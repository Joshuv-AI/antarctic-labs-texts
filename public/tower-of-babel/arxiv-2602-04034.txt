Abstract
. Clonoids are sets of finitary operations between two algebraic structures that are closed under composition with their term operations on both sides. We conjecture that, for finite modules 𝐀 \mathbf{A} and 𝐁 \mathbf{B} there are only finitely many clonoids from 𝐀 \mathbf{A} to 𝐁 \mathbf{B} if and only if 𝐀 \mathbf{A} , 𝐁 \mathbf{B} are of coprime order. We confirm this conjecture for a broad class of modules 𝐀 \mathbf{A} . In particular we show that, if 𝐀 \mathbf{A} is a finite k k -dimensional vector space, then every clonoid from 𝐀 \mathbf{A} to a coprime module 𝐁 \mathbf{B} is generated by its k k -ary functions (and arity k − 1 k-1 does not suffice). In order to prove this results, we investigate ‘uniform generation by ( 𝐀 , 𝐁 ) (\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}) -minors’, a general criterion, which we show to apply to several other existing classifications results. Based on our analysis, we further prove that the subpower membership problem of certain 2-nilpotent Mal’cev algebras is solvable in polynomial time.



1.
Introduction

A
clone
is a set of finitary operations on a given set that contains all projections and is closed under functional composition. Clones are a fundamental object in universal algebra, as they describe the set of term operations of any algebraic structure. Thus, starting with Post’s classification of clones on a two-element set
[
30
]
, there has been a rich history of structural results on finite algebras based on clone theory, see e.g.
[
32
]
. On the other hand, clones theory has also several applications in theoretical computer science. One of the most prominent examples is the study of polymorphism clones to determining the complexity of fixed template constraint satisfaction problems (see e.g. the survey
[
5
]
), which lead to Bulatov’s and Zhuk’s independent proofs of the CSP dichotomy theorem
[
6
]
,
[
34
,
35
]
.

In recent years, generalizations of clones such as
minions
and
clonoids
have received increasing attention. Minions are sets of finitary operations from a set
A
to a (possibly different) set
B
that are closed under permutation and identification of variables, as well as the addition of dummy variables. Research on minions gained much traction due to their application on (promise) constraint satisfaction, see e.g. the survey
[
4
]
. A
clonoid
from an algebra
\mathbf{A}
to an algebra
\mathbf{B}
is a minion that is closed under (pre-)composition with operations of
\mathbf{A}
and (post-)composition with the operations of
\mathbf{B}
. While the term ‘clonoid’ was first introduced by Aichinger and Mayr in
[
2
]
, the concept has appeared under various names earlier, e.g. in
[
9
]
.

Due to their weak structure, it is does not seem to be feasible to explicitly describe all minions between two given finite sets. A first hurdle is their sheer number: it was shown in
[
29
]
that there are already continuum many minions between two 2-element sets. However, depending on the expressiveness of the algebras
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, much more can be said about the clonoids between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
. Sparks showed in
[
31
]
, that there are only finitely many clonoids from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
if
\mathbf{B}
has a near-unanimity term, and at most countably many, if
\mathbf{B}
has an edge-term. However, also a rich source algebra
\mathbf{A}
can lead to finiteness results: As showed in
[
22
]
, every clonoids from an algebra
\mathbf{A}
with a discriminator term, to an algebra
\mathbf{B}
on the same set, gives rise to only finitely many clonoids.

We would also like to highlight a series of papers by Lehtonen and coauthors
[
21
,
22
,
10
,
23
,
24
]
, which culminated in
[
25
]
to a complete characterization of all two-element algebras
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
, for which there are at most countable clonoids, together with a complete classification of clonoids in all such cases.

In this paper, we are interested in classifying clonoids, for which
\mathbf{B}
is a module. Such clonoids often appear naturally when studying the term operations of Mal’cev algebras with a central congruence; this connection was recently made more explicit by Peter Mayr’s definition of the
difference clonoid
, see e.g.
[
19
]
. In particular, 2-nilpotent algebras give rise to clonoids between modules
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.

As an example of an application let us let us mention the classification of extensions of square-free groups in
[
1
]
and later
[
13
]
, which heavily relied on the classification of certain clonoids.

There is already a series of classification results in the literature. The clonoids between groups of prime order
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
were completely classified by Kreinecker
[
20
]
(in the case that
p=q
) and the first author, for
p\neq q

[
11
]
. Interestingly, in the latter case, there are only finitely many clonoids that are all generated by their unary functions (while for
p=q
one obtains infinitely many).

In subsequent generalizations of these results, it was also always observed that there are only finitely many clonoids between certain modules
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
of coprime order - the most recent, and most general such finiteness result is by Mayr and Wynne
[
26
]
, and applies to all cases where
\mathbf{A}
has a distributive lattice of submodules. This leads us to the following Conjecture:

Conjecture 1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
be finite modules. Then, there are only finitely many clonoids from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
, if and only if
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are of coprime order.

We remark that the “only if direction” follows from
[
26
, Theorem
1.3
]
, where the authors proved that for modules with a common divisor, one can always construct an infinite ascending chain of clonoids.

The first main contribution of our paper is to confirm Conjecture
1
if
\mathbf{A}
is a finite vector space. In the case where
\mathbf{B}
is coprime to the vector space
\mathbf{A}
, we are moreover able to give an explicit description of the lattice of all clonoids (Theorem
55
). In particular, this answers
[
26
, Question 1.2]
, which asked for a classification of clonoids, if
\mathbf{A}
is the group
({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})^{2}
, for some prime
p
. By combining our result with
[
26
]
, we are further able to confirm Conjecture
1
for all modules
\mathbf{A}={\mathbf{F}}_{1}^{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbf{F}}_{n}^{k_{n}}\times\mathbf{D}
as
{\mathbf{F}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathbf{F}}_{n}\times\mathbf{R}
-module, where
\mathbf{D}
is a distributive
\mathbf{R}
-module, and all
{\mathbf{F}}_{i}
are fields.

Our classification is based on proving that, in the case of a vector space
\mathbf{A}={\mathbf{F}}^{k}
and coprime
\mathbf{B}
, the set of all operations from
A
to
B
is
uniformly generated
by
k
-ary minors. While this technique was already hinted at in
[
26
]
, we are going to discuss it in great detail in Section
3
, and argue that many of the existing clonoid classifications follow the same pattern. We furthermore show that the arity
k
is optimal, i.e., not every clonoid from
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
to
\mathbf{B}
is generated by their
k-1
-ary functions. This coincides with a lower bound on the arity of generators of the
full
clonoid between modules that we derive in Section
6
, and was already pointed out in the Bachelor thesis by Jan Vaněček
[
33
]
.

Last, let us mention that, clonoids between affine algebras were also (sometimes implicitly) used to discuss some computational problems of Mal’cev algebras. The fact that some clonoids between coprime modules are generated by unary functions, which allows for a representation of their elements by nice normal forms, was used in
[
17
]
to prove that checking polynomial identities in a given 2-nilpotent algebra can always be done in polynomial time. In
[
28
]
and
[
18
]
such normal forms were similarly used to find finite equational basis for some 2-nilpotent loops.

While we are not going to discuss such syntactic aspects of clonoids, we will apply our results to the
subpower membership problem
of certain algebras. The subpower membership problem (SMP(
\mathbf{A}
)), for a fixed finite algebra
\mathbf{A}
, is the computational problem whether a given partial operation
f\colon A^{n}\to A
can be extended to a term operation of
\mathbf{A}
. In
[
16
]
it was asked, whether the subpower membership problem is always polynomial time solvable for algebras with few subpowers. This question, however remains unsolved even in the Mal’cev case. The easiest examples that are not covered by existing tractability results
[
27
,
7
]
, are 2-nilpotent algebras, for which it was shown in
[
19
]
that the problem is polynomial time equivalent to a similar ‘interpolation problem’ for their clonoids. In Section
5
we are going to use our results, to prove that the subpower membership problem of a big class of 2-nilpotent algebras is in P.

Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the necessary concepts from universal algebra and fix some notation. In Section

we develop a theory of ‘uniform generation’ and ‘uniform representation’ by minors, a concept introduced in

. In particular we provide a purely combinatorial criterion for all

-clonoids to be generated by their

-ary functions (Theorem

Section

Background

In this section, we introduce some necessary notation, key definitions, and foundational results from the literature concerning clone theory, clonoids, and module theory that will serve as the basis for our analysis. For further background in universal algebra we refer the reader to
[
8
]
.

Concerning the basic notation, tuples consisting of elements are represented in boldface, with their individual components denoted as, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
. Furthermore, we write
[n]
to indicate the set

and

2.1.
Clones and clonoids

Definition 2
.

For two sets
A,B
, we define
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}=\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}B^{A^{n}}
, that is,
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is the set of all finitary operations from
A
to
B
. Moreover, we are going to write
\mathcal{O}_{A}=\mathcal{O}_{A,A}
. For a set of operations
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
we denote its
n
-ary part by
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}
, i.e.
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}=\mathcal{C}\cap B^{A^{n}}
.

Definition 3
.

Let
A
be a set and
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}
.
Then
\mathcal{A}
is a
clone
on
A

if it contains all projections and
is closed under composition, i.e.

•

\pi_{i}^{n}\in\mathcal{A}
, for every
1\leq i\leq n
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\pi_{i}^{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=x_{i}

•

f\in\mathcal{A}^{(n)},g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{(k)}\Rightarrow f\circ(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})\in\mathcal{A}^{(k)}
for all
k,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Definition 4
.

An
algebraic structure
or
algebra

\mathbf{A}=(A,(f_{i})_{i\in I})
is a pair consisting of a set
A
(the
universe
of
\mathbf{A}
), and a family of finitary functions
f_{i}\colon A^{k_{i}}\to A

(the
basic operations
of
\mathbf{A}
). The
term clone of
\mathbf{A}
is the smallest clone containing all basic operations of
\mathbf{A}
. We are going to denote it by
\Clo(\mathbf{A})
. More generally, for a set of operations
F
on
A
we are also going to write
\Clo(F)
for the smallest clone containing
F
. We say two algebras
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
are
term-equivalent
if
\Clo(\mathbf{A})=\Clo(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
.

If not specified otherwise, we are going to use the same letter in different typeset for a clone/algebra and its domain, e.g.,
\mathcal{B}
is a clone on set
B
,
\mathbf{B}
is an algebra with universe
B
. An operation
m\colon A^{3}\to A
is called a
Mal’cev operation
, if it satisfies the identities
m(y,x,x)\approx m(x,x,y)\approx y
. An operation
v\colon A^{k}\to A
for
k\geq 3
is called a
near unanimity operation
, if it satisfies
v(y,x\ldots,x)\approx v(x,y,x,\ldots,x)\approx\ldots\approx v(x,x,\ldots,x,y)\approx x
.

We next introduce the main notion of this paper:

Definition 5

(Clonoid)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a clone on a set
A
, and
\mathcal{B}
be a clone on a set
B
. We then say that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is a
clonoid from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
(or
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid
, for short) if, for all
n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}

•

f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{(k)},g\in\mathcal{B}^{(n)}\Rightarrow g\circ(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in\mathcal{C}^{(k)}
,

•

f\in\mathcal{C}^{(k)},g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{(n)}\Rightarrow f\circ(g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})\in\mathcal{C}^{(n)}
.

If
\mathcal{A}=\Clo(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathcal{B}=\Clo(\mathbf{B})
for algebras
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
then we say that
\mathcal{C}
is a clonoid from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
or, equivalently, that
\mathcal{C}
is an
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-clonoid.

We remark that in the literature also alternative terminology is used; for example
[
9
]
describes
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoids as
function classes that are as left stable under
\mathcal{B}
and right stable under
\mathcal{A}
. The term

was coined by Aichinger and Mayr in
[
2
]
, and initially only applied to the case, in which

is the projection clone.

Every
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid is also a
minor-closed set
, or
minion
from
A
to
B
(in the sense of
[
29
]
, respectively
[
4
]
), i.e., it is closed under composition with projections from the inside. Conversely, minions can be seen as clonoids from the clone of projections on
A
to the clone of projections on
B
.

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
be clones on sets
A
and
B
. For a set of operations
F\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
from
A
to
B
, we define
\langle F\rangle_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
to be the
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid generated by
F
, i.e., the smallest
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid containing
F
.
We say that a clonoid
\mathcal{C}
from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
is

if there is a finite

with
\mathcal{C}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
.

It is not hard to see that, for fixed
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
, the clonoids from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
form a lattice with join and meet operations
\mathcal{C}\land\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{C}\lor\mathcal{D}=\langle\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{D}\rangle_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
.

We next introduce polymorphism minions, in orderto give a relational description of clonoids:

Definition 7
.

A
relational structure
is a pair
\mathbb{A}=(A,(R_{i}^{\mathbb{A}})_{i\in I})
consisting of a set
A
and a family of relations
R^{\mathbb{A}}_{i}\subseteq A^{k_{i}}
of finite arity
k_{i}\geq 1
. The family of relational symbols and their arity
((R_{i},k_{i}))_{i\in I}
is called the language of
\mathbb{A}
.

Let
\mathbb{A}=(A,(R_{i}^{\mathbb{A}})_{i\in I})
and
\mathbb{B}=(B,(R_{i}^{\mathbb{B}})_{i\in I})
be relational structures in the same language. A
homomorphism
for
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
is a map
h\colon A\to B
that preserves all relations, i.e.
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k_{i}})\in R_{i}^{\mathbb{A}}\Rightarrow h(\mathbf{a})=(h(a_{1}),\ldots,h(a_{k_{i}}))\in R_{i}^{\mathbb{B}}
, for all
i\in I
. An
(
n
-ary) polymorphism
from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
is a map
f\colon A^{n}\to B
, such that for each relation symbol
R_{i}
, and for all tuples
\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\in R_{i}^{\mathbb{A}}
, also
f(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n})\in R_{i}^{\mathbb{B}}
; here,
f
is evaluated coordinate-wise. This is equivalent to
f
being a homomorphism from the direct power
\mathbb{A}^{n}
to
\mathbb{B}
.

We denote the set of all polymorphisms from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
by
\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
. We further write
\Pol(\mathbb{A})=\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{A})
. It is well-known that
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
forms a clone, which we call the
polymorphism clone
of
\mathbb{A}
. It is also easy to see that
\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
is a clonoid from
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
to
\Pol(\mathbb{B})
. In the literature
\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
is commonly referred to as
polymorphism minion
from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
.

In
[
29
]
, Pippenger introduced a Galois-connection between the minions between two finite sets
A
and
B
, and pairs of relational structures on the other hand. His results in particular imply that every minion between
A
and
B
is equal to a polymorphism minion
\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
.

1

1

In fact, slightly more generally,
[
29
, Section 3]
, also already gave a relational description the clonoids between essentially unary clones

to the projection clone on

.
In
[

, Couceiro and Foldes generalized Pippenger’s Galois connection twofold - both to consider also sets of arbitrary cardinality, and relations that are invariant under given clones. For our paper, mainly the following consequence of

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be clones on two finite sets

and

. Then
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is a clonoid from
\mathcal{A}
to

2.2.
Modules and vector spaces

Next, we discuss some notation for modules and vector spaces. Rings in this paper are always assumed to be unital, i.e. every ring
\mathbf{R}=(R,+,-,0,\cdot,1)
contains a multiplicative identity
1
. All modules in this paper are going to be
left
modules. More precisely, an
\mathbf{R}
-module is a one-sorted algebra
\mathbf{A}=(A,+,0,-,(r)_{r\in\mathbf{R}})
, where
(A,+,0,-)
is the underlying abelian group, and every ring element is identified with the unary function
r(x)=rx
.

Note that for an
\mathbf{R}
-module
\mathbf{A}
,
\Clo(\mathbf{A})
consists exactly of the functions given by linear combinations over
R
, i.e.,
t(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}x_{i}
with
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\in R
. Using matrix multiplication, we will also write
t(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{r}^{T}\mathbf{x}
for short, where
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})^{T}\in R^{n}
, and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{T}\in A^{n}
. More generally, note that a function
t\colon A^{n}\to A^{m}
is in
t\in(\Clo(\mathbf{A})^{(n)})^{m}
if and only if
t(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}
, where
A\in R^{m\times n}
.

If
I=\{r\in R\mid\forall a\in A\colon r(a)=0\}
denotes annihilator of an
\mathbf{R}
-module
\mathbf{A}
and
R^{\prime}=R/I
, then
\mathbf{A}
is term equivalent to the module
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=(A,+,0,-,(r)_{r\in\mathbf{R}^{\prime}})
, with
(r+I)(a)=r(a)
. Thus, every module is term equivalent to a faithful module.

If
\mathbf{F}
is a field, then, following the convention for modules,
\mathbf{A}=(F^{k},+,0,-,(r)_{r\in F})
is the
k
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbf{F}
. We are going to slightly abuse notation, and also use the notation
\mathbf{A}={\mathbf{F}}^{k}
(although
\mathbf{A}
is not a direct power of
{\mathbf{F}}
in the universal-algebraic sense). We consider the elements of
\mathbf{A}
as
k
-dimensional
row
vectors
\mathbf{x}^{T}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
with
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in F
. We further identify tuples
(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{T})\in(A)^{n}
with the matrix
X\in\mathbf{F}^{n\times k}
, whose
i
-th row is equal to
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}
. We choose this notational convention, as it is consistent with what we already established for modules. In particular, every term operation
t\in\Clo(\mathbf{A})^{(n)}
, given by a linear combination
t(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{T})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}
can be compactly written as
t(X)=\mathbf{a}^{T}X
. Moreover, an operation
t\colon A^{n}\to A^{m}
is in
t\in(\Clo(\mathbf{A})^{(n)})^{m}
if and only if
t(X)=AX
for a matrix
A\in F^{m\times n}
.

We next recall some standard definitions from linear algebra:

Definition 9
.

For a ring
\mathbf{R}
and a matrix
X\in R^{k\times l}
, the rank
\rk(X)
of
A
is defines as the minimal
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
X=AB
for some
A\in R^{k\times n}
,
U\in R^{n\times l}
. If
n=\rk(X)
, we are going to refer to any decomposition
X=AB
with
A\in R^{k\times n}
,
U\in R^{n\times l}
as
rank factorization
of
X
.

In the case of matrices over a field,
\rk(X)
is equal to all the usual definition of
\rk(X)
as column or row rank (see e.g.
[
3
, Section 5.4]
). Moreover, the following well-known facts hold:

Lemma 10
.

Let
{\mathbf{F}}
be a field, let
k,l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
X\in F^{k\times l}
and let
n
be the rank of
X
. If
X=AU=A^{\prime}U^{\prime}
are two distinct rank factorizations of
X
then there exists an invertible matrix

T\in F^{n\times n}
,
with
A^{\prime}=AT
and
U^{\prime}=T^{-1}U
.

For a matrix
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k\times l}
over a field
{\mathbf{F}}
we let
C(X)=\{X\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{a}\in F^{l}\}
denote the
columnspace of
X
.

For any linear subspace
V\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
, we are going to write
V^{\bot}=\{\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k}\mid\forall\mathbf{x}\in V:\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{x}=0\}
for the space of vectors ‘orthogonal’ to
V
. Note that
(V^{\bot})^{\bot}=V
,
\dim(V)+\dim(V^{\bot})=k
and
(V\cap W)^{\bot}=V^{\bot}+W^{\bot}
hold. However (unlike in an actual inner product space)
V
and
V^{\bot}
need not be complementary.

3.
Uniformly generated functions

In this section, we discuss sets of operations that are
uniformly generated by their
n
-ary minors
, a notion that was first introduced in
[

Definition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a clone on a set
A
. For
k,l,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
R_{n}^{k,l}(\mathcal{A})
be the set of all the operations
r\in(\mathcal{A}^{(k)})^{l}
such that there exist
v\in(\mathcal{A}^{(n)})^{l}
and
w\in(\mathcal{A}^{(k)})^{n}
such that

r(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}r_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
r_{l}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}(w_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,w_{n}(\mathbf{x}))\\
\vdots\\
v_{l}(w_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,w_{n}(\mathbf{x}))\end{bmatrix}=v\circ w(\mathbf{x}).

We further define
R_{n}(\mathcal{A})=\bigcup_{l,k}R_{n}^{k,l}(\mathcal{A})
. For an algebra
\mathbf{A}
, we will also use the notation
R^{k,l}_{n}(\mathbf{A})=R^{k,l}_{n}(\Clo(\mathbf{A}))
and
R_{n}(\mathbf{A})=R_{n}(\Clo(\mathbf{A}))
.

In words,
R^{k,l}_{n}(\mathcal{A})
consists of all operations in
(\mathcal{A}^{(k)})^{l}
that factor through
\mathcal{A}^{(n)}
. As it was already pointed out in
[
26
]
, it follows straightforwardly from the definition that, for an
\mathbf{R}
-module
\mathbf{A}
:

R_{n}^{k,l}(\mathbf{A})=\{f\colon A^{k}\to A^{l}\mid\exists M\in R^{l\times k}:\rk(M)\leq n,f(\mathbf{x})=M\mathbf{x}\}.

So
R_{n}(\mathbf{A})
corresponds to the matrices over
\mathbf{R}
of rank at most
n
. Thus, the following lemma may be considered as a generalization of the fact that
\rk(MN)\leq\min(\rk(M),\rk(N))
for matrices
M
,
N
over a given ring:

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a clone. If
s\in R_{n}^{j,k}(\mathcal{A})
,
r\in R_{m}^{k,l}(\mathcal{A})
then
r\circ s\in R_{\min(n,m)}^{j,l}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

By definition, there are
u\in(\mathcal{A}^{(k)})^{m},t\in(\mathcal{A}^{(m)})^{l}
such that
r=t\circ u
, and there are
w\in(\mathcal{A}^{(j)})^{n},v\in(\mathcal{A}^{(n)})^{k}
such that
s=v\circ w
. Thus
r\circ s=t\circ(u\circ v\circ w)
. Since
t\in(\mathcal{A}^{(m)})^{l}
, and
(u\circ v\circ w)\in(\mathcal{A}^{(j)})^{m}
, we get that
r\circ s\in R_{m}^{j,l}(\mathcal{A})
. On the other hand
r\circ s=(t\circ u\circ v)\circ w
similarly implies that
r\circ s\in R_{n}^{j,l}(\mathcal{A})
. Thus
r\circ s\in R_{n}^{j,l}(\mathcal{A})\cap R_{m}^{j,l}(\mathcal{A})=R_{\min(n,m)}^{j,l}(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

are helpful when trying to characterize the clonoids generated by their

Lemma 13

Let

(1)

In particular,

and

such that

(2)

f=s\circ(f\circ r_{1},\ldots,f\circ r_{m}).

Proof.

Note that

For the second statement, note that

Theorem 18
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
be clones,

\mathcal{A}_{const}=\Clo(\mathcal{A}\cup\{a\}_{a\in A})
be the clone generated by
\mathcal{A}
and all constant operations over its set. Let
\mathcal{C}
a clonoid from
\mathcal{A}_{const}

to
\mathcal{B}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{C}^{(n+1)}
is
(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-UG

(2)

\mathcal{C}^{(k)}
is
(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-UG for some
k>n

(3)

\mathcal{C}
is
(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-UG

(4)

\forall k,l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, every partial operation

that is
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-UR is
(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-UR.

We would like to stress that, although we assume that
\mathcal{C}
in closed under composition with constant operations from

Proof of Theorem

The implications (
4

(

)

(
2
)

(

To prove (

)

(

, and let us assume that
\mathcal{C}
is uniformly generated by
n

(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-minors. Lemma

-ary
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-minors. By Lemma

(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

It only remains to prove the implication (

We first prove that any operation

-UR. Note that, by Lemma

be represented by

\mathcal{A}_{1}

)-minors.

Let

Since

\displaystyle g_{2}(f(\mathbf{x}_{1},s_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{2})),\dots,f(\mathbf{x}_{1},s_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{2})).

Combining these two equations, we obtain

(8)

\begin{split}f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=g_{1}(&g_{2}(f(r_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),s_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{2})),\ldots,f(r_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),s_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))),\\
&g_{2}(f(r_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),s_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{2})),\ldots,f(r_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),s_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))),\ldots,\\
&g_{2}(f(r_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),s_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{2})),\ldots,f(r_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),s_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{2})))),\end{split}

for all
f\colon A^{k}\to B
and
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})\in A_{1}^{k}\times A_{2}^{k}
. Since
\mathcal{A}^{(k)}=\mathcal{A}_{1}^{(k)}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}^{(k)}
, for every pair
r_{i}\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
,
s_{j}\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
there is a
t_{ij}\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathcal{A})
with
t_{i,j}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=(r_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),s_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))
. Thus (
8
) witnesses that
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is
(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-UG, which finishes the proof.
∎

We further would like to remark that, if
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
are clones on finite sets, and
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is
n
-UG, then every
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid can be described as the polymorphism minion of relational structures of maximal arity
\leq|A|^{n}
. In fact, we do not require
uniform
generation for this. A proof can be derived from the proof of Theorem
8
in
[
9
]
. We include it for completeness:

Lemma 22
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be clones on finite sets
A
and
B
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, let us define the relational structures
\mathbb{A}=(A,\mathcal{A}^{(n)})
, and, for every clonoid
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}}=(B,\mathcal{C}^{(n)})
(here, we regard
\mathcal{A}^{(n)}\subseteq A^{A^{n}}
, and
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}\subseteq B^{A^{n}}
as
A^{n}
-ary relation on
A
, respectively
B
). Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\forall(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}
:
\mathcal{C}=\langle\mathcal{C}^{(n)}\rangle_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
,

(2)

\forall(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}
:
\mathcal{C}=\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}})
.

Proof.

First, observe that for every
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}
the inclusions
\langle\mathcal{C}^{(n)}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{C}\subseteq\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}})
hold. Furthermore,
\mathbb{A}
is clearly invariant under
\mathcal{A}
; since
\mathcal{C}
is stable under composition with
\mathcal{B}
, also
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}}
is invariant under
\mathcal{B}
. Thus by Theorem
8
,
\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}})
is a
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid.

We next show that
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}=\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}})^{(n)}
. By the above, we only need to show the inclusion
\supseteq
. So let
f\in\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}})^{(n)}
. Note that
\pi_{1}^{n},\ldots,\pi_{n}^{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{(n)}
, where
\pi_{i}^{n}
denotes the
n
-ary projection to the
i
-th coordinate on
A
. Since
f\in\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}})^{(n)}
, we get
f(\pi_{1}^{n},\ldots,\pi_{n}^{n})\in\mathcal{C}^{(n)}
. In other words
f\in\mathcal{C}^{(n)}
, which
which is what we wanted to prove.

In order to prove the statement of the lemma, let us first assume that (1) holds. This implies in particular that every
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid is completely determined by its
n
-ary part. Hence
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}=\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}})^{(n)}
implies that
\mathcal{C}=\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}})
for every
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}
. Thus (2) holds.

Conversely assume that (2) holds. By its definition
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{B}_{\langle\mathcal{C}^{(n)}\rangle_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}}
holds, for every
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}
. Now (2) implies that
\mathcal{C}=\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}})=\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}_{\langle\mathcal{C}^{(n)}\rangle_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}})=\langle\mathcal{C}^{(n)}\rangle_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
, for every
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}
. Hence (1) holds.
∎

As a straightforward corollary of Lemma
22
and Corollary
20
, we get:

Corollary 23
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be clones on finite sets
A
and
B
, and assume
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(n+1)}
is
(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-UG. Then, every
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}
is the polymorphism minion of relational structures of maximal arity
\leq|A|^{n}
.

3.2.
Uniform generation for clonoids between affine clones

In this section, we are going to review some results from the literature on clonoids between affine clones under the light of uniform generation.

First, we would like to point out that all of the known classifications of clonoids between coprime modules are (implicitly) based on the proof that
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary minors.

Arguably the first such result was proven in
[
11
]
, where the first author classified all the clonoids between 1-dimensional vector spaces over coprime, finite fields. Although not explicitly stated in
[
11
]
, one can easily derive the following statement from it:

Lemma 24
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a one-dimensional vector space over a finite field
{\mathbf{F}}
, and let
\mathbf{B}
be a
\mathbf{S}
-module, such that
\chars({\mathbf{F}})
is invertible in
\mathbf{S}
. Then
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is uniformly generated by unary
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-minors.

Proof.

A proof of this lemma can be extracted from the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 4.3.]
. Although
[
11
, Theorem 4.3.]
is only stated for the case in which
\mathbf{B}
is also a one-dimensional vector space, it can easily be seen that the only required assumption on the module
\mathbf{B}
is that the
|F|
, or equivalently
\chars({\mathbf{F}})
, is invertible in
\mathbf{S}
.
∎

Lemma
24
together with Proposition
21
implies that the statement of Lemma
24
further holds, if
\mathbf{A}
is a direct product of finite fields, considered as a regular module. This was in part already observed in
[
12
]
. Using this observation, we can obtain the following slight generalization of
[
26
, Theorem 1.5.]
:

Theorem 25
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite, faithful
\mathbf{R}
-module over a commutative ring
\mathbf{R}
, and let
\mathbf{B}
be a faithful
\mathbf{S}
-module. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is uniformly generated by unary
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-minors.

(2)

Every
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-clonoid is generated by unary functions.

(3)

\chars(R)
is invertible in
\mathbf{S}
,
\mathbf{R}
is a direct product of finite fields and
\mathbf{A}
is isomorphic to the regular
R
-module.

Proof.

The implication (1)
\to
(2) holds by Corollary
20
. The implication (3)
\to
(1) follows from Lemma
24
and Proposition
21
. The implication (2)
\to
(3) was shown in
[
26
, Theorem 1.5.]
for the case that
\mathbf{B}
is finite. However, it is not hard to see that the exact same proof also works for general
\mathbf{B}
.
∎

More generally, in
[
26
]
the following was shown:

Theorem 26
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite distributive
\mathbf{R}
-module (i.e. its lattice of submodules is distributive), and let
\mathbf{B}
be an
\mathbf{S}
-module, such that
\chars(R)
is invertible in
\mathbf{S}
. Then
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-minors, where
n
is the nilpotence degree of the Jacobson radical of
\mathbf{R}
.

Proof.

This is proved in
[
26
, Theorem 1.4.]
, although the theorem does not explicitly mention
uniform
generation. A careful reading of
[
26
]
also shows, that, due to Proposition
21
, it is enough to prove the statement for uni-serial modules
\mathbf{A}
.
∎

Results for modules
\mathbf{A}
can be straightforwardly lifted to algebras that are polynomially equivalent to
\mathbf{A}
, i.e., algebras that, together with all constant operations, generate the same clone as
\mathbf{A}
. For some special cases, this was already observed in
[
26
, Theorem 4.1.]
.

Lemma 27
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=(A,+,0,-,(r)_{r\in\mathbf{R}})
be an
\mathbf{R}
-module, and let
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
polynomially equivalent to
\mathbf{A}
and let
\mathbf{B}
be an arbitrary algebra.

(1)

If
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-minors, then it is also uniformly generated by
(n+1)
-ary
(\mathbf{A}^{\prime},\mathbf{B})
-minors.

(2)

If
\mathcal{O}_{B,A}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{A})
-minors then it is also uniformly generated by
n
-ary
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
-minors.

Proof.

It is a folklore result in commutator theory that
\Clo(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
must contain the ternary operation
x-y+z
(the unique Mal’cev term in
\Clo(\mathbf{A})
) and all binary operations of the form
rx+(1-r)y
for
r\in\mathbf{R}
. For short, let us write
x+^{y}z=x-y+z
and
r^{y}(x)=rx+(1-r)y
. If we substitute
y
by some constant
a\in A
we obtain the algebra
\mathbf{A}^{a}=(A,+^{a},a,-^{a},(r^{a})_{r\in\mathbf{R}})
that is isomorphic to
\mathbf{A}
via the isomorphism
x\mapsto x-a
.

To every
l
-ary term operation
t(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l})=\sum_{i=1}^{l}r_{i}x_{i}\in\Clo(\mathbf{A})
we can similarly associate the
l+1
-ary operation
t^{y}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l},y)=r_{1}^{y}x_{1}+^{y}r_{2}^{y}x_{2}+^{y}\ldots+^{y}r_{l}^{y}x_{l}\in\Clo(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
, and, for fixed
a\in A
, the
l
-ary operation
t^{a}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l})=r_{1}^{a}x_{1}+^{a}r_{2}^{a}x_{2}+^{a}\ldots+^{a}r_{l}^{a}x_{l}\in\Clo(\mathbf{A}^{a})
. In particular, note that
r\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A})
implies that
r^{a}\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A}^{a})
and
r^{y}\in R_{n+1}^{k+1,k}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
.

We first show (
1
). Let
k>n+1
. By assumption, there exist
g\in\Clo(\mathbf{B})^{(l)}
and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{l}\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A})
such that for all
f\colon A^{k}\to B
:
f=g\circ(f\circ r_{1},\ldots,f\circ r_{l})
. Since
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{a}
are isomorphic for every
a\in A
, also
f=g\circ(f\circ r_{1}^{a},\ldots,f\circ r_{l}^{a})
holds for all
f\colon A^{k}\to B
and every
a\in A
. Alternatively, we can also write this fact as the equation
f(\mathbf{x})=g\circ(f(r_{1}^{y}(\mathbf{x})),\ldots,f(r_{l}^{y}(\mathbf{x}))
for all
f\colon A^{k}\to B
, where we regard
y
as a new variable. If we substitute
y
by
x_{k}
(the last coordinate of
\mathbf{x}
), we obtain the equation

f(\mathbf{x})=g\circ(f(r_{1}^{x_{k}}(\mathbf{x})),\ldots,f(r_{l}^{x_{k}}(\mathbf{x}))),

for all
f\colon A^{k}\to B
and
\mathbf{x}\in A^{k}
. It is easy to see that
r_{i}^{y}(\mathbf{x})\in R_{n+1}^{k+1,k}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
implies that
r_{i}^{x_{k}}(\mathbf{x})\in R_{n+1}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
. This finishes the proof.

To see (
2
), let
k>n
and let
g\in\Clo(\mathbf{A})
,
r_{1},\ldots,r_{l}\in R_{n}(\mathbf{B})
such that the formula
f=g\circ(f\circ r_{1},\ldots,f\circ r_{l})
witnesses that
\mathcal{O}_{B,A}^{(k)}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{A})
-minors. Then, again by isomorphism,
f=g^{a}\circ(f\circ r_{1},\ldots,f\circ r_{l})
holds for every
a\in A
. This in turn implies
f(\mathbf{x})=g^{y}(f(r_{1}(\mathbf{x})),\ldots,f(r_{l}(\mathbf{x})))
, for any choice of
y
. If we substitute
y
by any expression
f(q(\mathbf{x}))
, such that
q\in R_{n}(\mathbf{A})
(e.g.
y=f(x_{1},x_{1},\ldots,x_{1})
), we obtain a formula that witnesses that
\mathcal{O}_{B,A}^{(k)}
is uniform generation by
n
-ary
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
-minors.
∎

3.3.
Examples beyond affine clones

Also Spark’s finiteness result in the case in which the target
\mathcal{B}
has a near unanimity operation
[
31
]
can be derived using the criterion in Corollary
20
. We include a proof of this fact as the proof in
[
31
]
uses a different (relational) argument.

Lemma 28
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be clones on finite sets
A
,
B
such that
\mathcal{B}
contains a near unanimity operation of arity
l\geq 3
. Then
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is uniformly generated by
|A|^{l}
-ary
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-minors

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
\mathcal{A}
is the projection clone on
A
. Let
m=|A|
and
n=|A|^{l}
. By Theorem
18
, it is enough to prove that for some
k>n
,
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary minors.

For integers
a,b\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a
b
-ary tuple
\mathbf{x}
and a map
\alpha\colon[a]\to[b]
, let
\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}
denote the
a
-ary tuple defined by
\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}=(x_{\alpha(1)},x_{\alpha(2)},\ldots,x_{\alpha(a)})
. Note that
r\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathcal{A})
, if there is an
\alpha\colon[k]\to[k]
whose image has at most
n
elements, such that
r(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}
.

We claim that for every set of functions
S\subseteq[m]^{[k]}
there is an operation
I_{S}\colon\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}
that is uniformly representable by
n
-ary
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-minors, such that
I_{S}(f)(\mathbf{a}^{\alpha})=f(\mathbf{a}^{\alpha})
, for all
\alpha\in S
and
\mathbf{a}\in A^{k}
. We prove this claim by induction on
|S|
.

First, let us assume that
|S|\leq l
. Let
\beta\colon[k]\to[k]
be a map that maps every equivalence class of
\bigcap_{\alpha\in S}\ker(\alpha)
to the same representative, e.g.,
\beta(i)=\min(\{i^{\prime}\in[k]\mid\forall\alpha\in S\colon\alpha(i^{\prime})=\alpha(i)\})
. Since
|S|\leq l
, and
\ker(\alpha)
has at most
|A|
-many equivalence classes for every
\alpha\in S
, we get that
\beta
attains at most
\leq|A|^{l}=n
many values. Then, clearly
I_{S}(f)(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}^{\beta})
satisfies our claim.

For an induction step, let
|S|>l
. Let us fix
l
distinct elements
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{l}\in S
, and let
S_{i}=S\setminus\{\alpha_{i}\}
. By induction assumption, for every set
S_{i}
there exist operations
I_{S_{i}}
satisfying the claim.

We define
I_{S}=v\circ(I_{S_{1}},\ldots,I_{S_{l}})
, where
v
is the
l
-ary near unanimity operation of
\mathcal{B}
. By Lemma
17
,
I_{S}
is uniformly representable by
n
-ary
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-minors. For a map
\alpha\in S
and a tuple
\mathbf{a}\in A^{m}
, note that the equation
I_{S_{i}}(f)(\mathbf{a}^{\alpha})=f(\mathbf{a}^{\alpha})
holds for all but at most one index
i
. Since
v
is a near unanimity operation, we get
I_{S}(f)(\mathbf{a}^{\alpha})=f(\mathbf{a}^{\alpha})
. This finishes the proof of our claim.

Last, observe that every tuple
\mathbf{a}\in A^{k}
is of the form
\mathbf{b}^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in[m]^{[k]}
,
\mathbf{b}\in A^{m}
: to verify this, simply take some enumeration
A=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m}\}
of
A
, set
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m})
, and
\alpha(j)=i
if
a_{j}=b_{i}
. Hence, for
S=[m]^{[k]}
we conclude that
I_{S}(f)=f
for all
f\in\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}
. Since
I_{S}
had a uniform representation by
n
-ary minor, we are done.
∎

If the uniform generation requires the target clone
\mathcal{B}
to contain functions that are not essentially unary (i.e., that depend on more than one argument).

Lemma 29
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be clones on finite sets
A
,
B
with at least 2 elements and assume that
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-minors. Then
\mathcal{B}\neq\Clo(\mathcal{B}^{(1)})
.

Proof.

For contradiction, let assume that
\mathcal{B}=\Clo(\mathcal{B}^{(1)})
and
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
-minors. Thus, for every
k>n
there are
s\in\mathcal{B}^{(1)}
and
r\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathcal{A})
, such that
f=s\circ f\circ r
for all
f\colon A^{k}\to B
. For every pair
\mathbf{a}_{1}\neq\mathbf{a}_{2}
in
A^{k}
we can find a function
f
with
s(f(r(\mathbf{a}_{1})))=f(\mathbf{a}_{1})\neq f(\mathbf{a}_{2})=s(f(r(\mathbf{a}_{2})))
. It follows that
r(\mathbf{a}_{1})\neq r(\mathbf{a}_{2})
for all
\mathbf{a}_{1}\neq\mathbf{a}_{2}
, i.e.,
r
is injective. However, for
k>n
, no element of
R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathcal{A})
is injective - contradiction.
∎

Lehtonen and Szendrei showed in
[
22
]
that every clonoid from the discriminator clone to the projection clone on the same finite set is finitely generated. By Lemma
29
this finiteness result cannot be proven using the criterion in Corollary
20
.

3.4.
Interpolation by uniformly representable operations

A helpful step in proving that
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary minors may be to first construct
n
-UR operation
I
, such that
I(f)(\mathbf{x})
agrees with
f(\mathbf{x})
for all
f
and all tuples
\mathbf{x}\in K
from some ‘nice’ subset
K\subseteq A^{k}
.

An instance of this can be found in
[
26
, Lemma 2.14 (3)]
, where it is shown that for modules
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
, uniform generation from submodules of
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
can be lifted to such an ‘interpolating’ operation
I
that works on the union of said submodules. In the following, we generalize this result to the setting that only requires the target algebra to have a Mal’cev term:

Lemma 30
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
be algebras, such that
\mathbf{B}
has a Mal’cev term. Let
\mathbf{C}_{1},\mathbf{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{C}_{l}\leq\mathbf{A}
be subalgebras such that for every
i\in[l]
,
\mathcal{O}_{C_{i},B}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary minors. Then, there exists a
(n,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-UR operation
I
on
\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(n+1)}
with
I(f)(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})
for
\mathbf{x}\in\bigcup_{i\in[l]}C_{i}^{n+1}
.

Proof.

Let
m
be the Mal’cev term of
\mathbf{B}
, and let
k=n+1
. Our assumption implies, that for every
i\in[l]
there are
(n,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-UR operations
I_{i}\colon\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}\to\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}
such that
I_{i}(f)(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})
if
\mathbf{x}\in C_{i}^{k}
.

Let us inductively define the operations
J_{1}(f)=I_{1}(f)
and

J_{i+1}(f)=m(J_{i}(f)(\mathbf{x}),I_{i+1}(J_{i}(f))(\mathbf{x}),I_{i+1}(f)(\mathbf{x}))

for every
i<l
. We claim that, for every
i\in[l]
:
J_{i}(f)(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})
for all tuples
\mathbf{x}\in C_{1}^{k}\cup C_{2}^{k}\cup\cdots\cup C_{i}^{k}
. We prove this claim by induction on
i
.

For
i=1
the statement clearly holds. So let us consider an induction step
i\to i+1
. First, let
\mathbf{x}\in C_{j}^{k}
, for
j\leq i
. By induction assumption, we know that
J_{i}(f)(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})
. Furthermore, note that, for every
r\in R_{n}(\mathbf{A})
, we get
r(\mathbf{x})\in C_{j}^{k}
, since
C_{j}
is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
. Let
I_{i+1}(f)(\mathbf{x})=s(f(r_{1}(\mathbf{x})),\ldots,f(r_{l}(\mathbf{x})))
be a representation of
I_{i+1}
. Then, for
\mathbf{x}\in C_{j}^{k}
:

\displaystyle I_{i+1}(J_{i}(f))(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=s(J_{i}(f)(r_{1}(\mathbf{x})),\ldots,J_{i}(f)(r_{l}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=s(f(r_{1}(\mathbf{x})),\ldots,f(r_{l}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=I_{i+1}(f)(\mathbf{x}).

By the Mal’cev identities we obtain
J_{i+1}(f)(\mathbf{x})=m(f(\mathbf{x}),I_{i+1}(f)(\mathbf{x}),I_{i+1}(f)(\mathbf{x}))=f(\mathbf{x})
.

Next, assume that
\mathbf{x}\in C_{i+1}^{k}
. Then, by assumption,
I_{i+1}(J_{i}(f))(\mathbf{x})=J_{i}(f)(\mathbf{x})
holds, and therefore
J_{i+1}(f)(\mathbf{x})=m(J_{i}(f)(\mathbf{x}),J_{i}(f)(\mathbf{x}),f(\mathbf{x}))=f(\mathbf{x})
. We set
I=J_{l}
. By Lemma
17
(3) and (4)
I
can be uniformly represented by
n
-ary minors, thus we are done.
∎

We next show, how ‘uniform interpolation’ is also invariant under endomorphisms of the algebra
R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A})
, i.e., maps
e\colon A^{k}\to A^{k}
such that
e(r(\mathbf{x}))=r(e(\mathbf{x}))
holds for every
r\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A})
. Note that the algebra
(A^{k},R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A}))
is a (unary) term reduct of the
k
-th matrix-power
\mathbf{A}^{[k]}
of
\mathbf{A}
(in the sense of
[
15
, Exercise 3.12 (4)]
), so, this includes all endomorphisms of
\mathbf{A}^{[k]}
. If
\mathbf{A}={\mathbf{F}}^{l}
is a vector space, then all maps
X\mapsto XT
, for
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k\times l}
and
T\in{\mathbf{F}}^{l\times l}
are endomorphisms of
R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A})
.

Lemma 31
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
be arbitrary algebras, let
\mathbf{a}\in A^{n}
, and assume that
I\colon\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}\to\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}
is
(n,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-UR such that
I(f)(\mathbf{a})=f(\mathbf{a})
, for all
f\in\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}
. Then, for every
e\in\End(R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A}))
, also
I(f)(e(\mathbf{a}))=f(e(\mathbf{a}))
holds.

Proof.

Let
I(f)=s\circ(f\circ r_{1},\ldots,f\circ r_{l})
with
s\in\Clo(\mathbf{B})
and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{l}\in R_{n}(\mathbf{A})
be a representation of
I
. Let
e\in\End(R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A}))
. Then

\displaystyle I(f)(e(\mathbf{a}))

\displaystyle=s(f(r_{1}(e(\mathbf{a}))),\ldots,f(r_{l}(e(\mathbf{a}))))

\displaystyle=s(f(e(r_{1}(\mathbf{a}))),\ldots,f(e(r_{l}(\mathbf{a}))))

\displaystyle=I(f\circ e)(\mathbf{a})=f(e(\mathbf{a})).

∎

In the case that
\mathbf{B}
is a module, we can prove some additional results. We are going to use the following notation:

Definition 32
.

Let
\mathbf{B}
be a module, and
A
a arbitrary set. For
\mathbf{a}\in A^{k}
, and
b\in B
, we define the map
\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{b}\colon A^{k}\to B
by

\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{b}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}b&\text{if }\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{a}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

If
\mathbf{B}
is a regular module over a ring with unity
1
, we will write
\delta_{\mathbf{a}}=\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{1}
for short.

Definition 33
.

For a module
\mathbf{B}
, we define the
support

\supp(f)
of a function
f\colon A^{n}\to B
as
\supp(f)=\{\mathbf{a}\in A^{n}\mid f(\mathbf{a})\neq 0\}
.

The following lemma shows how (if the target algebra
\mathbf{B}
is a module) we can lift uniform generation of characteristic functions
\delta_{\mathbf{a}}
, to all functions, whose support contains only elements from the orbit of
\mathbf{a}
, under the action of
\Aut(R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A}))
:

Lemma 34
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite algebra,
T\subseteq A^{k}
, and
\mathbf{R}
be a ring such that
\{\delta_{\mathbf{a}}\mid\mathbf{a}\in T\}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{R})
-minors (where
\mathbf{R}
is considered as regular module).

Then, for
T^{\prime}=\{e(\mathbf{a})\mid e\in\Aut(R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A})),\mathbf{a}\in T\}
and every
\mathbf{R}
-module
\mathbf{B}
, also the set
\{f\colon A^{k}\to B\mid\supp(f)\subseteq T^{\prime}\}
is uniformly generated by
n
-ary
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-minors.

Proof.

Let
I
be an
(n,\mathbf{A},\mathbf{R})
-UR operation, such that
I(\delta_{\mathbf{a}})=\delta_{\mathbf{a}}
, for all
\mathbf{a}\in T
, and let
\mathbf{B}
be a general
\mathbf{R}
-module. Then
I
can be represented by a formula
I(f)(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{s\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A})}r_{s}f(s(\mathbf{x}))
, where every
r_{s}\in R
. Let
\mathbf{B}
be an arbitrary
\mathbf{R}
-module. The formula for
I
also induces operation
I\colon\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}\to\mathcal{O}_{A,B}^{(k)}
, which, for simplicity, we will denote by the same letter. It is straightforward to see that
I(\delta_{\mathbf{a}})=\delta_{\mathbf{a}}
implies

I(\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{b})(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{s\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A})}r_{s}\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{b}(s(\mathbf{x}))=\sum_{s\in R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A})}r_{s}\delta_{\mathbf{a}}(s(\mathbf{x}))\cdot b=I(\delta_{\mathbf{a}})\cdot b=\delta_{\mathbf{a}}\cdot b=\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{b}.

By Lemma
31
, also
I(\delta_{e^{-1}(\mathbf{a})}^{b})(\mathbf{x})=I(\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{b})(e(\mathbf{x}))=\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{b}(e(\mathbf{x}))=\delta_{e^{-1}(\mathbf{a})}^{b}(\mathbf{x})
holds, for all
e\in\Aut(R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A}))
. Thus
I(\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{b})=\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{b}
, for all
\mathbf{a}\in T^{\prime}
,
b\in B
. Next, note that every function
f
with support in
T^{\prime}
can be written as the sum
f=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in T^{\prime}}\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{f(\mathbf{a})}
. Since
\mathbf{B}
is a module,
I
is linear, i.e.,
I(f+g)=I(f)+I(g)
. Thus, we get that
I(f)=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in T^{\prime}}I(\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{f(\mathbf{a})})=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in T^{\prime}}\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{f(\mathbf{a})}=f
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma
34
implies two important facts: First, in order to prove that all operations from some algebra
\mathbf{A}
to a target
\mathbf{R}
-module
\mathbf{B}
are uniformly generated by
n
-ary minors, it suffices to prove this for characteristic functions
\delta_{\mathbf{a}}
, such that the orbits of the tuples
\mathbf{a}
under
\Aut(R_{n}^{k,k}(\mathbf{A}))
cover all of
A^{k}
.
Secondly, it is actually enough to only consider the regular module
\mathbf{R}
as target, in order to prove uniform generation for
all

\mathbf{R}
-modules
\mathbf{B}
:

Corollary 35
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite algebra and
\mathbf{R}
be a ring, considered as regular module. If
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{R}}
is uniformly generated by
k
-ary minors, then so is
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}
, for every
\mathbf{R}
-module
\mathbf{B}
.

4.
The main result

In this section, we are going to show that the
(k+1)
-ary functions from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
are uniformly generated by their
k
-ary
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-minors, if
\mathbf{A}={\mathbf{F}}^{k}
is a finite vector space, and
\mathbf{B}
is a module in which
\chars({\mathbf{F}})
is invertible. Corollary
20
then implies that every clonoid from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
is generated by its
k
-ary part.

Using the notation we introduced in the preliminary section, recall that
R_{n}(\mathbf{A})
consists of all operations
X\mapsto MX
, such that
M
is a matrix over
F
of rank less than or equal to
n
. Thus, our goal is to prove the existence of coordinates
\alpha_{M}\in R_{\mathbf{B}}
for every
(k+1)\times(k+1)
-matrix
M

over
{\mathbf{F}}
such that for each

f\colon{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}\to B
we have

\forall X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}\colon f(X)=\sum_{\rk(M)\leq k}\alpha_{M}f(MX).

Our proof proceeds, by first using the results from Section
3
, to reduce the problem to the question, whether we can generate a characteristic function
\delta_{X_{0}}
, for matrices of full rank, by their
k
-ary minors. We discuss this reduction in Subsection
4.1
. Then, we provide a proof of this fact, in Subsection
4.2
. In Subsection
4.3
we summarize the proof and discuss some immediate consequences. In Subsection
4.4
we use our results to obtain a more explicit description of the lattice of
({\mathbf{F}}^{n},\mathbf{B})
-clonoids.

4.1.
Reduction to functions of singleton support

We start with the following lemma that can be proved similarly to Lemma
34
:

Lemma 36
.

Let
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
be a matrix with
\rk(X_{0})=n
, and assume that
J
is a
(n,{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-UR operation, given by a formula

(9)

J(f)(X)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})}\alpha_{M}f(MX),

such that
J(\delta_{X_{0}})(X)=\delta_{X_{0}}(X)
, for all
X
of rank
\rk(X)\leq i
.

Then, for all
f
with
\supp(f)\subseteq\{X\mid\rk(X)\geq n\}
and every
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
with
\rk(X)\leq i
:

(10)

J(f)(X)=\begin{cases}f(X)&\text{if }\mathcal{C}(X)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0}),\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We first consider the behavior of
J
on certain characteristic functions. If
Z\in F^{m\times k}
is a matrix with
\mathcal{C}(Z)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
, then there is an invertible matrix
T\in F^{k\times k}
, such that
ZT=X_{0}
, so
\delta_{Z}({X})=\delta_{X_{0}}(XT)
. By Lemma
34
,
J_{H}(\delta_{Z})(X)=\delta_{Z}(X)
holds, for all
X
with
\rk(X)\leq i
.

Next, let
Z\in F^{m\times k}
be matrix with
\rk(Z)\geq n
and
\mathcal{C}(Z)\neq\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
. Note that this implies that
\mathcal{C}(MX)\subseteq\mathcal{C}(X_{0})\neq\mathcal{C}(Z)
, for all
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
and
M\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times m}
with
\mathcal{C}(M)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
. Since
\dim(\mathcal{C}(MX))\leq n\leq\dim(\mathcal{C}(Z))
, this further implies
Z\neq MX
. As a consequence,
J(\delta_{Z})(X)=0
holds for every
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
.

Next, let
f\in\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
be an arbitrary function, whose support contains only matrices of rank
\geq n
. Hence,
f
can be written as
f=\sum_{\rk(Z)\geq n}\delta_{Z}^{f(Z)}
, and therefore
J(f)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(Z)=\mathcal{C}(X)}J(\delta_{Z}^{f(Z)})
.

If we evaluate
J(f)
at some matrix
X
with
\mathcal{C}(X)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
we obtain, by the above,
J(f)(X)=\delta_{X}^{f(X)}=f(X)
. If we evaluate
J(f)(X)
at some
X
with
\rk(X)\leq i
and
\mathcal{C}(X)\neq\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
we similarly get
J(f)(X)=0
. This finishes the proof.
∎

While it is a relatively technical statement on its own, Lemma
36
has several very useful consequences that we will use throughout the paper. If we, for instance, assume that the equation (
9
) holds for all matrices
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
, we get the following consequence:

Lemma 37
.

Let
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
be a matrix with
\rk(X_{0})=n
and assume that
\delta_{X_{0}}
is generated by its
n
-ary
({\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{R})
-minors. Then

(1)

For every
H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{m}
with
\dim(H)=n
, there is a
(n,{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-UR operation
J_{H}
, such that for all
f
with
\supp(f)\subseteq\{X\in F^{m\times k}\mid\rk(X)\geq n\}
:

J_{H}(f)(X)=\begin{cases}f(X)&\text{ if }\mathcal{C}(X)=H\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

(2)

There exists a
(n,{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-UR operation
J_{n}
such that for all
f
with
\supp(f)\subseteq\{X\mid\rk(X)\geq n\}
:

J_{n}(f)(X)=\begin{cases}f(X)&\text{ if }\rk(X)=n\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Proof.

We first prove (1) for
H=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
. By our assumption, we know that there are
\alpha_{M}\in\mathbf{R}
such that

(11)

\delta_{X_{0}}(X)=\sum_{\rk(M)\leq n}\alpha_{M}\delta_{X_{0}}(MX)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})}\alpha_{M}\delta_{X_{0}}(MX).

For the second equation, note that for all matrices
M
of rank
\leq n
,
\mathcal{C}(M)\neq\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
implies that
\delta_{X_{0}}(MX)=0
for all
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
. So, without loss of generality, we can assume that
\alpha_{M}=0
if
\mathcal{C}(M)\neq\mathcal{C}(X_{0})
. Then, we obtain the statement by applying Lemma
36
to

(12)

J_{H}(f)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})}\alpha_{M}f(MX).

Next, let
H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{m}
be an arbitrary subspace of dimension
n
. Then, there is an invertible matrix
S\in F^{m\times m}
such that
\mathcal{C}(SX_{0})=H
. Note that
\delta_{SX_{0}}(X)=\delta_{X_{0}}(S^{-1}X)
, hence (
11
) implies that

\delta_{SX_{0}}(X)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})}\alpha_{M}\delta_{X_{0}}(MS^{-1}X)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})}\alpha_{M}\delta_{SX_{0}}(SMS^{-1}X).

Again, by Lemma
36
we can see that
J_{H}(f)(X)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=\mathcal{C}(X_{0})}\alpha_{M}f(SMS^{-1}X)
has the desired properties.

For (2) we define
J_{n}(f)=\sum_{H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{m},\dim(H)=n}J_{H}(f)
. For every function
f
, whose support contains only matrices of rank
n
or higher, note that by (1)
J_{n}(f)(X)=0
, if
\rk(X)>n
, and
J_{n}(f)(X)=J_{\mathcal{C}(X)}(f)(X)=f(X)
, if
\rk(X)=n
. So, we are done.
∎

We remark that the conditions of Lemma
37
are in particular satisfied, if, for a given rank decomposition
X_{0}=AU
we have that
\delta_{X_{0}}\in\langle\delta_{U}\rangle_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{R}}
holds. Then, there are coefficients
\alpha_{M}\in\mathbf{R}
such that

(13)

\delta_{X_{0}}(X)=\sum_{M\in F^{n\times m}}\alpha_{M}\delta_{U}(MX)=\sum_{M\in F^{n\times m}}\alpha_{M}\delta_{X_{0}}(AMX).

As
\rk(AM)\leq n
, for every matrix
M\in F^{n\times m}
, equation (
13
) clearly shows that
\delta_{X_{0}}
is generated by its
n
-ary
({\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{R})
-minors.

We next prove that it is further enough to only consider matrices
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(j+1)\times j}
of full rank, for every
j\in[k]
, in order to prove that
\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{R}}
is uniformly generated by
k
-ary minors:

Lemma 38
.

Let
{\mathbf{F}}
be a finite field,
\mathbf{R}
be a ring,
\mathbf{B}
be a
\mathbf{R}
-module, and
k\geq 0
. Assume that, for every
j\in[k]
, there is a matrix
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(j+1)\times j}
of rank
\rk(X_{0})=j
such that
\delta_{X_{0}}
is generated by its
j
-ary
({\mathbf{F}}^{j},\mathbf{R})
-minors. Then

(1)

\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}
is uniformly generated by
k
-ary minors.

(2)

For every
i\in[k]_{0}
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
there is a
(i,{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-UR operation
I_{i}\colon\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}\to\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
, with
I_{i}(f)(X)=f(X)
, for all
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
with
\rk(X)\leq i
.

Proof.

We first prove (1) by induction on
k=0,1,2,3,\ldots
For the base step
k=0
, the statement trivially holds by
f(X)=f(0)
. (Also,
k=1
can be observed by a careful reading of
[
11
]
).

So let us consider the induction step
k-1\to k
. For short, let us write
l=k+1
. Our goal is to prove that
\mathcal{O}_{F^{k},B}^{(l)}
is uniformly generated by
k
-ary minors. By the induction assumption, there is a
(k-1,{\mathbf{F}}^{k-1},\mathbf{B})
-UR operation

J(f)(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in{\mathbf{F}}^{l\times l}\\
\rk(M)\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M}f(MY)

such that
J(f)(Y)=f(Y)
, for all
f\in\mathcal{O}_{F^{k-1},B}^{(l)}
and
Y\in{\mathbf{F}}^{l\times(k-1)}
. Note that this representation formula also induces a
(k-1,{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-UR operation on
g\in\mathcal{O}_{F^{k},B}^{(l)}
. Next, let
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{l\times k}
be a matrix of rank
\leq k-1
; so it can be decomposed into
X=YU
, for
Y\in{\mathbf{F}}^{l\times(k-1)}
and
U\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k-1)\times k}
. If, for a function
g\in\mathcal{O}_{F^{k},B}^{(l)}
we define
g_{U}\colon F^{l\times(k-1)}\rightarrow B
by
g_{U}(Y)=g(YU)
, then we get

J(g)(X)=J(g)(YU)=J(g_{U})(Y)=g_{U}(Y)=g(YU)=g(X).

Thus,
J(g)
agrees with
g
on matrices of rank
\leq k-1
. Next, recall that we assume that there is a
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{l\times k}
such that
\delta_{X_{0}}
is generated by
k
-ary minors. Lemma
37
(2) implies, that there is an operation
J_{k}
on
\mathcal{O}_{F^{k},B}^{(l)}
that can be represented by
k
-ary minors, such that
J_{k}(g)=g
, for all functions
g\in\mathcal{O}_{F^{k},B}^{(l)}
with
\supp(g)\subseteq\{X\mid\rk(X)=k\}
. It follows that, for every
g\in\mathcal{O}_{F^{k},B}^{(l)}
:

g=J(g)+J_{k}(g-J(g))=J(g)+J_{k}(g)-J_{k}J(g).

Since both
J_{k}
and
J
can be represented by
k
-ary minors, we are done.

We next prove (2). For
m\leq i
, the statement is trivially true, by setting
I_{i}(f)=f
. So let us assume that
m>i
. By (1), we know that
\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{i},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
is uniformly generated by
i
-ary minors, so there exists a representation formula

I_{i}(f)(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times m}\\
\rk(M)\leq i\end{subarray}}\alpha_{M}f(MY),

such that
I_{i}(f)(Y)=f(Y)
for all
Y\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times i}
. Naturally
I_{i}
also induces an operation on
\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
that is representable by
i
-ary minors.

Every matrix
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
of rank at most
i
can be written as
X=YU
, for
Y\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times i},U\in{\mathbf{F}}^{i\times k}
. If we again, for every
g\in\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
define
g_{U}\colon F^{m\times i}\rightarrow B
by
g_{U}(Y)=g(YU)
, we get

I_{i}(g)(X)=I_{i}(g)(YU)=I_{i}(g_{U})(Y)=g_{U}(Y)=g(YU)=f(X).

Thus
I_{i}
indeed satisfies
I_{i}(f)(X)=f(X)
, for all
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
with
\rk(X)\leq i
.
∎

4.2.
Generating characteristic functions

By Lemma
38
, all there is left to prove our main result, is to show that for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there a matrix
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}
of rank
k
such that
\delta_{X_{0}}\in\langle\delta_{\Id_{k}}\rangle_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{R}}
, whenever
|F|
is invertible in
\mathbf{R}
.

So, throughout this subsection, we fix the following notation:

•

{\mathbf{F}}
denotes a fixed finite field

•

k\in{\mathbb{N}}
is a fixed natural number

•

\mathbf{R}
is a regular module, in which
|F|
is invertible.

•

X_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}
is a fixed matrix of rank
k
, e.g.

X_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\Id_{k}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}.

Our goal is to show that there are coefficients
\alpha_{M}\in R
for every
M\in F^{(k+1)\times k}
of rank
\rk(M)=k
such that
\delta_{X_{0}}(X)=\sum_{\rk(M)=k}\alpha_{M}\delta_{\Id_{k}}(MX)
. (Note that we only need to consider coefficient matrices
M\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k\times(k+1)}
of full rank
k
, as otherwise
\delta_{\Id_{k}}(MX)
is equal to
0
.) The function
X\mapsto\delta_{\Id_{k}}(MX)
evaluates to
1
only on the solution set of the equation
MX=\Id_{k}
. This motivates the following definition and lemma:

Definition 39
.

For every matrix
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}
with
\rk(X)=k
and for every vector
\mathbf{a}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k+1}\setminus\mathcal{C}(X)
, we define
\theta(X,\mathbf{a})=\{X+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T}\mid\mathbf{u}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k}\}
. We call every set of the form
\theta(X,\mathbf{a})
a
\theta
-space
.

Lemma 40
.

(1)

Let
M\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k\times(k+1)}
with
\rk(M)=k
. Then there is a
\theta
-space
V_{M}
such that
MX=\Id_{k}
if and only if
X\in V_{M}
.

(2)

Conversely, for every
\theta
-space
V
, there is a matrix
M_{V}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k\times(k+1)}
with
\rk(M_{V})=k
such that
M_{V}X=\Id_{k}
if and only if
X\in V
.

Proof.

We start with (
1
).
Since
M
has full rank,
\mathbf{x}\mapsto M\mathbf{x}
is a surjective map from
{\mathbf{F}}^{k+1}
to
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
. Hence, there is a matrix
Y
such that
MY=\Id_{k}
. Moreover, the kernel
\ker(M)
has dimension 1, and is therefore generated by a single vector
\mathbf{a}\neq 0
. It follows that the solution space of the homogeneous equation
MY=0
consists of all matrices of the form
\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T}
, for
\mathbf{u}\in F^{k}
.
Thus
V_{M}=\theta(Y,\mathbf{a})
is the solution space to the system of linear equations
MX=\Id_{k}
. Furthermore, assuming
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{C}(Y)
, that is,
\mathbf{a}=Y\mathbf{b}
for some
\mathbf{b}\neq 0
, would imply that
0=M\mathbf{a}=MY\mathbf{b}=\Id_{k}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}\neq 0
, a contradiction. Thus
V_{M}
is indeed a
\theta
-space.

For (
2
), let
Y\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}
and
\mathbf{a}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)}\setminus\mathcal{C}(Y)
be such that
V=\theta(Y,\mathbf{a})
. Let
\mathbf{n}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k+1}
be a generator of
\ker(Y^{T})
. Then, as in (
1
) we can see that there is a matrix
M_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k\times(k+1)}
such that
MY=\Id_{k}
holds if and only if
M=M_{0}+\mathbf{m}\mathbf{n}^{T}
, for some
\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
. Since
\mathbf{a}\notin\mathcal{C}(Y)
, we have
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{a}\neq 0
. Then, let us define
\mathbf{m}_{V}=-(\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{a})^{-1}M_{0}\mathbf{a}
, and
M_{V}=M_{0}+\mathbf{m}_{V}\mathbf{n}^{T}
. By definition,
M_{V}\mathbf{a}=0
, thus
M_{V}
is indeed the matrix we were looking for.
∎

We are going to use the following notation:

Definition 41
.

For a
\theta
-space
V
, let
\delta_{V}\colon{\mathbf{F}}^{k+1}\times k
denote the function given by
\delta_{V}(X)=1
if
X\in V
and
\delta_{V}(X)=0
else. Note that, by Lemma
40

\delta_{V}(X)=\delta_{\Id_{k}}(MX)
, for some matrix
M\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k\times(k+1)}
of rank
k
.

By definition, every
\theta
-space is an affine subspace of
{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}
of dimension
k
. However, in a different sense,
\theta
-spaces
exhibit some ‘line-like’ behavior, by the following observations:

Lemma 42
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}
, and let
\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k+1}
with
\mathbf{a}_{1}\notin\mathcal{C}(X_{1})
and
\mathbf{a}_{2}\notin\mathcal{C}(X_{2})
.

(1)

Either
|\theta(X_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1})\cap\theta(X_{2},\mathbf{a}_{2})|\leq 1
, or
\theta(X_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1})=\theta(X_{2},\mathbf{a}_{2})
.

(2)

For
X_{1}\neq X_{2}
there is at most one
\theta
-space containing both
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
.

(3)

All elements of a
\theta
-space have rank
k
.

Proof.

For (
1
), note that
\theta(X,\mathbf{a})=\theta(Y,\mathbf{a})
holds for every
Y\in\theta(X,\mathbf{a})
. Thus,
if there exists
X
in
\theta(X_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1})\cap\theta(X_{2},\mathbf{a}_{2})
, then without loss of generality we can assume
X=X_{1}=X_{2}
. It is then easy to see that
\theta(X_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1})=\theta(X_{2},\mathbf{a}_{2})
if
\mathbf{a}_{1}
and
\mathbf{a}_{2}
are collinear and
\theta(X_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1})\cap\theta(X_{2},\mathbf{a}_{2})=\{X\}
else.

For (
2
), let
\mathbf{a}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k+1}
. Then

X_{2}\in\theta(X_{1},\mathbf{a})
iff
X_{1}-X_{2}=\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T}
for some
\mathbf{u}\neq 0
.
In particular this can only happen if
\rk(X_{1}-X_{2})=1
. Therefore
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{C}(X_{1}-X_{2})
is, up to multiplication with a scalar, uniquely determined by
X_{1}-X_{2}
.

To see (
3
), let us consider a space

\theta(X,\mathbf{a})
, and let
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
. We show that the columns of
X+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T}
are linearly independent.
To this end, let us consider a vector
\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
such that
(X+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T})\mathbf{b}=0
.
If
\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{b}\neq 0
, then
\mathbf{a}=-X(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{b})^{-1}\mathbf{b}
, which contradicts the assumption that
\mathbf{a}\notin\mathcal{C}(X)
.
This leaves us with the case that
\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{b}=0
and hence
X\mathbf{b}=0
. Since
\rk(X)=k
, this implies
\mathbf{b}=0
, and we are done.
∎

In the following definitions we introduce
\theta
-spaces of a specific form, that will play a crucial role in the proof of the main result of the section.

Definition 43
.

Let
n\leq k
, and let
A\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times n}
,
U\in{\mathbf{F}}^{n\times k}
be matrices of rank
n
. Then, for
0\leq j\leq n
, let us set
X_{j}:=X_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}
,
where
\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k+1}
denote the columns of
A
, and
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n}^{T}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
denote the rows of
U
.
We call the rank factorization
AU=X_{n}-X_{0}

(
n
-)admissible
if

(1)

\mathbf{a}_{i+1}\notin\mathcal{C}(X_{i})
for all
0\leq i\leq n-1
;

(2)

\mathbf{a}_{i+2}\in\mathcal{C}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{i})
, for all
0\leq i\leq n-2
.

We say that
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}
is of
type
n
, if
X=X_{0}+AU
for some
n
-admissible product
AU
, and we call
X=X_{0}+AU
an
(
n
-)admissible representation of
X
. We write
\mathfrak{T}_{n}
for the set of matrices of type
n
, and
\mathfrak{T}=\mathfrak{T}_{0}\cup\mathfrak{T}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathfrak{T}_{k}
.

Definition 44
.

We say that a
\theta
-space
\theta(X,\mathbf{a})=\{X+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T}\mid\mathbf{u}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k}\}
is of
type
n
if
X\in\mathfrak{T}_{n}
with admissible representation
X=X_{0}+AU
, and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{C}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{1})\ldots\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{n-1})\setminus\mathcal{C}(X_{n})
.
We denote the set of all
\theta
-spaces of type
n
by
\Theta_{n}
, and we let
\Theta=\Theta_{0}\cup\Theta_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\Theta_{k}
.

The type of a matrix
X
or the
\theta
-space
\theta(X,\mathbf{a})
does not depend on the particular choice of admissible representation
AU=X-X_{0}
. The proof requires the following lemma:

Lemma 45
.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{T}_{n}
, and
let
X=X_{0}+AU=X_{0}+\tilde{A}\tilde{U}

be two admissible representations of it. Furthermore, let
X_{i}=X_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbf{a}_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{T}
and
\tilde{X}_{i}=X_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{i}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{T}
for
i\in[n]_{0}
. Then:

(1)

\mathcal{C}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{i})=\mathcal{C}(\tilde{X}_{0})\cap\mathcal{C}(\tilde{X}_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{C}(\tilde{X}_{i})
for
every
i\in[n]_{0}
.

(2)

there is an lower triangular matrix
T\in{\mathbf{F}}^{n\times n}
with
A=\tilde{A}T^{-1}
and
U=T\tilde{U}
.

Proof.

Since
AU=\tilde{A}\tilde{U}
are two rank factorizations of
X-X_{0}
we already know that there exists an invertible matrix
T=(t_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
with
A=\tilde{A}T
and
U=T^{-1}\tilde{U}
.

For
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, let
\mathbf{n}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}
denote vectors such that
\langle\mathbf{n}_{i}\rangle=\mathcal{C}(X_{i})^{\bot}=\ker(X_{i}^{T})
and
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}=\mathcal{C}(\tilde{X}_{i})^{\bot}=\ker(\tilde{X}_{i}^{T})
. We are going to prove by induction on
i=0,1,2,\ldots,n
that

(a)

\langle\mathbf{n}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{n}_{i}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}\rangle
.

(b)

\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i+1}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i+1}\rangle
.

By considering the orthogonal spaces, we can see that
(a)
is equivalent to
\mathcal{C}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{i})=\mathcal{C}(\tilde{X}_{0})\cap\mathcal{C}(\tilde{X}_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{C}(\tilde{X}_{i})
. Furthermore, if
\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{l}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{l}\rangle
holds for every
l\leq i+1
, then
TU=\tilde{U}
implies that
t_{lj}=0
for all
l,j
satisfying
i+1\geq l>j
; in other words,
T
looks like a lower triangular matrix on its first
i+1
rows. So, if we prove (a) and (b) for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
, we are done.

We first prove
(a)
and
(b)
for
i=0
. By
X_{0}=\tilde{X}_{0}
clearly
\langle\mathbf{n}_{0}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{0}\rangle
holds. For
(b)
, observe that
\mathbf{n}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{i}\neq 0\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{a}_{i}\not\in\mathcal{C}(X_{0})\Leftrightarrow i=1
. This implies that
\mathbf{n}_{0}^{T}X=\mathbf{n}_{0}(X_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{a}_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{T})=\mathbf{n}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}
. Symmetrically, we obtain
\mathbf{n}_{0}^{T}X=\mathbf{n}_{0}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{T}
. Thus
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{1}=(\mathbf{n}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{1})^{-1}(\mathbf{n}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{1})\mathbf{u}_{1}
, which finishes the proof of the base step.

Next, let us prove the induction step
1,\ldots,i-1\to i
. We start with
(a)
. By induction assumption
\langle\mathbf{n}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{n}_{i-1}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i-1}\rangle
, thus, it is enough to show that
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}\in\langle\mathbf{n}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{n}_{i-1},\mathbf{n}_{i}\rangle
. This is equivalent to finding coefficients
r_{0},\ldots,r_{i-1}
, such that the equation
(\mathbf{n}_{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}r_{j}\mathbf{n}_{j})^{T}\tilde{X}_{i}=0
holds.

By the induction assumption we know that
T
has the shape of lower triangular matrix on its first
i
rows. Recall that
\tilde{A}=AT
, and so
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{j}=\sum_{l\geq j}t_{lj}\mathbf{a}_{l}
for every
j\leq i
. For
j<i
this implies
\mathbf{n}_{j}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{j+1}=t_{j+1,j+1}\mathbf{n}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{j+1}\neq 0
. For all
j+1<l\leq i
this further implies
\mathbf{n}_{j}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{l}=0
holds. By both of these observations, for every
j<i
, we obtain

(14)

\mathbf{n}_{j}^{T}\tilde{X}_{i}=\mathbf{n}_{j}^{T}(\tilde{X}_{j}+\sum_{k>j}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{k}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{T})=c_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j+1}^{T},\text{ where }c_{j}=t_{j+1,j+1}\mathbf{n}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{j+1}\neq 0.

On the other hand, note that

(15)

\mathbf{n}_{i}^{T}\tilde{X}_{i}=\mathbf{n}_{i}^{T}(\tilde{X}_{i}-X_{i})=\mathbf{n}_{i}^{T}(\sum_{j\leq i}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{T}-\mathbf{a}_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{T})\in\langle\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{j}^{T}\rangle.

The latter follows from
\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{j}\rangle
, which holds by the induction hypothesis. Thus, there are coefficients
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
, such that
\mathbf{n}_{i}^{T}\tilde{X}_{i}=\sum_{j\leq i}d_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}
. We conclude that
(\mathbf{n}_{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}r_{j}\mathbf{n}_{j})^{T}\tilde{X}_{i}=0
is equivalent to the equation

(16)

\sum_{j=1}^{i}d_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{T}+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}r_{j}c_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j+1}^{T}=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}(d_{j-1}+r_{j}c_{j})\mathbf{u}_{j+1}^{T}=0

Equation (
16
) has the solution
r_{j}=-d_{j-1}c_{j}^{-1}
for
j=0,\ldots,i-1
(recall that
c_{j}
is invertible by (
14
)). Thus
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}\in\langle\mathbf{n}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{n}_{i-1},\mathbf{n}_{i}\rangle
, and we are done.

In order to prove
(b)
we are again going to use that we can write
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}
as linear combination
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{i}r_{j}\mathbf{n}_{j}
(with
r_{i}=1
). By multiplying with
X
we obtain
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}X=\sum_{j=0}^{i}r_{j}\mathbf{n}_{j}^{T}X=\sum_{j=0}^{i}r_{j}(\mathbf{n}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{a}_{j+1})\mathbf{u}_{j+1}^{T}
. On the other hand, clearly also
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{T}X=(\mathbf{n}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i+1})\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i+1}^{T}
holds. Since
\mathbf{n}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i+1}\neq 0
, this implies that
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j+1}\in\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{u}_{i+1}\rangle
. Since
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j+1}\notin\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{u}_{i}\rangle
, this implies that
\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i+1}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{u}_{i+1}\rangle
, so we are done.

Since items
(a)

(b)
hold for all
i=0,1,\ldots,n
, this finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

By Lemma
45
(
1
), we know that the set

\mathcal{C}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{1})\ldots\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{n-1})\setminus\mathcal{C}(X_{n})
of admissible directions
\mathbf{a}
in Definition
44
does not depend on the concrete representation of
X=X_{0}+AU
.

Furthermore, we get the following:

Lemma 46
.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{T}_{n}
with representation
X=X_{0}+AU
, let
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n}^{T}
be the rows of
U
, and let
\theta(X,\mathbf{a})\in\Theta_{n}
. Then

(1)

X+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T}\in\mathfrak{T}_{n+1}
if
\mathbf{u}\notin\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n}\rangle
;

(2)

X+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T}\in\mathfrak{T}_{n}
if
\mathbf{u}\in\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n}\rangle
.

Proof.

(
1
) directly follows from the definition of
\mathfrak{T}_{n+1}
. For (
2
) note that, if
\mathbf{u}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}
, then
X+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T}=X_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{a}_{i}+r_{i}\mathbf{a})\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}
, which is an
n
-admissible representation of
X+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}^{T}
.
∎

From the previous lemma it follows that, if
{\mathbf{F}}
is the field of order
q
, then
\theta
-spaces of type
n
consist of
q^{n}
matrices of type
n
, and
q^{k}-q^{n}
matrices of type
n+1
. On the other hand, also the following holds:

Lemma 47
.

Let
q=|{\mathbf{F}}|
and let
X\in\mathfrak{T}_{n}
, for
1\leq n\leq k
. Then

(1)

X
is in exactly one
\theta
-space of type
n-1
;

(2)

X
is in exactly
q^{k-n}

\theta
-spaces of type
n
;

(3)

X
is in no
\theta
-spaces of type
j\neq n-1,n
.

Proof.

Let
X=X_{0}+AU
be an admissible representation of
X
. So then
X=X_{n-1}+\mathbf{a}_{n}\mathbf{u}_{n}^{T}
, with
X_{n-1}=X_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}
. In particular
X\in\theta(X_{n-1},\mathbf{a}_{n})
, which is a
\theta
-space of type
n-1
. Next, assume
X
is in some other
\theta
-space of type
n-1
. This implies that there is a different admissible representation of
X=X_{0}+\tilde{A}\tilde{U}
, with
\theta=\theta(\tilde{X}_{n-1},\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n})
. By Lemma
45
, we know that representations are unique up to multiplication with lower triangle matrices
T
. In particular
\langle\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a}_{n}\rangle
. But this in turn implies that
\theta(X_{n-1},\mathbf{a}_{n})=\theta(X,\mathbf{a}_{n})=\theta(X,\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n})=\theta(\tilde{X}_{n-1},\mathbf{a}_{n})
, which completes the proof of (
1
).

For (
2
) we simply need to count the number of admissible directions
\langle\mathbf{a}\rangle
such that
\theta(X,\mathbf{a})
is a set of type
n
. Note that

\mathcal{C}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{n-1})

is a space of dimension
{k-n+1}
, thus

|\mathcal{C}(X_{0})\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{C}(X_{n-1})\setminus\mathcal{C}(X_{n})|=q^{k-n+1}-q^{k-n}=q^{k-n}(q-1).

Hence, up to multiplication with some non-zero scalar, there are
q^{k-n}
distinct choices for
\mathbf{a}
.

(
3
) clearly follows from the fact that every space of type
n
contains only matrices of type
n
or
n+1
, i.e., Lemma
46
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this subsection:

Theorem 48
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
{\mathbf{F}}
be a finite field of order
q
and let
\mathbf{R}
be a regular module, in which
q
is invertible. Let
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)\times k}
be of rank
\rk(X_{0})=k
, and let
\Theta_{n}
, for
n\in[k]_{0}
be as in Definition
43
. Then

\delta_{X_{0}}(X)=\sum_{n=0}^{k}(-1)^{n}q^{\binom{n}{2}-(1+n)k}\sum_{V\in\Theta_{n}}\delta_{V}(X).

Thus
\delta_{X_{0}}
is in the
({\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{R})
-clonoid generated by
\delta_{\Id_{k}}
. This further implies that
\delta_{X_{0}}
is generated by its
k
-ary
({\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-minors.

Proof.

Assume that there are some coefficients
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
, such that

\delta_{X_{0}}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\sum_{V\in\Theta_{i}}\delta_{V}(X)

holds for every
X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k\times(k+1)}
.

If
X\notin\mathfrak{T}
, then this equation clearly holds, as all functions then evaluate to
0
. If
X=X_{0}
, i.e.,
X
has type
0
, we get the equation
1=q^{k}\alpha_{0}
. Evaluating at some matrix of type
i
for
k\geq i>0
gives us the equation
\alpha_{i-1}+q^{k-i}\alpha_{i}=0
by Lemma
47
. By assumption
q
is invertible in
\mathbf{R}
, thus the equations are equivalent to
\alpha_{0}=q^{-k}
and
\alpha_{i}=-\alpha_{i-1}q^{i-k}
for
i\in[k]
. It is now easy to see that this system of equations has the solution
\alpha_{i}=(-1)^{i}q^{\frac{i(i+1)}{2}-(1+i)k}
. Since every
\delta_{V}
is an
(\mathbf{F}^{k},\mathbf{R})
-minor of
\delta_{\Id_{k}}
(see Definition
41
), and
\delta_{\Id_{k}}(X)=\delta_{X_{0}}(X_{0}X)
, this finishes the proof.

∎

4.3.
Summary

We now summarize the proof of our main result, and discuss some direct consequences.

Theorem 49
.

Let
{\mathbf{F}}
be a finite field,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and let
\mathbf{B}
be an
\mathbf{R}
-module, such that
|F|
is invertible in
\mathbf{R}
. Then
\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}
is uniformly generated by
k
-ary
({\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-minors.

Proof.

For every
j\in[k]
, let us pick some matrix
Z_{j}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{(j+1)\times j}
, such that
\rk(Z_{j})=j
. Then, Theorem
48
implies that
\delta_{Z_{j}}
is also generated by
j
-ary
({\mathbf{F}}^{j},\mathbf{R})
-minors. Lemma
38
(1) then implies that
\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}
is uniformly generated by
k
-ary
({\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-minors, for every
\mathbf{R}
-module
\mathbf{B}
.
∎

As immediate corollaries of Theorem
49
and Corollary
20
we obtain the following:

Corollary 50
.

Let
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
be the
k
-dimensional vector space over a finite field
{\mathbf{F}}
and let
\mathbf{B}
be a faithful
\mathbf{R}
-module, such that
|F|
is invertible in
\mathbf{R}
. Then every clonoid
\mathcal{C}
from
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
to
\mathbf{B}
is generated by its
k
-ary part, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}=\langle\mathcal{C}^{(k)}\rangle_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}
.

In particular, if
\mathbf{B}
is finite, we can prove the following result:

Corollary 51
.

Let
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
be the
k
-dimensional vector space over a finite field
{\mathbf{F}}
and let
\mathbf{B}
be a finite module of coprime order. Then

(1)

there are only finitely many clonoids from
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
to
\mathbf{B}
.

(2)

every clonoid from
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
to
\mathbf{B}
is finitely related by an at most
|F|^{k\times k}
-ary relation.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
\mathbf{B}
is a faithful
\mathbf{R}
-module. Then
|F|
is invertible in
\mathbf{R}
if and only if
\mathbf{B}
is of coprime order.
The statement of the corollary then directly follows from Corollaries
50
,
20
, and
23
.
∎

By combining our results with
[
26
]
we are able to prove the following statement, that corresponds to the most general setting, in which Conjecture
1
is now resolved:

Corollary 52
.

Let
\mathbf{A}={\mathbf{F}}_{1}^{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbf{F}}_{n}^{k_{n}}\times\mathbf{D}
as
{\mathbf{F}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathbf{F}}_{n}\times\mathbf{R}
-module, where all
{\mathbf{F}}_{i}
are finite fields, and
\mathbf{D}
is a finite distributive
\mathbf{R}
-module. Let
l
be the nilpotence degree of the Jacobson radical of
\mathbf{R}
, and let
k=\max(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n},l)
. Further let
\mathbf{B}
be an
\mathbf{S}
-module, such that
|A|
is invertible in
\mathbf{S}
. Then

(1)

every clonoid from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
is generated by its
k
-ary part.

(2)

If
\mathbf{B}
is finite then there are only finitely many clonoids from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
.

Proof.

Note that the clone of the source algebra factors over the clones of the single factors. Therefore, the stament follows from Theorem
49
, Theorem

and Proposition

.
∎

In particular, Conjecture
1
holds for modules
\mathbf{A}
like in Corollary
52
. For clonoids between polynomially equivalent algebras a similar statement for arity
k+1
can be derived, by Lemma
27
.

4.4.
A description of the lattice of clonoids from
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
to
\mathbf{B}

We next aim to refine Corollary
50
to obtain an explicit description of the lattice of clonoids from
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
to
\mathbf{B}
. By Corollary
50
, this is equivalent to classify the submodules of
\mathbf{B}^{F^{k\times k}}
that are invariant under composition with matrices
F^{k\times k}
. We are going to do so in Theorem
55
, using the following notation:

Definition 53
.

Let
{\mathbf{F}}
a finite field and let
\mathbf{B}
be an
\mathbf{R}
-module. Then

•

\mathbf{R}[\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})]
is the group ring of
\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})
over
\mathbf{R}
. We denote its elements by formal sums
\sum_{M\in\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})}\alpha_{M}M
, for
\alpha_{M}\in R
.

•

For every
1\leq i\leq k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let us denote by
\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})
the
\mathbf{R}[\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})]
-module of all functions
f\colon\{X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{i\times k}\mid\rk(X)=i\}\to\mathbf{B}
. The group ring naturally acts on the set by the action

(\sum_{M\in\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})}\alpha_{M}M)f(X)=\sum_{M\in\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})}\alpha_{M}f(MX).

We further define
\mathbf{M}_{0,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{B}
(as
\mathbf{R}
-module).

To prove Theorem
55
, we first prove the following characterization of
({\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-clonoids:

Proposition 54
.

Let
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
be the
k
-dimensional vector space over a finite field
{\mathbf{F}}
and let
\mathbf{B}
be an
\mathbf{R}
-module, such that
|F|
is invertible in
\mathbf{R}
.

Then, for all indices
0\leq i\leq k\leq m
, and every matrix
A\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times i}
of full rank
\rk(A)=i
, there is an
\mathbf{R}
-linear embedding
L_{A}\colon\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})\to\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
, such that

(1)

L_{A}(f)(AU)=f(U)
if
\rk(U)=i
,

(2)

L_{A}(f)(X)=0
if
\rk(X)\leq i
and
\mathcal{C}(X)\neq\mathcal{C}(A)
,

(3)

L_{TA}(f)(TX)=L_{A}(f)(X)
for all
T\in\GL_{m}({\mathbf{F}})
,

(4)

L_{A}(f)(X)
can be computed in time
O(m^{3})
,

(5)

\mathcal{C}
is an
({\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-clonoid, if and only if for every
i\in[k]_{0}
, the pre-images
L_{A}^{-1}(\mathcal{C})
are
\mathbf{R}[\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})]
-submodules of
\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})
that only depends on
i=\rk(A)
.

Proof.

Recall that by Theorem
48
, and Lemma
37
(2) there are operations
I_{0},I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
on
\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
, such that
I_{i}
is
(i,{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-UR and
I_{i}(f)(X)=X
for all matrices of rank
\rk(X)\leq i
. In particular, this implies that the assumptions of Lemma
36
are satisfied, for any matrix
X_{0}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
and
i=n=\rk(X_{0})
. So, for every
H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{m}
of dimension
\dim(H)\leq k
, Lemma
36
implies that there is an operation
J_{H}(f)(X)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=H}\alpha_{M}f(MX)
, such that for all
f
with support in
\{X\mid\rk(X)\geq i\}
we have
J_{H}(f)(X)=f(X)
if
\mathcal{C}(X)=H
and
J_{H}(f)(X)=0
if
\mathcal{C}(X)\neq H
and
\rk(X)\leq i
. Without loss of generality we can further assume that
J_{T(H)}(f)(X)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=H}\alpha_{M}f(TMT^{-1}X)
, for every
T\in\GL_{m}({\mathbf{F}})
(cf. the proof of Lemma
37
(1)).

We next define the operations
L_{A}
. Note that the map
f\mapsto L_{A}^{\prime}(f)=L_{A}(f)|_{\{X\mid\rk(X)\leq i\}}
is already uniquely determined by (1) and (2), and is an
\mathbf{R}
-linear map from
\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})
to the module
\{f\colon{\{X\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times m}\mid\rk(X)\leq i\}}\to\mathbf{B}\}
. By its definition
J_{H}(f)
only depends on the restriction of
f
to matrices
X
with
\mathcal{C}(X)\leq H
(and therefore of rank
\leq\dim(H)
). Thus,
L_{A}(f)=J_{\mathcal{C}(A)}(L_{A}^{\prime}(f))
is well-defined. It follows directly from this definition, that (1) and (2) hold. Property (3) follows from
L^{\prime}_{TA}(f)(TX)=L_{A}^{\prime}(f)(X)
, and
J_{T(\mathcal{C}(A))}(f)(X)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=A}\alpha_{M}f(TMT^{-1}X)
.

To see (4), note that (for fixed
k
, and
f\in\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})
), we can compute
L_{A}^{\prime}(f)(X)
by checking if
X
has a rank factorization
X=AU
(which can be done in time
O(m^{3})
). To compute
L_{H}(f)(X)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}(M)=H}\alpha_{M}L_{A}^{\prime}(f)(MX)
, involves computing the matrix products
MX
(which is again bounded by
O(m^{3})
) and evaluating
L_{A}^{\prime}(f)(MX)
, for every
M
with
\mathcal{C}(M)=H
. As the number of matrices
M\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times i}
with
\mathcal{C}(M)=H
does not depend on
m
, this finishes the proof.

We next prove (4). Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
({\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-clonoid, and
m\geq k
.

We first observe that every
f\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
can be decomposed into the sum
f=\sum_{i=0}^{k}K_{i}(f)
, where
K_{0}(f)=I_{0}(f)
, and
K_{i}(f)=I_{i}(f-K_{i-1}(f))
. By definition, for every
i\in[k]_{0}
we have that
K_{i}
is
(i,{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B})
-UR,
K_{i}(f)(X)=0
for
\rk(X)\leq i-1
. Let us define
\mathbf{C}_{i}=\{K_{i}(f)\mid f\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}\}
; clearly
\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
(as a
\mathbf{R}
-module) decomposes into the direct sum of
\mathbf{C}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{C}_{k}
. Note that the functions in
\mathbf{C}_{i}
are already determined by their values on rank
i
matrices. In fact,
\mathbf{C}_{i}
can be characterized as the set of all
f\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
that are generated by their
i
-ary minors, and satisfy
f(X)=0
for
\rk(X)<i
(as, for such functions,
K_{i}(f)=f
).

We can furthermore decompose every
\mathbf{C}_{i}
into a direct sum of
\mathbf{R}
-modules
\mathbf{C}_{H}=\{J_{H}(f)\mid f\in\mathbf{C}_{i}\}
, where
J_{H}
is defined as above. By construction, every function
J_{H}(f)\in\mathbf{C}_{H}
is completely determined by its restrictions to matrices of column-space
H
. On the other hand,
\mathbf{C}_{H}
can also be characterized as all
f\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
that are generated by their
i
-ary minors, and satisfy
f(X)=0
if
\mathcal{C}(X)\neq H
and
\rk(X)\leq i
. For any
A
with
\mathcal{C}(A)=H
, the image of
L_{H}
only consists of such functions. Therefore
L_{A}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{(m)})=L_{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{C}_{H})
, if
\mathcal{C}(A)=H
.

Next, note that for any
T\in\GL_{m}({\mathbf{F}})
, the map
I_{T}\colon\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{m},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}\to\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{m},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
defined by
I_{T}(f)(X)=f(TX)
is an isomorphism from
\mathbf{C}_{H}
to
\mathbf{C}_{T(H)}
. By property (3) we have
L_{TA}(f)=L_{A}(I_{T}(f))
, therefore
L_{A}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{(m)})=L_{TA}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{(m)})
, for every
T\in\GL_{m}({\mathbf{F}})
. In other words
L_{A}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{(m)})
only depends on
i=\rk(A)
. We can see that
L_{A}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{(m)})
is even an
\mathbf{R}[\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})]
-module, by only considering maps
T\in\GL_{m}({\mathbf{F}})
that fixes
\mathcal{C}(A)
setwise. Then
I_{T}
is an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}_{H}
, such that
I_{T}(f)
only depend on the restriction of
T
to
H
. In other words
\mathbf{C}_{H}
is module over
\mathbf{R}[\GL(H)]=\mathbf{R}[\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})]
, and so is its isomorphic copy
L_{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{C}_{H})
.

Conversely, for every
i\in[k]
, let
\mathbf{D}_{i}
be an
\mathbf{R}[\GL_{i}({\mathbf{F}})]
-submodule of
\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})
. Let
\mathbf{D}
be the linear closure of
\{L_{A}(\mathbf{D}_{i})\mid i\in[k],\rk(A)=i\}
. We then need to show that
\mathbf{D}
is the
m
-ary part of a clonoid. (Then, by Theorem
49
, and
m>k
we are done.)

We already know that
\mathbf{D}
is an
\mathbf{R}
-module. Thus, we only need to prove that it is closed under composition with matrices from the inside. So let
f\in\mathbf{D}_{i}
,
A\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times i}
with
\rk(A)=i
and
M\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times m}
. Then, we need to prove that also
L_{A}(f)(MX)\in\mathbf{D}
. If
\rk(MA)<i
, then we get
L_{H}(f)(MX)=0
, by definition of
L_{H}
, so we are done. Otherwise
\rk(MA)=i
, which implies that there is a linear transformation
T\in\GL_{m}({\mathbf{F}})
, such that
MA=TA
. Then, by property (3), we get
L_{A}(f)(MX)=L_{A}(f)(TX)=L_{T^{-1}A}(f)(X)
, which is also in
\mathbf{D}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Theorem 55
.

The lattice of clonoids from
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
to
\mathbf{B}
is isomorphic to
\prod_{i=0}^{k}\Sub(\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B}))
.

Moreover, for
j\leq k
, the sublattice
\prod_{i=0}^{j}\Sub(\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B}))
corresponds to the clonoids consisting of functions that are generated by their
j
-ary minors.

Proof.

Let
m=k
, and let
(A_{H})_{H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{k}}
be a family of matrices such that
\mathcal{C}(A_{H})=H
, and
A_{H}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{k\times\dim(H)}
. By Proposition
54
(3), the map
L\colon\prod_{i=0}^{j}\Sub(\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B}))\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\mathbf{B}}^{(k)})
given by
L(\mathbf{C}_{0},\mathbf{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{C}_{K})=\sum_{H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{K}}L_{A_{H}}(\mathbf{C}_{\dim(H)})
, is a lattice isomorphism from
\prod_{i=0}^{j}\Sub(\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B}))
to the lattice of
k
-ary parts of clonoids. By Theorem
49
, we are done.

To prove the second statement, note that by the proof Proposition
54
,
\mathcal{C}
is generated by
j
-ary minors if and only if
L_{A}^{-1}(\mathcal{C})=\{0\}
for some
A\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times i}
if and only if
i>j
.
∎

We remark that, in the special case where
k=1
, and
\mathbf{B}
is a field, this description of the clonoid lattice was already derived in
[
11
]
(in fact, an even more explicit description could be computed, as then
\mathbf{M}_{1,1}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{B}[{\mathbf{F}}^{*}]
is a cyclic module).

5.
Subpower Membership Problem

In this section, we discuss the Subpower Membership Problem for 2-nilpotent Mal’cev algebras
\mathbf{A}
. As it was pointed out in
[
14
, Chapter 7]
, to every 2-nilpotent Mal’cev algebra with central series
\mathbf{1}_{A}>\mu>\mathbf{0}_{A}
, one can associate two affine algebras
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{A}/\mu
and
\mathbf{L}=\mu/\Delta_{\mu,\mathbf{1}_{A}}
, such that
\mathbf{A}
is isomorphic to a
central extension
(also called
wreath product
)
\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{L}
, whose domain is
U\times L
, and whose basic operations are of the form

f^{\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{L}}\left(\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
l_{1}\end{bmatrix},\ldots,\begin{bmatrix}u_{n}\\
l_{n}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}f^{\mathbf{U}}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\\
f^{\mathbf{L}}(l_{1},\ldots,l_{n})+\hat{f}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\end{bmatrix},

for some
\hat{f}\colon U^{n}\to L
. In fact, then also all term operations can be represented as above.

Based on this representation, we define its
difference clonoid

\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{L})
, as the set of differences
\hat{t}-\hat{s}
, for terms
t,s
such that
t^{\mathbf{U}}=s^{\mathbf{U}}
and
t^{\mathbf{L}}=s^{\mathbf{L}}
. As it was shown in
[
19
]
,
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{L})
is indeed a clonoid from
\mathbf{U}
to
\mathbf{L}
extended by some constant
0
, and the subpower membership problem
\SMP(\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{L})
reduces in polynomial time to the following problem:

\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C})

Input:
tuples
\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m}\in A^{n}

Output:
a generating set of
\{f(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m})\mid f\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}\}\leq\mathbf{B}^{n}
.

Here
f(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n})
is computed component-wise. We are going to prove the following:

Proposition 56
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a clonoid between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, such that
\mathbf{A}
is polynomially equivalent to a finite vector space
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
, and
\mathbf{B}
is a coprime module. Then
\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C})
is solvable in polynomial time.

Proof.

Let us first assume that
\mathbf{A}={\mathbf{F}}^{k}
. By Proposition
54
(5), we know that there are submodules
\mathbf{C}_{i}\leq\mathbf{M}_{i,k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})
for all
i\in[k]_{0}
, such that
\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
is generated by
\{L_{A}(f)\mid A\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times i},\rk(A)=i,f\in\mathbf{C}_{i}\}
. In fact, if for every
H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{m}
of dimension
\dim(H)\leq k
, we denote by
A_{H}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{\dim{(H)}\times m}
a matrix with
\mathcal{C}(A_{H})=H
, then Proposition
54
implies that
f\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
can be uniquely written as
f=\sum_{H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\dim(H)=i}L_{A_{H}}(f_{H})
, for some
f_{H}\in\mathbf{C}_{i}
.

Now let us consider an instance of
\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C})
. Using our established matrix notation, for given input matrices
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{m\times k}
, the task is to compute a generating set of the module
\{(f(X_{1}),f(X_{2}),\ldots,f(X_{n}))\mid f\in\mathcal{C}^{m}\}\leq\mathbf{B}
.

Let
\mathfrak{H}=\{H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{m}\mid H\leq\mathcal{C}(X_{i}),
for some
i\in[n]\}
. Note that the size of
\mathfrak{H}
is linear in
n
. By picking a basis for every such space
H\in\mathfrak{H}
, we can enumerate matrices
(A_{H})_{H\in\mathfrak{H}}
as above in time
O(|A|^{k}\cdot n)=O(n)
.

From Proposition
54
(1)-(3) (and the decomposition
f=\sum_{H\leq{\mathbf{F}}^{k},\dim(H)=i}L_{A_{H}}(f_{H})
, for
f\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
), it follows that for all
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
, the equation
f(X_{i})=g(X_{i})
holds, if and only if
L_{A_{H}}(f_{H})=L_{A_{H}}(g_{H})
, for all
H
with
H\subseteq\mathcal{C}(X_{i})
. Hence
\{(f(X_{1}),f(X_{2}),\ldots,f(X_{n}))\mid f\in\mathcal{C}^{m}\}
is generated by

\{(g(X_{1}),g(X_{2}),\ldots,g(X_{n}))\mid H\in\mathfrak{H},g=L_{A_{H}}(g_{H}),g_{H}\in\mathbf{M}_{\dim(H),k}({\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{B})\}.

Computing
L_{A_{H}}(g_{H})(X_{i})
can be done in time
O(m^{3})
by Proposition
54
(4). Therefore
\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C})
is solvable in polynomial time
O(nm^{3})

In the case that
\mathbf{A}
is only polynomially equivalent to
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
, then we still know (cf. Lemma
27
), that it contains terms
x+^{y}z=x-y+z
,
-^{y}(x)=y-x+y
and
(r^{y})_{r\in{\mathbf{F}}}
with
r^{y}(x)=rx+(1-r)y
, such that the algebra
\mathbf{A}^{y}=(A,+^{y},y,-^{y},(r^{y})_{r\in{\mathbf{F}}})
is isomorphic to
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
for every substitution of
y
by some constant. As pointed out in Lemma
27
(1), the uniform generation by
k
-ary minors of operations from
{\mathbf{F}}^{k}
and
\mathbf{B}
, lifts to uniform generation by
k+1
-ary minors of operations from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
. Similar statements apply to the uniformly representable operations in Lemma
36
, and in the proof of Proposition
54
. We leave the proof to the reader. Following the very same argument as for
\mathbf{A}={\mathbf{F}}^{k}
, we get that
\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C})
is solvable in polynomial time
O(nm^{3})
.
∎

Theorem 57
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{L}
be a finite 2-nilpotent Mal’cev algebra in finite language, such that
\mathbf{U}
is polynomially equivalent to a vector space. Then
\SMP(\mathbf{A})
is solvable in polynomial time.

Proof.

By
[
19
, Theorem 10]
,
\SMP(\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{L})
reduces to
\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C})
in polynomial time, where
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{L})
is a clonoid from
\mathbf{U}
to
\mathbf{L}
, extended by a constant
0
. In particular,
\mathcal{C}
is a clonoid from
\mathbf{U}
to the abelian group
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=(L,+,0,-)
. If
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}
is of coprime order to
\mathbf{U}
, we are done by Proposition
56
. Else, note that
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}
can be directly decomposed into
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{1}\times\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{2}
, such that
\mathbf{L}_{1}^{\prime}
is coprime to
\mathbf{U}
, and
\mathbf{L}_{2}^{\prime}
is of size
p^{n}
, where the prime
p
is the characteristic of
\mathbf{U}
. Furthermore,
\Clo(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})
contains the unary maps
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
that project to
\mathbf{L}_{1}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2}^{\prime}
respectively.

Thus, every
f\in\mathcal{C}
can also be decomposed into
f=\pi_{1}f+\pi_{2}f
, where
\pi_{1}f
is from a
(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{L}_{1}^{\prime})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}_{1}
, and
\pi_{2}f
is from a
(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{L}_{2}^{\prime})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. We can solve
\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C}_{1})
in polynomial time by Proposition
56
. Moreover, it can be seen (similar to the proof that supernilpotent Mal’cev algebras have polynomial time solvable SMP in
[
27
]
) that
\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C}_{2})
can be solved in polynomial time. More precisely, we can be derived from the fact
\mathbf{A}
has a finite signature that there is a constant
C\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that every
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
can be written as a sum of functions
f_{I}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
with
I\subseteq[m]
and
|I|\leq C
, such that
f_{I}
only depend on the coordinates in
I
(see
[
17
, Proof of Theorem 6.1]
). This means that
\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C}_{2})
can be solved in time
O(nm^{C})
, by evaluating all operations
f_{I}\in\mathcal{C}
with essential coordinates
I
,
|I|\leq C
at the input. We leave the details of this proof to the reader. It follows that
\SMP(\mathbf{A})
is in P.
∎

6.
Sharp lower bounds for the full clonoid

In this section, we provide an elementary lower bound on the number of generators of the full clonoid between finite modules in general.

Theorem 58
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite
\mathbf{R}_{A}
-module and
\mathbf{B}
be a finite
\mathbf{R}_{B}
-module. Assume that the full clonoid
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}
is generated by the
m
-ary functions, for some
m
. Then
m\geq\frac{\log|A|}{\log|R_{A}|}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a generating set of
\mathbf{B}
of minimal cardinality. Then, for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
G_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}^{(n)}
consist of all functions of the form
\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{g}(X)
with
\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{g}(\mathbf{a})=g\in G
, and
\delta_{\mathbf{a}}^{g}(\mathbf{x})=0
else, with
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}^{n}
. Note that
G_{n}
is a generating set of the
\mathbf{R}_{B}
-module
\mathbf{B}^{\mathbf{A}^{n}}
of minimal cardinality
c\cdot|A|^{n}
.

Now, assume that for
n\geq m
,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}^{(n)}
is in the clonoid generated by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
(or, equivalently,
G_{m}
), and that
\mathbf{R}_{A}
is a right module. Then,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}^{(n)}
must be equal to the linear closure of all functions
g(M(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{t})
such that
M
is a
m\times n
-matrix over
\mathbf{R}_{A}
, with
g\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}^{(m)}
. There are at most
|G_{m}||R_{A}|^{mn}
many distinct such functions. By the minimality of
|G_{n}|
we get

|G_{m}||R_{A}|^{mn}=c|A|^{m}|R_{A}|^{mn}\geq c|A|^{n}=|G_{n}|.

Considering the logarithm of the second inequality we get:

(17)

m\geq\frac{n\log|A|}{\log|A|+n\log|R_{A}|}.

Since (
17
) holds for every
n\geq m
, we obtain
m\geq\frac{\log|A|}{\log|R_{A}|}
.
∎

We remark that for vector spaces
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, this was already pointed out in
[
33
]
, together with a proof that, in the coprime case,
k
-ary functions suffice to generate the full clonoid. (Note however that this can also be derived from Proposition
54
(5)).

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied clonoids between finite modules. We proved that there are only finitely many clonoids from a finite vector space
\mathbf{A}
to a coprime module
\mathbf{B}
, which is also in accordance to Conjecture
1
.

Our proof (and, as we argued in Section
3
, also the results for distributive modules
\mathbf{A}
in
[
26
]
) uses that the operations from
A
to
B
are uniformly generated by
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-minors. It is likely to expect that the same method works for all cases in the scope of Conjecture
1
. Thus, we also conjecture that the following statement is true:

Conjecture 59
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite abelian
p
-group, and let
\mathbf{R}
be a ring (considered as regular
\mathbf{R}
-module), such that
p
is invertible in
\mathbf{R}
. Then, there is a
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{R}}
is uniformly generated by
k
-ary
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{R})
-minors.

Note that, by Proposition
21
, Conjecture
59
implies the same statement holds for all abelian groups
\mathbf{A}
such that
|A|
is invertible in
\mathbf{R}
. Any module has clearly an abelian group as a reduct, thus we moreover get the same statement for modules
\mathbf{A}
. Last, by Corollary
35
, Conjecture
59
further implies that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}
is uniformly generated by
k
-ary
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-minors for some
k
, for
every

\mathbf{R}
-module
\mathbf{B}
. In particular, this means that Conjecture
59
implies Conjecture
1
.

If we consider only abelian
p
-groups
\mathbf{A}
, then Theorem
49
covers all elementary abelian groups
\mathbf{A}={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{n}
, while
[
26
]
covers all cyclic groups
\mathbf{A}={\mathbb{Z}}_{p^{n}}
. Therefore, the easiest open problem is the following:

Question 60
.

Does Conjecture
59
hold for
\mathbf{A}={\mathbb{Z}}_{p^{2}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, where
p
is a prime?

Any progress on this question (and Conjecture
1
in general) likely hinges on combining the uniformly representable operations discussed in
[
26
]
and the underlying paper. Note that, Theorem
58
and
[
26
, Lemma 5.1]
both only give us a lower bound of
k\geq 2
on the arity of the generating minors.

In the context of the subpower membership problem, the second author conjectured in
[
19
]
, that
\SMP(\mathbf{A})
of all finite 2-nilpotent Mal’cev algebras
\mathbf{A}
is polynomial time solvable. Our results in Section
5
confirm this for the case, in which the quotient of
\mathbf{A}/\mu
with respect to some central congruence
\mu
is polynomially equivalent to a vector space. By building on the results in
[
26
]
, we hope to also positively answer it in the case where
\mathbf{A}/\mu
is polynomially equivalent to a distributive module. This leads to the question:

Question 61
.

Is
\mathrm{CompRep}(\mathcal{C})
in P for every
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
-clonoid
\mathcal{C}
, with
\mathbf{A}
polynomially equivalent to a finite distributive module, and
\mathbf{B}
to a module?

We did not discuss any questions about equational theories in this paper. However, as it was exemplifies in
[
28
]
and Section 3 of
[
18
]
, finite basedness results for 2-nilpotent algebras can be derived from finite axiomatizations of the corresponding difference clonoids. Thus, last we ask:

Question 62
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
be finite modules, and let
\mathbf{C}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}
be a finitely generated clonoid. Does
\mathcal{C}
(or, more precisely, the multisorted algebra consisting of
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
and
F
) always have a finite equational basis?

Due to the examples in
[
28
]
and
[
18
]
it is natural to expect uniformly representable operations (or, more precisely the formulas defining them), to play also a central role in answering Question
62
.
