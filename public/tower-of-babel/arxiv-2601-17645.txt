: A Multimodal Multilingual Multicultural Benchmark for LLMs’ Contextual and Cultural Knowledge and Thinking
Xilin Jiang, Qiaolin Wang, Junkai Wu, Xiaomin He, Zhongweiyang Xu, Yinghao Ma, Minshuo Piao, Kaiyi Yang, Xiuwen Zheng, Riki Shimizu, Yicong Chen, Arsalan Firoozi, Gavin Mischler, Sukru Samet Dindar, Richard Antonello, Linyang He, Tsun-An Hsieh Xulin Fan, Yulun Wu, Yuesheng Ma, Chaitanya Amballa, Weixiong Chen, Jiarui Hai, Ruisi Li, Vishal Choudhari, Cong Han, Yinghao Aaron Li, Adeen Flinker, Mounya Elhilali, Emmanouil Benetos, Mark Hasegawa-Johnson, Romit Roy Choudhury, Nima Mesgarani, [4pt] Columbia University, University of Illinois Urbana - Champaign, University of Washington, [-2pt]Johns Hopkins University, New York University, Queen Mary University of London, [-2pt]Google, Meta[3pt]{xj2289, qw2443, xh2369}@columbia.edu, junkaiwu@uw.edu, zx21@illinois.edu, nima@ee.columbia.edu

Abstract
Internet audio-visual clips convey meaning through time-varying sound and motion, which extend beyond what text alone can represent. To examine whether AI models can understand such signals in human cultural contexts, we introduce AVMeme Exam, a human-curated benchmark of over one thousand iconic Internet sounds and videos spanning speech, songs, music, and sound effects. Each meme is paired with a unique Q&A assessing levels of understanding from surface content to context and emotion to usage and world knowledge, along with metadata such as original year, transcript, summary, and sensitivity. We systematically evaluate state-of-the-art multimodal large language models (MLLMs) alongside human participants using this benchmark. Our results reveal a consistent limitation: current models perform poorly on textless music and sound effects, and struggle to think in context and in culture compared to surface content. These findings highlight a key gap in human-aligned multimodal intelligence and call for models that can perceive contextually and culturally beyond the surface of what they hear and see. To bridge these gaps, we introduce
AVMeme Exam
, a manual collection of over one thousand iconic Internet sounds and videos annotated with human-annotated metadata and human-written questions probing surface understanding, contextual inference, emotion, humor, usage, and world knowledge. Evaluation of state-of-the-art MLLMs and human participants reveals consistent limitations in models’ ability to recognize, interpret, and culturally situate such clips. Together, our contributions establish AVMeme Exam as a comprehensive resource for diagnosing contextual and cultural weaknesses of AIs and guiding future progress in human-aligned multimodal intelligence.

2.1
Collection

The collection of AVMeme Exam is guided by three pillars:
Human-collected
: all clips are selected and annotated by 27 audio and NLP researchers, raised in the U.S., China, Japan, India, Middle East, and etc. They personally recognize and use these memes, ensuring that each entry is chosen with human judgment rather than automatic web scraping.
Audio-centric
: sound serves as the primary media of meaning, complemented by visual, spanning speech, songs, music, and wordless sound effects.
Multicultural-grounded
: the diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds of contributors enables coverage of memes originating from East and South Asia, Middle East, Europe, and North America. Collectively, these pillars ensure that AVMeme Exam reflects authentic, culturally embedded, real-world multimodal communication.

In total, we collect
1,032
audio-visual memes spanning more than ten languages and five sound categories, with their distributions shown in Figure
2
. Each meme consists of a video segment (URL, onset, offset) sourced from YouTube (86.1%) and Bilibili (13.9%), most exceeding one million views. It includes human-annotated metadata (summary, transcript, year, etc) and a multiple-choice Q&A. To maximize diversity, we place no restrictions on the videos (topic, region, etc), but safeguards still apply. We prohibit political materials and explicit depictions of sexual, violent, hateful, criminal, or drug-related content. Implicit depictions, verbal or contextual, are annotated using a sensitivity attribute drawn from {
sex, violence/gore, drug/alcohol, race/gender/geography/identity, disadvantaged/disability
}. Despite these safeguards,
some clips may remain sensitive for certain people, and discretion is advised.
The emotion attribute takes one or multiple from {
happy, sad, angry/annoyed, fearful/anxious, surprised/shocked, sarcastic/ironic, cringe/embarrassed, nostalgic, neutral
}. The question type is one of the seven (elaborated later). Below are two examples.

2.2
Verification

Verification is also conducted manually. Nine human verifiers first check every entry, reviewing all metadata fields: transcription, summary, usage, sensitivity, language, video integrity, and the multiple-choice Q&A, on a clip-by-clip basis, and return to contributors to fix if any issues are identified.

While this process ensures syntactic correctness and annotation quality, it is not sufficient to guarantee that the Q&A genuinely tests multimodal understanding, as some questions may still be solvable through unintended shortcuts
46
;
3
. We therefore begin with
text-cheat
detection. We run three LLMs in text-only modes: Gemini 2.5 Flash
6
, Grok 4
39
, and GPT-5.1
30
, with prompt “
without audio, guess the best option
.” Q&As that all three models guess correctly are flagged as
text_cheat
, for which the strong text priors allow the LLMs to infer the clip or the answer without listening or watching. For example,
a famous sci-fi movie from the late 1970s
almost inevitably points to
Star Wars
. Aware of this problem, we explicitly prohibit contributors from mentioning names, authors, historical events, or other giveaway keywords in the Q&A.
A small number of Q&As correctly guessed by all three LLMs are still retained, since they may be guessed by chance or by choosing the most well-known option. For example, when
John Williams
, the most famous film composer, appears among other composers, it is typically selected regardless of question phrasing. The full dataset is denoted
meme-full
(1032 memes), and removing all
text_cheat
items yields
meme-main
(846 memes). From
meme-full
to
meme-main
, the deaf-and-blind guessing accuracy drops from
34.7
\rightarrow
20.4
,
37.9
\rightarrow
24.3
, and
40.9
\rightarrow
27.9
for Gemini 2.5 Flash, Grok 4, and GPT-5.1, respectively, showing that
meme-main
represents a more exclusive test of multimodal understanding.

Next, we assess
visual-cheat
. While we treat visual as a supplemental signal, it may trivially reveal the answer, especially through
on-screen text
. We manually inspect each video and assign a
visual_hint
attribute with one or multiple values: {
no text
,
irrelevant text
,
transcription
,
title/name
,
visual contains solution
}. The last and the most severe category (labeled as
visual_cheat
) denotes cases where the visual stream collapses the intended reasoning problem into simple OCR or object detection instead of genuine multimodal understanding. For
visual_cheat
clips, we omit the visual input when evaluating audio–visual models.

These measures together ensure that we are evaluating MLLMs’ genuine multimodal understanding abilities without text or visual shortcuts.

2.3
Question Types

Q&As are categorized into seven question types by human verifiers, not by contributors, ensuring consistent labeling while allowing contributors to draft Q&As creatively without prior constraints.

\ulcorner
The first two types ask for the immediate information of the clip: what is heard or said. It reflects the most basic level of multimodal comprehension.
\lrcorner

A
udio Analysis
focuses on what the sound alone reveals: prosody, rhythm, style, or other audible patterns. They check whether a model can genuinely analyze audio rather than rely on the text.
Example
:
What kind of musical processing was applied to the human voice?

L
anguage Analysis
tests recognition and parsing of the spoken words, including what is said and how it functions within a sentence or conversation. The answer depends directly on the linguistic content of the clip.
Example
:
What one thing does the speaker claim about himself?

\ulcorner
The middle three types move beyond literal content to how humans grasp meaning, feeling, and humor in a clip. It often requires educated thinking.
\lrcorner

C
ontextual Inference
evaluates whether a model can understand the situation behind the clip: what the speaker intends, what they mean, or what is happening in the scene. The correct answer comes from interpreting the context, not just rephrasing the words.
Example
:
Which situation best matches the tone and intent of this moment?

E
motion Analysis
asks the model to identify the feeling expressed, based on tone, delivery, pacing, or the effect on the audience. They check whether the model can pick up how the moment is meant to feel.
Example
:

What will people likely feel when they first listen to this piece?

H
umor & Popularity
explores why a clip became funny, iconic, or widely shared. They involve noticing unexpected reactions, exaggerated behavior, or other traits that made the moment memorable online.
Example
:
Which of the following is not a reason that the clip is humorous?

Discussion
. Taken together, our results and analyses reveal a consistent pattern: MLLMs perform reliably on content-level questions, particularly when rich linguistic cues are present. Their performance drops sharply when understanding requires contextual thinking or cultural grounding, and on textless audio or less common languages. Even the strongest models like Gemini 3 Pro exhibit the largest errors in these categories, and longer thinking often does not help. This underscores that pragmatic and cultural comprehension remain open challenges for multimodal intelligence.

Lesson
. Beyond increasing exposure to Internet audio and video, our findings suggest that multimodal intelligence should be more firmly grounded in the human world. Understanding audio–visual signals is not merely about perceiving isolated sounds or images, but about connecting them to how people interpret, feel about, and use them within context and culture. This points to the importance of richer, human-centered annotations that capture emotion, usage, and cultural meaning, along with training objectives that prioritize alignment with human interpretation over surface-level recognition.

4
Conclusion

We presented AVMeme Exam, a multimodal, multilingual, and multicultural benchmark that evaluates whether MLLMs can understand not only what is heard or seen, but also how meaning is constructed through context, emotion, usage, and shared cultural knowledge. Our analysis reveals consistent limitations: models perform substantially worse on textless audio, struggle with contextual and cultural reasoning, and often fail to align with human interpretations. These findings highlight a key gap between current multimodal capabilities and the human expectations of intent-aware, culturally grounded understanding. In future work, we plan to expand AVMeme Exam to broader cultures, linguistic communities, and media ecosystems, creating a more globally representative testbed for multimodal understanding. We also aim to develop methods that could address the challenges revealed by our benchmark, advancing progress toward human-aligned multimodal intelligence.

Limitations

While our AVMeme Exam spans diverse languages and media, several limitations remain. First, cultural coverage is inevitably shaped by our contributors, who are highly-educated researchers aged 22–35 and whose perspectives do not fully represent the global, intergenerational landscape of meme culture. Second, meme meaning shifts over time. Our annotations capture contemporary (by the end of 2025) interpretations and cannot anticipate future cultural drift (which is impossible). Third, most current MLLMs impose strict audio and video length limits, requiring clips to be truncated to 30 seconds, which may omit context essential for real-world understanding. Fourth, our evaluation focuses on controlled multiple-choice Q&A on single clips, whereas real-world application often requires multi-turn dialogue, personalization, and open-ended scenarios. Fifth, meme interpretation is inherently subjective. Although viewers may converge on a majority understanding, alternative readings are equally valid and cannot be fully reflected in a standardized benchmark. Taken together, these considerations suggest that AVMeme Exam should be viewed as a reference benchmark, diagnostic and comparative, rather than an absolute ground truth for human multimedia culture. Nevertheless, the lesson from AVMeme Exam, that current multimodal AIs remain weaker at audio–visual understanding than text and at contextual & cultural thinking than surface content, still holds.

Ethical Considerations

AVMeme Exam is designed as a research benchmark to study multimodal understanding of audio–visual memes, with careful attention to ethical considerations in data collection, annotation, and verification. All audio–visual clips are human-curated by contributors who personally recognize and use these memes, rather than being automatically scraped, and are drawn exclusively from publicly available online videos, with no private, paywalled, or confidential content included. To mitigate potential harm, we prohibit political materials and explicit depictions of sexual, violent, hateful, criminal, or drug-related content, and clearly annotate clips with implicit or contextual sensitivity using sensitivity tags to enable filtering and informed use. The benchmark is intended for research and evaluation, not for safety-critical or high-stakes deployment, and any biases or cultural limitations it reveals should be understood as limitations of current models and of the dataset itself, motivating further work toward more human-aligned, culturally grounded multimodal systems.

Human evaluation was conducted under an Institutional Review Board (IRB) protocol. All participants completed the evaluation online in approximately 30 minutes and were compensated $15.

Appendix A
Collection & Verification Procedures

AVMeme Exam is curated by 27 audio and NLP researchers affiliated with universities and companies in the US and the UK, aged between 22 and 35, the majority of whom are current PhD students. To coordinate collection, all raw entries are first recorded in a shared Google Sheet, organized into separate sheets by language and sound category, which helps prevent duplicate submissions across different contributors. The complete raw data collection process took approximately three months.

All contributors participated voluntarily and with informed consent. They are listed as co-authors of the paper. To mitigate potential ethical risks in data curation, contributors were instructed to rely only on publicly available content, avoid political or religious material, and exclude explicit sexual, graphic violent, or hateful content. No personal data of contributors are collected or released.

All contributors were given and agreed to follow the same collection and annotation guidelines:

•

Meme selection:
Memes are chosen based on broad public recognition and sourced from original or highly circulated online videos, preferably from YouTube. A view count of at least one million is a soft requirement.

•

Clip extraction:
Each meme is trimmed to a short segment (1–30 seconds). At most two distinct memes may be extracted from the same source video.

•

Audio-centric requirement:
Memes must be identifiable primarily from audio alone. Clips whose meaning relies heavily on visual information are excluded.

•

Multilingual and cross-cultural coverage:
Memes may span multiple languages and cultures, and are explicitly labeled while retaining their original sound category.

•

Standardized metadata:
Each entry includes structured annotations such as source URLs, temporal boundaries, original appearance date, language, sound category, transcription (when applicable), summary, emotion, sensitivity, and typical usage.

•

Content safeguards:
Political and religious content, explicit sexual material, and graphic violence are excluded. Mild or implicit sensitive content is allowed only with explicit sensitivity labels.

•

Question design:
Each meme is paired with a unique multiple-choice question targeting different levels of understanding. Contributors are encouraged to design challenging and creative questions, and are not required to assign questions to any predefined categories. Question types are labeled later during verification by independent annotators.

•

Leakage prevention:
Questions and answer choices must not disclose identifying information (e.g., titles, names, slogans, or sources) to prevent textual shortcuts and ensure genuine audio-visual evaluation.

For leakage prevention, we label Q&As that can be correctly guessed by all of Gemini 2.5 Flash, Grok 4, and GPT-5.1 in text-only settings, with prompt “

.”, and return them to the original contributors for revision. This process is repeated for two rounds.

AVMeme Exam is verified by nine human verifiers. To reduce bias and confirmation effects, we assign each meme to verifiers who did not author the corresponding entry whenever possible. For less common languages (e.g., Persian and Telugu), where linguistic expertise is limited, original contributors assist in verification to ensure correctness.

The verification process is conducted in a distributed manner through a web-based interface. For each meme, verifiers review all annotated attributes and the multiple-choice Q&A. For each attribute, verifiers select one of several actions:
Ok
,
Wrong
, or
Wrong, but I can fix
. Identified issues are either corrected directly by the verifier (if just a simple typo or video-question mismatch) or returned to the original contributor for revision.

After validating all metadata fields, verifiers assign the question to one of the predefined question types. Only after completing all checks and assigning a question type can a verifier proceed to the next sample. This structured, attribute-level verification ensures annotation consistency, correctness, and alignment with the intended evaluation goals.

Finally, one human verifier aggregates all verified entries into a single file, manually reviews the merged results to remove duplicate entries, and performs a final pass to ensure consistency in question type assignments across the dataset.

Appendix B
Human Evaluation Details

We conduct a controlled human evaluation to establish a human reference for AVMeme Exam, following Columbia University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) protocol. A total of 20 participants were recruited, including 10 native English speakers and 10 native Chinese speakers, all aged 18–35 and self-reported frequent users of online video platforms (e.g., YouTube, Bilibili, TikTok). Participants grew up in the U.S. or China and were residing in the U.S. at the time of the study, due to practical recruitment constraints. All participants completed the study individually, without web search, collaboration, or external assistance.

Each evaluation session lasted approximately 30 minutes, and participants were compensated with a $15 prepaid card upon completion. Each participant was assigned 37 or 38 audio-visual meme clips, resulting in 750 total human-evaluated samples. To ensure linguistic and cultural appropriateness, participants only evaluated clips in their native language and cultural context, as well as clips without spoken language (e.g., music or sound effects). Screenshots of the session are shown in Figure
6
.

Before the main evaluation, participants completed a background survey collecting demographic information (age range, native language), Internet usage frequency, and self-reported familiarity with online memes and viral media. Each participant was assigned a unique anonymous identifier, and no personally identifiable information was collected. Survey responses were linked only to these anonymous identifiers and were used solely to describe the participant pool and report aggregate evaluation metadata.

For each sample, participants were first shown the video clip alone (or the audio clip for the visual cheating condition, as we enforce for LLMs), without the associated question or answer options. They were asked whether they had previously seen and were familiar with the clip. Only after recording this response were participants presented with the corresponding multiple-choice question and answer options. This two-stage design prevents participants’ familiarity judgments from being influenced by question difficulty.

Participants then answered the same multiple-choice questions used for model evaluation, under identical answer formats and constraints. Human responses were recorded and later aggregated by familiarity category for comparison with model performance, as illustrated in Fig.
4
. This protocol ensures that human evaluation is conducted under controlled, reproducible conditions aligned with the LLM evaluation setting.

C.1
Study on On-screen Text

Real-world videos often contain on-screen text that may partially assist or hack multimodal understanding. To analyze how such visual text affects model performance, we conduct a focused study on clips with different degrees of on-screen textual hints.

We (9 human verifiers) manually label each clip based on the strongest type of text appearing in the visual stream. Specifically, we categorize clips into five groups: (1)
visual contains (part of) solution
(
visual_cheat
) (2)
title, name, or other keywords
, which reveal identifiable entities (3)
transcription
, where the spoken content or its translation is displayed as subtitles (4)
no text
. These categories form a spectrum of visual hints, ranging from the most severe to the mildest.

For each category, we aggregate model accuracy across all applicable clips and report the results in Figure
7
. Accuracy peaks when the visual stream directly reveals the solution and decreases monotonically as text hints weaken from titles and keywords to transcription and ultimately to no text. This pattern is consistent across models, indicating that on-screen text acts as a strong shortcut. For some models, the no-text condition is slightly worse than transcription, as many textless clips involve music or sound effects, which are inherently more challenging.

C.2
Study on Meme’s Year

We additionally annotate each meme clip with its original year, defined as the upload year of the source YouTube or Bilibili video, or the debut or premiere year for music, songs, and movies. We group clips into coarse temporal spans and aggregate model accuracy within each group, as shown in Figure
8
.

Across models, performance peaks on memes originating between 1980 and 2000, while accuracy drops for both older (
<
1980) and more recent (
>
2020) clips. We hypothesize that this pattern reflects uneven Internet coverage: middle-era memes are well represented and repeatedly circulated online, whereas very old or very recent content is less consistently captured in training data.
