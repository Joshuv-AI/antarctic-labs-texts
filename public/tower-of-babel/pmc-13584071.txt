Do papers tell the whole story? A benchmark and framework for uncovering hidden implementation gaps in bioinformatics
Authors: Tianxiang Xu, Xiaoyan Zhu, Xin Lai, Xin Lian, Sizhe Dang, Hangyu Cheng, Jiayin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs bioinformatics software is increasingly applied across a broader range of scenarios and the rapid development of large language models (LLMs) further lowers the barriers to software use and development, the composition of the bioinformatics research community is undergoing substantial change. Consequently, a growing number of researchers require a deeper understanding of methodological details and software behavior. In this context, systematically analyzing the relationship between paper descriptions and code implementations is emerging as an important new challenge in the field. To address this challenge, we introduce paper-code consistency analysis as a new research perspective and construct BioCon, the first benchmark dataset for paper-code consistency analysis in bioinformatics. Furthermore, we develop a unified cross-modal analysis framework to systematically investigate this problem from three perspectives: sentence-level detection, cross-modal retrieval, and project-level assessment. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed framework can effectively model the semantic relationships between scientific publications and software implementations. Further case studies reveal that paper-code inconsistency is not a single phenomenon but arises from multiple underlying causes, among which Author-Perceived Non-Essential Details represents the most prevalent category. These findings suggest that paper-code consistency analysis is not merely a technical problem but also raises broader discussions regarding knowledge dissemination, the boundaries of code disclosure, and community norms. We hope that this work will encourage the bioinformatics community to re-examine the relationship between scientific publications and software implementations while providing a foundation for future research in paper-code consistency analysis.Keywords: bioinformatics software, cross-modal analysis, paper-code consistency

Introduction

Bioinformatics, as a critical interdisciplinary field integrating modern biology and information technology, has become an indispensable foundation for life sciences research [1, 2]. With the completion of the Human Genome Project and the rapid advancement of high-throughput sequencing technologies, the continuous accumulation of massive biological data has significantly accelerated progress in bioinformatics research [3]. Against this backdrop, an increasing number of bioinformatics research outputs are disseminated not only through scientific publications but also through open-source software repositories [4], and are widely applied in practical scenarios such as genomic analysis, genetic disease screening, cancer diagnosis and treatment, and precision medicine [5–7]. Software is no longer merely a by-product of scientific research; rather, it has increasingly become an important medium for knowledge dissemination and application in bioinformatics [8].

Meanwhile, the user population of bioinformatics software is undergoing significant transformation. Traditionally, such software primarily served professional researchers with strong computational backgrounds and domain expertise, who were generally capable of understanding software implementation logic by reading papers, examining source code, and leveraging their domain knowledge. However, as bioinformatics expands beyond specialized research settings into broader application domains, its user base has gradually extended to clinicians, healthcare practitioners, and interdisciplinary researchers. In particular, with the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies and large language models (LLMs), an increasing number of researchers without formal training in bioinformatics are becoming involved in the development and use of bioinformatics analysis workflows. While the barriers to software usage are decreasing, the demand for understanding methodological details, parameter configurations, implementation logic, and algorithmic behavior continues to grow among these users [9].

In the field of bioinformatics, scientific publications and software code play distinct yet complementary roles. Publications typically focus on algorithmic design, theoretical foundations, and experimental results, whereas code translates these methods into executable software systems and contains numerous implementation-level details, including parameter settings, preprocessing procedures, and engineering optimization strategies [10, 11]. For the growing number of researchers without extensive bioinformatics backgrounds, relying solely on publications often fails to provide complete implementation information, while analyzing code alone may not adequately convey the methodological motivations and theoretical foundations behind the software. Consequently, establishing correspondence between paper descriptions and code implementations has become increasingly important for understanding and using bioinformatics software.

In fact, this demand is already widespread within the bioinformatics community. For well-known software projects such as AlphaFold [12], Seurat [13], and STAR [14], users frequently raise questions in community forums and GitHub Issues (https://github.com/google-deepmind/alphafold/issues/1071)(https://github.com/satijalab/seurat/issues/10297) (https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR/issues/2684) regarding issues such as which code components implement a specific step described in a paper, where default parameters are defined, or whether a particular algorithmic detail is executed exactly as described in the publication. These questions do not necessarily imply the existence of software defects or errors between papers and code. Rather, they reflect the growing need among an expanding user population to understand the correspondence between scientific publications and software implementations. Implementation details that may be implicitly understood by domain experts are often far less obvious to users from diverse backgrounds. Therefore, systematically analyzing the relationship between papers and code, and helping researchers understand the actual implementation process of software, is emerging as an important new challenge.

We argue that, driven by these ongoing changes in bioinformatics, the relationship between papers and code is extending beyond the traditional concept of reproducibility [15] toward broader issues involving software understanding, knowledge dissemination, and scientific traceability. As software is increasingly reused, extended, and deployed in diverse application settings, researchers are becoming concerned not only with whether software can be executed successfully, but also with how methods are implemented, which details are explicitly documented, which knowledge remains embedded in the code, and how different forms of knowledge are transferred between papers and software implementations.

Motivated by this observation, we propose paper-code consistency analysis as a new research perspective for systematically analyzing the semantic correspondence between methodological descriptions in scientific publications and software implementations. It is important to emphasize that paper-code consistency analysis is not intended to determine whether software is correct, nor is it designed to identify scientific misconduct. Instead, it aims to help researchers better understand software implementations and the underlying processes of knowledge representation by analyzing inconsistencies between papers and code and exploring their potential causes.

Nevertheless, research on paper-code relationships remains at an early stage. On the one hand, the emergence of LLMs has made automated consistency analysis increasingly feasible. On the other hand, the bioinformatics community still lacks systematic studies regarding the definition, boundaries, and evaluation criteria of paper-code consistency. Therefore, before deeper discussions can be conducted, it is necessary to establish a unified data foundation and analytical framework.

To this end, we construct BioCon, the first benchmark dataset for paper-code consistency analysis in bioinformatics, and propose a unified cross-modal framework for modeling semantic consistency between paper descriptions and software implementations. Based on this benchmark, we systematically investigate paper-code consistency analysis from three perspectives: sentence-level detection, cross-modal retrieval, and project-level assessment. Furthermore, we conduct case studies on real-world bioinformatics software projects to investigate the underlying causes and manifestations of paper-code inconsistency in practical scenarios.

Based on these research questions, this study not only demonstrates the feasibility of automated paper-code consistency analysis but also reveals the underlying causes of inconsistencies between paper descriptions and code implementations in real-world bioinformatics software. For example, in some projects, developers may consider certain implementation details as non-essential information and therefore choose not to fully disclose the corresponding code. Such inconsistencies may reflect different understandings between software developers, researchers, and users regarding knowledge dissemination and software development practices, for which a unified consensus has not yet been established. As bioinformatics applications continue to expand and user demands continue to evolve, important questions remain regarding which information should be considered essential implementation knowledge, which aspects should be consistently represented across papers and code, and how appropriate community standards should be established. We hope that this work will not only advance automated consistency analysis techniques but also encourage the bioinformatics community to reconsider the boundaries of implementation disclosure, mechanisms of knowledge dissemination, and the development of consistency standards.

Related work

In recent years, AI and large-scale pretrained models have achieved remarkable progress, demonstrating strong performance in various tasks, including natural language processing, code retrieval, and code generation [16–18]. These advances have made it increasingly feasible to automatically analyze the relationship between scientific publications and software implementations. However, to the best of our knowledge, existing studies have not systematically investigated the consistency between paper descriptions and code implementations. Instead, related research has primarily focused on three adjacent areas: research reproducibility, code-text alignment, and software quality assessment.

Research Reproducibility. Research on reproducibility primarily investigates whether scientific findings can be independently reproduced, including aspects such as the availability of datasets and source code, the reconstructability of experimental environments, and the executability of analytical workflows [19, 20]. For example, Siddiq et al. [21] analyzed papers published in the software engineering domain over the past five years and found that only 13.3% of papers were free from reproducibility-related issues.

However, such studies typically consider whether experiments can be reproduced as the primary objective, focusing mainly on software while paying relatively limited attention to the corresponding scientific publications. Importantly, successful reproduction does not necessarily indicate consistency between methodological descriptions and code implementations. A paper may omit critical implementation steps, simplify methodological details, or even contain discrepancies with the actual implementation. These issues may not directly result in reproduction failure, but they can affect how subsequent researchers understand the underlying methodology, correctly utilize the software, and further extend or reuse the approach. Therefore, the objective of paper-code consistency analysis differs fundamentally from traditional reproducibility research. Rather than determining whether an experiment can be repeated, it focuses on analyzing the semantic correspondence between knowledge representations in scientific publications and their actual realization in software implementations.

Code-Text Alignment. In software engineering, extensive research has been conducted on modeling the relationship between natural language descriptions and source code [22], including tasks such as code retrieval [23, 24] and code generation [25]. These studies generally assume that a meaningful correspondence exists between natural language descriptions and code, aiming to learn cross-modal representations to improve retrieval accuracy or code generation performance [26]. However, the problem investigated in this work is fundamentally different. Instead of asking which code segment is most relevant to a given textual description, paper-code consistency analysis further asks: Is the scientific sentence described in the paper actually implemented in the corresponding code?

Even when existing retrieval models successfully identify relevant code fragments, they cannot determine whether the corresponding paper description contains omissions, deviations, or inaccuracies. In other words, code retrieval and related tasks aim to discover potential associations between text and code, whereas paper-code consistency analysis aims to verify the authenticity, completeness, and semantic alignment of such relationships. Therefore, paper-code consistency analysis is not a direct extension of existing code retrieval tasks but represents a fundamentally different research problem. Consequently, existing software engineering methods and datasets cannot be directly transferred to this emerging task without additional considerations.

Software Quality Assessment. In the bioinformatics domain, previous studies have extensively investigated software quality from various perspectives, including usability, maintainability, documentation completeness, coding practices, and long-term sustainability [8, 27–29]. These studies have significantly advanced software engineering practices for bioinformatics applications. However, existing software quality assessments primarily focus on the engineering quality of software itself, while paying limited attention to the relationship between software implementations and scientific publications as a means of knowledge dissemination. As bioinformatics software continues to expand from specialized research environments to broader application scenarios, including clinical practice and interdisciplinary collaborations, an increasing number of users need to rely on both publications and source code to understand software behaviors. Under these circumstances, whether papers and code remain consistent is no longer merely a software engineering concern, but also involves broader issues related to the accuracy of scientific knowledge representation and reliable knowledge dissemination.

Benchmark construction

To systematically study the problem of paper-code consistency analysis, we construct a benchmark dataset for the bioinformatics domain, termed BioCon. This section presents the construction pipeline in detail, including data source collection, paper sentence extraction, code function extraction, and sentence-code pair generation. The overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data source collection

We begin by collecting bioinformatics software projects along with their corresponding research papers. Following the selection criteria established in prior work [27], we identify 54 bioinformatics projects from GitHub that are implemented in Python and are explicitly associated with published papers. The full-text versions of the corresponding publications are then retrieved.

To ensure data quality, we perform rigorous manual filtering. First, all papers are carefully reviewed, and three projects lacking clearly defined methodological sections are excluded. Next, all code repositories are analyzed, and three additional projects with parsing failures or significant structural issues are removed. As a result, 48 high-quality bioinformatics software projects are retained, each containing both a complete paper (PDF) and a functional codebase.

Statistical details are summarized in Table 1, including average code size, number of files, number of functions, cyclomatic complexity, as well as project popularity (measured by stars) and paper citation counts. The statistics indicate that these projects exhibit substantial code scale and structural complexity, rendering manual consistency analysis impractical in real-world scenarios. Furthermore, in terms of impact (stars and citations), the selected projects are representative and influential within the domain, providing a reliable foundation for the paper-code consistency analysis task. Paper sentence extraction

To obtain text units relevant to code implementation, we extract sentence-level content from the full text of each paper. Specifically, we first employ GROBID to perform structured parsing of PDF documents, enabling accurate extraction of textual content along with section information. GROBID is chosen due to its optimization for scholarly documents, allowing reliable identification of sections such as Methods and Experiments.

The extracted text is then segmented into sentence-level units. Based on section metadata, we remove content unrelated to implementation, including references, appendices, conclusions, and acknowledgments. However, even within retained sections, some sentences may still be irrelevant (e.g. background descriptions or result analysis). Therefore, we further apply keyword-based filtering to prioritize sentences closely related to methodological implementation, thereby improving the effectiveness of subsequent sentence-code matching.

Ultimately, for each paper, we obtain a set of high-quality candidate sentences, which are treated as potential methodological descriptions and will be aligned with code functions in subsequent steps.

Code function extraction

Given the software repositories, we perform function-level parsing of source code to construct semantically meaningful code units. Specifically, Python source code is analyzed using abstract syntax trees (AST), from which function-level code snippets are extracted.

Function-level granularity strikes a balance between semantic completeness and manageable input length, making it well-suited for the paper-code consistency analysis task. For each function, we retain the full function body as the primary representation, along with auxiliary metadata such as function name, file path, and comments. These additional features provide useful semantic cues that facilitate both model training and human annotation.

We also apply standard normalization procedures, such as removing empty lines and redundant formatting symbols, to ensure consistency of input sequences. As a result, each software project is represented as a collection of functions, where each function serves as a potential implementation unit for subsequent sentence-code alignment.

Sentence-code pair generation

After obtaining the sets of paper sentences and code functions, we construct sentence-code pairs as samples for the consistency analysis task. For each sentence, we first retrieve the top-10 most semantically similar functions from the corresponding project using cosine similarity. This step reduces the candidate space and lowers the cost of manual annotation.

Next, each sentence is paired with its top-1 candidate function and evaluated by three domain experts (from bioinformatics, software engineering, and computer science). A pair is labeled as consistent only if all three experts unanimously agree that the function correctly implements the functionality described in the sentence. All other cases are excluded from the labeled dataset to minimize noise introduced by subjective judgment. Detailed annotation procedures and representative examples are provided in Supplementary Section A.

This process yields a set of high-confidence positive samples, ensuring clear semantic correspondence between paper descriptions and code implementations while avoiding noise from irrelevant combinations.

Negative sampling strategy

To construct negative samples and enhance the discriminative capability of the framework, we design a hybrid negative sampling strategy that combines hard negatives and random negatives. For each positive sentence-code pair, negative samples are generated from two sources:

Intra-project hard negatives: Functions from the same project that are semantically related but inconsistent with the sentence (e.g. functions ranked from Top-5 to Top-10 in cosine similarity retrieval). These samples exhibit high semantic similarity but do not implement the described functionality, thereby improving the model’s ability to distinguish fine-grained semantic differences.

Intra-project hard negatives: Functions from the same project that are semantically related but inconsistent with the sentence (e.g. functions ranked from Top-5 to Top-10 in cosine similarity retrieval). These samples exhibit high semantic similarity but do not implement the described functionality, thereby improving the model’s ability to distinguish fine-grained semantic differences.

Cross-project random negatives: Functions randomly sampled from other projects and paired with the sentence. This strategy simulates cross-project semantic noise and increases the diversity and realism of the data distribution.

Cross-project random negatives: Functions randomly sampled from other projects and paired with the sentence. This strategy simulates cross-project semantic noise and increases the diversity and realism of the data distribution.

For each sentence, we construct two hard negatives and three random negatives. Compared to random negatives, hard negatives are more challenging and prevent the model from relying on superficial lexical cues, thereby improving its ability to capture deeper semantic consistency. This ratio provides sufficiently challenging supervision while maintaining adequate sample diversity during training. We further conduct ablation experiments by varying the proportion of hard negatives and random negatives. The results demonstrate that the 2:3 configuration achieves stable and competitive performance across multiple evaluation levels, and is therefore adopted as the default negative sampling strategy. Detailed experimental results are provided in Supplementary Section B.

Experiments

Experimental setup

Encoders

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed framework and the BioCon benchmark, we compare several representative pre-trained models as backbone encoders, including CodeBERT [32], UniXcoder [30], CodeT5+ [31], Qwen3 [34], and CodeGen [35]. These models have demonstrated strong performance in code understanding and cross-modal learning tasks, making them suitable baselines for this study.

To ensure a fair comparison, all models adopt a unified input format, where the paper sentence and code function are concatenated into a single sequence. Considering differences in maximum input length across encoders, the sequence length is uniformly limited to 512 tokens. For sequences exceeding this limit, the code portion is truncated first to preserve the complete sentence, thereby avoiding the loss of critical semantic information. In addition, all models are trained using the weighted Focal Loss, with the focusing parameter γ =2 and class weights α =[1.0,5.0], to mitigate the impact of class imbalance in the dataset.

Implementation details

All experiments are conducted on eight NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. Pre-trained model weights are loaded via Hugging Face [36]. We use the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of , a batch size of 16, and train each model for 10 epochs. Early stopping is applied, and the best model is selected based on validation performance. To reduce randomness, all reported results are averaged over three runs with different random seeds. Due to differences in model sizes, the training time per epoch ranges approximately from 1 min 36 s to 3 min 14 s.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively assess the framework, we adopt multiple evaluation metrics tailored to different task settings.

Sentence-level classification. In addition to Accuracy (Acc) and macro-F1 (F1), we report the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) [37, 38] as a key metric. Unlike Acc and F1, MCC provides a balanced evaluation by considering all elements of the confusion matrix, making it particularly robust under class imbalance. Acc and F1 range from [0,1], while MCC ranges from [-1,1].

Cross-modal retrieval. To evaluate the ability to locate correct implementations within candidate sets, we use Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) and Hit@k (). MRR measures the relative ranking position of the correct code, assigning higher scores to higher ranks, with values in [0,1]. Hit@k evaluates whether the correct implementation appears within the top-k retrieved results.

Project-level consistency. For project-level evaluation, we consider both classification and regression perspectives. The aggregated consistency score is thresholded at 0.5 to produce discrete labels, evaluated using Acc and F1. Additionally, accounting for the inherent uncertainty of ground-truth consistency boundaries, we report Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) to quantify the deviation between predicted and ground-truth inconsistency ratios. Both metrics range from [0, +∞ ), with lower values indicating better performance.

Under the intra-project setting, all models achieve relatively strong performance, indicating that they can capture sentence-code alignment when the candidate space is restricted. Among all models, UniXcoder achieves the best results in terms of MRR (0.5452), Hit@5 (0.7108), and Hit@10 (0.7389), demonstrating its ability to rank the correct implementation near the top in most cases.

In contrast, Qwen3 achieves the highest Hit@1 (0.4618) but performs slightly worse on Hit@5 and Hit@10, suggesting a tendency toward more aggressive ranking behavior. Specifically, the model assigns high confidence to a limited number of candidates but exhibits relatively weaker overall ranking stability. Overall, UniXcoder and CodeBERT exhibit more balanced performance across metrics, reflecting stronger robustness in ranking.

Under the cross-project setting, performance drops significantly for all models. For example, UniXcoder’s MRR decreases from 0.5452 to 0.3509 (-35.6%), while CodeBERT drops from 0.5205 to 0.3558 (-31.6%). Similar declines are observed for Hit@1, Hit@5, and Hit@10. This performance degradation is not solely caused by the increased number of candidate functions but also results from the absence of project-specific semantic and structural priors in cross-project retrieval. In intra-project retrieval, consistent naming conventions, coding styles, domain-specific terminology, and function organization patterns provide additional contextual information, thereby reducing semantic ambiguity among candidate functions. In contrast, cross-project retrieval introduces substantial variations in implementation strategies, abstraction levels, and function organization patterns. As a result, similar functionalities may be implemented through different function structures across projects, while semantically similar functions may serve different purposes in different software systems, leading to increased semantic ambiguity.

Interestingly, although UniXcoder achieves the best performance under the intra-project setting, CodeBERT slightly outperforms UniXcoder under the cross-project setting in terms of MRR (0.3558) and Hit@10 (0.5341). This observation suggests that retrieval performance depends not only on local semantic matching capability but also on model robustness to implementation variations across different projects. The intra-project and cross-project settings represent two distinct challenges in paper-code consistency analysis. Intra-project retrieval primarily evaluates whether models can perform semantic alignment within a relatively consistent project context. In contrast, cross-project retrieval requires models to generalize across substantial differences in coding conventions, function decomposition strategies, abstraction levels, and project-specific contexts. Therefore, cross-project retrieval is not merely an expanded candidate search problem; rather, it introduces additional challenges in cross-project semantic generalization and better reflects practical deployment scenarios.

In contrast, under the cross-project setting, all curves shift to the right, indicating a significant decline in ranking quality. Moreover, the performance gaps between models become more pronounced, suggesting that some models struggle under more challenging retrieval conditions.

Overall, retrieval evaluation provides a stricter assessment than sentence-level classification. While existing models perform well in constrained candidate spaces, they exhibit clear limitations in cross-project scenarios. Future work should focus on improving cross-project generalization, for example, by incorporating structured code representations or leveraging larger-scale cross-domain pre-training data, to better support real-world bioinformatics applications.

These results indicate that, beyond strong sentence-level discrimination, UniXcoder can effectively capture the overall consistency of a project.

Case study

Overall results

To investigate the causes and implications of paper-code inconsistency in real-world bioinformatics software, we conduct a case study on publicly available bioinformatics projects. Specifically, we select articles from the most recent issues of three representative journals, namely Bioinformatics, Genome Biology, and PLOS Computational Biology, as analysis targets. Among these studies, the code links provided in five papers were no longer accessible, while the remaining 44 projects released codebases implemented in Python. Therefore, the following analyses are conducted based on these 44 real-world projects.

For each project, we automatically extract sentences related to methods or implementation following the pipeline described earlier, and pair them with all functions in the corresponding code repository. The trained framework is then used to compute consistency scores. For each sentence, we retrieve the top-10 most relevant functions as candidates and apply the following decision rules:

If none of the top-10 candidates has a high-confidence match (i.e. all consistency scores are below a threshold of 0.5), the sentence is considered potentially inconsistent with the code;

If none of the top-10 candidates has a high-confidence match (i.e. all consistency scores are below a threshold of 0.5), the sentence is considered potentially inconsistent with the code;

Otherwise, the sentence is considered potentially consistent.

Otherwise, the sentence is considered potentially consistent.

It should be noted that this process does not rely on manually constructed sentence-function correspondences. Instead, it simulates the process by which researchers locate implementation details from paper descriptions when examining software in practice. Therefore, the focus of this section is not whether the software is implemented correctly, but whether a clear correspondence can be established between paper descriptions and publicly available code.

Based on the above analysis, we further calculated the paper-code consistency ratio, inconsistency ratio, and average consistency score for each project. The detailed project statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S7 (Supplementary Section C), while Fig. 4 summarizes the inconsistency ratios across the 44 projects. We observed substantial variation in paper-code relationship characteristics across projects. Some projects [39, 40] exhibited relatively high consistency levels, indicating that most methodological descriptions could be clearly mapped to corresponding implementations in the code. In contrast, other projects [41, 42] showed relatively high inconsistency ratios, suggesting potential discrepancies between paper descriptions and code implementations. Further analysis revealed that projects published in Bioinformatics and Genome Biology exhibited lower average inconsistency ratios than those published in PLOS Computational Biology.

Overall, the average observable inconsistency ratio among the 44 projects is approximately 22.4%, suggesting that most methodological descriptions can be mapped to corresponding implementations in publicly available repositories, while localized inconsistencies remain prevalent. However, this estimation should be interpreted with caution. First, the current analysis only includes projects whose code remains publicly accessible, whereas five projects with unavailable code links were excluded from automated analysis. From the perspective of paper-code consistency, inaccessible code itself represents a fundamental barrier to verifying whether methodological descriptions can be confirmed at the implementation level. Therefore, such projects may suffer from more severe limitations in terms of implementation traceability and verifiability. Second, the analyzed projects were collected from three highly recognized bioinformatics journals, and these publications may generally represent projects with relatively stronger research practices, software maintenance, and code availability standards. Consequently, the current sample may not fully represent the broader bioinformatics software ecosystem. Taken together, the observed inconsistency ratio of 22.4% should be regarded as a conservative estimate among currently accessible projects published in high-quality bioinformatics journals. The actual prevalence of paper-code inconsistency in the broader bioinformatics software landscape may be higher.

Furthermore, similar discrepancies between scientific descriptions and software implementations have also been reported in other research domains, including clinical research [43] and software engineering [21]. Although these studies adopt different definitions and evaluation methodologies from ours, their findings suggest that the relationship between scientific descriptions and computational implementations may represent a broader challenge across research disciplines rather than an issue unique to bioinformatics.

Human validation

Since the analysis of 44 projects primarily relies on the automated consistency framework, we further conduct an independent human validation study to evaluate the reliability of the obtained results. Specifically, we select 10 projects from the three journals for validation, including four projects from Bioinformatics, four from Genome Biology, and two from PLOS Computational Biology. For each selected project, we manually review the framework predictions and compare them with human assessment results. It should be noted that these projects are not included in the BioCon dataset and are therefore not involved in model training or testing. Consequently, this independent validation provides an additional evaluation of the framework’s generalization capability on unseen real-world projects.

As shown in Table 7, across the 10 independently validated projects, the framework achieves an average Acc of 0.7858, with an F1 and MCC of 0.7552 and 0.5757, respectively. These results indicate that the proposed framework can reliably identify potential consistency relationships between paper descriptions and code implementations, although its predictions are not completely error-free. In particular, false-positive cases indicate that some samples classified as inconsistent by the framework may actually have corresponding implementations consistent with the descriptions in the papers. Therefore, the estimated inconsistency ratio derived from large-scale analysis should be interpreted as an approximation of the potential inconsistency level rather than an exact measurement without uncertainty.

Operational Issues During Development and Release. This category accounts for 26.67% of the observed inconsistencies. These cases mainly arise from operational issues during software development, version management, or public release processes. Specifically, certain implementations described in the paper may exist during the development stage but are absent from the final publicly released repository due to issues such as incomplete synchronization, incorrect version uploading, or insufficient code maintenance. For example, one project [41] described in the paper that model training was performed using a composite loss function consisting of BCE Loss and Weighted Positive Loss. However, the implementation of Weighted Positive Loss was not found in the publicly available repository. After communication with the authors, they confirmed that an earlier version of the code had been mistakenly uploaded, resulting in only the BCE Loss implementation being released while the Weighted Positive Loss component was missing. Such cases reflect potential limitations in current bioinformatics software development and release workflows, particularly regarding version management, code organization, and long-term maintenance. Since software development often involves multiple rounds of experimentation and iterative modification, discrepancies may emerge between the final released code and the specific implementation version used for reported experiments, resulting in paper-code inconsistency.

Author-Perceived Non-Essential Details. This category represents the largest proportion of observed inconsistencies (55%) and primarily reflects differences in authors’ perceptions regarding which components should be publicly disclosed. Specifically, certain elements, including training procedures, dataset preprocessing pipelines, auxiliary analyses, or parameter configurations, may be considered by authors as non-essential components of the software and therefore are not included in the released code. For example, some projects [42, 44] provide detailed descriptions of training strategies and data preprocessing procedures in their publications; however, their publicly available repositories only contain trained models and processed datasets, without the corresponding training or preprocessing code. Importantly, such cases do not necessarily indicate that the authors failed to implement these components. Instead, they may reflect different interpretations of the minimum publicly available implementation required for software disclosure. For some researchers, training scripts, preprocessing workflows, auxiliary analyses, and parameter configurations may be regarded as experimental details rather than essential software components that must be released. This phenomenon highlights a broader question regarding modern bioinformatics software: which types of implementation details should be considered essential knowledge and therefore require public disclosure? Currently, no unified consensus exists regarding this issue. As the user community of bioinformatics software continues to expand beyond traditional domain experts, establishing clearer community expectations for implementation disclosure remains an important topic for further discussion.

Limited Code Accessibility Due to Resource Constraints. This category accounts for 18.33% of the observed inconsistencies and is mainly associated with constraints arising from research environments and software ecosystems. Specifically, some implement-ations depend on resources that cannot be directly released, including internal servers, private computational environments, or external software components. Consequently, these components are not included in publicly available repositories. For example, one project [42] described a filtering strategy in the paper, but the corresponding implementation was not identified in the released code. After communication with the authors, we learned that this component was executed within a server environment and was not included in the public repository. Similarly, some components in another project [45] relied on external libraries or existing frameworks and therefore did not contain explicit implementations within the project repository. These cases indicate that paper-code inconsistency does not always originate from errors or omissions. Instead, it may result from modular software dependencies and resource limitations within real-world software ecosystems. Nevertheless, for researchers attempting to independently reproduce or extend these methods, such hidden dependencies may still create practical barriers.

False Positives Caused by Differences in Code Implement-ation Forms. The current framework primarily relies on function-level code units for analysis and therefore performs well in identifying explicitly encapsulated implementations. However, in practical software development, not all computational procedures are implemented as independent functions. For example, one project [42] described the use of a confusion matrix for metric calculation in the paper. However, the corresponding code was directly embedded within a script rather than implemented as an independent function. Although the functionality was actually present, it was not captured during function-level code extraction, causing the framework to incorrectly classify the sentence as inconsistent. This phenomenon indicates that real-world software often contains diverse implementation patterns, including different levels of abstraction, code reuse, and inline implementations. Therefore, function-level analysis alone may not fully capture implicit implementations. Future approaches should incorporate multi-granularity code representations and program dependency relationships to reduce such false positives.

False Negatives Caused by Implicit Methodological Descriptions. Some paper descriptions involve model training configurations or regularization strategies but are expressed using general terminology or highly abstract descriptions. As a result, the framework may incorrectly match them with semantically similar code implementations. For example, one project [44] described learning rate settings and L2 regularization strategies, and the framework predicted that these components had corresponding implementations in the code. However, human validation revealed that the corresponding parameter configurations and implementations were not present. This observation suggests that the current training data may still be insufficient to capture diverse forms of methodological descriptions. Future work should incorporate more projects from broader application domains and enrich the diversity of paper-code relationships represented in training data.

Insufficient Filtering of Non-Implementation Sentences. The third category of errors mainly originates from the upstream sentence selection stage. Some paper sentences may involve computational, biological, or AI concepts but essentially represent background introduction, research motivation, or result interpretation rather than specific software functionalities or computational procedures. For example, sentences such as “... to computational and Artificial Intelligence (AI) methods …” or “… metric arises when the model …” do not correspond to specific code implementations. If such sentences are not effectively filtered during preprocessing, the framework may incorrectly treat them as claims requiring code support. This finding demonstrates that paper-code consistency analysis depends not only on cross-modal semantic modeling capabilities but also on the quality of upstream scientific text understanding. Future improvements may incorporate more fine-grained document structure analysis and methodological sentence identification techniques to further enhance analysis accuracy.

Temporal trend analysis

To further investigate how paper-code consistency has evolved in recent years, we extend the temporal scope of our analysis by examining bioinformatics software projects published in Genome Biology over the past five years (2022-2026). A total of 89 projects were included in this extended analysis, including 15, 14, 21, 24, and 15 projects published in 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025, and 2026, respectively. Note that the 2026 collection only covers projects published between January and May. Compared with the previous case study, this extended analysis provides continuous yearly coverage and enables a preliminary investigation of temporal variations in paper-code inconsistency.

As shown in Fig. 5, both the number of relevant software projects and the observed inconsistency ratio in Genome Biology exhibit an overall increasing trend over time. Specifically, although fluctuations exist across different years, the average inconsistency ratio increases from 15.5% in 2022 to 22.8% in 2026. One possible explanation is the presence of a selection effect in early open-source projects. During periods when public code availability was not yet widely required, researchers who voluntarily released their software may have represented a relatively selective group with greater confidence in software maturity, maintainability, and development quality. In other words, early publicly available projects may not represent the average quality of all software projects developed during that period, but rather projects whose developers were more willing and prepared to share their implementations. This selection effect may have resulted in an underestimation of paper-code inconsistency in earlier years.

Temporal trends of inconsistency ratios among 89 bioinformatics software projects published in Genome Biology from 2022 to 2026. The numbers of projects included in 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025, and 2026 are 15, 14, 21, 24, and 15, respectively. All values are rounded to three decimal places.

With the recent development of open science practices and increasing requirements from journals regarding code availability, the coverage of publicly accessible software has expanded substantially. Consequently, a broader range of projects with different scales, development stages, and levels of maturity have entered the open-source ecosystem. Under this broader inclusion environment, code availability may increase the diversity of analyzable projects and consequently introduce more projects with incomplete documentation, partial mismatches between paper descriptions and implementations, or insufficient code maintenance. This may contribute to the observed increase in inconsistency ratios over time.

However, it is important to emphasize that this temporal trend should not be interpreted simply as evidence that stronger code disclosure requirements have reduced paper-code consistency. Instead, the findings should be understood from a broader perspective: increased code accessibility improves the visibility and verifiability of software implementations, but code accessibility alone does not guarantee consistency between papers and code. Indeed, as more software projects become publicly available, researchers are increasingly able to identify implementation differences, undocumented procedures, and previously hidden gaps that could not be observed when code was inaccessible. Therefore, open-source practices may simultaneously enhance reproducibility and reveal previously invisible paper-code inconsistencies.

Taken together, these findings highlight that as bioinformatics software ecosystems continue to expand, establishing systematic approaches for evaluating the consistency between scientific descriptions and software implementations remains an increasingly important need.

Discussion

Core findings

Through a systematic analysis of the consistency relationship between paper descriptions and code implementations, this work reveals an overlooked issue in current bioinformatics software ecosystems: the availability of source code does not necessarily guarantee a clear correspondence between published methodologies, workflows, and experimental procedures and their actual implementations. This issue reflects broader changes in how scientific knowledge is represented and disseminated. In bioinformatics, software is no longer merely a tool for implementing algorithms; instead, it has increasingly become an essential component of scientific contributions. Scientific publications typically serve the roles of methodological explanation and theoretical communication, whereas software code preserves concrete implementation details and computational workflows. Therefore, papers and code together constitute an integrated knowledge representation system in modern bioinformatics. When inconsistencies exist between these two forms of knowledge representation, the interpretability, understandability, and reusability of scientific findings may be affected.

However, the boundary of paper-code consistency is not always absolute. Our case study demonstrates that some training procedures, parameter configurations, and auxiliary analysis scripts may not be publicly released because authors consider them to be non-essential components of the main contribution. Such cases account for 55% of the observed inconsistencies. Therefore, paper-code consistency should not be simply equated with previous concepts such as “whether code is complete” or “whether experiments are reproducible.” Instead, it involves a broader question regarding how researchers, developers, and users collectively define which types of information constitute essential scientific knowledge. Future bioinformatics communities need further discussion and consensus-building regarding reasonable consistency boundaries for different types of software.

Practical implications for different bioinformatics users

Paper-code inconsistency not only affects how different user groups understand and utilize bioinformatics software but also influences the broader ecosystem of scientific knowledge dissemination.

For researchers, inconsistencies between paper descriptions and code implementations may increase the cost of reproducing methods and conducting further development. When training procedures or data processing steps cannot be traced through the released code, researchers may need to infer software behaviors independently, potentially leading to incorrect interpretations. This not only reduces the efficiency of reusing existing methods but may also result in subsequent studies being developed based on incomplete information.

For clinical practitioners, bioinformatics software is increasingly being applied in scenarios such as disease prediction, omics analysis, and clinical decision support, where interpretability and reliability are particularly important. If the functionalities described in publications cannot be verified through publicly available implementations, clinical users may find it difficult to assess the applicability, limitations, and potential risks of these tools, thereby affecting their practical adoption.

For students and interdisciplinary researchers, source code has become an important pathway for learning bioinformatics methodologies. Clear correspondence between papers and code can help these users understand algorithmic principles, experimental workflows, and engineering implementations. Conversely, hidden inconsistencies may further increase the learning barriers for newcomers entering the field.

Toward FAIR-oriented paper-code consistency guidelines

In recent years, the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable) have become important guidelines for promoting scientific data sharing. However, for bioinformatics software, ensuring that code can be discovered and accessed is insufficient to guarantee that it can be effectively understood and reused. We argue that paper-code consistency provides an opportunity to further extend the interpretation of FAIR principles in the context of bioinformatics software.

First, from the perspective of Findability, users should not only be able to locate software repositories but also identify where key methodological descriptions in publications are implemented within the codebase.

Second, regarding Accessibility, publicly available code should not merely be downloadable but should also provide sufficient documentation to help users understand software behaviors and implementation logic.

Third, for Interoperability, clearly defined computational workflows, module relationships, and interface descriptions can facilitate software integration across different research environments.

Finally, in terms of Reusability, complete and traceable implementations provide essential support for method transfer, extension, and secondary research.

Therefore, future efforts toward software openness in bioinformatics should move beyond the question of “whether code is provided” toward the broader goal of “whether scientific implementations are understandable, verifiable, and reusable.” To achieve this goal, journals and research communities could explore lightweight consistency guidelines. For example, authors could provide mappings between methodological descriptions and corresponding code modules; peer-review processes could encourage checking whether critical methodological claims have corresponding implementations; and software repositories could provide clearer version histories and computational workflow documentation. These practices should not be viewed as additional publication burdens, but rather as mechanisms to promote more transparent dissemination of scientific knowledge.

Applications and recommendations

The framework proposed in this work can not only support large-scale analysis of bioinformatics software ecosystems but also serve as an auxiliary tool in practical software development and evaluation processes.

For researchers, the framework can be incorporated into pre-submission self-check workflows. By providing methodological descriptions from manuscripts, researchers can automatically identify potentially missing or difficult-to-trace implementation components and discover possible issues affecting reproducibility before publication.

For journals and reviewers, the framework can serve as an auxiliary tool for rapidly checking whether key methodological claims in manuscripts have corresponding implementations in publicly available repositories. Importantly, this tool is not intended to replace human review but rather to help reviewers identify potentially problematic areas requiring further examination.

For software maintainers, the proposed approach can also be used to detect long-term divergence between publications and evolving codebases. As software undergoes multiple rounds of updates, published descriptions may gradually become inconsistent with current implementations. Automated consistency analysis can help identify such implementation drift.

For the bioinformatics community as a whole, we believe that one of the most important priorities is to promote continued discussion regarding the boundaries of paper-code consistency. Currently, researchers still hold different interpretations regarding which components constitute essential parts of software and should theoretically be disclosed, and no unified consensus has yet been established. Therefore, we suggest that future communities gradually develop clearer consistency standards through mechanisms such as focused discussions, practical guidelines, and publicly available benchmarks. Compared with pursuing incremental performance improvements on existing tasks, establishing reasonable and actionable consistency standards may ultimately have a more profound impact on the future development of the bioinformatics ecosystem.

Limitations

This work represents the first exploration of paper-code consistency analysis and inevitably has several limitations.

First, BioCon currently only includes bioinformatics software implemented in Python. Although Python has been increasingly adopted in bioinformatics applications, other programming languages, such as R and C/C++, remain widely used in the field. Therefore, the current findings may not fully represent the characteristics of all types of bioinformatics software. Future work should extend BioCon to support multiple programming languages and enable more comprehensive investigations.

Second, the current analysis operates at the granularity of paper sentences and function-level code implementations. However, bioinformatics software often contains complex architectures in which a complete functionality may be achieved through interactions among multiple modules, or implementations may exist without being explicitly encapsulated as independent functions. Consequently, function-level analysis may still introduce detection errors. Future work will incorporate additional code structures, function dependency relationships, and documentation information to improve the analytical capability and accuracy of the framework.

Finally, although the proposed framework substantially reduces the cost of large-scale analysis, the current results still require interpretation with human validation. Future research will explore more advanced cross-modal consistency analysis approaches to improve accuracy, robustness, and interpretability when applied to increasingly complex software systems.
