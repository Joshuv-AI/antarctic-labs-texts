Drug‐Induced Sleep Endoscopy in Infants With Obstructive Sleep Apnea: Current Evidence and Knowledge Gaps
Authors: Melissa Bouhraoua, Christophe Salois, Mathieu Bergeron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo map and synthesize the available evidence and evidence gaps on drug‐induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) in infants with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), focusing on reported sites of obstruction, DISE‐associated management decisions, postoperative outcomes, safety, and limitations in current reporting.Data SourcesPubMed/MEDLINE/Embase/Google Scholar was searched for studies published in English and French from January 1980 to September 2025.Review MethodsThis scoping review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) guidelines. Eligible studies included original reports describing infants under 12 months with OSA who underwent DISE. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, extracted data with a standardized form, and synthesized results descriptively. Extracted variables included available polysomnography (PSG) parameters, DISE methodology, comparator airway evaluations when reported, DISE‐directed interventions, postoperative outcomes, and complications.ResultsEight observational studies published between 2013 and 2024 met inclusion criteria, involving 151 infants. DISE often revealed multilevel obstruction, most commonly involving the supraglottis (epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds, arytenoids; including sleep‐state laryngomalacia) and retrolingual airway. Contributors from the nasal and adenotonsillar regions were also reported. DISE findings were associated with variable interventions, most frequently supraglottoplasty and adenoidectomy/tonsillectomy, with selected cases requiring mandibular distraction, tongue‐lip adhesion, or tracheostomy. When postoperative PSG was available, indices generally improved (mean apnea‐hypopnea index [AHI] approximately 10.6 events/h preoperatively and 3 events/h postoperatively). No major DISE‐related complications were consistently described.ConclusionIn infants with OSA, DISE appears feasible and may identify dynamic, often multilevel obstruction, particularly supraglottic and retrolingual collapse. However, current evidence remains limited by small retrospective series, inconsistent PSG reporting, variable DISE protocols, and limited objective postoperative outcomes. Prospective studies with standardized DISE methods, uniform PSG reporting, defined outcome measures, and comparator airway evaluations are needed.Level of Evidence2.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is the most serious form of sleep‐disordered breathing (SDB). It involves repeated episodes of partial or complete collapse of the upper airway during sleep [1]. Infants have specific anatomical features compared to older children and adults that make them more prone to obstruction [2]. Notably, a low, posteriorly positioned soft palate and a relatively enlarged epiglottis cause them to be primarily nasal breathers [3]. Additionally, the infant's tongue is proportionally larger relative to the oral cavity, which further contributes to airway narrowing [3]. OSA in infancy is a serious condition linked to a wide range of complications, including failure to thrive, developmental delays, learning difficulties, metabolic alkalosis, cardiorespiratory issues, and, in severe cases, death [4, 5, 6].

Polysomnography (PSG) remains the diagnostic gold standard for OSA; however, in infants, its application is often constrained by technical challenges and logistical limitations [7]. Conventional awake laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy also provide limited insight, as these procedures fail to capture the dynamic nature of upper airway collapse during sleep, given that they are performed in the upright awake state [8]. These shortcomings have led to the increasing adoption of drug‐induced sleep endoscopy (DISE), a flexible endoscopic technique performed under pharmacologically induced sleep, which enables direct identification of the anatomic sites of obstruction [9].

DISE has been shown to identify obstruction sites not seen during awake examinations, including tongue base collapse and sleep‐state dependent laryngomalacia [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. This modality is especially useful in infants, who often have multilevel obstruction and frequently experience persistent OSA after supraglottoplasty or adenotonsillectomy [13]. Nevertheless, despite its expanding role in pediatric otolaryngology, the evidence supporting DISE in infants younger than 12 months remains sparse, with most available series constrained by small sample sizes and heterogeneous methodologies [14].

The objective of this scoping review is to map and synthesize the existing evidence on the use of DISE in infants under 12 months with OSA, with particular attention to gaps in the evidence that limit clinical translation. Specifically, this review aimed to identify anatomical sites of obstruction detected through DISE, describe how DISE findings were associated with management decisions, summarize available postoperative and safety outcomes, and determine whether studies reported sufficient PSG data, DISE methodology, and comparisons with awake or in‐office airway evaluation to inform clinical decision‐making. By systematically gathering the available evidence, this study seeks to clarify what is currently known, what remains uncertain, and what should be standardized in future research on DISE in this distinct and understudied patient group.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) guidelines [15]. Eligible studies were original reports describing infants younger than 12 months with OSA who underwent DISE. We included prospective and retrospective designs (including cohort studies, case series, and case reports). Editorials, expert opinions, and review articles without original patient‐level data were excluded. Studies enrolling older children were excluded unless outcomes for infants < 12 months were reported separately. The study selection process is summarized in a PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram protocol for drug‐induced sleep endoscopy in infants under one year of age.

A systematic search was performed primarily on PubMed/MEDLINE/Embase/Google Scholar to identify studies published between January 1980 and September 2025. Articles published in English or French were eligible. The search used combinations of keywords and synonyms for DISE and infancy, including (“drug induced sleep endoscopy” OR “DISE”) AND (“infant” OR “newborn” OR “under 1 year”). Reference lists of included articles were also hand‐searched to identify additional eligible studies.

Selection of Sources of Evidence

After duplicate removal, titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers using prespecified eligibility criteria. Full texts were then assessed independently by the same two reviewers to confirm inclusion. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved by discussion and consensus.

Data Charting and Data Items

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form, with results compiled in Microsoft Excel for descriptive synthesis. Extracted variables included: study characteristics (design, setting, sample size), patient demographics and comorbidities, available PSG parameters (including apnea‐hypopnea index [AHI] or obstructive AHI, oxygen saturation nadir, central apnea index, sleep study type, and postoperative PSG values when reported), DISE protocol details (anesthetic agent, sedation approach, patient positioning, scoring system, and anatomical subsites assessed, when available), findings from awake flexible laryngoscopy, in‐office scope, or another airway evaluation modality when reported, interventions associated with DISE findings, postoperative outcomes, follow‐up duration, and complications/adverse events. Synthesis of Results

Analyses were conducted from September 2025 through December 2025. Findings were synthesized descriptively and presented in narrative form, emphasizing (1) obstruction patterns in infants aged 0–12 months, (2) the degree to which DISE findings were associated with management and surgical decision‐making, (3) the completeness of PSG and DISE methodology reporting, (4) comparisons with awake or in‐office airway evaluation when available, and (5) postoperative outcomes and complications. Where feasible, quantitative summaries (e.g., counts/percentages and measures of central tendency and range) were reported. A meta‐analysis was not planned due to anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity, small sample sizes, inconsistent PSG reporting, and variable outcome definitions across included studies. Figure 1 shows in detail the stages of the literature review presented according to the PRISMA guideline [16].

Results

Study Selection

The literature search identified 59 records. After removal of duplicates and application of inclusion criteria, 8 studies were included in this scoping review, encompassing 151 infants. The studies were published between 2013 and 2024 and originated from multiple countries, including the USA, Belgium, and France. All included articles were observational in design, most commonly retrospective case series. Sample sizes were limited and heterogeneous, ranging from 1 to 58 infants per study. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as an evidence map of reported experience rather than as a comparative effectiveness analysis. Data are summarized in Table 2.

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

All infants underwent DISE within the first year of life. The reported age at the time of DISE ranged from 3.6 to 12 months. A total of 94 boys and 57 girls were included. The majority are Caucasian.

Several studies included infants with relevant comorbid conditions. Ma et al. reported a high prevalence of gastroesophageal reflux disease (9/15) and laryngomalacia (7/15), although asthma and congenital malformations were less frequently observed (1/15 each) [17]. Williamson et al. reported a high prevalence of gastroesophageal reflux disease (14/35), swallowing dysfunction (10/35), and prior supraglottoplasty (18/35), with additional comorbidities including congenital malformations, gastrostomy tube dependence, allergic rhinitis, congenital heart disease, and less frequent conditions such as cleft palate, facial vascular malformation, intraventricular hemorrhage, and intrauterine drug exposure [12]. Love et al. reported trisomy 21 or other syndromic conditions in 14/41 infants [20]. Overall, 17 patients across the included studies are premature at < 37 weeks without a range available.

Preoperative Sleep Study Characteristics

Preoperative sleep evaluation demonstrated heterogeneous presentations of sleep‐disordered breathing across the included studies. PSG data were inconsistently reported, and several studies did not provide complete baseline values for AHI, obstructive AHI, oxygen saturation nadir, central apnea index, sleep architecture, or study type. Among studies reporting numeric data, the mean preoperative AHI was approximately 10.6 events per hour (Table 3). The mean obstructive AHI (oAHI) was 8.9 events per hour. Furthermore, the preoperative mean oxygen saturation nadir was 85.6%. Williamson et al. study reported a preoperative central AHI of 1.13 per hour [12]. Most infants were diagnosed with OSA (n = 70), while others were categorized as having obstructive sleep‐disordered breathing (n = 22), noisy breathing during sleep (n = 7), or a history of brief resolved unexplained events (n = 12).

DISE Procedural Characteristics

Anesthetic protocols and DISE methodologies varied across the included studies and were incompletely reported. Most procedures were performed using intravenous anesthesia, most commonly propofol‐based regimens, either alone or in combination with other agents. Sevoflurane was frequently used, particularly for mask induction, followed by intravenous maintenance. In some studies, remifentanil was administered in combination with propofol. However, important methodological details such as depth of sedation, patient positioning, oxygen supplementation, use of topical anesthetic, scoring system, and whether the assessment was video‐recorded or reviewed by multiple raters were not consistently described.

DISE Findings: Patterns of Upper Airway Obstruction

DISE revealed varied, often multilevel patterns of, upper airway obstruction across the included studies. In the largest group, Ma et al. reported obstructions at several anatomical sites, including the nasal cavity (n = 26), adenoids (n = 16), palate (n = 12), tonsils (n = 18), retrolingual airway (n = 24), lingual tonsils (n = 17), vallecula (n = 11), epiglottis (n = 26), aryepiglottic folds (n = 22), and arytenoids (n = 10) [17]. Other studies have identified more localized obstruction patterns such as the ones by Lee et al. who noted retrolingual airway obstruction in two patients and McArdle et al. who reported lingual tonsillar obstruction in 21 patients [18, 19]. Conversely, Blanc et al. described laryngomalacia as the main obstructive finding in a single patient [22].

Comparison With Awake or In‐Office Airway Evaluation

Few studies reported whether DISE findings were compared with awake flexible laryngoscopy, in‐office scope, PSG patterns, or another modality of airway evaluation. When comparator information was available, DISE primarily contributed by identifying sleep‐state‐dependent or dynamic obstructions, including retrolingual collapse, epiglottic or supraglottic collapse, and laryngomalacia that may be less apparent during awake assessment. However, no included study provided a standardized diagnostic accuracy analysis or a consistent comparison between DISE and another evaluation modality.

Interventions Following DISE

Management strategies implemented following DISE varied across the included studies and covered a wide range of surgical interventions. Airway‐directed procedures were commonly reported, including supraglottoplasty, performed in multiple cohorts with frequencies ranging from 1 to 37 patients. Adenoidectomy and tonsillectomy were also frequently undertaken, with adenoidectomy reported in up to 18 patients and tonsillectomy in up to 11 patients in individual studies. In selected cases, more invasive airway procedures were described, including mandibular distraction osteogenesis (n = 14), tracheostomy (n = 6), and tongue–lip adhesion (n = 4). Less often reported procedures included aryepiglottic fold division (n = 1), isolated adenoidectomy or supraglottoplasty in single patients, and proton pump inhibitor therapy (n = 2).

Postoperative Outcomes

Postoperative outcomes were reported inconsistently across the included studies. When available, postoperative PSG showed a mean AHI of approximately three events per hour and a mean postoperative oxygen saturation of 88.7%. Table 3 shows that two of the included studies reported both preoperative and postoperative AHI values, demonstrating a reduction in postoperative events.

Safety and Complications

No major complications related to DISE were consistently reported across the included studies. However, adverse‐event reporting was limited and often non‐systematic.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes the existing literature on the use of DISE in infants younger than 12 months with OSA and highlights both recurring observations and major evidence gaps. Across the included studies, DISE frequently revealed multilevel patterns of upper airway obstruction, with prominent supraglottic and retrolingual involvement. These findings are consistent with the distinctive pathophysiology of infant OSA, which differs from that of older children because of anatomical predispositions, such as a higher‐positioned larynx and increased chest wall compliance, and physiological vulnerabilities, including ventilatory control instability and ventilation–perfusion mismatch [24]. Age‐related differences in obstruction patterns are well described, with younger children exhibiting higher rates of multilevel obstruction than older pediatric populations [25]. However, the available studies remain too small and heterogeneous to establish standardized DISE‐directed treatment pathways or to determine whether DISE improves outcomes compared with awake airway assessment or other evaluation strategies.

Anatomical Patterns of Obstruction in Infancy

Supraglottic obstruction was a key finding across multiple studies, most often involving the epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds, and arytenoids. The prevalence of laryngomalacia as a cause of infant OSA is significant, with earlier studies indicating that about 77%–79% of infants with laryngomalacia experience OSA [24, 25]. Significantly, the pattern of upper airway obstruction changes quickly during early childhood, shifting from mainly dynamic collapse in infancy, such as laryngomalacia and omega‐shaped epiglottis, to more structural causes, including adenotonsillar hypertrophy, in older toddlers [24, 25, 26, 27]. Ma et al. showed that supraglottic collapse often coexisted with obstruction at other anatomical levels, highlighting the multilevel nature of sleep‐related airway collapse in this age group [17]. Multilevel obstruction was widespread in infants and very young children. Blancke et al. reported multilevel collapse in about one‐quarter of children under 2 years old, a proportion significantly higher than in older children, where adenotonsillar obstruction usually prevails [21]. These findings underscore the limitations of relying solely on awake airway examination or age‐based surgical guidelines while assessing infants with suspected OSA.

Clinical Implications of DISE‐Guided Intervention

The studies included in this review suggest that DISE may contribute to individualized assessment by identifying sleep‐specific sites of obstruction; however, the present evidence does not establish that DISE‐directed management improves outcomes compared with conventional evaluation. The American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery (AAO‐HNS) Expert Consensus Statement on pediatric DISE endorses its use in selected children with persistent OSA, complex anatomy, or small tonsils, emphasizing its value in identifying sleep‐specific sites of obstruction [9]. Similarly, pediatric literature outside the infant‐specific population suggests that DISE can alter surgical planning in some children [28]. In the infant studies included here, DISE findings were associated with a broad range of interventions, including supraglottoplasty, adenotonsillar procedures, mandibular distraction, tongue–lip adhesion, and tracheostomy. Nevertheless, because studies were retrospective, heterogeneous, and generally lacked comparator groups, the clinical implications should remain cautious; DISE should be viewed as a potentially useful adjunctive evaluation tool.

Postoperative Outcomes and Outcome Reporting

Postoperative outcomes were inconsistently reported across studies, and the limited availability of complete pre‐ and postoperative PSG data was among the most important barriers to interpretation. When available, PSG parameters generally improved after DISE‐associated interventions, but studies rarely used standardized outcome definitions, uniform follow‐up intervals, or consistent reporting of obstructive AHI, oxygen saturation nadir, central apnea index, symptoms, quality of life, or growth. The American Thoracic Society Clinical Practice Guideline on persistent pediatric OSA emphasizes standardized outcome measures, including resolution of OSA (oAHI < 1 event/h), improvement of OSA (oAHI < 5 events/h), quality of life, and neurocognitive outcomes [29]. Future infant DISE studies should adopt similar outcome frameworks to permit comparison across centers and interventions.

The absence of standardized comparison with awake flexible laryngoscopy, in‐office scope, imaging, or PSG‐based obstruction patterns further limits clinical application. Although DISE may identify sleep‐state‐dependent findings that are not obvious during awake examination, the current infant literature does not define the diagnostic yield, incremental value, or cost‐effectiveness relative to these other modalities. Prospective studies should therefore include paired awake and sleep‐state airway assessments when feasible and should report how DISE findings changed management beyond information already available from clinical examination, PSG, and awake endoscopy.

Safety Considerations and Anesthetic Agents

Available evidence indicates that DISE is safe and well tolerated in pediatric populations, including infants with comorbidities such as hypotonia, obesity, and trisomy 21, when performed using appropriate anesthetic protocols and monitoring [30]. The AAO‐HNS consensus statement endorses the use of both propofol‐based and dexmedetomidine‐based sedation strategies for pediatric DISE, acknowledging that each agent has unique advantages and limitations [28]. However, systematic reporting of perioperative adverse events remains limited, particularly in infants, and should be addressed in future studies.

Evidence Gaps, Limitations, and Future Directions

This scoping review highlights several limitations within the current literature. Most included studies were small retrospective case series, and reporting was inconsistent for PSG parameters, DISE methodology, obstruction scoring, comparator airway evaluation, indications for intervention, follow‐up duration, and objective postoperative outcomes. The lack of standardized pediatric DISE scoring remains a significant challenge. Recent efforts have led to the development and validation of scoring frameworks such as the International Pediatric Sleep Endoscopy Scale (IPSES) and the NAVOTEL system, although widespread adoption has yet to occur [31, 32]. In addition, the natural history of OSA in infants, particularly those with laryngomalacia, may include maturation‐related improvement in obstructive and central respiratory events, making it difficult to attribute postoperative improvement solely to DISE‐directed surgery without prospective controls.

A minimum reporting framework for future studies should include age at DISE, prematurity and comorbidities, indication for DISE, complete baseline PSG values, awake or in‐office airway findings, anesthetic and sedation protocol, patient positioning, scoring system, obstruction sites and severity, planned management before and after DISE, postoperative PSG values, complications, and patient‐centered outcomes such as feeding, growth, symptoms, and quality of life. Multicenter prospective studies using standardized DISE and PSG reporting are needed to determine which infants derive clinically meaningful benefit from DISE and whether DISE‐directed management improves outcomes beyond conventional evaluation.

Pre‐ and postoperative PSGs were not uniformly detailed, follow‐up duration varied, and studies did not consistently report obstructive AHI, oxygen saturation nadir, central apnea index, symptom scores, growth parameters, quality‐of‐life outcomes, or standardized definitions of surgical success. These limitations reduce the ability to attribute postoperative improvement specifically to DISE‐directed intervention and represent a gap for future studies.

Conclusion

DISE appears feasible in reported infant cohorts and may provide sleep‐specific information about dynamic, often multilevel upper airway obstruction, particularly at the supraglottic and retrolingual levels. Important gaps include incomplete PSG reporting, variable DISE protocols, inconsistent obstruction scoring, limited comparison with awake or in‐office airway evaluation, and sparse objective postoperative outcome data. Future prospective multicenter studies with standardized DISE methodology, uniform PSG and outcome reporting, defined comparator evaluations, and longer follow‐up are necessary to clarify the clinical utility, safety, and best indications for DISE in this vulnerable population.
