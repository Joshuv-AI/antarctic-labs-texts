The problem was first posed more than 100 100 years ago for the special case k = 2 k=2 and has remained open ever since. Introduction

A set of points in the plane is said to be in
general position
if no three of the points lie on a common line. Following the riddle of Dudeney concerning the placement of 16 chess pieces on the chessboard in general position, the no-three-in-line problem was formulated, asking to determine the maximum number of grid points of an
n\times n
grid so that the points are in general position. For the history and background of this celebrated problem, we refer to the excellent book of Brass, Moser, and Pach
[
Brass2005
]
and that of Eppstein
[
Eppstein:2018
]
.
It is easy to see using the pigeonhole principle that
2n
is an upper bound. This theoretical upper bound can be attained when
n
is small
[
anderson1979update
,
flammenkamp1998progress
]
.
Surprisingly, although it has been studied extensively, no progress has been reported concerning the lower bound, up to our best knowledge, since the result of
Hall, Jackson, Sudbery, and Wild
[
hall1975some
]
. They showed fifty years ago that one can choose
1.5n-o(n)
points in general position from the
n\times n
grid.

Several conjectures were formulated concerning
the
asymptotical value
of the answer to the no-three-in-line problem, and it is far from clear whether it is
1.5n
,
2n
or somewhere in between
[
Brass2005
,
Eppstein:2018
,
green100
,
Guy/Kelly:1968
]
.

Our main focus is to continue the investigation of the generalization of this problem.

Definition 1.1
.

Let

denote the maximum size of a point set chosen from the points of the

grid
{\mathcal{G}}=[1,n]\times[1,n]

Using this notation, the aforementioned cornerstone result is the following.

(Hall, Jackson, Sudbery, and Wild
[

1.5n-o(n)\leq f_{2}(n)\leq 2n.

Their construction relies on a well-chosen subset of the modular hyperbola

Later Lefmann, using graph-theoretic tools, proved that for fixed

Here we note that actually, simply taking the disjoint union of

\displaystyle\text{ if\hskip 10.22217pt}2\nmid k.

However, the present authors were recently able to show much stronger results, as we determined

Preliminaries

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{F}_{p}=\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
denotes the set of integers modulo
p
, and
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}=\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus\{0\}
. We let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of nonnegative integers.

2.1
Grids and secants

Let us recall the following definitions and notation from the previous paper by the authors
[
kovacs2025settling
]
.

The notation
[a,b]
refers to the integer interval
\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:a\leq x\leq b\}
. A
grid
is a set
{\mathcal{G}}=I_{1}\times I_{2}
where
I_{1}=[a_{1},b_{1}]
and
I_{2}=[a_{2},b_{2}]
are integer intervals. We say that
{\mathcal{G}}
is an
n_{1}\times n_{2}
grid, where
n_{j}=|I_{j}|=b_{j}-a_{j}+1
for
j=1,2
. We call
{\mathcal{G}}
a
square grid
if
n_{1}=n_{2}
.

A secant
\ell
for a grid
{\mathcal{G}}
is a line of the Euclidean plane that meets
{\mathcal{G}}
in at least two points. We call a line
\ell

generic
if it is not horizontal or vertical. Each generic secant
\ell
has a unique direction vector
\mathbf{v}=(v_{x},v_{y})
such that
v_{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
v_{y}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
\gcd(v_{x},v_{y})=1
. The
modulus
of
\ell
is
M=\max(|v_{x}|,|v_{y}|)
.

Given an integer
k\geq 2
, a set
S\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}
is a
no-
(k+1)
-in-line set
if
|S\cap\ell|\leq k
for every Euclidean line
\ell
(or equivalently, for every secant
\ell
of
{\mathcal{G}}
).

2.2
The no-three-in-line construction of Hall, Jackson, Sudbery and Wild

In this subsection, we recall the main construction of Hall, Jackson, Sudbery and Wild in
[
hall1975some
]
, which gives a no-three-in-line set of size
3(p-1)
in a
2p\times 2p
grid for any prime
p\geq 3
. We start by recalling their most important definitions and lemmas, as our new constructions are also based on these.

Definition 2.1

(Congruent points in
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
)
.

Two points
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
are called
congruent mod
p
, or simply
congruent
, if
x_{1}\equiv x_{2}\pmod{p}
and
y_{1}\equiv y_{2}\pmod{p}
. A
class
is the set of all points in
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
congruent to a certain point. Note that classes naturally correspond to elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}
. The
base point
of a class is its unique element in
[0,p-1]\times[0,p-1]
.

Definition 2.2

(Modular hyperbola)
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
. Define the
modular hyperbola
to be

H(c,p):=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}:xy\equiv c\penalty\ (\mathrm{mod}\penalty\ p)\}.

A point
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
will be called
normal
if
p\nmid x
and
p\nmid y
. In
[
hall1975some
]
, Hall et al. introduce the following way to partition the normal points of
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
into subgrids of size
\frac{p-1}{2}\times\frac{p-1}{2}
, which we will call
blocks
.

Definition 2.3

(Blocks)
.

Define the following four
\frac{p-1}{2}\times\frac{p-1}{2}
grids:

\displaystyle A_{0,0}

\displaystyle=\left[1,\frac{p-1}{2}\right]\times\left[\frac{p+1}{2},p-1\right],

\displaystyle B_{0,0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{p+1}{2},p-1\right]\times\left[\frac{p+1}{2},p-1\right],

\displaystyle C_{0,0}

\displaystyle=\left[1,\frac{p-1}{2}\right]\times\left[1,\frac{p-1}{2}\right],

\displaystyle D_{0,0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{p+1}{2},p-1\right]\times\left[1,\frac{p-1}{2}\right].

For
r,s\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
A_{r,s}=A_{0,0}+(rp,sp)
, and similarly define
B_{r,s}
,
C_{r,s}
,
D_{r,s}
. We let
A=\bigcup\limits_{r,s\in\mathbb{Z}}A_{r,s}
, which is the union of all classes that have their base point in
A_{0,0}
; and we similarly define
B
,
C
,
D
. Note that the disjoint union
A\sqcup B\sqcup C\sqcup D
is equal to the set of all normal points of
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
.

Recall the following observation from
[
hall1975some
]

Observation 2.4
.

For any

If

Proposition 2.11
.

f_{3}(2p)\geq|S_{3}|\geq 3.5(p-1)
and
f_{4}(2p)\geq|S_{4}|=4(p-1)
for every prime

.

Proof.

We prove that
S_{k}
contains at most
k
points on every line
\ell
for
k\in\{3,4\}
. Clearly,
S_{2}\subseteq S_{3}\subseteq S_{4}
.

Take any line
\ell
; the points of
\ell\cap S_{k}
lie in at most two classes. By
2.6
, if any two points in
{\mathcal{G}}
are congruent then they determine a line of slope
0
,
\infty
or
\pm 1
, and on this line there cannot be any more points of
{\mathcal{G}}
from the same class. So
|\ell\cap S_{4}|\leq 4
, and if
\ell
is of modulus
\geq 2
, then
|\ell\cap S_{4}|\leq 2
.

Horizontal and vertical lines only meet one class of
H(c,p)
, so they meet
S_{4}
in at most
2
points. So it remains to show that
|\ell\cap S_{3}|\leq 3
for
\ell
of slope
\pm 1
.

We already know that
|\ell\cap S_{2}|\leq 2
, and now we show that
|\ell\cap(S_{3}\setminus S_{2})|=|\ell\cap(M\cap E)\cap H(c,p)|\leq 1
. Supposing the contrary,
\ell
must meet
(M\cap E)\cap H(c,p)
in two noncongruent points, as no two points of
M
are congruent.

If
\ell
has slope
+1
, then considering the possibilities in
2.5
(b) for the two classes of
\ell\cap H(c,p)
, we cannot have one class in
B
and the other in
C
, as such points are not on the same slope
+1
line in
M
. However we also cannot have two classes in
A
(or two in
D
), since the corresponding points would be reflections of each other in
x+y=p
(or
x+y=2p
), but one point from every such pair in
A_{0,0}
and
D_{0,1}
is missing from
E
.

If
\ell
has slope
-1
, we get an analogous contradiction, giving that
S_{3}
is indeed no-
4
-in-line.

Now as
H(c,p)
consists of
p-1
full normal classes, and
T_{4}
contains
4
points of every normal class,
|S_{4}|=4(p-1)
. The set
T_{3}
consists of
T_{2}
(having
3
points of each normal class), and

(where
M
has
1
point of each normal class). By
2.9
, normal classes
(x,y)
that do not lie on the diagonals

can be subdivided into groups of two classes in
E
and two in
F
, with the value of

being invariant modulo
p
within each group.

Altogether
p-1
classes satisfy
xy\equiv c\pmod{p}
, and on the diagonals
y-x\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and

, there are at most two such classes each. Half of the remaining classes are in

|S_{3}\setminus S_{2}|\geq\frac{p-1}{2}
and hence
|S_{3}|\geq 3.5(p-1)
holds.
∎

3
Randomised algebraic construction for every
k

In this section, we prove
Theorem 1.5
by giving a random point set of the grid, for which we select a set of random modular hyperbolae, and then delete some points from the union of their point sets lying in an
n\times n
grid in order to obtain our no-
(k+1)
-in-line set.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime.
In the
2p\times 2p
grid
{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}(p)\coloneqq\left[-\frac{p-1}{2},\frac{3p-1}{2}\right]\times[0,2p-1]
,
consider the
2p\times(2p-1)
subgrid
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\left[-\frac{p-1}{2},\frac{3p-1}{2}\right]\times[1,2p-1]
which we get from
{\mathcal{G}}
by removing the bottom row.
Let
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
be the set of lines of slope
\pm 1
intersecting
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
.
Define
\Omega_{p}
to be the set of subsets of
\{1,\dots,p-1\}
.

In the following, we build on
2.10
.

Construction 3.2

(Randomised construction using multiple modular hyperbolae)
.

Let
W\in\Omega_{p}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 2\left|W\right|
.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}(W)\coloneqq\bigcup_{c\in W}S_{4}(c,p)\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
,
and pick a subset
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}(W)
of minimal size such that

|\ell\cap{\mathcal{R}}|\geq|\ell\cap{\mathcal{S}}(W)|-k

for every line
\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
.
Our construction is
{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(W)\coloneqq{\mathcal{S}}(W)\setminus{\mathcal{R}}
.

The remaining part of the section aims to determine the size of the construction above.

Lemma 3.3

(Construction size)
.

The trimmed point set

{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(W)\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}
from
3.2
is a no-
(k+1)
-in-line set of size

\left|{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(W)\right|\geq 4(p-1)\left|W\right|-X_{k,p}(W),

(3.1)

where

X_{k,p}(W)\coloneqq\sum_{\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}\max\{0,\left|\ell\cap{\mathcal{S}}(W)\right|-k\}
.

Proof.

We prove that
\left|\ell\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(W)\right|\leq k
for every line
\ell
.
Indeed, if
\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
, this holds by construction.
Otherwise,
\left|\ell\cap S_{4}(c,p)\right|\leq 2
for every
c\in W
by
2.5
(a) and
2.6
for horizontal or vertical
\ell
, and by
2.4
and
2.6
for
\ell
of modulus
\geq 2
.
Hence

\left|\ell\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(W)\right|\leq\sum_{c\in W}\left|\ell\cap S_{4}(c,p)\right|\leq 2\left|W\right|\leq k
.

For the bound on
\left|{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(W)\right|
, note that
\left|{\mathcal{S}}(W)\right|=4(p-1)\left|W\right|
by
2.11
.
On the other hand, for every
\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
, pick a subset
R_{\ell}\subseteq\ell\cap{\mathcal{S}}(W)
of size
\max\{0,\left|\ell\cap{\mathcal{S}}(W)\right|-k\}
.
Then

\left|{\mathcal{R}}\right|\leq\sum_{\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}|R_{\ell}|=X_{k,p}(W)
by definition.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Note that
\frac{1}{2}X_{k,p}(W)\leq\left|{\mathcal{R}}\right|\leq X_{k,p}(W)
.
Indeed, in the proof of
3.3
, we must remove the necessary excess points
R_{\ell}
from
{\mathcal{S}}(W)
along the disjoint lines

of slope
+1
. Considering the symmetries of slope

and slope

lines (c.f.
), this gives the lower bound on

. The upper bound follows from

.
Choosing these

carefully to stay close to the lower bound on

(i.e. to avoid removing further points due to lines of slope

) could give an improvement on the estimate (

).

When
\left|W\right|
and

are small, the underlying number theory enables finding this optimum, see
Subsection 2.2
and
Subsection 2.3
. We will use similar ideas in
Section 4
to gain some improvement for small constant values of

.
In the general treatment of the current section, we will simply work with the above upper bound on
|{\mathcal{R}}|
.

Definition 3.5
.

For
\Omega_{p}
from
3.1

to be the set of subsets of

, and

Now

For

One wishes to choose

with

so that (

) gives the largest lower bound for the construction size.
Unfortunately, finding this optimal

So instead, we use the standard averaging argument

\min\{X_{k,p}(W):W\in A_{t}\}\leq{\mathbb{E}}(X_{k,p}\mid A_{t})=\frac{1}{|A_{t}|}\sum_{W\in A_{t}}X_{k,p}(W),

(3.2)

which together with (

4(p-1)t-{\mathbb{E}}(X_{k,p}\mid A_{t})\leq f_{k}(2p).

(3.3)

In

Their proof is computationally heavy, so it is separated to

.
In this section, along the idea of
3.4
, we alter the process of finding
{\mathcal{R}}
from
Section 3

to obtain a stronger lower bounds for
f_{k}(n)
when
k
is small, at least for certain values of
k
.

For example, in the case
\left|W\right|=1
and
k=2
,

Subsection 2.2
essentially details an optimal choice for the points
{\mathcal{R}}
to be removed.
Thus this value of
\left|{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(W)\right|
is a sharpening of the estimate of (

) we used in

\left|W\right|=1
and

.

For arbitrary

1.

Start by picking some of the hyperbolae forming
{\mathcal{S}}(W)
.
For each such chosen hyperbola
S_{4}
, pick

and remove manually the points of
S_{4}
located in the middle of the grid as in the example above corresponding to the selected

, see
Subsection 2.2
and
Subsection 2.3
.

Finish by applying the suboptimal averaging argument as in

to remove the remaining excess points.

It is useful to have this extra first manual step of artificially subdividing the optimisation problem of finding
{\mathcal{R}}
into local problems, because we can solve these subtasks optimally.
However, this first step is limited as there are many other ways of subdividing the problem.
On the other hand, the averaging second step considers the global problem in its entirety, but fails to provide the optimal solution.
We expect the effect of the first step to be relevant for small
k
, and
suspect that the second step will dominate for large

.

We now expand the ideas above formally.
The development and structure are very similar to
Section 3
,
so here we are only highlighting the key steps.

As in
3.1
,
define

to be the set consisting of triplets

Recall the hyperbola

from

for

and

.

Construction 4.1

(Extended randomised construction, c.f.

)
.

Let

and

,
