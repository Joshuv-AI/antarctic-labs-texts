Computational thresholds in high-dimensional statistics: the case of graph alignment
Laurent Massoulié

Abstract
Graph alignment is an instance of the NP-hard quadratic assignment problem. It consists, given the adjacency matrices A 1 A_{1} , A 2 A_{2} in ℝ n × n \mathbb{R}^{n\times n} of two graphs, in finding a permutation matrix Π \Pi that minimizes the Frobenius norm ‖ Π ​ A 1 − A 2 ​ Π ‖ F \|\Pi A_{1}-A_{2}\Pi\|_{F} . In this article we consider the problem from the perspective of high-dimensional statistics: we aim to estimate an unknown permutation π ∗ \pi^{*} in the symmetric group 𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{n} from the observation of two correlated random adjacency matrices A 1 A_{1} , A 2 A_{2} . We establish the following computational thresholds. We first consider the case where A 1 A_{1} , A 2 A_{2} are the adjacency matrices of two correlated Erdős-Rényi random graphs 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , p ) \mathcal{G}(n,p) in the sparse regime with average degree λ := n ​ p = O ⁡ ( 1 ) \lambda:=np=O(1) and edge correlation parameter s ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) s\in(0,1) . We identify a critical threshold s ∗ ​ ( λ ) s^{*}(\lambda) for s s above which a message-passing, local algorithm succeeds at alignment –that is, recovers a fraction Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \Omega(1) of the entries of π ∗ \pi^{*} , and below which no local algorithm succeeds. This result crucially depends on an associated model of correlated random trees. We then consider the case where A 1 A_{1} , A 2 A_{2} are two correlated Gaussian Wigner matrices with correlation parameter expressed as s = 1 / 1 + σ 2 s=1/\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}} where σ \sigma is the “noise” parameter. In this setting we consider a fast spectral algorithm based on the leading eigenvectors of the matrices A 1 A_{1} , A 2 A_{2} , and identify the critical scaling for noise parameter σ \sigma at which the fraction of entries of π ∗ \pi^{*} correctly recovered goes from 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) 1-o(1) to o ⁡ ( 1 ) o(1) . We next consider the convex relaxation approach which obtains the doubly stochastic matrix X X that minimizes ‖ X ​ A 1 − A 2 ​ X ‖ F \|XA_{1}-A_{2}X\|_{F} . We obtain upper and lower bounds on the critical noise parameter σ \sigma at which a simple post-processing of X X correctly recovers a fraction 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) 1-o(1) of entries of π ∗ \pi^{*} . We finally identify promising future directions on i) computational thresholds for spectral methods and convex relaxation methods of practical interest, and ii) impossibility results for broad classes of algorithms, notably low degree polynomial algorithms and local search algorithms.



1
Introduction.

High-dimensional statistics focuses on statistical estimation problems where the observed data, that we shall denote by
A
, and the signal to be estimated, that we shall denote by
\pi^{*}
, are both “high-dimensional”. A prevalent phenomenon in this context is the so-called “informational-computational gap”: For many problems of interest, there exist parameter ranges where brute-force search yields some non-trivial estimator
\hat{\pi}
, while all known polynomial-time algorithms fail to do so.

A major objective of the field is to identify efficient polynomial-time algorithms and determine under which conditions they give non-trivial estimators
\hat{\pi}
. Convex relaxations, spectral methods, message-passing algorithms and local search methods such as Monte-Carlo Markov Chains (MCMC) are the natural candidates in this endeavour.

An equally important objective is to determine conditions under which a whole family of algorithms must fail. One approach consists in studying the “energy landscape”
\pi\to\mathbb{P}(\pi^{*}=\pi|A)
, assuming a natural topology on the space
\mathcal{S}
to which
\pi^{*}
belongs. This may allow one to rule out local search algorithms that search for local maxima of the posterior distribution
\pi\to\mathbb{P}(\pi^{*}=\pi|A)
. One then says that the corresponding problem is “local search-hard”. Bandeira et al.
[
4
]
provide criteria for this failure mode of local search algorithms based on constructs from statistical physics, namely the so-called Franz-Parisi potential.

Another fruitful approach consists of ruling out estimates
\hat{\pi}
that depend polynomially on the observation
A
, the degree of the polynomial being bounded by
D=O(\ln n)
where
n
captures the problem’s dimension; see Wein
[
37
]
for a recent survey and Hopkins
[
24
]
for an early reference. One then says that the problem at hand is “LDP-hard”, where LDP stands for Low Degree Polynomial.

Correspondence between the two notions of hardness was investigated in Bandeira et al.
[
4
]
, where they showed that the two types of hardness coincide for problems of detecting whether some signal has been added or not to a vector of Gaussian noise.

Together, positive results on some algorithm and negative results on a whole family may combine to show that this specific algorithm is optimal within that family.

Our goal is to explain results on the efficiency of illustrative algorithms and the underlying mathematical techniques in the context of graph alignment. The graph alignment problem is popular because it arises in a variety of applications, e.g. network de-anonymization (see Narayanan and Shmatikov
[
30
]
), computational biology (see Singh et al.
[
34
]
), pattern recognition (see Conte et al.
[
7
]
) to name a few. It has also attracted attention in high-dimensional statistics recently as its analysis required the development of new techniques.

In graph alignment, one observes two adjacency matrices
A_{1},\;A_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, generated in a two-step procedure. In a first step,
A_{1}
is generated at random and a noisy version
A^{\prime}_{2}=A_{1}+W
of
A_{1}
is obtained through addition of noise matrix
W
. In a second step, a permutation
\pi^{*}
is sampled uniformly at random from the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, independently of
(A_{1},A^{\prime}_{2})
, and matrix
A_{2}
is then defined via
A_{2}(i,j)=A^{\prime}_{2}(\pi^{*}(i),\pi^{*}(j))
.

Consistent with previous notation,
\pi^{*}
is the signal of interest, to be estimated
from observation
A=(A_{1},A_{2})
. The quality of estimator
\hat{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is assessed by its so-called overlap

\hbox{ov}(\pi^{*},\hat{\pi}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}1\!\!{\sf I}_{\pi^{*}(i)=\hat{\pi}(i)},

that is the fraction of correctly estimated entries of
\pi^{*}
.
Several objectives could be envisioned, expressed in terms of
\hbox{ov}(\pi^{*},\hat{\pi})
: exact reconstruction corresponds to
\hbox{ov}(\pi^{*},\hat{\pi})=1
and almost exact reconstruction to
\hbox{ov}(\pi^{*},\hat{\pi})=1-o(1)
.
Of particular interest is partial reconstruction, that is when when
\hbox{ov}(\pi^{*},\hat{\pi})\geq\Omega(1)
as
n\to\infty
.

Graph alignment as described is an instance of Bayesian estimation:
\pi^{*}
is the signal –or structure– planted in the observation, sampled from a prior distribution, that we aim to estimate.
As is typical in high-dimensional statistics, one can associate to this estimation problem a related hypothesis testing, or decision problem: the couple
(A_{1},A_{2})
generated as just described depends on signal
\pi^{*}
; alternatively one could have generated
A_{1},A_{2}
independently of one another, but with the same marginal distributions. The latter situation would be the null hypothesis
H_{0}
(no signal), and the former the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
(signal present). The decision problem then consists in testing from sample
(A_{1},A_{2})
whether one is under
H_{0}
or
H_{1}
. This correspondence between a reconstruction and a decision problem is reminiscent of computational complexity theory where optimization problems are also associated with decision problems.

The two most studied versions of the problem are the following:

Gaussian ensembles
:
A_{1}
is a Gaussian Wigner matrix, also known as the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE), that is:
(A_{1}(i,j))_{i\leq j\in[n]}
are mutually independent,
A_{1}(i,j)=A_{1}(j,i)
,
A_{1}(i,i)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,2/\sqrt{n})
,
i\in[n]
, and
A_{1}(i,j)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/\sqrt{n})
,
i<j\in[n]
. Matrix
A^{\prime}_{2}
reads
A^{\prime}_{2}=A_{1}+\sigma Z
, where
Z
is independent of
A_{1}
and identically distributed to
A_{1}
and
\sigma
is the noise parameter.

Erdős-Rényi random graphs
: here
A_{1}
is the adjacency matrix of an Erdős-Rényi random graph, i.e.
(A_{1}(i,j))_{i<j}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli
(p)
for some
p\in[0,1]
,
A_{1}(i,i)\equiv 0
, and
A_{1}(i,j)=A_{1}(j,i)
,
i<j\in[n]
. For a given entry
(i,j)
,
i<j
, the pair
(A_{1}(i,j),A^{\prime}_{2}(i,j))
follows the Bernoulli
(p,s)
distribution defined via

(A_{1}(i,j),A^{\prime}_{2}(i,j))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1,1)&\hbox{with probability }ps,\\
(1,0)&\hbox{with probability }p(1-s),\\
(0,1)&\hbox{with probability }p(1-s),\\
(0,0)&\hbox{with probability }1-p(2-s).\end{array}\right.

These two versions typically rely on different sets of tools; random matrix theory for the former, and random graph theory for the latter.

1.1
Outline:

In
Section
2
we focus on the Erdős-Rényi model in the sparse regime where the average degree
\lambda:=np
is of constant order
O(1)
. This regime is of special interest for several reasons: i) it is challenging for the design of efficient algorithms; ii) sparse graphs occur in application scenarios; iii) its analysis can be done by the study of well-defined limiting local processes, random trees as it will appear.

We introduce MP-Align, a message-passing algorithm which matches vertex
i
in
A_{1}
to vertex
u
in
A_{2}
if their local neighborhoods in their respective graphs are “sufficiently similar”. We leverage the local structure of Erdős-Rényi graphs in the sparse regime to characterize the critical threshold
s^{*}(\lambda)
for
s
above which MP-Align succeeds. We also establish that for
s
below this threshold, for a suitable notion of local algorithm, no
O(\ln(n))
-local algorithm succeeds.

In
Section
3
we relate the success of MP-ALign to properties of a hypothesis testing problem, deciding whether a pair of random trees is correlated or independent. We provide an asymptotic characterization of this tree correlation testing problem in the limit
\lambda\to\infty
, establishing in particular that
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}s^{*}(\lambda)=\sqrt{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is Otter’s constant
[
31
]
.

Section
4
is devoted to the study of a specific fast spectral algorithm that relies on the leading eigenvectors of both matrices
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
. There we place ourselves in the Gaussian ensemble setting. We establish that for any
\epsilon>0
, the algorithm achieves overlap
1-o(1)
for noise parameter
\sigma=n^{-7/6-\epsilon}
, and instead achieves overlap
o(1)
for noise parameter
\sigma=n^{-7/6+\epsilon}
.

Section
5
is devoted to the convex relaxation where rather than minimizing
\|XA_{1}-A_{2}X\|_{F}
over permutation matrices
X
, one relaxes this to minimization over the convex hull of permutation matrices, also known as the Birkhoff polytope, or equivalently the set of doubly stochastic matrices. Despite this relaxation being very successful and popular in practice, there are few rigorous results to date on their performance.
Focusing on the Gaussian ensemble case, we prove that for any
\epsilon>0
, denoting by
\Pi^{*}
the optimal permutation matrix, with high probability
\|\Pi^{*}-X\|^{2}_{F}=o(n)
if
\sigma=n^{-1-\epsilon}
and
\|\Pi^{*}-X\|^{2}_{F}=\Omega(n)
if instead
\sigma=n^{-1/2+\epsilon}
. Thus algorithms based on this convex relaxation undergo a qualitative change in performance for
\sigma
between
n^{-1}
and
n^{-1/2}
.

Section
6
concludes by reviewing promising future directions. Specifically, Sections
2
and
3
lead to conjectures on LDP-hardness as well as local search-hardness of graph alignment for sparse correlated Erdős-Rényi graphs. Sections
4
and
5
lead to several questions respectively on the spectra of composite random matrices, and on finer performance guarantees for convex relaxations.

1.2
Related works:

Study of graph alignment for Erdős-Rényi random graphs initially focused on informational thresholds, i.e. feasibility of the problem irrespective of computational complexity. Cullina and Kyavash
[
8
]
showed that exact recovery of unknown permutation
\pi^{*}
is feasible with high probability if and only
ps-\ln(n)=\omega(1)
, or equivalently if and only if the intersection graph (whose adjacency matrix
A_{\cap}
is given by
A_{1}\odot A^{\prime}_{2}
) is fully connected with high probability. The study of partial recovery (achieving overlap
\Omega(1)
) started with Hall and Massoulié
[
23
]
; partial recovery is feasible if and only if
snp>1
or equivalently if and only if the intersection graph admits a giant (i.e. comprising
\Omega(n)
vertices) connected component. The necessary part was established by Ganassali, Lelarge and Massoulié
[
21
]
and the sufficient part by Ding and Du
[
10
]
.

A series of works proposed polynomial-time algorithms and proofs of their success, for either exact or partial recovery of
\pi^{*}
. In particular Ding et al.

[
13
]
obtain exact recovery for
np\geq\ln^{\alpha}(n)
and
1-s\leq\ln^{-\beta}(n)
for suitable constants
\alpha,\;\beta>0
; Mao et al.
[
28
]
relaxed the condition on
s
to
1-s\leq c
for some constant
c\in(0,1)
. It was then shown by Mao et al.
[
29
]
that partial recovery can be done in polynomial-time for graphs with average degree
np
sufficiently large, as soon as
s>\sqrt{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is Otter’s constant.

Concerning impossibility results, Ding, Lu and Li
[
11
]
showed that the hypothesis testing problem of distinguishing between correlated graphs and independent graphs cannot be done with low degree polynomials whenever
s<\sqrt{\alpha}
. Li
[
26
]
then showed that partial recovery of
\pi^{*}
is impossible with low degree polynomials when
s<\sqrt{\alpha}
.

For correlated Gaussian Wigner matrices,
Ganassali
[
18
]
established that the threshold for informational feasibility of recovery is for correlation parameter
1/\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}
between the two matrices equal to
2\sqrt{\ln n/n}
. A spectral polynomial-time algorithm, coined GRAMPA, was proposed by Fan et al.
[
15
]
, achieving recovery for
\sigma=O(1/\ln(n))
. The authors extended the results for GRAMPA to the setting of Erdős-Rényi graphs in
[
16
]
. Ding and Li
[
12
]
then obtained a polynomial-time algorithm that succeeds at recovery for any non-vanishing correlation parameter.

Much less is known about the performance of convex relaxation approaches in the settings we consider. Araya and Tyagi
[
3
]
consider convex relaxation from permutation matrices to both the Birkhoff polytope
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and the simplex
\Delta_{n}:=\{M\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n^{2}}:\sum_{i,j\in[n]}M_{ij}=n\}
in the noiseless case
\sigma=0
, and show that these relaxations then recover
\pi^{*}
exactly.

The theorems stated in Sections
2
and
3
stem from Ganassali, Lelarge and Massoulié
[
20
]
and Ganassali, Massoulié and Semerjian
[
22
]
respectively, and we refer to these for proofs (see also Maier and Massoulié
[
27
]
for detailed proofs, and generalization of the results to a model of asymmetric random graphs with differing numbers of vertices and densities of edges). The results of Section
4
appeared in Ganassali, Lelarge and Massoulié
[
19
]
, to which we refer for detailed proofs. The result of Section
5
is from Varma, Waldspurger and Massoulié
[
36
]
and we refer to that paper for its proof.

2
Sparse Erdős-Rényi graphs and a message-passing algorithm.

To motivate the algorithm, consider the so-called union graph
A_{\cup}
of
A_{1}
and
A^{\prime}_{2}
, where we let

A_{\cup}(i,j)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{1,2\}&\hbox{ if }A_{1}(i,j)=A^{\prime}_{2}(i,j)=1;\\
\{1\}&\hbox{if }A_{1}(i,j)=1,\;A^{\prime}_{2}(i,j)=0;\\
\{2\}&\hbox{if }A_{1}(i,j)=0,\;A^{\prime}_{2}(i,j)=1;\\
\emptyset&\hbox{if }A_{1}(i,j)=A^{\prime}_{2}(i,j)=0.\end{array}\right.

A classical result of random graph theory states that for an Erdős-Rényi random graph
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
with
\lambda=np
of order 1, the neighborhood of a fixed vertex
i
of the graph up to distance
d=O(\ln n)
converges, for the total variation distance, to a Galton-Watson branching tree of depth
d
and with offspring distribution following the Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda
, that we denote by
\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}
. See for instance Alon and Spencer
[
2
]
. Writing
d=\kappa\ln(n)
, the constant
\kappa
ought to be chosen such that
\lambda^{d}\ll\sqrt{n}
, i.e.
\kappa\ln(\lambda)<1/2
for this property to hold.

This result easily generalizes to a situation where edges have multiple types such as the model of the union graph
A_{\cup}
we just defined, where edge types are
\{1\}
,
\{2\}
or
\{1,2\}
, an entry of
A_{\cup}
equal to
\emptyset
corresponding to absence of an edge; see Bollobás Janson and Riordan
[
5
]
. Up to distance
d\leq\kappa\ln n
, where
\kappa\ln(\lambda(2-s))<1/2
, the neighborhood of a vertex in the union graph converges in total variation distance to a multi-type Galton-Watson branching process with edge types
\{1\}
,
\{2\}
or
\{1,2\}
. In this multivariate version, the distribution of the number
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{12})
of children of respective types
\{1\}
,
\{2\}
,
\{1,2\}
(where the type of a child corresponds to the edge between itself and its parent), for any given individual follows the product distribution
\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda(1-s)}\otimes\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda(1-s)}\otimes\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda s}
.

We shall use the following

Definition 2.1
.

Denote by
\mathcal{Y}_{d}
the collection of rooted, labeled trees of depth at most
d
, where the root label is
\bullet
and for any vertex with label
i
and
k
children, its children are labeled as
(i;1),\ldots,(i;k)
.

Denote by
GW_{d}^{(\lambda)}
the distribution over
\mathcal{Y}_{d}
of a depth
d
-Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}
.

Let us now define a distribution over correlated pairs of Galton-Watson trees as follows:

Definition 2.2
.

Let parameters
\lambda>0
,
s\in(0,1)
and depth
d\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed. The
(\lambda,s,d)

augmentation
of any tree
t\in\mathcal{Y}_{d}
is defined as follows: first, for each
h=0,\ldots,d-1
, endow each depth
h
-vertex of
t
with an additional number of children distributed according to
\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda(1-s)}
. Next, to each such new vertex, whose depth is
h+1
, graft a descendance tree of depth
d-h-1
sampled according to
GW^{(\lambda)}_{d-h-1}
, all child additions and tree graftings being performed independently.

The
shuffling
of any tree
t
in
\mathcal{Y}_{d}
is obtained by successively performing independent uniform permutations of the labels of the children of one vertex, starting from the root and proceeding towards the leaves. The result is a tree from
\mathcal{Y}_{d}
sampled uniformly at random from those trees
t^{\prime}
that are isomorphic to

, for the natural notion of graph isomorphism between rooted labeled trees.

\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
is then defined as the distribution over
\mathcal{Y}_{d}\times\mathcal{Y}_{d}
of a pair
(\mathcal{T}_{d},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{d})
obtained as follows. Start from some intersection tree
\mathcal{T}_{\cap,d}
drawn from
GW^{(\lambda s)}_{d}
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{d}
be the shuffling of a
(\lambda,s,d)
augmentation of
\mathcal{T}_{\cap,d}
, and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{d}
be similarly obtained via a shuffling and an augmentation of
\mathcal{T}_{\cap,d}
independently performed.

By the previous statement on the neighborhood of some vertex in the union graph
A_{\cup}
, one readily obtains the following

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider two vertices
i
,
u
of
A_{1}
,
A_{2}

\pi^{*}(i)=u
. Consider their respective graph neighborhoods

. Then for

, where

\kappa\ln(\lambda(2-s))<1/2
, the corresponding neighborhoods are with high probability trees the pair of which, thus labeled, converges in total variation to

In contrast, if we instead pick two arbitrary vertices
i
,
u
from each graph without enforcing the constraint

Lemma 2.4
.

Consider two vertices
i
,
u

\mathcal{B}_{1}(i,d)

.

Consider a pair of vertices

, one has

for some suitable constant threshold

, where for any two trees

, we let

denote the likelihood ratio

L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(t,t^{\prime}):=\frac{\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(t,t^{\prime})}{\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda)}_{d}(t,t^{\prime})}\cdot

Before we state the main theorem of this section, some remarks are in order.

Remark 2.7
.

Although not obvious at first sight, MP-Align can be implemented as a message-passing algorithm. Indeed, assume that the roots of trees
t
,
t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{d}
have respectively
c
and
c^{\prime}
children. For
i\in[c]
, let
t_{i}
denote the subtree of
t
rooted at the
i
-th child of
t

\mathcal{S}(k,c)
the number of injections from
[k]
to
[c]
, it follows from the definitions of
\mathcal{P}^{\lambda,s}_{d}
,
\mathcal{P}^{\lambda}_{d}
that

L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=0}^{c\wedge c^{\prime}}\psi(k,c,c^{\prime})\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(k,c),\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(k,c^{\prime})}\prod_{u=1}^{k}L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d-1}(t_{\sigma(u)},t^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}(u)}),

where

\psi(k,c,c^{\prime}):=\frac{\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda s}(k)\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda(1-s)}(c_{k})\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda(1-s)}(c^{\prime}-k)}{\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}(c)\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}(c^{\prime})}\cdot

This readily gives an iterative method for computing

for any two oriented edges
(j\to i)
,
(v\to u)
of
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
respectively and for any
\delta\in[d]
. Initializing with
L^{(\lambda,s)}_{0}\equiv 1
,
L^{(\lambda,s)}_{\delta}(\mathcal{B}_{1}(j\to i,d),\mathcal{B}_{2}(v\to u,d))
is obtained as a sum of a product of likelihood ratios
L^{(\lambda,s)}_{\delta-1}(\mathcal{B}_{1}(k\to j,d),\mathcal{B}_{2}(w\to v,d))
for

a neighbor of

distinct from

and

u
.

The recursive operation can then be viewed as “passing messages” from pairs of oriented edges
(k\to j),(w\to v)
to neighboring pairs of oriented edges
(j\to i),(v\to u)
. Indeed, this message-passing approach to computing likelihood ratios has been implemented and numerically tested in Piccioli et al.
[
32
]
and in
[
20
]
. In this sense it is reminiscent of
belief propagation
, a celebrated algorithm and powerful analytical tool in the study of graphical models. See Jordan and Wainwright
[
25
]
for an introduction to belief propagation, and Decelle et al.
[
9
]
for its application as an analytical tool from statistical physics to high-dimensional statistics. More precisely, belief propagation is used in
[
9
]
to predict that the threshold for computational hardness of performing
community detection
, or equivalently clustering, in a random graph known as the Stochastic Block Model, occurs precisely at the so-called Kesten-Stigum threshold from branching process theory.

Remark 2.8
.

MP-Align is a polynomial-time algorithm. Indeed, the number of operations performed in order to update some
L^{(\lambda,s)}_{\delta}(\mathcal{B}_{1}(j\to i,d),\mathcal{B}_{2}(v\to u,d))
is upper-bounded by
c^{2}c!c^{\prime}!
, where
c
,
c^{\prime}
are the degrees of vertices
j
and
v
respectively. For sparse Erdős-Rényi random graphs, it holds with high probability that the largest node degree is
(1+o(1))\ln n/\ln(\ln n)
. Thus

c!\leq\exp((1+o(1))\ln(\ln n)\times\ln n/\ln(\ln n)))=\exp((1+o(1))\ln n).

Thus for
d=O(\ln n)
, the number of operations needed to compute
L_{d}^{\lambda,s}
is
O(n^{2+o(1)})
, hence the polynomial complexity of MP-Align.

We now need the following the following definition, which was suggested to us by Jiaming Xu
[
38
]
:

Definition 2.9
.

For integer
d\geq 0
, we say that an algorithm for graph alignment is
d
-local if it returns a collection
\mathcal{C}
of pairs of vertices
(i,u)
from graphs
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
obtained by letting

(i,u)\in\mathcal{C}\hbox{ if and only if }f(\mathcal{B}_{1}(i,d),\mathcal{B}_{2}(u,d))=1,

where
f
is a function from pairs of labeled graphs with a distinguished “root” vertex to
\{0,1\}
.

Note that this definition of a local algorithm differs from the notion of a
local search
algorithm mentioned in the Introduction.

Clearly, MP-Align is a
d
-local algorithm in the sense just defined, where we enforce
d\leq\kappa\ln n
for
\kappa
such that
\kappa\ln(\lambda(2-s))<1/2
.
We are now ready to state the following

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
KL_{d}(\lambda,s)
denote the Kullback-Leibler divergence from
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
to
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda)}_{d}
,

KL_{d}(\lambda,s):=\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}_{d}^{2}}\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(t,t^{\prime})\ln\left(L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(t,t^{\prime})\right).

Assume that

(2.1)

\lim_{d\to\infty}KL_{d}(\lambda,s)=+\infty.

Then for suitable choice of threshold parameter
\tau=\Theta(1)
, for
d=\Theta(\ln n)
,
d\leq\kappa\ln n
where
\kappa\ln(\lambda(2-s))<1/2
, with high probability MP-Align provides
\Omega(n)
correct vertex matches, i.e. pairs
(i,u)
such that
u=\pi^{*}(i)
, and returns only
o(n)
incorrectly matched pairs.

If instead (
2.1
) does not hold, then any
d
-local algorithm which provides
\Omega(n)
correct matches with high probability must also provide
\Omega(n^{2})
incorrect matches with high probability.

For detailed proofs of this result and the other statements in this Section we refer the reader to
[
20
]
. A sketch of the argument is as follows.

For the negative part, assume some function
f
is such that with high probability,

\sum_{i\in[n]}f(\mathcal{B}_{1}(i,d),\mathcal{B}_{2}(\pi^{*}(i),d))=\Omega(n).

Then by Lemma
2.3
, necessarily for
(t,t^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
, one must have

x:=\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(f(t,t^{\prime})=1)=\Omega(1).

Let now
y:=\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda)}_{d}(f(t,t^{\prime})=1)
denote the probability of the same event under distribution
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda)}_{d}
. Since we are under the assumption that (
2.1
) fails, the Kullback-Leibler divergence
KL_{d}(\lambda,s)
is upper-bounded by some constant
C
. Classical monotonicity properties of the Kullback-Leibler divergence then imply that

x\ln(x/y)+(1-x)\ln((1-x)/(1-y))\leq C,

and elementary inequalities allow to deduce

y\geq x\exp(-C-1/e),

hence
y=\Omega(1)
.

The number of incorrectly matched pairs reads

\sum_{i,j\in[n],\pi^{*}(i)\neq j}f(\mathcal{B}_{1}(i,d),\mathcal{B}_{2}(j,d)).

The expectation of this quantity is, by Lemma
2.4
,
(1+o(1))n^{2}y
. Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
2.4
, one also gets that the second moment of the previous sum equals
(1+o(1))n^{4}y^{2}
. Bienaymé-Tchebitchev inequality then ensures that with high probability, the number of incorrect matches is
(1+o(1))n^{2}y=\Omega(n^{2})
, thus obtaining the negative part of the Theorem.

The positive part of Theorem
2.10
is shown by expressing the likelihood ratio
L_{d}^{(\lambda,s)}(t,t^{\prime})
, for
(t,t^{\prime})
drawn according to
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
in a factorized form with factors
L_{h}^{(\lambda,s)}(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i})
for
i
in range
[\Theta((\lambda s)^{d})]
, where the pairs
(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i})
are drawn according to
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{h}
. Taking logarithms, the law of large numbers ensures that

\ln\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\Theta((\lambda s)^{d})}L_{h}^{(\lambda,s)}(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i})\right)\approx\Theta((\lambda s)^{d})KL_{h}(\lambda,s),

and this is the leading contribution to
\ln(L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(t,t^{\prime}))
for sufficiently large
h
, the latter point following from the assumption (
2.1
) that
\lim_{h\to\infty}KL_{h}(\lambda,s)=+\infty
.
A direct consequence of Theorem
2.10
is the following

Corollary 2.11
.

MP-Align succeeds for
s>s^{*}(\lambda)
where the threshold
s^{*}(\lambda)
is defined via

(2.2)

s^{*}(\lambda):=\inf\{s\in[0,1]:\lim_{d\to\infty}KL_{d}(\lambda,s)=+\infty\}.

3
Tree correlation testing: Gaussian limits and Otter’s constant

.
Let us introduce the following

Definition 3.1
.

Given two series of probability distributions
\{p_{0,d}\}_{d\geq 0}
,
\{p_{1,d}\}_{d\geq 0}
on probability spaces that may depend on
d
, an associated series of tests
\{F_{d}\}
taking values in
\{0,1\}
is said to be one-sided if

\liminf_{d\to\infty}p_{1,d}(F_{d}=1)>0\hbox{ and }\lim_{d\to\infty}p_{0,d}(F_{d}=1)=0.

We then have the following characterization for existence of one-sided tests:

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume there is a one-sided family of tests for
\{p_{0,d}\}_{d\geq 0}
against
\{p_{1,d}\}_{d\geq 0}
. Then necessarily, the Kullback-Leibler divergence
KL(p_{1,d}||p_{0,d})
goes to infinity as
d\to\infty
.

Assume that there is a single probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
endowed with two probability distributions
p_{1}
,
p_{0}
and a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{d}\}_{d\geq 0}
. Let
p_{1,d}
(respectively,
p_{0,d}
) be the restriction of
p_{1}
(respectively of
p_{0}
) to
\mathcal{F}_{d}
. If the Kullback-Leibler divergence
KL(p_{1,d}||p_{0,d})
goes to infinity as
d\to\infty
, then one-sided testability holds between
\{p_{1,d}\}_{d\geq 0}
and
\{p_{0,d}\}_{d\geq 0}
.

The first statement easily follows from the expression of the Kullback-Leibler divergence between two distributions
p,q
as the supremum over finite measurable partitions of the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the discrete distributions induced by
p
and
q
on this finite partition, while the second follows from Doob’s martingale convergence theorem.

In the present context, let
\Omega=\mathcal{Y}_{\infty}\times\mathcal{Y}_{\infty}
, where
\mathcal{Y}_{\infty}
is the space of rooted labeled trees, with no restriction on their depth, and take for
\mathcal{F}_{d}
the sigma-field generated by restriction to depth
d
of the two trees. Taking
p_{0,d}=\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda)}_{d}
and
p_{1,d}=\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
, Proposition
3.2
applies and yields that Condition (
2.1
) is equivalent to one-sided testability between correlated pairs of trees with distribution
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
and independent pairs of trees with distribution
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda)}_{d}
.

To gain further understanding into the threshold
s^{*}(\lambda)
in (
2.2
), a fruitful approach consists in studying its limit as
\lambda\to\infty
. This will be done by establishing convergence to limiting Gaussian distributions of trees
t,t^{\prime}
sampled from
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
as
\lambda\to\infty
.

To that end one needs to consider rooted, unlabeled trees, i.e. equivalence classes of rooted labeled trees, rather than rooted labeled trees as in the last Section. Let then
\mathcal{X}_{d}
denote the collection of rooted unlabeled trees of depth at most
d
. An element of
\mathcal{X}_{d}
can be seen as a measure on
\mathcal{X}_{d-1}
with weights in
\mathbb{N}
, which is adequate for establishing convergence to limiting Gaussian distributions in the spirit of the central limit theorem, as we are now dealing with elements from some vector space
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}_{d-1}}
.

For two elements
(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{X}_{d}
, denote by
\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime})
,
\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda)}_{d}(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime})
the probability weights that distributions
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
,
\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda)}_{d}
respectively put on all pairs
(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}_{d}
such that
t\in\mathfrak{t}
,
t^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
, where we write
t\in\mathfrak{t}
to say that
t
belongs to the equivalence class
\mathfrak{t}
. We abuse notation and still write
L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
for the likelihood ratio of these two distributions:

L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime})=\frac{\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime})}{\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda)}_{d}(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime})},

and also keep notation
GW^{(\lambda)}_{d}(\mathfrak{t})
to denote the probability that a depth
d
Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}
belongs to class
\mathfrak{t}
.

The key step towards obtaining a tractable Gaussian limit is the following diagonalization of the likelihood ratio
L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
on a suitable basis of orthogonal polynomials:

Theorem 3.3
.

For all
\lambda>0
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
s\in[0,1]
, there exists a collection
\left\{\left(f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(x)\right)_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{d}},\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}\right\}
indexed by
\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}
of vectors
f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}
whose entries
f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(x)
are polynomial in the coordinates of
x
, that do not depend on
s
, and such that

(3.1)

\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}_{d},\;L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(x,y)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}s^{|\beta|-1}f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(x)f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(y),

where for any tree
\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}
,
|\beta|
denotes its number of vertices.
The polynomials
f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}
further satisfy the following properties:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X}_{d},\;f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\bullet}(x)=1\quad\hbox{(value at trivial tree)},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\forall\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{d},\;\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}GW^{(\lambda)}_{d}(x)f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(x)f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta^{\prime}}(x)=1\!\!{\sf I}_{\beta=\beta^{\prime}},\quad\hbox{(first orthogonality relation)},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{d},\;\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(x)f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(x^{\prime})=\frac{1\!\!{\sf I}_{x=x^{\prime}}}{GW^{(\lambda)}_{d}(x)},\quad\hbox{(second orthogonality relation)},

and finally for all
m\geq 2
,
d\geq 1
,
\beta^{(1)}=\{\beta^{(1)}_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\mathcal{X}_{d-1}}
,…,
\beta^{(m)}=\{\beta^{(m)}_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\mathcal{X}_{d-1}}
, the mixed moments of order
m
admit the following limits:

(3.5)

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}GW^{(\lambda)}_{d}(y)f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta^{(1)}}(y)\cdots f^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta^{(m)}}(y)=\prod_{\gamma\in\mathcal{X}_{d-1}}\sqrt{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{\gamma}^{(i)}!}\left[x_{1}^{\beta^{(1)}_{\gamma}}\cdots x_{m}^{\beta^{(m)}_{\gamma}}\right]e^{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}x_{i}x_{j}},

where
[x^{\alpha}]G
refers to the coefficient of monomial
x^{\alpha}
in the power series expansion of some function
G
.

The construction of polynomials
f_{d,\beta}^{(\lambda)}
is done in
[
22
]
by induction on
d
, as are shown the corresponding properties.

Remark 3.4
.

We now provide the intuition for the diagonalization formula (
3.1
) and how it separates dependency on
s
via the terms
s^{|\beta|-1}
from dependency on
\lambda
via the terms
f^{(\lambda)}_{\beta}
. Define a Markov process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
on
\mathcal{X}_{d}
as follows. At unit rate, each edge of the current tree is suppressed as well as the sub-tree below that edge. At rate
\lambda
, for each
h=0,\ldots,d-1
, each vertex of the tree of depth
h
obtains a new child and that child’s genealogy tree up to depth
d-h-1
, the genealogy tree of that child being distributed according to
GW^{(\lambda)}_{d-h-1}
.

It is readily seen that this defines a reversible Markov process with invariant distribution
GW^{(\lambda)}_{d}
.
Initializing the Markov process by taking
Z_{0}\sim GW^{(\lambda)}_{d}
, then a pair of trees
\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
drawn from
\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
is obtained by letting
(\mathfrak{t},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime})=(Z_{0},Z_{-\ln(s)})
.

Denote now by
H
the infinitesimal generator of this Markov process. Formally (this is always true for Markov jump processes on finite state spaces, but requires a proof for countably infinite state spaces such as
\mathcal{X}_{d}
), the matrix
P
of transition probabilities
P_{xy}=\mathbb{P}(Z_{u}=y|Z_{0}=x)
in time
u
is obtained as
P=\exp(uH)
. Let now
D
denote the diagonal matrix
D_{x,y}=GW^{(\lambda)}_{d}(x)1\!\!{\sf I}_{x=y}
. Reversibility entails that matrix
M:=D^{1/2}HD^{-1/2}
is symmetric. One thus expects
M
to be diagonalizable, with real spectrum
\{\nu_{i}\}_{i}\in\mathcal{I}
for some countable index set
\mathcal{I}
, and associated collection of orthonormal eigenvectors
g_{i}
:

M=D^{1/2}HD^{-1/2}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\nu_{i}g_{i}g_{i}^{\top},

and thus the transition probabilities matrix
P
reads

P=D^{-1/2}\exp(uM)D^{1/2}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e^{u\nu_{i}}D^{-1/2}g_{i}g_{i}^{\top}D^{1/2}.

Now, the likelihood ratio
L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}(x,y)
equals
(PD^{-1})_{x,y}
for the specific choice
u=-\ln(s)
, so that

L^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}s^{-\nu_{i}}(D^{-1/2}g_{i})(D^{-1/2}g_{i})^{\top}.

In this light, taking
\mathcal{X}_{d}
for indexing set
\mathcal{I}
, we interpret (
3.1
) as follows: the spectrum of infinitesimal generator
H
is given by
\{-|\beta|\}_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}
, and the eigenvector
g_{\beta}
of
M
associated to
\nu_{\beta}=-|\beta|
is given by
D^{1/2}f_{\beta}
.

The expression (
3.5
) for the limits of mixed moments implies the following asymptotic Gaussianity result:

Theorem 3.5
.

For
\mathcal{T}\in\mathcal{X}_{d+1}
,
\mathcal{T}=\{N_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}
, consider its representation
\{\phi^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(\mathcal{T})\}_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}
in the dual basis
\{f_{d,\beta}^{(\lambda)}\}_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}
defined by

\forall\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d},\;\phi^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(\mathcal{T}):=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}f_{d,\beta}^{(\lambda)}(\tau)\left[N_{\tau}-GW^{(\lambda)}_{d}(\tau)\right].

Then for a pair

(\mathcal{T}=\{N_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{d}},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\{N^{\prime}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{d}})
drawn from
\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d+1}
, the joint dual representations
\{\phi^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(\mathcal{T}),\phi^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\}_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}
admit the following weak limit as
\lambda\to\infty
:

(3.6)

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d+1}\left(\left\{\{\phi^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(\mathcal{T}),\phi^{(\lambda)}_{d,\beta}(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\right\}_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}\in\cdot\right)=\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}\limits_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&s^{|\beta|}\\
s^{|\beta|}&1\end{array}\right)\right).

Note that the distribution
\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda)}_{d}
corresponds to the uncorrelated version (
s=0
) of
\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
, and in the dual domain it thus also admits a Gaussian limit with the same structure as above, but with parameter
s=0
. Letting, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

J(k)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&s^{k}\\
s^{k}&1\end{array}\right),

and
I_{2}
the
2\times 2
identity matrix,
the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the limiting Gaussian distributions then evaluates to

(3.7)

\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}KL\left(\mathcal{N}\left(0,J(|\beta|)\right)||\mathcal{N}\left(0,I_{2}\right)\right)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-s^{2|\beta|})=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{(s^{j})^{2|\beta|}}{j}.

Let
A_{d,n}
denote the number of elements of
\mathcal{X}_{d}
with exactly
n
vertices, and let for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

(3.8)

\phi_{d}(x):=\sum_{n\geq 1}A_{d,n}x^{n-1}.

Using the correspondence between elements
\mathfrak{t}
of
\mathcal{X}_{d+1}
and vectors
\{N_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{d}}
in
\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{X}_{d}}
, it is shown in
[
22
]
that

(3.9)

\phi_{d+1}(x)=\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{x^{j}}{j}\phi_{d}(x^{j})\right).

It is further established in
[
22
]
that the limit as
\lambda\to\infty
of
KL_{d}(\lambda,s)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the limiting distributions. We then obtain from (
3.7
), (
3.9
) the following

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
KL_{d}(\infty,s)
denote the limit
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}KL_{d}(\lambda,s)
.
One then has

(3.10)

KL_{d}(\infty,s)=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}A_{d,n}(s^{2})^{n-1}\right).

Denote by
A_{n}
the number of rooted unlabeled trees with
n
vertices. The celebrated result of Otter
[
31
]
establishes that for some constant
C>0
one has

A_{n}\sim\frac{C}{n^{3/2}}\alpha^{-n},

where
\alpha=0.338...
is now known as Otter’s constant. This together with (
3.10
) in turn yields, since
A_{n,d}
increases to
A_{n}
as
d\to\infty
:

Corollary 3.7
.

If
s\leq\sqrt{\alpha}
, then
\lim_{d\to\infty}KL_{d}(\infty,s)<+\infty
.

If
s>\sqrt{\alpha}
, then
\lim_{d\to\infty}KL_{d}(\infty,s)=+\infty
.

The relationship between Criterion (
2.1
) for success of Algorithm MP-Align and Otter’s constant is then obtained by proving that the following interchange of limits holds:

\lim_{d\to\infty}\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}KL_{d}(\lambda,s)=\infty\Leftrightarrow\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lim_{d\to\infty}KL_{d}(\lambda,s)=\infty.

This finally yields the following result, previously conjectured in Piccioli et al.
[
32
]
:

Theorem 3.8
.

For
s\leq\sqrt{\alpha}
, MP-Align fails. For
s>\sqrt{\alpha}
, there is
\lambda(s)
such that, for
\lambda>\lambda(s)
, MP-Align succeeds. As a consequence,
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}s^{*}(\lambda)=\sqrt{\alpha}
.

Remark 3.9
.

In
[
29
]
, Mao et al. propose an algorithm based on counting specific trees that they call chandeliers, and show that for correlated Erdős-Rényi random graphs, with fixed
s>\sqrt{\alpha}
and average degree
\lambda
sufficiently large, their algorithm succeeds at partial alignment. The arguments differ from those in our present approach and provide a more direct justification for the appearance of Otter’s constant. Also, their scheme is suited to non-sparse graphs (
\lambda\to\infty
) which MP-Align cannot handle.

In contrast, our approach is specifically targeted at the sparse regime
\lambda=O(1)
. Also, Condition (
2.1
) for success of MP-Align coincides by Proposition
3.2
with one-sided testability between the correlated distributions
\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda,s)}_{d}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{(\lambda)}_{d}
, and by Theorem
2.10
with existence of some successful
d
-local algorithm for
d=O(\ln n)
. It is thus tempting to conjecture that Condition (
2.1
) precisely characterizes the onset of polynomial tractability in the sparse regime. This will be further discussed in Section
6
.

4
Correlated Wigner matrices and a fast spectral method.

Our aim in this Section is to identify a computational threshold for a spectral method called EIG1, to be defined shortly. EIG1 is the fastest among several such methods proposed in
Feizi et al.
[
17
]
. We study it here in the specific context of correlated Gaussian random matrices

A_{1}
,
A_{2}
. Specifically, we are given two independent identically distributed matrices Gaussian Wigner
A_{1}
,
Z
. That is to say entries
\{Z(i,j)\}_{i\leq j}
are independent, with
Z(i,j)=Z(j,i)
for all
i<j\in[n]
,
Z(i,i)
i.i.d. with distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,2/\sqrt{n})
for
i\in[n]
, and
Z(i,j)
i.i.d. with distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1/\sqrt{n})
for
i<j\in[n]
.

As before,
\pi^{*}
is sampled uniformly at random from the set
\mathcal{S}_{n}
of permutations of
[n]
. We finally let

A^{\prime}_{2}=A_{1}+\sigma Z,

and

A_{2}(i,j)=A^{\prime}_{2}(\pi^{*}(i),\pi^{*}(j)),\quad i\leq j\in[n].

The spectral method EIG1 proceeds as follows. Let
v_{1}
(respectively,
v_{2}
) be a normalized eigenvector of
A_{1}
(respectively,
A_{2}
) associated with its largest eigenvalue. Let
\pi_{+}
(respectively,
\pi_{-}
) denote the permutation from
\mathcal{S}_{n}
that sorts the entries of eigenvectors
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
in the same (respectively, in opposite) order, i.e. assuming that the entries of
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
are sorted by permutations
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2}
, i.e.

v_{i}(\tau_{i}(1))>v_{i}(\tau_{i}(2))>\cdots>v_{i}(\tau_{i}(n)),\quad i=1,2,

then

\forall j\in[n],\;\pi_{+}(\tau_{1}(j))=\tau_{2}(j)\hbox{ and }\pi_{-}(\tau_{1}(j))=\tau_{2}(n-j+1).

Finally, the algorithm EIG1 returns the permutation
\hat{\pi}\in\{\pi_{-},\pi_{+}\}
chosen according to the criterion

\hat{\pi}\in\hbox{argmax}_{\pi\in\{\pi_{-},\pi_{+}\}}\left\{\sum_{i,j\in[n]}A_{1}(i,j)A_{2}(\pi(i),\pi(j))\right\}.

EIG1’s implementation requires computation of eigenvectors, which takes
\tilde{O}(n^{2})
operations, and sorting of the eigenvectors’ entries, which takes
O(n\ln n)
operations, thus an overall computational complexity of
\tilde{O}(n^{2})
. As such it is faster to implement than competing proposals in the literature.

Our main result for EIG1 is the following

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider the correlated Gaussian Wigner matrices
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
with noise parameter
\sigma
. For any fixed
\epsilon>0
, if
\sigma\leq n^{-7/6-\epsilon}
, then with high probability,

(4.1)

\hbox{ov}(\hat{\pi},\pi^{*})=1-o(1).

If instead
\sigma\geq n^{-7/6+\epsilon}
, then with high probability,

(4.2)

\hbox{ov}(\hat{\pi},\pi^{*})=o(1).

The result is established in
[
19
]
. The proof strategy consists in controlling the perturbation of the leading eigenvector of
A^{\prime}_{2}
that results from the addition of noise matrix
\sigma Z
to
A_{1}
. In particular one establishes the following

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume that for some fixed
\alpha>0
,
\sigma\leq n^{-1/2-\alpha}
. Then the leading eigenvector
v^{\prime}_{2}
of
A^{\prime}_{2}
can be written

(4.3)

v^{\prime}_{2}=(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))\left(v_{1}+S\frac{1}{\|w\|}w\right),

where
S
is a random variable such that
S=(\sigma n^{1/6})^{1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)}
, and
w
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
and is independent of
v_{1}
.

Proposition
4.2
essentially relies on writing the eigenvalue-eigenvector equation for eigenvector
v^{\prime}_{2}
of
A^{\prime}_{2}
in the basis of the eigenvectors of
A_{1}
. The evolution of the leading eigenvector
v^{\prime}_{2}
as
\sigma
increases has been studied for instance by Allez and Bouchaud
[
1
]
, who establish stochastic differential equations for the evolution of the coordinates of a specific eigenvector of
A^{\prime}_{2}
in the eigenbasis of
A_{1}
.

In order to obtain Proposition
4.2
, we need quantitative estimates on the magnitude of the perturbation. We obtain these by approximating the perturbed eigenvector via an iterative Picard scheme, whose convergence we analyze by exploiting fine rigidity estimates for the eigenvalues of Wigner matrices established in Erdős, Yau and Yin
[
14
]
.

Equipped with Proposition
4.2
, the performance of EIG1 is reduced to the study of a toy problem, that consists in studying how the ranking of entries of a vector
x
whose entries are i.i.d. with distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1/n)
is affected by adding to each entry
x(i)
a perturbation
Sw(i)
where
S=(\sigma n^{1/6})^{1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)}
and the
w(i)
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and independent of the
x(i)
. For the analysis of this toy problem, the proof of Proposition
4.2
and how they combine to yield Theorem
4.1
, we refer the reader to
[
19
]
.

5
Correlated Wigner matrices and convex relaxation.

In this Section we consider convex relaxations for the estimation of the unknown permutation
\pi^{*}
, placing ourselves again in the context of correlated Gaussian random matrices
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
.

The algorithm considered in this Section returns matrix
X^{*}
, obtained as the minimizer of
X\to\|XA_{1}-A_{2}X\|_{F}
over the convex set
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is the set of doubly stochastic matrices, also known as the Birkhoff polytope:

(5.1)

X^{*}\in\hbox{argmin}_{X\in\mathcal{B}_{n}}\|XA_{1}-A_{2}X\|_{F}.

Other convex relaxations are possible: for instance Araya and Tyagi
[
3
]
consider the above minimization over
M\in\Delta_{n}
, defined as the simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
:

\Delta_{n}:=\left\{X\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n^{2}}:\sum_{i,j\in[n]}X_{ij}=n\right\}.

Surprisingly few theoretical results are available about the performance of such convex relaxations, despite their empirical success. Indeed, Araya and Tyagi
[
3
]
considered the noise-free case where
\sigma=0
, for which they show that for both relaxations to
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and to
\Delta_{n}
, the unique minimizer
X^{*}
is given by permutation matrix
X^{*}=\Pi^{*}
associated with
\pi^{*}
, for which the objective function
\|MA_{1}-A_{2}M\|_{F}
trivially equals zero.

The following result is established in Varma, Waldspurger and Massoulié
[
36
]
:

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider
X^{*}
that solves minimization problem (
5.1
) over the Birkhoff polytope, for
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
two correlated Gaussian Wigner matrices with noise parameter
\sigma\leq 1
.
Then for any fixed
\epsilon>0
,
X^{*}
verifies with high probability:

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sigma\leq n^{-1-\epsilon}\Rightarrow\|X^{*}-\Pi^{*}\|^{2}_{F}=o(n)

(5.3)

\displaystyle\sigma\geq n^{-1/2+\epsilon}\Rightarrow\|X^{*}-\Pi^{*}\|_{F}^{2}=\Omega(n).

Remark 5.2
.

In the first case (
5.2
), property
\|X^{*}-\Pi^{*}\|^{2}_{F}=o(n)
readily guarantees that a very crude post-processing of
X^{*}
, for instance letting

(5.4)

\forall i\in[n],\;\hat{\pi}(i)\in\hbox{argmax}_{j\in[n]}X^{*}_{ij},

achieves overlap
\hbox{ov}(\hat{\pi},\pi^{*})
of
1-o(1)
, i.e., it correctly recovers all but a vanishing fraction of the entries of
\pi^{*}
. Indeed, for any
i
such that
\hat{\pi}(i)\neq\pi^{*}(i)
, it must be that
X^{*}_{i,\pi^{*}(i)}\leq 1/2
. Thus

\|X^{*}-\Pi^{*}\|^{2}_{F}\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i\in[n]}1\!\!{\sf I}_{\hat{\pi}(i)\neq\pi^{*}(i)},

hence the lower bound
1-o(1)
on the overlap
\hbox{ov}(\hat{\pi},\pi^{*})
when
\|X^{*}-\Pi^{*}\|^{2}_{F}=o(n)
. Since (
5.2
) is less stringent than Condition
\sigma\leq n^{-7/6}
needed for the success of EIG1 in (
4.1
), it follows that the convex relaxation has a better robustness to noise than EIG1, at the expense of higher computational complexity.

In contrast, under Condition (
5.3
), Property
\|X^{*}-\Pi^{*}\|_{F}^{2}=\Omega(n)
is compatible with an overlap
\hbox{ov}(\hat{\pi},\pi^{*})
equal to
o(1)
.

We briefly sketch how (
5.3
) is established. Thanks to invariance of Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
and of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
by left- or right-multiplication by a permutation matrix, we can assume without loss of generality that
\pi^{*}
is the identity. Let
J
be the all-ones matrix. Since
n^{-1}J\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, by optimality of
X^{*}
one has

\|A_{1}X^{*}-X^{*}A_{2}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|A_{1}J-JA_{2}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{n^{2}}\left[\|A_{1}J\|_{F}^{2}+\|JA_{1}\|_{F}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\|JZ\|_{F}^{2}\right].

The three Frobenius norms in the right-hand side are all of the form

\sum_{i,j\in[n]}Y_{ij}^{2},

for
Y_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1+1/n)
. With high probability, all these Gaussian random variables are in absolute value less than
n^{\epsilon/2}
. This together with
\sigma\leq 1
ensures that with high probability,

(5.5)

\|A_{1}X^{*}-X^{*}A_{2}\|_{F}^{2}\leq 9n^{\epsilon}.

A companion lower bound on
\|A_{1}X^{*}-X^{*}A_{2}\|_{F}^{2}
is obtained in terms of
\|X^{*}-I\|_{F}^{2}
by writing

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|A_{1}X^{*}-X^{*}A_{2}\|_{F}^{2}&=\|A_{1}(I-X^{*})-(I-X^{*})A_{2}+\sigma Z\|_{F}^{2}\\
&=\|A_{1}(I-X^{*})-(I-X^{*})A_{2}\|_{F}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\|Z\|_{F}^{2}-2\sigma\langle A_{1}Z-ZA_{1},X^{*}\rangle-2\sigma^{2}\langle Z^{2},I-X^{*}\rangle.\end{array}

The first term
\|A_{1}(I-X^{*})-(I-X^{*})A_{2}\|_{F}^{2}
is non-negative, the second term
\sigma^{2}\|Z\|_{F}^{2}
is with high probability larger than
\sigma^{2}n/2
. We thus obtain, using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the fourth term:

\|A_{1}X^{*}-X^{*}A_{2}\|_{F}^{2}\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}n}{2}-2\sigma\max_{i\neq j}|(A_{1}Z-ZA_{1})_{ij}|\sum_{i\neq j}X^{*}_{ij}-2\sigma^{2}\|Z^{2}\|_{F}\|I-X^{*}\|_{F}.

It can be shown that with high probability,
\max_{i\neq j}|(A_{1}Z-ZA_{1})_{ij}|=O(n^{\epsilon/2-1/2})
. Noting further that
\sum_{i\neq j}X^{*}_{ij}\leq n
, this yields

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|A_{1}X^{*}-X^{*}A_{2}\|_{F}^{2}&\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}n}{2}-O(\sigma n^{\epsilon/2+1/2})-2\sigma^{2}\|Z^{2}\|_{F}\|I-X^{*}\|_{F}\\
&\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}n}{2}-O(\sigma n^{\epsilon/2+1/2})-5\sigma^{2}\sqrt{n}\|I-X^{*}\|_{F},\end{array}

where we used that
\|Z^{2}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{n}\|Z^{2}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\|Z\|^{2}_{2}
and that
\|Z\|_{2}=2+o(1)
with high probability. By combining (
5.5
) with this last display, and using that
\sigma\geq n^{-1/2+\epsilon}
, one gets

5\sigma^{2}\sqrt{n}\|I-X^{*}\|_{F}\geq\Omega(\sigma^{2}n),

hence the announced lower bound (
5.3
). We refer the reader to
[
36
]
for the detailed proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Remark 5.3
.

Post-processing (
5.4
) is crude, and a more refined post-processing could be proposed. In particular one could let
\hat{\pi}
be chosen as the permutation in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
that solves

(5.6)

\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sum_{i\in[n]}X^{*}_{i\pi(i)}.

This is known as the Linear Assignment Problem (LAP), or maximum weight bipartite matching problem. Empirically LAP appears to achieve overlap
1-o(1)
for
\sigma
well above
n^{-1/2}
, potentially as large as
\Omega(1/\ln n)
, but there is so far no theoretical understanding of LAP’s performance in the present setup.

6
Perspectives.

Theorem
5.1
from Section
5
predicts a threshold between
\sigma=n^{-1}
and
\sigma=n^{-1/2}
for the squared Frobenius norm
\|X^{*}-\Pi^{*}\|_{F}^{2}
to go from
o(n)
to
\Omega(n)
, a change which may compromise the accuracy of estimating
\pi^{*}
by the simple post-processing of
X^{*}
previously described by (
5.6
). We are currently investigating the precise localization of this threshold, which we expect to be at
\sigma=n^{-1/2}
.

As already mentioned, it is not known at which level of noise parameter
\sigma
do more sophisticated methods such as LAP break down. Numerical experiments suggest that LAP produces a permutation
\hat{\pi}
with overlap
1-o(1)
for
\sigma
potentially as large as
O(1/\ln n)
, and one promising direction consists in trying to get a precise characterization of the threshold for such methods.

Section
4
focused on EIG1, one among several spectral methods proposed in Feizi et al.
[
17
]
. Another family of spectral methods described in
[
17
]
is as follows: construct an
n^{2}
by
n^{2}
matrix
M
, by letting

M_{(i,k),(j,\ell)}=F(A_{1}(i,j),A_{2}(k,\ell))

for some function
F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. Proceed then to extract the eigenvector
\{x(i,k)\}_{i,k\in[n]}
of
M
corresponding to its largest eigenvalue. This can also be seen as a matrix, which may then be post-processed by using for instance LAP to obtain a permutation. The study of the spectrum of such random matrices is largely open. An exception is the case where
F(a,b)=a\times b
, which corresponds to
M=A_{1}\otimes A_{2}
and for which the spectrum of
M
is the tensor product of the spectra of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, and the eigenvectors of
M
are the tensor products of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
’s eigenvectors. Then essentially the method just described reduces to EIG1. However for more general functions
F
, the question of characterizing the spectral properties of the associated matrix
M
for correlated GOE matrices
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
constitutes an interesting challenge.

Sections
2
and
3
suggest another line of research, namely to try and obtain hardness results for graph alignment of sparse correlated Erdős-Rényi graphs. Indeed Condition (
2.1
) is, as shown in Section
2
, the threshold for feasibility of partial alignment with
O(\ln n)
-local algorithms. It is tempting to guess that (
2.1
) is also the threshold for other notions of hardness, which would lend support to the belief that it is the threshold for polynomial feasibility of partial alignment. Progress along these lines would be of interest not just for graph alignment but also more broadly in high-dimensional statistics, by shedding new light on the relationships between the various notions of hardness.

A first objective would aim to prove that partial alignment cannot be done with local search algorithms when (
2.1
) fails. More specifically, denote the posterior distribution of the sought permutation
\pi^{*}
by
\mathbb{P}_{post}(\tau):=\mathbb{P}(\pi^{*}=\tau|A)
, where again
A=(A_{1},A_{2})
is the observation. An impossibility result for local search algorithms would follow if under
\mathbb{P}_{post}
, the overlap with
\pi^{*}
satisfies a large deviations principle:

\forall C\subset[0,1],\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln(\mathbb{P}_{post}(\{\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{n}:\hbox{ov}(\tau,\pi^{*})\in C))=-\inf_{x\in C}\phi(x),

for some rate function
\phi
, and if furthermore 0 is a local minimum of
\phi
.

A second objective could consist in showing that partial alignment cannot be done with low-degree polynomial algorithms when (
2.1
) fails.

As mentioned in the Introduction, Ding, Du and Li
[
11
]
have established low-degree hardness of the hypothesis testing version of graph alignment, that consists given a pair of graphs
(A_{1},A_{2})
of deciding whether they are correlated Erdős-Rényi graphs with parameters
(n,p,s)
or alternatively whether they are independent
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
Erdős-Rényi graphs when
s<\sqrt{\alpha}
.

More recently, Li
[
26
]
has shown, under an appropriate low degree conjecture, that positive overlap
\hbox{ov}(\hat{\pi},\pi^{*})=\Omega(1)
cannot be achieved with low degree polynomials when
s<\sqrt{\alpha}
. Li’ approach
[
26
]
reduces the task of proving LDP (low-degree polynomial) impossibility of estimation to proving LDP impossibility of an associated hypothesis testing task, modulo an adequate conjecture about LDP hardness. More precisely, the hypothesis testing problem considered in
[
26
]
is to distinguish between two independent Erdős-Rényi graphs and two correlated Erdős-Rényi graphs conditioned on the event
\pi^{*}(1)=1
.

The fact established in Section
2
that the threshold
s^{*}(\lambda)
corresponding to (
2.1
) converges to
\sqrt{\alpha}
as
\lambda\to\infty
, together with the aforementioned results
[
11
]
and
[
26
]
lends credit to the belief that, in the sparse regime
\lambda=O(1)
, partial alignment is LPD-hard when (
2.1
) fails.

Two possibilites can be envisioned to attack this problem. First, one could follow the approach of
[
26
]
which reduces the problem to establishing LDP hardness of a hypothesis testing problem. Second, one could aim to establish directly LDP hardness of partial alignment, without relying on the conjecture used in
[
26
]
. To that end, one could try the novel proof technique developed by Wein and Sohn
[
35
]
for showing LDP hardness of estimation, which they applied successfully to many problems (among which estimation of planted dense subgraphs and planted submatrices). The method of
[
35
]
in some sense leverages conditional independence properties of the models at hand, and may be applicable to graph alignment.

Finally, the two models we considered, namely correlated Erdős-Rényi graphs and correlated Gaussian Wigner matrices, both display an extreme amount of symmetries in the observations
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
. While they constitute a natural starting point for mathematical analysis, less symmetrical models may be closer to real-world data, and their analysis may reveal new phenomena of computational hardness. To date, few attempts at analyzing computational feasibility of graph alignment for less symmetrical models of graphs have been made. The main exceptions address correlated random graphs drawn from the Stochastic Block Model rather than Erdős-Rényi graphs; see e.g. Li
[
26
]
and Chen et al.
[
6
]
.
In this respect, Semerjian
[
33
]
provides an inspiring discussion of the role of symmetries in high dimensional statistics, focusing on how symmetries impact the feasibility of non-trivial estimation without any constraints on computational resources. Further investigations could consider the impact of symmetries on computational hardness of estimation, and aim to identify computational thresholds for less symmetrical yet tractable models.
