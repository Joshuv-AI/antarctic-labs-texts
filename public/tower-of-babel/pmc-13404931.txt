Neural population dynamics of direct electrical stimulation of neocortex
Authors: Jordan L Hickman, Grant Hughes, Eashan Sahai, Moriah Miles, Daniel J Denman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYIntracranial electrical stimulation is foundational in neuroscience and clinical neuromodulation, yet it remains unclear as to how applied electrical fields engage neural tissue and influence perception. Using three orthogonal Neuropixels probes in mouse visual areas, we record extracellular voltages and spikes with sub-millisecond resolution in three dimensions around a stimulation source. The evoked potential extends asymmetrically and increases sub-linearly with amplitude, influenced by anatomical heterogeneity. Increasing amplitude increases the density, but not spatial extent, of directly responsive neurons; however, fewer than 5% of neurons are activated. Fast-spiking interneurons are recruited nearer the source, whereas pyramidal neurons show anisotropic activation. Direct responses are polarity and amplitude selective. Sparse direct spiking drive large, asymmetric cortical responses and modulates >50% of neurons at higher amplitudes. Perceptually, mice weakly detect single pulses, and performance does not improve with amplitude. Thus, anatomy and spatiotemporal patterning—not spike counts—govern physiological and perceptual impact, informing biomimetic, multi-contact prostheses.

INTRODUCTION

Intracranial electrical stimulation is a powerful tool for modulating neural activity, with widespread applications in basic neuroscience, to assess neural function and behavior,1-3 and clinical medicine. Clinically, intracranial electrical stimulation is used to treat movement disorders including Parkinson’s,4-6 essential tremor,7 and dystonia8; disorders of mental health including depression,9 obsessive-compulsive disorder,10 and schizophrenia11; and epilepsy.12 Beyond these indications for therapeutic neuromodulation, there is also growing interest in using electrical stimulation in cortical prostheses, devices that aim to restore lost perception by encoding information through stimulating the brain.13-22

Electrical stimulation has long been used as a tool to interrogate and restore sensory function. Early work demonstrated that direct electrical stimulation of the human visual cortex can evoke rudimentary percepts termed “phosphenes,”23 laying the groundwork for further exploration of cortical visual prostheses.24-28 Similarly, electrical stimulation in the somatosensory cortex elicits localized tactile percepts,15 suggesting feasibility for somatosensory neuroprosthetics and for providing sensory feedback to improve motor BCIs.14 However, a major limitation of sensory cortical prosthetics to date is the inability to generate percepts that closely resemble natural sensory experiences.20,29 Increasing the number of stimulating electrodes has expanded coverage of retinotopic and somatotopic space, enabling perception of a greater number of stimuli, and facilitated simultaneous stimulation at multiple sites to create additive percepts, yet the quality of percepts remains limited.18,20,29,30

Approaches to enhancing the quality of electrically evoked percepts generally seek to leverage spatial and temporal interactions of multiple electrical stimuli. For example, exploiting putative spatial specificity to isolate laminar structure and stimulating from multiple sites within functional column can improve detection thresholds in rodents.31,32 Others focus on refining stimulation paradigms to temporally mimic sensory stimuli: dynamic current steering in the primary visual cortex (V1) of sighted and blind humans can evoke percepts of letter forms, whereas static stimulation is unable to evoke the same.33 In the somatosensory cortex, spatially and temporally structured pulse trains that mimic structured spike trains derived from natural touch have been shown to evoke percepts that more closely resemble real tactile experiences.34,35 While these stimulation approaches can improve the perceptual quality and the number of discriminable features, they remain far from recreating naturalistic stimuli. A major limitation in the design of these spatiotemporal stimulation paradigms is the incomplete empirical understanding of the direct impact of electrical stimulation on intact neural tissue.

Direct measurements of evoked fields and neural response to electrical stimulation have been technically challenging due to electrical artifacts in electrophysiological recordings, spatial limits of electrophysiology techniques, temporal imprecision in imaging approaches, and ambiguity in the transfer function relating voltage or calcium changes to the recorded fluorescence. Still, studies have long sought to characterize the neural response to electrical stimulation.36 Classic work by Stoney et al. in cat motor cortex demonstrated that electrical stimulation excites neurons within a roughly spherical volume that expands with increasing current intensity by using pyramidal tract activation as a proxy for the affected volume.37 This led to the hypothesis that higher currents recruit neurons at increasing distances. Four decades later, Histed et al. revisited this with two-photon calcium imaging and arrived at a different picture. They found that even low-current electrical stimulation (5–10 μA) sparsely activates neurons distributed hundreds of microns to millimeters away from the electrode. These activated cells were scattered in space and their pattern changed with small (tens of μm) electrode movements, suggesting that the volume of affected tissue is small and stimulation primarily triggers local axonal and dendritic processes rather than cell bodies.38 These studies, using distinct approaches, framed two contrasting views: one of an expanding volume of uniform tissue activation with higher currents37 and another of a fixed local stimulation zone yielding widespread but sparse neuronal activation patterns.38 Computational modeling studies have attempted to reconcile these differences, arguing that the spatial distribution of activated somata remains relatively constant, with higher currents mainly increasing the density of activated neurons within that area39; this hypothesis remains to be empirically verified.

Here, we aim to measure the spatial and temporal extents of the effects of electrical stimulation on underlying neural tissue—the induced voltage changes and the resulting responses of populations of single neurons. To do so, we use multiple spatially registered high-density Neuropixels electrophysiological devices to capture electrically evoked activity at fine spatial (~10 μm) and temporal (sub-millisecond) resolution, with varying intracranial stimulation amplitude and polarity of single pulses. With these data, we quantify the spatial properties of stimulation-evoked neural activity, how single neurons directly respond to stimulation, and how single-neuron direct responses propagate into circuit-wide activity. These measures allow us to evaluate models of brain conductivity and the specificity of electrical stimulation for all uses of intracranial electrical stimulation that seek to modulate neural dynamics, including both deep brain stimulation and cortical prostheses. Finally, we evaluate the relationship between induced neural dynamics and perception using matched electrical stimulation in a visual detection test paradigm. Because artificial perception ultimately depends on how stimulation recruits neurons in space and time, resolving longstanding uncertainty about the spatial extent and structure of recruitment is not only a basic biophysical question but a prosthesis-relevant one. Our approach revisits foundational studies37,38 and models,39,40 with three-dimensional, sub-millisecond population multi-Neuropixels recordings, and then directly tests how these dynamics relate to perceptual detection, providing an empirical bridge from stimulation biophysics to perceptual outcomes.

RESULTS

Orthogonal multi-Neuropixels recordings for spatial measurement of electrical stimulation in awake mice

To measure the effects of electrical stimulation in neocortex, we simultaneously inserted three Neuropixels 1.0 probes,41 orthogonally to each other, to measure neural activity in three dimensions around a stimulation source (a 16-channel stimulating electrode; see STAR Methods) within the V1 in awake, head-fixed mice (Figures 1A and 1B). This configuration allowed continuous sampling within and across multiple brain regions including the V1, higher order visual areas such as anteromedial (AM), lateral (LM), and posterolateral (PL), as well as sub-neocortical regions across a white matter tract including hippocampus (CA1, dentate gyrus, subiculum) and superior colliculus (Figure 1C). The corpus callosum (CC)—a dense sheet of white matter—separates cortical and subcortical structures and provides a distinct anatomical reference point to quantify the influence of anatomical heterogeneity on the spatial spread of electrical stimulation. We targeted stimulation to layer 5 of V1 due to its role in cortical output pathways, dense activity, and anatomical proximity to the white matter boundary (Figure 1I). Each recording Neuropixels probe and the stimulating electrode array were registered in the mouse common coordinate framework (CCF) brain coordinate space42 to measure anatomical and distance effects of stimulation responses and to allow comparisons across recordings. To align each probe and the stimulating electrode accurately, we combined an a priori stereotactic plan based on fixed probe geometry with post hoc verification using histology and electrophysiological landmarks (Figures 1A-1E, STAR Methods). This alignment assigns each Neuropixels channel and stimulating electrode contact a CCF coordinate; these coordinates are mapped to anatomical brain regions and also allow three-dimensional spatial measurements across recordings (Figures 1F and 1G).

During each recording session, we systematically varied electrical stimulation parameters of single constant-current electrical pulses, focusing on current amplitude and polarity (Figure 1H, STAR Methods). We used a linear 16-channel stimulating electrode (Neuronexus, A1x16-5mm-50-703) with iridium oxide site activation to decrease contact impedance and increase charge delivery. The average contact impedance was 0.10 ± 0.06 MΩ (Figure 1J, top), and the average contact charge capacity was 25.90 ± 9.69 mC/cm2 (Figure 1J, bottom). We describe the charge magnitude in amperes (μA) but include a conversion to charge density (Figure 1K), allowing findings to be better extrapolated to other electrode configurations. Simultaneous electrophysiology and electrical stimulation can result in saturating electrical artifacts that obscure neural data. In our recordings, we exploited Neuropixels 1.0 band-specific amplifier gain and the rapid settling of Neuropixels amplifiers to observe the neural response almost immediately following stimulation. On average, following a 0.2 ms total-duration electrical pulse, saturation persisted for approximately 0.14 ± 0.00 ms at 5 μA, 0.24 ± 0.01 ms at 25 μA, 0.26 ± 0.01 ms at 50 μA, and 0.30 ± 0.01 ms at 100 μA (Figure 1L, one-way ANOVA, F = 105.62, p < 0.001). In rare cases, saturation at channels extremely close to the stimulating electrode extended up to 1 ms for cathodal-leading monopolar and cathodal-leading bipolar stimulation (Figure S1A, i-iii). Overall, we were able to observe neural activation across polarities and amplitudes (Figure S1B) despite increases in saturation with amplitude (two-way ANOVA, F = 236.04, p < 0.001), and polarity alone did not significantly change saturation duration (F = 2.48, p = 0.09).

Evoked potential spatial extent increases sub-linearly with amplitude

We first sought to characterize the spatial spread of stimulation-evoked extracellular potentials generated by a single electrode or a pair of adjacent electrodes during electrical stimulation. where ρ is the tissue resistivity (Ω·m), I is the injected current (in amperes), and r is the radial distance from the source (in meters). Applications of Gauss’s law to models of brain stimulation assume uniform brain conductivity, neglecting potential anatomical and conductive variations such as differences in axonal myelination, dendritic branching, and somatic size.43 Consequently, these models predict isotropic spatial boundaries for extracellular fields that increase linearly with current amplitude.39,43 Conversely, the current required for neural activation increases with the square of distance from the electrode,36 highlighting that the spatial extent of the modeled extracellular field and the spatial extent of neural activation are not equivalent. Local anatomical structures and heterogeneous tissue conductivity could significantly influence both the magnitude and spatial profile of electrically evoked potentials, but direct measurements of this have been elusive. We first employed the idealized point-source model with a uniform cerebral cortex resistivity of 5.8 Ω·m44,45 to calculate the instantaneous evoked field and isotropic spatial bounds at experimental currents (5, 25, 50, and 100 μA) (Figures 2A-2C, STAR Methods). This model serves to provide a geometric reference point for symmetry and amplitude-dependent extent against which to compare in vivo observations.

Next, we empirically measured the spatial extent and magnitude of stimulation-evoked potentials using raw extracellular voltages recorded with Neuropixels probes (Figure 2D). We recorded and processed (STAR Methods) extracellular potentials that followed a brief, saturating electrical artifact and evaluated these in a window from 0.3–3 ms post-stimulation onset, a window that excludes the saturated portion (Figure 1L). These evoked extracellular potentials are a biological readout shaped by the stimulation-induced electric field and the resulting neuronal transmembrane currents. We compare these extents to the radial isotropic extents of an instantaneous induced electric field from the point source model.

We characterized the extracellular potential for each probe (n = 25) across recordings (N = 9) superficial (or dorsal) to the stimulation source (denoted as negative distances) and deep (or ventral) to the stimulation source (denoted as positive distances). We aligned each probe to the peak response and averaged the evoked potential for 5, 25, 50, and 100 μA for cathodal (Figure 2E), anodal, and bipolar polarities (Figure S2); increasing stimulation amplitude led to larger evoked potentials, both in spatial spread and magnitude of potential, for all polarities (STAR Methods). As expected, cathodal-leading monopolar Figure 2E) and bipolar stimulation (Figure S2C) predominantly induced negative extracellular potentials, corresponding to depolarizing membrane potentials.36,43 Anodal-leading monopolar stimulation led to an early positive electrically evoked potential, consistent with a hyperpolarizing effect on membrane potential, but was followed by negative potential deflections beginning around 2 ms (Figure S2B). This observation aligns with previous literature suggesting early inhibitory effects of anodal stimulation but paradoxical somatic activation.36,43,46,47

To quantify the magnitude and spatial spread, we examined the extracellular potentials at a fixed time point of 1 ms, chosen to reflect a stable phase of the evoked potential across stimulation conditions (Figure 2F). We measured the magnitude of the evoked potential by calculating the mean maximum evoked potential and the area under the curve (AUC). For all polarities, both the maximum evoked potential and AUC increased nearly linearly (Figures S2G and S2H; see Table S1). Next, we calculated the average spatial spread across all probes, both superficial and deep to the stimulation source (STAR Methods). Both the superficial and deep distance from stimulation increased with amplitude (Figure 2G, superficial: 5 μA: −424.21 ± 209.65 μm, 25 μA: −683.18 ± 252.64 μm, 50 μA: −741.94 ± 267.22 μm, 100 μA: −743.84 ± 264.17 μm; deep: 5 μA: 510 ± 214 μm, 25 μA: 591 ± 261 μm, 50 μA: 662 ± 263 μm, 100 μA: 712 ± 286 μm, p = 0.002, linear mixed-effects model; see STAR Methods and Table S1 for full model details). While the point-source model suggests linear increases in spatial extent to increasing applied current, the increase in spatial extent of the biologically observed evoked potential was sublinear and poorly fit by linear regression (superficial R2 = 0.02 and deep R2 = 0.17). This deviation most likely reflects nonlinearities in the biological response (recruitment and transmembrane currents). Notably, spatial extents did not differ across polarities (Figures 2J and S2E, linear mixed-effects model, p = 0.151, see STAR Methods and Table S1 for more details). This suggests that the spatial limit of stimulation spread is primarily determined by amplitude rather than polarity, in contrast to some predictions suggesting greater spatial focus with bipolar stimulation, but consistent with previous evidence and modeling expecting similar spatial extents.40,43,48 Altogether, we found that spatial extent increased sublinearly with amplitude both superficially and deep to the stimulation source, with no significant polarity effects.

To evaluate asymmetries in the spatial profile of evoked potentials, we compared the decay of extracellular potentials measured from different probe identities (probe A, probe B, and probe C), which differ in their trajectory and anatomical sampling; probe A and probe B cross the white matter boundary and extend into subcortical areas, while the probe C trajectory allows it to remain in cortex for the full extent of its insertion. The point-source model predicts radial isotropy in spatial extent of the evoked field. However, we observed probe trajectory had a significant effect on average spatial extent, most notably with an increase in spatial extent for probe C (Figure 2H, probe A vs. probe C: p < 0.001, probe B vs. probe C: p < 0.001, linear mixed-effects model, see STAR Methods and Table S1 for more details). These findings suggest that anatomical heterogeneity, such as dense white matter, alters tissue conductive properties to produce an anisotropic spatial profile of electrically evoked potentials. We further explored this asymmetry relative to underlying neural tissue using the alignment of evoked fields to mouse common brain coordinates.

Anisotropic volumetric profile of the induced electrical field

To further explore the anisotropy and identify anatomical factors shaping the electrically evoked potential (Figure 3A), we quantified the volumetric distribution of evoked potentials (Figure 3B, STAR Methods). Volume, measured as the three-dimensional convex hull spanning the one-dimensional extents recorded on orthogonal Neuropixels probes (Figure 2, STAR Methods), trended to increase sublinearly with stimulation amplitude (Figure 3C; cathodal polarity: −25 μA: 0.31 ± 0.20 mm3, −50 μA: 0.43 ± 0.19 mm3, −100 μA: 0.61 ± 0.22 mm3; linear R2 = 0.259; see Table S1 for full results across polarities).

Inspection of reconstructed volumes in brain space revealed asymmetries at the gray-white matter boundary: evoked potentials extended broadly within cortex but were compressed ventrally at the corpus callosum (Figure 3D). To formally assess these asymmetries, we first established a symmetric reference by fitting the data with isotropic spheres (Figure 3D, gray spheres). This provided a baseline null expectation for isotropic evoked-potential shape against which to compare the observed profiles. Because convex-hull volumes underestimate extent along unsampled directions, we also constructed convex hulls from the fitted spheres, ensuring symmetry in both fit and measurement geometry. We then compared the observed convex hulls from evoked potentials to the imputed symmetric sphere-based hulls by calculating the Euclidean distance from each point on the symmetric hull to the nearest facet of the observed evoked potential hull (Figure 3E). This analysis extended the visual impression from individual examples (Figure 3D) to a condition-averaged measure of asymmetry. Deviations consistently localized to the ventral aspects of the fields: when probes remained in cortex (probe C, Figure 3E), responses extended beyond the symmetric fit, whereas when probes traversed into subcortical white matter (probe B, Figure 3E), responses were truncated relative to the sphere, confirming anatomical influence hypotheses generated from differences in spatial extents by probe identity in Figure 2H. We then used the volumetric fits from Figure 3E to quantify anisotropy. First, we computed the anisotropy strength (Figure 3F, STAR Methods), defined as the norm of a directional bias term relative to the isotropic baseline radius. This dimensionless metric is 0 for perfectly spherical profiles and increases with directional variance. Across all amplitudes and polarities, anisotropy strength averaged 0.493 ± 0.288 and did not vary systematically with stimulation amplitude or polarity (Figure 3G, see Table S1). Second, we quantified the vector displacement between the stimulating contact and the center of mass (COM) of the evoked-potential volume relative to the corpus callosum (Figure 3H). The displacement magnitude exceeded zero (one-sample t test vs. 0, p = 0.02), indicating anisotropy. The dorsal (superficial) component averaged 105 ± 46 μm away from the corpus callosum and, while directionally consistent, did not reach significance (one-sample t test vs. 0, p = 0.1095). This superficial bias is consistent with ventral compression of evoked potentials at the white matter (corpus callosum) boundary.

Together, these analyses demonstrate that the induced electrically evoked potential is not isotropic but is shaped by anatomical constraints, including gray-white matter boundaries. Translationally, this anisotropy implies that in both deep brain stimulation and cortical prostheses, activation will preferentially spread along tissue planes and fiber pathways, indicating that current steering, electrode orientation, and patient-specific anatomy must be considered to achieve focal recruitment and stable percepts.

Evoked spiking is sparse and increases in density with amplitude

Our measurements indicate that the potential produced by the induced electrical field from a stimulating electrode grows sublinearly with amplitude and is anisotropic, influenced by brain substrate. The neural responses of each neuron within this field, based on its morphology and physiology, may exacerbate or alleviate these spatial nonlinearities and/or asymmetries. To understand the relationship between electrical stimulation and single-neuron activation, we examined the spiking of single units directly following stimulation onset and how these responses relate spatially to the stimulation source, anatomical boundaries, and stimulation amplitude. Specifically, we measured single-unit reliability, spatial uniformity and density, and across-class uniformity in response to electrical stimulation, and asked how the spatial distribution of activated units compared to the volume defined by evoked potentials.

Historically, early neural responses to electrical stimulation have been difficult to measure due to saturating electrical artifacts and amplifier properties,49,50 thus, we verified that single unit spiking could be reliably captured and appropriately clustered with sub-millisecond precision by overlaying raw extracellular voltage traces with Kilosort (see STAR Methods) identified clusters during a 10 ms post-stimulation window (Figure 4A). To more broadly survey evoked spiking, we plotted each unit during the 10 ms following each −50 μA cathodal pulse, ordering units by cortical depth and annotating anatomical layers for each probe (Figure 4B, i-iii), and rasters from representative single units across the full amplitude range (Figure 4C, i-iii). Within this 10 ms window, we noted both spikes that occurred almost immediately after the pulse and spikes that were delayed by several milliseconds, the latter suggesting synaptically propagated activity. We sought to isolate spiking activity that was directly influenced by the electrical pulse and not derived from synaptic propagation, which we refer to as a “direct” response. We defined a direct response as spiking activity occurring within a 2 ms window immediately following stimulation onset. This window was chosen based on the distribution of first-spike latencies, which exhibited a bimodal shape: an early peak immediately after stimulation, followed by a brief pause occurring at a median value of 1.95 ms across parameters and a broader, and sustained peak beginning around 2.5 ms (Figures 4D and S3B). The 2 ms cutoff provides an estimate intended to capture most direct activation while minimizing spiking from synaptically propagated activity. To validate this approximation, we fit a Gaussian-mixed model to the distribution of first spike latencies for each stimulation amplitude for cathodal stimulation to create a direct response window for each amplitude (5 μA: 0.68 ms; 25 μA: 1.14 ms; 50 μA: 1.22 ms, and 100 μA: 1.47 ms, Figure S3C); there was no statistical difference in the overall proportion of direct responders compared to the 2-ms window used (Figure S3D).

For each single unit, we calculated direct-response probability, normalized for differences in firing rates (see STAR Methods). Single units with a direct-response probability change following electrical stimulation greater than 0.15 were classified as “direct responders.” When visualized in three-dimensional anatomical brain space, direct responders were sparse, amplitude-dependent, and, while not spatially restricted, predominantly clustered within cortex near the stimulation site (Figure 4E). To quantify the relationship between distance from stimulation and single-unit direct-response probability, we measured the Euclidean distance of each single unit from the stimulation source and calculated direct-response probability as a function of distance across stimulation amplitudes (Figure 4F). These measures allowed us to assess the density of neuronal activation within the applied field and the relationship between distance from stimulation and the probability of direct activation. Direct responders were sparse and generally constrained within the average boundaries of the evoked potential (33.3% for 5 μA, 74.1% for 25 μA, 88.2% for 50μA, and 100% for 100 μA; Figure 4F, shaded region). Notably, at the lowest amplitude (5 μA), more direct responders existed outside of the boundary compared to higher amplitudes, consistent with previous observations38 of antidromic or orthodromic somatic activation outside of the evoked potential via dendrites or axons near the stimulation source.

Despite increases in the spatial extent of measured evoked potentials (Figure 4F, shaded region; from Figure 2), the spatial extent of single-unit responders remained relatively constant across amplitudes (5 μA: 1180 ± 559 μm, 25 μA: 884 ± 652 μm, 50 μA: 965 ± 451 μm, and 100 μA: 522 ± 313 μm, One-way ANOVA, F = 1.265, p = 0.312) (Figures 4H and 4I, ii). Stimulation amplitude significantly decreased the average distance from stimulation of responsive units (5 μA: 426 ± 500 μm, 25 μA: 300 ± 436 μm, 50 μA: 137 ± 394 μm, and 100 μA: 146 ± 260 μm; one-way ANOVA, F = 4.15, p = 0.007), driven specifically by differences between 5 μA and 50 μA and between 5 μA and 100 μA (post hoc Tukey tests: 5 vs. 50 μA, p = 0.015; 5 vs. 100 μA, p = 0.019; p > 0.05 for other pairs). Further, 5 μA and 100 μA recruited distinct spatial populations (pairwise two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, corrected for six comparisons using the Holm-Šidák procedure: 5 vs. 25 μA, padj = 0.566; 5 vs. 100μA, padj = 0.016; 25 vs. 50μA, padj = 0.566; 25 vs. 100μA, padj = 0.363; and 50 vs. 100μA, padj = 0.083; Figures 4H and 4I, ii). Together, these results indicate that increasing stimulation amplitude does not expand the spatial reach of directly responsive neurons but instead biases recruitment toward neurons located closer to the stimulation site, engaging partially distinct local populations.

The proportion of responders within the evoked potential increased with amplitude (5 μA: 0.014 ± 0.12, 25 μA: 0.020 ± 0.14, 50 μA: 0.031 ± 0.172, and 100 μA: 0.030 ± 0.170; linear ANOVA, F = 5.41, p = 0.020, Figure 4I), and among responders, direct-response probability increased with amplitude (5 μA: 0.12 ± 0.20, 25 μA: 0.14 ± 0.21, 50 μA: 0.19 ± 0.25, and 100 μA: 0.23 ± 0.31; F = 12.78, p = 0.0004, Figure 4J), suggesting that increasing amplitude both recruits more neurons and increases the spiking probability of recruited neurons. Altogether, these results demonstrate that electrical stimulation induces direct neural firing in a sparse subpopulation of neurons (less than 5%) within the spatial extent of the induced electrical field.

While the 2 ms window was conservatively set to minimize contributions from synaptic activity, we additionally explored the proportion of single units that were responsive within 5 and 10 ms windows (“early” responders), which may reflect a combination of direct and early synaptically mediated spiking. As expected, broader windows increased the proportion of responsive units (0.092 ± 0.01 for the 5 ms window and 0.175 ± 0.01 for the 10 ms window at 100μA, FDR-corrected p < 0.001, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests; see Table S1 for full comparisons; Figure S3E). Notably, the amplitude dependence was stronger for the 5 and 10 ms windows than for the 2 ms window, consistent with two non-exclusive interpretations: (i) increasing amplitude increases the probability of recruiting synaptically mediated spiking captured by broader windows and (ii) larger stimulation artifacts at higher amplitudes may obscure some spikes within the 2 ms window, resulting in underestimating direct responsiveness for higher amplitudes. In summary, the population of direct and early responders remains sparse even while this population becomes denser with increasing amplitude. Next, we considered the composition of the sparse subpopulations directly activated by electrical stimulation near the stimulation site in cortex.

Stimulation amplitude and polarity tune unit-specific recruitment

Because amplitude modulated not only how many neurons responded but also where they were located, we next asked whether stimulation parameters (amplitude and polarity) could be used to selectively recruit single units. We expected that single units would exhibit a dose-dependent increase in direct-response probability with increasing stimulation amplitude,36 such that the observed increase in density (Figure 4) resulted from increased recruitment of a uniform underlying population. To test this, we clustered single units classified as direct responders at any amplitude based on their direct-response probability across amplitudes (Figures 5A-5C, STAR Methods), expecting most clusters to be dose-responsive; indeed, we have already shown that, on average within this subgroup of units, response probability increased with amplitude (Figure 4I). Hierarchical clustering produced six clusters (Figure S5A; cophenetic correlation coefficient (CCC) = 0.702, mean silhouette score = 0.352) that were broadly either dose-dependent or amplitude-selective. Surprisingly, while 58.6% of direct responders exhibited dose-dependent increases in response probability (Figures 5A-5C, i-iii; see Table S1 for cluster-wise comparisons), we observed a subset of single units demonstrating amplitude selectivity, responding maximally—and almost exclusively—at specific intermediate amplitudes rather than monotonically increasing with amplitude (19.2% for 5 μA, 15.2% for 25 μA, and 7.1% for 100 μA; Figures 5A-5C, iv-vi; see Table S1 for cluster-wise comparisons).

We next asked whether stimulus polarity shapes which neurons are recruited. Clustering units by their normalized probability of firing in response to cathodal, anodal, and bipolar pulses (Figures 5D-E and S5B, STAR Methods; number of clusters = 6, CCC = 0.806, silhouette score = 0.519) revealed a striking polarity bias: 69.1% of directly activated units were polarity-specific, preferring bipolar (27.7%, Figure 5F, i), anodal (23.6%, Figure 5F, ii), or cathodal (17.7%, Figure 5F, iii) stimulation. The remaining units fell into smaller groups that were largely insensitive to polarity: 10.9% were dose-selective but polarity-agnostic (Figure 5F, iv), 12.7% responded only when the cathodal phase of the pulse led (phase-selective, dose-dependent; Figure 5F, v), and 3.3% were driven exclusively by monopolar pulses (Figure 5F, vi; see Table S1 for a comprehensive breakdown of each cluster). Together, these findings demonstrate that polarity and amplitude sharply tune single-unit recruitment, raising the possibility that intrinsic neuronal properties—beyond spatial location alone—determine which single units respond to specific electrical stimulation parameters.

Cell type correlates with direct activation distance distributions and response probability with amplitude dependence

To probe what makes some neurons, but not their neighbors, susceptible to specific pulses, we next compared how the two major cortical classes—fast-spiking (FS) interneurons and regular-spiking (RS) pyramidal cells—respond across space and stimulation parameters.

We classified single neurons by their electrophysiologically recorded waveforms as FS, RS, or axonal51 (Figures 5G and 5H) and analyzed the direct responses of FS and RS cells. The proportion of direct responders remained sparse across amplitudes for both FS (5– 100 μA: 0.032 ± 0.177, 0.035 ± 0.183, 0.030 ± 0.170, and 0.040 ± 0.196) and RS neurons (0.013 ± 0.115, 0.033 ± 0.178, 0.039 ± 0.194, and 0.037 ± 0.188), with a significant main effect of amplitude but no effect of waveform class or interaction (two-way ANOVA: amplitude F = 3.04, p = 0.028; waveform class F = 1.33, p = 0.262; interaction F = 1.34, p = 0.210, Figure 6I).

We observed differences in the spatial distribution of FS and RS responders at 5, 50, and 100 μA. In line with our previous findings (Figure 4), increasing amplitude constrained the average distance from stimulation for both FS responders (5 μA: 205 ± 311 μm; 25 μA: 203 ± 405 μm; 50 μA: −2 ± 122 μm; and 100 μA: 60 ± 163 μm) and RS responders (5 μA: 567 ± 603 μm; 25 μA: 337 ± 455 μm; 50 μA: 208 ± 406 μm; and 100 μA: 185 ± 296 μm; two-way ANOVA, Famplitude = 4.10, p = 0.008). FS responders were clustered significantly closer to the stimulation site than RS responders (two-way ANOVA, Fwaveform = 8.04, p = 0.005).

FS responders were almost entirely constrained within the evoked-potential boundary, even at low amplitudes (75.0% for 5 μA; 83.3% for 25 μA; 100% for 50 μA; and 100% for 100 μA). In contrast, only 33.0% of RS responders were within the evoked potential at 5 μA, with this proportion increasing with amplitude (74.1% for 25 μA; 88.2% for 50 μA; and 100% for 100 μA; Figures 5I and 5J). Differences in the spatial distributions of FS and RS single units likely reflect morphological and anatomical distinctions between the classes. These results suggest that FS interneurons near the stimulation site—with compact axonal and dendritic arbors—are preferentially and locally recruited, whereas RS pyramidal neurons—with broader dendritic fields and longer-range projections—are more readily activated at a distance, revealing distinct spatial modes of excitability across cell types.

To assess how these spatial differences in the direct response (within <2 ms) contribute to spiking that includes early synaptic integration, we overlaid spiking from all cortical units, color-coded by waveform class and sorted by depth within a 10 ms window (Figure S6A) and averaged their firing rates (Figure S6B). Within this broader window, we observed pronounced differences in spiking between waveform classes across amplitudes that evolved with stimulation strength (Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests at each amplitude: 5 μA, padj = 0.010; 25 μA, padj = 0.007; 50 μA, padj = 0.019; and 100 μA, padj = 0.010; Figure S6B). These early differences raise questions about how the direct response to a single stimulation pulse propagates through the network over longer timescales.

Single pulse electrical stimulation leads to extensive circuit response

While the direct response to stimulation was sparse, with less than 5% of recorded neurons responding within the defined 2 ms window (Figure 4), this gave way to substantial amplitude- and region-dependent circuit activity. At higher amplitudes, stimulation triggered a global, highly synchronous response among cortical neurons that persisted for hundreds of milliseconds (Figures 6A and 6B). This circuit-wide activity propagated asymmetrically, shaped by anatomical connectivity: subcortical units largely maintained their spontaneous firing rate, whereas neurons in visual cortical areas, well beyond the spatial reach of the initial stimulation, were robustly engaged (Figure 6B).

Cortical single units displayed a response consisting of early excitation, suppression, and at least one rebound excitation (Figure 6C), consistent with previous descriptions in the literature.19,49,52-54 The magnitude of spiking modulation (STAR Methods) increased with amplitude for cortical units (5 μA: 12.60 ± 8.43, 25 μA: 13.37 ± 8.41, 50 μA: 15.11 ± 8.80, and 100 μA: 20.21 ± 10.32) but remained relatively constant for subcortical units (5 μA: 12.27 ± 7.21, 25 μA: 12.60 ± 8.00, 50 μA: 12.08 ± 7.53, and 100 μA: 13.48 ± 7.70; Figure 6C). A two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of brain region (Fregion = 106.28, p < 0.001), stimulation amplitude (Famplitude = 487.36, p < 0.001), and their interaction (Famplitude:region = 90.88, p < 0.001). The overall magnitude of modulation differed between cortical and subcortical units at 50μA (Student’s t test, p < 0.001) and 100 μA (Student’s t test, p < 0.001; Figure 6C). This overall trend persisted across all polarities with minor differences (Figure S7D; multivariate ANOVA, Fpolarity = 96.14, p < 0.001).

Notably, differences in modulation were not driven solely by distance from stimulation but instead reflected connectivity. Higher visual areas (HVAs) exhibited responses at higher amplitudes (magnitude of modulation = 15.82 ± 9.97) that were significantly different from those of subcortical units (magnitude of modulation = 13.48 ± 7.70) at 100 μA (Tukey HSD, p = 0.004; see Table S1 for full comparisons; Figures 6D and S8A), despite originating from a similar distance-from-stimulation distribution (Mann-Whitney test, p = 0.511; see Table S1 for full comparisons). This suggests that a single pulse of electrical stimulation in V1 results in rapid, distributed engagement of multiple cortical areas. Further, the proportion of cortical neurons participating in this circuit response increased with amplitude (5 μA: 0.14 ± 0.05, 25 μA: 0.21 ± 0.08, 50 μA: 0.52 ± 0.26, and 100 μA: 0.79 ± 0.21; Famplitude = 175.18, p < 0.001), such that at the highest amplitudes, over half of all recorded cortical units were recruited (Figure 6E). Despite being less responsive overall, the number of circuit responders in subcortical regions also increased with amplitude (5 μA: 0.08 ± 0.02, 25 μA: 0.09 ± 0.03, 50 μA: 0.17 ± 0.07, and 100 μA: 0.35 ± 0.19; Famplitude = 175.18, p < 0.001; Famplitude:region = 15.61, p < 0.001; Fregion = 40.14, p < 0.001), consistent with the volume increase observed in evoked potentials (Figure 2), yet surprising, given the spatial constraints of the direct response (Figure 4). Small but significant differences were observed across polarities; however, overall responses followed the same regional and amplitude-dependent trends (multivariate ANOVA, Fpolarity = 5.19, p = 0.006; see Table S1 for full comparisons; Figure S7C).

To determine whether this circuit response reflected a triggered, propagating wave, we sorted single units as a function of distance from stimulation and visualized their average modulation indices. Contrary to expectations for a propagating wave, no apparent temporal structure was observed. This was confirmed statistically by the absence of a relationship between distance from stimulation and time to peak inhibition (Figure 6F, ii; Pearson r = 0.09) or time to peak rebound excitation (Figure 6F, iii; Pearson r = −0.03). These findings suggest that the observed circuit dynamics reflect synchronous recruitment of a recurrent network, either cortico-cortical55 or corticothalamo-cortical,56,57 spanning at least ~1 mm—a spatial scale comparable to that of the evoked potential. The millimeter scale evoked potential provides a spatially extended, near-simultaneous perturbation that could synchronize recurrent cortical circuitry, rapidly engaging distributed areas without requiring directional propagation. Antidromic recruitment of interareal projection fibers near the stimulation site may further contribute to widespread engagement.

Cortical state can modulate neural responses to visual58 and electrical stimuli59 and may potentially influence perception (60,61). We classified trials as “high” or “low” state by the cumulative population spiking during the 500 ms preceding stimulation (see STAR Methods, Figure S9A) and explored the potential effect on direct response probability and circuit response. DRP was slightly higher during high cortical states across stimulation amplitudes (−5 μA: low = 0.051 ± 0.112, high = 0.056 ± 0.110, p < 0.001; −25 μA: low = 0.061 ± 0.122, high = 0.065 ± 0.109, p < 0.001; −50 μA: low = 0.082 ± 0.151, high = 0.084 ± 0.153, p = 0.010; and −100 μA: low = 0.104 ± 0.197, high = 0.107 ± 0.192, p = 0.030; paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test across units with false discovery rate correction; see Table S1; Figure S9B). Although statistically significant at each amplitude, the absolute differences were small. When examining firing rates over a 500 ms post-stimulation window, differences between high and low state trials were modest, and overall response profiles were broadly similar (Figure S9C).

Finally, we sought to link circuit-wide responses to potential encoding capacity. To do so, we examined the dimensionality of neural population activity following electrical stimulation, because high population dimensionality is a hallmark of visual processing.62 We explored population dimensionality using two principal-component analysis (PCA)-derived metrics: participation ratio (PR), which reflects the evenness of variance distribution across principal components, and effective dimensionality, which describes the number of dimensions required to explain 95% of the variance (STAR Methods). Despite distinct temporal phases marked by fluctuations in firing rate (Figure 6G, i, black) that increased with stimulation amplitude, the response window was associated with a pronounced reduction in neural dimensionality, as reflected by decreases in both PR and effective dimensionality (Figure 6G, ii, purple and blue; STAR Methods). This finding suggests that cortical responses to single-pulse electrical stimulation, in contrast to responses to visual stimulation, become highly coordinated, with individual neurons exhibiting stereotyped activity during the evoked response, even across periods of fluctuating firing rates. Notably, at 5, 25, and 50 μA, we observed small transient increases in effective dimensionality within 20 ms of stimulation onset, which may reflect a brief window of enhanced encoding capacity before subsequent collapse of dimensionality, providing optimism for potential sustained expansion of neural dimensionality when delivering trains of electrical stimulation (especially those varied across both space and time). The dense increase in firing rate concurrent with a collapse in dimensionality creates diverging expectations for the behavioral accessibility of neural activity driven by single-pulse electrical stimulation.

Single pulses of electrical stimulation are weakly detected, similar to low contrast visual stimuli

Given the extensive cortical effects of a single pulse of electrical stimulation, we sought to determine whether this widespread modulation of cortex yields behaviorally relevant information without explicit training to detect electrical stimulation. We first trained mice to detect visual stimuli and then examined whether single pulses of electrical stimulation in V1 could be behaviorally reported in a manner analogous to visual stimuli (STAR Methods). Mice were trained to indicate detection of a stimulus by licking during a fixed response window (Figure 7A). Based on the licking behavior, trials were classified as hits (rewarded detections), lapses (missed responses), or false alarms (licking before stimulus presentation; Figure 7B, STAR Methods). Mice were initially trained to detect static gratings (Figure 7C, phase 1), after which we introduced a different visual stimulus—a moving circle—to test whether detection generalized across stimulus classes (Figure 7C, phase 2). Mice that successfully demonstrated detection of both visual stimulus classes advanced to phase 3, in which trials were intermixed and included the previously trained visual stimuli as well as single pulses of electrical stimulation at varying amplitudes. This design allowed us to directly compare the behavioral detectability of electrical stimulation pulses with visual stimuli of known contrast and perceptual characteristics (Figure 7C, phase 3).

Mice successfully learned to detect high-contrast static gratings (>30% contrast) over an average of 1353.8 ± 496.8 rewarded high-contrast trials and 14802 ± 5154 total trials (n = 4 mice; Figure 7C, STAR Methods). These mice generalized detection of static gratings to the moving-circle stimulus before testing electrical stimulation, achieving a sensitivity index of d′ = 2.35 ± 0.30, comparable to their performance on high-contrast grating stimuli (d′ = 1.62 ± 0.92, p = 0.113). In one mouse, the moving-circle task was introduced at a time when performance on high-contrast grating stimuli exceeded d′ = 6; this mouse immediately performed the moving-circle task at a learned rate (d′ >2), and within 500 trials, its d′ matched that of the static grating stimuli (Figures S10B-S10I), suggesting that mice were capable of generalizing across visual stimulus classes without explicit training.

Following visual training, mice were chronically implanted with stimulating electrodes (Neuronexus, CM16) in V1 using the same stereotactic coordinates and depths validated in the previous acute electrophysiological recordings. To confirm accurate electrode placement within visually responsive cortical areas and verify electrode functionality, we simultaneously recorded from non-stimulating contacts on the implanted stimulating electrode during behavioral sessions (Figure 7F, i). Recorded channels exhibited visually evoked potentials in response to high-contrast visual stimuli (Figure 7F, ii), confirming placement within visually responsive cortical regions. We additionally verified effective delivery of electrical stimulation by recording neural activity from non-stimulated electrode contacts during stimulation trials. As expected, we observed large electrical artifacts coincident with stimulation events in both the local field potential (LFP) band (Figure 7F, iii, top) and the action potential (AP) band (Figure 7F, iii, bottom). On specific contacts, we also observed increases in multi-unit activity following stimulation, confirming that electrical pulses reliably modulated neural activity during task performance (Figure 7F, iii, bottom).

Mice detected single pulses of electrical stimulation at similar rates across amplitudes (d′ for 5 μA: 0.37 ± 0.04, 25 μA: 0.32 ± 0.14, 50 μA: 0.36 ± 0.06, and 100 μA: 0.45 ± 0.14; Famplitude = 1.74, p = 0.21, Figure 7G) and above chance levels (average pulse d′ = 0.37 ± 0.06; one-sample t test compared to d′ = 0, p = 0.001, Figure 7H). Anodal-leading waveforms were marginally more detectable than cathodal-leading waveforms (anodal d′ = 0.44 ± 0.06, cathodal d′ = 0.33 ± 0.09; paired t test, p = 0.028, Figure S10C). High-frequency stimulation trains (100–300 Hz; 10–50 pulses), similar to those commonly used in visual cortical prostheses,20 yielded higher detection sensitivity than single pulses (d′ = 0.66 ± 0.11 vs. 0.37 ± 0.06; paired t test, p = 0.019). Within stimulation trains, detection sensitivity did not differ significantly by frequency (100 Hz: 0.72 ± 0.13, 200 Hz: 0.46 ± 0.14, 300 Hz: 0.61 ± 0.09; one-way ANOVA, F = 3.22, p = 0.112), although frequencies were unevenly sampled.

Performance (d’) on visual stimuli increased with contrast for both static gratings (2%: 0.20 ± 0.16, 4%: 0.42 ± 0.23, 8%: 0.63 ± 0.33, 16%: 0.70 ± 0.50, 32%: 0.89 ± 0.61, 64%: 1.17 ± 0.65, 80%: 1.30 ± 0.66, and 100%: 1.30 ± 0.65) and moving circles (2%: 0.27 ± 0.11, 4%: 0.43 ± 0.14, 8%: 0.86 ± 0.37, 16%: 1.15 ± 0.14, 32%: 1.50 ± 0.31, 64%: 1.54 ± 0.31, 80%: 1.48 ± 0.15, and 100%: 1.77 ± 0.35), as confirmed by one-way ANOVA (static gratings: Fcontrast = 18.28, p < 0.001; moving circles: Fcontrast = 46.52, p < 0.001; Figure 7I, refer to Figure S10G for raw hit rates of catch and varying intensities for stimuli and Table S1 for full details). Perceptual detection in highly trained mice emerged at 4% contrast for static gratings (one-sample t test, p = 0.035) and 2% contrast for moving circles (one-sample t test, p = 0.018, Figure 7I). Single pulses of electrical stimulation performed comparably to visual contrasts between 2% and 4%, whereas stimulation trains performed near the level of an 8% visual contrast (Figure 7I). We interpret this equivalence as a behavioral detectability benchmark rather than evidence that stimulation evokes a percept matched to a 4%–8% visual contrast; mice may instead be reporting the occurrence of a salient event (potentially shaped by arousal or widespread population responses) rather than specific visual content.60,61

Within individual sessions, electrical stimulation became less detectable across trials (mixed linear model regression: intercept = 0.56, pintercept<0.001, zintercept = 6.61; slope = −0.004, pslope<0.001, zslope = −15.18, Figure 7J), suggesting habituation or desensitization to repeated electrical stimulation, consistent with previous reports in somatosensory and visual cortex.21,63 Reaction times decreased with increasing contrast for both classes of visual stimuli, whereas reaction times for electrical stimuli were similar to those for low-contrast visual stimuli (Figure S10F see Table S1), indicating low perceptual salience of electrical stimulation.

Percepts evoked by electrical stimulation are often described as unnatural but learnable over time.64 Although our behavioral paradigm was not designed to assess learning of electrical stimulation, electrical stimuli were tested across many sessions, providing ample opportunity for improvement. We observed no evidence of such learning: detection performance did not increase across sessions (ordinary least-squares regression, R2 = 0.01, F = 1.12, slope = −0.004, pslope = 0.29, intercept = 0.39, pintercept<0.001).

Overall, mice weakly detected electrical stimulation, at levels comparable to low-contrast visual stimuli. These findings suggest that single pulses of electrical stimulation, despite inducing widespread cortical modulation persisting for hundreds of milliseconds, are only weakly perceptible behaviorally, revealing a disconnect between cortical activation magnitude and perceptual salience.

DISCUSSION

Electrical stimulation is a cornerstone of both basic neuroscience1,46,65,66 and clinical neuromodulation,4,5,9,22,23,25,67,68 and significant effort has been made to understand how electrical stimulation interacts with neural tissue.36,37,47,69-71 Yet the field lacks a quantitative link between the biophysics of stimulation (i.e., how fields spread and which elements are directly recruited) and the resulting circuit dynamics and perception.

Previous experimental attempts to measure electrically-evoked neural activation have been limited by stimulation artifacts that obscure the earliest responses, by limited spatial context during electrophysiology, and/or by the temporal resolution of imaging techniques.72 Neuropixels probes help overcome limitations from electrical artifacts through band-specific amplifier gain settings.41 We found that integrated amplifiers with rapid recovery allow the observation of neural activity within 300 μs after pulses, even at high amplitudes, and within hundreds of microns from the stimulation source. The orthogonal geometry in our experiments and alignment to Allen CCF brain space42 allowed three-dimensional sampling, overcoming historical difficulties with spatial information with electrophysiology. The combination of millisecond-scale timing, high density sampling, and 3D spatial registration provides a framework to connect evoked potentials, direct single unit activation, and circuit-wide activity with unprecedented resolution.

We measured both the spatial extent of stimulation-evoked extracellular potentials and the distribution of single neurons affected by single-pulse electrical stimulation. Classic work has argued that the volume of activated tissue expands with stimulation amplitude, inferred from measuring the spiking activity of a single pyramidal neuron to varying amplitudes,37 and computational models in largely uniform media predict that the induced electric field grows with injected current39,71 (see Figure 2B). Congruent with these expectations and optical measurements using voltage dyes,73 we observed evoked potentials spanning 0.5–1 mm in radius whose magnitude increased with amplitude, but with a sublinear scaling that places empirical constraints on point-source approximations. In contrast to the expanding-volume interpretation of recruitment, two-photon calcium imaging in cortex suggested that directly driven spiking arises from a compact zone whose spatial footprint is relatively insensitive to amplitude, with higher currents primarily increasing the density of recruited neurons rather than the activated volume.38 Using Neuropixels probes, we confirm that single unit recruitment increases in density, not spatial extent, with increasing stimulation amplitude, extending prior work to sub-millisecond resolution and sampling in three dimensions. These results closely match the reconciliation proposed by anatomically detailed microstimulation models: an amplitude-dependent growth of the extracellular field can coexist with a distance-invariant somatic activation that fills in with increasing density.39 A key difference, however, is that these models predict that most recruited spikes are directly evoked, whereas our data indicate that directly activated units constitute a minority of the total spiking response, with the majority arising from circuit-mediated activity. Together, our measurements help reconcile previous studies and models to provide a unified bridge between field spread and neuronal recruitment. These measurements offer a mechanistic framework for human psychophysics in which higher currents increase percept intensity, but percept size quickly saturates.74

Computational models of electrical stimulation typically assume a uniform excitable medium even when incorporating cytoarchitecture.39,48,69,71,75 However, neural tissue is structurally and biophysically heterogeneous: conductivities differ across gray vs. white matter and across layers44,45; axons, especially at the axon initial segment, reach threshold more readily than somata36,43,76; myelination and ion-channel composition shift excitability both within cellular compartments69,77-79 and across cell classes80,81; and morphology/orientation govern field-membrane coupling.36,40,80,82 While thresholds for specific elements can be measured in isolation, extending those measurements to population-scale in vivo, where diverse elements and orientations intermix, is not tractable. Our data show that anatomical heterogeneity, both broadly from white-gray matter boundaries (Figure 3) and from cellular diversity (Figure 5), creates anisotropic neural responses to electrical stimulation. White matter boundaries limited evoked potential propagation. At a cellular level, FS (putative inhibitory interneurons) and RS (putative pyramidal) units exhibited distinct spatial patterns. These differences may arise from their distinct morphologies,83-85 but also may be influenced by morphoelectric properties derived from differences in ion-channels and conductances.80,81,86,87 While we did not see differences in the spatial extent across polarities, we observed polarity- and amplitude-selective units, suggesting that these complex interactions occur in heterogeneous brain tissue create amplitude- and polarity-selective responses in some neurons beyond simple dose- and distance-dependence that may be expected.88,89 This specificity has strong implications that the parameters of electrical neuromodulation could be algorithmically controlled to enhance the activity of certain neurons over others by adjusting polarity and amplitude, resulting not only in changing the number of neurons in stimulated ensembles (Figure 4D) and their level of activation (Figure 4B), but also the constituents of those ensembles (Figure 5B). Collectively, these findings highlight the role of anatomical and cellular heterogeneity in determining neural responses to electrical stimulation, which could be exploited in future efforts to precisely control information delivery as in sensory prosthetics.

Electrical stimulation has been successfully used to elicit percepts for decades, from somatosensory tingles13,90 to visual phosphenes,23,91 yet the resulting sensations remain coarse and unnatural.29 Most strategies to improve perceptual outcomes scale electrode count and/or coverage across representational space in early sensory cortices.92-95 However, in V1 and other cortical areas, multiple maps of features are overlaid, with areas and even single cells encoding multiple features and mixed information,96,97 and perception depends on distributed, time-evolving population activity.33,98 Our results show that despite eliciting a large, synchronous cortical response that spreads across V1 and HVAs, and persists for hundreds of milliseconds, single-pulse microstimulation was weakly detected across all amplitudes. This dissociation implies that the strength and number of neurons activated are not limiting factors for sensory perception. Instead, perception of artificial inputs may benefit from matching distributed, temporally structured patterns of activation,99-101 consistent with improvements from biomimetic sensory BCI paradigms.22,33

Visual stimuli recruit distributed, temporally structured patterns in primate98,102,103 and mouse V1,104 whereas single-pulse electrical stimulation elicits a highly synchronous wave of activity and reduces population dimensionality, despite fluctuations in firing rates (Figure 6). This neural difference is consistent with perceptual observations: reliable detection of electrical stimulation in V1 typically requires extensive training and trains of many electrical pulses.20,105-108 Such detectability may reflect learned associations rather than native neural interpretability of evoked activity,109,110 further demonstrated by learned detection of single neuron activation.111,112 In our study, we found that without explicit training, artificial stimulation of V1 is weakly behaviorally salient: mice rapidly generalized to novel visual stimuli but showed weak generalization to electrical stimulation. Relatedly, work in non-human primates has demonstrated that training to detect V1 microstimulation impaired visual contrast detection, and conversely, retraining on visual contrast impaired microstimulation detection.108 Because visual and electrical stimulation trials were intermixed during our testing phase, this interference may have limited perceptual learning of electrical stimulation in our paradigm. We observed that detectability did not increase with stimulation amplitude, contrasting with human studies where higher currents typically increase phosphene detectability and apparent size (until saturation).113 While trains were more detectable than single pulses in our paradigm, we did not systematically vary train amplitude, limiting direct comparison to the classic amplitude-detectability relationship. The low detectability we observed may be influenced by several factors: the task paradigm that interleaved electrical and visual stimuli, species differences between mouse and primate vision,114 and the use of primarily single pulses rather than trains. Notably, detection may depend on cortical state (e.g., arousal or widespread synchrony), whether present pre-stimulation or induced by stimulation itself.53,60,61 Further, in sensory cortical prostheses, humans often report habituation to percepts, where percepts become less detectable over repeated deliveries.21 We noted habituation to detection of electrical stimuli within a behavior session, despite no apparent changes in the evoked neural activity across stimulation pulses, suggesting that the stimuli lose perceptual salience. Moreover, when electrical stimulation is used to bias ongoing sensation (e.g., Middle Temporal area microstimulation shifting motion judgments1), effects are robust even with small currents, reinforcing perceptual salience depends more on naturalistic cortical dynamics than the absolute magnitude of driven activity.115

In this study, we chose to limit measures to the neural responses to single pulses of electrical stimulation, despite widespread clinical and experimental applications employing trains of pulses (typically 100–300 Hz, 30–100 pulses). Our design isolates the neural effects of individual stimulation events without the confounds of temporal summation of high-frequency stimulation.89 By characterizing the fundamental unit of electrical stimulation—single pulses—we provide a framework for predicting the neural effects of more complex stimulation paradigms from trains to biomimetic paradigms that use spatiotemporally patterned pulses.22

Our findings motivate several avenues for improvement of neural devices to encode neural information to cortical circuits. Directly comparing the statistics of naturalistic neural dynamics to artificial stimuli may provide quantitative benchmarks for prosthetic design that inform spatiotemporally patterned modulation. Exploring alternative stimulation waveforms (beyond biphasic pulses, such as sinusoidal or other continuous forms) may unlock regimes of sensory neuromodulation. Future work could leverage machine learning approaches to optimize stimulation parameters for neural or behavioral targets—for example, reproducing cortical response patterns of natural visual stimuli or enhancing detectability in behavioral tasks, a strategy that is beginning to be explored.116 Temporally varied stimulation from multiple contacts, distributed not only across horizontal cortical space but also across laminar depth, may be essential to recreating high-dimensional visual inputs with consideration to the temporal flow of spiking and anatomical connectivity.

Together, our results suggest that the amplitude of neural activation does not straightforwardly predict perception. Instead, we hypothesize that the spatiotemporal pattern of activity is a determinant of perceptual impact. For cortical prosthetics and other neuromodulatory technologies, this implies that more current or more synchronous firing is not necessarily better; rather, effective stimulation will require tailoring inputs to mimic the spatial and temporal structure of natural neural signals. Advances in technology (e.g., high-density electrode arrays, current steering, and adaptive closed-loop control) and leveraging anatomical heterogeneity for selective neural responses open the door to such biomimetic stimulation strategies. Ultimately, understanding the principles of how electrical stimulation engages neural circuits and leveraging these principles in neuromodulatory devices will be essential for building neuroprostheses capable of delivering reliable naturalistic percepts.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, neural activity occurring during the stimulation pulse itself (around 200 μs) is not captured, given the brief amplifier saturation. As a result, the earliest components of direct neuronal activation and evoked extracellular potentials may be underrepresented. Second, while all recording probes were registered in the CCF 10 μm mouse atlas, this alignment is subject to uncertainty in the order of tens of microns, reflecting limitations in histological reconstruction and registration methods. Finally, the orthogonal probe geometry enables three-dimensional sampling, but it does not sample cortical laminar dynamics within individual cortical columns.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

A total of thirteen C57BL6 mice (7 female, 6 male) between P83D and P361D were used for acute multi-Neuropixels recordings with electrical stimulation (n = 9 mice) and behavioral assays (n = 4 mice). Animals were group-housed. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus and were conducted in accordance to guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

METHOD DETAILS

Surgical methods

Surgical procedures included headbar affixation and habituation, craniotomies, and chronic implantation of linear 16-channel electrode arrays.

Mice underwent surgery to fix a metal headbar to the skull for head fixation. Mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and placed on a stereotactic frame (Model 940, Kopf Instruments). Isoflurane was maintained at 1.5–2% (Somnosuite, Vetflo) and body temperature was held at 37°C using a temperature controller (TC-1000, CWE). Anesthesia depth and mouse wellness were checked every 15 min, ensuring a stable breathing rate between 15 and 20 breaths per minute, temperature, and the absence of a toe pinch reflex. Carprofen (5 mg/kg) and lactated ringers were delivered for pain management and surgery recovery. The hair above the skull was removed, and the surgery site was sterilized with iodine and ethanol scrub. A portion of the skin and periosteum above the skull were removed. The skin surrounding the skull was secured with VetBond adhesive glue. The skull was cleaned and dried with phosphate-buffered saline. The skull was leveled by aligning lambda and bregma in the anteroposterior and mediolateral axes. The headbar was placed on the skull with the headbar opening approximately centered on the skull above the left V1. The headbar was fixed to the skull using translucent CI&B Metabond (Parkell) dental cement. Once the dental cement dried, mice were returned to a home cage with a heating pad and moist chow and monitored for 30 min or until full recovery.

Following headbar affixation, mice were allowed at least one week to fully recover. Mice were habituated to head fixation for acute electrophysiological recordings and behavioral experiments (see Behavior section for behavior-specific habituation). On the first day, mice were handled and allowed to freely explore the head-fixation apparatus. On subsequent days, mice were head-fixed beginning at 15 min and gradually increasing to 90–120 min. Habituation sessions were terminated if mice exhibited signs of distress, including vocalizations, physical agitation, or porphyrin secretion from the eyes. During later habituation sessions, recording conditions were simulated, including exposure to direct light from the stereoscope and high-frequency noise from the piezoelectric micromanipulators (New Scale Technologies). Following each habituation session, mice were given peanut butter as a reward.

Craniotomies for acute electrophysiological recordings or chronic multichannel stimulating electrode implantation (see “Visual and electrical stimulation behavioral detection assay” subsection) were performed on mice that had previously undergone headbar affixation. Mice were anesthetized and monitored as described in the headbar affixation procedure. Lambda and bregma were identified through the clear dental cement by applying a thin layer of saline over its surface. The skull was leveled, and stereotactic coordinates were determined. For acute electrophysiological recordings, either a small craniotomy (2 × 2 mm) encompassing the insertion stereotactic coordinates or individual burr holes at predetermined stereotactic coordinates for each probe were made. In each case, the dura was removed to facilitate probe insertion. For chronic multichannel stimulation electrode implantation (Neuronexus A1x16-5mm-50-703-A16, plated with IrOx to improve current delivery), a small burr hole with dura removal was made at (−3860 AP, −2580 ML). The multichannel stimulation electrode was held using a custom 3D-printed holder and inserted 900 μm into the brain. The burr hole and exposed shank were sealed with Kwik-Sil (World Precision Instruments), and the electrode base was secured to the headbar using clear C&B Metabond (Parkell) dental cement.

Electrophysiological recordings

Electrophysiological recordings used multi-Neuropixels with concurrent electrical stimulation exploring a vast parameter space. Neural recordings were performed using Neuropixels 1.0 probes,41 with the 383 electrode sites closest to the tip active. Signals from each recording site (channel) were split into a spike band (30 kHz sampling rate with a 500 Hz first-order high-pass filter) and an LFP band (2.5 kHz sampling rate with a 1000 Hz low-pass filter). The experimental rig was assembled using Thorlabs components and designed to insert three Neuropixels probes at 45° angles orthogonal to a stimulating electrode inserted at 90°. Neuropixels probes and stimulating electrodes were mounted on three-axis piezoelectric micromanipulators (New Scale Technologies). The manipulators were secured to a round breadboard attached to a two-dimensional microscope stage (Thorlabs) to allow fine X and Y motor control. Probe geometry and relative spacing were set prior to insertion, and probes were positioned at least 1000 μm above their target depth before lowering. The mouse platform, with adjustable height, was situated beneath the probe platform and raised to the appropriate height for insertion. X and Y positions were adjusted using the microscope stage to center probes above the insertion sites. Probes were lowered to the brain surface and then advanced to target depths (2000–3000 μm for Neuropixels probes and 1000 μm for the stimulating electrode) at a rate of 50–100 μm/min. Neuropixels data were acquired using the Open Ephys GUI software.118

Electrical stimulation was delivered using a combined waveform generator and isolated current output device (AM4100, A-M Systems). Stimulation during electrophysiological recordings was controlled using a modified version of example MATLAB code provided by A-M Systems. This code used serial commands to update parameters, trigger stimulation, and save stimulation parameters as CSV files. Stimulation for the behavioral detection task was controlled using custom Python code interfacing with the stimulator via PySerial commands. MATLAB and Python code for the stimulator interface are available at github.com/denmanlab/am4100_code.

Stimulation was delivered through contacts on a linear 16-channel Neuronexus electrode (Neuronexus, A1x16-5mm-50-703). To increase stimulation efficacy, each site was activated with iridium oxide. The contact area was 703 μm 2, with 50 μm spacing between contacts. As previously noted, the average contact impedance was 0.10 ± 0.06MΩ (Figure 1J, top), and the average contact charge capacity was 25.90 ± 9.69mC/cm2 (Figure 1J, bottom).

The stimulation parameter space was systematically explored. Each unique stimulation condition was delivered 75 times, with at least 2 s between single pulses or pulse trains. Each pulse consisted of a biphasic square wave, in which the sign of the amplitude indicates the phase of the first component of the waveform. Each recording tested a range of amplitudes, including −100, −50, −25, −5, 5, 25, 50, and 100μm A, across both monopolar and bipolar configurations. Negative amplitudes indicate cathodal-leading pulses, whereas positive amplitudes indicate anodal-leading pulses. Here, “bipolar” refers to cathodal-leading bipolar configurations, whereas “anodal” and “cathodal” refer to anodal-leading and cathodal-leading monopolar configurations, respectively.

For the analyses presented in the main text, we focused on single pulses with a pulse width of 100μm s per phase, delivered from contacts located in or near Layer 5 of V1. Additional stimulation parameters were tested in select recordings—including varying frequencies, stimulation depths, and pulse widths—but are not included in the present analyses and are available in the dataset.

Visual and electrical stimulation behavioral detection assay

We trained mice to report detection of visual stimuli across multiple stimulus classes and then tested whether mice that were proficient at reporting visual stimulus detection could also detect electrical stimulation of V1. Here we describe the task protocol, trial structure, task design and stimuli (visual and electrical), task software and hardware, and the approach for electrophysiology during behavioral sessions. Statistical analyses are described in the subsection “Quantification and statistical analyses.”

Task protocol:

Mice were surgically implanted with a headbar for head-fixation and allowed one week of recovery before behavioral training (see “Head affixation procedure” subsection). Following recovery, mice were weighed daily for three consecutive days to establish a stable baseline weight. Mice were then placed on a controlled water-restriction schedule, receiving approximately 1 mL of water daily to maintain body weight at approximately 85% of baseline, adjusted for expected growth curves. Mice underwent habituation to head-fixation and learned to associate a visual stimulus with water reward. Once mice were habituated to head-fixation and had associated visual stimuli with water delivery, they were initiated on the detection task.

Trial structure:

Mice learned and performed a detection task in which they reported stimulus detection by licking during a response window (0–1.5 s) following stimulus onset. A trial was initiated after a 1–5 s period with no detected licks. Following trial initiation, a variable delay of 1–10 s preceded stimulus presentation. If mice licked before stimulus presentation, the trial was terminated and labeled a “false alarm.” If the trial progressed to stimulus presentation (i.e., no false alarm occurred), the stimulus was presented and, for visual stimuli, remained on the monitor until a lick was detected or the response window (1.5 s) elapsed. If mice licked during the response window, the trial was labeled a “hit,” and a small water reward (5–20 μL) was delivered. If mice did not lick during the response window, the trial was labeled a “lapse,” and no reward was delivered (Figure 7B). “Catch” trials followed the same structure but no stimulus was presented at the onset of the response window. If mice licked during this window, the trial was labeled a “catch false alarm” and was not rewarded.

Task design and stimuli:

The behavioral task consisted of three phases (Figure 7C). In the initial phase, mice were trained to detect static gratings presented at varying contrasts (0, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 80, and 100%). During Phase 1 training, multiple strategies were used to facilitate task acquisition. Mice often developed timing strategies in which they licked repetitively, resulting in frequent false alarms. At the trainer’s discretion, triggered by excessive false alarms, these strategies were discouraged either by implementing a “false alarm timeout,” during which access to the lick spout was temporarily restricted by servo-motor positioning for 3–10 s and no trials were initiated, or by delivering a mild aversive stimulus coincident with false alarms, including an air puff to the whiskers or a mild electrical shock.

During Phase 1, high-contrast trials were overrepresented to promote learning; however, dedicated “test” days included all contrast levels to evaluate detection thresholds. In Phase 2, a new visual stimulus, termed a “moving circle,” was introduced, and moving circle trials were interleaved with static grating trials. Moving circle stimuli consisted of circles presented at varying contrasts (0, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 80, and 100%) and sizes that appeared at random positions on the monitor and moved slowly in random directions. The goal was to approximate spatially confined, perceptually subtle visual percepts (often referred to as “phosphenes”) reported in human V1 electrical stimulation studies.24 Mice that demonstrated successful learning during Phase 1 and generalization to the moving circle stimulus in Phase 2 were implanted with stimulating electrodes and advanced to Phase 3 (see behavioral analysis in the subsection “Quantification and Statistical Analyses” for details regarding performance quantification across phases).

In Phase 3, static grating trials with varying contrasts, moving circle trials with varying contrasts, and electrical stimulation trials with varying amplitudes were presented. Electrical stimulation generally consisted of single pulses at varying amplitudes; however, some sessions specifically tested high-frequency pulse trains (100–300 Hz, 10–50 pulses), which were analyzed independently.

Software and hardware implementation:

The behavioral detection task was implemented using custom Python scripts based on the Pyglet library to control stimulus timing, visual presentation, and behavioral event handling with precise temporal accuracy. Task hardware was interfaced via an Arduino microcontroller (Arduino MEGA), which managed peripheral devices including an infrared beam sensor (Z4352-ND, DigiKey) for lick detection, a servo motor (2000 Series 5-Turn, Dual Mode Servo 2000-0025-0502, ServoCity) for spout positioning, and a solenoid valve (LHDA1233215H, Lee Co.) for water delivery. Visual stimuli were displayed on two adjacent vertical monitors (ASUS VG277Q1A) positioned 30 cm in front of the mice. Detailed documentation of the software and hardware, Python task scripts, and parts lists for hardware assembly are available in our public repository (https://github.com/denmanlab/mouse_behavior).

Electrophysiology recordings during detection assay:

During select behavioral sessions, we recorded from contacts not used for stimulation on the chronically implanted 16-contact linear stimulating electrode (Neuronexus, A1x16-5mm-50-703-CM16). In these sessions, two electrode contacts were designated for electrical stimulation, while the remaining contacts were routed for electrophysiological recording. The recorded contacts were connected to an RHD 16-channel recording headstage (Intan C3334) via an 18-pin electrode adapter board (Intan C3418) and 18-pin wire adapter (Intan B7600). Neural signals were acquired at 30 kHz using an Open Ephys acquisition system.118 Post hoc analysis separated the data into an LFP band (first-order low-pass filtered at 300 Hz) and an AP band (first-order high-pass filtered at 300 Hz). Relevant task timestamps, including stimulation onset, reward delivery times, and lick times, were recorded on analog and digital lines during acquisition.

Raw binary data can be provided upon request. Statistical details can be found in the Figure legends, Results, and Table S1 included in the Supplemental.

Alignment to CCF coordinates

We used a semi-rigid probe geometry in which probe positions remained fixed relative to one another. Each recording Neuropixels probe was inserted at a 45° angle relative to the brain surface, and the stimulating electrode was inserted perpendicular to the surface. Recording probes were positioned 100–200 μm horizontally from the stimulating electrode and 100–200 μm vertically from the nearest probe at the crossing point. Probe geometry and relative positioning were reconstructed using the Neuropixels trajectory planning software Pinpoint119 to establish planned trajectories and stereotactic coordinates for surgery.

We used the Allen Common Coordinate Framework (CCFv3, 10 μm mouse brain atlas) as a standardized three-dimensional anatomical reference volume and registered each experiment’s probe trajectories to this reference space.42 Specifically, we first obtained a priori estimates of probe entry and tip coordinates by mapping planned stereotactic coordinates and the semi-rigid probe geometry into CCF space using Pinpoint. These planned coordinates provided an initial approximation of probe trajectories and expected recording locations in CCF coordinates (Figure S11A).

We then refined these estimates post hoc using histology. Before insertion, probe tips were coated with the fluorescent tracer CM-DiI to label the probe track. After the experiments, brains were fixed, sectioned, and imaged, and probe tracks were identified in the fluorescence channel (Figure S11B). Track locations were aligned to the Allen reference using HERBS (an open-source Neuropixels atlas registration tool; https://github.com/Whitlock-Group/HERBS) or BrainGlobe Atlas API tools (https://brainglobe.info/documentation/brainglobe-atlasapi/index.html), yielding a best-fit probe trajectory in CCF space for each probe (Figure S11C). Final probe depths and orientations were additionally cross-validated using electrophysiological landmarks, including depth-dependent changes in spike rate and LFP power (Figure S11D;120).

The CCF alignment provides an atlas-referenced estimate of recording and stimulation locations (and inter-probe geometry). The combination of rigid geometry constraints, fluorescent track reconstruction, and electrophysiological confirmation supports accurate identification of the targeted cortical region and relative distances between probes, enabling measurement of distance- and region-dependent effects within and across recordings.

3D plotting in anatomical brain space

Three-dimensional plots in CCF mouse anatomical brain space were generated using custom Python code leveraging Brainrender121 (https://github.com/brainglobe/brainrender) and Vedo (https://github.com/marcomusy/vedo). For 3D renders showing individual single units (Figures 4D and 6E), unit locations were assigned to the CCF coordinates of the channel with the maximal waveform amplitude, with small random mediolateral jitter added for visualization. Sphere size represents the amplitude of the single-unit waveform.

Evoked potential analysis

Defining the evoked potential

Evoked potential analyses were performed on raw extracellular voltages from the AP band. Across all channels and stimulation conditions, we observed an initial saturating phase—likely due to brief amplifier saturation—that increased with stimulation amplitude (Figure 1L, statistics) and closely matched the duration and polarity of the biphasic pulse (100 μs per phase, 200 μs total). This correspondence suggests that, despite brief saturation, the amplifiers recovered nearly instantaneously (Figure 2B, example waveform). This saturated artifact was consistent across conditions and varied minimally with distance from the stimulation source, except in rare cases, indicating that it does not reflect physiological neural activity.

Following this saturation period, we observed a non-saturating waveform component that rapidly returned to baseline due to hardware-imposed high-pass filtering in the AP band (Figure 2B). This non-saturating phase scaled with stimulation amplitude and distance from the stimulation source, supporting a physiological origin rather than an artifact component. This non-saturating phase (Figure 2D, shaded region) was defined as the evoked potential.

Measuring evoked potential in AP and LFP bands

The evoked potential was recorded in both the AP band (acquired at 30,000 samples per second) and the LFP band (acquired at 2,500 samples per second). We quantified the spatial extent using the AP band because its higher sampling rate allowed more precise estimation of time points immediately surrounding stimulation onset. However, the hardware high-pass filtering (300 Hz) applied in the AP band attenuates slow, sustained voltage deflections and therefore limits characterization of the full temporal profile of the evoked potential. Accordingly, our analysis of the evoked potential was restricted to its spatial extent within the first several milliseconds following stimulation. This constraint also serves a scientific purpose: measuring the evoked potential within 3 ms of stimulation onset minimizes contributions from synaptic propagation and other circuit-mediated effects that emerge at longer latencies.

Algorithmically determining the spatial extent of the evoked potential

The spatial extent of evoked potentials was algorithmically determined from raw AP-band data. Each trial for a given electrical stimulation condition was aligned to stimulation onset using threshold detection of the saturating portion of the electrical artifact, compensating for microsecond-level imprecision in synchronized stimulation timestamps. After alignment, a response window from 0.3 to 3 ms was selected for each trial to exclude the saturating artifact and organized into a three-dimensional array (trials × samples × channels), including the first 20 channels located outside the brain in saline.

For each sample, a common signal was defined as the median voltage across the saline channels and subsequently subtracted from all channels (Figures S12A and S12B). This common signal decreased and approached baseline within the first 3 ms following stimulation (Figure S12C). The common signal showed a weak trend toward decreasing with distance from the stimulation source, but this effect was not statistically significant (Figure S12D). This signal likely reflects residual volume conduction in saline or electronic artifact; subtracting it improved spatial specificity.

The mean across the trial dimension was then computed. For spatial characterization and alignment, the sample at 1 ms post-stimulation was extracted (yielding a one-dimensional array of the trial-averaged voltage at each channel). For each probe across all recordings, the 1 ms data were aligned to the channel exhibiting the maximum voltage, padding edges as needed to maintain a consistent array shape. This alignment was then applied to the mean 0.3–3 ms window data to generate a peak-aligned average evoked potential (samples × channels) for each probe. Finally, evoked responses were averaged across probes to obtain the mean evoked potential across all probes.

Aligning and averaging the evoked potential

To enable spatial averaging across probes, we normalized probe distances by assigning 0 μm to the channel exhibiting the peak evoked response. Channels superficial (toward the pia) relative to this peak were assigned negative distances, whereas deeper channels were assigned positive distances, enabling consistent spatial comparison across probes. These normalized distances facilitated alignment and averaging of the evoked potential across probes.

For anatomical precision, we subsequently transitioned to absolute Euclidean distances derived from CCF coordinates. The trends relating evoked potential magnitude to distance from the stimulation source were similar when using peak-aligned relative distance (Figure S2E) and Euclidean CCF distance (Figure S2F). The mean difference between the channel exhibiting the maximum evoked potential and the channel with the minimum Euclidean distance to the stimulation site (based on CCF coordinates) was 8.14 ± 25.37 channels, corresponding to approximately 80 μm (Figures S2D, i). The channel offset between peak response and minimum CCF distance did not vary significantly with stimulation amplitude (one-way ANOVA: F = 0.883, p = 0.519, Figure S2D, ii) but did vary with recording (one-way ANOVA: F = 23.16, p < 0.001, Figure S2D, iii). Under this normalized distance scheme, every 10 channels toward the surface corresponded to −100μm m, and every 10 channels deeper than the zero point corresponded to +100μm m. Spatial extent and magnitude of the evoked potential were quantified using voltages at 1 ms post-stimulation. For each probe and direction, spatial extent was defined as the distance at which the evoked potential returned to 20 μV. Area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated from the boundaries of the evoked potential at 1 ms (Figure S2H).

Statistical approaches for measuring spatial extent

To account for the hierarchical structure of the spatial extent data, we used a linear mixed-effects model to examine the relationship between stimulation parameters and spatial extent. Spatial extent measurements were nested within recording sessions (9 recordings total), with ventral and dorsal measurements obtained for each probe within a recording (n = 25 probes). Recording was therefore included as a random intercept to account for session-specific offsets and non-independence of observations within recordings. Fixed effects included stimulation polarity, stimulation amplitude, boundary type (dorsal or ventral measurements along a probe), probe identity, and their interactions. This specification allowed us to test (i) the main effects of amplitude, polarity, and boundary type on spatial extent and (ii) whether the relationship between amplitude and spatial extent differed across polarity or boundary type. Models were fit using maximum likelihood estimation. Statistical significance of fixed effects was evaluated using Wald z-tests, and 95% confidence intervals were derived from model estimates. Including recording as a random effect allowed partitioning of variance attributable to session-level variability, thereby improving inference on fixed effects.

Anisotropic volumetric analyses

Volumetric calculations

To calculate volumes, we leveraged the orthogonal probe geometry that sampled the space surrounding the stimulation source. The six total one-dimensional spatial measurements that could be generated from the three Neuropixels (one measurement of the spatial decay superficial to the stimulation source and one deep from the stimulation source on each probe) were used to calculate the volumetric space. We successfully calculated all six spatial extents possible for four of the nine recordings, for all polarities and amplitudes except 5 μA. The CCF coordinates of the six one-dimensional spatial extents were used to fit a convex hull, and we calculated the volume of the convex hull, a conservative estimate of volume. The stimulation site served as the fixed anatomical reference (the “electrode center”). All distances are reported in μm.

Single unit stability metric

Single unit stability (i.e., the ability to record a single unit across the duration of the recording) was essential for the interpretation and accuracy of our results, specifically when comparing responses from different stimulus conditions. To ensure that only stable single units were included in downstream analyses, we quantified firing rate consistency across the duration of each recording session and filtered out unstable units. For each unit, spike times were binned into 60-s non-overlapping intervals across the entire session. Firing rates were calculated as spike count per bin divided by bin duration (Hz). To quantify stability, we fit a linear regression model to each unit’s binned firing rate as a function of time using ordinary least squares (scikit-learn LinearRegression). The resulting slope, intercept, mean squared error (MSE), and coefficient of determination (R2) were recorded. Additionally, we computed the ratio of the y-intercept to the unit’s average firing rate (y-intercept/FR) to assess drift: values near 1 indicate stationarity, while values below or above 1 suggest increasing or decreasing firing rate trends, respectively. Units were excluded if their firing rate trajectory was identified as an outlier using the interquartile range (IQR) method. Specifically, units with either y-intercept/FR falling beyond 1.5 times the IQR from the first or third quartile were labeled as unstable and removed from further analysis. Outlier detection was performed separately for each recording session.

Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) applied to first spike latencies to estimate direct response window

First-spike latencies (0–15 ms post-stimulation) were modeled separately for each polarity–amplitude condition using one-dimensional Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) (Figure S3). For each condition, latency distributions were fit with 4 Gaussian components using maximum likelihood estimation, and the optimal number of components was selected by minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Components were sorted by mean latency for interpretability. To define latency boundaries between putative response classes, we computed posterior probabilities across a dense latency grid and identified crossover points where adjacent component responsibilities were equal. These intersection points were taken as data-driven direct response boundaries. Only conditions with at least 200 latencies were fit. Model parameters (means, standard deviations, weights) and boundary estimates were extracted for downstream comparisons across polarity and amplitude.

Hierarchical clustering of direct responders

We performed hierarchical agglomerative clustering using Ward’s method on normalized feature vectors. The dendrogram was cut into k clusters using the “maxclust” criterion. The clustering quality was evaluated using three complementary metrics: (i) the cophenetic correlation coefficient (CCC), quantifying how well the dendrogram preserved pairwise distances; (ii) the silhouette score, assessing within-cluster cohesion and between-cluster separation; and (iii) the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) across different cluster numbers, visualized in an elbow plot.

Waveform (FS/RS) classification

Average waveforms were computed for each single unit. Fast-spiking (FS) waveforms, characterized by short, rapidly decaying action potentials, are considered putative parvalbumin-positive (PV+) inhibitory interneurons. Regular spiking (RS) waveforms are characterized by larger, slower waveforms (51). Classification was based on spike duration, peak-to-trough ratio, and repolarization slope. Units were initially assigned FS or RS categories by k-means clustering on normalized waveform features. Spikes with positive polarity (peak-to-trough ratio ≥1.0) were reclassified as “up” units, and narrow upward spikes (<0.4 ms) were further labeled as putative axonal signals (“axon”), which were not included in analyses unless otherwise stated.

Quantifying task performance of electrical and visual stimuli in learned mice

In Phase 3, we evaluated mice that successfully learned to report detection of both visual stimulus classes. In this phase, we included trials with all contrasts of both static gratings and moving circles, and electrical stimulation pulses with varying amplitudes (see “Task design and stimuli”). Notably, sufficient catch trials were introduced, allowing for an accurate assessment of false positive rates during this phase. Here, false positives (F) were defined explicitly as the hit rate on 0% contrast (catch) trials, while true positives (H) were defined as the proportion of rewarded trials at each given contrast or amplitude level.

Sessions from Phases 1 and 2 were analyzed to demonstrate task learning and generalization, while all training sessions conducted after successful behavioral shaping (Phase 3) were included in the analysis for determining detection thresholds across stimulus classes. Sessions or intervals characterized by poor task engagement, defined quantitatively by excessive licking without stimulus presentation (false-alarm rate) or failure to respond to high-contrast stimuli (lapse rate), were excluded. Specifically, periods where either the false-alarm rate or lapse rate exceeded 0.7, calculated using a rolling mean over 10 consecutive trials, were omitted from further analyses.
