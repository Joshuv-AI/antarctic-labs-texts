Prognostic Utility of Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance–Based Phenotyping in Patients With Muscular Dystrophy
Authors: Niharika Kashyap, Anish Nikhanj, Dina Labib, Easter Prosia, Sandra Rivest, Jacqueline Flewitt, Gerald Pfeffer, Jeffrey A Bakal, Zaeem A Siddiqi, Richard A Coulden, Richard Thompson, James A White, Gavin Y Oudit
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic utility of cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging, including strain analysis and tissue characterization, has not been comprehensively investigated in adult patients with muscular dystrophy.Methods and ResultsWe prospectively enrolled 148 patients with dystrophinopathies (including heterozygotes), limb‐girdle muscular dystrophy, and type 1 myotonic dystrophy (median age, 36.0 [interquartile range, 23.0–50.0] years; 51 [34.5%] women) over 7.7 years in addition to an age‐ and sex–matched healthy control cohort (n=50). Cardiovascular magnetic resonance markers, including 3‐dimensional strain and fibrosis, were assessed for their respective association with major adverse cardiac events. Our results showed that markers of contractile performance were reduced across all muscular dystrophy groups. In particular, the dystrophinopathies cohort experienced reduced left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction and high burden of replacement fibrosis. Patients with type 1 myotonic dystrophy showed a 26.8% relative reduction in LV mass with corresponding reduction in chamber volumes. Eighty‐two major adverse cardiac events occurred over a median follow‐up of 5.2 years. Although LV ejection fraction was significantly associated with major adverse cardiac events (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 3.0 [95% CI, 1.4–6.4]) after adjusting for covariates, peak 3‐dimensional strain amplitude demonstrated greater predictive value (minimum principal amplitude: aHR, 5.5 [95% CI, 2.5–11.9]; maximum principal amplitude: aHR, 3.3 [95% CI, 1.6–6.8]; circumferential amplitude: aHR, 3.4 [95% CI, 1.6–7.2]; longitudinal amplitude: aHR, 3.4 [95% CI, 1.7–6.9]; and radial strain amplitude: aHR, 3.0 [95% CI, 1.4–6.1]). Minimum principal strain yielded incremental prognostic value beyond LV ejection fraction for association with major adverse cardiac events (change in χ 2=13.8; P<0.001).ConclusionsCardiac dysfunction is observed across all muscular dystrophy subtypes; however, the subtypes demonstrate distinct phenotypic profiles. Myocardial deformation analysis highlights unique markers of principal strain that improve risk assessment over other strain markers, LV ejection fraction, and late gadolinium enhancement in this vulnerable patient population.Keywords: cardiovascular magnetic resonance, major adverse cardiac events, muscular dystrophy, prognosisSubject Categories: Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Imaging, Prognosis

Heart disease is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with muscular dystrophy (MD). 1 , 2 The major types of MD, including Duchenne MD (DMD), Becker MD (BMD), limb‐girdle MD (LGMD), and type 1 myotonic dystrophy (DM1), are driven by distinct genetic mutations and pathophysiology. 1 , 2 From a cardiac perspective, patients with dystrophinopathies (DMD and BMD) and LGMD are conventionally characterized by ventricular dilation, reduced systolic function, and ventricular arrhythmias. 2 , 3 , 4 In contrast, conduction disease and arrhythmias are more prevalent in patients with DM1, and left bundle‐branch block correlates with reduced left ventricular (LV) systolic performance. 5 , 6

The accuracy, reproducibility, and versatility of cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging make it a valuable phenotyping tool for patients with MD. 7 , 8 Beyond reference standard volumetric quantification of cardiac chamber structure and contractile function, CMR imaging provides unique insights into myocardial health across the spectrum of diffuse through regional replacement fibrosis. The versatile delivery of these markers without limitation from poor imaging windows, a common challenge for ultrasound‐based assessments in patients with MD attributable to scoliosis, obesity, and lung disease, 9 , 10 , 11 offers strong potential for CMR imaging to deliver personalized phenotype‐driven risk modeling in this population. However, the distribution and predictive value of contemporary CMR‐derived markers in patients with MD has not been well studied to date.

In this observational, multicenter study, we explored the utility of contemporary, multiparametric CMR phenotyping, inclusive of 3‐dimensional myocardial deformation analysis, late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), and tissue mapping, to differentiate unique phenotypes of MD across a large relevant referral population. CMR markers were compared with a concurrently recruited age‐ and sex‐matched health volunteer cohort and assessed for their associations with future adverse major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) over a median 5.2 years of clinical follow‐up.

METHODS

Study Cohort

This was a multicenter, prospective, observational cohort study that recruited 148 patients with MD from the Neuromuscular Multidisciplinary clinic at the Kaye Edmonton Clinic, University of Alberta (Edmonton, Alberta, Canada) and the Cardiovascular Imaging Registry of Calgary (CIROC; NCT04367220), Libin Cardiovascular Institute, University of Calgary (Calgary, Alberta, Canada) over 7.7 years from 2014 to 2022. Patients were followed up over a median of 5.2 years from the time of CMR imaging. Neurologic assessment, muscle biopsy, and genetic testing confirmed the diagnosis of all 148 patients with MD, including dystrophinopathies (64 [43.2%] of patients; 24 patient with DMD, 27 patients with BMD, 9 patients with DMD heterozygote, and 4 patients with BMD heterozygote), LGMD (38 [25.7%] of patients), and DM1 (46 [31.1%] of patients). For this investigation, patients diagnosed with DMD and BMD, including heterozygotes, were grouped as dystrophinopathies given their similarity in pathogenesis, management practices, and vulnerability to experience MACEs, which was confirmed by clinical profile and outcome data stratified on the basis of the types of dystrophinopathies. 12

Patients were referred to our centers at various stages of their disease and recruited to this study with no bias toward having overt cardiac symptoms. All patients received collaborative multidisciplinary care from specialist physicians who implemented guideline‐based medical therapy, including device implantation and follow‐up care. In addition, only patients with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging performed at our centers were recruited. Clinical data, such as clinical assessment and history, 12‐lead ECG parameters, medical therapy, including pharmacologic therapies and device implantation, and outcomes, for this investigation were obtained by electronic medical record review. The study was approved by the Health Research Ethics Board at the University of Alberta and University of Calgary, and all patients provided informed and written consent at the time of study enrollment in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. On reasonable request, all data about this study are available from the corresponding authors. All the research procedures followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology cohort reporting guidelines. 13

Healthy Reference Cohort

A healthy reference cohort (n=50) was prospectively recruited through the University of Calgary and was sex‐ (P=0.95) and age‐ (P=0.44) matched based on agreement to median and interquartile range to the MD cohort. All healthy controls were recruited from the local community with no history of cancer, cardiovascular disease, moderate or severe obesity (body mass index, ≥35.0 kg/m2), hypertension, diabetes, kidney disease, or collagen vascular disease. CMR imaging was performed with identical protocol and compared with the MD cohort. Healthy controls were not assessed in follow‐up or included in risk analysis.

CMR Imaging

CMR imaging was performed at the Mazankowski Alberta Heart Institute (1.5‐T Aera or Avanto scanner; Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) and the Stephenson Cardiac Imaging Centre of the Libin Cardiovascular Institute (3.0‐T Prisma or Skyra; Siemens Healthcare, except for 2 patients scanned at 1.5‐T Avanto). All patients underwent standardized imaging protocols, including routine cine imaging in sequential short‐axis and 3 long‐axis views and slice‐matched LGE imaging. The latter was performed 7 to 10 minutes following administration of intravenous gadolinium contrast (Gadovist; 0.1–0.15 mmol/kg; Bayer Canada Inc). 14 , 15 A subgroup of patients (n=46 at 1.5 T, and n=43 at 3.0 T) underwent precontrast and postcontrast T1 mapping using a MOLLI‐ (n=81) or ShMOLLI‐ (n=8) based sequence to determine extracellular volume (ECV). Healthy volunteers underwent matched imaging protocols at 3 T, with 29 participants agreeing to contrast administration for the estimation of ECV.

All images were anonymized and digitally transferred to a core laboratory for blinded analysis. Cardiac chamber volumes, systolic and diastolic function, LGE, and ECV were assessed using commercial software (cvi42 v5.12; Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc, Calgary, Alberta, Canada). 16 Short‐axis cine images were used for biventricular volumetric assessments with LV mass inclusive of the papillary muscles. Volume and mass values were indexed by both height and body surface area to assess influence of body composition in this population. Short‐axis LGE images were analyzed using a ≥5‐SD threshold versus normal reference myocardium to estimate total LGE burden. T1 mapping was added to the imaging protocol following initial study launch. A total of 89 patients successfully completed precontast and postcontrast T1 mapping to support ECV map reconstruction and analysis. Remote tissue ECV was calculated to assess for diffuse myocardial fibrosis. This was accomplished by segmental ECV calculation at a midventricular slice using a dedicated T1 mapping module, followed by excluding segments with >10% by volume abnormal LGE. The mean ECV value of remaining segments was then reported. Deformation analysis was performed using validated 3‐dimensional (3D) strain analysis software, as previously described. 17 , 18 This provided measures of 3D global circumferential strain (3D‐GCS), 3D global longitudinal strain (3D‐GLS), and 3D global radial strain (3D‐GRS) in addition to 3D minimum principal strain (3D‐minPS) and 3D maximum principal strain (3D‐maxPS). The latter markers of 3D‐maxPS and 3D‐minPS uniquely describe maximal tissue thickening and shortening occurring in the tissue's local axis of maximal deformation, reflecting the net local direction of myofibril forces. 18 , 19 , 20 Principal strain analysis aims to eliminate the inherent off‐axis error introduced by conventional strain estimates performed using predefined axes (ie, longitudinal, radial, and circumferential) that present varying local deviations from helically orientated myofibrils (Figure S1). As such, these measures aim to deliver greater accuracy for the description of tissue deformations. 21 All strain markers were expressed globally and segmentally according to the 17‐segment American Heart Association model, excluding the apical cap.

Primary Study End Points

Major adverse cardiac events were defined as a composite of incident heart failure, atrial or ventricular arrhythmia, need for device implantation, cardiac‐related hospitalization, and cardiac mortality. Incident heart failure was diagnosed on the basis of comprehensive cardiac assessments, which considered symptoms and signs, such as dyspnea, orthopnea, peripheral edema, or abdominal distention. Atrial arrhythmias (ie, atrial fibrillation/flutter) and ventricular tachyarrhythmias (VTs) (ie, nonsustained VT or sustained VT) required visual confirmation by 12‐lead ECG, Holter monitoring, or device interrogation. Nonsustained VT was defined as ≥3 consecutive beats with a duration of <30 seconds, and sustained VT was defined as ventricular tachycardia with a duration of ≥30 seconds. Holter monitoring was recommended every 1 to 2 years, and 12‐lead ECG and device interrogation were recommended on an annual basis; however, this is not mandated as part of our study protocol. Implanted devices included implantable cardiac defibrillators, pacemakers, and cardiac resynchronization therapy. Cardiac‐related hospitalization includes chest pain/angina, myocardial infarction, hypotension, stroke, and syncope. Cardiac hospitalization, arrhythmias, heart failure symptoms, and cardiac mortality were identified through manual review of provincial electronic health records and death certificates.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians (interquartile ranges) and analyzed using Kruskal‐Wallis test, and categorical variables were summarized as number (percentage) and compared using Pearson χ2 tests. Post hoc analysis was conducted using a Dunn test for pairwise comparisons of. We assessed the association of 3D‐minPS with LGE and ECV using bivariate correlation analysis and Spearman correlation coefficient. For risk analysis, patients with MD were stratified on the basis of optimal cutoff values from each CMR‐based marker, which were calculated from receiver‐operating characteristic curves and corresponding Youden indexes. 22 LV ejection fraction (LVEF) <55.0% was used to define a diagnosis of cardiomyopathy based on the lower limit of normal from our previously published healthy reference population. 18 , 22 A composite end point for all MACEs was evaluated in a time‐to‐first‐event analysis with right censorship. Univariate Cox regression analysis was used to identify CMR markers with predictive value for MACEs; these were included in multivariate Cox regression analysis to derive independent models and respective adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs). Each model was adjusted for age in 5‐year increments, assigned‐at‐birth sex, MD genotype group, presence of respiratory disease, and baseline use of cardiac medications, which were chosen on the basis of clinical or statistical significance. Respiratory disease was defined as respiratory muscle weakness, restrictive lung disease, obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, or recurrent aspiration pneumonia. Model performance for predicting MACEs was assessed using Akaike information criterion and concordance index. In addition, the incremental value of 3D strain to LVEF to predict MACEs was evaluated with the likelihood ratio test. Collinearity test determined LVEF is not associated with strain markers. All statistical analyses were performed in R, version 4.0.3, and SPSS statistics software, 28.0.1.0. P<0.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Clinical Characteristics

Baseline characteristics of 148 patients with MD revealed a median age of 36 (interquartile range, 23.0–50.0) years, with 51 (34.5%) being women (Table 1). Within the dystrophinopathies cohort, DMD and BMD hemizygote patients were exclusively younger men, whereas heterozygotes were exclusively women, distinguishing this cohort from other types of MD (Table S1). Conduction delays, inclusive of first‐degree atrioventricular block and left bundle‐branch block, were predominantly seen in patients with DM1 (Table S2). Comorbidities, including hypertension, respiratory disease, and sleep‐disordered breathing, were prevalent, with a comparable high use of respiratory therapies across the various cohorts of MD; in addition, patients with DM1 exhibited the lowest need for wheelchair assistance (Table 1). The use of cardiac medications at time of CMR imaging was higher among patients with dystrophinopathies, whereas cardiac devices were more prevalent in patients with DM1 (Table 1).

Cardiac Assessment and Differential Remodeling

Although all MD groups had lower LVEF relative to healthy controls, systolic function was significantly reduced in dystrophinopathies compared with LGMD and DM1 (P=0.001 and P<0.001, respectively; Figure 1A and Table 2). Dystrophinopathies exhibited mildly increased chamber volumes versus LGMD and DM1, whereas the overall MD cohort showed indexed LV end‐systolic volume to be different from healthy controls (Figure 1B and 1C and Table 2). In addition, recognizing heterogeneity in the dystrophinopathy cohort, we observed that the heterozygote population had reduced LV end‐systolic volume indexed to body surface area; however, there was mild chamber dilation observed in the dystrophinopathies as a whole (Figure 1, Table 2, and Table S1). Dystrophinopathy heterozygotes showed lower LV mass indexed to body surface area compared with hemizygotes, but this may be indicative of age‐ and sex–based differences (Table S1). Patients with DM1 showed smaller chamber volumes and reduced LV mass (Figure 1D and Table 2), which was maintained when stringent age‐ and sex–matched healthy controls were used (Figure S2). Left bundle‐branch block was associated with reduced LVEF, elevated LV end‐systolic volume, and reduced 3D‐minPS in patients with DM1 (Table S3).

The traditional, geometry‐dependent directions of deformation demonstrated reduced peak strain amplitude and strain rate all across all MD cohorts compared with healthy controls (Figure 2, Table 2, and Table S4). Radial strain amplitude was the only strain measure that was reduced in dystrophinopathies compared with patients with LGMD and DM1. To gain further insight into the remodeling process, we used a novel 3D representative model to capture the geometry‐independent direction of deformation, which reflects local shortening and thickening of tissue in our cohort (Figure 3 and Figure S1). In our comparison of healthy control patients and MD cohort, peak 3D principal strain amplitude was reduced across all MD cohorts (Figures 2 and 3 and Table 2). Dystrophinopathies subgroup analysis revealed maximum principal strain amplitude was significantly reduced in the dystrophinopathies hemizygote group compared with the heterozygotes (Table S1).

Prognostic Utility of CMR Markers

Over a median follow‐up of 5.2 (interquartile range, 1.7–6.3) years, 82 incident MACEs occurred in 47 (31.8%) of patients. This included 17 cases of atrial flutter or fibrillation, 10 cases of nonsustained or sustained VT, 13 device implantations, 23 cardiac‐related hospitalizations, 15 incident heart failure diagnoses, and 4 cardiac deaths across the various MD types (Figure 5). Patients with myotonic dystrophy experienced a higher, but not statistically significant, occurrence of arrhythmias and device implantations compared with the other MD subtypes (Figure 5). There was no significant difference in the distribution of primary end points or in event‐free survival over time for specific subcohorts of the dystrophinopathies (Table S1 and Figure S4). Patients who experienced MACEs were marginally older, with a higher proportion receiving baseline cardiac medications (Table S6).

Multivariable risk modeling was performed to assess the predictive utility of all CMR parameters following their assessment by univariate Cox regression (Table S7). Candidate CMR markers were assessed according to optimal binary thresholds, established by receiver‐operating characteristic curve analysis. Following adjustment for age, sex, MD type, respiratory disease, and baseline cardiac medication use, the following CMR markers remained significantly associated with MACEs: LVEF <55% (aHR, 3.0 [95% CI, 1.4–6.4]; P=0.006), 3D‐GCS <59.31% (aHR, 3.4 [95% CI, 1.6–7.2]; P=0.001), 3D‐maxPS <59.31% (aHR, 3.3 [95% CI, 1.6–6.8]; P=0.002), 3D‐GLS <−14.00% (aHR, 3.4 [95% CI, 1.7–6.9]; P<0.001), and 3D‐GRS <42.58% (aHR, 3.0 [95% CI, 1.4–6.1]; P=0.003) (Table 3 and Figure 6). Greatest performance was provided by 3D‐minPS amplitude <−24.44% (aHR, 5.5 [95% CI, 2.5–11.9]; P<0.001) with the lowest Akaike information criterion and highest concordance index (Table 3). Chamber volumes, LGE presence, LGE burden, and ECV were not independently associated with MACEs in the overall cohort (Table S7). Although subgroup analysis identified LGE presence, including all patterns of fibrosis, was not associated with MACEs in patients with hemizygous DMD and BMD (P=0.47), LGE presence, excluding subepicardial pattern, was associated with risk of MACEs (P=0.03; Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we examined the distribution and predictive utility of CMR‐derived markers for the prediction of MACEs in patients with MD (Figure 7). Supported by the largest adult study cohort examined to date by CMR imaging, we identified clinically relevant phenotypic differences between MD subtypes, showing a prominent replacement fibrosis with LV dysfunction phenotype in patients with dystrophinopathy, a milder but similar phenotype in LGMD, and reduced cardiac mass with minimal fibrosis phenotype in DM1. Of all investigated CMR imaging markers, 3D myocardial strain offered the strongest capacity to predict cardiovascular outcomes in our cohort of patients with MD. In particular, the geometry‐independent strain marker of 3D‐minPS amplitude identified patients at a 5.5‐fold elevated risk of MACEs, independent of baseline characteristics, showing superior predictive performance over LVEF. Similarly, the composite model of 3D‐minPS and LVEF had significant additive value over LVEF alone for risk stratification.

Reduced 3‐dimensional (3D) strain amplitudes and ventricular dysfunction lead to increased risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) in patients with muscular dystrophies.

We observed both geometry‐dependent and geometry‐independent 3D markers of myocardial deformation to be uniformly reduced compared with healthy controls, and they were predictive of future events. Following adjustment, 3D‐minPS delivered optimal performance for the prediction of outcomes, a marker describing the maximal amplitude of local tissue shortening when measured along its innate direction of deformation. 18 , 19 , 20 Superior performance for this marker relative to geometry‐dependent strain estimates is postulated to reflect more accurate estimation of local myofibril contractile health through elimination of off‐axis errors. 21 Our technique is validated against tagged magnetic resonance imaging and speckle‐tracking echocardiography, 19 has had reference healthy values published, 18 and has characterized various disease cohorts inclusive of large international studies. 17 , 23 , 24 Several studies have examined the role of myocardial strain in patients with MD. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Of particular relevance, a recent study also applied a 3D strain analysis technique to study conventional, geometry‐dependent measures in 111 patients with a broad range of neuromuscular diseases inclusive of, but not limited, to MD. 29 Similar to our study, they observed both 3D‐GCS and 3D‐GRS are associated with the presence of replacement fibrosis. Incremental to this publication, our study provided a greater focus on MD, assessed geometry‐independent 3D strain markers, and followed up patients for MACEs to determine the predictive value of these markers. LVEF in patients with neuromuscular disease is valuable for risk stratification and associated with plasma markers for adverse cardiac remodeling. 22 Recognizing LVEF is a well‐established prognostic marker in patients with MD, our study provided objective evidence that myocardial strain delivers incremental prognostic value to LVEF in this vulnerable patient population. 27 , 28 Although speckle‐tracking echocardiography can be used for strain‐based evaluations, its performance can be hindered because of limited acoustic windows as a result of scoliosis, obesity, and lung disease, 9 , 30 which are highly prevalent in patients with MD. 5 CMR imaging is the preferred method for myocardial strain analysis as a high‐resolution, accurate, and reproducible alternative to echocardiography, 11 , 31 , 32 which supports the impact of CMR imaging as part of routine clinical care in patients with MD. 33 , 34 However, historically, the application of CMR imaging in the MD population can be complicated because of extensive musculoskeletal involvement, and presence of incompatible devices with CMR imaging, such as spinal steel rods or noninvasive ventilators. 12 , 35 , 36 However, enhancements in CMR technology and widespread use of CMR compatible devices have improved the accessibility for patients with MD. 11 , 32 , 36

We observed important differences in cardiac remodeling among the different types of MD in our cohort. Cardiac phenotypes in the dystrophinopathies cohort were distinguishable from LGMD and DM1 cohorts, given larger LV volumes, greater prevalence and burden of LGE, and greater reduction in LVEF with corresponding lower 3D strain amplitudes. Although the heterozygote population in dystrophinopathies is frequently overlooked or dismissed as a milder disease cohort, our results demonstrated heterozygotes are at risk of MACEs and exhibit impaired systolic function, reduced geometry‐dependent amplitudes, and reduced 3D‐minPS amplitudes, consistent with the dystrophinopathies phenotype. 37 The LGMD cohort experienced similar features, including elevated volumes, reduced systolic function, and presence of fibrosis, to a milder extent. However, these cohorts lacked objective evidence of eccentric hypertrophy, which is commonly observed in other cardiomyopathy states. In particular, patients with DM1 showed a unique phenotype with lower LV mass compared with healthy controls with reduced atrial and ventricular chamber volumes. This condition is a multisystemic disorder caused by a CTG repeat expansion within the 3′‐untranslated region of the myotonic dystrophy protein kinase (DMPK) gene. Interestingly, the primary defect associated with this genetic variant is impaired myogenesis, 38 , 39 , 40 suggesting that impaired cardiac growth is primarily responsible for the reduced LV mass and volume. Moreover, physical impairment was less abundant in the DM1 cohort; therefore, a sedentary lifestyle with limited exercise ability is not likely leading to cardiac atrophy. Although the cardiomyopathy in patients with MD is reminiscent of a dilated cardiomyopathy phenotype, 41 , 42 , 43 our data show relatively mild chamber dilation, small left atrial volumes, and minimal LV hypertrophy. This contrasts with other cardiomyopathy states, whereby adverse and progressive eccentric or concentric hypertrophy is commonly observed during disease progression. 44 , 45

Assessment of replacement fibrosis by LGE and diffuse fibrosis by T1 mapping showed a high prevalence of the former among patients with dystrophinopathies. The pattern and distribution of these findings were as described by prior reports with subepicardial or transmural replacement fibrosis of the inferolateral segments. 46 , 47 In contrast, replacement fibrosis was less prevalent in patients with DM1 and LGMD and, when present, was localized to the lateral wall subepicardial regions. Although LGE presence has prognostic utility in other cardiomyopathies and is associated with adverse remodeling in patients with MD, our investigation showed that LGE was not a significant predictor of MACEs, with the exception of LGE presence with transmural pattern in the dystrophinopathies cohort. 48 , 49 , 50 Furthermore, early‐onset myocardial fibrosis in pediatric patients with DMD is associated with a corresponding reduction in systolic function and increase in mortality risk; however, the associated risk of mortality declines with age. 51 Meanwhile, the link between myocardial fibrosis and heart disease in patients with DM1 is unclear. Although LGE presence has been reported as an independent predictor of atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter events, another study demonstrated LGE presence was not associated with ECG or Holter monitor abnormalities. 52 , 53 Considering the low prevalence and burden of fibrosis and lack of predictive value for MACEs in our cohort, myocardial fibrosis is not likely to be the primary driver of heart disease experienced by patients with DM1. Diffuse fibrosis has been previously observed in patients with MD using native (noncontrast) T1 mapping compared with healthy controls. 54 , 55 In contrast to prior investigations, our ECV‐based assessment of diffuse fibrosis did not reveal elevated values in our MD cohorts relative to healthy controls. It is important to recognize a priori excluded segments with replacement fibrosis, isolating analysis to segments not already classified as abnormal by LGE‐based techniques. The management of comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia), LV systolic dysfunction, and respiratory disease can minimize the risk of heart damage in patients with MD. 56 , 57 Indeed, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers are known to reverse myocardial fibrosis, and the prevention of nocturnal hypoxemia (respiratory therapy) and comorbidities can minimize myocardial ischemia. 58 , 59 , 60 , 61

Study Limitations

We recognize certain limitations associated with our study. Although multicenter and the largest adult MD cohort to date evaluated by CMR imaging, our cohort size remains modest and reflects the challenge of conducting CMR‐based studies in patients with MD. Ongoing recruitment of patients to our cohort will increase size for enhanced validation in future studies, including permitting subgroup analyses. Targeted recruitment of matched healthy controls to fulfill an n:1 ratio would provide important insight in the comparison of healthy controls to patients with MD. In addition, collecting follow‐up data on healthy controls for time‐to‐event analysis would further enhance the quality of these comparative assessments. Our multicenter design also recognizes differences in imaging hardware. This prevented our capacity to assess the predictive utility of native T1 values, given inherent reliance on field strength and pulse sequence. However, this was not a limitation for ECV estimation.

CONCLUSIONS

MD represents a collection of rare genetic diseases, each associated with a distinct cardiomyopathy phenotype that can be identified by comprehensive CMR imaging. We showed prominent contractile dysfunction (with replacement fibrosis) among dystrophinopathies, intermediate expression of a similar phenotype in LGMD, and reduced cardiac growth without replacement fibrosis phenotype in DM1. Of all studied CMR‐based markers, 3D‐minPS delivered the strongest association with future MACEs and was incremental to LVEF.
