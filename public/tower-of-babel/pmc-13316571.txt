A versatile VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP platform for precise genome editing and durable epigenome silencing in cancer
Authors: Sungjin Ju, Jang Hyeon Lee, Jiyun Yang, Tae Yeong Jeong, Chang Geun Choi, Yerim Lee, Seongkwang Cha, Jeongeun Park, Sol Pin Kim, Sanghun Kim, Je Kyung Seong, Hyunji Lee, Kyoungmi Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based genome and epigenome editing hold great promise for precision therapy, yet their clinical translation is limited by delivery challenges. Here, we present a transient, DNA-free virus-like particle (VLP) platform for the delivery of CRISPR ribonucleoprotein (RNP) across diverse cancer applications. VLP-mediated genome editing achieved high editing efficiency and broad applicability across multiple cancer types. Notably, VLP-mediated mutation-selective targeting of oncogenic alleles induced potent on-target activity and high-fidelity mutation-selective effects in cancer cells, with minimal effects on normal cells. Beyond genome editing, VLP delivery of the epigenome editor CRISPRoff effectively suppressed cancer cell growth and induced stable gene silencing maintained for over 120 days. Moreover, it provided a strategy to overcome knockout escape, a key limitation of conventional genome editing. In comparison with DNA vector-based delivery systems, VLPs enabled transient expression while achieving lower off-target activity and higher efficiency. Furthermore, the VLP platform exhibited a favorable in vivo safety profile, characterized by transient exposure and no hepatotoxicity. In a mouse xenograft model, the platform successfully suppressed tumor growth via precision targeting. Collectively, these findings establish VLP-mediated CRISPR delivery as a versatile and safe integrated delivery platform, providing a comprehensive modality for both genome and epigenome editing in precision therapeutics.Keywords: MT: delivery strategies, cancer-selective genome editing, durable epigenome silencing, VLP, CRISPR-RNP delivery, versatile delivery platform, overcoming knockout escape resistance

Introduction

The CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats) system is a versatile genome engineering platform that has transformed the fields of biology and medicine.1 By enabling the precise manipulation of genomic loci or epigenomic features, it provides powerful means to interrogate gene function and develop therapies for various diseases. Specifically, genome editing achieves this through targeted DNA sequence alterations. Epigenome editing modulates gene expression without altering the DNA sequence by writing epigenetic marks such as DNA methylation, thereby avoiding permanent genomic modification and reducing the risks of unintended mutations.2

These precise manipulation capabilities of the CRISPR system have emerged as a powerful tool to overcome the hurdles in cancer therapy. While advances in chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy have greatly improved cancer outcomes, intrinsic and acquired drug resistance continue to be major barriers to long-term efficacy.3 Consequently, the development of novel therapeutic strategies with distinct mechanisms is becoming increasingly important. In this context, the CRISPR system represents a promising therapeutic strategy that directly targets the genetic origins of tumorigenesis either by targeting cancer-specific mutations or suppressing key oncogenic drivers.4,5

This rapid expansion of CRISPR application underscores the need for safe and efficient delivery systems. Currently, viral vectors are widely utilized vehicles for CRISPR delivery due to their established efficiency. However, significant safety concerns arise from the use of viral genomes for delivery. These include the risk of genomic integration leading to insertional mutagenesis and prolonged nuclease expression that increases off-target activity.6 To circumvent these safety concerns, virus-like particles (VLPs) have emerged as an alternative. VLPs are non-infectious nanostructures that mimic the architecture of native viruses, maintaining high transduction efficiency while entirely lacking viral genetic material.7,8

Leveraging these advantages, recent developments have focused on packaging active CRISPR ribonucleoprotein (RNP), consisting of the Cas9 nuclease complexed with guide RNA directly into VLPs.9 This approach utilizes the functional advantages of RNP, specifically its transient activity, which minimizes the risk of off-target effects, and its ability to initiate immediate genome editing upon cellular entry, thereby avoiding the delays and prolonged exposure associated with transcription and translation.10,11,12

Building upon these capabilities, recent studies have demonstrated the promise of VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP delivery in editing the liver, brain, and retina, as well as in T cell engineering.13,14,15 However, the therapeutic potential of VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP delivery for cancer-specific genome and epigenome editing remains largely unexplored. Therefore, we investigated a retrovirus-derived VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP delivery platform to determine its efficacy for precise genomic and epigenomic editing across various cancer types. We further assessed its precise editing and delivery efficiency in comparison to conventional DNA vector-based approaches, including DNA and adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based systems. Additionally, we investigated the in vivo safety and efficiency to evaluate the translational potential of this platform for future clinical applications.

In this study, we demonstrate that the VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP platform offers three advantages for cancer therapy: (1) the platform enables the selective targeting of cancer-specific mutations and effectively overcomes knockout (KO) escape, a critical limitation of conventional gene editing, through CRISPRoff-mediated epigenetic silencing. (2) Compared to DNA vector-based delivery systems, it achieves high editing efficiency while exhibiting superior mutation-selective precision at on-target sites and a significant reduction in off-target activity. (3) The VLP platform exhibits a systemic safety profile, characterized by the rapid clearance of luciferase-packaging VLPs and the absence of hepatotoxicity, ensuring minimal off-target risks associated with prolonged exposure. Distinctly, in a therapeutic context, the administration of cancer mutation-targeting VLPs into a mouse xenograft model resulted in suppression of tumor growth, a direct consequence of efficient targeted insertions or deletions (indels) within the oncogenic locus. Collectively, these results support the potential of VLP-mediated delivery as a versatile platform for precision genome and epigenome editing.

Results

Efficient PLK1 gene editing by VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNPs across diverse cancer cell types

To achieve efficient genome editing, the VLP system relies on four key functional modules that operate in a coordinated manner. The process is initiated by co-transfection of producer cells with plasmids encoding VSV-G, Gag-Pol, Gag-Cargo, and a target-specific single guide RNA (sgRNA). Within the producer cell, the cargo protein and the sgRNA assemble into a functional RNP complex, which is subsequently incorporated into the VLP core through the inherent assembly capacity of Gag, driving particle budding from the producer cell membrane. Concurrently, Gag-Pol provides the structural framework necessary for particle assembly and maturation into fully formed VLPs. The resulting particles are coated with VSV-G on their surface, enabling broad cellular tropism via low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR)-mediated endocytosis. Upon entry into recipient cells, the RNP complex is released into the cytoplasm and imported into the nucleus, where Cas9 is directed by the sgRNA to the intended genomic locus for precise targeted gene modification (Figure S1).

We first evaluated the applicability of VLP-mediated delivery of CRISPR-RNPs utilizing Streptococcus pyogenes (SpCas9) by measuring editing efficiency at the TTN locus under varying VLP doses and observed a dose-dependent increase in indel frequencies across multiple cell lines, including HEK293T (non-malignant human embryonic kidney), NHDF (normal human dermal fibroblast), Huh7 (hepatocellular carcinoma), U87 (glioblastoma), H1975 (non-small cell lung cancer), SKBR-3 (breast cancer), A375 (melanoma), and SW480 (colorectal cancer) cells. As a result, high indel frequencies confirmed that VLP-mediated delivery of CRISPR-RNPs is effective across diverse cell types (Figure S2).

Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1), a serine/threonine kinase essential for centrosome maturation, spindle assembly, chromosome segregation, and cytokinesis, is frequently overexpressed in tumors and correlates with progression, lymph node metastasis, and reduced patient survival. PLK1 inhibition has been proposed to overcome chemoresistance and shows potential as a monotherapy or in combination with radiation or immunotherapy.16,17,18 Given its biological and clinical relevance, PLK1 was selected as a target. Accordingly, we designed sgRNAs targeting exons 2 and 3 of PLK1 and conducted a screen to select highly effective sgRNAs for growth suppression. Among the three candidates, the PLK1-1 sgRNA showed the strongest growth suppression (Figures S3A and S3B) and was selected for subsequent experiments, hereafter referred to as PLK1 (KO) (Figure S3C).

Increasing doses of PLK1 (KO) produced dose-dependent increases in indel frequencies, confirming efficient VLP-mediated PLK1 KO (Figure S3D). In the following functional analysis, the reduction in cell viability indicated that PLK1 disruption induced cellular dysfunction across various cancer cell types (Figure S3E). Consistently, the marked decrease in colony formation indicates impaired long-term proliferative ability and reduced capacity to form viable cells (Figures S4A and S4B). Unlike DNA-based delivery, VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP delivery is characterized by transient exposure, which facilitates repeated dosing. Based on this advantage, we investigated whether sequential VLP treatments could further enhance genome editing efficiency. In H1975 cells, which initially exhibited low editing efficiency, repeated VLP treatments resulted in a stepwise increase in editing frequency (Figures S5A and S5B). Consistently, subsequent cell viability assays showed a corresponding stepwise decrease. These findings suggest that repeated VLP administration serves as a practical strategy to improve editing outcomes (Figure S5C). Given that PLK1 peaks during mitosis and its loss triggers apoptosis, we investigated whether VLP-mediated PLK1 KO induces apoptotic death. A marked induction of apoptotic cell death was evident in the experimental group compared to the control group (Figures S6A and S6B). Successful KO of PLK1 was further evidenced by the diminished protein expression observed in western blot analysis (Figures S7A and S7B). Collectively, these data show that VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP delivery efficiently enables targeted PLK1 editing across multiple cancer cell types, resulting in effective growth suppression and cell death.

Selective suppression of BRAF (V600E)-mutant cancer cells by VLP-mediated precision genome editing

Cancer is characterized by the accumulation of multiple genetic mutations, and these cancer-associated mutations are attractive targets for CRISPR-based therapeutic strategies, enabling cancer mutation-selective genome editing.19,20,21 The V600E mutation in the B-Raf proto-oncogene, a serine/threonine kinase, is a clinically relevant gain-of-function alteration commonly observed in melanoma, thyroid cancer, and colorectal cancer, where it promotes tumor progression.22 On this basis, we adopted a mutation-selective targeting strategy that leverages the high on-target specificity and low off-target activity of VLP-mediated delivery.23 We designed an sgRNA targeting the BRAF (V600E) mutation and produced VLPs for selective BRAF (V600E) editing in A375 melanoma cells harboring the mutation, with no effect on NHDF cells with BRAF wild type (WT) (Figure 1A). With increasing VLP dose, A375 melanoma cells harboring the BRAF (V600E) mutation exhibited dose-dependent increases in indel frequencies, whereas editing was barely detectable in NHDF, BRAF WT cells. Additionally, to validate previously reported VLP variants that enhance packaging and delivery, we compared v4 and v5 variants in our system and observed that v4 achieved higher editing efficiency at the BRAF (V600E) locus (Figure 1B).24

To further evaluate whether this mutation-selective targeting occurs within a heterogeneous cell population, we co-cultured A375 melanoma cells (harboring three copies of the BRAF-containing chromosome) together with NHDF (harboring two copies of the BRAF-containing chromosome).25 Specifically, 2.5 × 103 cells of each type were seeded per well in 96-well plates to assess the mutation-selective targeting capability of our VLP platform in a mixed cellular environment. Although variations in cell growth and BRAF chromosome counts initially led to a high abundance of the BRAF (V600E) mutation genotype, at day 3, the VLP-treated group exhibited a modest 5.51% increase in the WT cell proportion compared to control. As the culture progressed, this increase accelerated, leading to a more substantial differentiation between the two groups. By day 10, the WT proportion in the control group had decreased to 6.72% from its initial level of 14.19% at day 3, indicating an accelerated dominance of cancer cells. In contrast, the WT proportion in the VLP-treated group rose significantly to 37.57%. Consequently, at day 10, the VLP group achieved a substantial 30.85% increase in the WT population relative to the control. Notably, the indel frequencies showed a gradual decline after its day 3 peak, reaching 17.86% by day 10 (Figure 1C). These findings suggest that VLP-mediated BRAF (V600E) targeting selectively depletes mutant cancer cells, implying a progressive increase in the WT cell fraction within a heterogeneous cell population.

Functionally, BRAF (V600E)-targeting VLPs markedly reduced cell viability and colony formation in A375 cells, with no effects in NHDF cells (Figures 1D and 1E). Consistently, BRAF mRNA expression was significantly reduced in A375 cells but remained unchanged in NHDF cells (Figure 1F). Additionally, treatment with BRAF (V600E)-targeting VLPs significantly induced apoptosis, as evidenced by a marked increase in Annexin V/PI-positive cells and elevated caspase-3/7 activity relative to the control (Figures 1G and 1H; Figure S8). Evaluation of 10 predicted off-target sites showed no evidence of unintended editing in NHDF cells (Figure 1I). Together, these results demonstrate efficient and specific VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP editing of the BRAF (V600E) mutation, resulting in mutation-selective growth inhibition and apoptosis in BRAF (V600E) mutant cancer cells, with minimal effects in normal cells. This underscores the robustness of our VLP-mediated strategy in distinguishing between mutant and WT cells, supporting its feasibility for further therapeutic development.

Extending VLP-mediated editing to the oncogenic KRAS (G12V) mutation

Building on the BRAF (V600E) results, we extended our strategy to the well-characterized oncogene KRAS (G12V), a frequent driver in colorectal cancer that promotes tumor cell proliferation and survival.26 To evaluate mutation-selective editing, we produced VLPs packaging an sgRNA specifically targeting the KRAS (G12V) mutation and assessed editing efficiency in SW480 cells harboring the KRAS (G12V) mutation and NHDF cells with KRAS WT (Figure S9A). Increasing VLP dose induced dose-dependent increases in indel frequencies in KRAS (G12V) mutant colorectal cancer cells (SW480), whereas editing remained minimal in NHDF cells. Consistent with our prior evaluation (Figure 1B), we compared the v4 and v5 VLP systems and found that v5 exhibited greater editing efficiency for this target (Figure S9B). We observed variation in editing performance across targets, with v4 showing higher activity at BRAF. These results suggest that selection between v4 and v5 should consider target-specific efficiency. Functionally, the KRAS (G12V) KO significantly reduced cell viability in SW480 cells without affecting NHDF cells (Figure S9C) and markedly suppressed colony formation in SW480 cells, with no detectable effects in NHDF cells (Figure S9D). We confirmed a substantial decrease in KRAS mRNA levels only in SW480 cells (Figure S9E). Targeted disruption of KRAS (G12V) resulted in the selective induction of apoptosis in SW480 cells, with negligible changes in NHDF (Figures S9F and S10). We confirmed minimal to no detectable unintended editing at five potential off-target loci in NHDF cells (Figure S9G). These findings indicate that the high on-target specificity of VLP-mediated delivery is broadly applicable across distinct oncogenic mutations, underscoring its potential for diverse cancer targets.

Durable gene silencing through VLP-mediated delivery of an epigenome editor

CRISPR, originally developed for nuclease-based genome editing, now enables epigenome editing by fusing catalytically inactive Cas9 (dCas9) to effector domains that install epigenetic marks. Among available tools, CRISPRoff enables sustained gene silencing through the fusion of three transcriptional repressors: DNA methyltransferase 3 alpha (DNMT3A), DNA methyltransferase 3 like (DNMT3L), and Krüppel-associated box (KRAB). Alternatively, the CRISPRoff (D3A-E765A) variant can be utilized for transcriptional repression without long-term persistence, as it carries the E765A mutation in the catalytically inactive DNMT3A that abolishes its maintenance capability.27 However, these additional domains exceed the packaging limits of common viral vectors like AAV (∼4.7 kb), limiting their clinical application. To overcome these limitations, we employed a VLP system, which accommodates larger cargo, to deliver CRISPRoff or CRISPRoff (D3A-E765A) (Figure 2A).

Although CRISPRoff enables durable gene silencing, transient RNP activity and the reversibility of epigenome editing have raised concerns about durability. To directly test whether VLP delivery can sustain stable CRISPRoff-mediated silencing, we targeted PCSK9 as a representative locus.28 In Huh7 cells, a single treatment with CRISPRoff-VLPs produced a sustained reduction in PCSK9 mRNA levels for more than 120 days, whereas expression in the CRISPRoff (D3A-E765A) group gradually recovered (Figure 2B). In addition, the DNA methylation status at day 120 showed that the methylation patterns induced by CRISPRoff and CRISPRoff (D3A-E765A) correlated with the corresponding PCSK9 mRNA expression levels (Figure 2C). These results demonstrate that epigenome editors in RNP form can be functionally packaged into VLPs. Furthermore, a CRISPRoff-VLP single treatment can achieve durable, long-term silencing of target genes, supporting VLPs as an efficient vehicle for delivering epigenome editors.

Building on our observation that PLK1 KO induces cell death across diverse cancer cell types, we evaluated whether VLP-mediated epigenome editing could achieve PLK1 knockdown (KD) and consequently induce cell death. Accordingly, we screened five sgRNAs targeting the PLK1 transcription start site (TSS) and promoter region and selected the most effective sgRNA (Figure 2D). Among them, sgRNA PLK1-1 induced the largest reduction in cell viability and was designated PLK1 (KD) for subsequent experiments (Figure 2E).

Furthermore, a colony formation assay confirmed that the PLK1 (KD)-treated group significantly inhibited cell growth (Figure 2F). Consistent with this, PLK1 expression decreased at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figures 2G and 2H), and epigenetic profiling revealed increased DNA methylation at promoter-proximal CpG sites (Figure 2I). These findings indicate successful CRISPRoff-RNP packaging into the VLP, which enables PLK1 repression via promoter methylation and growth inhibition, while demonstrating the platform’s versatile applications.

CRISPRoff-mediated epigenome editing overcomes KO escape-driven resistance

Although genome editing technologies enable efficient gene disruption, accumulating evidence indicates that CRISPR-mediated KO can fail to abolish gene function. This phenomenon, known as KO escape, can result from in-frame transcript generation, translation reinitiation, or functionally silent nucleotide changes.29 These mechanisms lead to alterations in the target sequence, disrupting sgRNA targeting capacity. Consequently, drug resistance may arise in genome editing-mediated cancer therapy. As a strategy to circumvent the unpredictable repair outcomes and limited control inherent to conventional genome editing, we employed epigenome editing.

As a proof of concept, to generate a KO-resistant Huh7 cell pool, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well. Each well was subsequently treated with 5 × 108 PLK1 (KO) VLPs. Surviving cells from multiple wells were harvested and pooled into a single population. This process of treatment and enrichment was repeated serially to model the development of resistance under sustained genome editing pressure (Figure 3A). Targeted deep sequencing of the resistant Huh7 cell pool revealed heterogeneous editing outcomes. At the initial stage, established following a single PLK1 (KO) VLP treatment, missense mutations comprised the largest fraction (41.35%), followed by insertions (21.67%), in-frame deletions (14.09%), deletions (12.14%), silent mutations (7.11%), and WT alleles (3.65%) at this stage. As selection progressed toward the terminal stage, repeated editing pressure ultimately established a resistant Huh7 cell pool by completely eliminating WT alleles (Figure 3B). These editing patterns indicate that PLK1 function persisted in a subset of edited cells, allowing survival despite repeated KO attempts. Subsequently, to overcome this resistance, we applied PLK1 (KD)-mediated epigenetic repression to the resistant cell pool. Consistent with prior results, PLK1 KD significantly reduced cell viability and colony formation (Figures 3C and 3D), decreased PLK1 mRNA level (Figure 3E), and increased apoptosis (Figure 3F). These findings confirm the emergence of resistance to KO-based therapeutics and demonstrate that transcriptional repression can eliminate KO escape variants, providing a complementary strategy to conventional genome editing.

Evaluating the safety and efficiency of VLP-mediated RNP delivery against DNA vector-based systems

To assess whether VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP offers safety advantages over DNA vector-based delivery regarding off-target risk,30 we first measured the duration of intracellular Cas9 expression after VLP-mediated RNP delivery and compared it with plasmid-based Cas9 expression. Cas9 levels declined rapidly and were nearly absent within 1 week following VLP delivery, whereas plasmid-based delivery sustained prolonged Cas9 expression. These results indicate that VLP delivery reduces risks associated with persistent Cas9 nuclease exposure (Figures 4A and 4B).

We next compared the off-target activity of VLP and DNA vector-based delivery systems targeting the cancer-specific mutations BRAF (V600E) or KRAS (G12V). For this evaluation, we utilized HEK293T cells, which carry WT sequences at both the BRAF and KRAS loci. Using these cells as a mutation-negative control, we were able to directly assess the ability of each delivery system to discriminate base-mismatched off-target sites. Relative to VLP delivery, DNA vector-based delivery produced markedly higher off-target effects, with increases up to 53.88-fold for BRAF and 5.9-fold for KRAS (Figures 4C and 4D). These findings indicate that VLP delivery enables mutation-selective targeting, minimizes unintended editing in normal cells, and provides superior safety, whereas DNA vector-based delivery exhibits higher off-target activity and reduced specificity.

To further evaluate safety across diverse human-derived normal cell types, we treated HaCaT (keratinocyte), ARPE-19 (retinal pigment epithelium), and HEK293T cells with previously validated cancer-specific BRAF- (V600E) or KRAS (G12V)-targeting VLPs. VLPs showed negligible effects on viability at any dose or in any cell type (Figure S11).

Given the widespread use but limited cargo capacity of AAV, CRISPR delivery often relies on dual-vector strategies, including dual AAV systems (Dual AAV) that separate Cas9 and sgRNA across two vectors and intein-mediated split-Cas9 systems (Split AAV) that divide Cas9 into N- and C-terminal fragments.31 These approaches increase viral dose and complicate manufacturing, and the requirement for transcription and translation before nuclease activation leads to delayed functional onset and substantially lower overall efficiency.

In this context, we anticipated that VLP-mediated delivery of preassembled RNPs would induce a more rapid and efficient functional response than AAV and compared the two delivery systems. To minimize delivery-related bias, we assessed AAV serotype-dependent transduction efficiency in A375 cells using GFP-expressing AAVs. As a result, we found that AAV serotype 6 exhibited the highest transduction efficiency and selected AAV serotype 6 for subsequent comparison experiments (Figure S12).

We next compared genome editing efficiency after BRAF (V600E) targeting using Split AAV, Dual AAV, and VLP at two indicated doses (5 × 107 and 5 × 108). For the Split and Dual AAV systems, these doses refer to the amount of each individual vector, resulting in total titers of 1 × 108 and 1 × 109 viral genomes, respectively. Notably, despite the higher total viral load in the AAV groups, VLP-mediated delivery induced markedly higher indel frequencies in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 4E). Consistent with the observed indel frequencies, we further evaluated the effect of each delivery system on cell viability and long-term proliferative capacity. While both Split and Dual AAV systems failed to induce any significant reduction in cell viability across doses and time points, VLP-mediated delivery exhibited a distinct, dose-dependent reduction in cell survival (Figure 4F). These observations were further supported by a colony formation assay (Figures 4G and 4H). To verify the versatility of the VLP platform, we extended our comparative analysis by targeting the KRAS (G12V) mutation in SW480 cells. Consistent with our previous findings, the VLP-treated group exhibited significantly higher indel frequencies compared to both Split and Dual AAV delivery systems (Figure S13A). Correspondingly, while both Split and Dual AAV systems failed to induce any significant reduction in cell viability across doses and time points, the VLP treatment resulted in potent, dose-dependent growth inhibition (Figure S13B). This inhibitory effect was further corroborated by colony formation assays, in which VLP-treated cells showed a decrease in colony-forming capacity, whereas the AAV-treated groups remained unaffected (Figures S13C and S13D). Collectively, these data again underscore the superior efficacy of the VLP platform over conventional AAV-based strategies. Notably, Split AAV consistently exhibited lower indel frequencies than Dual AAV, highlighting the inherent inefficiency of reconstituting Cas9 through an intein-mediated split system. The use of split systems becomes necessary for delivering large editors such as CRISPRoff.32,33,34 However, the reduced efficiency associated with Split AAV systems reveals limitations of AAV-based delivery and highlights the need for VLP delivery, which has no packaging capacity constraints. Collectively, these results highlight practical advantages of VLP delivery, including reduced packaging constraints, high editing efficiency, and potential production cost benefits.

In vivo safety evaluation of VLPs

Building upon our in vitro observations (Figure 4A), we investigated the in vivo persistence and safety of the VLP-mediated delivery using intravenous injection in mice. VLPs packaging a luciferase reporter were administered at distinct dose levels (1 × 1011 and 5 × 1011 VLPs), resulting in a dose-responsive bioluminescence signal (Figures 5A, 5B, and 5C). In the group treated with 5 × 1011 VLPs, the signal peaked at 2 h post-administration and subsequently diminished to near baseline levels by 12 h, indicating transient expression. In vivo imaging revealed that the signal was predominantly localized to the liver and spleen.

Given the significant signal localization in the liver, we assessed in vivo safety by measuring hepatotoxicity. Serum was harvested at 24 h post-injection to analyze the levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). The enzyme activities in the VLP-treated groups showed no clinically significant alterations compared to the control groups (Figures 5D and 5E), and histopathological examination by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining likewise revealed no signs of tissue damage. Furthermore, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for cleaved caspase-3 showed no significant induction of apoptosis in any of the VLP-treated groups, confirming the safety of the VLPs (Figure 5F). Together, these results demonstrate that the VLP delivery system maintains a favorable profile in vivo, showing no evidence of treatment-related adverse effects across biochemical and histopathological evaluations.

In vivo efficiency of BRAF (V600E)-targeting VLPs in a xenograft cancer model

Building upon the precise and efficient anti-tumor activity observed in vitro, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of BRAF (V600E)-targeting VLPs in an in vivo environment. We utilized an A375 xenograft cancer model.35 Once the tumor volume reached approximately 45 mm3, a total of 1 × 1011 BRAF (V600E)-targeting VLPs were administered via intratumoral injection every 2 days for a total of five times (Figure 6A). The administration of 1 × 1011 BRAF (V600E)-targeting VLPs demonstrated successful tumor growth inhibition compared to PBS and Cas only, validating the therapeutic efficacy of the platform in vivo. The lack of a significant difference between the Cas only and PBS groups confirms that the observed anti-tumor effect is specifically driven by BRAF (V600E) targeting, while further indicating that the VLP platform is devoid of intrinsic toxicity (Figure 6B). The body weights of the mice remained stable throughout the study, suggesting that the VLP treatment was well tolerated without apparent systemic toxicity (Figure 6C). Subsequent analysis of indel frequencies confirmed that BRAF (V600E) gene editing occurred within the harvested tumor tissues (Figures 6D and 6E). Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of our VLP-mediated delivery system as an effective platform capable of achieving genome editing within an in vivo environment.

Discussion

In this study, our comprehensive evaluations demonstrate the versatility, high efficiency, safety, and precision of the VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP platform. PLK1 targeting across various cancer cell types showed high indel frequencies along with significant cell death and growth suppression. This indicates that our platform can effectively control the survival of cancer cells. Furthermore, when targeting major oncogenic hotspots such as BRAF (V600E) and KRAS (G12V), genome editing was selectively induced only in mutant cancer cells, with almost no editing efficiency in normal cells. This high selectivity means that the VLP system ensures safety for cancer-specific therapy.

Regarding epigenome editing, we confirmed that CRISPRoff-VLP could stably suppress target gene expression for a long period with a single treatment, and we observed anticancer effects when targeting PLK1. This shows that the platform can effectively control cancer cell survival through strong gene expression suppression. This suppression strategy is especially valuable as a complementary approach to overcome KO escape resistance, which can occur in conventional genome editing.

Additionally, our research confirmed technical superiority by comparing the VLP platform with other DNA vector-based delivery systems. Compared to DNA vector-based delivery, VLPs showed markedly superior discrimination of mutation sequences, lowering off-target activity and minimizing the impact on normal cells. In comparison with AAV systems, VLPs also achieved faster and higher editing efficiency.

During in vivo evaluations using luciferase-packaged VLPs, we observed a transient expression pattern where the signal peaked immediately after intravenous injection and disappeared quickly within a few hours. This transient exposure ensured that, despite hepatic accumulation, no clinically significant alterations in serum biochemical parameters (AST and ALT) were observed, and there were no detectable histopathological lesions or apoptotic markers (cleaved caspase-3). These results highlight the VLP platform as a next-generation delivery system, characterized by transient exposure and a favorable safety profile in vivo.

While this study demonstrates the potential of the VLP platform, several critical limitations and challenges must be addressed for practical clinical translation. First, there is the issue of non-specific toxicity resulting from target gene suppression. Although PLK1 inhibition exhibited potent anticancer activity in our experiments, the essential role of this gene in regulating the normal cell cycle raises concerns regarding non-specific cytotoxicity. To mitigate this risk, we employed mutation-selective targeting of cancer cells as a promising strategy to enhance specificity. Additionally, the pseudotyping of the VLP envelope protein, which determines cellular entry, offers a promising solution.36,37,38 By engineering the delivery vehicle to enhance uptake in cancer cells while minimizing delivery to normal cells, the precision and safety of the treatment can be substantially improved.39,40 In this regard, it is also important to note that the in vivo safety evaluation in the present study was conducted using luciferase-packaged VLPs administered via intravenous injection, which does not recapitulate tumor-targeted delivery. Given that systemic intravenous administration lacks inherent cancer selectivity, direct assessment of VLP efficiency within the tumor microenvironment was not feasible. Future studies employing pseudotyped VLPs in tumor-bearing models will therefore be essential to comprehensively evaluate both therapeutic efficiency and the safety profile of targeted in vivo genome editing.37,41

Second, the scope of application is limited by the rarity of specific mutations and various technical constraints.42 Targeting oncogenic hotspots is a powerful therapeutic strategy. However, its clinical utility is restricted to specific patient populations harboring these drivers since not all malignancies are caused by these mutations.43 Furthermore, technical constraints such as PAM availability and the impact of the mutation position within the sgRNA on selective precision further narrow the targetable range. To overcome these hurdles, integrating the VLP platform with engineered CRISPR variants, such as PAM-flexible SpRY or NG-Cas9 to expand targetable sites and high-fidelity variants to improve single-nucleotide discrimination, will be a necessary future direction.44,45,46

Third, there is a potential for additional resistance due to tumor heterogeneity. While we have demonstrated that epigenome editing can effectively circumvent the KO escape resistance observed in conventional genome editing, the complex heterogeneity of cancer suggests that secondary resistance to epigenetic repression may still occur. Cancer cells might bypass transcriptional silencing by activating alternative signaling pathways or through epigenetic adaptation. To prevent such resistance and achieve complete tumor suppression, it is essential to develop combination strategies that integrate epigenome editing with conventional chemotherapies or targeted drugs.

Collectively, the present study advances VLP-mediated CRISPR-RNP delivery as an integrated platform capable of supporting both genome and epigenome editing with high precision and a favorable in vivo safety profile. Although challenges for clinical translation remain, including tumor heterogeneity and limited target scope, further optimization of targeting strategies and delivery specificity, along with expanded in vivo evaluation encompassing broader therapeutic endpoints such as long-term anti-tumor efficacy, is expected to enhance therapeutic precision and safety. These results support the clinical translational potential of this platform for future cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Vector design and cloning

sgRNAs were designed to include a 5′ guanine nucleotide followed by a 20-nucleotide target sequence (GX20)47 to enhance transcription efficiency. sgRNA design was performed using Cas-Designer (CRISPR RGEN Tools; rgenome.net). The selected sgRNA sequences were cloned by digesting the pRG2 vector (Addgene, 104174) with BsaI-HFv2 (New England Biolabs, R3733S) and ligating with T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs, M0202S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To generate the MMLVgag-3xNES-CRISPRoff v.2.1 and MMLVgag-3xNES-Luciferase vectors, the corresponding gene fragments from CRISPRoff v.2.1 (Addgene, 167981) and pCAG-luciferase (Addgene, 55764) were cloned into the MMLVgag-3xNES-Cas9 backbone (Addgene, 181752) via HiFi DNA Assembly (New England Biolabs, E2621S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Oligonucleotides were synthesized by Bionics and IDT. Detailed sgRNA sequences are provided in Table S1.

Cell culture

Huh7, U87, A375, NHDF, HaCaT, and HEK293T were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Welgene, LM001-05) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Welgene, S101-01). H1975, SW480, and ARPE-19 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Welgene, LM011-03) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. SKBR-3 was cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium (Welgene, LM005-01) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. All cell types were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2.

VLP production

Gesicle Producer 293T cells (Takara, 632617) were used to generate VLPs. Cells (5 × 106) were seeded in T-75 flasks (SPL, 70075) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. At 24 h after seeding, cells were transfected with jetPRIME (Polyplus, 101000001) and a mixture of VSV-G (400 ng), pBS-CMV-gagpol (3,375 ng), and MMLVgag-3xNES-based vectors (MMLVgag-3xNES-Cas9 [Addgene, 181752], MMLVgag-3xNES-CRISPRoff v2.1 [OPT], or MMLVgag-3xNES-luciferase) (1,125 ng). For the gene-editing constructs, 4,400 ng of sgRNA was co-transfected, whereas the luciferase mixture was transfected in the absence of sgRNA. The medium was replaced 24 h post-transfection. Supernatants were harvested at 48 h after transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) filter (Millipore, S2HVU02RE), and mixed with PEG-it Virus Precipitation Solution (SBI, LV810A-1) and stored overnight at 4°C. The mixture was centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 30 min at 4°C. For in vivo experiments, supernatants were concentrated through 20% (w/v) sucrose cushion buffer by centrifugation at 115,151 × g using Optima XE-100 (Beckman Coulter, A94516) equipped with an SW 32 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter, 369650). Pellets were resuspended in chilled PBS (Welgene, LB001-02) or Opti-MEM (Gibco, 31985070) and stored at −80°C.14,48,49

VLP titration

VLP titers were measured with the MuLV Core Antigen ELISA kit (Cell Biolabs, VPK-156) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each VLP reportedly contains approximately 1,800 copies of p30; about 20% of total p30 is VLP associated.14,49,50 VLP values are listed in Table S4.

AAV production

Cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 106 cells per 100 mm dish (Sarstedt, 83.3902) 24 h prior to transfection. Plasmids encoding the AAV vector genome (3 μg), the serotype 6-specific rep/cap genes (3 μg), and adenoviral helper functions (6 μg) were introduced into the cells using polyethyleneimine (Polysciences, 24765-1) transfection reagent. After 48 h of culture, cells were harvested, and viral particles were extracted using the AAVpro Extraction Solution (Takara, 6235) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for subsequent vector purification. Viral titers were then determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using primers specific to the AAV2 ITR region.

Cell transfection

HEK293T cells were transfected with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015). Cells (5 × 103) were plated in 96-well plates (Sarstedt, 83.3924) in 100 μL complete medium. After 24 h, cells were transfected with 150 ng Cas9 plasmid and 50 ng sgRNA plasmid according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell viability assay

5 × 103 cells were seeded at 100 μL medium per well in a 96-well plate. After 24 h, cells were transduced with VLPs (5 × 107 to 1 × 109 particles). Cell viability was measured with the Viability Assay Kit (Cellrix, B1007-1500) on a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, A51119700DPC) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded at 5 × 103 per well in 96-well plates. After 24 h, cells were transduced with VLPs (5 × 107 or 5 × 108 particles). At 3 days post-transduction, cells were transferred to 6-well plates. After 8–15 days, cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosesang, PC2031-100-00) for 10 min. Fixed cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet (Merck, V5265) for 10 min at room temperature, and then wells were washed twice with PBS. Colonies were quantified in ImageJ.

Relative mRNA quantification

RNA was extracted using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, 9767A). cDNA was synthesized with the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, 1708890). Quantitative PCR was performed on 50 ng cDNA with AccuPower 2× Greenstar (Bioneer, K-6254) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Thermal cycling was 95°C for 3 min, then 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. Oligonucleotides were synthesized by Bionics and IDT. Primer sequences are provided in Table S3.

Bisulfite sequencing PCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from Huh7 cells seeded at 2 × 104 per well in 24-well plates using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, 69506) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following day, cells were treated with VLPs, and genomic DNA was collected 2 days post-treatment. For each condition, 1 μg genomic DNA was subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EZ DNA Methylation Lightning Kit (Zymo Research, D5001). Purified bisulfite-converted DNA was amplified by PCR with EpiMark Hot Start Taq (New England Biolabs, M0490S). PCR products were purified with the Expin PCR SV Kit (GeneAll, 103-102) and cloned using the TOPO TA Cloning Kit (Invitrogen, 451641). Recombinant colonies were selected by blue-white screening on X-gal/IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside) plates. Individual colonies were Sanger sequenced (Bionics), and reads were analyzed in QUMA (quma.cdb.riken.jp).51 Detailed bisulfite primer sequences are provided in Table S3. The promoter region was sourced from EPD (Eukaryotic promoter database).

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed with the Dead Cell Apoptosis Kit with Annexin V Alexa Fluor 488 and propidium iodide (PI) (Invitrogen, V13241) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were collected, washed with cold PBS, and resuspended in Annexin V binding buffer. Cells were stained with Alexa Fluor 488 and PI for 15 min at room temperature, diluted in binding buffer, and analyzed immediately on a BD FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences) and a Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences, equipment registration no.: NFEC-2024-09-299163). Data were processed in FlowJo v.10.7.

Caspase-3/7 activity assay

Caspase-3/7 activity was measured using the Caspase-Glo 3/7 assay (Promega, G8091) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well in 100 μL complete medium and incubated overnight to allow attachment. The following day, cells were treated with VLPs. After 72 h of VLP treatment, an equal volume (100 μL) of Caspase-Glo 3/7 reagent was then added directly to each well. Plates were mixed gently and incubated at room temperature for 2 h in the dark. Luminescence was measured using a GloMax Discover microplate reader (Promega).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Sigma, R0278) supplemented with Xpert protease inhibitor cocktail (100×; GenDEPOT, P3100-001). Lysates were mixed with 2× protein sample buffer (ELPIS-BIOTECH, EBA-1051) and denatured at 95°C. Proteins were separated on NuPAGE Bis-Tris, 1.0–1.5 mm, mini protein gels (4%–12%, 1.0–1.5 mm; Invitrogen, NP0321BOX) and transferred to Immobilon-P PVDF membrane (Sigma, IPVH00010) using NuPAGE transfer buffer (Thermo Scientific, NP00061). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (Sigma, A2153) in 1× TBS-T (GenDEPOT, T8058-001) or with 5% skim milk (Difco, 232100) in TBS-T for 1 h. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C on a rocker: anti-PLK1 (Abcam, ab17057; 1:1,000 in BSA), anti-β-actin (N-term; AbFrontier, LF-PA0207; 1:2,500 in BSA), and Cas9 (7A9-3A3; Santa Cruz, sc-517386; 1:1,000 in skim milk). After three washes in TBS-T, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature: goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), HRP-conjugated (Invitrogen, 31460; 1:5,000 in BSA), and polyclonal anti-mouse IgG Fc-HRP (AbFrontier, LF-SA8001; 1:5,000 in BSA). After three additional TBS-T washes, signals were developed with Pierce ECL Plus western blotting substrate (Thermo Scientific, 32209).

Targeted deep sequencing

Target loci were amplified using a three-round PCR strategy to append Illumina adapters and unique sample barcodes. Genomic DNA served as a template to amplify target sites with Platinum SuperFi PCR Master Mix (Invitrogen, 12368050), KAPA polymerase (Roche, 7958897001), or SUN PCR Blend (SUN GENETICS, SG-PT02). Amplicons were subjected to paired-end sequencing on MiSeq and MiniSeq instruments (Illumina). Sequencing data were analyzed with the EUN program (daeunyoon.com/). Detailed targeted deep sequencing primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Off-target analysis

Cas-OFFinder (CRISPR RGEN Tools; rgenome.net) was used to identify potential off-target sites in the Homo sapiens genome (GRCh38/hg38), allowing up to three mismatches. On- and off-target editing was quantified by targeted deep sequencing.

In vivo luciferase assay

All animal experiments were conducted with the approval of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Seoul National University (SNU-250617-1-2). 5- to 6-week-old Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) female mice were administered various doses of VLPs (n = 3 per group). The VLPs were delivered via intravenous (i.v.) injection (200 μL over 5–7 s). Following intraperitoneal injection of diluted luciferin (Promega, P1043) at 150 mg/kg, bioluminescence signals were measured at defined time intervals.

In vivo xenograft cancer model study

Male BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old) were used to establish the xenograft model. 1 × 106 A375 cells were suspended in 100 μL of PBS and administered via subcutaneous injection. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 45 mm3 or greater, mice were randomly divided into three treatment groups (n = 3 per group). BRAF-targeting VLPs (1 × 1011 particles) were prepared in a total volume of 30 μL using PBS and delivered via intratumoral injection. The treatment was administered every 2 days for a total of five times. Tumor dimensions were measured every 2 days starting from the day of the initial injection. All mice were sacrificed at 26 days for subsequent analysis.

H&E staining

The tissue sections were cleared in xylene to remove paraffin and gradually rehydrated through a descending ethanol series (100%–70%). The slides were then incubated with Gill 2 hematoxylin (Epredia, 6765007) followed by eosin counterstaining (BBC Biochemical, 3610). Excess stain was briefly differentiated using 1% HCl (DUKSAN GENERAL SCIENCE, 1129). Afterward, the slides were passed through graded ethanol for dehydration, rinsed in xylene, and coverslipped using mounting medium (Leica, 1404630011). Images were acquired using a Pannoramic Scanner 250 Flash III system (3DHISTECH) and visualized with CaseViewer software (3DHISTECH).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three or four independent biological replicates. For in vivo experiments, data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3 mice per group). Comparisons were performed using unpaired Student’s t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).
