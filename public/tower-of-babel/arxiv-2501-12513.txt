Major index distribution
Michael Coopman

Abstract
. For 0 < q < 1 0<q<1 , let Maj ⁡ ( n , q ) \mathrm{Maj}(n,q) be the distribution on the symmetric group 𝔖 n \mathfrak{S}_{n} such that a permutation π ∈ 𝔖 n \pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n} is selected with probability proportional to q maj ⁡ ( π ) q^{\mathrm{maj}(\pi)} . The distribution has connections to q q -Plancherel measure. We describe an algorithm that realizes Maj ⁡ ( n , q ) \mathrm{Maj}(n,q) , and use it to prove known results of q q -Plancherel measure without the need of representation theory. This sampler is transparent and elegant, allowing properties of Maj ⁡ ( n , q ) \mathrm{Maj}(n,q) about its limit shape, pattern normality, and cycle structure to be obtained.

Random permutations are ubiquitous in combinatorics, probability, and statistics with applications to all quantitative disciplines.
In many of these applications, the distributions of these permutations are not uniform.
Some distributions assign each permutations
\pi
a probability proportional to
q^{f(\pi)}

q>0
and
f(\pi)
is some statistic on the symmetric group.
When the statistic is inversion count, we recover the Mallows distribution, which was first used to answer questions in statistical ranking models
[
14
]
and more recently in genomics
[
7
]
.
When the statistic is cycle count, we recover the Ewens distribution, which was first used to answer questions in neutral allele theory
[
6
]
.

This paper will focus on the case where the statistic is the major index. This gives us the major index distribution, and we will answer questions regarding
q
-Plancherel measure with it.
For
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the
major index

\mathrm{maj}(\pi)
of a permutation
\pi
is the sum of its descents.
For
q>0
, we say that
\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)
follows the
major index distribution

\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)

2.1.
Geometric random variables

Given a random variable
X:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we will say that
X

follows a geometric law
, denoted
X\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)
if
Pr(X=k)=q^{k}(1-q)
.
The relevant properties of geometric random variables for this article are provided below. For further background, see
[
5
,
13
]
.

Proposition 2.1

(Memorylessness)
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(X>m\mid X\geq k\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X+k>m\right).

For our purposes, we want a more general version of memorylessness, which can be proven is a similar fashion as Proposition
2.1
.

Corollary 2.2

(Memorylessness II)
.

Let
X,Y
be random variables such that
X
is independent from
Y
. Let
E(X,Y)\subset\Omega
be an event that depends on random variables
X
and
Y
. If
X\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(E(X,Y)\mid X\geq k\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(E(X+k,Y)\right).

In Section 3, we will perform comparisons between geometric random variables. As such, the following facts will be used.

Proposition 2.3

(Geometric races)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be independent random variables such that
X\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-a)
and
Y\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-b)
.
Then,

(1)

\min(X,Y)\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-ab)

(2)

\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq Y\right)=\dfrac{1-b}{1-ab}

(3)

\mathbb{P}\left(X>Y\right)=\dfrac{a(1-b)}{1-ab}

Proof.

(i) Let
Z=\min(X,Y)
. For
k\leq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq k\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq k\right)\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq k\right)=a^{k}b^{k}=(ab)^{k}.

Therefore,
\mathbb{P}\left(Z=k\right)=(ab)^{k}(1-ab)
.

(ii) A straightforward calculation gives the following:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq Y\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\sum_{y=0}^{x}a^{x}(1-a)b^{y}(1-b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}a^{x}(1-a)(1-b^{x+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}a^{x}(1-a)-(ab)^{x}b(1-a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-b}{1-ab}.

(iii) A similar argument as (ii) applies for (iii).
∎

2.3.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
We will show that if given

As

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n-1}\bigg\lvert\min_{i\in[n-1]}G_{\pi(i)}\underset{(n{-}1)}{>}G_{\pi(n)}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{i\in[n{-}1]}G_{\pi(i)}\underset{(n-1)}{>}G_{\pi(n)}\right).

For the first factor,
\underset{i\in[n-1]}{\min}G_{\pi(i)}\underset{(n-1)}{>}G_{\pi(n)}
is equivalent to
G_{\pi(i)}\underset{(n-1)}{>}G_{\pi(n)}
for all

Thus, using Proposition
2.2
to obtain (3.5),

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(A_{n-1}\bigg\lvert

\displaystyle\min_{i\in[n-1]}G_{\pi(i)}\underset{(n{-}1)}{>}G_{\pi(n)}\Big)

(3.3)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n-1}\bigg\lvert\forall i\in[n-1],G_{\pi(i)}\underset{(n{-}1)}{>}G_{\pi(n)}\right)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n-1}\bigg\lvert\forall i\in[n-1],G_{\pi(i)}\underset{(n-1)}{>}k\right)\mathbb{P}\left(G_{\pi(n)}=k\right)

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n-1}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(G_{\pi(n)}=k\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n-1}\right).

The second factor follows by Lemma

This completes the proof.

\hat{L}_{i}(t)\overset{\mbox{dist.}}{=}\sqrt{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}(t).

By Proposition
2.6
, the supremum (and infimum due to symmetry) of
W(t)
follows a distribution with a rapidly decaying tail. More precisely,

(4.3)

\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{0\leq t\leq 1}{\sup}|W(t)|\geq\dfrac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\epsilon/\sqrt{n}}^{\infty}e^{\frac{-u^{2}}{2}}\;du=o(n^{-1/2})

Combining these two facts gets

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{0\leq t\leq 1}{\sup}|\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)]|>\epsilon\right)

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{0\leq t\leq 1}{\sup}|n^{-1/2}\hat{L}_{i}(t)-\mathbb{E}[n^{-1/2}\hat{L}_{i}(t)]|>\epsilon\right)

(4.6)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{0\leq t\leq 1}{\sup}|\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)-\mathbb{E}[n^{-1/2}\hat{L}_{i}(t)]-n^{-1/2}W(t)|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{0\leq t\leq 1}{\sup}|n^{-1/2}W(t)|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)

(4.7)

\displaystyle=o(n^{-1/2}).

Now, we need to show that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)]=\frac{1}{n}(p_{i}t+L_{i}(0))
converges in probability to the line segment starting at
(q^{i+1},0)
and ending at
(q^{i},1)
.
By the construction of the lattice paths,
L_{i}(y)
is equal to the cardinality of the disjoint union of the sets
\{k\leq y\mid G_{k}<i\}
and
\{k>y\mid G_{k}\leq i\}
.
Both of these cardinalities are independent and follow a binomial distribution with success probability
q_{i+1}
and
q_{i}
respectively.
Together,
\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{n}L_{i}(y))
has mean
p_{i+1}(y/n)+q^{i+1}
.
This describes the desired line segment.
In addition, note that the variance of
\frac{1}{n}L_{i}(y)
is the sum of the variances of these binomial distributions, which is
O(1/\sqrt{n})
.
Thus, we obtain via triangle inequality

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}\left\lvert\frac{L_{i}(t)-p_{i+1}y-q^{i+1}n}{n}\right\rvert>\epsilon\right)\leq O(1/\sqrt{n}).

Therefore,
\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)
converges in probability to the desired line segment.

As the deviation from the expectation vanishes to 0, take
n
large enough such that w.h.p.,
L_{0},L_{1},\ldots,L_{i}
are within
\epsilon
of their corresponding line segments, where
i
satisfies
q_{i+1}<\epsilon
.
Then w.h.p., all other walks
L_{i+1},L_{i+2},\ldots
must take values less than
L_{i}(0)<q^{i+1}+\epsilon<2\epsilon
.
Thus, all lattice paths fall within
2\epsilon
of their corresponding line segment.
Therefore, w.h.p., a permutation selected under major index distribution will be arbitrarily close to the support described in the hypothesis.
∎

5.
Pattern Normality

With the establishment of a permuton, pattern density for major index distribution is known to converge as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
This section proves a stronger result: the pattern densities for a fixed pattern
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
is constant once
n\geq k
.
In addition, pattern occurrence can be shown to be asymptotically normal as well.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\pi\sim\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)
. For
\pi^{-1}
, the probability that set of indices
I=(i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k})
form the pattern
\sigma^{-1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
is

\frac{q^{\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)}}{[k]_{q}!}

Proof.

Set
\pi=\Gamma_{n}(G)
. In order for
\pi^{-1}(I)
to be order isomorphic to
\sigma^{-1}
, we need

G_{i_{\sigma(1)}}\underset{(1)}{>}G_{i_{\sigma(2)}}\underset{(2)}{>}\ldots\underset{(k-1)}{>}G_{i_{\sigma(k)}}

such that
\underset{(j)}{>}
is a strict inequality if
j\in Des(\sigma)
and weak otherwise.
The proof then follows from the proof of Theorem
1.1

∎

Let
T_{\sigma}(\pi)
denote the number of occurrences of the pattern of
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
in the permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
The expectation of
T_{\sigma}(\mathrm{Maj}(n,q))
follows from Proposition
5.1
and linearity of expectation.
Computing its variance requires tracking two copies of
\sigma
in the
\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)
.
To accomplish this, we use a variant of Stanley’s Shuffling Theorem.

Let
\pi=\pi_{1}\ldots\pi_{m}
and
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n}
be permutations on disjoint values whose union is
[m+n]
.
The
shuffle
of
\pi
and
\sigma
, denoted
\pi\shuffle\sigma
, is the set of permutations
\tau
of
[m+n]
such that
\pi
and
\sigma
appear as subsequences in
\tau
.

Proposition 5.2

(Stanley’s Shuffling Theorem, Th 1.1
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\pi
and
\sigma
be permutations on disjoint values whose union is
[m+n]
.
Then

\sum_{\tau\in\pi\shuffle\sigma}q^{\mathrm{maj}{\tau}}=\frac{[m+n]_{q}!}{[m]_{q}![n]_{q}!}q^{\mathrm{maj}(\pi)+\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)}.

Theorem 5.3
.

For
\pi\sim\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)
, then

(i)

\mathbb{E}(T_{\sigma}(\pi))=\binom{n}{k}\frac{q^{\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)}}{[k]_{q}!}=\Theta(n^{k}).

(ii)

\mathrm{Var}\left(T_{\sigma}(\pi)\right)=O(n^{2k-1}).

Proof.

Proposition
5.1
and linearity of expectation makes (i) trivial.
For (ii), we will show that the variance has no terms of order
n^{2k}
or greater.
First note that
T_{\sigma}(\pi)^{2}
is a sum of pattern occurrences
\tau
, where
\tau
can be expressed as a (not necessarily disjoint) union of two copies of
\sigma
.
As
\mathbb{E}[T_{\tau}(\pi)]=O(n^{|\tau|})
, the only
\tau
’s that contribute terms of
n^{2k}
or higher are those that have length
2k
.
Thus, we only need to consider such
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{2k}
, which must be a disjoint union of two copies of
\sigma
. By counting the number of such
\tau
and calculating the expected number of each one in
\pi
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}(T_{\sigma}(\pi)^{2})=\sum_{S\in\binom{[2k]}{k}}\sum_{\tau\in\sigma(S)\shuffle\sigma(S^{C})}\binom{n}{2k}\frac{q^{\mathrm{maj}(\tau)}}{[2k]_{q}!}+O(n^{2k-1}).

By using Stanley’s Shuffling Theorem , we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T_{\sigma}(\pi)^{2})

\displaystyle=\binom{2k}{k}\frac{[2k]_{q}!}{([k]_{q}!)^{2}}\binom{n}{2k}\frac{q^{2\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)}}{[2k]_{q}!}+O(n^{2k-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)}}{(k!)^{2}([k]_{q}!)^{2}}n^{2k}+O(n^{2k-1}).

Finally, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T_{\sigma}(\pi))^{2}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}^{2}\frac{q^{2\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)}}{([k]_{q}!)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)}}{([k]_{q}!)^{2}(k!)^{2}}n^{2k}+O(n^{2k-1}).

has the same leading term as
\mathbb{E}(T_{\sigma}(\pi)^{2})
.
Thus, the highest order term of
\mathrm{Var}(T_{\sigma}(\pi))
is at most order
n^{2k-1}
.
∎

Note that
\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{n}
is an i.i.d.​​ sequence of random variables and that pattern occurrences of
\pi^{-1}
is a U-statistic of this sequence.
Thus, Theorem
2.8
directly leads to the following corollary.

Corollary 5.4
.

Pattern occurrence for
\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)
is asymptotically normal (or degenerate).

Remark 5.5
.

As major index distribution deforms to the uniform distribution as
q\rightarrow 1
, we know that the
n^{2k-1}
term for variance can be written as a nonnegative polynomial in
q
that is not identically zero.
However, it stands to reason some combinations of
q
and
\sigma
may reduce to a lower degree polynomial in
n
.
This is the only case where the corollary above would give a degenerate distribution.

6.
Fixed points and 2-cycles

Using the lattice walk sequence defined in Section 3,
the cycle structure of major index distribution can be found.
For fixed points, this rests on two ideas.

•

Each
L_{k}
crosses the
y=x
line exactly once.

•

The number of steps that
L_{k}
stays on the
y=x
line is roughly a geometric random variable.

This is done in a more rigorous fashion below.
Recall from Section 1 that
c_{p}=c_{p}(\pi)
is the number of
p
-cycles of a permutation
\pi
, and
p_{i}=q^{i}(1-q)
.

Proposition 6.1
.

We have

\mathbb{E}[c_{1}(\mathrm{Maj}(n,q))]=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{(1-q)^{\ell-1}}{[\ell]_{q}},

\mathrm{Var}[c_{1}(\mathrm{Maj}(n,q))]\overset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{\ell(1-q)^{\ell-1}}{[\ell]_{q}}.

Proof.

Consider
G\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{n}
and
\pi=\Gamma_{n}(G)
.
We will find the distribution of fixed points for each
\mathcal{G}_{i}
.

Let
D_{i}=D_{i}(G)=\{i\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\mid\pi(i)=i\}
.
As each
L_{i}
consists of vertical and diagonal steps,
D_{i}
must be a consecutive set of integers.

Expectation:
Fix
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
k\geq 0
.
Let
A_{(k,i)}=\{G\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\mid|D_{i}|\geq k\}
.
Define
\phi_{(k,i)}:A_{(k,i)}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n-k}
by removing the smallest
k
elements of
D_{i}
.
Explicitly, if
a=\min D_{i}
, then
\phi_{k}(G)=G^{\prime}=(G^{\prime}_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{n-k}
where

G^{\prime}_{\ell}=\begin{cases}G_{\ell}&\ell<a\\
G_{\ell+k}&\ell\geq a\end{cases}

In terms of
\pi
, the elements
a,a+1,\ldots,a+k-1
are removed and all remaining integers larger than
a+k
are shifted down accordingly.
Note that both
|D^{\prime}_{i}|=|D_{i}|-k
and
\phi_{k}
is reversible.
Considering
G^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n-k}
, there is a minimal element
a
such that the lines
y=x
and
L_{i}
intersect at
(a,a)
.
Simply set

G_{\ell}=\begin{cases}G^{\prime}_{\ell}&\ell<a\\
i&a\leq\ell<a+k\\
G^{\prime}_{\ell-k}&\ell\geq a+k\end{cases}.

This produces a
G\in A_{k}
who maps to
G^{\prime}
via
\phi
.

In addition, for all
G\in A_{k}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(G\mid G\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{n}\right)=(q^{i}(1-q))^{k}\mathbb{P}\left(G^{\prime}\mid G^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{n-k}\right).

Thus,
\mathbb{P}\left(G\in A_{(k,i)}\right)=p_{i}^{k}
, and so
|D_{i}|\geq k
with probability
p_{i}^{k}
when
k\leq n
and
0
otherwise.
This implies that
|D_{i}|\sim\min(\mathrm{Geom}(1-p_{i}),n)
.

Thus, the expected number of fixed points would be

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathbb{E}[|D_{i}|]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{i}>\ell\right)=\sum_{i\geq 0}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}q^{\ell i}(1-q)^{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{(1-q)^{\ell}}{1-q^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{(1-q)^{\ell-1}}{[\ell]_{q}}.

Variance:
Fix
i<j\in\mathbb{N}
and let
k,\ell\geq 0
.
Similar to the previous argument, we will find the probability that
G
is in the set
B_{k,\ell}=A_{(k,i)}\cap A_{(\ell,j)}
. Let
\phi_{(k,i)}
be the same function as the previous argument that maps
G
into
G^{\prime}
.
As
j>i
, every element of
D_{j}(G)
is smaller than every element of
D_{i}(G)
, so we have that
D_{j}(G^{\prime})=D_{j}(G)
.
Define
\phi^{\prime}_{(\ell,j)}:\phi_{(k,i)}(B_{k,\ell})\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n-k-\ell}
be obtained by removing the
\ell
smallest elements of
D_{j}(G^{\prime})
and shifting the larger elements accordingly, just as
\phi_{(k,i)}
does.
It is clear that
\phi^{\prime}_{(\ell,j)}
is reversible by the same argument that
\phi_{(k,i)}
is reversible.
As the composition of reversible operations are reversible, we find that for all
G\in B_{k,\ell}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(G\mid G\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{n}\right)=p_{i}^{k}p_{j}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}\left(G^{\prime}\mid G^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{n-k}\right).

Explicitly, this means that the joint survival function of
|D_{i}|
and
|D_{j}|
is

S(x,y)=\mathbb{P}\left(|D_{i}|\geq k,|D_{j}|\geq\ell\right)=\begin{cases}p_{i}^{k}p_{j}^{\ell}&k+\ell\leq n\\
0&\mbox{else}\end{cases}

As
n\rightarrow\infty
, the joint survival function converges uniformly to that of two independent geometric random variables.
This establishes that
\mathrm{cov}(|D_{i}|,|D_{j}|)
vanishes as
n\rightarrow\infty
if
i\neq j
.
Using a crude bound, we bound the covariances (and variances) above by

\displaystyle|\mathrm{cov}(|D_{i}|,|D_{j}|)|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(|D_{i}|)\mathrm{Var}(|D_{j}|)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\dfrac{p_{i}}{(1-p_{i})^{2}}\dfrac{p_{j}}{(1-p_{j})^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sqrt{p_{i}p_{j}}}{(1-p_{i})(1-p_{j})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{q}^{i+j}(1-q).

Since

\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{q}^{i+j}(1-q)=(1-q)(1-\sqrt{q})^{-2}
,
we have that the covariances, when treated as a function on the domain
\mathbb{N}
, is dominated by the integrable function
g(i,j)=\sqrt{q}^{i+j}(1-q)
. Thus, by the dominated convergence theorem, we get the following

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{Var}(c_{1}(\pi))

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i,j\leq 0}\mathrm{cov}(D_{i},D_{j})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i\leq 0}\mathrm{Var}(D_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{p_{i}}{(1-p_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\ell p_{i}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\ell q^{i\ell}(1-q)^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{\ell}{(1-q^{\ell})}{(1-q)^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{\ell(1-q)^{\ell-1}}{[\ell]_{q}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

For
0<q<1
, the limit of the expectation does not meet the variance. Thus, the distribution of fixed points in
\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)
is not Poisson.

The expectation for
c_{1}
can be rewritten into the following recurrence.
Let
\mathrm{FP}(n,q)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}c_{1}(\pi)q^{\mathrm{maj}(\pi)}
.
It would be interesting to find a combinatorial interpretation for the following result.

Corollary 6.2
.

For
0<q\leq 1
and
n\geq 1
,

\mathrm{FP}(n+1,q)=[n+1]_{q}\mathrm{FP}(n,q)+\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}(1-q^{i})

For 2-cycles, a similar procedure can be followed except that a pair of runs needs to be considered instead of a single run.

Proposition 6.3
.

The expected number of 2-cycles under
\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)
is

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{q^{\ell}(1-q)^{2\ell-2}}{[\ell]_{q}[2\ell]_{q}}.

Proof.

Let
G\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{n}
.
The indices of any 2-cycle forms an inversion.
As elements of any
\mathcal{G}_{k}
form an increasing run, elements of a 2-cycle must inhabit different
\mathcal{G}_{k}
’s. For a lattice walk
L
, define its transpose
L^{T}
such that
L^{T}(b)=a
if and only if
L(a)=b
.

Fix
i,j
such that
i<j
.
On the lattice walks
L_{i}
and
L_{j}
, let
a
be the minimal integer such that there exists points
(a,b)\in L_{i}
and
(b,a)\in L_{j}
.
This can be seen by reflecting
L_{j}
across the
y=x
line to form
L_{j}^{T}
.
Furthermore,
L_{i}
takes steps with slope at least
1
and
L_{j}^{T}
takes steps with slope at most
1
.
So, the intersection set between
L_{i}
and
L_{j}^{T}
are connected by consecutive diagonal steps.
Let
D_{i,j}
denote the number of consecutive diagonal steps necessary to form the intersection set (alternatively, this is one less than the size of the intersection set).
We claim that
D_{i,j}\sim\min(\mathrm{Geom}(1-p_{i}p_{j}),\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor)
.

Let
A_{k}=\{G\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\lvert D_{i,j}\geq k\}
.
Define the function
\phi_{k}:A_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n-2k}
by removing the first
k
diagonal steps counted by
D_{i,j}
.
Explicitly,
A_{k}(G)=G^{\prime}=(G^{\prime}_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{n-2k}
where

G^{\prime}_{\ell}=\begin{cases}G_{\ell},&\ell\leq b\\
G_{\ell+k},&b<\ell\leq a-k\\
G_{\ell+2k},&a-k<\ell\leq n-2k\end{cases}.

Let
a^{\prime}
be the minimal integer such that there exist a point
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in L^{\prime}_{i}\cap L_{j}^{{}^{\prime}T}
.
It can be shown that
(a^{\prime},b)=(a^{\prime}-k,b)
by noting the following.
Removing the diagonal steps from
L_{i}
caused the point
(b,a)\in L_{j}
to be shifted to
(b,a-k)\in L^{\prime}_{j}
.
Removing the diagonal steps from
L_{j}
caused the point
(a,b)\in L_{i}
to be shifted to
(a-k,b)\in L^{\prime}_{i}
.
Since
L^{\prime}_{i}\cap L_{j}^{T^{\prime}}
is connected by diagonal steps, any intersection before
(a^{\prime},b)
here would imply an intersection earlier than
(a,b)
in
L_{i}\cap L_{j}^{T}
.
Thus,
a
and
b
can be found from the image alone.
Therefore,
\phi_{k}
is reversible.

Finally, note that for
2k\leq n

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{ij}\geq k\right)=\sum_{G\in A_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(G)=\sum_{G\in A_{k}}q^{ik}(1-q)^{k}q^{jk}(1-q)^{k}\mathbb{P}_{n}(\phi_{k}{G})=q^{(i+j)k}(1-q)^{2k},

and 0 otherwise.
This proves the earlier claim about the distribution of
D_{i,j}
. The expected number of 2-cycles can then be obtained by linearity of expectation

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{E}(D_{ij})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{ij}\geq\ell\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}q^{(i+j)\ell}(1-q)^{2\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(1-q)^{2\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{n}q^{i\ell}\frac{q^{(i+1)\ell}}{1-q^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(1-q)^{2\ell}\frac{q^{\ell}}{(1-q^{2\ell})(1-q^{\ell})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{q^{\ell}(1-q)^{2\ell-2}}{[\ell]_{q}[2\ell]_{q}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

7.
q-Plancherel Measure

To recover the main result in
[
8
]
, recall that
\lambda(\pi)=(\lambda_{1}(\pi),\lambda_{2}(\pi),\ldots)
were defined such that
\lambda_{1}(\pi)+\lambda_{2}(\pi)+\ldots+\lambda_{i}(\pi)
is the maximum total length amongst all
i
-tuples of disjoint increasing subsequences.
In essence, our argument will rely on the fact that the rightmost runs of a permutation under
\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)
is dense enough that using anything other than the rightmost runs is inefficient in the limit.
The following lemma will help leverage this idea.

Lemma 7.1
.

Fix
q\in(0,1)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
let
G=(G_{j})_{j=1}^{m}\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{m}
.

(i)

Among all
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, the
k
largest sets are
\mathcal{G}_{0},\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k-1}
with probability
1-O(e^{-m})
.

(ii)

The expected number of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
that are nonempty is
O(\log(m))
.

Proof.

Fix
q
and
k
as above.

(i) Let
P
be any value strictly between
p_{k-1}
and
p_{k}
.
We will show that with high likelihood,
\mathcal{G}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k-1}
have cardinalities larger than
Pm
while all other
\mathcal{G}_{i}
’s have cardinalities smaller than
Pm
.

Each
|\mathcal{G}_{i}|
is a binomial distribution with
n
trials with success rate
p_{i}

For
i\geq k
, it follows from the Chernoff bounds on the
\mathcal{G}_{i}
’s and elementary calculus that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{G}_{i}|>Pm\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left(\frac{p_{i}}{P}\right)^{P}\left(\frac{1-p_{i}}{1-P}\right)^{1-P}\right)^{m}<1.

Let
C_{i}=\left(\frac{p_{i}}{P}\right)^{P}\left(\frac{1-p_{i}}{1-P}\right)^{1-P}
. Observe that

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C_{i+1}}{C_{i}}=q^{P}<1.

So,
\sum_{i\geq k}C_{i}<\infty
, and there exists a
j\geq k
such that
\sum_{i\geq k}C_{i}<0.5
.
Thus, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq k}C_{i}^{m}

\displaystyle=C_{k}^{m}+C_{k+1}^{m}+\ldots+C_{j-1}^{m}+\sum_{i\geq j}C_{i}^{m}

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}^{m}+C_{k+1}^{m}+\ldots+C_{j-1}^{m}+\left(\sum_{i\geq j}C_{i}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}^{m}+C_{k+1}^{m}+\ldots+C_{j-1}^{m}+0.5^{m}

\displaystyle=O(e^{-m}).

For the case of
i<k
, we can use the identity
\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{G}_{i}|<Pm\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(m-|\mathcal{G}_{i}|>(1-P)m\right)
and get a similar result.

(ii) Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\#\{\mathcal{G}_{k}\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}_{k}\neq\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\min(\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{G}_{k}),1)

\displaystyle=|\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid mq^{k}(1-q)\geq 1\}|+\sum_{mq^{k}(1-q)<1}mq^{k}(1-q)

\displaystyle\leq\log_{q}(m(1-q))+\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}q^{\ell}

\displaystyle=O(\log(m)).

This completes the proof.
∎

Even with the rigidity from the limit shape of the major index distribution, increasing subsequences will stray off from their expected
\mathcal{G}_{i}
for a small segment.
Though this deviation is of a negligible order, it does make calculations less tractable.
So, we create a more tractable class of subsequences that generalize increasing subsequences.

Let
\pi=\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)=\Gamma_{n}(G)
.
Let a
net

\mathcal{A}
of
n
be any set partition of
[n]
into contiguous subsets
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{m}
.
Let the
inner/outer width
of
\mathcal{A}
be the smallest/largest cardinality of the parts of
\mathcal{A}
.
An (entry-wise) subsequence
\tau
of
\pi
is
blocked with respect to
\mathcal{A}
if the following properties holds:

(1)

For all
i\in[m]
,
\tau
when restricted to
A_{i}
is an increasing subsequence.

(2)

Let
k=|\{j\in\mathbb{N}\mid\mathcal{G}_{j}>0\}|
. With at most
k
exceptions, for
i\in[m]
, there exists
j_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
\mathcal{G}_{j}\cap A_{i}=\tau\cap A_{i}
.

In other words, a subsequence blocked with respect to
\mathcal{A}
is effectively
\mathcal{G}_{j}
when restricted to some
A_{i}
in all but a small number of cases dependent on
G
.
We will call these exceptional
A_{i}

bad blocks
.
In the case where
\mathcal{A}
is unambiguous, we will call
\tau
a
blocked
subsequence.
Note that due to the geometry of
\mathrm{Maj}(n,q)
, an increasing subsequence can only “jump” between
\mathcal{G}
’s at most
k
times.
Thus, increasing subsequences are blocked with respect to
\mathcal{A}
, regardless of the choice of
\mathcal{A}
.
As a result, the length of the longest blocked subsequence is weakly larger than that of the longest increasing subsequence.
As such, define
\lambda^{*}(G)=(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G),\lambda_{2}^{*}(G),\ldots)
such that
\lambda_{\leq i}^{*}(G)=\lambda_{1}^{*}(G)+\lambda_{2}^{*}(G)+\ldots+\lambda_{i}^{*}(G)
to be the maximum length of
i
disjoint blocked (w.r.t.
\mathcal{A}
) subsequences of
\pi=\Gamma(G)
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Fix
0<q<1
and let
G=(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{n})~\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}
be a fixed sequence of nets such that the inner and outer width of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is
\Theta(n^{\epsilon})
for
0<\epsilon<1
.
For all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\lambda_{\leq i}^{*}(G)-|\mathcal{G}_{<i}|\right)^{k}\right]=O(n^{\epsilon k}\log(n)^{k}).

Proof.

Fix
q
,
k
,
\epsilon
, and
i
as above.
As
\mathcal{G}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{i-1}
is an
i
-tuple of disjoint blocked subsequences,
\lambda_{\leq i}^{*}(G)-|\mathcal{G}_{\leq i-1}|\geq 0
.
Thus, we only need to bound the moments above.
Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
be the parts of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
Necessarily,
m=\Theta(n^{1-\epsilon})
.
Let
\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}(G)
be a set of elements of
[n]
such that
\tau_{i}
can be partitioned into
i
disjoint set of blocked subsequences of
\pi
that witness
\lambda_{\leq i}^{*}(G)
.
Fix this
i
-disjoint set as
T
.
Let
|\tau_{i}|_{j}=|\tau_{i}\cap A_{j}|
and define
|\mathcal{G}_{<i}|_{j}
similarly.
Observe that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\lambda_{\leq i}^{*}(G)-|\mathcal{G}_{\leq i-1}|\right)^{k}\right]=\sum_{1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\leq m}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(|\tau_{i}|_{j_{\ell}}-|\mathcal{G}_{\leq i-1}|_{j_{\ell}})\right].

Typically,
\lambda^{*}_{<j}(G)=|\mathcal{G}_{\leq j-1}|
in all but two cases.
The first is when the runs
\mathcal{G}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{i-1}
are not the
i
largest runs after being intersected with
B_{j_{\ell}}
, which can happen with probability
O(e^{-n^{\epsilon}})
by Lemma
7.1
.
The second is when
A_{j}
is a bad block for some element of
T
.
This gives us

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(|\tau_{i}|_{j_{\ell}}-|\mathcal{G}_{\leq i-1}|_{j_{\ell}})\right]\leq\sum_{K\subseteq[k]}P(K)O(n^{k\epsilon}e^{-(k-|K|)n^{\epsilon}}),

where
P(K)
is the probability that
A_{j_{\ell}}
is a bad block for each
\ell\in K
. Note that
j_{\ell}
’s need not be distinct.

By Lemma
7.1
, we have that
\sum_{j=1}^{m}P(j)\leq O(\log n)
.
Thus, we have an upper bound for summing up all
\ell
-tuples of distinct indices

\sum_{K\subseteq[m],|K|=\ell}P(K)\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}P(j)\right)^{\ell}=O(\log(n)^{k}).

Among
k
-tuples of indices with repeated elements, each repeated element loses a factor of
\log
and gains a factor that depends only on
k
. As
k
is fixed, this term does not contribute towards the leading asymptotic.
Thus, adding up all expectations together, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\lambda_{\leq i}^{*}(G)-|\mathcal{G}_{<i}|\right)^{k}\right]=O(n^{\epsilon k}\log(n)^{k}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we can recover the main result from
[
8
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be distributed under major index law.
That is,
\pi=\Gamma_{n}(G)
for
G\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)^{n}
.
Set
k
and
i
as in the earlier theorem.
By the definition of increasing subsequences and blocked subsequences, we have

(7.1)

|\mathcal{G}_{<i}|\leq\lambda_{\leq i}(\pi)\leq\lambda_{\leq i}^{*}(G)

As
\lambda_{i}(\pi)=\lambda_{\leq i}(\pi)-\lambda_{\leq i-1}(\pi)
(where we treat
\lambda_{-1}(\pi)=0
), we can bound the quantity as follows

(7.2)

\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{<i}|-\lambda_{\leq i-1}^{*}(G)}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{i}(\pi)}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\leq i}^{*}(G)-|\mathcal{G}_{<i-1}|}{\sqrt{n}}.

Using Proposition
7.2
with
\epsilon<0.5
, we have by methods of moments that for all
k
,

(7.3)

\lim_{n}\dfrac{|\mathcal{G}_{<k}|}{\sqrt{n}}=\lim_{n}\dfrac{\lambda_{\leq k}^{*}(G)}{\sqrt{n}}

Note that since
|\mathcal{G}_{<j}|
is a binomial distribution with fixed success rate,
\lim_{n}\dfrac{|\mathcal{G}_{<k}|}{\sqrt{n}}
is the normal distribution with expectation
\sum_{j<k}q^{j}(1-q)
.
Finally, taking the limit of
7.2
and shifting the mean by
p_{i-1}
, we can conclude that

(7.4)

Y_{i}=\lim_{n}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}(\pi)-p_{i-1}}{\sqrt{n}}=\lim_{n}\dfrac{|\mathcal{G}_{i-1}|-p_{i-1}}{\sqrt{n}}=\mathcal{N}\left(0,p_{i-1}(1-p_{i-1})\right)

This covers
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]
and
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]
.
For the covariance, observe that we can also take the limit of
Y_{i}+Y_{j}
.

(7.5)

Y_{i}+Y_{j}=\lim_{n}\dfrac{|\mathcal{G}_{i-1}|+|\mathcal{G}_{j-1}|-p_{i-1}-p_{j-1}}{\sqrt{n}}=\mathcal{N}\left(0,(p_{i-1}+p_{j-1}(1-p_{i-1}-p_{j-1})\right)

So, the the covariance would be

(7.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}(Y_{i},Y_{j})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Var}(Y_{i}+Y_{j})-\mathrm{Var}(Y_{i})-\mathrm{Var}(Y_{j})\right)

(7.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((p_{i-1}+p_{j-1})(1-p_{i-1}-p_{j-1})-p_{i-1}(1-p_{i-1})-p_{j-1}(1-p_{j-1})\right)

(7.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(-p_{i-1}p_{j-1}-p_{j-1}p_{i-1}\right)

(7.9)

\displaystyle=-(1-q)^{2}q^{i+j-2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

8.
Future directions

Due to the simplicity of the sampler, it is natural to ask what if
\mathrm{Geom}(1-q)
is replaced with a different distribution.
Only distributions with some discreteness will give nontrivial results as any continuous distribution can be reduce to the uniform distribution on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Of note, the case of uniform discrete distributions is effectively the study of random words of
n
letters with a fixed alphabet.
In the case that the size of the alphabet is of
\Theta(n^{\alpha})
, this has been studied from the angle of representation theory for
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
in
[
1
]
and
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
in
[
8
]
.

Another direction to take this is to have
q
change with
n
.
Similar research of the longest increasing subsequence has been done with Mallows distribution by
[
15
,
18
]
.
Based off of how Mallows developed, the next natural setting is to take
q=1-\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
for
\alpha>0
.

Conjecture 8.1
.

For
q=1-\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
for
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\lim_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\mathrm{LIS}(\mathrm{Maj}(n,q))}{n^{1-\alpha}}\right]=(1-q).

The proof outline in Section 7 is expected to work, contingent on a modification of Lemma
7.1
holding.

For
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, it is expected that the disparity between the
|\mathcal{G}_{k}|
are no longer relevant and the behavior is similar to that of the uniform distribution.

Conjecture 8.2
.

For
q=1-\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\lim_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\mathrm{LIS}(\mathrm{Maj}(n,q))}{\sqrt{n}})\right]=2.

The true interest lies within
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, where both the disparity of
|\mathcal{G}_{k}|
and the uniform measure behavior are present.
