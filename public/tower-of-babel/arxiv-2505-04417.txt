Localized Diffusion Models
Georg A. Gottwald, Shuigen Liu, Youssef Marzouk, Sebastian Reich, Xin T. Tong

Abstract
Diffusion models are state-of-the-art tools for various generative tasks. Yet training these models involves estimating high-dimensional score functions, a task that in principle suffers from the curse of dimensionality. It is therefore important to understand how low-dimensional structure in the target distribution can be exploited in these models. Here we consider locality structure , which describes certain sparse conditional dependencies among the target random variables. Given some locality structure, the score function is effectively low-dimensional, so that it can be estimated by a localized neural network with significantly reduced sample complexity. This observation motivates the localized diffusion model , where a localized score matching loss is used to train the score function within a localized hypothesis space. We prove that such localization enables diffusion models to circumvent the curse of dimensionality with dimension-independent error bounds, at the price of additional localization error. Under realistic sample size scaling, we then show both theoretically and numerically that a moderate localization radius can balance the statistical and localization errors, yielding better overall performance. Locality structure also facilitates parallel training, making localized diffusion models potentially more efficient for large-scale applications.

Introduction

Over the past decade, numerous neural network (NN)-based sampling algorithms have emerged in the machine learning literature, demonstrating remarkable performance in varied applications. These methods, often referred to as generative models, include normalizing flows
[
46
]
, variational auto-encoders
[
31
]
, generative adversarial networks
[
23
]
, and diffusion models
[
49
,
27
,
50
]
.
Among generative models, diffusion models (DMs)—a term usually referring to “denoising diffusion probabilistic models”
[
27
]
—are a state-of-the-art and widely used approach.
They have gained great popularity due to their ability to generate high-quality samples, particularly in tasks such as image synthesis
[
27
,
16
]
.
Several recent studies
[
35
,
10
,
5
,
43
,
8
,
58
]
theoretically justify the approximation and generalization capabilities of DMs over a broad class of target distributions.
However, our understanding of their effectiveness in representing high-dimensional distributions remains limited.

DMs are known to be expensive when training with high-dimensional data.
The training sample size needs to grow exponentially with the problem dimension
[
52
,
43
]
, a scaling known as the
curse of dimensionality
(CoD) in the literature.
Various attempts have been made to avoid this scaling by leveraging low-dimensional structure within the target distribution.
The
manifold hypothesis

[
19
]
, which postulates that the data lie on a low-dimensional manifold, is often invoked in these settings.
For such data,
[
43
,
8
,
54
,
1
]
show that the sample complexity of DMs depends on the dimension of the manifold rather than the ambient dimension, and that DMs can thus avoid the CoD with appropriate NN structure.
There are also studies considering Gaussian mixtures
[
48
,
60
,
21
]
to avoid the CoD.
In both the manifold hypothesis and Gaussian mixture models, although the ambient space is high-dimensional, there is a low-dimensional latent structure that effectively characterizes the target distribution.

While these concepts of low “effective dimension” cover many applications, there are still important cases left open.
One large class of high-dimensional distributions consists of those with
locality structure

[
6
,
55
,
20
,
15
]
.
We say a joint distribution has locality structure if each coordinate random variable
has strong conditional dependence only on a small subset of the other coordinates.
Such sparse dependence structure arises naturally in spatial models, and has been successfully applied in various fields such as spatial statistics
[
6
]
, data assimilation
[
45
]
, quantum mechanics
[
32
]
, and sampling
[
55
]
.
We refer to
[
15
]
for a detailed review on locality structure.

An important property of distributions with locality structure, or
localized distributions
, is that their score functions are effectively low-dimensional
[
55
,
15
]
.
Due to conditional independence, the
j
th component of the score function,
s_{j}(x)=\nabla_{j}\log p(x)
, depends only on
x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}}
, where
x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}}
contains the components conditionally dependent on
x_{j}
.
If the conditional dependencies are sparse, the dimension of
x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}}
is much smaller than the ambient dimension
D
, so that the score function can be regarded as a collection of low-dimensional functions
\{s_{j}\}_{j=1}^{D}
. This suggests that learning the score functions of localized distributions does not suffer from the CoD.

Motivated by this, we propose the
localized diffusion model
, which embeds the locality structure into the hypothesis space of the score function, reducing a high-dimensional score matching problem to a low-dimensional one.
With small effective dimension, the statistical error of score estimation is significantly reduced.
On the other hand, localizing the hypothesis space introduces additional localization error.
Through a complete approximation and generalization analysis, we show that by adjusting the localization radius, one can balance the trade-off between the statistical error and the localization error to achieve smaller overall error.
This can be interpreted as a trade-off between variance and bias. Such a trade-off is validated by numerical experiments on high-dimensional time series data.
Finally, we find that the localized diffusion models can be interpreted as a collection of diffusion models on low-dimensional marginals. That is, we construct the samplers by combining local samplers for the marginals of the localized distributions.
This allows the localized diffusion models to be trained in parallel, which is practically important for large-scale applications.

The paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

2
, we review diffusion models and the locality structure, and show that the locality structure is approximately preserved in the forward diffusion process.
In
Section

3
, we introduce the localized diffusion model and analyze its approximation and statistical error.
We also discuss how to modify the localized diffusion model for mixture distributions.
In
Section

4
, we present numerical experiments to validate our theoretical results.

Related work

1.1.1
Analysis of diffusion models

Since the introduction of DMs
[
49
,
27
,
50
]
, there has been a surge of interest in understanding their theoretical properties.
Our work is built on two main lines of research: the convergence of DMs and the statistical analysis of DMs.
A comprehensive review of all related work is beyond the scope of this paper; we refer to
[

,
21
]
for an in-depth overview.

The convergence analysis of DMs studies error bounds for the sampled distribution given the learned score function.
Early work
[
35
]
provides a TV guarantee by assuming a log-Sobolev inequality.
Using the Girsanov theorem, later work relaxes this condition to bounded moment conditions
[

,
7
]

A growing body of work seeks to further relax assumptions and improve error bounds.
For instance,
[

[
12
]
uses a relative score approach and derives bounds without early stopping; and
[
44
]
considers manifold data and improves the bound of the discretization error to scale linearly with the manifold dimension.

The statistical analysis of DMs essentially studies the sample complexity of estimating the score function.

[
43
,
58
]
prove that the diffusion model reaches the minimax rate for distribution estimation.
To avoid the CoD,
[
43
,
8
]
consider linear subspace data, and later
[
54
,
1

extend it to general manifold data.
Recently,
[
59
]
relaxes the manifold assumption and improves the ambient dimension dependence in the generalization bound.
Other types of low-dimensional structures are also considered.

[
48
]
considers certain Gaussian mixtures, and shows that the sample complexity does not depend exponentially on the dimension.
[
21
]
further extends it to general Gaussian mixtures with edited diffusion models.
See
[
51
]
for a comprehensive review on the statistical analysis of DMs.

Recently,
[
34
,
18
]
show that diffusion-model estimation can avoid the CoD for factorizable densities.
The class of factorizable densities is broader than the localized distributions considered here, but their results focus on the original diffusion models and study how sparse networks can be used to efficiently estimate the score function.
In contrast, we consider the design and analysis of diffusion models tailored to the locality structure, and establish dimension-independent error bounds for low-dimensional marginals, which are not provided in
[
34
,
18
]
.

[
39
]
studies a similar high-dimensional graphical model setting and proves that diffusion models can avoid the CoD. However, the analysis relies on an explicit solution of a denoising algorithm used to approximate the score function, and is restricted to Ising-type models. Our method applies more broadly to general high-dimensional graphical models.

Locality in diffusion models has also been studied from a different perspective, i.e., as a mechanism for “creativity” or generalization of DMs.

[
30
,
42
]
suggest that the inductive bias of localized denoisers, arising from architectures such as convolutional neural networks, may enable DMs to generalize rather than memorize.
However, their localized denoisers are introduced to explain this phenomenon, not to improve sample complexity, and their focus is empirical rather than rigorous numerical or statistical analysis.

[
38

argues that locality emerges from the data statistics rather than from the inductive bias of the architecture.
They provide empirical evidence that the learned score exhibits locality across different architectures, which is consistent with our main theoretical results on localized diffusion models.

Localized samplers

In recent years, there has been growing interest in sampling methods that leverage locality structures

,

,

. These localized samplers follow the general strategy of building samplers by combining local samplers for the marginals.

2
Diffusion models and localized distributions

2.1
Diffusion models

Diffusion models operate by simulating a process that gradually transforms a simple initial distribution, often Gaussian noise, into a complex target distribution, which represents the data of interest.
The core formulation involves two processes: a forward diffusion process which evolves data samples from the data distribution
p_{0}
to noisy samples, and a reverse diffusion process that learns to progressively denoise the samples and effectively reconstruct the original data distribution.

Consider a forward Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process
(X(t))_{t\in[0,\overline{T}]}
that is initialized with the target distribution
p_{0}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{d}X(t)=-X(t)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad X(0)\sim p_{0}.

(2.1)

Here
W(t)
is a standard Brownian motion.
Let
(Y(t))_{t\in[0,\overline{T}]}
denote its reverse process, so that
Y(t)=X(\overline{T}-t)
. Under mild conditions,
Y(t)
follows the reverse SDE
[
50
]

\mathrm{d}Y(t)=\left(Y(t)+2\nabla\log p_{\overline{T}-t}(Y(t))\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}(t),\quad Y(0)\sim p_{\overline{T}},

(2.2)

where we denote
p_{t}=\mathsf{Law}(X(t))
. The target distribution
p_{0}
can then be sampled by first sampling
Y(0)\sim p_{\overline{T}}
and then evolving
Y(t)
according to (
2.2
) to obtain a sample
Y(\overline{T})\sim p_{0}
.

To implement the above scheme, several approximations are needed:

•

Score estimation. The score function
s(x,t):=\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
is not accessible, and needs to be estimated from the data via the denoising score matching scheme
[
57
,
49
,
27
]

\widehat{s}=\argmin_{s_{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(s_{\theta}),

(2.3)

\mathcal{L}(s_{\theta}):=\int_{0}^{\overline{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{0}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x(t)\sim p_{t|0}(x(t)|x)}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(x(t),t)-\nabla_{x(t)}\log p_{t|0}(x(t)|x)\right\|^{2}\right]\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(2.4)

Here
p_{t|0}(\cdot|x)
denotes the conditional distribution of
X(t)
given
X(0)=x
.
In the sampling process, the true score
\nabla\log p_{\overline{T}-t}
in (
2.2
) is approximated by the estimated score
\widehat{s}(\cdot,\overline{T}-t)
.

•

Approximation of
p_{\overline{T}}
. The initial distribution
p_{\overline{T}}
in the reverse process is intractable. But since the OU process converges exponentially to
p_{\infty}=\mathsf{N}(0,I)
, we can approximate
p_{\overline{T}}
by
\mathsf{N}(0,I)
in (
2.2
).

•

Early stopping. The reverse process is usually stopped at
t=\overline{T}-\underline{T}
for some small
\underline{T}>0
to avoid potential blow up of the score function
s(t)
as
t\to 0
. The early stopped samples satisfy
Y(\overline{T}-\underline{T})\sim p_{\underline{T}}
, which is close to
p_{0}
when
\underline{T}
is small.

•

Time discretization. The Euler-Maruyama scheme is used to discretize (
2.2
). Pick time steps
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=\overline{T}-\underline{T}
, and evolve
n=0,1,\dots,N-1
by

Y(t_{n+1})=Y(t_{n})+\Delta t_{n}\left(Y(t_{n})+2\widehat{s}(Y(t_{n}),\overline{T}-t_{n})\right)+\sqrt{2\Delta t_{n}}\xi_{n},

(2.5)

where
\Delta t_{n}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
and
\xi_{n}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)
. Design of the time steps (the schedule) is crucial for the empirical performance of the sampling process.

Note the OU process admits an explicit transition kernel

p_{t|0}(x(t)|x)=\mathsf{N}(x(t);\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I),\quad\alpha_{t}:=\mathrm{e}^{-t},\quad\sigma_{t}:=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}.

(2.6)

Thus
\nabla_{x(t)}\log p_{t|0}(x(t)|x)=-\sigma_{t}^{-2}(x(t)-\alpha_{t}x)
, and
p_{t|0}(x(t)|x)
can be realized as

x(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm d}}{{=}}\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t),\quad\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I).

(2.7)

Here
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution.
Thus the denoising score matching loss (
2.4
) can be written as

\mathcal{L}(s_{\theta})=\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t),t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t,

(2.8)

where we introduce the early stopping truncation
\underline{T}
.
The above loss provides a convenient form for implementation
[
27
]
.

2.2
Locality structure

We will use the undirected graphical model
[
36
,
33
]
, also known as Markov random field, to describe the locality structure.
In this model, the conditional dependencies of a collection of random variables are encoded in the underlying dependency graph.
Thus, the sparsity of the graph can be used to characterize the locality structure in the joint distribution of these random variables.
We will define the localized and approximately localized distributions based on the dependency graph.

2.2.1
Sparse graphical models

Following
[
15
]
, consider an undirected graph
\mathsf{G}=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
and an associated random variable

X=(X_{i})_{i\in\mathsf{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},\quad X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}},\quad D=\sum_{i\in\mathsf{V}}d_{i}.

(2.9)

Here we assume that the dimension of each component
d_{i}
is small, but the total dimension
D
is large. We say
X
has
dependency graph

\mathsf{G}
if, for any nonadjacent vertices
i,j\in\mathsf{V}
,
X_{i},X_{j}
are conditionally independent given the rest of the components
(X_{k})_{k\neq i,j}
, i.e.,

X_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp X_{j}\mid(X_{k})_{k\neq i,j}.

(2.10)

X
is called a sparse graphical model if the dependency graph
\mathsf{G}
is sparse, which essentially encodes the sparse local dependencies in
X
.
The following equivalent characterization
[
55
]
of the sparse graphical models will be crucial. Let
p=\mathsf{Law}(X)
. If
p(x)
is twice differentiable, then (
2.10
) is equivalent to

\forall\text{ nonadjacent }i,j\in\mathsf{V}~\Rightarrow~\nabla_{ij}^{2}\log p(x)=0.

(2.11)

Let
b=|\mathsf{V}|
, and attach each vertex in
\mathsf{V}
with a unique index
j\in[b]
. Denote

\mathcal{N}_{j}:=\{i\in\mathsf{V}:(i,j)\in\mathsf{E}\}

(2.12)

as the neighboring vertices of
j
. For simplicity, we require that
\mathsf{E}
includes all the self-loops in
\mathsf{G}
; i.e.,
j\in\mathcal{N}_{j}
. We further define the extended neighborhood of
j
as

\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}=\{i\in\mathsf{V}:\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{G}}(i,j)\leq r\},

(2.13)

where
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{G}}(i,j)
is the graph path distance between
i,j\in\mathsf{V}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{G}}(i,j)=\min\{n\geq 0:\exists~\text{path of length }n\text{ from }i\text{ to }j\}.

(2.14)

2.2.2
Localized distributions

Now we define localized and approximately localized distributions. The former corresponds precisely to sparse graphical models, and the latter is a relaxation based on (
2.11
), which allows exponentially small long-range dependencies.

Definition 2.1
.

A distribution
p
is called localized w.r.t. an undirected graph
\mathsf{G}
if it satisfies (
2.10
). A distribution
p
is called approximately localized w.r.t.
\mathsf{G}
, if there exist dimension-independent constants
c_{p},C_{p}>0
such that

\left\|\nabla_{ij}^{2}\log p\right\|_{\infty}\leq C_{p}\exp\left(-c_{p}\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{G}}(i,j)\right).

(2.15)

Here
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
denotes the
L^{\infty}
-norm, and
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{G}}(i,j)
is the graph distance (
2.14
).

For a localized distribution, consider the
j
-th component of its score function

s_{j}(x)=\nabla_{j}\log p(x).

(2.16)

Note that it is only a function of
x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}}
, since by (
2.11
), for any
i\notin\mathcal{N}_{j}
,

\nabla_{i}s_{j}(x)=\nabla^{2}_{ij}\log p(x)=0.

(2.17)

For a sparse graph
\mathsf{G}
,
|\mathcal{N}_{j}|\ll|\mathsf{V}|
, so that
s_{j}
is essentially a low-dimensional function, which implies that estimating
s_{j}
does not suffer from the curse of dimensionality. This motivates us to leverage the locality structure in the hypothesis space of the score function and to localize the score matching procedure. The detailed methods will be discussed in
Section

3
.

However, the low dimensionality of the score functions only holds for localized distributions. For approximately localized distributions, the score functions can only be approximated by low-dimensional functions. To improve the approximation accuracy, we can use the expanded neighborhood (
2.13
) for the approximate scores, i.e.,

s_{j}(x)\approx s_{\theta,j}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}).

(2.18)

Here
r
is the radius of the neighborhood and can be tuned to balance the approximation accuracy and the sample complexity. Note by (
2.15
), the approximation error decays exponentially with the radius
r
, while the dimension of
s_{\theta,j}
only grows polynomially with
r
.
Section

3
will provide a detailed analysis of the approximation error and the trade-off in the choice of
r
.

In this work, we assume that the dependency graph, or at least a simple neighborhood structure, is available
a priori
.
This is natural in many applications with spatial or temporal structure, as in standard localization methods in data assimilation
[
28
,
26
]
, where neighborhoods are often prescribed by geometry or time ordering.
When such prior structure is unavailable, one could first estimate the graph using existing graph learning methods
[
17
,
40
,
3
,
37
]
and then apply our method based on the learned graph.
We do not pursue this direction in this paper.

2.3
Locality structure in diffusion models

We show in this section that the locality structure is preserved in the forward OU process, which lays the foundation for the localized score matching in diffusion models.

The explicit transition kernel (
2.6
) of the OU process implies that
p_{t}
has an explicit density

p_{t}(y)=\int\mathsf{N}(y;\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I)p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

(2.19)

p_{t}
can be viewed as an interpolation between
p_{0}
and
p_{\infty}=\mathsf{N}(0,I)
. Suppose
p_{0}
is a localized distribution w.r.t. an undirected graph
\mathsf{G}
.
The limit distribution
p_{\infty}
is localized, but the interpolation
p_{t}
may not remain strictly localized. However,
p_{t}
is still approximately localized, as proved in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose
p_{0}
is localized w.r.t. an undirected graph
\mathsf{G}
. Assume additionally that
p_{0}
is log-concave and smooth, i.e.,
\exists 0<m\leq M<\infty
such that
mI\preceq-\nabla^{2}\log p_{0}(x)\preceq MI
. Then for any
t\in(0,\overline{T}]
,
p_{t}
is approximately localized w.r.t.
\mathsf{G}
. Specifically, for any
i\neq j
,

\left\|\nabla_{ij}^{2}\log p_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}\left(m\sigma_{t}^{2}+\alpha_{t}^{2}\right)}\left(1-\frac{m\sigma_{t}^{2}+\alpha_{t}^{2}}{M\sigma_{t}^{2}+\alpha_{t}^{2}}\right)^{\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{G}}(i,j)}.

(2.20)

Here
\alpha_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-t}
and
\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}
(cf. (
2.6
)), and
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{G}}(i,j)
is the graph distance (
2.14
).

See
Section

C.1
for the proof. The first step is to show that when
i\neq j
,

\nabla_{ij}^{2}\log p_{t}(y)=\alpha_{t}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{-4}\mathsf{Cov}_{p_{0|t}(x|y)}\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right).

(2.21)

Here
p_{0|t}(\cdot|y)
is the conditional distribution of
X(0)
given
X(t)=y
.
The bound then directly follows
Proposition

2.2
below, which establishes the exponential decay of correlations between
x_{i},x_{j}
w.r.t. their graph distance
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{G}}(i,j)
for localized distributions. This is a ubiquitous property for distributions with locality structure
[
32
,
45
,
15
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

In
Theorem

2.1
, log-concavity is used through
Proposition

2.2
to obtain the exponential decay of correlations with explicit quantitative control.
We expect similar locality properties to hold beyond the log-concave setting, but establishing such results would require different assumptions and a more delicate analysis, which is outside the scope of this paper.
In
Section

3.4
, we discuss a special case of non-log-concave distributions, where the distribution is a mixture of localized distributions.
We show that the score function can still be approximated by low-dimensional functions effectively.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose
p
is localized w.r.t. an undirected graph
\mathsf{G}
and is log-concave and smooth, i.e.,
\exists 0<m\leq M<\infty
such that
mI\preceq-\nabla^{2}\log p(x)\preceq MI
. Then for any
i,j
and Lipschitz functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}\to\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

\left|\mathsf{Cov}_{p(x)}\left(f(x_{i}),g(x_{j})\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{m}\left(1-\frac{m}{M}\right)^{\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{G}}(i,j)}\left|f\right|_{\rm Lip}\left|g\right|_{\rm Lip}.

(2.22)

See
Section

C.2
for the proof.

Remark 2.2
.

We note that the condition number
\kappa:=M/m
of typical localized distributions is independent of the dimension
D
.
This is in contrast to the distributions satisfying the manifold hypothesis, where the condition number typically grows with the ambient dimension
D
.
The key difference is how the dependence structure scales with the dimension.
See
Section

A.1
for an illustrative example.

3
Localized diffusion models

3.1
Localized denoising score matching

3.1.1
Localized hypothesis space

To exploit locality structure in diffusion models, we introduce the localized hypothesis space

\mathscr{H}_{r}=\left\{s_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{D+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}\mid s_{\theta,j}(x,t)=u_{\theta,j}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},t),~u_{\theta,j}\in\mathscr{U}_{j},~j\in[b]\right\},

(3.1)

where
r
denotes the localization radius,
\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}
is the extended neighborhood (
2.13
), and
\mathscr{U}_{j}
is the hypothesis space for the
j
-th component of the score function.
Here we use
s_{\theta,j}(\cdot,t)
with an extended neighborhood to approximate the score function of
p_{t}
in light of
Theorem

2.1
.
In practice,
\mathscr{H}_{r}
can be realized by a neural network (NN) with locality constraints; for instance, we can take each
\mathscr{U}_{j}
as a sparse ReLU NN introduced in
Section

A.2
.

Define the
effective dimension
of
s_{\theta}
as

d_{\rm eff}:=\max_{j\in[b]}d_{j,r},\quad d_{j,r}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}d_{i}.

(3.2)

Since
s_{\theta}(\cdot,t)
can be viewed as a collection of functions
\{u_{\theta,j}(\cdot,t):\mathbb{R}^{d_{j,r}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}\}_{j\in[b]}
, it is essentially a function of
d_{\rm eff}
variables. For a sparse graph,
d_{\rm eff}\ll D
, so intuitively estimating
s_{\theta}
in
\mathscr{H}_{r}
does not suffer from the CoD.

3.1.2
Localized score matching

Given the hypothesis space
\mathscr{H}_{r}
(
3.1
) with localized function classes
\{\mathscr{U}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{b}
, we can learn the localized score function by minimizing the denoising score matching loss (
2.8
).
Given an i.i.d. sample
\{X^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from
p_{0}
, the population loss (
2.8
) is approximated by the empirical loss

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(s_{\theta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(\alpha_{t}X^{(i)}+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t),t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(3.3)

Note that
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}
is decomposable:
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(s_{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{b}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}^{N}(u_{\theta,j})
, where

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}^{N}(u_{\theta,j})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left[\left\|u_{\theta,j}(\alpha_{t}X_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}^{(i)}+\sigma_{t}\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}(t),t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(3.4)

Thus, one can minimize
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}^{N}
for each
j
to learn
u_{\theta,j}
.
This allows for
parallel training
of the localized loss, i.e., the components of the score function can be trained independently. Note the score function need not be a gradient field, which introduces considerable flexibility in designing the hypothesis space.
Also note that the components of the score function are correlated for general distributions, so that
\{s_{\theta,j}(x)\}_{j=1}^{b}
should be trained simultaneously.
However, for approximately localized distributions, most components of
s_{\theta}
are almost uncorrelated, so that they can be trained in parallel.

3.2
Error analysis

In this section, we consider the error of the sampling process with the localized score function.
We do not consider time discretization here for simplicity.
The sampling process is

\mathrm{d}\widehat{Y}(t)=\left(\widehat{Y}(t)+2\widehat{s}(\widehat{Y}(t),\overline{T}-t)\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad\widehat{Y}(0)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I).

(3.5)

We take the early-stopped distribution
\widehat{q}_{\overline{T}-\underline{T}}=\mathsf{Law}(\widehat{Y}_{\overline{T}-\underline{T}})
as the approximation of
p_{0}
. It suffices to consider the error between
\widehat{q}_{\overline{T}-\underline{T}}
and
p_{\underline{T}}
, as it is easier to control the early-stopping error, i.e., the distance between
p_{\underline{T}}
and
p_{0}
.

3.2.1
Error decomposition

The following error decomposition is standard
[
10
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Under Novikov’s condition
[
10
]
:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{Q}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\overline{T}-\underline{T}}\left\|\widehat{s}(Y(t),\overline{T}-t)-s(Y(t),\overline{T}-t)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)\right]<\infty,

(3.6)

where
\mathsf{Q}=\mathsf{Law}((Y(t))_{t\in[0,\overline{T}-\underline{T}]})
denotes the path measure of the reverse process (
2.2
). It holds that

\mathsf{KL}(p_{\underline{T}}\|\widehat{q}_{\overline{T}-\underline{T}})\leq\mathrm{e}^{-2\overline{T}}\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}\|\mathsf{N}(0,I))+\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x(t)\sim p_{t}}\left[\left\|\widehat{s}(x(t),t)-s(x(t),t)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(3.7)

See
Section

D.1
for the proof. We note that the first term in (
3.7
) can be replaced by
\mathrm{e}^{-2(\overline{T}-\underline{T})}\mathsf{KL}(p_{\underline{T}}\|\mathsf{N}(0,I))
when
p_{0}
is singular w.r.t.
\mathsf{N}(0,I)
, so that it always decays exponentially in
\overline{T}
regardless of
p_{0}
.
The key is to control the second term, i.e., the score approximation error.

3.2.2
Localized score function

As discussed in
Section

2.3
, strict locality might not be preserved in the forward OU process, so that the true score
s\notin\mathscr{H}_{r}
in general.
We first control the optimal approximation error in
\mathscr{H}_{r}
.

Let
\mathscr{H}_{r}^{*}
denote the hypothesis space (
3.1
) when we take
\mathscr{U}_{j}=\mathscr{U}_{j}^{*}:=C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{j,r}+1})
.
For a
C^{1}
function
s
to be in
\mathscr{H}_{r}^{*}
, the only constraint is the locality structural constraint.
Thus, the best possible approximation error in
\mathscr{H}_{r}^{*}
can be identified as the
localization error
of the score function.
Motivated by (
3.7
), we consider the optimal approximation in the
L^{2}(p_{t})
sense, i.e.,

s^{*}=\argmin_{s_{\theta}\in\mathscr{H}_{r}^{*}}\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\int\left\|s_{\theta}(x,t)-s(x,t)\right\|^{2}p_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

(3.8)

\Leftrightarrow~\forall j,~s^{*}_{j}(x,t)=u_{j}^{*}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},t),\quad u_{j}^{*}=\argmin_{u_{\theta,j}\in\mathscr{U}_{j}^{*}}\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\int\|u_{\theta,j}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},t)-s_{j}(x,t)\|^{2}p_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

(3.9)

Using the property of conditional expectation, it is straightforward to show that the minimizer is

\begin{split}u_{j}^{*}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},t)=~&\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p_{t}}\left[s_{j}(x^{\prime},t)~\big|~x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}^{\prime}=x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}\right]\\
=~&\frac{1}{\int p_{t}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}})\mathrm{d}x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}}}\int\nabla_{j}\log p_{t}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}})p_{t}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}})\mathrm{d}x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}}.\end{split}

(3.10)

Here we denote
\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}:=[b]\setminus\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}
.

Due to the approximate locality (
Theorem

2.1
), one can expect that the approximation error decays exponentially with the radius
r
.
Before presenting the approximation result, we introduce a quantitative condition
[
15
]
characterizing the sparsity of the graph
\mathsf{G}
.

Definition 3.1
.

An undirected graph
\mathsf{G}
is called
(S,\nu)
-local if

\forall j\in\mathsf{V},~r\in\mathbb{N},\quad|\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}|\leq 1+Sr^{\nu}.

(3.11)

In the above definition,
S
denotes the maximal size of the immediate neighbor, and
\nu
denotes the
spatial dimension
of the graph, which controls the growth rate of the neighborhood volume with the radius. Here we require it growing at most polynomially to ensure effective locality. Note the spatial dimension
\nu
is typically a small number.
A motivating example for
Definition

3.1
is the lattice model
\mathbb{Z}^{\nu}
, where a naive bound of the neighborhood volume is

|\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}|=|\{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{\nu}:\left\|i\right\|_{1}\leq r\}|\leq(2r+1)^{\nu}<1+(3r)^{\nu}.

(3.12)

Thus,
\mathbb{Z}^{\nu}
is
(3^{\nu},\nu)
-local.

Now we state the approximation result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
p_{0}
satisfy the conditions in
Theorem

2.1
, and suppose its dependency graph is
(S,\nu)
-local. Consider the hypothesis space
\mathscr{H}_{r}^{*}
(
3.1
) with
\mathscr{U}_{j}=C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{j,r}+1})
. Then there exists an optimal approximation
s^{*}\in\mathscr{H}_{r}^{*}
such that

\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\|s_{j}^{*}(x,t)-s_{j}(x,t)\|_{L^{2}(p_{t})}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\leq Cd_{j}(r+1)^{\nu}\mathrm{e}^{-c(r+1)},

(3.13)

where
C
and
c
are some dimension-independent constants depending on
m,M,S,\nu
, i.e.,

C=2S\max\{1,m^{-1}\}\nu!\kappa^{2\nu+1}\log\kappa,\quad c=-2\log(1-\kappa^{-1}).

(3.14)

Note (
3.13
) is independent of
\underline{T},\overline{T}
.
Moreover, for any
s_{\theta}\in\mathscr{H}_{r}^{*}
, the Pythagorean equality holds

\left\|s_{\theta,j}(x,t)-s_{j}(x,t)\right\|_{L^{2}(p_{t})}^{2}=\|s_{\theta,j}(x,t)-s_{j}^{*}(x,t)\|_{L^{2}(p_{t})}^{2}+\|s_{j}^{*}(x,t)-s_{j}(x,t)\|_{L^{2}(p_{t})}^{2}.

(3.15)

See
Section

D.2
for the proof.
(
3.13
) provides an upper bound for the hypothesis error of using a localized score function to approximate the true score function.
Note that the bound has no explicit dependence on the ambient dimension
D
, although the true score
s_{j}(x,t)
is a
D
-dimensional function.
We also comment that condition number
\kappa
of typical localized distributions is independent of
D
; see
Section

A.1
.
Since the bound decays exponentially (up to a polynomial factor) w.r.t. the radius
r
, a small
r
is sufficient to achieve a good approximation.
Finally, taking the sum over
j\in[b]
in (
3.13
) gives the total approximation error

\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\left\|s^{*}(x,t)-s(x,t)\right\|_{L^{2}(p_{t})}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\leq CD(r+1)^{\nu}\mathrm{e}^{-c(r+1)}.

(3.16)

3.3
Sample complexity

In this section, we show the key advantage of localized diffusion models, i.e., that they do not suffer from the CoD in terms of sample complexity.
We will show that the denoising score matching with the localized hypothesis space
\mathscr{H}_{r}
is equivalent to learning the
L^{2}
-optimal localized score (
3.10
).
Since the localized scores are low-dimensional functions, the sample complexity should be independent of
D
.

3.3.1
Equivalence to diffusion models for marginals

A key observation is that the localized denoising score matching loss (
3.4
) is
equivalent
to the loss for the
j
-th score component in a standard diffusion model applied to the
marginal distribution

\pi_{0}^{j}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}):=\int p_{0}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}})\mathrm{d}x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}}
.
First, notice that since each component
X_{j}(t)
in the forward process (
2.1
) evolves independently, the marginal distribution

\pi_{t}^{j}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}})=\int p_{t}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}})\mathrm{d}x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}}

(3.17)

coincides with the time-
t
distribution of the same forward process initialized from
\pi_{0}^{j}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The localized denoising score matching is equivalent to denoising score matching for the marginal distributions.
To be precise, the following identity holds:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{j}(u_{\theta,j}):=~&\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{0}}\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left[\left\|u_{\theta,j}\left(\alpha_{t}x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}+\sigma_{t}\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}(t),t\right)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t\\
=~&\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{0}^{j}}\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\mathbb{E}_{z(t)\sim\pi_{t|0}^{j}(z(t)|z)}\left[\left\|u_{\theta,j}(z(t),t)-\nabla_{j}\log\pi_{t|0}^{j}(z(t)|z)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t\\
=~&\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\mathbb{E}_{z(t)\sim\pi_{t}^{j}}\left[\left\|u_{\theta,j}(z(t),t)-u_{j}^{*}(z(t),t)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t+{\rm const}.\end{split}

(3.18)

Here
\pi_{t|0}^{j}(\cdot|z)
is the conditional distribution of
z(t)
given
z(0)=z
when
z(t)
follows the forward OU process (
2.1
) with initial distribution
\pi_{0}^{j}
;
u_{j}^{*}(\cdot,t)
is the optimal localized score (
3.10
), which coincides with
\nabla_{j}\log\pi_{t}^{j}
;
and the constant depends only on
\pi_{0}^{j}
.

See
Section

D.3
for the proof.

Proposition

3.3
implies that the localized score matching can be regarded as
b
diffusion models, each of which aims to fit (one component of) the score function of a low-dimensional marginal distribution
\pi_{0}^{j}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}})
.
Using the minimax results for diffusion models, e.g.
[
43
]
, one immediately obtains that the sample complexity of the localized score matching is essentially independent of the ambient dimension
D
.

3.3.2
A complete error analysis

In this section, we provide a complete error analysis of localized diffusion models, which combines the localization error and the statistical error.
The error analysis clearly illustrates the trade-off associated with the choice of localization radius
r
, and shows that a moderate localization radius can balance the statistical and localization errors, yielding better overall performance.

We assume that the target distribution
p_{0}
belongs to a probability family
\mathcal{P}
that satisfies the following two assumptions.
The first assumption is to make sure that
Theorem

2.1
holds.

Assumption 3.1
.

There exists a
(S,\nu)
-local graph
\mathsf{G}
such that any
p_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
is localized w.r.t.
\mathsf{G}
in the sense of
Definition

2.1
.
Moreover, there exist
m,M>0
such that for any
p_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
,

mI\preceq-\nabla^{2}\log p_{0}(x)\preceq MI,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

(3.19)

Next, we assume some regularity conditions that ensure good approximation properties of the score function.
By
Proposition

3.3
, each localized score matching problem is equivalent to a denoising score matching problem for the marginal distribution
\pi_{0}^{j}
.
Thus, it suffices to assume that the score functions of
\pi_{t}^{j}
can be approximated well by some hypothesis space
\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon}
.

To measure the size of a function class, we use the
covering number

\mathscr{N}

[
56
]
defined as

\mathscr{N}(t,\mathscr{U},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{\infty}}):=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\exists u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in\mathscr{U},~\forall u\in\mathscr{U},~\min_{i\in[n]}\left\|u-u_{i}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}<t\}.

(3.20)

\log\mathscr{N}(t,\mathscr{U},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{\infty}})
is usually called the
metric entropy
of
\mathscr{U}
.

Assumption 3.2
.

Suppose that there exists
\gamma>0
such that, for every sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
0<\underline{T}^{\varepsilon}<\overline{T}^{\varepsilon}
,
\underline{T}^{\varepsilon}\asymp\varepsilon^{\alpha}
and
\overline{T}^{\varepsilon}\asymp\log\varepsilon^{-1}
with some
\alpha>0
.
For any
j\in[b]
, suppose also that there exists a function class
\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon}\subseteq\{u:\mathbb{R}^{d_{j,r}}\times[\underline{T}^{\varepsilon},\overline{T}^{\varepsilon}]\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}\}
satisfying the following conditions:

(1) Uniform boundedness. There exists
M_{j}^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}(\log\varepsilon^{-1})
such that

\forall u\in\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon},\quad\sup_{t\in[\underline{T},\overline{T}]}\sigma_{t}\left\|u(\cdot,t)\right\|_{\infty}\leq M_{j}^{\varepsilon}.

(3.21)

(2) Approximation capacity.
Let
\pi_{t}^{j}
denote the marginal distribution of
p_{t}
on
x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}
(
3.17
).
It holds that

\sup_{p_{0}\in\mathcal{P}}\inf_{u\in\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon}}\int_{\underline{T}^{\varepsilon}}^{\overline{T}^{\varepsilon}}\int\|u(z,t)-\nabla\log\pi_{t}^{j}(z)\|^{2}\pi_{t}^{j}(z)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}t\leq\varepsilon.

(3.22)

(3) Metric entropy condition. There exists
A_{j}^{\varepsilon}
and
\Gamma_{j}^{\varepsilon}
such that for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\log\mathscr{N}(t,\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{\infty}})\leq

\displaystyle A_{j}^{\varepsilon}\log_{+}(\Gamma_{j}^{\varepsilon}/t),

(3.23)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad A_{j}^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-d_{j,r}/(2\gamma)}{\rm poly}\log\varepsilon^{-1}),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}^{\varepsilon}=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}({\rm poly}\log\varepsilon^{-1})\right)

Here
\log_{+}(x):=\max\{0,\log x\}
.
Note
M_{j}^{\varepsilon},A_{j}^{\varepsilon},\Gamma_{j}^{\varepsilon}
are constants independent of
D
.

Remark 3.1
.

(1) The boundedness assumption (
3.21
) is used in
[
43
,
51
]
and is natural, since the score function
s(\cdot,t)
typically scales as
\sigma_{t}^{-1}
.
This condition can be enforced by clipping the function values.

(2) The key assumption above is the entropy condition (
3.23
).
By enlarging the function class
\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon}
, one can achieve small approximation error in (
3.23
), but it leads to a larger metric entropy.
The scaling of
A_{j}^{\varepsilon},\Gamma_{j}^{\varepsilon}
in (
3.23
) controls how fast the size of
\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon}
grows in order to achieve
\varepsilon
-approximation capacity.

(3) Since
\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon}
is a class of functions from
\mathbb{R}^{d_{j,r}+1}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
, the
typical scaling of
A_{j}^{\varepsilon}
should be
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-(d_{j,r}+1)/(2\gamma)})
instead of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-d_{j,r}/(2\gamma)})
.
But as a function of
t
,
\nabla\log\pi_{t}^{j}(z)
is completely determined by
\nabla\log\pi_{0}^{j}(z)
through the forward process, so that its effective dimension is
d_{j,r}
.
Based on this observation, several previous works
[
43
,
59
,
51
,
34
]
have derived the improved rates through constructive approximation using neural network classes.
We refer to
Section

A.2
for more details on the construction of
\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that
p_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
where
\mathcal{P}
satisfies
Assumptions

3.1
and
3.2
.
Given sample size
N\gg 1
, take
\varepsilon=N^{-2\gamma/(d_{\rm eff}+2\gamma)}
where
d_{\rm eff}
is the effective dimension (
3.2
), and
\underline{T}=\underline{T}^{\varepsilon},\overline{T}=\overline{T}^{\varepsilon}
as in
Assumption

3.2
.
Consider the empirical minimizer of (
3.3
) in a localized hypothesis space
\mathscr{H}_{r}^{N}
, i.e.,
\widehat{s}\in\argmin_{s_{\theta}\in\mathscr{H}_{r}^{N}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(s_{\theta})
, where

\mathscr{H}_{r}^{N}:=\left\{s:\mathbb{R}^{D+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}~\big|~\forall j,~s_{j}(x,t)=u_{j}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},t),~u_{j}\in\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\varepsilon}\right\}.

(3.24)

Let
\widehat{q}_{\overline{T}-\underline{T}}
denote the sampled distribution using the learned score
\widehat{s}
.
Then for any
\tau\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(p_{\underline{T}}\|\widehat{q}_{\overline{T}-\underline{T}})\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-2\overline{T}}\mathsf{KL}(p_{0}\|\mathsf{N}(0,I))+CD(r+1)^{\nu}\mathrm{e}^{-c(r+1)}

(3.25)

\displaystyle+C_{1}bN^{-2\gamma/(d_{\rm eff}+2\gamma)}(\log N)^{C_{2}}+\left(\tau+\log b\right)bN^{-1}(\log N)^{C_{2}}.

Here
C,c,C_{1},C_{2}
are dimension-independent constants.

See
Section

D.5
for the proof. There are three sources of error in (
3.25
):

•

Approximation error of
p_{\overline{T}}
, which decays exponentially in terminal time
\overline{T}
;

•

Localization error of the score function, which decays exponentially in localization radius
r
;

•

Statistical error, which decays polynomially in
N
, with statistical rate
\frac{2\gamma}{d_{\rm eff}+2\gamma}
.

The last term in (
3.25
) is a high-probability term of order
N^{-1}
, and is therefore negligible compared to the statistical error in the large-
N
regime.

Remark 3.2
.

(1) Compared to the vanilla method, the localized diffusion models achieve a much faster statistical rate
\frac{2\gamma}{d_{\rm eff}+2\gamma}\gg\frac{2\gamma}{D+2\gamma}
, and thus potentially mitigate the curse of dimensionality.

(2) (
3.25
) indicates a trade-off in the choice of localization radius
r
. A smaller
r
leads to smaller statistical error but induces larger localization error. Note
d_{\rm eff}\asymp r^{\nu}
(see
Definition

3.1
), so that the optimal choice is
r^{*}=\mathcal{O}((\log N)^{\frac{1}{\nu+1}})
.
Let
d_{\rm eff}^{*}
be the effective dimension with
r=r^{*}
.
When
\log N\ll D^{\frac{\nu+1}{\nu}}
, one can show that the overall error is greatly reduced compared to the usual statistical error:

\mathrm{e}^{-cd_{\rm eff}^{*}}+N^{-\frac{2\gamma}{d_{\rm eff}^{*}+2\gamma}}\ll N^{-\frac{2\gamma}{D+2\gamma}}.

(3.26)

This is the practical scenario, as one cannot obtain a sample size
N
exponential in
D
.

(3) We compare the sampled distribution to the early-stopped distribution
p_{\underline{T}}
by convention. In fact, the early-stopping error can be controlled straightforwardly in Wasserstein distance. For instance, by Lemma 3 in
[
10
]
, it holds that
\mathsf{W}_{2}^{2}(p,p_{\underline{T}})\lesssim D\underline{T}
.
Thus, the overall error is

\mathsf{W}_{2}^{2}(p,\widehat{q}_{\overline{T}-\underline{T}})\lesssim\mathsf{W}_{2}^{2}(p,p_{\underline{T}})+\mathsf{W}_{2}^{2}(p_{\underline{T}},\widehat{q}_{\overline{T}-\underline{T}})\lesssim DN^{-\alpha}+\mathsf{KL}(p_{\underline{T}}\|\widehat{q}_{\overline{T}-\underline{T}}).

(3.27)

Here the second inequality uses Talagrand’s inequality.
The early-stopping error does not deteriorate the convergence rate if we take
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

3.4
Localized diffusion models for mixture distributions

In this section, we consider exploiting locality structure in diffusion models for mixture distributions.
Suppose the target distribution
p_{0}
is a mixture of
K
localized distributions
\{p_{0}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
:

p_{0}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}p_{0}^{(k)}(x),\quad w_{k}>0,\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}=1.

(3.28)

Such mixture structure is common in practice, and can be used to model multi-modal data distributions that go beyond the log-concave setting of
Theorem

2.1
.

3.4.1
Localized score approximation

Let
p_{t}
be the marginal distribution at time
t
of the forward process (
2.1
) with initial distribution
p_{0}
.
Direct computation shows that the score function of
p_{t}
can be written as

\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{t}^{(k)}(x)\nabla\log p_{t}^{(k)}(x),\quad\lambda_{t}^{(k)}(x):=\frac{w_{k}p_{t}^{(k)}(x)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}w_{\ell}p_{t}^{(\ell)}(x)}

(3.29)

where
p_{t}^{(k)}
is the marginal at time
t
of the forward process with initial distribution
p_{0}^{(k)}
, and
\lambda_{t}^{(k)}(x)
is the posterior probability of the
k
-th component given
x
.

If each component
p_{0}^{(k)}
satisfies the assumptions in
Theorem

2.1
, then
p_{t}^{(k)}
is approximately localized, and thus
s_{j}^{(k)}:=\nabla_{j}\log p_{t}^{(k)}(x)
can be approximated as in (
2.18
).
Thus, if
\{\lambda_{t}^{(k)}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
can be approximated by
\{\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(k)}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
, then one can approximate
s_{j}(x,t):=\nabla_{j}\log p_{t}(x)
by

s_{\theta,j}(x,t)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(k)}(x)u_{\theta,j}^{(k)}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},t).

(3.30)

Although the posterior weights
\{\lambda_{t}^{(k)}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
still depend on the full variable
x
, estimating them can be substantially easier in certain regimes.
This problem is closely related to the density-ratio estimation problem
[
53
]
.
For example, when there is a sufficient statistic
T(x)
for the mixture, the posterior weights can be approximated by functions of the sufficient statistic, i.e.,
\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(k)}(x)=f^{(k)}(T(x),t)
for some functions
f^{(k)}
.
This composite structure can be exploited to design efficient estimators of the posterior weights.
A detailed discussion in the case of Gaussian mixture models is provided in
Section

A.3
.
In the numerical experiments in
Section

4.2
, we also consider a mixture of stochastic processes.
The trajectory average
\overline{x}=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}x_{i}
acts as an approximate sufficient statistic for the mixture, and the posterior probabilities can therefore be efficiently approximated by functions of
\overline{x}
.

3.4.2
Localized score matching with approximate posterior weights

Suppose we have an oracle estimator
\widehat{\lambda}:=\{\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(k)}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
of the posterior weights.
We can then apply the localized score matching method using the localized hypothesis space

\mathscr{H}_{r}^{\widehat{\lambda}}=\Big\{s_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{D+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}~\Big|~s_{\theta,j}(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(k)}(x)u_{\theta,j}^{(k)}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},t),~u_{\theta,j}^{(k)}\in\mathscr{U}_{j},~j\in[b],~k\in[K]\Big\}.

(3.31)

Denote for simplicity
{\bm{u}}_{\theta,j}=(u_{\theta,j}^{(1)},\dots,u_{\theta,j}^{(K)})\in\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\otimes K}
.
We can then approximate the score function
s_{\theta}
by minimizing the empirical loss
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(s_{\theta})
(
3.3
), which is equivalent to minimizing the following loss for each component
j
:

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}^{N}({\bm{u}}_{\theta,j};\widehat{\lambda})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(k)}(x)u_{\theta,j}^{(k)}(\alpha_{t}X_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}^{(i)}+\sigma_{t}\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}(t),t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(3.32)

Let its empirical minimizer be

\widehat{{\bm{u}}}_{j}=(\widehat{u}_{j}^{(1)},\dots\widehat{u}_{j}^{(K)})\in\argmin_{{\bm{u}}_{\theta,j}\in\mathscr{U}_{j}^{\otimes K}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}^{N}({\bm{u}}_{\theta,j};\widehat{\lambda}).

(3.33)

The corresponding learned score function is denoted as

\widehat{s}_{j}(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(k)}(x)\widehat{u}_{j}^{(k)}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},t).

(3.34)

Numerical experiments

4.1
Gaussian model

In this section, we verify the quantitative results obtained above using Gaussian models. First, we use randomly generated Gaussian distributions to show that locality is approximately preserved in the OU process. Second, we consider sampling a discretized OU process and show that a suitable localization radius is important for balancing localization and statistical errors.

4.1.1
Approximate locality

Consider a localized Gaussian distribution
p_{0}=\mathsf{N}(0,C_{0})

Consider sampling

The score function is given by

(4.2)

4.1.2
Balance of localization error and statistical error

Consider a discretized OU process
X\in\mathbb{R}^{D}~(D=101)

When employing the localization strategy to this bimodal distribution, the diffusion model generates chimera trajectories where parts are generated from one of the two models and other parts from the other model. Moreover, for two well separated distributions, a considerable amount of the generated samples fall into the range between the two peaks that does not lie in the effective support of the original data distribution. The localization prevents the diffusion model from separating the two modes, especially when they merge during the forward noising process. To mitigate this effective averaging of the diffusion model, we augment the input for training the network to determine the score function by the mean of each sample trajectory, which serves as a proxy for a label of the mode. 5
Conclusions

In this work, we study how locality structure can be exploited in diffusion models to sample high-dimensional distributions.
We show that the locality structure is approximately preserved in the forward diffusion process, which guarantees that localization error decays exponentially in the localization radius.
We propose the localized diffusion model, where we learn the score function within a localized hypothesis space by optimizing a localized score matching loss.
We show that the localized diffusion model avoids the curse of dimensionality, and the rate of the statistical error depends on the effective dimension rather than the ambient dimension.
Through both theoretical analysis and numerical experiments, we demonstrate that a suitable localization radius can balance the localization and statistical error to reduce the overall error.
This validates the effectiveness of the localization method in diffusion models for localized distributions.

However, several interesting questions remain open.
First, the locality structure should not rely on the log-concavity of the distributions, and it would be interesting to extend the theoretical results to non-log-concave distributions.
Second, designing the localized hypothesis space requires prior knowledge of the locality structure. Although it can be learned by many existing methods, it would be interesting to investigate how to combine them, or even learn the locality structure adaptively in the diffusion model.
We leave these questions for future work.

A.1
Manifold structure vs. locality structure

We compare the scaling of the condition number
\kappa
in two different regimes, which are typical of distributions with manifold structure and locality structure, respectively.
Consider a simplified Ginzburg–Landau model for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
with density

p(x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{D}x_{i}^{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{D-1}\left(\frac{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}{h}\right)^{2}\right),

(A.1)

Here the first term describes the potential energy, and the second term describes the interaction energy with mesh size
h>0
and interaction strength
\gamma>0
.
Note that

-\nabla^{2}\log p(x)=I+\frac{\gamma}{h^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&0&\cdots&0\\
-1&2&-1&\cdots&0\\
0&-1&2&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}.

(A.2)

Its eigenvalues are
\lambda_{k}=1+4\gamma h^{-2}\sin^{2}\frac{(k-1)\pi}{2D}
(
k=1,2,\dots,D
).
When
D\gg 1
, it holds that

\lambda_{\min}=\lambda_{1}=1,\quad\lambda_{\max}=\lambda_{D}\asymp 1+4\gamma h^{-2}.

(A.3)

Different scaling of
h
with
D
leads to different types of high-dimensional distributions, reflected in how the condition number
\kappa:=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}
scales with
D
.

•

Manifold type. Suppose
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a discretization of a field on a
fixed
spatial domain
[0,1]
, with grid points
s_{k}=kh
(
k=0,1,\dots,D-1
) and
h=(D-1)^{-1}
.
As
D
increases, the domain remains fixed while the mesh becomes finer, so nearby components of
x
become increasingly strongly correlated.
In this regime, the condition number grows with
D
, which is typical of distributions exhibiting a manifold-type structure:

\kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}\asymp 1+4\gamma h^{-2}\asymp 4\gamma D^{2},\quad\text{as }D\to\infty.

(A.4)

•

Locality type. Suppose instead
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a discretization of a field on an
expanding
spatial domain
[0,L]
, with fixed mesh size
h_{0}>0
, i.e.,
s_{k}=kh_{0}
(
k=0,1,\dots,D-1
) and
L=(D-1)h_{0}
.
As
D
increases, the system size grows, but the local interaction range remains unchanged. Thus the components of
x
remain only locally correlated.
In this regime, the condition number stays bounded uniformly in
D
, which is typical of distributions with locality structure:

\kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}\asymp 1+4\gamma h_{0}^{-2},\quad\text{as }D\to\infty.

(A.5)

A.2
Sparse ReLU neural network

Following
[
43
]
, we introduce the hyperparameters of a sparse NN as follows:

•

\mathsf{L}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
denotes the depth of the NN.

•

\mathsf{W}=(\mathsf{w}_{0},\dots,\mathsf{w}_{\mathsf{L}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{L}+1}
denotes the width vector of the NN.

•

\mathsf{S},\mathsf{B}
denote the sparsity and boundedness of the parameters.

Consider the ReLU NN class with hyperparameters
(\mathsf{L},\mathsf{W},\mathsf{S},\mathsf{B})

\begin{split}&\mathsf{N}\kern-2.70004pt\mathsf{N}(\mathsf{L},\mathsf{W},\mathsf{S},\mathsf{B})=\{u_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{w}_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{w}_{\mathsf{L}}}\mid\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}(\mathsf{L},\mathsf{W},\mathsf{S},\mathsf{B})\},\\
\mathsf{\Theta}(\mathsf{L},\mathsf{W},\mathsf{S},\mathsf{B})=~&\left\{\theta=\{W_{l},b_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\mid W_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{w}_{l}\times\mathsf{w}_{l-1}},b_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{w}_{l}},\left\|\theta\right\|_{0}\leq\mathsf{S},\left\|\theta\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mathsf{B}\right\},\\

The sparse ReLU NN class can be used as the hypothesis space of the score function.

The localized hypothesis space

sparse ReLU NNs.

It is known that, with a suitable choice of hyperparameters, sparse ReLU neural network classes can approximate smooth functions at the

For learning the score function

Theorem A.1

(Score approximation by sparse ReLU NNs)
.

Let

Smoothness: there exist

•

Boundedness: there exists

such that

for all

•

Boundary regularity: there exist
\delta,L>0
such that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|p\right\|_{C^{n}([-1,1]^{d}\setminus[-1+\delta,1-\delta]^{d})}<L

Given a sample size
N\geq 2
, choose

\underline{T}_{N}=N^{-c_{\underline{T}}},\qquad\overline{T}_{N}=c_{\overline{T}}\log N,\qquad I_{N}=[\underline{T}_{N},\overline{T}_{N}],

(A.7)

where

(A.8)

with hyperparameters chosen so that

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{N}=\mathcal{O}((\log N)^{C_{\mathsf{L}}}),\qquad\left\|\mathsf{W}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(N(\log N)^{C_{\mathsf{W}}}),

(A.9)

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}_{N}=\mathcal{O}(N(\log N)^{C_{\mathsf{S}}}),\qquad\mathsf{B}_{N}=\exp(\mathcal{O}((\log N)^{C_{\mathsf{B}}})).

such that

\sup_{p_{0}\in\mathcal{P}}\inf_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}}\int_{I_{N}}\left\|f(\cdot,t)-s(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{2}(p_{t})}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\leq CN^{-\frac{2\gamma}{d}}(\log N)^{c}.

(A.10)

Here

is the score function of the forward process initialized with

.

To verify the conditions in Assumption 3.3, it suffices to assume that each marginal distribution
\pi_{0}^{j}
(the marginal of

.
One can take

where

and

.
It then remains to verify the entropy condition (3.23), but this is standard
[
47
,
43

due to the following entropy bound for sparse ReLU NNs.

Proposition A.2
.

Consider the sparse ReLU NN class (
A.6
). Then there exists a constant

such that for any

\log\mathscr{N}(\mathsf{N}\kern-2.70004pt\mathsf{N}(\mathsf{L},\mathsf{W},\mathsf{S},\mathsf{B}),\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty},t)\leq C\,\mathsf{S}_{N}\left(1+\log_{+}(\mathsf{L}_{N}\left\|\mathsf{W}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\mathsf{S}_{N}\mathsf{B}_{N}/t)\right).

A.3
Gaussian mixture model

Consider a Gaussian mixture with known
K
,

p_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}p_{0}^{(k)},\quad p_{0}^{(k)}=\mathsf{N}(m^{(k)},C^{(k)}),

(A.13)

with component-dependent means and covariances. Under the forward noising,

\displaystyle p_{t}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{N}(\alpha_{t}m^{(k)},C_{t}^{(k)}),\qquad C_{t}^{(k)}:=\alpha_{t}^{2}C^{(k)}+\sigma_{t}^{2}I,

(A.14)

\displaystyle\log p_{t}^{(k)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log\det C_{t}^{(k)}-\frac{1}{2}(x-\alpha_{t}m^{(k)})^{\rm T}(C_{t}^{(k)})^{-1}(x-\alpha_{t}m^{(k)})+{\rm const}.

The constant is independent of
k\in[K]
, hence cancels in the posterior weights. Including
\log w_{k}
, define the quadratic logits

\displaystyle T_{t}^{(k)}(x)

\displaystyle:=x^{\rm T}A_{t}^{(k)}x+(a_{t}^{(k)})^{\rm T}x+b_{t}^{(k)},

(A.15)

\displaystyle A_{t}^{(k)}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}(C_{t}^{(k)})^{-1},\qquad a_{t}^{(k)}:=\alpha_{t}(C_{t}^{(k)})^{-1}m^{(k)},

\displaystyle b_{t}^{(k)}

\displaystyle:=\log w_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det C_{t}^{(k)}-\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{2}(m^{(k)})^{\rm T}(C_{t}^{(k)})^{-1}m^{(k)}.

Then the posterior weights in (3.29) are

(\lambda_{t}^{(1)}(x),\dots,\lambda_{t}^{(K)}(x))={\rm softmax}(T_{t}^{(1)}(x),\dots,T_{t}^{(K)}(x)).

(A.16)

Here

{\rm softmax}(x_{1},\dots,x_{K}):=\left(\frac{\exp(x_{1})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\exp(x_{\ell})},\dots,\frac{\exp(x_{K})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\exp(x_{\ell})}\right).

Thus the posterior weight map is a quadratic feature map followed by softmax, and therefore can be estimated efficiently.

A.3.1
Oracle estimator of posterior weights

We estimate this composite map by first fitting the mixture. Fix constants
w_{\min},M,c_{0},c_{1}
such that

w_{k}\geq w_{\min}>0,\quad\|m^{(k)}\|\leq M,\quad c_{0}I\preceq C^{(k)}\preceq c_{1}I,\qquad k\in[K].

(A.17)

Let
\Sigma=(\Sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\Sigma^{(K)})
and define the parameter space

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm GMM}=\{(v,\mu,\Sigma):

\displaystyle v_{k}\geq w_{\min},\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{k}=1,

(A.18)

\displaystyle\|\mu^{(k)}\|\leq 2M,\ \Sigma^{(k)}=(\Sigma^{(k)})^{\rm T},\ c_{0}I\preceq\Sigma^{(k)}\preceq c_{1}I,\ k\in[K]\},

Given independent samples
X^{(1)},\dots,X^{(N)}
from
p_{0}
, we take the constrained MLE

(\widehat{v},\widehat{\mu},\widehat{\Sigma})\in\argmax_{(v,\mu,\Sigma)\in\Theta_{\rm GMM}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{k}\mathsf{N}(X^{(i)};\mu^{(k)},\Sigma^{(k)})\right).

(A.19)

The plug-in logits are

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{t}^{(k)}(x)

\displaystyle:=x^{\rm T}\widehat{A}_{t}^{(k)}x+(\widehat{a}_{t}^{(k)})^{\rm T}x+\widehat{b}_{t}^{(k)},

(A.20)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)}

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{t}^{2}\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}+\sigma_{t}^{2}I,\quad\widehat{A}_{t}^{(k)}:=-\frac{1}{2}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)})^{-1},\quad\widehat{a}_{t}^{(k)}:=\alpha_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)})^{-1}\widehat{\mu}^{(k)},

\displaystyle\widehat{b}_{t}^{(k)}

\displaystyle:=\log\widehat{v}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)}-\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{2}(\widehat{\mu}^{(k)})^{\rm T}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)})^{-1}\widehat{\mu}^{(k)}.

The estimated posterior weights are then

(\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(1)}(x),\dots,\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(K)}(x))={\rm softmax}(\widehat{T}_{t}^{(1)}(x),\dots,\widehat{T}_{t}^{(K)}(x)).

(A.21)

This automatically satisfies the feasibility condition (3.35).

In the analysis, to avoid the label permutation redundancy, consider the optimal label alignment

\widehat{\pi}\in\argmin_{\pi\in{\rm S}_{K}}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\log\widehat{v}_{\pi(k)}-\log w_{k}\right|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\widehat{\mu}^{(\pi(k))}-m^{(k)}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(\pi(k))}-C^{(k)}\|_{\rm F}^{2}\right\},

(A.22)

where
{\rm S}_{K}
is the symmetric group of order
K
.
After relabeling the MLE according to
\widehat{\pi}
, we still denote it by
(\widehat{v},\widehat{\mu},\widehat{\Sigma})
.
We write
p_{v,\mu,\Sigma}
for the Gaussian mixture with parameters
(v,\mu,\Sigma)
(
A.13
), with the same label-alignment convention.

Proposition A.3
.

Assume that
p_{0}
satisfies the parameter bounds (
A.17
).
Assume also the usual local nonsingularity condition: there exist
r_{0},\kappa>0
such that, after optimal label alignment as in (
A.22
),

\mathsf{KL}(p_{w,m,C}\|p_{v,\mu,\Sigma})\geq\kappa\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\log v_{k}-\log w_{k}\right|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\mu^{(k)}-m^{(k)}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\Sigma^{(k)}-C^{(k)}\|_{\rm F}^{2}\right\}

(A.23)

whenever the expression in braces is at most
r_{0}^{2}
, while the KL divergence is bounded away from zero outside this neighborhood.
Let
(\widehat{v},\widehat{\mu},\widehat{\Sigma})
be the aligned constrained MLE (
A.19
), and let
\widehat{\lambda}
be the composite estimator (
A.20
)–(
A.21
).
Denote

N_{\rm GMM}:=K-1+KD+K\frac{D(D+1)}{2}.

Let

J_{N}:=\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\sigma_{t}^{-2}\mathrm{d}t,\quad\mathfrak{m}_{4}:=\sup_{t\in[\underline{T},\overline{T}]}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{t}}\left(1+\left\|x\right\|^{2}\right)^{2}.

(A.24)

Then for any
\tau>0
, with probability at least
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
, the posterior weight error satisfies

\Delta_{\lambda}^{N}(\tau):=\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\sigma_{t}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{t}}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(k)}(x)-\lambda_{t}^{(k)}(x)\right|\right)^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t\leq C\mathfrak{m}_{4}J_{N}\frac{N_{\rm GMM}+\tau}{N}.

(A.25)

Here
C
depends on
K,w_{\min},M,c_{0},c_{1},\kappa
but not on
N
.
Under (
A.17
),
\mathfrak{m}_{4}\leq C(1+D^{2}+M^{4})
.

Proof.

For regular finite-dimensional mixtures on
\Theta_{\rm GMM}
, the centered log-likelihood class has entropy of order
N_{\rm GMM}\log(A/\epsilon)
and sub-exponential envelopes. A standard empirical-process bound for the constrained MLE gives, with probability at least
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
,

\mathsf{KL}(p_{w,m,C}\|p_{\widehat{v},\widehat{\mu},\widehat{\Sigma}})\leq C\frac{N_{\rm GMM}+\tau}{N}.

The nonsingularity condition (
A.23
) implies

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\log\widehat{v}_{k}-\log w_{k}\right|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\widehat{\mu}^{(k)}-m^{(k)}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}-C^{(k)}\|_{\rm F}^{2}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{N_{\rm GMM}+\tau}{N}.

(A.26)

Let
\underline{c}=\min\{c_{0},1\}
and
\overline{c}=\max\{c_{1},1\}
.
The parameter bounds and the definition of
\Theta_{\rm GMM}
imply that, for all
k\in[K]
and
t\in[\underline{T},\overline{T}]
,

\underline{c}I\preceq C_{t}^{(k)}\preceq\overline{c}I,\quad\underline{c}I\preceq\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)}\preceq\overline{c}I.

Hence the resolvent identity gives

\|(\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)})^{-1}-(C_{t}^{(k)})^{-1}\|\leq\|(\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)})^{-1}\|\,\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)}-C_{t}^{(k)}\|\,\|(C_{t}^{(k)})^{-1}\|\leq C\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}-C^{(k)}\|_{\rm F},

and the same spectral bounds imply

|\log\det\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)}-\log\det C_{t}^{(k)}|\leq C\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}-C^{(k)}\|_{\rm F}.

Set
\rho_{\lambda}:=\sqrt{(N_{\rm GMM}+\tau)/N}
.
Together with (
A.26
) and
\left\|m^{(k)}\right\|\leq M
, this yields

\max_{k}\|\widehat{A}_{t}^{(k)}-A_{t}^{(k)}\|\leq C\rho_{\lambda},\quad\max_{k}\|\widehat{a}_{t}^{(k)}-a_{t}^{(k)}\|\leq C\rho_{\lambda},\quad\max_{k}|\widehat{b}_{t}^{(k)}-b_{t}^{(k)}|\leq C\rho_{\lambda},

again uniformly in
t
.
Therefore

\max_{k}\left|\widehat{T}_{t}^{(k)}(x)-T_{t}^{(k)}(x)\right|\leq C\rho_{\lambda}(1+\left\|x\right\|^{2}).

Since softmax is Lipschitz from
\ell^{\infty}
to
\ell^{1}
with a constant depending only on
K
, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\widehat{\lambda}_{t}^{(k)}(x)-\lambda_{t}^{(k)}(x)\right|\leq C\rho_{\lambda}(1+\left\|x\right\|^{2}).

Square the above inequality, take expectation under
p_{t}
, and integrate over
[\underline{T},\overline{T}]
against
\sigma_{t}^{-2}\mathrm{d}t
.
Using the definition of
\mathfrak{m}_{4}
, we obtain (
A.25
).

.
It holds that

\nabla_{j}\log\pi_{t|0}^{j}(z(t)|z)=-\sigma_{t}^{-2}(z_{j}(t)-\alpha_{t}z_{j}).

Note one can realize
z(t)=\alpha_{t}z+\sigma_{t}\eta(t)\sim\pi_{t|0}^{j}(z(t)|z)
if
\eta(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)
, so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z(t)\sim\pi_{t|0}^{j}(\cdot|z)}

\displaystyle\left[\left\|u_{\theta,j}(z(t),t)-\nabla_{j}\log\pi_{t|0}^{j}(z(t)|z)\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\eta(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left[\left\|u_{\theta,j}(\alpha_{t}z+\sigma_{t}\eta(t),t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\eta_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}\right].

This verifies the first equality.
For the second equality, we first claim that

u_{j}^{*}(z,t)=\nabla_{j}\log\pi_{t}^{j}(z).

(D.1)

Given this, the third equality follows from the basic trick in denoising score matching: take
y=z(t)
and
\nu(y,z)=\pi_{0}^{j}(z)\pi_{t|0}^{j}(y|z)
in the following identity:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\nu(z)}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\nu(y|z)}\left\|s_{\theta}(y)-\nabla_{y}\log\nu(y|z)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\nu(z)}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\nu(y|z)}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(y)\right\|^{2}-2(s_{\theta}(y))^{\rm T}\nabla_{y}\log\nu(y|z)+\left\|\nabla_{y}\log\nu(y|z)\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\nu(z)}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\nu(y|z)}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(y)\right\|^{2}+2\text{tr}\left(\nabla s_{\theta}(y)\right)+\left\|\nabla_{y}\log\nu(y|z)\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\nu(y)}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(y)\right\|^{2}+2\text{tr}\left(\nabla s_{\theta}(y)\right)+\left\|\nabla_{y}\log\nu(y)\right\|^{2}\right]+{\rm const}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\nu(y)}\left\|s_{\theta}(y)-\nabla_{y}\log\nu(y)\right\|^{2}+{\rm const}.

Here the second inequality follows from integration by parts; in the third inequality, we take

{\rm const}=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\nu(z)}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\nu(y|z)}\left\|\nabla_{y}\log\nu(y|z)\right\|^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\nu(y)}\left\|\nabla_{y}\log\nu(y)\right\|^{2},

which is independent of
\theta
; the last equality follows from the same integration by parts trick.

It then suffices to prove (
D.1
).
Note that by definition,

u_{j}^{*}(z,t)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim p_{t}}\left[s_{j}(y^{\prime},t)~\Big|~y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}^{\prime}=z\right]=\dfrac{\int\nabla_{j}p_{t}(z,y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}})\mathrm{d}y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}}}{\int p_{t}(z,y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}})\mathrm{d}y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}}}.

Since
p_{t}(y)=\int\mathsf{N}(y;\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I)p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x
, it holds that

\nabla_{j}p_{t}(y)=\int-\sigma_{t}^{-2}(y_{j}-\alpha_{t}x_{j})\mathsf{N}(y;\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I)p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Plugging the above relation into the expression of
u_{j}^{*}(z,t)
and marginalizing out
x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}},y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle u_{j}^{*}(z,t)=~

\displaystyle\dfrac{\int\left(-\sigma_{t}^{-2}(z_{j}-\alpha_{t}x_{j})\right)\mathsf{N}((z,y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}});\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I)p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}}}{\int\mathsf{N}((z,y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}});\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I)p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r_{\bot}}}}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\dfrac{\int\left(-\sigma_{t}^{-2}(z_{j}-\alpha_{t}x_{j})\right)\mathsf{N}(z;\alpha_{t}x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},\sigma_{t}^{2}I)\pi_{0}^{j}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}})\mathrm{d}x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}}{\int\mathsf{N}(z;\alpha_{t}x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}},\sigma_{t}^{2}I)\pi_{0}^{j}(x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}})\mathrm{d}x_{\mathcal{N}_{j}^{r}}}.

On the other hand, by the definition of
\pi_{t}^{j}(z)
and the same marginalization, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}

\displaystyle\log\pi_{t}^{j}(z)=\frac{\nabla_{j}\pi_{t}^{j}(z)}{\pi_{t}^{j}(z)}=\frac{\int\nabla_{j}\mathsf{N}(z;\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I)\pi_{0}^{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{\int\mathsf{N}(z;\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I)\pi_{0}^{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\int\left(-\sigma_{t}^{-2}(z_{j}-\alpha_{t}x_{j})\right)\mathsf{N}(z;\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I)\pi_{0}^{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x}{\int\mathsf{N}(z;\alpha_{t}x,\sigma_{t}^{2}I)\pi_{0}^{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x}=u_{j}^{*}(z,t).

This completes the proof.
∎

D.4
Prototype error analysis for denoising score matching

We present an error analysis for the denoising score matching estimator of (3.4) in a general function class.
The key result is
Theorem

D.2
, which will be used in the proof of Theorem 3.4 and Theorem 3.5.
To adapt the analysis for the localized diffusion models, we consider denoising score matching for a component of the score function, instead of the full score.

D.4.1
Problem setup

Consider a target distribution
p_{0}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
.
Fix
j\in[b]
, and write the
j
-th component of the true score function as
s_{j}(y,t)
.
We will omit the subscript
j
when there is no confusion.
Let
I_{N}=[\underline{T},\overline{T}]
be the time interval where
\underline{T}\asymp N^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
and
\overline{T}\asymp\log N
.
We aim to approximate
s_{j}
by a function

f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}\subseteq\{f:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times I_{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}\}.

Here we do not require
f
to be localized.
For any
f:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times I_{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
, define the loss function

\ell_{f}(x):=\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left\|f(\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t),t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

(D.2)

Given samples
X^{(1)},\dots,X^{(N)}
, define the empirical distribution by

\mathbb{P}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{(i)}}.

Write
\mathbb{P}f
for the expectation of
f
over
X\sim p_{0}
unless otherwise specified, and
\mathbb{P}_{N}f
for the expectation of
f
over the empirical distribution
\mathbb{P}_{N}
.
The population loss and empirical loss of
f
are given by

\mathcal{L}(f):=\mathbb{P}\ell_{f},\quad\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(f):=\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{f}.

Define the population loss and empirical loss minimizers by

f^{\circ}\in\argmin_{f}\mathcal{L}(f),\quad\widehat{f}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(f).

(D.3)

We aim to control the
j
-th component of the score estimation error

\mathcal{E}(f):=\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{t}}\left[\left\|f(y,t)-s_{j}(y,t)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(D.4)

Note that the true score function
s_{j}
satisfies

s_{j}(y,t)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{0},\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left[-\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)~\Big|~\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t)=y\right].

Therefore, we can show that

\mathcal{E}(f)=\mathcal{L}(f)-\mathcal{L}(s_{j}).

In fact, denote
y=\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t)
where
x\sim p_{0}
and
\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f)-\mathcal{L}(s_{j})=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{I_{N}}\left[\left\|f(y,t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}-\left\|s_{j}(y,t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|f(y,t)-s_{j}(y,t)\right\|^{2}+2(f(y,t)-s_{j}(y,t))^{\rm T}(s_{j}(y,t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t))~\Big|~y\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{I_{N}}\left\|f(y,t)-s_{j}(y,t)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t=\mathcal{E}(f).

We aim to control the generalization error
\mathcal{E}(\widehat{f})
by the approximation error
\mathcal{E}(f^{\circ})
and the statistical error (or the excess risk).

D.4.2
Main results

We need the following assumption on
\mathscr{F}_{N}
.

Assumption D.1
.

(1) Uniform boundedness. There exists
M_{N}=\mathcal{O}(\log N)
such that

\forall f\in\mathscr{F}_{N},\quad\sup_{t\in[\underline{T},\overline{T}]}\sigma_{t}\left\|f(\cdot,t)\right\|_{\infty}\leq M_{N}.

(D.5)

(2) There exists
A_{N},\Gamma_{N}>0
such that for any
t>0
,

\log\mathscr{N}(t,\mathscr{F}_{N},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{\infty}})\leq A_{N}\log_{+}(\Gamma_{N}/t).

(D.6)

Moreover,
A_{N}=\mathcal{O}(N^{p}{\rm poly}\log N)
and
\Gamma_{N}=\exp\left(\mathcal{O}({\rm poly}\log N)\right)
for some
p>0
.

Theorem D.2
.

Assume that
p_{0}\in C^{2}
, and
\mathscr{F}_{N}
satisfies
Assumption

D.1
.
Then for any
\tau\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
, it holds that

\mathcal{E}(\widehat{f})\leq C\left(\varepsilon_{\rm stat}+\mathcal{E}(f^{\circ})\right),\quad\varepsilon_{\rm stat}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{\tau}{N}+N^{-(1-p)}\right){\rm poly}\log N\right).

(D.7)

Here
C
is a universal constant.
Moreover, if the constants in
Assumption

D.1
are independent of
D
, then
\varepsilon_{\rm stat}
is also independent of
D
.

Remark D.1
.

Since
f
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
,
M_{N}
usually depends on
d_{j}
rather than
D
.
When
\mathscr{F}_{N}
is induced by a low-dimensional function class (as in Section 3.4), its entropy can be independent of
D
.

D.4.3
Proofs

First, we state two elementary lemmas.

Lemma D.3
.

It holds that

J_{N}=\int_{I_{N}}\sigma_{t}^{-2}\mathrm{d}t=\mathcal{O}(\log N).

(D.8)

Proof.

Since
\overline{T}\asymp\log N
, direct computation gives

\displaystyle J_{N}=~

\displaystyle\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}\mathrm{d}t=\overline{T}-\underline{T}+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\underline{T}}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\overline{T}}}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\overline{T}+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\overline{T}}}\leq C\log N.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma D.4
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that
\forall a,b>0
,

\int_{0}^{a}\sqrt{\log_{+}(b/t)}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq Ca\left(1+\sqrt{\log_{+}(b/a)}\right).

Proof.

First note
\log_{+}(ab)\leq\log_{+}(a)+\log_{+}(b)
.
Let
t=as
, then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\sqrt{\log_{+}(b/t)}\,\mathrm{d}t=~

\displaystyle a\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log_{+}(b/(as))}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle a\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log_{+}(b/a)}\,\mathrm{d}s+a\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log(1/s)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle Ca\left(1+\sqrt{\log_{+}(b/a)}\right),

since
\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log(1/s)}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty
is a universal constant.
∎

For
f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}
, define (recall (
D.3
))

\delta_{f}(y,t):=f(y,t)-f^{\circ}(y,t),\quad\Delta^{2}(f):=\int_{I_{N}}\left\|\delta_{f}(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{2}(p_{t})}^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

(D.9)

Note the upper bounds of
\delta_{f}
and
\Delta^{2}(f)
:

\left\|\delta_{f}(y,t)\right\|\leq 2M_{N}\sigma_{t}^{-1},\quad\Delta^{2}(f)\leq\int_{I_{N}}4M_{N}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{-2}\mathrm{d}t=4M_{N}^{2}J_{N}.

(D.10)

The following lemma is a key lemma that controls
\sup_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}}|(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N})g_{f}|
for certain classes of
f
-dependent function
g_{f}
with a
fast rate

\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
, following the standard local Rademacher complexity theory with a peeling argument.

Lemma D.5
.

Let
g_{f}(x)
be a
f
-dependent function such that there exists
B_{N}>0
,

\forall f\in\mathscr{F}_{N},\quad\left\|g_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\leq B_{N},\quad\mathbb{P}g_{f}^{2}\leq B_{N}\Delta^{2}(f).

(D.11)

Moreover, there exists
L_{N}>0
such that

\forall f,f^{\prime}\in\mathscr{F}_{N},\quad\left\|g_{f}-g_{f^{\prime}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}\leq L_{N}\left\|f-f^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}.

(D.12)

Then for any
\tau>0
, with probability at least
1-2\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
, it holds that

\sup_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}}|(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N})g_{f}|\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}(f)}{4}+\frac{CB_{N}}{N}\left(\tau+A_{N}\log_{+}(NL_{N}\Gamma_{N}/B_{N})\right).

Proof of
Lemma

D.5
.

Denote the function class

\mathscr{G}_{N}=\{g_{f}:f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}\},\quad\mathscr{G}_{N}(a)=\{g_{f}:f\in\mathscr{F}_{N},\Delta^{2}(f)\leq a\}.

By the Lipschitz condition (
D.12
), we have

\log\mathscr{N}(t,\mathscr{G}_{N},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})})\leq\log\mathscr{N}(t/L_{N},\mathscr{F}_{N},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty})\leq A_{N}\log_{+}(L_{N}\Gamma_{N}/t).

For
g_{f}\in\mathscr{G}_{N}(a)
, by (
D.11
), we have

\left\|g_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\leq B_{N},\quad\left\|g_{f}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}=\sqrt{\mathbb{P}g_{f}}\leq\sqrt{B_{N}a}.

Denote the supremum of the empirical process

Z_{a}:=\sup_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}(a)}|(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N})g_{f}|.

By Bousquet’s inequality, for any
\tau>0
, with probability at least
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
, it holds that

Z_{a}\leq\mathbb{E}Z_{a}+\sqrt{\frac{2\tau B_{N}(a+2\mathbb{E}Z_{a})}{N}}+\frac{B_{N}\tau}{3N}.

On the other hand, by the maximal inequality for empirical processes
[
56
]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z_{a}\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{B_{N}a}}\sqrt{\log\mathscr{N}(t,\mathscr{G}_{N},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})})}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle C\sqrt{\frac{A_{N}}{N}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{B_{N}a}}\sqrt{\log_{+}(L_{N}\Gamma_{N}/t)}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{A_{N}B_{N}a}{N}}\left(1+\sqrt{\log_{+}(L_{N}\Gamma_{N}/\sqrt{B_{N}a})}\right)=:\psi(a).

Here we use
Lemma

D.4
in the last step.
Let
a_{0}=\min\{a>0:\psi(a_{0})=a_{0}/32\}
.
This implies

a_{0}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\frac{A_{N}B_{N}}{N}\log_{+}(NL_{N}\Gamma_{N}/B_{N}).

(D.13)

Since
\psi(a)/a
is non-increasing, we have
\psi(a)\leq a/16
for any
a\geq a_{0}
.
Therefore, for any
a\geq a_{0}
, with probability at least
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
, it holds that for some universal constant
C>0
,

Z_{a}\leq\frac{a}{16}+\sqrt{\frac{3\tau B_{N}a}{N}}+\frac{B_{N}\tau}{3N}\leq\frac{a}{8}+C\frac{B_{N}\tau}{N}.

Here we use Young’s inequality.
Denote
a_{j}=a_{0}2^{j}
and
\tau_{j}=\tau+2\log(j+1)
for
j\geq 0
. Then

\Pr\left(\forall j\geq 0,~Z_{a_{j}}>\frac{a_{j}}{8}+C\frac{B_{N}\tau_{j}}{N}\right)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau_{j}}\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}

Therefore, with probability at least
1-2\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
, for any
g_{f}
such that
\Delta^{2}(f)\geq a_{0}
, there exists
j\geq 0
such that
a_{j}\leq\Delta^{2}(f)<a_{j+1}
, and we have

|(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N})g_{f}|\leq Z_{a_{j+1}}\leq\frac{a_{j+1}}{8}+C\frac{B_{N}\tau_{j+1}}{N}\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}(f)}{4}+C\frac{B_{N}(\tau+\log(j+1))}{N}.

The upper bound (
D.10
) implies that
j\leq\log_{2}(4M_{N}^{2}J_{N}/a_{0})
.
When
\Delta^{2}(f)<a_{0}
, we have

|(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N})g_{f}|\leq Z_{a_{0}}=\frac{a_{0}}{8}+C\frac{B_{N}\tau}{N}.

Thus, for all
f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}
, with probability at least
1-2\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
, it holds that

|(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N})g_{f}|\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}(f)}{4}+\frac{CB_{N}}{N}\left(\tau+A_{N}\log_{+}(NL_{N}\Gamma_{N}/B_{N})\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we are ready to prove
Theorem

D.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

D.2
.

Recall the definition of
\delta_{f}
and
\Delta(f)
in (
D.9
), and define

\mathcal{Q}_{f}=\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left\|\delta_{f}(\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t),t)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

\mathcal{C}_{f}=\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left\langle\delta_{f}(\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t),t),f^{\circ}(\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t),t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t.

Note that
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{Q}_{f}=\Delta^{2}(f)
. Denote
y=\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t)
where
x\sim p_{0}
and
\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}\mathcal{C}_{f}\right|=~

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\int_{I_{N}}\left\langle\delta_{f}(y,t),f^{\circ}(y,t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\delta_{f}(y,t),f^{\circ}(y,t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\rangle|y\right]\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\int_{I_{N}}\left\langle\delta_{f}(y,t),f^{\circ}(y,t)-s_{j}(y,t)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\int_{I_{N}}\left\|\delta_{f}(y,t)\right\|\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}\int_{I_{N}}\left\|f^{\circ}(y,t)-s_{j}(y,t)\right\|\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\Delta(f)\cdot\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(f^{\circ})}.

Direct computation shows that (recall (
D.2
) for the definition of
\ell_{f}
)

\ell_{f}-\ell_{f^{\circ}}=\mathcal{Q}_{f}+2\mathcal{C}_{f}.

Since
\widehat{f}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{N}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(f)
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{N}(\ell_{\widehat{f}}-\ell_{f^{\circ}})=\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{f})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(f^{\circ})\leq 0.

\Rightarrow~\mathbb{P}(\ell_{\widehat{f}}-\ell_{f^{\circ}})=\left(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N}\right)(\ell_{\widehat{f}}-\ell_{f^{\circ}})+\mathbb{P}_{N}(\ell_{\widehat{f}}-\ell_{f^{\circ}})\leq\left(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N}\right)(\ell_{\widehat{f}}-\ell_{f^{\circ}}).

Since
\mathbb{P}(\ell_{\widehat{f}}-\ell_{f^{\circ}})=\mathbb{P}\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{f}}+2\mathbb{P}\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{f}}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}(\widehat{f})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{f}}\leq\left(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N}\right)(\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{f}}+2\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{f}})-2\mathbb{P}\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{f}}

(D.14)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N}\right)(\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{f}}+2\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{f}})+2\Delta(\widehat{f})\cdot\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(f^{\circ})}.

It remains to control the empirical process term
\left(\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}_{N}\right)(\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{f}}+2\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{f}})
.
We will apply
Lemma

D.5
to control it, and thus it suffices to verify the conditions therein.
Denote
g_{f}=\mathcal{Q}_{f}+2\mathcal{C}_{f}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|g_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\leq~

\displaystyle\int_{I_{N}}4M_{N}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{-2}\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left[2M_{N}\sigma_{t}^{-1}\left(M_{N}\sigma_{t}^{-1}+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\left\|\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\|\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle CM_{N}(M_{N}+\sqrt{d_{j}})J_{N}.

Moreover,
\mathbb{P}g_{f}^{2}\leq 2\mathbb{P}\mathcal{Q}_{f}^{2}+8\mathbb{P}\mathcal{C}_{f}^{2}
, and

\mathbb{P}\mathcal{Q}_{f}^{2}\leq\left\|\mathcal{Q}_{f}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathcal{Q}_{f}\leq 4M_{N}^{2}J_{N}\Delta^{2}(f).

By Cauchy–Schwarz, denote
y=\alpha_{t}x+\sigma_{t}\epsilon(t)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathcal{C}_{f}^{2}\leq~

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left\|f(y,t)-f^{\circ}(y,t)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\cdot\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left\|f^{\circ}(y,t)+\sigma_{t}^{-1}\epsilon_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{I_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon(t)\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)}\left\|f(y,t)-f^{\circ}(y,t)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\cdot\int_{I_{N}}\left(2M_{N}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{-2}+2\sigma_{t}^{-2}d_{j}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle 2\left(M_{N}^{2}+d_{j}\right)J_{N}\Delta^{2}(f).

Combined, we obtain

\mathbb{P}g_{f}^{2}\leq C(M_{N}+\sqrt{d_{j}})^{2}J_{N}\Delta^{2}(f).

Thus, we can take
B_{N}=C(M_{N}+\sqrt{d_{j}})^{2}J_{N}
in (
D.11
).
