A note on the gravitational dark matter production
Jaume de Haro, Supriya Pan

Abstract
Dark matter, one of the fundamental components of the universe, has remained mysterious in modern cosmology and particle physics, and hence, this field is of utmost importance at present moment. One of the foundational questions in this direction is the origin of dark matter which directly links with its creation. In the present article we study the gravitational production of dark matter in two distinct contexts: firstly, when reheating occurs through the gravitational particle production, and secondly, when it is driven by the inflaton’s decay. We establish a connection between the reheating temperature and the mass of dark matter, and from the reheating bounds, we determine the range of viable dark matter mass values.

Dark matter, a fundamental component of the universe, remains one of the most profound mysteries in modern cosmology and particle physics. Gravitational dark matter production leverages the unique role of gravity, a universal interaction, as the primary mechanism for generating dark matter particles. This process requires no additional couplings or interactions with the Standard Model, relying solely on the dynamics of the expanding universe. Such production is especially relevant during the reheating phase following cosmic inflation, where the universe transits from an early inflationary epoch to a radiation-dominated era.

Two primary scenarios dominate discussions of gravitational dark matter production: one in which reheating occurs through the copious production of heavy particles

To begin with we consider a potential which near the minimum

\varphi^{2n}

\varphi^{2n}
, hence, with the use of virial theorem, the effective Equation of State (EoS) parameter,
w_{\rm eff}
, becomes
w_{\rm eff}=\frac{n-1}{n+1}
. Note that
w_{\rm eff}
is higher than

because, as a function of the scale factor, the energy density of matter decays as
a^{-3}
, the one of radiation as
a^{-4}
, and for the fluid with EoS parameter
w_{\rm eff}

In order to clearly realize the mechanism of gravitational reheating, it is essential to understand the decay process. The decay process can be described with the use of the dynamics governed by the Boltzmann equations:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{d\rho_{X}(t)}{dt}+3H\rho_{X}(t)=-\Gamma_{X}\rho_{X}(t)\\
\\
\frac{d\rho_{r}(t)}{dt}+4H\rho_{r}(t)=\Gamma_{X}\rho_{X}(t),\end{array}\right.

where
\rho_{X}(t)
stands for the energy density of the produced
X
-particles,
\rho_{r}(t)
is the energy density of the radiation (it is the the energy density of the decay products),
and
\Gamma_{X}
is the decay rate of
X
-particles into SM particles and it is assumed to be a constant.
For example,
considering an interaction of the
X
-field
with fermions, this leads to the decay of the
X
-particles with the decay rate

\Gamma_{X}=\frac{\hat{h}^{2}m_{X}}{8\pi}
, where
\hat{h}
is a dimensionless constant
[
35
]
.

We proceed with the solution which describes the energy density of the heavy massive particles as follows

\displaystyle\rho_{X}(t)=\rho_{X,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a(t)}\right)^{3}e^{-\Gamma_{X}(t-t_{\rm END})},\quad t\geq t_{\rm END}.

(5)

Here, this solution represents that the decay begins at the end of inflation, as discussed in
[
12
]
. This is due to the fact that the heavy
X
-particles are produced at the end of inflation, when the inflaton starts to oscillate. Consequently, the decay of these particles commences immediately upon their creation.

Now, inserting (
5
) to the second equation of (
II
) and considering the fact that decay starts
at the end of inflation, one can obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{r}(t)=\rho_{X,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a(t)}\right)^{4}\int_{t_{\rm END}}^{t}\frac{a(s)}{a_{\rm END}}\Gamma_{X}e^{-\Gamma_{X}(s-t_{\rm END})}ds.

(6)

We now proceed by defining
t_{\rm dec}
as the time where decay ends, that is, when
\Gamma_{X}(t_{\rm dec}-t_{\rm END})\sim 1
. This quickly gives
t_{\rm dec}\sim t_{\rm END}+\frac{1}{\Gamma_{X}}
, (
\Gamma_{X}\neq 0
), and
we focus on the investigation of the evolution of the decay products for
t\gg t_{\rm dec}
.
We presume that background dominates and with such assumption we have

a(s)\cong a_{\rm END}\left(s/t_{\rm END}\right)^{\frac{n+1}{3n}}
since
w_{\rm eff}=\frac{n-1}{n+1}
. Now, bearing in mind that

\displaystyle\int_{t_{\rm END}}^{\infty}\left(s/t_{\rm END}\right)^{\frac{n+1}{3n}}\Gamma_{X}e^{-\Gamma_{X}(s-t_{\rm END})}ds

\displaystyle\cong\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{-\frac{n+1}{6n}}\Gamma\left(\frac{4n+1}{3n}\right)\cong\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{-\frac{n+1}{6n}},

(7)

where
\Gamma
denotes the Euler’s Gamma function, we reach at the conclusion that for
t>t_{\rm dec}
, the following approximation can be made

\displaystyle\rho_{r}(t)\cong\rho_{X,\rm END}\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{-\frac{n+1}{6n}}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a(t)}\right)^{4},

(8)

where we have used the definition of the decay efficiency of
X
-particles.
Now since,
w_{\rm eff}=\frac{n-1}{n+1}
,
the energy density of the background, that is, the energy density of the inflaton field,
satisfies the equation
\dot{\rho}_{B}=(1+w_{\rm eff})\rho_{B}
, and thus,
it
evolves as

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm B}(t)=\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a(t)}\right)^{\frac{6n}{n+1}}.

(9)

Therefore, since
the universe becomes reheated when

\rho_{\rm B}\sim\rho_{r}
, we get

\displaystyle\Theta_{X}=\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{\frac{n+1}{6n}}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{\rm reh}}\right)^{\frac{2(n-2)}{n+1}},

(10)

and as a result of which, the energy density of the decay products at the time of reheating is given by the following

\displaystyle\rho_{r,\rm reh}\cong\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{-\frac{n+1}{2(n-2)}}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{3n}{n-2}}.

(11)

Now, using the Stefan-Boltzmann law

T_{\rm reh}=\left(\frac{30}{\pi^{2}g_{\rm reh}}\right)^{1/4}\rho_{r,\rm reh}^{1/4}
(here
g_{\rm reh}=106.75

denotes the effective number of degrees of freedom in the SM),
one gets the following reheating temperature,

\displaystyle T_{\rm reh}=\left(\frac{90}{\pi^{2}g_{\rm reh}}\right)^{1/4}\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{-\frac{n+1}{8(n-2)}}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{3n}{4(n-2)}}\sqrt{H_{\rm END}M_{\rm pl}}.

(12)

It is essential to calculate the range of values for
\Gamma_{X}
. We first note that
\Gamma_{X}\ll H_{\rm END}
because the decay occurs well after the end of inflation. Additionally, we have assumed that the energy density of the background dominates at the end of decay, that means,

\rho_{r,\rm dec}\ll\rho_{\rm B,\rm dec}\cong 3M_{\rm pl}^{2}\Gamma^{2}_{X}
. Therefore, using the relation

\rho_{\rm B,\rm dec}\cong 3M_{\rm pl}^{2}\Gamma^{2}_{X}
, we get,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{\rm dec}}\right)^{4}\cong\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{\frac{2(n+1)}{3n}},

(13)

and inserting the above relation to

\rho_{r,\rm dec}\ll 3M_{\rm pl}^{2}\Gamma^{2}_{X}
, one arrives at

\displaystyle\Theta^{\frac{n}{n-1}}_{X}\ll\sqrt{\bar{\Theta}_{X}}\ll 1.

(14)

Moreover, combining (
14
)
with the following bound of the reheating temperature,

5\times 10^{-22}M_{\rm pl}\leq T_{\rm reh}\leq 5\times 10^{-10}M_{\rm pl}
,
which states that
the reheating temperature
remains in an interval consistent with the Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) which occurs at about
\sim 1
MeV scale, and attains an upper bound at about
\sim 10^{9}
GeV in order to mitigate the issues related to the gravitino problem
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
,
one gets four distinct cases.
However, the only viable case is the following

\displaystyle\Theta_{X}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}\ll\sqrt{\bar{\Theta}_{X}}\leq 10^{\frac{84(n-2)}{n+1}}\left(\frac{H_{\rm END}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{\frac{2(n-2)}{n+1}}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{3n}{n+1}},

(15)

provided that the inequality

\displaystyle 10^{-\frac{42(n-1)}{n}}\left(\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{H_{\rm END}}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\ll\Theta_{X}\ll 10^{-\frac{28(n-2)}{n}}\left(\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{H_{\rm END}}\right)^{\frac{2(n-2)}{3n}},

(16)

holds.
Finally, we comment on the maximum value of the reheating temperature. The maximum reheating temperature is obtained at the epoch when the decay coincides with the end of the inflaton’s domination, that means, when
\rho_{r,\rm reh}\sim 3\Gamma^{2}_{X}M_{\rm pl}^{2}
. Now, considering eqn. (
11

\displaystyle\sqrt{\bar{\Theta}_{X}}\sim\Theta_{X}^{\frac{n}{n-1}},

(17)

and inserting this into (
12

maximum reheating temperature becomes

\displaystyle T_{\rm reh}^{\rm max}\cong 5.4\times 10^{-1}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{n}{2(n-1)}}\sqrt{H_{\rm END}M_{\rm pl}},

(18)

where
\Theta_{X}
, due to the bounds of the reheating temperature, is constrained as follows

\displaystyle 10^{-\frac{42(n-1)}{n}}\left(\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{H_{\rm END}}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq\Theta_{X}\leq 10^{-\frac{18(n-1)}{n}}\left(\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{H_{\rm END}}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}.

(19)

Gravitational production of dark matter

This section deals with the gravitational production of dark matter in the framework of gravitational reheating. We consider two quantum scalar fields,
X
and
Y
which are conformally coupled to the Ricci scalar. The
X
-field produces heavy

X

m_{X}

The
Y

Y

III.1
Maximum reheating temperature

The case when the
X
-particles decay close to the onset of radiation leads to the maximum reheating temperature.
We begin by considering the energy density of the dark matter at the present time which is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{Y,0}=\rho_{Y,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{0}}\right)^{3}=\Theta_{Y}\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{0}}\right)^{3}.

(20)

Now, with the use of the following

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{0}}\right)^{3}=\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{\rm reh}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a_{0}}\right)^{4}\frac{a_{0}}{a_{\rm reh}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{\rm reh}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a_{0}}\right)^{4}\frac{T_{\rm reh}^{\rm max}}{T_{0}},

(21)

where the adiabatic expansion of the universe after reheating has been considered, i.e.
a_{0}T_{0}=a_{\rm reh}T_{\rm reh}^{\rm max}
, we now calculate

\displaystyle\rho_{r,0}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a_{\rm reh}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a_{\rm END}}\right)^{3}

\displaystyle=\rho_{\rm B,\rm reh}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a_{\rm END}}\right)^{3}=\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\Theta_{X},

(22)

wherein we make use of

\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{\rm reh}}\right)^{3}=\Theta_{X}^{\frac{n+1}{n-1}}

[
26
]
,
which arises under the assumptions of the background evolves as

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm B}(t)=\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(a_{\rm END}/a(t)\right)^{\frac{6n}{n+1}},

(23)

and the decay of the
X
-particles is at the onset of radiation. With these, we find the following relation between the energy density of the Y-particles and the maximum reheating temperature

\displaystyle\rho_{Y,0}=\frac{\Theta_{Y}}{\Theta_{X}}\rho_{r,0}\frac{T_{\rm reh}^{\rm max}}{T_{0}},

(24)

that is:

\displaystyle\Omega_{Y}h^{2}=\Omega_{r}h^{2}\frac{\Theta_{Y}}{\Theta_{X}}\frac{T_{\rm reh}^{\rm max}}{T_{0}}.

(25)

Now, inserting the value of the
maximum reheating temperature and the observational values of
\Omega_{Y}h^{2}
and
\Omega_{r}h^{2}
, one arrives at the following bound

\displaystyle 8.63\times 10^{-28}\sqrt{\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{H_{\rm END}}}=\Theta_{Y}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{2-n}{2(n-1)}}

(26)

Next, we deal with scalar particles conformally coupled to gravity (for the non-conformally coupled case, specially the minimally coupled case, see for instance
[
40

41
]

Using the results obtained in
[
26

,
we get

\displaystyle\Theta_{A}=\frac{1}{12\pi^{3}}\left(\frac{m_{A}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{5/2}\sqrt{\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{\sqrt{2}H_{\rm END}}}\cong 2.26\left(\frac{m_{A}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{5/2},

(27)

where we have used the usual value of the Hubble rate at the end of inflation, i.e.
H_{\rm END}\sim 10^{-6}M_{\rm pl}
.
Then, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{m_{Y}}{M_{\rm pl}}\cong 1.7\times 10^{-10}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{n-2}{5(n-1)}}

\displaystyle\cong 1.7\times 10^{-10}\left(2.26\left(\frac{m_{X}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{5/2}\right)^{\frac{n-2}{5(n-1)}},

(28)

and taking into account the constraint,

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle 0.7\times 10^{-\frac{72(n-1)}{5n}}\leq\frac{m_{X}}{M_{\rm pl}}\ll 10^{-6},

\displaystyle\Theta_{X}^{\frac{n}{n-1}}\ll\sqrt{\bar{\Theta}_{X}}\leq 10^{\frac{72(n-2)}{n+1}}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{3n}{n+1}},

(45)

where we continue using

H_{\rm END}\cong 10^{-6}M_{\rm pl}
.
From this last constraint, it
is also possible to obtain the bound for
m_{Y}
as,

\displaystyle 1.7\times 10^{-10}10^{-\frac{36(n-2)}{5n}}\leq\frac{m_{Y}}{M_{\rm pl}}\ll 2\times 10^{-10}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{n-2}{5(n-1)}},

(46)

with
\Theta_{X}\cong 2.26\left(\frac{m_{X}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{5/2}
and

\displaystyle 10^{-\frac{72(n-1)}{5n}}\ll\frac{m_{X}}{M_{\rm pl}}\ll\mbox{min}(10^{-\frac{48(n-2)}{5n}};10^{-6}).

(47)

III.2.1
Quintessential Inflation

In the case with
n=\infty
, the formulas get simplifies as follows.
The reheating temperature in this case is given by

\displaystyle T_{\rm reh}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{90}{\pi^{2}g_{\rm reh}}\right)^{1/4}\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{-\frac{1}{8}}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{3}{4}}\sqrt{H_{\rm END}M_{\rm pl}}

\displaystyle\cong 5.4\times 10^{-4}\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{-\frac{1}{8}}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{3}{4}}\sqrt{H_{\rm END}M_{\rm pl}}.

(48)

The relation between different heating efficiencies and the decay efficiency is given by

\displaystyle 8.67\times 10^{-25}=\Theta_{Y}\Theta_{X}^{-3/4}\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{\frac{1}{8}},

(49)

which leads to

\displaystyle\frac{m_{Y}}{M_{\rm pl}}\cong 1.7\times 10^{-10}\Theta_{X}^{3/10}\bar{\Theta}_{X}^{-\frac{1}{20}},

(50)

with the constraints:

\displaystyle 10^{-36}\ll\Theta_{X}\ll 10^{-{24}},

(51)

and

\displaystyle\Theta_{X}\ll\sqrt{\bar{\Theta}_{X}}\leq 10^{{72}}\Theta_{X}^{{3}}.

(52)

Finally, we have the following bound for the masses:

\displaystyle 4\times 10^{-14}\ll\frac{m_{X}}{M_{\rm pl}}\ll 3\times 10^{-10},

(53)

and

\displaystyle 1.07\times 10^{-17}\leq\frac{m_{Y}}{M_{\rm pl}}\ll 2\times 10^{-10}\Theta_{X}^{\frac{1}{5}},

(54)

with
\Theta_{X}\cong 2.26\left(\frac{m_{X}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{5/2}
.
Taking into account the bound of
\Theta_{X}
, one can conclude that the viable masses for dark matter are of the
order
(10^{-17}-10^{-16})M_{\rm pl}\sim(10^{1}-10^{2})
GeV.

IV
Gravitational production of dark matter + reheating via inflaton decay

As we have already discussed, gravitational reheating works only for
n\geq 3
.
The reason behind this is that the energy density of the background must decrease faster than that of the decay products for radiation to eventually dominate and for the universe to become reheated.
As we have already discussed,
this condition is satisfied only when, after inflation, the effective EoS parameter of the background is greater than

1/3
, which corresponds to
n\geq 3
.

Here we investigate the case with
n=1
,
where the potential behaves like
\varphi^{2}
near its minimum, corresponding to models such as Higgs or Starobinsky inflation.
To achieve a successful reheating, we assume that the inflaton field decays into SM particles with a decay rate

\Gamma_{\varphi}
.
In this scenario, we first assume that the decay is instantaneous, that means, all the energy stored in the inflaton field is immediately converted to radiation, which is quite different than a delayed decay as we will see in section
IV.1
. Reheating is achieved when

\Gamma_{\varphi}\cong H_{\rm reh}
, yielding a reheating temperature
given by
[
58
]
:

\displaystyle T_{\rm reh}\cong 5.4\times 10^{-1}\sqrt{\Gamma_{\varphi}M_{\rm pl}}.

(55)

Next, we consider the scalar
Y
-particles, which are created gravitationally and are responsible for the present dark matter.
For
n=1
, the background evolves as

\rho_{\rm B}(t)=\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a(t)}\right)^{3}
, and immediately after the decay which coincides with the reheating time, this energy density transforms to

\rho_{r}(t)=\rho_{r,\rm reh}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a(t)}\right)^{4}
. Then,
following step by step the calculation done in section
III.1
, we obtain,

\displaystyle\Omega_{Y}h^{2}=\Omega_{r}h^{2}{\Theta_{Y}}\frac{T_{\rm reh}}{T_{0}},

(56)

which coincides with the result used in
[
2
]
.
By inserting the value of the reheating temperature along with the observational data, we obtain:

\displaystyle\Theta_{Y}\cong 8.63\times 10^{-28}\sqrt{\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{\Gamma_{\varphi}}}.

(57)

At this point, we note that for

m_{Y}\ll H_{\rm END}
, when

n=1
, we have
[
26
,
4
,
40
]

\displaystyle\rho_{Y,\rm END}\cong\frac{9}{4\pi}\Gamma^{-4}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)m_{Y}^{2}H_{\rm END}^{2}\cong 4.14\times 10^{-3}m_{Y}^{2}H_{\rm END}^{2},

(58)

where we have used
\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\cong 3.6254
. Therefore,
\Theta_{Y}\cong 1.38\times 10^{-3}\left(\frac{m_{Y}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{2}
, which leads to the following relation between
m_{Y}
and
\Gamma_{\varphi}
as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m_{Y}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{2}\cong 6.25\times 10^{-25}\sqrt{\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{\Gamma_{\varphi}}}.

(59)

Finally, we derive the relation between the reheating temperature and the dark matter mass which goes as

\displaystyle T_{\rm reh}\cong 3.37\times 10^{-25}\left(\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{m_{Y}}\right)^{2}M_{\rm pl},

(60)

and taking this into account, the constraint of the reheating temperature we find to be,

\displaystyle 2.59\times 10^{-8}\leq\frac{m_{Y}}{M_{\rm pl}}\ll 10^{-6},

(61)

where we have taken into account that
m_{Y}\ll H_{\rm END}\sim 10^{-6}M_{\rm pl}
.
Therefore, the viable masses for dark matter are of the order:
m_{Y}\sim 10^{-7}M_{\rm pl}\sim 2\times 10^{11}
GeV.

IV.1
Delayed decay

This situation is more involved than the previous case because it requires to solve the Boltzmann equations:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{d\rho_{\rm B}(t)}{dt}+3H\rho_{\rm B}(t)=-\Gamma_{\varphi}\rho_{\rm B}(t),\\
\\
\frac{d\rho_{r}(t)}{dt}+4H\rho_{r}(t)=\Gamma_{\varphi}\rho_{\rm B}(t),\end{array}\right.

where for simplicity, we assume that the decay rate is constant.
Following the reasoning depicted in
[
12
]

we can write the second equation as follows

\displaystyle\dfrac{d(\rho_{r}a^{3})}{da}=\dfrac{\Gamma_{\varphi}\rho_{\rm B}}{H}a^{3},

(66)

and assuming that the decay occurs during the inflaton’s domination, i.e., when
H\cong\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{\rm B}}{3M_{\rm pl}^{2}}}
, one has

\displaystyle\dfrac{d(\rho_{r}a^{3})}{da}=\sqrt{3\rho_{\rm B}}\Gamma_{\varphi}M_{\rm pl}a^{3}.

(67)

Next, following
[
12
]
, we also assume that the background scales as

\rho_{\rm B}=\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(a_{\rm END}/a\right)^{3}
, and inserting it into (
67
), which after integration leads to

\displaystyle\rho_{r}=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{5}\Gamma_{\varphi}M_{\rm pl}\sqrt{\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a}\right)^{3/2}\left[1-\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a}\right)^{5/2}\right],

(68)

where we can see that for
a\gg a_{\rm END}
, the energy density of radiation evolves as

\displaystyle\rho_{r}=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{5}\Gamma_{\varphi}M_{\rm pl}\sqrt{\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a}\right)^{3/2},

(69)

which does not match with the real evolution of radiation at late times, which scales as
a^{-4}
.
The problem in this approach is that the authors assume,
for all the time, an evolution of the background which only holds at early times when
\Gamma_{\varphi}\ll H
as is discussed in the earlier works
[
59
,
2
,
29
,
60
]
,
because as we can see from the first equation of (
IV.1
), its exact evolution, for all time
t\geq t_{\rm END}
, is

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm B}(t)=\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a(t)}\right)^{3}e^{-\Gamma_{\varphi}(t-t_{\rm END})},

(70)

which means that at very late times, since the right hand side of the second equation of (
IV.1
)
decays exponentially, it becomes

\frac{d\rho_{r}(t)}{dt}+4H\rho_{r}(t)=0
, which is the conservation equation for radiation.
Therefore, as we will demonstrate, the reheating temperature obtained in this work will differ slightly—by less than an order of magnitude—from the values reported in previous studies.

Having clarified this point, we now address the exact evolution of radiation, which is described by:

\displaystyle\rho_{r}(t)=\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a(t)}\right)^{4}\int_{t_{\rm END}}^{t}\frac{a(s)}{a_{\rm END}}\Gamma_{\varphi}e^{-\Gamma_{\varphi}(s-t_{\rm END})}ds.

(71)

Equating both energy densities at the reheating time, one has,

\displaystyle e^{-\Gamma_{\varphi}t_{\rm reh}}=\int_{t_{\rm END}}^{t_{\rm reh}}\frac{a(s)}{a_{\rm reh}}\Gamma_{\varphi}e^{-\Gamma_{\varphi}s}ds,

(72)

which is impossible to solve analytically,
because to find the evolution of the scale factor one needs to analytically solve (
IV.1
) with

H(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\rho_{\rm B}(t)+\rho_{r}(t)}}{\sqrt{3}M_{\rm pl}}
, which can only be solved numerically.
Therefore, to find an approximate solution of (
72
), we follow the strategy to assume that before the reheating the evolution is dominated by the background, and thus, the evolution of the scale factor is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}\cong H_{\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a(t)}\right)^{3/2}e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{\varphi}}{2}(t-t_{\rm END})},

(73)

which leads to

\displaystyle\frac{a(t)}{a_{\rm END}}\cong\left(\frac{3H_{\rm END}}{\Gamma_{\varphi}}\right)^{2/3}\left[1-e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{\varphi}}{2}(t-t_{\rm END})}+\frac{\Gamma_{\varphi}}{3H_{\rm END}}\right]^{2/3},

(74)

which under the assumption of
\Gamma_{\varphi}\ll H_{\rm END}

can be approximated by

\displaystyle\frac{a(t)}{a_{\rm END}}\cong\left(\frac{3H_{\rm END}}{\Gamma_{\varphi}}\right)^{2/3}\left[1-e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{\varphi}}{2}t}\right]^{2/3}.

(75)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{t_{\rm END}}^{t}\frac{a(s)}{a_{\rm END}}\Gamma_{\varphi}e^{-\Gamma_{\varphi}(s-t_{\rm END})}ds

\displaystyle\cong\left(\frac{3H_{\rm END}}{\Gamma_{\varphi}}\right)^{2/3}\int_{0}^{t\Gamma_{\varphi}}\left(1-e^{-x/2}\right)^{2/3}e^{-x}dx,

(76)

where we have assumed that
t_{\rm END}\Gamma_{\varphi}\sim\Gamma_{\varphi}/H_{\rm END}\ll 1
.
Taking into account that a primitive, which can be obtained integrating by parts, of the integrand is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{3}{20}\left(1-e^{-x/2}\right)^{5/3}\left(3+5e^{-x/2}\right),

(77)

we have

\displaystyle\rho_{r}(t)\cong\frac{3}{20}\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(\frac{3H_{\rm END}}{\Gamma_{\varphi}}\right)^{2/3}\left(1-e^{-t\Gamma_{\varphi}/2}\right)^{5/3}

\displaystyle\times\left(3+5e^{-t\Gamma_{\varphi}/2}\right)\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a(t)}\right)^{4}.

(78)

For
t>t_{\rm reh}
the contribution of the term

\displaystyle\int_{t_{\rm reh}}^{t}\frac{a(s)}{a_{\rm END}}\Gamma_{\varphi}e^{-\Gamma_{\varphi}(s-t_{\rm END})}ds

(79)

appearing in (
71
) can be disregarded, which means that, at late times
\rho_{r}(t)\cong\rho_{r,\rm reh}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a(t)}\right)^{4}
.

Returning to (
72
),
(
75
) and (
78
), after introducing the notation,

z_{\rm reh}\equiv e^{-t_{\rm reh}\Gamma_{\varphi}/2}
, we find:

\displaystyle 35z_{\rm reh}^{2}-6z_{\rm reh}-9=0,

(80)

whose solution is
z_{\rm reh}=3/5
, and leads to the following reheating temperature

\rho_{r,\rm reh}\cong\frac{3}{4}\Gamma_{\varphi}^{2}M_{\rm pl}^{2}
, which is of the same order, the difference is to replace
3/4
by
1/2
, than the result obtained in
[
12
]

using the method explained above.
Having in mind all of these results, we calculate:

\displaystyle\rho_{Y,0}=\rho_{Y,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{0}}\right)^{3}=\Theta_{Y}\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{0}}\right)^{3},

(81)

and write

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{0}}\right)^{3}=\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{\rm reh}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a_{0}}\right)^{4}\frac{a_{0}}{a_{\rm reh}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{\rm reh}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a_{0}}\right)^{4}\frac{T_{\rm reh}}{T_{0}},

(82)

obtaining:

\displaystyle\rho_{Y,0}=\Theta_{Y}\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\left(\frac{a_{\rm END}}{a_{\rm reh}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a_{0}}\right)^{4}\frac{T_{\rm reh}}{T_{0}}.

(83)

Now, multiplying the right hand side by
\dfrac{\rho_{r,0}}{\rho_{r,0}}
, we find,

\displaystyle\rho_{Y,0}=\Theta_{Y}\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\rho_{r,0}\frac{T_{\rm reh}}{T_{0}}\frac{1}{\rho_{r,0}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a_{\rm reh}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a_{\rm END}}\right)^{3}}

\displaystyle\cong\Theta_{Y}\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}\rho_{r,0}\frac{T_{\rm reh}}{T_{0}}\frac{1}{\rho_{r,\rm reh}\left(\frac{a_{\rm reh}}{a_{\rm END}}\right)^{3}}\frac{25}{9}\Theta_{Y}\rho_{r,0}\frac{T_{\rm reh}}{T_{0}},

(84)

where we have used that

\rho_{r,\rm reh}\left(a_{\rm reh}/a_{\rm END}\right)^{3}\cong\frac{9}{25}\rho_{\rm B,\rm END}
.
Therefore, we conclude that the relation between the density parameters is,

\displaystyle\Omega_{Y}h^{2}\cong\frac{25}{9}\Theta_{Y}\Omega_{r}h^{2}\frac{T_{\rm reh}}{T_{0}},

(85)

and inserting the observational data, we get,

\displaystyle 1.68\times 10^{-28}\cong\Theta_{Y}\frac{T_{\rm reh}}{M_{\rm pl}},

(86)

and using that
\Theta_{Y}\cong 1.38\times 10^{-3}\left(\frac{m_{Y}}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)^{2}
, we find,

\displaystyle T_{\rm reh}\cong 1.22\times 10^{-25}\left(\frac{M_{\rm pl}}{m_{Y}}\right)^{2}M_{\rm pl},

(87)

which is of the same order as the result obtained in the previous case of instantaneous decay. Consequently, the viable masses of dark matter are of the same order as those obtained in (
61
).

V
Conclusions

In this article, we have studied the gravitational production of dark matter particles resulting from the conformal coupling of a massive scalar field to the Ricci tensor in two distinct scenarios:
(i)
when the reheating occurs via gravitational production of heavy particles conformally coupled to gravity, which subsequently decay into SM particles, and
(ii)
when reheating is driven by the decay of the inflaton field into SM particles.

In both scenarios, we relate the reheating temperature to the mass of dark matter particles. By considering the constraints on the reheating temperature imposed by the success of the BBN, we identify the range of viable masses for dark matter particles, obtaining new bounds (see sections
III
and
IV
).

A significant result emerges when reheating is driven by the gravitational production of scalar particles conformally coupled to gravity. In this case, as described in section
III
, the range of viable dark matter masses is relatively low and narrow, depending on the effective EoS parameter during the oscillations of the inflaton field. For example, in the case of Quintessential Inflation, where
w_{\rm eff}=1
, the maximum viable mass is below the TeV scale.

Conversely, in the second scenario where the potential is approximately quadratic near its minimum, as we have shown in section
IV
, the viable mass range for dark matter particles is significantly higher, centered around
10^{11}
GeV, though still within a narrow range. This highlights the strong dependence of viable dark matter properties on the specific dynamics of reheating in the early universe.
