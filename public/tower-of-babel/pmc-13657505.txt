Pancreatic solid papillary neoplasm with high-grade malignant transformation and multiple hepatic metastases in an elderly male: a case report
Authors: Kuang-Ting Liu, Yueh-Ching Chang, Yu-Chieh Lin, Junn-Liang Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and clinical importance:Solid pseudopapillary neoplasms (SPNs) are rare pancreatic tumors that predominantly affect young women and generally exhibit low malignant potential. High-grade malignant transformation with distant metastasis is uncommon, particularly in elderly males.Case presentation:A 66-year-old male with hepatitis B virus–related cirrhosis presented with obstructive jaundice, abdominal pain, and significant weight loss. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a large pancreatic head mass with multiple hepatic lesions. Histopathological examination demonstrated mixed solid and pseudopapillary architecture with marked nuclear atypia, high mitotic activity, and extensive necrosis. Immunohistochemical analysis showed nuclear β-catenin positivity and expression of CD56 and CD99, with negative neuroendocrine markers, and a proliferative Ki-67 labeling index of approximately 60% in the hot spot together, these findings confirmed SPN with high-grade malignant transformation and hepatic metastases.Clinical discussion:This case represents an aggressive and atypical presentation of an SPN. Advanced disease, extensive metastases, and underlying liver cirrhosis significantly limited therapeutic options; only palliative management was feasible.Conclusion:High-grade metastatic SPNs may exhibit aggressive clinical behavior and a poor prognosis. Elderly male sex may represent a potential adverse prognostic factor for aggressive SPN variants. Early recognition and accurate diagnosis are essential, particularly in atypical populations with significant comorbidities.

Introduction

Solid pseudopapillary neoplasms (SPNs) are rare, low-grade malignant epithelial neoplasms of the pancreas, previously referred to as solid and papillary epithelial neoplasms, papillary cystic tumors, or solid cystic tumors. First described by Frantz in 1959[1], SPNs were officially designated as “solid pseudopapillary tumors” by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1996[2,3]. SPNs account for approximately 0.9–2.7% of all pancreatic neoplasms and approximately 5% of cystic pancreatic tumors. SPNs may arise throughout the pancreas, most commonly in the tail, and are frequently detected incidentally. Although many patients remain asymptomatic, symptomatic individuals usually present with nonspecific abdominal pain, abdominal fullness, or gastrointestinal discomfort. Metastatic disease occurs in approximately 10–15% of cases, most commonly involving the liver, followed by the peritoneum, lymph nodes, and distant organs[4–27]. Despite metastatic spread, SPNs generally demonstrate favorable biological behavior, with reported 5-year survival rates exceeding 95%[9,20–24,26,28].

However, a small subset of SPNs may undergo high-grade malignant transformation and exhibit more aggressive clinical behavior. According to the WHO Classification of Tumours, 5th edition (2019/2022), high-grade malignant transformation in SPN is characterized by diffuse solid growth, marked nuclear atypia, increased mitotic activity, tumor necrosis, elevated proliferative activity, and, in some cases, undifferentiated or sarcomatoid transformation[2,23]. Compared with conventional SPNs, these high-grade tumors are associated with poorer clinical outcomes. SPNs occurring in elderly male patients are exceptionally uncommon[20,21,27,28], and cases demonstrating high-grade malignant transformation with extensive metastatic disease have only rarely been reported, as summarized in Table 1[10,23,24]. Herein, we report a rare case of pancreatic SPN (PSPN) with WHO-defined high-grade malignant transformation in a 66-year-old male presenting with multifocal hepatic and peritoneal metastases, obstructive jaundice, and hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related Child–Pugh class C cirrhosis. This report highlights the diagnostic challenges, radiologic–pathologic correlation, and the importance of comprehensive immunohistochemical (IHC) evaluation in distinguishing aggressive SPN from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor (PNET), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). This report was prepared in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[29].

Case presentation

A 66-year-old man presented with a 1-month history of intermittent abdominal distension, progressive abdominal fullness, poor appetite, generalized weakness, and unintentional weight loss of more than 10 kg. Because of persistent symptoms and progressive functional decline, he was admitted for further evaluation.

On admission, his vital signs were stable; however, he appeared chronically ill and cachectic. His medical history was significant for HBV-related liver cirrhosis, classified as Child–Pugh class C, and dilated cardiomyopathy, complicated by prior ventricular fibrillation that required placement of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. He had no history of abdominal surgery, alcohol consumption, smoking, or illicit drug use. He reported no known drug allergies. His family history was unremarkable for malignancy or hereditary disorders.

Laboratory findings

Initial laboratory investigations included complete blood count, serum biochemistry, viral serology, and tumor marker analysis. Abnormal biochemical findings included elevated aspartate aminotransferase at 72 U/L (reference range: 10–30 U/L) and alanine aminotransferase at 82 U/L. Tumor marker analysis showed elevated alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) at 13.3 ng/mL (reference range: ≤7 ng/mL), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) at >1000 U/mL (reference range: ≤35 U/mL), and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) at 25.3 ng/mL (reference range: ≤5.2 ng/mL).

Serologic testing demonstrated a reactive hepatitis B surface (HBs) antigen with negative anti-HBs. Hepatitis C virus polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing was negative. Additional testing for Epstein–Barr virus, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), and HIV was also negative.

Initial supportive management included intravenous hydration, albumin supplementation, and empirical antibiotic therapy.

Imaging studies

Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated coarse hepatic echotexture, irregular liver surface contour, and blunt hepatic margins, consistent with cirrhosis. Multiple heterogeneous hepatic masses measuring up to 6.1 cm were identified in both hepatic lobes, accompanied by gallbladder sludge and small-volume ascites without splenomegaly.

Subsequent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CE-CT) revealed a large, heterogeneous mass in the pancreatic head–body region, measuring 7.9 × 6.7 cm, with incomplete capsular formation and mixed solid-necrotic morphology. Central low-attenuation areas were suggestive of cystic or hemorrhagic degeneration, whereas the peripheral solid components showed heterogeneous enhancement (Fig. 1A–B). The lesion encased the celiac trunk but showed no definite portal vein invasion. No pancreatic duct dilatation, biliary duct invasion, or intralesional calcification was identified. Multiple heterogeneous hepatic lesions with enhancement characteristics similar to those of the pancreatic mass were present in both hepatic lobes. The gallbladder and biliary tree were otherwise unremarkable, and no significant lymphadenopathy was observed. Based on these radiologic findings, the differential diagnoses included PDAC with hepatic metastases, metastatic carcinoma from another primary site, HCC associated with HBV-related cirrhosis, PNET, and SPN with hepatic metastases.

Cytologic and surgical evaluation

Ascitic fluid cytology using the cell-block technique demonstrated malignant cells; however, the findings were insufficient for definitive classification. Although the patient had Child–Pugh class C cirrhosis and metastatic disease, definitive tissue diagnosis was considered necessary because imaging findings were inconclusive and the differential diagnoses included HCC, metastatic pancreatic malignancy, and PNET.

Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) was considered; however, concerns remained regarding limited architectural assessment, insufficient tissue for extensive IHC characterization, and potential difficulty in distinguishing a high-grade SPN from other pancreatic neoplasms with overlapping features. After a multidisciplinary discussion, diagnostic laparoscopy was selected because it allowed direct intra-abdominal assessment and procurement of adequate tissue from both pancreatic and hepatic lesions. Given the progressive obstructive jaundice and inconclusive ascitic fluid cytology, diagnostic laparoscopy with pancreatic biopsy and wedge resection of the left lateral hepatic lobe was performed to obtain sufficient tissue for comprehensive histopathological and IHC evaluation, despite the patient’s poor hepatic reserve.

Intraoperative findings revealed an irregular, firm, necrotic-appearing pancreatic head mass with multiple hepatic and peritoneal metastatic nodules, accompanied by moderate ascites. Two Jackson–Pratt drains were placed. Postoperatively, obstructive jaundice worsened, and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography demonstrated external compression of the bile duct requiring biliary stent placement.

Histopathological findings

Microscopic examination of the pancreatic and hepatic specimens demonstrated a neoplasm with heterogeneous mixed solid and pseudopapillary architecture. The solid areas consisted of relatively uniform polygonal tumor cells arranged around delicate, capillary-sized blood vessels, whereas the pseudopapillary areas showed fibrovascular stalks lined by loosely cohesive neoplastic cells forming pseudopapillary and rosette-like structures (Fig. 2A–B). Extensive hemorrhage and tumor necrosis were present. The tumor cells exhibited marked nuclear atypia, pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, and frequent mitotic figures (Fig. 2C–D), supporting a high-grade malignant transformation according to the WHO criteria. No undifferentiated or sarcomatoid component was identified despite extensive tissue sampling. The differential diagnosis included PNET, poorly differentiated PDAC, metastatic carcinoma, and HCC arising in the setting of HBV-related cirrhosis. The metastatic hepatic lesions showed an immunophenotype similar to that of the pancreatic tumor. Ki-67 quantification was performed by hot-spot analysis across multiple representative high-power fields (Fig. 3E–F). The estimated labeling index represented the average percentage of positively stained tumor nuclei within the most proliferative areas and was approximately 60% in both pancreatic and hepatic tumors (Fig. 3C), indicating markedly increased proliferative activity.

The characteristic nuclear accumulation of β-catenin, together with CD56, vimentin, CD10, and focal CD99 positivity, supported the diagnosis of SPN. PNET was excluded based on negative chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and INSM1 staining. PDAC was considered unlikely because of the absence of glandular differentiation and a negative CK7/CK19 immunoprofile. HCC was excluded based on negative HepPar-1, arginase-1, glypican-3, and AFP staining. Mesenchymal tumors were also excluded based on negative desmin and CD34 immunostaining.

Additional IHC analysis demonstrated p16 overexpression and loss of RB expression. However, molecular testing for CTNNB1, TP53, CDKN2A, and RB1 alterations was not performed because of limited tissue availability and the patient’s rapidly deteriorating clinical condition. Therefore, the interpretation of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation and p16/RB pathway alterations in this report was based on IHC findings rather than molecular confirmation. Nevertheless, the characteristic nuclear accumulation of β-catenin strongly supported aberrant Wnt signaling, consistent with previously reported SPN biology.

Collectively, the characteristic pseudopapillary morphology and distinctive immunophenotype established the diagnosis of PSPN with high-grade malignant transformation and hepatic metastases. According to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM staging system, the tumor was classified as metastatic stage IV disease (pM1).

Outcome and follow-up

Given the advanced disease stage, extensive metastatic burden, severe cirrhosis, and poor overall prognosis, further oncologic evaluation and systemic therapy were declined by the patient and his family. He subsequently received supportive palliative care with a hospice consultation and opioid-based pain management. His clinical condition deteriorated rapidly, and he was discharged to home-based hospice care 1 week after surgery. He died approximately 1 month after diagnosis.

Discussion

This case represents an exceptionally rare presentation of PSPN in an elderly man with WHO-defined high-grade malignant transformation, synchronous hepatic and peritoneal metastases, and advanced HBV-related Child–Pugh class C cirrhosis. Although SPNs account for less than 3% of pancreatic neoplasms and predominantly occur in young women, those arising in elderly men appear to exhibit more aggressive biological behavior and poorer clinical outcomes than conventional SPNs[10,23,24,26–28].

The uniqueness of this case lies in the coexistence of multiple adverse features, including advanced age, male sex, high-grade transformation, extensive metastatic disease at presentation, decompensated cirrhosis, and rapidly progressive, fatal disease without the opportunity for systemic therapy. To our knowledge, this constellation of findings has rarely been reported.

Establishing the preoperative diagnosis was particularly challenging because the clinical, radiologic, and laboratory findings strongly favored PDAC or HCC. The markedly elevated serum CA19-9 level (>1000 U/mL), although uncommon and nonspecific in SPN, further increased suspicion for PDAC in the setting of an infiltrative pancreatic mass. However, CA19-9 elevation may also occur secondary to biliary obstruction or advanced pancreatic malignancy and should therefore be interpreted cautiously within the overall clinical context rather than as a disease-specific biomarker.

Radiologically, SPNs typically appear as heterogeneous, well-circumscribed masses with hemorrhagic or cystic degeneration. In the present case, CE-CT demonstrated a heterogeneous pancreatic head–body mass with multiple hepatic lesions but no characteristic pancreatic duct dilatation, resulting in a broad differential diagnosis. Because ascitic fluid cytology was nondiagnostic, histological confirmation was required. Although EUS-FNA was considered, diagnostic laparoscopy was selected following multidisciplinary discussion. Despite the patient’s advanced Child–Pugh class C cirrhosis, laparoscopy enabled direct evaluation of peritoneal dissemination, simultaneous biopsy of representative hepatic lesions, and procurement of sufficient tissue for definitive histopathological and IHC assessment, thereby maximizing diagnostic accuracy.

Definitive diagnosis relied on integrating morphologic and immunophenotypic findings. In this unusual clinical setting, comprehensive IHC evaluation was essential to distinguish high-grade SPN from PDAC, PNET, and metastatic HCC, as summarized in Table 2. This diagnostic approach was particularly important because of the patient’s HBV-related cirrhosis, multifocal hepatic lesions, and atypical clinical presentation.

Molecular analyses of CTNNB1, TP53, CDKN2A, and RB1 were not performed because of limited tissue availability and the patient’s rapidly deteriorating clinical condition[2,6,7,9,10,15,20,24,26]. Consequently, dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin and p16/RB pathways was inferred from IHC findings rather than confirmed by genomic analysis. Future studies integrating molecular profiling with histopathological features may further elucidate the mechanisms underlying high-grade transformation in SPN.

Complete surgical resection remains the standard treatment for SPN and is associated with excellent long-term survival, even in selected patients with metastatic disease. In the present case, however, curative surgery was precluded by extensive hepatic and peritoneal metastases, severe cirrhosis, obstructive jaundice, and poor functional reserve. Although chemotherapy and other locoregional therapies have been reported in selected patients with unresectable SPN, evidence remains limited, particularly for tumors with high-grade malignant transformation[4,11,12,21–23]. Because of poor performance status and advanced hepatic dysfunction, the patient and his family elected supportive and hospice-directed care.

Several clinicopathological features have been associated with aggressive SPN, including older age, male sex, large tumor size, diffuse solid architecture, high proliferative activity, tumor necrosis, vascular invasion, and distant metastases[10,23,24,30]. The present patient exhibited nearly all recognized adverse prognostic factors, which likely accounted for the rapidly progressive clinical course and death within 1 month of diagnosis[15,31–33].

Conclusion

This case underscores that high-grade SPN should remain a diagnostic consideration in atypical pancreatic masses, even in elderly men with markedly elevated CA19-9 levels and underlying HBV-related cirrhosis. When clinical, radiologic, and laboratory findings are inconclusive, adequate tissue acquisition and integration of morphologic and IHC findings are essential for establishing the correct diagnosis and guiding appropriate management.
