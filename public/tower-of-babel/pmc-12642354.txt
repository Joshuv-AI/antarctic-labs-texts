ABSTRACT
BackgroundInvasive pneumococcal disease (IPD), caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, remains a major global health concern, particularly for children. Among more than 100 pneumococcal serotypes, 19A is known for its multidrug resistance (MDR) and increased incidence following PCV7/PCV10 introduction in many countries. Bangladesh introduced PCV10 in 2015 considering the low burden of 19A in the country.MethodsUtilizing our IPD surveillance from 2004 to 2023, we investigated the hospital incidence of serotype 19A before and after PCV10 introduction among < 5 years old children in Bangladesh. Whole-genome sequencing was done for 153 serotype 19A isolates from IPD, otitis media, carriage, and urine samples. Background

Invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) in children remains a major global health challenge and a leading cause of mortality and severe illness. Annually, approximately 9.2 million IPD cases occur every year globally, resulting in an estimated 320,000 deaths among children under the age of five years [1, 2]. The causative agent, Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus), comprises over 100 distinct serotypes [3]. Among these, serotype 19A is particularly concerning due to its multidrug resistance (MDR) and substantial contribution to the incidence of IPD, pneumonia, acute otitis media, and hemolytic uremic syndrome [4].

To protect children against IPD, multiple pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs), including 7-, 10-, 13-, 15-, and 20-valent, have been licensed, which provide protection against 7, 10, 13, 15, and 20 different pneumococcal serotypes, respectively [5, 6]. The first pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, the 7-valent PCV (PCV7), was introduced in the United States in 2000 and in Europe in 2001 [7, 8]. However, the introduction of PCV7 was followed by rising MDR pneumococcal infections, particularly from serotype 19A, which was not included in the vaccine [9]. The primary driver of rising 19A-MDR cases was the emergence of sequence type 320 (ST320) [10–12]. Isolates of 19A-ST320, part of global pneumococcal sequence cluster-GPSC1, commonly exhibit resistance to penicillin, cotrimoxazole, carbapenem, macrolide, and tetracycline [4, 13]. Evolutionarily, 19A-ST320 is a double-locus variant of ST236, a serotype 19F clone (Taiwan19F-14) that was MDR and had spread globally before PCV7’s introduction [4, 14–16]. Serotype 19A also exhibits notable genetic diversity, with subtypes I, II, and III showing sequence variation in their capsular loci, cps [17]. However, these variations within the serotype remain rarely studied. In contrast, the emergence of the 19A-ST320 lineage has been reported from multiple countries including Brazil, Japan, Poland, USA, and several Asian countries [9, 12, 18–20]. Role of this lineage in replacing vaccine serotypes has prompted the introduction of PCVs that include 19A, such as PCV13, in many of the countries.

In Bangladesh, policymakers introduced PCV10 (Synflorix, GlaxoSmithKline) into its national expanded programme on immunization (EPI). Between 2007 and 2013, only 4% of IPD cases (17/442) were attributed to 19A [2]. Consequently, PCV10, which protects against serotypes 1, 4, 5, 6B, 7F, 9V, 14, 18C, 19F, and 23F, but not 19A, was introduced into the routine vaccination program in 2015 [21]. Since then, we have remained vigilant about the potential emergence of the MDR 19A-ST320 lineage in Bangladesh.

In this study, we investigate the trends, population structure, and evolution of serotype 19A in Bangladesh before and after PCV10 introduction. Using whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of 153 pneumococcal 19A isolates from diverse sources, including blood and cerebrospinal fluid, otitis media, nasopharyngeal carriage, and urine, we examine circulating 19A subtypes, STs, and GPSCs and associated resistance genes. Through phylogenetic and Bayesian analyses, we explore 19A’s evolution and resistance markers, contextualizing findings against a global dataset of 1,753 publicly available genomes.

Results

No increase in invasive serotype 19A infections observed in the post-PCV10 era in Bangladesh

Between 2004 and 2023, a total of 47 serotype 19A cases were detected among 1,879 confirmed IPD cases, including both culture- and ICT-positive detections. For disease burden calculations, we focused on data collected since 2010, as patient eligibility/ enrollment criteria and laboratory methods for pneumococcal detection and serotyping were standardized that year. From 2010 to 2023, 34 serotype 19A cases (25 culture and nine ICT-positive) were identified among 1,106 IPD cases, representing 3% of all cases (Additional file 1: Table S1). The yearly hospital incidence of 19A cases per 10,000 eligible suspected IPD cases among < 5 years old children declined over time, from 8.3 in 2010 to 4.05 in 2023 (Fig. 1 and Additional file 1: Table S2). The lowest number of hospitalized IPD cases was observed in 2020 (n = 29; a 44% reduction from 2019, with no 19A cases), and this trend continued in 2021 (n = 31, with one 19A), and 2022 (n = 32, with no 19A). This decline of IPD cases was likely due to the COVID-19 pandemic and nationwide lockdown, which disrupted hospital visits. IPD case numbers started rising again in 2023 (Additional file 1: Table S2).

Absence of GPSC1/ST320 amongst 19A isolates

We sequenced all culture-positive serotype 19A isolates (n = 37), detected in the IPD surveillance between 2005 and 2023. No culture-positive serotype 19A was detected in 2004. Additionally, we sequenced 51 isolates from otitis media (OM) surveillance (2014–2019), 62 isolates from NP carriage surveillance (2012–2020), and three isolates from urine samples collected in our hospital microbiology laboratory at Bangladesh Shishu (Children) Hospital and Institute (BSHI) (2018–2021). In total, 153 serotype 19A isolates were sequenced for this study.

Sequence type (ST) analysis of the WGS data identified 41 different STs circulating in Bangladesh, including 11 new STs reported in this study. Notably, neither ST320 nor its progenitor, ST236—both associated with MDR, were detected [22]. ST12888 was the dominant ST (n = 57), followed by ST12473 (n = 16), and ST2464 (n = 15) (Additional file 1: Table S3). GPSC1 was not detected, which is related to both ST320 and ST236 [23]. Instead, GPSC84 was the most prevalent (n = 83), followed by GPSC10 (n = 26), and GPSC5 (n = 15) (Additional file 1: Table S3). Two of the three 19A isolates from urine samples did not match any known GPSC, leading to the definition of a new lineage, GPSC1123.

The distribution of GPSCs has shifted over time (Fig. 2). Between 2005 and 2013, GPSC53 accounted for 56% (10/18) of the invasive cases. However, it disappeared in later years, with GPSC84 becoming dominant, representing 93% (13/14) of the invasive cases during 2018–2023 (Fig. 2A). The yearly distribution of GPSCs among invasive isolates also corroborates to this change (Fig. 2B). A similar trend was observed for both OM and NP carriage samples. In 2014, OM isolates were primarily GPSC10 (n = 9) or GPSC5 (n = 4), but GPSC84 became dominant later, accounting for 76%; (28/37) of isolates (Fig. 2A, and C). For NP isolates, GPSC84 represented 21% (4/19) of pre-2017 isolates but increased to 77% (33/43) in subsequent years (Fig. 2A, and D). These findings highlight changes in GPSC distribution across all sample types in recent years. A comparable shift was seen in ST distribution among invasive samples as well. Before 2013, 44% (7/16) of invasive isolates were ST12891 or ST5282, but since 2017, 65% (11/17) of isolates have been ST12888 (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).

Dominance of macrolide, cotrimoxazole, and tetracycline resistance

To analyze antimicrobial resistance (AMR) patterns, we used the inferred resistance phenotypes from GPS pipeline for all 153 19A isolates. Among these, only two isolates were penicillin-resistant belonging to GPSC10 (from 2013) and GPSC53 (from 2008) (Fig. 3A). Only one isolate, GPSC84, from 2012 was chloramphenicol-resistant. No isolates were resistant to meropenem or fluroquinolones. Cotrimoxazole resistance was common (92%; 140/153) and observed in > 99% of GPSC84, GPSC10, and GPSC5 isolates (Fig. 3A, and B). Tetracycline resistance was also prevalent (86%; 131/153) across different GPSCs, except for GPSC53 (present in only 1/11; 8%).

Macrolide resistance (inferred using erythromycin resistance) was identified in 85% (125/153) isolates (Fig. 3A). However, the resistance varied across GPSCs. GPSC84, 10, and 5 had high macrolide resistance (94%; 117/124), driven predominantly by the ermB gene in GPSC10 (22/26) and GPSC84 (75/83). GPSC5 isolates (n = 15) carried mefA and msrD genes. In contrast, macrolide resistance was negligible in GPSC53 and GPSC307 (5%; 1/19). Ten isolates (7%) lacked resistance to any antibiotics, 70% (7/10) of which were invasive and associated with GPSC53 or GPSC307 (Fig. 3A-C).

Resistance to three antibiotic classes – cotrimoxazole, macrolides (erythromycin), and tetracycline (abbreviated as the “CotR, EryR, TetR” profile or triple-resistant) – became highly prevalent among 19A invasive isolates after 2015 (95%; 18/19) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). This profile was absent among pre-2011 isolates (0/13) and became dominant from 2014 (96%; 117/122), particularly within GPSC84 (93%; 77/83) (Fig. 3A, and B). For OM and NP carriage isolates, triple-resistance profile likely emerged much earlier, as a few isolates from 2012 (50%; 3/6) already exhibited the profile (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B, and S2C). These changes in resistance profiles paralleled shifts in the GPSC distribution, as non-resistant clusters like GPSC53 and GPSC307 disappeared while GPSC84, associated with the CotR, EryR, TetR profile, became dominant (Figs. 2A and 3). A corresponding shift in STs was observed as well. ST5282 (n = 4) and ST12891 (n = 7), which exhibited no resistance or only cotrimoxazole resistance, declined after 2013. In contrast, ST12888, showing the triple-resistance profile, increased significantly, accounting for 56 isolates after 2014 (Fig. 3C, and Additional file 1: Fig. S1A).

GPSC84 acquired resistance before PCV10 introduction in Bangladesh

To better understand the emergence of macrolide resistance, we performed a Bayesian analysis of all GPSC-84 genomes from Bangladesh (n = 83) alongside 81 global GPSC-84 genomes from the Monocle database, totaling 164 genomes. The global dataset included 62 genomes (77%; 62/81) of serotype 19F. Two primary clades, A and B, were identified. Clade A predominantly comprised serotype 19F subtype I (19F-I) genomes, while Clade B included both 19F and 19A genomes of various subtypes (Fig. 4 and Additional file 3: Data S2). A clear distinction was noted in the macrolide resistance pattern between the two clades. In Clade A, all five macrolide-resistant isolates carried the mefA-msrD gene combination, including two subtype 19A-I genomes from Bangladesh. In contrast, 95% (94/99) of the macrolide-resistant genomes were in Clade B, with 93 carrying the ermB gene. BEAST analysis estimated the origins of both clades in the 1970s (Fig. 4).

Clade B was further divided into three secondary clades - B1, B2, and B3. Clades B1 and B2 were primarily composed of 19F and 19F-I genomes from The Gambia (92%; 34/37). Clade B3 had two subclades - B3.1 and B3.2, with 19F-I and 19A-I genomes, respectively. Among 19A-I genomes, 97% (94/97) was placed in subclade B3.2.1, with 85% (80/94) originating from Bangladesh and the rest from Nepal and India (Fig. 4). Notably, one of its subclades, B3.2.1.2, which originated between 2007 and 2011, was the largest in the tree. This subclade included 89 macrolide-resistant 19A-I genomes, with ermB gene present in all except one, carrying mefA-msrD gene combination. The majority of these genomes were ST12888 (75%; 67/89) and isolated in Bangladesh (84%; 75/89), with the remaining from Nepal (n = 12) and India (n = 2) (Fig. 4). The acquisition of ermB-gene-based macrolide-resistance within the 19A-I GPSC84 population likely occurred as an independent event, which subsequently spread to other South Asian countries, including India and Nepal. In contrast, macrolide resistance based on the mefA-msrD genes did not spread widely and remained sporadic within GPSC84. Our study supports this timeline of ermB-based macrolide resistance, as the earliest resistant 19A-I GPSC84 genome we identified was from a nasopharyngeal carriage sample collected in 2012 (Figs. 2D and 3A). This finding indicates that the emergence of macrolide resistance within the 19A-I GPSC84 population in Bangladesh predates the introduction of PCV10 in 2015 and cannot be attributed to the vaccine.

Dominance of 19A subtype-I in Bangladesh

To classify the capsular subtypes of serotype 19A circulating in Bangladesh, we employed Pneumokity, following the classification system proposed by Elberse et al. [17], which identifies three subtypes: I, II, and III. Analysis revealed that 90% (138/153) of the 19A isolates belonged to subtype-I (19A-I), while the remaining were standard 19A (Additional file 2: Data S1). A comparison of the cps loci between standard reference 19A (accession: LR216061.1) and subtype 19A-I (ID: SPN_0109; Sample Accession: ERS22716356) revealed smaller cps loci in 19A-I (19,849 bp, compared to 21,424 bp in standard 19A). In addition, a pseudogene, glf was deleted in 19A-I cps loci and the orientation of the dTDP-4-dehydrorhamnose reductase (rmlD) gene reversed, though its length remained nearly the same, 855 vs. 852 bp (Fig. 5A). The comparison also showed that one of the flanking transposase (tnp) genes in subtype 19A-I was reversed and shorter in length (522 vs. 846 bp). Despite these structural changes, the wzy and wzx genes, which determine the capsular phenotype, remained unaltered (Fig. 5A). This explains why subtype 19A-I phenotypically resembles serotype 19A.

The prevalence of 19A-I remained low among 19A (phenotype) isolates in our study till 2011. Since then, 96% of 19A isolates were 19A-I (96%; 133/138), compared to earlier isolates, which were primarily standard 19A (66%; 10/15). This change was even more distinct among invasive 19A isolates, where the proportion of 19A-I increased from 44% (8/18) in the pre-PCV to 100% (19/19) in the post-PCV period (Fig. 5B). Notably, the GPSC distribution also shifted during this time, from GPSC53 and GPSC307 to GPSC84 (Fig. 2A, and 5C). However, for OM and NP carriage samples, 19A-I was dominant throughout, regardless of the PCV timeline (Fig. 5B).

Evolutionary origin of 19A-I in Bangladesh

To understand the evolutionary divergence of subtype 19A-I, we conducted a whole-genome SNP-based phylogenetic analysis. The annotated phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5C) highlights that the origin of 19A-I in Bangladesh marks a significant event for serotype 19A in the country, as subtype-I started to dominate and expand independently from standard 19A. The tree showed two distinct primary clades for standard 19A and 19A-I. For standard 19A isolates, no significant clustering was observed, as the isolates were predominantly GPSC53, from invasive sources, and the pre-2012 period. In contrast, primary clade of 19A-I isolates expanded into four major subclades, each characterized by its own dominant GPSC, STs, and timelines (A to D, as shown in Fig. 5C).

Subclade A consisted of pre-2014 invasive 19A-I isolates, characterized by GPSC307 and primarily ST5282. Subclades B and C mostly included isolates from OM and NP samples, associated with GPSC5 and GPSC10, respectively, from the post-2014 period. The largest subclade D contained all 83 GPSC84 isolates, dominated by ST12888, spanning 2012–2023. This subclade included four isolates sourced from blood and NP samples collected between 2012 and 2014, suggesting that GPSC84 was already present within the 19A population of Bangladesh prior to the introduction of PCV10.

Subtype 19A-I is dominant in many countries

A global analysis of 1,906 serotype 19A genomes revealed the dominance of subtype 19A-I (46%; n = 881), followed by standard 19A (42%; n = 806), 19A subtype II (19A-II; 10%; n = 201), and 19AF (1%; n = 18) (Fig. 6A and Additional file 4: Data S3). Subtype 19AF isolates has a cps loci resembling 19A, but with a 19F-like wzy gene, thus displays 19F in Quellung reactions [22]. Globally, 19A-I is dominant in countries such as Argentina, Bangladesh, India, Israel, Mozambique, Nepal, Qatar, and South Africa, while The Gambia and USA report higher prevalence of standard 19A. Countries like Ireland and Malawi showed co-existing populations of standard 19A, 19A-I, and 19A-II subtypes (Fig. 6A and Additional file 4: Data S3).

Among GPSCs, GPSC17 is the most frequently isolated lineage globally (19%; n = 371), followed by GPSC1 (17%; n = 325), and GPSC4 (13%; n = 249). In South Asia, GPSC10 is dominant in India (51%; 22/43), while GPSC84 is prevalent in Nepal (47%; 14/30) and Bangladesh (54%; 83/153). GPSC1, a major contributor to pneumococcal MDR, is common in the USA (38%; 106/281), Cambodia (95%; 69/73), Ireland (16%; 48/302), and China (69%; 22/32), and is significantly correlated with standard 19A (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6B, Additional file 1: Table S4 and Additional file 4: Data S3). For global ST distribution, ST320 is the most dominant type globally (14%; n = 273). In South Asia, ST12474 is dominant in India (23%; 10/43), while ST12888 is prevalent in Nepal (34%; 10/29) and Bangladesh (38%; 57/153). The presence of ST320 is also significantly correlated with standard 19A and GPSC1 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6C and Additional file 4: Data S3).

Discussion

Pneumococcal serotype 19A has garnered attention for its persistence and emergence in various countries following the introduction of PCVs, particularly due to MDR lineages such as ST320 or GPSC1 [18, 20, 24, 25].

Our analysis of IPD surveillance data from four hospitals in Bangladesh during 2010–2023 revealed decrease in the hospital incidence of serotype 19A cases following PCV10 introduction in 2015. In fact, the hospital incidence declined from 8.3 to 4.05 cases per 10,000 eligible suspected IPD cases over this period. Genomic analysis of 153 serotype 19A isolates from invasive, otitis media, nasopharyngeal (NP) carriage, and urine samples confirmed the complete absence of MDR lineages such as ST320 or GPSC1. These findings indicate that, unlike other countries, Bangladesh did not experience a post-PCV10 increase in 19A burden or associated MDR [18, 20, 24, 25]. They also underscore the importance for evidence-based vaccine policies informed by local epidemiological and genomic data. This approach is not unique though, as countries like Belgium and Brazil have utilized genomic data to evaluate the efficacy of PCVs within their routine immunization programs and make adjustments accordingly [18, 26, 27]. Similarly, Vietnam assessed both PCV10 and PCV13 to compare their effectiveness in children and is planning to introduce PCV10 based on this evaluation [28, 29].

The serotype 19A population in Bangladesh has undergone notable shifts in STs and GPSCs. GPSC84 and ST12888 now dominate across IPD, otitis media, and NP carriage isolates. Before 2015, GPSC53 and GPSC10 were prevalent, with ST12891 and ST5282. This replacement likely caused the lower 19A hospital incidence (per 10,000 eligible suspected IPD cases) observed in 2013–2014 (5.7-4.4, pre-PCV10) and again in 2019 (5.9) and 2023 (4.05; Fig. 1), suggesting background lineage turnover (GPSC53/GPSC10 to GPSC84/ST12888). Although these changes do not align precisely with the introduction of PCV10 in Bangladesh, similar shifts have been observed in Belgium after switching from PCV13 to PCV10 in 2015–2016 [30]. In Belgium, dominant types such as ST193 (GPSC11) and ST276 (GPSC10) in 2011 were replaced by ST416 (GPSC4) and ST994 (GPSC146), which accounted for 66%-76% of IPD 19A cases during 2017–2018. This finding highlights that emerging lineages and serotype dynamics vary across countries and cannot always be predicted based on trends elsewhere. Therefore, local genomic surveillance is essential to monitor serotype evolution and inform evidence-based vaccine strategies.

Our study also revealed that most Bangladeshi serotype 19A isolates belong to the 19A-I subtype (90%; 138/153). Although these different subtypes were described in 2011 [17], the importance of subtyping within the 19A population had not been fully appreciated. Analysis of the capsular regions of standard 19A and 19A-I showed deletion of the glf pseudogene, inversion of the rmlD gene, and reversed and shorter tnp sequences in the latter subtype. This may indicate historical recombination or deletion events at the capsule locus boundary. Within the global serotype 19A population, ST320 or GPSC1 is significantly correlated with presence of standard 19A (p < 0.001) and negatively correlated with subtype 19A-I (p < 0.001). Phylogenetic analysis indicates that standard 19A and 19A-I have independent evolutionary origins in Bangladesh, with 19A-I expanding rapidly before the PCV10 introduction. The dominance of 19A-I within the Bangladeshi serotype 19A population is strongly correlated to GPSC84 and ST12888 (p < 0.001). Both lineages exhibit high resistance to macrolide, cotrimoxazole, and tetracycline (94%; 78/83), indicating multidrug resistance, MDR. In contrast, the globally dominant MDR clones of GPSC1/ST320 are completely absent in the country, underscoring the unique dynamics of serotype 19A in this setting. These findings suggest that future outbreaks of serotype 19A in Bangladesh, if current trends persist, could potentially emerge from the MDR 19A-I/GPSC84/ST12888 lineage which is resistant to macrolide, tetracycline, and cotrimoxazole.

Macrolide resistance within the Bangladesh 19A population has emerged over the past decade, significantly correlated with the dominance of GPSC84 in the country (p < 0.001). BEAST analysis of the global GPSC84 population showed its prevalence in neighboring countries such as Nepal and India. We estimate the origin of the macrolide-resistant subclade, heavily burdened by ST-12888, to be between 2007 and 2011 (B3.2.1.2 in Fig. 5C). This aligns with the detection of the first macrolide-resistant isolate detected in our study in 2012 (Fig. 3). These findings suggest that macrolide resistance in the 19A-I/GPSC84/ST12888 lineage emerged independently of PCV10 introduction. The widespread use of azithromycin, a macrolide drug with a long half-life (approximately three days), may have contributed to the selection and persistence of resistant pneumococcal strains [31]. Low drug concentrations at carriage sites over extended periods promote resistance, which can further spread through healthy carriers [32]. Mass distribution and unregulated use of azithromycin can also act as a driver of increased macrolide resistance [33]. A recent study reported a 38-fold increase (0.1 to 3.8 DDD/1000 person/day) in the use of azithromycin in Bangladesh over the last 22 years (1999–2020) [34] and the timeline of increasing macrolide resistance amongst 19A isolates in Bangladesh (Fig. 3) correlated with increasing consumption of azithromycin in the country (p < 0.001). Taken together, the rising macrolide use providing strong selection pressure, coupled with the clonal expansion of GPSC84/ST12888 lineage (carrying ermB- gene), likely drove the increase in macrolide-resistant 19A in Bangladesh.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the otitis media and carriage studies were conducted only during specific time periods. While invasive surveillance provided genomic data from 2005 to 2023, otitis media surveillance was limited to 2014–2019, and carriage data were available from 2012 to 2019. This constrained our ability to precisely date and compare population-level changes in serotype 19A across these sampling sources. Second, within our invasive surveillance, a large number of pneumococcal meningitis cases (upto 96%) are typically culture-negative, detected by either ICT or qPCR [2, 35]. As a result, no genomic information could be retrieved from most 19A-positive meningitis cases. Third, we lacked a systematic antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) dataset for all serotype 19A isolates across the study period, which precluded a concordance analysis between resistance phenotypes and WGS-inferred resistance.

Conclusions

This study provides the first comprehensive genomic analysis of pneumococcal serotype 19A in Bangladesh, encompassing invasive, otitis media and NP carriage isolates. The absence of MDR lineages such as GPSC1 and ST320, combined with the dominance of subtype 19A-I and GPSC84, underscores the importance of continued genomic surveillance to track population dynamics and resistance patterns. Our findings support the decision to introduce PCV10 in the national immunization program of Bangladesh, and emphasize the value of locally generated genomic and epidemiological data in shaping vaccination strategies. To inform updates to vaccination policies, future efforts are needed focusing on monitoring serotype 19A to identify any significant increases in disease burden. Although there has been no policy announcement on transitioning to PCV15 or PCV20, the rapid evolution and rising macrolide resistance of serotype 19A observed here may serve as evidence for evaluating potential benefits to introduce broader-coverage vaccines in the future if invasive pneumococcal surveillance indicates a growing burden of non-PCV10 serotypes.

Methods

Pneumococcal surveillance at CHRF

The Child Health Research Foundation (CHRF) has been conducting invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) surveillance since 2004 at four different hospitals in Bangladesh– Bangladesh Shishu (Children) Hospital and Institute (BSHI, formerly known as Dhaka Shishu Hospital, or DSH) and Dr. M R Khan Shishu Hospital (MRKSH, formerly known as Shishu Shastho Foundation Hospital, or SSFH) in Dhaka, ChittagongMaa O Shishu (Mother and Children) Hospital, or CMOSH in Chittagong, and Kumudini Women’s Medical College and Hospital (KWMCH) in Mirzapur (Additional File 1: Fig. S3). Of these, BSHI and MRKSH are dedicated pediatric hospitals, while surveillance at CMOSH and KWMCH was limited to the pediatric and neonatal wards. At all four sites, hospitalized children under 5 years were screened for eligibility using WHO case definitions for meningitis, pneumonia, and sepsis. Eligible children were enrolled after written parental/guardian consent, and blood and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were collected as previously described [2]. More details are described in Supplementary methods (Additional file 1).

CHRF conducted otitis media (OM) surveillance at BSHI between 2014 and 2019. In short, children under 18 years visiting the hospital’s Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT) outpatient department were screened, and those with suspected OM were enrolled by study physicians based on WHO-recommended clinical and otoscopic criteria [36]. CHRF also conducted nasopharyngeal (NP) carriage surveillance through three studies between 2012 and 2020: a hospital study at KWMCH, and a longitudinal study, and a cross-sectional study at a population-based site in Mirzapur upazilla (subdistrict) of Tangail district. All 19A carriage isolates included in this study were obtained from < 5 years children. Details of CHRF pneumococcal surveillance programs of all modalities (invasive, otitis media, and nasopharyngeal carriage) are described in Supplementary Methods (Additional File 1; Supplementary Methods).

Laboratory detection of pneumococcus and serotype determination

Blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from suspected IPD patients, ear discharge specimens from OM patients, and NP swabs from study participants were cultured at CHRF-collaborating microbiology laboratories in all four hospitals using previously described standardized methodologies [37]. Cultures were performed on blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar. Suspected pneumococcal colonies were confirmed by optochin sensitivity (zone ≥ 14 mm), while strains with a zone of 9–13 mm were further tested for bile solubility [37]. Urine samples, processed as a part of the routine hospital services at the CHRF microbiology lab at BSHI, were also cultured on the same media.

Pneumococcal isolates from culture-positive samples were serotyped using the Quellung reaction and tested for antibiotic susceptibility via disc diffusion and E-test methods, following CLSI guidelines [2, 38]. For the culture-negative CSF samples (no bacterial growth on culture plates), pneumococcal detection was done using immunochromatography-based test (ICT), BinaxNOW Streptococcus pneumoniae Antigen Card (Abbott, Chicago, IL, USA) and quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting lytA gene [35, 39–41]. All ICT-positive samples were serotyped using multiplex qPCR [42, 43]. Notably, all qPCR-positive samples were also ICT-positive. All culture-positive serotype 19A isolates from blood and CSF samples (referred as invasive samples) were selected for whole genome sequencing (WGS) and analysis. From OM and NP carriage studies, total 99 and 96 serotype 19A isolates were identified, respectively. Half of the 19A isolates from OM (51/99) were randomly selected for WGS based on the year of isolation, while 65% (62/96) of the 19A isolates from NP were selected (based on year of isolation) randomly for WGS. All serotype 19A isolates from urine specimens (n = 3) were included for WGS.

Serotype 19A hospital incidence calculation

The proportion of serotype 19A amongst all pneumococcus from hospitalized IPD patients was calculated from the four different study hospitals. Culture-negative cases detected using ICT were also included. Yearly hospital incidence of serotype 19A (per 10,000 eligible suspected IPD cases in children < 5 years) was calculated as the proportion of 19A among eligible-enrolled children (with collected specimen), adjusted by the enrolment ratio (enrolled/eligible), and scaled to 10,000 eligible suspected IPD cases. Details are provided in the Supplementary Methods, and year-wise values are shown in Table S2 (Additional file 1). The line graphs showing the adjusted hospital incidence of serotype 19A per 10,000 eligible suspected IPD cases (Fig. 1) were created using the Python Matplotlib library (https://matplotlib.org/).

Whole-genome sequencing

All selected pneumococcal isolates were subcultured on blood agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C. In case of any visible contamination on plates, a single colony was selected and subcultured again. Colonies were then swabbed and suspended in 800 µL of 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by centrifugation at 9,000Xg for 2 min. The supernatant was discarded, and pellet was used for DNA extraction using an optimized protocol with the Qiagen DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The protocol includes a pre-incubation phase with a mutanolysin and lysozyme enzyme mix (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 60 min at 37 °C, followed by another incubation with proteinase K for 30 min at 56 °C.

WGS was conducted under three projects: the Global Pneumococcal Sequencing Project (GPS), GPS2, and the CHRF Pneumococcal Research and Sequencing Effort (CHRF-PARSE).

GPS project: Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (150-bp paired-end, PE150) at the Oxford Genomics Centre, UK (2005–2018; 28 isolates).CHRF-PARSE project: DNA was sent to Novogene, Hong Kong, for sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (PE125; 2005–2020; 68 isolates).GPS2 project: Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NextSeq2000 platform (PE150) at the CHRF Genomics Center in Dhaka, Bangladesh (2014–2023; 57 isolates).

In total, 153 isolates were sequenced, with metadata available in Data S1 (Additional File 1). All Illumina sequencing output were received in fastq files.

Long-read sequencing of one serotype 19A isolate (ID: SPN_0109; Sample Accession: ERS22716356) was performed using the Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) sequencing platform at the CHRF Genomics Center in Dhaka, Bangladesh. Nanopore libraries were generated using a protocol reported earlier [44], following the Rapid sequencing DNA-PCR barcoding protocol (SQK-RPB004) and sequencing on a MinION MK1B device (R9 flow cell) using the MinKNOW platform (2000-bp single-end, SE). The guppy basecaller v6.0.1 (https://nanoporetech.com/document/Guppy-protocol) and qcat v1.1.0 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/qcat) were used for base-calling and to generate fastq files [45].

Using the sequence data, we conducted analyses to confirm the serotype, detect GPSC, ST, resistance markers, and subtypes, performed hybrid assembly to reconstruct the cps loci. Phylogenetic analyses (RAxML and BEAST) were also conducted to investigate the evolutionary pattern of 19A in Bangladesh (Additional File 3: Data S2). Detailed methodologies for these analyses are provided in the Supplementary Methods (Additional File 1).

Global comparison of 19A, visualization, and statistical analysis

For global comparison, a WGS dataset of 1,753 serotype 19A were acquired from the GPS project [23] and the Monocle database (https://data-viewer.monocle.sanger.ac.uk/project/gps) (Additional File 4: Data S3). Subtypes of 19A, ST, and GPSC for all global isolates were detected using the same tools described above. For these global data, GPSCs and 19A-subtypes are presented in Table S4 (Additional File 1), while the total number of cases per country and years of isolation are shown in Table S5 (Additional File 1). The global distribution of different subtypes of 19A, as well as GPSCs and STs, were visualized using R version 4.3.3. Comparative alluvial plots were generated with the alluvial package v0.1-2. Additionally, comparisons of genes within the capsular (cps) loci between serotype 19A and its subtype were performed using the gggenes package v0.5.1. All figures and statistical tests were conducted using R version 4.3.2. All codes used to create the figures can be found in CHRF-Genomics Github (https://github.com/CHRF-Genomics/Spn19A ).
