High-dimensional ridge regression with random features for non-identically distributed data with a variance profile
Issa-Mbenard Dabo & Jérémie Bigot

Abstract
The behavior of the random feature model in the high-dimensional regression framework has become a popular issue of interest in the machine learning literature. This model is generally considered for feature vectors x i = Σ 1 / 2 ​ x i ′ x_{i}=\Sigma^{1/2}x_{i}^{\prime} , where x i ′ x_{i}^{\prime} is a random vector made of independent and identically distributed (iid) entries, and Σ \Sigma is a positive definite matrix representing the covariance of the features. In this paper, we move beyond this standard assumption by studying the performances of the random features model in the setting of non-iid feature vectors. Our approach is related to the analysis of the spectrum of large random matrices through random matrix theory (RMT) and free probability results. We turn to the analysis of non-iid data by using the notion of variance profile which is well studied in RMT. Our main contribution is then the study of the limits of the training and prediction risks associated to the ridge estimator in the random features model when its dimensions grow. We provide asymptotic equivalents of these risks that capture the behavior of ridge regression with random features in a high-dimensional framework. These asymptotic equivalents, which prove to be sharp in numerical experiments, are retrieved by adapting, to our setting, established results from operator-valued free probability theory. Moreover, for various classes of random feature vectors that have not been considered so far in the literature, our approach allows to show the appearance of the double descent phenomenon when the ridge regularization parameter is small enough.



Keywords:
Two-layers neural networks; Random features; Ridge regression; Non-identically distributed data; Double descent; Variance profile; Heteroscedasticity; Random Matrices; Deterministic equivalents; Mixture models.

1
Introduction

This study focuses on the classical statistical learning problem in a regression framework. In this setup, we have a dataset consisting of independent pairs of training data
(y_{i},x_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, where each input
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a vector of random variables representing features (or predictors), and each
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is a scalar response variable that we aim to predict. The primary goal in such contexts is to construct an estimator
\hat{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that leverages the training data to make accurate responses for new inputs,
where
\hat{f}
belongs to a given functional class
\mathcal{F}
.
In this study, our focus is on fitting the training data using the Random Features (RF) model
[
RR07
]
. This corresponds to the choice of the following functional class of estimators:

\mathcal{F}_{\text{RF}}^{W}=\left\{f_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{j}h\left(w_{j}^{\top}x/\sqrt{p}\right):(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m},x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\right\},

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the trainable parameters (or weights) of the model, and
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an activation function such as ReLU, sigmoid, or tanh, which introduces non-linearity into the model. The vectors
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
represent the so-called random features, and they are organized into an
m\times p
matrix
W=(w_{1}|w_{2}|\cdots|w_{m})^{\top}

where the entries of the matrix
W
are centered and independent random variables that are identically distributed. These random features are fixed during the training process, and they serve as a way of projecting the input data into a new space where linear methods can be applied more effectively. Importantly, the entries of
W
are uncorrelated with the training data
(y_{i},x_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, meaning that the random feature transformation is independent of the specific realization of the data.

The RF model can be seen as a simplified form of a two-layer neural network, where the weights in the first layer (that is the random features
W
) are kept fixed, and only the second layer’s weights
\theta
are trained. This model has gained considerable attention in recent years, and it serves as a theoretical basis for studying the generalization error in deep learning models. In particular, the RF model has become a popular tool for understanding the double descent phenomenon in machine learning
[
BHMM19
]
, as discussed in several key studies
[
AP20
,
ALP22
,
LCM20
,
HL23
,
PW17
,
dSB20
,
MM22
,
HMRT22
,
DRBK20
,
MYC24
]
.

Existing theoretical studies generally explore the double descent phenomenon within the traditional framework of random matrix theory (RMT). This framework assumes that the number
n
of training samples , the number
p
of predictors, and the number
m
of random features all grow proportionally. This approach has been particularly useful in examining the behavior of various models in high-dimensional statistical learning
[
CL22
]
, where the complexity of the model increases with the dimensions of the data. However, a common assumption in this research area is that the training data
(y_{i},x_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
consists of independent and identically distributed (iid) observations. Specifically, it is often assumed that the random predictors
x_{i}
follow the sampling model

x_{i}=\Sigma^{1/2}x_{i}^{\prime},

(1.1)

where
\Sigma
is a positive definite covariance matrix, and
x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n}^{\prime}
are random vectors with iid entries that have mean zero and unit variance.
Several works, including
[
Péc19
,
BP21
,
PW17
,
AP20
]
, have studied the performances of the RF model under these two assumptions through the prism of RMT. The results established in
[
Péc19
,
BP21
]
are focused on the behavior of the spectrum of the random matrix

H=h\left(\frac{WX_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},

as the dimensions
n,m
and
p
tend to infinity comparably, where
X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
denote the matrix of predictors, and the function
h
is applied element-wise to the matrix
\frac{WX_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}
. These works prove the convergence of the empirical distribution of singular values of
H
towards a limit that is characterized by a fixed-point equation for the related Stieltjes transform. Nevertheless, the non-linearity induced by the activation function
h
makes
H
difficult to study with conventional methods in RMT. To circumvent this issue, these works provide a simpler matrix, that we refer to as the “linear-plus-chaos” approximation of
H
, that shares the same limiting singular-value distribution as that of
H
. This powerful result appeared to be very useful in a statistical context as it was used in various works, see e.g.
[
AP20
,
MM22
,
dSB20
,
HL23
]
, to obtain asymptotic equivalents of the training and prediction risks in the regression framework for the RF model.

In this paper, we aim at providing asymptotic equivalents of the training and test risks in a broader context involving variance profiles (a notion to be defined later) to model settings where the rows of the random matrix
X_{n}
are not necessarily iid. In order to achieve this goal, our approach strongly relies on an extension of the “linear-plus-chaos” approximation of the matrix
H
recently proposed in
[
DM24
]
when the data matrix
X_{n}
and the random features matrix
W
are endowed with variance profiles.

1.1
Main contributions and related works

1.1.1
The use of random matrices with a variance profile

Building on our previous work
[
BDM24
]
on high-dimensional linear regression, we now move beyond the traditional iid assumption by introducing a more flexible framework where the random predictors
x_{i}
are independent but not identically distributed. To this end, we shall assume that

x_{i}=\Sigma_{i}^{1/2}x_{i}^{\prime},

where
\Sigma_{i}
is a diagonal matrix defined as

\Sigma_{i}=\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\text{diag}}(\gamma_{ij}^{2}).

Here, the terms
\gamma_{i,1}^{2},\ldots,\gamma_{i,p}^{2}
represent the variances of the individual components of the random predictors
x_{i}
, allowing for non-identical distributions across different feature vectors. Under this generalized framework, the matrix
X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
of predictors can be expressed as

X_{n}=\Upsilon_{x}\circ X_{n}^{\prime},

where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product (element-wise multiplication) of two matrices. In this formulation,
X_{n}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{\prime\top}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}^{\prime\top}\end{pmatrix}
is a random matrix with iid entries, centered with variance one, representing the underlying randomness of the predictors, while
\Upsilon_{x}=(\gamma_{ij})
is a deterministic matrix that modulates the variances of the individual entries in
X_{n}
. The so-called variance profile matrix

\Gamma_{n}=(\gamma_{ij}^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},

captures the variance of each element in
X_{n}
, and it allows for heterogeneous distributions across different samples. This generalization is useful for modeling elements in a data set for which the assumption of identical distributions may not hold.

1.1.2
Ridge regression with random features

Our analysis begins by using the functional class
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{RF}}^{W}
to fit the training data using ridge regression. This amounts to solve the following optimization problem:

\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\left\|Y_{n}-h\left(\frac{WX_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right)^{\top}\theta\right\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda\|\theta\|^{2}\right),

where
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter, and
Y_{n}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To assess the performances of RF ridge regression in a context where the data may not be identically distributed, we make the assumption that the response variable follows the linear model:

y_{i}=x_{i}^{\top}\beta_{\ast}+\epsilon_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq n.

(1.2)

Here,
\varepsilon_{n}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents a noise vector that is independent of the matrix of predictors
X_{n}
, with
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{n}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{T}]=\sigma^{2}I_{n}
. The vector of regression coefficients
\beta_{\ast}
is also random, and it is assumed to be independent of both
X_{n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}
, characterized by
\mathbb{E}[\beta_{\ast}]=0
and

\mathbb{E}[\beta_{\ast}\beta_{\ast}^{T}]=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{p}I_{p},

where
\alpha>0
represents the average amount of signal strength. By assuming randomness of the regression coefficients
\beta_{\ast}
, we thus focus on the random-effect hypothesis, which corresponds to an average case analysis over a set of dense regression coefficients as argued in
[
DW18
]
. Note the generalization of our approach to data sampled from the regression model
y_{i}=f_{\ast}(x_{i})+\epsilon_{i}
, with
f_{\ast}
a non-linear function, is beyond the scope of this paper. We leave this perspective for future works, and for hints in this direction we refer to
[
MM22
]
where the estimation of non-linear models using RF ridge regression has been studied the iid setting.

The primary aim of this paper is then to evaluate the performance of RF ridge regression within the framework of the linear model stated above, by specifically analyzing the training risk
E_{train}(\lambda)
and the testing (or predictive) risk
E_{test}(\lambda)
. These risks are defined as follows:

\displaystyle E_{train}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|Y_{n}-h\left(\frac{WX_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right)^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(1.3)

\displaystyle E_{test}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\tilde{y}_{i}-h\left(\frac{W\tilde{x}_{i}}{\sqrt{p}}\right)^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}\right)^{2}\right],

(1.4)

where the pairs
(\tilde{x_{1}},\tilde{y}_{1}),...,(\tilde{x}_{\tilde{n}},\tilde{y}_{\tilde{n}})
represent new samples that are independent of the training data
(Y_{n},X_{n})
, and that also follows the linear model
\tilde{y}_{i}=\tilde{x}_{i}^{\top}\beta_{\ast}+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,\tilde{n}
. In this context,
\tilde{x}_{i}
is also defined as
\widetilde{\Sigma}^{1/2}_{i}\tilde{x}^{\prime}_{i}
, where
\tilde{x}^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a random vector with iid centered components, each having unit variance. The covariance structure of
\tilde{x}_{i}
is represented by
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{i}=\text{diag}(\tilde{\gamma}_{i,1}^{2},\ldots,\tilde{\gamma}_{i,p}^{2})
.

The central contribution of this paper lies in deriving two types of asymptotic equivalents for
E_{train}(\lambda)
and
E_{test}(\lambda)
. The first (respectively the second) type of asymptotic equivalents referred to as “lozenge equivalents” (respectively “square equivalents”) consists in two values, denoted as
E^{\lozenge}_{train}(\lambda)
and
E^{\lozenge}_{test}(\lambda)
(respectively
E^{\square}_{train}(\lambda)
and
E^{\square}_{test}(\lambda)
), that approximate the training and predictive risks in the high dimensional regime in the following sense:

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty,\;p/n\to c_{p}\\
m/n\to c_{m}\end{subarray}}|E_{train}(\lambda)-E^{\bullet}_{train}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty,\;p/n\to c_{p}\\
m/n\to c_{m}\;\tilde{n}/n\to\tilde{c}\end{subarray}}|E_{test}(\lambda)-E^{\bullet}_{test}(\lambda)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(1.6)

for
\bullet\in\{\lozenge,\ \square\}
.

The derivation of these asymptotic equivalents is facilitated by the fact that
\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}
has the explicit expression:

\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}=(HH^{\top}+n\lambda I_{m})^{-1}HY_{n}=H(H^{\top}H+n\lambda I_{n})^{-1}Y_{n},

where
H=h\left(\frac{WX_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. To analyse the predictive risk, we also introduce the notation

\tilde{X}_{\tilde{n}}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{x}_{1}^{\top}\\
\ldots\\
\tilde{x}_{\tilde{n}}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\tilde{\Upsilon}_{x}\circ\tilde{X}_{\tilde{n}}^{\prime}},\;\tilde{Y}_{\tilde{n}}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{y}_{1}\\
\ldots\\
\tilde{y}_{\tilde{n}}\end{pmatrix},\mbox{ and }\tilde{H}=h\Big(\Big\{\frac{W\tilde{X}_{\tilde{n}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\Big\}\Big).

Now, introducing the resolvent

Q(-\lambda)=(H^{\top}H/n+\lambda I_{n})^{-1},

one has that
\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}=HQ(-\lambda)Y_{n}/n
. The resolvent matrix
Q(-\lambda)
is well known in RMT as it is associated with understanding the singular values distribution of the matrix
H/\sqrt{n}
, providing critical insights into the spectral behavior of high-dimensional random matrices. As such, both
\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}
and the associated training and predictive risks are intrinsically linked to the spectral distribution of the matrix
H^{\top}H/n
via its resolvent
Q(-\lambda)
.

The asymptotic equivalents that are derived in this paper extend the results of
[
AP20
]
to the case where
X_{n}
is a random matrix with a variance profile. One of our contributions is then to show that, for some variance profiles, the expression of
E_{train}
matches with the case of constant variance profiles (corresponding to iid data) dealt by
[
AP20
]
.
The derivation of
E^{\lozenge}_{train}(\lambda)
and
E^{\lozenge}_{test}(\lambda)
mainly rely on the results of
[
DM24
]
that uses the so-called traffic theory in free probability
[
Mal20
]
to prove that the expressions of
E^{\lozenge}_{train}(\lambda)
and
E^{\lozenge}_{test}(\lambda)
are simply obtained by replacing
H
with its “linear-plus-chaos” approximation
H^{\lozenge}
(defined in Section
1.1.5
) in the closed-form expressions of
E_{train}(\lambda)
and
E_{test}(\lambda)
that are given in Section
3.1
. Nevertheless, these first asymptotic equivalents remain expectations of random quantities that depend on the random matrices
X_{n}
and
W
. To the contrary, the second set of asymptotic equivalents
E^{\square}_{train}(\lambda)
and
E^{\square}_{test}(\lambda)
only depend on the variance profiles
\Gamma_{n}=(\gamma_{ij}^{2})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}=(\tilde{\gamma}_{ij}^{2})
. The construction of this second set of asymptotic equivalents heavily relies on results related to the notion of amalgamation over the diagonal in free probability
[
ACD+21
]
of block matrices whose blocs are diagonal. These asymptotic equivalents are discussed in more detail in Sections
1.1.5
and
1.1.6
.

1.1.3
Application to features following a mixture model

As an application, our methodology offers a novel framework for analyzing predictors that arises from a mixture model, that is when the data come from multiple underlying subpopulations. Indeed, consider latent class variables
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
(assumed to be fixed in our analysis), which determine the class membership of each feature vector
x_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Within each class, the random predictor
x_{i}
is then assumed to have a specific covariance structure as follows. If
x_{i}
belongs to the
k
-th class among
K
possibilities, that is
C_{i}=k
for some
1\leq k\leq K
, we model this predictor as

x_{i}=S_{k}^{1/2}x_{i}^{\prime},

where
S_{k}=\text{diag}(s_{k,1}^{2},\ldots,s_{k,p}^{2})
is a diagonal matrix that characterizes the covariance structure of the predictors within the
k
-th class. The variances
s_{k,j}^{2}
of the predictors vary across classes, reflecting potential heterogeneity in the data. Then, given the class labels
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
, the resulting matrix of predictors
X_{n}
exhibits a variance profile governed by the matrix

\Gamma_{n}=(s_{C_{i},j}^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},

where each entry
s_{C_{i},j}^{2}
corresponds to the variance of the
j
-th coordinate of the
i
-th feature vector, determined by its class membership
C_{i}
.

E_{test}(\lambda)

, a well-known image classification dataset comprising handwritten digits from 0 to 9. This dataset follows a 10-class mixture model where the class

S_{k}=\text{diag}(s_{k})
with

of dimension

. These diagonal matrices are normalized so that they satisfy Assumption

below. Then, we simulate data from the regression model (

) for the variance profiles

\mathbf{1}_{n_{0}}s_{2}^{\top}\\

\vdots\\
\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{n}_{0}}s_{K}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times p}\;\mbox{ with }\tilde{n}=K\tilde{n}_{0},

(1.7)

where
\mathbf{1}_{n_{0}}
denotes the vector of length
n_{0}
with all entries equal to one. We then apply RF ridge regression with
h(x)=x^{3}
for several values of
\lambda
. In all the numerical experiments reported in the paper, the entries of

,

and

are chosen as iid random variables sampled from the standard Gaussian distribution

1.1.4
The linear plus chaos approximation

The inherent non-linear randomness of the matrix
H
induced by the activation function
h
complicates the analysis of the spectrum of
H^{\top}H/n
when the dimensions
n,p
and
m
tend to infinity proportionally. For iid predictors, these complexities have already been addressed in the literature, notably in
[
Péc19
,
BP21
]
, which build on the fundamental results established in
[
PW17
,
AP20
]
. These studies focus on the setting where the entries of both matrices
W
and
X_{n}
are iid centered random variables with variances
\sigma^{2}_{w}
and
\sigma^{2}_{x}
respectively, effectively addressing scenarios involving iid data. The results from
[
BP21
]
reveal that the spectral distribution of
H/\sqrt{n}
converges to a limiting distribution that coincides with the limiting spectral distribution of the matrix
H_{lin}
, referred to as the “linear-plus-chaos” approximation of
H
, that is defined by:

\frac{H_{lin}}{\sqrt{n}}=\sqrt{\theta_{2}(h)}\frac{W{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{n}^{\top}}}{\sqrt{np}}+\sqrt{\theta_{1}(h)-\theta_{2}(h)}\frac{Z^{G}}{\sqrt{n}},

(1.8)

where the terms
\theta_{1}(h)
and
\theta_{2}(h)
are defined as

\theta_{1}(h)=\mathbb{E}\left[h\left(\sigma_{x}\xi\right)^{2}\right],\quad\theta_{2}(h)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{x}h^{\prime}\left(\sigma_{x}\xi\right)\right]^{2},

for
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
Z^{G}
denotes an independent matrix with standard Gaussian iid entries. The matrix
H_{lin}
simplifies the analysis as it excludes the non-linear transformations introduced by the activation function
h
applied to entries of the random matrix
\frac{WX^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}
. The “linear-plus-chaos” approximation (
1.8
), also referred to in the literature as a Gaussian equivalence model, serves as a foundation in various studies
[
AP20
,
ALP22
,
dSB20
,
MM22
,
HL23
]
that analyze the training and predictive risks of RF ridge regression with predictors following the model
x_{i}=\Sigma^{1/2}x_{i}^{\prime}
.

In the recent work
[
DM24
]
, significant progress have been made to derive a “linear-plus-chaos” approximation of
H
in the setting where

W={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Upsilon_{w}\circ W^{\prime}}\quad\text{and}\quad X_{n}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Upsilon_{x}\circ X^{\prime}},

(1.9)

with matrices
\Upsilon_{w}
and
\Upsilon_{x}
inducing arbitrary variance profiles, and
W^{\prime}
and
X^{\prime}
are matrices made of iid entries, each having zero expectation and variance one.

Recalling that

H=h{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left(\frac{WX_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right)},

the results in
[
DM24
]
[Theorem 3.1] reveal that the matrix
H/\sqrt{n}
can be asymptotically approximated by the following “linear-plus-chaos” decomposition

\frac{H^{\lozenge}}{\sqrt{n}}=\Theta_{lin}(h)\circ\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{np}}\right)+\Theta_{chaos}(h)\circ\frac{Z^{G}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(1.10)

In the above equation,
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{X}_{n}
are random matrices defined as

\mathcal{W}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Upsilon_{w}\circ W^{G}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{X}_{n}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Upsilon_{x}\circ X^{G}},

(1.11)

where
W^{G}
,
X^{G}
, and
Z^{G}
are independent matrices, each made of iid entries sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution. The decomposition (
1.10
) emphasizes the role of the variance profiles
\Upsilon_{w}^{\circ 2}
and
\Upsilon_{x}^{\circ 2}
in influencing the behavior of the matrix
H
. The matricial terms
\Theta_{lin}(h)
and
\Theta_{chaos}(h)
are critical to the approximation of
H
by
H^{\lozenge}
, and they are defined as follows

\Theta_{lin}(h)=\mathbb{E}\left(h^{\prime}\left(\xi M_{2}\right)\right),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Theta_{chaos}(h)=\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}\geq 2}\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell}!}\mathbb{E}\left(h^{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell})}\left(\xi M_{2}\right)\right),

(1.12)

where
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The quantity
M_{2}
is a
m\times n
matrix defined as:

M_{2}=\sqrt[\circ]{p^{-1}\Upsilon_{w}^{\circ 2}(\Upsilon_{x}^{\top})^{\circ 2}},

where the notation
A^{\circ 2}=A\circ A
refers to the Hadamard product of the matrix
A
with itself, while
\sqrt[\circ]{A}=(\sqrt{a_{ij}})
indicates an element-wise square root of the matrix
A
. We define
\tilde{H}^{\lozenge}
accordingly by replacing, in Equation (
1.10
), the quantities depending on the training data by equivalent quantities depending on the test data and the variance profile
\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}=(\tilde{\gamma}_{ij}^{2})
, that is

\frac{\tilde{H}^{\lozenge}}{\sqrt{\tilde{n}}}=\tilde{\Theta}_{lin}(h)\circ\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}\tilde{\mathcal{X}_{n}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{\tilde{n}p}}\right)+\tilde{\Theta}_{chaos}(h)\circ\frac{\tilde{Z}^{G}}{\sqrt{\tilde{n}}}.

(1.13)

The results from
[
DM24
]
indicate that
\frac{H}{\sqrt{n}}
and
\frac{H^{\lozenge}}{\sqrt{n}}
have asymptotically the same spectral distribution. Furthermore, their asymptotic behavior under polynomial transformations are identical thanks to the following proposition that is a direct consequence of
[
DM24
]
[Theorem 3.1].

Proposition 1.1
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
2.3
, given in Section
2
, and for any polynomial

1.1.6
Square equivalents

When
W
has a constant variance profile, it can be seen from Equation (

becomes a non-commutative matrix polynomial in the variables
\mathcal{X}_{n},\mathcal{W},D_{lin}(h),D_{chaos}(h)
and

. Therefore, using the so-called linearization trick
[
MS17
]
[Chapter 10] in free probability, we are able to obtain a deterministic equivalent of the spectral distribution of the random matrix

, which leads to the construction of the second set of asymptotic equivalents of the training and predictive risk in RF regression. This linearization trick is detailed in Section

.

For specific classes of variance profile and choices of activation function

\Gamma_{n}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}
.

Assumption 1.1
.

There exists a constant

such that the rows of the variance profile of

and

1.1
means that the matrices

and

are row stochastic. Under Assumption

,

and

) and (

\displaystyle H^{\lozenge}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{lin}(h)\frac{\mathcal{W}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}+\theta_{chaos}(h)Z^{G}

\displaystyle\tilde{H}^{\lozenge}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{lin}(h)\frac{\mathcal{W}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\tilde{n}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}+\theta_{chaos}(h)\tilde{Z}^{G},

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha^{2}}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\lozenge^{\prime}}(-\lambda)\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{p}\right]\Bigg]+\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\lozenge^{\prime}}(-\lambda)\right]\Bigg],

\displaystyle E^{\lozenge}_{test}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{p\tilde{n}}\Tr\Bigg[\mathrm{deg}(\tilde{\Gamma}_{\tilde{n}})\Bigg]+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\tilde{n}}\Tr\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{pn^{2}}\tilde{H}^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)H^{\lozenge\top}\tilde{H}^{\lozenge}\right]\Bigg]

The proof of Theorem

[

[Chapter 10] (sometimes called the linear pencil

(3.1)

In the following theorem, it is shown that the resolvent

Theorem 3.2
.

Under Assumptions

2.5

for any

4.1
Derivation of the Lozenge asymptotic equivalents using the traffic theory

The expressions of the training and predictive risks provided by Lemma
3.1
are not tractable enough because of the non-linearity caused by
h
in
H
,
\tilde{H}
and
Q(-\lambda)
. Fortunately, Proposition
1.1
allows us to find more convenient approximations for
E_{train}
and
E_{test}
by replacing
H,X_{n},Q
with
H^{\lozenge},\mathcal{X}_{n},Q^{\lozenge}(\lambda)
as precisely stated in Theorem
3.1
that we prove below.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a complex such that
\Im m(z)>2\ \underset{n\geq n_{0}}{\mathrm{sup}}\{\parallel\frac{H^{\top}H}{n}\parallel;\parallel\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{n}\parallel\}
for some sufficiently large
n_{0}
. Under Assumption
2.2
and Assumption
2.3
, we have that the entries of the diagonal matrices
D_{lin}(h)
and
D_{chaos}(h)
appearing in the “linear-plus-choas” approximation (
1.16
) are uniformly bounded. Hence, arguing as in Remark
2.1
, we may prove that, for
H^{\lozenge}
satisfying (
1.16
), there exists a constant
C>0
such that, with probability one,
\parallel\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{n}\parallel\leq C
for all sufficiently large values of
n
. Moreover, Assumption
2.4
asserts that there exists a constant
C>0
such that, with probability one,
\parallel\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{n}\parallel\leq C
. These two inequalities ensure the existence of such a
z
. Consequently, we can differentiate
Q(z)=\frac{1}{z}\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(\frac{H^{\top}H}{zn}\right)^{k}
and
Q^{\lozenge}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{zn}\right)^{k}
term by term, which yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\prime}(z)\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{p}-Q^{\lozenge^{\prime}}(z)\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{p}\right]\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z^{2}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{zn}\right)^{k}\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{p}-\left(\frac{H^{\top}H}{zn}\right)^{k}\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{p}\right]\Bigg]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{z^{2}}\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{k}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{zn}\right)^{k}\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{p}-\left(\frac{H^{\top}H}{zn}\right)^{k}\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{p}\right]\Bigg].

Each terms of these sums tends to zero as
n
goes to
+\infty
as stated by (
1.14
). Moreover,
A,B\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{n}\Tr[AB^{\ast}]]
being an Hermitian product, one has from the Cauchy-Schwarz and triangle inequalities that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\left(\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{zn}\right)^{k}\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{p}-\left(\frac{H^{\top}H}{zn}\right)^{k}\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{p}\Bigg]\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\frac{H^{\top}H}{zn}\right)^{k}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{zn}\right)^{k}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]},

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{H^{\top}H}{zn}\right\|^{2k}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{zn}\right\|^{2k}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}.

Since
\Im m(z)>2\ \underset{n\geq n_{0}}{\mathrm{sup}}\{\parallel\frac{H^{\top}H}{n}\parallel;\parallel\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{n}\parallel\}
, one has that
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\frac{H^{\top}H}{zn}\right)^{k}\right\|^{2}\right]},\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{zn}\right)^{k}\right\|^{2}\right]}<\frac{1}{2}
, then

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\left(\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{zn}\right)^{k}\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{p}-\left(\frac{H^{\top}H}{zn}\right)^{k}\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{p}\Bigg]\right]\right|<\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k}\left[\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}\right].

The terms
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]
are actually the second moment of
\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}
and
\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}
. The matrices
X^{\prime}
and
X^{G}
(See Equations (
1.9
) and (
1.11
)) being random matrices whose iid entries are centered with finite moments of all order, the second moment
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{X^{\prime}X^{{}^{\prime}\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\mathcal{X}^{G}\mathcal{X}^{G\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]
can be proved to converge as
n\rightarrow+\infty
from the moment method. One can deduce from this that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]
are also bounded since it is proved in
[
DM24
]
that the presence of variance profiles does not affect the proof of convergence of these quantities using the moment method. Then

\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k}\left[\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}\right]+\sum_{k\geq 1}k\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k}\left[\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\right]}\right]<+\infty.

Thus, we obtain from the dominated convergence theorem, that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\prime}(z)\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{p}-Q^{\lozenge^{\prime}}(z)\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{p}\right]\Bigg]\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0

(4.1)

Let us now prove that this result holds true for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
, to this end, let us define the function

f_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\prime}(z)\frac{X_{n}X_{n}^{\top}}{p}-Q^{\lozenge^{\prime}}(z)\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{p}\right]\Bigg]

for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Proving that (
4.1
) holds for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
is equivalent to prove that
f_{p}(z)
converges uniformly to zero. This function is analytic on
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
since
Q(z)
and
Q^{\lozenge}(z)
are analytic
[
HLN07
]
[Proposition 5.1]. We deduce from
[
HS12
]
[Theorem 5.8] that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\parallel Q_{p}(z)\parallel_{F}\leq\parallel Q_{p}(z)\parallel\leq\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(z,\mathbb{R}^{+})}
and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\parallel Q^{\lozenge}(z)\parallel_{F}\leq\parallel Q^{\lozenge}(z)\parallel\leq\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(z,\mathbb{R}^{+})}
.
Moreover,
\left\|\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}
and
\left\|\frac{X_{n}}{\sqrt{p}}\right\|_{F,n}
are bounded, hence for each compact subset
C\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
f_{p}
is uniformly bounded on
C
with

|f_{n}(z)|\leq\frac{2K}{\delta_{C}},

where
\delta_{C}
is the distance between
C
and
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then, by the normal family Theorem
[
Rud87
]
[Theorem 14.6] there exists a sub-sequence
f_{p_{k}}
which uniformly converges to
f^{\ast}
that is an analytical function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Let
(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence with an accumulation point in
\{z\in\mathbb{C}|\Im m(z)>\mathrm{max}\{\parallel\frac{H^{\top}H}{n}\parallel;\parallel\frac{H^{\lozenge\top}H^{\lozenge}}{n}\parallel\}\}
. Then, for each
k
,
f_{p}(z_{k})\rightarrow 0
with probability one as
p
tends to
+\infty
. This implies that
f^{\ast}(z_{k})=0
for each
k
. We finally obtain that
f^{\ast}
is identically zero on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Therefore
f_{p}
converges uniformly to zero which proves that (
4.1
) holds for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

The other term
\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{{}^{\prime}}(-\lambda)\right]\Bigg]
appearing in the expression of
E_{train}(\lambda)
in Lemma
3.1
can be handled the same way, which finally gives us

E_{train}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha^{2}}{n}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\lozenge^{\prime}}(-\lambda)\frac{\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathcal{X}_{n}^{\top}}{p}\right]\Bigg]+\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathrm{Tr}[\mathbb{E}[Q^{\lozenge^{\prime}}(-\lambda)]]+o(1).

One can proceed the same way for
E_{test}(\lambda)
using its decomposition given in Lemma
3.1
. Indeed, one has that
\mathbb{E}[\|n^{-1/2}H\|_{F,n}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{n}^{-1/2}\tilde{H}\|_{F,n}]
can be shown to be bounded using again arguments from
[
DM24
]
, and we argued earlier that
\mathbb{E}[\|n^{-1/2}X\|_{F,n}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{n}^{-1/2}\tilde{X}\|_{F,n}]
are bounded. Finally, one can prove that
\|Q(-\lambda)\|_{F,n}
is bounded from
[
HS12
]
[Theorem 5.8]. Therefore, one can proceed as previously for
E_{test}(\lambda)
by combining Equation (
1.14
) and the dominated convergence theorem to prove the convergence of each of the term in the expression of
E_{test}(\lambda)
, which completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
. ∎

4.2
Derivation of the Square asymptotic equivalents using free probabiliy

In order to prove Theorem
3.3
, we now construct a deterministic equivalent of
Q^{\lozenge}(\lambda)
, since
E^{\lozenge}_{train}
and
E^{\lozenge}_{test}
depend on this matrix. The linearization trick
[
MS17
]
is often used to approximate resolvent matrices such as
Q^{\lozenge}(\lambda)
. In our case, it turns out that this method allows to approximate more complicated expressions that are matrix polynomials composing the expressions of the training and predictive risks that involve
Q^{\lozenge}(\lambda),H^{\lozenge},\tilde{X},\tilde{W}
. The linearization trick, that we apply in this paper, consists in using the matrix
L
defined by (
2.3
) which is a matrix polynomial of degree one depending on
W,X
and
Z
. Following
[
MS17
]
, the choice of
L
is motivated by Definition
A.1
of the linearization of a matrix that is recalled in the Appendix. This trick is a natural way to ease computations whenever confronted to rational fraction of matrices. Moreover, a systematic way of obtaining a linearization of a polynomial matrix is described in
[
MS17
]
and the case of rational fraction as been studied in
[
Yin18
]
. Now, we recall that, when assuming that
W
has a constant variance profile, the “linear-plus-chaos” approximation writes as Equation (
1.16
).
This polynomial expression for
H^{\lozenge}
has guided us to propose the matrix
L
given by (
2.3
) as its linearization, which is not unique but relevant for our purposes.

Let
\Lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
be a diagonal matrix such that
L-\Lambda
is invertible (the condition
\Im m(\Lambda)>0
ensures the invertibility, see Lemma
2.1
). Recall that
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)=(L-\Lambda)^{-1}
is the resolvent matrix of
L
. We need to compute this resolvent for
\Lambda_{\lambda}=-\lambda I_{n}\otimes E_{11}
, and the resuting expression of
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})
is obtained using the Schur complement formula:

\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})=\begin{pmatrix}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)&\frac{Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)H^{\lozenge\top}}{\sqrt{n}}&-\frac{Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)H^{\lozenge\top}\mathcal{W}}{\sqrt{np}}&-\frac{Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)D_{lin}(h)\mathcal{X}_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\\
\frac{H^{\lozenge}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)}{\sqrt{n}}&\frac{H^{\lozenge}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)H^{\lozenge\top}}{n}-I_{m}&\frac{\mathcal{W}}{\sqrt{p}}-\frac{H^{\lozenge}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)H^{\lozenge\top}\mathcal{W}}{n\sqrt{p}}&-\frac{H^{\lozenge}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)\mathcal{X}_{n}D_{lin}(h)}{n}\\
-\frac{\mathcal{W}^{\top}H^{\lozenge}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)}{\sqrt{np}}&\frac{\mathcal{W}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}}+\frac{\mathcal{W}^{\top}H^{\lozenge}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)H^{\lozenge\top}}{n\sqrt{p}}&\frac{\mathcal{W}^{\top}H^{\lozenge}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)H^{\lozenge\top}\mathcal{W}}{np}-\frac{\mathcal{W}^{\top}\mathcal{W}}{p}&\frac{\mathcal{W}^{\top}H^{\lozenge}Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)D_{lin}(h)\mathcal{X}_{n}}{n\sqrt{p}}-I_{p}\\

(4.2)

As shown in Section

to obtain the second set of asymptotic equivalents for the training and testing errors. This deterministic equivalent of

is obtained by following the methodology developed in

. This work studies deformed GUE matrices with a variance profile defined as

M_{N}=\Upsilon\circ A_{N}+B_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},

where

is a GUE matrix (that is

(a_{ij})_{i<j}
form a sequence of iid standard complex Gaussian variables and its diagonal elements
(a_{ii})

\Upsilon=(\gamma_{N}(i,j))
and

are deterministic Hermitian matrices and
\Gamma=(\gamma_{N}^{2}(i,j))
is a variance profile matrix with non-negative real entries. Under this framework, a deterministic equivalent of the diagonal of the resolvent of

is provided in
[
BM20
]
in the sense of Theorem
A.1
that is recalled in the Appendix for completeness.

Now, as explained in Section

, the linearization

of

can be interpreted as a deformed variance profiled GOE matrix, see the key relation (

).
It would be convenient to use Theorem
A.1
on the decomposition (

) of the linearization

to obtain a relevant deterministic approximation of its resolvent
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})
. However, we cannot use Theorem

since

in (

) is not a GUE matrix. Moreover, Theorem

provides a deterministic equivalent of the diagonal of the resolvent whereas we need a deterministic equivalent for the diagonal of the blocks of the resolvent

via the map

. Nevertheless, by changing some of the proofs in
[
BM20
]
, we have been able to adapt this result to our setting leading to Theorem

\displaystyle+

First, the convergence of the two terms appearing in the decomposition of

\lim_{n\to+\infty}|E_{train}^{\lozenge}(\lambda)-E_{train}^{\square}(\lambda)|=0

E_{train}^{\square}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha^{2}}{\theta_{lin}^{2}(h)p}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathfrak{Q}^{\square^{\prime}}_{44}\right]+\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathrm{Tr}[\mathfrak{Q}^{\square^{\prime}}_{11}].

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbbm{R}^{+}
, we denote by
\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z})
,
\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{N}I_{N})
and
\mathfrak{Q}^{\square^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{N}I_{N})
the respective derivatives of
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{-z})
,
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{N}I_{N})
and
\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})
with respect to
z
, where
\eta_{N}
is defined in (

\displaystyle\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z})-\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{N}I_{N})\right\|_{F,n}\xrightarrow{}0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\square^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})-id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})]\right\|_{F,n}\xrightarrow{}0.

Proof of Corollary
4.1
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbbm{R}^{+}
such that
\Im m(z)>0
and denote
\tilde{\Lambda}_{z}=\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{N}I_{N}
. We first need to notice that
\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z})
,
\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{N}I_{N})
and
\mathfrak{Q}^{\square^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})
are analytic functions, see
[
BM20
]
[Lemma 6.4 and Theorem 1.1], then the Cauchy integral formula yields that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\rho}\frac{\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{w})}{(w-z)^{2}}dw,\quad\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{N}I_{N})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\rho}\frac{\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{w}+i\eta_{N}I_{N})}{(w-z)^{2}}dw

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}^{\square^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{-z}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\rho}\frac{\mathfrak{Q}^{\square^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{w}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})}{(w-z)^{2}}dw,

where
\rho
is a path around
z
in
\{w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}|\Im m(w)>0\}
.

Hence, one has from the triangle inequality that

\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z})-\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})\right\|_{F,n}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\rho}\frac{\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{w})-\mathfrak{Q}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{w})\right\|_{F,n}}{|w-z|^{2}}dw

It is proved in Lemma
A.10
that
\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{w})-\mathfrak{Q}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{w})\right\|_{F,n}
tends almost surely to
0
and that there exists a constant C such that
\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{w})-\mathfrak{Q}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{w})\right\|_{F,n}\leq C\eta_{N}
.
Since
\eta_{N}
tends to 0,
\frac{C\eta_{N}}{|w-z|^{2}}\leq\frac{\bar{C}}{|w-z|^{2}}
with
\bar{C}
a constant independent from
n
. The bound
\frac{\bar{C}}{|w-z|^{2}}
being integrable, we deduce from the dominated convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{z})-\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{z}+i\eta_{N}I_{N})\right\|_{F,n}\xrightarrow{}0\quad\mbox{almost surely}.

(4.4)

The dominated convergence theorem allows us to prove similarly that

\left\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})\right]-\mathfrak{Q}^{{}^{\prime}}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})]\right\|_{F,n}
tends almost surely to
0
. As earlier, one need to bound
\left\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{w})\right]-\mathfrak{Q}^{{}^{\prime}}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{w})]\right\|_{F,n}
by a constant.

One can express
\mathfrak{Q}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})
as a block matrix as we did for
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{z})
allowing us to prove that
\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})\right\|_{F,n}
is bounded independently from
n
as we proved that
\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{z})\right\|_{F,n}
is bounded in the proof of Lemma
A.10
. Thus there exists
\tilde{C}
, such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})\right]-\mathfrak{Q}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})\right\|_{F,n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{C}.

Then, we deduce from (
A.10
) and the dominated convergence theorem, that

\displaystyle\left\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})\right]-\mathfrak{Q}^{{}^{\prime}}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})\right]\right\|_{F,n}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0\quad\mbox{almost surely}.

(4.5)

Once again we prove that
\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Lambda})\right]-\mathfrak{Q}^{\mathrm{\square^{\prime}}}(\tilde{\Lambda})\right\|
tends almost surely to
0
with the dominated convergence theorem.
One has from Lemma
A.2
, that, for
\Lambda
such that
\Im m(\Lambda)\geq\eta_{N}
,

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]-\mathfrak{Q}^{\mathrm{\square}}(\Lambda)\right\|\leq\frac{1}{n}(1+\gamma_{max}^{2}\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\|^{2}/\delta)(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\|^{4}+\kappa\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\|^{2}),

for
0<\delta<1
.
For
N
large enough,
\delta_{N}=\eta_{N}^{2}
, one has that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}(1+\gamma_{max}^{2}\|(\Im m(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z}))^{-1}\|^{2}/\delta_{N})(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}\|(\Im m(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z}))^{-1}\|^{4}+\kappa\|(\Im m(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z}))^{-1}\|^{2})\leq\frac{1}{n}(1+\gamma_{max}^{2})(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}(\eta_{N}^{4}+\kappa\eta_{N}^{2}).

(4.6)

Since the right term of this inequality tending to
0
it is bounded independently from
n
and
\Lambda
, then one has from the dominated convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})\right]-\mathfrak{Q}^{\mathrm{\square^{\prime}}}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})\right\|\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0.

(4.7)

One can combine the triangle inequality with (

) and (

), to obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}^{\square^{\prime}}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})-\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})]\right\|_{F,n}\xrightarrow{}0\quad\mbox{almost surely}.

(4.8)

Since we have bounded
\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{z})-\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{z}+i\eta_{N}I_{N})\right\|_{F,n}
and
\left\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}^{\square^{\prime}}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{z})-\mathfrak{Q}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{-z})]\right\|_{F,n}
independently from

, one can prove (

) and (

) for

as in the proof of Theorem

.
∎

Now, to prove the convergence of the various terms appearing in the decomposition of
E_{test}^{\lozenge}(\lambda)
, we need further results beyond the one of Corollary
4.1
that are given below.
First, since
\frac{1}{n}\Tr(AA^{\star})\leq\|A\|^{2}
for every matrix

that, for

\left\|\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)]-\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}^{\square}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F,n}^{2}\xrightarrow{}0\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

(4.9)

Furthermore, one can deduce from the above equation and Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality that, for any diagonal matrix

with uniformly bounded entries,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\Tr[D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)-D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}^{\square}(\Lambda)]\xrightarrow{}0\quad\mathrm{a.s.}.

(4.10)

At last, we will also need to use the following corollary.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\Lambda\in\mathrm{D}_{N}(\mathbb{C})^{+}
such that
\Im m(\Lambda)>\eta_{N}
, where

is defined in (

). Then, one has that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F}^{2}\right]-\frac{1}{n}\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F}^{2}\xrightarrow{}0\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

1\leq I,J\leq 4
and any deterministic diagonal matrix
D

Proof of Corollary
4.2
.

We first remark that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left(\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F}-\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|_{F}\right)\left(\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F}+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|_{F}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|-\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|\right)\left(\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|D\right\|\left\|D\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[D\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|\left(\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|D\right\|^{2}\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|\left(\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right\|+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|\right)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|=O(\eta_{N}^{2}).

(4.11)

Note that the blocks

are made of products of

,

,
\mathcal{X}/\sqrt{n}
and

.
These matrices can be proved to be bounded in operator norm, indeed

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}_{IJ}(\Lambda)\|\leq\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda)\|\leq\eta_{N}^{-1}.

tends to

5
Numerical experiments

This section gathers numerical experiments that illustrate the theoretical results of this work. These experiments are conducted for simulated data following a mixture model with variance profiles as in (
1.7
) that are computed from the MNIST database as explained in Section
1.1.3
.

In order to compute
E^{\square}_{\text{train}}
and
E^{\square}_{\text{test}}
, it is needed to calculate the matrix
\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}
. Although we do not possess an explicit analytical expression for this matrix, it can be effectively approximated using a fixed-point algorithm to solve Equation (
3.2
). This algorithm plays a central role in our numerical experiments, as it enables us to obtain accurate approximation of
E^{\square}_{\text{train}}
and
E^{\square}_{\text{test}}
. Moreover, it provides a basis for comparison between different error metrics, namely
E_{\text{train}}
,
E^{\square}_{\text{train}}
, and
E^{\lozenge}_{\text{train}}
(respectively
E_{\text{test}}
,
E^{\square}_{\text{test}}
, and
E^{\lozenge}_{\text{test}}
). In particular, the construction of
E^{\square}_{\text{test}}
follows from a structured modification of the original expression of
E_{\text{test}}
. Specifically, we replace the sub-blocks of the matrix
\mathfrak{Q}
with their corresponding sub-blocks in
\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}
. This substitution ensures that
E^{\square}_{\text{test}}
incorporates the refined approximation encoded in
\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}
, thereby allowing for a direct comparison between the original and approximated errors. In all the figures of this section, dashed curves correspond to the true predictive error

f
the following function

f(M)=\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(\Delta\left[\left(\sum_{i\leq j}m_{i,j}F_{ij}+C_{N}-\Lambda\right)^{-1}\right]\right).

Since
\mathcal{R}_{N}
is linear, one has that

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i,j}^{\prime}}f_{2}\left(\left(x_{i,j}^{\prime}\right)_{i,j}\right)=\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(-\left(L-\Lambda\right)^{-1}F_{ij}\left(L-\Lambda\right)^{-1}\right)=\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(-\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)F_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right).

Thus we obtain from Proposition
A.1
(Gaussian Poincaré inequality) that

\displaystyle\mathbbm{V}ar(D_{n}(k,k))\leq\frac{\gamma_{max}^{2}}{n}\mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{i\leq j}\left|\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)F_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right)(k,k)\right|^{2}\right].

(A.3)

We denote by
(e_{k})_{k\in[N]}
the canonical basis of
\mathbbm{C}^{N}
. Recalling that the entry
(ij)
of a matrix
M
is equal to
e_{i}^{\ast}Me_{j}
and that
E_{ij}=e_{i}e_{j}^{\ast}
. Since
F_{ij}
is a Hermitian matrix, one has that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)F_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right)(k,k)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)F_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right)(k,k)\times\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)F_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)\right)(k,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\frac{\gamma^{2}_{kk_{1}}\gamma^{2}_{kk_{2}}}{n^{2}}e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)F_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)e_{k_{1}}e^{\ast}_{k_{2}}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)F_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}

Note that for any matrix
M=(m_{ij})
, one has that

\displaystyle E_{ij}ME_{ij}=m_{ji}E_{ij},

\displaystyle E_{ij}ME_{ji}=m_{jj}E_{ii}

\displaystyle E_{ij}ME_{ij}=e_{j}e_{j}^{\ast}Me_{i}e_{i}^{\ast},

\displaystyle E_{ij}ME_{ji}=e_{i}e_{j}^{\ast}Me_{j}e_{i}^{\ast}.

Let us define
M=\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)e_{k_{1}}e^{\ast}_{k_{2}}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)
, then

\displaystyle e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)E_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)e_{k_{1}}e^{\ast}_{k_{2}}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)E_{ji}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)E_{ij}ME_{ji}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)e_{i}e_{j}^{\ast}Me_{j}e_{i}^{\ast}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)(k_{1},i)\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)(j,k_{1})\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)(k_{2},j)\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)(i,k_{2}),

which gives us

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\delta_{ij}}\sum_{i\leq j}e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)E_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)e_{k_{1}}e^{\ast}_{k_{2}}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)E_{ji}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}+e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)E_{ji}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)e_{k_{1}}e^{\ast}_{k_{2}}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)E_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)(k_{1},i)\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)(j,k_{1})\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)(k_{2},j)\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)(i,k_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2})(\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda))(k_{2},k_{1}).

We also have the following

\frac{1}{1+\delta_{ij}}\sum_{i\leq j}E_{ij}ME_{ij}+E_{ji}ME_{ji}=M^{\top},

thus

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\delta_{ij}}\sum_{i\leq j}e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)E_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)e_{k_{1}}e^{\ast}_{k_{2}}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)E_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}+e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)E_{ji}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)e_{k_{1}}e^{\ast}_{k_{2}}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)E_{ji}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\delta_{ij}}\sum_{i\leq j}e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)E_{ij}ME_{ji}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)M^{\top}\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\bar{Q}_{L}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}e^{\ast}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)e_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\bar{Q}_{L}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2})\times(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2}),

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\left|\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)F_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right)(k,k)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{max}^{4}}{n^{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left|(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2})(\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda))(k_{2},k_{1})\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma_{max}^{4}}{n^{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left|(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\bar{Q}_{L}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2})\times(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2})\right|.

we obtain from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\left|\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)F_{ij}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right)(k,k)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{max}^{4}}{n^{2}}\left(\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left|(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\times\left(\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left|(\mathfrak{Q}^{\ast}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma_{max}^{4}}{n^{2}}\left(\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left|(\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\bar{Q}_{L}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\times\left(\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left|(\hat{Q}_{L}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda))(k_{1},k_{2})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{max}^{4}}{n^{2}}\left(\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\bar{Q}_{L}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)Q^{\ast}_{L}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\gamma_{max}^{4}}{n}\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{4}.

Hence, by combining the above inequality with (
A.3
), one has that

\mathbbm{V}ar(D_{n}(k,k))\leq\frac{2\gamma_{max}^{6}}{n^{2}}\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{4}.

This inequality along with (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), finally give us

\left\|O_{n}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{n}(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{3}+\kappa\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|),

and completes the proof of Lemma
A.8
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

We have from Lemma
A.7
that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]=\left(C_{N}-\Omega_{L}(\Lambda)\right)^{-1}+\Theta_{N},

with
\Theta_{N}=\left(C_{N}-\Omega_{L}(\Lambda)\right)^{-1}O_{N}
. Moreover, since
\Im m(\Omega_{L}(\Lambda))>\Im m(\Lambda)
one has that
\|\Im m(\Omega_{L}(\Lambda))^{-1}\|\leq\|\Im m(\Lambda)^{-1}\|
, then we deduce from Lemma
A.8
that

\displaystyle\|\Theta_{N}\|\leq\frac{1}{n}(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{4}+\kappa\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{2}).

(A.4)

We define the two following diagonal matrices

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{L}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]-\Theta_{N}

\displaystyle\tilde{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda+\mathcal{R}_{N}(\Theta_{N})=\Lambda+\mathcal{R}_{N}(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]-\tilde{Q}_{L}(\Lambda)).

We need
\tilde{(}\Lambda)>0
to use the former lemmas, Let us find when this is true

\displaystyle\Im m(\tilde{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Im m(\Lambda)+\Im m(\mathcal{R}_{N}(\Theta_{N}))

(A.5)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Im m(\Lambda)-\|\mathcal{R}_{N}(\Theta_{N})\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Im m(\Lambda)-\frac{1}{n}(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{4}+\kappa\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{2}).

The last quantity is positive as soon as
\frac{1}{n}(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{4}+\kappa\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{2})
is less than
\lambda_{min}
the diagonal entry of
\Lambda
with the least norm.
Moreover
\lambda_{min}=\frac{1}{\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|}
, thus
\tilde{(}\Lambda)
belongs to
D_{N}(\mathbb{C})^{+}
if

\frac{1}{n}(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{5}+\kappa\left\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\right\|^{3}).

Provided that
\tilde{\Lambda}
belongs to

, one has that

\tilde{Q}_{L}(\Lambda)=\left(C_{N}-\Lambda-\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]\right)\right)^{-1}=\left(C_{N}-\tilde{\Lambda}-\mathcal{R}_{N}\left(\tilde{Q}_{L}(\Lambda)\right)\right)^{-1}=\psi_{\tilde{\Lambda}}(\tilde{Q}_{L}(\Lambda)).

Therefore by Lemma
A.1

\tilde{Q}_{L}(\Lambda)=\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\tilde{\Lambda})
and so

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]=\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\tilde{\Lambda})+\Theta_{N}.

The maps
\Lambda\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]
,
\Lambda\mapsto\Theta_{N}
are analytic, thus
\Lambda\mapsto\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\tilde{\Lambda})
and
\Lambda\mapsto\tilde{\Lambda}

and
\tilde{\Lambda}>0

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]-\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\tilde{\Lambda})-\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda)\|+\|\Theta_{N}\|.

(A.6)

we obtain from Lemma
A.1
that

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\tilde{\Lambda})-\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda)\|\leq\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\|\|(\Im m(\tilde{\Lambda}))^{-1}\|\|\Lambda-\tilde{\Lambda}\|.

(A.7)

We have from Lemma
A.3
that
\|\Lambda-\tilde{\Lambda}\|=\|\mathcal{R}_{N}(\Theta_{N})\|\leq\gamma^{2}_{max}\|\Theta_{N}\|

A.5
Concentration of the resolvent

The following step consists in proving that the block of the resolvent concentrate around their diagonal.

Lemma A.9

(Lemma 7.3 from
[
BM20
]
)
.

Let
\Lambda\in\mathrm{D}_{N}(\mathbb{C})^{+}
. Then, for any pair of unit vectors
v,w
(that is
\|v\|_{2}=

\left.\|w\|_{2}=1\right)
, one has that, for all
t>0

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|v^{*}\left(\left(L-\Lambda\right)^{-1}-\mathbb{E}\left[\left(L-\Lambda\right)^{-1}\right]\right)w\right|\geq t\right)\leq 4\exp\left(-N\frac{t^{2}\left\|(\Im m\Lambda)^{-1}\right\|^{-4}}{2\gamma_{\max}^{2}}\right)

where
\gamma_{\max}^{2}
is the maximum of the variances in the profile
\Gamma_{N}
.

Note that
id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]]
is the following matrix with diagonal blocs

id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]]=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}_{11}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{11}\right]]&0&0&0\\
0&\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}_{22}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{22}\right]]&0&0\\
0&0&\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}_{33}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{33}\right]]&\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}_{34}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{34}\right]]\\
0&0&\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}_{43}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{43}\right]]&\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}_{44}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}_{44}\right]]\end{pmatrix},

where
Q_{IJ}
denotes the
IJ
sub-block of
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)
.
Let us denote the
IJ
block of
id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]]
by
M_{IJ}
, then

\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]]\|\leq\|M_{11}\|+\|M_{22}\|+\|M_{33}\|+\|M_{44}\|+\|M_{34}\|+\|M_{43}\|.

The sub-blocks
M_{IJ}
being diagonal matrices, one has that
\|M_{IJ}\|=\max\limits_{k}|M_{IJ}(k,k)|
, thus

\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]]\|\leq 6\max_{I,J,k}|M_{IJ}(k,k)|.

This maximum is taken over
N+2p
elements, thus combining Inequality (7.3) with a union bound yields the following concentration inequality: for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]]\right\|\geq t\right)\leq 4(N+2p)\exp\left(-N\frac{t^{2}\left\|(\Im m\Lambda)^{-1}\right\|^{-4}}{72\gamma_{\max}^{2}}\right)

(A.10)

Hence, taking

\displaystyle t_{N}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}(1+\gamma_{max}^{2}\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\|^{2}/\delta)(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\|^{4}+\kappa\|(\Im m(\Lambda))^{-1}\|^{2})

\displaystyle t_{N}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 6\gamma_{max}\|\Im m(\Lambda)^{-1}\|^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}\log\left(\frac{N+2p}{N^{1-d}}\right)},

(for some
\left.d>1\right)
, one finally obtains from (
A.10
) and Lemma
A.2
that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta\left[\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda)-\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right]\right\|\geq t_{N}^{(2)}+t_{N}^{(1)}\right)\leq 4N^{1-d},

which finishes the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Note that
\Lambda_{\lambda}
is a real matrix then
\Im m(\Lambda_{\lambda})=0
, which means that it does not meet with the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
. Therefore one cannot obtain a deterministic equivalent of
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})
from this theorem. However one can bypass this issue by going thanks to
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})
, with
\eta_{n}=\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}_{max}}{(1-\delta)n}(2\sqrt{2}\gamma_{max}^{3}+\kappa)\right)^{\frac{1}{5}}
. As a matter of fact,
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})
satisfies the assumption of Theorem
3.2
, thus one has a deterministic equivalent for this matrix, which is
\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})
. Moreover we prove in the following lemma the
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})
is close from
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})
when
n
goes to infinity so that
\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})
is a good approximation of
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})
in high dimension.

Lemma A.10
.

For
\lambda>0
the following limits hold almost surely

\displaystyle\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})-\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})\right\|_{F,n}\xrightarrow{}0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\left\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda_{\lambda})-\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})]\right\|_{F,n}\xrightarrow{}0.

Proof of Lemma
A.10
.

We remark that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda+i\eta_{n}I_{N})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)-i\eta_{n}\mathfrak{Q}^{2}(\Lambda)+\eta^{2}_{n}\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda+i\eta_{n}I_{N})\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda).

Thus one has from the triangle inequality and Lemma
2.2
that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda+i\eta_{n}I_{N})-\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F,n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\eta_{n}\left\|\mathfrak{Q}^{2}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F,n}+\eta^{2}_{n}\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda+i\eta_{n}I_{...})\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F,n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\eta_{n}\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F,n}^{2}+\eta^{2}_{n}\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F,n}\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right\|\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda+i\eta_{n}I_{N})\right\|.

Denote by
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)^{ij}
the
ij
block of
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)
, then one has that
\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right\|_{F,n}\leq\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)\right\|\leq\sum_{ij}\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)^{ij}\right\|
. Note that the blocks
\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)^{ij}
are made of products of
Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)
,
H^{\lozenge}/\sqrt{n}
,
\mathcal{X}/\sqrt{n}
and
\mathcal{W}/\sqrt{p}
.
These matrices can be proved to be bounded in operator norm, indeed
\|H^{\lozenge}/\sqrt{n}\|
,
\|\mathcal{X}/\sqrt{n}\|
and
\|\mathcal{W}/\sqrt{p}\|
are bounded thanks to Assumption
2.3
and by
[
BS98
]
[Theorem 1.1] as argued in Remark
2.1
, and
\|Q^{\lozenge}(-\lambda)\|\leq\lambda^{-1}
by
[
HS12
]
[Theorem 5.8]. Hence, we deduce by sub-multiplicativity of the operator norm that the blocks
\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda)^{ij}\|
are bounded, which means that there exists a constant
c
, such that
\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})\|_{F,n}\leq c
. Moreover, one can deduce from Lemma
A.4
states that
\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda+i\eta_{n}I_{N})\right\|\leq\eta_{n}^{-1}
.
Thus, there is a constant
C
, such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})-\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})\right\|_{F,n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\eta_{n}.

Since
\eta_{n}
tends to
0
as
n
goes to
+\infty
, one has that

\left\|\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda})-\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})\right\|_{F,n}\xrightarrow{}0\quad\mbox{ almost surely}.

Finally, combining this above equation with the triangle inequality and Corollary
3.1
, gives us that

\left\|id_{4}\otimes\Delta[\mathfrak{Q}^{\square}(\Lambda_{\lambda})-\mathfrak{Q}(\Lambda_{\lambda}+i\eta_{n}I_{N})]\right\|_{F,n}\xrightarrow{}0\quad\mbox{ almost surely}.

This completes the proof of Lemma
A.10
.
∎

, which concludes this Appendix.
