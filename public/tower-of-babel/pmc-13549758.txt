The first case of cardiac urogenital syndrome presenting with ambiguous genitalia in Korea
Authors: Bina Kim, Sukdong Yoo, Chong Kun Cheon
Published: 2026

To the editor,

Cardiac urogenital syndrome (CUGS) (OMIM #618280) is a rare multisystem disorder associated with variants in MYRF (myelin regulatory factor) that affect cardiovascular, urogenital, pulmonary, and diaphragmatic development [1-4]. We report the first Korean patient with ambiguous genitalia and a novel MYRF variant.

A 3-month-old boy who had received neonatal intensive care after preterm birth was referred to our institution. He was born at 31+0 weeks' gestation by cesarean delivery because of maternal preeclampsia, with a birth weight of 960 g (<3rd percentile). His family history was notable for intellectual disability in his mother. The patient was referred to pediatric endocrinology for evaluation of ambiguous genitalia.

At a corrected age of 36 days, his growth parameters were as follows: weight, 4.99 kg (50th–90th percentile); head circumference, 36.9 cm (10th percentile); and length, 53.5 cm (10th percentile). Physical examination revealed bilateral exotropia, a reducible umbilical hernia, and a bifid scrotum. Genital examination showed a 0.8-cm glans, a 1.2-cm urethral defect, and a urethral opening at the penoscrotal junction. The stretched penile length was 1.5 cm, and both testes had a volume of 2 mL (Fig. 1). The karyotype was 46,XY.

Based on these findings, a 46,XY disorder of sex development (DSD) was initially suspected. Hormonal testing revealed a luteinizing hormone level of 5.6 mIU/mL (normal range, 0.02–7 mIU/mL), a follicle-stimulating hormone level of 4.3 mIU/mL (normal range, 0.16–4.1 mIU/mL), a testosterone level of 1.12 ng/mL (normal range, 0.37–1.98 ng/mL), an anti-Müllerian hormone level of 24 ng/mL (normal range, 32.77–262.69 ng/mL), and a 5α-dihydrotestosterone level of 41 ng/dL (normal range, 10–53 ng/dL), with a testosterone/5α-dihydrotestosterone ratio of 2.73. The androstenedione level was 0.62 ng/mL (normal range, 0.5–4.49 ng/mL). Abdominal ultrasonography revealed bilateral hydroceles and a cystic lesion in the right epididymal head, suggestive of an epididymal cyst. Imaging revealed no congenital diaphragmatic hernia or Müllerian structures.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified a novel MYRF variant, c.3313C>T (p.Arg1105Trp). Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant in both the patient and his mother (Fig. 2A), whereas his asymptomatic father did not carry it. The patient had no siblings available for testing. The variant was absent from population databases, including gnomAD v4.1.0 and the Korean Reference Genome Database, and was initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) (PM2+PP3).

In silico predictions were mixed: the Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant score was 0.07 (tolerated), whereas the PolyPhen-2 score was 0.964 (probably damaging). After segregation analysis and phenotype matching, the variant remained classified as a VUS (PM2+PP1+PP4) but was considered potentially associated with CUGS.

Protein structural modeling indicated that the R1105W variant may alter hydrophobic interactions with surrounding residues and thereby affect local protein stability (Fig. 2B and C). After identification of the variant, a systemic evaluation was performed. Highresolution chest computed tomography revealed bronchopulmonary dysplasia. Echocardiography demonstrated asymmetric septal hypertrophy and mild left pulmonary artery stenosis. Brain magnetic resonance imaging findings were unremarkable. The patient underwent first-stage chordectomy at 12 months of age, with urethroplasty and hormonal therapy planned at 18 months. At 15 months of age (corrected age, 12 months), he had right-sided torticollis and global developmental delay (Bayley Developmental Test scores: cognitive, 55; language, 59; motor, 46) and was receiving rehabilitation therapy.

CUGS is a rare syndrome associated with MYRF variants. MYRF plays a pivotal role in embryonic development, particularly in the urogenital and cardiac systems [1,2,5,6]. The MYRF variant identified in our patient, c.3313C>T (p.Arg1105Trp), was present in both the proband and his mother, consistent with an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. The mother had a 46,XX karyotype, intellectual disability, and a congenital heart defect, features that have been observed in individuals with MYRF variants. Internal or external genital anomalies are the most common clinical manifestations of CUGS and have been reported in up to 89% of patients [1,2,5,7-9]. Individuals with a 46,XY karyotype may present with a wide spectrum of urogenital abnormalities, ranging from isolated unilateral cryptorchidism to female-appearing external genitalia. Some affected individuals may also have Müllerian structures, such as a uterus or vagina, as well as intellectual disability or motor delay [2,7-9]. CUGS has rarely been reported in individuals with a 46,XX karyotype; only 6 cases have been documented, 3 of which involved atypical internal genitalia. MYRF variants are associated with a broad phenotypic spectrum encompassing cardiac, urogenital, pulmonary, diaphragmatic, and neurodevelopmental abnormalities, with marked variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance. Both 46,XY and 46,XX individuals may present with DSD, and genital anomalies may occur with or without Müllerian structures [10]. These observations further support the clinical variability demonstrated by our patient and his mother. In summary, this case illustrates the broad phenotypic spectrum of CUGS and may represent the first reported Korean patient with ambiguous genitalia possibly associated with a MYRF variant. It also highlights the value of NGS for the early diagnosis and individualized management of rare urogenital anomalies, while acknowledging that functional studies are needed to clarify the pathogenicity of this variant.
