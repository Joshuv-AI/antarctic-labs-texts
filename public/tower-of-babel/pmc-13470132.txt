Intrathecal amylin reverses morphine tolerance through site-specific DNA methylation of Pdyn and Bdnf promoters in rats
Authors: Sara Edalat Behbahani, Hajar Jaberie, Mohsen Tatar, Fatemeh Zal, Ali Akbar Owji, Zahra Khoshdel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic use of opioids such as morphine can lead to tolerance, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, and addiction. Amylin has demonstrated analgesic properties and the ability to reverse morphine properties when injected intrathecally in rats. Our previous study correlated these effects with reduced spinal cord Bdnf expression. To further investigate the mechanisms underlying amylin’s reversal of morphine tolerance, we analyzed the mRNA expression of Pdyn and DNA methyltransferases (Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b), along with the methylation status of Pdyn and Bdnf exon IV promoters in the lumbar spinal cord of rats. Quantitative RT-PCR showed that morphine elevated Pdyn mRNA levels, an effect reversed by co-administration of amylin (1–60 pmol) between days 6 and 10 post-tolerance induction. The highest amylin dose also significantly upregulated Dnmt3a expression. Methylation-specific PCR (MSP-PCR) revealed unmethylated Pdyn and Bdnf promoters in saline-treated and morphine-only rats, but 20–100% methylation in rats treated with both morphine and amylin, and 100% methylation with amylin alone. Bisulfite sequencing confirmed site-specific methylation at CpG − 197 in the Bdnf promoter and CpG − 110 in the Pdyn promoter. These molecular changes, observed in animals with behaviorally confirmed morphine tolerance and amylin- induced reversal, suggest that amylin reverses morphine tolerance through mechanisms associated with Dnmt3a upregulation and site-specific methylation of Pdyn and Bdnf promoters, revealing a novel epigenetic correlation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54573-2.

Introduction

Amylin belongs to the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) family. Members of the family include calcitonin, salmon calcitonin, calcitonin gene-related peptides (α-CGRP and β-CGRP), adrenomedullin and AM2 (also known as intermedin). Amylin is co-secreted with insulin by pancreatic β-cells and exerts various biological functions such as glucose homeostasis, the sensation of satiety and the control of food intake1. This peptide has also been reported to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB)2 and is supposedly able to bind to its receptors in several brain areas to produce effects such as anorexia3. Beyond its metabolic role, amylin is also expressed in the nervous system primarily within CGRP peptidergic neurons of the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) that project to the spinal dorsal horn, indicating a potential involvement in nociceptive signaling4. Amylin receptors consist of a calcitonin-receptor (CTR) core associated with a receptor activity-modifying protein (RAMP)5. Amylin acts at the spinal cord, where CTR and RAMP are expressed in rats4,6. Studies suggest that amylin may be involved in nociceptive processing; however, its effects vary depending on the pain model and the route of administration4. For instance, Intrathecal (i.t.) administration of amylin suppresses the spinal expression of c-fos, a neuronal protein typically upregulated in response to painful stimuli. This downregulation of c-fos in the spinal cord correlates with a reduction in pain induced by acetic acid or by formalin7,8. Amylin administered via intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection markedly increased paw withdrawal latency (PWL) in the hot plate test, but did not alter responses in the tail flick assay9,10. The antinociceptive effects of amylin were shown to be inhibited by amylin antagonists such as salmon calcitonin11. Furthermore, results from our laboratory have also revealed that i.t. amylin, when co-administered with morphine from days 6 to 10, reversed morphine tolerance in rats12.

Chronic administration of opioids such as morphine results in enduring behavioral alterations, including opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH), the development of tolerance, and physical dependence13. These phenomena restrict the therapeutic use of morphine as a potent painkiller. Tolerance is a state of reduced analgesic effect of morphine in which an increase in dose is required to maintain its primary analgesic effects. It is highly desirable to avoid or at least attenuate the development of tolerance14. A number of spinal signaling molecules including neuropeptides15,16, cyclooxygenase inhibitors17, excitatory amino acids18, neurotrophins19 and endogenous opioids20 have been reported to modulate pain sensitivity and opioid tolerance. Consistent with this, molecules like brain- derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and prodynorphin (PDYN) are involved in the control of opioid tolerance21.

The Pdyn gene encodes a precursor protein that, upon processing yields, multiple active peptides of the opioid family, including dynorphin A, dynorphin B, and α- and β-neo-endorphin22. In addition to their antinociceptive effects, mediated through kappa opioid receptors, these peptides also exert non-opioid effects mainly through NMDA receptors. The non opioid effects of dynorphins appear to be relevant to several pathophysiological processes including chronic neuropathic pain and spinal cord injury23. Thus, spinal cord levels of Pdyn mRNA and dynorphin immunoreactivity are elevated in cases of peripheral nerve injury and spinal cord trauma24–26. A recent study has demonstrated that chronic opioid exposure and hind paw incision interact through spinal epigenetic mechanisms to up-regulate Bdnf and Pdyn which are well-established pain related- genes. Specifically, chromatin immunoprecipitation assays revealed that the Bdnf and Pdyn promoters were associated with acetylated histone H3K9 after morphine exposure combined with incision27.

The Bdnf gene in rats consists of eight 5’ untranslated exons (I-VIII) and one translated 3’ exon (IX) which contains the coding sequence for the preproBDNF protein28. This neurotrophin has been suggested to play important roles in the transmission of both physiologic and pathologic pain29. BDNF may also counterbalance the action of morphine in the brain, because as chronic morphine treatment up-regulates Bdnf expression in the brain and lumbar spinal cord30,31, and central injection of anti-BDNF antibody abolishes morphine tolerance32. Furthermore, Bdnf knockout mice fail to develop morphine tolerance33. The promoter of exon IV of the Bdnf gene contains binding sites for transcriptional regulators that have demonstrated implications in neuroplasticity and drug addiction34,35.

Morphine exposure has been shown to influence the epigenetic regulation of genes associated with addiction and tolerance in both human and animal studies36. It is widely known that epigenetic regulation of genes occurs through DNA methylation, histone modification and non-coding RNAs37. DNA methylation is a reversible epigenetic process in which methyl groups are added to the fifth carbon of cytosine at CpG sites in gene promoters, thereby influencing gene expression. This process is catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) which transfer methyl groups from S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM) to specific sites on DNA. DNMT3A and DNMT3B are enzymes responsible for establishing initial methylation patterns on unmethylated DNA and play a crucial role in the early stages of embryonic development and normal cellular differentiation38. DNA methylation can be affected by several factors, including diet, environmental stress, drugs, hormones39. The results of one study indicated that methadone was associated with increased DNA methylation and reduced BDNF serum levels40, while another study demonstrated that repeated morphine use leads to demethylation of the BDNF exon IV promoter and elevated Bdnf gene expression the dorsal root ganglion of rats41. We focused specifically on Bdnf exon IV for several compelling reasons. First and most importantly, our previous work using this exact model demonstrated that exon IV is the predominant Bdnf transcript upregulated in the spinal cord during morphine tolerance, and its suppression by amylin correlates with behavioral reversal of tolerance12. While morphine can affect multiple Bdnf exons in various brain regions, exon IV has emerged as particularly critical in the spinal cord for activity-dependent plasticity and opioid-related processes. Second, exon IV contains a CREB-responsive element and is strongly regulated by neuronal activity and calcium signaling pathways known to be engaged by chronic opioid exposure34,35. Third, in the context of pain and opioid tolerance, several studies have specifically implicated exon IV-containing BDNF transcripts in mediating spinal sensitization processes12,41,42. Therefore, based on our previous findings and the established literature on spinal cord plasticity, we selected exon IV as the most relevant target for investigating amylin’s epigenetic mechanisms in morphine tolerance.

Using spinal cord tissue from the same behaviorally characterized animals from our previous study12, This study is the first investigation to assess the effects of intrathecal amylin on the expression of Pdyn, Dnmt3a, and Dnmt3b genes, as well as the methylation patterns of the Pdyn and Bdnf exon IV promoters in the lumbar spinal cord of rats during the development of morphine tolerance.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Rat amylin was obtained from Bachem Americas, Inc. (Torrance, CA). Morphine sulfate was procured from Temad Company. All other chemicals and reagents utilized in this study were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (Munich, Germany).

Animal surgery and treatment

Adult male Sprague-Dawley rats, with a body weight of 250 ± 20 g, were procured from the Laboratory Animal Center at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Shiraz, Iran. The study received approval from the Medical and Research Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (approval number IR.SUMS.REC.1395.S808). All experimental procedures were compiled with the ARRIVE guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals to ensure transparency and ethical standards. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Rats were anesthetized with ketamine (50 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg), followed by intrathecal catheterization according to the method described by Yaksh and Rudy43. In brief, an incision was made in the cisternal membrane, and a polyethylene catheter (PE-10, Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA) measuring 7.5 cm in length was inserted into the subarachnoid space. The rostral end of the catheter was secured to the adjacent musculature to prevent displacement, and the incision was closed in two layers using 4 − 0 silk sutures. The placement of the caudal tip of the catheter was verified after the animals were euthanized.

Animals displaying neurological impairments during the seven-day recovery period were excluded from further analysis. Subsequently, the rats were divided into eight experimental groups (Table 1).

Drugs and vehicle solutions were administered via intrathecal (i.t.) injection in a volume of 10 µL, followed by a subsequent 10 µL flushe of normal saline to ensure catheter clearance. The final doses of morphine (22 nmol per rat) and amylin (1, 10 and 60 pmol per rat) were each prepared in 10 µL of sterile saline for intrathecal administration. The specific injection protocols were as follows: Saline (S) and Morphine (M) groups: Received a single daily injection of saline or morphine, respectively. Amylin-alone groups (A1, A10, A60): Received a single daily injection of the specified amylin dose. Amylin + Morphine groups (A1 + M, A10 + M, A60 + M): Received two consecutive injections daily: first the specified dose of amylin, followed immediately by the dose of morphine. It is important to note that a control group receiving a vehicle injection prior to morphine was not included. This is a limitation of the study, as it precludes the distinction between the specific pharmacological effects of amylin and potential non-specific effects associated with the dual-injection procedure itself.

All experimental procedures were carried out in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and received approval from the Medical and Research Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Shiraz, Iran.

Behavioral test

The antinociceptive effects of morphine and amylin were evaluated using the tail-flick test according to the method of D’Amour and Smith method44. A radiant heat source (analgesia meter, Tajhiz Gostar, Iran) was applied to the distal tail, and the latency to tail withdrawal was recorded. Baseline latency was set at 2–3 s, with a 10 s cut-off to prevent tissue damage. Results were expressed as the maximal possible effect (MPE%), calculated as:

Chronic morphine tolerance was induced by i.t. morphine (15 µg per rat or 22 nmol) administered once daily for 10 days (9–11 AM). Saline served as the control. Antinociception was assessed 30 min before and after injection. On day 11, acute morphine tolerance was evaluated via cumulative dose-response curves (four ascending doses at 30 min intervals), with tail flick measurements taken 25 min after injection. Tolerance was confirmed through multiple indicators: diminished daily antinociceptive responses, rightward-shifted dose-response curves, and elevated ED50 values. To evaluate amylin’s ability to reverse established tolerance, rats initially received morphine for 5 consecutive days. On days 6–10, experimental groups were pretreated with amylin (1, 10, or 60 pmol per rat) 10 min prior to morphine administration. Behavioral monitoring continued throughout the intervention period, with comprehensive dose-response analysis performed on day 11. Successful reversal of tolerance was demonstrated by both the recovery of antinociceptive responses and the normalization of ED50 values to baseline levels.

Isolation of the Lumbar spinal cord

To euthanize the rats, we used a controlled flow of CO2. The gas was administered at a rate that displaced 30%–70% of the chamber volume per minute. This protocol ensures that the rats rapidly lose consciousness without experiencing distress, following the standards set by the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals45. After sacrificing of the rats, the lumbar spinal cords were isolated, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C until mRNA and DNA extractions were performed. The lumbar spinal cord tissues used for all molecular analyses (qRT-PCR, MSP-PCR, and Bisulfite Sequencing) were collected from the same rats in which the development of morphine tolerance and its reversal by intrathecal amylin were behaviorally characterized. The detailed behavioral data, including daily antinociception and dose-response curves, have been previously published12. This confirms that the molecular data presented herein are directly linked to the behaviorally defined physiological states.

Measurement of mRNA levels by real time reverse transcriptase PCR

Total RNA from the lumbar spinal cords was extracted using the TriPure isolation reagent (Roche Co., Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration and purity were then quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using the Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Fermentas, EU) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression of Pdyn, Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b was determined by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using the ABI 7500 sequence Detection system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Real-time PCR was performed with 12.5 µL RealQ Plus 2X master mix with SYBR Green (Amplicon, Denmark), 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer (0.1 µmol/L) and 1 µL of cDNA template in a final reaction volume of 25 µL. The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR of the aforementioned genes are listed in Table 2. β-actin was used as an internal control for normalization. The expression level of each specific gene was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method46.

Pdyn, Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b genes were amplified under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles consisting of a denaturation step at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 57 °C for 1 min. Each PCR reaction was performed in duplicate. Amplification efficiencies were also calculated from a relative standard curve derived from 10- fold serial dilutions of pooled cDNA.

Methylation-specific PCR(MSP-PCR) analysis

MSP-PCR was used to investigate the DNA methylation status in the promoter regions of the Pdyn gene and exon IV of the Bdnf gene. For this purpose, genomic DNA was extracted from the lumbar spinal cord of rats using the standard method of proteinase K digestion and phenol/chloroform extraction, followed by purification and modification with bisulfite treatment, as previously described47. Bisulfite treatment converts unmethylated cytosine residues to uracil, while methylated cytosines remain unchanged48. The modified DNA samples were amplified using specific primers designed with MethPrimer software (http://urogene.org/methprimer/) for either the methylated (M) or unmethylated (U) alleles of each gene49. The primer sequences and annealing temperatures used for MSP-PCR are listed in Table 3. PCR reactions were performed using a Mastercycler Gradient (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). For the Pdyn gene and exon IV of the Bdnf gene, reactions were carried out in a 50 µL volume containing 1 µL DNA (10 pmol), 1.5 mmol/L MgCl2, 0.2 mmol/L dNTPs, 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer (0.1 µmol/L) and 1.5 IU Taq polymerase. The cycling conditions for the PCR program were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at the specified temperature for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. In each PCR reaction, DNA from peripheral blood lymphocytes and CpGenome rat methylated genomic DNA standard (Millipore, CA) were included as unmethylated and methylated controls, respectively. MSP-PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel, visualized by staining with GelRed, and photographed under UV illumination. In Figs. 1 and 4, related with MSP-PCR products of gel agarose electrophoresis, no lanes were spliced or rearranged and no additional bands of interest were present outside the cropped region that would alter interoperation of the Bdnf of Pdyn methylation data. Full length, and uncropped images of all gels (from wells to dye front) are provided in supplementary section.

The specific region of the Bdnf exon IV promoter (from − 205 to + 19 relative to the transcription start site (TSS)) was selected because it contains well- known regulatory elements and a validated CpG island. A CpG island was defined according to standard criteria (sequence length > 200 bp, CG content > 50%, observed/expected CpG ratio > 0.6) using MethPrimer software.

The Pdyn promoter region was identified from the rat genomic database (NCBI) and defined as the 5’-flanking region containing known regulatory sequences. The analyzed region encompasses from.

-300 to + 50 relative to the TSS and contains a characterized CpG island meeting standard criteria. Primer positions for both MSP-PCR and bisulfite sequencing were designed using MethPrimer software to cover these defined regions and are detailed in the tables.

Bisulfite sequencing-PCR

Bisulfite-treated samples (as described above) were prepared and amplified using primers for the promoter region of the Pdyn gene and exon IV of the BDNF gene. The PCR protocol for the Pdyn gene involved an initial denaturation step (5 min at 95 °C), followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (45 s at 95 °C), annealing (45 s at 60 °C), and extension (45 s at 72 °C), and concluded with a final extension step (10 min at 72 °C) before cooling to 4 °C, resulting in a 250 bp PCR product. The PCR protocol for Bdnf exon IV involved an initial denaturation step (5 min at 95 °C), 50 cycles of denaturation (1 min at 95 °C), annealing (1 min at 60 °C), and extension (1 min at 72 °C), followed by a final extension step (5 min at 72 °C) and cooling to 4 °C, resulting a 250 bp PCR product.

The PCR products for both regions were purified using a gel extraction kit (Qiagen) and sequenced with the Pdyn and Bdnf exon IV forward primers, respectively. The percentage of CpG site methylation was calculated from the electropherogram by measuring the ratio of peak heights of cytosine (C) to thymine (T), using the equation C/(C + T) × 100, with Chromas software. The sequences of primers for amplification and sequencing of the promoter regions are listed in Table 4.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism program (version 9, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and SPSS software version 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Real-Time PCR data for genes such as Pdyn, Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b.were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical analysis of data pertaining to alterations in methylation status between different groups was evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc analysis. Differences were considered significant if p < 0.05.

Results

Behavioral confirmation of morphine tolerance and its reversal by amylin

The efficacy of the drug treatment regimen in inducing morphine tolerance and the ability of amylin to reverse it were established in our prior study12. In brief, daily intrathecal morphine (22 nmol) initially produced robust antinociception, which progressively declined over five days, confirming the development of tolerance. Subsequent co-administration of amylin (1–60 pmol) with morphine from days 6–10 partially restored analgesic efficacy in a dose-dependent manner, with the 60 pmol dose recovering approximately 70% of the original effect. A dose-response analysis on day 11 confirmed a rightward shift in the morphine-tolerant rats that was reversed by amylin co-treatment. The molecular analyses presented below were performed on tissue from these behaviorally characterized animals.

The effects of AMY on nociception and on morphine tolerance

The antinociceptive effects of daily intrathecal morphine (22 nmol) were initially robust but progressively declined over five days, indicating the development of morphine tolerance, as assessed by the tail-flick test. Subsequent co-administration of amylin (1– 60 pmol) with morphine partially restored analgesic efficacy in a dose-dependent manner, with the 60 pmol dose recovering approximately 70% of the original effect by day 10. Additionally, amylin alone demonstrated dose-dependent antinociceptive activity. Dose-response analysis confirmed that amylin shifted morphine potency leftward, restoring sensitivity in tolerant rats. Detailed methodology and complete data for these findings have been reported previously12.

Methylation status of the promoter related to exon IV of the Bdnf gene

MSP-PCR and BS-PCR were conducted to determine whether methylation status of the promoter of the exon IV of the BDNF gene is associated with changes in the spinal cord levels of the exon IV mRNA observed after repeated (10- day) intrathecal (i.t.) treatment of rats with morphine (22 nmol) alone or in combination with amylin. Non parametric analysis of variance (Kruskal-Wallis) within groups was significant (H = 39, p < 0.0001). As demonstrated in Fig. 1, the results of MSP-PCR products on a 2% agarose gel revealed the exon IV promoters were 100% unmethylated at CpG islands in either groups of morphine or saline (p > 0.05, n = 5). Whereas the same promoter showed to be methylated in 100% of its CpG islands in rats treated with amylin alone (at doses of 1.0, 10 and 60 pmol) or combined with morphine (p < 0.01, n = 5). Data are summarized in Table 5.

To confirm our MSP-PCR data and to examine the exact site of methylation within the promoter region of exon IV, we conducted direct bisulfite sequencing PCR (BS-PCR). There are 12 CpG site in the 5’-flanking region (-205 to + 19, which covers the promoter region with the ATG start codon designated as + 1 of exon IV promoter (Fig. 2). Regulation of Pdyn gene expression in spinal cord during morphine tolerance and reversal of tolerance by spinal amylin treatment

The lumbar spinal cord was isolated from rats after the last administration of either morphine or saline. Corresponding samples were then subjected to real-time PCR to quantify levels of Pdyn mRNA. A two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Morphine, F(1, 32) = 32.788, p < 0.001,ƞ2 = 0.557, a significant main effect of amylin, F(3,32) = 14.394, p < 0.001,ƞ2 = 0.545 and a significant morphine x amylin interaction, F(3, 32) = 6.831, p = 0.004,ƞ2 = 0.363. As shown in Fig. 3, samples from morphine tolerant rats showed significantly higher levels of Pdyn mRNA (p < 0.05, n = 5), as compared to those from the saline group. However, co-administration of morphine and amylin (1, 10 or 60 pmol) on days 6–10 after established morphine tolerance led to a significant decrease in the mRNA level of Pdyn compare to morphine alone (p < 0.01, n = 5). Also, we evaluated effects of amylin alone on mRNA levels of Pdyn. When administered alone, amylin did not significantly alter Pdyn mRNA levels compared to the saline control group at any dose tested (p > 0.05, n = 5). However, in the presence of morphine, amylin co-administration significantly reversed the morphine-induced upregulation of Pdyn mRNA.

Methylation status of Pdyn gene promoter

To determine whether the changes in levels of Pdyn mRNA observed in this study were associated with promoter methylation/demethylation of the gene, we examined the status of cytosine methylation within the Pdyn gene promoter by MSP-PCR and BS-PCR. Non parametric analysis of variance (Kruskal-Wallis) within groups was significant (H = 32.62, p < 0.0001). Dunn’s post-hoc analysis of our MSP-PCR data showed no significant differences in between the methylation status of the Pdyn gene promoter region in rats treated with either morphine or saline (p > 0.05). Both regions were 100% unmethylated at the studied CpG islands (n = 5). However, while 100% of rats treated with A10 + M and/or A60 + M showed, methylated CpG islands in the promoter region of their Pdyn gene (p < 0.05), only 20% of animals receiving A1 + M had methylated cytosines at this location (p > 0.05, n = 5). The MSP-PCR analysis revealed that amylin alone induced detectable promoter methylation in 100% of rats only at the highest dose (60 pmol), while lower doses showed a lower frequency or no methylation (Table 6). To quantify this effect, we performed BS-PCR, which confirmed that the level of methylation at the specific CpG − 110 site was highest in the A60 group compared to lower doses.

Discussion

The present study investigated the epigenetic and transcriptional modulation in the lumbar spinal cord underlying morphine tolerance and its reversal by amylin, focusing on DNA methylation dynamics in the promoter regions of the Bdnf exon IV and Pdyn genes, as well as expression changes in Dnmt3a, Dnmt3b and Pdyn genes. Our findings elucidate a novel epigenetic mechanism by which amylin may modulates spinal gene regulation to reverse chronic opioid tolerance.

Our methylation-specific PCR (MSP-PCR) and bisulfite sequencing results revealed a striking pattern: chronic intrathecal morphine administration did not affect methylation of the Bdnf exon IV promoter, which remained fully unmethylated in morphine and saline groups. In contrast, amylin treatment at all tested doses (1, 10 and 60 pmol), both alone and combined with morphine, induced significant cytosine methylation at a specific CpG site (-197) in the Bdnf exon IV promoter region. This site-targeted methylation was consistent across animals in these groups and absent in morphine-only or saline controls. These findings suggest that amylin treatment was associated with epigenetic modification of the Bdnf gene as evidenced by the addition of methyl groups to the Bdnf gene promoter, which may explain its modulation of gene expression pertaining to morphine tolerance. Bdnf signaling is well-known to promote the neuroplastic changes driving opioid tolerance14,50; and therefore, methylation could be a means of restraining the detrimental expression of Bdnf, thus attenuating the rate of tolerance development41,51.

The absence of methylation in morphine-tolerant animals alone aligns with previous literature showing that morphine may alter Bdnf expression via other mechanisms rather than promoter methylation31. Our focus on Bdnf exon IV was guided by our previous demonstration that this specific transcript is dynamically regulated in our model12, and its promoter is particularly responsive to activity-dependent regulation relevant to opioid tolerance. Morphine tolerance was also associated with a significant upregulation of Pdyn mRNA in the lumbar spinal cord, verifying Pdyn’s role in opioid tolerance development through dynorphin peptide modulation of opioid receptors. Notably, amylin co-administration during tolerance induction reversed the morphine-induced upregulation of Pdyn mRNA. This reversal was accompanied by an increase in the frequency of animals showing Pdyn promoter methylation (as per MSP-PCR, Table 6) and, more importantly, a quantitative increase in the level of methylation at a specific CpG site (CpG − 110, as measured by BS-PCR) with higher amylin doses (10 and 60 pmol), suggesting a dose-dependent epigenetic effect This methylation was localized to a specific CpG site, and occurred alongside gene expression changes consistent with potential epigenetic silencing.

Amylin alone at the highest dose (60 pmol) also induced promoter methylation and tended to reduce Pdyn mRNA, although this effect was not statistically distinct from saline controls, implying that amylin’s modulatory impact on Pdyn is context-dependent, and particularly effective during morphine tolerance states. Partial methylation at the lowest amylin dose (1 pmol) combined with morphine showed less consistent methylation, indicating a dose threshold for this epigenetic effect. Together, these results indicate that reversal of morphine tolerance by amylin is a consequence of epigenetic repression of Pdyn expression through methylation of the promoter, likely reducing dynorphin’s negative effect on opioid analgesia.

The detected methylation changes prompted examination of Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b, the enzymes responsible for de novo methylation. Chronic morphine tolerance decreased Dnmt3a mRNA but tended to increase Dnmt3b with only the latter change being statistically significant.

The co-administration of amylin at the highest dose reversed Dnmt3a down-regulation, but only weakly and non-significantly suppressed DNMT3b up-regulation. The divergent regulation of Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b suggests these enzymes may serve distinct roles in the epigenetic mechanisms of morphine tolerance, with Dnmt3a downregulation potentially being a key target for amylin’s effects. Together, these results suggest that the establishment of morphine tolerance introduces a disruption of epigenetic homeostasis, potentially through the down-regulation of Dnmt3a that may drive abnormal hypomethylation or dysregulation of gene expression. The restoration of the Dnmt3a expression by amylin may allow normal methyltransferase activity, setting the stage for remethylation of important gene promoters such as Pdyn and Bdnf. The lack of substantial change in Dnmt3b may reflect either separate functional roles for these Dnmts or that Dnmt3b may serve a compensatory role during the expression of opioid tolerance. Overall, this study provides evidence that amylin acts on the spinal cord epigenome and acts, at least in part, to elicit reversal of, or reduction of, morphine tolerance through modulation Dnmt3a expression. These mechanistic insights provide a credible molecular framework for amylin’s observed pharmacological effects on reducing morphine tolerance, extending beyond receptor-level interactions to epigenetic gene regulation. The selective methylation at discrete CpG sites signifies precise epigenomic targeting by amylin, which could be therapeutically exploited.

This study uniquely identifies promoter-specific DNA methylation changes as signatures of tolerance reversal, highlighting epigenetic plasticity as a novel therapeutic target. The Dnmt expression changes, especially Dnmt3a suppression by morphine, align with emerging evidence of opioids influencing methyltransferase pathways. Our findings reveal an interesting dissociation in the regulation of de novo DNA methyltransferases during morphine tolerance. While Dnmt3a expression was significantly suppressed, Dnmt3b showed a contrasting pattern of upregulation. This divergent regulation suggests that these two enzymes may serve distinct, and potentially opposing, functions in the epigenetic adaptations underlying opioid tolerance. Dnmt3a has been particularly implicated in experience-dependent epigenetic plasticity in neurons, and its downregulation by morphine may contribute to the aberrant gene expression patterns observed in tolerance. The trend toward Dnmt3b upregulation, while not statistically significant in our study, aligns with emerging evidence that Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b can be differentially regulated and may have non-overlapping genomic targets. We acknowledge that our study did not assess Dnmt1 expression. While traditionally characterized as a maintenance methyltransferase in dividing cells, recent evidence indicates that Dnmt1 also plays active roles in post-mitotic neurons, including in activity-dependent methylation changes and interactions with other epigenetic regulators. Future studies should include comprehensive analysis of all Dnmt isoforms to fully characterize the methyltransferase dynamics in opioid tolerance.

Furthermore, the data suggests that amylin or its analogs could be developed as adjuvant therapies to prevent or reverse morphine tolerance, potentially improving long-term opioid efficacy and reducing dose escalation. Future research should investigate causal links between methylation status and gene expression, as well as the translation of these findings into behavioral pain outcomes. It is important to acknowledge a limitation in our experimental design. Our study lacked a control group that received a vehicle injection followed by morphine (Vehicle + Morphine). This group would be crucial to rule out potential non-specific effects stemming from the procedure of administering two consecutive intrathecal injections, such as mild stress or mechanical disturbance. Therefore, while our data robustly demonstrate that the co-administration regimen of amylin with morphine reverses morphine-induced changes in gene expression and promoter methylation, we cannot definitively conclude that all observed effects are solely due to the specific pharmacological action of amylin, independent of the injection procedure. Future studies should include this critical control to isolate the precise contribution of the amylin molecule itself. Several technical aspects of our methylation analyses warrant consideration when interpreting our findings. First, our MSP-PCR and bisulfite sequencing approaches specifically targeted discrete CpG sites within the promoter regions, and therefore do not provide a comprehensive map of methylation across the entire regulatory domains. Second, conventional bisulfite sequencing cannot distinguish between 5-methylcytosine and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, both of which are biologically significant epigenetic marks particularly relevant in neuronal tissue. The modifications we detected at specific CpG sites (-197 in Bdnf; -110 in Pdyn) therefore represent either stable methylation or hydroxymethylation intermediates. Third, we focused exclusively on CpG methylation, while non-CpG methylation (known to be abundant in neurons) could also contribute to the epigenetic regulation of these genes in our model. Future studies employing more comprehensive approaches such as whole-genome bisulfite sequencing or oxidative bisulfite sequencing (which distinguishes 5mC from 5hmC) would provide a more complete epigenetic characterization. While our study demonstrates compelling associations between amylin treatment, site-specific promoter methylation, and normalized gene expression patterns, we acknowledge that our experimental design does not establish causality. The observed correlations could reflect direct mechanistic relationships, parallel processes, or epiphenomena. Several lines of evidence suggest the methylation changes may be functionally significant: the specificity of the affected CpG sites, the concomitant changes in Dnmt expression, and the established role of promoter methylation in gene silencing. However, definitive proof of causation would require additional experiments such as: (1) Dnmt inhibition studies to determine if blocking methylation prevents amylin’s effects on gene expression and tolerance reversal; (2) targeted epigenetic editing using CRISPR/dCas9 tools to specifically methylate or demethylate the identified CpG sites and examine consequent effects on gene expression and behavior; (3) time-course studies to establish the temporal sequence of methylation versus expression changes. Future studies employing these approaches will be necessary to establish whether the methylation changes we observed directly mediate amylin’s effects on morphine tolerance.

Conclusion

In summary, using a behaviorally validated model of morphine tolerance and reversal, we found that amylin administered during chronic morphine exposure selectively induces methylation of the Bdnf exon IV and Pdyn gene promoters and reverses morphine tolerance-induced changes in gene expression. This epigenetic regulation is linked with an increase in Dnmt3a expression. suggesting that amylin’s effects on spinal cord neuroplasticity are accompanied by site-specific epigenetic modifications at key regulatory regions. These findings offer new and valuable insights into the molecular basis of opioid tolerance and emphasize the therapeutic potential of modulating epigenetic pathways to enhance opioid analgesia.
