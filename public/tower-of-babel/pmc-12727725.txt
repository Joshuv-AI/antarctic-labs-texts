Chest CT muscle Volume + Body weight: a two-parameter index to operationalize sarcopenia screening in older inpatients
Authors: Zhiwei Hu, Hua Dong, Yuchang Fei
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

We read with interest Schluessel et al.’s geriatric cohort study (n = 208; mean age 81 ± 7 years) showing that fully automated 3D segmentation of routine thoracic CT yields muscle volume that correlates with DXA-derived appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM: r = 0.669, p < 0.001); in regression, CT muscle volume alone explained 44.5% of ASM variance, which rose to 68.9% when body weight was added (βweight = 0.531; βCT = 0.318), while sex was non-informative. The authors also mapped EWGSOP2 thresholds onto practical CT cut-offs (e.g., ASM < 20 kg ≈ 3.2 L for men; ASM < 15 kg ≈ 1.9 L for women) [1]. Our single thesis is that these data are best translated by centering a simple, two-parameter clinical index—thoracic CT muscle volume plus body weight, rather than treating CT alone or height-normalized ratios as the primary screen.

This focus follows the numbers. First, the multivariable model indicates weight carries the largest standardized effect (β ≈ 0.53) with CT muscle volume as an independent contributor (β ≈ 0.32), lifting explained variance by + 24.4% points beyond CT alone [1]. Critically, Schluessel et al. also show that muscle volume/weight ratios correlate poorly with ASM (|r| ≤ 0.07), arguing against ratio normalizations and for an additive (or logistic) combination of raw CT muscle volume and body weight. Second, aligning such an index to EWGSOP2 which defines sarcopenia by low muscle strength plus low muscle quantity/quality, it keeps the tool consistent with current geriatric practice and preserves handgrip strength as the functional gate [2]. Third, because CT additionally captures myosteatosis that DXA cannot, the same pipeline can be extended with a quality modifier if needed, but the core screening decision should rest on the two-parameter quantity signal to remain parsimonious and widely deployable [3].

We therefore propose making the study’s result immediately actionable through a Two-Parameter Chest CT Sarcopenia Index (TCSI): a prespecified linear predictor using (a) thoracic CT muscle volume (mL) and (b) body weight (kg) [1, 2]. The initial cut-offs can be anchored exactly as the authors did, by regressing to EWGSOP2 ASM thresholds (e.g., < 3.2 L men, < 1.9 L women) and then mapping TCSI values to probability bands of low ASM [1, 2]. In a prospective cohort, we suggest reporting AUC, calibration in the large/slope, and net reclassification improvement versus DXA only surrogates; this will show whether TCSI improves triage where DXA is unavailable (a frequent reality on acute geriatric wards) [2, 4]. Because thoracic CT is often acquired for other indications, a TCSI “flag” in the radiology report could route patients to confirmatory strength testing and falls-risk interventions without additional imaging.

Two practical details keep the index clinically tight while avoiding scope creep. Timing: retain the authors’ short median CT–DXA interval (~ 9–10 days) and aim for ≤ 14 days in prospective work to limit biological drift. Positioning: standardize arm position where feasible or adjust the model if arm-up versus arm-down subtly shifts thoracic volumes; a brief sensitivity analysis can quantify any offset without complicating use at the bedside [1]. Finally, given the rising prevalence of sarcopenic obesity, stratified calibration across BMI categories will help ensure that adding weight enhances discrimination rather than re-introducing bias from ratio-based normalizations that underperformed here [1, 5].

In sum, Schluessel et al. deliver the essential ingredients for translation: a robust CT–DXA link (r = 0.669), large gain in R² when weight is added, and sex-specific CT cut-offs aligned to EWGSOP2. We argue that formalizing these into a two-parameter TCSI and validating its discrimination, calibration, and workflow impact, it offers the most direct path to operational sarcopenia screening in hospitalized older adults while conserving ACER’s emphasis on methods that clinicians can use tomorrow.
