Fractional CO₂ vaginal laser therapy for genitourinary syndrome of menopause in breast cancer survivors: a scoping review
Authors: Sine Jacobsen, Emma Kristine Eliasen, Julie Kjær Stokvad, Pinar Bor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGenitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) is highly prevalent among breast cancer survivors (BCSs), largely due to treatment-induced hypoestrogenism. Hormonal therapies are often contraindicated, creating a need for non-hormonal options. This scoping review aimed to map and critically summarize the evidence on the efficacy and safety of vaginal CO₂ laser therapy for GSM in women with current or previous breast cancer.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines. PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and APA PsycInfo were searched from inception to June 2025. Randomized controlled trials and observational studies investigating vaginal CO₂ laser therapy for GSM among BCSs were included. Data were extracted on study design, laser protocols, outcomes, follow-up, and adverse events.ResultsTwenty-two study reports representing approximately 1,099 BCSs participant observations were included. Most observational studies reported within-group improvements in GSM symptoms, FSFI, VAS, and Vaginal Health Index scores. Treatment was generally well tolerated in the short term. However, study designs, populations, outcome measures, and follow-up durations were heterogeneous. The two sham-controlled randomized trials did not demonstrate a clinically meaningful benefit over sham treatment, whereas the two active-comparator randomized trials found no clear differences between CO₂ laser therapy, radiofrequency, and promestriene.ConclusionVaginal CO₂ laser therapy appears well tolerated in the short term, but current evidence remains limited. Further adequately powered sham-controlled trials with standardized outcomes and long-term follow-up are needed to establish treatment-specific efficacy and safety in BCSs with GSM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10103-026-05029-6.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women worldwide, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases reported in 2022, and approximately one in twelve women expected to receive a breast cancer diagnosis during their lifetime [1]. Advances in early detection and adjuvant therapies have markedly improved survival, with five-year survival rates reaching up to 90% in high-income countries [2]. Consequently, increasing attention has shifted toward the long-term sequelae of breast cancer and its treatment, particularly conditions that compromise quality of life among breast cancer survivors (BCSs).

Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) is a chronic and progressive condition resulting from oestrogen deprivation, characterized by symptoms such as vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, irritation, burning, urinary incontinence, and recurrent urinary tract infections [3]. GSM is particularly prevalent and often more severe among BCSs due to iatrogenic hypoestrogenism induced by chemotherapy, ovarian suppression, and adjuvant endocrine therapies. It is estimated that 50–75% of BCSs experience moderate to severe GSM symptoms [4]. Importantly, GSM symptoms tend to worsen over time if left untreated [3] and may significantly impair sexual health, intimate relationships, and overall quality of life.

The clinical burden of GSM in this population is further underscored by its impact on adherence to adjuvant endocrine therapy. Approximately 20% of BCSs discontinue endocrine treatment prematurely due to treatment-related side effects, including severe genitourinary symptoms [5]. Given that nearly 80% of breast cancers are oestrogen-receptor positive [6], effective hormonal treatment options for GSM are often contraindicated or used with caution in this patient group [3]. As a result, first-line non-hormonal therapies, such as vaginal moisturizers and lubricants, are commonly recommended; however, these interventions frequently provide insufficient and short-lived symptom relief in BCSs [4].

In recent years, vaginal energy-based therapies have emerged as potential non-hormonal alternatives for the management of GSM. Vaginal light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation (laser) therapy—most commonly using fractional carbon dioxide (CO₂) lasers—has gained increasing attention [7]. Fractional CO₂ laser therapy exerts both ablative and thermal effects on vaginal tissue, leading to controlled micro-injury of the epithelium and thermal stimulation of the lamina propria. This process promotes neoangiogenesis, collagen synthesis, and tissue remodelling, which may restore vaginal elasticity, hydration, and epithelial integrity, thereby alleviating GSM symptoms [8].

Evidence from studies in postmenopausal women without cancer suggests that vaginal laser therapy may improve both subjective and objective markers of GSM [9–12]. Improvements have been reported in validated outcome measures, including the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores for vaginal dryness and dyspareunia, and the Vaginal Health Index (VHI) [9–12].

Despite these promising findings, the use of vaginal laser therapy remains controversial, particularly in oncological populations. The ablative nature of CO₂ laser treatment raises concerns regarding potential adverse effects, including epithelial damage, scarring, and burns, especially in tissues already compromised by hypoestrogenism and prior cancer treatments. In 2018, Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a warning highlighting the lack of robust evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of vaginal laser devices marketed for the treatment of GSM and related conditions [13]. Since then, the evidence base has expanded markedly with a growing number of studies specifically addressing GSM in BCSs. However, the available evidence remains heterogeneous, with wide variation in study design, laser protocols, outcome measures, and follow-up duration. Importantly, high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with long-term follow-up are still limited, and recent RCTs have reported mixed results [14–17]. Consequently, uncertainty persists regarding the true clinical efficacy, durability of effect, and safety of vaginal laser therapy in BCSs.

The aim of this scoping review is to map and summarize clinical evidence on the efficacy and safety of vaginal laser therapy for the treatment of GSM in women with current or previous breast cancer. Furthermore, this review seeks to characterize study designs, patient populations, laser protocols, outcome measures, and follow-up durations, as well as to identify key knowledge gaps and methodological limitations in the current literature.

Methods

Review design

This scoping review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [18](see Supplementary PRISMA-ScR checklist). A review protocol was not prospectively registered, as this study was designed as a scoping review intended to broadly map the available literature rather than perform a formal quantitative evidence synthesis.

Search strategy

In collaboration with an experienced medical librarian from Central Denmark Region, a comprehensive search strategy was developed to identify relevant literature. The electronic databases PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and APA PsycInfo were searched from database inception to June 2025, which represented the final search cut-off date for the review. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords related to laser therapy, vaginal treatment and breast cancer. A broad search approach was chosen to maximise sensitivity and capture the heterogeneous terminology used for vaginal laser therapy and GSM in the breast cancer literature.

The following search strategy was adapted for each database:

In addition, reference lists of included articles were manually screened to identify further relevant studies not captured by electronic searches. No grey literature searches were performed.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they investigated the efficacy of vaginal CO₂ laser therapy for GSM in women with current or previous breast cancer. Both fractional microablative and non-ablative continuous-wave CO₂ laser systems were eligible for inclusion. No restrictions were applied regarding study design, laser protocol, follow-up duration, or the proportion of breast cancer survivors within mixed study populations.

The review included randomized controlled trials, prospective observational studies, and retrospective cohort studies involving human participants. Only peer-reviewed full-text articles published in English were included.

Exclusion criteria comprised review articles, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, editorials, letters, unpublished studies, case reports, and studies investigating non-CO₂ laser modalities or non-laser energy-based interventions.

Study selection

All identified records were imported into the review management software Covidence (Melbourne, Australia), which was used for duplicate removal and study screening. Title and abstract screening were performed independently by the two authors (SJ and JKN). Full-text screening and eligibility assessment were conducted independently by JKN and EKME. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus among the authors. No formal calibration exercise was performed, and inter-rater agreement was not assessed.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two authors (JKN+EKME) using a predefined data extraction form. Extracted data included publication year, country, study design, sample size, participant characteristics, type of breast cancer treatment, laser modality and protocol (including number of sessions and energy settings), outcome measures, follow-up duration, and reported adverse events. Extracted data were compared and discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Narrative methodological quality assessment

As this review was designed as a scoping review, no formal risk-of-bias assessment was prespecified. However, to improve transparency and contextualize interpretation of the available evidence, key methodological limitations and potential sources of bias were narratively assessed during data extraction. Particular attention was given to study design, comparator groups, randomization and blinding procedures, sample size, follow-up duration, attrition, reliance on subjective patient-reported outcomes, and completeness of adverse-event reporting. These methodological considerations informed interpretation of the findings and are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Data synthesis

The results were synthesized descriptively to provide an overview of the scope, characteristics, and methodological heterogeneity of the available evidence. Given the substantial heterogeneity in study populations, treatment protocols, outcome measures, and follow-up periods, no quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis was performed. Outcomes were grouped according to the reported clinical domains, and breast cancer-specific data were used whenever available. Where multiple publications were identified as originating from the same or an overlapping cohort, they were treated as separate study reports but not as independent patient populations.

Results

Study selection

The database searches identified 440 records. After removal of 168 duplicates, 272 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 217 were excluded. Fifty-five full-text articles were assessed for eligibility; 33 were excluded (conference abstracts n = 17; inappropriate intervention n = 8; inappropriate study design n = 2; insufficient sample size n = 3; other reasons n = 3). In total, 22 studies were included in this scoping review. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Across the 22 included study reports, BCS-specific sample sizes summed to approximately 1,099 participant observations. This figure was derived using the reported number of BCSs in each study, including the BCS subgroup only for mixed-population studies. Because some publications may represent follow-up reports from overlapping cohorts, this number should be interpreted as study-level participant observations rather than confirmed unique individuals. Sample size ranged from 8 to 135 BCSs, and the reported mean age across studies ranged from 42 to 62 years. Most studies were conducted in Europe [17, 19–21, 23, 27–31, 34–36], followed by the United States [24–26, 32, 33], South America [14, 15], Australia [22], and the Middle East [16].

Reporting of breast cancer-specific clinical variables varied substantially across studies. To improve transparency, Supplementary Table S2 summarizes reported information on endocrine therapy type and duration, menopausal status, prior oncological treatment, and time since breast cancer diagnosis or treatment.

Eligibility criteria varied considerably between studies, including differences in symptom definitions (broad GSM/VVA definitions versus threshold-based inclusion using instruments such as VHI, VAS, or vaginal pH), and in restrictions related to concomitant or previous treatments (e.g., local oestrogen washout periods and restrictions on moisturizer/lubricant use). Considering hormonal treatments with oestrogen several studies demanded a wash-out period of six months [15, 17, 21, 22, 27, 29], but the required period varied from six weeks [24–26] to twelve months [16]. Two studies had no time definition but excluded systemic and vaginal oestrogen [20, 30]. Several studies excluded patients with usage of lubricants and moistures within the last 30 days [17, 19, 22, 23, 29, 35], while Angioli et al. [30] excludes usage within the last 15 days.

Only two studies [17, 21] specified exclusion based on former radio- or laser therapy. Mension et al. [17] defined no former usage within two years, while Lami et al. [21] excluded former usage of energy-based devices with no time definition.

Interpretation of efficacy outcomes should be undertaken cautiously, as most included studies were observational. Moreover, the two sham-controlled randomized trials did not demonstrate a clinically meaningful benefit of active treatment over sham procedures, while the two active-comparator randomized trials found no clear differences between CO₂ laser therapy, radiofrequency, and promestriene but lacked sham or untreated control groups.

Variation in laser modality and treatment protocol

Marked heterogeneity was observed in laser protocols. Most studies used fractional CO₂ laser systems with intravaginal power settings typically ranging from 30 to 40 W [14, 15, 17, 19, 22–30, 32, 34–36]. In contrast, a subset of studies additionally included treatment of the introitus and/or vulva, generally using lower power setting, most often between 15 and 30 W [21, 27, 29, 32, 36]. In some studies, detailed information on laser settings was not reported [16, 31, 33]. The number of treatment sessions ranged from a minimum of two [19, 20] to a maximum of five [17, 29] with inter-session intervals generally spanning 20–45 days. The most frequently reported protocol consisted of three sessions delivered at approximately monthly intervals [14–16, 21–28, 30, 34, 35].

Laser delivery parameters were inconsistently reported, but however, when available, they most included dwell times 1000 µs and dot spacing 1000 μm [14, 15, 17, 19, 22–26, 28, 30, 32, 34, 35]. Considerable variation was also observed in stacking modes and application techniques, including differences in probe rotation (e.g., 360° versus 90°) and treatment extent (intravaginal only versus combined intravaginal and vulvar treatment). Several studies reported the use of SmartStack levels 1–3 [27–29, 34–36], without specifying whether SmartStack 1 was used during the first session, SmartStack 2 during the second, and SmartStack 3 during the third. In contrast, some studies applied SmartStack 1 for all treatment sessions [19, 23], others consistently used SmartStack 2 throughout [14, 15, 17, 22], while a subset employed SmartStack 1 for the initial session followed by StackSmart 3 for subsequent treatments [24–26, 32]. Two studies used a non-ablative continuous-wave CO₂ system with substantially lower energy delivery [20, 21]. This modality delivers homogeneous thermal stimulation without epithelial ablation, differing fundamentally from fractional micro-ablative CO₂ lasers in both tissue interaction and presumed mechanism of action.

Outcomes reported across studies

Across the included literature, outcomes were predominantly patient reported. Commonly reported instruments included the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) [16, 17, 19, 21, 22, 24–26, 28, 29, 32, 33] and Visual Analogue Scales (VAS) for dyspareunia and/or vaginal dryness [14–17, 19, 22, 23, 25–32, 34–36]. Several studies also incorporated quality-of-life instruments (e.g., QoL SF-12) and treatment satisfaction measures [17, 21, 22, 29].

A smaller subset of studies reported objective or clinician-assessed outcomes, including the Vaginal Health Index (VHI) [14, 16, 17, 19–21, 23, 25, 26, 28, 29, 36], vaginal pH [16, 17, 30, 31], cytology, and/or histological measures (e.g., epithelial maturation indices, biopsies, tissue hydration/elasticity metrics) [14–17, 19, 28].

Patient-reported outcomes

Female sexual function index

Twelve studies assessed sexual function using the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) questionnaire [16, 17, 19, 21, 22, 24–26, 28, 29, 32, 33] (Fig. 2). However, four studies did not provide extractable baseline and follow-up FSFI data [19, 24, 26, 33]. Becorpi et al. [19] reported median values only, while Gittens et al. [33] and Quick et al. [26] reported qualitative improvements without reporting absolute FSFI scores; consequently, these studies were excluded from Fig. 2. For Gardner et al. [32], BCS-specific FSFI subgroup data were available and used.

The FSFI comprises six domains—desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain—with total scores ranging from 2 to 36, where higher scores indicate better sexual function.

[28] reported that FSFI was significantly improved at six and 18 months.

Visual analogue scale, dyspareunia and vaginal dryness

VAS outcomes were frequently reported across the included studies. Fourteen studies assessed VAS scores for dyspareunia [14–17, 22, 23, 27, 29–32, 34–36] (Fig. 3), and twelve studies assessed VAS scores for vaginal dryness [14, 16, 21, 22, 27, 29–32, 34–36] (Fig. 4). Cantarelli et al. [14] reported VAS outcomes as the proportion of women with moderate-to-severe symptoms (VAS ≥ 4) rather than as continuous mean scores; therefore, these data could not be included in Figs. 3 and 4. For most studies, VAS values were reported for BCSs specifically. However, in three mixed-population studies included in Figs. 3 and 4—Filippini et al. [31], Gardner et al. [32], and Siliquini et al. [27]—VAS values were reported primarily for the full study cohort rather than separately for the BCS subgroup. Across studies reporting dyspareunia (Fig. 3), VAS scores were assessed at baseline and short-term follow-up, most commonly one month after the final laser session [15, 17, 22, 29–31, 34, 35]. All included studies reported lower dyspareunia VAS scores at follow-up compared with baseline values.

Objective outcomes

Biopsies, pH and vaginal elasticity in tissue

Only eight included studies assessing objective tissue-related outcomes, including vaginal pH, histological parameters from biopsies, or measures related to tissue elasticity [14–17, 19, 28, 30, 31].

Two studies incorporated vaginal biopsies [14, 17]. Mension et al. [17] obtained paired full-thickness vaginal mucosal biopsies before and after treatment to assess vaginal epithelial thickness and epithelial elasticity using atomic force microscopy. At six months (one month after the last treatment), no significant between-group differences were observed for epithelial thickness (mean change + 0.021 mm in the laser group vs. + 0.013 mm in the sham group; p = 0.30) or epithelial elasticity (mean change − 1373 Pa vs. − 2103 Pa; p = 0.64). Cantarelli et al. [14] performed histomorphometric analyses of paired vaginal biopsies in BCSs treated with CO₂ laser, radiofrequency, or promestriene. Mean vaginal epithelial thickness did not differ significantly between treatment groups (p = 0.697) and no significant overall change over time was observed (p = 0.648). In the CO₂ laser group, mean epithelial thickness decreased from 2.5 ± 1.1 to 2.0 ± 0.9, remained unchanged in the radiofrequency group (2.5 ± 0.9 to 2.5 ± 1.3), and increased in the promestriene group (2.2 ± 0.8 to 2.9 ± 1.3).

Fernandes et al. [15] evaluated histomorphometric parameters in paired vestibular biopsies before and after treatment with CO₂ laser, radiofrequency, or promestriene. Mean epithelial thickness did not change significantly over time (CO₂ laser: 2.36 ± 0.87 to 2.68 ± 0.94 μm; radiofrequency: 2.52 ± 0.80 to 2.75 ± 1.25 μm; promestriene: 2.55 ± 1.10 to 2.32 ± 0.76 μm; p = 0.460).

Becorpi et al. [19] assessed objective tissue-related outcomes by analysing immune mediators and vaginal microbiome composition before and after fractional CO₂ laser treatment. They reported changes in several cytokines implicated in tissue remodelling, including increases in IL-17 (2.3 to 9.5 pg/mL; p = 0.028), LIF (21.8 to 30.4 pg/mL; p = 0.025), and M-CSF (21.4 to 42.9 pg/mL; p = 0.028), alongside decreases in IL-7 (1.39 to 0.48 pg/mL; p < 0.001) and IL-2 (1.13 to 0.94 pg/mL; p = 0.018). In contrast, vaginal microbiome diversity remained unchanged (Shannon index 1.6 to 1.8; p = 0.702). These immunological findings should be interpreted cautiously and as exploratory, as they were derived from multiple comparisons without reported correction for multiplicity.

Vaginal pH was reported in four studies [16, 17, 30, 31]. In the trial by Jaber et al. [16], vaginal pH decreased significantly following CO₂ laser treatment. At one-month follow-up, pH decreased by − 0.46 ± 0.22 (p = 0.041) compared with baseline, and the reduction was significantly greater in the laser group than in the sham group (laser effect − 0.31 ± 0.14, p = 0.021). In the trial by Mension et al. [17], vaginal pH decreased over time in both the laser and sham groups, from 7.7 ± 0.9 to 7.1 ± 1.0 in the laser group and from 7.8 ± 0.9 to 7.0 ± 1.3 in the sham group, with no significant between-group difference in pH change at six months (p = 0.29). In the remaining studies reporting vaginal pH [30, 31], baseline values were within the atrophic range and decreased significantly following CO₂ laser treatment, although post-treatment pH values generally remained above the atrophic range (> 4.5).

Vaginal health index

Ten studies reported the Vaginal Health Index (VHI) as an outcome measure [14, 16, 17, 19–21, 23, 27, 29, 36], which is a clinician assessed evaluation including five domains: vaginal elasticity, secretions, vaginal pH, epithelial integrity and hydration, each scored from 1 to 5 with higher scores indicating better vaginal health. A total score < 15 is used to indicate vulvovaginal atrophy. For mixed-population studies, BCS-specific VHI values were used when available. However, Siliquini et al. [27] reported VHI outcomes for the full cohort rather than separately for the BCS subgroup; these whole-cohort values were therefore used descriptively and are identified in Supplementary Table S2. Across all studies, VHI scores were higher at follow-up compared with baseline values (Fig. 5).

Four studies [14, 16, 19, 21, 23, 29] reported mean VHI scores exceeding 15 at one-month follow-up, indicating a clinically relevant improvement. In contrast, Mension [17] reported mean VHI values remaining below 15 at one month despite improvement from baseline. Hersant et al. [20] and both studies by Siliquini et al. [27, 36] demonstrated VHI scores above 15 at three, six- and twelve-months follow-up respectively.

Jaber et al. [16] reported a mean increase of 3.35 points at one month follow-up. The largest improvement of VHI was observed by Pieralli et al. [23], with mean VHI increasing from 8.9 at baseline to 21.6 at one month (+ 12.7). The smallest improvement was reported by Hersant et al. [20], with a mean increase of 2.8 points (baseline 10.6; one-month follow-up 13.6).

Follow-up periods

Follow-up duration after completion of treatment varied across studies, ranging from one month [14–17, 19, 21–23, 26, 29–31, 33–35], to three months [20, 32], six months, twelve months [25, 36], fifteen months [27], eighteen months [28] and up to twenty-four months [24].

None of the studies with follow-up beyond six months were randomized controlled trials. All randomized controlled trials [15–17] assessed outcomes at one month following the final treatment session. In Mension et al. [17], this corresponded to the six-month follow-up, as outcomes were assessed one month after the fifth monthly treatment session.

Studies with longer follow-up periods generally reported that outcome measures were most favorable within the first year after treatment [25, 27, 28, 36]. Thereafter, outcome measures showed a gradual decline over time but generally remained above baseline values during follow-up [24, 25, 27, 36].

Safety

No severe adverse events related to vaginal CO2 laser therapy were reported in any of the 22 included studies in BCSs with GSM, regardless of current or previous endocrine therapy use. Mild to moderate adverse events were reported in several studies and included transient vaginal itching or burning, mild pain during or after treatment, temporary heating sensation during laser application, post-treatment vaginal bleeding, leukorrhea, urinary tract infections, and vaginal candidiasis.

Most studies reported that treatment was generally well tolerated in the short term. However, long-term mucosal and oncologic safety data remain limited. In the sham-controlled trial by Mension et al. [17], both vaginal CO₂ laser treatment and first-line therapy combined with sham laser were generally well tolerated, however tolerance scores were significantly lower in the laser group compared with the sham group (3.3 ± 1.3 vs. 4.1 ± 1.0; p = 0.007). Related adverse events were reported in 37% of women in the laser group and 27% in the sham group (p = 0.67), with no significant differences in adverse-event severity between groups (p = 0.39) and no severe events observed.

Discussion

This scoping review mapped and critically appraised the current clinical evidence on vaginal CO₂ laser therapy for the treatment of GSM in women with current or previous breast cancer. Across 18 non-randomized studies [19–36], within-group improvements were reported in patient-reported GSM symptoms, sexual function, and VHI scores after vaginal CO₂ laser treatment. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the predominance of uncontrolled study designs. Among the randomized studies, the two sham-controlled trials did not demonstrate a clinically meaningful benefit of CO₂ laser therapy over sham treatment [16, 17], whereas the two active-comparator trials found no clear differences between CO₂ laser therapy, radiofrequency, and promestriene [14, 15]. Importantly, the active-comparator trials did not include a sham or untreated control group and therefore cannot distinguish treatment-specific effects from non-specific effects, placebo responses, or regression to the mean. Vaginal CO₂ laser therapy was generally reported to be well tolerated in the short term, with predominantly mild and transient adverse events and no serious treatment-related complications reported.

Interpretation of efficacy outcomes

Across prospective and retrospective studies, improvements were consistently reported in patient-reported outcomes such as vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, and sexual function, most assessed using VAS for dyspareunia/vaginal dryness and the FSFI [22, 24–26, 32–35]. Objective measures, including vaginal pH and VHI, were also higher or more favourable at follow-up in several studies [19–21, 23, 29–31]. These findings are clinically appealing in BCSs, a population in whom local oestrogen therapy is often contraindicated or used cautiously.

However, the interpretation of these findings is limited by the predominance of non-randomized study designs and the reliance on subjective outcome measures. Therefore, findings from uncontrolled studies should primarily be interpreted as within-group changes over time rather than definitive evidence of a treatment-specific effect. Importantly, sham-controlled RCTs [16, 17] did not demonstrate a statistically significant superiority in objective outcomes or clinically meaningful benefit of vaginal CO₂ laser therapy over sham treatment. In the trial by Mension et al. [17], improvements from baseline to one-month follow-up were observed in VHI, vaginal pH, vaginal maturation index, and vaginal elasticity in both the laser and sham groups, without significant between-group differences. Importantly, all participants received first-line non-hormonal therapies for GSM and were allowed to continue these treatments throughout the study period, which may have contributed to symptom improvement in both groups. In addition, follow-up duration was relatively short and focused primarily on early post-treatment outcomes, potentially limiting the detection of delayed or cumulative tissue effects. In the sham-controlled trial by Jaber et al. [16], dyspareunia and reductions in vaginal pH were observed in both laser and sham groups, with no consistent evidence of a clinically meaningful advantage of laser treatment for patient-reported outcomes. Together, these findings underscore that improvements observed in sham-controlled trials may reflect a combination of non-specific treatment effects, concomitant therapies, and short-term follow-up rather than a distinct clinical benefit attributable solely to laser intervention. Additional contributing factors may include placebo responses, participant expectations, repeated clinical contact, regression to the mean, and vaginal manipulation associated with probe insertion and repeated examinations.

The two active-comparator randomized trials should be interpreted differently from the sham-controlled trials. Cantarelli et al. [14] and Fernandes et al. [15] compared CO₂ laser therapy with radiofrequency and topical oestrogen treatment with promestriene and reported comparable improvements across treatment groups. However, because these trials did not include a sham or untreated control group, the absence of between-group differences should not be interpreted as evidence that CO₂ laser therapy lacks treatment-specific efficacy. Rather, the findings are compatible with several explanations, including similar efficacy of the active interventions, non-specific procedural effects, placebo responses, regression to the mean, or natural symptom fluctuation. This limitation is particularly important when interpreting active-comparator trials in a condition characterized by subjective symptoms and substantial expectation effects.

Notably, despite post-treatment improvements, mean FSFI scores generally remained below or close to the established threshold for sexual dysfunction [16, 17, 21, 22, 25, 28, 32], although Salvatore et al. [29] reported a mean follow-up FSFI score slightly above the commonly used cut-off of 26.55. In contrast, several studies [14, 16, 19, 21, 23, 27, 29, 36] reported improvements in VHI scores that exceeded criteria for vulvovaginal atrophy. This divergence highlights the multifactorial nature of GSM in BCSs, in whom sexual dysfunction may be influenced not only by local vaginal changes but also by systemic hypoestrogenism, psychological factors, body image concerns, and the long-term consequences of cancer treatment. These findings suggest that isolated vaginal laser treatment may be insufficient to fully restore sexual function in this population.

Objective tissue outcomes

Objective tissue outcomes, including histology, cytology, vaginal pH, VHI, and measures of vaginal elasticity, were reported inconsistently across studies. A few studies described tissue-level changes after vaginal CO₂ laser therapy, including epithelial thickening, altered epithelial maturation, stromal remodelling, changes in vaginal pH, and/or changes in elasticity measures [14, 15, 17, 28].

However, objective findings were heterogeneous and did not consistently correspond with patient-reported symptom improvement [37]. In the two sham-controlled trials, objective tissue-related outcomes were heterogeneous. In Mension et al. [17], several objective measures improved over time in both active and sham groups, without significant between-group differences. In Jaber et al. [16], pH, VHI, and vaginal hydration improved over time, with statistically significant laser effects for these objective measures, although this was not accompanied by a consistent clinically meaningful advantage in the co-primary patient-reported outcomes. Moreover, baseline histological or cytological atrophy was not uniformly present across study populations, limiting conclusions regarding mechanism of action in BCSs exposed to prolonged hypoestrogenism. Overall, the available evidence does not establish a consistent correlation between objective tissue changes and sustained clinical improvement.

Adverse events

Across the 22 included studies, no severe adverse events related to vaginal CO₂ laser therapy were reported. However, safety reporting was heterogeneous and often limited by small sample sizes, observational designs, short follow-up, and incomplete adverse-event ascertainment. Reported side effects were generally mild and transient, including discomfort during treatment, spotting, and short-term irritation. These findings are reassuring but should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of safety. Long-term mucosal safety, effects of repeated or maintenance treatments, and oncologic safety in BCSs, particularly among women receiving prolonged endocrine therapy, remain insufficiently established.

Interestingly, the most recent RCT [16] reported moderate rates of mild adverse events, including minor spotting and vaginal discomfort, also in the sham arm (n = 7, 46.7%). This suggests that some minor symptoms may be related to probe insertion, vaginal manipulation, topical preparation, or other procedural factors rather than active laser energy alone.

Strengths and limitations of this review

This scoping review has several strengths. A comprehensive and systematic search strategy was developed in collaboration with an experienced medical librarian and applied across multiple major databases, ensuring broad capture of the available literature. The review was conducted and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines, with independent study selection, data extraction, and methodological quality, thereby enhancing transparency and reproducibility. By including RCTs alongside prospective and retrospective observational studies, this review provides a comprehensive overview of the scope, evolution, and methodological heterogeneity of research on vaginal CO₂ laser therapy for GSM in BCSs. Importantly, this scoping review integrates findings from the most recent sham-controlled trials, allowing a balanced and up-to-date interpretation of the evidence.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. As a scoping review, no quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis was performed. The review was limited to studies published in English, which may have introduced language bias. In addition, grey literature was not searched, and publication bias therefore cannot be excluded. Considerable heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, laser protocols, outcome measures, and follow-up duration limited direct comparison between studies. In addition, the overall quality of evidence was constrained by the predominance of observational studies and the limited number of adequately powered randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up. Variability in the reporting of breast cancer–specific characteristics, including type, timing and duration of endocrine therapy, restricted the ability to draw firm conclusions regarding differential effects in clinically relevant subgroups. Some included studies involved mixed populations in which BCS-specific outcomes were not always reported separately. For these studies, the total number of BCSs was extracted from the reported subgroup size, but outcome data included in the descriptive figures were sometimes available only for the whole study cohort. This limits the precision of the breast cancer-specific synthesis and may reduce the applicability of some plotted FSFI, VAS, and VHI findings to BCSs specifically. To improve transparency, Supplementary Table S2 indicates whether outcomes were reported separately for the BCS subgroup.

Finally, most included studies were observational, creating a high risk of selection bias, particularly as participants were often recruited from specialized clinics or volunteered for intervention studies. Such women may have more severe symptoms, higher health literacy, or greater motivation for treatment, potentially leading to overestimation of treatment effects. Recall bias further limits the validity of subjective outcome measures such as VAS and FSFI, especially in retrospective studies or those with longer follow-up periods.

Clinical implications and future research directions

Recent reviews of the available evidence have reached differing conclusions depending on study design and inclusion criteria. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Löczi et al. [38] pooling observational laser studies in BCSs reported significant improvements in VHI and symptoms such as dyspareunia and vaginal dryness, while no significant effects were observed for FSFI or vaginal pH, and substantial heterogeneity and methodological limitations were acknowledged. Similarly, scoping review by Ferreira et al. [39] concluded that vaginal CO₂ and Er: YAG laser therapies appear safe and effective in women with breast cancer and GSM; however, this interpretation largely reflects the predominance of uncontrolled observational studies in the available literature. In contrast, a systematic review restricted to RCTs by Serquiz et al. [40] found that although within-group improvements were common, laser and radiofrequency treatments generally did not demonstrate statistically significant superiority over control interventions, and the overall certainty of evidence was rated as low to very low.

Clinically, the current evidence does not support strong conclusions regarding treatment-specific efficacy. Sham-controlled evidence has not demonstrated a clinically meaningful benefit of CO₂ laser therapy over sham treatment, while active-comparator evidence shows no clear differences between CO₂ laser therapy and other active interventions but cannot determine whether observed improvements are treatment-specific. This cautious interpretation is consistent with current clinical guidance. The 2025 AUA/SUFU/AUGS guideline highlights the limited evidence supporting energy-based interventions for GSM, including vaginal CO₂ laser therapy [41]. Similarly, NICE recommends that vaginal laser treatment for GSM should only be offered as part of a randomised controlled trial, including for individuals with a personal history of breast cancer [42] .

Future research should prioritise adequately powered, sham-controlled RCTs with standardised laser protocols, clearly defined and clinically meaningful endpoints, and longer follow-up periods. Stratification by baseline GSM severity, type and duration of endocrine therapy, and time since cancer treatment may help identify patient subgroups who could derive clinically meaningful benefit. Integration of patient-reported outcomes with objective, histological, microbiome, and immunological measures may further clarify mechanisms and the clinical relevance of observed changes.
