Integrative bioinformatics analysis reveals novel insights into osteoarthritis pathogenesis and diagnostic biomarkers
Authors: Qipeng Chen, Xiaodong Li, Pengfei Li, Hongpeng Liu, Qi Zhang, Linqin He, Zonghan Tang, Hanbing Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent joint disorder characterized by degeneration and inflammation. Understanding its molecular mechanisms is crucial for diagnosis and treatment.MethodsWe employed bioinformatics analyses to study OA using gene expression data. Differential expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis were conducted. Enrichment analyses were performed to elucidate the biological significance of identified genes. Additionally, signature genes were identified using LASSO regression analysis, and a diagnostic nomogram was developed. qRT-PCR was conducted to confirm the expression levels of signature genes.ResultsWe identified 200 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and a lightgreen module strongly correlated with OA. Within this module, 97 core genes were identified. Fifteen core lipopolysaccharide-related genes (LRGs) were found, enriched in immune and inflammatory pathways. Three hub genes (CCL3, ZFP36, and CCN1) emerged as potential biomarkers for OA diagnosis, with a nomogram showing high predictive accuracy, and validated by using clinical samples. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed distinct signaling pathways associated with the signature genes. Immunological analysis indicated altered immune profiles in OA, with the signature genes influencing immune cell infiltration and immune response pathways.ConclusionOur study provides insights into OA pathogenesis and identifies potential diagnostic biomarkers. The developed nomogram shows promise for accurate OA diagnosis. Furthermore, the signature genes play crucial roles in modulating the immune microenvironment in OA, suggesting their therapeutic potential.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-024-08124-3.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common form of arthritis, affecting millions worldwide with joint pain, stiffness, and loss of mobility [21]. It is characterized by the degeneration of joint cartilage and underlying bone, leading to pain and functional impairment [30]). This degenerative joint disease not only reduces the quality of life of those affected but also imposes a significant socio-economic burden due to its high prevalence in the aging population [14]. According to recent studies, OA is not merely a wear-and-tear condition; it involves complex biological processes, including inflammation and changes in joint structure [32, 39]. Furthermore, as per the World Health Organization (WHO), the likelihood of OA developing in individuals globally who are aged 60 and above stands at a staggering 10% (Global, regional, and national incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability for 354 diseases and injuries for 195 countries and territories, 1990–2017: a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2017 2018). OA is a whole joint disease involving all joint tissues, including cartilage and meniscal degenerations, subchondral bone remodeling, inflammation, and fibrosis of both infrapatellar fat pad and synovial membrane [38, 53]. The synovial membrane plays a crucial role in OA pathogenesis due to its involvement in inflammatory and fibrotic processes, serving as an anatomy-functional unit with the infrapatellar fat pad [20, 43]. Despite its widespread impact, the etiology of OA remains incompletely understood, and diagnosis is often delayed until significant joint damage has occurred [1, 24]. This delay can lead to irreversible changes in joint structure and function, emphasizing the need for early detection. Current diagnostic tools rely primarily on clinical assessment and imaging techniques, which may not detect early molecular changes that precede visible joint changes [15]. It is therefore important to delve into the molecular biology of OA and actively search for potential biomarkers. This research has significant implications for the early detection and subsequent treatment of OA, ensuring improved patient outcomes.

Several risk factors contribute to the development of OA, including age, obesity, joint injuries, and genetic predisposition [40]. Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) are a critical element found on the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria. It plays an important role in the activation of various cells of the innate immune system. These activated cells then produce pro-inflammatory factors as well as free radicals and MMPs. This cascade of events ultimately leads to severe tissue inflammation, causing significant damage [36, 37, 55]. Mice that do not have functional LPS receptors (Cd14-knock-out mice) exhibit resistance towards the development of obesity and associated metabolic syndrome when exposed to subcutaneous LPS [9]. Due to its pathophysiological characteristics, exogenous LPS has been widely employed alongside collagen in the induction of arthritis in experimental animal models for a considerable period [54]. Recent insights into the pathogenesis of OA suggest that inflammation plays a crucial role, particularly the involvement of LPS. LPS has been implicated in triggering inflammatory pathways that may contribute to cartilage degradation and joint inflammation [18]. Bacterial toxins play a crucial role in connecting the rise in gut permeability with low-grade inflammation in OA [12]. Furthermore, recent research has revealed the existence of bacterial nucleic acids, proteoglycan, and LPS within the synovial fluid of individuals undergoing total knee replacement surgery [3]. The development of obesity-associated OA may be attributed to enhanced absorption of LPS caused by increased intestinal permeability [35]. These studies suggest that biomarkers related to LPS show promise in the diagnosis and treatment of OA. By identifying these biomarkers, healthcare professionals can better understand the underlying mechanisms of OA and develop more targeted and effective treatment approaches.

Bioinformatics is an interdisciplinary field that combines biology, computer science, and information technology to analyze and interpret biological data [10]. At present, bioinformatics has emerged as a powerful tool to dissect the complexity of diseases like OA [16]. In the context of OA research, bioinformatics enables the identification of novel biomarkers and the elucidation of biological pathways involved in disease pathogenesis through high-throughput data analysis [33, 42]. This study will use advanced bioinformatics techniques to mine publicly available datasets for LPS-related genetic and molecular factors associated with OA. Methods

Data downloading and preprocessing

We obtained the datasets from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Specifically, we utilized three OA datasets (GSE12021, GSE55457, and GSE55235) as listed in Table 1. The raw matrix files were processed using the Affy (v.3.8) software for extraction and standardization. To convert probe data to gene symbols, we used the platform’s annotation file. For genes associated with multiple probes, the average value of the probes was selected as the gene expression value. We reviewed the expression matrix to remove genes and samples with over 50% missing values. To fill in the remaining gaps, we utilized the impute package in R, employing the k-nearest neighbors method with K set to 10. Each dataset underwent median normalization to standardize expression levels. The three datasets were merged using the inSilicoMerging R package [47]. To adjust for batch effects, we applied the method by Johnson et al., using the ComBat function from the “sva” package, which corrects batch differences while maintaining biological variation [23]. We employed the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, http://ctdbase.org/) to gather a comprehensive list of genes associated with LPS exposure.

Differential gene screening

We utilized the limma package to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the HC and OA groups. DEGs were defined as genes with a p-value adjusted to be less than 0.05 and an absolute log fold change (|logFC|) exceeding 1.5 [44, 57]. Afterward, the ggplot2 and ComplexHeatmap packages were employed to create a visual volcano plot and heatmap, respectively.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA served as the analytical method to explore the clusters of genes most strongly associated with the illness [27]. We began by selecting the top 50% of genes with the highest median absolute deviation (MAD). We applied a soft-thresholding power (β) to assess the adjacency of gene co-expression patterns. Using dynamic tree cuts and hierarchical clustering, we developed a new method to create a topological overlap matrix (TOM) for grouping genes into distinct modules, represented by a randomized color scheme. This TOM was then used to build a gene dendrogram, utilizing phase dissimilarity to accurately depict the hierarchical relationships among genes. Finally, we analyzed the correlation between module eigengenes and the clinical characteristics of the disease, which helped identify gene clusters that illustrate the connection between OA and HC.

Identification and analysis of core lipopolysaccharide-related genes (LRGs)

To generate a Venn diagram illustrating the genes identified in the DEGs, LRGs, and core lightgreen module, we employed the Venny tool. The genes that were shared among all three groups were designated as core LRGs. The STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was utilized to construct the PPI network for the core LRGs, with a confidence score threshold set at 0.7. The network visualization was achieved using the Cytoscape software. To determine the significance of the importance of each gene, we employed the R package “GOSemSim” to perform Friends analysis. GO enrichment analysis consists of three primary elements, namely biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). To conduct GO functional annotation and KEGG enrichment analysis, we employed the clusterProfiler and enrichplot packages. A statistically significant threshold was established, indicating an adjusted p-value of < 0.05.

Identification of signature genes

We utilized the glmnet package to perform LASSO regression analysis. By adopting a binomial model and setting lambda to the lowest average cross-validation error, we conducted LASSO regression to uncover the signature genes from the core LRGs. Afterward, the identification of signature genes was refined using a Cox regression analysis.

Development and evaluation of a predictive nomogram

To develop the nomogram, we employed the rms R package, which incorporated the signature genes [58]. The diagnostic efficacy of the nomogram in detecting OA was determined by constructing a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. Additionally, we assessed the robustness of the model by reporting the Area Under the Curve (AUC) along with its 95% confidence intervals. To evaluate the calibration of the nomogram, we utilized calibration plots generated through the rms and ResourceSelection R packages. We implemented a bootstrapping technique involving 200 resampling iterations to construct these calibration plots.

Single gene GSEA

To perform single gene GSEA, we utilized the GSEA software to categorize samples into groups with high or low expression, using the median values of gene expression levels as the threshold. We obtained the subset “c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt” from the Molecular Signatures Database, which enabled us to evaluate the pertinent molecular pathways associated with gene expression profiles and phenotypic grouping.

Immune microenvironment analysis

In this research, we investigated the associations between signature genes and immune characteristics, such as diverse subsets of immune cells and pathways responsible for immune responses. To evaluate the levels of 24 immune cell subpopulations and 16 immune reaction pathways in OA samples, we employed single-sample gene-set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). We obtained sets of genes that were utilized to assess the level of enrichment of different subgroups of immune cells based on a previous study [13]. Additionally, we downloaded gene sets about immune response pathways from the ImmPort database (http://www.immport.org) [2]. Pearson correlation analysis was utilized to calculate the associations between signature genes and fractions of immunocytes, as well as the immune reaction pathway.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assessment

This study involved collecting blood samples from individuals diagnosed with OA and healthy controls to assess target gene expression levels. OA inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 40 years old with a confirmed diagnosis through imaging and clinical evaluation. Exclusion criteria included individuals with other joint diseases like rheumatoid arthritis or gout, severe cardiovascular conditions, renal or liver dysfunction, active infections, and those who had intra-articular injections, surgery, or treatments affecting gene expression within the last three months. The patients who were enrolled in the study and diagnosed with OA included two individuals in the middle stage of the disease and four individuals in the late stage. All blood samples were obtained from individuals who had fasted, and the collections took place in the morning, between 8:00 AM and 10:00 AM. The specimens were procured from the Heilongjiang University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, following the acquisition of written informed consent from all participants before the commencement of the study. The Ethics Committee of Heilongjiang University of Traditional Chinese Medicine approved this study on 19 October 2023 (approval number: HZYLLKY20230131). Total RNA isolation from these specimens was performed utilizing the TRIzol reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). The isolated RNA underwent complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis facilitated by a cDNA synthesis kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, qRT-PCR was conducted on the Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) employing the SYBR GREEN PCR Master Mix. The relative mRNA expression levels of the hub genes CCL3, ZFP36, and CCN1 were quantified utilizing the 2–ΔΔCt method. Table S1 presents the primers for the target genes.

Results

Identification of DEGs

Based on the volcano map (Fig. 2A) and heatmap (Fig. 2B), we employed the “limma” package to identify a total of 200 DEGs from the combined dataset. Among these DEGs, 132 were found to be down-regulated, while 68 were up-regulated. This comprehensive screening process provides valuable insights into the gene expression patterns.

Identification of core module in OA

In OA research, WGCNA was employed to isolate key modules. Figure 2C illustrates that WGCNA pinpointed 29 gene modules. Of these, the lightgreen module demonstrated a significant correlation (r = 0.77, p < 0.001) with OA, as depicted in Fig. 2D. Subsequent examination yielded 97 core genes within the lightgreen module for further research.

Identification and analysis of core LRGs

By utilizing the Venny tool, we identified a collective of 15 core LRGs in the DEGs, LRGs, and lightgreen module (Fig. 3A). To examine the connections among these LRGs, we performed a PPI network analysis (Fig. 3B). Friends analysis showed that the genes were arranged in descending order according to the mean value of similarity with other genes, and the rightmost gene (GADD45B) indicated that it had the highest similarity with other genes, and was the key gene (Fig. 3C). Our results demonstrated that these LRGs exhibited significant down-regulation in the OA group (Fig. 3D-E).

Enrichment analysis of core LRGs

To explore the biological significance of LRGs, we performed intensive examinations using GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. As depicted in Fig. 4A, the BP terms of the LRGs were predominantly associated with cellular response to molecules of bacterial origin, cellular response to lipopolysaccharide, etc. The CC terms of the LRGs were primarily concentrated on the RNA polymerase transcription regulator complex. As for the MF terms, they were predominantly linked to cytokine activity, CXCR chemokine receptor binding, and so forth. The KEGG analysis revealed that immune and inflammatory pathways were significantly enriched with LRGs. These pathways include the TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptors, and more (Fig. 4B-C).

Identification of signature genes

We conducted LASSO regression analysis on the mentioned 15 LRGs. By screening, we identified 8 LRGs (CCL3, ZFP36, JUN, SOCS3, GADD45B, CCN1, RGS16, and NR4A2) with non-zero coefficients among patients with OA (Fig. 5A-B). Next, we employed a regression analysis using these 8 genes and discovered that three hub genes, namely CCL3, ZFP36, and CCN1, exhibited p values < 0.05, suggesting that these three genes have the potential to serve as biomarkers for OA patients (Table 2). To improve the precision of OA progression prediction, we have developed a nomogram incorporating three signature genes (CCL3, ZFP36, and CCN1) (Fig. 5C). Evaluation of this nomogram utilizing ROC curve analysis resulted in a remarkable AUC value of 0.933, indicating an exceptional predictive capacity (Fig. 5D). Additionally, the reliability of the nomogram model in the prediction of OA advancement was validated by the calibration curve (Fig. 5E).

The immunological landscape of OA

To delve deeper into the immune profiles in OA groups, we utilized the ssGSEA algorithm to compute the proportions of 24 distinct immune cell subsets and 16 immune response pathways in every specimen. Figure 7A and C illustrate that the OA group exhibited notably higher levels of B cells, iDC, macrophages, mast cells, T cells, and Tgd compared to the HC group. Conversely, the OA group had lower CD8 T cells, eosinophils, NK CD56bright cells, pDC, Tcm, and Tem. Furthermore, the OA group had lower scores for chemokines, cytokine receptors, interleukins, and TGFb family member receptors, while TNF family members had higher scores (Fig. 7B and D) in comparison to the HC group. Moreover, the three signature genes exhibited a strong correlation with immune cell infiltration and multiple immune response pathways in the cohorts of individuals with OA (Fig. 8). These findings imply that the signature genes play a crucial role in influencing the immune microenvironment in OA, underscoring their importance in the immune characteristics associated with the disease.

Discussion

OA, a prevalent joint tissue degeneration disorder, involves articular cartilage and bone damage. Aging, injuries, and muscle atrophy around joints contribute to OA’s progression, exacerbated by synovial membrane inflammation, fatty pad breakdown under the kneecap, and meniscal tissue decline [46]. The mechanisms behind OA’s progression involve various types of cell death, including cuproptosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis [51]. However, the role of LPS in the development of OA is not yet fully understood. The emerging field of transcriptomics has allowed for a more nuanced understanding of the complex biological processes underlying diseases such as OA [17]. In this study, we have utilized transcriptome analysis to elucidate the role of bacterial LPS-related gene signatures in the progression of OA and their correlation with immune cell infiltration.

In the present study, our results suggest that 15 bacterial LPS-related core genes are downregulated in OA-affected tissues, which is consistent with previous studies showing that elevated serum levels of LPS could potentially play a role in the progression of obesity-related OA [35]. The involvement of LPS, a component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, implicates a potential trigger for innate immune responses that may exacerbate the inflammatory environment within the joint [19]. Furthermore, our enrichment analysis revealed that these LRGs played a crucial role in various pathways associated with the development of OA, including the TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and NF-kappa B signaling pathway. These pathways have been extensively studied and shown to be closely associated with OA progression [5, 29, 49, 50].

In comparison to existing biomarkers, our findings highlight the potential of LRGs as novel indicators for OA diagnosis and progression. For instance, while traditional biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) have been widely studied, the specific downregulation of CCL3, ZFP36, and CCN1 in OA patients suggests a more targeted approach to understanding the inflammatory processes involved [22, 41]. Through the application of LASSO regression analyses, we have effectively pinpointed three LRGs (CCL3, ZFP36, and CCN1) that demonstrate the potential for diagnosing OA. In patients with OA, all of these genes exhibited a significant decrease in expression levels. CCL3 is a chemokine found in inflammatory cells, mainly immune cells involved in inflammation. It stimulates the movement of inflammatory agents towards synovial tissue, leading to bone and joint deterioration and angiogenesis. Thus, CCL3 plays a crucial role in the development and progression of rheumatoid arthritis [52]. ZFP36 is a protein that binds to AU-rich elements and inhibits the inflammatory reaction [8]. Mice lacking ZFP36 exhibit elevated levels of TNF-α and are recognized for their susceptibility to developing a severe inflammatory syndrome [48]. In addition, ZFP36 has the potential to serve as a promising predictive biomarker and innovative therapeutic target for patients with osteosarcoma and prostate cancer [45, 59]. CCN1, a protein that is released into the extracellular matrix and belongs to the CCN family, plays a crucial role in various cellular processes such as cellular growth and differentiation [28, 34]. CCN1 has been demonstrated to induce aberrant cell growth and play a pivotal role in the development of arthritis [56]. A previous study has highlighted that CCN1 could be a promising target for OA treatment [4]. The findings of the present study suggest a potential link between bacterial LPS and the development of OA, as indicated by the dysregulation of these genes.

In recent years, the importance of inflammation in OA has gained significant recognition as a fundamental pathophysiological mechanism [25]. It is becoming increasingly clear that a variety of inflammatory cells are attracted to the synovial joint, playing a critical role in the development of pathological changes within this joint [30, 39]. Interestingly, we observed that the down-regulation of ZFP36 and CCN1 correlated with increased immune cell infiltration, particularly of mast cells, Tgd, macrophages and T cells. This is consistent with the previous report, which reported that LPS can enhance the recruitment of macrophages in the synovial tissue, leading to the production of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [11, 19]. Moreover, the correlation between LPS-related signature genes and immune reaction pathways underscores the potential for these biomarkers to not only reflect the inflammatory state of the joint but also to guide therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating immune responses in OA patients. The peripheral blood, synovial fluid and synovial membranes of patients with OA have been shown to have a markedly abnormal T-cell composition. These findings strongly suggest a link between T cells and the development of OA [31]. In the inflamed synovium of OA, mast cells, along with macrophages and T cells, play a crucial role [6]. Studies on animals have also confirmed the significant involvement of mast cells in the development of OA [26]. In addition, the correlation between LPS-related signature genes and immune reaction pathways highlights a potential mechanism by which bacterial components may contribute to OA progression. The fibroblast-like synoviocytes of OA patients were found to secrete a significantly higher amount of chemokines (CXCL6, CXCL10, and CXCL16) when stimulated by LPS or TNF-α compared to the control cells [7]. Our transcriptomic data provide further evidence for this pathway, as we observed a downregulation of chemokines in samples with a low LPS-related gene signature. Our findings suggest that the LPS-related gene signature may serve as a biomarker for the inflammatory state of the joint and the extent of immune cell infiltration.

However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. The dataset selection may introduce potential biases, as the samples used for transcriptomic analysis were sourced from specific populations, which may not fully represent the broader OA patient demographic. Additionally, the reliance on existing datasets may limit the ability to control for confounding variables that could influence gene expression profiles. Furthermore, while collecting blood samples is easier and less invasive compared to synovial membrane sampling, it is essential to highlight that experimental validation ideally should be performed on the same tissue type to ensure consistency and reliability of the findings. Due to we were unable to collect synovial membrane samples and therefore using blood samples. This constraint underscores the need for future studies to incorporate samples derived from synovial membrane to validate our results more robustly. Building on our findings, several promising avenues for future research and potential clinical trials could be explored to further develop the field. Conducting in vitro and in vivo studies to clarify the precise mechanisms through which CCL3, ZFP36, and CCN1 interact with LPS and affect the pathogenesis of OA could uncover novel therapeutic targets. Additionally, large-scale clinical trials are needed to confirm the diagnostic significance of the identified LPS-related gene signature across various OA patient populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our comprehensive transcriptome analysis highlights the pivotal role of bacterial LPS-related genes (CCL3, ZFP36, and CCN1) in the progression of osteoarthritis (OA) and their significant correlation with immune cell infiltration. These findings not only enhance our understanding of OA’s underlying mechanisms but also suggest that these biomarkers could serve as innovative therapeutic targets. Future therapeutic strategies may leverage the modulation of these gene expressions to develop more effective treatments for OA, ultimately improving patient outcomes and advancing clinical practice in this field.
