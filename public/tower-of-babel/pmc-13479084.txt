Exploring Participation as a Mechanism of Domain-Specific Learning During Otolaryngology Clinical Clerkships
Authors: Teruyuki Sato, Kaoru Toshima, Fumi Shishido, Yutaka Tateda, Takahiro Suzuki, Naoya Noguchi, Muneharu Yamazaki, Akina Shirotori, Fumi Shoji, Shunsuke Kawamoto, Hirohito Metoki, Tomomitsu Miyasaka, Isao Ohno, Yoko Shimotani, Takaaki Tanaka, Nobuo Ohta, Yutaka Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical clerkships are a form of workplace-based learning in which authentic participation is considered a key contributor to learning. However, it remains unclear which domains of clinical learning depend on authentic participation and which can be supported through structured instructional activities. Taking advantage of the educational changes associated with the COVID-19 pandemic as a natural educational experiment, this study examined how differences in opportunities for authentic participation were associated with domain-specific learning outcomes during otolaryngology clinical clerkships.MethodsThis comparative cross-sectional study analyzed prospectively collected educational evaluation data from 237 fourth- and fifth-year medical students who completed otolaryngology clinical clerkships between January 2021 and March 2023 (196 in-person and 41 online). Both clerkship formats shared identical educational objectives but differed in opportunities for authentic workplace participation. Learning experiences were assessed using an eight-item questionnaire representing participation-dependent and participation-independent learning domains. Group comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U-tests and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance.ResultsMedian scores were generally comparable between the in-person and online clerkships across most domains. However, response variability differed significantly for clinical documentation and patient status reporting (p = 0.0047), with higher variance in the online group. No significant differences were observed for clinical reasoning, professionalism, understanding of otolaryngology, or overall satisfaction.ConclusionWithin the limitations of this exploratory study, our observations tentatively suggest that clinical learning may not be uniformly dependent on authentic workplace participation. Cognitive learning outcomes may be supported through structured instructional activities, whereas experiential learning may depend more strongly on opportunities for authentic participation. These findings may provide a conceptual framework for future studies and for the design of hybrid clinical clerkships that intentionally integrate structured instruction with meaningful workplace participation. Further multicenter studies using objective assessments are warranted to confirm these observations.Keywords: clinical clerkship, workplace-based learning, authentic participation, experiential learning, situated learning, medical education

Introduction

Clinical clerkships constitute the core of undergraduate medical education because they provide opportunities for students to participate in authentic clinical practice while progressively developing the knowledge, skills, and professional behaviors required for independent patient care. This process is understood as workplace-based, experience- driven learning in which participation in authentic clinical activities is central to learning.1,2 Unlike classroom-based instruction, clinical learning occurs within the workplace, where students learn through supervised participation in real clinical activities rather than through observation alone. Consequently, contemporary medical education increasingly recognizes clinical clerkships as a form of workplace-based learning in which participation itself is considered a central mechanism of learning rather than merely a context in which learning occurs.

From the perspective of situated learning theory, learners gradually become members of a community of practice through legitimate peripheral participation, assuming increasing responsibility while engaging in authentic workplace activities.3 This process facilitates not only the acquisition of clinical knowledge and reasoning but also the development of professional identity, teamwork, communication, and responsibility for patient care. Similarly, competency-based medical education emphasizes progressive participation in authentic clinical practice through Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs), whereby learners acquire competence by performing supervised clinical tasks with increasing levels of responsibility.4 Together, these educational frameworks suggest that meaningful participation in clinical practice is fundamental to many aspects of clinical learning.

At the same time, advances in digital education have demonstrated that well-designed online instruction can effectively support knowledge acquisition, clinical reasoning, and other cognitive learning outcomes.5 During the COVID-19 pandemic, medical schools worldwide rapidly introduced online clerkships to maintain educational continuity.6,7 Previous research has largely examined differences between instructional modalities, whereas the specific educational contribution of authentic participation has received comparatively less attention. Consequently, an important educational question remains unresolved: which domains of clinical learning depend on authentic participation in clinical practice, and which can be achieved primarily through structured instructional design?

The COVID-19 pandemic unintentionally created a unique educational setting in which opportunities for authentic participation were markedly reduced while many structured learning activities, including case discussions, clinical reasoning exercises, and didactic teaching, continued through online instruction. This circumstance may therefore be viewed as a natural educational experiment that allows examination of the contribution of authentic participation to different domains of clinical learning. Rather than simply comparing online and in-person instruction, such comparisons provide an opportunity to explore how participation influences learning across different educational domains. Otolaryngology provides a particularly suitable context for addressing this question because the specialty combines participation-dependent clinical activities, such as patient examination, clinical documentation, and patient status reporting, with cognitive learning tasks including diagnostic reasoning, interpretation of clinical findings, and discussion of diverse clinical cases.8 This combination enables comparison of learning domains that may differ in their dependence on authentic clinical participation within a single clerkship.

Therefore, this study used the educational changes associated with the COVID-19 pandemic as a natural educational experiment to investigate how differences in opportunities for authentic participation were associated with domain-specific learning outcomes during otolaryngology clinical clerkships. We anticipated that learning domains requiring authentic workplace participation would be more strongly influenced by reduced participation opportunities than domains primarily supported through structured instructional activities.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a comparative cross-sectional design using educational evaluation data prospectively collected from undergraduate medical students who completed an otolaryngology clinical clerkship at Tohoku Medical and Pharmaceutical University. The study was designed to examine whether differences in opportunities for authentic participation in clinical practice were associated with domain-specific learning outcomes. Although the data were collected prospectively during routine clerkships, each participant contributed only a single post-clerkship assessment. Accordingly, the study was analyzed as a comparative cross-sectional study. The educational changes introduced during the COVID-19 pandemic created naturally occurring differences in participation opportunities while maintaining similar curricular learning objectives, allowing comparison of learning domains under different levels of workplace participation.

Educational Setting

The otolaryngology clinical clerkship is a compulsory rotation for fourth- and fifth-year medical students. The educational objectives include the development of clinical reasoning, understanding of common otolaryngologic diseases, communication with patients, participation in healthcare teams, clinical documentation, and professional behavior.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the clerkship was temporarily converted to an online format because of infection control measures. Although learning objectives remained unchanged, opportunities for authentic participation in clinical activities differed substantially between instructional formats.

Participants

Participants included all fourth- and fifth-year medical students who completed the otolaryngology clinical clerkship between January 2021 and March 2023. During the study period, the instructional format changed in response to institutional COVID-19 infection-control measures. Because the timing of each student’s clerkship had been predetermined within the curriculum, students completed either the in-person or online clerkship according to the format in place at the time of their scheduled rotation rather than by student preference or academic characteristics. No students could select their clerkship format. Because this study analyzed routinely collected educational evaluation data from consecutive clerkship cohorts, no formal sample-size calculation was performed.

Clerkship Formats

Both clerkship formats shared identical educational objectives and were delivered by the same faculty members. The in-person clerkship emphasized authentic participation in workplace activities, including supervised patient interviews, physical examination, clinical documentation, patient status reporting, and participation in team discussions (Table 1). The online clerkship emphasized structured educational activities, including case-based discussions, clinical reasoning exercises, review of clinical images and videos, and exposure to a broad spectrum of otolaryngologic diseases (Table 2). However, opportunities to participate directly in patient care, clinical documentation, and workplace communication were necessarily limited. Thus, the principal educational difference between the two clerkship formats was not the intended learning objectives but the degree of authentic workplace participation.

Questionnaire Development

Learning experiences were evaluated using an eight-item questionnaire developed by faculty members responsible for undergraduate medical education and the otolaryngology clerkship based on the educational objectives of the rotation (Table 3). Each item was rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (4), without a neutral response option. Because direct patient interaction (Q1) and participation in healthcare teams (Q2) were not feasible during the online clerkship, these items were excluded from comparative statistical analyses. The questionnaire was developed for educational program evaluation and had not undergone formal psychometric validation. This limitation is addressed in the Discussion.

Conceptual Framework

The questionnaire was interpreted using a conceptual framework derived from workplace-based learning and situated learning theory. Items were categorized according to whether successful achievement depended primarily on authentic workplace participation or could reasonably be achieved through structured instructional activities. Participation-dependent domains included clinical documentation and patient status reporting, whereas participation-independent domains primarily reflected knowledge acquisition, clinical reasoning, and understanding of clinical cases. This categorization was performed a priori based on educational theory rather than on statistical findings.

Statistical Analysis

Because questionnaire responses were measured using ordinal Likert scales, comparisons of central tendencies between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. However, similar median scores may mask important differences in response variabilities. Therefore, Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance was additionally performed to compare response variabilities for each questionnaire item. Effect sizes are reported to facilitate interpretation of practical significance in addition to statistical significance.

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 19 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

The study analyzed routinely collected educational evaluation data. Informed consent for participation was obtained through an institutional review board-approved opt-out procedure in accordance with institutional ethical standards.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 237 medical students completed the otolaryngology clinical clerkship during the study period and were included in the analysis. Of these, 196 students completed the in-person clerkship and 41 completed the online clerkship. The two clerkship formats shared identical educational objectives and faculty members but differed in opportunities for authentic participation in workplace activities, as described in the Methods.

Comparison of Learning Outcomes

Median questionnaire scores were generally comparable between the in-person and online groups across all analyzed learning domains (Table 4). To compare the responses between two groups, the Mann–Whitney U-test was performed because the data were ordinal and not normally distributed. Mann–Whitney U-tests demonstrated no statistically significant differences in case exposure (Q3), clinical competence scores (Q4), clinical documentation and patient status reporting (Q5), professionalism (Q6), understanding of otolaryngology (Q7), or overall satisfaction (Q8) between groups (Table 4).

Variability of Responses

When the variability of responses was evaluated, Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance revealed a statistically significant difference in variance for Q5 (clinical documentation and patient status reporting) (p = 0.0047). The online group demonstrated a wider dispersion of scores for Q5 compared to the in-person group. No statistically significant differences in response variability were observed for case exposure (Q3), clinical competence (Q4), professionalism (Q6), understanding of specialty characteristics (Q7), or overall satisfaction (Q8).

Summary of Findings

Although median self-reported learning outcomes were generally comparable between the instructional formats, response variability differed significantly for clinical documentation and patient status reporting, indicating greater heterogeneity in perceived learning within these participation-dependent domains.

Discussion

The present study examined how differences in opportunities for authentic participation in clinical practice were associated with domain-specific learning outcomes during undergraduate otolaryngology clerkships. Although overall self-reported learning outcomes were largely comparable between in-person and online clerkships, differences in response variabilities were observed for clinical documentation and patient status reporting, suggesting that some learning domains may be more dependent on authentic workplace participation than others.

Rather than demonstrating the superiority of one instructional modality over another, our findings support the view that authentic participation represents an important educational mechanism through which selected clinical competencies are developed.9 From the perspective of workplace-based learning, clinical competence is acquired not simply through exposure to clinical environments but through progressively participating in authentic clinical activities under supervision.2 The present findings can be interpreted within this theoretical perspective, as learning domains that required direct engagement in workplace activities appeared to be more sensitive to reductions in participation opportunities than predominantly cognitive learning domains. An important finding of this study is that learning outcomes were not uniformly influenced by changes in instructional format. Cognitive domains, including clinical reasoning, knowledge acquisition, and understanding of clinical cases, appeared to be maintained despite reduced workplace participation. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that structured online learning can effectively support knowledge acquisition and clinical reasoning when educational design is appropriate.5,10 In contrast, experiential domains involving clinical documentation and patient status reporting demonstrated different response patterns, suggesting that these competencies may depend more heavily on authentic participation within clinical workplaces. One possible explanation for this distinction is that authentic workplace participation provides opportunities for informal and incidental learning that are difficult to reproduce through structured online instruction. In clinical workplaces, students not only participate in planned educational activities but also learn from unplanned observations of patient care, feedback, interprofessional communication, clinical documentation, and day-to-day decision making. Such informal learning experiences may contribute particularly to participation-dependent competencies,11 whereas predominantly cognitive learning outcomes may be more readily supported through structured educational activities.

The present findings suggest that authentic workplace participation may not primarily increase average learning outcomes but instead may reduce variability by providing more consistent experiential learning opportunities across students. In other words, authentic participation may function not only as a mechanism for learning but also as a mechanism for equalizing learning opportunities within workplace-based education. This perspective provides a theoretical rationale for designing hybrid clerkships in which cognitive learning is intentionally supported through digital education while experiential competencies are developed through supervised workplace participation.6 The proposed conceptual framework presented in Figure 1 illustrates this interpretation. This framework builds upon experience-based learning theory.2 Authentic workplace participation may function as an educational mechanism that contributes particularly to experiential learning domains, whereas structured instructional activities may adequately support cognitive domains. Although this framework requires further empirical validation, it provides a useful perspective for interpreting differences in learning outcomes observed across instructional formats.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this was a single-institution study with unequal group sizes and a non-randomized design, limiting generalizability. Second, learning outcomes were evaluated using a locally developed questionnaire that had not undergone formal psychometric validation. Third, outcomes were based on self-reported perceptions rather than objective assessments of clinical performance. Finally, multiple statistical comparisons were performed in an exploratory study, and only one learning domain demonstrated a statistically significant difference. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. In addition, the relatively short duration of the clerkship may have limited observable changes in higher-order outcomes such as professional identity formation, regardless of instructional format.

Future research should examine whether the conceptual distinction between participation-dependent and participation-independent learning domains can be confirmed using objective assessments such as OSCE performance, workplace-based assessments, and entrustment decisions across multiple institutions and clinical specialties.4,12 Such investigations may help clarify how opportunities for authentic participation contribute to competency development within contemporary hybrid clinical clerkships.

Conclusion

This study suggests that clinical learning may differ in its dependence on authentic workplace participation across learning domains. While cognitive learning outcomes appeared to be maintained through structured instructional activities, experiential learning related to clinical documentation and patient status reporting showed different response patterns associated with opportunities for authentic participation. Rather than demonstrating the superiority of one instructional modality over another, these findings support a conceptual framework in which different domains of clinical learning require different educational strategies. This perspective may inform the design of future hybrid clinical clerkships that intentionally combine structured instruction with authentic workplace participation. Further multicenter studies using objective assessments, including OSCEs and workplace-based assessments, are needed to determine whether participation-dependent learning domains translate into measurable differences in clinical performance.
