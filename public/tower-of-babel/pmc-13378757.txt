Guanylyl Cyclase 2C–Targeted Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-Cell Therapy in Patients With Metastatic Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Changsong Qi, Chang Liu, Jifang Gong, Xicheng Wang, Zhenghang Wang, Ting Xu, Dan Liu, Panpan Zhang, Jiarui Li, Miao Zhang, Xiaotian Zhang, Ming Lu, Jun Zhou, Zhihao Lu, Zhi Peng, Yanshuo Cao, Jiajia Yuan, Yakun Wang, Xin'an Lu, Ting He, Yanping Ding, Fei Wu, Dongqun Liu, Jian Li, Lin Shen

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEThe clinical efficacies of third- or later-line therapies for metastatic colorectal cancer (CRC) are extremely limited. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of a chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR T) cell targeting guanylyl cyclase 2C (GUCY2C) that was steadily expressed in all stages of CRC in a phase I study.PATIENTS AND METHODSThis was an open-label, single-center, phase I study, consisting of a 3 + 3 pattern dose-escalation phase and a dose-expansion investigation. Tumor tissues were histologically confirmed positive for GUCY2C expression. Four dose levels were tested in the dose-escalation phase, including 3 × 108 (DL1), 6 × 108 (DL2), 12 × 108 (DL3), and 20 × 108 (DL4) CAR T cells. The primary end points were safety and tolerability within 28 days after the first infusion.RESULTSIn the dose-escalation phase, dose-limiting toxicity was not observed. DL3 was chosen for dose-expansion study. A total of 20 patients with metastatic CRC were infused with GUCY2C CAR T after lymphodepletion, and only one patient (5.0%) showed grade 3 cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity. Grade 3 diarrhea occurred in 11 patients (55.0%). Of 19 evaluable patients, the objective response rate (ORR) was 26.3%, with all responding patients in DL3 and DL4 groups. Of 10 patients in the DL3 group, the ORR was 40.0% and the median progression-free survival time (mPFS) was 7.0 months. Among patients showing medium-to-high GUCY2C expression in the DL3 group, the ORR achieved 50.0% and the mPFS was 9.0 months.CONCLUSIONGUCY2C CAR T showed acceptable safety profile and high response rate in patients with third- or later-line CRC, and the clinical efficacy was associated with CAR T dose level.

INTRODUCTION

Despite the great breakthrough of chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR T) cells in the treatment of hematologic malignancies,1-4 efficacy in solid tumors remains limited.5-7 Key challenges include tumor heterogeneity, stromal barriers, immunosuppressive microenvironment, and avoidance of on-target-off-tumor effect regarding tumor-associated antigens (TAAs).8,9 Nevertheless, several CAR T cells have achieved promising results in early-phase clinical trials against late-stage GI cancers, neuroblastoma, and liver cancer,10-18 proving the potential of CAR T therapy in intractable solid tumors.

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, and distant metastasis after curative resection remains the major cause of death.19,20 Chemotherapies combined with vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitor or epidermal growth factor inhibitor have long been the standard frontline therapies for metastatic CRC.21 However, the third-line treatment options and benefits are extremely limited, with the objective response rate (ORR) of 1.6%-4.7% and the median progression-free survival time (mPFS) of 1.9-3.7 months.22-25 Thus, significant unmet needs are apparent in patients with third- or later-line CRC.

Guanylyl cyclase 2C (GUCY2C) is a steadily expressed TAA in all stages of CRC and other intestinal metaplasia–derived tumors.26,27 It is strictly expressed at the apical side of normal intestinal epithelium tight junctions,28 making this TAA a feasible target for CAR T therapy. We previously reported a GUCY2C-targeted CAR T cell (named IM96, Data Supplement, Fig S1A) featured with adequate tonic signal, which was induced by suitable clustering of CAR molecules. This unique character enabled IM96 CAR T with moderate autoactivation, long-term survival, and durable antitumor effect.29 Here, we initiated an exploratory phase I trial to investigate the safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetics of IM96 in patients with third- or later-line metastatic CRC (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05287165).

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Design and Patients

This open-label, single-center, phase I study included dose-escalation and dose-expansion phases (Data Supplement, Figs S1B and S1C). Eligible patients were adults (age 18-75 years) who had advanced cancers of the digestive system confirmed by pathology and had failed at least second-line treatment. Tumor tissues were histologically confirmed positive for GUCY2C expression. Details regarding enrollment and GUCY2C expression are provided in the Data Supplement.

The study was initiated by the investigator and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and International Conference on Harmonisation guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. The protocol was approved by the Independent Ethics Committee of Peking University Cancer Hospital (2022YJZ04). All patients provided written informed consent.

Treatment

All patients underwent apheresis for the manufacture of IM96, and details are shown in the Data Supplement. Before IM96 infusion, lymphodepletion therapy was performed as the following: days -4, -3, -2 given fludarabine 25 mg/m2 once per day; days -4, -3, -2 given cyclophosphamide 250 mg/m2 once per day (Data Supplement, Fig S1B).

End Points

The primary end points were safety and tolerability within 28 days after the first infusion of IM96. Dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) and maximum tolerated dose (MTD) were reported accordingly. Secondary end points included pharmacokinetics of IM96, antitumor efficacy and duration, changes in cytokines and carcinoembryonic antigen before and after IM96 infusion, and the positive rate of antidrug antibody (ADA) after CAR T infusion. Full details are provided in the study protocol.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size was determined based on clinical considerations and a 3 + 3 dose-escalation design. Safety analyses included all the treated patients and were graded according to CTCAE version 5.0. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were graded and managed according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy criteria. Efficacy was assessed per RECIST 1.1 in patients with at least one evaluable tumor on days 28 and 90, and every 60 days thereafter. The Clopper-Pearson method was used to calculate 95% CIs for ORR and disease control rate (DCR). Kaplan-Meier estimates were provided for time-to-event end points. For the pharmacokinetics and biomarkers analyses, P values should not be overinterpreted due to the inflated Type I error from multiple hypothesis testing.

RESULTS

The clinical trial consists of a 3 + 3 pattern dose-escalation phase and a dose-expansion investigation (Data Supplement, Figs S1B and S1C). Four dose levels of IM96 were designed for dose-escalation and three patients were included for each dose: 3 × 108 (DL1), 6 × 108 (DL2), 12 × 108 (DL3), and 20 × 108 CAR T cells (DL4). Based on discussions with the Data Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC), the DL3 was recommended for dose-expansion and eight patients were further included.

Patients and Treatment

From May 10, 2022, to April 23, 2023, 26 participants signed informed consent, among whom 23 patients underwent apheresis and 20 patients finally received IM96 infusion (Fig 1). The baseline characteristics were shown in Table 1 and the Data Supplement (Table S1). All patients were mismatch repair–proficient CRC, including 12 patients with colon cancer and eight patients with rectal cancer. The median age was 52.5 (range, 25-73) years, and 11 (55.0%) patients were male. Eleven (55.0%) patients had liver metastases. Fifteen patients (75.0%) had received at least three lines of prior treatments. A total of 19 patients (95.0%) received one-cycle bridging therapies.

Safety

As of the data cutoff on 31 December 2024, all 20 patients experienced at least one treatment-emergent adverse event (Table 2; Data Supplement, Table S2). In dose-escalation phase, DLT was not observed, and the MTD of IM96 was not detected. No treatment-related death occurred. The most common adverse events ≥ grade 3 were hematologic toxicities (100%), including lymphopenia (20/20), leukopenia (20/20), neutropenia (9/20), and anemia (3/20). Complete hematologic recovery was observed within 60 days after IM96 treatment. In total, 17 of 20 patients (85.0%) showed CRS, with grade 1-2 in 16 patients (80.0%), and grade 3 in one patient (5.0%). CRS occurred with a median time to onset of 2 days (range, 0-6 days). Four patients received tocilizumab and three received glucocorticoids for control of CRS, while fever was controlled with antipyretics alone in the remaining 10 patients. There was no difference of frequency and severity of CRS in four dose levels of IM96. Diarrhea was observed in 14 patients (70.0%), with 11 patients of grade 3 (55.0%). All patients recovered with a median time of 10 days (range, 3-33) after treatments including montmorillonite powder, intestinal probiotics, or loperamide, without the need for glucocorticoids. Six patients with grade 3 diarrhea additionally underwent fasting for solids and liquids and received total parenteral nutrition, with grade 3 lasting 1-5 days. Rash appeared in 14 patients (70.0%), with two patients (10.0%) of grade 3. For grade 3 rash, topical treatment with calamine lotion and halometasone ointment led to gradual symptom improvement, without the need for systemic glucocorticoids. Grade 1-3 stomatitis occurred in 7/20 patients (35.0%), only in DL2, DL3, and DL4 groups, with three patients of grade 1, 2 patients of grade 2, and two patients of grade 3. Management for grade 3 stomatitis included oral hygiene care, topical agents, coating agents, local anesthetics (eg, lidocaine) for pain control, and local prophylactic antibiotic treatment. The stomatitis symptoms were alleviated in all patients with a median time of 9 days (range, 4-14).

Efficacy

Nineteen patients were evaluable for efficacy after the treatment of IM96, and tumor regression was observed in 10 patients (Figs 2A and 2B). Five patients achieved partial response (PR) and nine patients showed stable disease (SD). The ORR was 26.3% (95% CI, 9.1 to 51.2) and the DCR was 73.7% (95% CI, 48.8 to 90.9; Table 3). The median time to PR was 12.9 weeks (range, 4-20 weeks). PR appeared only in DL3 and DL4 groups. Among 10 patients in the DL3 group, the ORR was 40.0% (95% CI, 12.2 to 73.8), and among the five patients with liver metastases, ORR was 40.0% (95% CI, 5.3 to 85.3). The ORR reached 50.0% (95% CI, 11.8 to 88.2) among six patients with medium to high expression of tumoral GUCY2C (Data Supplement, Table S3).

The follow-up of progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in the entire cohort and DL3 group was shown in Figures 2C and 2D. The durability of efficacy was summarized in Table 3. In all patients, the mPFS was 3.0 months (95% CI, 1.0 to 5.0). The median duration of response (mDOR) was 5.8 months (95% CI, 4.3 to 7.3). The median duration time of SD was 1.9 months (95% CI, 1.0 to 3.4). The median overall survival time (mOS) was 15.6 months (95% CI, 7.9 to 23.3). In the DL3 group, patients showed the mPFS of 7.0 months (95% CI, 0.0 to 20.7), the mDOR of 5.1 months (95% CI, 4.3 to 5.9), and the mOS not achieved. In this cohort, patients with liver metastases showed the mPFS of 7.0 months (95% CI, 0.0 to 15.7), the mDOR of 5.0 months, and the mOS not achieved. Patients in DL3 with medium and high GUCY2C expression had superior mPFS of 9.0 months (95% CI, 0.0 to 24.7; Data Supplement, Table S3).

Subgroup analyses were limited by small sample sizes, but ORR remained consistent across factors, including age, sex, lines of prior therapies, GUCY2C expression, tumor sites, burden, and mutation (Data Supplement, Fig S3). We evaluated the T-cell subsets of each CAR T formulation based on flow cytometry analysis of CD45RA and CD62L expression. No significant correlation was found between response or the Cmax and subset frequencies in the IM96 CAR T products (Data Supplement, Figs S4A and S4B).

Pt09, a 60-year-old man with rectal cancer who had received three prior systemic therapies, achieved PR 3 months after IM96 infusion (12 × 10^8). Computer tomography scans showed continuous shrinkage of pulmonary and hepatic metastases from 1 to 9 months after infusion (Fig 2E). At 12 months, only a liver lesion progressed, while others continued to regress. A second IM96 infusion was administered, but tumor progression occurred 1 month later.

CAR T Expansion

CAR T cells were detectable in peripheral blood by day 4 after IM96 infusion (Fig 3A). Among all 20 treated patients, the median Cmax was 24.4 × 106 CAR T cells/L (range, 1.22-799.58). The median Tmax was 12 days (range, 7-90) and the median persistence was 28 days (range, 4-259) after the first infusion of IM96 CAR T cells (Fig 3A). Cmax was similar between the DL3 and DL4 group, but differed significantly between low- and high-dose groups (7.0 × 106/L v 42.0 × 106/L, P = .0200; Fig 3B). Significant association was observed between Cmax and tumor response (nonresponse 12.5 × 106/L v response 137.4 × 106/L, P = .0103; Fig 3C). Persistence of CAR T cells was also observed in the high-dose groups based on the vector copy number of CAR T cells in the peripheral blood (Fig 3D).

DISCUSSION

This study showed a broad prospect of CAR T-cell therapy in late-stage solid tumors and provided an effective treatment for patients with third- or later-line CRC. For a decade, metastatic CRC largely depends on systemic chemotherapy and targeted therapy which eventually develop drug resistance. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors has made substantial improvements in response rates and survival time of patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) CRC.30,31 However, patients with MSI-H CRC account for <15% of all advanced CRC cases.32 The majority of CRC patients with microsatellite-stable (MSS) genotype gain very limited therapeutic benefits from immune checkpoint inhibitors.33 Notably, the third-line treatment options for patients with MSS CRC are only restricted to regorafenib, fruquintinib, and TAS-10222-25. Poor clinical outcomes have been reported with the ORR of 1.6%-4.7% and the mPFS of 1.9-3.7 months. Even the combination therapy of TAS-102 and bevacizumab only showed the ORR of 6.1% and the mPFS of 5.6 months.34 Liver metastasis is the most common distant spread, accounting for nearly 50% of metastatic CRC cases,35-37 and is extremely intractable to above standard of therapies. In comparison, our study showed the ORR of IM96 at the RP2D dose level as 40.0% and the mPFS as 7.0 months in the patients with third- and later-line MSS CRC. Although cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution, given the nonrandomized design in this phase I study, the observed response rate and durability of IM96 appear encouraging relative to currently available treatment options. Most importantly, the efficacy of IM96 was not affected by the RAS status, tumor mutation, and site, indicating the feasible application of IM96 for a broad patient population.

The pharmacokinetics of CAR T cells is distinct between patients with solid and hematologic cancers. The high expansion and long-term persistence of CAR T cells is crucial for the tumor regression and long-term survival of patients with hematologic tumor.38 Long-term follow-up showed that CAR T was detectable in the peripheral blood of some hematologic patients even 10 years after infusion.39 However, CAR T expansion and persistence are generally limited in patients with solid tumor due to low chance of interaction with target antigen and the immunosuppressive microenvironment.5,7,17 In this study, some patients, particularly responders, showed striking CAR T expansion levels comparable with those in patients with hematologic tumor (Fig 3C), with a median Cmax of 24.4 × 106 CAR T cells/L (range, 1.22-799.58), and many maintained CAR T presence for over 3 months (Fig 3A). Additionally, CAR T cells must infiltrate solid tumors for effective antitumor activity, and the number and retention time within the tumor are crucial. One limitation of this study is the lack of the data on intratumoral CAR T infiltration, which requires repeated biopsies for immunohistochemical analysis at different time points of follow-up. These clinical samples are difficult to collect and may increase the risk of patient infection. A novel in vivo radiolabeled probe strategy could enable safer tracking of CAR T cells and warrants future investigation.40

Like CAR T therapy in hematologic tumors, common adverse events, including hematologic toxicity, CRS, and ICANS, were also observed after IM96 treatment. Hematologic toxicity is the most typical adverse event over grade 3, which occurs due to a combined effect of lymphodepleting chemotherapy and CAR T–induced inflammatory responses. According to previous studies on CD19 CAR T cells, cytopenia invariably occurred after CAR T infusion and persisted mostly for 30-90 days.41 In our study, complete hematologic recovery from lymphopenia, leukopenia, neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia was observed within 60 days. The incidence of CRS was high but mostly grade 1-2, which is consistent with previous reports.12,17 The incidence of neurotoxicity was low for CAR T in solid tumors, but was more commonly observed in CAR T treatments for hematologic malignancies.2,3 Diarrhea is a distinctive adverse event for IM96, which is a notable on-target off-tumor toxicity of GUCY2C-targeted therapy. The incidence rate of diarrhea was 70%, with 55% of grade 3, and was manageable with appropriate interventions. Since GUCY2C is expressed in the apical side of normal intestinal mucosa, IM96 can somehow attack normal intestinal mucosa, causing intestinal edema and congestion (Data Supplement, Fig S7). Although no DLT was observed, DL3 was chosen for dose expansion. The rationale was based on the severity of adverse events, the tumor response rate, the pharmacokinetics of IM96 CAR T cells, and the evaluation of future manufacturing challenge among all dose groups. Tumor response was observed only in DL3 and DL4 groups, and both groups showed similar CAR T expansion and persistence. However, DL4 showed much higher incidence of severe adverse events than DL3, especially diarrhea and stomatitis. Most importantly, the manufacturing time and vein-to-vein time for DL4 (10 days) was longer than those for DL3 (8 days), which would increase the risk of manufacture failure, CAR T-cell exhaustion, and patient disease progression.

The chance of CAR T cells to encounter and interact with target antigen in solid tumors is much lower than in hematologic tumors. It is important for CAR T cells to retain longer survival and possess higher antigen sensitivity in patients with solid tumor. Suitable tonic signaling is critical for the survival, hemostasis, and readiness of T cells. The promising efficacy of IM96 was exactly due to this unique mechanism. We have proved that IM96 showed adequate tonic signaling, leading to high antigen sensitivity, long-term survival, and expansion.29 In this study, the primary resistance to IM96 therapy is mostly associated with the CAR T infusion dose, the expression levels of GUCY2C in multiple tumor lesions, and the fitness of T cells from each patient. We already observed the correlation of response rate with CAR T dose levels (Data Supplement, Fig S3). As a tumor-associated protein, GUCY2C is positively expressed in over 80% of patients with CRC27 and is the prerequisite for initiating the IM96 CAR T function. All patients in this study were diagnosed with multiple metastatic lesions, and we only detected the expression of GUCY2C in the primary tumor sections due to the limited clinical samples. Thus, GUCY2C expression may be heterogenous in different metastatic lesions and in different patients, causing the primary escape of GUCY2C-negative tumor cells. Antigen loss after CAR T therapy is a cause of tumor relapse. Future development of dual-targeted or multitargeted CAR T will be a strategy for overcoming antigen heterogeneity and antigen loss in solid tumors. In this study, we detected the expression of GUCY2C in the progressed tumor lesions of Pt09 who initially showed PR after IM96 infusion. During the screening period, Pt09 showed 40% of tumor cells with GUCY2C intensity 1+, and 10% of tumor cells with GUCY2C intensity 2+ and 3+. Decreased expression of GUCY2C was observed in the progressed tumor lesion of Pt09, showing only 25% of tumor cells with intensity 1+ and no expression of intensity 2+ or 3+. Moreover, the phenotype and function of T cells from patients are also crucial for the effectiveness of final CAR T formulation. A recent study demonstrated the stemness of T cells in the leukapheresis was associated with the clinical efficacy of final CAR T cells in patients with large B-cell lymphoma.42 Thus, further investigation on the correlation of the T-cell phenotypes and clinical responses is worth studying.

A recent study has also reported the clinical application of GUCY2C-targeted CAR T in metastatic CRC10; however, several distinct points differ from our study in CAR molecule mechanism, clinical study design, the safety profile, and the long-term efficacy. The previous report investigated a coupled CAR T cell with a complicated expression of both GUCY2C CAR and CD19 CAR, largely relying on CD19 recognition by B cells for expansion in vivo. By contrast, our study used a CAR T cell with a simple molecule design as a second-generation CAR but representing a unique mechanism as adequate tonic signaling for long-term survival and high antigen sensitivity. Our phase I study included both dose-escalation and dose-expansion phases, while the previous study had two dose groups. Infection and grade 4 neurotoxicity were not observed for IM96, but were reported in the CoupledCAR-T. These severe adverse events were probably due to the design of CD19 CAR that induced much higher secretion of cytokines. IM96 and CoupledCAR-T showed comparable ORR (40% v 40%), but IM96 had superior durability, with mPFS of 7.0 versus 6.0 months.

In conclusion, IM96 shows acceptable safety profile and promising antitumor activity at dose levels of 12 × 108 CAR T cells in mCRC with positive expression of GUCY2C, and further studies will be conducted at this dose level.
