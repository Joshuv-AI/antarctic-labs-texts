Outer membrane vesicles from Fusobacterium necrophorum elicit protective immunity in mice and robust immune responses in sheep
Authors: Pengyu Zhao, Jiancheng Jiang, Ning Yang, Rongqi Liu, Kai Jiang, Lan Bi, Xianjing He, Donghua Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFusobacterium necrophorum is a major opportunistic pathogen of ruminants that causes foot rot and liver abscesses and contributes to substantial economic losses. Current vaccines provide limited protection. Here, we prepared and evaluated outer membrane vesicles (OMVs)-based vaccine candidate derived from F. necrophorum. OMVs were purified by density-gradient centrifugation and were characterized for morphology, composition, and immunological activity. The purified OMVs showed intact vesicle structure and a uniform particle size distribution and contained virulence-associated antigens and pathogen-associated molecular patterns. OMVs were rapidly taken up by dendritic cells and promoted dendritic cell maturation, as indicated by activation of PI3K/AKT signaling and increased expression of MHC I, MHC II, CD80, and CD86. In vivo imaging showed persistent fluorescence at the injection site and signal accumulation in draining lymph nodes and the spleen. In mice, OMVs immunization induced high titers of antigen-specific IgG and elicited a Th1-biased cellular immune response. In a lethal challenge, OMVs immunization achieved 100% survival, significantly reduced liver bacterial loads, and alleviated inflammatory and necrotic liver lesions. In sheep, OMVs immunization also induced progressively increased antigen-specific IgG and a predominantly Th1 type cellular immune response, supporting strong immunogenicity in a natural host. Together, these results support F. necrophorum OMVs as a promising vaccine candidate containing multiple native antigens for the prevention and control of F. necrophorum - associated diseases in ruminants.

Introduction

Fusobacterium necrophorum is a strictly anaerobic, Gram-negative bacterium that is generally considered as an opportunistic pathogen [1]. It commonly colonizes mucosal surfaces in animals, including the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and urogenital tract, and is associated with necrotic and suppurative diseases [2,3]. In ruminants, liver abscesses in feedlot cattle and foot rot in cattle and sheep represent two of the most frequently reported clinical outcomes and impose substantial health and economic burdens [4,5]. Epidemiological surveys indicate that foot rot is globally distributed, with incidence varying by region and husbandry conditions; in high-risk settings, incidence can reach 25% [6], and the estimated economic loss per case is approximately US$120.70 [7]. In the United States, liver abscess prevalence in feedlot cattle has been estimated at 10% to 20%, with annual losses to the beef industry of approximately US$256 million [8,9]. Emerging evidence further links F. necrophorum to production-limiting disorders in dairy cattle, including mastitis and endometritis [10–12], highlighting a broader impact of this pathogen on ruminant health and productivity.

At present, prevention and treatment of F. necrophorum-associated diseases still rely predominantly on antibiotics in many production settings [13]. However, antimicrobial use is increasingly constrained by regulatory and stewardship policies, and antimicrobial resistance remains a growing concern. Together, these pressures reduce the sustainability of antibiotic-centered control and underscore the need for prevention-oriented strategies that protect animal health and support food safety. Vaccination is therefore widely considered a practical and cost-effective intervention [14]. Nevertheless, vaccine development against F. necrophorum remains challenging, as reported protection is often incomplete, immunity may be short-lived, and no broadly adopted, highly effective commercial vaccine is currently available [15,16].

Most vaccine candidates fall into two broad classes: inactivated whole-cell bacterins and recombinant subunit vaccines that target selected virulence determinants [15,17]. Inactivated bacterins have reduced liver abscess incidence in some experimental and field settings, but efficacy has been variable across feeding regimens and management conditions. For example, a randomized, blinded field trial found a reduction in severe liver abscesses in vaccinated cattle fed an ad libitum forage-based growing diet, whereas no significant effect was observed in cattle limit-fed a grain-based growing diet [16]. Recombinant subunit vaccines targeting leukotoxin, hemolysin, or the 43-kDa outer membrane protein (43K OMP) have demonstrated immunogenicity in murine models and decrease pathology or bacterial burden in some contexts [17,18]. However, F. necrophorum pathogenesis is driven by coordinated actions of multiple virulence-associated and metabolic determinants [19]. Therefore, formulations limited to a small antigen set may not sufficiently capture this complexity, resulting in protection that is frequently partial and inconsistent across experimental models and field conditions.

Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) offer a vaccine platform that can address several limitations of bacterins and narrowly targeted subunit formulations. OMVs are nanoscale lipid-bilayer vesicles naturally shed from the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria and typically range from 20 to 250 nm in diameter [20]. This size supports transit through interstitial spaces and lymphatic drainage in vivo and facilitates efficient uptake by antigen-presenting cells, including dendritic cells, with subsequent trafficking to draining lymph nodes [21,22]. OMVs function as multivalent antigen carriers and provide intrinsic immunostimulatory adjuvanticity because they contain lipopolysaccharide (LPS), outer membrane proteins (OMPs), lipoproteins, periplasmic proteins, virulence factors, and nucleic acids [23]. The native lipid bilayer can presents surface-exposed antigens in near-native conformations alongside pathogen-associated molecular patterns, thereby supporting innate immune activation and coordinated induction of humoral and cellular immunity [24]. Importantly, OMVs are non-replicative, which supports their development as an acellular vaccine platform, although reactogenicity associated with OMV components such as LPS requires formulation-specific evaluation [25]. Consistent with these attributes, OMV-based vaccines have demonstrated immunogenicity and protection across multiple pathogen models [26,27], and licensed serogroup B meningococcal vaccines include an OMV component with demonstrated immunogenicity and an acceptable safety profile [28].

Here, we characterized F. necrophorum OMVs and evaluated their ability to activate antigen-presenting cells and induce protective immunity in mice, with immune profiling in sheep to define host-relevant signatures. These findings support F. necrophorum OMVs as a multivalent vaccine candidate with intrinsic immunostimulatory activity for preventing F. necrophorum-associated diseases in ruminants.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Heilongjiang Bayi Agricultural University (approval No. DWKJXY2022035). This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Female BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks, 20 g) were purchased from Changchun Yisi Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Sheep (25–30 kg) were obtained from Qiqihar City, Heilongjiang Province, China. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with ad libitum food and water (12-h light/dark cycle, environmental enrichment), and monitored routinely. After challenge, mice were monitored at least daily; humane endpoints (≥20% body-weight loss and severe clinical signs/score) triggered euthanasia. Mice were euthanized by sodium pentobarbital injection (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. Sheep were subjected to blood collection only and maintained under routine husbandry.

Bacterial strain

Fusobacterium necrophorum (ATCC 25,286) was cultured at 37°C under anaerobic conditions in a gas mixture containing 85% nitrogen, 10% hydrogen, and 5% carbon dioxide. The bacterium was grown in brain heart infusion (BHI) medium (HB8478, HopeBio Technology) for 12–24 h to OD600 0.7–0.9.

Isolation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs)

SPF C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were euthanized by sodium pentobarbital injection (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. Femurs and tibias were aseptically collected, and bone marrow cells were flushed with culture medium. After red blood cell lysis, bone marrow cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (12633020, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; A15-101, PAA) and recombinant mouse granulocyte macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF; HY-P7361, MedChemExpress; 20 ng/mL). Fresh medium containing GM-CSF at the same concentration was added on days 3 and 7. Cells were harvested on day 8 for subsequent experiments.

Isolation and purification of F. necrophorum OMVs

OMVs were isolated from F. necrophorum cultures according to a previously described protocol [29]. Briefly, 2 L of bacterial culture was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected and passed through a 0.22 μm filter (SLGP033RB, Millipore). The clarified filtrate was ultracentrifuged at 213,000 × g for 2 h at 4°C (Type 50.2 Ti rotor, Beckman Coulter), and the pellet was resuspended in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). To further purify OMVs, crude vesicle preparations were subjected to density-gradient centrifugation. Briefly, discontinuous OptiPrep (1114542, Axis Shield) gradients were prepared at concentrations of 20%, 25%, 30%, 35%, and 40%. The OMVs suspension was loaded onto the gradient and centrifuged at 135,000 × g for 16 h at 4°C (SW41 Ti rotor, Beckman Coulter). Each fraction was carefully collected, diluted at least tenfold with sterile PBS, and ultracentrifuged at 200,500 × g for 2 h at 4°C to remove residual OptiPrep. Pellets from each fraction were resuspended in sterile PBS, and OMVs size distribution and morphology were assessed by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Transmission electron microscopy

OMVs samples were examined by TEM. Copper grids were placed on drops of the sample and allowed to adsorb for 5 min. Excess liquid was removed with filter paper, after which the grids were placed on phosphotungstic acid staining solution for 30 s. The grids were then blotted again with filter paper and vesicle morphology was observed using a transmission electron microscope (H-7650, Hitachi).

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

The size distribution and concentration of OMVs were measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (PMX 110, Particle Metrix, 405 nm laser). Samples were diluted in sterile PBS to achieve a concentration of 1 × 107/mL − 1 × 109/mL. For each sample, videos were recorded for 1 min at 30 frames per second and measurements were repeated 3 times. Particle trajectories were analyzed using NTA software (ZetaView 8.02.28).

Proteomic analysis of OMVs

OMVs proteins were lysed in SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 100 mM DTT) and quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins (100 μg per sample) were processed by filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) on 10 kDa ultrafiltration units (Pall), reduced with DTT, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and digested overnight with trypsin (Promega; 1:50 enzyme-to-protein ratio, w/w) at 37°C. Peptides were desalted using C18 cartridges (Waters), lyophilized, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. For LC – MS/MS analysis, peptides (5 μg) were separated on a C18 reversed-phase column (Thermo Scientific EASY Column, 10 cm × 75 μm i.d., 3 μm resin) using an EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile). Peptides were eluted with a 120-min gradient of increasing solvent B at a nanoflow rate. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Q Exactive HF-X Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated in data-dependent acquisition mode. Each sample was analyzed in biological triplicate with technical duplicates. Raw data were processed using MaxQuant (version 2.0.1.0) and searched against the UniProt F. necrophorum protein database (ATCC 25,286 strain). Search parameters included trypsin digestion with up to two missed cleavages, carbamidomethylation of cysteine (fixed modification), and oxidation of methionine and N-terminal acetylation (variable modifications). A false discovery rate of 1% was applied at peptide and protein levels. Gene Ontology annotation was performed using Blast2GO (version 5.2), KEGG pathway mapping using KAAS, and subcellular localization was predicted using Cell-PLoc 2.0.

Western blotting for OMVs protein validation

Proteins were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in PBST containing 5% (w/v) nonfat milk for 2 h at room temperature, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, washed, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, signals were developed using ECL substrate (BL523B, Biosharp) and imaged with a gel documentation system (Amersham Imager 600, GE Healthcare Life Sciences). A prestained protein marker (26616, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was run in parallel to the samples to estimate protein molecular weights. For OMVs protein analysis, western blotting was performed using a mouse monoclonal antibody against leukotoxin and a rabbit polyclonal antibody against the 43K OMP. These antibodies were obtained from laboratory stocks and were generated as previously described [30,31].

Analysis of OMVs uptake by BMDCs

OMVs were labeled with the lipophilic dye DiL (D3911, Sigma) by incubation for 30 min at 4°C. Labeled OMVs were collected by ultracentrifugation (200,500 × g, 2 h, 4°C), resuspended in PBS, and centrifuged again under the same conditions to remove unbound dye. DiL-labeled OMVs were then incubated with BMDCs for 3 h or 6 h. Cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After washing, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min at room temperature. OMVs uptake was visualized by fluorescence microscopy. For flow cytometric quantification, BMDCs were incubated with DiL-labeled OMVs for 6 h and collected for acquisition on an LSRFortessa SORP flow cytometer (BD). Intracellular DiL fluorescence was detected in the PE channel and quantified.

Immunoblotting analysis of PI3K/AKT signaling in BMDCs

For BMDCs signaling analysis, BMDCs were lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (BL1439A, biosharp). Lysates were probed with antibodies against β-actin (4967S, CST, 1:2 000), AKT (9272S, CST, 1:1 000), p-AKT (4060S, CST, 1:1 000), PI3K (20584–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1 000), and p-PI3K (bs-6417 R, Bioss, 1:1 000). HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (7074, CST, 1:5 000) was used as the secondary antibody. A prestained protein ladder was included on each gel for molecular weight estimation (PM008, novoprotein). For inhibitor experiments, BMDCs were pretreated with LY294002 (Merck) for 1 h at 37°C, followed by stimulation with OMVs for the indicated times. For pathway activation experiments, BMDCs were treated with the AKT activator SC79 (HY-18749, MedChemExpress) for the indicated time. Cells were then washed once with ice-cold PBS and immediately lysed for immunoblotting. Western blot images were acquired using a Tanon 5200 Multi imaging system (Tanon 5200 Multi, Tanon).

Flow cytometric analysis of BMDC activation and maturation

BMDCs were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with OMVs, SC79, or LY294002 plus OMVs as indicated. For inhibitor experiments, BMDCs were pretreated with LY294002 for 1 h at 37°C before OMVs stimulation. For pathway activation experiments, BMDCs were treated with the AKT activator SC79 for the indicated time. After treatment, cells were collected, washed with PBS, and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies on ice for 30 min in the dark. The following antibodies were used: APC-Cy7 anti-mouse CD11c (117324, BioLegend, 1:100), FITC anti-mouse H-2Kb/H-2Db/MHC class I (114706, BioLegend, 1:100), PE-Cy7 anti-mouse MHC II (116420, BioLegend, 1:200), PE anti-mouse CD80 (104708, BioLegend, 1:200), and APC anti-mouse CD86 (105012, BioLegend, 1:100). Cells were then washed with PBS, centrifuged at 1,750 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in PBS. Samples were fixed by adding 3% paraformaldehyde to achieve a final paraformaldehyde concentration of 1.5%. Data were acquired on an LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) using FACSDiva software and analyzed with FlowJo software (v10; FlowJo LLC). CD11c+ cells were gated as BMDCs, and the expression levels of MHC I, MHC II, CD80, and CD86 were used to evaluate BMDC activation and maturation.

In vivo tracking of DiL labeled OMVs in mice

BALB/c mice were injected with 50 μg DiL-labeled OMVs into the medial side of the right hind limb. Fluorescence at the injection site was monitored at 12-h intervals. For each imaging time point, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (RWD Life Science, R510-22–10), and whole-body fluorescence signals were acquired using a small-animal in vivo imaging system (AniView 600 SE, Boluteng). At 72 h post-injection, mice were euthanized, and draining lymph nodes and spleen were collected for ex vivo biodistribution analysis. Fluorescence intensities in each tissue were quantified using the manufacturer’s imaging analysis software.

Recombinant protein expression and purification

Recombinant 43K OMP, PL4, and H2 were expressed and purified as previously described [17]. Briefly, pET-32a-43K OMP, pGEX-6p-1-PL4, and pET-32a-H2 were individually transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) for protein production. Protein expression was induced with IPTG, and bacterial pellets were harvested and lysed by sonication in PBS supplemented with protease inhibitors. For PL4 purification, clarified lysate was loaded onto glutathione Sepharose 4B resin pre-equilibrated with PBS to capture the GST-PL4. After extensive washing, the GST tag was removed by on-column cleavage using PreScission protease. Cleaved PL4 was collected in the flow-through and further passed over glutathione resin to remove residual GST and uncleaved fusion protein. His-tagged H2 and 43K OMP were purified under native conditions using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (Qiagen). Purified proteins were dialyzed against PBS, and endotoxin was removed using Detoxi-Gel columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein purity (>85%) was verified by SDS – PAGE, endotoxin levels were < 1 EU/ µg, and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay prior to vaccine formulation.

Mouse immunization and challenge experiments

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 10 per group), including PBS, inactivated F. necrophorum (Inactivated F.n), recombinant multicomponent subunit vaccine (F.n-3Sub: 43K OMP + PL4 + H2), and OMVs. All mice were immunized three times at 14 days intervals by subcutaneous multipoint injection on the back. For the primary immunization, each preparation was emulsified 1:1 (v/v) with Freund’s complete adjuvant (F5881, Sigma-Aldrich) and booster immunizations were emulsified 1:1 (v/v) with Freund’s incomplete adjuvant (F5506, Sigma-Aldrich). Each mouse received a total volume of 250 μL per immunization. The PBS group received 250 μL PBS formulated with adjuvant. The Inactivated F.n group received 125 μL of an inactivated bacterin per immunization before emulsification. The F.n-3Sub group received 30 μg recombinant proteins per immunization (10 μg of each component). The OMVs group received 30 μg purified OMVs per immunization. The bacterin was prepared by formalin inactivation (0.2%, 24 h at 37°C), and complete inactivation was confirmed by the absence of bacterial growth after 72 h of culture. Serum samples and spleens were collected on days 14, 28, and 42.

Challenge experiments were performed 14 days after the third immunization as previously described [17]. Briefly, F. necrophorum was cultured to mid-log phase and harvested by centrifugation (3,000 × g, 25 min). The bacterial pellet was resuspended in sterile saline to a final concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL. Mice were challenged by intraperitoneal injection of 0.25 mL of the bacterial suspension per mouse. After challenge, mortality was monitored for 14 days. Clinical scores were recorded as described previously [32]. On day 14 post-challenge, surviving mice were euthanized and livers were collected for bacterial load and histopathology analysis.

Sheep immunization

Sheep (25–30 kg) were randomly assigned to two groups (OMVs and PBS control; n = 12 per group). Animals in the OMVs group were immunized by subcutaneous multipoint injection with 100 μg OMVs formulated 1:1 (v/v) with Freund’s adjuvant. Complete Freund’s adjuvant was used for the primary immunization, and incomplete Freund’s adjuvant was used for the second and third immunizations. Booster immunizations were administered 14 days after the primary immunization and 21 days after the second. Serum samples were collected on days 14, 35, and 56 for downstream analyses.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs)

Antigens used for immunization preparation were used as coating antigens for ELISAs. 96-well plates (3590, Corning) were coated overnight at 4°C with OMVs (1 μg/mL), 43K OMP (0.25 μg/mL), H2 (0.25 μg/mL), PL4 (0.5 μg/mL), or F. necrophorum (1 × 107 CFU/mL). Plates were washed and blocked for 2 h at 37°C with PBS containing 5% (w/v) nonfat milk and 0.05% Tween-20. Serum samples were serially diluted in blocking buffer, added to coated plates, and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Plates were washed three times with PBST and incubated for 1 h at 37°C with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (A16066, Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:5 000) or HRP-conjugated donkey anti-sheep IgG (A16041, Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:10 000). After washing, TMB substrate (PR1200, Solarbio) was added for 15 min, and reactions were stopped with stop solution (C1058, Solarbio). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Model ELX800, BioTek).

Cytokine measurement

Serum cytokine concentrations in mice, including TNF-α (MM-0132M1), IFN-γ (MM-0182M1), IL-12 (MM-44769M1), IL-4 (MM-0165M1), IL-10 (MM-0176M1), and IL-13 (MM-0173M1), were quantified using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Enzyme Immunoassay Industry) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For sheep, serum TNF-α (MM-36938O1), IFN-γ (MM-2299O1), IL-12 (MM-80064O1), IL-4 (MM-33373O1), IL-10 (MM-80013O1), and IL-13 (MM-1753O1) were measured using ELISA kits from the same manufacturer, following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Detection of T-lymphocyte frequencies

Lymphocytes were isolated from mouse spleens and sheep peripheral blood using a Mouse Spleen Lymphocyte Isolation Solution Kit (P8860, Solarbio) or a Sheep Peripheral Blood Lymphocyte Isolation Solution Kit (P3090, Solarbio), respectively. For surface staining, mouse lymphocytes were labeled with FITC-conjugated anti-mouse CD3 (100203, BioLegend), PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD4 (116005, BioLegend), and APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD8 (100711, BioLegend). Sheep lymphocytes were stained with FITC-conjugated anti-sheep CD4 (MA1-8090, Invitrogen) and PE-conjugated anti-sheep CD8 antibodies (MA5-28420, Invitrogen). All staining steps were performed on ice for 30 min. Cells were washed with PBS (2.5 mL), centrifuged at 1750 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in PBS (250 µL). Samples were fixed by adding 3% paraformaldehyde (250 µL) to achieve a final paraformaldehyde concentration of 1.5%. Data were acquired on an LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) using FACSDiva (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo (v10; FlowJo LLC).

Bacterial load quantification in mouse livers

Mouse livers were collected aseptically, weighed, and transferred to a tissue grinder containing 3 mL sterile saline. Tissues were homogenized and serially diluted in sterile saline (10-fold dilutions to 10−1, 10−2, 10−3, and 10−4). For each dilution, 50 μL aliquots were plated onto BHI agar plates and incubated under anaerobic conditions for 24 h at 37°C. Colonies were counted, and bacterial burdens were calculated using plates containing 30–300 colonies and expressed as CFU per gram of tissue (CFU/g).

Histopathology of mouse liver tissue

Collected mouse liver tissues were fixed in 4% formaldehyde. Tissue blocks were trimmed to approximately 1 cm × 1 cm × 0.5 cm and processed through standard dehydration, clearing, paraffin infiltration, and embedding procedures. Paraffin sections (4–5 μm) were cut using a rotary microtome, mounted on glass slides, deparaffinized and rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (G1120, Solarbio). Slides were mounted, air dried, and examined by light microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for comparisons among multiple groups at a single time point. Two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to analyze experiments with two independent variables. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Differences were considered significant at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Results

Characterization of F. necrophorum OMVs

To optimize purification conditions for F. necrophorum OMVs, we evaluated different OptiPrep density gradients using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and SDS-PAGE analysis. Spherical vesicles with uniform morphology were consistently observed in the 20–30% OptiPrep fractions, exhibiting mean diameters of 132.7 nm (Figure 1(A,B)). SDS-PAGE analysis revealed multiple protein bands (40–170 kDa) in these fractions, while no vesicular structures were detected in the 35–40% fractions (Figure 1(C)). To evaluate the reproducibility of the OMVs purification method, three independent batches of F. necrophorum OMVs were prepared under identical culture conditions, at the same harvest time, and using the same ultracentrifugation purification procedure. BCA analysis showed that the protein yield of each OMVs batch was approximately 1 mg/L of culture. NTA and SDS-PAGE analyses showed that the particle size distributions of the three OMVs batches were mainly concentrated within the 100–200 nm range, with similar protein banding patterns among batches (Figure S1A – B). Proteomic analysis identified 162 proteins in purified OMVs (Supplementary Table 1). Subcellular localization prediction assigned 41.49% to inner membrane, 22.34% to cytoplasm, 12.77% to outer membrane, 11.17% to periplasm, 9.04% to extracellular compartments, and 3.19% to fimbriae (Figure 1(D)). Western blot validation confirmed the presence of two representative proteins, 43K OMP and leukotoxin (Figure 1(E)).

Gene Ontology analysis of molecular function and biological process indicated that OMVs associated proteins were mainly enriched in functions related to transmembrane transport and energy metabolism. These functions included substrate specific transport, ATPase activity, peptide and proton transport, phosphorylation, and ATP metabolism (Figure 2(A)). KEGG pathway analysis further suggested that these proteins are primarily involved in energy metabolism pathways, such as carbohydrate metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation, as well as in fundamental biological processes like protein transport and RNA degradation. This indicates that F. necrophorum OMVs carry a wide range of functional proteins related to transmembrane transport and energy metabolism (Figure 2(B)). Together, these results indicate that structurally intact F. necrophorum OMVs can be reproducibly enriched using the 20%-30% OptiPrep fractions and that the resulting OMVs proteome is complex and functionally diverse, including proteins annotated as virulence-associated factors. Evaluation of the antigen presentation capacity of F. necrophorum OMVs

To assess antigen-presentation capacity, we quantified OMVs uptake by BMDCs over time and evaluated BMDCs activation. Fluorescence microscopy revealed efficient uptake as early as 3 h, with red fluorescence predominantly localized to the perinuclear cytoplasm, and a further increase at 6 h (Figure 3(A)). Consistently, flow cytometry analysis confirmed robust uptake, with 49.69% OMVs-positive cells at 6 h (Figure 3(B)). OMVs treatment markedly upregulated antigen-presentation and costimulatory molecules on BMDCs, including MHC I (47.4-fold), MHC II (78.9-fold), CD80 (34.5-fold), and CD86 (24.1-fold) compared with controls (p < 0.05, Figure 3(C)). Evaluation of the immunoprotective effect of F. necrophorum OMVs in mice

To evaluate the immunoprotective effect of F. necrophorum OMVs in mice, BALB/c mice were immunized and compared with the Inactivated F.n and F.n–3Sub groups (Figure 6(A)). Serological analysis showed that after the third immunization, when inactivated F.n was used as the ELISA coating antigen, the OMVs and Inactivated F.n groups induced the highest levels of antigen-specific IgG responses, followed by the F.n-3Sub group. In contrast, when 43K OMP, hemolysin (H2), or PL4 were used as coating antigens, the F.n-3Sub group induced the strongest IgG responses, followed by the OMVs group and then the Inactivated F.n group. When OMVs were used as the coating antigen, the OMVs group induced the highest OMV-specific IgG levels, followed by the F.n-3Sub group and the Inactivated F.n group (Figure 6(B)). These results suggest that OMVs immunization induces strong humoral responses directed against both the whole F. necrophorum and key protective antigens. Serum IgG subclass profiling showed that after the third immunization, both IgG1 and IgG2a levels were significantly higher in the OMVs group than those in the Inactivated F.n group and F.n-3Sub groups (p < 0.05, Figure 6(C)). Under this Freund’s-adjuvanted immunization regimen, the IgG1/IgG2a ratio in the OMVs group was 0.89, indicating a Th1-biased immune profile. In parallel, serum cytokines were quantified by ELISA. After the third immunization, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-12, IL-10, and IL-13 concentrations were significantly elevated in the OMVs group compared with the Inactivated F.n group and the F.n–3Sub group (p < 0.05). IL-4 levels were significantly higher in the OMVs group than in the Inactivated F.n group (p < 0.05), but did not differ significantly from the F.n-3Sub group (p > 0.05, Figure 6(D)). These results suggest that the Freund’s-adjuvanted OMVs formulation induces a predominantly Th1-associated response while simultaneously enhancing both Th1- and Th2-related cytokine production.

Flow cytometry analysis of splenic lymphocytes showed that after the first immunization, the frequencies of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the OMVs group were significantly higher than those in the Inactivated F.n group (p < 0.05). With successive boosts, this difference became more pronounced: after the third immunization, CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell frequencies in the OMVs group were significantly higher than those in both the Inactivated F.n group and F.n-3Sub group (p < 0.05, Figure 6(E,F)). These results indicate that OMVs immunization more effectively promotes the expansion of CD4+ helper T cells and CD8+ effector T cells.

In lethal challenge experiments, all mice in the F. necrophorum + PBS group succumbed between days 4 and 13, resulting in 0% survival by day 14. In the F. necrophorum + Inactivated F.n group, deaths occurred on days 7, 9, and 12, corresponding to a 70% survival rate by day 14. In contrast, no mortality was observed in either the F. necrophorum + OMVs group or the F. necrophorum + F.n-3Sub group, and both groups maintained 100% survival through day 14 (Figure 7(A,B)). Clinical symptom scores peaked at days 9–10 in the PBS group but remained low in OMVs and F.n-3Sub groups. The PBS group showed progressive weight loss, while vaccinated groups maintained stable weight (Figure 7(C)). Consistent with improved outcomes, bacterial load quantification showed that the OMVs group exhibited a significantly reduced bacterial burden compared with the F. necrophorum + Inactivated F.n and F. necrophorum + F.n-3Sub groups (p < 0.05, Figure 7(D)). Cytokine profiling further showed that, after the third immunization, serum TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-12, IL-4, IL-10, and IL-13 levels in the OMVs group were significantly higher than those in the PBS group (Figure 8(D)). Discussion

Fusobacterium necrophorum remains a challenging vaccine target because its pathogenesis is multifactorial and current bacterin- or subunit-based approaches can provide variable protection [15,16,19]. In addition, formulation safety and adjuvant selection are important considerations for translational livestock vaccine development [33]. In this study, we evaluated F. necrophorum OMVs as a multivalent vesicular vaccine platform. The mouse and sheep experiments were designed to address complementary questions. The mouse model enabled controlled comparison of OMVs with an inactivated bacterin and a recombinant three-component subunit vaccine followed by lethal challenge, whereas the sheep experiment was used to assess host-relevant immunogenicity in a natural ruminant host. Thus, the mouse data provide proof-of-concept evidence for protection, while the sheep data support immunogenicity but do not yet establish protective efficacy in the natural host.

A major vaccinological contribution of OMVs is broad antigen coverage. F. necrophorum virulence depends on the combined activity of leukotoxin, hemolysin, OMPs, adhesion-related factors, and metabolism-associated determinants [19]. Vaccines based on one or a few recombinant antigens may therefore fail to capture the full antigenic and pathogenic complexity of this organism, which may contribute to incomplete or inconsistent protection in some settings [16,17]. This rationale is also consistent with recent multiepitope vaccine design work incorporating F. necrophorum transmembrane proteins [34]. By contrast, OMVs arise from the bacterial envelope and can package membrane-associated and periplasmic proteins in a native-like lipid environment together with pathogen-associated molecular patterns [23–25]. Consistent with this concept, our proteomic analysis identified 162 OMV-associated proteins, including the representative virulence-associated antigens 43K OMP and leukotoxin, broadly consistent with the proteomic and lipidomic characterization of F. necrophorum OMVs reported previously [35]. OMVs immunization also induced antibodies against whole F. necrophorum, OMVs, and selected recombinant antigens. These findings support the use of OMVs as a multicomponent antigen-delivery platform, although the individual protective contribution of each antigen remains unresolved.

OMVs-associated immunostimulatory molecules are also important for interpretation of the vaccine response. Components such as LPS and lipoproteins can provide innate immune signals that promote dendritic-cell activation, antigen presentation, and downstream adaptive immunity [24,25]. At the same time, excessive innate immune stimulation, especially from endotoxin-active LPS, can increase vaccine reactogenicity. Therefore, OMVs-associated LPS should be interpreted as a dual contributor to both immune enhancement and potential safety concerns. In the present study, we did not quantify endotoxin activity or perform a systematic reactogenicity analysis, such as injection-site histopathology or detailed post-immunization inflammatory profiling. Future studies should measure OMVs-associated endotoxin activity and compare native OMVs with endotoxin-reduced or genetically detoxified OMVs in veterinary-compatible formulations.

Efficient delivery to antigen-presenting cells is another strength of OMVs. The purified F. necrophorum OMVs had a predominant size of approximately 125–135 nm, a range compatible with lymphatic drainage and dendritic-cell uptake [36]. In vitro, OMVs were internalized by BMDCs within 3 h and increased the proportion of OMVs-positive cells by 6 h. In vivo, fluorescently labeled OMVs accumulated in draining lymph nodes and spleen, suggesting access to immune-inductive tissues. Functionally, OMVs upregulated MHC I, MHC II, CD80, and CD86 on BMDCs. Mechanistically, PI3K/AKT signaling is an important regulator of dendritic-cell and innate immune-cell activation [37,38]. In our study, LY294002 reduced OMVs-induced PI3K/AKT activation and maturation-marker expression, whereas SC79 partially reproduced the maturation phenotype. These data support functional involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway in OMVs-induced BMDC maturation, but they do not exclude additional pathways. These findings are also compatible with the broader role of dendritic-cell maturation in antigen presentation and cross-presentation, although direct cross-presentation assays were not performed [39,40]. Upstream receptor engagement by OMVs-associated PAMPs, including LPS and lipoproteins, should be investigated in future work.

In mice, the Freund’s-adjuvanted OMV formulation induced broad humoral responses and a Th1-associated cellular profile. Similar Th1/Th17-associated immune responses and protective effects have been reported for OMVs vaccines derived from other Gram-negative bacteria [41–44]. Importantly, both OMVs and the F.n-3Sub vaccine achieved 100% survival after lethal challenge, but OMVs further reduced hepatic bacterial burdens compared with the inactivated bacterin and F.n-3Sub groups. This pattern suggests that OMVs may provide an advantage in bacterial clearance, likely because they combine multiple native antigens with vesicle-mediated delivery and innate immune stimulation. However, the in vivo Th1-associated profile should be interpreted as the response to a Freund’s-adjuvanted OMVs formulation rather than to OMVs alone. Because all mouse antigen groups were formulated with the same Freund’s adjuvant, the group differences support an OMVs-associated contribution under a common adjuvant background, but the independent contribution of OMVs requires direct testing.

The sheep experiment strengthens the translational relevance of the study by showing that OMVs are immunogenic in a natural ruminant host. OMVs-specific total IgG increased after successive immunizations, whereas IgG1 and IgG2 subclass titers peaked after the second immunization and declined at the third sampling point. This apparent discrepancy likely reflects different kinetics of total and subclass-specific antibody responses rather than loss of immunogenicity, because total IgG remained elevated, the IgG1/IgG2 ratio remained below 1, and Th1-associated cytokines and CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell frequencies increased after the third immunization. Additional sampling points, longer follow-up, and functional antibody assays will be needed to define the durability and biological significance of these subclass kinetics in sheep.

Several limitations should be addressed before translational application. First, Freund’s adjuvant is not suitable for commercial livestock vaccination because of reactogenicity and translational safety concerns [33]; therefore, future studies should compare OMVs alone, Freund’s-adjuvanted OMVs, and OMVs formulated with veterinary-compatible adjuvants. Second, although 162 OMVs-associated proteins were identified, the relative abundance, surface exposure, epitope accessibility, and protective contribution of most candidate antigens remain unclear. Quantitative proteomics, surface accessibility assays, mutant strains, antigen overexpression, and engineered OMVs could help define the antigen combinations that drive protection. Third, protection was demonstrated only in mice. Controlled challenge studies in sheep and other ruminants are necessary to evaluate field-relevant efficacy, safety, dose requirements, reactogenicity, breadth of protection, and durability under production conditions.

In summary, F. necrophorum OMVs provide a multivalent vesicular platform that combines broad native antigen delivery, nanoscale lymphoid trafficking, and intrinsic innate immune stimulation. The current data demonstrate strong protection in mice and robust immunogenicity in sheep, supporting further development of OMVs-based vaccination strategies against F. necrophorum-associated diseases. The next critical steps are formulation optimization, safety assessment, antigen deconvolution, and natural-host challenge validation.
