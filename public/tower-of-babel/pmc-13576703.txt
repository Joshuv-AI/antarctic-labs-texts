Stressed-out immune cells
Authors: Jessica D. Rosarda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Connections between chronic stress, inflammation, and human diseases are well-established but poorly understood. In this issue of Cell Genomics, the effects of stress hormones on the immune system are investigated using single-cell RNA sequencing to better understand how stress-regulated immunological signaling contributes to disease.

Main text

A major source of stress is the environment, in which the presence of predators and pathogens or the absence of food and water directly impacts an organism’s ability to survive. The physiological response of humans to stress includes increases in arousal, sharpened focus, enhanced reaction times, and altered immune functions.1,2 These responses may confer an evolutionary advantage to detect and flee danger, store nutrients, and mitigate debilitating pathogens. In the absence of selective pressures, these adaptations may have become a liability by promoting disorders such as anxiety, depression, obesity, and chronic inflammation.1

The perception of danger is converted into a physiological response through the central nervous system (CNS)-mediated production of hormones. The hormonal mediators of the “fight-or-flight” response, adrenaline and noradrenaline, are produced by the sympathetic adrenomedullary (SAM) system. Adrenaline primarily acts peripherally, while noradrenaline can act as both a neurotransmitter when released by the locus coeruleus in the brain and as a hormone when released by the adrenal glands (Figure 1).2 The adrenal glands are also integral to the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which produces the stress hormone cortisol. A complex set of feedback mechanisms permits close control of the release of these hormones (Figure 1).3 While transient increases in stress hormones can be beneficial by elevating blood glucose concentrations, inhibiting protein synthesis, and suppressing inflammation, sustained upregulation can promote glucose intolerance, obesity, and hypertension.1,3 While the underlying cellular mechanisms by which physiologic stress progresses to a pathologic state remain poorly understood, recent work has highlighted the contributions of stress-induced inflammation to the pathogenesis of numerous human diseases.1,2,4Figure 1Stress signaling crosstalk between central, peripheral, and immune systemsGraphic illustration of the sympathetic adrenomedullary (SAM) system and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis signaling and the downstream impact of the adrenal hormones noradrenaline and cortisol on the transcriptomes of immune cell populations.

Prior studies established a role for these hormones in modulating inflammatory responses. However, many of these studies relied on animal models, which may not fully recapitulate human immune responses.4 In this issue of Cell Genomics, Sofia Benavides et al. sought to define the impact of stress hormones on human immune cells by examining the response of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from eight human donors, including four male and four female specimens, to physiologic concentrations of norepinephrine and cortisol using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). This approach allowed for within-donor comparisons to assess individual and sex-based differences in the cellular responses to the two hormones.

First, the authors defined the individual cell populations detected in the scRNA-seq dataset. The data showed that certain immune cell subpopulations exhibited significant shifts in size in response to treatment with the different hormones.5 For example, naive CD8+ T cells decreased in response to cortisol but not norepinephrine. Conversely, memory B cells were reduced with norepinephrine but not cortisol. This suggested that some of the observed effects of these hormones on the immune system could occur through alterations in the abundance of these cells, consistent with other studies.4,5

Alterations in the transcriptomes of the cell populations were also assessed. Among the most differentially expressed genes in response to cortisol were the co-chaperone FKBP5, which regulates glucocorticoid receptor folding, and VSIG4, a complement receptor that regulates immune cell reactivity.5,6,7 RNU-4 was indicated as a novel marker of norepinephrine exposure across cell types. The pro-inflammatory cytokine IL1B showed sexually dimorphic changes in expression, with upregulation in females following cortisol exposure but unchanged or having reduced expression in males. Males and females frequently showed opposing pathway enrichment, indicating distinct immune remodeling programs between the sexes. Across different cell types, female cells displayed more expansive pathway changes in response to stress hormones, while male cells exhibited more restrained alterations primarily involving genes associated with the suppression of immune responses. Despite the variability between individuals and the sexes, the authors were able to define a transcriptional signature associated with these two hormonal indicators of environmental stress.5

The authors used this signature to develop an inflammation-related stress gene panel named the cell-type-specific (CD163+) stress-inflammation score (CytSIS). The score was generated using the transcriptional signature from the particularly reactive CD163+ subset of monocytes and macrophages (MonoMacs) intersected with the MSigDB Hallmark Inflammatory Gene Set.5,8 This approach allowed for the generation of a transcriptional risk score that could be applied across other mononuclear cell datasets and worked in both mouse and human models.5 Further, the CytSIS panel proved to be predictive of disease state in patients with cardiovascular disease and rheumatoid arthritis, two environmental-stress-associated diseases.1,5 This suggests that targeted sequencing of patient-derived CD163+ MonoMacs from routine blood samples could be used to detect stress-associated diseases. However, the prognostic value has not yet been evaluated.

Although much remains to be discovered regarding how stress and inflammation cause disease, this research provides insights into the mechanisms by which stress hormones influence inflammatory signaling and human disease states.5 The complexity of stress hormone regulatory mechanisms (Figure 1), along with sex-, cell-, and tissue-specific responses to both hormones and inflammation, suggests that the relationship between the two is far more complicated in living systems.2,4,5 While many studies indicate that environmental stress is a factor in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease, arthritis, depression, and other non-hereditary disorders, the extent to which stress causes the disease or exacerbates the underlying pathology remains poorly understood.1,4 Further elucidating these pathologic relationships will be an essential step toward developing effective interventions for stress-related diseases.
