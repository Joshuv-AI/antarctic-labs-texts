Synthesis, In Vitro Cytotoxic Evaluation, DFT Calculation and Molecular Docking for Novel Pyridine Derivative Platinum Complexes
Authors: Emine Kutlu, Derya Kılıç, Fatih Mehmet Emen, Canan Vejselova Sezer, Neslihan Kaya Kınaytürk, Hatice Mehtap Kutlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNovel pyridine derivative platinium complexes, ‐bis(2,4‐dimethylpyridine)dichloroplatinum(II), [PtCl2L1 2], bis(2‐amino‐5‐ chloropyridine)dichloroplatinum(II), [PtCl2L2 2] and bis(3‐amino‐2‐chloropyridine)dichloroplatinum(II), [PtCl2L3 2]‐ were synthesized and characterized via FT‐IR, UV‐Vis, 1H‐NMR, 13C‐NMR techniques and elemental analysis. The spectral assignment to structural data were corroborated via DFT/ MPW1PW91/LanL2DZ basic set. Vibrational transitions were conferred to the complexes by potential energy distribution analysis. Molecular electrostatic potential maps, frontier molecular orbitals and Mulliken charge distribution were calculated and the active regions of the molecules were determined. The interaction between 2ITO –a lung cancer protein‐ and the complexes was investigated by molecular docking. Protein‐ligand interactions established that the lung cancer cell interact more strongly with the [PtCl2L1 2] and the [PtCl2L2 2] complexes than the [PtCl2L3 2] complex. Cytotoxicity of the complexes against A549 and Beas‐2B normal lung cells via MTT colorimetric assay revealed that [PtCl2L1 2], [PtCl2L2 2] complexes most effective against lung cancer cell linesA549 and Beas‐2B over the incubation period of 24 h. These results suggest promising in‐vitro toxicity against the A549 and Beas‐2B that warrants furthest test in an animal model.Keywords: A549, Beas‐2B, cytotoxic effect, molecular docking, pyridine Pt(II) derivative complexes

Introduction

Cancer, which is the third leading cause of death characterized by the uncontrolled division and proliferation of mutated cells. In 2020 19.3 million new cancer cases were reported. Breast cancer in females is surpassed lung cancer as the most frequently diagnosed cancer with an estimated 2.3 million new cases (11.7%), followed by lung (11.4%), colorectal (10.0%), prostate (7.3%), and stomach (5.6%) cancers. Lung cancer with an estimated 1.8 million deaths (18%) remains the leading cause of cancer‐related death and is followed by colorectal (9.4%), liver (8.3%), stomach (7.7%), and female breast (6.9%) cancers [1]. Although new paradigm‐shifting treatments for cancer have been reported, lung cancer remains a deadly disease, and improving outcomes requires overcoming three major challenges that limit long‐term survival: (1) late diagnosis, (2) disparities in cancer care, and (3) limited treatment advances for early‐stage cancer. Chemotherapy remains one of the most widely used treatment options; however, continued research and development are needed to advance more effective and efficient anticancer drugs with fewer side effects for clinical use [2].

Most of the drugs marketed for therapeutic purposes are organic compounds that do not contain metal atoms. However, there is an increasing interest in the development of metal complexes in medicinal chemistry as drugs or diagnostic agents. Metal complexes offer mechanisms of drug action that organic agents cannot achieve through their kinetic properties, variable transition metal coordination numbers, and a wide range of geometries. Transition metal complexes have distinct chemical properties and are important for various therapeutic applications [3]. The platinum compound cisplatin, one of the best known and most sold metal complex in the treatment of various malignancies, is a well‐established anticancer agent. The antitumor activity of cisplatin was discovered by Rosenberg et al. [4, 5] and since then, it has been one of the most effective drugs used in cancer chemotherapy. Besides cisplatin, a few platinum complexes, including carboplatin, oxaliplatin, nedaplatin, and lobaplatin, have been approved for cancer treatment. However, it is reported that only certain tumor types can be treated with platinum‐based anticancer drugs, while patients may experience severe side effects, such as gastrointestinal and hematologic toxicity, and may develop drug resistance [6, 7]. Oxaliplatin, carboplatin, and nedaplatin, which are cisplatin analogs, have been widely used in cancer treatment [8, 9, 10]. Although trans‐diaminedichloroplatin(II) (trans‐platin) binds to DNA and forms mono‐adducts and cross‐links, it was initially considered an ineffective antitumor agent because it was thought to lack sufficient biological activity [11]. This viewpoint began to change in the 1990s, and some trans‐platin complexes have been reported to have antitumor activity against some cis‐platin resistant tumor cells [12]. Subsequently, platinum and palladium complexes with trans geometry containing moieties such as pyridine and iminoethers, rather than only amine groups, were investigated for their biological activity [13, 14]. Recently, new coordination complexes have been synthesized by incorporating bioactive heterocyclic ligands containing N‐donor atoms into their structures. Among these heterocyclic structures, pyridines are among the most well‐known heterocyclic nitrogen ligands, and their coordination chemistry has been extensively studied. The Cambridge Structural Database (CSD, ConQuest Version V5.41, Aug 2020) [15] lists almost 150.000 coordination compounds containing a metal and a pyridine ring. Of these, 5313 are Pt(II)‐pyridine complexes. Many of these compounds have shown antitumor activity in vitro and in vivo. The pyridine complexes mostly have a planar square planar geometry. However, octahedrally coordinated complexes with Pt(IV) or palladium(IV) have also been reported [16]. Much effort is devoted to studies on pyridine derivatives including 2‐chloropyridine, 2‐bromopyridine [17], 3,4‐dimethylpyridine [18], 2‐(p‐tolyl)pyridine [19], 3‐hydroxypyridine [20] and metal halide complexes. However, there is limited research on the antitumor activity of pyridine‐derived metal halide complexes [21, 22, 23, 24]. To the best of our knowledge, although a wide range of Pt(II) complexes containing pyridine and substituted pyridine ligands have been extensively investigated, a systematic study of cis‐[PtCl2L2] complexes incorporating 2,4‐dimethylpyridine, 2‐amino‐5‐chloropyridine, and 3‐amino‐2‐chloropyridine ligands has not been reported. In particular, the influence of the type and positional arrangement of methyl, amino, and chloro substituents on the structural, spectroscopic, physicochemical, and biological properties of closely related Pt(II)–pyridine complexes remains insufficiently explored. This represents an important gap in establishing relationships between ligand substitution patterns and the resulting properties of Pt(II) complexes.

In this study, bis(2,4‐dimethylpyridine)dichloroplatinum(II), [PtCl2L1 2], bis(2‐amino‐5‐chloropyridine)dichloroplatinum(II), [PtCl2L2 2], and bis(3‐amino‐2‐chloropyridine)dichloroplatinum(II), [PtCl2L3 2], were synthesized and characterized using FT‐IR, UV–Vis, 1H‐NMR, 13C‐NMR spectroscopy, and elemental analysis. DFT calculations were performed to obtain further insights into the structural, electronic, and reactivity properties of the synthesized complexes, their vibrational behavior, and the molecular sites susceptible to electrophilic attack. Furthermore, the cytotoxic effects of these complexes were investigated against the non‐small cell lung cancer cell line A549 and the normal lung fibroblast cell line BEAS‐2B.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals and solvents used for syntheses were of reagent grade and they were used without further purification. K2[PtCI4] (Alpha‐Aesar), 2,4‐dimethylpyridine (Merck), 2‐amino‐5‐chloropyridine (Merck), 3‐amino‐2‐chloropyridine (Merck) and ethanol (Merck) were commercially obtained. Fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin‐streptomycin, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5 diphenyl‐2H‐tetrazolium bromide (MTT) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI‐1640) was purchased from GIBCO (Grand Island, NY, USA). The A549 (ATCC, CCL‐185) and Beas‐2B (ATCC, CRL‐3588) cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA).

Measurements

Melting points and decomposition temperatures were determined using an Electrothermal 9100 Digital Melting Point Apparatus. The UV‐vis spectra were obtained in DMSO solutions (10−3 mol/L) of the complexes by a Varian‐Cary 5000 spectrometer in the range from 1000 to 190 nm. The FT‐IR spectra were recorded in the range from 4000 to 400 cm−1 via Perkin Elmer Fronter FT‐IR spectrometer using KBr pellets. The NMR studies were performed via a Bruker Ultrashield Plus Biospin Avance III 400 MHz NanoBay FT‐NMR instrument and d6‐DMSO was used as the solvent. Elemental analysis (C, H, and N) was performed using a Costech ECS 4010 elemental analyzer equipped with a silver wool trap for halogens. Bio‐Tek HTX Synergy ELISA reader was used to quantification of the formazan crystals at a wavelength of 540 nm. Leica TCS‐SP5 II, Wetzlar confocal microscope was used to examine the morphology of the nucleus, membrane, cytoplasmic and other structural changes.

Preparation of the Complexes

The [PtCl2L2] complexes were synthesized according to a previously reported method [25]. For this, 0.025 mol of K2[PtCl4] was dissolved in 50 mL water under constant stirring for 2 h at room temperature. 0.05 mol of pyridine derivatives (L1; 2,4‐dimethylpyridine, L2; 2‐amino‐5‐choloropyridine and L3; 3‐amino‐2‐choloropyridine) dissolved in 20 mL ethanol were added onto the platinum solution. Then the mixture was stirred under reflux for 2 h at constant temperature 70°C. The complexes were precipitated by cooling the solutions to room temperature. Then the precipitates were filtered, washed with ethyl alcohol and dried in a desiccator.

Evaluation of Biological Activity

Cell Culture Studies

The A549 (ATCC CCL‐185) and BEAS‐2B (ATCC CRL‐3588) cell lines used in the cytotoxicity studies of the synthesized platinum complexes were commercially obtained. The cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 under standard cell culture conditions. The cultured cells were detached by trypsinization and passaged twice a week. Cells at approximately 80% confluence were used for all experiments.

MTT Colorimetric Assay

The MTT assay (3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide) was used to evaluate the cytotoxic effects of the synthesized platinum complexes. For this purpose, A549 and BEAS‐2B cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin–1 mM EDTA solution and seeded into 96‐well cell culture plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well. The cells were then incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was removed, and the synthesized platinum complexes at different concentrations (3–100 µmol L− 1) were added to the wells and incubated under the same conditions for 24 and 48 h. After the incubation periods, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg mL− 1), prepared in distilled water, was added to each well, and the cells were further incubated at 37°C for 2 h. The medium was then removed, and 200 µL of DMSO was added to each well and incubated at room temperature until the formazan crystals were completely dissolved. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using an ELISA microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate to minimize experimental variability. Confocal Microscopy Analysis

The potential morphological changes caused by the platinum complexes synthesized in this study on the A549 (ATCC, CCL‐185) and Beas‐2B (ATCC, CRL‐3588) cells were examined by confocal microscopy. The cells were seeded on sterilized covers lips at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well and incubated at 37°C for 24 h in 5% CO2 environment for 24 h by applying determined IC50 concentrations of platinum complexes. A group of the untreated A549 and Beas‐2B cell were used as control cells. At the end of the incubation period, the media were removed and the cells were washed in phosphate buffer (PBS) and were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde/paraformaldehyde solution. The cells were then again washed with PBS and were stained with a binary of phalloidin and acridine orange. The morphology of the nucleus and the membrane and the cytoplasmic and other structural changes were examined via a confocal microscope.

Computations

Gaussian 09 [26] software was used for theoretical calculations. Geometry optimizations of the synthesized platinum(II) complexes were carried out within the framework of density functional theory (DFT) employing the MPW1PW91 exchange–correlation functional together with the LanL2DZ basis set and effective core potential (ECP). The MPW1PW91 functional was selected because it has demonstrated reliable performance in describing the structural and electronic properties of transition‐metal coordination compounds, particularly Pt(II) complexes, while maintaining a reasonable computational cost [27, 28]. The LanL2DZ basis set incorporates relativistic ECPs for heavy atoms such as platinum, allowing an efficient treatment of core electrons without significantly compromising the accuracy of the calculated molecular geometries and electronic properties. Although larger basis sets and more sophisticated relativistic ECPs (e.g., Def2‐TZVP or SDD) may improve quantitative accuracy, the MPW1PW91/LanL2DZ methodology remains a widely adopted approach for comparative theoretical investigations of platinum coordination compounds. A scaling factor of 0.9706 was used for wavenumbers up to 1700 cm−1, while a scaling factor of 0.9467 was used for wavenumbers above 1700 cm−1 [29]. The 1H‐ and 13C‐NMR values were calculated by using the GIAO method in the same basic set. The time‐dependent DFT (TD‐DFT) was used to determine the UV‐Vis spectra and the electronic properties. The results were visualized via the Gauss View program [30]. The potential energy distributions (PEDs) corresponding to the vibrational frequencies observed were calculated via the VEDA 4 software [31]. Molecular docking simulations were performed using HEX 8.0.0 software to explore the possible binding orientations of the synthesized platinum(II) complexes within the ATP‐binding pocket of the EGFR kinase domain (PDB ID: 2ITO) [32]. The crystal structure of the receptor was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The resulting docking poses were analyzed according to intermolecular interaction patterns and visualized using the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System [33]. Since conventional docking algorithms model the intact complexes through non‐covalent interactions, they do not explicitly account for ligand exchange (aquation), covalent metal–protein coordination, or other chemical transformations that characterize the biological activation of platinum‐based drugs. Therefore, the docking calculations presented herein should be interpreted as providing structural insight into possible ligand–protein recognition rather than a complete representation of the molecular mechanism of action.

Statistical Evaluation

In vitro test results were expressed as mean ± SD of independent experiments performed in triplicate. Differences were tested using two‐way ANOVA GraphPad Prism 6 Software demo version. Values of p < 0.05 were considered as statistically significant compared to controls.

The aromatic ν(C═C) and ν(C═N) stretching vibrations appeared as broad overlapping bands between 1629 and 1531 cm− 1, in agreement with the spectral characteristics commonly reported for structurally related square‐planar platinum(II) complexes containing pyridine donor ligands. The calculated ν(Pt─Cl) stretching vibrations (343–328 cm− 1) also fall within the characteristic range previously reported for cis‐dichloridoplatinum(II) complexes coordinated by monodentate pyridine derivatives, further supporting the proposed coordination geometry around the platinum(II) center [34, 35].

DFT calculations reproduced the experimental spectra with good agreement, particularly in the fingerprint region, confirming the reliability of the proposed structural model. The structures of the synthesized platinum(II) complexes were further confirmed by 1H and 13C‐NMR spectroscopy. The observed chemical shifts were fully consistent with the proposed coordination of the pyridine‐based ligands to the Pt(II) center and showed good agreement with the DFT‐calculated values. Similar coordination‐induced chemical shift changes have been reported for structurally related cis‐platinum(II) and cis‐palladium(II) complexes containing pyridine, picoline, lutidine and aminopyridine ligands, confirming that coordination through the pyridine nitrogen atom produces only moderate perturbations of the ligand electronic environment while preserving the characteristic spectral features of the aromatic framework [38, 39].Rochon et al. reported comparable 1H and 13C‐NMR behavior for cis‐ and trans‐[Pt(Ypy)2] complexes (Ypy = substituted pyridines), whereas Pazderski et al. demonstrated similar coordination‐induced proton and carbon chemical shift trends for Pt(II) and Pd(II) pyridine complexes. Furthermore, the NMR characteristics observed for the amino‐substituted complexes are in good agreement with those previously described for aminopyridine‐containing platinum(II) complexes [35, 40].

The UV–Vis spectra of the synthesized platinum(II) complexes were recorded in DMSO and exhibited the characteristic electronic transitions expected for square‐planar Pt(II) complexes coordinated by pyridine‐based ligands (Figure S1). The spectra were dominated by intense ligand‐centered π→π* transitions in the 258–260 nm region together with broader low‐energy absorption bands between 325 and 345 nm accompanied by weak shoulders extending to approximately 400–423 nm. These spectral features are in good agreement with those reported for structurally related platinum(II) and palladium(II) complexes containing pyridine, picoline and chloropyridine ligands, where the higher‐energy absorptions arise predominantly from ligand‐centered transitions, whereas the lower‐energy bands are associated with metal‐involved charge‐transfer transitions [41].

The minor differences observed among the three complexes are attributed to the electronic effects of the substituents on the pyridine ring, which slightly influence the transition energies without altering the characteristic electronic structure of the square‐planar Pt(II) coordination environment. Complete absorption data together with the detailed assignment of the electronic transitions and comparative discussion with related Pt(II)/Pd(II) pyridine complexes are provided in the Supporting Information (S2: Electronic Absorption Spectral Analysis).

In Vitro Cytotoxicity Study of the Complexes

MTT Assay

MTT assay was exploited for evaluating the in vitro cytotoxicity effect of the synthesized complexes. The result of the MTT assay expressed as viability percentages showed that the viability of both A549 and Beas‐2B cells decreased when they were exposed to the [PtCl2L1 2] complex over 24 h. Statistically significant decrease was observed for both of the test cell lines tested in the concentration range from 12.5 to 100 µM (Figure 1) and the cell viability decrease was dose‐dependent. Half maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of the [PtCl2L1 2] complex over 24 h were detected to be 10.4 and 9.2 µM for A549 and Beas‐2B cells, respectively. The IC50 value of the [PtCl2L1 2] complex on A549 cells over 24 h was found to be slightly higher than that for the Beas‐2B cells. Based on IC50 values the selectivity index (SI) was calculated to be 0.8 for [PtCl2L1 2] for 24 h that indicate no apparent selectivity between cancer and normal cells for exposure time of 24 h. This behavior differs from several recently reported pyridine‐derived Pt(II) complexes that display preferential cytotoxicity toward A549 cells while exhibiting lower toxicity toward normal epithelial cells, suggesting that the nature and electronic properties of the coordinated pyridine substituents play an important role in determining biological selectivity [42]. Similar dose‐dependent antiproliferative responses have been reported for structurally related Pt(II) complexes containing pyridine‐based ligands, which generally exert their cytotoxic effects through platinum‐mediated DNA damage followed by inhibition of cell proliferation and induction of apoptosis [43].

Cell viability percentage‐ Concentration graph of the [PtCl2L1 2] complex for 24 h. * = p < 0.05 (GraphPad Prism 6.0).

The cytotoxic effect of [PtCl2L1 2] after 48 h of exposure was comparable to that observed after 24 h (Figure 2). The IC50 values remained essentially unchanged, being 10.5 µM for A549 cells and 9.3 µM for Beas‐2B cells. The SI value (SI = 0.8) calculated for exposure time of 48 h for [PtCl2L1 2] shows no apparent selectivity on cancer and normal cells. The absence of a marked time‐dependent reduction in the IC50 values suggests that the antiproliferative activity of [PtCl2L1 2] is established during the early stage of treatment and is maintained upon prolonged exposure. A similar time‐dependent cytotoxic profile has been reported for several pyridine‐based Pt(II) complexes, which rapidly exert their biological effects following cellular uptake and interaction with intracellular targets [42, 44].

Growth inhibition graph of [PtCl2L1 2] complex over 48 h. * = p < 0.05 (GraphPad Prism 6.0).

The comparable IC50 values obtained after 24 and 48 h further indicate that extending the incubation time does not substantially enhance the cytotoxic response of [PtCl2L1 2] under the present experimental conditions. Similar observations have been reported for pyridine‐containing Pt(II) complexes evaluated against A549 cells, where the major antiproliferative effect was established during the initial period of exposure and only minor changes in cytotoxicity were observed upon prolonged incubation [45].

The dose‐dependent cytotoxicity effect of the [PtCl2L1 2] complex on the A549 and Beas‐2B cells over 48 h is presented in Figure 2.

As shown in Figure 3, the [PtCl2L2 2] complex inhibited the proliferation of both A549 and Beas‐2B cells in a concentration‐dependent manner after 24 h of treatment. A statistically significant reduction in cell viability was observed over the concentration range of 12.5–100 µM for A549 cells, whereas significant inhibition of Beas‐2B cells was detected between 6.25 and 100 µM. The IC50 values were determined to be 8.6 µM for A549 cells and 10.3 µM for Beas‐2B cells, indicating a slightly greater sensitivity of the lung adenocarcinoma cells toward [PtCl2L2 2] than the normal bronchial epithelial cells.

The results of the cell viability study of the [PtCl2L2 2] complex over 24 h, * = p < 0.05 (GraphPad Prism 6.0).

The antiproliferative activity observed for [PtCl2L2 2] is comparable to that reported for structurally related Pt(II) complexes containing aminopyridine and pyridine‐derived ligands, which generally exhibit IC50 values in the low micromolar range against A549 cells. Li et al. reported A549 IC50 values between approximately 5 and 15 µM for amino‐pyridine Pt(II) complexes [46], whereas Bhaduri and Moi described pyridine‐based Pt(II) complexes exhibiting similar cytotoxic potency together with favorable DNA‐binding properties [42]. The IC50 value obtained in the present study therefore falls within the activity range reported for analogous platinum(II) pyridine complexes.

After 48 h of treatment, the [PtCl2L2 2] complex exhibited enhanced antiproliferative activity, with IC50 values of 6.34 and 6.33 µM against A549 and Beas‐2B cells, respectively. A significant inhibition of cell proliferation was observed in both cell lines over the concentration range of 6.25–100 µM (Figure 4). Compared with the 24 h treatment, the lower IC50 value obtained for A549 cells indicates that prolonged exposure enhances the cytotoxic efficacy of [PtCl2L2 2]. This complex indicates preferential selectivity toward cancer cells with SI indexes 1.2, 1.0 obtained for 24 and 48 h time of exposure, respectively. The cytotoxic potency of [PtCl2L2 2] is comparable to that reported for structurally related platinum(II) complexes containing pyridine‐ and aminopyridine‐based ligands, which generally exhibit antiproliferative activity in the low micromolar range against A549 lung cancer cells. For example, Li et al. reported several amino‐pyridine Pt(II) complexes with pronounced cytotoxic activity toward A549 cells, while Bhaduri and Moi demonstrated that pyridine‐derived Pt(II) complexes possessing similar cytotoxic potency also exhibited favorable DNA‐binding characteristics supported by DFT and molecular docking analyses [42, 46].

Antiproliferative activity of the [PtCl2L2 2] complex for 48 h, * = p < 0.05 (GraphPad Prism 6.0).

Although [PtCl2L2 2] was the most active complex synthesized in the present study, its nearly identical IC50 values toward A549 and Beas‐2B cells after 48 h indicate that further optimization of the ligand framework may be required to improve cancer cell selectivity while maintaining its high antiproliferative activity. This observation is consistent with previous studies emphasizing that subtle modifications of pyridine‐based carrier ligands can markedly influence both cytotoxic potency and cellular selectivity of Pt(II) complexes [44, 47].

The [PtCl2L3 2] complex exhibited lower antiproliferative activity than the other synthesized platinum(II) complexes. After 24 h of treatment, [PtCl2L3 2] did not reduce the viability of either A549 or Beas‐2B cells to 50% within the investigated concentration range (Figure 5), and no statistically significant decrease in cell viability was observed. Therefore, IC25 values of 34.3 and 18.5 µM were calculated for A549 and Beas‐2B cells, respectively. These values indicate substantially lower cytotoxic potency than those obtained for [PtCl2L1 2] and [PtCl2L2 2] under identical experimental conditions. Based on the obtained IC25 concentrations the SI values of for [PtCl2L3 2] complex calculated for 24 and 48 h of exposure were 0.5 and 2.2. These findings clearly indicated the [PtCl2L3 2] complex has no selectivity toward cancer cells in 24 h but may indicate low‐to‐moderate selectivity toward A549 cancer cells. Compared with recently reported platinum(II) complexes containing pyridine‐based ligands, the antiproliferative activity of [PtCl2L3 2] is relatively modest. For example, fluorine‐containing pyridine Pt(II) complexes described by Zhao et al. exhibited significantly lower IC50 values against A549 cells together with improved selectivity toward cancer cells, while platinum(II) complexes bearing 2‐(diphenylphosphino)pyridine ligands also demonstrated markedly stronger antiproliferative activity accompanied by apoptosis induction and DNA interaction [48].

Antiproliferative activity graph of [PtCl2L3 2] for 24 h.

The lower activity of [PtCl2L3 2] therefore suggests that, although the square‐planar coordination environment is preserved, the position of the amino substituent on the pyridine ring substantially influences the biological response of the complex. Similar structure‐dependent variations in cytotoxic activity have been reported for platinum(II) complexes containing pyridine‐derived ligands, where relatively small modifications of the auxiliary ligand significantly affect the antiproliferative properties of the resulting complexes [47, 49, 50].

The [PtCl2L3 2] complex inhibited the viability of both A549 and Beas‐2B cells in a concentration‐dependent manner after 48 h of treatment. Statistically significant reductions in cell viability were observed only at the higher concentrations of 50 and 100 µM for both cell lines, whereas Beas‐2B cells also exhibited a significant decrease in viability at 25 µM (Figure 6). These findings indicate that prolonged exposure enhances the antiproliferative effect of [PtCl2L3 2], although considerably higher concentrations are required compared with [PtCl2L1 2] and [PtCl2L2 2].

A similar concentration‐ and time‐dependent cytotoxic response has been reported for several Pt(II) complexes containing pyridine‐based ligands. Ari et al. demonstrated that Pt(II) complexes bearing hydroxymethyl‐ and hydroxyethylpyridine ligands exhibited dose‐dependent inhibition of A549 cell proliferation, with prolonged exposure leading to enhanced antiproliferative activity through apoptosis induction [51]. Likewise, Pt(II) bipyridine complexes have been shown to display increased cytotoxicity after extended incubation periods, highlighting the importance of exposure time in determining the biological response of square‐planar platinum(II) complexes. Despite this time‐dependent increase in activity, [PtCl2L3 2] remained less potent than the other synthesized complexes, suggesting that the position of the amino substituent on the pyridine ring significantly influences the antiproliferative efficacy. This observation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that relatively small structural modifications of pyridine‐based ligands can markedly alter the biological activity of platinum(II) complexes [46, 52].

The [PtCl2L3 2] complex exhibited greater selectivity toward A549 lung cancer cells than toward normal Beas‐2B cells. The IC25 values were determined to be 23.0 µM for A549 cells and 50.7 µM for Beas‐2B cells, indicating that approximately two‐fold higher concentrations were required to produce a comparable inhibitory effect in normal bronchial epithelial cells. Although [PtCl2L3 2] displayed lower overall cytotoxic potency than [PtCl2L1 2] and [PtCl2L2 2], its preferential inhibition of cancer cells suggests a more favorable selectivity profile.

Improved selectivity toward malignant cells is considered a desirable characteristic for platinum‐based anticancer agents because it may reduce toxicity toward normal tissues. Similar observations have been reported for pyridine‐containing Pt(II) complexes, where appropriate modification of the auxiliary ligand enhanced cancer cell selectivity without necessarily increasing overall cytotoxic potency [53]. For example, Mojaddami et al. described Pt(II) complexes containing 2‐(diphenylphosphino)pyridine ligands that combined potent antiproliferative activity with significantly lower toxicity toward normal cells, while recent studies on monofunctional pyridine Pt(II) complexes have also demonstrated that the nature and substitution pattern of the pyridine ligand plays an important role in determining both cytotoxicity and cellular selectivity [34].

The present findings therefore suggest that, for the synthesized complexes, ligand substitution influences not only the magnitude of the antiproliferative response but also the balance between cytotoxic potency and selectivity, an aspect that is further discussed in relation to the molecular docking and theoretical calculations.

DFT Calculation and Molecular Docking Study of the Complexes

Molecular Electrostatic Potential Surface (MEP)

Charge distribution on the molecules can be determined via the molecular electrostatic potential surface of map (MEP). Information on charge distribution can be used for determining the interactions among the molecules, the electrophilic and the nucleophilic reactivity, the reactivity of the molecular behavior, structural activity and the hydrogen bonds. Interpretation of the MEP maps play a key role in determining the active sites taking place in the chemical binding of the molecules and in the synthesis of new chemicals. The blue, red and the green colors indicate nucleophilicity, electrophilicity and the neutral or zero electrostatic potential region for hydrogen bond interactions. In Figure 11 are presented the MEP maps of the complexes. The MEP surface of the [PtCl2L1 2] complex reveals that the minimum electrostatic potential (−0.129 a.u., red) is localized around the chlorine atoms, whereas the maximum electrostatic potential (+0.129 a.u., blue) is mainly distributed around the hydrogen atoms. Here, the blue color indicates the strongest attraction and the red color indicates strong repulsion. In a similar way, for the [PtCl2L2 2] and [PtCl2L3 2] complexes while the regions with negative potential are concentrated on the Pt‐Cl atoms, the regions with positive potential are concentrated on NH2. These active regions clearly indicate the biological activity of the complexes.

The color coded picture indicating the electrostatic potential surface of the complexes (a) [PdCl2L1 2], (b) [PdCl2L2 2], and (c) [PdCl2L3 2].

In addition to identifying electron‐rich and electron‐deficient regions, the MEP distribution provides qualitative insight into the potential chemical reactivity of the platinum(II) complexes. Consistent with the well‐established activation mechanism of platinum‐based anticancer agents, the coordinated chloride ligands are expected to represent the most labile sites and may undergo aquation under physiological conditions, generating reactive aqua species capable of coordinating to biological nucleophiles such as the N7 atoms of guanine residues in DNA. It should be noted, however, that these activation processes cannot be directly inferred from the MEP surface itself, which reflects the electronic distribution of the intact complexes. Therefore, the MEP analysis should be interpreted as providing complementary electronic information rather than direct evidence of ligand substitution or biological activation.

HOMO‐LUMO Analysis

The HOMO‐LUMO values can be used to determine the polarization, electronegativity and reactivity of a molecule. The difference between the HOMO and LUMO energy levels, which is also named as the energy gap, offer information regarding the chemical reactivity and the kinetic stability of the molecule [54]. Electronegativity is the value that shows the power of a molecule to attract the valence electrons. If the electronegativity of a molecule is high the molecule attracts more valence electrons [55].

The frontier molecular orbital (HOMO–LUMO) energies were calculated to evaluate the electronic properties and relative chemical reactivity of the synthesized platinum(II) complexes (Table 3) and their surface pictograms are given in Figure 12. The HOMO–LUMO energy gap (ΔE) is commonly used as a qualitative descriptor of molecular stability and electronic reactivity, where smaller energy gaps generally indicate increased charge‐transfer capability and enhanced electronic responsiveness. Among the investigated complexes, the HOMO–LUMO energy gap gradually decreased in the order [PtCl2L1 2] (5.329 eV) > [PtCl2LL2 2] (4.983 eV) > [PtCl2L3 2] (4.842 eV). A similar trend was observed for the chemical hardness (η), which decreased from 2.665 to 2.421 eV, whereas the chemical softness (S) increased from 0.188 to 0.207 eV. These results suggest a progressive increase in the electronic flexibility and charge‐transfer capability from [PtCl2L1 2] to [PtCl2L3 2]. Consistent with this trend, the electrophilicity index (ω) and the maximum electronic charge transfer parameter (ΔNmax) also increased across the series, indicating an enhanced tendency of [PtCl2L3 2] to accept electronic charge during molecular interactions.

Nevertheless, the experimental cytotoxicity results revealed that [PtCl2L22] exhibited higher anticancer activity than [PtCl2L32], indicating that biological efficacy cannot be explained solely by frontier molecular orbital descriptors. For platinum(II) compounds, cellular uptake, chloride ligand substitution, DNA coordination efficiency, and intracellular stability are equally important determinants of biological activity. Therefore, the calculated DFT descriptors should be regarded as complementary indicators of intrinsic electronic properties rather than direct predictors of cytotoxic potency.

The calculated electronic descriptors are comparable to those reported for structurally related cis‐platinum(II) complexes containing aromatic mono‐ and bidentate nitrogen donor ligands, where HOMO–LUMO energy gaps in the range of approximately 4–6 eV have been associated with moderate electronic reactivity and sufficient thermodynamic stability for biological applications. The presence of aromatic nitrogen‐containing ligands contributes to stabilization of the coordination sphere while preserving the electronic characteristics required for interaction with biological targets.

It should be emphasized, however, that the frontier molecular orbital parameters describe the electronic properties of the intact platinum(II) complexes and provide only qualitative information regarding their relative reactivity. Although the relatively small HOMO–LUMO energy gaps suggest moderate electronic reactivity, these descriptors alone cannot be used to conclude whether the complexes are kinetically labile or inert under physiological conditions. The kinetic activation of platinum‐based anticancer agents is primarily governed by chloride ligand substitution (aquation), Pt–ligand bond strengths, and activation barriers, which are beyond the scope of conventional HOMO–LUMO analysis. Therefore, the present results indicate that the synthesized complexes possess favorable electronic characteristics for biological interactions, while detailed mechanistic studies are required to evaluate their kinetic behavior.

Docking studies

Molecular docking technique can explain drug‐enzyme interactions. Thus, recently it has become a frequently used technique in intelligent drug design [25]. It has been reported that platinum derivative have been used as drugs in treatment of types of many cancer alone or in combination with radiotherapy and other antitumor and antiangiogenic drug combinations [56]. The DFT/MPW1PW91/LanL2DZ basic set was used for to elucidate the structures of the synthesized complexes. The Hex (version 8.0.0) software was used for molecular docking with the following parameters: FFT mode—3D quick life, distance range—40, twist range—360, correlation type—shape + electro + DARS, grid dimension—0.6, receptor range—180, and ligand range—180.

To complement the experimental cytotoxicity studies and obtain structural insight into the possible protein‐binding behavior of the synthesized platinum(II) complexes, molecular docking calculations were performed using the kinase domain of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR; PDB ID: 2ITO), a clinically relevant molecular target in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [57, 58]. EGFR plays a pivotal role in regulating cell proliferation, survival, differentiation, and apoptosis, and its dysregulation has been closely associated with lung cancer progression [59]. Consequently, inhibition of EGFR signaling has become an established therapeutic strategy in NSCLC. In the present study, docking calculations were performed to explore whether the synthesized complexes could adopt energetically favorable binding poses within the ATP‐binding pocket of EGFR.

Conventional molecular docking evaluates reversible non‐covalent interactions and does not account for ligand exchange (aquation), covalent platinum–DNA coordination, or other activation processes known for platinum(II)‐based anticancer agents. Therefore, the present docking results should be considered as complementary computational evidence of potential ligand–protein recognition rather than a direct representation of the established pharmacological mechanism of these complexes.

The docking results showed that all platinum(II) complexes could be accommodated within the binding cavity of the receptor and formed several non‐covalent interactions with amino acid residues located in the active site (Figure 13 and Table 4). The [PtCl2L1 2] complex exhibited interactions with ASP855, LYS745, GLU762, PHE723, and GLU785, whereas the [PtCl2L2 2] complex interacted predominantly with PHE723, GLU746, LEU747, and GLU758. The [PtCl2L3 2] complex established fewer interactions, mainly involving LYS745 and ILE759. These differences suggest that structural variations among the ligands influence their predicted binding orientations and interaction profiles within the EGFR binding pocket.

It is important to emphasize that the present docking calculations describe only the possible non‐covalent recognition of the intact platinum complexes by the target protein. Conventional docking algorithms evaluate ligand binding under simplified conditions and do not account for ligand exchange (aquation), covalent coordination to amino acid residues or DNA, changes in platinum coordination geometry, or other chemical transformations that are known to occur for platinum‐based anticancer agents under physiological conditions. Therefore, the obtained docking poses should not be interpreted as representing the complete mechanism of action of these complexes.

The experimental cytotoxicity results demonstrated that the synthesized platinum complexes possess promising anticancer activity against A549 lung cancer cells. Although the docking calculations indicate that the complexes are capable of forming favorable interactions within the EGFR binding pocket, these computational observations alone cannot establish EGFR inhibition as the primary mechanism responsible for the observed cytotoxicity. The biological activity of platinum‐based compounds is influenced by multiple processes, including cellular uptake, aquation kinetics, intracellular transport, DNA coordination, induction of apoptosis, oxidative stress, and interactions with various intracellular biomolecules. These factors are beyond the scope of conventional molecular docking simulations. In addition to the molecular docking results should be considered complementary to the DFT calculations and the experimental biological assays rather than independent evidence of the mechanism of action.

Overall, the docking study provides complementary structural information regarding the potential recognition of the synthesized platinum(II) complexes by a therapeutically relevant lung cancer target. Rather than demonstrating a definitive molecular mechanism, the computational results should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating evidence that supports the experimental findings and identifies plausible ligand–protein interactions for future biochemical and mechanistic investigations.

When interpreted together with the DFT‐derived electronic descriptors, the docking findings provide complementary insights into the potential biological behavior of the synthesized platinum(II) complexes. Nevertheless, neither computational approach alone is sufficient to explain the experimentally observed cytotoxic activity, which is governed by multiple physicochemical and biological processes.

Conclusions

In this study, bis(2,4‐dimethylpyridine)dichloroplatinum(II), [PtCl2L1 2], bis(2‐amino‐5‐chloropyridine)dichloroplatinum(II), [PtCl2L2 2] and bis(3‐amino‐2‐chloropyridine)dichloroplatinum(II), [PtCl2L3 2] were synthesized. The complexes were characterized experimentally by FT‐IR, UV–Vis, 1H NMR, 1 3C NMR spectroscopy, and elemental analysis, while their molecular structures were investigated theoretically using DFT calculations at the MPW1PW91/LANL2DZ level of theory. The structures of the synthesized complexes were elucidated via DFT/ MPW1PW91/LanL2DZ basic set. The results of the theoretical and the experimental studies were in good conformity.

The MEP results showed that for the [PtCl2L2 2] and [PtCl2L3 2] complexes the regions with negative potential are concentrated on the Pt‐Cl atoms and the regions with positive potential are concentrated on NH2. These active regions suggest potential sites for electrophilic and nucleophilic interactions and provide qualitative support for the experimentally observed biological activity; however, they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of biological efficacy. The molecular docking study demonstrated that the synthesized platinum(II) complexes were capable of adopting energetically favorable binding poses within the active site of the EGFR kinase domain and establishing several non‐covalent interactions with key amino acid residues. Further biochemical and mechanistic studies are therefore required to validate the proposed ligand–protein interactions and to elucidate the molecular basis of the observed anticancer effects.

To contextualize the docking findings, it is important to note that several studies have compared newly synthesized platinum complexes with clinically approved drugs such as cisplatin, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin. Ruswanto et al. demonstrated that bis‐thiourea platinum(II) complexes exhibited lower binding energies and more stable interactions with breast, lung, and prostate cancer receptors compared to cisplatin, highlighting the potential of structural modifications to enhance protein recognition [60]. Similarly, Menon and Manoj reported that thiocarbohydrazone derivatives showed significant cytotoxicity against breast and lung cancer cell lines, with docking studies confirming favorable binding to EGFR (PDB ID: 2ITO), a clinically relevant target for non‐small cell lung cancer [61]. In another comparative study, Elmorsy et al. synthesized triphenylamine‐linked heterocycles and found that several analogues displayed superior cytotoxicity to cisplatin against A549 lung cancer cells, with docking simulations revealing strong binding affinities within EGFR kinase domains [62]. Taken together, these reports indicate that the non‐covalent interactions observed for our platinum(II) complexes within the EGFR ATP‐binding pocket are consistent with the binding profiles described for clinically established platinum drugs [63]. Thus, while the primary cytotoxic mechanism of platinum‐based agents remains DNA coordination, the observed EGFR recognition provides complementary structural evidence that situates our complexes within the broader pharmacological framework of platinum chemotherapy.

Complexes, [PtCl2L1 2]and [PtCl2L2 2] exhibited higher dose‐dependent cytotoxicity against the A549 lung adenocarcinoma and Beas‐2B normal lung cells after 24 h incubation. The morphologies of the lung adenocarcinoma A549 and Beas‐2B cells after 24 h treatment with the complexes showed chromatin condensation holes on their cytoskeletons and the cells shrunk. These were indications that the platinum complexes triggered programmed cell death.

The platinum complexes synthesized in this study can be used as potent cytotoxic agent in treatment of A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cells and hence, further development may produce a promising candidate for use as a small‐lung chemotherapeutic agent.
