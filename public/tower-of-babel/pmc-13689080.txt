Reduced neural sensitivity to emotional faces and voices in preterm five-year-olds: a comparative study
Authors: Rowena Van den Broeck, Lisa Gistelinck, Silke Vos, Bieke Bollen, Els Ortibus, Gunnar Naulaers, Stephanie Van der Donck, Bart Boets
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate emotion processing is crucial for social development. Preterm birth is associated with neurodevelopmental alterations and increased vulnerability to subtle socio-emotional difficulties, often described within the preterm behavioral phenotype. Previous studies have reported impaired emotion recognition in preterm populations, but have largely relied on behavioral tasks and primarily focused on facial expressions. Because explicit emotion recognition is still developing in early childhood, objective neural markers of implicit emotion discrimination may be particularly informative for early detection.MethodsWe administered a series of frequency-tagging electroencephalography (EEG) paradigms to investigate neural sensitivity to brief changes in emotional expressions in a cohort of five-year-old preterm children (N = 66), and age-matched full-term peers (N = 32). Frequency-tagging EEG utilizes fast periodic stimulation to elicit synchronized brain responses measurable in the frequency domain. Neural sensitivity to changes in emotional expressions was examined in both the visual and auditory modality, using oddball paradigms with neutral faces or voices presented at base frequency and emotional expressions (either fearful or happy) presented as periodic oddballs. Linear mixed models were used to investigate the effects of group, emotion, and prematurity severity.ResultsAll children showed implicit neural discrimination between neutral and emotional expressions. Compared to full-term peers, children born before 32 weeks of gestation, but not those born between 33 and 36 weeks of gestation, showed reduced neural sensitivity to facial emotional expression changes. In contrast, all preterm children (< 37 weeks of gestation) showed reduced neural sensitivity to vocal emotional expression changes. Across modalities, reduced neural sensitivity to happy emotional expressions was associated with higher parent-reported social difficulties.ConclusionsFive-year-old children born preterm show reduced neural sensitivity to brief changes in facial and vocal emotional expressions compared with full-term peers. Strikingly, the impact of prematurity severity differed between the visual and auditory modalities, indicating differential maturation of higher-level visual and auditory processing following preterm birth. These findings contribute to a more detailed understanding of socio-emotional development after preterm birth and highlight the importance of early, modality-sensitive monitoring of socio-emotional development across the entire preterm population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05097-z.

Background

Accurate emotion processing is fundamental for social functioning. From early childhood onward, the ability to implicitly and explicitly discriminate emotional cues supports successful social interactions and the development of stable relationships [1–3]. When these skills are compromised, children may face interpersonal challenges. For example, autistic individuals often show difficulties in recognizing and processing emotions, as demonstrated in behavioral tasks [4–7] and using frequency-tagging electroencephalography (EEG) studies [8–10]. Using this same EEG method, reduced discrimination between angry and neutral faces has also been demonstrated among adolescents exposed to childhood adversity, illustrating the influence of early environmental stressors on emotion processing [11, 12].

Premature birth has likewise been proposed to alter emotion processing abilities. Infants born before 37 weeks of gestation [13] are at heightened risk for altered neurodevelopment [14–17]. These difficulties are often subclinical yet pervasive and are collectively captured within the preterm behavioral phenotype, comprising inattention, anxiety, and socio-emotional difficulties [18–20]. Preterm (PT) individuals show a 3-to-4-fold increased prevalence of psychiatric conditions, such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [21–23]. Both idiopathic ASD and ADHD, as well as the preterm behavioral phenotype include difficulties in emotion recognition and altered processing of emotional stimuli [4, 18, 19, 24–26].

Most research on socio-emotional development following PT birth employs behavioral tasks. Several studies have reported that PT children have difficulties identifying emotional facial expressions. For instance, school-aged PT children performed worse than their full-term (FT) peers on an Emotion Recognition Task (ERT) involving facial expressions of pictures of peers [27]. They scored particularly low in recognizing positive emotions, and poorer performance was linked to increased peer problems. The authors hypothesized that early life experiences of PT children may be marked by increased exposure to negative affect (e.g. parental anxiety), potentially leading to heightened sensitivity to negative expressions and specific difficulties in recognizing positive expressions [27]. Research involving PT toddlers indicated challenges in labeling emotions, although children were able to select appropriate emotional expressions for a given context [28]. In broader social perception tasks, school-aged PT children performed worse than FT peers when interpreting other’s emotions, mainly showing difficulties in interpreting facial and bodily cues [29]. These difficulties may persist into adolescence [30] and adulthood, where individuals born very preterm and with extremely low birth weight exhibit slower responses and reduced accuracy in emotion recognition tasks [31, 32].

Neurophysiological studies similarly point to altered emotion processing in PT populations. Neural differences in emotional face processing emerge as early as infancy [24] and remain evident in school-age, where PT children show atypical neural network activity when viewing angry and fearful faces [26, 33].

Research on vocal emotional processing, however, is limited in PT populations. A mismatch-negativity study at term-equivalent age found no group differences in neural responses to emotional speech sounds between PT and FT infants, but did report atypical processing of non-speech auditory stimuli [34]. At later developmental stages, PT toddlers exhibit reduced neural discrimination of prosody [35], a deficit associated with delayed language development in both FT and PT children. In typically developing FT school-aged children, vocal prosody decoding has also been linked to social functioning [36], underscoring the importance of accurate prosody processing for socio-emotional development.

More broadly, emotion research in PT populations has largely relied on behavioral tasks and a predominant focus on visual emotional cues. As explicit emotion recognition continues to develop until early adolescence [37–39], behavioral tasks may lack the sensitivity to detect early or subtle deficits in emotion discrimination. This highlights the need for objective neurophysiological measures that can capture implicit emotion processing across both visual and auditory modalities in young children.

In the present study, we use frequency-tagging EEG to investigate implicit emotion discrimination in five-year-old PT and FT children. This approach uses fast periodic stimulation to elicit synchronized brain activity that can be quantified in the frequency domain [40] and has proven effective to investigate emotion sensitivity in young children [41] and clinical populations [8–12]. We assess neural responses to brief changes in facial and vocal expressions of fear and happiness. These emotions were chosen as they have been proven to elicit differential responses in children with autism compared to typically developing peers [8, 10], and PT children are potentially exposed to more fearful and less happy expressions during the first years of their life [27].

To explore potential gradient effects of prematurity based on gestational age (GA), we further distinguish between very preterm (VPT; GA ≤ 32 weeks) and moderate-to-late preterm (MPT; GA 33–36 weeks) (subdivision similar to [42, 43]), as the latter is often underrepresented in previous research.

Previous work in this same preschool cohort revealed a reduced neural bias for voices, but not faces, in PT children [44]. Similarly, a recent frequency-tagging EEG study in a school-aged sample reported comparable neural sensitivity to facial expressions in PT and FT children [45]. Based on these findings and the broader literature, we expect reduced implicit emotion discrimination in PT children, potentially more pronounced in, or specific to, the auditory domain. Finally, we investigate whether neural sensitivity to emotional cues is linked to prematurity indices (gestational age and birth weight), and to behavioral outcomes, including social responsiveness and autistic traits.

Methods

This study is part of the Resilience study, which monitors a cohort of PT children since their stay at the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) at University Hospital Leuven [46]. The current study is embedded within a larger research protocol consisting of three laboratory sessions. The EEG paradigms were administered in the first session, while intelligence and autism characteristics were assessed in the third session. The second session consisted of multiple face-to-face interaction tasks with the child, which are not addressed in the current report.

Participants

The sample comprised 66 PT children and 32 FT controls, matched for age and sex (Table 1). All children were 5.5 years old, native Dutch speakers, and had normal or corrected-to-normal hearing and vision. Intellectual ability was measured using an abbreviated version of the Dutch Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence, fourth edition (WPPSI-IV-NL) [47, 48]. The Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI) was estimated using the Information and Similarities subtests, the Fluid Reasoning Index (FRI) using the Matrix Reasoning and Picture Concepts subtests, and Working Memory (WM) using the Picture Memory and Zoo Locations subtests. EEG experiments

Five different frequency-tagging EEG experiments were conducted, of which two are discussed below. The order of the experiments was counterbalanced over participants.

Stimuli

Visual stimuli: faces

Visual stimuli were identical as in Van der Donck et al. (2020) [8]. The stimulus set comprised colored photographs of full front faces of 7 male and 7 female individuals selected from the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces database [53], displaying neutral, fearful, and happy expressions. All images were sized to 300 × 450 pixels and placed against a gray background. They were standardized by equalizing mean pixel luminance and contrast across faces. Stimuli were presented at a viewing distance of 79 cm on a 60 Hz 23.8-inch LED-backlit LCD monitor with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. All faces subtended a visual angle of 6 × 9° and were displayed using a custom-built Java script (SinStim).

Auditory stimuli: voices

Auditory stimuli were identical as in Vos et al. (2023), and consisted of vocal utterances of 5 male and 5 female speakers selected from the Emotional Voices and Identity Database [54]. Utterances were 250 ms long, including 10 ms linear fade-in and fade-out ramps to avoid clipping, and were normalized for root mean square (RMS) overall energy. Stimuli mainly contained short words and phonemes conveying neutral, fearful, or happy emotional prosody. Stimuli were presented at a comfortable listening level in free field via a Genelec 8040 BPM speaker positioned 50 cm in front of the child, using a custom Matlab script and an RME fireface UC.

Design

The design and presentation frequencies of both the visual [8, 10, 11, 45] and auditory [54, 55] paradigm were identical to previous frequency-tagging research.

Visual paradigm: neutral and emotional faces

Neutral faces were rapidly presented through sinusoidal contrast modulation at a 6 Hz base rate. Every fifth face was expressive (i.e., fearful or happy, in separate sequences), resulting in a 1.2 Hz oddball rate (Fig. 1A). Facial identity varied with every image. The paradigm included eight 64-second sequences (four per emotion), including 2s fade-in and fade-out periods, resulting in four minutes of total stimulus presentation per emotion. The eight sequences were presented in random order, with short breaks as needed. To maintain attention, children were instructed to press a button whenever the black fixation cross on the nasion of the face briefly (0.3 s) turned red, occurring ten times per sequence at pseudo-random intervals.

Auditory paradigm: neutral and emotional voices

Neutral vocal utterances were presented periodically, at a 4 Hz base rate. Expressive utterances (i.e., fearful or happy, in separate sequences) were interleaved every third stimulus, resulting in a 1.333 Hz oddball rate (Fig. 1B). The paradigm included twelve 64-second sequences (six per emotion), including a 2s fade-in/out period, resulting in six minutes of stimulation per emotion. Each sequence contained utterances from a single speaker. Across sequences, six different speakers were used per condition (three males and three females). The twelve sequences were presented in random order, with breaks provided as needed. To maintain attention, children were instructed to close their eyes and press a button whenever they heard a predefined alarm sound, occurring four times per sequence at random intervals.

EEG recording

EEG was recorded at 512 Hz using a BioSemi Active-Two amplifier system with 32 Ag/AgCl electrodes. The common mode sense (CMS) and driven right leg (DRL) electrodes were used for reference and ground.

EEG analysis

Preprocessing

All data was processed using Letswave 6 (https://www.letswave.org/; RRID: SCR_016414) and Matlab 2021b (RRID: SCR_001622). Preprocessing steps were consistent with previously reported frequency-tagging analyses (e.g. [8–11, 44, 45, 54]). EEG data was segmented per sequence, including 2s before and after stimulation, resulting in 68s segments for both paradigms. Noisy electrodes were re-estimated using linear interpolation of the three spatially nearest electrodes, with no more than two electrodes per participant. Interpolation was done for 8/32 (25%) FT children and 13/66 (~ 24%) PT children. Data was bandpass filtered (0.1–100 Hz) using a fourth-order Butterworth filter, down-sampled to 256 Hz and re-referenced to a common average reference. In line with other frequency-tagging research, we did not perform any blink correction (e.g. [56–59]) because the technique yields responses with a high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at specific frequency bins, which are typically not influenced by broadband blink artefacts [60].

Frequency-domain analyses

The pre-processed segments were further cropped after the fade-in period ensuring an exact number of frequency-bins to avoid overspill (15,360 and 15,172 bins for the visual and auditory paradigms, respectively). Per modality, the resulting segments were averaged separately for each emotion condition (fearful and happy), and transformed to the frequency domain using a Fast Fourier Transform.

Amplitudes at the oddball frequencies (1.2 Hz and 1.333 Hz for the visual and auditory paradigms, respectively) and their harmonics (integer multiples) were used as indices of expression discrimination [8, 61, 62]. Baseline-subtracted amplitudes (BSA) were used to describe the response in relation to surrounding noise. BSA were computed by subtracting the average amplitude of the 20 surrounding frequency bins (10 bins on each side of the target frequency, excluding directly adjacent bins and the two bins with the most extreme values) from the amplitude at the frequency bin of interest.

Harmonics with amplitudes significantly above noise level were identified using Z-scores, calculated relative to the mean and standard deviation of the same 20 surrounding bins, averaged across electrodes within predefined regions of interest (ROIs). Harmonics were considered significant until Z-scores for two consecutive harmonics fell below 1.64 (p < 0.05). For the visual paradigm, the oddball response included the sum of eight harmonics (1.2, 2.4, 3.6, 4.8, 7.2, 8.4, 9.6 and 10.8 Hz), and the base response included the sum of six harmonics (6, 12, 18, 24, 30 and 36 Hz). For the auditory paradigm, both oddball and base responses consisted of the summed amplitudes of three harmonics (1.333, 2.666 and 5.332 Hz for the oddball response, and 4, 8 and 12 Hz for the base response).

For the visual paradigm, two PT children were excluded due to technical failure, resulting in the inclusion of 64 PT and 32 FT children in the group-level analysis. For the auditory paradigm, three PT children did not participate, one FT and four PT children were excluded due to technical failure, and one PT child due to poor hearing. Consequently, 58 PT and 31 FT children were included in the group-level auditory analysis.

Determination of regions of interest (ROIs)

To ensure equal weighting of both emotional conditions, responses to fearful and happy stimuli were combined when determining the ROIs. To avoid bias toward either participant group, ROIs were defined using an iterative subsampling approach, randomly selecting 1000 subsets of 40 children (20 FT and 20 PT per subset). For the visual paradigm, electrodes were included if responses were significantly above the mean in 95% of 1000 iterations, using significance thresholds of α = 0.0001 for base responses and α = 0.0015 for oddball responses. The resulting visual ROIs included occipital-temporal electrodes: O1, O2 and Oz for base responses, and P7 (i.e., left ROI) and P8 (i.e., right ROI) for oddball responses.

For the auditory paradigm, electrodes were included if responses were significantly above the mean in 70% of 1000 iterations, using significance thresholds of α = 0.0015 for base responses and α = 0.05 for oddball responses. The auditory ROIs comprised O1, Oz, P7 and P8 for base responses; O1, O2, P7 and P8 for dorsal oddball responses; and Fc5 and Fc6 for frontal oddball responses. All ROIs were consistent with previous frequency-tagging research [8–10, 45, 54] and were validated by visual inspection of the topographical maps (Additional file 1: Fig. S1 and S2).

Statistical analysis

Group-level analyses on BSA were conducted using linear mixed models (LMM; ‘Afex’ package, v1.2-1) [63] implemented in R (v4.2.0) [64]. Neural responses at base and oddball frequency were examined separately. First, categorical models examined potential group differences with Group (PT vs. FT; or VPT vs. MPT vs. FT) as a fixed between-subject factor and Emotion (fear vs. happy) as a fixed within-subject factor. For the oddball responses, ROI was also included as a fixed within-subject factor. Second, continuous models examined developmental effects using GA as a continuous predictor while retaining Emotion (and ROI for oddball responses) as fixed factors. In all analyses, a random intercept per participant accounted for repeated measures. Post-hoc tests (‘emmeans’ package, v1.8.5) were performed using the Kenward-Roger method to approximate the degrees of freedom [65], and Tukey-correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

Outlying data points were identified using Cook’s Distance and removed before conducting the group-level analyses. The cutoff for the removal of outlying data points was calculated using the formula: cutoff=4/(N-k-1), where N is the total number of observations, and k is the number of explanatory variables in the model.

Associations between neural oddball responses and prematurity indices (GA, BW) were assessed in the PT group using Spearman correlations. Associations between neural oddball responses and ADOS-2, and SRS-2 scores were assessed in the whole group using partial Spearman correlations controlling for GA.

Repeated measures sensitivity analyses in G*Power (v3.1.9.7) indicated that small-to-medium effect sizes (d = 0.396) could be detected for the main group comparison (PT vs. FT), and medium effect sizes (d = 0.442) for the subgroup comparison (MPT vs. VPT vs. FT). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Using frequency-tagging EEG, we explored whether PT birth is associated with altered neural sensitivity for brief changes in facial and vocal expressions in five-year-old children. We further examined the effect of prematurity severity by distinguishing VPT (GA ≤ 32 weeks) from MPT (GA 33–36 weeks) children and by relating neural responses to continuous indices of prematurity (GA and BW). Lastly, we examined whether neural sensitivity to emotional expressions was related to individual differences in social responsiveness and autistic traits.

Reduced neural sensitivity to emotional expressions in preterm children

Robust neural synchronization at the base presentation rates was observed across all groups and modalities, indicating intact basic processing of faces and voices. The absence of group differences at base frequencies indicates that any group effects found at the oddball frequencies are attributable to differences in neural sensitivity to expression changes rather than differences in general face or voice processing.

Overall, across both modalities, children in both groups demonstrated successful implicit emotion discrimination. However, neural oddball responses in the VPT group were consistently smaller in the visual paradigm, and in the PT group in the auditory paradigm. Interestingly, the influence of prematurity severity differed by modality. In the visual paradigm, VPT children demonstrated significantly lower sensitivity for facial emotion expressions compared with both MPT and FT children. This prematurity effect was confirmed by significant positive associations between neural oddball responses and both GA and BW, indicating that deficits in implicit facial emotion discrimination were more pronounced with increasing prematurity. This finding aligns with extensive evidence linking lower GA and BW to poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes [66–69].

In contrast, the auditory paradigm revealed a more general group difference, with all PT children (irrespective of GA) displaying lower responses to changes in vocal expressions compared with FT children. This result was supported by the absence of associations between prematurity indices and neural responses to emotional voices within the PT group. Although subgroup analyses suggested descriptively lower responses in both VPT and MPT children compared with the FT group, these differences did not reach statistical significance. However, neural responses to vocal expression changes were similar across the gestational range of the PT group, and neural sensitivity only increased with increasing GA when both preterm and full-term children were considered. Overall, these results may indicate that PT birth is associated with reduced implicit sensitivity for vocal emotions, independent of prematurity severity. Future studies should investigate this further with larger sample sizes and a wide range of GA, from extremely preterm (GA < 28 weeks) to late preterm (GA 33–36 weeks).

These findings are consistent with and expand our previous research showing reduced implicit sensitivity to voices in PT children when voices compete with non-social object sounds [44]. The absence of group differences on the auditory base frequency further suggests that PT children can adequately process vocal stimuli per se, with difficulties emerging at higher-level processing stages such as emotional prosody discrimination. Deficits in prosody discrimination have previously been reported in PT populations, and have been associated with poorer language development and reduced social functioning [35, 36]. In line with this literature, we also found that reduced neural sensitivity to positive vocal expressions was related to greater social difficulties.

Taken together, our findings emphasize the potential importance of early auditory experience of preterm infants rather than prematurity status itself. Previous research reports that the noisy NICU environment may alter auditory development [70, 71]. Increasing evidence suggests that enhanced exposure to voices and especially maternal speech during NICU hospitalization supports the maturation of brain regions important for language and social communication [72–75]. Continued, social interaction and vocal engagement after discharge may further support sensitivity to voices, higher-level prosody processing, and socio-emotional development [17, 76]. Furthermore, we note that, although non-significant, the neural responses to vocal expression changes were visibly smaller in the MPT versus the VPT group. It is thus worth investigating if infants who were biologically more vulnerable but received more intensive neurodevelopmental support in the NICU (via structured programs such as NIDCAP and FNI; [14, 77–80]) show relatively better neural sensitivity to vocal social cues compared to MPT infants.

All group differences were observed irrespective of ROI or emotion. However, in line with previous research, ROI and emotion effects did emerge. In the visual paradigm, the significant ROI effect revealed higher neural responses over the right vs. left occipito-temporal region for expressive faces. Such a right hemispheric dominance has previously been reported in studies investigating (emotional) face processing, and typically reflects higher-level visual processing [8, 10, 45, 61, 81]. In the auditory paradigm, a significant main effect of emotion was observed with higher neural responses to fearful compared to happy utterances. This relative attenuation of responses to happy voices parallels observations in adult populations [54, 82], and likely reflects the evolutionary importance of quick and easy detection of threat-related emotions such as fear and anger [54, 83].

Importantly, neural responses were not correlated across the visual and auditory modalities. This suggests that, despite their similar design, the paradigms primarily capture modality-specific perceptual processes rather than a supra-modal emotion sensitivity trait. Although the stimuli were controlled for low-level, non-emotional features [54, 61], the tasks likely tap into early perceptual categorization rather than abstract emotion representations.

Prematurity effects are modality-dependent

The severity of prematurity differentially influenced implicit facial and vocal emotion discrimination: reduced neural sensitivity to facial emotional expressions was only observed in children born before 32 weeks of gestation, while reduced sensitivity to vocal emotional expressions was present in all PT children (i.e. born before 37 weeks of gestation). This modality-specific pattern may be explained by differences in maturation rates of higher-order sensory cortices. Although the auditory system functionally develops before the visual system, maturation of higher-level auditory cortices extends over a longer period than the visual cortical maturation [84–87]. In line with this interpretation, previous work using the same neural paradigm has shown intact facial emotion discrimination in 8-to-12-year-old PT and FT children [45], suggesting the possibility of a developmental catch-up in higher-level visual processing. Our findings may indicate that by 5 years of age, MPT children have already caught-up with their FT peers in visual emotion processing, whereas VPT children have not yet reached this level of maturation. In contrast, given the more protracted development of the auditory system, a comparable catch-up in vocal emotion discrimination may occur later in development, potentially explaining why reduced neural sensitivity to changes in vocal emotional expressions was observed across the entire PT group at this age.

This interpretation aligns with our previous findings in the same cohort, which demonstrated a reduced neural bias for voices, but not for faces, in PT vs. FT 5-year-olds [44]. Together, these results are consistent with the notion of modality-specific delays in brain maturation following PT birth. Specifically, it has been suggested that maturation of occipital regions in PT children might catch-up by approximately 4 years of age, whereas temporal areas may still lag behind [88].

Further longitudinal research is needed to investigate whether reduced neural sensitivity for emotional prosody and vocal emotion discrimination persist into later childhood and adolescence, or whether neural responses normalize over time.

Importantly, even if a developmental catch-up in implicit expression discrimination were to occur, this may not necessarily translate into typical emotion processing during real-life social interactions [45]. Emotion recognition follows a protracted developmental trajectory, with explicit identification and labeling continuing to mature into adolescence [89, 90]. Early in life, sensitivity to emotional cues is primarily implicit and perceptual, as infants already show rapid neural discrimination of emotional expressions within the first year of life [91]. These early perceptual processes are thought to scaffold the later emergence of explicit emotion recognition, alongside the growing involvement of conceptual knowledge and prefrontal systems supporting higher-order social cognition [92, 93]. Social cognition develops within the context of repeated caregiver-infant interactions, through which children gradually acquire emotion understanding. Such early caregiving experiences have been shown to predict later neural functioning in emotion-processing systems [94]. PT birth may disrupt both early neural specialization and the timing and/or quality of these early social interactions, potentially altering how children engage with social input. These early differences may, in turn, cascade into later difficulties in explicit emotion recognition. Consistent with this view, both children and adults born PT have been reported to exhibit persistent deficits in explicit emotion recognition and interpretation [27, 29–31]. Future research should therefore examine these developmental pathways more directly and test whether early implicit sensitivity to emotional expressions predicts later explicit emotion recognition abilities.

Implicit discrimination of happy cues is linked to social responsiveness

Neural sensitivity to facial and vocal emotion expressions was associated with social difficulties, as measured with the SRS-2. PT children who exhibited greater social difficulties showed reduced neural sensitivity to changes in emotional expression. Importantly, this association was specific to the implicit discrimination of happy expressions, as no relationship was found for fearful expressions. Moreover, no significant correlations were found between implicit emotion discrimination and ADOS-2 scores, complicating the interpretation of these results.

Although PT children showed higher levels of social difficulties and autism-related traits than FT peers on both the parent reported SRS-2 and the clinician-administered ADOS-2, scores largely remained within the normative range and did not warrant further diagnostic evaluation [52]. As the ADOS-2 reflects a single observational time point, it is plausible that the subtle social difficulties associated with reduced neural sensitivity to happy expressions are more rapidly captured by parents in daily life contexts than by clinicians in our laboratory environment. This may be especially relevant in light of evidence that children often show adaptive or masking behaviors outside of the home environment [95–97]. Together, these findings are consistent with the concept of a preterm behavioral phenotype [18], as reflected by increased but still subclinical scores on both the SRS-2 and ADOS-2. This highlights the importance of incorporating sensitive and objective tools to identify subtle socio-emotional difficulties in PT populations.

Although causal inferences cannot be drawn, the observed relationship aligns with previous reports of poorer social skills in PT children with reduced prosody discrimination [36]. Similarly, explicit facial emotion recognition abilities have been linked to broader social skills within school-aged PT children [98].

Strikingly, associations with social responsiveness were observed exclusively for happy and not for fearful expression changes. This finding aligns with earlier evidence suggesting that PT children show poorer recognition of positive, but not negative, emotions compared with FT peers [27], as well as reports of a fear bias in extremely low birth weight survivors [32]. Adequate or even enhanced discrimination of negative expressions has been proposed to result from early postnatal experiences characterized by increased exposure to negative affect after PT birth [27, 32]. However, emotion-specific effects are not always assessed [28, 29] and have not been consistently observed across studies on PT populations [30, 31]. Moreover, our own data did not reveal a Group-by-Emotion interaction effect in the neural responses to expression changes, warranting careful interpretation of the observed emotion-specific association with social responsiveness.

The observed associations between reduced neural sensitivity to (happy) emotional stimuli and the presence of social difficulties and autism-related traits in PT children are reminiscent of findings in autistic children, where reduced neural discrimination of facial expression changes has also been reported using identical frequency-tagging EEG paradigms [8–10]. However, in autistic children, reduced neural sensitivity is not limited to happy expressions, as observed in the associations with social dysfunction in the PT group, but is typically more pronounced for angry and fearful expressions. This subtle discrepancy may support the notion that the autism-like phenotype observed in the PT populations does not fully overlap with the symptom profile of idiopathic ASD [18, 99–101]; see also [102]. Together, these observations underscore the need for caution when interpreting emotion-specific neural effects across neurodevelopmental conditions.

Alternatively, the emotion-specific association may reflect developmental factors. Recognition of positive emotions such as happiness typically emerge earlier in childhood, whereas accurate recognition of negative emotions often reaches adult-like levels later in development [37, 41, 103]. In line with this, Naumann and colleagues reported stronger neural responses to changes in happy versus angry facial expressions in typically developing five-year-olds [41]. It is therefore possible that reduced social skills are preferentially reflected in aberrant implicit discrimination of happy expressions, as discrimination of fearful expressions is still difficult for all preschoolers regardless of their social abilities. However, this interpretation remains speculative and requires further investigation, especially given the absence of Group-by-Emotion effects in neural responses to expression changes in the present study.

Limitations

A first limitation concerns the use of controlled, artificial paradigms that may not fully capture emotion recognition and discrimination as they occur in real-life settings. Nonetheless, neural sensitivity to happy expressions was negatively associated with parent-reported social responsiveness, suggesting ecological relevance of the observed neural measures. As the study did not include an explicit emotion recognition task, future research should examine the relationship between implicit neural sensitivity and explicit emotion recognition abilities within the PT population. This is further underscored by the absence of cross‑modal correlations in neural responses, suggesting that these measures primarily reflect early perceptual categorization processes rather than abstract, modality‑general emotion representations. Other task-related limitations include the use of static adult faces, expressing only basic emotions (fear or happiness). Future research should also consider including more diverse and nuanced expressions to investigate emotion- or valence-specific effects.

Second, the PT group contained relatively healthy children without major health issues or neurological disorders, which may limit generalizability of our findings to the broader PT population. Importantly, however, even with this healthy PT sample, reduced neural sensitivity to emotional expressions was evident. Children born before 37 weeks of gestation showed impairments in vocal emotion discrimination, while those born before 32 weeks of gestation showed impairments in both facial and vocal emotion discrimination. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring socio-emotional development in all PT children, including those born moderate-to-late preterm and those without major health concerns post-discharge.

Third, the current sample size is limited, especially for the subgroup comparisons between MPT, VPT, and FT children. Effect sizes indicated substantial uncertainty for detecting potential group differences at the base frequency of the visual paradigm. In contrast, the observed oddball response effects were small-to-moderate, suggesting that the study was reasonably sensitive to group-level effects of this magnitude. Nonetheless, larger samples will be needed to estimate subgroup effects and interactions more precisely.

Finally, due to limited compliance in this young cohort, we were unable to use the orthogonal button-press task to investigate attention during stimulus presentation. Instead, children were closely monitored throughout data collection, and behaviors indicative of inattention (e.g., looking away from the screen or talking/singing) were documented. During EEG preprocessing, neural signals of children displaying such behaviors were checked for the presence of robust base-frequency responses, as lack of attention would be expected to reduce neural synchronization at the base stimulation frequency. No children were excluded due to inattention or an absence of base-frequency responses. Moreover, no group differences were observed between PT and FT children at the base frequencies of either paradigm, indicating that the observed group differences in sensitivity to emotional expression changes at the oddball frequencies are unlikely to be driven by differences in attention. We also note that the orthogonal task in the visual paradigm likely imposed slightly higher cognitive demands (requiring continuous monitoring of a fixation cross) than the auditory paradigm, which involved detecting a salient alarm sound. Such differences in monitoring load may have interacted with top-down modulation of implicit emotion processing and could contribute to the generally higher neural responses observed in the visual paradigm compared to the auditory paradigm.

Conclusions

Using frequency-tagging EEG, we demonstrate reduced neural sensitivity to emotional expressions in five-year-old children born preterm compared with full-term peers. Strikingly, the effect of prematurity severity was modality-specific: facial emotion discrimination deficits were only present for those born VPT (GA ≤ 32 weeks), whereas all PT children showed deficits in vocal emotion discrimination. Furthermore, reduced neural sensitivity to positive emotional expressions was associated with increased parent-reported social difficulties. However, there were no group-by-emotion effects on visual or auditory neural sensitivity itself. From a public health perspective, these findings are important given the large number of children born moderate-to-late preterm, who are often considered at low risk and receive limited long-term follow-up. Together, these findings provide novel insight into visual and auditory socio-emotional development following preterm birth and highlight the importance of monitoring socio-emotional functioning in all PT children, including those without major medical complications or those born moderate-to-late preterm.
