Analysis of the Technical Feasibility of Sustainable Concrete Production Using Waste Foundry Sand as a Fine Aggregate
Authors: Emily Noronha Marques, Carlos Perez Bergmann, Angela Borges Masuero
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWaste foundation sand (WFS) is one of the most abundant residues in the foundation industry. Currently, its annual production is estimated to be three million tons. This material has properties that make it an attractive candidate for implantation as an alternative constituent to a natural fine aggregate in concrete applications. This application can promote greater sustainability, as it would establish a noble destination for the waste generated in large quantities by the metallurgical industry in addition to reducing the exploitation of a natural resource widely used by the civil construction industry. Given this, the present study observed the test of three different proportions of replacement, 25, 50, and 100% by mass, of natural sand by WFS in concrete. To assess the feasibility of these replacements, several tests were carried out covering mechanical properties and aspects related to the durability of concrete. The results indicated a significant improvement in the mechanical performance, with a resistance gain of 25% in relation to the reference concrete. As for the modulus of elasticity, there was no significant variation. As for aspects related to durability, both the absorption test and the alkali aggregate reaction test did not show statistically significant disparity, which attests to the technical feasibility of using nonconcrete WFS.

Introduction

As informed by the Associação Brasileira de Fundição (ABIFA), the national production of iron castings in the first quarter of 2022 alone was 904 564 tons, obtaining a growth of 12% in relation to the same period of 2021, thus guaranteeing Brazil’s position as the 10th largest world producer of castings.1 It is known that this activity is highly polluting due to the high amount of solid waste produced, of which sand discarded from the waste foundation sand (WFS) foundry represents a significant fraction, currently generating about three million tons of WFS per year. First of all, it is important to emphasize that the improper disposal of this waste can cause problems for the environment, and disposal in industrial landfills can imply additional costs for the industries involved.2,3 For this reason, companies that have foundry processes in their production processes and, as a consequence, generate WFS waste, have sought solutions that minimize the costs associated with the final disposal of their waste as well as meet the growing rigor of environmental requirements.4,5 In this scenario, WFS recycling processes help minimize waste and costs with industrial landfills and thus reduce the accumulation of environmental liabilities.

Parallel to this, with regard to the civil construction industry, recycling and reuse of materials are excellent alternatives for the preservation of natural resources and consequently the release of smaller amounts of pollutants into the environment. This is particularly pertinent since the sector is one of the largest consumers of natural resources, with concrete being one of the most consumed materials in the world with an estimated production of 52.7 million tons per year.6 From this perspective, the incorporation of WFS in concrete becomes very attractive because it is a collaborative alternative resource between two industries, where the waste generated by one becomes a resource for the other.7

In this scenario, research emerged some time ago to address the replacement of natural resources with alternative materials, including the reuse of WFS. This is because this residue is very similar to a natural aggregate, being basically composed of high-quality silica, with a very uniform granulometric distribution, obtaining 85–95% of the material retained between the 0.6 and 0.15 mm sieves.8−10 As for the chemical properties, it is known that the properties of WFS are directly influenced by the production method from which they originate and may vary, mainly, according to the molded metal and the type of binder used. However, according to previous research, it is possible to observe that WFS is usually rich in silica and coated with a thin film of burnt carbon, powdery material, and residual binders such as, for example, bentonite, resins, or coal. Due to the high silica content, this material has a hydrophilic character, attracting water to its surface; however, the silica content is lower than the levels found in natural sands, which is due to the presence of additives.

The mentioned properties made this residue attract the interest of several researchers, who in turn demonstrated that it can be used in different ways in the construction industry, such as, for example, use in roadwork to fill sanitary landfills,11 application in soil stabilization,12 in the manufacturing of self-compacting concrete,7 in the production of conventional concrete,13 and in the production of ceramic blocks3 and cementitious soil blocks.2

Based on the bibliography addressed, this study aims to investigate more broadly the behavior of WFS in concrete in order to investigate aspects related not only to mechanical performance as well as issues related to durability such as absorption by immersion and occurrence of alkali reaction aggregate—an issue little explored and much suggested by previous studies.7 In addition, the characterization of concrete samples with 100% replacement of natural sand by WFS was also carried out through the leaching and solubilization test, given that it is a noninert industrial waste with toxic materials in its composition. The originality of the study resides in the dosage methodology in which traces composed exclusively of fine sand as a fine aggregate were studied in order to obtain a more reliable comparison with foundry sand, in addition to enabling satisfactory results to be obtained for dosages of more heights of natural sand by WFS, thus allowing an in-depth study on the behavior of concrete containing different percentages of WFS.

Materials and Methods

Cement

The CPV-ARI-type cement was used to evaluate the performance of the waste as a substitute for the fine aggregate of natural origin as it is the national cement with the lowest content of mineral additions. The cement used is CPV ARI according to ABNT NBR 5733:1991.14

Foundry Sand

The waste foundry sand (WFS) used in this study constitutes a solid waste that arises from an industrial plant located in Nova Petrópolis, in the state of Rio Grande do Sul. According to data provided by the company, the weekly production stood at approximately 100 tons. The material was collected and properly conditioned to avoid external contamination. Subsequently, the characterization process started through the chemical analysis of the casting sand performed by X-ray fluorescence (XRF), calculated for oxides, using a SHIMADZU device, an XRD 1800 model. Mineralogical analysis was performed using X-ray diffraction (XRD), a nonoriented method, using a PHILIPS diffractometer, X’PERT model. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on a SHIMADZU TG50 device. The results are presented in Table 1, Figures 1, and 2.

Dosage of Traces

The study of the concrete dosage was conducted following the methodology proposed by Helene and Terzian,16 whereby an ideal dry mortar content(α), set at 50, was initially determined through an experimental trace with natural sand. Subsequently, the reference trace was replicated with the substitution of natural sand by WFS at mass percentages of 25, 50, and 100%.

Molding of Specimens

Specimens were molded with substitutions of 25, 50, and 100% of natural sand by WFS. To characterize the concrete in the fresh state, the Slump Test ABNT NBR NM 67:199817 was performed, which consists in determining the consistency by the abatement of the cone trunk. A limit of 100 ± 20 mm was established.

The decrease in the water/cement ratio, shown in Table 5, may be associated with the shape of the grains. The surface texture of natural sand was found to be rougher than that of foundry sand, as depicted in Figure 4. This characteristic, together with the MF, is responsible for reducing the water/cement ratio of the traces with substitution to the detriment of the reference trace.

Results and Discussion

Compressive Strength

Figure 5 shows the compressive strength results for the tested mixtures.

Influence of the content of small aggregate replacement by WFS on the compressive strength of concretes at ages of 3, 7, and 28 days.

The results of the axial compression strength test indicate an improvement as the replacement content of natural sand by WFS increases. To verify whether this improvement presents a significant difference, a two-way ANOVA statistical analysis was performed with repetition, using the compressive strength results for all ages and replacement levels. The results of the tests revealed a statistically significant influence on the strength of concrete for replacing 100% natural sand by WFS.

This fact can be explained by the presence of powdery material, such as clay dust and coal dust. These materials could help in the compaction factor (packaging) of the mixtures, which could lead to an increase in the mechanical resistance and a decrease in the pore diameter.19,20

In addition, another factor that may have corroborated the result concerns the texture and shape of the grains, as shown in Figure 4, which provided, for concrete with 100% replacement of natural sand by WFS, a reduction in the water/cement ratio for the same abatement. Results were similar to those found by Mavroulidou and Lawrence and Martins et al.19,20

Elastic Modulus

Figure 6 illustrates the modulus of the elasticity of concrete with various WFS replacement percentages.

Modulus of elasticity of concretes with WFS at 28 days.

The results indicate that the modulus of elasticity did not vary significantly for the studied substitution contents, as demonstrated by ANOVA statistical analysis. No drastic changes were observed in this property for the different substitution contents. Similar results were also found by other authors6,20 who attest that the WFS influences the modulus of elasticity in the same way that it influences the compressive strength—with a tendency to increase proportionally with the increase in the used contents. The main hypothesis is that this is due to the similarity of the WFS with the natural sand used in this study. In addition, as observed by the analysis of the visual aspect and by the absorption test, the concretes with WFS proved to be more compact than the conventional concrete, which provides greater density, and consequently, it may have corroborated with the tendency of growth of the elastic modulus as well as with the gains in the compressive strength.

However, this effect is less significant with regard to the modulus of elasticity since the growth is not statistically significant, so it can be inferred that concrete with WFS behaves very similarly to conventional concrete in terms of this property.

Absorption by Immersion

As for absorption by immersion, Figure 7 illustrates that there was a small reduction in the percentage of absorption as the percentage of replacement of natural sand by WFS increased. However, based on the statistical analysis, this reduction was not significant, showing behavior similar to the concrete reference.

Percentage of absorption by total immersion of concretes with WFS.

It is believed, when relating the values of compressive strength of concrete and water absorption, that there was an increase in the value of the first variable due to the tendency to decrease the value of the second, similar results to those found by Martins et al., 2022,22 which can be justified by the presence of fine particles that provide greater packing in concretes with WFS so that concretes with this residue are denser than concretes with fine sand of natural origin, which increases the quality mechanics, in addition to positively contributing to characteristics related to the durability of concrete, such as resistance to absorption.

Alkali Aggregate Reaction

The investigation of the occurrence of the alkali aggregate reaction revealed that there were no statistically significant differences between the bars and the studied substitution contents. Furthermore, the percentage values of expansion are below the normative limit of 0.19% (see Figure 8) and are therefore considered potentially innocuous.19 This result must be closely linked to the result obtained in the immersion absorption test, given that the passage of water is fundamental for the occurrence of expansion, and in the case of the concrete produced with WFS, denser and less porous concrete was obtained, which may have contributed to the inhibition of the alkali aggregate reaction

Percentage expansion per RAA of mortar bars with WFS.

About the values, it is noted that the bars that came closest to the normative expansion limit (0.19%), curiously, were those with a substitution content of 50% WFS that reached the mark of 0.18% (see Figure 8). Based on this, greater caution is suggested regarding certain uses of this replacement percentage, such as, for example, its use in concrete for foundation elements. A safer alternative may be its use combined with mitigating materials such as mineral additions and pozzolanic materials.21,22

Characterization through Solubilization and Leaching

Regarding the characterization through solubilization (Table 7) and leaching (Table 8) tests, the neutralization of the contaminant Al initially present in the foundry sand and the reference concrete was carried out. The solubilization and stabilization process in cement matrices allows for the control of heavy metal leaching through chemical reactions between hydrated calcium silicate (C–S–H), carbon dioxide, and portlandite (Ca(OH)2), resulting in the formation of calcite (CaCO3). This process changes the solubility of the components and immobilizes the heavy metals, which can occur through adsorption or chemical interactions with the cementitious matrix.23 Therefore, it is possible to classify foundry sand and reference concrete as nonhazardous class II and noninert class II A and concrete with 100% WFS as nonhazardous waste class II and inert class II B according to the classification of ABN NBR 10004 of 2004.24

Surface Aspect

Finally, in terms of the surface aspect, it is observed that there was no meaningful change, and the final coloration of the specimens was very similar when comparing the reference concrete to the concrete containing 100% WFS as a replacement for natural sand (Figure 9). It is important to note that the color of concrete is related not only to the type of aggregate used but also to the type of cement and its respective additions as well as other crucial variables such as the water/cement ratio.25 In this context, it is valid to point out that the present study limited the use of the materials mentioned above.

Conclusions

Based on the results and analyses conducted, the following conclusions can be drawn:

The contents studied had strong results in terms of mechanical properties with a significant gain in resistance to resistance. The modulus of elasticity of the materials was also similar to that of the reference concrete, with no statistically significant difference being observed, which can be attributed to the granulometric similarity of the two types of sand used in this study. However, despite the observed growth not being statistically significant, a growth trend was observed that can be explained by the increase in the density of the concretes obtained with the replacement of natural sand by WFS.

However, despite the observed growth not being statistically significant, a growth trend was observed that can be explained by the increase in the density of the concretes obtained with the replacement of natural sand by WFS.

The absorption tests found that there was no statistically significant difference between the mixes, indicating that the substituted concretes present similar behavior to the reference concrete. In spite of this, even though it was not significant, a trend toward a reduction in absorption was observed as the WFS content increased, due to the reduction in porosity caused by the incorporation of this residue in the concrete.

The AAR test demonstrated that there was no statistically significant difference between the concretes with the replacements and the reference mix. It is an expected result, since it was observed through the immersion absorption test that there was a reduction in water absorption due to the reduction in the porosity of the concrete, which may have inhibited the occurrence of an alkali aggregate reaction. Regarding the values, the results found that the trait closest to the normative limit (0.19%), curiously, was the trait with an additional 50% WFS content, which presented 0.18% expansion. In view of this, we suggest greater caution regarding its use in certain applications as foundations and/or its use in combination with attenuating agents such as pozzolans. We emphasize the need to carry out further studies regarding this phenomenon, approaching in detail its mechanism of occurrence in the case of concretes with WFS.

This finding represents an important way of mitigating the environmental effects of conventional waste disposal (in industrial waste). This makes it possible to use this waste as a fine aggregate with a replacement percentage, by mass, of up to 100%.
