Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation inhibition by 9α,11α-dihydroxy-kaurenoic acid promotes ROS-associated apoptosis and suppresses cancer stemness in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Kun-Chang Wu, Yu-Chi Liu, Jing-Ru Weng, Te-Ling Lu, Fang-Pin Chang, Yu-Xin Cai, Bing-Yan Tsai, Meng-Tien Lu, Po-Chen Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. In this study, we investigated the anticancer activity and molecular mechanism of ent-9α,11α-dihydroxy-15-oxo-kaur-16-en-19-oic acid (9α,11α-dihydroxy-kaurenoic Acid; 9α,11αOH-KA, also known as adenostemmoic acid B), a natural kaurane-type diterpenoid isolated from Adenostemma lavenia, against NSCLC. 9α,11αOH-KA exhibited potent and preferential cytotoxicity toward NSCLC cells compared with non-tumorigenic fibroblasts, accompanied by inhibition of cell migration and induction of apoptosis. Moreover, 9α,11αOH-KA significantly suppressed tumorsphere formation and reduced the expression of cancer stem cells (CSCs)-associated markers, including CD44, CD133, and Notch-1, indicating impairment of stemness-associated properties. Mechanistically, 9α,11αOH-KA induced mitochondrial dysfunction associated with impaired oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), as evidenced by reduced basal and maximal oxygen consumption rate (OCR), depletion of intracellular ATP, and dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential. These mitochondrial alterations were accompanied by pronounced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, which contributed to apoptotic cell death, as antioxidant pretreatment attenuated both ROS generation and apoptosis. Importantly, oral administration of 9α,11αOH-KA significantly suppressed A549 xenograft tumor growth without observable systemic toxicity. Collectively, 9α,11αOH-KA exhibits strong anti-NSCLC activity through dual suppression of mitochondrial bioenergetics and CSCs-associated properties. These findings highlight 9α,11αOH-KA as a promising phytochemical candidate for the development of mitochondria-targeting therapeutics for NSCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55357-4.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most common cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. In 2026, an estimated 229,410 new cases of lung cancer will be diagnosed and 124,990 people will die from the disease in the United States1. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases and continues to impose a substantial global health burden2. Although recent advances in molecularly targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors have improved clinical outcomes in subsets of patients, the prognosis of advanced or metastatic NSCLC remains poor, with five-year survival rates below 20%3. Tumor heterogeneity, acquired therapeutic resistance, and limited efficacy in certain molecular subtypes substantially compromise the long-term success of current treatment strategies4,5. While tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors have provided substantial benefit in selected genomic and immunological contexts, their clinical effectiveness is frequently limited by resistance mechanisms and disease progression6,7. These challenges underscore the urgent need to develop novel therapeutic agents with distinct mechanisms of action that may overcome current treatment limitations and improve durable clinical benefit in patients with NSCLC.

Mitochondria play critical roles in ATP production, calcium (Ca2+) concentration, redox homeostasis, and the regulation of apoptotic cell death8. Oxidative phosphorylation is a major cellular process for ATP generation, occurring through the electron transport chain (ETC) complexes located in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The ETC complexes, including complex I (NADH dehydrogenase), complex II (succinate dehydrogenase), complex III (ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase), complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), and complex V (ATP synthase), catalyze the phosphorylation of adenosine diphosphate (ADP) to adenosine triphosphate (ATP)9. Electrons donated by NADH at complex I and FADH2 at complex II are transferred to complex III, complex IV and finally to oxygen through complex V. At the same time, positively charged protons (H+) are pumped from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space, resulting in creating an electrochemical proton gradient known as the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The membrane potential is then dispersed by the re-entry of protons back into the matrix through Complex V that catalyzes the generation of ATP from ADP10.

About a century ago, Otto Warburg first described a distinctive metabolic phenotype in cancer cells characterized by preferential reliance on aerobic glycolysis rather than mitochondrial OXPHOS for ATP production, a phenomenon now known as the Warburg effect11. Defective mitochondrial OXPHOS has been proposed in Warburg effect to explain the metabolic shift of tumor cells12. However, accumulating evidence over recent decades has demonstrated that intact and active mitochondrial metabolism remains essential for tumor growth and survival13–17, highlighting the complex metabolic adaptations that support tumorigenesis and cancer progression. Actually, accumulating evidence has indicated that besides the strong dependence on glycolysis, several tumor cells also utilize OXPHOS for biosynthesis and the mitochondrial metabolism plays a critical role in tumor growth and survival18–20. Moreover, upregulated OXPHOS has been shown to be required for the tumorigenic potential, such as cancer stem cells (CSCs)21, and is associated with tumor development, progression, and resistance of chemotherapy22–24. Consequently, mitochondrial metabolism has emerged as an attractive therapeutic target for cancer treatment.

Despite considerable progress in cancer therapy, the identification of novel and effective anticancer agents remains critically important due to the limitations of existing treatments, including systemic toxicity, acquired resistance, and insufficient long-term efficacy. Natural compounds derived from medicinal plants have therefore attracted increasing attention in anticancer drug discovery because of their remarkable chemical diversity, historical medicinal use, and broad pharmacological activities25–28. Indeed, approximately 70% of anticancer compounds are sourced from medicinal plants or their derivatives, underscoring their pivotal roles in the development of anticancer therapeutics29,30. Adenostemma lavenia (L.) Kuntze belongs to the family Asteraceae and is widely distributed in the temperate to tropical parts of Asia and the Pacific Islands31. In Taiwan, aqueous extracts prepared from the aerial parts of A. lavenia have traditionally been used for the treatment of inflammatory disorders, including pulmonary congestion, pneumonia, hepatitis, respiratory tract infections, and fever32. Previous studies have identified that ent-11α-hydroxy-15-oxo-kaur-16-en-19-oic acid (11αOH-KA), which exerted various in vitro and in vivo bioactivities, including anti-melanogenic, anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidative, and antitumor activities33–36, was one the major kaurene-type diterpenes in the aqueous extract of A. lavenia33,37. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated the anticancer effects of 11αOH-KA in liver, lung, stomach, colon, breast, and anaplastic thyroid cancer cells38–43. In addition to 11αOH-KA, Taiwanese A. lavenia contains higher level of ent-9α,11α-dihydroxy-15-oxo-kaur-16-en-19-oic acid (9α,11α-dihydroxy-kaurenoic Acid; 9α,11αOH-KA, also known as adenostemmoic acid B) than 11αOH-KA33. Although 9α,11αOH-KA shares a similar structure and bioactivities with 11αOH-KA, including anti-inflammatory, anti-melanogenic, antioxidant, and cytotoxic activities33,37, its anticancer activity and underlying molecular mechanisms in human NSCLC remain largely unexplored.

In the present study, we uncovered the potent in vitro and in vivo anticancer activities of 9α,11αOH-KA, isolated from the leaves of Taiwanese A. lavenia, against human NSCLC. Our findings provide evidence supporting the novel mitochondria-targeted effect of 9α,11αOH-KA, including downregulation of mitochondrial respiratory chain-associated genes, depolarization of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), reduction of ATP production, and excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), ultimately leading to apoptotic cell death in NSCLC cells. In addition, we found that 9α,11αOH-KA significantly suppressed tumorsphere formation and reduced CSCs-associated properties accompanied by inhibition of Notch-1 signaling. Taken together, these findings highlight the novel effects of 9α,11αOH-KA and its potential translational application in developing new therapeutic strategies for NSCLC patients.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and antibodies

The human lung cancer cell lines, A549 (ATCC, Cat#CCL-185), H1975 (ATCC, Cat#CRL-5908), HCC827 (ATCC, Cat#CRL-2868), H1299 (ATCC, Cat#CRL-5803), and H1650 (ATCC, Cat#CRL-5883) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The human skin fibroblast CCD966-SK cell line (BCRC, Hsinchu, Taiwan, Cat#60153) was a kind gift from Prof. Louis Kuoping Chao (China Medical University, Taichung, Taiwan). All these cells were maintained in the Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA, Cat#12800017), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#A5670701) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#15140122), and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. The passage number of all cells used in this study did not exceed p20. Antibodies used in this study and their sources were as follows: p70S6K (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat#sc-8418) and p-4EBP1 (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat#sc-293124) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA); PARP-1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#9542), Caspase 9 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#9502), Caspase 3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#14220), mTOR (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#2983), p-mTOR (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#5536), Notch-1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#3608), p-p70S6K (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#9205) (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA); MT-ND1 (1:500, ProteinTech, Cat#19703-1-AP), MT-ND2 (1:500, ProteinTech, Cat#19704-1-AP), MT-CYB (1:500, ProteinTech, Cat#55090-1-AP), GAPDH (1:5000, ProteinTech, Cat#60004-1-Ig) (ProteinTech, Chicago, IL, USA); CD133 (1:1000, GeneTex, Cat#GTX32496) and CD44 (1:2000, GeneTex, Cat#GTX637231) (GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA); HRP-Goat Anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs, Cat#115-035-146) and HRP-Goat Anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs, Cat#111-035-144) (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA).

Extraction and isolation of bioactive compounds from A. lavenia

The dried herbal material of A. lavenia (L.) Kuntze (AL) was purchased from the herbal medicine store in Taichung, Taiwan, and authenticated by professor Kun-Chang Wu (China Medical University), and the specimens were deposited in the School of Pharmacy at China Medical University. Solvents used for partition and separation, including ethyl acetate (EtOAc, ≥ 99.4% purity), n-butanol (n-BuOH, ≥ 99.4% purity), and dichloromethane (CH₂Cl₂, ≥ 99.5% purity), were of ACS grade and purchased from DUKSAN Pure Chemicals Co., Ltd. (Ansan, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). The extraction, partition and separation procedures are illustrated in Fig. 1A. The crushed leaves (AL1), stems (AL2) and aerial part (AL3) of AL (30 g, respectively) were extracted with boiling water (0.45 L, two times) and then freeze-drying. The extract of AL1 (11.41 g) was suspended in distilled water and the aqueous suspension was sequentially partitioned with EtOAc and n-BuOH to obtain the EtOAc-soluble layer (AL1-1), n-BuOH-soluble layer (AL1-2), H2O-soluble layer (AL1-3) and chylus (AL1-4). The extract of AL1-1 was chromatographed by normal phase medium pressure liquid chromatography (MPLC; CombiFlash Rf 75, Lincoln, Nebraska, U.S.A.) with mixture of increasing polarity of CH2Cl2 : EtOAc to elute crude fractions AL1-1-1 ~ AL1-1-10. The fraction of AL1-1-4 was further chromatographed on silica gel with CH2Cl2 : EtOAc to afford ent-9α,11α-dihydroxy-15-oxo-kaur-16-en-19-oic acid (9α,11αOH-KA; adenostemmoic acid B) and ent-11α-hydroxy-15-oxo-kaur-16-en-19-oic acid (11αOH-KA). The fraction of AL1-1-4 was also analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a dC18 column (Waters, Atlantis™ dC18, 5 μm, St., Milford, MA, USA, Cat#SKU: 186001346) with Waters 2695 HPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a Waters 2998 photodiode array detector, a Waters e2695 separations module, and a column heater module. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (LC grade, ≥ 99.9% purity; Merck, Taipei, Taiwan) and phosphoric acid in water (0.1%, v/v; phosphoric acid purity 85%; Honeywell Fluka™, Charlotte, NC, USA). The elution conditions utilized a chromatographic fingerprinting method with a gradient from 5% to 95% acetonitrile over a duration of 105 min. The 9α,11αOH-KA and 11αOH-KA detection was performed by determining the ultraviolet (UV) absorption at 245 nm.

Cell viability assays

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in the presence of 10% FBS. After overnight incubation, cells were exposed to indicated extracts/fractions/compounds or vehicle (0.1% DMSO) for 72 h. After treatment, cells were incubated with 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Biomatik, Wilmington, DE, USA, Cat#A3338) for 1 h. The medium was then removed from each well and replaced with DMSO to dissolve the MTT formazan for subsequent colorimetric measurement of absorbance at 570 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M2, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Cell viabilities are expressed as percentages of viable cells relative to the corresponding vehicle-treated control group.

Western blot analysis

The cell lysates were collected and solubilized in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were then determined by BCA method (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Chicago, IL, USA, Cat#23227). Equal amounts of protein samples were resolved by 8%-12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#GE10600002). Membranes were blocked for 1 h in TBST (TBS with 0.1% Tween 20) containing 5% nonfat milk, followed by overnight incubation at 4 ℃ with specific primary antibodies. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were treated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (PK-NEL105, PerkinElmer, Boston, MA, USA).

Apoptotic cell analysis

Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) apoptosis detection kit (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA, Cat#556547) was used to measure the apoptotic cells. Human lung cancer cells (4 × 105 cells/plate) were treated with 9α,11αOH-KA at specified concentrations or vehicle (DMSO) for 72 h. Following the treatment, cells were harvested and stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) in binding buffer for 20 min. The stained cells were analyzed using the BD FACSCanto™ flow cytometer (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) with data acquisition and analysis performed using BD FACSDiva™6.2 software. The apoptotic cells were defined as the sum of Annexin V-FITC positive/PI negative (early apoptosis) and Annexin V-FITC positive/PI positive (late apoptosis) populations.

Tumorsphere formation assays

To investigate the effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on the stemness of lung cancer cells, human lung cancer cells (2 × 103 cells/well) were cultured in ultra-low attachment 24-well plates (Corning Inc., Union City, CA, USA, Cat#3473) using MammoCult™ Human Medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada, Cat#05620) containing the indicted concentrations of 9α,11αOH-KA or vehicle (DMSO). Tumorsphere development was monitored over 8–12 days, and the formed tumorspheres were observed and captured under a microscope at 100× magnification (Motic AE2000 Inverted Microscope, Motic Deutschland GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). Tumorsphere formation was quantified by counting viable spheroids using trypan blue exclusion under a microscope with hemacytometer. Relative tumorsphere formation (%) was calculated by normalizing the number of viable spheroids in each treatment group to that of the corresponding DMSO-treated control group, which was defined as 100%.

Wound healing assay

Human lung cancer cells (7 × 105 − 1 × 106 cells) were plated in 6-well plates. Upon reaching confluence, scratch wounds were created across each well using a sterile pipette tip. The wells were washed once with PBS to remove debris, and the cells were incubated in medium containing 9α,11αOH-KA at specified concentrations. Images of the scratches were captured at 0, 6, 9, 15, and 18 h using the microscope at 40x magnification. The wound areas were analyzed and quantified using ImageJ software (Version 1.52a).

Transwell migration assays

The effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on migration activity in lung cancer cells was assessed using transwell chambers with 8 μm pore-sized membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA, Cat#SCWP09025). The 1 × 105 cells were suspended in 300 µL of serum-free medium with indicated concentrations of 9α,11αOH-KA and seeded into the upper chambers of the transwell inserts. Meanwhile, 800 µL of culture medium containing 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#A5670701) was added to the lower chambers to act as a chemoattractant. After incubation at 37 °C for 16–24 h, non-migrated cells were removed with a cotton swab and the transwell membranes were fixed in methanol, stained with Giemsa (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#32884), and imaged. Migrated cells were quantified by counting cell numbers in randomly selected fields under a light microscope at 100x magnification (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The results were expressed as percentages of migrated cells relative to the DMSO-treated control group.

RNA-sequencing and analysis

RNA-sequencing was performed on independent biological replicates of control (DMSO-treated) and 9α,11αOH-KA-treated A549, HCC827, and H1975 cells. For each cell line and treatment condition, separate cell cultures were prepared and processed independently to generate biological replicate samples. Library preparation, sequencing, and downstream bioinformatic analyses were conducted by Welgene Biotech Co. Ltd. (Taipei, Taiwan). Briefly, total RNA was extracted using RNeasy kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany, Cat#ID. 74104) according to the instruction manual. Purified RNA was quantified at OD260 nm using a ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technology, USA) and quantified by using a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technology, USA) with RNA 6000 LabChip kit (Agilent Technology, USA, Cat#5067 − 1511). Agilent’s SureSelect Strand-Specific RNA Library Preparation Kit was used for library construction followed by AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA, Cat#A63882) size selection. The sequence was determined using Illumina’s sequencing-by-synthesis (SBS) technology (Illumina, USA). Sequencing data (FASTQ reads) were generated using Welgene Biotech’s pipeline based on Illumina’s base-calling program bcl2fastq v2.20. Both adaptor clipping and sequence quality trimming were performed using Trimmomatic (v0.36). HISAT2 program was used for mRNA alignment. Differential expression analysis was performed using StringTie (StringTie v2.1.3) and DEseq (DEseq v1.39.0) or DEseq2 (DEseq2 v1.28.1) with genome bias detection/correction and Welgene Biotech’s in-house pipeline. Functional enrichment assay in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of each experiment design was performed using clusterProfiler v3.6. Genes with |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and p < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Cells were seeded in 60 mm dishes at a density of 4 × 105–5 × 105 cells. After treatment with 9α,11αOH-KA at the indicated concentrations or vehicle (DMSO) for 48 hours, total RNA was extracted using RNeasy kit (QIAGEN, Cat#ID. 74104), and then was reverse transcribed to cDNA using TOOLS Easy Fast RT kit (Biotools, Taipei, Taiwan, Cat#KRT-BA18) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the StepOne Real-time PCR Detection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Fast SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#4385612). The primer sequences used for real-time PCR analysis were MT-ND1: 5’-CCACCTCTAGCCTAGCCGTTTA-3’ and 5’-GGGTCATGATGGCAGGAGTAAT-3’; MT-ND6: 5’-GCGATGGCTATTGAGGAGTATCC-3’ and 5’-CACAGCACCAATCCTACCTCCA-3’; MT-CYB: 5’-ATCACTCGAGACGTAAATTATGGCT-3’ and 5’-TGAACTAGGTCTGTCCCAATGTATG-3’; MT-CO1: 5’-TCTCAGGCTACACCCTAGACCA-3’ and 5’-ATCGGGGTAGTCCGAGTAACGT-3’; MT-ATP6: 5’-TAACCATACACAACACTAAAGGACGA-3’ and 5’-GGGCATTTTTAATCTTAGAGCGAAA-3’; 18s ribosomal RNA: 5’-ACCCGTTGAACCCCATTCGTGA-3’ and 5’-GCCTCACTAAACCATCCAATCGG-3’. Relative gene expression was normalized to 18s rRNA.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) Assay

Cells were seeded in 60 mm dishes at a density of 4 × 105 − 5 × 105 cells. After overnight incubation, cells were exposed to 9α,11αOH-KA at the indicated concentrations or vehicle (DMSO) for 48 h. After treatment, cells were collected and stained with 2 µM JC-1 (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA, Cat#HY-15534) in PBS at 37℃, 5% CO2, for 20 min according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The stained cells were then analyzed by using BD FACSCanto™ (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) with BD FACSDiva™ 6.2 software (BD) for acquisition and analysis, or observed and imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE 80i, Nikon, Japan) equipped with a DS-Qi1MC CCD camera (Nikon).

Measurements of mitochondrial OXPHOS

The oxygen consumption rate (OCR), which can serve as the indicator of OXPHOS, was measured using Seahorse XFe24 analyzer and XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA, Cat#103015-100). Cells (1 × 104) were seeded in Seahorse XFe24-well microplate for 24 h. After overnight incubation, cells were treated with vehicle control (DMSO) or 7.2 µM and 14.4 µM 9α,11αOH-KA for 24 h. After washing the cells with culture medium without sodium bicarbonate, the base OCR was then determined through real-time measurement under basal conditions. After measuring the basal respiration, mitochondrial function was examined by sequentially adding four compounds that affect bioenergetics, including: Oligomycin (1 µM, injection A), FCCP (1.5 µM, injection B), and Rotenone/Antimycin A (1 µM, injection C). At the end of recording, the OCR values were calculated after normalization with the number of cells.

Measurement of intracellular ATP content

Cells were seeded in 60 mm dishes at a density of 4 × 105 − 5 × 105 cells. After overnight incubation, cells were exposed to 9α,11αOH-KA at the indicated concentration or vehicle control (DMSO) for 24 h. The intracellular ATP level was quantitatively determined using an ATP Colorimetric/Fluorometric Assay Kit (Biovision, Milpitas, CA, USA, Cat#MA-0113) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vivo animal model

All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of China Medical University (Approval No. CMUIACUC-2021-306-1), and all experimental procedures were performed according to the regulations of the IACUC guidelines of China Medical University. A549 cells (2 × 106) were harvested and mixed with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA, Cat#356234), and then subcutaneously injected into BALB/c nude mice (National Institutes of Applied Research National Center for Biomodels, Taipei, Taiwan). After 14 days of injection, the mice were randomly assigned to administrate 100 mg/kg or 200 mg/kg 9α,11αOH-KA or the vehicle DMSO solution in sterile water containing 0.5% methylcellulose (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#M0512) and 0.1% Tween-80 (v/v) (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#P1754) via oral gavage daily. The body weight of each mouse was monitored every week. Tumor volumes were calculated weekly from caliper measurements (width2 × length × 0.52) after administration. At the termination of the experiment, mice were anesthetized with 2.5–3.5% isoflurane inhalation anesthesia prior to tumor collection, and euthanasia was subsequently performed by carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. The tumors were weighted, photographed, and processed to Western blot analysis.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses in this study were performed by using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied when data met the assumptions of normality. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for normally distributed data, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test to correct for multiple testing. The difference is considered significant when * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001.

Results

Bioactivity-guided fractionation of A. lavenia reveals leaf-derived extract containing the most potent cytotoxic compounds against NSCLC Cells

In order to isolate and identify potential anticancer agents from Taiwanese A. lavenia, a systematic bioactivity-guided fractionation strategy was performed (Fig. 1A). Plant materials of A. lavenia were initially separated into leaves (AL1), stems (AL2), and aerial parts (AL3), followed by hot water extraction. To identify extracts with potent cytotoxic properties, we performed cytotoxicity screening using MTT assays in A549 and H1975 NSCLC cell lines. Among the three primary extracts, the leaf extract (AL1) displayed the strongest cytotoxic activity, significantly reducing cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner compared with the stem (AL2) and aerial (AL3) extracts (Fig. 2A and B). These results suggest that cytotoxic constituents against NSCLC cells are highly enriched in the leaves of Taiwanese A. lavenia. The crude leaf extract was therefore subjected to a multistep solvent-partitioning procedure to obtain four fractions, including ethyl acetate (AL1-1), n-butanol (AL1-2), aqueous (AL1-3), and chylus (AL1-4). Subsequent screening of these fractions revealed that the ethyl acetate-soluble fraction (AL1-1) exhibited the most potent cytotoxic activity among the four fractions (AL1-1 to AL1-4) in both A549 and H1975 cells (Fig. 2C and D), indicating that compounds with intermediate polarity present in the ethyl acetate fraction are primarily responsible for the cytotoxic activity observed in the crude extract.

Further chromatographic separation of AL1-1 yielded ten subfractions (AL1-1-1 to AL1-1-10), among which AL1-1-4 displayed the strongest cytotoxic activity against A549 and H1975 cells (Fig. 2E and F). Spectroscopic and chromatographic analyses of the AL1-1-4 subfraction revealed two major structurally related kaurane-type diterpenoids: ent-9α,11α-dihydroxy-15-oxo-kaur-16-en-19-oic acid (9α,11α-dihydroxy-kaurenoic Acid; 9α,11αOH-KA) and ent-11α-hydroxy-15-oxo-kaur-16-en-19-oic acid (11αOH-KA) (Fig. 1B), which were subsequently purified and structurally confirmed via HR-ESI-Orbitrap-MS and NMR (Supplementary Fig. S1-S6).

9α,11αOH-KA exhibited more potent cytotoxicity than 11αOH-KA and induced apoptotic cell death in NSCLC cells

We next compared the cytotoxic activities of 9α,11αOH-KA and 11αOH-KA (Fig. 3A) using MTT assays in A549 and H1975 cells. The MTT results showed that 9α,11αOH-KA exhibited greater cytotoxic potency than 11αOH-KA in both A549 (Fig. 3B) and H1975 (Fig. 3C) cells. Beyond A549 and H1975 cells, the cytotoxic effect of 9α,11αOH-KA was further examined in H1299, H1650, and HCC827 NSCLC cell lines, as well as in non-tumorigenic human fibroblast CCD966-SK cells. Among these three additional NSCLC cell lines, H1299, H1650, and HCC827 cells also showed high susceptibility to the cytotoxic effect of 9α,11αOH-KA, with cell viability reduced to 15.4 ± 9.2%, 14.3 ± 2.7%, and 12.3 ± 1.6%, respectively, following treatment at 28.7 µM for 72 h (Fig. 3D). In contrast, non-tumorigenic CCD966-SK fibroblasts retained substantially higher viability (73.0 ± 8.0%) compared to NSCLC cells (Fig. 3D), indicating preferential cytotoxicity toward NSCLC cells compared with CCD966-SK fibroblasts. These findings suggest that 9α,11αOH-KA exhibits greater cytotoxicity toward NSCLC cells than the fibroblast control line examined, although additional studies using origin-matched normal lung epithelial cells will be required to further define its therapeutic selectivity. To determine whether the suppressive effects of 9α,11αOH-KA on NSCLC cell viabilities were mediated through apoptosis, Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) dual staining assays were conducted to detect apoptotic cell death. Compared with the DMSO control group, the proportion of apoptotic cells significantly increased in a concentration-dependent manner following 72 h of treatment with 9α,11αOH-KA in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells (Fig. 3E). As shown in Fig. 3F, the percentage of apoptotic cells in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells increased from 5.2 ± 0.7%, 12.1 ± 3.8%, and 23.1 ± 1.7% to 52.3 ± 10.8%, 49.4 ± 2.9%, and 43.7 ± 1.8%, respectively, as the concentration of 9α,11αOH-KA increased from 2.8 µM to 28.7 µM. Furthermore, Western blot analyses revealed increased levels of cleaved PARP-1, caspase-3, and caspase-9 in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells treated with 9α,11αOH-KA (Fig. 3G), further confirming the induction of apoptosis in NSCLC cells.

Suppressive effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on cancer stemness and motility in NSCLC cells

Given the critical role of CSCs in tumor initiation, recurrence, metastasis, prognosis, and resistance to conventional therapies44, we further investigated the impact of 9α,11αOH-KA on cancer stemness using tumorsphere formation assays in NSCLC cells. Treatment with 9α,11αOH-KA significantly reduced both the size and number of tumorspheres in a concentration-dependent manner in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells (Fig. 4A). This suppressive effect on CSCs was further validated by decreased expression levels of CSCs-associated markers, including Notch-1, CD44, and CD133, in these NSCLC cells (Fig. 4B). Notch-1 signaling pathway has been known to regulate the self-renewal and survival of lung CSCs45,46. To explore whether the inhibitory effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on CSCs properties in NSCLC cells was mediated through suppression of Notch-1 signaling, the Notch-1 intracellular domain (NICD) was ectopically overexpressed in 9α,11αOH-KA-treated A549 cells (Fig. 4C). Tumorsphere formation assays showed that NICD overexpression significantly alleviated the inhibitory effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on tumorsphere formation in A549 cells (Fig. 4D). These findings indicate that 9α,11αOH-KA suppresses CSCs-associated properties in NSCLC cells, at least in part, through downregulation of Notch-1 signaling.

Metastasis is the primary cause of lung cancer-related mortality, therefore, targeting metastasis represents a potential therapeutic strategy for lung cancer47,48. To evaluate the effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on NSCLC cell motility, wound healing and transwell migration assays were performed. Compared with the DMSO control, wound healing assays showed that treatment with 9α,11αOH-KA (7.2, 14.4, and 28.7 µM) significantly reduced wound closure in a concentration-dependent manner in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells (Fig. 4E-G). Transcriptome analysis of NSCLC cells treated with 9α,11αOH-KA

To elucidate the potential molecular targets and signaling pathways affected by 9α,11αOH-KA in NSCLC cells, transcriptomic analysis was performed in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells treated with 9α,11αOH-KA or vehicle (DMSO). Heat map analysis clearly distinguished the significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between untreated (A549 DMSO, H1975 DMSO, and HCC827 DMSO) and treated (A549 9α,11αOH-KA, H1975 9α,11αOH-KA, and HCC827 9α,11αOH-KA) groups (Fig. 5A). Volcano plot analysis revealed a total of 2,573 statistically significant DEGs were identified between the treated and untreated groups, including 1,210 upregulated genes (fold change ≥ 2, p < 0.05) and 1,363 downregulated genes (fold change ≤ − 2, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5B), suggesting that 9α,11αOH-KA treatment induced substantial transcriptional alterations in NSCLC cells.

In order to further investigate the molecular pathways involved in the anticancer effects of 9α,11αOH-KA, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed based on the transcriptomic data49,50. The top 20 enriched KEGG pathways were summarized in Fig. 5C, and several cancer-related pathways were significantly enriched, including basal cell carcinoma (p = 0.0013), microRNAs in cancer (p = 0.0018), the MAPK signaling pathway (p = 0.0041), oxidative phosphorylation (p = 0.0050), apoptosis, and the mTOR signaling pathway (p = 0.0062) (Fig. 5C). To validate the KEGG results, we examined the regulatory effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on the mTOR signaling pathway in NSCLC cells. Western blot analyses showed that 9α,11αOH-KA treatment significantly decreased the phosphorylation levels of mTOR (p-mTOR) and its downstream targets, p-p70S6K and p-4EBP1, in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells (Fig. 5D). Notably, the oxidative phosphorylation pathway was also significantly enriched, comprising 7 upregulated and 13 downregulated mitochondrial OXPHOS-related genes (Fig. 5E). The thirteen downregulated mitochondrial OXPHOS-related genes, including MT-ND1, MT-ND2, MT-ND3, MT-ND4, MT-ND4L, MT-ND5, MT-ND6, MT-CYB, MT-CO1, MT-CO2, MT-CO3, MT-ATP6, and MT-ATP8 (Fig. 5E), encode subunits of mitochondrial ETC complexes I, III, IV, and V. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis corroborated that 9α,11αOH-KA treatment significantly reduced the transcript levels of several mitochondrial OXPHOS-related genes, including MT-ND1 and MT-ND6 (complex I), MT-CYB (complex III), MT-CO1 (complex IV), and MT-ATP6 (complex V) in NSCLC cells (Fig. 5F). Consistent with these transcriptional changes, Western blot analysis demonstrated concentration-dependent decreases in the protein expression of OXPHOS components, particularly MT-ND1, MT-ND2, and MT-CYB, in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells following 9α,11αOH-KA treatment (Fig. 5G). The concordant suppression of both mRNA and protein levels across multiple mitochondrial ETC-associated genes supports the transcriptomic findings and suggests that 9α,11αOH-KA induces broad mitochondrial functional impairment associated with disruption of mitochondrial bioenergetics in NSCLC cells. Accumulating evidence has shown that elevated mitochondrial OXPHOS promotes tumorigenesis and chemoresistance in NSCLC51,52. Therefore, targeting mitochondrial OXPHOS has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for suppressing tumor growth and metastasis in NSCLC53.

Effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on mitochondrial membrane potential and oxygen consumption rate

According to the above findings, we next examined the effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on mitochondria function by measuring mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP, ∆Ψm) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR). JC-1 fluorescence staining revealed that treatment with 9α,11αOH-KA significantly increased MMP depolarization from 37.5 ± 5.0% and 21.0 ± 1.8% to 64.9 ± 3.9% and 62.8 ± 0.7% (p < 0.0001) in A549 and H1975 cells, respectively, as the concentration of 9α,11αOH-KA increased from 14.4 µM to 28.7 µM (Fig. 6A). The reduction of MMP, indicated by increased green fluorescence of monomeric form of JC-1, in 9α,11αOH-KA treated cells was further confirmed and observed by fluorescence microscope (Fig. 6B), suggesting that 9α,11αOH-KA treatment significantly disrupts MMP in NSCLC cells. Since MMP is essential for maintaining mitochondrial respiratory chain function and ATP production54, we next evaluated the effect of 9α,11αOH-KA on mitochondrial bioenergetics through measuring the cellular oxygen consumption rate (OCR), an indicator of OXPHOS, in A549 cells. As shown in Fig. 6C, the real-time measurement of OCR plotting curves revealed that 9α,11αOH-KA treatment significantly reduced mitochondrial respiration. Compared with the DMSO control, treatment with 7.2 µM and 14.4 µM 9α,11αOH-KA significantly inhibited basal respiration (107.6 ± 2.4 and 74.9 ± 4.3 vs. 125.7 ± 14.5 pmol/min/10⁴ cells, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 6D) and maximal respiration (106.7 ± 3.3 and 66.3 ± 6.2 vs. 128.9 ± 8.8 pmol/min/10⁴ cells, p < 0.05 and p < 0.0001) (Fig. 6E) in a concentration-dependent manner. Moreover, treatment with 9α,11αOH-KA decreased the intracellular ATP levels up to 25–40% in A549, H1975, and HCC827 NSCLC cells (Fig. 6F). Taken together, these findings suggest that 9α,11αOH-KA induces mitochondrial dysfunction associated with impaired OXPHOS and disruption of mitochondrial bioenergetics in NSCLC cells.

Induction of ROS-dependent cytotoxicity by 9α,11αOH-KA in NSCLC cells

In light of mitochondria are the major source of cellular ROS, particularly through electron leakage from mitochondrial ETC complexes55, and excessive ROS accumulation can trigger oxidative damage and cell death, we hypothesized that the anticancer activity of 9α,11αOH-KA against NSCLC cells may be associated with ROS overproduction. To test this hypothesis, intracellular ROS levels were measured by flow cytometry using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. As shown in Fig. 7A, treatment with 9α,11αOH-KA significantly increased ROS production in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells, reaching levels approximately 1.7- to 3.4-fold higher than those observed in the DMSO control group. Furthermore, pretreatment with N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a commonly used ROS scavenger, markedly suppressed the ROS accumulation induced by 9α,11αOH-KA (Fig. 7A). In addition to reducing ROS levels, NAC significantly attenuated 9α,11αOH-KA-induced apoptotic cell death in A549, H1975, and HCC827 cells (Fig. 7B). Consistently, NAC pretreatment also significantly restored cell survival and viability in response to 9α,11αOH-KA-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. 7C). These finding suggest that ROS overproduction plays a critical role in mediating 9α,11αOH-KA-induced apoptosis in NSCLC cells.

In vivo efficacy of 9α,11αOH-KA in suppressing the growth of NSCLC xenograft tumor model

In addition to the in vitro findings, we further examined the in vivo antitumor efficacy of 9α,11αOH-KA using a human NSCLC xenograft model established by subcutaneous implantation of A549 cells in nude mice. Tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group) and treated once daily by oral gavage with vehicle (DMSO) or 9α,11αOH-KA at doses of 100 or 200 mg/kg. Compared with the vehicle control, 9α,11αOH-KA treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth in vivo after 35 days of treatment (Fig. 8A), as evidenced by reduced tumor volumes and representative tumor sizes (Fig. 8B). Consistently, tumor weights at the endpoint were significantly decreased in the 9α,11αOH-KA-treated groups compared with the control group (Fig. 8C). At the study endpoint, tumor volume was reduced by 78.4–83.7% relative to vehicle-treated mice (p < 0.0001). No significant differences in the body weight were observed between the vehicle- and 9α,11αOH-KA-treated groups throughout the treatment period, suggesting good tolerability and the absence of overt systemic toxicity (Fig. 8D). Furthermore, histological examination of major organs, including liver, spleen, and kidney, using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed no apparent pathological abnormalities in the 9α,11αOH-KA-treated groups compared with the control group (Fig. 8E), supporting a favorable preliminary safety profile in vivo. Additionally, Western blot analysis of tumor lysates revealed that treatment with 9α,11αOH-KA significantly decreased the expression of mitochondrial OXPHOS-related proteins, including MT-ND1, MT-ND2, and MT-CYB, compared with the vehicle control (Fig. 8F), suggesting that the tumor-suppressive activity of 9α,11αOH-KA is associated with inhibition of mitochondrial OXPHOS and disruption of mitochondrial bioenergetics in vivo.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates the potent anticancer activity of 9α,11αOH-KA, a kaurane-type diterpenoid isolated from Taiwanese A. lavenia, against NSCLC. Through a comprehensive series of in vitro and in vivo experiments, we show that 9α,11αOH-KA exerts preferential cytotoxic effects toward NSCLC cells compared with a non-tumorigenic human fibroblast cell line (CCD966-SK), suggesting a degree of tumor-selective activity. However, given that CCD966-SK cells are not derived from lung epithelium, this observation should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, the use of fibroblast cells as a non-tumorigenic control represents a limitation of the present study, as these cells differ from lung epithelial cells in tissue origin, lineage, and metabolic characteristics, and therefore may not fully recapitulate the physiological responses of normal lung tissue to mitochondrial perturbation. Future studies incorporating origin-matched non-tumorigenic human lung epithelial cells, such as BEAS-2B or primary small airway epithelial cells, will be necessary to more accurately define the tissue specificity and therapeutic window of 9α,11αOH-KA. Nevertheless, 9α,11αOH-KA effectively suppresses tumor growth through a dual mechanism involving mitochondrial dysfunction associated with impaired OXPHOS and disruption of mitochondrial bioenergetics, together with suppression of cancer stemness properties, highlighting its potential as a mitochondria-targeting therapeutic candidate for NSCLC.

A key aspect of this study is the comparison of the cytotoxic activity of 9α,11αOH-KA with its structurally related analog 11αOH-KA. Although both compounds share a kaurane-type diterpenoid backbone, the presence of an additional hydroxyl group at the C-9 in 9α,11αOH-KA markedly enhanced its anticancer potency. Cytotoxicity assays consistently demonstrated stronger growth inhibition across multiple NSCLC cell lines by 9α,11αOH-KA compared with 11αOH-KA. From a structure-activity relationship (SAR) perspective, the additional C-9 hydroxyl group may alter the physicochemical properties of 9α,11αOH-KA, such as polarity, hydrogen-bonding capacity, and molecular conformation. These changes could influence membrane permeability, intracellular distribution, and protein-ligand interactions. In particular, the increased hydrogen-bonding potential may enhance interactions with target proteins, whereas changes in polarity and conformation may affect cellular uptake and subcellular localization. Given the mitochondrial functional changes observed in this study, altered mitochondrial distribution or interaction with mitochondrial-associated proteins is a plausible explanation for the enhanced activity of 9α,11αOH-KA. However, direct evidence for mitochondrial localization or target binding is currently lacking. Therefore, this SAR interpretation should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Future studies incorporating target identification, cellular uptake analysis, and structure-based modeling will be required to elucidate the mechanistic basis underlying this SAR.

An important clinical implication of our findings is the efficacy of 9α,11αOH-KA in NSCLC cells harboring distinct EGFR mutations, including those associated with resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Although EGFR-targeted therapies such as erlotinib, gefitinib, and osimertinib have significantly improved outcomes for patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC, acquired resistance frequently develops through secondary mutations (e.g., EGFR T790M) or activation of bypass signaling pathways56,57. In this study, 9α,11αOH-KA exhibited robust cytotoxicity across EGFR wild-type (A549), EGFR exon 19 deletion (HCC827), and EGFR L858R/T790M double mutant (H1975) NSCLC cell lines. The consistent cytotoxic effects observed across EGFR wild-type and mutant cell lines suggest that 9α,11αOH-KA acts independently of EGFR signaling status, thereby potentially overcoming TKI resistance. This EGFR-independent mechanism may provide therapeutic benefit for patients who have progressed on current targeted therapies.

Accumulating evidence indicates that mitochondrial OXPHOS remains functional and often essential in many tumors, particularly in CSCs, therapy-resistant subpopulations, and metastatic lesions52,53,58–63. Conventional mitochondrial OXPHOS inhibitors, such as metformin and atovaquone, have demonstrated anticancer activity but are often associated with limited tumor selectivity due to the fundamental requirement of mitochondrial respiration in normal tissues. In this study, our findings suggest that 9α,11αOH-KA may exert a relatively greater impact on NSCLC cells, potentially reflecting cancer-specific metabolic dependencies. Moreover, transcriptomic and functional analyses demonstrated that 9α,11αOH-KA significantly impaired mitochondrial respiration, reduced ATP production, and disrupted mitochondrial membrane potential. These effects were accompanied by downregulation of mitochondrial-encoded genes, including MT-ND1, MT-CYB, MT-CO1, and MT-ATP6. Although these findings strongly support substantial mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics, the current data do not definitively distinguish between direct inhibition of mitochondrial OXPHOS and broader alterations involving mitochondrial biogenesis and/or mitochondrial transcriptional regulation. In particular, the coordinated suppression of multiple mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-encoded OXPHOS-associated genes across several ETC complexes may reflect broader mitochondrial regulatory perturbations rather than selective inhibition of a single respiratory complex. Although baseline mitochondrial activity was not directly compared between normal and cancer cells in this study, the observed suppression of mitochondrial respiration supports the notion that mitochondrial bioenergetics represents a critical vulnerability in NSCLC cells. Consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of the apoptotic cascade was observed, including cleavage of caspase-9, caspase-3, and PARP-1. Notably, flow cytometry detected apoptotic populations at lower concentrations than Western blot analysis of apoptotic markers. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in assay sensitivity, as Annexin V-based flow cytometry detects early apoptotic events at the single-cell level, whereas Western blot reflects population-averaged protein changes associated with later stages of apoptosis. In addition, increased ROS production following 9α,11αOH-KA treatment and its attenuation by NAC suggest that oxidative stress contributes to the cytotoxic response. Nevertheless, NAC is a pleiotropic antioxidant that can influence multiple aspects of cellular redox regulation and mitochondrial function. Therefore, the present data do not definitively establish whether ROS accumulation is a primary driver or a secondary consequence of mitochondrial dysfunction. Instead, our findings support a model in which mitochondrial impairment and ROS accumulation are closely interconnected processes that cooperatively promote apoptotic cell death in NSCLC cells. Although multiple lines of evidence indicate mitochondrial dysfunction associated with impaired OXPHOS, the direct molecular target of 9α,11αOH-KA within mitochondria remains to be identified. Moreover, the current findings do not exclude the possibility that broader alterations in mitochondrial biogenesis and/or mitochondrial transcriptional regulation contribute to the observed mitochondrial phenotype.

CSCs are increasingly recognized as key contributors to tumor initiation, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy64. In this study, tumorsphere assays revealed that 9α,11αOH-KA significantly suppressed cancer stemness-associated properties in NSCLC cells, as evidenced by reduced sphere-forming capacity and downregulation of CSC-associated markers, including CD44, CD133, and Notch-1. However, these analyses were based on bulk protein measurements and do not directly reflect changes in CSC frequency at the single-cell level. Future studies using flow cytometry-based quantification of CSC subpopulations and in vivo functional assays will be required to confirm CSC targeting. Given the established dependence of CSCs on mitochondrial metabolism60,62, the mitochondrial dysfunction induced by 9α,11αOH-KA may preferentially impair CSC-associated metabolic programs in NSCLC cells; however, this hypothesis requires further validation. Comparative metabolic profiling of CSC and non-CSC populations, including measurements of oxygen consumption rate and mitochondrial membrane potential, will be important to define the metabolic basis of CSC vulnerability to mitochondrial targeting. In addition to its effects on stemness-associated traits, 9α,11αOH-KA significantly inhibited NSCLC cell migration in wound healing and transwell assays, suggesting potential anti-metastatic activity. Further studies are warranted to determine whether this effect arises from impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics, disruption of cytoskeletal signaling, or suppression of CSC-associated pathways.

The in vivo antitumor efficacy observed in the A549 xenograft model further supports the translational potential of 9α,11αOH-KA. Oral administration of 9α,11αOH-KA led to significant tumor growth suppression without detectable systemic toxicity, and ex vivo analyses confirmed reduced expression of mitochondrial OXPHOS proteins in tumors. Importantly, histological examination of major organs revealed no overt pathological abnormalities, supporting a favorable preliminary safety profile. However, mechanistic validation of ROS accumulation and CSC suppression in tumor tissues was not performed, and thus the in vivo mechanism remains to be confirmed.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although in vivo efficacy of 9α,11αOH-KA was observed at 100 and 200 mg/kg, the pharmacokinetic and bioavailability data are currently lacking, leaving the systemic exposure, metabolic stability, and tissue distribution of 9α,11αOH-KA undefined. In addition, the absence of in vivo validation of ROS levels and CSC-associated markers limits mechanistic interpretation within the tumor microenvironment. Second, although transcriptomic and functional analyses implicate mitochondrial dysfunction associated with impaired OXPHOS and Notch-1 signaling as key pathways affected by 9α,11αOH-KA, additional signaling cascades, including MAPK and mTOR pathways identified by KEGG enrichment analysis, may also contribute to its anticancer effects. Furthermore, although coordinated suppression of mtDNA-encoded OXPHOS-associated genes was observed, the current study did not directly evaluate mitochondrial mass, mtDNA copy number, or mitochondrial biogenesis-associated pathways. Therefore, future studies will be required to distinguish between impaired mitochondrial biogenesis and direct mitochondrial respiratory inhibition as contributors to the mitochondrial dysfunction induced by 9α,11αOH-KA. Third, direct evaluation of CSC subpopulations using single-cell approaches and functional assays will be required to more precisely define the extent and specificity of CSC targeting by 9α,11αOH-KA. Finally, while 9α,11αOH-KA exhibited preferential cytotoxicity toward NSCLC cells compared with a non-tumorigenic fibroblast cell line, broader evaluation of normal cell types and in vivo toxicity will be required to fully establish its therapeutic window, particularly given the essential role of mitochondrial metabolism in high-energy tissues such as the heart, skeletal muscle, and nervous system.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that 9α,11αOH-KA exerts potent anti-NSCLC activity both in vitro and in vivo through mitochondria-associated mechanisms. 9α,11αOH-KA induces mitochondrial dysfunction associated with impaired OXPHOS and disruption of mitochondrial bioenergetics, leading to ATP depletion, ROS accumulation, and activation of caspase-dependent apoptosis, while also suppressing cancer stemness-associated properties. However, the current findings do not definitively distinguish between direct inhibition of mitochondrial respiratory function and broader alterations involving mitochondrial biogenesis and/or mitochondrial transcriptional regulation, which warrant further investigation. Importantly, oral administration significantly inhibited tumor growth without overt systemic toxicity, supported by histological evaluation of major organs. These findings identify mitochondrial bioenergetics and redox homeostasis as actionable vulnerabilities in NSCLC and highlight 9α,11αOH-KA as a promising natural-product scaffold for the development of mitochondrial-targeted anticancer therapeutics.
