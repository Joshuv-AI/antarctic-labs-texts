Imperfect blockade in Rydberg superatoms
Valentin Magro, Sébastien Garcia, Alexei Ourjoumtsev

Abstract
Ensembles of atoms interacting via their Rydberg levels, known as “superatoms” for their ability to encode qubits and to emit single photons, attract increasing attention as building blocks for quantum network nodes. Assessing their performance requires an accurate, physically informative and numerically scalable description of interactions in a large and disordered ensemble. We derive such a description from first principles and successfully test it against brute-force numerics and experimental data. This model proves essential to make quantitative predictions about gate fidelities or photon emission efficiencies, and to guide experiments towards large-scale superatom-based systems.



Photon exchange between quantum emitters is a fundamental physical process underlying many applications in quantum technologies. Its efficiency improves with the ratio between the scattering cross-section of the emitter and the characteristic cross-section of the photon. While confining the photon in a waveguide or in a small-volume cavity is the most explored route towards this goal
Haroche and Raimond 2006
;
Chang et al. 2014
, increasing the effective size of the atom emerged as an interesting alternative
Lukin et al. 2001
;
Dudin and Kuzmich 2012
;
Firstenberg et al. 2016
;
Kumlin et al. 2023
;
Shao et al. 2024
. It consists in using a “superatom” formed by a small atomic ensemble, where the anharmonicity required to isolate a single qubit is provided by strong interactions between Rydberg excitations.

Building superatom-based quantum nodes requires accurately predicting their imperfections, in particular those related to Rydberg blockade. Existing models often focus on correlations between the emitted photons, insensitive to linear losses, and consider either continuous driving or instantaneous excitation
Gorshkov et al. 2011
;
Bariani et al. 2012
;
Stanojevic et al. 2012
;
Gorshkov et al. 2013
;
Bienias et al. 2014
;
Grankin et al. 2015
;
Grankin et al. 2016
;
Georgakopoulos et al. 2018
. Semi-phenomenological models, accounting for dynamical control and non-Markovian interaction-induced qubit decay, successfully fit existing data
Stiesdal et al. 2020
but lack universality and thus predictive ability. This makes it difficult to describe the non-Gaussian photonic states produced with these superatoms
Magro et al. 2023
, to predict the success rates of superatom-based multi-photon gates
Stolz et al. 2022
and, more generally, to draw conclusions about the scalability of this approach.

Here we show that a dynamically driven and imperfectly blockaded superatom allows an accurate, low-dimensional, bottom-up description, scalable towards multi-node quantum networks. Derived from first principles and tested against brute-force simulations and experimental data, it precisely describes the system’s dynamics. We explain how this model is obtained from microscopic physics, give the equations required to implement it numerically, and show how it compares with brute-force numerics and with an experiment using a single superatom.

In most experiments, the atomic ensemble is prepared using a dipole trap formed by a single or two crossed Gaussian beams. As it is composed of many disordered atoms, we approximate it with a continuous Gaussian atomic density distribution, with a radius
\sigma

x

\sigma_{z}

z

{\bf r}=(x/\sigma,y/\sigma,z/\sigma_{z})

\mu({\bf r})={\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}/(2\pi)^{3/2}

{\hat{S}^{\dagger}}=\int\hbox{{d}}^{3}{\bf r}\sqrt{\mu({\bf r})}{\hat{\sigma}}_{rg}({\bf r})

We have shown, theoretically and experimentally, that the complex behavior of imperfectly-blockaded Rydberg superatoms can be reproduced by a numerically efficient model derived from first principles in a physically intuitive way. This ability is essential to adjust experimental parameters
Vaneecloo et al. 2022
, to optimize control pulse shapes, and to guide future developments of superatom-based quantum technologies. The foundations of the model are not specific to van-der-Waals interactions in Rydberg gases and can be generalized to ensembles of emitters interacting with a known potential, which does not need to be central. For example, resonant dipole-dipole interactions
V\propto(1-3\cos^{2}\theta)/r^{3}
can be described via a simple modification of the function
Q
in Eq.
11
.

Incidentally, this model explains why, in Gaussian atomic clouds, Rydberg blockade is much weaker than one could expect. The factor
3\sqrt{2}\approx 4.2
between the Gaussian radius
\sigma
and the range
R_{e}
, corresponding to the peak of the density of states and providing a correct estimation of the interactions, is of the order of
1
as expected from dimensional analysis, but
C_{6}/R_{e}^{6}
is weaker than
C_{6}/\sigma^{6}
by nearly four orders of magnitude. Preliminary simulations confirm that removing the atoms located at the edges of the cloud strongly improves the blockade without significantly degrading the superatom-photon coupling. The generalization of this model to non-Gaussian density profiles, mitigating this issue, is underway.

Appendix A
3D harmonic oscillator eigenstates

To describe spatial excitations in the atomic cloud, our model uses the eigenstates of a three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator. To construct them algebraically, let us first introduce the one-dimensional bosonic creation operator
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{x}={\hat{x}}/2-i{\hat{p}}_{x}=x/2-\partial_{x}
where
{\hat{p}}_{x}=-i\partial_{x}
is the momentum along
x
, as well as
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{y}
and
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{z}
for the
y
and
z
coordinates. They satisfy
[{\hat{a}}_{i},{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{j}]=\delta_{i,j}
, and their conjugates annihilate the fundamental fully-symmetric state
\left|\psi_{0,0,0}\right\rangle
with
\psi_{0,0,0}({\bf r})={\textrm{e}}^{-{\bf r}^{2}/4}/(2\pi)^{3/4}
. The “Cartesian” eigenstates of the Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}_{3D}={\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{x}{\hat{a}}_{x}+{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{y}{\hat{a}}_{y}+{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{z}{\hat{a}}_{z}
are thus of the form
({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{x})^{n_{x}}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{y})^{n_{y}}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{z})^{n_{z}}\left|\psi_{0,0,0}\right\rangle/\sqrt{n_{x}!n_{y}!,n_{z}!}
, with corresponding eigenvalues
n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}
. The structure of “cylindrical” eigenstates is the same if one replaces
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{x}
and
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{y}
with
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho}=({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{x}+i{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{y})/\sqrt{2}
and
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho^{*}}=({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{x}-i{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{y})/\sqrt{2}
. Expressing the projections
{\hat{L}}_{x}
,
{\hat{L}}_{y}
and
{\hat{L}}_{z}
of the angular momentum
\hat{\bf L}
as
{\hat{L}}_{i}=\epsilon_{ijk}\hat{r}_{j}{\hat{p}}_{k}=-i\epsilon_{ijk}{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{j}{\hat{a}}_{k}
, and observing that
{\hat{L}}_{z}={\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho}{\hat{a}}_{\rho}-{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho^{*}}{\hat{a}}_{\rho^{*}}={\hat{n}}_{\rho}-{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}
where
{\hat{n}}_{j}={\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{j}{\hat{a}}_{j}
, shows that
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho}
and
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho^{*}}
create excitations with, respectively,
+1
and
-1
unit of angular momentum along
z
.

In spherical coordinates, the states
\left|\psi_{n,l,m}\right\rangle
with eigenfunctions

\displaystyle\psi_{n,l,m}({\bf r})=

\displaystyle~R_{n,l}(r)Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\phi),

\displaystyle R_{n,l}(r)=

\displaystyle~\sqrt[4]{\frac{2}{\pi}}\mathcal{N}_{n,l}r^{l}{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/4}L_{n}^{(l+1/2)}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{n,l}=

\displaystyle~\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}n!}{(2n+2l+1)!!}},

(16)

conveniently separate into a radial part
R_{n,l}
involving generalized Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}^{(l+1/2)}
, multiplied by a spherical harmonic
Y_{l}^{m}
. They co-diagonalize
{\hat{H}}_{3D}
,
{\hat{L}}^{2}
and
{\hat{L}}_{z}
for the eigenvalues
2n+l,l(l+1)
and
m
, respectively. To construct them algebraically, we define the usual angular momentum ladder operators
{\hat{L}}_{+}={\hat{L}}_{x}+i{\hat{L}}_{y}=\sqrt{2}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{z}{\hat{a}}_{\rho^{*}}-{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho}{\hat{a}}_{z})
and
{\hat{L}}_{-}={\hat{L}}_{x}-i{\hat{L}}_{y}=\sqrt{2}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho^{*}}{\hat{a}}_{z}-{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{z}{\hat{a}}_{\rho})
such that
{\hat{L}}_{\pm}|\psi_{n,l,m}\rangle=\sqrt{l(l+1)-m(m\pm 1)}|\psi_{n,l,m\pm 1}\rangle
, and verify that
{\hat{L}}_{+}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\hat{\rho}})^{l}\left|\psi_{0,0,0}\right\rangle=0
and
{\hat{L}}_{z}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\hat{\rho}})^{l}\left|\psi_{0,0,0}\right\rangle=l({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\hat{\rho}})^{l}\left|\psi_{0,0,0}\right\rangle
. With the Condon-Shortley convention, we thus have
\left|\psi_{0,l,l}\right\rangle=(-{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho})^{l}\left|\psi_{0,0,0}\right\rangle/\sqrt{l!}
. Writing
l(l+1)-m(m-1)=(l-m+1)(l+m)
, we get, for
|m|\leq l
,

\left|\psi_{0,l,m}\right\rangle=\prod_{k=0}^{l-m-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2l-k)(k+1)}}{\hat{L}}_{-}^{l-m}\left|\psi_{0,l,l}\right\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{(l+m)!}{l!(2l)!(l-m)!}}{\hat{L}}_{-}^{l-m}(-{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho})^{l}\left|\psi_{0,0,0}\right\rangle.

(17)

For the radial part, we introduce the operator

{\hat{K}}={\hat{a}}_{x}^{2}+{\hat{a}}_{y}^{2}+{\hat{a}}_{z}^{2}=2{\hat{a}}_{\rho}{\hat{a}}_{\rho^{*}}+{\hat{a}}_{z}^{2}
which commutes with
\hat{\bf L}
, preserving
l
and
m
. Because
{\hat{H}}_{3D}={\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho}{\hat{a}}_{\rho}+{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho^{*}}{\hat{a}}_{\rho^{*}}+{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{z}{\hat{a}}_{z}={\hat{n}}_{\rho}+{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}+{\hat{n}}_{z}
verifies
[{\hat{H}}_{3D},{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}]=2{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}
, we have
{\hat{H}}_{3D}{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}\left|\psi_{n,l,m}\right\rangle=(2n+l+2){\hat{K}^{\dagger}}\left|\psi_{n,l,m}\right\rangle
. Thus, as
{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}
preserves
l
,
{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}\left|\psi_{n,l,m}\right\rangle\propto\left|\psi_{n+1,l,m}\right\rangle
. To determine the normalization factor, we expand
{\hat{K}}{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}=4{\hat{n}}_{\rho}{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}+4{\hat{n}}_{\rho}+4{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}+{\hat{n}}_{z}^{2}+3{\hat{n}}_{z}+2({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{z})^{2}{\hat{a}}_{\rho}{\hat{a}}_{\rho^{*}}+2{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho}{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho^{*}}({\hat{a}}_{z})^{2}+6

and use
{\hat{L}}_{+}{\hat{L}}_{-}={\hat{L}}_{x}^{2}+{\hat{L}}_{y}^{2}-i[{\hat{L}}_{x},{\hat{L}}_{y}]={\hat{L}}^{2}-{\hat{L}}_{z}^{2}+{\hat{L}}_{z}
, which gives

\displaystyle{\hat{L}}^{2}=

\displaystyle~2({\hat{n}}_{\rho}{\hat{n}}_{z}+{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}{\hat{n}}_{z}-{\hat{n}}_{\rho}{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}})-2({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{z})^{2}{\hat{a}}_{\rho}{\hat{a}}_{\rho^{*}}-2{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho}{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho^{*}}({\hat{a}}_{z})^{2}+{\hat{n}}_{\rho}^{2}+{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}^{2}+{\hat{n}}_{\rho}+{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}+2{\hat{n}}_{z}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow{\hat{K}}{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}+{\hat{L}}^{2}=

\displaystyle~{\hat{n}}_{\rho}^{2}+{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}^{2}+{\hat{n}}_{z}^{2}+5({\hat{n}}_{\rho}+{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}+{\hat{n}}_{z})+2{\hat{n}}_{\rho}{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}+2{\hat{n}}_{\rho}{\hat{n}}_{z}+2{\hat{n}}_{\rho^{*}}{\hat{n}}_{z}+6=({\hat{H}}_{3D}+2)({\hat{H}}_{3D}+3)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow{\hat{K}}{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}\left|\psi_{n,l,m}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle~[(2n+l+2)(2n+l+3)-l(l+1)]\left|\psi_{n,l,m}\right\rangle=2(n+1)(2(n+1)+2l+1)\left|\psi_{n,l,m}\right\rangle

The sign convention for Laguerre polynomials then gives
\left|\psi_{n,l,m}\right\rangle=-{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}\left|\psi_{n-1,l,m}\right\rangle/\sqrt{2n(2n+2l+1)}
. Recursively,

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{n,l,m}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle~\sqrt{\frac{(2l+1)!!}{2^{n}n!(2n+2l+1)!!}}(-{\hat{K}^{\dagger}})^{n}\left|\psi_{0,l,m}\right\rangle=\mathcal{K}_{n,l,m}(-{\hat{K}^{\dagger}})^{n}{\hat{L}}_{-}^{l-m}(-{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho})^{l}\left|\psi_{0,0,0}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n,l,m}=

\displaystyle~\sqrt{\frac{(l+m)!}{l!(2l)!(l-m)!}\frac{(2l+1)!!}{2^{n}n!(2n+2l+1)!!}}=\sqrt{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+l\\
n\end{array}\right)\frac{(l+m)!}{(l-m)!}\frac{2l+1}{l!(2n+2l+1)!}}.

In particular, we recover
\psi_{n,l,m}(-{\bf r})=(-1)^{l}\psi_{n,l,m}({\bf r})
, as
{\hat{K}^{\dagger}}
and
{\hat{L}}_{-}
are invariant by parity while
{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho}
changes sign.

Equation
A
is the algeabraic definition of the 3D harmonic oscillator eigenstates we were looking for. It will be used it in Appendix
B
to calculate laser coupling matrix elements between singly- and doubly-excited states.

Appendix B
Doubly-excited states in center-of-mass and relative coordinates

When the Rydberg blockade is imperfect, the laser can add a symmetric excitation to a singly-excited state
\left|\psi_{n,l}\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{n,2l,0}\right\rangle
, creating the doubly-excited state

\frac{{\hat{S}^{\dagger}}}{\sqrt{1+\delta_{n+l}}}\left|\psi_{n,l}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\delta_{n+l}}}\int\hbox{{d}}{\bf r}_{a}^{3}\hbox{{d}}{\bf r}_{b}^{3}\psi_{n,2l,0}({\bf r}_{a})\psi_{0,0,0}({\bf r}_{b}){\hat{\sigma}}_{rg}({\bf r}_{a}){\hat{\sigma}}_{rg}({\bf r}_{b})\left|G\right\rangle

(20)

In this Appendix, following the standard treatment of a two-body problem with interactions depending only on the relative position
{\bf r}_{a}-{\bf r}_{b}

\displaystyle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho}=

\displaystyle~\frac{{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{x}+i{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{y}}{\sqrt{2}},

\displaystyle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho^{*}}=

\displaystyle~\frac{{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{x}-i{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{y}}{\sqrt{2}},

\displaystyle{\hat{L}}_{a-}=

\displaystyle~{\hat{L}^{\dagger}}_{a+}=\sqrt{2}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho^{*}}{\hat{a}}_{z}-{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{z}{\hat{a}}_{\rho}),

\displaystyle{\hat{K}}_{a}=

\displaystyle~2{\hat{a}}_{\rho}{\hat{a}}_{\rho^{*}}+{\hat{a}}_{z}^{2}

Using

||\left|\Phi_{n_{c},l_{c},m_{c},n_{d},l_{d},m_{d}}\right\rangle||^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int\hbox{{d}}{\bf r}_{a}^{3}\hbox{{d}}{\bf r}_{b}^{3}\hbox{{d}}{{\bf r}^{\prime}_{a}}^{3}\hbox{{d}}{{\bf r}^{\prime}_{b}}^{3}\psi_{n_{c},l_{c},m_{c}}^{*}({\bf r}_{c})\psi_{n_{c},l_{c},m_{c}}({\bf r}^{\prime}_{c})\psi_{n_{d},l_{d},m_{d}}^{*}({\bf r}_{d})\psi_{n_{d},l_{d},m_{d}}({\bf r}^{\prime}_{d})\\

{\hat{K}}^{p}{\hat{L}}_{+}^{q}({\hat{K}^{\dagger}})^{n}{\hat{L}}_{-}^{l-m}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho})^{l}\left|0\right\rangle=\frac{\mathcal{K}_{n-p,l,m+q}^{2}}{\mathcal{K}_{n,l,m}^{2}}({\hat{K}^{\dagger}})^{n-p}{\hat{L}}_{-}^{l-m-q}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho})^{l}\left|0\right\rangle

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n+l}}\frac{\mathcal{K}_{l_{c}+l_{d}-l,2l,0}^{2}}{\mathcal{K}_{n,2l,0}^{2}}\left\langle{\hat{a}}_{\rho}^{2l}{\hat{L}}_{+}^{2l}{\hat{K}}^{l_{c}+l_{d}-l}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho})^{2l_{c}}{\hat{L}}_{-}^{2l_{c}+2l_{d}}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}_{\rho})^{2l_{d}}\right\rangle

(33)

The operator
{\hat{L}}_{+}
commutes with
{\hat{K}}

Appendix C
Effective description of interactions in a restricted subspace

In this Appendix, we derive an effective non-Hermitian potential describing the effect of Rydberg interactions in a subspace. The decay rates obtained from its anti-Hermitian part are then injected in a standard master equation.

C.1
Effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian

We restrict the Hilbert space of the system to a small subspace
\{{\hat{P}}\}
formed by the ground state
\left|G\right\rangle
, a few low-lying singly-excited states
\left|\psi_{n,l}\right\rangle
with
n+l\leq n_{max}
, and a few low-lying doubly-excited states
\left|\Psi_{n_{c},l_{c},n_{d},l_{d},m}\right\rangle
defined in Appendix
B
, where all indices are positive integers,
0\leq m\leq\min(2l_{c},2l_{d})
and
n_{c}+n_{d}+l_{c}+l_{d}\leq n_{max}
. This subspace corresponds to a projector
{\hat{P}}=\left|G\middle\rangle\middle\langle G\right|+\sum_{n,l}\left|\psi_{n,l}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{n,l}\right|+\sum_{\bf k}\left|\Psi_{\bf k}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\Psi_{\bf k}\right|
where we adopted the compact notation
{\bf k}=(n_{c},l_{c},n_{d},l_{d},m)
. The Rydberg interaction potential (Eq.
10
)

\displaystyle{\hat{V}}=\int\hbox{{d}}{\bf r}_{a}^{3}\hbox{{d}}{\bf r}_{b}^{3}\frac{V_{0}}{8r_{d}^{6}(1-\beta\cos^{2}(\theta_{d}))^{3}}\frac{{\hat{\sigma}}_{rr}({\bf r}_{a}){\hat{\sigma}}_{rr}({\bf r}_{b})}{2},

(66)

couples these doubly-excited states to many higher-lying ones, treated here as a broad memory-less continuum. The coupling to this continuum leads to Lamb shifts and decays, which we obtain from an effective non-Hermitian potential
{\hat{V}}_{e}=\hat{\Lambda}-i\hat{\Gamma}
. Its Hermitian part
\hat{\Lambda}
describes the unitary part of the interactions, including Lamb shifts, while
\hat{\Gamma}
contains time-independent decay rates which we can integrate is a standard Gorini - Kossakowski - Sudarshan - Lindblad (GKSL) master equation
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
given in Eq.
15
of the main text.

To calculate
{\hat{V}}_{e}
, we follow Ref.
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 1998
and use the resolvent of
{\hat{V}}/V_{0}
:

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z-{\hat{V}}/V_{0}}=\int\hbox{{d}}{\bf r}_{a}^{3}\hbox{{d}}{\bf r}_{b}^{3}G(z,{\bf r}_{d})\frac{{\hat{\sigma}}_{rr}({\bf r}_{a}){\hat{\sigma}}_{rr}({\bf r}_{b})}{2},

\displaystyle G(z,{\bf r}_{d})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z-1/[8r_{d}^{6}(1-\beta\cos^{2}(\theta_{d}))^{3}]}

(67)

In the limit
\text{Im}(z)\rightarrow 0^{+}
assumed in the following,
{\hat{G}}(z)
is related to the forward propagator
{\hat{U}}(t\geq 0)={\textrm{e}}^{-i{\hat{V}}t}
via

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}(z)=

\displaystyle-iV_{0}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t\,{\textrm{e}}^{i(V_{0}z-{\hat{V}})t}=-iV_{0}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t\,{\hat{U}}(t){\textrm{e}}^{iV_{0}zt},

\displaystyle{\hat{U}}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}z\,{\hat{G}}(z){\textrm{e}}^{-iV_{0}zt}.

(68)

Defining
{\hat{V}}_{e}(z)=V_{0}[z-({\hat{P}}{\hat{G}}(z){\hat{P}})^{-1}]
gives the exact evolution in the subspace of interest as

{\hat{P}}{\hat{U}}(t){\hat{P}}=\frac{i}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}z\,\frac{{\hat{P}}}{z-{\hat{V}}_{e}(z)/V_{0}}{\textrm{e}}^{-iV_{0}zt}

(69)

where

is a characteristic energy rescaled by
V_{0}

{\hat{P}}{\hat{U}}(t){\hat{P}}={\textrm{e}}^{-i{\hat{V}}_{e}(z_{e})t}

, choosing an appropriate

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}r{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}r^{2l+2}L_{j}^{(l+1/2)}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)L_{j^{\prime}}^{(l+1/2)}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\frac{x^{3}}{r^{6}/8-x^{3}}.

(86)

The expansion

\displaystyle\frac{x^{3}}{r^{6}/8-x^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{x{\textrm{e}}^{-2i\pi k/3}}{r^{2}/2-x{\textrm{e}}^{-2i\pi k/3}},

(87)

shows that
Q
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle Q(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}T(x)+\frac{2}{3}\text{Re}\left(T\left(x{\textrm{e}}^{-2i\pi/3}\right)\right),

\displaystyle T_{j,j^{\prime},l}(x)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}r{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}r^{2l+2}L_{j}^{(l+1/2)}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)L_{j^{\prime}}^{(l+1/2)}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\frac{x}{r^{2}/2-x}.

(88)

For
\text{Im}(x)<0
, which is the case for
x=z^{-1/3}/(4(1-\beta u^{2}))
and for
x{\textrm{e}}^{-2i\pi/3}
as
\text{Im}(z)\rightarrow 0^{+}
,

\displaystyle T_{0,0,0}(x)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}r\frac{x{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}r^{2}}{r^{2}/2-x}=2x\left[1+\sqrt{\frac{x}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}r{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}\left(\frac{1}{r-\sqrt{2x}}-\frac{1}{r+\sqrt{2x}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2x\left[1-i\sqrt{\frac{x}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}r{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}{\textrm{e}}^{-it\sqrt{2x}}\left({\textrm{e}}^{itr}+{\textrm{e}}^{-itr}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2x\left[1-i\sqrt{2x}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t{\textrm{e}}^{-t^{2}/2-it\sqrt{2x}}\right]=2x\left[1-i\sqrt{\pi x}{\textrm{e}}^{-x}\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{i\sqrt{x}}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}t{\textrm{e}}^{-t^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2x\left[1-i\sqrt{\pi x}w(-\sqrt{x})\right]

(89)

where
w(z)={\textrm{e}}^{-z}\hbox{{e}rfc}(-iz)
is the Faddeeva function. Using
r^{2}=2(r^{2}/2-x+x)
, we then obtain recursively

\displaystyle T_{0,0,l+1}(x)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}r{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}r^{2l+4}\frac{x}{r^{2}/2-x}=2x\left(T_{0,0,l}(x)+\mathcal{N}_{0,l}^{-2}\right)=2x\left(T_{0,0,l}(x)+(2l+1)!!\right).

(90)

Using the symmetry
T_{j,j^{\prime},l}=T_{j^{\prime},j,l}
, this allows us to construct the family of functions
T_{j,j^{\prime},l}
via the recurrence relation of Laguerre polynomials:

\displaystyle L_{j+1}^{(\alpha)}(y)=

\displaystyle\frac{(2j+1+\alpha-y)L_{j}^{(\alpha)}(y)-(j+\alpha)L_{j-1}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{j+1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow T_{j+1,j^{\prime},l}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{2j+l+3/2-x}{j+1}T_{j,j^{\prime},l}(x)-\frac{j+l+1/2}{j+1}T_{j-1,j^{\prime},l}(x)

\displaystyle-\frac{x}{j+1}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}r{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}r^{2l+2}L_{j}^{(l+1/2)}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)L_{j^{\prime}}^{(l+1/2)}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2j+l+3/2-x}{j+1}T_{j,j^{\prime},l}(x)-\frac{j+l+1/2}{j+1}T_{j-1,j^{\prime},l}(x)-\frac{x}{(j+1)\mathcal{N}_{j,l}^{2}}\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}.

(91)

The explicit calculation of
T_{0,0,0}(x)
eliminates the apparent divergence of the integrand in Eq.

and makes Eq.
C.1
easy to integrate numerically. We thus recover Eq.
11
in the main text.

For a spherical cloud with
\beta=0
, the interactions conserve angular momentum
(\Rightarrow l^{\prime}=l)
, and the integral in Eq.

gives
\delta_{l^{\prime\prime}}Q_{j,j^{\prime},2l}(z^{-1/3}/4)
. After simplification,

\displaystyle z\left\langle\Psi_{n_{c}^{\prime},n_{d}^{\prime},l^{\prime}}\middle|{\hat{G}}(z)\middle|\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d},l}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\delta_{n_{c},n_{c}^{\prime}}\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\left(\delta_{n_{d},n_{d}^{\prime}}+\mathcal{N}_{n_{d},2l}\mathcal{N}_{n_{d}^{\prime},2l}Q_{n_{d},n_{d}^{\prime},2l}\left(z^{-1/3}/4\right)\right),

(92)

giving Eq.

in the main text.

C.2
Characteristic energy scale

To obtain the effective Hamiltonian
{\hat{V}}_{e}(z_{e})=V_{0}[z_{e}-({\hat{P}}{\hat{G}}(z_{e}){\hat{P}})^{-1}]
using the matrix elements of
{\hat{G}}(z)
calculated above, we need to make an appropriate choice for the dimensionless energy
z_{e}
. From Eq.
68
, it is easy to see that if the system’s evolution is Markovian, i.e. if
{\hat{P}}{\hat{U}}(t){\hat{P}}=\exp(-\hat{\Gamma}t-i\hat{\Lambda}t)
, we get
{\hat{V}}_{e}=\hat{\Lambda}-i\hat{\Gamma}
independently on
z_{e}
. Therefore, the choice of
z_{e}
mainly influences our ability to reproduce the non-Markovian part of the evolution. In many problems,
z_{e}
is defined as the mean value of
{\hat{V}}/V_{0}
,
z_{e}=\text{Tr}({\hat{P}}{\hat{V}}{\hat{P}})/\text{Tr}(V_{0}{\hat{P}})
. In our case, the interactions in the fully symmetric doubly-excited state
\left|\Psi_{\bf 0}\right\rangle
diverge. This effect is non-physical as it arises in a short-range limit where the van-der-Waals approximation is no longer valid. Our approach, providing a finite
{\hat{V}}_{e}
, conveniently eliminates it without introducing cutoffs, but this choice of
z_{e}
is inappropriate. Instead, as shown in the main text, the brute-force simulations are very well reproduced with
z_{e}=z_{\Omega}=\Omega/(2V_{0})
which corresponds to the power-broadened linewidth. In this case, if
\Omega
depends on time,
{\hat{V}}_{e}
will be time-dependent. Alternatively, we can note that the singly-excited qubit state
\left|R\right\rangle
is only laser-coupled to the Gaussian doubly-excited state
\left|\Psi_{{\bf 0}}\right\rangle
. Considering that atomic pairs are excited at random positions, which is of course a crude approximation ignoring blockade effects, we obtain a probability distribution for the dimensionless interaction energy
z
as

p(z)=\int\frac{\hbox{{d}}{\bf r}^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}\delta\left(\frac{1}{8r^{6}(1-\beta\cos^{2}\theta)^{3}}-z\right)\\
=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}\hbox{{d}}u\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}r\,r^{2}{\textrm{e}}^{-r^{2}/2}\delta\left(\frac{1}{8r^{6}(1-\beta u^{2})^{3}}-z\right)=\frac{1}{12\sqrt{\pi}z^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\hbox{{d}}u}{(1-\beta u^{2})^{3/2}}{\textrm{e}}^{-1/[4z^{1/3}(1-\beta u^{2})]}

(93)

which can be integrated using a change of variable
y=\sqrt{1/(1-\beta u^{2})-1}
, giving Eq.
14
in the main text:

\displaystyle p(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{{\textrm{e}}^{-1/(4z^{1/3})}}{12\sqrt{\pi\beta}z^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\beta/(1-\beta)}}\hbox{{d}}y{\textrm{e}}^{-y^{2}/(4z^{1/3})}=\frac{{\textrm{e}}^{-1/(4z^{1/3})}}{12\sqrt{\beta}z^{4/3}}\hbox{{e}rf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}}\frac{1}{2z^{1/6}}\right).

(94)

In the limit
\beta\rightarrow 0
of a spherical cloud, this reduces to
p(z)={\textrm{e}}^{-1/(4z^{1/3})}/(12\sqrt{\pi}z^{3/2})
which has a maximum in
z_{0}=18^{-3}\approx 1.7\times 10^{-4}
. This value changes little when the cloud flattens along the
z
axis: for
\beta\rightarrow 1
(
\sigma_{z}/\sigma\rightarrow 0
), this maximum is in
16^{-3}\approx 2.4\times 10^{-4}
. In the opposite, prolate regime, the limit
\beta\rightarrow-\infty
of an infinitely long cloud is irrelevant as the blockade vanishes in this case. As shown in the main text, choosing
z_{e}=z_{0}
provides a good approximation of the brute-force simulations when the blockade is not too weak.

C.3
Spherical cloud

For a spherical cloud the effective interaction potential
{\hat{V}}_{e}=\hat{\Lambda}-i\hat{\Gamma}
is block-diagonal in
n_{c}
and
l
(see Eq.
92
). We show here that inside each of these blocks the decay matrix
\hat{\Gamma}
has a single non-zero eigenvalue, allowing one to use a single collapse operator per
(n_{c},l)
block in the master equation. From Eqs.
92
,
C.1
and
70
,

\left\langle\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d}^{\prime},l}\middle|{\hat{G}}(z)\middle|\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d},l}\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}rr^{2}\frac{R_{n_{d},2l}(r)R_{n_{d}^{\prime},2l}(r)}{z-1/(8r^{6})}.

(95)

In the limit
\text{Im}(z)\rightarrow 0^{+}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d}^{\prime},l}\middle|{\hat{G}}(z)\middle|\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d},l}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}rr^{2}R_{n_{d},2l}(r)R_{n_{d}^{\prime},2l}(r)\left(\mathcal{P}\frac{1}{z-1/(8r^{6})}-i\pi\delta\left(z-\frac{1}{8r^{6}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathcal{P}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}rr^{2}\frac{R_{n_{d},2l}(r)R_{n_{d}^{\prime},2l}(r)}{z-1/(8r^{6})}-i\frac{4\pi r_{c}^{9}}{3}R_{n_{d},2l}(r_{c})R_{n_{d}^{\prime},2l}(r_{c}),

\displaystyle r_{c}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}z^{1/6}},

(96)

where
\mathcal{P}
stands for the Cauchy principal value. For given
n_{c}
and
l
, this allows us to rewrite
{\hat{G}}(z)={\hat{G}}_{r}(z)+i{\hat{G}}_{i}(z)
where
{\hat{G}}_{r}=\text{Re}({\hat{G}}(z))
and
{\hat{G}}_{i}=\text{Im}({\hat{G}}(z))
are both Hermitian, with

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}_{r}(z)=

\displaystyle~\sum_{n_{d},n_{d}^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hbox{{d}}rr^{2}\frac{R_{n_{d},2l}(r)R_{n_{d}^{\prime},2l}(r)}{z-1/(8r^{6})}\left|\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d}^{\prime},l}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d},l}\right|,

(97)

\displaystyle{\hat{G}}_{i}(z)=

\displaystyle~\alpha_{n_{c},l}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|,

\displaystyle\alpha_{n_{c},l}=

\displaystyle~-\frac{4\pi r_{c}^{9}}{3}\sum_{n_{d}}R_{n_{d},l}^{2}(r_{c}),

\displaystyle\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{n_{d}}R_{n_{d},l}^{2}(r_{c})}}\sum_{n_{d}}R_{n_{d},l}(r_{c})\left|\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d},l}\right\rangle.

(98)

where the sums over
n_{d}
run from
0
to
n_{d,max}=n_{max}-n_{c}-2l
. By design,
{\hat{G}}
has an inverse
{\hat{G}}^{-1}=z-\hat{\Lambda}/V_{0}+i\hat{\Gamma}/V_{0}
such that, still in this (
n_{c},l
) subspace,

V_{0}{\hat{G}}^{-1}{\hat{G}}=V_{0}\hat{1}=(V_{0}z-\hat{\Lambda}){\hat{G}}_{r}-\hat{\Gamma}{\hat{G}}_{i}+i\left((V_{0}z-\hat{\Lambda}){\hat{G}}_{i}+\hat{\Gamma}{\hat{G}}_{r}\right),

(99)

Defining the projector
{\hat{P}}_{\perp}=\hat{1}-\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|
, the imaginary part of this equation gives
\hat{\Gamma}{\hat{G}}_{r}{\hat{P}}_{\perp}=0
. As
\det({\hat{G}})=\det({\hat{G}}_{r})+i\alpha_{n_{c},l}\det({\hat{P}}_{\perp}{\hat{G}}_{r}{\hat{P}}_{\perp})\neq 0
,
\text{rank}({\hat{G}}_{r}{\hat{P}}_{\perp})=\text{Tr}({\hat{P}}_{\perp})=\text{Tr}(\hat{1})-1
. Since
\hat{\Gamma}
annihilates a subspace of dimension
\text{Tr}(\hat{1})-1
, it can be written as
\hat{\Gamma}=\Gamma_{n_{c},l}\left|\zeta_{n_{c},l}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\zeta_{n_{c},l}\right|
where
{\hat{P}}_{\perp}{\hat{G}}_{r}\left|\zeta_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle=0
, which concludes the proof.

When
{\hat{G}}_{r}(z)
is invertible, which is the case except for a finite number of
z
values,

\left|\zeta_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle=\frac{{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-1}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-2}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle}}.

(100)

To find the decay rate
\Gamma_{n_{c},l}
, as well as
\hat{\Lambda}
, we can use the real and imaginary parts of Eq.
99
to express
\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{V_{0}z-\hat{\Lambda}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle
in two different ways:

\displaystyle\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{V_{0}z-\hat{\Lambda}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle~\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\middle|V_{0}{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-1}+\hat{\Gamma}{\hat{G}}_{i}{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-1}\middle|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle=V_{0}\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-1}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle+\alpha_{n_{c},l}\Gamma_{n_{c},l}\frac{\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-1}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle^{3}}{\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-2}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{V_{0}z-\hat{\Lambda}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle~-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n_{c},l}}\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{\hat{\Gamma}{\hat{G}}_{r}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle=-\frac{\Gamma_{n_{c},l}}{\alpha_{n_{c},l}}\frac{\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-1}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-2}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\Gamma_{n_{c},l}=

\displaystyle~-V_{0}\frac{\alpha_{l}\left\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}\right|{{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-2}}\left|\chi_{n_{c},l}\right\rangle}{1+\alpha_{l}^{2}\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}|{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-1}|\chi_{n_{c},l}\rangle^{2}}

(101)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hat{\Lambda}=

\displaystyle~V_{0}(z-{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-1})-\alpha_{n_{c},l}\langle\chi_{n_{c},l}|{\hat{G}}_{r}^{-1}|\chi_{n_{c},l}\rangle\hat{\Gamma}.

(102)

Appendix D
Experimental Protocol

This appendix presents additional details of our experimental protocol. More information can be found in references
Vaneecloo et al. 2022
and
Covolo et al. 2025
.

We prepare a small cloud of cold
87
Rb atoms in
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
through a sequence of cooling, transport and loading into a crossed dipole trap (CDT), as described in detail in Ref.
Vaneecloo et al. 2022
. Within the CDT, we find
N\approx 800
atoms at a temperature of
3\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro K}
, distributed with a Gaussian profile of rms radius
\sigma=((2\sigma_{\rho}^{2}+\sigma_{z}^{2})/3)^{1/2}=$5.20(2)\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
, where
\sigma_{\rho}=$5.62(2)\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
is the radius in the plane of the CDT beams and
\sigma_{z}=$4.22(2)\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
is the radius along the orthogonal axis. The cloud is coupled to the Gaussian optical mode of a single-ended running-wave medium-finesse cavity with a waist
w=$21\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
and a field decay rate
\kappa=2\pi\times$2.89\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
.
The relevant atomic states in the system are the ground state
g:\{5S_{1/2},F=1,m_{F}=1\}
, the Rydberg state
r:\{nS_{1/2},J=1/2,m_{J}=1/2,I=3/2,m_{I}=3/2\}
where
n\in\{80,95,109\}
, and the intermediate state
e:\{5P_{1/2},F=2,m_{F}=2\}
which has a dipole decay rate of
\gamma=2\pi\times$2.87\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
.

The main part of the experimental protocol, where Rydberg blockade imperfections play a major role, consists in driving a two-photon transition from
g
to
r
with a pair of laser beams. As described in the main text, for perfect Rydberg blockade the system should oscillate between the ground state
\left|G\right\rangle
and the singly-excited symmetric Rydberg state
\left|R\right\rangle
with a collective Rabi frequency
\Omega=\sqrt{N_{0}}\Omega_{1}\Omega_{2}/2\Delta
, where
N_{0}=N/(1+4\sigma_{\rho}^{2}/w^{2})\sim 620
is the effective number of atoms coupled to the cavity mode,
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are the respective Rabi frequencies on the
g-e
and
e-r
transitions, and
\Delta=-2\pi\times$500\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
is the detuning from the state
e
. Blockade imperfections create a leakage into a large manifold of asymmetric Rydberg states. This drive sequence lasts
t_{d}=$500\text{\,}\mathrm{ns}$
, shaped by the temporal profile of
\Omega_{1}
encompassed by
\Omega_{2}
as shown on the left graph of Fig.
3
(c) in the main text. The rotation angle
\Omega t_{d}
of the qubit is controlled by setting
\Omega_{2}=2\pi\times$7.8(1)\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
and adjusting
\Omega_{1}
between 0 and
2\pi\times$22\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
.

Once the driving sequence is completed, we perform one of the two following measurements:

•

G/not(G): To detect if the qubit is in
\left|G\right\rangle
, we follow the protocol described in detail in Ref.
Vaneecloo et al. 2022
and measure the transmission of a weak probe beam, resonant with the cavity and with the
g-e
transition, in presence of a control beam resonantly coupling
e
with the Rydberg state
r^{\prime}=78S_{1/2}
(Fig.
3
(b) in the main text).

When the qubit is in
\left|G\right\rangle
, the control beam opens an electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT) window for the probe photons, transmitted through the cloud as dark Rydberg polaritons. Keeping the probe power sufficiently low to avoid Rydberg blockade between these polaritons, we then detect a constant photon flux
\phi_{G}
at the output of the cavity. The probability to detect
n
photon during an integration time
t_{i}
is then given by a Poissonian law
P_{G}(n)=\mathcal{P}(n,t_{i}\phi_{G})=e^{-t_{i}\phi_{G}}(t_{i}\phi_{G})^{n}/n!
.

On the other hand, when the cloud contains a Rydberg excitation, the blockade between
r
and
r^{\prime}
suppresses the EIT and the transmitted photon flux drops to a residual value
\phi_{r}
. Eventually, the Rydberg excitation decays and the photon flux
\phi_{G}
is restored. As shown in Ref.
Vaneecloo et al. 2022
appendix C, the probability to detect
n
photons during a time
t_{i}
, accounting for the finite Rydberg lifetime
\tau_{r}
, is then

P_{R}(n)=\mathcal{P}(n,t_{i}\phi_{r})e^{-t_{i}/\tau_{r}}+\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\mathcal{P}(n,t\phi_{r}+(t_{i}-t)\phi_{G})e^{-t/\tau_{r}}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\tau_{r}}

(103)

In practice, the detection step begins
5.01\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
after the driving sequence. For given experimental settings, we reconstruct the distribution
P(n)
of photon counts integrated during
t_{i}=$45\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$
, fit it with the function
\eta_{r}P_{r}(n)+(1-\eta_{r})P_{G}(n)
, and retrieve the Rydberg population
\eta_{r}=1-\text{Tr}({\hat{\rho}}\left|G\middle\rangle\middle\langle G\right|)
from this fit, together with
\phi_{r}
. The best-fitted values
\eta_{r}
are plotted in Fig.
3
(b) in the main text, with a correction factor 1.012 accounting for the decay during the
5.01\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
between the drive pulse and the beginning of the detection.

•

R/not(R): To estimate the population in the qubit state
\left|R\right\rangle
, we increase the Rabi frequency
\Omega_{m}
of the mapping beam, resonant with the transition
e-r
, from
0
to
2\pi\times$7.4(1)\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
(see cyan arrows and curve in Fig.
3
(c)). As detailed in
Magro et al. 2023
, the strong collective coupling
g=2\pi\times$9.70(6)\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
between the atoms and the cavity mode (
g\gg\gamma,\kappa
) enables an efficient mapping of
\left|R\right\rangle
to an intracavity photon, which is then detected by a single-photon detector (SPD) at the cavity output.

Appendix E
Numerical simulations

This Appendix provides details about the numerical simulations of the experimental results in Fig.
3
. We perform
2\times 3\times 37
simulation runs corresponding to two types of measurements (G/not(G) and R/not(R)), three Rydberg states (80S, 95S and 109S), and 37 different values of the Rabi frequency
\Omega_{1}
on the lower branch of the two-photon transition, ranging from 0 to
2\pi\times$22\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
. We neglect the weak ellipticity of our cloud and, to reduce the Hilbert space, consider it as spherically symmetric with a Gaussian r.m.s radius
\sigma
defined in Appendix
D
. We verified that this assumption has little effect on the precision of the simulations given our experimental uncertainties, but it makes them much faster. We adapt the basis size to the chosen Rydberg state, making it larger when the blockade is weaker and asymmetric states are populated faster: we set
n_{max}=(3,6,8)
for the states
(109,95,80)S_{\textbullet 1/2}
, respectively. The basis then contains
n_{max}+1
singly-excited states
\left|\psi_{n}\right\rangle
and

\sum_{n=0}^{n_{max}}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor n_{max}/2\rfloor}(n-2l+1)=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n_{max}}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n_{max}}{2}\right\rfloor+2\right)\left(n_{max}-\frac{4}{3}\left\lfloor\frac{n_{max}}{2}\right\rfloor+\frac{1}{2}\right)

(104)

doubly-excited states
\left|\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d},l}\right\rangle
with
n_{c}+n_{d}+2l=n\leq n_{max}
. The integration of the master equation is performed on a desktop computer using the Python package QuTiP
Johansson et al. 2013
. The solver supports time-dependent Rabi frequencies, with temporal profiles obtained by fitting the experimental pulses to theoretical curves (see Fig.
3
(c) in the main text). To keep the Lamb-shifted interaction matrix
\Lambda
and the decay matrix
\Gamma
time-independent, we set the effective energy to
z_{e}=z_{0}
. We simulate the total Rydberg population (G/non(G) measurement) and the number of extracted photons (R/not(R) measurement) with two slightly different versions of the master equation, described below.

E.1
G/not(G) measurement: simulating the total Rydberg population

For the G/not(G) measurement, which estimates the probability for at least one Rydberg excitation to be present in the cloud, we simulate only the driving part of the experimental protocol. Errors occurring during the EIT probing, related to the finite Rydberg lifetime and to the residual transmission in the not(G) case, are accounted for via a semiclassical model described in Appendix
D
and in Ref.
Vaneecloo et al. 2022

As the two-photon driving, with an effective time-dependent Rabi frequency
\Omega=\sqrt{N_{0}}\Omega_{1}\Omega_{2}/2\Delta

\left|e\right\rangle

\left|G\right\rangle
, the singly- and doubly-excited manifolds
\{\left|\psi_{n}\right\rangle\}
and
\{\left|\Psi_{n_{c},n_{d},l}\right\rangle\}
with
n_{c}+n_{d}+2l=n\leq n_{max}
and the doubly-excited “continuum” state
\left|C\right\rangle
populated by the interactions), to which we add a singly-excited “continuum” state
\left|c\right\rangle
to account for thermal dephasing as explained below.

In addition to the laser driving and to the Rydberg interactions, described by the operators
{\hat{S}}
,
\hat{\Lambda}
and
\hat{\Gamma}
, the simulations include two experimental imperfections. We neglect the
\sim$100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$
lifetime of Rydberg excitations, much longer than the experimental timescale
\sim$2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$
, but we include the pure dephasing rate
\gamma_{r}=2\pi\times$40\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
between the Rydberg manifold and the ground state, measured in Ref.
Magro et al. 2023
. In addition, the finite temperature
T
of the cloud leads to a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution of atomic velocities. This translates into a Gaussian distribution
{\textrm{e}}^{-\omega^{2}/(2\omega_{0}^{2})}/\sqrt{2\pi\omega_{0}^{2}}
of Doppler shifts
\omega
with a width
\omega_{0}=k\sqrt{k_{B}T/m}=2\pi\times$57\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
where
k
is the spin wave vector,
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant and
m
is the atomic mass. This inhomogeneous dephasing is negligible in the doubly-excited manifold, where interaction-induced dephasing dominates, but it is included as a correction for singly-excited states. In Ref.
Covolo et al. 2025
, we have shown that this non-Markovian process can be accurately simulated by harmonically coupling a ladder of singly-excited Dicke states
\left|\phi_{n}\right\rangle
with
\left|\phi_{0}\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle
, constructed through successive applications of a displacement Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}_{d}=\omega_{0}\sum_{n}\sqrt{n}\left|\phi_{n-1}\right\rangle\left\langle\phi_{n}\right|+\text{h.c.}
. In principle, as the interaction-induced and the temperature-induced dephasing processes are uncorrelated, the asymmetric states
\left|\phi_{n>0}\right\rangle
should be maximally orthogonal to the states
\left|\psi_{n>0}\right\rangle
:
\forall(n,n^{\prime})>0,\;|\langle\phi_{n}|\psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle|^{2}\sim 1/N
. As thermal dephasing is a small correction to our dynamics, to avoid increasing the Hilbert space we form the ladder of states
\{\left|\phi_{n>0}\right\rangle\}
via a discrete Fourier transform
\left|\phi_{n}\right\rangle=\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n_{max}}{\textrm{e}}^{2i\pi nn^{\prime}/n_{max}}\left|\psi_{n^{\prime}}\right\rangle/\sqrt{n_{max}}
, which ensures
\forall(n,n^{\prime})>0,\;|\langle\phi_{n}|\psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle|^{2}=1/n_{max}
. As shown in Ref.
Covolo et al. 2025
, the last state
\left|\phi_{n_{max}}\right\rangle
of the ladder decays in a singly-excited continuum
\left|c\right\rangle
with a rate
\gamma_{T}=\omega_{0}(2/\pi)^{(-1)^{n_{max}}/2}n_{max}!!/(n_{max}-1)!!
.

E.2
R/not(R) measurement: mapping to a photon

For the R/not(R) measurement, the master equation includes additional terms describing the mapping of the symmetric state
\left|R\right\rangle
onto a photon and the decay of the latter out of the cavity.

The cavity mode, described by the photon annihilation operator
{\hat{a}}
, is strongly coupled to symmetric excitations in the intermediate state, described by the annihilation operator
{\hat{T}}=\int\hbox{{d}}^{3}{\bf r}\,\sqrt{\mu({\bf r})}{\hat{\sigma}}_{ge}({\bf r})
analogous to
{\hat{S}}
, via the Hamiltonian term
g({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}{\hat{T}}+{\hat{T}^{\dagger}}{\hat{a}})
where
g=2\pi\times$9.70(6)\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
. Considering that the system contains two excitations at most, and writing
\left|G\right\rangle
the collective ground state of the whole system, the conjugates of these operators generate the states
\left|1\right\rangle={\hat{a}^{\dagger}}\left|G\right\rangle
,
\left|2\right\rangle=({\hat{a}^{\dagger}})^{2}\left|G\right\rangle/\sqrt{2}
,
\left|e\right\rangle={\hat{T}^{\dagger}}\left|G\right\rangle
,
\left|E\right\rangle=({\hat{T}^{\dagger}})^{2}\left|G\right\rangle/\sqrt{2}
,
\left|e1\right\rangle={\hat{T}^{\dagger}}{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}\left|G\right\rangle
,
\{\left|\psi_{n}e\right\rangle={\hat{T}^{\dagger}}\left|\psi_{n}\right\rangle\}
, and
\{\left|\psi_{n}1\right\rangle={\hat{a}^{\dagger}}\left|\psi_{n}\right\rangle\}
. In this basis,

\displaystyle{\hat{S}}=

\displaystyle\left|G\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{0}\right|+\sum_{\bf k}S_{n;n_{d},l}\left|\psi_{n}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\Psi_{\bf k}\right|+\left|1\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{0}1\right|+\left|e\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{0}e\right|,

\displaystyle\hat{n}_{r}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\left(\left|\psi_{n}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{n}\right|+\left|\psi_{n}e\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{n}e\right|+\left|\psi_{n}1\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{n}1\right|\right)+2\sum_{\bf k}\left|\Psi_{\bf k}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\Psi_{\bf k}\right|+\left|c\middle\rangle\middle\langle c\right|+2\left|C\middle\rangle\middle\langle C\right|,

\displaystyle{\hat{a}}=

\displaystyle\left|G\middle\rangle\middle\langle 1\right|+\left|e\middle\rangle\middle\langle e1\right|+\sqrt{2}\left|1\middle\rangle\middle\langle 2\right|+\sum_{n}\left|\psi_{n}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{n}1\right|,

(106)

\displaystyle{\hat{T}}=

\displaystyle\left|G\middle\rangle\middle\langle e\right|+\left|1\middle\rangle\middle\langle e1\right|+\sqrt{2}\left|e\middle\rangle\middle\langle E\right|+\sum_{n}\left|\psi_{n}\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{n}e\right|.

(107)

Other operators in the driving part of the master equation (Eq.
105
) remain unchanged. In particular, we neglect the thermal dephasing of the states containing an intermediate or a photonic excitation, where the decay is dominated respectively by the atomic dipole decay rate
\gamma=2\pi\times$2.87\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
and the field decay rate
\kappa=2\pi\times$2.89\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
. These decays translate into additional terms
2\gamma\mathcal{L}[{\hat{T}}]({\hat{\rho}})+2\kappa\mathcal{L}[{\hat{a}}]({\hat{\rho}})
in the master equation.

The mapping laser, with a Rabi frequency
\Omega_{m}
increasing from
0
to
2\pi\times$7.4(1)\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
, converts Rydberg excitations into intermediate-state ones via the Hamiltonian term
(\Omega_{m}{\hat{Q}}+\Omega_{m}^{*}{\hat{Q}^{\dagger}})/2
where
{\hat{Q}}=\int\hbox{{d}}^{3}{\bf r}{\hat{\sigma}}_{er}({\bf r})
(see cyan arrows and curve in Fig.
3
(c)). In principle,
{\hat{Q}}
generates the states
{\hat{Q}}\left|\psi_{n}\right\rangle
,
{\hat{Q}}\left|\Psi_{\bf k}\right\rangle/\sqrt{2}
and

{\hat{Q}}^{2}\left|\Psi_{\bf k}\right\rangle/2
. This doubles the basis size quite uselessly, these states being short-lived and weakly populated. Instead, we keep only the states defined above, where the intermediate excitation is symmetric, to describe the superatom-to-photon mapping without assuming its adiabaticity, and treat the other states generated by
{\hat{Q}}
as decay channels. For this, we rewrite
{\hat{Q}}={\hat{T}^{\dagger}}{\hat{S}}+{\hat{Q}}_{\perp}
where
{\hat{T}^{\dagger}}{\hat{S}}
converts a symmetric Rydberg excitation into its intermediate counterpart, while
{\hat{Q}}_{\perp}
does the same for asymmetric excitations. We consider that the latter lie outside of our Hilbert space, described by a projector
{\hat{P}}
, and that in absence of laser coupling their evolution is described by a non-Hermitian decay Hamiltonian
-i\gamma
. The readout term
\Omega_{m}{\hat{Q}}/2+\text{h.c.}
is now separated in two parts:
\Omega_{m}{\hat{T}^{\dagger}}{\hat{S}}/2+\text{h.c.}
is kept in the Hamiltonian, while
\Omega_{m}{\hat{Q}}_{\perp}/2+\text{h.c.}
is converted, using the resolvent formalism
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 1998
, into a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian

{\hat{P}}\frac{\Omega_{m}{\hat{Q}}_{\perp}+\Omega_{m}^{*}{\hat{Q}^{\dagger}}_{\perp}}{2}\frac{1}{z+i\gamma}\frac{\Omega_{m}{\hat{Q}}_{\perp}+\Omega_{m}^{*}{\hat{Q}^{\dagger}}_{\perp}}{2}{\hat{P}}\approx-i\frac{|\Omega_{m}|^{2}}{4\gamma}{\hat{P}}{\hat{Q}^{\dagger}}_{\perp}{\hat{Q}}_{\perp}{\hat{P}}=-i\frac{|\Omega_{m}|^{2}}{4\gamma}\left(\hat{n}_{r}-{\hat{S}^{\dagger}}{\hat{S}}\right)

(108)

where we approximated the effective energy
z
by
0
for a resonant readout, and used
{\hat{P}}{\hat{Q}^{\dagger}}{\hat{Q}}{\hat{P}}=\hat{n}_{r}
and
{\hat{P}}{\hat{Q}^{\dagger}}{\hat{T}^{\dagger}}{\hat{S}}{\hat{P}}={\hat{S}^{\dagger}}{\hat{S}}
. As it is purely anti-Hermitian, it translates into decay terms in the master equation, with a rate
|\Omega_{m}|^{2}/(4\gamma)
. The different decay channels should in principle correspond to spatially localized states but, for simplicity, we use our basis states and consider that the states containing two Rydberg excitations decay in
\left|c\right\rangle
.

The master equation governing the whole process, describing both the driving and the superatom-to-photon mapping, becomes
\dot{{\hat{\rho}}}=\mathcal{Z}_{D}({\hat{\rho}})+\mathcal{Z}_{M}({\hat{\rho}})
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{M}({\hat{\rho}})=

\displaystyle~-i\left[\frac{\Omega_{m}}{2}{\hat{T}^{\dagger}}{\hat{S}}+g{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}{\hat{T}}+\text{h.c.},{\hat{\rho}}\right]+2\gamma\mathcal{L}[{\hat{T}}]({\hat{\rho}})+2\kappa\mathcal{L}[{\hat{a}}]({\hat{\rho}})

\displaystyle~+2\frac{|\Omega_{m}|^{2}}{4\gamma}\left(\sum_{n>0}\mathcal{L}[\left|G\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{n}\right|+\left|e\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{n}e\right|+\left|1\middle\rangle\middle\langle\psi_{n}1\right|]({\hat{\rho}})+\mathcal{L}[\left|G\middle\rangle\middle\langle c\right|]({\hat{\rho}})+2\mathcal{L}[\left|c\middle\rangle\middle\langle C\right|]({\hat{\rho}})\right)

\displaystyle~+2\frac{|\Omega_{m}|^{2}}{4\gamma}\sum_{{\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime}}(2\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime}}-\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}S_{n;n_{d},l}S_{n;n_{d}^{\prime},l^{\prime}})\mathcal{D}[\left|c\middle\rangle\middle\langle\Psi_{\bf k}\right|,\left|c\middle\rangle\middle\langle\Psi_{\bf k^{\prime}}\right|]({\hat{\rho}}).

(109)

When the driving and the mapping laser pulses are well separated in time, which is the case in our experiment (see Fig.
3
(c)), the asymmetric states play no role in the mapping process, and the decay terms on the second and third lines of the above equation can be neglected to speed up numerics.

The number of emitted photons is given by
n_{phot}=2\kappa_{0}\int\text{Tr}({\hat{a}^{\dagger}}{\hat{a}}{\hat{\rho}}(t))\hbox{{d}}t
where
\kappa_{0}=2\pi\times$2.58\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
is the measured leakage rate of the cavity field through the input/output coupler. The simulation thus accounts for losses arising from the finite cooperativity, the imperfectly adiabatic mapping, and the cavity extraction efficiency. An additional efficiency factor
\eta_{mm}=0.82
, included in the right plot in Fig.
3
(c), can be attributed to the imperfect mode-matching of the off-resonant drive beam
1
and to wavefront inhomogeneities of the drive beam
2
.
