Covid- 19 and gender inequality: A critique of the working woman's struggle
Authors: Mais Qutami, Yousef Hamdan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere has been a dramatic change in everyday life due to COVID-19 around the world. The lockdowns, closure of schools and universities, and the loss of jobs has placed a lot of pressure on the family, particularly women. One of the main outcomes of the pandemic in Jordan has been the increase in the workload of women outside and inside the home. Despite the efforts made to empower women throughout the years, they continue to struggle and gender inequality remains overlooked and even normalized in Jordanian society. The aim of the study is to unfold the way Covid- 19 has disrupted women's lives and made gender equality less achievable, and explore the challenges they have faced in terms of the added paid and unpaid work they have been forced to do during the pandemic and to this end, in-depth interviews were carried out with 20 working mothers who were selected using purposive sampling. The study is largely informed by feminist standpoint theory whose main concern is the female voice and making it a valuable instrument in the process of knowledge production. The findings show that most women suffered from traditionally prescribed gender roles imposed by society, pressure and discrimination at work, and a lack of understanding and empathy at home and the workplace. Very few shared responsibilities with their husbands and their burdens with coworkers and employers, and an even smaller number was able to use the pandemic to their advantage and found ways to regain female power and self-assertion. The findings indicate that serious changes in policies need to be made to protect women's rights at work and in the domestic world, and the institution of marriage reconstructed within the Jordanian imaginary, and gender roles redefined.Keywords: Covid- 19, Gender roles, Feminist standpoint theory, Jordan, Knowledge production

Introduction

Women have always been subject to unfair expectations by the patriarchal system governing their lives long before Covid- 19. They have taken on multiple responsibilities to meet the demands of their spouses, children, and work outside the home. It is well-known that during global crises of famine, war, and disease outbreak, women's burdens tend to increase and worsen adding to their pre-disaster “gendered burdens” [1]. United Nations studies conducted on Jordanian women's conditions during the pandemic reflect the exacerbating inequalities within society. One study shows that the unpaid work time for women who are married and have children at school age has increased and been estimated to be up to “18–24 h per week” and those who are employed their total workload became 80–85 h per week. On the other hand, the impact on men's paid and unpaid work time is not as significant as it has risen to 47 h per week at most [2]; p. 23).

Several studies have been conducted on the way the pandemic has been handled in Jordan and its impact in terms of employment opportunities, domestic violence, policies, emergency lockdowns, and education [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. The existing research, however, reveals a lack of qualitative studies on gender inequality and women's frustrations and suffering while juggling work and house responsibilities during the pandemic in Jordan [10]. The aim of the current study is to examine the challenges Jordanian working mothers have faced during the pandemic and raise awareness about their current dilemma. It highlights the impact of traditional gender constructions that have contributed to the worsening of gender inequality in Jordan and led to more female marginalization at home and the workplace. In producing knowledge about women, feminist standpoint theory is utilized to uncover the ways power and oppression operate within a patriarchal society and bring the female voice to the forefront so that their frustrations and concerns are addressed.

Theoretical background

Women in the past had been excluded from the process of knowledge production, and so what was written was largely by men, about men, and for men [11]; p.18). The problem lies in the fact that mainstream science assumed that its representations of reality are reliable and the researcher is an objective knower who merely reflects the conclusions arrived at by the study. This traditional approach to science did not acknowledge the way context and the knower's perspectives influenced the kind of knowledge produced. Feminist standpoint theorists reject this traditional approach as they believe that the researcher is never a “detached knower” and science is certainly not “value-free” [12]. They explain that this is due to the fact that certain biases naturally appear throughout the research process as it is the researcher who determines which questions are asked or not asked, and points (de)emphasized, published or circulated which eventually leads to the privileging of certain produced knowledge over other possible ones [13]. Therefore, many feminists were dissatisfied with the knowledge produced by men as it reflected their perspectives which did not necessarily take into account women's perceptions and view of the world, yet their knowledge was assumed to be universal. This is precisely what feminist standpoint theorists objected to as they believe in the plurality of situated standpoints rather than any single approach that homogenizes the female experience or any other [14].

Feminists argued that the female experience and perspective has been misrepresented by the mainstream because they have been addressed and interpreted by dominant male conceptualization, and so their experiences were introduced mainly through a lens and language developed by white men [11]. Smith [11] explains that women have a “bifurcated consciousness” as they live through their experiences on daily basis but only get to perceive and interpret them within a male conceptual framework. This has often led to distorted truth about women since they have been unable to express their own concerns using their own language and theorization. Therefore, standpoint theorists do not base their research on assumptions or hypotheses formerly set but begin their scientific inquiry from the lived experience of marginalized groups who do not represent the voices of the mainstream or patriarchy [15]. Harding states:

The point here is not that every poor or otherwise marginalized person already can or does ‘see the truth,’ but rather that discourses oppositional to the dominant ones can arise as marginalized groups begin to articulate their histories, needs, and desires ‘for themselves’ instead of only in the ways encouraged by their ‘masters’ favored conceptual frameworks (pp. 445–6).

This epistemological approach was developed as an alternative to knowledge produced from the position of privilege within racist, sexist, or colonialist discourses. The social situation of privileged groups is perceived scientifically and epistemologically as a disadvantage in the process of knowledge production as these individuals fail to critically interrogate their privileged positions and draw insight regardless of the good intentions they may have [16]. In other words, a privileged group who has not experienced oppression may not be able to fully grasp the complexity of another's suffering. Accordingly, in analyzing the situation, the privileged may be blind to certain aspects of the injustice inflicted upon the others because they have never had to go through anything similar and their accounts of reality would not be completely authentic or reflect comprehensive knowledge. The current study does not claim that a female can always produce better knowledge of other female lives but suggests that a feminist researcher invested in the lives of marginalized women, their empowerment, and the betterment of their conditions is more likely to be capable of producing objective knowledge that does not include distorted truth about them and the injustices they encounter. This is because of the lack of interest in maintaining the status quo or protecting the privileged position from which a male would speak.

Method

Because the study was designed to produce authentic knowledge and stemmed from a recognition of the necessity of reflecting the female experience through narratives of their own making, qualitative research in the form of in-depth interviews was found the most appropriate method to achieve the desired goal in mind. This interview-based qualitative study was carried out to fill the gap in the literature on Jordan that is highly quantitative (e.g. Refs. [5,17,18].; [19]. The qualitative method is preferable to explore, in an in-depth manner, conditions and personal matters that are unique to the experiences of the researched subjects rather than generate a general understanding of larger groups of people while many critical aspects may never be scrutinized or addressed. As such, a study based on interviews creates an opportunity to hear the voices of marginalized groups in society that may not be heard elsewhere [20]. In examining the female struggle and her multiplying responsibilities during the pandemic with the aim to make it visible to society, the researcher uses feminist standpoint theory as it provides the necessary tools to foreground the challenges Jordanian women face and give their concerns the legitimacy the patriarchal system denies and invalidates because they disrupt the status quo. This study confirms the idea that having female subjects examined by a female researcher is epistemically advantageous since women are able to interpret their world through their own perspective and their own conceptual framework. They are given the space and comfort from which they can speak freely without doubting themselves or worrying about the underlying politics that govern gender relations. However, if a male were to conduct such research, responses may have taken a different direction since most managers and those in power in Jordan are men, and women would have felt intimidated, judged, and even anxious to share their real experience in fear of losing their jobs. In other words, the gender of the researcher would have affected the nature of their responses and female competition and the attempt to present a positive female image would have come into play as some would represent themselves as capable and flexible at dealing with the crisis and the workload and would have avoided projecting any vulnerabilities again to protect their positions at work. As a result, the outcome would have been a distorted version of the truth shaped by fear and personal consideration to the proper approach to be adopted in handling the existing gender politics.

Sampling

Because the study deals with working mothers' struggle, it was important that the selected participants were well-established in their careers and marriage so that the focus remains on the challenges presented by the pandemic and not other factors that may change their lives dramatically such as being recently hired or newly married or having a newborn. Studies show that purposive sampling has been highly effective in documenting events or experiences that not everyone undergoes or witnesses, including private events that many in the community may not even be aware of [21,22]. Purposive sampling was used in this study as random sampling is not always efficient and many individuals are unwilling to participate and may not even take the subject seriously especially that Jordan is a patriarchal society ([21]; [22]. In this case, many Jordanian women have internalized patriarchal values over time, and so would not be able to understand the significance of the current study or provide insightful information as they are accustomed to traditional gender roles and inequality. Accordingly, and for the purpose of this study, educated working women who showed an understanding of the goals of the study and women's rights and who met the criteria were selected for the interview process. Interviews were conducted with 25 women who were aged (30–45) years old. They were recruited through Facebook groups that included academics at various public and private universities in Jordan and non – academics as well. Their educational backgrounds varied as some had a bachelor's degree, while others had their master's or doctorate degree. They also belonged to different fields such as accounting, education, international relations, medicine, business, and administration. They all had (1–3) children who were between the ages of (5–12) and did not have maids at home. This age group specifically is significant as the children still needed parental supervision and help with online learning during Covid- 19. Generally, they all belonged to the middle class with an average annual income ranging between USD 31.700–35.200 for an average household of 5 members and their children went to private schools [23]. While the sample does not reflect the entire population, it represents real life experiences of a group of women that is usually understudied. The sample size was chosen based on recommendations made by scholars in the fields of social sciences, psychology, and healthcare who used the term “theoretical saturation” that suggests that once no further themes emerged in participant responses, that is the point of saturation at which the researcher should stop searching for participants as responses get repetitive after that [24]; pp. 441–443). It was concluded that ‘For most research enterprises […] in which the aim is to understand common perceptions and experiences among a group of relatively homogeneous individuals, twelve interviews should suffice’ [25]; p. 79). Some studies found that saturation occurred after 6 interviews (e.g. Refs. [26,27]. Another got to that point at a higher number of 17 interviews [28]. Accordingly, there is no specific saturation point that can be generalized to apply to all fields but may vary depending on the subject of the research and the research questions. Based on these conclusions and for the purpose of this study, 25 participants was the number of women who were reachable and accepted to be part of the study, in spite of the challenges presented by the pandemic, and who actually met the criteria set by the researcher.

Interviews

The interviews were mostly conducted in English while some used codeswitching, shifting back and forth between English and Arabic. They were 25 and held by the researcher, sometimes face to face when restrictions and lockdowns were reduced, and many took place through video conferencing, WhatsApp voice messages, and phone calls. These interviews lasted approximately 3 h as many shared similar experiences and needed to vent because of the little support and understanding they have been receiving during these critical times. Three of the participants had initially expressed an interest in being part of the study but were under a lot of pressure and could not continue with us due to their responsibilities during covid- 19. Two other women were part of the conversation at the beginning but when personal issues were raised, they did not want to reveal any more about their family life and marriage, and so decided to leave the conversation. The interviews were semi-structured and guided to focus on gender inequality and ensure that each participant responded to the research questions. The interviews were recorded and transcribed by the researcher and notes were taken as well. The researcher explored gender inequalities and issues arising during Covid- 19 from the perspective of working mothers. Results and discussion

Traditionally, it was assumed that scientists needed to maintain emotional distance toward their subjects to avoid errors, however, this view was refuted later by Refs. [29,30] who believe that emotional distance is not the solution as it may have epistemic defects. That is, a “feeling for the [individual] organism” may help a scientist gain further insight into the plight of the researched and get more sensitized to critical data which total detachment would have led to the loss of valuable information and serious conclusions [29,30]. This is why feminist standpoint theory urges researchers to fully engage in the conversation with the subjects of the study. It prioritizes building trust rather than focusing on numbers and statistics and maintaining distance like empiricists who do not directly communicate with the researched on any personal level. They strongly believe that when trust is built, it contributes to the generation of authentic evidence and further access to individuals’ private lives. This process of connecting with the subjects helps unpack crucial issues that may have gone unnoticed historically [31].

The current study addresses the existing gap in the body of research about Jordanian working mothers' frustrations and challenges during the pandemic. Most conducted studies on Jordan are empirical, and focused on statistics, but do not capture the essence of working mothers’ struggle. In most, the empiricist approach was applied, and emotional distance with the subjects was maintained. By contrast, and in line with feminist standpoint theory, the current study took shape out of personal interaction and deep engagement with the female participants in addition to a genuine interest in their well-being and betterment of their lives. The researched subjects were able to connect with the researcher during the course of the study and knowing she herself is a working mother gave them a sense of relief because they shared a common interest. That is, they are both part of this struggle of reconstructing the female image in Jordan and challenging traditional stereotypes of femininity and masculinity.

The stereotypical gender roles and conventional constructions of femininity and masculinity are important within the Jordanian context, as the findings reveal, because of the negative impact they have on an individual's life, whether male or female. Due to traditional stereotypes, a man who helps his wife or does house chores is misrepresented and even devalued. He is either seen as extraordinary and worthy of respect, or as feminine and not “man” enough. Women, however, are merely viewed as doing their job as housewives when they work at home and are not admired for it, but when they work outside the house, they are often perceived negatively as not doing enough for their home and children and are made to feel guilty about it [32]; p. 492). These rigid cultural constructions of gender by which women are suffocated impede any progress made or attempt at gaining independence for women in the world at a time they should be appreciated and not blamed for all the roles they play. In fact, men would refrain from thinking women are not doing “enough” if they themselves were stripped of their privileged positions and were given the same responsibilities women are forced to handle on daily basis. Once they have experienced a day in the life of working women, and having occupied their social location of marginalization, they would eventually come to recognize their own inability to fill their shoes, and would come to a better understanding of all that working mothers do.

Evidence suggests that although males may work for longer hours compared to working mothers, these women can be more productive since they have advanced people management skills which they have acquired from their mothering experience [32]; P. 492). It is crucial that such a conclusion be emphasized and circulated through the media to reinforce a positive image of female productivity and skillfulness. The continuous reproduction of such an image can alter the perspective from which problematic gender constructions are viewed and bring about social change.

These issues of privilege and marginalization were raised throughout the interview process. The female interviewees spoke of their mothering experience and their longing to be understood and viewed through a positive lens by their spouses and society in general. The interviews focused on the female role at home, and her struggle within the family circle and at the workplace. Our discussions also delved into the issue of support or lack of it in the lives of these over-tired working women. Gender roles

Patriarchal discourses have traditionally supported unequal gender roles within the family circle and outside of it. That is, women have not been treated equally to men and their lives have been limited by discourses of ‘natural mothering’ [33] which view women as having innate nurturing abilities. This, unfortunately, makes the children entirely her responsibility as she is seen fit for it while men are not expected to take charge of any caring activities related to children because of their perceived roles as breadwinners and protectors of their families [34]. Many women around the world have rejected these conventional roles and the lack of appreciation attached to them. In an article entitled “A day without women? This is what it would look like,” [35] explains that American women went on strike on International Women's Day of 2017 to show the way their “cheap labor” has been undervalued. That day none of the expected tasks that are usually taken care of by women were done like grocery shopping, cooking meals, doing laundry and cleaning. The point was to get their unpaid work to be noticed or the cost it would take to hire someone else to do the job they usually did around the house. It only proved that through their work, they contribute financially to the household not to mention the income made by working women that is often spent on children's education and needs.

Another common perception the world still needs to challenge is the notion that real “masculine” men do not normally do “feminine” chores such as housework [36]. suggests that men do not feel motivated to do housework because they find it boring and it requires time they feel is better spent on paid work that involves financial gain. Men, in this situation, fail to understand the importance of their role in alleviating women's pain. They forget that by actually doing “feminine” chores, they relieve their wives of the multiple burdens they shoulder and by showing compassion, they contribute to their emotional and mental well-being and achieving gender equality and social reform. Ironically, while more men now support the idea of gender equality, it is not necessarily reflected at home where traditional values are dominant. According to Ref. [37]; this became visible in the responses of one-quarter of high school students when they thought their fathers' working full time and their mothers staying at home was the “ideal” arrangement. This leads to the conclusion that “perhaps young people are open to women's equality at work not because their attitudes about gender roles have evolved, but because they need a second income, so they still act more traditional at home” [37]. This explains why husbands may encourage their wives to have a job but this does not mean they value their work or that they believe in the division of labor at home.

Unfortunately, because society's expectations of women are limited to the traditional role of housewife and mother, her work outside the home is sometimes viewed as a “social promotion.” Men, however, are expected to be providers and work outside but when they do more household chores, they are faced with the perception of it being a “social demotion” for them [38]. These perceptions of a promotion for one and a demotion for another are problematic and are rather a disservice to both women and men. That is, when women's work is merely viewed as a social promotion, her hard work is not recognized enough in terms of professional growth and advancement, but as a mere addition to her life that does not hold much value since her original place is supposedly at the home. Men, on the other hand, and because of such perceptions and the need to prove their masculinity, are discouraged to have active roles at home which in turn leaves the women burdened by all the duties relevant to childcare and unpaid work.

Jordanian society, like most Middle Eastern countries, is dominated by a patriarchal system which imposes certain gender roles and shapes the way men and women are perceived [39]. Jordanian working women and mothers, in particular, are among those whose status is highly affected by stereotypical gender roles. One study conducted by the UNDP examines the various perceptions toward women's work and its research subjects suggested that their work was not appreciated because it was considered beneficial only to women, and does not financially contribute to the family or community. This assumption stems from the traditional perception that the female is not the main breadwinner, and so this makes her “work seem worthless” [10]; p. 41). Therefore, a woman is unable to complain in fear of losing her supposedly “unnecessary” job and at home, she is expected to be a housewife and not a career woman, and thus, her work is merely treated like a privilege that can be taken away easily. This is supported by the belief that for women to have a job “means being less attentive to household duties” which is rendered unacceptable under patriarchal rule. This attitude constantly undermines the importance of a woman's job and she is made to feel guilty for caring for anything other than her household. [40]; p. 37). It is such ideas and the notion of “superiority in mothering done by ‘stay at home mothers’” that have remained in the minds of many in the 21st century and continue to reinforce the patriarchal discourse of inequality [32].

The existing inequality in Jordan is reflected in the responses of many of the researched subjects. Around 85 % of the interviewees stated that their husbands did not do much work around the house because they viewed themselves as the main providers of the family. They agreed that their assistance is mostly visible when it comes to paying the bills and grocery shopping, but they did not follow up on their children's education online, keep any checklists on doctors' appointments, or involve the children in activities since the lockdown, neither did they clean the house or cook. 75 % of the interviewees agreed that the husband's presence at home often plays a negative role in raising and disciplining the children because they lack experience. Participant A stated:

When my husband is in charge and our children are left to him to take care of, they do not spend any quality time. They actually get a lot of screen time that is also unsupervised and house rules are not enforced. This has created tensions at home because of the conflicting methods practiced in raising the children.

Other participants explained that while their husbands hardly helped at home, they interfered and made more demands as they spent more time at home since the pandemic started. They also criticized their wives for the way they did things and did not approve of their parenting strategies although disciplining the children was normally left to the wives prior to the pandemic. This only added to these women’s stress as they had to deal with the pressure at the workplace while their homes were not a source of comfort or relief. Participant B commented that:

The responsibility of childcare mostly falls on us. I think it is much harder for a female to succeed than it is for a male. I think they are rewarded more often for smaller things but we would have to do so much more to possibly get the same reward. Because we are expected to handle childcare and family related issues, our job outside the home is not anyone’s concern and so men are not looking for us to succeed.

The participant's comment is indicative of society's expectations in terms of gender roles. These expectations have increased the challenges women face in their attempt at succeeding at work. The traditional role as mothers and housewives discourages women from seeking a career or an independent life for themselves outside the domestic sphere. Jordanian society continues to believe in traditional gender roles as a lot more value is placed on a woman's job at home. Her success at the workplace, however, is deemed insignificant and irrelevant. Participant C shared similar concerns:

We are expected to do everything at home and at work without complaining to either side; not to your colleagues and not to your husband or other family members. It is as if they are telling you that you asked for it so woMan up! Men will never get the pressure and the stress we have to live with day in and day out. They have many advantages that they themselves do not realize.

This image of the female as “super woman” who can somehow manage everything on her own and without any objection is a recurring issue most participants brought up in the interviews and were infuriated by. The interviews reflected that 70% feel the same way as they are unable to ask for help or complain when they are under a lot of pressure. It is as if they are being punished by society for the choice they made as working women since their place is “supposedly” the home. When they choose to work outside, it is a choice they have to come to terms with because they will not be excused from their responsibilities at home and would still be expected to make certain arrangements to fulfill their duties inside and outside the home.

Tensions at work

There are no laws in Jordan against gender equality, or women having jobs, yet that does not mean their voices are heard. Women's concerns are often ignored and if they complain about the pressure they undergo at work, they are told to leave work and fulfil their duties at home as mothers and housewives. It is common within a patriarchal society to perceive a woman's job a privilege granted to women by their husbands and not a necessity for their growth, independence, sense of achievement, or well-being. Therefore, if having this privilege generates any complaints or forms of dissatisfaction, it may be taken away from them by being replaced by a male employee or some women may be forced to become stay-at- home mothers by husbands who prioritize their needs and housework over their spouses' interest in growing personally and professionally. In other words, gender discrimination may not be widely discussed or easily identified at home or the workplace, nevertheless, it is often practiced in various subtle ways in Jordanian society.

Discrimination can take on the form of stereotypes that are imposed on both genders. One example is the way Jordanian women suffer from traditional stereotypes through which they are depicted as weak and feminine and if they step outside that image, they are viewed as having masculine features and not strong independent characters. One woman in a study on Jordan states she had to “desexualize” her identity as female to be taken seriously [10]. Others agreed and expressed their frustration about having to constantly prove themselves and their ability at work while Jordanian men did not experience the same pressure [10]; p. 41). Not only did these women feel pressured, but also felt oppressed when they were not taken seriously by male coworkers and were even ridiculed, and jokes were made about their work. They were perceived as having taken on jobs only because they had extra time on their hands and not because they had dreams or goals to fulfil like males did [10]; p. 41). Women found this offensive and complained about being underestimated but could not do much to dismantle these deep-rooted misperceptions.

In the same study, males and females agreed that Jordanian women work harder than men and do not waste time fighting or mingling at the workplace. They also thought that “women are much more active, do their tasks on time, and are more committed to the work” while men did not need to work very hard as they knew they would easily get promoted merely because they are men or have a certain family name with some power attached to it. In addition, many men mentioned they preferred working with women over other men [10]; p. 40). This may seem flattering to women, but there seems to be a growing consensus among 14 of the interviewees about the reasons behind such a preference. They believe that more and more women are being hired often because they are hardworking and subordinate. They do not stand up against the oppression or injustice they face whereas most Jordanian men cannot be silenced nor can their concerns be disregarded in a predominantly patriarchal society.

The interviews revealed that 80 % of the women suffered immensely as their work has doubled at the workplace and at home but they were unable to express their frustrations or anxiety to their employers. They put in more effort at work so they do not lose their jobs during the pandemic and their responsibilities at home, simultaneously, increased while their working hours have not been reduced. One participant reported that she receives no assistance, physically, financially, or psychologically, and neither does the administration offer any support for working mothers. She has had to do extra work so they do not feel she is falling behind because she is a mother. Since the pandemic, she has been making double the effort to avoid becoming expendable, and easily replaceable at work putting more pressure on herself, in addition to all the extra responsibilities relevant to schooling and attending to children's needs. She stated that she cannot afford leaving the job either because the family needs the financial assistance even if her paycheck is not a major contribution.

Around 80 % of the women complained that they cannot afford being perceived not fit for the job, but felt that when they do double the work, they are still not appreciated for it. Males, however, are appreciated doing half the job which is clearly a sign of gender discrimination. Most participants noticed that due to COVID-19, many men have been fired and female employees have been given more work to do because employers know they need the job, and will do the work without asking for a raise. They are even made to feel lucky that they were able to have kept the job, but if male employees had been approached with the extra amount of work to do, they would not have accepted it without a pay raise or a promotion in store. Accordingly, female job opportunities do not emerge from a position of power or belief in their potential, but rather their disadvantaged location as a marginalized group in society.

On the other hand, 20 % of the interviewees pointed out that they shared their concerns with coworkers and bosses and they were supportive allowing leaves, late arrival to meetings, and working from home when others were at the office. It is important though to recognize that the working conditions of 15 % are quite unique as they worked at non-governmental organizations which were led by foreign administrations and not Jordanian ones. This plays a major role in the way the pandemic and its resulting stress is being handled. Participant D mentioned:

Before Covid it was weird to talk about this as it was an obvious challenge but now the challenge is Global and even people with dogs address their issues! I don’t have to explain myself anymore … I’ll say I’ll be late to the meeting or whatever is causing me to create a balance that didn’t have to exist before … people at work became somewhat more tolerant and understanding.

They were even offered counselling services for their support. The other one worked under Jordanian management but also felt some empathy and understanding from coworkers and those in charge. She reflected on her situation and asserted that:

Discussing worries as a mother with coworkers or boss is a must simply because if I pretend to act as wonder woman at work and keep hiding my worries at home, I will lose control over the two and distraction will eventually find its way to work. We are playing mother-employee-wife "3-in-one role" and being the three is so powerful. Having to address them all does not make us less focused nor does it unfold any weaknesses. Handling the above should never imply, on the other hand, that we are supernatural heroines! We are absolutely human … it is ok to get tired, frustrated, or simply not be in the mood! Participant E

Fortunately, this working woman, unlike most, has been able to share her concerns at work. This is common when the administration cares about the well-being of their employees and women do not feel blamed for their anxiety or exhaustion because of their multiple responsibilities. Women working in similar environments feel a sense of support and security as their needs are taken into consideration. They are not constantly under the threat of losing their jobs because of their conditions as working mothers. On the contrary, working for a supportive administration makes them proud and more productive because they feel appreciated and understood.

An understanding of women's plight

Many mothers were under the assumption that working from home during the pandemic would make things easier for them, but, eventually, they found out that juggling responsibilities like a full-time job, childcare, domestic duties, and supervision of children's education is very overwhelming especially for mothers of young children. Research shows it is not just house chores that are a burden on working mothers' lives, but it is the “emotional labor” of planning, keeping track of family and children's activities and needs. Recognizing the nature of the challenge and stress that is caused by all the “emotional labor” women do which goes unnoticed [41], believe men should take on some of these responsibilities as the mental lists women maintain are a form of unpaid work and this includes “Grocery lists, holidays, birthdays, children's school requirements, children's clothing, medicines, pets' needs” and so much more [41]. Therefore, the men should take this “emotional labor” into consideration as it adds to women's anxiety besides the physical effort made to keep up with the family's demands and housework.

Working women in Jordan, like many others around the world, have had to juggle their careers along with their responsibilities at home regardless of whether the people by whom they are surrounded sympathized with them. These responsibilities include helping their children with online schoolwork, finding activities for them during lockdowns, controlling their screen time and what they are being exposed to through media, addressing technical difficulties during classes and online exams, parenting, cooking, cleaning, grocery shopping, attending to the needs of the elderly at times, in addition to their duties as wives. In Jordan and other countries such as Korea, for instance, sharing a limited space with the children, preparing study spaces for them at home and monitoring their online education also became a dilemma especially for those living in small apartments and on a limited income [42]; p. 305). In fact, many have been faced with the inconvenience of sharing a limited space with the children while working from home. Sometimes, noise and interruptions are unavoidable and working around it all can become a nuisance not to be underestimated. Another challenge women have encountered beyond meeting expectations within the family circle and gender discrimination at work is the lack of support and an understanding of their struggle. Males' inability to identify with working mothers can be traced back to the way they were raised by figures who have internalized patriarchy and treated males as a superior species worthy of respect. Being raised by parents with such biases and values, males have become so comfortable with being served by women, finding no faults with that, and enjoy being supported privileges on the basis of their gender while paying no attention to the needs of the females in the family.

The female's challenge lies in the fact that men's experience is defined through their position of privilege which does not place them under the same pressure women feel at home or the workplace. A lot of men are unable to relate to working mothers' situation because they have never had to commit to so many responsibilities all at once and neither have they been expected to the way women have long before Covid-19. There is also a fact that is often overlooked by most men which is their gender never being a cause for their oppression. They have not experienced being marginalized and underestimated due to their gender the way women have which makes it difficult for them to identify with the female situation [43]. asserted that men hardly ever comprehend female concerns and struggles because they themselves have never had to deal with the kind of discrimination females may be exposed to all their lives. Therefore, most men have not lowered their expectations in terms of the unpaid work their wives did even after witnessing the drastic changes COVID-19 brought upon their lives and their children's. Most of the researched subjects expressed their frustration with the indifference with which their plight has been handled in Jordan. The interviews revealed that those who felt that no one understood what they were going through were 80 % of the interviewees. They felt they were suffering alone and did not get any support from husbands or family:

You’re always expected to be able to meet the expectations and beyond by your family and society. With online education for children, it feels like having a second job for which you’re actually paying and no decrease in your actual workload or working hours. Participant F

The participant refers to all the time spent on following up on children's classes, their homework, and the assistance they need in meeting their teachers demands and carrying out projects, all of which is expected of mothers to ensure their success at school. In this regard, the children's education has become another cause of concern not to mention the amount of work that has been added to mothers' former responsibilities which didn't come with added support.

It is worth noting that feminist point theorists think marginalized groups, such as women, enjoy a “double vision” enabling them to understand the reality of the privileged as they experienced their own reality and witness the others simultaneously while remaining subjugated by their dominant ideologies [44]. This “double vision” can be sensed through the following statement by one female participant who is able to critique the female situation and contrast it with the male world in which their work is valued and needs are respected. Participant G stated:

The case of denial and pretending to be okay with all the responsibilities and new roles we took on during COVID is very important and worth a deeper look. It’s like the golden chance to prove that we are super- heroes but we have certainly failed. This failure brought many hidden issues to the discussion table like should we handle all this during COVID or even before to prove that we are worthy and successful women? Why are men appreciated for going to work every day and are exempted from many responsibilities because they certainly are tired and excused, while on the other hand, some working women feel guilty both at home and at work for not being enough. During a working day, we juggle between online classes and the supervision of our kids' education. We think we can replace the school, the teacher, the kids' companions, because we don’t have a choice and because we are expected to do it.

This comment touches upon serious issues addressed by feminists that continuously haunt women's lives such as proving their worthiness, feelings of guilt, and males' superiority over females. In this society, women who are feminist and support equality get punished for their beliefs, and so when they fail at work or experience difficulty, they are met with a lack of empathy and understanding by patriarchal figures. This is visible at the workplace and at home where women are viewed as having left their home and children to pursue personal goals and dreams by getting a job, and therefore, received no sympathy or support by their husbands or in-laws when they are desperately in need for it. In their eyes, these women are selfish for putting their careers ahead of family. Furthermore, 85 % of the interviewees agreed that most men are unable to comprehend what a working mother experiences on daily basis because they do not play an equal role in marriage or society. One woman believes this is only with men in the Arab world. Another one thinks:

It’s rare to find a man who really understands, not to mention their inability to understand our hormonal fluctuations, and our physiological and physical vulnerability, so at certain days or periods of time we have half the energy we usually have, and yet are expected to be functioning at the same rate. Participant H

One interviewee explains that “husbands or males in general do not feel they need to understand our situation as working women because we have been seeking equality with them at work for so long, and now that we have it, we should not expect any special treatment or get to complain about it.” Not only do these women lack support at work, but also their advancement and promotion are often hindered. According to Ref. [32]; working mothers are even “penalized in attempting to meet the demands of parent and worker roles” (p. 494). This is applicable to the Jordanian context where progress has been made in terms of women seeking careers outside the home, but the cost has been high as their responsibilities have doubled while support for them in their endeavor has been jeopardized by males and society at large.

One of the interviewed women has felt conflicted about sharing her troubles with individuals at work. She has been unable to complain about her tiresome situation because of the lack of trust at the workplace.

Sharing my agony over work and life balance has been a struggle as I need to be selective in terms of whom I can trust and open up to. In general, I do feel like I cannot be honest and say ‘this is too much.’ Or ‘I can’t handle this way of life anymore, I just have to keep going. Life has become so complicated that I have had to give up on my dream to pursue graduate school because of all the responsibilities and stress I am supposed to be able to handle. Participant I

In general, it has been difficult for many of the women to find comfort through sharing their struggles with others especially men because most support patriarchal values. Nevertheless, a few interviewees have disrupted the narrative of male oppression and indifference and since one of the most prominent principles the feminist standpoint theory prides itself on is “multiple subjectivities” or “plurality,” it is crucial that alternative points of view are presented to reinforce its strategies for objective knowledge production [45]. Twenty percent of the interviewees assessed the situation from a different perspective and mentioned that they believed that the current situation involving covid- 19 may contribute positively to men's understanding of the female experience:

I see because many men had to work from home and try to mute the kids' voices in the background noise during an online meeting, for example. They for once had to experience what their working wives go through. They became more involved in the kids' education and housework. This might bring power and change to working women as they now share a similar experience to some extent. Participant J

Ironically, the pandemic has produced a positive outcome necessary to the subversion of patriarchal constructions of gender. It has provided males with the opportunity to experience another world, the female world, even if partially, leading them to a recognition of the unbelievable work women do. In light of what they have learned from such an experience, their levels of sympathy and empathy have risen, and the wives felt the improvement in their husbands’ behavior.

Participant responses show that the pandemic has had an impact on gender relations and has influenced Jordanians' attitude toward marriage. This is one of the issues that have surfaced during the interviews as several participants mentioned that their siblings have witnessed their suffering within the marriage relationship and accordingly, have started to lose faith in the constitution of marriage. Many of these young individuals no longer perceive it as a partnership or a sacred relationship between males and females. They see it as a relationship fraught with difficulties and burdens they wish to do without. These sentiments lead us to predict a further rise in the rate of divorces in Jordan and an increased desire among adolescents to remain single free of the hassles married couples are exposed to. This breakdown in marriage relationships became more visible during the pandemic due to the pressure caused by lockdowns. The pandemic has exposed critical problems within the family which should lead to redefining the whole concept of marriage and family within the Jordanian context and the Middle East at large where patriarchy's influence is so profound. These concerns reflect a need for more attention to be paid to the way children are raised and how gender is constructed in the family to prevent future victimization of both genders.

Limitations

While the study discusses important issues relevant to the lives and well-being of women in Jordan, there are certain limitations to this study. First, the inability to go into more critical and personal issues about the impact of the pandemic on marriage and the husband-wife relationship, and possible divorces. This is because it is a stigma in Jordan and the participants were not ready to share any further as the culture regards these issues highly private that should not be discussed outside the family circle, and so the women spoke with unease. A second limitation is some of the women could not be very open about the dilemma at work because they want to protect their jobs and are afraid that some incidents at the workplace relevant to their issues with family and work will get out and cause them trouble if they spoke up and, thus, did not want to put themselves at risk. This is because many extended families are connected with each other in Jordan, and there is that fear that the news can possibly spread quickly. A third limitation is the limited privacy participants had as the entire family may be at home at all times and the children are receiving online classes which limits their opportunities to have time for themselves and freedom of movement. In other words, being part of this conversation and interview process was not an easy task for some especially when they were not able to speak freely but wanted to. In addition, taking breaks from the family may have been possible but not without difficulty. The last limitation is the inability to follow up on these women's conditions at a much later stage to trace any progress or regression after perhaps adapting better to the current situation. This can be attributed to their lack of time, privacy, and an unwillingness to be part of a larger project and a more time-consuming one.

Conclusion

The study reveals that most women have struggled to juggle responsibilities of paid and unpaid work while hardly getting any support or understanding from home or the workplace due to the patriarchal beliefs dominating Jordanian society. One of the findings of this study is that the challenges they have faced inside and outside the home during COVID-19 have taken a toll on their lives and well-being. The study indicates that Jordanian society continues to be highly influenced by traditional gender roles regardless of women's opportunities to work outside the home. That is, even if many husbands have working wives, that does not mean they will participate in the division of labor at home or childcare. These working mothers have suffered tremendously during the pandemic and have taken on so much more than they can actually handle on their own. Nevertheless, they have not been excused for any oversight, tardiness, or lapse in judgement by spouses or employers for that matter.

Moreover, it was found that while many employers choose to hire women, it is not because they value their work or qualifications, but due to their willingness to take on the extra workload which employers would not have been able to impose on their male counterparts and for less pay. In fact, their need to prove themselves and their capabilities is what enables those in power to take advantage of them. These findings reflect that Jordan has developed in several domains, but insufficient progress has been made in terms of patriarchy's strong control over women's lives and the future of their daughters. This is a critical issue which should be addressed as most working mothers have become victims to these harsh conditions in which gender discrimination is practiced with such great ease and their daughters will most likely face even worse conditions if the current situation continues as the demands of life continue to grow. Unfortunately, serious efforts need to be made by policy makers, employers, and husbands to alleviate women's pain and suffering and address their concerns as equal partners worthy of respect.

These women have provided great insight into the female position in Jordan and the battles she constantly fights to make her voice heard. It is their marginalization that has given them an epistemic privilege over males and an ability to see through power relations and the dynamics within which patriarchy operates. These insights can lead to social transformation if taken seriously. Findings indicate that although many women are encouraged to have jobs, it is not because their husbands are open minded or progressive, but because they want a second income to relieve them of financial stress. This can have negative implications for the future of gender equity.

Conversely, female empowerment resulting from exerting extreme efforts to overcome the pressure of having to deal with the global crisis of COVID-19 was one of the themes which appeared in the interviews and is worth further investigation. The interviews show that a few participants were tired of the guilt they felt no matter how much they worked and sacrificed for the family and realized some changes needed to be made. They decided to show themselves some appreciation and make time for themselves and their own needs. They found that society and their husbands had failed them often, and so they had to take control of their own lives and destiny rather than remain victims and feel pity for themselves. Their lives have been transformed by this realization and it has led them to regain an identity that was slowly being erased. Once they felt they were losing themselves as they attended to their children's and husband's needs while neglecting their own, they became determined to take back their lives and take steps to feel empowered again. Some have reunited with old friends whom they have lost contact with for a long period of time. Others followed their interests and pursued their hobbies such as doing yoga, painting, or playing a musical instrument. Finally, it can be concluded that this is the best and most positive outcome of the pandemic for Jordanian females and the promotion of female self-awareness and gender equality.
