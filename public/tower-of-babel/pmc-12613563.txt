The thighs have it: evidence for the importance of lower body regions in female body size judgments
Authors: Lia Marinko, Briana L Kennedy, Kei-Kei Koh, Laura Dondzilo, Jason Bell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the body features underpinning accurate size judgments of female bodies, and whether judgment accuracy varies with body size. Previous research indicates several body features can influence size judgments; however, there is uncertainty around which specific parts are important, if those vary with size, and whether body perception involves holistic processing (i.e., the whole body) or relies on specific cues (i.e., certain body parts). To examine these questions, we used the bodyline task, which measures two underlying sources of perceptual error in body size judgments: regression to the mean and serial dependence.ResultsIn Experiment 1 (N = 99), we compared judgments of whole bodies to those made viewing the top-half or bottom-half only. Viewing the bottom-half only produced judgments as accurate as those for the whole body, suggesting holistic processing of the whole body is not required for body size judgments. Experiment 2 (N = 116) built on that result by comparing judgments when only the inner or outer thigh region were visible, compared to the whole body. Both isolated thigh regions led to significantly poorer accuracy in judgments compared to whole-body stimuli, indicating that accurate size judgments require more body features than those alone.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that accurate judgment of female body size does not require holistic processing but does involve integration of multiple features within the lower body region. These results have implications for understanding the perceptual processes involved in body size estimation with potential for broader considerations of body image disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02444-z.Keywords: Size judgment, Body perception, Serial dependence, Regression to the mean Perceptual error, Body integration, Holistic processing

Background

Body size perception plays an important role in maintaining health, but research indicates that incorrect judgments of ones’ own and others’ body sizes can be related to greater health issues [1]. For instance, individuals with eating disorders tend to perceive their current body size less accurately than those without such disorders [2]. Such misperceptions are considered an aspect of body image disturbance, which is comprised of several factors, including evaluative (i.e., negative thoughts and feelings about bodies resulting in body dissatisfaction; [3], behavioral (i.e., body checking and avoidance; [4, 5]), and perceptual components (i.e., an altered perception of one’s own and others’ bodies; [6, 7]). Given the clinical relevance and that the underlying causes of body image disturbance remain unclear [8], understanding the mechanisms underlying body size judgments could have valuable applications [8]. In this study, we borrowed techniques from vision science to better understand the perceptual processes involved in viewing and judging bodies. In particular, we investigated which parts of a body—that is, the specific visual information from body stimuli—individuals use to judge a body for its size, to better understand the perceptual processes involved in making body size judgments.

Body size integration theories

Some theories propose that errors in body size judgments are related to perceptual difficulties integrating body features together. One such theory focuses on multisensory integration, such that errors in body size judgment are related to challenges combining information from different sensory modalities of body stimuli to form a coherent perception [9]. Of clinical relevance, people who experience difficulties in multisensory integration may be vulnerable to eating disorders. Individuals with eating disorders often struggle to integrate various types of information effectively, whereby they show impairments combining and processing information from multiple sensory inputs simultaneously [10–12]. Individuals with eating disorders may have difficulties combining visual information (how they see their body, i.e., the size and shape of the body), proprioceptive information (the sense of where their body parts are in space), interoceptive information (internal bodily sensations), and tactile information (touch sensations on the skin) to form a coherent body representation [11]. For example, a person with an eating disorder might struggle to accurately integrate the visual perception of their body size in a mirror with the proprioceptive sense of the space their body occupies, leading to a distorted overall body image.

Another theoretical concept related to perceptual integration is known as weak central coherence theory [13], which proposes that differences in accuracy of body judgments might be related to a tendency to process information in a piecemeal (i.e., “parts-processing” style) rather than a holistic manner. In the context of this theory, individuals with eating disorders tend to focus on details rather than seeing the “big picture” [13]. Weak central coherence has been defined by two components: a poorer ability in global processing (i.e., forming a holistic representation) and enhanced local (feature-specific) processing abilities [14], and therefore is characterized as a local bias towards specific information and a failure to adequately integrate this information into a global percept [15–17].

Overall, these findings suggest that individuals who make more inaccurate size judgments have difficulties integrating information when making these judgments, although less clear is exactly which visual features of a body are necessarily being integrated or not integrated to facilitate such misjudgments.

Insights from body perception research

An open question in body size judgment research is whether bodies are processed holistically or in a parts-based processing manner. Research that has been done to investigate how we perceive and integrate body features when healthy participants judge body size has yielded mixed results. In one such study, using an adaptation paradigm, Brooks et al. [18] found body size aftereffects transfer from image to image despite scale and orientation changes; such transfer effects are consistent with the idea that bodies are processed as whole objects rather than simply by their low-level visual features. Additionally, a recent study by Marinko et al. [19] found that errors in body size judgments increased when participants were shown a silhouette, in comparison to a natural image containing internal features. This suggests that the outline shape of the body was not sufficient to judge size but rather, a combination of internal and external features is required for accurate size judgments, consistent with a holistic processing account. On the other hand, Alexi et al. [20] measured body size judgment accuracy by examining patterns of error for upright and inverted bodies, with the latter thought to be less effectively processed in higher visual object regions [21]. Error was similar regardless of whether body images were presented in an upright or inverted orientation, suggesting that judgments of body size rely on local visual features (parts-based processing) unaffected by overall configuration, rather than holistic body representations.

In a similar vein, some researchers have considered whether certain body parts are more important than others in the way people make body size judgments. Irvine et al. [22] recorded participants’ eye movements while they made body size judgments and found that accurate observers attended to more body parts when they judged size, and especially when their gaze was focused on the body center and sides of the torso and inner thigh. George et al. [23] corroborated these findings by showing that accurate observers had increased fixations around the lower to mid torso. In another study, Cornelissen et al. [24] identified stomach depth as a critical visual cue for judging body mass, especially for bodies presented in side-on or three-quarter views. Overall, these studies indicate that there may be several different body parts that are crucial for making accurate size judgments and that they “typically” present in the lower torso. Still other research considers a conjunction of features. For example, some evidence indicates that the waist-to-hip ratio is particularly important for body size judgments [25–27]. Together, limited research indicates that several particular features of body stimuli are critical for body size judgments; however, clarity is still needed about which features or subsets of features are the most important. In addition, the studies to date have predominantly involved healthy weight participants and body stimuli, meaning little is known about whether the contribution from these features might vary across different body sizes, e.g., thigh gap is not a visible cue for larger body sizes.

Vision science has a long history of tackling these kinds of holistic versus part processing questions to elucidate the processes underpinning perception of basic shapes [28, 29], visual patterns [30, 31], objects [32, 33], and also human faces [34, 35]. A common technique among these studies is to occlude or isolate specific features and to measure changes in detection or recognition, compared to the whole unmodified stimulus. Such methods can quantify which features are important and whether they are integrated and thus could be adapted to resolve the debate between holistic and parts-based processing of bodies. By applying similar techniques to body perception research, we can gain a more comprehensive understanding of how people judge body size and shape.

Perceptual biases contributing to errors in body size judgments

Previous literature has revealed several perceptual biases that contribute to errors in body size judgments and conveniently, there is a perceptual task, known as the bodyline task, that can simultaneously measure several of them, such as central tendency [36], or regression to the mean, and serial dependence [37]. We tend to make judgments about complex stimuli, such as bodies and faces, with reference to a template based on the average of all that class of stimuli which we have seen previously [36]. This experience can lead to perceptual errors in which judgments of magnitude are biased towards the mean of a set [38], and this is known as a central tendency, or regression to the mean bias. With regard to body size perception, Cornelissen et al. [36] and Alexi et al. [37] have shown that healthy females systematically underestimate the weight of large females, and overestimate the weight of small females, so errors in magnitude consistently regress or contract towards the mean of a set. Recent research shows that assessing regression to the mean bias can help to explain changes in judgment accuracy when body stimuli vary in appearance [19] because it is sensitive to changes in stimulus certainty (i.e., how precisely a characteristic is perceived; [19, 39, 40].

The bodyline task also measures a second, separate source of perceptual bias in body size judgments, termed serial dependence. Serial dependence occurs when the appearance of an object is distorted towards that of a previously viewed stimulus [41]. Serial dependencies have been shown to occur for a range of perceptual processes, e.g., numerosity [39], orientation [41, 42], face identity [43, 44] and attractiveness [45], body size [37], and also see recent reviews [46–48]. Serial dependence is theorized to help stabilize perception when there are subtle disruptions (e.g., blinking, occlusions, eye, and head movements; 41) and thus these biases are maximal when there are small stimulus changes from moment to moment. Specifically, in body size judgments serial dependence causes a body to be perceived as smaller when it is preceded by a slightly smaller body, and larger when preceded by a slightly larger body. Like regression to the mean, serial dependence has also been used to explain changes in task accuracy [39, 42, 49], including when stimuli vary in appearance [19] or certainty. Assessing serial dependence induced biases has also proven useful in helping to explain the causes of larger perceptual errors in certain groups, such as exaggerated body judgment errors by those with elevated eating disorder symptoms [20] or poorer face recognition by those with elevated autistic symptoms [50].

Current study

The overarching aim of the current study was to identify the critical body feature, or features, that underpin accurate judgments of female body size. To do so, we used the bodyline task [37], which is a well-established method for evaluating body size judgments and associated errors. The body stimuli used in the task represent a broad range of female body sizes (very small to very large), which enables direct comparison of judgment errors for different sizes. This study focused on female participants and body stimuli to ensure a homogeneous sample and reduce confounding variables. Additionally, this focus aligns with the broader trend in the literature, where research on body size judgments and related body image disorders has predominantly centered on females (e.g., [2, 20, 22, 23]). While such disorders can affect individuals of all genders, they are disproportionately represented in females [51, 52]. This overrepresentation likely stems from sociocultural pressures and gender-specific ideals surrounding thinness, which are more pervasive among women. Research indicates that men and women differ in body size ideals and critical features for size judgments: women generally idealize thinner physiques, focusing on overall thinness and specific body parts, while men prefer mesomorphic shapes with developed musculature, emphasizing muscle mass and upper body development [53–60].

To examine the role of different body features in female body size judgments, we used a masking paradigm to isolate distinct areas of female body stimuli across two experiments. In Experiment 1, we examined the coarser question as to whether accurate body size judgments require a view of the whole body (i.e., performance benefits from holistic integration of body features), or whether accurate judgments can be made using either the upper- (from the navel to the head), or lower-half (from the navel to the feet) of the body only. If body size judgments require holistic integration [18], then it is hypothesized that the whole-body condition should result in greater body size judgment accuracy than either half-body condition. On the other hand, if body size judgments rely on specific body parts (i.e., not holistic processing [20], then it is hypothesized that either of the two half-body conditions would provide sufficient information to make accurate body size judgments, compared with the whole-body condition.

Guided by the findings of Experiment 1, in Experiment 2, we aimed to investigate the relative importance of specific features in the lower half-body. To do so, we further restricted the visible body regions to assess the relative importance of specific candidate features: the inner thigh region, and the outer thigh region. We chose these two body parts based on eye tracking research indicating that both regions are critical when making accurate body size judgments [22]. Based on those findings, we hypothesized that viewing only the inner or outer thigh would lead to significantly less accurate body size judgments compared with viewing the whole body. Alternatively, if a single feature (e.g., width across hips) supports accurate judgments of body size, then it is hypothesized that presenting a single thigh feature should produce similar levels of judgment accuracy, compared with the whole body. Finally, we were also interested in whether the relative utility of these cues varied with body size, e.g., inner thigh features might be less important for larger bodies.

Experiment 1 results

Data screening

Consistent with previous studies using the bodyline task [19, 20, 37], each participant’s mean body size judgments were separately assessed to verify they could differentiate between various body size categories. A linear regression was performed separately on each participant’s mean body size category judgments and repeated for all three body type conditions: whole-body, top-half, and bottom-half. A regression slope significantly greater than zero indicated the participant could discriminate the body size categories. Conversely, a slope not statistically different from zero suggested the participant could not perceive differences in body size across categories. The illustrations of our body stimuli in Fig. 5 help demonstrate that this was a very basic performance check. Any participant whose data had one or more non-significant from zero slopes was excluded from the analysis. This process resulted in the exclusion of data from five participants.

Participant data were then combined and assessed for outliers, using the outlier labelling rule g = 2.2 [61], such that data that went beyond 2.2 standard deviations from the mean were considered outliers. Fifty-nine individual data points were identified as outliers, which represented 1.1% of the data (including mean body size judgments and serial dependence difference scores), and one case within the AQ measure. All were subsequently winsorised [62] using +/− 1 rule [63]. All variables were assessed for normality and all variables used satisfied guidelines for skew <|2.0| and kurtosis <|9.0| [64], and all assumptions were met for analyses. When sphericity assumptions were violated in our ANOVA analysis, results were interpreted using Huynh–Feldt adjusted values [63].

We examined participant characteristics based on their questionnaire responses.1 Participants’ BMI (M = 22.05, SD = 3.15, range 15.57–29.41), indicated that, on average, participants were in the normal to healthy body range. The EDEQ global scores of the participants (M = 2.21, SD = 1.41, range from 0 to 5.65) were slightly higher in comparison to Australian norms for young adult women [65]. On the DASS-21 questionnaire, participants’ scores indicated mild depressive symptoms (M = 5.64, SD = 4.58, range 0–21), mild to moderate anxiety symptoms (M = 5.03, SD = 3.99, range 0–15) and normal to mild stress symptoms (M = 7.30, SD = 4.09, range 0–18). These were slightly elevated DASS-21 scores in comparison to Australian norms for young-adults [66]. The AQ global scores for the participants (M = 61.40, SD = 10.78, range 37–93) were indicative of relatively low autistic traits as Russell-Smith et al. [67] scoring ranges from 50 to 200. Despite showing elevated levels of distress, the participant sample largely mirrored the typical young adult female demographic employed in previous body image studies [19, 37, 68].

Comparing body size judgments for half- and whole-body conditions

To investigate whether body size judgments differed for whole-body and half-body conditions, we conducted a 3 (body type condition: whole-body, top-half, bottom-half) × 7 (body size category: 1–7) within-subjects analysis of variance (ANOVA), using body size judgments as the dependent variable (see Fig. 1A). This analysis was conducted to determine whether the experimental manipulation (i.e., whole-body, top-half and bottom-half) had any impact on our dependent variables (i.e., body size judgments). The analysis revealed a significant main effect of body size category, F(2.48, 242.83) = 2164.72, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.957, demonstrating that, as expected, participants could readily discriminate between the seven body size categories. The main effect of body type condition (whole-body, top-half, bottom-half) was not significant, F(2, 196) = 2.74, p = 0.067, ηp2 = 0.027, indicating the half-body conditions were not simply judged as systematically larger or smaller than their whole body counterparts. However, there was a significant interaction between body size category and body type condition, F(7.28, 713.32) = 37.99, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.279, indicating the influence of viewing whole versus partial bodies varied across body size categories. To illustrate these differences and their direction, Fig. 1B shows the mean difference scores between the whole-body and half-body conditions, plotted by body size category. For judgments of the smallest bodies (categories 1 and 2), top-half body stimuli appeared to be judged larger than the bottom-half and whole-body conditions. By contrast, for the largest categories (6 and 7) top-half body stimuli appeared to be judged smaller than the other conditions. Then, for more average sized bodies (categories 3, 4, and 5), all three body type conditions appeared to produce near identical size judgments. Bottom-half bodies appear to be consistently judged similarly to whole-bodies. While these interactions could be followed up with post hoc analyses, consistent with prior research [20, 40], we sought to understand the causes of these differences between conditions by unpacking the bodyline task into its two underlying measures of perceptual bias (i.e., regression to the mean and serial dependence).

In certain perceptual domains, measures of serial dependency have correlated with and have been used to help explain overall magnitude estimation errors (i.e., central tendency/regression to the mean; [39, 42, 49]). In judgments of body size, however, the two processes have been found to be independent of one another [37], with serial dependence biases predicting almost none of the substantial regression to the mean observed in body size estimation. To assess their possible relationship and showcase individual variation in these biases in the current study data, we ran a correlational analysis on the full data from Experiment 1, comparing serial dependence and regression to the mean magnitudes across participants (see Fig. 1C). The majority of participants exhibited both biases (data points fall in the left upper quadrant); however, we found no evidence of a correlation between regression to the mean and serial dependence magnitudes, r(297) = 0.014, p = 0.81, consistent with previous literature with this task [37]. We proceeded to consider the analyses of these perceptual biases separately.

Changes in body size judgment accuracy related to regression to the mean

To calculate the average regression to the mean magnitude in each of our body type conditions, we fit separate linear regressions to each condition. The data were a very good fit to the linear model (whole-body: R2 = 0.95; top-half: R2 = 0.97; bottom-half: R2 = 0.99). We defined the regression index within the constraints of 0 to 1.0 (where 0 = total regression to the mean and 1 = assumed veridical perception/no error), i.e., a flatter slope signifies greater regression to the mean errors. The linear regression slope for the whole-body condition fit was 0.74, 95% CI [0.72, 0.76], which was comparable with previous research using full featured whole bodies as stimuli [36, 37]. The slope for the bottom-half stimuli was identical at 0.74, 95% CI [0.72 to 0.76]; however, the slope for top-half stimuli was shallower, at 0.66, 95% CI [0.64 to 0.68], indicating larger regression to the mean errors for this body type condition.

To formally compare regression to the mean magnitude across our body type conditions, we used an extra sums-of-squares F-test. This analysis compares a two-model fit to a single model, for a pair of data sets. Each participant’s data is treated as an individual data point. Our parameter of interest was slope. The F-test determines whether the data from both conditions is better fit by a single model with a common slope or two models with different slopes. The F-test comparing whole-body and top-half conditions supported two models, with a significantly more shallow slope/greater errors made when viewing the top-half only, compared with the whole-body, F(1,1382) = 31.01, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.02. The next analysis compared the whole- and bottom-half body conditions. Here, the analysis favored a single model, indicating that there was no difference in slope/error magnitude between whole-body and bottom-half conditions, F(1,1382) = 0.01, p = 0.915, ηp2 < 0.01. The final analysis compared the top-half and bottom-half conditions. The F test favored two models, with the top-half condition again producing a significantly more shallow slope/greater errors compared to bottom-half condition, F(1,1382) = 29.27, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.02. Taken together our findings indicate support for our local feature (rather than holistic) hypothesis. Body size judgment errors due to regression to the mean were the same whether viewing the whole-body or only the bottom-half of a body. By contrast, judgment errors were significantly greater when viewing the top-half of a body only, compared with the whole- body, or bottom-half.

Changes in body size judgment accuracy related to serial dependencies

To calculate serial dependence judgments biases, we took an approach consistent with previous research [19, 20, 37], whereby data were reorganized into difference scores. First, mean judgments were calculated for each body size category separately. That mean was then subtracted from individual trial judgments of that category, binned according to the relative size of the body on the previous trial (i.e., all conditions have trials where a body size is preceded by its own size (0 relative size difference) and by each other size category). These calculated difference scores now reflect how the judged size of the current body stimulus varies as a function of the relative size of the body on the previous trial. The data from all seven body sizes can then be combined into a single normalized data set. The above transformations were repeated separately for all three body type conditions (whole-body, top-half and bottom-half), to allow their comparison. The resulting serial dependence bias values are presented in Fig. 2.

Serial dependence bias was evident in all three body type conditions. Namely, bodies were judged as somewhat smaller when preceded by a smaller body on the previous trial and larger when the previous trial contained a larger body. Note that these biases did not reflect simple trial to trial averaging as the bias in size judgments occurred more when the body on the previous trial was similar in size but not when the trial-to-trial difference was large (e.g., compare data at differences 1–3 with 4–5).

To statistically estimate and compare serial dependence biases across the three conditions, the data in each condition were fit with a first derivative-of Gaussian (D1) function in GraphPad Prism, using a similar method to Fischer and Whitney [41]. The equation is outlined in Eq. 1, and the resulting fits are plotted as the solid lines in Fig. 2.

C was a constant representing the average response bias, θ centered the function on the x-axis. Both were set to zero. Amplitude, A, one of the parameters to be solved, indicates the magnitude of error, or bias due to the previous image: zero meant no influence, negative values indicate adaptation, and positive values indicate serial dependence. The other solved parameter, σ, was the width of the function, with a larger σ suggesting a broader range of biases, and vice versa.

Overall, our results indicated serial dependence in all body type conditions. The fit of the D1 function in each conditions was strong (whole-body: R2 = 0.90; top-half: R2 = 0.95; bottom-half: R2 = 0.98). The solved parameters for amplitude (bias magnitude) were all positive, and similar (whole-body: A = 0.37, 95% CI [0.32, 0.42]; top-half: A = 0.38, 95% CI [0.32, 0.43]; bottom-half: A = 0.41, 95% CI [0.35, 0.47]; likewise, the bandwidth (range of bias) was similar across conditions (whole-body: σ = 1.87, 95% CI [1.68, 2.08]; top-half: σ = 2.03, 95% CI [1.81, 2.29]; bottom-half: σ = 1.95, 95% CI [1.76, 2.17]). An extra sums-of-squares F-test was again used to investigate whether the serial dependence data in each pair of comparisons was better fit by a single model, or two models differing in the selected parameter. Separate F tests comparing pairs of conditions in terms of bias magnitude (amplitude A) and range (sigma σ) were all non-significant (all p values > 0.3), indicating no differences in serial dependence bias across body type conditions. In light of these findings, we concluded that serial dependence biases were present but did not differ according to whether participants saw a whole or half-body. Therefore, serial dependence biases (in contrast to regression to the mean errors) were not meaningfully contributing to the poorer discrimination of top-half only bodies, observed in Fig. 2.

Discussion

In Experiment 1, we aimed to determine whether body size judgment accuracy benefits from viewing the whole-body shape or if the top-half or bottom-half of a body image is sufficient for such judgments. Our findings indicate that the lower half of the body provides sufficient information, enabling size judgments with the same level of accuracy as those made when the whole-body was presented. Viewing the top-half of a body, however, led to significantly greater size judgment errors, evidenced by larger regression to the mean errors. This performance-based analysis provides evidence that viewing the bottom-half of a body allows for equivalent accuracy as when a full-body image is seen. This is consistent with the idea that specific features of a body image—rather than the full, holistic image of the body per se—are sufficient to judge body size.

Experiment 2

Although Experiment 1 indicated that the bottom half of a body image was sufficient to make body judgments as accurately as when the full body was shown, it remains unclear whether a single body part (e.g., hip width) or multiple body parts (e.g., hip width and thigh gap) within that bottom-half of body are used and integrated by our visual system to judge size and shape. In Experiment 2, we aimed to clarify which body part/s in the lower half of a body are used to judge body size. We chose to focus on two possible regions, based on previous research [22]—the inner and outer thigh. In addition to being areas of the body proposed by previous research to be important for judgments, the focus on these features generally aligns with principles from object perception theory [23, 69, 70], which suggests that curved features (both convex and concave) around the object’s center are particularly important for coding shape. Based on these literatures suggesting both thigh features to be important, it was hypothesized that viewing either thigh area alone (i.e., the inner or outer thigh only) would lead to significantly less accurate body size judgments, compared with viewing the whole body. Alternatively, if a single feature contained in our restricted-view stimuli (e.g., width across hips in the outer thigh only condition) is sufficient to make body size judgments, then it is hypothesized that judgments of body size when viewing a single thigh feature/region would produce similar levels of judgment accuracy compared with the whole-body stimuli. We were also interested in how the relative importance of thigh features varied across different body size categories.

Experiment 2 results

Data screening

Prior to the analyses, each participant’s data were screened, and outliers were identified using the same techniques as Experiment 1. Data from 11 participants were removed, as they were unable to minimally differentiate between body size categories. Across the 21 image conditions (including mean body size judgments and serial dependence difference scores), fifty-eight individual data points were identified as outliers. These were subsequently winsorised using Tukey’s [62] +/− 1 rule [63]. All variables were evaluated for normality and all variables used satisfied guidelines for skew <|2.0| and kurtosis <|9.0| [64], and all assumptions were met for analyses. In cases where sphericity assumptions were violated in our ANOVA analysis, we interpreted results using Huynh–Feldt adjusted values [63].

Descriptive statistics

Participants’ BMI (M = 22.51, SD = 3.40, range 16.89–32.56), indicated that, on average, participants were in the normal to healthy body range. The EDEQ global scores of the participants (M = 1.99, SD = 1.59, range from 0 to 6) were comparable to Australian norms for young adult women [65]. On the DASS-21 questionnaire, participants’ scores indicated mild depressive symptoms (M = 4.92, SD = 4.44, range 0–19), mild to moderate anxiety symptoms (M = 5.10, SD = 4.84, range 0–19), and normal to mild stress symptoms (M = 7.07, SD = 4.59, range 0–20). These were slightly elevated DASS-21 scores in comparison to Australian norms for young-adults [66]. Thus, although the sample of participants indicated elevated levels of distress, they mostly represented a stereotypical sample of young adult females used in similar previous literatures of body image [19, 37, 68] and similar to participants in Experiment 1.

Comparing body size judgments for inner-thigh, outer-thigh, and whole-body conditions

To formally determine whether the experimental manipulation (i.e., whole-body, inner-thigh and outer-thigh) had any impact on our dependent variables (i.e., body size judgments) a 3 (body type condition: whole-body, outer-thigh, inner-thigh) × 7 (body size category: 1–7) within-subjects ANOVA (see Fig. 3A) was conducted. The result revealed a significant main effect of body size category, F(2.17, 250.36) = 1975.86, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.945, indicating that participants were able to distinguish between the seven body size categories. The main effect of body type condition was also significant, F(1.81, 208.104) = 31.656, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.216, suggesting systematic differences in judgments between body type conditions. However, there was also a significant interaction between body size category and body type condition, F(6.82, 784.29) = 129.01, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.529, indicating that the influence from viewing different body parts varied across body size categories. To help interpret these effects, Fig. 3B illustrates the difference scores between the whole-body, and the inner- and outer-thigh conditions, plotted by body size category. For thin bodies (categories 1–3), viewing only the inner or outer thigh region led to a body being judged larger, compared with the whole body. However, for larger bodies (categories 5–7), viewing only the inner or outer thigh region led to the body being judged smaller, compared with the whole body. Like Experiment 1, we explored the cause of the differences between the three conditions by unpacking the bodyline task into two underlying sources of perceptual bias: regression to the mean and serial dependence.

Changes in body size judgment accuracy related to regression to the mean

As in Experiment 1, to estimate regression to the mean for each body type condition we fit a separate linear regression to the data in each condition. Data from each body type condition was a very good fit to the linear model (whole-body: R2 = 0.98; outer-thigh: R2 = 0.96; inner-thigh: R2 = 0.98; see Fig. 3). Consistent with Experiment 1 and prior research [19, 20, 36, 37, 68], the linear regression slope for the whole-body stimuli was 0.75 (95% CI [0.72, 0.77]). The outer-thigh condition produced a slope of 0.56 (95% CI [0.54, 0.59]) and the inner-thigh condition produced a slope of 0.52 (95% CI [0.50, 0.54]), indicating much larger judgment errors in both part-body conditions, relative to the whole-body condition. To formally compare regression to the mean error magnitude across body type conditions, we again used the extra sums-of-squares F-test. The F-test comparison between the whole-body and outer-thigh condition favored two models with different slopes, F(1, 1620) = 152.8, p < 001, ηp2 = 0.086, with a more shallow slope/greater errors in the outer-thigh condition, relative to the whole-body condition. Similarly, the F-test between the whole-body and inner-thigh conditions favored two models with different slopes, F(1,1620) = 262, p < 0.139, ηp2 = 0.139 with a more shallow slope/greater errors in the inner-thigh condition, relative to the whole-body condition. Taken together this pair of findings demonstrates that judgments of body size are significantly less accurate when we are presented with a single body part around the thigh region, compared to when viewing a whole body. This finding supports Irvine et al. [22] and the multiple features hypothesis that judgments of body size would be less accurate (compared with whole bodies) when participants could only see one thigh feature.

We also compared regression to the mean errors across the two part-body conditions. The F test favored two models, differing in slope, F(1,1620) = 7.09, p = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.004. This difference indicates that viewing the inner thigh only led to a shallower slope/larger errors in size judgment compared with when only the outer thigh was visible. We note here that the inner-and outer-thigh conditions differed in the size of body parts presented (outer thigh regions are intuitively greater in size or length) and the implications of this for our findings are considered in the general discussion.

Changes in body size judgment accuracy related to serial dependencies

The serial dependence data was transformed using the same procedure outlined in Experiment 1 (see Fig. 4).

The fit of the D1 function to each body type condition was again strong (whole-body: R2 = 0.95; outer-thigh: R2 = 0.95; inner-thigh: R2 = 0.90) and the solved parameters for amplitude (A, the magnitude) were all positive (whole-body: A = 0.43, 95% CI [0.39, 0.48]; outer-thigh: A = 0.47, 95% CI [0.40, 0.54]; inner-thigh: A = 0.64, 95% CI [0.54, 0.75]). Thus, data from the three body type conditions were consistent with serial dependence induced biases in perception. The breadth of biases (σ) for the whole-body condition was 1.68, 95% CI [1.82 to 1.54], for the outer-thigh condition it was 2.23, 95% CI [2.54, 1.98] and for the inner-thigh condition it was 2.72, 95% CI [2.38, 3.20], where larger values indicate a broader range of serial dependence biases (i.e. occurring across a larger range of trial to trial size differences).

To assess differences in the magnitude and range of serial dependence across the three body type conditions, we compared their respective amplitude (A) or sigma (σ) parameters in separate analyses. For amplitude, the extra sums-of-squares F-test consistently favored two models, differing in amplitude. For the whole-body and inner-thigh conditions F(1,2544) = 17.26, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.008, the two model solution indicated greater serial dependence bias in the inner-thigh condition. For the whole-body and outer-thigh conditions, F(1,2544) = 297.8, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.105, the two model solutions indicated greater bias when only the outer thigh was visible. Finally, the comparison between the two part-body conditions also favored two models, F(1,2544) = 8.05, p = 0.005, ηp2 = 0.003, with slightly greater serial dependence bias when only the inner thigh was visible.

With regards to the range across which serial dependence biases occur, the F-test comparing sigma (σ) favored two models, differing in sigma, in all comparisons. Comparing the whole-body and inner-thigh condition, F(1,2544) = 38.71, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.015, the preferred two model solutions indicated a broader range of biases (larger σ) when only the inner thigh was seen. Comparing the whole-body and outer-thigh conditions, F(1,2544) = 297.8, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.105, the preferred two model solution indicated that there was also a broader range of serial dependence biases when only the outer thigh was seen. Finally we compared the two thigh conditions, F(1,2544) = 4.39, p = 0.037, ηp2 = 0.002, with the preferred two model solution indicating the inner-thigh visible condition produced a slightly broader range of judgment biases. Overall, the magnitude and range of serial dependence induced biases in size judgments for both part-body conditions being larger than for the whole-body conditions, consistent with Irvine et al. [22] and our multiple feature hypothesis that body size judgment accuracy would be lower when participants could not view both the inner and outer thigh regions together.

Discussion

Experiment 2 aimed to investigate which specific aspects of the bottom half of a body are crucial for accurate body size judgments, focusing particularly on the inner and outer thigh regions. This approach built on our previous finding that the bottom half of the body provides sufficient information for size estimation (compared with whole bodies) and sought to pinpoint the key area/s around the thigh region that people utilize when making body size judgments. Our findings demonstrate that viewing either the inner- or outer- thigh region only resulted in significantly worse (i.e. more error or bias) body size judgments in comparison to the whole body, consistent with our multiple features hypothesis. Our follow-up analyses then showed that both regression to the mean and serial dependence biases appear to contribute to the increase in overall judgment error. This result held across the broad range of body sizes tested- i.e. both thigh regions appear important, whether judging large or small bodies. We also found slightly larger errors for the inner-thigh condition relative to the outer-thigh condition.

General discussion

This study aimed to identify the critical body features that underpin judgments of female bodies. We investigated this using the bodyline task to uncover how body size judgments and underlying sources of perceptual bias (serial dependence and regression to the mean) differed when viewing various body regions/parts. In Experiment 1, we investigated whether accurate body size judgments required viewing the whole body (i.e., evidence of holistic integration) or if half of the body provided sufficient information (i.e., evidence of parts-processing integration). The results of Experiment 1 showed that viewing the bottom-half of the body resulted in judgments as accurate as those for the whole body, suggesting that bodies are processed in a parts-based manner rather than holistically. These findings compliment previous research by Alexi et al. [20] indicating parts-based processing of bodies, while contrasting with Brooks et al. [18] suggestion of holistic processing. Furthermore, this finding also indicates that the bottom-half of the body contains the necessary information to judge size. However, this is still a coarse interpretation as the bottom half of a body contains multiple potential features, such as hip width, inner thigh gap, outer thigh curvature etc. Therefore, Experiment 2 sought to determine if a combination of lower body features, as opposed to a specific candidate feature (e.g., width across the outer thighs) were critical for accurate body size judgments. If multiple features are integrated, it was hypothesized that viewing only the inner or outer thigh would lead to less accurate judgments than viewing the whole body. Alternatively, if a single feature provides sufficient information, one thigh region could produce similar accuracy to the whole body.

The results from Experiment 2 showed that viewing only the inner or outer thigh did not provide enough information for accurate size judgments, equivalent to those for the whole body. These findings indicate that both thigh features need to be visible and perceptually integrated to make accurate size judgments and argues against the use of a single critical feature (such as width across outer thighs). Our findings align with previous research on important body features for size judgments that highlight that multiple features are required. For instance, Irvine et al. [22] found that accurate observers attended to multiple body parts, particularly the torso edges and thigh regions. Similarly, George et al. [23] highlighted the importance of the lower and mid torso regions. Our study complements these findings and add to this literature by showing that the importance of both thigh features is relatively consistent across a broad range of body sizes.

Overall, the findings from Experiment 1 and 2 add to the existing literature cataloging the specific characteristics of body stimuli that can substantially influence size judgments, such as three dimensionality [40]; internal features [19] and color [71]. Our results also reveal how errors in body size judgments change across different body sizes, with judgment errors occurring in the opposite directions for small and large bodies. Specifically, we observed large over estimation errors for the smallest bodies (categories 1 & 2) and large under estimation errors for largest bodies (categories 6 & 7). These patterns of errors were further exacerbated when viewing only the top-half of a body in Experiment 1, or a single thigh region in Experiment 2. This pattern for greatest error for non-average body sizes effectively mirrors evidence in the literature, with some studies reporting greater errors for larger body sizes [36, 40] and others finding larger errors for smaller body sizes [19] but judgments of average sized bodies appear relatively accurate. Our findings reiterate that more extreme body sizes are generally more challenging to judge accurately, possibly due to individuals having less everyday experience processing these different body types, but possibly also related to them requiring more extreme responses on the measurement scale.

The bodyline task allows for the investigation of judgment accuracy across multiple body sizes; hence it is important to consider how specific physical features may contribute differently across varying sizes. One feature that changes significantly is the thigh gap, which is the space between the inner thighs when standing with feet together. This feature is particularly interesting because its presence and visibility can vary significantly across different body sizes and shapes. For smaller bodies (i.e., categories 1 to 3), a visible gap between the thighs serves as a reliable cue for smaller size. However, as body size increases, this gap diminishes and is no longer present (such as in category 5 and above, See Fig. 5). Despite this consideration, our results indicate that judgments of small and large bodies were equally compromised when viewing the inner thigh only, even though this feature diminishes in larger bodies. One possible explanation is that other types of surface information retained in the images, such as cellulite, volume, shadows and absence of visible bones, may assist with size judgments for larger bodies. This concept is supported by research from Alexi et al. [68], which compared body size judgment accuracy between computer-generated and real bodies. They found that computer-generated bodies were more difficult to discriminate in size, in comparison to real bodies. This difficulty was interpreted as being due to the computer-generated bodies lacking textural details (i.e., cellulite, shadows, visible presence of bones), resulting in increased error in size judgments, including serial dependence induced errors.

Our findings also have potential implications for understanding body size perception and its relation to body image disturbances and integration theories. Our results suggest that accurate body size judgments rely on the integration of multiple visual features, particularly from the lower body region. To extend on our current research, future studies could examine the links between perceptual accuracy of size judgments (based on lower body features) specifically in clinical populations with eating disorders. While our exploratory analyses (reported in supplementary materials) touched on the relationship between accuracy and eating disorder symptoms (with no significant relationships identified) a more targeted investigation of clinical populations could provide deeper insights. This approach would allow for a direct comparison of perceptual accuracy between individuals diagnosed with eating disorders and healthy controls, potentially revealing more pronounced differences and clinical implications. Further, individuals exhibiting elevated body image disturbance are often themselves very thin (e.g. anorexia) or overweight (e.g. binge eating disorder) and it remains to be seen if their eating disorder symptoms relate to any difficulty in integrating multiple features to judge size. This approach also aligns with the weak central coherence theory, which suggests that individuals with eating disorders may have a bias towards local rather than global processing [72]. Considering this in relation to body size judgments, it is possible that individuals with eating disorders may not be effectively integrating body parts for perception. Future research could also employ eye-tracking methodologies, akin to Irvine et al. [22], to investigate whether individuals with eating disorders show different patterns of visual attention when judging body size, compared to control groups.

This study focused exclusively on female body stimuli and participants. It would be valuable to replicate this experiment with a male population to determine if the lower body is equally vital for accurate size judgments in males. Previous research has shown that the upper body is typically idealized or focused on in male populations [53]. Specifically, men typically idealize a mesomorphic shape (i.e., having a robust muscular body shape) and believe that women prefer muscular physiques in their ideal male partner [54–57, 73]. Studies have validated this belief as women generally rate men with more developed pectoral muscles and an inverted triangle torso shape as more physically attractive compared to men with less muscular upper bodies [58]. Thus, the most desirable male body shape emphasizes muscle mass and physical bulk, particularly in the upper body, which researchers refer to as the muscular mesomorph ideal. Therefore, it is possible that the critical features for accurate size judgments may differ between males and females. Such a comparison could provide further insights into gender-specific aspects of body size perception and potentially inform tailored approaches for addressing body image concerns in different populations.

It is important to acknowledge several factors that may have influenced our findings. One consideration is related to the masking technique used to isolate specific body regions in Experiment 2. While we view our natural image set and broad range of body sizes as a strength, it caused a challenge in consistently masking the inner and outer thigh regions across the different body sizes and poses (See Fig. 5). For instance, smaller bodies typically exhibited a larger gap between the thighs, while larger bodies often had no visible thigh gap. These anatomical differences made it challenging to apply a uniform masking approach (i.e., the mask was the same size across all body stimuli, however, would mask different amounts of the body representation depending on pose and body size differences), and may have introduced some variability in our results, particularly when comparing judgments across different body sizes. This limitation highlights the complexity of isolating specific body features while maintaining consistency across a diverse range of body sizes and shapes. We also acknowledge that our study was unable to quantify the extent to which contextual features contributed to the increased judgment errors that we report. However, based on the existing literature, the removal of contextual visual cues around body features (such as cellulite, shadows, bone structure, etc.) likely plays a role. Further research could explore alternative masking techniques to isolate features differently (such as the variable sized bubble-masking technique; [22]).

Despite these considerations, our study had several strengths. The use of the bodyline task and masking paradigm allowed us to measure multiple error types (regression to the mean and serial dependence) concurrently. This approach provides a methodology for investigating the perceptual processes involved in body size judgment ability and goes beyond simply reporting judgment changes, to also assess the possible underlying perceptual causes of performance change. To that end, the current findings add to a growing volume of studies demonstrating the utility of assessing regression to the mean and serial dependence biases when considering factors that influence task accuracy overall [37, 39–42, 44, 49] and within specific populations [20, 50]. One discrepancy that is apparent is that while regression to the mean and serial dependence appear independent processes during body size judgments [37], the two biases appear to be related for several other visual processes and judgments [39, 42, 49]. There is reason to believe the nature of this relationship may be process specific. For instance, using analogous magnitude estimation tasks and analysis, researchers have demonstrated a functional relationship between the two biases in numerosity perception [39] but no association between them in body size perception [37]. Alexi and colleagues [37] suggested that estimates of body size may not reflect an optimal perceptual encoding strategy, and that other factors, may also contribute to the large central tendency biases. Alternatively, the serial dependence bias observed on the bodyline task appears relatively small, which may work to obscure any relationship. There is some evidence for this when inspecting individual variations in each bias in Fig. 1C. While nearly all participants exhibit regression to the mean, a subset of participants do not exhibit serial dependence, implying a weaker bias by comparison, at least in our paradigm. Studying body size judgments through another paradigm or assessing the relationship between these two biases in person perception more broadly, could assist our understanding on this discrepancy. Returning to our specific context, the bodyline task is particularly valuable because it allows for the assessment of judgments across a broad range of body sizes, from very small to very large, something seldom done in a single study. This range is crucial for understanding how perception may vary across the spectrum of body sizes, rather than focusing solely on average or idealized body types. Further, our findings underscore the importance of using realistic body stimuli, rather than computer-generated or schematic figures to enhance the ecological validity. The complexity of cues used in natural body size judgments suggests that simplified or schematic images may not capture the nuances of body size perception [19].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that accurate judgments of female body size do not require the whole body to be visible but does require a view of multiple visual features. Across a broad range of body sizes, the required features for accurate body size perception appear to reside within the lower body region, specifically the combination of hip width, thigh gap, and outer thigh contours. This emphasizes the need to consider multiple visual features when studying how body size is judged rather than focusing on a single feature. The results contribute to the growing understanding of the perceptual factors that influence body size estimation, underscoring the complexity of this process. By investigating the visual information used in body size judgments, this research provides insights that may inform future studies on body image and size perception and of differences in perception within those exhibiting body image disturbances.

Experiment 1 methods

Participants

One hundred and thirty undergraduate students from the University of Western Australia were recruited to participate in the experiment. Participants were recruited from two separate sources. Participants from the first recruitment source were students enrolled in a 3rd year research topics course who voluntarily participated as part of their coursework. To achieve a larger sample size, additional participants were recruited from the broader undergraduate student population, who received course credit for their participation. Although the first cohort eventually discussed the study findings to complete a course assessment, at the time of participation, all participants were entirely naïve to the specific experimental aims and manipulations.

We analyzed and present data only from participants who identified as female, for the following reasons. First, there are known gender differences in body size judgments [59, 60] and our paradigm only involved female body images, consistent with most previous research examining perceptual biases and body image disturbances [24, 36, 37, 40, 68]. Second, due to the nature of psychology cohorts from which we recruited, our gender ratio of participants was heavily skewed towards female (88%), precluding any meaningful analysis of gender differences. Therefore, although they participated as part of a course activity, 15 participants who identified as male were excluded from the dataset. Participant data were also removed from the final dataset if participants were aged outside of the 17–25 year old study requirement (n = 5), had missing or incomplete data (n = 5), or could not discriminate between body size categories (n = 5; see Results for further explanation). We aimed to collect 100 participants data; however, with the above exclusions the final sample was 99 participants. A sensitivity analysis in G*Power was run prior to recruitment that showed a sample of 100 participants would be sufficient to detect potentially small effect sizes, f = 0.08, with an alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.80 [74]. The experimental procedure was approved by the institutional Human Research Ethics Committee at the University of Western Australia, Protocol 2021/ET000004, and the experiment was performed in accordance with their guidelines and regulation. The experimental program and anonymized data are available on OSF: https://osf.io/w7nc6/ [75].

Materials

Apparatus

Participants used their own computers to complete the experiment. The experimental task was programmed and run within Inquisit 6 [76] software, while the questionnaires were administered through Qualtrics [77]. Subsequently, the data was transformed in Matlab [78] and then analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9 [79] and IBM SPSS statistics [80] software.

Stimuli

In Experiment 1, there were three stimulus types: whole-body stimuli, top-half stimuli, and bottom-half stimuli. The whole-body stimuli were the same as those used by Alexi and colleagues [37] (see example in Fig. 6A). The set was comprised of 35 color images depicting front facing, real, female bodies (399 × 390 pixels), clothed in either swimwear or underwear. The images of the bodies were depicted with their heads removed to avoid attention to faces and/or differences in facial attractiveness [81, 82]. The female bodies represented seven distinct body size categories, ranging from extremely thin (category 1) to extremely large (category 7), and categories of images were those established by Alexi and colleagues [37]. Five images were used to represent each size category, resulting in 35 female body stimuli in total. To maximize experimental control and to enable a direct comparison between conditions, the other half-body conditions depicting only parts of the whole body (which we termed “restricted-view”) were made using the same 35 whole-body images.

Example stimuli. Note. Example images used in the sets of A whole-body stimuli, B top-half stimuli, and (C) bottom-half stimuli. Note that the images presented here were not used in the experiment but serve as examples of the stimuli used in the experiment (Figure A was sourced from Pexels, a copyright free photo website; B & C were adapted in the same way as experimental top and bottom-half stimuli)

Restricted-view body stimuli were created using Adobe Photoshop (Inc., 2019). The “half” of each image was determined from the navel; top-half stimuli were created by occluding everything below the navel, whereas bottom-half stimuli were created by occluding everything above the navel. We chose the navel as a cut-off in order to isolate the waist from the hips in our half body conditions, as the waist-to-hip ratio is suggested to be a crucial feature combination for judging female attractiveness and body size [25, 26, 58].

The bodyline task

The bodyline task [37] was used to assess the accuracy of participants’ perceptual judgments of female body size. On each trial, a single female body stimulus was presented for 250 ms, and participants were required to judge the perceived size (see Fig. 7 for a visual representation of the bodyline task). To make their response, participants clicked on the “bodyline”: a visual analog scale that consisted of an unmarked solid line (scored linearly from 1 to 7). This scale was displayed throughout the experiment, alongside anchor body images that were offset by one additional unit (1/7th of the scale) on each end of the bodyline. Anchor images depicted a very small body on the left and a very large body on the right; anchor images were not used as experimental stimuli in the experiment and depicted body sizes that were more extreme than those used in the experiment.

Bodyline trial example. Note. A visual representation of the bodyline task. The bodyline task required participants to judge the size of CG body stimuli that were presented for 250 ms. Participants recorded their body size estimations by left-clicking their mouse along the bodyline, which showed an extreme body anchor displaced from each end of the scale. The anchor images were more extreme in size than all the body images presented throughout the bodyline task. The bodyline was continuously presented throughout the task. The body images presented here were not those shown in the experiment and were created using CG imagery software, Poser (Smith Micro Software, 2015). For copyright reasons, these are CGI image illustrations of the bodies used in the bodyline task

The task consisted of 14 practice trials, followed by nine blocks of 50 experimental trials (450 experimental trials in total). This design allowed us to effectively run the typical 150-trial bodyline task for each body type condition (top-half, bottom-half, or whole-body). We blocked the experiment by body type condition (three blocks total) but the order of the three conditions was counterbalanced across participants. Practice trials included two example stimuli from each of the seven body size categories (i.e., randomized to select stimuli from the three body type conditions); hence encompassing the full spectrum of body sizes participants would view in the task, while also introducing the three body types. Practice trials presented the seven body sizes in a fixed, randomized, order for each participant. In the main bodyline task, the images from each of the seven body size categories were also presented in a fixed order. This sequence ensured that in each run of 50 trials, each body size category was both preceded and followed by each other category, including its own (49 combinations plus an initial trial). For example, in each block, a certain body image size category was equally likely to be preceded by another body image of the same size category as it was to be preceded by any of the other body size categories. Thus, across the bodyline task, there was an equal number of every trial-to-trial body size combination, which was important to balance the trial categories across each block and also especially for assessing serial dependencies from image to image.

Questionnaires

Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale 21 (DASS-21; [83])

The DASS-21 is a self-report questionnaire designed to measure the frequency and severity of negative emotions depression, anxiety, and stress experienced over the past week. Depression and anxiety are the most frequent comorbid diagnoses in eating disorders [84, 85], and research has shown that higher impairment in anxiety and depression is associated with more severe eating disorder symptoms [86]. Consequently, we incorporated the DASS-21 as a way to characterize our sample demographics, recognizing the relevance of these attributes to issues related to body disturbance. The questionnaire consists of 21 items (7 per subscale) that are rated on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (did not apply to me at all) to 3 (applied to me very much, or most of the time), with higher scores signifying greater levels of depression, anxiety, or stress. Example items include “I found it difficult to relax,” “I felt life was meaningless,” and “I felt close to panic.” In previous research, the DASS-21 has demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.82–0.97; [83, 87]). The Cronbach alpha for each of three subscales in the present study was good: depression: α = 0.91, anxiety: α = 0.84, and stress: α = 0.82, indicating strong reliability.

Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire 6 (EDEQ; [88])

EDEQ is a widely used self-report questionnaire designed to assess both attitudes towards and frequency of eating disorder symptoms experienced in the past 28 days. The questionnaire includes four subscales relating to dietary restraint, eating concerns, weight concerns, and shape concerns, which are derived from 22 items rated on a 7-point rating scale ranging from 0 (no days) to 7 (everyday). An additional 6 items are used to measure the frequency of engaging in eating disorder behaviors; however, these data were not included in the analyses. The four subscales were summed to produce a total score, and higher scores signified greater levels of eating disorder symptomatology. An example item included: “have you felt fat?”. In previous research, the EDEQ has demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency (α = 0.70–0.93; [89]), concurrent and criterion validity, and appropriately distinguishes clinical from non-clinical cases [65]. The Cronbach alpha for the total EDEQ score in the present study was α = 0.96, indicating high reliability.

The Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ; [90])

The AQ is a widely used self-report questionnaire used to measure autistic traits in adults. Autistic characteristics have been shown to relate to eating disorder symptoms [91]; for example, research indicates strong relationships between autistic traits (e.g., cognitive inflexibility, theory of mind, impaired global processes, and emotion intelligence) and anorexia nervosa symptoms [92–95]. Additionally, individuals with eating disorders show higher autistic traits in comparison to healthy controls [92]. Consequently, we incorporated the AQ as a way to characterize our sample demographics in Experiment 1, recognizing the relevance of these attributes to issues related to body disturbance. The AQ consists of 50 descriptive statements, with participants rating their agreement with each item using a 4-point scale (definitely agree, slightly agree, slightly disagree, definitely disagree). For each participant, we calculated the three domains of the AQ [67], social skills (AQ_social skill; maximum score of 52), communication and mind reading (AQ_comm/mind; maximum score of 32), and attention to details and patterns (AQ_detail/patterns; maximum score of 28), as well as a total AQ score (AQ_total; maximum score of 200). We adopted a 1–4 scoring strategy as recommended by Austin [96], which results in total scores ranging from 50 to 200. Higher scores reflected a higher level of autistic traits. Previous research indicates that the AQ has good reliability (α = 0.88, 0.62, and 0.73, respectively; 68), and the Cronbach alpha for the total AQ score in the present study was α = 0.85, indicating satisfactory reliability.

Demographic questionnaire

A brief questionnaire was used to collect basic demographic information, such as age, gender and self-reported height and weight. Participant’s height and weight were collected to calculate their body mass index (BMI) using the formula: BMI = kg/m2.

Procedure

Participants started by reading an information sheet outlining the details of the experiment and gave their digital informed consent. They used their computer and mouse/track pad to complete the experiment. Before commencing the task, participants were instructed that they would view bodies for a very brief time (250 ms) and asked to judge the size of female bodies along the “bodyline” (visual analog scale). Furthermore, participants were told that the anchors on either edge of the bodyline were more extreme (smaller or larger) than any of the body images to be shown in the experiment. Once participants read the instructions, they completed the practice trials, followed by the experimental trials in the bodyline task with the three body type conditions. After completion of the bodyline tasks, participants were redirected to Qualtrics and completed the demographic questionnaire. Participants then completed the DASS-21, EDEQ, and AQ. Lastly, participants were provided with a debrief sheet and given the opportunity to provide feedback on their experience of the experiment. The experiment was self-paced but took approximately 50 min for participants to complete.

Experiment 2 methods

Participants

One hundred and forty-five female undergraduate students from The University of Western Australia were recruited for Experiment 2. Participants’ data were removed if they had incomplete data (n = 15), did not identify as female (n = 3), or could not discriminate between body size categories (n = 11; see Experiment 2 Results for further explanation). This resulted in a final sample size of 116 participants.

We aimed to recruit 100 participants in our final sample. This was calculated with a sensitivity analysis in G*Power, which showed that a sample of 100 participants would be sufficient to detect potentially small effect sizes, f = 0.08, with an alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.80 [74]. The experimental procedure was approved by the same ethics protocol as Experiment 1 (Protocol 2021/ET000004) and was performed in accordance with the University of Western Australia’s guidelines and regulation. Participants were reimbursed for their participation with university course credit. The experimental program and anonymized data are available on OSF: https://osf.io/w7nc6/ [75].

Materials

The materials in Experiment 2 were the same as Experiment 1, with exceptions as outlined below.

Body stimuli

The whole-body stimuli were the same as in Experiment 1 (see Fig. 8A). To make the restricted-view body stimuli for Experiment 2, we used the bottom-half stimuli from Experiment 1. To isolate the thigh regions, our challenge was to design a fixed size mask that could consistently, effectively isolate the target candidate feature across body stimuli. This was made difficult due to largescale variations in pose across our natural body images (e.g., crossed legged, open stance), so we chose to remove body content below the knee, including the feet, to allow for such restricted-view body stimuli types to be made. The outer-thigh stimuli included the two outer thigh regions without any inner thigh information being present, and a single white rectangular mask (180 × 90 pixels) was applied over the area spanning the crotch to the knees. Through trial and error, this mask size was determined to be effective on each of the 35 body images (see Fig. 8B). The inner-thigh stimuli included the inner thigh region without any outer thigh information being present, and two rectangular masks (180 × 30 pixels in size, separated by a fixed distance, of 35 pixels) were positioned like a saddle, over the area of body extending from the waist/navel to the knees. Through trial and error, this mask configuration was determined to be effective on each of the 35 body images (see Fig. 8C).

The bodyline task

The bodyline task worked in the same manner as described in Experiment 1, except the stimuli presented in the three body type conditions now represented whole-body, outer-thigh, and inner-thigh conditions.

Questionnaires

We did not include the AQ in Experiment 2 due to experimental time constraints, but otherwise used the same questionnaires as in Experiment 1. The Cronbach alpha for the EDEQ was 0.92 and for each of DASS-21 subscales in this experiment was good: depression: α = 0.91, anxiety: α = 0.90, and stress: α = 0.88, indicating good reliability.

Procedure

The procedure was the same as Experiment 1 and took approximately 40 min to complete.
