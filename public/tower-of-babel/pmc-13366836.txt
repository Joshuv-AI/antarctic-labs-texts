Background

This report describes a critical complication following the first stage of an unplanned ALPPS procedure (Associating Liver Partition and Portal vein Ligation for Staged hepatectomy) and highlights the novelty of using a rescue transsplenic mechanical thrombectomy and balloon dilation to restore flow, demonstrating a successful interventional radiology approach to salvage a complex surgical plan. While ALPPS is designed to induce rapid hypertrophy of the future liver remnant (FLR) [1, 2], the development of portal vein thrombosis (PVT) in the remnant liver can be catastrophic, jeopardizing the success of the second stage [3].

Case presentation

We present the case of a 68-year-old male with a history of rectal adenocarcinoma (stage pT2) previously treated with endoanal resection, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Follow-up computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed a hepatic lesion in segment 5, measuring 6 cm in maximum diameter, infiltrating the right portal vein distal to the origin of the segment 4 branch. Biopsy findings were consistent with pancreatobiliary-type adenocarcinoma (Fig. 1). The case was discussed by the multidisciplinary team, and a right hepatectomy was indicated.

Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (A) and computed tomography (B) scans. A 6 cm hepatic lesion is observed in segment 5 (white arrows). The portal branch corresponding to liver segment 4, originating from the right portal vein, is indicated (blue arrow)

Intraoperatively, the tumor appeared to have increased in size and infiltrated the portal branches of segment 4, with insufficient collateralization from the left portal vein (Fig. 2A). Consequently, the first stage of a tourniquet ALPPS procedure was performed (Fig. 2B). The right portal vein was ligated. A 2 cm-deep hepatic transection was carried out 1 cm from the ligamentum teres, and a cotton tape was passed between the middle and left hepatic veins, traversing segment 1. Finally, the right hepatic vein, right hepatic artery, and right hepatic duct were identified and referenced.

A. Intraoperative findings: The lesion (black arrow) infiltrates the portal branches of segment 4 (S4). B. First stage of a tourniquet ALPPS procedure: The right portal vein is ligated (white arrow). The right hepatic vein (blue), right hepatic artery (red), and right hepatic duct (green) are referenced. A 2 cm-deep hepatic transection is performed, and a tape is passed between the middle and left hepatic veins

At our institution, a follow-up CT scan is routinely performed 10 days after the first stage of the ALPPS procedure. If an adequate FLR volume is achieved, the completion hepatectomy is scheduled two weeks after the initial intervention. In the present case, the preoperative CT scan showed a baseline FLR volume—comprising segments 2 and 3—of 506 cc, representing 22% of the total liver volume (TLV). Prior to the second stage, the follow-up CT scan performed 10 days postoperatively revealed a partially occlusive thrombosis of the main portal vein and insufficient volumetric growth, with an FLR of 516 cc (27% of the TLV). The patient had received prophylactic-dose low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) until the diagnosis of thrombosis, at which point therapeutic-dose anticoagulation was initiated, and interventional radiology was indicated and scheduled 48 h later.

Percutaneous transsplenic access was obtained under ultrasound guidance using a Neff set (Cook Medical) with an initial 22G Chiba needle puncture. Portography confirmed the presence of a partially occlusive thrombus within the main portal vein (Fig. 3). Based on angiographic findings and the 10-day evolution of the thrombus, pharmacomechanical thrombectomy was indicated. A total of 90 mg of Alteplase was administered (comprising a 45 mg initial bolus followed by a 45 mg infusion). Mechanical thrombectomy was then performed using the Aspirex device (Becton Dickinson), achieving complete recanalization. The stenotic area was dilated with a 10-mm diameter balloon. Following the embolization protocol to prevent hemorrhagic complications, the splenic parenchymal tract was filled with Embocube Embolization Gelatin (Merit Medical)—a gelfoam slurry mixed with intravenous contrast—as the sheath was withdrawn. Given the heightened risk of bleeding, an Amplatzer Vascular Plug 4 (Abbott) was also deployed for vascular occlusion.

A. Transsplenic portal embolectomy. B. Identification of a partially occlusive thrombus in the main portal vein (arrow). Mechanical thrombectomy was performed following alteplase instillation. C. Balloon dilation of the stenotic area after mechanical thrombectomy. D. Post-thrombectomy portography

A perisplenic hematoma developed at the puncture site within 12 h post-intervention and necessitated selective splenic artery embolization. The patient was discharged after 10 days without further complications, after the progressive reintroduction of therapeutic-dose anticoagulation. However, the patient was readmitted two weeks later with upper gastrointestinal bleeding, hemodynamic instability, and hypotension. A CT angiography revealed bleeding from short gastric vessels arising from the splenic artery, along with 14 cm splenomegaly. Gastroscopy showed a large clot in the gastric fundus. A second selective splenic embolization was performed, resulting in cessation of the bleeding.

Finally, after confirming a FLR volume of 43.7% (762 cc) three weeks following the thrombectomy, the second stage of the ALPPS procedure was carried out (Fig. 4). The patient’s recovery was prolonged due to the development of ascites through the drains and the surgical wound, which persisted for four weeks and required negative pressure wound therapy for definitive resolution.

Second stage of the ALPPS procedure. The right and middle hepatic veins were sectioned. The volume of the future liver remnant was 43.7%

Histopathological examination revealed a moderately differentiated intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, stage pT2N0. After a 24-month follow-up, the patient remains disease-free.

This case highlights transsplenic pharmacomechanical thrombectomy as a viable rescue therapeutic strategy following the first stage of a tourniquet-ALPPS procedure, where the development of PVT led to insufficient hypertrophy of the FLR and potential procedural failure. Following the interventional radiological procedure, adequate compensatory growth was achieved, enabling the successful completion of the hepatectomy.

Discussion

Unplanned ALPPS

The ALPPS technique has been proposed as a method that promotes rapid growth of the liver remnant, thereby increasing resection rates—especially in patients without underlying liver disease [1, 2]. It also shortens the time to surgery compared to non-surgical techniques, such as hepatic and portal vein embolization, although it is associated with higher morbidity and mortality rates [4]. In our case, the intraoperative tumor anatomy appeared to differ from preoperative imaging findings, leading to a choice between proceeding with the ALPPS procedure or reconsidering embolization. An intraoperative conversion to stage 1 ALPPS was decided upon, with the aim of shortening the interval to definitive hepatectomy.

Post-hepatectomy portal vein thrombosis

The incidence of PVT following hepatectomy for primary or metastatic hepatic malignancies is low, with studies reporting rates between 2% and 10% [5–9].

Several factors associated with this complication have been described, including right hepatectomy [5, 6], caudate lobe resection [6], right lateral sectorectomy [7], larger resection volumes [5], concomitant biliary resection or splenectomy [6], and the extent of portal vein resection and type of reconstruction [7, 9]. Intraoperative parameters, such as prolonged operative time, duration of the Pringle maneuver [5, 10], and blood transfusion, have also been implicated. Additionally, clinical and laboratory factors—such as ascites, liver cirrhosis, altered INR and prothrombin time, elevated D-dimer in the early postoperative period [10], or bile leakage [6]—are recognized risk factors. Aside from the unexpected intraoperative conversion to ALPPS, our patient did not present with these established risk factors.

Patients who develop post-hepatectomy PVT face a higher risk of insufficient hepatic regeneration and functional liver failure. Since hepatectomy can lead to increased bleeding risk and coagulopathy, routine anticoagulation in the early postoperative period is not universally practiced [5]. Once a diagnosis of PVT is established, treatment is individualized based on thrombus extent, clinical symptoms, and hemodynamic stability [11], with options ranging from observation and anticoagulation to thrombectomy [7, 11].

To date, no specific literature has been published regarding the management of portal vein thrombosis following the first stage of the ALPPS procedure.

In a multicenter study comprising 62 patients, this complication—alongside insufficient hepatic hypertrophy and mortality—accounted for the cancellation of the second stage in 3 cases (4.8%) [12]. Furthermore, in an analysis of the International ALPPS Registry involving 320 patients, PVT was not reported as a complication during the inter-stage period [13].

Indication for endovascular thrombectomy

According to international guidelines, endovascular thrombectomy for acute or subacute thrombosis in cirrhotic patients is indicated when anticoagulant therapy fails to produce clinical improvement, particularly in cases of suspected intestinal ischemia, presumably secondary to mesenteric venous system obstruction [14, 15]. Furthermore, in patients with an obstruction greater than 50% or involvement of multiple drainage areas, the benefit of recanalization is higher, as portal flow reduction can reach 94% [15], and thrombosis progression may exacerbate portal hypertension or complicate a potential transplant. According to AASLD guidelines, anticoagulation should be considered in non-cirrhotic patients to prevent the development of portal hypertension and intestinal ischemia, noting that the risk of hemorrhage in this patient group is low [12]. However, complete recanalization is achieved in only 40% of cases [16] and a minimum of 4–6 weeks is required to assess the response to treatment; therefore, interventional radiology procedures should be considered in high-risk patients.

Literature regarding endovascular thrombectomy and thrombolysis in non-cirrhotic patients is limited. Case series have reported successful portal thrombus recanalization via the mesenteric axis following hepatobiliary surgery through a small laparotomy [17–19] or via transhepatic portography [20, 21]. The transjugular approach following TIPS placement has also been evaluated, showing optimal recanalization rates, though associated with complications such as encephalopathy or ascites [22]. Recently, Mota C et al. published a series of three cases of portomesenteric mechanical thrombectomy or thrombolysis, two of which were in non-cirrhotic patients without TIPS placement [23].

Transsplenic access has been studied primarily for the management of thrombotic events in cirrhotic patients. Al-Ogaili et al. evaluated transsplenic access for treating portal venous trunk pathology, including thrombectomy, in 30 patients, reporting favorable results and minor complications [24]. This group did not describe hemorrhagic events related to the access site, which was a complication associated with the procedure in our case; however, they did report two cases of hepatic bleeding and portal re-thrombosis. In a comparative study between transhepatic and transsplenic access involving 124 and 24 patients, respectively, Haddad et al. found no significant differences regarding hemorrhagic complications between the two groups, with a major bleeding rate of 4.2% (one patient) [25]. Similarly, Zhu et al. evaluated the safety of transsplenic access for portal vein catheterization in a retrospective study of 46 patients; they reported major hemorrhagic complications in 6.5% of cases, including intraperitoneal hemorrhage in one patient and subcapsular hemorrhage in two, while minor complications occurred in 13% (six patients), specifically splenic subcapsular hemorrhage [26].

Rössle et al. retrospectively compared the outcomes of medical management with heparin versus interventional treatment (transjugular fragmentation and thrombolysis, with or without stent placement) in 65 patients with acute portal vein thrombosis—defined by the authors as treatment initiated within 28 days of symptom onset. They reported a complete response rate of 30% in the anticoagulation group compared to 54% in the interventional group. In the latter, intra-abdominal hemorrhage was the predominant complication [27].

Similarly, Cai et al. retrospectively evaluated the outcomes of aspiration mechanical thrombectomy combined with urokinase-based thrombolysis via transjugular or transhepatic routes. Complete recanalization was achieved in 8 out of 13 patients (61.5%)—including both cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic individuals—presenting with acute thrombosis of the portosplenomesenteric axis that had failed to respond to anticoagulant therapy. Among these, three patients underwent TIPS placement, and one was treated with balloon dilation [28]. Regarding surgical thrombectomy, Kuboki et al. reported a complete recanalization rate of 80% with anticoagulant therapy for cases diagnosed within the first 5 postoperative days and 88.9% for late diagnoses; surgical thrombectomy achieved recanalization rates of 100% and 50%, respectively [6].

In our case, given the radiological findings identifying portal vein thrombosis as the cause of insufficient FLR growth, interventional therapy was considered the primary treatment option. This decision was prompted by the high risk of procedural failure, the potential inability to complete the hepatectomy, and the risk of tumor progression in a patient with cholangiocarcinoma.

The role of splenic embolization

Portal thrombosis in patients without underlying liver disease leads to an increase in blood flow resistance and portal pressure, which can be highly pronounced even in cases of incomplete obstruction [16, 29]. These patients develop collateral communications—both portosystemic, similar to cirrhotic patients, and porto-portal, which reach the intrahepatic circulation and whose long-term consequences remain unknown. In patients undergoing hepatectomy, an excessive or sudden increase in portal pressure constitutes a risk factor for liver failure and can cause congestion of the remnant parenchyma [30]. In a hyperperfused liver, cellular oxygenation is impaired, triggering a state of hypermetabolism and hypoxia [31]. According to the hyperperfusion theory, increased flow ultimately triggers the inhibition of cellular proliferation [32]. In addition to this increased resistance, elevated flow to the portal system can contribute to the development of portal hypertension. Thus, splenic embolization—particularly in cases of hypersplenism—may promote the normalization of portal pressure by reducing this flow. However, the relationship between portal hypertension and splenic flow has not been clearly established [33].

Limitations

As a single-case report, the findings cannot be generalized to all patients undergoing ALPPS who suffer PVT. The biological behavior of the FLR after pharmacomechanical thrombectomy may vary significantly between individuals. Additionally, this procedure requires a highly specialized Interventional Radiology unit and a multidisciplinary team, which may not be available in all hepatobiliary centers, thus limiting its reproducibility.

Conclusions

Transsplenic mechanical thrombectomy performed by interventional radiology is a viable option for the treatment of PVT following the first stage of an ALPPS procedure. In our case, it enabled portal recanalization and facilitated hypertrophy of the FLR.
