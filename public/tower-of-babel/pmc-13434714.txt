Unveiling the novel role of PGAM5 in rewiring metabolism through PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling in acute myelogenous leukemia
Authors: Guangfeng He, Yewei Xiao, Yu Wang, Qian Peng, Shiqiang Hou, Chunjing Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a major clinical challenge, with patients often having poor prognoses, especially in subtypes with high-risk genetic profiles. PGAM5 plays a critical role in the progression of various malignancies; however, its biological function and underlying molecular mechanisms in AML remain unclear. The study aimed to systematically investigate the role and potential mechanisms of PGAM5 in AML. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that PGAM5 expression was significantly upregulated in AML patients compared with healthy controls, and high PGAM5 expression was closely associated with unfavorable prognosis. Further analysis suggested that PGAM5 may be related to the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. By establishing AML cell models with PGAM5 knockdown, functional experiments demonstrated that suppressing PGAM5 expression significantly arrested cell cycle progression, inhibited proliferation, and induced apoptosis. Mechanistic studies indicated that PGAM5 is closely associated with glycolytic metabolism in AML and enhances glycolytic flux through the transcription factor HIF-1α. Upon PGAM5 knockdown, AML cells exhibited significant reductions in glucose consumption, ATP production, and lactate output. Furthermore, treatment with the PI3K activator 740Y-P in PGAM5-knockdown cells provided additional evidence that PGAM5 plays a critical role in supporting AML cell proliferation and metabolic reprogramming, suggesting that PGAM5 may represent a candidate molecule for further functional investigation in AML. In summary, this study elucidates the key role of PGAM5 in metabolic remodeling in AML, suggesting its potential as a novel therapeutic target for AML treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55582-x.Keywords: PGAM5, Glycolysis, Acute myelogenous leukemia, PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, Signaling pathwaySubject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Oncology

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a blood cancer marked by significant clinical and biological diversity. It is defined by the clonal expansion of poorly differentiated myeloid precursors, which impairs normal hematopoiesis and leads to rapid disease progression1. Despite recent advances in novel targeted agents and risk-adapted treatment strategies, AML patients continue to face substantial risks of relapse and poor overall survival, particularly among the elderly and those ineligible for intensive chemotherapy2. Although targeted therapies directed against specific molecular subgroups-such as FLT3 inhibitors (e.g., midostaurin, gilteritinib) and IDH1/2 inhibitors (e.g., ivosidenib, enasidenib)-have improved clinical outcomes in certain patient subsets3, their efficacy remains limited. These constraints underscore the urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets for AML.

Altered energy metabolism is a hallmark of tumor cells, frequently characterized by enhanced aerobic glycolysis, also known as the Warburg effect. The process involves converting glucose to lactate preferentially, even when oxygen is available, and is accompanied by increased glucose uptake and lactate production4,5. Such metabolic reprogramming not only supplies energy to sustain rapid tumor proliferation but also provides intermediate metabolites for the biosynthesis of macromolecules6. Additionally, lactate accumulation creates an acidic microenvironment that promotes tumor invasion and metastasis7. In AML, upregulation of aerobic glycolysis has been shown to maintain mitochondrial integrity and suppress apoptosis in response to therapy-induced metabolic stress8. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is crucial in controlling the metabolism of tumors, and its activation strongly promotes aerobic glycolysis9,10. Consequently, targeting this pathway to modulate glycolysis represents a promising therapeutic strategy against tumors.

The development and progression of AML are closely associated with the aberrant activation of multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, which drive metabolic reprogramming-particularly enhanced glycolysis-and thereby accelerate disease progression11. In metabolically active AML cells, key glycolytic enzymes like hexokinase 2 (HK2), pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) are often increased12. HIF-1α, a master regulator of the cellular response to hypoxia, can activate a range of genes that promote glycolysis and tumor progression, making it a potential therapeutic target11,13. Recent studies further support the strong link between glycolysis and AML progression14, suggesting that modulating glycolytic pathways may offer novel strategies to suppress AML development and improve patient prognosis.

Phosphoglycerate Mutase Family Member 5 (PGAM5) serves as a serine/threonine phosphatase and is mainly localized to the mitochondria, where it participates in the regulation of mitophagy, cell death, and other cellular processes. Recent studies have further revealed its multifaceted roles in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and modulating inflammatory responses15,16. Through alternative splicing, PGAM5 exists in two isoforms-a long form (PGAM5-L) and a short form (PGAM5-S)-predominantly localized to the outer mitochondrial membrane, with potential dynamic localization at sites of inter-membrane contact. This structural diversity and variable subcellular distribution underlie its functional complexity17,18. As a multifunctional protein, PGAM5 plays key roles in regulating mitochondrial dynamics, quality control, and programmed cell death19. Nevertheless, the specific function of PGAM5 in metabolic reprogramming in AML remains unclear.

This study aimed to investigate the role of PGAM5 in aerobic glycolysis and disease progression in AML. Our findings demonstrated that PGAM5 expression is elevated in AML cells and positively correlated with the activity of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Knockdown of PGAM5 impaired AML cell viability, induced cell cycle arrest, and promoted apoptosis. At the metabolic level, PGAM5 depletion led to significant reductions in glucose consumption, ATP production, and lactate output. Rescue experiments using the PI3K/AKT/mTOR agonist 740Y-P further confirmed that PGAM5 promotes aerobic glycolysis by activating this pathway, thereby driving AML progression (Graphical Abstract). In summary, our study elucidates the critical role of PGAM5 in glycolytic reprogramming in AML, providing new experimental insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying aerobic glycolysis in this malignancy and suggesting PGAM5 as a potential novel target for metabolism-directed therapy in AML.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Analysis of bulk RNA-seq data was performed using the Sangerbox platform, which integrates multiple validated bioinformatics workflows20. First, utilizing the pan-cancer dataset from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), we systematically assessed the differential expression patterns of the PGAM5 gene across various malignancies and their corresponding normal tissues. To clarify the prognostic value of PGAM5 expression levels, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards models were applied to analyze their correlation with patient overall survival. Second, based on the DNA methylation profiles of tumor samples, the DNA stemness score (DNAss), which reflects cancer stem cell-like characteristics, was calculated. Spearman correlation analysis was used to determine the link between PGAM5 expression and the DNAss stemness index. Finally, co-expression analysis of the target gene set was conducted using the GEPIA online database to explore potential correlations between PGAM5 and molecules involved in relevant signaling pathways.

Cell culture and treatments

The THP-1 and MOLM-13 human AML cell lines obtained from ProCell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd, were grown in RPMI-1640 complete medium (Gibco, USA) with an addition of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). Cells were maintained in a standard cell culture incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. Subculturing was performed every 2–3 days at a split ratio of 1:2 to 1:3 to ensure cells remained in the logarithmic growth phase. Cells were treated with 50 µg/mL of the PI3K activator 740Y-P for 48 h. All experiments were conducted using cells in good condition, with low passage numbers and confirmed absence of mycoplasma contamination.

Cell transfection

To establish a PGAM5 knockdown cell model, RNA interference was employed using small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting PGAM5 in THP-1 and MOLM-13 cells. Transfection was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol provided with the transfection reagent (Yisheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). In brief, THP-1 and MOLM-13 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at an appropriate density in culture plates. When cells reached approximately 70% confluence, transfection was initiated. Two groups were included: an experimental group transfected with PGAM5-targeting siRNA (si-PGAM5) and a control group transfected with non-targeting control siRNA (si-Control). Following the recommended protocol, siRNA and transfection reagent were each diluted in a medium without serum and left at room temperature for 5 minutes, and then combined to form siRNA-transfection reagent complexes. The complexes were left at room temperature for another 20 minutes to ensure stable complex formation, after which they were evenly added to the cell culture wells. Six hours after transfection, the medium was replaced with a complete medium to maintain the culture. The knockdown efficiency of PGAM5 was confirmed by Western blot analysis. The siRNA sequences used were as follows: 5’-GCAGUAUUACGAAGACGGA-3’ (si-PGAM5#1), 5’-CGUCCAAGCUGGACCACUA-3‘(si-PGAM5#2), and 5’-GACAGUUACGAGAUCUUCA-3’ (si-PGAM5#3).

Survival analysis

To assess the prognostic role of PGAM5 in AML, we utilized the GEPIA database for online bioinformatic analysis. Within the “Survival Analysis” module, the “Survival Plots” function was selected. “PGAM5” was entered into the gene input field, and the cancer type was set to “LAML”. “Overall Survival” was chosen as the survival endpoint.

Cell viability assay

To assess how PGAM5 knockdown affects cell proliferation and viability, the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was used to evaluate cell survival. THP-1 and MOLM-13 cells, in their logarithmic growth phase, were seeded into 96-well plates and subjected to either PGAM5 knockdown or a control treatment, followed by incubation under appropriate conditions for specified durations. At the designated time points, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was directly added to each well, avoiding bubble formation. The plate was then incubated in a 37℃, 5% CO2 incubator for 2 h in the dark. Following incubation, the absorbance of each well was immediately measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

ATP measurement

THP-1 and MOLM-13 cells were treated with PGAM5 knockdown or control during their logarithmic growth phase and harvested at the specified time points. The cells were rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS and then lysed. As per the protocol, the ATP detection working solution was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37℃ for 5 min. The absorbance of each sample was then measured at 636 nm using a multifunctional microplate reader.

Glucose uptake and lactate production analysis

THP-1 and MOLM-13 cells were seeded at a suitable density in culture plates during their logarithmic growth phase to establish a stable growth state. Subsequently, cells were subjected to PGAM5 knockdown or control treatment as described previously and cultured for an additional 48 h. After incubation, the cell culture supernatant was collected. Glucose consumption was measured using a glucose assay kit (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), and lactate production was determined with a lactate assay kit (Sevier Biotechnology, China), following the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance was measured at the wavelengths specified by the respective kits using a microplate reader.

Western blot analysis

After the treatment, cells were collected, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. The lysates underwent centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4℃, and the supernatant was collected as the total protein sample. Protein concentration was determined by the BCA assay, and samples were adjusted to equal concentrations using lysis buffer and 5× loading buffer, followed by denaturation at 100℃ for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein were loaded for separation by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane using the wet transfer method. Following transfer, the membrane was blocked at room temperature for 2 h with 5% non-fat milk in TBST. The membrane was then incubated overnight at 4℃ using the relevant primary antibodies. After washing the membrane three times with TBST for 10 min each on the next day, it was incubated with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for two hours. The protein bands were visualized post-wash using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent in a gel imaging system, and the intensities of these bands were analyzed with Image J software. All original Western blot bands are included in the supplementary files.

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry using the Annexin V/PI double staining method. Treated cells were stained and analyzed by flow cytometer. By detecting the exposure of phosphatidylserine on the cell surface and membrane integrity, cells were distinguished into live cells, early apoptotic cells, late apoptotic cells, and necrotic cells. In the flow cytometry apoptosis scatter plots, cells in the lower-left quadrant are considered viable cells. Cells in the lower-right quadrant are considered early apoptotic cells. Cells in the upper-right quadrant are considered late apoptotic cells. Cells in the upper-left quadrant are considered necrotic cells.

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by PI staining combined with flow cytometry. Treated cells underwent fixation, RNase digestion, and PI staining to detect intracellular DNA content. Based on DNA fluorescence intensity histograms, NovoExpress software was used to fit and analyze the proportions of cells in the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data in this study are presented as the mean±standard deviation (mean ± SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival curves were generated. Differences between groups were assessed for significance using the log-rank test. To comprehensively evaluate the impact of multiple prognostic factors on survival, multivariate analysis was performed using the Cox proportional hazards regression model. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Elevated PGAM5 as a potential prognostic biomarker in AML patients

To elucidate the clinical relevance of PGAM5 in AML, we first analyzed transcriptomic sequencing data from public databases. The results revealed that PGAM5 expression is widely upregulated across pan-cancer samples and is significantly higher in AML patients compared with healthy individuals (Fig. 1A). To further evaluate the prognostic value of PGAM5, pan-cancer survival analysis indicated that high PGAM5 expression is associated with unfavorable outcomes in multiple cancer types (Fig. 1B). Subsequent survival analysis using the GEPIA database confirmed that elevated PGAM5 expression correlates with significantly shorter overall survival in AML patients (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the relationship between PGAM5 expression and tumor stemness was investigated, demonstrating a positive correlation between high PGAM5 levels and enhanced tumor stemness characteristics in AML (Fig. 1D). The HPA database revealed a trend of high PGAM5 expression across various cancer cell lines (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, Western blot analysis showed that PGAM5 expression levels in KG1A, THP-1, HL60, and MOLM-13 cells were higher than those in bone marrow stromal HS-5 cells (Fig. 1F, G). Collectively, these findings suggest that PGAM5 may be broadly involved in the progression of various malignancies, warranting further investigation into its clinical prognostic significance and functional mechanisms in AML.

PGAM5 regulates proliferation and apoptosis in AML cells

After establishing the clinical link between high PGAM5 expression and poor prognosis in AML patients, we aimed to investigate its functional role. Gene knockdown of PGAM5 was performed in two AML cell lines, THP-1 and MOLM-13, using specific siRNA. Western blot results indicated that si-PGAM5#1 demonstrated the highest knockdown efficiency. Therefore, we selected si-PGAM5#1 as the target for subsequent experiments. (Fig. 2A, B,C, D). To evaluate the effect of PGAM5 on cell proliferation, we performed Western blot and CCK-8 assays. Results demonstrated that PGAM5 knockdown in AML cells reduced expression of the cell proliferation marker Ki67 (Fig. 2E, F,G, H). Furthermore, PGAM5 knockdown led to decreased viability in both THP-1 and MOLM-13 cells (Fig. 2I, J), suggesting that PGAM5 is essential for maintaining AML cell growth. To further assess if its anti-proliferative effect was linked to apoptosis, flow cytometry was conducted after Annexin V/PI staining. The data indicated that PGAM5 knockdown caused a marked rise in the total percentage of early and late apoptotic cells in both THP-1 (Fig. 2K, L) and MOLM-13 (Fig. 2M, N) cells relative to the control group. These findings provide initial evidence that PGAM5 is key in regulating proliferation and apoptosis in AML cells, indicating its possible oncogenic role.

PGAM5 inhibits cell cycle progression in AML cells

To investigate whether PGAM5 influences cell proliferation by regulating the cell cycle, we first conducted a correlation analysis. The results revealed that in AML, PGAM5 expression levels showed significant positive correlations with the expression of multiple key cell cycle proteins, such as CDK4, CDK6, and Cyclin D1 (Fig. 3A). Flow cytometry was used to further explore cell cycle distribution. The results showed that PGAM5 knockdown in THP-1 cells increased the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase and decreased those in the G2/M phase (Fig. 3B, C), indicating that PGAM5 deficiency induces G0/G1 arrest. Consistent with this, a similar cell cycle arrest was observed in MOLM-13 cells upon PGAM5 knockdown (Fig. 3D, E). Moreover, Western blot analysis demonstrated that PGAM5 knockdown markedly downregulated the protein levels of CDK4, CDK6, and Cyclin D1 in THP-1 cells (Fig. 3F, G). The same result was obtained in MOLM-13 cells (Fig. 3H, I). The combined results of these functional experiments indicate that PGAM5 may promote cell cycle progression by regulating the expression of cell cycle-related proteins, thereby driving the proliferation of AML cells. This provides a mechanistic basis for its carcinogenic effects.

PGAM5 enhances glycolytic flux in AML cells

Although the oncogenic effect of PGAM5 in AML has been established, its precise molecular mechanism remains unclear. We hypothesized that PGAM5 may drive AML progression by promoting glycolysis. To test this, we first analyzed the association between PGAM5 expression and glycolysis-related molecules via bioinformatics. The results revealed a significant positive correlation between PGAM5 and the expression of several key glycolytic molecules, including HK2, PFKP, GLUT1, and LDHA (Fig. 4A), suggesting that PGAM5 may positively regulate aerobic glycolysis in AML cells. To further elucidate the underlying mechanism, we examined changes in the expression of key glycolytic enzymes and relevant regulators. Western blot analysis demonstrated that knockdown of PGAM5 in THP-1 cells downregulated the protein levels of glycolysis-promoting factors HK2, GLUT1, and LDHA (Fig. 4B, C). Concurrently, the level of HIF-1α, a major transcriptional activator of glycolytic genes, was also significantly reduced (Fig. 4B, C). Similar results were observed in MOLM-13 cells (Fig. 4D, E). Moreover, we measured key indicators of glycolytic metabolism after PGAM5 knockdown. Silencing PGAM5 in THP-1 and MOLM-13 cells significantly suppressed glycolytic activity, as evidenced by decreased glucose uptake, lactate production, and intracellular ATP levels (Fig. 4F, G). These results reveal that PGAM5 is a important regulator of glycolytic metabolism in AML cells, likely promoting glycolytic reprogramming through modulation of HIF-1α and downstream glycolytic enzyme expression, thereby sustaining energy metabolism and malignant proliferation in AML.

PGAM5 knockdown inhibits the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in AML cells

Given the established role of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in cellular metabolic reprogramming, we further investigated the effect of PGAM5 on this pathway to elucidate its oncogenic mechanism in AML. First, bioinformatic analysis demonstrated that the expression of PGAM5 in AML patient samples was found to be significantly positively correlated with the mRNA levels of key components of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, including PIK3CA, AKT1, and mTOR(Fig. 5A). Subsequently, Western blot was performed to examine changes in the phosphorylation levels of key proteins in this pathway upon PGAM5 knockdown. The results showed that in THP-1 cells, PGAM5 knockdown significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR, while the total protein levels of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR remained largely unchanged (Fig. 5B, C). To verify the generality of this finding, we repeated the experiment in MOLM-13 cells and obtained similar results (Fig. 5D, E). These findings suggest that in AML, PGAM5 may act as a positive regulator of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. By modulating the phosphorylation and activation status of this pathway, it participates in regulating the growth and metabolism of AML cells.

PGAM5 drives AML progression by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR‑mediated glycolytic pathway

To determine whether PGAM5 promotes AML progression via the glycolytic pathway mediated by PI3K/AKT/mTOR, we conducted a series of recovery experiments. First, THP-1 and MOLM-13 cells with PGAM5 knockdown were treated with the PI3K specific activator 740Y-P. The results showed that 740Y-P treatment effectively restored the reduced phosphorylation levels of p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR caused by PGAM5 knockdown, confirming reactivation of downstream signaling; similarly, these phosphorylation levels were markedly increased in the control groups treated with 740Y-P alone (Fig. 6A, B). In contrast, total protein levels of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR remained largely unchanged across all groups. Furthermore, this pharmacological rescue reversed the inhibitory effect of PGAM5 silencing on key glycolytic indicators. In both AML cell lines, treatment with 740Y-P significantly upregulated the expression of glycolysis‑related molecules, including HK2, GLUT1, LDHA, and the transcription factor HIF-1α (Fig. 6C, D). Furthermore, to reveal the potential role of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in AML cell apoptosis, flow cytometry results demonstrated that treatment with 740Y-P partially reversed the pro-apoptotic effect of PGAM5 knockdown in AML cells (Fig. 6E, F). Together, these rescue experiments provide direct evidence for the oncogenic mechanism of PGAM5, indicating that PGAM5 primarily promotes the expression of downstream glycolytic molecules by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby driving metabolic reprogramming and disease progression in AML cells.

Discussion

AML is a highly heterogeneous hematologic malignancy. Although targeted therapies (such as FLT3 and IDH inhibitors) have achieved some progress in recent years, the 5-year overall survival rate for patients remains below 30%21. Studies have indicated that aerobic glycolysis plays a key role in the initiation and progression of AML, and its aberrant activation can promote stem-cell-like properties in AML cells. However, whether PGAM5 plays a similar role in AML is still unclear and has not been systematically investigated. This study reveals the oncogenic function of PGAM5 in AML: TCGA data analysis indicates that PGAM5 is significantly overexpressed in AML patients and is closely associated with poor prognosis. Functional experiments further confirm that knocking down PGAM5 inhibits AML cell proliferation and induces cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase. Additional mechanistic research indicates that PGAM5 promotes aerobic glycolysis in AML cells by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway.

Abnormal glucose metabolism is a hallmark of malignant tumors, particularly AML. Dysregulated glycolysis provides AML cells with the essential energy and biosynthetic precursors required for rapid proliferation22. Glycolysis not only supplies crucial energy in hypoxic tumor microenvironments and promotes oncogenic progression23, but also contributes to immune dysfunction and immune evasion24–26. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in tumor cells has been demonstrated to play a crucial role in regulating rapid tumor cell growth and energy metabolism27. In AML cells, this pathway is often constitutively activated and directly promotes a glycolytic phenotype via AKT signaling28. Through a cascade of phosphorylation events, this pathway coordinates cellular metabolic demands: PI3K activates AKT, which in turn phosphorylates the downstream target mTORC1, leading to enhanced HIF-1α expression and driving glycolysis29. Studies have demonstrated that HK and GLUT1 are markedly overexpressed in AML, particularly in drug-resistant subtypes of the disease30, facilitating high glucose uptake and glycolytic activity, which makes them potential therapeutic targets. Our study demonstrates that PGAM5 maintains glycolytic activity and pathogenic mechanisms in AML cells by promoting the phosphorylation and activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, thereby providing novel molecular insights and identifying a potential therapeutic target for this disease.

Under normoxic conditions, HIF-1α is typically maintained at low levels through PHD-mediated hydroxylation and subsequent ubiquiti-dependent degradation; however, under hypoxia or following PI3K/AKT pathway activation, its stability increases and it accumulates intracellularly31,32. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is involved in a variety of pathological processes, such as liver fibrosis, nephrotoxicity33, heart failure34, and non-small-cell lung cancer35. In this pathway, class IA PI3Ks are heterodimers composed of a catalytic and a regulatory subunit16. AKT, a key downstream effector36, is activated by phosphorylation at Ser473 and Thr308. mTOR acts as a downstream target, forming the mTORC1/2 complexes, and recent studies have increasingly focused on its role in regulating aerobic glycolysis in multiple cancers. Former research has found that the PI3K/AKT pathway raises GLUT1 expression and aids in glucose uptake in thyroid cancer cells37. A significant reduction in the expression of glycolysis-related genes, including GLUT1, HK2, and PKM2, is observed with the combined inhibition of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR38. In this study, analysis of public databases revealed that PGAM5 is upregulated in AML and positively correlated with the expression of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR. Experiments showed that PGAM5 knockdown suppresses the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Noteworthy is that the PI3K activator 740Y-P partially countered the inhibitory effects of PGAM5 knockdown on aerobic glycolysis. The results imply that PGAM5 may facilitate aerobic glycolysis in AML cells by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.

In previous studies, PGAM5 has been confirmed to be closely associated with the development and progression of various solid tumors. Specifically, PGAM5 has been identified as significantly correlated with immune scores in liver cancer patients and can effectively predict their prognosis39. Additionally, this molecule attenuates the sensitivity of liver cancer cells to ferroptosis by inhibiting lipid peroxidation and intracellular iron accumulation40. In clear cell renal cell carcinoma, PGAM5 is closely linked to immune suppression within the TME41. Furthermore, PGAM5 is involved in regulating mitochondrial dynamics in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and colorectal cancer42,43. Similarly, given the importance of immune regulation in cancer patients44, further studies have shown that PGAM5 promotes M1 macrophage polarization and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway45. Meanwhile, PGAM5 is closely associated with M2-TAMs, and its knockdown enhances CD8+ T cell activation and improves the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy46. This study explores the potential role of PGAM5 in AML, revealing that PGAM5 may serve as a potential biomarker for AML patients and promotes AML cell proliferation by regulating glycolysis, highlighting its critical role in AML progression and its potential therapeutic value.

This study has several limitations. First, the lack of functional experiments in normal hematopoietic cells and PGAM5 overexpression experiments represents a key limiting factor. To rigorously validate the specificity of PGAM5 in AML, future studies should compare the effects of PGAM5 knockdown or knockout in normal hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells with those in primary AML blasts. In this study, only two AML cell lines, THP-1 and MOLM-13, both of which belong to the M5 subtype according to the FAB classification, were selected. Given the high heterogeneity of AML in terms of morphology, immunophenotype, and genetics, the generalizability of our conclusions beyond the M5 subtype requires further validation. Future studies should include additional AML cell lines representing different subtypes as well as primary patient samples. Moreover, this study has not yet explored the combined effects of PGAM5-targeted intervention with standard AML chemotherapy regimens. The development of specific small-molecule inhibitors of PGAM5 and evaluation of their synergistic effects with existing therapies in vitro and in vivo will be a critical step in advancing the clinical translation of PGAM5 as a therapeutic target. Finally, Metabolic reprogramming is closely associated with stem cell function, and PGAM5 regulates glycolysis through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF-1α axis, a pathway that plays a critical role in maintaining LSC stemness. In-depth investigation of the potential value of PGAM5 in LSCs will be an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that high expression of PGAM5 plays a critical role in maintaining the malignant phenotype of AML cells. Mechanistically, PGAM5 promotes aerobic glycolytic metabolism in AML cells by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. The findings enhance our understanding of PGAM5’s role in AML progression. While our results suggest that PGAM5 regulates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis to promote aerobic glycolysis, further investigations using primary AML samples and normal hematopoietic cells are required to assess the translational potential of targeting PGAM5. Nevertheless, this study identifies PGAM5 as a candidate molecule for future functional and therapeutic studies in AML.
