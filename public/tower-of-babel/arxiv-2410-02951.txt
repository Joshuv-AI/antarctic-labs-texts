Non-Asymptotic Analysis of Classical Spectrum Estimators with L L -mixing Time-series Data
Yuping Zheng, Andrew Lamperski

Abstract
Spectral estimation is a fundamental problem for time series analysis, which is widely applied in economics, speech analysis, seismology, and control systems. The asymptotic convergence theory for classical, non-parametric estimators, is well-understood, but the non-asymptotic theory is still rather limited. Our recent work gave the first non-asymptotic error bounds on the well-known Bartlett and Welch methods, but under restrictive assumptions. In this paper, we derive non-asymptotic error bounds for a class of non-parametric spectral estimators, which includes the classical Bartlett and Welch methods, under the assumption that the data is an L L -mixing stochastic process. A broad range of processes arising in time-series analysis, such as autoregressive processes and measurements of geometrically ergodic Markov chains, can be shown to be L L -mixing. In particular, L L -mixing processes can model a variety of nonlinear phenomena which do not satisfy the assumptions of our prior work. Our new error bounds for L L -mixing processes match the error bounds in the restrictive settings from prior work up to logarithmic factors.

Spectral estimation is the problem of estimating the power spectral density of a time series from a finite record of data samples. It is widely applied in economics, speech analysis, seismology, control systems, and more. II-B

L
-mixing processes

We will assume that the data,
\mathbf{y}[k]
, is an
L
-mixing process.
Here, we introduce some background for
L
-mixing processes. We start with the classical definitions in continuous time and describe how they change for discrete time.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an increasing family of
\sigma
-algebras.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{+}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+})_{t\geq 0}
be a decreasing family of
\sigma
-algebras such that for all
t\geq 0
,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+}
are independent,

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+}=\mathcal{F}_{0}^{+}
for all
t\leq 0
,
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+}=\sigma\left\{\bigcup_{\epsilon>0}\mathcal{F}_{t+\epsilon}\right\}
.
A continuous-time stochastic process
\mathbf{y}_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
is called
L
-mixing
with respect to
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{+})
if

•

\mathbf{y}_{t}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0

•

M_{q}(\mathbf{y}):=\sup_{t\geq 0}\|\mathbf{y}_{t}\|_{L_{q}}<\infty
for all
q\geq 1

•

\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{q}(\tau,\mathbf{y})d\tau<\infty
for all
q\geq 1
, where
\gamma_{q}(\tau,\mathbf{y})=\sup_{t\geq\tau}\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-\tau}^{+}]\|_{L_{q}}
.

The number,
\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y})
characterizes the speed at which dependencies decay over time.

Now we sketch the discrete-time case.
Let
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be an increasing sequence of
\sigma
-algebras and let
\mathcal{F}^{+}=(\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+})_{k\geq 0}
be a decreasing sequence of
\sigma
-algebras such that
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
are independent for all
k\geq 0
.
We say that the discrete-time process
\mathbf{y}_{k}
is
L
-mixing with respect to
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{+})
, if the continuous-time process defined by
\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{y}_{\lfloor t\rfloor}
is
L
-mixing with respect to the continuous-time
\sigma
-algebras defined by
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{\lfloor t\rfloor}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+}=\mathcal{F}_{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{+}
.

The moment bounds remains the same as in continuous time, but the discrete-time counterpart of
\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y})
is defined by

\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{q}(k,\mathbf{y}).
Note that

\gamma_{q}(\tau,\mathbf{y})=\max\{\gamma_{q}(\lfloor\tau\rfloor,\mathbf{y}),\gamma_{q}(\lfloor\tau\rfloor+1,\mathbf{y})\}
, which implies that

\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y})\leq\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y})\leq 2\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y}).

In particular,
\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y})
is finite if and only if
\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y})
is finite.

II-C

Problem and Algorithm

Given a stationary zero-mean discrete-time stochastic process
\mathbf{y}[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the autocovariance sequence and power sepctral density are

R[k]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}[i+k]\mathbf{y}[i]^{\top}]
and
\Phi(s)=\sum_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-j2\pi sk}R[k]
,
where
s\in[-1/2,1/2]
.

The batch-form
Bartlett and Welch estimators are

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}w_{k}(s)\mathbf{y}[iK+k]

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{L}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(s)\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(s)^{\star}

where the Bartlett method has
K=M
and
w_{k}(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}e^{-j2\pi ks}
, while the Welch method may have
K\neq M
and uses
w_{k}(s)=\frac{v_{k}}{\|v\|_{2}}e^{-j2\pi ks}
for some window vector
0\neq v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
In both the Bartlett and Welch estimators,
w(s)
is a Euclidean unit vector for all
s
:
\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}|w_{k}(s)|^{2}=1
.

Choose
\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{k+1}
, then the general iterative algorithm is:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k+1}(s)=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}(s)+\alpha_{k}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}(s)\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}(s)^{*}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}(s)).

\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]\|_{L_{q}}\leq 2M_{q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}).

When
\ell K\geq(M-1)
, we have that
(i-\ell)K+(M-1)=\left(iK+k\right)-\left(\ell K+k-(M-1)\right)
, where
\ell K+k-(M-1)\geq 0
for all
k=0,\ldots,M-1
. In this case, using the triangle inequality, and that
|w_{k}|\leq 1
gives:

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]\|_{L_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\|\mathbf{y}[iK+k]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}[iK+k]|\mathcal{F}_{(i-\ell)K+(M-1)}^{+}]\|_{L_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\gamma_{q}(\ell K+k-(M-1),\mathbf{y}).

Now, since at most
M/K
values of
\ell
have
\ell<(M-1)/K
, the bound on
\Gamma_{q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})
follows by summing over
\ell
.
∎

Now we can use the
L
-mixing properties of
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
to derive the
L
-mixing properties of the outer product,
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{\star}
.

Proof of Proposition
3

Note that for any vectors,
\|xy^{\star}\|_{F}=\|x\|_{2}\|y\|_{2}
. In particular,

\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{\star}\|_{L_{q}}=\|\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|_{L_{q}}=\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\|_{L_{2q}}^{2}.

The bound on
M_{q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})
now follows.

To bound the mixing constant, first note that for
0\leq\ell\leq i
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{\star}-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{\star}\middle|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}\right]

\displaystyle=(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])^{\star}

\displaystyle+(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]^{\star}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}](\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])^{\star}

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])^{\star}\middle|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}\right].

This equality is derived by plugging in
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])+\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]
and then simplifying.

Now, taking the
L_{q}
norm gives

\left\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{\star}-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{\star}\middle|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}\right]\right\|_{L_{q}}\\
\leq 2\left\|(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]^{\star}\right\|_{L_{q}}\\
+2\left\|(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}])^{\star}\right\|_{L_{q}}.

(1)

The first term on the right can be bounded by:

\displaystyle\left\|\left\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]\right\|\left\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]\right\|\right\|_{L_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]\right\|_{L_{2q}}\left\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]\right\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{2q}(\ell,\hat{\mathbf{y}})M_{2q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}).

The second term on the right is bounded similarly by:

\displaystyle\left\|\left\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]\right\|\left\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]\right\|\right\|_{L_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\|_{L_{2q}}\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}|\mathcal{G}_{i-\ell}^{+}]\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq 2M_{2q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})\gamma_{2q}(\ell,\hat{\mathbf{y}}).

Plugging the bounds into (
1
) and summing over
\ell
gives:

\Gamma_{d,q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})\leq 6M_{2q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})\Gamma_{d,2q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}).

The result now follows by plugging in the expressions for
M_{2q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})
and
\Gamma_{d,2q}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})
from Lemma
3
.
∎

V-A

Proof of Theorem
1

Let
\mathbf{x}_{k}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}(s)
,
\mathbf{z}_{k}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}(s)\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}(s)^{*}
,
\bar{z}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_{k}]
,
\mathbf{e}_{k}=\mathbf{x}_{k}-\bar{z}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{k}=\mathbf{z}_{k}-\bar{z}
.
Then,
\mathbf{e}_{k+1}=(1-\alpha_{k})\mathbf{e}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{k}
.

Setting
\beta_{i,k}=\Pi_{j=i+1}^{k-1}(1-\alpha_{j})\alpha_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}
,
\tau_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\alpha_{j}
and for all
q\geq 1
, iterating and taking the
L_{2q}
norm gives

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq\Pi_{i=k_{0}}^{k-1}(1-\alpha_{i})\|\mathbf{e}_{k_{0}}\|_{L_{2q}}+\|\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k-1}\beta_{i,k}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k_{0}})}\|\mathbf{e}_{k_{0}}\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k-1}\beta_{i,k}^{2}}2\sqrt{2(2q-1)M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})\Gamma_{d,2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k_{0}})}\|\mathbf{e}_{k_{0}}\|_{L_{2q}}+\sqrt{\sum_{i=k_{0}}^{k-1}\alpha_{i}^{2}}C_{q},

where
C_{q}=2\sqrt{2(2q-1)2M_{2q}(\mathbf{z})\Gamma_{d,2q}({\mathbf{z}})}
.
The first inequality above uses
1-x\leq e^{-x}
,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
and Corollary
2
.

Choose integers
1=s_{0}<\cdots<s_{m}<s_{m+1}=k
and iterate the bound above:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{q}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\sqrt{\sum_{i=s_{l}}^{s_{l+1}-1}\alpha_{i}^{2}}e^{-(\tau_{k}-\tau_{s_{l+1}})}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt+e^{-(\tau_{k}-\tau_{s_{0}})}\|\mathbf{e}_{s_{0}}\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{q}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{l}}}\frac{s_{l+1}}{k}+\frac{2}{k}\|\mathbf{e}_{1}\|_{L_{2q}}.

The last inequality uses the Riemann sum bounds: For
s_{l}\geq 1
,
l\in[0,m]
,
\sum_{j=s_{l}}^{s_{l+1}-1}\alpha_{j}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{s_{l}}
and
e^{-\sum_{i=s_{l}}^{k-1}\alpha_{i}}\leq\frac{s_{l}+1}{k+1}\leq 2\frac{s_{l}}{k}

Let
s_{l}=k^{\frac{2^{l}-1}{2^{l}}}

\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{l}}}\frac{s_{l+1}}{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}
. Then, setting

k^{\frac{2^{m}-1}{2^{m}}}\geq\frac{k}{2}\Leftrightarrow m\geq\log_{2}(\log_{2}k)
when
k>1
.

\|\mathbf{e}_{1}\|_{L_{2q}}=\|\mathbf{z}_{0}-\bar{z}\|_{L_{2q}}
gives
\|\mathbf{e}_{1}\|_{L_{2q}}\leq 2M_{2q}(\mathbf{z})
. Furthermore, when
k\geq 4
, we have
\log_{2}(\log_{2}k)\geq 1
. Then,
we choose

to obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq(6\sqrt{2}C_{q}+4M_{2q}(\mathbf{z}))\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\log_{2}(\log_{2}k).

Plugging the bounds from Proposition
3

norm

Proof of Proposition

For
0\leq i\leq j
,

\displaystyle\|R[j-i]\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{y}[j]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}[j]|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{+}])\mathbf{y}[i]^{\top}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}[j]|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{+}]\mathbf{y}[i]^{\top}\right]\right\|_{2}.

(2)

Then, use Jensen’s inequality, norm inequalities and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to bound the first term of (

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{y}[j]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}[j]|\mathcal{F}_{i}^{+}])\mathbf{y}[i]^{\top}\right]\right\|_{2}

V-C

Proof of Theorem
2

For all
\epsilon>0
and all
q\geq 1
, Markov’s inequality, followed by Theorem
1
implies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\bar{z}\|>\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{-q}\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\bar{z}\|_{L_{q}}^{q}\leq\epsilon^{-q}f_{k}^{q}q^{rq}

(3)

where

f_{k}=c\frac{\log_{2}(\log_{2}(k))}{\sqrt{k}}.

The logarithm of the upper bound in (
3
) is:

g(q)=q\ln(\epsilon^{-1}f_{k})+rq\ln q,

which is convex in
q
. The global minimum is given by
q^{\star}=e^{-1}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{f_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}

with
q^{\star}\geq 1
when
\epsilon\geq f_{k}e^{r}
.
Thus, as long as
\epsilon\geq f_{k}e^{r}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\bar{z}\|>\epsilon)\leq(\epsilon^{-1}f_{k}(q^{\star})^{r})^{q^{\star}}=e^{-\frac{r}{e}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{f_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}}.

Fix
\nu\in(0,1)
. Then
e^{-\frac{r}{e}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{f_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}}\leq\nu
iff

\epsilon\geq f_{k}e^{r}\frac{\left(\ln\nu^{-1}\right)^{r}}{r^{r}}.

Thus, if
\epsilon=f_{k}e^{r}\max\left\{1,\frac{\left(\ln\nu^{-1}\right)^{r}}{r^{r}}\right\}
,
\mathbb{P}(\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\bar{z}\|>\epsilon)\leq\nu
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1

Let
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}[k]=\mathbf{v}
be a
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian random variable.
We follow the basic steps of Proposition 2.5.2 in
[
23
]
, but track the constants more specifically.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{v}|^{q}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{v}>\epsilon^{1/q})+\mathbb{P}(-\mathbf{v}>\epsilon^{1/q}))d\epsilon.

For all
\lambda>0
, we have:

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{v}>\epsilon^{1/q})=\mathbb{P}(e^{\lambda\mathbf{v}}>e^{\lambda\epsilon^{1/q}})\leq e^{-\lambda\epsilon^{1/q}}\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\mathbf{v}}]\\
\leq e^{-\lambda\epsilon^{1/q}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}\>\overset{\lambda=\epsilon^{1/q}/\sigma^{2}}{\implies}\>\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{v}>\epsilon^{1/q})\leq e^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2/q}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

The same upper bound holds for
\mathbb{P}(-\mathbf{v}>\epsilon^{1/q})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{v}|^{q}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2/q}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)d\epsilon=q(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{q}\Gamma(q/2)

\displaystyle\leq 3q(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{q}(q/2)^{q/2}=eq\sigma^{q}q^{q/2}

where the second inequality uses the Stirling bound
\Gamma(x)\leq ex^{x}
for
x\geq 1/2
.
It follows that

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L_{q}}\leq e^{\frac{1}{q}}q^{\frac{1}{q}}\sigma\sqrt{q}\leq e^{2}\sigma\sqrt{q}\leq 8\sigma\sqrt{q},

where the second inequality uses that
q^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq e
for
q\geq 1
.
Now, using the triangle inequality gives
M_{q}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\leq 8m\sigma\sqrt{q}
.

Using the bound on
M_{q}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})
, we can bound
M_{q}(\mathbf{y})
. Indeed,
\|h[k-\ell]\boldsymbol{\zeta}[\ell]\|_{2}\leq\|h[k-\ell]\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}[\ell]\|_{2}
implies that

\|\mathbf{y}[k]\|_{L_{q}}\leq M_{q}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\|h[\ell]\|_{2}.

The bound on
M_{q}(\mathbf{y})
now follows.

To bound
\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y})
, let
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}[\ell]|\ell\leq k\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}=\sigma\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}[\ell]|\ell>k\}
. Then for
0\leq\ell\leq k
,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{y}[k]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}[k]|\mathcal{F}_{k-\ell}^{+}]\right\|_{L_{q}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{p=-\infty}^{k-\ell}h[k-p]\boldsymbol{\zeta}[p]\right\|_{L_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq M_{q}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\sum_{p=\ell}^{\infty}\|h[p]\|_{2}.

Summing the bound above over
l
completes the proof.

To verify the obtained error bounds for the two classical spectrum estimators, samples are generated from measurements of a finite-state Markov chain.
The stochastic process
\mathbf{y}[k]\in\{0,1\}

P=\begin{bmatrix}0.3&0.7\\

In the simulation, we shift measurements by the mean
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}[k]]=7/12

It can be proved that such an ergodic finite-state markov chain

[

]
also calculates an upper bound of the

\Gamma_{d,4q}(\mathbf{y})\leq 4G_{\max}\frac{1}{1-(1-\delta)^{\frac{1}{4q}}}\leq\frac{4G_{\max}}{\delta}4q

2

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we showed that the finite-time convergence rate for two classical spectrum estimators is of order
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\log_{2}(\log_{2}k))
, where
k
is the number of chunks of data used in the algorithm. The error bounds corresponding to the variance of the estimators can be quantified by the
L
-mixing properties of the data. For Bartlett estimator, the concentration bound is independent of the window length
M
, while for Welch estimator, it depends on the ratio between
K
and
M
, which is usually fixed as
0.5
in practice.

One limitation is that the zero-mean assumption on the time series often will not hold.
Therefore, in the future, we will extend the current analysis to more general time series which are not necessarily zero-mean. Another future direction is to improve the constant factors.
