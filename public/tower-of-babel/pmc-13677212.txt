Selective Prescribing of First‐Line SGLT2 Inhibitor and GLP‐1 RA in Adults With Severe Mental Illness and Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Yueh‐Yi Chiang, Chixiang Chen, Xiaojuan Li, Charmaine D. Rochester‐Eyeguokan, Wendy Camelo Castillo, Susan dosReis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeSGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and GLP‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RA) provide cardiovascular (CV) benefits, but their first‐line use among adults with severe mental illness (SMI) is not well characterized. We evaluated selective prescribing of SGLT2i, GLP‐1 RA, and metformin among adults with SMI and type 2 diabetes (T2D).MethodsUsing 2017–2022 Medicare claims, we identified adults with SMI and T2D who initiated first‐line SGLT2i, GLP‐1 RA, or metformin during 1/1/2018–12/31/2021. By calendar year, standardized mean differences (SMD) assessed baseline characteristic differences, and propensity score overlap coefficients (OVL) assessed group comparability between SGLT2i or GLP‐1 RA initiators and metformin initiators.ResultsWe identified 1322 SGLT2i, 2306 GLP‐1 RA, and 21 144 metformin initiators. From 2018 to 2021, SGLT2i initiators shifted from younger to older than metformin initiators (SMD, −0.11 to 0.19), while differences in CVD (0.10 to 0.50) and CKD (0.18 to 0.62) increased. GLP‐1 RA initiators consistently had higher prevalence of obesity (SMD, 0.33 to 0.41) and CKD (0.42 to 0.28) than metformin initiators. Compared with metformin initiators, both SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA initiators had lower prevalence of schizophrenia, antipsychotic use, and recent psychiatrist visits but higher prevalence of major depressive disorder; these differences generally persisted over time. OVL increased from 42.7% to 50.7% for SGLT2i and from 42.3% to 50.0% for GLP‐1 RA versus metformin.ConclusionsFirst‐line SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA prescribing among adults with SMI was selective according to cardiometabolic and mental health‐related characteristics. Future comparative studies should account for these differences and temporal prescribing changes to mitigate confounding.

Introduction

Severe mental illness (SMI), defined as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder [1], is associated with substantial burden of physical illness [2]. Individuals with SMI have a 15‐ to 20‐year reduction in life expectancy [3], with much of the excess mortality attributable to premature cardiovascular disease (CVD) [4]. Adults with SMI have a 2‐ to 4‐fold higher prevalence of CVD risk factors (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome) than the general population [5]. High rates of health‐risk behaviors, social disadvantage, and metabolic adverse effects of psychotropic medications may further increase cardiometabolic risk [6, 7].

Over the past decade, clinical trials have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits of two novel classes of antihyperglycemic agents in adults with or without type 2 diabetes (T2D). Sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events and hospitalization for heart failure, while glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RA) reduce the risk of stroke, myocardial infarction, and death [8, 9, 10, 11]. These findings have led to guideline updates and expanded indications for their use beyond glycemic control [12, 13].

Despite these advances, evidence on how newer antihyperglycemic agents have been incorporated into diabetes care for adults with SMI remains limited. Individuals with SMI are less likely than those without SMI to receive recommended diabetes monitoring and may experience poorer quality of care [14, 15]. Studies have also found lower use of recommended cardiovascular medications in individuals with SMI versus without SMI, suggesting potential gaps in cardiovascular risk management [16]. Metformin has long been the preferred first‐line treatment for T2D [17], and clinical guidance supports managing diabetes in individuals with SMI according to established diabetes treatment standards [18]. A Danish registry based study found that individuals with SMI and T2D were more likely to receive antihyperglycemic treatment, particularly metformin, than those without SMI [XX]. However, little is known about the use of SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA as first‐line treatment among adults with SMI, particularly how prescribing patterns have evolved with their expanded use in the general T2D population.

Understanding first‐line treatment selection is important because adults with SMI may particularly benefit from the cardiometabolic effects of SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA but may also face barriers to their use. Clinical factors, socioeconomic circumstances, prescriber‐related factors, and health insurance policies, may influence the uptake of these newer agents and contribute to selective prescribing (i.e., channeling) [19, 20, 21]. To date, evidence is lacking on whether SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA are selectively prescribed as first‐line treatment according to patient characteristics within the SMI population.

To address this gap, we investigated first‐line antihyperglycemic prescribing patterns among adults with SMI and comorbid T2D, focusing on selective prescribing. We characterized adults who initiated SGLT2i, GLP‐1 RA, or metformin from 2018 through 2021 and compared baseline CVD and other chronic conditions across initiation groups. We hypothesized that, over time, SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA initiators would have a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors than metformin initiators.

Methods

Data Sources

We used Medicare fee‐for‐service administrative claims from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW) for January 1, 2017, through December 31, 2022, representing a 20% random sample of Medicare beneficiaries. Medicare is the US federal health insurance program for adults aged 65 years or older and for younger individuals with qualifying disabilities or conditions. A unique beneficiary identifier enabled linkage across data files for person‐level health care utilization histories. Medical claims include dates, care settings, and associated diagnosis and procedure codes. Part D prescription drug event claims capture dispensing transactions, including dispensing date, National Drug Code, generic names, and days' supply. Demographic and enrollment characteristics were obtained from the Master Beneficiary Summary File.

Study Design and Study Cohort

We implemented a new‐user design to identify a retrospective cohort of individuals with SMI and comorbid T2D. Eligible individuals initiated first‐line monotherapy with an SGLT2i, a GLP‐1 RA, or metformin from January 1, 2018 through December 31, 2021. The index date (Day 0) was the first dispensing for the study drug. First‐line monotherapy was the initiation of a single study drug (SGLT2i, GLP‐1 RA, or metformin) with no dispensing of any antihyperglycemic agents—including metformin, SGLT2i, GLP‐1 RA, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, or thiazolidinediones—during the 365‐day baseline period (Day −365 to Day −1). Generic names of the study drugs are listed in Table S1.

Individuals were included if they (1) had continuous Medicare enrollment with complete medical and pharmacy benefits (Parts A, B, and D) during the 365‐day baseline period before the index date; (2) were aged 18 years or older on the index date; and (3) had evidence of SMI and T2D during baseline and/or on the index date. SMI was defined by at least one International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) diagnosis code for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or major depressive disorder. Individuals could meet the criteria for more than one SMI condition (e.g., an individual could have both schizophrenia and major depressive disorder). In the absence of a single validated claims‐based algorithm for SMI, we used CMS CCW definitions [22]. Operational definitions for SMI and T2D are provided in Table S2.

We excluded individuals who (1) initiated more than one antihyperglycemic drug class on the index date; (2) had evidence of type 1 diabetes, gestational diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome, organ transplant, end‐stage renal disease (ESRD), or HIV/AIDS during baseline and/or on the index date; or (3) had ESRD as the current or original reason for Medicare entitlement. These conditions were excluded because they may represent contraindications to the study drugs or complex clinical circumstances that could introduce confounding.

Baseline Characteristics

We measured baseline characteristics from Day −365 through Day 0 (the index date), including sociodemographic characteristics, comorbidities, medication use, specialist visits in the 7 days before the index date, and health care resource utilization (HCRU). SMI‐related clinical characteristics included diagnoses of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder; mental health comorbidities; psychotropic medications; and psychiatry visits in the 7 days before the index date. We used quartiles of the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) as an area‐level measure of social vulnerability, with higher quartiles indicating greater vulnerability [23]. Variables were selected a priori informed by published literature on factors associated with antihyperglycemic treatment initiation and cardiovascular outcomes [24, 25]. Operational definitions for all baseline measures are provided in Table S3.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized cohort characteristics overall and by index drug. We assessed between‐group differences using chi‐squared tests for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables. We considered two‐sided p < 0.05 statistically significant. To evaluate temporal changes in characteristics of individuals who initiated an SGLT2i, GLP–1 RA, or metformin, we stratified the study period into four calendar‐year time blocks: T1 (January 1–December 31, 2018), T2 (January 1–December 31, 2019), T3 (January 1–December 31, 2020), and T4 (January 1–December 31, 2021). These time blocks aligned with guideline updates, cardiovascular outcomes trial publications, and US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) drug label changes that may have influenced prescribing of SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA. Table S4 summarizes key events from 2018 through 2021 that may have affected prescribing.

To assess selective prescribing, we conducted pairwise comparisons using metformin as the reference group (SGLT2i vs. metformin; GLP‐1 RA vs. metformin). For each calendar year, we calculated standardized mean differences (SMD) in baseline CVD risk factors. Positive SMDs indicated that the prevalence (or mean values) of a given characteristic was higher among SGLT2i or GLP‐1 RA initiators than among metformin initiators, whereas negative SMDs indicated that the prevalence (or mean values) was higher among metformin initiators than among SGLT2i or GLP‐1 RA initiators. By convention, an absolute SMD < 0.1 was considered to indicate a negligible difference between groups. We plotted SMDs across the four time blocks for selected CVD risk factors and indicators of disease severity. To assess temporal trends, we regressed SMDs on time block (coded 0–3) using ordinary least squares. We considered 2‐sided p < 0.05 statistically significant.

Within each time block, we estimated propensity scores (PS) to summarize differences in baseline characteristics across treatment groups and to evaluate overlap (i.e., potential clinical equipoise). We used logistic regression to estimate the probability of treatment initiation, with metformin as the reference group, and included baseline characteristics specified in Table S3. We assessed overlap of PS distributions using the overlapping coefficient (OVL), which quantifies the degree of overlap between groups' PS distributions [26]. An OVL close to 100% indicates substantial common support and greater comparability between groups. We conducted all analyses in SAS Studio version 9.4 (SAS Institute). This study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Maryland, Baltimore (HP‐00112780).

Results

Study Cohort Characteristics

We identified adults with SMI and T2D who initiated first‐line SGLT2i (n = 1322), GLP‐1 RA (n = 2306), or metformin (n = 21 144) from January 1, 2018, through December 31, 2021 (Table S5). Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Most were aged ≥ 50 (n = 27 972; 88.7%), female (n = 15 256; 61.6%), and White (n = 19 389; 78.3%). More than half originally qualified for Medicare through disability insurance benefits (n = 13 844; 55.9%). The cohort had a high prevalence of obesity (n = 13 137; 53.0%), CVD (n = 14 924; 60.2%), hyperlipidemia (n = 20 451; 82.6%), and hypertension (n = 22 177; 89.5%). The SMI cohort mainly represented individuals with major depressive disorder (n = 20 487; 82.7%). Over half had an anxiety disorder (n = 14 364; 58.0%). Psychotropic use in this cohort included antidepressants (n = 19 580; 79.0%), antipsychotics (n = 7704; 31.1%), and to a lesser extent mood stabilizers (n = 3823; 15.4%). In the 7 days before the index date, primary care visits were more common than psychiatrist visits (71.2% vs. 5.8%). Most individuals (n = 20 335; 82.1%) resided in areas within SVI quartiles 2 through 4, representing higher levels of social vulnerability. Baseline characteristics differed across treatment groups for most prespecified variables (p < 0.05; Table 1).

SGLT2i Versus Metformin

In 2018, SGLT2i initiators were younger (SMD = −0.11) and had a higher prevalence of obesity (SMD = 0.25), diabetic nephropathy (SMD = 0.20), CVD (SMD = 0.10), and chronic kidney disease (CKD; SMD = 0.18) than metformin initiators. SGLT2i initiators had a similar prevalence of cardiologist visits in the 7 days before the index date (SMD = 0.03) and a lower prevalence of baseline hospitalizations (SMD = −0.16). SGLT2i initiators had a lower prevalence of schizophrenia (SMD = −0.21), antipsychotic use (SMD = −0.14), mood stabilizer use (SMD = −0.11), and psychiatrist visits in the 7 days before the index date (SMD = −0.15), but a higher prevalence of major depressive disorder (SMD = 0.17).

Age differences reversed over time: in 2018, SGLT2i initiators were younger than metformin initiators (mean, 64.2 vs. 65.5 years; SMD = −0.11), whereas in 2021 they were older (mean, 69.4 vs. 67.0 years; SMD = 0.19). SMDs increased over time for diabetic nephropathy (SMD, 0.20 in 2018 vs. 0.60 in 2021; p = 0.02), CVD (0.10 vs. 0.50; p = 0.01), and CKD (0.18 vs. 0.62; p = 0.02), but decreased for obesity (0.25 vs. 0.07; p = 0.01). By 2021, SGLT2i initiators also had a higher prevalence of cardiologist visits (SMD, 0.03 in 2018 vs. 0.49 in 2021; p = 0.13) and hospitalizations (−0.16 vs. 0.14; p = 0.06) than metformin initiators, although these temporal trends were not statistically significant. Differences in SMI‐related characteristics generally persisted through 2021, including a lower prevalence of schizophrenia (SMD = −0.33), antipsychotic use (SMD = −0.32), mood stabilizer use (SMD = −0.17), and recent psychiatrist visits (SMD = −0.17), and a higher prevalence of major depressive disorder (SMD = 0.22). However, none of these SMI‐related differences changed significantly over time.

GLP‐1 RA Versus Metformin

In 2018, GLP‐1 RA initiators had a similar mean age to metformin initiators (SMD = −0.08) but a higher prevalence of obesity (SMD = 0.33), diabetic nephropathy (SMD = 0.35), CVD (SMD = 0.13), CKD (SMD = 0.42), and insulin use (SMD = 1.17). GLP‐1 RA initiators also had a higher prevalence of endocrinologist visits in the 7 days before the index date (SMD = 0.40) and a lower prevalence of baseline hospitalizations (SMD = −0.12). GLP‐1 RA initiators had a lower prevalence of schizophrenia (SMD = −0.26), alcohol use disorder (SMD = −0.17), antipsychotic use (SMD = −0.17), mood stabilizer use (SMD = −0.12), and recent psychiatrist visits (SMD = −0.19), but a higher prevalence of major depressive disorder (SMD = 0.24) and antidepressant use (SMD = 0.15).

From 2018 to 2021, GLP‐1 RA initiators remained younger than metformin initiators, with a widening age difference (2018: mean, 64.5 vs. 65.5 years; SMD = −0.08; 2021: mean, 65.2 vs. 67.0 years; SMD = −0.15). SMDs were similar between 2018 and 2021 for obesity (0.33 vs. 0.41), diabetic nephropathy (0.35 vs. 0.22), CKD (0.42 vs. 0.28), and insulin use (1.17 vs. 0.87), whereas the imbalance in CVD decreased (0.13 vs. 0.03). In 2021, GLP‐1 RA initiators continued to have a higher prevalence of endocrinologist visits (SMD, 0.40 in 2018 vs. 0.30 in 2021) and a lower prevalence of hospitalizations (−0.12 vs. −0.23) than metformin initiators. Differences in several SMI‐related characteristics persisted through 2021, including a lower prevalence of schizophrenia (SMD = −0.26), alcohol use disorder (SMD = −0.16), antipsychotic use (SMD = −0.13), and recent psychiatrist visits (SMD = −0.16), and a higher prevalence of major depressive disorder (SMD = 0.13). None of these SMI‐related differences changed significantly over time. Only endocrinologist and primary care physician visits showed statistically significant trends over time (p < 0.05).

PS Overlap by Calendar‐Year

Figure 2 shows PS distributions for the SGLT2i versus metformin comparison by calendar year. In 2018, metformin initiators' PS values were concentrated between 0.0 and 0.05, indicating a low estimated probability of SGLT2i initiation. By 2021, the PS distribution among metformin initiators shifted upward to approximately 0.1–0.2, indicating higher estimated probabilities of SGLT2i initiation. We observed a similar shift for the GLP‐1 RA versus metformin comparison (Figure 3). For SGLT2i versus metformin, the OVLs were 42.7% (2018), 48.0% (2019), 51.0% (2020), and 50.7% (2021). For GLP‐1 RA versus metformin, the corresponding OVLs were 42.3% (2018), 43.4% (2019), 41.5% (2020), and 50.0% (2021). Summary statistics for PS values are provided in Table S8.

Discussion

This study found evolving selective prescribing of SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA among adults with SMI and comorbid T2D, reflected by systematic between‐group differences in baseline characteristics that shifted over time. Overall, SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA initiators had a higher comorbidity burden, more microvascular complications, and greater baseline insulin use than metformin initiators, suggesting more advanced and/or uncontrolled diabetes. Over time, SGLT2i were increasingly prescribed to older adults and those with diabetic nephropathy, CKD, HF, and CVD, whereas GLP‐1 RA were consistently prescribed to adults with CKD and obesity. Differences in mental health related characteristics were also evident. Compared with metformin initiators, SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA initiators had a lower prevalence of schizophrenia, antipsychotic use, and recent psychiatrist visits but a higher prevalence of major depressive disorder. These differences generally persisted over time. Although PS overlap increased modestly, it remained limited, indicating persistent selective prescribing.

Our findings share several patterns with first‐line SGLT2i prescribing in the broader T2D population [27]. Shin et al. found that SGLT2i initiators were initially younger than metformin initiators, but this age difference narrowed over time. They also found that CVD and recent cardiologist visits became more prevalent among SGLT2i initiators over time. We observed similar patterns. In our study, SGLT2i initiators shifted from being younger than metformin initiators in 2018 to older in 2021. CKD, CVD, HF, and recent cardiologist visits also became more prevalent among SGLT2i initiators over time. These similarities suggest that emerging clinical evidence, regulatory approvals, and guideline recommendations influenced first‐line SGLT2i prescribing among adults with SMI in ways broadly consistent with prescribing in the general T2D population.

Our findings also identify treatment‐selection patterns that may be particularly relevant to the SMI population. We found that individuals with schizophrenia, antipsychotic treatment, substance use disorders, or greater involvement in mental health care were less likely to initiate SGLT2i and GLP‐1 RA as first‐line treatment. Notably, antipsychotic use was less prevalent among GLP‐1 RA initiators despite the potential weight‐related benefits of GLP‐1 RA for individuals experiencing antipsychotic‐induced weight gain [28]. Mental illness severity, challenges in managing complex medication regimens, and fragmentation between mental and somatic health care may contribute to these patterns [19, 29, 30]. However, we could not measure these factors directly; therefore, we cannot determine if they explain the observed prescribing patterns. Studies incorporating detailed clinical measures and patient and prescriber perspectives are needed to better understand how mental health characteristics influence diabetes treatment selection.

To investigate overall between‐group differences, we examined PS distributions separately for each calendar year from 2018 through 2021. Although PS overlap increased modestly, it remained around 50% in 2021, indicating limited comparability between treatment groups. Limited PS overlap suggests that future comparative studies should carefully consider the choice and application of PS methods, such as matching or weighting, to improve comparability between treatment groups. In addition, the observed temporal shifts in patient characteristics suggest that calendar time should be explicitly addressed. Sequential cohort designs with time‐stratified PS estimation may help account for changes in prescribing over time [31, 32]. PS methods can then be applied within each time stratum to improve covariate balance.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to characterize first‐line initiation of SGLT2i, GLP‐1 RA, and metformin specifically among adults with SMI and comorbid T2D. We evaluated a comprehensive set of clinical and mental health characteristics and assessed temporal shifts in selective prescribing. PS distributions provided summary measures of between‐group comparability and may inform control confounding in future observational studies. Finally, nationwide Medicare claims allowed us to examine a large, clinically complex population of Medicare‐eligible adults with SMI. This study has limitations. We assessed prescribing at the drug‐class level and did not examine agent‐specific patterns. Therefore, some temporal changes may reflect evidence emerging for specific agents within a class. In addition, our cohort identification period ended in 2021, preventing assessment of more recent prescribing patterns. Because uptake of newer medications may occur gradually after new evidence, indications, or guideline changes, studies using more recent data are needed to determine whether these prescribing patterns have continued to evolve.

Conclusion

Among adults with SMI and comorbid T2D, characteristics of SGLT2i initiators shifted over time toward individuals with higher baseline CVD risk. In contrast, characteristics of GLP‐1 RA initiators consistently differed from those of metformin initiators, with differences remaining relatively stable from 2018 to 2021. Our findings suggest that mental health‐related clinical profiles and care contexts may also influence first‐line antihyperglycemic selection. Future observational studies in adults with SMI and comorbid T2D should account for both cardiometabolic and mental health‐related factors that contribute to selective prescribing to minimize confounding and support valid causal inference.
