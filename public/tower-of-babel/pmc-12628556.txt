Ablation versus drug therapy in atrial fibrillation patients with or without prior Pharmacological or direct current cardioversion: results from the CABANA trial
Authors: Wenli Dai, Zejun Yang, Sitong Li, Mingxiao Li, Chao Jiang, Ning Zhou, Caihua Sang, Deyong Long, Xin Du, Jianzeng Dong, Changsheng Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCatheter ablation is superior to drug therapy in selected atrial fibrillation (AF) patients, yet the impact of prior non-ablation rhythm control remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed data from Catheter Ablation vs. Antiarrhythmic Drug Therapy for Atrial Fibrillation (CABANA) trial, which assessed whether catheter ablation was more effective than drugs for improving outcomes in AF patients, including paroxysmal, persistent and long-standing persistent AF. In this study, patients were stratified by whether they had pharmacological or direct current cardioversion (P/DCCV) before randomization. The primary outcome was a composite of all-cause death, disabling stroke, serious bleeding, or cardiac arrest. The secondary outcomes included all-cause death, death or cardiovascular hospitalization, cardiovascular death, AF recurrence and composite of all-cause death, stroke, or cardiovascular hospitalization.ResultsIn CABANA trial, 1,035 (47.0%) had a history of P/DCCV. The effect of catheter ablation on primary outcome did not differ significantly between patients with prior P/DCCV and those without (HR = 1.00, 95%CI [0.66–1.51] and HR = 0.68, 95%CI [0.45–1.03], respectively). For the secondary outcome of death or cardiovascular hospitalization, catheter ablation reduced risk in both groups, with a greater effect observed in patients with prior P/DCCV (HR = 0.72, 95%CI [0.61–0.85] vs. HR = 0.91, 95%CI [0.78–1.07], respectively; P for interaction = 0.032). Ablation also significantly reduced AF recurrence in both groups and improved AF-related quality of life over 60 months of follow-up.ConclusionIn this post-hoc analysis of the CABANA trial, catheter ablation was associated with greater benefit in reducing the composite outcome of death or cardiovascular hospitalization among patients with prior P/DCCV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05283-6.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia encountered in clinical practice and is associated with increased risks of stroke, heart failure, and death [1–5]. Rhythm control strategies, including antiarrhythmic drugs, direct current cardioversion and catheter ablation, are frequently employed to restore and maintain sinus rhythm in symptomatic AF patients to reduce symptoms, recurrence, progression of AF and improve prognosis [6–15]. In selected patients, catheter ablation demonstrated the superior efficacy in reducing AF recurrence and improving quality of life, compared to drug therapy [6, 16–21]. However, some randomized controlled trials have shown that catheter ablation does not consistently improve long-term clinical outcomes [16]. While catheter ablation has been associated with better cardiovascular outcomes in certain studies, it remains essential to identify the patient subgroups who are most likely to benefit from this intervention.

Therefore, we utilized data from the Catheter Ablation vs. Antiarrhythmic Drug Therapy for Atrial Fibrillation (CABANA) trial to investigate whether prior pharmacologic or electrical cardioversion influences the clinical efficacy of catheter ablation.

Methods

Trial design

The CABANA data set used in this analysis was available at the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute BioLINCC Biologic Specimen and Data Repository (https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/home). The CABANA trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT0091150) design and methods have been previously reported in detail [16, 22]. Each site’s Institutional Review Board and/or Ethics Committee approved the CABANA study and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Study population and follow-up

Patients either age ≥ 65, or age < 65 and had at least one risk factor for stroke with electrocardiographic documentation of at least 2 episodes of paroxysmal AF or 1 episode of persistent AF in the 6 months prior to enrollment, and who were suitable candidates for either catheter ablation or drug therapies were eligible for enrollment in CABANA study. In this study, patients were stratified based on their history of pharmacological or direct current cardioversion into the group with and without prior P/DCCV.

Follow-up in all patients occurred at 3-, 6-, and 12-months following randomization during the first year and every 6 months thereafter, with clinic visits or phone follow-up. After enrollment, subjects either received a loop recorder to be used throughout the study, or were followed using ambulatory event monitoring and Holter ECGs as conducted during routine clinical care by their attending physician.

Study outcomes

Consistent with the CABANA trial, the primary outcome in this study was a composite of all-cause death, disabling stroke, serious bleeding, or cardiac arrest. Secondary outcomes included all-cause death, the composite of all-cause death or cardiovascular hospitalization, the composite of all-cause death, stroke, or cardiovascular hospitalization, cardiovascular death, quality of life and AF recurrence.

The quality of life was evaluated by the Mayo AF-Specific Symptom Inventory (MAFSI), as reported previously [9]. The MAFSI is a modification of the Bubien-Kay Symptom Checklist and in the version used for CABANA includes a 10-item symptom checklist that assesses both frequency and severity of each symptom over the past month. According to previous CABANA analysis of QOL based on MAFSI, a change of 1.6 (Frequency) and 1.3 (Severity) in any individual patient was considered minimal clinically important differences [23, 24].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables were described as number (%). The Kruskal-Willis test and one-way ANOVA were used to analyze differences in continuous variables. Pearson’s Chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test were used to analyze differences in categorical variables.

The effect of catheter ablation was evaluated between the groups according to the intention-to-treat principle based on a time-to-first-event analysis using the log-rank test. Treatment effect sizes were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and were estimated using multivariate Cox proportional hazards models. To evaluate the heterogeneity of treatment effect in patients with or without prior P/DCCV, an interaction term (treatment group × history of P/DCCV) was included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards models. The quality of life (MAFSI Frequency and Severity scores) were analyzed with multivariable linear regression models. For each follow-up point, we generated point estimates for each treatment group as well as treatment group mean differences (ablation score – drug score). Precision of estimates was assessed with 95% CIs. Variables adjusted in the Cox and linear regression models were similar with those in the CABANA trial, including age, sex, ethnicity, AF type, years since onset of AF, history of heart failure, structural heart disease, CHA2DS2-VA score, history of coronary artery disease, and hypertension. Missing data were handled using complete case analysis without imputation. Considering that left atrial diameter (LAD) and body mass index (BMI) are important determinants of AF ablation outcomes, we additionally adjusted for these two continuous variables in the model.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0. A two-tailed P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Clinical outcome comparisons by intention-to-treat

The CABANA primary outcome event (death, disabling stroke, serious bleeding, or cardiac arrest) occurred in 89/1035 (8.60%) and 97/1167 (8.31%) patients with and without prior P/DCCV (HR = 1.00, 95%CI [0.66 − 0.51], and HR = 0.68, 95%CI [0.45–1.03], respectively, P for interaction = 0.221) (Table 2; Fig. 1). As for secondary outcomes, catheter ablation significantly reduced the risk of all-cause death or cardiovascular hospitalization in patients with prior P/DCCV (HR = 0.72, 95%CI [0.61–0.85], P < 0.001), but the effect was nonsignificant in patients without prior P/DCCV (HR = 0.93, 95%CI [0.79–1.09], P = 0.368). A significant interaction was observed in patients with and without prior P/DCCV (P for interaction = 0.032) (Table 2; Fig. 2). For the secondary outcome of composite of all-cause death, stroke or cardiovascular hospitalization, a greater effect of catheter ablation was also observed in patients with prior P/DCCV (HR = 0.72, 95%CI [0.38–1.86] vs. HR = 0.92, 95%CI [0.78–1.08], respectively; P for interaction = 0.037). In sensitivity analysis, LAD and BMI were additionally included as a covariate in Cox model in 794 patients with available data (Table S1). AF recurrence

AF recurrence occurred in 79.61% (n = 742) of patients with prior P/DCCV and 77.36% (n = 810) of those without, respectively. Catheter ablation significantly reduced risk of AF recurrence (HR = 0.53, 95%CI [0,46-0.62.62.62] and HR = 0.42, 95%CI [0.37–0.49], P for interaction = 0.028) (Figure S1).

AF-related quality of life outcomes

Over the 60 months of follow-up in patients with prior P/DCCV, the average adjusted MAFSI Frequency score difference was − 1.04 (95% CI, − 1.65 to − 0.43) points and MAFSI Severity score difference was − 0.77 (95% CI, −1.26 to −0.28) points favoring ablation (Figure S2). The results were similar in patients without prior P/DCCV (Figure S3). In patients without prior P/DCCV, 766 (75.64%) and 736 (63.07%) achieved minimal clinically important differences in MAFSI Frequence and Severity score. In patients with prior P/DCCV, 662 (63.96%) and 637 (61.55%) achieved minimal clinically important differences in MAFSI Frequency and Severity score.

Discussion

In this post hoc analysis of the CABANA trial, we observed a significant interaction between prior rhythm control therapy and the efficacy of catheter ablation. Patients with a history of P/DCCV experienced greater clinical benefit from ablation, particularly with respect to the composite outcome of death or cardiovascular hospitalization. These findings suggest that prior rhythm control attempts may identify a subgroup of patients more likely to benefit from invasive rhythm control strategies.

These findings may have important implications for clinical decision-making. The 2024 ESC guidelines recommend first-line catheter ablation in selected patients with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation to reduce arrhythmia recurrence and delay disease progression. However, this recommendation is not based on evidence of prognostic benefit and does not extend to patients with persistent atrial fibrillation. Our results suggest that initial rhythm control using antiarrhythmic drugs or cardioversion, followed by catheter ablation, may improve long-term outcomes. In clinical practice, the decision to perform P/DCCV prior to catheter ablation is often made through shared decision-making between physicians and patients, taking into account the patient’s clinical status and preferences. This stepwise approach may be particularly useful in elective settings, where ablation is delayed and rhythm stabilization can be achieved pharmacologically or electrically. For patients who could tolerate initial rhythm control and ultimately require further intervention, early P/DCCV for rhythm control is reasonable.

The EAST-AFNET 4 trial revealed that early rhythm control therapy is associated with a lower risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes [6]. Previous research from China-AF registry suggested that a shorter diagnosis-to-ablation time was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular events and recurrent AF for early-onset AF patients [25]. Another study indicated that the more drugs failed pre-ablation, the lower the freedom from AF post-procedure, possibly due to AF progression during drug trials [26]. Patients who have failed antiarrhythmic drugs may represent a more challenging subgroup for ablation, likely due to disease progression and transition to persistent AF during the time taken to fail one or more medications, which is known to reduce ablation success [27, 28]. In our study, AF recurrence rates remained high in both groups, yet were significantly reduced by ablation, with a more pronounced relative effect in those without prior P/DCCV. A recently developed strategy combined linear ablation and ethanol infusion via the vein of Marshall in addition to pulmonary vein isolation has been shown to further reduce AF recurrence [29–32]. However, the absolute reduction in AF-related symptoms and event rates was greater in patients with prior rhythm control, potentially reflecting a higher symptomatic burden or more refractory arrhythmic phenotype. Notably, in patients with prior P/DCCV, the prevalence of persistent AF was higher and the duration of AF was longer compared with those without prior P/DCCV—both of which are factors typically linked to worse outcomes after ablation. However, despite these adverse clinical characteristics, patients with prior P/DCCV still experienced greater cardiovascular benefit from ablation. This highlights the potential value of P/DCCV in the disease course, suggesting that it may contribute to improved long-term outcomes even in patients with more progressive AF.

This finding may reflect several underlying mechanisms. The use of antiarrhythmic drugs or cardioversion during the waiting period for ablation may help stabilize rhythm and delay atrial remodeling, potentially preserving procedural efficacy. Patients with prior exposure to rhythm control strategies may represent a subgroup with a greater arrhythmia burden, more symptomatic AF, or a higher motivation to maintain sinus rhythm, making them more likely to benefit from definitive rhythm control via catheter ablation. Furthermore, these patients may have demonstrated partial responsiveness or tolerability to prior rhythm control attempts, suggesting a more modifiable electrophysiological substrate that responds favorably to ablation. Conversely, patients without prior rhythm control may include those with asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic AF, or those with contraindications to antiarrhythmic drugs, potentially limiting the observable benefit of ablation in this group.

This study has several important limitations. As a post-hoc, secondary analysis, our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. The CABANA trial was not originally randomized or powered to detect an interaction based on prior cardioversion status. Although we adjusted for numerous key baseline covariates, the potential for unmeasured confounding variables remains. Secondly, we lacked granular data on the specifics of the prior cardioversion, such as the number of attempts, the time elapsed since the procedure, the antiarrhythmic drugs used, the results of cardioversion or the clinical indication (e.g., elective vs. emergent), which could also affect the results of catheter ablation. Thirdly, baseline differences between the groups existed, notably in AF type and duration, which may have influenced the outcomes despite statistical adjustment.

In conclusion, our results suggest that a history of rhythm control attempts may modify the clinical response to catheter ablation. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and refine patient selection strategies for AF ablation.

Conclusion

In this post-hoc analysis of the CABANA trial, catheter ablation was associated with greater benefit in reducing the composite outcome of death or cardiovascular hospitalization among patients with a history of P/DCCV. Incorporating non-ablation rhythm control before ablation decision-making may help improve the effectiveness of ablation for cardiovascular outcome in AF.
