Spermidine, a chondrocyte apoptotic metabolite, delays osteoarthritis by regulating macrophage M2c polarization
Authors: Jiajia Lu, Hongbiao Sun, Fan Zhang, Guangnan Wang, Xianan Song, Qinling Jiang, Zhibin Zhou, Jun Ma, Qiang Fu, Lei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChondrocyte apoptosis and synovial macrophage polarization represent two key pathological hallmarks of osteoarthritis (OA), but the interplay between them remains largely unknown. This study aimed to investigate the roles and mechanisms of the chondrocyte apoptotic secretome in mediating macrophage polarization during the pathological process of OA.MethodsIn vitro cultures of chondrocytes and macrophages, along with an in vivo surgically induced OA mouse model, were used to evaluate the effect of the chondrocyte apoptotic secretome. Untargeted metabolomics, mitochondrial function assessment, and macrophage-specific AMPKα knockout mice were used to identify key metabolites and their regulatory mechanisms. A sustained-release delivery system for spermidine (SPD) was constructed using lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and sodium hyaluronate.ResultsApoptotic chondrocytes increased the secretion of SPD in the metabolite secretome. SPD acts as a signaling metabolite to increase AMPKα activation in macrophages, which in turn drives increased oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and improves mitochondrial function, ultimately promoting M2c macrophage polarization during OA. Exogenous supplementation with SPD via a ROS-responsive SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel alleviated OA progression by promoting M2c macrophage polarization in vivo. Furthermore, elevated SPD levels in synovial fluid correlated with milder clinical symptoms and less severe pathological features.ConclusionsOur findings underscore a significant protective role of chondrocyte apoptosis in early OA mediated through the secretion of SPD, which acts on macrophages. Mechanistically, SPD-induced enhancement of AMPKα activation and OXPHOS occurs specifically in macrophages, driving their beneficial M2c polarization. Background

Osteoarthritis (OA), the most prevalent degenerative joint disorder, is caused mainly by aging and mechanical injury [1, 2]. Recent statistics indicate that more than one-third of individuals aged 45 and above are estimated to be afflicted with OA, leading to a significant health and economic burden [3, 4]. The hallmark features of OA include progressive degeneration of the articular cartilage, which is accompanied by subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, and inflammation within the synovial membrane at the comprehensive joint level [5]. Despite numerous studies shedding light on factors implicated in the disease mechanism, clinical interventions for OA continue to demonstrate limited efficacy.

Apoptosis represents a fundamental biological process that manifests throughout the course of embryonic development, the regulation of tissue homeostasis, and various pathological conditions [6–8]. Apoptosis has long been considered a “negative event” in the pathological process of OA; thus, targeting chondrocyte apoptosis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for the management of OA [9, 10]. The correlation between synovial inflammation and the progression of OA is widely acknowledged, with synovitis being significantly associated with OA severity [11]. The infiltration of polarized macrophages is the principal morphological characteristic of synovitis; as OA progresses, the population of proinflammatory M1 macrophages gradually increases, whereas the population of anti-inflammatory M2-polarized macrophages decreases [12, 13]. Therefore, maintaining the balance between M1 and M2 macrophages is considered an optimal therapeutic approach for OA. Numerous studies have revealed the pivotal roles of chondrocyte apoptosis and macrophage polarization in OA; however, treatments targeting the articular cartilage or synovial components in isolation have yielded unsatisfactory outcomes [14].

Given that OA affects all joint tissues, therapies targeting multiple pathological changes have attracted increased attention. However, the crosstalk between chondrocytes and macrophages, as well as the underlying mechanisms, remain largely unknown. In this study, we aimed to investigate whether and how metabolite-mediated communication between chondrocytes and macrophages contributes to OA pathogenesis and to explore potential therapeutic strategies targeting this intercellular crosstalk.

Methods

Patients and specimens

The study involving human samples was approved by the Ethics Committee of Navy Medical University (Shanghai, China, Approval No.240064). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Human synovial fluids were collected from OA patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty or normal patients receiving arthroscopy, and used to determine the concentrations of spermidine (SPD). Patient information, including sex, age, height, weight, and BMI were obtained, along with Knee Society Score were also collected. KL grades [15] and minimal medial joint space width were measured X-ray of the knee joint to evaluate severity of OA.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shanghai Fourth People’s Hospital, Tongji University (protocol No. TJBH16525402). Animal care and experimental procedures followed the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Shanghai Slake Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM)-induced mouse OA models were established as previously described [16]. Macrophage-specific AMPKα knockout mice were generated by crossing AMPKαflox/flox mice with Lyz2-Cre transgenic mice, yielding AMPKαflox/flox; Lyz2-Cre + (AMPKα-CKO) offspring. Littermate AMPKαflox/flox; Lyz2-Cre− (AMPKαf/f) mice served as controls. To establish the post-traumatic OA model, 8-week-old male AMPKαf/f or AMPKα-CKO mice were intraperitoneally injected with 75 μg/g body weight TMX for five consecutive days before DMM surgery. The whole knee joint in different groups were harvested for radiological examination, and then cartilage and synovium samples were collected for further analyses.

Preparation of SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel composite system

To prepare SPD-loaded responsive nanoparticles (LNP@SPD), DSPC, cholesterol, DSPE-Se-Se-PEG, and DSPE-PEG-FA (molar ratio 84:10:3:3) were dissolved in chloroform/methanol. The solvent was evaporated at 50 °C using a rotary evaporator to form a lipid film, which was further dried under vacuum. The film was hydrated with deionized water containing SPD (10 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 1 h to obtain a multi-lamellar liposome suspension. The suspension was sonicated (40% power, 2 s on/off, 5 min) and extruded 20 times through a 100 nm polycarbonate membrane. Unencapsulated SPD and free lipids were removed by dialysis (7 kDa MWCO, 4 °C, 24 h). Subsequently, the Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading capacity (DLC) were determined by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) quantification of free SPD in the supernatant.

For the hydrogel composite, sodium hyaluronate (3% w/v) was dissolved in deionized water under magnetic stirring. LNP@SPD (1 mg/mL) was mixed into the polymer solution, followed by addition of glutaraldehyde (0.5% w/v) as a crosslinker. After stirring for 30 min, the resulting hydrogel was rinsed three times with deionized water (10 × sample volume), allowed to stand for 10 min, and dialyzed against excess deionized water (50 × volume, 10 kDa MWCO, room temperature, 6 h intervals for 24 h) to yield the SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel.

In this construct, DSPE-Se-Se-PEG resides in the outer lipid shell of the nanoparticles, with the DSPE moiety anchored in the hydrophobic lipid layer and the PEG chain conferring hydrophilic shielding and colloidal stability. Upon H₂O₂-triggered cleavage of the Se–Se bond, the linkage between the DSPE anchor and PEG is disrupted, causing PEG shedding and loss of nanoparticle stability. This leads to LNP destabilization and structural disintegration, thereby promoting SPD release from both the nanoparticles and the hydrogel network.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Cartilage tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration, decalcification in 0.5 M EDTA, embedding in paraffin, and sectioning. The sections were then subjected to H&E staining, safranin O/fast green staining, and immunohistochemistry. Assessment of cartilage destruction was conducted using the OARSI grade[17] based on safranin O/fast green staining, while evaluation of synovial hyperplasia was performed using the synovial score according to H&E staining. For immunohistochemistry analysis, the sections underwent deparaffinization and hydration before being incubated with BSA and cultured with specified antibodies overnight: MertK (1:500; AF100; R&D), CD206 (1:500; GB153497-100; Servicebio), MMP13 (1:500; GB11247-100; Servicebio), COL2A1 (1:500; GB150010-100; Servicebio). This was followed by secondary antibody conjugation with HRP staining. All sections were analysis by a Panoramic DESK scan system (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary).

Cell culture and treatment

Primary mouse articular chondrocytes and primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated and cultured as previously described [18, 19]. For chondrocytes treatment, cells were treated with ultraviolet (UV) light, IL-1β (10 ng/mL), Tensile strain (10% elongation), and H₂O (50 mU) for indicated time. For all conditions, experimental parameters were rigorously optimized to maintain late-stage apoptosis at ≤ 5%, as confirmed by dual staining for annexin V (apoptosis marker) and propidium iodide (PI; membrane integrity indicator). This ensured that the majority of chondrocytes retained intact plasma membranes (PI negative) while exhibiting early-stage apoptotic features (annexin V positive). For co-culture of chondrocytes and BMDMs, macrophages (in the upper chamber) were co-cultured with chondrocytes (in the lower chamber) within a transwell chamber featuring a pore size of 0.4 μm. For Oligomycin experiment, BMDMs were pretreated with oligomycin (1 μM, Sigma-Aldrich, #75,351) or an equivalent volume of DMSO (vehicle control) for 1 h, followed by stimulation with SPD for 24 h. cells were harvested at 24 h post-stimulation for Western blot and OCR analysis, while culture supernatants were collected at 48 h post-stimulation for ELISA.

Spermidine immunodepletion

SPD immunodepletion was performed using an anti-SPD antibody conjugated to protein G‑Sepharose beads. Briefly, 500 µL of cell culture supernatant or synovial fluid was incubated with 2 µg of rabbit anti-SPD monoclonal antibody at 4 °C for 2 h under gentle rotation, followed by addition of 50 µL of protein G‑Sepharose bead slurry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and overnight incubation at 4 °C with continuous mixing. The beads were pelleted by centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected as the SPD-depleted sample. For mock-depletion controls, normal rabbit IgG (isotype control) was used instead of the anti-SPD antibody. Depletion efficiency was assessed by quantifying SPD levels before and after depletion using HPLC with fluorescence detection after pre-column derivatization with dansyl chloride. Across three independent experiments, the average depletion efficiency was 93.6 ± 3.1%, with residual SPD below 8% in all depleted samples, confirming effective removal of endogenous SPD.

Immunofluorescence assay

The immunofluorescence assay was used to detect protein levels. Briefly, samples were deparaffinized and hydrated, and cultured with the following antibodies: c-CASP3 (1:500; GB11532-100; Servicebio), F4/80 (1:500; GB113373-100; Servicebio), iNOS (1:500, GB11119-100, Servicebio), MertK (1:500; AF100; R&D). Then, the species-matched secondary antibodies were used, and the nucleus was stained with DAPI.

Cell apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis was evaluated through the utilization of flow cytometry. Briefly, chondrocytes were collected using 0.25% trypsin, followed by PBS washes, and subsequent incubation with Annexin V buffer and PI for duration of 5 min. Subsequently, apoptotic cells were examined utilizing flow cytometry.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using an RNA isolation kit, and rRNA was depleted prior to cDNA library construction. Stranded sequencing libraries were prepared and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina platform. Raw reads were quality-filtered and aligned to the reference genome using STAR, and gene counts were quantified with featureCounts. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 with criteria of |Fold change|≥ 2 and p ≤ 0.05. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed utilizing the DAVID online tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted on all genes ranked by log₂(fold change) against predefined KEGG gene sets, and significantly enriched gene sets were determined at FDR < 0.25.

Radiological evaluation

The knee joints were obtained for X-ray and micro-CT examination. Plain radiographs of whole knee joints were acquired with an MX-20 Cabinet X-ray System (Faxitron, Tucson, AZ, USA), or the knee joints were scanned using micro-CT analysis system (SkyScan 1176, Bruker, Germany).

Metabolomics analysis

For metabolomics analysis, samples were filtered through a 5-kDa filter and measured using via CE-TOFMS and CE-MS/MS, or UPLC-MS/MS respectively. Metabolites that satisfy the stringent criteria of a p-value less than 0.05, a Variable Importance in Projection exceeding 1, and an absolute log2(fold change) of at least 1 were deemed to be differentially expressed.

Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR) analysis

OCR of BMDMs was assessed by OCR Fluorometric Assay Kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). OCR was assessed under basal conditions, followed by the administration of oligomycin (1 μM), FCCP (1 μM), and a combination of rotenone (0.5 μM) with antimycin (0.5 μM).

Measurement of mtROS content

mtROS content were measured by MitoSOX assays. BMDMs were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 4 × 104 per well and subjected to various culture conditions. Cells were treated with MitoSOX Red (Invitrogen) and MitoTracker Green, then transferred to confocal dishes for analysis of mitochondrial ROS using confocal microscopy.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) levels measurement

JC‑1 staining was performed via manufactures’ protocol. Briefly, BMDMs were incubated with JC-1 staining buffer, centrifuged to remove the buffer, and then re-suspended in cell medium. Next, they were incubated with JC-1 dye solution in a CO2 incubator for 15–30 min. After removing the dye solution, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson) for mean fluorescence.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells and expression of mRNAs was determined using SYBR Premix Ex Tag Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data were analyzed by calculating relative fold change using the formula: 2 ‒ ΔΔCt.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Antibodies used in this study were as follows: AMPKα (1:3000; GB112669-100; Servicebio), p-AMPKα (1:5000; GB114323-100; Servicebio), CD86 (1:1000; GB150054-100; Servicebio), MertK (1:1000; AF100; R&D), Arg1 (1:1000; GB11285-100; Servicebio), IL-10 (1:1000; GB11108-100; Servicebio), while GAPDH (1:1000; GB12002; Servicebio) were used as internal controls.

ELISA analysis

BMDMs were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. The culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove cell debris. The levels of IL-10 and TGF-β in the supernatants were quantified using ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols (IL-10: GEM0003-96 T, Servicebio; TGF-β: GEM0051-96 T, Servicebio). The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD; categorical variables (sex) are shown as counts. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Parametric data were analyzed using paired and unpaired Student’s t‑tests; nonparametric data using the Mann‑Whitney U test and Wilcoxon signed‑rank test. One‑way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD was used for multiple group comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‑square test. A p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Chondrocyte apoptosis and M2 macrophage infiltration are upregulated during OA progression

To investigate the role of chondrocyte apoptosis in mouse OA, we established a mouse model of knee joint OA through surgical destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM). The surgery was conducted on 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, and cartilage tissues were harvested at 3, 6, and 9 weeks postsurgery. Prior to the experimental evaluation, safranin-O/fast green staining was performed on the cartilage tissues of mice in the various groups. As demonstrated in Fig. 1A, the articular cartilage of mice in the Sham group displayed a consistent morphological structure, whereas mice with OA exhibited a gradual loss of chondrocytes and cartilage thickness, leading to an irregular structure after DMM surgery. Similarly, compared with those in the Sham group, the OARSI scores in the DMM group were notably greater, suggesting that articular cartilage degradation in mice progressively worsened (Fig. 1B). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining revealed increased cleaved-caspase3 (c-CASP3) expression in the pathological groups compared with that in the Sham group (Fig. 1A and C). Notably, compared with the other time points, c‑CASP3 expression peaked at 6 weeks post‑surgery, implicating a significant role for apoptosis in early OA.

In addition, histological examination revealed high levels of hyperplasia in the synovium and increased synovitis scores, further indicating accelerated progression of OA following DMM surgery (Fig. 1D and E). Compared with those in the Sham group, the percentages of macrophages (F4/80-positive cells) and M1 macrophages (iNOS-positive cells) in the synovium were significantly greater in mice post-DMM surgery, suggesting abundant macrophage infiltration in the synovium during OA progression (Fig. 1D, F and G). Interestingly, the proportion of M2 macrophages (CD206-positive cells) was in accordance with the expression trend of c-CASP3 in chondrocytes (Fig. 1D, H and I), suggesting a potential correlation between chondrocyte apoptosis and M2 macrophage polarization.

The metabolite secretome of apoptotic chondrocytes activates macrophage M2c polarization

Previous work has indicated that metabolites released from apoptotic cells can act as novel tissue messengers and actively modulate tissue outcomes [20]. Therefore, we explored whether chondrocyte apoptosis influences macrophage polarization. We constructed a coculture system of chondrocytes and macrophages (Fig. 2A), and Western blot analysis revealed that the protein expression levels of MertK and IL-10 (M2c macrophage markers) were significantly increased in macrophages cocultured with chondrocytes pretreated with ultraviolet (UV) light (a method to induce caspase-dependent apoptosis), whereas no significant difference in the expression levels of M1 (CD86) and M2a (Arg1) macrophage markers were observed (Fig. 2B). Flow cytometry further revealed that the proportion of M2 macrophages (CD86 − CD206 +) significantly increased following coculture with chondrocytes pretreated with UV light, whereas the content of M1 macrophages (CD86 + CD206 −) remained unchanged (Fig. 2C, D and E). Moreover, among the increased M2-polarized macrophages, the proportion of the M2c subtype (CD206 + MertK +) was significantly increased, whereas the changes in the M2a subtype (CD206 + Arg1 +) did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2C, F and G). Next, we tested the influence of the supernatant from apoptotic chondrocytes on macrophage polarization. We confirmed that the supernatant of chondrocytes pretreated with UV light also resulted in increased induction of M2c macrophages (Fig. 2H). In addition, both Western blot and flow cytometry results indicated that the expression levels of M2c macrophage markers were comparably inhibited in macrophages incubated with supernatant from UV light-pretreated chondrocytes following treatment with the caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Fig. 2H–K). These findings suggest that the changes in apoptotic chondrocyte supernatant, which can be inhibited by Z-VAD-FMK, may be a potential cause of M2c polarization of macrophages.

To investigate the association between chondrocyte apoptosis and macrophage polarization under OA-related stress, we extended our validation to three additional stimuli: IL-1β (10 ng/mL), tensile strain (10% elongation), and H₂O₂ (50 mU). As shown in Figure S1A–B, IL-1β induced approximately 18% annexin V + /PI − cells, which were reduced to ~ 5% by the pancaspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK. Consistent with the results of the UV exposure experiments, macrophages cocultured with IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes or the conditioned medium supernatant showed no changes in the expression levels of canonical M1 or M2a markers but did significantly upregulate the expression levels of the M2c-associated markers MertK and IL-10 (Figure S1C–F). This M2c polarization was markedly diminished by Z-VAD-FMK treatment (Figure S1E–F), indicating that this activity is dependent on chondrocyte apoptosis. Similar results were obtained under tensile strain (Figure S1G–L) and H₂O₂ exposure (Figure S1M–R). Collectively, these data demonstrate that diverse OA-related stressors, such as inflammation, mechanical overloading, and oxidative stress, can also indirectly drive M2c macrophage polarization via chondrocyte apoptosis.

To address whether apoptosis mediated the alteration of the metabolite secretome in chondrocytes, untargeted metabolomics was used to profile the metabolite secretome in the supernatants of chondrocytes pretreated with UV light. As shown in Fig. 2L and M, untargeted metabolomics analysis identified 116 enriched metabolites and 267 reduced metabolites, with the top 10 enriched metabolites displayed in Fig. 2N. Among the top 10 upregulated metabolites, we subsequently validated the top five metabolites (adenosine, AMP, inosine, spermidine, and UDP-glucose) and found that four of them were significantly increased in the supernatant of UV-stimulated chondrocytes (Fig. 2O). Surprisingly, results showed that, among the top five upregulated metabolites, SPD exhibited the most pronounced change, and it was the only metabolite whose secretion decreased following Z-VAD-FMK treatment (Fig. 2O), suggesting a close association with chondrocyte apoptosis. Besides that, we also confirmed that the SPD levels were also elevated in the supernatant of chondrocytes induced to undergo apoptosis by IL-1β, tensile strain, or H₂O₂, and this increase was markedly reduced by the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Fig. 2P-R). Consequently, we selected SPD for further investigation.

To test whether the effect of the supernatant of apoptotic chondrocytes on M2c macrophage polarization is caused by SPD, we performed immune depletion of SPD in the supernatant of UV light-pretreated chondrocytes (Fig. 2S), which blunted the M2c-polarizing effect, and strikingly, the inhibitory effect of the caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK on MertK and IL-10 expression was reversed by incubating macrophages with SPD (Fig. 2T). In addition, flow cytometry results confirmed that eliminating SPD from the supernatant inhibited the M2c macrophage polarization induced by UV light, and the inhibitory effect of Z-VAD-FMK on M2c polarization was reversed by incubating macrophages with SPD (Fig. 2U–V). Together, these data suggest that UV light-induced apoptosis in chondrocytes alters the metabolite secretome, mainly SPD, and that SPD activates the polarization of macrophages toward the M2c phenotype.

Spermidine regulates macrophage M2c polarization by enhancing mitochondrial function and promoting OXPHOS through AMPKα

To elucidate the mechanism through which macrophage polarization is regulated by alterations in the metabolite secretome, we further performed RNA sequencing analysis on macrophages cocultured with UV light-induced chondrocytes with or without Z-VAD-FMK pretreatment. Differential expression analysis was conducted to compare the UV + Z-VAD-FMK group to the UV group, yielding a group of 867 significantly upregulated genes and 1451 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). KEGG pathway analysis of the DEGs revealed that metabolic pathways were enriched in the genes whose expression levels were significantly downregulated (Fig. 3B). Among these significantly downregulated genes, GSEA further revealed enrichment of these genes in the OXPHOS pathway (Fig. 3C). Moreover, compared with the expression in macrophages cocultured with UV light-treated chondrocytes, the expression levels of OXPHOS-related genes decreased in macrophages cocultured with UV light-treated chondrocytes following treatment with the caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Fig. 3D). Further RT‒qPCR analysis confirmed that following Z-VAD-FMK treatment, macrophages cocultured with UV light-treated chondrocytes exhibited lower expression levels of OXPHOS-related genes, whereas the expression levels of glycolysis-related genes remained similar (Fig. 3E). Therefore, we hypothesized that changes in the metabolite secretome regulate M2c polarization by enhancing OXPHOS instead of glycolysis. To confirm this hypothesis, we measured steady-state ATP levels in macrophages and detected lower ATP concentrations in macrophages cultured with SPD-immunodepleted supernatant or Z-VAD-FMK-treated chondrocytes, whereas these inhibitory effects were reversed by exogenous supplementation with SPD (Fig. 3F). In addition, we examined the OCR and assessed basal respiration, proton leakage, and nonmitochondrial respiration. As shown in Fig. 3G and H, decreased SPD in the supernatant caused by Z-VAD-FMK treatment or SPD immune depletion suppressed macrophage respiration, but this effect was abolished by SPD treatment. Moreover, MitoSOX and JC-1 assays revealed that mitochondria from SPD-deficient supernatant-cultured macrophages led to increased ROS levels (Fig. 3I and J) and decreased MMP levels (Fig. 3K and L), and these effects were reversed by supplementation with SPD. Together, these data support the idea that SPD regulates M2c polarization by enhancing OXPHOS activity.

Previous studies have demonstrated that AMPKα is involved in the metabolic reprogramming of M2-like macrophages [21]. We found that SPD treatment also effectively activated AMPKα and increased the expression of M2c-associated markers (MertK and IL-10) in wild-type macrophages, whereas the knockout of AMPKα in macrophages blocked the promoting effect of SPD on AMPKα phosphorylation and the expression of MertK and IL-10 (Fig. 3M). ELISA analysis further confirmed that SPD promoted the secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β, while the knockout of AMPKα also blocked the SPD-stimulated secretion of these cytokines (Fig. 3N). Furthermore, we found that knocking out AMPKα in macrophages reduced the ATP concentration and OCR of macrophages to a similar extent as those observed in macrophages without SPD treatment (Fig. 3O–Q). Moreover, MitoSOX and JC-1 assays revealed that AMPKα deficiency clearly eliminated the increasing effect of ROS levels and the decreasing effect of MMP levels on macrophages caused by SPD (Fig. 3R and U). Moreover, we further evaluated the role of OXPHOS in the ability of SPD to promote M2c polarization using oligomycin (an OXPHOS inhibitor). As shown in Fig. 3V, OCR analysis confirmed that oligomycin fully abolished the stimulatory effect of SPD on OXPHOS in macrophages. As determined by Western blotting, pretreatment with oligomycin also completely abolished the SPD-induced upregulation of MertK and IL-10 at the protein level (Fig. 3W). Importantly, oligomycin did not affect SPD-induced AMPKα phosphorylation, indicating that OXPHOS acts downstream of AMPKα. ELISA further confirmed that oligomycin blocked the SPD-stimulated secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β (Fig. 3X). Collectively, these data suggest that SPD may act as an additional metabolite to increase AMPKα activation, which could account for the enhanced OXPHOS and mitochondrial functions to promote M2c macrophage polarization.

Conditional knockout of AMPKα reduces macrophage M2c polarization in DMM-Induced mice with OA

To elucidate the role of AMPKα in OA pathogenesis in vivo, we used AMPKαf/f Lyz2-CreERT2 (AMPKα-CKO) mice. Safranin-O/fast green staining revealed that the morphology of the knee joint did not significantly differ between the AMPKα-CKO mice and their AMPKαf/f littermates (Fig. 4A). We subsequently used a tamoxifen-inducible system to delete AMPKα in adult male mice. Western blotting confirmed effective knockout in synovial tissues of macrophage-specific AMPKα knockout mice after tamoxifen injection, whereas cartilage AMPKα levels remained unchanged (Fig. 4B–D). We then further tested the effect of AMPKα knockout on the process of DMM-induced OA in mice. Histological examination revealed high levels of hyperplasia in the synovium and increased synovitis scores in AMPKα-CKO mice, further indicating accelerated progression of OA following DMM surgery (Fig. 4E and F). Compared with that in the Sham-operated mice, the percentage of macrophages in the synovium, particularly M1 macrophages, was significantly greater in AMPKαf/f mice after DMM surgery. Similarly, the proportion of CD206-positive cells in the synovium of AMPKαf/f mice was also increased, suggesting abundant macrophage infiltration in the synovium during OA progression (Fig. 4E and G–I). Interestingly, macrophage-specific conditional knockout of AMPKα markedly decreased the proportions of F4/80-positive cells and CD206-positive cells, whereas the percentage of iNOS-positive cells in the synovium remained unchanged (Fig. 4E and G–I), suggesting that the knockout of AMPKα in macrophages attenuates M2 macrophage polarization in the synovium.

To further elucidate the alteration of synovial macrophages, scRNA-seq analysis of synovial tissues from AMPKαf/f and AMPKα-CKO mice was performed (Fig. 4J). Consistent with the histological results, a decreased proportion of M2-polarized macrophages was observed in the synovial tissues of AMPKα-CKO mice than in those of AMPKαf/f mice (Fig. 4K). Specifically, among all the macrophage subpopulations, M2c macrophages (MertK + macrophages) markedly decreased in the synovial tissues of mice after the conditional knockout of AMPKα (Fig. 4K). To further validate these findings, we analyzed the transcriptional profiles of synovial tissues from AMPKα-CKO and AMPKαf/f mice following DMM surgery. Compared with controls, we observed a significant increase in the expression of M1-related markers, including IL-1β, IL-12, TNFα, and IFNγ, alongside a marked decrease in M2-associated markers, including the M2a-related genes CCL1, Arg1, and Ym1, as well as the M2c-specific marker MertK. Notably, MertK showed a particularly pronounced reduction (Fig. 4L). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining revealed a sharp reduction in the protein expression of MertK in the synovium of AMPKα-CKO mice (Fig. 4M). Together, these data illustrate that macrophage-specific conditional knockout of AMPKα could decrease the infiltration of M2c macrophages in the synovium of DMM-induced OA mice.

Characterization of the fundamental physicophysiological properties of ROS-responsive SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel

To further explore the translational potential of SPD in OA therapy, we developed a ROS-responsive sustained-release hydrogel system that mimics the sustained release of endogenous SPD by apoptotic chondrocytes. LNP@SPD nanoparticles prepared via the thin-film hydration method were confirmed by HPLC analysis to be uniformly dispersed (Fig. 5A), with an encapsulation efficiency of 82.33 ± 2.76% and a drug loading capacity of 6.63 ± 0.53%, and their morphology was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 5B). During the synthesis process, the LNP@SPD nanoparticles exhibited a highly uniform particle size distribution and zeta potential (Fig. 5C). LNP@SPD nanoparticles could achieve rapid and efficient drug release in a ROS environment in a concentration-dependent manner, which provides promising application prospects for the precise treatment of ROS-related diseases, including OA (Fig. 5D–E). The SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel composite system was prepared by compounding and crosslinking LNP@SPD with sodium hyaluronate (Fig. 5F). Its appearance is shown in Fig. 5G, and the SEM image results are shown in Fig. 5H. We further evaluated the degradation behavior and drug release profile of the hydrogel system in PBS and ROS (1 mM H₂O₂) environments within 14 days. In the presence of ROS, the hydrogel exhibited significantly accelerated degradation, with a total mass loss of 86.75 ± 7.91% at day 14, compared with 72.86 ± 2.4% in PBS (Fig. 5I). Correspondingly, the cumulative release of SPD in the ROS environment reached 82.46 ± 3.35% within 14 days, markedly higher than the 44.79 ± 2.49% observed in PBS (Fig. 5J). Biocompatibility of the materials was assessed using a CCK-8 assay and calcein-AM/PI double staining to evaluate the viability of RAW 264.7 cells. The CCK-8 results showed that SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel exhibited no significant cytotoxicity, with cell survival rates exceeding 90% (Fig. 5K). This favorable biocompatibility was further confirmed by calcein-AM/PI staining (Fig. 5L–N). Collectively, the SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel composite system demonstrates ROS-responsive degradation, sustained SPD release over an extended period, low toxicity, and excellent biocompatibility. These features suggest that this ROS-responsive SPD delivery system may represent a useful therapeutic strategy for OA.

Spermidine alleviates OA progression by targeting AMPKα and serves as a potential diagnostic marker

To confirm the protective role of SPD during OA progression, we further performed in vivo experiments with free SPD and SH@LNP@SPD hydrogels in AMPKαf/f or AMPKα-CKO mice (Fig. 6A). We detected a decrease in osteophyte formation in AMPKαf/f mice that received DMM surgery following free SPD and SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel treatment, whereas SPD did not improve osteophyte formation in AMPKα-CKO mice (Fig. 6B and C). In addition, alleviated cartilage degeneration and a lower OARSI grade were observed in AMPKαf/f mice treated with free SPD post-DMM surgery, and the therapeutic effect of the SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel was comparable to that of free SPD, whereas no such improvement effect was observed in AMPKα-CKO mice in the free SPD or SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel treatment groups (Fig. 6D and E). Consistent with the previous results, HE staining revealed relatively normal synovium and decreased synovitis scores in AMPKαf/f mice treated with SPD, and the conditional knockout of AMPKα in the synovial tissue not only exacerbated the severity of synovial inflammation but also eliminated the alleviating effect of SPD (Fig. 6D and F). Similarly, we found that SPD treatment (both free SPD and the SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel) restored the balance between cartilage ECM anabolism and catabolism, as evidenced by increased expression of COL2A1 and decreased expression of MMP13, whereas conditional knockout of AMPKα abolished these effects (Fig. 6D and G–H). Moreover, immunohistochemistry assay revealed that SPD treatment significantly increased MertK expression in AMPKαf/f mice, and notably, the SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel induced even stronger M2c polarization (more MertK⁺ cells in the synovium) compared with the free SPD injection group (Fig. 6D and I). These observations may be attributed to the capacity of the hydrogel to sustain a therapeutically effective concentration of SPD within the joint cavity. However, in AMPKα-CKO mice, the expression of MertK did not differ between the SH@LNP hydrogel and SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel groups (Fig. 6D and I). Pain behavioral analysis revealed significant increases in the paw withdrawal response threshold, locomotion distance, locomotion speed, and rearing frequency in the AMPKαf/f mice treated with free SPD and SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel, whereas the knockout of AMPKα in the synovial tissue not only exacerbated the degree of pain in the mice after DMM surgery but also eliminated the pain relief effect of SPD (Fig. 6J–M). Collectively, these data indicate that SPD attenuates the development of OA and OA pain by targeting AMPKα.

Finally, we investigated whether the levels of SPD were elevated in human synovial fluids. We retrospectively studied the relationships between the levels of SPD in synovial fluids and the clinical characteristics of patients with OA (Fig. 7A and B). As shown in Fig. 7C, 95 patients were enrolled according to the inclusion criteria and were classified using Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grades, and no significant differences in sex or BMI were observed among the five groups. Although age differed significantly across KL grades, post hoc analyses indicated that these differences were limited to comparisons between KL 0 vs. KL IV and KL I vs. KL IV (Supplementary Table S1). Given that OA is a well-established age-related disease, this pattern of intergroup variation is biologically plausible. We subsequently found that the level of SPD in human synovial fluids tended to have a unimodal curve depending on the KL grade (Fig. 7D), indicating that SPD might play a role in the early stage of OA. We then further divided these patients into two groups: the Control group, normal and KL I; and the OA group, KL II-IV. We found that the level of SPD was greater in the OA group than in the Control group (Fig. 7E). However, when we analyzed the data from all the patients, the results revealed that SPD was not associated with any of the clinical characteristics (Fig. 7F–I), including age, BMI, mJSW or KSS. To eliminate the impact of data from patients in KL IV, we assessed the relationship using data from normal individuals and KL I–III patients. We discovered that SPD was moderately positively correlated with age but was not significantly associated with BMI (Fig. 7J–K). Furthermore, the presence of SPD in the synovial fluid was significantly negatively correlated with both mJSW and KSS (Fig. 7L–M), suggesting that SPD was is significantly correlated with OA, as determined by both radiographic assessments and clinical evaluations. Therefore, we conducted a further diagnostic evaluation. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the results revealed that the presence of SPD in synovial fluids strongly differentiated patients afflicted with OA (OA group) from their relatively healthy counterparts (Control group) (Fig. 7N). Notably, when we further divided these patients into normal, early-stage (KL I), advanced-stage (KL II–III) and terminal-stage (KL IV) disease groups, we discovered that SPD could markedly differentiate patients with OA classified as KL I–IV from healthy individuals (Fig. 7O). Furthermore, it demonstrated significant advantages in the early diagnosis of OA, as it had comparable sensitivity and specificity when normal subjects were compared to those with KL I stage disease (Fig. 7P). These findings imply that SPD present in synovial fluids may serve as a promising diagnostic biomarker for OA, particularly in its early stage.

Discussion

The etiology of osteoarthritis is complex and involves a multitude of factors, such as the mechanical stresses exerted on the joint and the predisposing elements associated with the aging process [1]. Progressive degradation of articular cartilage and inflammation of the synovium are the two primary pathological manifestations observed during OA progression [1, 5]. Emerging evidence suggests that apoptosis is a hallmark of chondrocytes undergoing degenerative changes, whereas synovitis is mechanistically linked to the infiltration of polarized macrophages [9, 11]. Although numerous studies have elucidated the roles of chondrocyte apoptosis and macrophage polarization in OA, the interplay between chondrocytes and macrophages during the pathological process of OA, as well as the underlying mechanisms, remain largely enigmatic. Our findings demonstrate that apoptotic chondrocytes actively participate in the resolution of synovial inflammation by promoting macrophage M2c polarization through the release of SPD, thereby potentially initiating an endogenous protective response during the early stage of OA progression.

Chondrocyte apoptosis has long been viewed as a deleterious event that directly contributes to cartilage matrix loss and structural damage [22], while the imbalance in M1/M2 polarization of macrophages is the main cause of synovial inflammation during OA process [12, 23, 24]. Recently, accumulating evidence indicates that apoptosis is not merely a silent or pro-inflammatory process; rather, apoptotic cells can release a variety of metabolites, nucleic acids, and proteins that modulate the function of neighboring cells [25, 26]. For example, Niemann B et al. reported that inosine is a metabolite released during apoptosis with a “replace me” signaling function that regulates thermogenic fat and counteracts obesity [20]. Apoptotic tumor cell metabolites can promote tumor growth and immune evasion. This paradox of apoptosis induction and the protumorigenic effects of tumor cell apoptosis raises the question of whether apoptosis is a suitable cancer therapy [27]. Yu et al. found that SPD secreted by apoptotic cells enhances chemotherapy resistance in osteosarcoma and that targeting SPD may offer a promising therapeutic strategy to increase the effectiveness of chemotherapy [28]. In addition, apoptotic metabolites ameliorate bone aging phenotypes via TCOF1/FLVCR1-mediated mitochondrial homeostasis in age-related diseases [29]. However, the functional significance of apoptotic chondrocyte-derived factors on synovial macrophages has not been previously explored in OA. Our results reveal that apoptotic chondrocytes (induced by UV light, inflammation, mechanical overloading, and oxidative stress), secrete SPD as a key signaling molecule that instructs macrophage polarization toward the M2c phenotype and ultimately alleviate OA progression. This finding introduces a novel paradigm in which early apoptotic chondrocytes serve as a source of endogenous protective signals that temper synovial inflammation and promote tissue homeostasis in OA. It is noteworthy that this protective effect appears to be stage-dependent, as the transition from early to late apoptosis may coincide with the loss of SPD secretion, potentially explaining the divergent roles of apoptosis at different stages of disease progression.

SPD, a naturally occurring polyamine, has garnered considerable attention for its anti-inflammatory effects, antioxidant properties, and improvements in respiratory function [30, 31]. Although exogenous administration of SPD has been reported to exert chondroprotective and anti-synovitic effects in OA models [32, 33], the endogenous source of SPD within the OA joint has remained elusive. Our study identifies early apoptotic chondrocytes as a major cellular source of endogenous SPD. More importantly, we demonstrate that endogenous SPD derived from apoptotic chondrocytes functions as a paracrine mediator that directs macrophage polarization toward the M2c subtype, characterized by high expression of MertK and IL-10, and enhanced secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines. Collectively, these findings establish a previously unrecognized chondrocyte–macrophage communication axis in which early apoptotic chondrocytes may contribute to an anti-inflammatory synovial environment SPD -mediated M2c polarization.

AMPKα is a master regulator of cellular energy homeostasis and has been implicated in macrophage polarization, with emerging evidence suggesting that AMPK activation promotes M2-like phenotypes [21, 34], However, the specific role of AMPKα in M2c polarization and its relationship with SPD signaling had not been characterized. Our data reveal that SPD activates AMPKα phosphorylation in macrophages, and genetic ablation of AMPKα abolishes SPD-induced M2c polarization, establishing AMPKα as an essential upstream mediator of this process. Besides that, we further uncovered the downstream metabolic program that drives M2c polarization. We found that SPD selectively enhances OXPHOS, as evidenced by increased OCR, reduced JC-1 green/red ratio, and decreased mitoSOX. Importantly, AMPKα deficiency abrogated all of these metabolic changes, indicating that SPD may contribute to a metabolic switch toward OXPHOS in an AMPKα-dependent manner in macrophage. Using pharmacological inhibition with oligomycin, we confirmed that OXPHOS activity is required for SPD-induced M2c polarization, as blockade of ATP synthase completely abrogated the upregulation of MertK and IL-10 without affecting upstream AMPKα phosphorylation. Collectively, these data suggest that SPD activates AMPKα and reprograms macrophage glucose metabolism toward enhanced oxidative phosphorylation, thereby promoting M2c polarization.

To explore the potential elevation of SPD levels in human synovial fluids, we meticulously examined samples from patients afflicted with OA alongside those from healthy individuals. Our findings revealed a noteworthy and progressive increase in SPD concentrations in the synovial fluids of patients at the early stages of OA. Based on our animal experiments, we speculate that SPD secretion by apoptotic chondrocytes may play a protective role during the early stages of OA. As the disease progresses, extensive chondrocyte loss might reduce the endogenous SPD pool, potentially contributing to insufficient protective effects–a hypothesis that warrants further investigation. Currently, methodologies for the early assessment of OA or for monitoring its pathological progression are limited. Furthermore, the majority of reported biomarkers lack specificity because they are derived from plasma or urea [35, 36]. Nonetheless, our data reveal a marked and progressive increase in SPD concentrations in the synovial fluids of patients diagnosed with early-stage OA. The results of our statistical analyses indicate that SPD can effectively differentiate OA patients from relatively healthy individuals. Furthermore, owing to its commendable sensitivity and specificity, SPD has notable advantages for the early diagnosis of OA. Besides that, we developed a ROS-responsive sustained-release hydrogel system to explore the translational potential of SPD in OA therapy. The comparable efficacy of the SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel to free SPD, coupled with its superior ability to sustain M2c polarization, underscores the importance of maintaining adequate SPD concentrations in the joint space-a principle that may inform future clinical formulation strategies. Consequently, these findings not only substantiate the therapeutic potential of SPD as a clinically translatable agent for OA treatment but also indicate its promise as a biomarker for the early diagnosis of OA.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we demonstrate that apoptotic chondrocytes secrete SPD and promote M2c macrophage polarization in vitro, and exogenous supplementation with SPD, alleviated OA progression by promoting M2c macrophage polarization in vivo, the in vivo relevance of chondrocyte-derived SPD to synovial macrophage polarization and OA progression requires further investigation using cell-specific depletion or pharmacological blockade strategies. Second, while OXPHOS is identified as a critical downstream effector of SPD signaling, the molecular links between AMPKα activation and mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming remain to be elucidated. Third, our study focused exclusively on early apoptotic chondrocytes; the contribution of other joint-resident cells, such as synovial fibroblasts and osteoblasts, to endogenous SPD production in OA remains unexplored. Fourth, our clinical analysis was conducted using a single-center cohort of 95 patients without an independent validation set, and the specificity of synovial fluid SPD for OA versus other arthritic conditions was not examined. Fifth, the long-term safety and degradation kinetics of the SH@LNP@SPD hydrogel in large animal models remain unexplored. Addressing these issues will require lineage-tracing, cell-type-specific knockout, and prospective multicenter studies.

In conclusion, in the present study, we demonstrated that the apoptotic chondrocyte metabolite SPD plays an essential role in early OA through the activation of macrophage M2c polarization (Fig. 7Q). We assert that SPD serves as a pivotal regulator of chondrocyte apoptosis and macrophage M2c polarization, mediates the crosstalk between chondrocytes and macrophages and governs OA pathogenesis. Synovial fluid SPD may hold diagnostic value for early-stage OA and strategies aimed at sustaining SPD may represent effective therapeutic and preventive approaches for OA.
