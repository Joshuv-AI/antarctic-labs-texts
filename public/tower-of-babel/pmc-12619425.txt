Volatile sedation in critically ill adults undergoing mechanical ventilation: not all sedatives are equivalent, in sustainability!
Authors: Florence Boissier, Nicolas Massart, Thomas Frapard, Marie Lecronier, Alexandre Demoule, David Grimaldi, Alain F. Kalmar, Laurent Zieleskiewicz
Published: 2025

A recently published systematic review and meta-analysis, comparing volatile sedation to intravenous sedation, suggested that volatile sedation may increase mortality in mechanically ventilated critically ill adults [1]. The results were mainly driven by a recent large randomized trial assessing sevoflurane in patients with moderate to severe ARDS [2]. As stated in the comment of the meta-analysis by L’Her et al. [3], this limits the generalizability in other settings and with others volatile sedation, such as isoflurane, that are being evaluated in completed or ongoing trials, such as INSPiRE-ICU 1 and 2, and INASED (ClinicalTrial.gov NCT05312385, NCT05327296, NCT04341350).

Beyond the lack of evidence supporting a benefit of volatile anesthetics, Yamamoto et al. underlined that volatile anesthetics are powerful greenhouse gas and hence contribute to global warming. They suggested that future research should include these outcomes to inform clinical decision-making [1].

We felt it necessary to highlight this critical climatic issue. Compared to CO2, inhaled sedations have a Global Warming Potential at a 100-year time horizon (GWP100) which is 2,590 higher for desflurane, 195 for sevoflurane, and 539 for isoflurane [4].

This metric reflects the cumulative heat absorption over a century. However, because most of the heat-trapping occurs in the first decades, the GWP20 is considerably higher and more relevant for clinical decision-making, since it captures the urgent near-term consequences for climate tipping points [5].

The carbon dioxide equivalent footprint of hypnotic drugs per intervention when using a total intravenous anesthesia strategy is 20 times lower than when using a mixed strategy in the operative room [6]. Even when Sevoflurane is used, the halogenated anaesthetic with the lowest emissions, the carbon footprint of inhaled sedation is still eight times higher than that of propofol.

Similar results were observed in ICU setting, with an estimation of a carbon footprint of 72.9 kg CO2Eq per day for sevoflurane, and 171.5 kg CO2Eq per day for isoflurane for an average ICU patient of 70 kg, whereas propofol 2% releases 2.8 kg CO2Eq [7]. Moreover, isoflurane has an ozone-depleting effect due to its chlorine atom [7].

Not all volatile sedatives are equivalent: similar calculation assuming 0.3 MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration) of desflurane, would represent a carbon footprint of 2,400 kg CO2Eq per day [7]. The disproportionate environmental impact of desflurane led the European Commission to ban it from January 2026 (https://climate.ec.europa.eu/eu-action/fluorinated-greenhouse-gases/f-gas-legislation_en).

Compared to the estimated carbon footprint of one day of ICU care, which ranges between 60 and 178 kg CO2 per patients per day, the carbon footprint of volatile sedation is high [8, 9]. Importantly, sedation is one of the most used medications in ICU and inhaled sedation implementation, even with sevoflurane or isoflurane, would dramatically increase greenhouse gas emissions linked to ICU stays [8]. Considering an average number of 2000 of ventilator-days per year, based on a recent publication of a 21-bed polyvalent French intensive care unit [10], we estimated that, assuming that the number under sedation would be 1000 days per year, the annual carbon footprint of total sedation by isoflurane would be 171,500 Kg CO2Eq, compared to 5,500 Kg CO2 Eq with propofol 2%. The estimated annual carbon footprint of this ICU being 355,602 kg CO2 Eq. (8), this would lead to an increase of around 50%.

Finally, although propofol elimination contributes to ecotoxicity, the environmental impact of halogenated gases may be significantly higher. Due to their carbon–fluorine bonds, halogenated gases are classified as “forever chemicals.” Their use leads to local water pollution through excreted metabolites and global pollution via the deposition of polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), such as trifluoroacetic acid [11, 12]. For example, HFIP (hexafluoroisopropanol), a metabolite of sevoflurane, and TFA, the terminal degradation product of halogenated gases, accumulate indefinitely in surface waters and even food products, as shown by the exponential rise of TFA concentrations in wine bottles from 1945 to 2025 [13]. In the case of our 21-bed unit, the amount of isoflurane used could contaminate 382 billion liters of water up to the European upper safety threshold limit for PFAS of 500 ng/l [11, 14].

Intensivists should consider the environmental implications when selecting ICU sedation strategies.

To date, and despite robust and well-conducted clinical trials, scientific data do not support routine inhaled sedation in critically ill adult patients. Given the urgency of the climate crisis, we recommend restricting its use to well-designed clinical trials that rigorously assess both clinical efficacy and environmental impact.
