Effects of oleic acid supplementation on ileal fatty acid digestibility, intestinal barrier function, and cecal microbiota in Pekin ducks fed a high-fat diet
Authors: W.X. Zhang, G. Tian, K.Y. Zhang, Y. Xuan, Q.F. Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the effects of a diet supplemented with oleic acid (OA) on apparent ileal digestibility (AID) of fatty acids, intestinal barrier function, and the cecal microbiota as well as its metabolites in Pekin ducks fed a high-fat diet (HFD). A total of 240 male ducks (14 d of age) were randomly assigned to three groups: control group (Con), high-fat diet (HFD), and the HFD plus 0.50% OA (HFD_OA). The experimental duration was 28 days. Results demonstrated that HFD significantly decreased the AID of ether extract and palmitic acid (P < 0.05), whereas OA supplementation effectively restored these levels and further increased the AID of total saturated fatty acids (SFAs) (P < 0.05), which led to a decreased flow of unabsorbed SFAs at the terminal ileum. Meanwhile, OA reinforced the intestinal epithelial barrier by decreasing endotoxin concentrations in both serum and ileal mucosa (P < 0.05) and upregulating the mRNA expression of tight junction (TJ) proteins (Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1) and lysozyme (LYZ), along with suppressing MLCK mRNA expression (P < 0.05). Moreover, OA intervention specifically enriched the abundance of Parabacteroides and significantly increased the concentrations of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) (P < 0.05). Spearman correlation analysis further revealed that Parabacteroides enrichment was positively correlated with LYZ mRNA expression and SCFA production (P < 0.05) and was negatively correlated with serum endotoxin levels. These results suggest that OA enhances proximal assimilation of SFAs to reduce hindgut lipid overload, thereby ameliorating dysbiosis, suppressing endotoxin concentrations in both serum and ileal mucosa, and restoring TJs to reverse HFD-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction in Pekin ducks.

Introduction

The Pekin duck is a cornerstone of the global waterfowl industry, where a high-fat diet (HFD) is essential to support its rapid growth trajectory (Chen et al., 2023). However, an oversupply of dietary lipids often exceeds the innate digestive and absorptive capacity of the ducks, leading to the excessive accumulation of undigested lipids within the intestinal lumen. This metabolic surcharge induces lipotoxicity-mediated barrier dysfunction (Tanaka et al., 2020). Notably, undigested dietary lipids can significantly alter the composition of the gut microbiota (Conlon et al., 2014; Singh et al., 2017). HFD feeding significantly reduces the abundance of beneficial bacteria associated with barrier function, while promoting the overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens that disrupt barrier homeostasis (Cani et al., 2007; Derrien et al., 2004; Heisel et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2017). Emerging evidence suggests that the physiological benefits of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) are often linked to their ability to modulate the gut microbiota (Millman et al., 2020; Patterson et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2019). Meanwhile, it is well established that UFAs and saturated fatty acids (SFAs) exhibit a synergistic effect during lipid digestion (Tancharoenrat et al., 2013). Building on these findings, enhancing dietary lipid digestibility through UFA supplementation to reduce luminal fat accumulation may represent a targeted strategy to alleviate microecological dysbiosis and protect the intestinal barrier of birds fed an HFD.

Oleic acid (OA), a typical monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA), is known to enhance the digestibility of dietary SFAs in poultry by promoting synergistic micelle formation (Garrett et al., 1975; Tutunchi et al., 2020). Moreover, OA is recognized for its protective role in the intestine, as it has been shown to preserve epithelial integrity in models of inflammatory bowel disease (Basson et al., 2020). In our previous study, we revealed that OA supplementation mitigates systemic lipid disorders by promoting the conversion of excess dietary lipid into cytosolic lipid droplets stored within the intestine (Zhang et al., 2025). Nonetheless, it remains unclear whether OA supplementation can alter the gut microbiota and enhance intestinal barrier function specifically by improving the ileal digestibility of dietary fatty acids. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of OA supplementation on apparent ileal digestibility (AID) of fatty acids, intestinal barrier function, and the cecal microbiota as well as its metabolites in Pekin ducks fed an HFD.

Materials and methods

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University, China (ethics code permit no. SAUPNZ-23-08).

Birds, diet, and management

A total of 240 male Pekin ducks aged 14 d were randomly divided into three groups with 8 replicates of 10 ducks each. The three groups were control diet (Con) with 3% palm oil, high-fat diet (HFD) with 9% palm oil, and the HFD plus 0.50% OA (HFD_OA). The experimental duration was 28 days. The experimental dietary formulations were prepared according to our previous study (Zhang et al., 2025). The analyzed nutrient levels and fatty acid profiles of the three experimental diets are presented in Table 1. Palm oil and OA were purchased from Yihai Kerry Arawana Holdings Co., Ltd. and Shanghai BioEngineering Co., Ltd., respectively. All diets were provided in mash form. The ducks were housed in cages (1.0 × 0.8 × 0.6 m) within a temperature- and humidity-controlled room. Sample collection and measurement

Throughout the experimental period, feed consumption was recorded on a per-replicate basis to calculate the cumulative feed intake (CFI) per bird. At 42 d of age, eight birds per group (one per replicate) were randomly selected to collect blood samples from the jugular vein and centrifuged at 3500 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting serum was stored at −20°C for endotoxin analysis. Then all birds were eeuthanized by cervical dislocation to harvest ileal mucosal tissues for endotoxin analysis and evaluation of the mRNA expression of barrier-related genes. Additionally, cecal contents were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for the determination of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and 16S rDNA sequencing.

Apparent ileal digestibility of dietary fat and fatty acid analysis

To determine the AID of dietary fat and fatty acid, eight birds from each treatment group were selected on d 44 based on the average body weight of their respective pens. The ducks were fasted for 24 h and then fed their respective experimental diets supplemented with titanium dioxide (TiO2; 5 g/kg) as an indigestible marker ad libitum. Porvide feed for at least 4 h before euthanasia to ensure sufficient ileal digesta content. The ileal digesta was promptly frozen at –20°C and subsequently freeze-dried. Dry matter (DM) and ether extract (EE) contents were determined in accordance with AOAC (2006) protocols. The concentrations of 36 individual FAs were analyzed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. Briefly, 0.2 g of freeze-dried digesta was homogenized with chloroform and a methanol-water mixture, followed by centrifugation. The lower organic layer was collected and evaporated to dryness under vacuum for 12 h. Following saponification and methylation, the resulting FA methyl esters were extracted. The samples were analyzed using an Agilent 8890-5977B GC-MS system, with data acquisition and processing performed using the MassHunter software. AID coefficients were calculated as previously described (Tan et al., 2020).

Furthermore, to evaluate the total amount of undigested or unabsorbed fatty acids entering the hindgut, the cumulative terminal ileal flow of individual fatty acids over the entire experimental period was calculated using the following equation:

CumulativeFAflow=CFI×C_diet×(1−AID)where cumulative FA flow represents the total unabsorbed flow of a specific fatty acid at the terminal ileum; CFI is the cumulative feed intake per bird during the entire trial; C_diet is the analyzed concentration of the specific fatty acid in the experimental diets; and AID is the apparent ileal digestibility coefficient of that corresponding fatty acid.

Intestinal barrier function evaluation

Endotoxin concentrations in the serum and ileal mucosa were determined using commercial assay kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. To evaluate the mRNA expression of genes related to intestinal barrier function, total RNA was extracted from the ileal mucosa using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Shiga, Japan). The PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara) was used to reverse transcribe the RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA). Cecal short-chain fatty acid analysis

Cecal SCFA concentrations were analyzed following a previously described protocol with minor modifications (Fang et al., 2009). Briefly, cecal samples were homogenized in deionized water and deproteinized using 25% metaphosphoric acid. The resulting supernatants were spiked with crotonic acid as an internal standard and subsequently analyzed via a high-performance gas chromatograph (Varian CP-3800, USA).

Cecal microbiota analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted from cecal digesta using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit. The 16S rRNA V3-V4 region was amplified (primers 338F/806R) and sequenced on an Illumina Nextseq2000 platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology, China). Raw sequences were denoised into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using DADA2 within the QIIME 2 pipeline, followed by taxonomic assignment against the SILVA database (v138). Microbial diversity analysis, including Bray-Curtis distance-based Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) and LEfSe for identifying differential taxa, was performed using standard bioinformatics pipelines.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SAS 9.2 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and results are presented as the mean ± standard error. The normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. For data meeting the assumptions of parametric analysis, an independent-samples Student's t-test was employed to compare specific groups (Con vs. HFD; HFD vs. HFD_OA). Otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was performed. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05, and a trend was considered at 0.05 < P < 0.10.

Effects of OA on the cecal microbiota

As shown in Fig. 6A, regarding α-diversity, no significant differences were observed in the Ace, Shannon, and Simpson indices of the cecal microbiota across all experimental groups (P > 0.05). PCoA revealed a high degree of spatial overlap among samples from different treatments, indicating that the β-diversity of the cecal microbiota remained unaffected (P > 0.05) (Fig. 6B). Venn diagram analysis illustrated the distribution of ASVs (Fig. 6C): 651 core ASVs were shared among the three groups, whereas the Con, HFD, and HFD_OA groups harbored 2554, 2458, and 2433 unique ASVs, respectively. Discussion

The efficiency of dietary lipid assimilation heavily depends on the physicochemical properties of fatty acids. Due to their high melting points and poor aqueous solubility, long-chain SFAs (particularly PA) readily chelate with divalent cations to form insoluble metallic soaps (Burch et al., 2021). These soaps strongly resist enzymatic hydrolysis and micellar solubilization, severely impairing intestinal digestion and absorption (Brink et al., 1995; Glasser et al., 2008). Consistent with this, the Pekin ducks fed HFD exhibited a significant reduction in the AID of EE and total SFAs, resulting in a substantial influx of undigested lipids into the hindgut, such as the cecum. Conversely, OA supplementation significantly improved total SFA digestibility and concurrently reduced their terminal ileal flow. Mechanistically, OA optimizes luminal emulsification and expands the loading capacity of mixed micelles for hydrophobic SFAs, thereby accelerating the partitioning of PA into the absorptive phase (Freeman 1969; Ockner et al., 1972; Ravindran et al., 2016). Ultimately, this enhances absorption in the small intestine, effectively reducing the residual burden of unabsorbed lipids entering the hindgut.

The influx of unassimilated lipid substrates into the distal intestine serves as a critical environmental determinant shaping the hindgut microbiota. Notably, de Wit et al. (2012) demonstrated that when the proximal intestine is overloaded with poorly digestible SFAs (such as palm oil), the distal small intestine undergoes compensatory metabolic adaptations. They further postulated that such an abnormal luminal lipid environment and associated metabolic perturbations might precipitate downstream microbial dysbiosis. Consistent with this hypothesis, the significant influx of undigested SFAs observed in the HFD ducks in the present study induced marked microecological shifts in the cecum. This was initially evidenced by a perturbed B/B ratio (Bacillota/Bacteroidota ratio, formerly known as the F/B ratio). Although a high B/B ratio is traditionally associated with obesity, recent meta-analyses and studies have highlighted that the direction of this ratio can be highly variable and context-dependent, with a significant decrease also serving as a signature of HFD-induced dysbiosis (Magne et al., 2020). In this study, the altered B/B ratio represents a clear departure from microbial homeostasis (Xiao et al., 2022). Moreover, HFD feeding significantly depleted key taxa crucial for lipid metabolism and cholesterol biotransformation, most notably the [Eubacterium] coprostanoligenes group (Wei et al., 2021). Concurrently, a significant reduction was observed in core SCFA-producing taxa, including Fusobacterium, Thomasclavelia, and Ruminococcaceae (Dahlstrand Rudin et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2024; Rong et al., 2025).

Conversely, by significantly reducing the influx of unabsorbed lipid residues into the hindgut, OA supplementation attenuated the HFD-induced dysbiosis. Although overall diversity indices remained unchanged, OA intervention selectively enriched beneficial SCFA-producing consortia. Notably, the HFD_OA group exhibited a significant enrichment of Parabacteroides, a genus well-documented to mitigate inflammation and promote SCFA synthesis (Liu, J. et al., 2025). Consistent with this microbial shift, OA intervention elevated the concentrations of total SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, isobutyrate, and butyrate, in the cecal digesta. As key metabolites of the gut microbiota (Mann et al., 2024), SCFAs play a central role in remodeling the intestinal microenvironment and maintaining the integrity of the epithelial barrier. These SCFAs, particularly butyrate, serve as the primary energy source for enterocytes and are known to enhance the assembly of tight junctions while inhibiting the MLCK-mediated contraction of the perijunctional actomyosin ring (Liu, L. et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2009). Collectively, this OA-driven restoration of the cecal microbial architecture and its derived SCFA metabolites provides the essential biochemical substrates for repairing the compromised epithelial barrier.

Indeed, OA supplementation effectively mitigated this barrier impairment by upregulating the mRNA expression of TJ-related genes through targeted modulation of the gut microecology. Crucially, Spearman correlation analysis revealed that the OA-induced enrichment of Parabacteroides exhibited significant positive correlations with LYZ mRNA expression and cecal SCFA concentrations. This highlights its essential contributions to innate immune defense and intestinal energy metabolism (Wang et al., 2016), supplying the epithelium with substrates vital for cellular turnover and repair. While a compromised barrier architecture typically facilitates the passive paracellular translocation of endotoxins (Guo et al., 2015; Leaphart et al., 2007), OA attenuated this effect by optimizing the specific microbe-metabolite axis to effectively restrict the paracellular leakage of endotoxin. This was evidenced by the significantly reduced endotoxin levels in both the serum and ileal mucosa of the HFD_OA ducks, effectively mitigating HFD-induced metabolic endotoxemia. Collectively, these data indicate that OA prevents HFD-induced intestinal damage by enriching Parabacteroides and optimizing the metabolite profile, thereby actively suppressing MLCK transcription to restore tight junctions and block endotoxin leakage.

Conclusion

OA supplementation to the HFD can mitigate undigested lipid overload in the hindgut by enhancing the proximal assimilation of saturated fatty acids, which ameliorates intestinal dysbiosis through the enrichment of Parabacteroides and increased SCFA production to restore TJs and restrict endotoxin leakage. These findings highlight that OA can act as an effective nutritional intervention to preserve gut homeostasis and combat intestinal health problems caused by dietary lipids in poultry.
