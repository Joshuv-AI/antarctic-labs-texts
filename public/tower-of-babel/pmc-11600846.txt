Dietary patterns and premature coronary artery disease: result from the Iran premature coronary artery disease (IPAD) study
Authors: Ghazal Ghasempour Dabaghi, Ehsan Zarepur, Mehrdad Rabiee Rad, Noushin Mohammadifard, Fahimeh Haghighatdoost, Alireza Khosravi, Nahid Azdaki, Nahid Salehi, Masoud Lotfizadeh, Samad Ghaffari, Arsalan Salari, Mostafa Cheraghi, Ahmadreza Assareh, Nizal Sarrafzadegan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPremature coronary artery disease (PCAD) is one of the major health concerns leading to considerable death and disabilities. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship of dietary patterns with risk of PCAD.MethodsIran premature coronary diseases (IPAD) is a case-control study consists of 3159 participants. Obstructive CAD in ≥ 75% of at least one coronary artery or ≥ 50% in the left main artery based on coronary angiography in women under 70 and men under 60 years old was considered PCAD. The habitual dietary intake of participants was collected using a semi-quantitative validated food frequency questionnaire (SFFQ). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to extract dietary patterns.ResultsThree main dietary patterns including healthy, Western and high-fat, high-sugar were identified. Patients categorized in the last tertile of the healthy dietary pattern had a decreased risk of PCAD (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.64–0.93), while those in the top tertile of Western (OR = 2.38, 95 CI:1.97–2.86) and High-fat simple carbohydrate (HFSC) (OR = 3.10, 95% CI:2.57–3.75) diets had increased risk of PCAD.ConclusionsA healthy dietary pattern was significantly related to a decreased risk of PCAD presence, whereas Western and HFSC diets were connected to a higher risk of PCAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04333-9.Keywords: Coronary disease, Coronary heart disease, Dietary patterns, Healthy diet, Western diet

Background

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a leading cause of death globally, accounting for over 350,000 deaths annually [1]. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study has found that more than 30% of cardiovascular disease burden is attributed to CAD [2]. There has been a notable rise in hospitalizations for CAD among women under 50 in recent years during the last two decades [3]. Premature coronary artery diseases (PCAD) are generally defined as CAD occurring in women and men younger than 65 and 55 years, respectively [4], although onset ages may vary in different studies [4–6]. Identifying risk factors for PCAD is crucial due to the potential for greater life expectancy in these professionally productive younger patients who may face challenges in diagnosis [7].

Unhealthy dietary habits, such as excessive consumption of meat, eggs, and snacks, have been linked to a significant portion of CAD risk in the population [8]. It is claimed that an improvement in the diet quality from current global situation to healthy, could prevent 11 million premature deaths worldwide [9, 10]. Therefore, investigating the relationship between dietary patterns and premature cardiovascular diseases is crucial. There is previous research on the association between cardiovascular diseases and diets, for example suggesting a positive relationship between Western dietary patterns and cardiovascular diseases [11, 12]. However, the investigation on the connection between dietary patterns and premature artery diseases and whether unhealthy dietary patterns can significantly cause cardiovascular diseases in younger people is limited.

In addition, due to variations in dietary intakes and lifestyle factors between different populations, it is worthwhile to explore the association of major dietary patterns with diseases. To our knowledge, most of available studies in this regard among Iranians have examined the association of dietary patterns with cardiometabolic risk factors like metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, and adiposity [13]. Although the results of these studies are investigated with others conducted in other populations [14], identifying the properties of most deleterious or favorable dietary patterns in relation to cardiovascular disease (CVD) in young ages would be informative for that population. Therefore, despite a lack of research on the impact of dietary patterns on PCAD, this study aimed to assess how different dietary patterns may influence the risk and severity of PCAD using data from the Iran Premature Coronary Artery Disease (IPAD) study [15]. This approach allows for a comprehensive examination of the association of dietary patterns with CAD.

Methods

Study design and population

This study is conducted in the framework of Iran premature coronary artery disease (IPAD). IPAD is a case-control study investigating the prevalence and risk factors of PCAD among nine different ethnicities in Iran, whose study design has previously been discussed elsewhere [15]. In brief, the IPAD information was collected between 2020 and 2022 from 3159 participants who underwent coronary angiography and were aged ≤ 70 and ≤ 60 years for women and men, respectively. Participants were enrolled from 15 Iran field centers (Isfahan, Tabriz and Maragheh, Kermanshah, Khorramabad, Ahvaz, Shahrekord, Zahedan, Birjand, Yazd, Rasht, Zanjan, and Shiraz) and belonged to nine different ethnicities in Iran including Fars, Azari, Kurd, Arab, Lor, Gilak, Balouch, Qashqaei, and Bakhtiari. Participants were asked to fill out questionnaires on the ethnicity of their parents. We consider each ethnicity positive if they indicate a similar ethnicity with both parents. The exclusion criteria were any previous history of coronary artery diseases such as coronary artery bypass surgery, balloon angioplasty, or percutaneous coronary intervention and pregnancy. Having an occlusion of at least single coronary artery equal and above 75% or left main coronary of equal or more than 50% and normal coronary artery were our criteria for case and control groups, respectively. IPAD was approved by Medical Sciences institutional review boards (IR.MUI.REC.1396.2.055), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measurements

Habitual dietary intake of participants was collected using a semi-quantitative validated 112-item food frequency questionnaire (SFFQ) for adults [16]. FFQ was validated against twelve 24-hour dietary recalls (24DRs), and the correlation between food items ranged from 0.48 (oils and fats) to 0.65 (sweets). Trained dietitian completed the questionnaires on the one-one basis. The SFFQ consists of 11 domains: (1) dairy, (2) fruits, (3) vegetables, (4) meat, (5) legumes and cereals, (6) fast foods, (7) sweets, (8) oils and fats, (9) nuts (10) sauces, and 11) soft drinks. Participants were asked to report the frequency of food consumption based on ten categories: seldom/never, 1–3 per month, once per week, 2–4 per week, 5–6 per week, 1 per day, 2–3 per day, 4–5 per day, and 6 or more per day. For the analysis, the reported consumption frequency of each food was converted into daily frequency and multiplied it by corresponding portion sizes. Seldom and never were calculated as “zero.” All data were entered into the Iranian Food Consumption Program (IFCP) to calculate the daily intake of energy and nutrients [17].

CAD was defined as 75% stenosis or greater in ≥ 1 vessel or ≥ 50% in the left main artery. CAD severity, indicate by the number of obstructed arteries according to angiographic result including single vessel disease (SVD), two-vessel disease (2VD), and three-vessel disease (3VD). For the present analysis, the following covariates were assessed using standardized questionnaires: age, sex, religion, income, education, physical activity, anxiety level, status of smoking, alcohol consumption, the medication used, medical history, and family history of illness. Moreover, we considered the following biochemical parameters after 12 h fasting: triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), and fasting blood sugar (FBS). Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined as having two FBS levels of 126 mg/dL or higher, a 2-hour blood sugar level exceeding 200 mg/dL, or using medications to control blood sugar levels. Participants with TC levels of at least 200 mg/dL, TG of at least 150 mg/dL, LDL-c levels of more than 130 mg/dL, HDL-C levels below 40 mg/dL for men and below 50 mg/dL for women, or those who were taking lipid-lowering agents due to a previous diagnosis of dyslipidemia were identified as dyslipidemia. Anthropometrics including height (with stadiometer after taking off the shoes to the nearest of 0.5 cm), weight (while participants wearing minimal clothes to the nearest of 0.5 kg using a Seca scale) and waist circumference (WC) were assessed by trained personnel directly. Body-mass index (BMI) was defined as weight (kilograms) divided by the square of height (meters). General obesity was defined as having a BMI of 30 or higher, while abdominal obesity was determined by a WC exceeding 102 cm in men and 88 cm in women [18]. Sitting blood pressure was measured two times in both arms after 10 min of rest, and the mean pressures were calculated. Hypertension (HTN) was characterized by the presence of at least one of the following conditions: having an average systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher, an average diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or higher, or taking medications to manage hypertension.

Statistical analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to extract dietary patterns based on 32 food groups derived from FFQ (Table 1). The interpretability of factors based on the similarity of foods loaded beside each other, eigenvalues > 1 and the Scree test determined which factors should be retained. Specific food items with a factor loading exceeding |0.2| were emphasized as composing that factor for ease of interpretation. Each participant received a factor score for each identified pattern. Finally, we reached three dietary patterns which could explain 23% of variance. The estimated factor scores for dietary patterns were grouped into tertiles and the participants were categorized by the tertiles of dietary patterns. The tertiles of dietary pattern was determined based on pooled all participants. Categorical and continues variables presented as frequency (percentage) and mean ± standard deviation (SD), respectively. Chi-square tests or one-way ANOVA were used for comparison between groups. Food intakes were adjusted for age, sex and energy intake, and compared with ANCOVA across the tertiles of each identified factor. The association between dietary pattern and PCAD were examined using binary logistic regression and the relationship between dietary pattern and PCAD severity was evaluated using ordinal regression. For evaluation of PCAD and dietary pattern Model 1 was Adjustment for age, sex, total energy and ethnicity. Model 2 was further adjusted for education, smoking, physical activity, alcohol, marital status. BMI, DM, HTN, Hypercholesterolemia, Aspirin were additionally adjusted in Model 3. For evaluation of the connection between dietary pattern and PCAD severity full adjusted model included adjustment for age, sex, ethnicity, total energy, smoking, alcohol, education, physical activity, marital status, BMI, aspirin, DM, HTN and hypercholesterolemia. SPSS version 26 software was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Three major dietary patterns according to the scree plot were identified by factor analysis. These factors explained 22.98% of the total variance in food items. Table 1 summarizes the primary food items and factor loadings for each dietary pattern extracted from factor analyses. The healthy pattern was demonstrated by high consumption of green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, yellow red vegetables, fruits, legumes, dough, poultry, garlic, fruit juice, fish and seafood, low-fat dairy, pickles, and potatoes. The Western diet was characterized by a high intake of fast food and processed meat, sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB), sweet dessert, mayonnaise, refined grain, snacks, and French fries. Meanwhile, the high fat simple carbohydrate (HFSC) pattern was loaded by trans saturated fatty acids (SFA) fats, red meat, butter, organ meat, sugars, condiments, and high-fat dairy (Table 1).

Demographic and clinical characteristics of 3159 subjects consisted of 1427 (45%) females and 1732 (55%) males are shown in Table 2. The 1915 participants were categorized in the case group and 1244 participant categorized in control group. Participants were categorized into three groups based on the tertiles of identified dietary patterns with 1053 (33.3%) participants in each group. number of PCAD cases presented significant difference among tertiles of healthy, western and HSFC dietary patterns. The mean age differed significantly among three levels of dietary patterns in the healthy and Western dietary patterns (p < 0.01). Physical activity (p = 0.04), total cholesterol (p = 0.001), LDL (p = 0.003), and HDL (p = 0.047), smoking (p < 0.001), alcohol use (p < 0.001), aspirin (p < 0.001), DM (p < 0.001), HTN (p = 0.004) and hyperlipidemia (p < 0.001) varied significantly between the three levels of the HFSC diet. Significant variations were seen for FBS (p = 0.043), HDL (p < 0.001), LDL (p = 0.045), cholesterol (p = 0.002), TG (p = 0.024), WC (p = 0.022), BMI (p = 0.259) and systolic blood pressure (SBP) (p = 0.001), marital status (p = 0.025), sex, ethnicity, smoking, alcohol, level of education, DM, HTN, hyperlipidemia (all Ps < 0.001) in Western dietary pattern. Patients in the last tertile of Western and HFSC diets reported more smoking and alcohol consumption. Also, patients in the higher tertiles of all three dietary patterns were more likely to have higher physical activity. HTN, DM, and hyperlipidemia were more frequent in patients categorized in the first tertile of Western and HFSC dietary patterns. Also, participants in the second tertile of the healthy diet were more likely to have HTN (p = 0.04) (Table 2).

The adjusted analysis (full adjustment for age, sex, total energy, education, marital status, smoking, alcohol, physical activity, BMI, DM, HTN, hypercholesterolemia and aspirin) revealed that higher adherence to the Western and HFSC diets was associated with an increased risk of PCAD. Discussion

The goal of this study was to assess the risk of PCAD and its severity among three different dietary patterns: healthy, Western, and HFSC. Our findings indicated that individuals with higher adherence to a healthy diet had a decreased risk of PCAD, whereas those with higher scores of the Western and HFSC diets were more likely to have the disease. Additionally, a healthy diet was associated with less severe forms of PCAD, while Western and HFSC diets increased the likelihood of severe cases.

Previous research on the correlation between CAD and diets has been conducted [11, 12, 19, 20], but limited information is available on the impact of diets on PCAD. Our study found that a diet high in fast food, processed meats, french fries, and snacks (all types of ultra-processed foods) categorized as Western diet can increase the risk of PCAD. The relationship between a Western diet - characterized by low fruit and vegetable intake, and high consumption of meat, carbohydrates, and fats - and the severity of CAD was also significant in a cohort on 188 symptomatic stable patients with CAD [11]. In addition, a higher intake of ultra-processed foods inclined patients to PCAD presence, and increased the risk of severe forms of PCAD [20, 21]. Another study by Dimitriou et al., suggested a positive correlation between Western dietary pattern and cardiovascular diseases which was strengthened even further after adjusting for other confounding factors [12].

This study revealed a significant association between healthy dietary pattern and decreased risk and severity of PCAD. Hou et al. found an inverse relation between healthy dietary pattern and risk of CAD [22]. A case-control study compromising 550 participants showed that higher intakes of fruits, cruciferous vegetables, green leafy vegetables, other vegetables is associated with a significant reduction in risk of CAD in women [23]. Also, studies evaluating dietary pattern underline the importance of vegetables, fruits, legumes and whole grains, lean protein sources, with reducing the processed food as protective methods to cardiovascular diseases [24, 25]. Furthermore, A study by Panwar et al. [26] supported our findings, showing a direct link between low fruit and vegetable consumption, high fat intake, and the risk of PCAD. Our study, which includes a larger patient sample and more in-depth analysis of dietary factors, reinforces the importance of healthy diet in relation to PCAD.

Various mechanisms support the link between diet and PCAD. Postprandial lipemia, which can lead to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases through endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation [27–29], is greatly influenced by dietary choices [30].

It is also suggested that, despite having a similar nutritional composition as other foods, the processing of foods leads to significant alterations in the food matrix that can result in serious health issues. This processing can affect oxidative stress, inflammation, gut microbiota, dysglycemia, and insulin resistance, ultimately contributing to cardiovascular diseases [31]. Changes to the food matrix, along with the fat and fiber content of foods, and the addition of specific food additives during processing, can affect the composition and function of gut microbiota. This altered gut microbiota may result in increased production of atherogenic metabolites such as choline, trimethylamine N-oxide, and betaine; low-grade systemic inflammation caused by endotoxemia; changes in the host’s immune system; and weight gain. These factors are associated with excess body fat, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [32, 33]. Additionally, inorganic phosphate salts used as additives in industrial food processing, commonly found in Western diets, are absorbed more readily than organic phosphates. This excessive absorption of phosphate can lead to aortic calcification due to increased secretion of parathyroid hormone and fibroblast growth factor 23, as well as oxidative stress in endothelial cells, ultimately causing endothelial dysfunction [34, 35]. Another relate marker is the effect of dietary nitrate induced by vegetable and fruits consumption on nitric oxide (NO) status. Multiple studies have established a connection between dietary nitrate of a diet rich in fruit and vegetables and the cardioprotective effect [31, 36]. Furthermore, diets high in carbohydrates, such as the Western and HFSC diets, have a higher glycemic load which is associated with an increased risk of CAD [27, 36, 37]. Diets with a low glycemic index, on the other hand, can lead to lower postprandial glucose levels, cholesterol, C-reactive protein, and blood pressure, thereby lowering the risk of cardiovascular diseases [38, 39]. In our study, garlic was a key component of the healthy dietary pattern. Garlic has been shown to have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cardiovascular protective, and anti-obesity properties [40]. Additionally, garlic can help reduce cholesterol levels, platelet aggregation, and blood pressure, all factors that contribute to lower cardiovascular disease risk [41, 42].

The strengths of this study lie in a large and diverse sample group and data collection from multiple urban and rural areas in Iran. Besides, consideration of confounding variables in examining the relationship between PCAD and dietary patterns. Additionally, identifying three various dietary patterns in this study also provides a comprehensive understanding of this connection. Our findings should also be interpreted in the context of several limitations. Firstly, reliance on self-reported dietary intake data, which may introduce recall bias. Secondly, the study design was case-control, which limits the ability to establish causality connection between dietary patterns and PCAD. Further longitudinal studies on this regard are warranted to establish this causal relationship. Additionally, our study primarily focused on a specific population in Iran, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups or regions. Furthermore, while we controlled for several confounding factors, there may still be residual confounding variables not accounted for in our analysis like supplement intake. Future studies should employ advanced statistical methods, such as propensity score matching or stratified analyses, to better control for these variables. Lastly, to enhance the accuracy of dietary assessments, future research could incorporate more objective measures of dietary intake, such as food diaries or biomarkers, in addition to self-report questionnaires. This could help mitigate recall bias and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the dietary patterns associated with PCAD.

Conclusions

Our investigation demonstrated that dietary pattern can significantly impact presence and severity of PCAD. While healthy dietary including more consumption of fruits and vegetables, garlic, and legume may have protective effect against early onset of CAD, Western and HFSC diets with high intake of fast food, fat, and sweet deserts may lead to higher risk of PCAD.
