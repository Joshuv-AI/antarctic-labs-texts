Lack of Effect of Chlordiazepoxide on the Number of Falls in a Novel Falling‐Trap Test in Male C57BL/6N Mice
Authors: Shunsuke Kidani, Masatoshi Ukezono, Yuji Takano, Yasuhide Mitsumoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBehavioral tests in mice and other animals are widely used to study anxiety and to evaluate the efficacy of anxiolytic compounds. However, the validity of behavioral tests used to assess anxiety‐like behavior has been questioned because their results are often inconsistent and not readily replicated across studies. We developed a simple falling‐trap test to examine whether the number of falls could serve as an index of anxiety‐like behavior. The test is designed to produce falls from an elevated path, based on the hypothesis that less‐anxious mice would be more prone to fall. Male C57BL/6N mice were given vehicle or chlordiazepoxide (CDP), and their behavior was evaluated using both the novel test and the elevated plus maze (EPM). The outcomes were the number of falls in the falling‐trap test and open‐arm time in the EPM. In the falling‐trap test, CDP‐treated mice did not show a statistically significant increase in the number of falls compared with control mice. In contrast, CDP significantly increased open‐arm time in the EPM. Thus, under the present experimental conditions, these findings did not support the hypothesis that the number of falls in the falling‐trap test could serve as a measure of anxiety‐like behavior. Further studies are needed to determine the conditions under which this assay could be useful and to establish its reliability and validity.

Introduction

Anxiety has a significant influence on behavior and can arise from, as well as contribute to, a wide range of disease processes. Accordingly, considerable work has focused on its mechanisms and on evaluating anxiolytic drugs. Anxiety‐like behavior in mice is commonly assessed using the elevated plus maze (EPM; [1, 2]), the light‐dark box [3], and the open‐field test [4, 5].

Aside from these, other paradigms, such as the elevated zero‐maze [6] and the hole‐board test [7], have been proposed. However, a meta‐analysis reported that the effects of many anxiolytics vary significantly in magnitude and even direction across assays, raising concerns about consistency and validity [8]. To address this, we introduce a simple behavioral assay—the falling‐trap test—that characterizes mouse behavior on an elevated apparatus with a dark tunnel.

Many anxiety‐like‐behavior tests exploit rodents' aversion to brightly lit, open spaces [9]. Our approach uses the opposing tendency to seek darkness to reduce confounding by sedation, which is particularly important for benzodiazepines [10]. In this study, we investigated whether anxiolytic treatment and sedation would have different effects on behavior directed toward a dark area on an elevated apparatus.

Specifically, a behavioral measure that requires active, directed movement toward a dark area should decrease under sedation and increase when anxiety is reduced.

In accordance with this logic, the falling‐trap test counts the number of falls from an elevated apparatus as mice move toward a darkened tunnel. We evaluated male C57BL/6N mice given saline or chlordiazepoxide (CDP) and compared their performance in the falling‐trap test with that in the EPM.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Drug Administration

Male C57BL/6N mice aged eight weeks were purchased from Charles River Laboratories Japan Inc. Animal and protocol procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee at Hokuriku University (HU) (No. 18‐04) and the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry (NCNP) (No. 2023009R1). A total of 102 mice were housed in groups of 4–5 mice each and acclimated to the laboratory for 1 week before behavioral testing. All mice were kept under controlled conditions with an artificial 12/12 h light/dark cycle (lights on from 7:00 am to 7:00 pm). Mice were fed standard mouse food (CE‐2, CREA Japan Inc.) and tap water ad libitum.

CDP (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was dissolved in saline and administered via i.p. injection in a volume of 10 mL/kg. To draw dose‐response curves, CDP was administered at 2.5, 5.0, 10, and 20 mg/kg. CDP‐treated mice received a single dose of CDP [11, 12], whereas control mice received saline, 20–30 min before behavioral testing.

Equipment

We developed a novel falling‐trap test to examine the balance between approach to an attractive dark tunnel and potential avoidance after experiencing an aversive fall. The number of falls (NFs) served as the primary behavioral outcome measure in the falling‐trap test.

As shown in Figures 1a and 1b, the apparatus was based on the EPM and placed at the center of a fluorescently lit room (illuminance: 120 lx at HU; 90 lx at NCNP). The open and closed arms were each 65 cm long and elevated 50 cm above the floor. A cylindrical black‐paper tunnel was installed at the end of one open arm; the other open arm remained unchanged, and entry to the closed arms was prevented. The tunnel measured 25 cm in length and ~8 cm in diameter, with half of the cylinder extending beyond the edge of the open arm. The top was covered with black cloth to create a dark environment, in line with mice's preference for darkness. Half of the tunnel was suspended, allowing for a controlled fall into a padded area. With experience, mice may learn to avoid this area. The EPM served as a comparator, with its apparatus matching the base platform used in the fall test.

Apparatus used for the falling‐trap test. (a) Equipment configuration. The black cloth‐covered space on the far left contains a tunnel. When the mouse moves to its end, it falls. The closed arms were blocked by barriers. (b) The length of the open arm and tunnel.

Behavioral Experiment

At the start of the falling‐trap test, a mouse was placed at the midpoint of the open arm before the tunnel was attached, and the 10‐min test began immediately after the tunnel was positioned. After each fall, the mouse was placed in a cage on the floor for 1 min, during which time recording was paused; the mouse was then returned to the initial location, and recording was resumed. The total number of falls over 10 min was recorded. Mice that did not move from their starting point during the session were classified as immobile and excluded from the primary analysis.

For the EPM, each mouse was placed in the central area facing an open arm and allowed to explore the maze for 10 min [1, 2]. The maze was cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials. The time spent in the open and closed arms was measured using a stopwatch.

The falling‐trap test was first conducted at HU using six mice per condition under the saline and 10 mg/kg CDP conditions. Subsequently, at NCNP, ten mice per condition were tested in the falling‐trap test under five conditions (saline and 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 mg/kg CDP), and eight mice per condition were tested in the EPM under the same five conditions. Thus, a total of 62 mice were tested in the falling‐trap test and 40 mice were tested in the EPM.

Statistical Analysis

Data are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the HU site data, differences in NF between the control and CDP groups were evaluated using a two‐sided Welch's t‐test, which was selected a priori because of its robustness to unequal variances. For the NCNP‐site data, NF in the falling‐trap test and open‐arm time in the EPM were analyzed separately using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's test for comparisons with saline. Closed‐arm time was presented descriptively and was not subjected to inferential statistical analysis. As a sensitivity analysis, the falling‐trap test data were reanalyzed after including all assigned animals using their observed NF values. In addition, data from the saline and 10 mg/kg conditions shared by the two sites were analyzed using a two‐way ANOVA with treatment condition and site as factors. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using MATLAB (Mathworks, ver. 2023a) and R (ver. 4.4.3) through RStudio.

Results

Figure 2 shows the HU site results. The mean NF values were 4.00 ± 0.58 in the control group and 7.17 ± 3.24 in the CDP group. There was no statistically significant difference between the groups (two‐sided Welch's t‐test, t(5.32) = 2.15, p = 0.081).

Additional Analysis

To examine whether the results differed between the two experimental sites, we conducted an additional two‐way ANOVA with only the treatment conditions shared by both sites (saline and 10 mg/kg CDP), with treatment condition and site as factors. This analysis showed a significant main effect of treatment condition, F(1,26) = 4.47, p = 0.04, but no significant main effect of site, F(1,26) = 1.04, p = 0.32, and no significant treatment condition × site interaction, F(1,26) = 3.09, p = 0.09. These findings should not be taken as evidence of consistency across sites. Rather, the significant pooled main effect of treatment should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect increased statistical power when the two sites were combined, whereas the primary per‐site analyses found no statistically significant effect in the falling‐trap test.

Discussion

In the present study, CDP did not significantly increase NF at the HU site. Similarly, at the NCNP site, the falling‐trap test showed no significant effect of dose, whereas the EPM detected a significant effect of CDP. Although the additional two‐way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of treatment condition, this pooled result should be interpreted cautiously. The primary analyses were the site‐specific analyses, which did not detect a statistically significant effect in the falling‐trap test. The pooled treatment effect may therefore reflect increased statistical power when the two sites were combined, rather than reproducible evidence of an effect across sites. These findings indicate that, under the present experimental conditions, the falling‐trap test was less sensitive than the EPM for detecting the effect of CDP. Further studies will be required to determine the utility of NF as an endpoint in this system.

There are several limitations to consider. Individual variability in CDP effects could have increased the SD of NFs under CDP conditions. It also remains unclear whether all control mice were in an anxiety‐like state on the elevated apparatus. Consequently, a clearer separation might be observed if testing were restricted to mice in experimentally induced anxiety‐like states. The behavior quantified here could represent both unlearned (innate) and learned aspects of anxiety.

At the highest dose (20 mg/kg), both the falling‐trap test and the EPM showed decreases in their respective indices, consistent with sedation. In the falling‐trap test, NF values at 20 mg/kg were lower than those in the control condition. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution as three of the ten mice in the 20 mg/kg group were excluded from the primary analysis due to immobility. The exclusion of the most profoundly affected animals may have influenced the observed mean NF in the analyzed animals, complicating the interpretation of the reduction in falls at this dose. To assess this possibility, a sensitivity analysis including all animals without exclusions was also performed; this analysis revealed no significant main effect of dose. As a result, the overall conclusion was unchanged, although the 20 mg/kg findings should be interpreted cautiously and investigated further.

To further evaluate the utility of this system, future studies should examine a broader range of anxiolytics and anxiety‐induction models and compare the performance of the falling‐trap test with that of other commonly used assays. These studies will help determine how well NFs reflect the combined drives to enter darkness and avoid falling.

The present study does not establish the falling‐trap test as a validated assay for anxiety‐like behavior. When evaluating behavioral assays, predictive, face, and construct validity should all be considered [13]. In terms of face validity, the avoidance behavior and falls observed in the dark tunnel may resemble behavioral responses to a potentially hazardous environment; however, it is unclear if these behaviors are specifically anxiety‐like. Regarding predictive validity, no significant difference between the saline and CDP (10 mg/kg) groups was observed in the falling‐trap test. Thus, the present data do not provide sufficient evidence that the assay is consistently sensitive to a known anxiolytic drug. As for construct validity, the test may involve approach–avoidance conflict and defensive behavior, but the present data do not directly demonstrate this. Therefore, it would be premature to equate the falling‐trap test with established anxiety‐like behavioral assays. Further pharmacological, mechanistic, and multi‐site studies are required to determine its utility.

In conclusion, under the present experimental conditions, these findings did not support the hypothesis that the falling‐trap test provides a new measure of anxiety‐like behavior in C57BL/6N mice.
