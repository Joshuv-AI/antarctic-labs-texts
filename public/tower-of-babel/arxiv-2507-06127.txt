PrefixAgent : An LLM-Powered Design Framework for Efficient Prefix Adder Optimization
Dongsheng Zuo, Jiadong Zhu, Yang Luo, Yuzhe Ma

Abstract
Prefix adders are fundamental arithmetic circuits, but their design space grows exponentially with bit-width, posing significant optimization challenges. Previous works face limitations in performance, generalization, and scalability. To address these challenges, we propose PrefixAgent , a large language model (LLM)-powered framework that enables efficient prefix adder optimization. Specifically, PrefixAgent reformulates the problem into subtasks including backbone synthesis and structure refinement, which effectively reduces the search space. More importantly, this new design perspective enables us to efficiently collect enormous high-quality data and reasoning traces with E-graph, which further results in an effective fine-tuning of LLM. Experimental results show that PrefixAgent synthesizes prefix adders with consistently smaller areas compared to baseline methods, while maintaining scalability and generalization in commercial EDA flows.



I
Introduction

Adders are fundamental arithmetic components used in digital systems like processors, digital signal processors, and AI chips.
Among various adder architectures, the prefix adder is well-known for its parallel structure and efficiency in addition operations.
The prefix adder’s internal operation is represented by a prefix graph, where each node computes group propagate and generate signals for rapid parallel carry computation.
However, the design space of prefix adders grows exponentially with bit-width, making exhaustive exploration impractical.
Traditionally, adder designs rely on regular structures or cumbersome manual optimization.
Therefore, developing efficient and flexible adder design methodologies is essential.

Previous works on adder design primarily involve regular structures and automated design methods.

Practical design requires balancing area, delay, and power while considering timing constraints as well as various input arrival time profiles.

In addition, a non-trivial gap remains between structural-level metrics and physical-implementation metrics.
Machine learning (ML) methods have been proposed to bridge this gap.
Recent works

Inspired by manual design practices, experienced engineers typically choose a suitable initial structure based on design specifications and refine it using detailed feedback from EDA tools.
Although effective, this process requires significant engineering effort.
An intelligent agent with strong reasoning and interactive capabilities can automate the design process, addressing generalization challenges and enabling more efficient, scalable optimization.
Motivated by this, emerging large language models (LLMs) provide a promising solution to further automate and optimize the prefix adder design process.

Specifically, the node count in a prefix graph theoretically scales quadratically with bit-width, and these graphs must adhere to stringent structural constraints, further complicating the generation process.
The second challenge is the lack of high-quality data, which is essential for fine-tuning LRMs on the prefix adder design task—an inherently complex and previously unseen problem.
Recent studies highlight the importance of high-quality data in improving the reasoning capabilities of LRMs

The backbone, representing the critical structural component of the adder, significantly simplifies the overall design complexity.
The new perspective also enables the LLMs to efficiently generate zero-deficiency

This enables us to gather high-quality data and interpretable reasoning traces using E-graphs, successfully addressing the second challenge in applying LLM agents.
By fine-tuning the LLM on these carefully curated datasets,
PrefixAgent
learns to efficiently optimize adders under various scenarios and design requirements.
Experimental results demonstrate that

Furthermore, under commercial physical design flows, it achieves better results than adders generated by commercial synthesis tools.

•

•

Owing to the new design perspective, we can utilize the E-graph-based method to systematically generate comprehensive, interpretable, and high-quality optimization traces for this problem, enabling effective fine-tuning of the agent.

•

Experimental results show that

II-A

Prefix Adder

Given two
n
-bit binary numbers,
A=a_{n-1}a_{n-2}\dots a_{0}
and
B=b_{n-1}b_{n-2}\dots b_{0}
, the prefix adder computes the sum
S=s_{n-1}s_{n-2}\dots s_{0}
and the carry-out bit
C_{out}
.
The operation is performed in three stages: preprocessing, prefix computation, and post-processing.

Preprocessing:
Each bit position independently computes propagate (
p_{i}
) and generate (
g_{i}
) signals:

p_{i}=a_{i}\oplus b_{i},\quad g_{i}=a_{i}\cdot b_{i}

(1)

Prefix Computation:
The propagate and generate signals are combined recursively using the associative prefix operator
\mathit{o}
defined as:

\displaystyle(G,P)_{i:j}

\displaystyle=(G,P)_{i:k}\;\mathit{o}\;(G,P)_{k-1:j}

\displaystyle=\Big(G_{i:k}+P_{i:k}\cdot G_{k-1:j},\;P_{i:k}\cdot P_{k-1:j}\Big)

(2)

As the prefix operator
\mathit{o}
is associative, it allows flexibility in grouping strategies.
Each
\mathit{o}
operation corresponds to a node in the prefix graph, and different groupings lead to different graph topologies.
This recursive process continues until each bit position
i
is assigned an output node to compute
G_{i,0}
, forming the final prefix structure.

Post-processing:
The final carry and sum bits are computed using the prefix results:

c_{i}=G_{[i:0]},\quad s_{i}=p_{i}\oplus c_{i-1}

(3)

We adopt the notation from
[
13
]
, where each prefix node
(i,j)
represents the computation of
(i,k)
and
(k\!-\!1,j)
, with
i\geq k>j
.
Here,
i
and
j
denote the most significant bit (MSB) and least significant bit (LSB), respectively.
We refer to
(i,k)
as the
upper parent (up)
and
(k\!-\!1,j)
as the
lower parent (lp)
of node
(i,j)
.
We further classify the fanouts of each node into
trivial fanout (tf)
and
non-trivial fanout (ntf)
:
A fanout is labeled as
tf
if it shares the same MSB as its parent, and
ntf
if the MSB is larger.
For example, node
(i,j)
is a tf of
(i,k)
and an ntf of
(k\!-\!1,j)
.

A zero-deficiency adder aims for a balanced design with minimal cost in both prefix graph size and depth.
According to Snir’s theorem
[
41
]
, a prefix adder is defined as zero-deficiency if its size and depth satisfy the equation:

s_{C}(n)+d_{C}(n)=2n-2

(4)

where
S
and
L
represent the size and logic level of an
n
-bit prefix adder, respectively.

II-C

E-graphs

E-graphs
[
45
]
are data structures that represent equivalence classes of expressions.
E-nodes in the E-graph represent variables, constants, or operators, and are grouped into equivalence classes (e-classes).
One of the key advantages of E-graphs is their ability to generate solutions through equivalence-preserving rewrites.
These rewrites enable E-graphs to explore the full solution space while avoiding the phase ordering problem.
This process, known as equality saturation
[
46
]
, enables efficient exploration and representation of large design spaces.
In the EDA domain, E-graphs have been used for logic optimization tasks, such as
[
47
,
48
]
.

The Egg library
[
49
]
provides powerful extraction tools, including an explanation feature that yields interpretable rewrite traces.
By applying different extraction strategies, E-graphs become a powerful tool for generating high-quality optimized designs and capturing the corresponding optimization traces.

III-A

Overview

Fig. 4:
PrefixAgent
framework overview.

An overview of the
PrefixAgent
framework is shown in
Figure

.
The
PrefixAgent
framework operates in two phases.
In Phase I, an LRM iteratively refines an initial prefix adder backbone structure to achieve an optimized backbone.
Upon completion of Phase I, the framework constructs a full adder based on the optimized backbone, transitioning into Phase II.
In Phase II, the LRM further performs local structural refinement, guided by feedback from the physical implementation to optimize timing performance.

By decomposing the complex adder design task into these two phases, we simplify the complex prefix design task.
The backbone refinement process directly corresponds to E-graph rewrites, enabling efficient generation of high-quality optimization data for fine-tuning the LRM.
Rather than directly generating a fully valid prefix adder structure,
PrefixAgent
employs function calling, allowing the LRM to focus more on high-level decision-making, such as where and what type of optimization to perform.
The actual execution of structural modifications is handled by external tools.
The LRM determines the end of each phase by explicitly invoking the corresponding completion tools.

III-B

Backbone

In
PrefixAgent
, we simplify the prefix graph optimization task to a backbone generation problem.

Definition 1

(Backbone)
.

Given an
N
-bit prefix graph, the backbone is defined as the subgraph consisting of nodes that compute the carry for the most significant bit (MSB), denoted as node
(N-1,0)
.

As illustrated in the left part of
Figure

5
, the backbone for an 8-bit prefix adder includes nodes specifically structured for computing the MSB carry. The backbone dominates the fundamental architecture of the prefix adder, significantly influencing its overall performance and complexity.
In this example, the backbone corresponds to the following grouping structure:

G_{7:0}=(i_{0}\,\mathit{o}\,i_{1})\,\mathit{o}\,(i_{2}\,\mathit{o}\,i_{3})\,\mathit{o}\,(i_{4}\,\mathit{o}\,i_{5})\,\mathit{o}\,(i_{6}\,\mathit{o}\,i_{7})

(5)

The backbone can be viewed as a full binary tree with
N
leaves, where pairs of input nodes from
i_{0}
to
i_{N-1}
are recursively grouped.
It is well known that such a binary tree has exactly
N-1
nodes.
Therefore, we let
S_{B}
denote the number of backbone nodes, and
S_{B}=N-1
.

To derive the full prefix adder from a given backbone, each bit position must be associated with an output node.
For a backbone with level
L_{B}
, there already exist
L_{B}
output nodes corresponding to the backbone level.
Therefore, for the remaining
N-1-L_{B}
bit positions that lack outputs, we insert auxiliary nodes to complete the prefix adder as shown in
Figure

5
.
The total number of auxiliary nodes is thus
S_{A}=N-1-L_{B}
.
This construction ensures that all intermediate nodes have non‑trivial fanout, i.e., each intermediate propagate–generate signal is utilized by subsequent nodes.
According to Snir
[
41
]
, this is a necessary condition for a zero-deficiency adder.
By appropriately designing the backbone structure, the completed prefix adder can achieve a level
L
equal to the backbone level
L_{B}
, and the total size and level jointly satisfy
Equation

4
, corresponding to a zero-deficiency adder.
In scenarios involving non-uniform input arrival times, the final level
L
may exceed
L_{B}
.
In such cases, subsequent local refinements using the
level opt
tool in Phase II can yield a low-deficiency adder.

Focusing on the backbone reduces the design complexity while resulting in zero- or low-deficiency adders.
For an
N
-bit prefix adder, the full solution space grows exponentially as a product of Catalan numbers.
Restricting optimization to the backbone reduces this to a single Catalan number.
For example, in the 16-bit case, the design space drops from approximately
10^{48}
to
10^{6}
.

To further improve the backbone structure,
PrefixAgent
employs a regrouping strategy that iteratively modifies the grouping pattern within the binary tree.
A regroup operation alters the backbone by reorganizing two existing subtrees.
As illustrated in
Figure

6
, regrouping nodes
(7,6)
and
(5,4)
introduces a new node
(7,4)
and removes node
(5,0)
, effectively restructuring the backbone.
In this example, the grouping structure of the backbone changes from
Equation

5
to:

G_{7:0}=((i_{0}\,\mathit{o}\,i_{1})\,\mathit{o}\,(i_{2}\,\mathit{o}\,i_{3}))\,\mathit{o}\,((i_{4}\,\mathit{o}\,i_{5})\,\mathit{o}\,(i_{6}\,\mathit{o}\,i_{7}))

(6)

The detailed implementation of regroup operations, including how regroup candidates are identified within a backbone, is presented in
Section

III-D
.

Notably, each regroup operation corresponds exactly to an equivalence-preserving rewrite in an E-graph.
This observation enables us to leverage E-graph to generate high-quality backbone structures along with their corresponding regrouping traces, which can be used to construct effective fine-tuning data for the LRM.

III-C

E-graph based Data Generation

E-graph based optimization trace generation.

Optimizing prefix adder backbones under specific delay constraints and non-uniform input arrival times remains highly complex.
For LRMs, this task constitutes an unseen scenario, highlighting the necessity of high-quality optimization trace data for effective fine-tuning.
To enable effective fine-tuning of the LRM, we propose an E-graph-based data generation framework, as illustrated in
Figure

7
.
This framework consists of three main components:
(1) using E-graphs to generate optimized backbone solutions along with their corresponding rewrite traces;
(2) employing commercial large reasoning models to generate structured
<think>
reasoning content based on the trace; and
(3) combining the
PrefixAgent
prompt format with tool feedback to synthesize complete training samples for LRM fine-tuning.

III-C
1
Backbone Representation in E-graphs

The
egg
library represents expressions using nested S-expressions, where both operators and operands must be explicitly defined.
To describe prefix adder backbones, we define a domain-specific intermediate language,
BackboneLang
, to encode backbone structures in a structured and concise form.
In
BackboneLang
, each input node is represented as a
Leaf
annotated with its input arrival time.
The language supports a single binary operator
\mathit{o}
, which denotes a grouping operation between two operands—either leaf nodes or previously grouped sub-expressions.
For example, grouping inputs
i_{0}
and
i_{1}
is expressed as
(
\mathit{o}
i0 i1)
.
The backbone structure corresponding to
Equation

6
can be represented in
BackboneLang
as:

(o
(o (o i0 i1) (o i2 i3))
(o (o i4 i5) (o i6 i7)))

We define a single associativity rewrite rule as follows:

(\mathit{o}\;(\mathit{o}\;\texttt{?x}\;\texttt{?y})\;\texttt{?z})\;\Longleftrightarrow\;(\mathit{o}\;\texttt{?x}\;(\mathit{o}\;\texttt{?y}\;\texttt{?z}))

(R1)

This associativity rewrite rule on S-expressions is semantically equivalent to a regroup operation in the prefix backbone.
That is, rewriting an expression according to Rule
R1
corresponds exactly to restructuring the backbone by modifying the regroup of two subtrees.
Starting from an initial fully serial backbone expression, we employ
egg
’s equality saturation
[
46
]
to apply the associativity rewrite rule
R1
.
This process compactly encodes the complete solution space of backbone structures within a single E-graph
\mathcal{G}
.

III-C
2
Timing-based Cost Function for Extraction

From the generated E-graph
\mathcal{G}
, we extract multiple backbone candidates satisfying specific input arrival times and delay constraints.
We employ the
egg
extractor to identify optimized expressions based on a predefined cost function.
Prior work
[
16
,
8
]
has shown that a linear approximation provides accurate estimates of path delays in prefix adders.
Given that each backbone node has a fixed fanout of one, the delay is primarily determined by the longest path in the structure.
Consequently, we define the delay cost as a linear function of the path length:

y=kx+b

where
x
denotes the number of nodes on the path.
Accordingly, we define the cost function
C
to guide E-graph extraction under non-uniform input arrival times.
This function recursively computes the cost of each internal node based on the arrival times of its inputs and the linear delay model:

C(n)=\begin{cases}t_{i},&\text{if }n=\texttt{Leaf}(x_{i})\\[4.0pt]
\max(C(l),\ C(r))+d+\lambda,&\text{if }n=\texttt{Op}(l,r)\end{cases}

(7)

Here,
t_{i}
denotes the input arrival time at leaf
x_{i}
,
d
is a constant node delay, and
\lambda
is a timing margin used to model fanout or layout uncertainty.

III-C
3
Optimization Trace Generation

The cost function satisfies decomposability and monotonicity, two essential properties required by
egg
’s optimal extraction algorithm.
We therefore adopt the default bottom-up extractor provided by
egg
to retrieve the optimal backbone solution.
The optimal solution corresponds to the tightest timing constraint scenario.
To emulate more general area–delay trade-off conditions, we additionally generate suboptimal solutions by introducing controlled perturbations during the extraction process.
This modification allows the extractor to explore a broader set of feasible backbone structures that reflect a wider range of relaxed timing constraints.

The
egg
extraction framework naturally produces explanation traces in the form of rewrite sequences, describing how each backbone structure is transformed from the initial serial expression.
These sequences serve as interpretable optimization traces, explicitly capturing the sequence of regroup operations applied to reach a given structure.
Among all backbone solutions encoded in the E-graph, we select training samples that yield minimal increases in the final prefix adder level
L
relative to the backbone level
L_{B}
.
Such samples are more likely to correspond to zero-deficiency or low-deficiency adders.

III-C
4
Data Synthesis and Fine Tuning

To enable fine-tuning of LRMs, each training sample must include explicit chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning annotated within
<think>
…
</think>
tags.
We generate these reasoning traces by prompting GPT-o1
[
25
]
and Claude 3.7 Sonnet
[
50
]
with
PrefixAgent
’s internal prompt and corresponding regroup decisions at each step.
As illustrated in the lower part of
Figure

7
, the resulting CoT segments are combined with function-calling formatted regroup traces, the system prompt, and feedback from invoked tools to form complete fine-tuning samples.

Highly repetitive CoT templates can limit the model’s ability to generalize.
In such cases, the model tends to overfit to surface-level textual patterns rather than attend to structural features.
This behavior results in reduced sensitivity to function-call accuracy, despite achieving low training loss that does not reflect actual reasoning fidelity.
To jointly supervise reasoning generation and structural decision accuracy, we define a composite training objective:

L_{\mathrm{total}}=L_{\mathrm{CoT}}+\lambda\cdot L_{\mathrm{function\_call}}

Here,
L_{\mathrm{CoT}}
supervises the generation of coherent chain-of-thought reasoning, while
L_{\mathrm{function\_call}}
promotes accurate invocation of regroup via function calls.

III-E

PrefixAgent Phase II: Local Structural Refinement

After the backbone is optimized and the prefix adder is completed by adding auxiliary nodes, timing violations may still occur.
These violations are primarily caused by three factors:
(1) the overall level
L
of the completed prefix adder may exceed the backbone level
L_{B}
;
(2) the addition of auxiliary nodes may increase fan-out at certain nodes; and
(3) layout-related effects introduced during physical implementation.

To resolve these timing violations,
PrefixAgent
enters Phase II, which applies local structural refinements to improve timing.
In this phase, the LRM identifies timing-critical nodes and invokes one of the three timing optimization tools.
The LRM determines the appropriate optimization target and tool type, while the execution of structural modifications is delegated to external tools.
As illustrated in
Figure

8
, the three tools include:

(1)
level-opt
, which reduces the target node’s level by inserting an additional node;

(2)
fanout-opt
, which inserts a node to alleviate excessive fanout at the target node;

(3)
node clone
, which creates a copy of a high-fanout node to share the load.

Fig. 8:
Illustration of timing optimization tools: level-opt, fanout-opt and node clone.

To support effective reasoning during structural refinement, we introduce the
Enhanced Prefix Representation
(EPR).
Preliminary experiments show that explicitly encoding node-level attributes—such as logic level, parent relationships, and fanout—significantly improves the LRM’s ability to analyze and optimize prefix graph structures.
The EPR compactly captures both global properties and detailed per-node information of a prefix adder.
It includes input and non-input node lists, level and connection relationships.
An example EPR for a 4-bit prefix adder is shown below:

Bitwidth: 4
Non-input nodes: 3
Max level: 3
Max fanout: 1

Input nodes:
(0,0), tf: [], ntf: [(1,0)]
(1,1), tf: [(1,0)], ntf: []
(2,2), tf: [(1,0)], ntf: []
(3,3), tf: [(1,0)], ntf: []

Non-input nodes:
(3,0),lvl:3,up:(3,3),lp:(2,0),tf:[],ntf: []
(2,0),lvl:2,up:(2,2),lp:(1,0),tf:[],ntf: [(3,0)]
(1,0),lvl:1,up:(1,1),lp:(0,0),tf:[],ntf: [(2,0)]

During timing refinement, critical path information is essential for guiding optimization decisions.
Based on the start and end points of the critical path, we reconstruct the full sequence of nodes along the critical path and generate a structured analysis.
An example for critical path analysis is as follows:

Lvl 0: (0,0), [INPUT] tf: [], ntf: [(1,0)]
Lvl 1:(1,0),up:(1,1),lp:(0,0), tf:[], ntf:[(2,0)]
Lvl 2:(2,0),up:(2,2),lp:(1,0), tf:[], ntf:[(3,0)]
Lvl 3:(3,0),up:(3,3),lp:(2,0), tf:[], ntf:[]

- Lvl efficiency:2/3(theoretical min:2, actual:3)

In each refinement iteration, the
PrefixAgent
framework constructs a prompt using current physical metrics, tool feedback, EPR, and the critical path analysis.
Based on this information, the LRM performs reasoning and then either invokes an appropriate optimization tool or issues a termination call to finalize the design and prevent over-design.

IV-B

Comparing with Baselines

Under the uniform arrival time setting, we evaluate each method across six target delay values per bit-width, representing a range of area–delay trade-off scenarios.
For each target delay,
PrefixAgent
generates a single optimized adder structure.
In contrast, each baseline (MCTS, RL, and VAE) is executed with 5000 samples per delay target, yielding 30,000 designs per method.
The resulting Pareto fronts are illustrated in
Figure

PrefixAgent
consistently achieves high-quality solutions.
Notably, in the 32-bit and 64-bit cases, it Pareto-dominates all baseline approaches, demonstrating superior scalability.

For the non-uniform arrival time scenario, we evaluate two distinct arrival time profiles: (i)
LSB-first
, where the least significant half of the input bits arrive earlier, and (ii)
Random

For each arrival profile and bit-width configuration, we set a fixed target delay.
Each baseline method samples 5000 candidate designs under the given delay constraint.
Among them, we select the adders with the smallest area that meet the timing constraints, allowing at most 1% timing violation.

IV-D

Discussion

Previous LLM-based methods PrefixLLM
[
37
]
fail to directly generate complete prefix structures with complex graph constraints.
In contrast,
PrefixAgent
decomposes the task into backbone generation and local refinement, and leverages high-quality optimization traces from E-graph rewrites.
Search-based baseline methods require retraining for each new scenario and do not scale well with bitwidth due to the exponential design space.
As reported in the PrefixRL paper
[
18
]
, optimizing a 64-bit adder requires over 500,000 training steps, taking several weeks.
Moreover, we observe that these methods frequently explore high-deficiency structures that lead to excessive area consumption.

PrefixAgent
provides a flexible and efficient alternative with high generalization and scalability.

V
Conclusion

We propose
PrefixAgent
, a framework for prefix adder optimization. The design process is decomposed into two phases—backbone generation and local refinement.
To guide the LRM, we leverage high-quality optimization traces generated through E-graph rewrites.
Experimental results demonstrate that
PrefixAgent
outperforms prior methods with generalization and scalability.
Moreover, we introduce a novel paradigm for generating high-quality supervision traces, which remains valuable as LRM capabilities continue to advance.
The just-released model GPT-o3
[
26
]
now supports multi-step tool invocation during reasoning, further highlighting the relevance of high-quality trace data for future LRM-driven EDA workflows.
This paradigm of high-quality supervision data collection also enables flexible extension of E-graph-based logic rewriting to other arithmetic circuits, including multiplier design and general datapath synthesis.
