CD155 and its receptors in glioma: prospects for emerging immune checkpoint inhibitors
Authors: Mingyao Huang, Youchen Cui, Xiaoqian Wang, Yang Weng, Huiyan Huang, Weiwei Su, Shan Zhao, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioma is an aggressive brain malignancy characterized by a markedly immunosuppressive microenvironment and limited response to conventional treatments. CD155, also known as the poliovirus receptor, is frequently overexpressed in glioma and plays a key role in immune escape. By interacting with inhibitory receptors such as TIGIT and CD96, as well as the activating receptor CD226, CD155 modulates the tumor immune landscape. These interactions suppress the cytotoxic activity of T cells and natural killer (NK) cells while promoting immunoregulatory phenotypes, thereby impairing immune-mediated tumor cell elimination and supporting tumor progression. The balance among these receptor–ligand interactions is critical in determining immune outcomes. Targeting the CD155 axis has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy. Approaches including monoclonal antibodies, bispecific antibodies, and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-engineered immune cells are being developed to counteract immune suppression and restore antitumor responses. This review summarizes the structural and functional features of CD155 and its associated receptors, examines their roles in glioma immune evasion, and discusses recent advances and challenges in developing therapies targeting this pathway. Modulation of CD155-related signaling may offer new opportunities to improve treatment outcomes in glioma. Keywords: CD155, PVR, Glioma, Immunotherapy, Immune checkpoint inhibitors

Introduction

Glioma, particularly glioblastoma (GBM), remains one of the most lethal primary brain tumors, with a median overall survival of only 12–15 months despite maximal surgical resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide-based chemotherapy [1, 2]. The aggressive nature of glioma is largely attributed to its profound immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which not only dampens endogenous antitumor immunity but also limits the efficacy of conventional immunotherapies [3, 4]. Recent advances in immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the treatment of several solid tumors; however, their clinical benefits in glioma have been disappointing, underscoring the urgent need to identify alternative or complementary immune evasion pathways [5, 6].

Among the emerging targets, CD155 (also known as the poliovirus receptor, PVR, or Necl-5) has attracted considerable attention due to its frequent overexpression in glioma and its central role in modulating immune cell function [7, 8]. CD155 interacts with three main receptors on immune cells: the activating receptor CD226 (DNAM-1) and the inhibitory receptors TIGIT and CD96 [9, 10]. The balance between these interactions determines whether immune cells become activated or suppressed. In glioma, the high-affinity binding of CD155 to TIGIT often outcompetes CD226 engagement, leading to impaired T cell and natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity, metabolic dysfunction of exhausted CD8+T cells, and enhanced regulatory T cell (Treg) activity [11–13].

Compared with classical immune checkpoint pathways such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, the CD155 axis exhibits distinct regulatory features. Whereas PD-1 primarily delivers inhibitory signals to effector T cells and CTLA-4 competitively antagonizes CD28-mediated co-stimulation during T cell priming, the CD155 axis operates through a tripartite receptor system in which inhibitory (TIGIT, CD96) and activating (CD226) receptors compete for a shared ligand. This architecture creates a dynamic balance between immune activation and suppression, rather than a unidirectional inhibitory pathway. Notably, TIGIT is frequently co-expressed with PD-1 on exhausted CD8+ T cells in GBM [14, 15], and dual blockade of TIGIT and PD-1 has demonstrated synergistic efficacy in preclinical glioma models [15], suggesting functional interplay between these pathways. However, the limited efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in GBM [5, 6] underscores the need to explore targets such as the CD155 axis that may be uniquely positioned to address the distinctive immunosuppressive landscape of the CNS.

Despite growing preclinical evidence supporting the CD155 axis as a promising immunotherapeutic target, several critical questions remain unresolved. First, the relative contribution of CD155–TIGIT versus CD155–CD96 signaling in glioma-associated immune suppression is not fully understood, and conflicting reports suggest that CD96 may exert both inhibitory and co-stimulatory functions depending on the context [16, 17]. Second, most mechanistic studies rely on overexpression systems or murine models that may not faithfully recapitulate the human glioma microenvironment, particularly the blood–brain barrier and the predominance of myeloid-derived suppressor cells [18]. Third, early-phase clinical trials targeting TIGIT or CD155 have shown only modest single-agent activity, and predictive biomarkers for patient selection are largely lacking [19, 20]. These limitations highlight the need for a more integrated and context-specific understanding of the CD155 signaling network.

Importantly, although the TIGIT/CD155 axis has been widely reviewed across multiple cancer types, most existing studies remain largely descriptive, focusing on cataloging receptor–ligand interactions and therapeutic strategies without systematically addressing the underlying regulatory logic of this pathway. In glioma, however, the situation is likely more complex. The presence of the blood–brain barrier, limited infiltration of effector lymphocytes, and a myeloid-dominated immune microenvironment may fundamentally reshape the functional interactions among CD155 and its receptors (TIGIT, CD226, and CD96). These context-specific features have not been adequately considered in previous reviews.

In this review, we therefore move beyond a purely descriptive framework and focus on several key aspects. First, we reinterpret the CD155 axis from the perspective of receptor competition, emphasizing the dynamic balance among TIGIT, CD226, and CD96 rather than a unidirectional inhibitory pathway. Second, we highlight emerging but underappreciated mechanisms, including CD155-induced CD226 degradation and the functional duality of CD96. Third, we examine how these mechanisms operate within the glioma-specific immune microenvironment and discuss their implications for therapeutic translation. Through this approach, we aim to more clearly define the functional landscape of the CD155 axis in glioma and identify critical gaps that should be addressed in future studies.

Structural characteristics and functional interactions of CD155 and its receptors

CD155, also referred to as Necl-5 or PVR, belongs to the nectin and nectin-like protein families (nectin1–4 and Necl1–5), which are classified within the Ca2+-independent immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) and are primarily associated with cell adhesion processes. It was originally identified as the cellular entry receptor for poliovirus [21]. The CD155 gene is located on chromosome 19 at position q13.31–13.32 and is transcribed into a precursor mRNA comprising eight exons. Alternative splicing of this transcript gives rise to four distinct isoforms, namely CD155α, CD155β, CD155γ, and CD155δ [22, 23]. CD155α and CD155δ exist as transmembrane isoforms, each consisting of three extracellular Ig-like domains (V–C2–C2), a membrane-spanning segment, and a cytoplasmic domain. By comparison, CD155β and CD155γ lack the transmembrane region and are secreted as soluble forms, which can be found in circulating fluids including serum and cerebrospinal fluid [24]. Of note, the intracellular region of CD155α contains an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) [25]. The production of soluble CD155 occurs independently of proteolytic processing [26].

Current research on CD155 has largely centered on its membrane-bound variants, with particular emphasis on CD155α, the largest isoform that contains an ITIM motif [24]. CD155 is expressed not only in tumor cells but also in DCs [27], and its levels are further increased in tumor-associated myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [28]. Notably, CD155 is markedly upregulated in glioblastoma, whereas it is essentially undetectable in normal brain tissue. Elevated CD155 expression in glioma has also been linked to enhanced immune cell infiltration, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for immunomodulation [29].

CD155 engages with multiple receptors, including TIGIT, CD96, and CD226, with distinct binding affinities that contribute to the regulation of immune responses (Fig. 1). Among these interactions, TIGIT exhibits the strongest binding to CD155 [30]. Originally identified in 2009 as an immune checkpoint receptor, TIGIT—also referred to as WUCAM or VSIG9—belongs to the type I transmembrane protein family [31]. Structurally, it consists of an extracellular IgV domain and an intracellular tail harboring an ITIM motif. The suppressive activity of TIGIT requires signaling through both ITIM and ITT motifs [30]. Expression of TIGIT is observed in a range of immune cells, such as CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, follicular helper T cells, and NK cells [32]. By comparison, CD226 (DNAM-1), which also interacts with CD155, exerts an opposing effect by promoting immune activation rather than suppression. CD226 is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) and is characterized by two consecutive IgV domains, along with an ITT or ITT-like signaling motif within its intracellular region [33]. CD226 is expressed on various immune cells, including T cells, NK cells, and monocytes [34]. Functionally, CD226 is considered a marker of highly active CD8+T cells and plays a key role in NK cell–mediated immune surveillance and antitumor responses [35]. CD96 represents a third receptor for CD155, further increasing the complexity of this regulatory network. Similar to TIGIT and CD226, CD96 is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) and contains three extracellular Ig-like domains, an intracellular ITIM, and, in humans, a YXXM motif [36, 37]. Functionally, CD96 is generally considered to mediate inhibitory signaling upon interaction with CD155. However, accumulating evidence indicates that it may also exert co-stimulatory effects under specific conditions [38]. CD96 is primarily expressed on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, as well as NK cells, suggesting its role as a context-dependent regulator within the CD155 signaling axis [39].

CD155 expressed on tumor cells interacts with multiple immune receptors, including the inhibitory receptor TIGIT, the activating receptor CD226, and the context-dependent receptor CD96. CD155 and its receptors in glioma immune regulation

The prevailing model of the CD155 axis in glioma immunity—whereby CD155 engagement with TIGIT dominates to suppress effector functions while CD226-mediated activation is passively outcompeted—provides a useful framework but fails to capture the full complexity of this signaling network. A critical examination of the literature reveals substantial heterogeneity in receptor expression patterns, context-dependent functional plasticity, and important discrepancies between in vitro findings and in vivo observations. Moreover, most mechanistic studies have relied on overexpression systems or subcutaneous murine models that do not recapitulate the unique immune landscape of the central nervous system, including the blood–brain barrier and the predominance of glioma-associated microglia/macrophages. The following sections critically evaluate the evidence for each receptor-ligand interaction, highlighting unresolved questions and methodological caveats that must be addressed to advance therapeutic development.

CD155–TIGIT interaction in immune suppression

TIGIT, also termed WUCAM, Vstm3, or VSIG9, is a member of the PVR/nectin family within the immunoglobulin superfamily. Structurally, it consists of an extracellular IgV domain, a type I transmembrane region, and a cytoplasmic tail harboring both ITT and ITIM motifs. Engagement of TIGIT with its ligand CD155 delivers inhibitory signals that suppress immune cell activation, thereby contributing to tumor immune evasion [19, 40]. Mechanistically, this interaction can attenuate downstream T cell receptor (TCR) signaling and reduce cytokine production, ultimately impairing effector function. TIGIT is broadly expressed across immune cell subsets, including CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells, NK cells, Tregs, and follicular helper T cells, whereas naïve T cells exhibit relatively low expression levels [41]. Notably, TIGIT expression is often upregulated upon chronic antigen stimulation, which is a hallmark of tumor-associated immune exhaustion.

Analyses of freshly resected glioblastoma tissues and matched peripheral blood samples have demonstrated a pronounced increase in TIGIT expression on tumor-infiltrating T cells [42]. Furthermore, in glioma, TIGIT is frequently co-expressed with PD-1 on CD8⁺ T cells exhibiting an exhausted phenotype, indicating a severely impaired antitumor immune response within the tumor microenvironment [14]. This co-expression pattern also suggests potential synergistic effects between TIGIT- and PD-1-mediated inhibitory pathways, providing a rationale for combined immune checkpoint blockade strategies. However, the frequent co-expression of TIGIT with PD-1 raises the question of whether TIGIT blockade alone can meaningfully restore effector function, or whether its effect is largely redundant without concurrent PD-1 inhibition; the synergistic efficacy of combined TIGIT/PD-1 blockade in the GL261 glioma model [15] suggests TIGIT may function as a co-maintainer rather than a primary driver of exhaustion. Indeed, TIGIT monotherapy has shown limited efficacy in GBM, and disrupting its inhibitory network may be required to achieve measurable responses [43]. Engagement of TIGIT with CD155 induces receptor phosphorylation and promotes the recruitment of SHIP-1, an SH2 domain–containing phosphatase. This interaction negatively regulates key signaling pathways such as NF-κB and ERK, ultimately leading to decreased cytokine production and impaired effector function of CD8+T cells [44]. Furthermore, accumulating evidence indicates that TIGIT-mediated T cell exhaustion in the TME is tightly associated with metabolic dysregulation, particularly impaired glucose utilization in CD8⁺ T cells [11, 45]. In TIGIT+CD8+T cells, diminished activation of the AKT/mTOR signaling axis results in decreased expression of critical metabolic regulators, including GLUT1, HK1, HK2, and c-Myc. This reduction disrupts glycolytic flux and limits biosynthetic capacity, ultimately leading to impaired metabolic fitness. Such metabolic constraints are closely associated with T cell functional exhaustion, weakening their effector responses. Importantly, interventions such as glucose supplementation or inhibition of TIGIT signaling have been reported to restore GLUT1 levels, revive the functionality of exhausted T cells, and increase IFN-γ production, thereby enhancing antitumor immune activity [11, 12]. Nevertheless, whether similar metabolic constraints operate in the glucose-restricted, lipid-rich microenvironment of glioma remains to be rigorously tested, as the metabolic vulnerabilities of glioma-infiltrating T cells may differ substantially from those in extracranial tumors where these mechanisms were originally characterized [46] (Fig. 2).

Among CD155-binding receptors, TIGIT has attracted considerable attention as a potential target for NK cell–based immunotherapy, largely due to its prominent expression on NK cells across a wide range of malignancies [47, 48]. Due to its higher binding affinity for CD155 relative to the co-stimulatory receptor CD226, TIGIT can effectively outcompete CD226 for ligand engagement, leading to inhibition of NK cell activation and cytotoxic function [40]. Together with the well-established understanding of its structural features and downstream signaling mechanisms, this suppressive capacity highlights TIGIT as an important therapeutic target [49, 50]. Beyond its strong interaction with CD155, TIGIT is also capable of binding CD112, albeit with lower affinity. This interaction interferes with DNAM-1–mediated co-stimulatory signaling, ultimately reducing NK cell–mediated immune responses [51–53]. Although the downstream signaling mechanisms of TIGIT remain incompletely defined, current evidence indicates that ligand engagement with CD155 leads to phosphorylation of its ITIM motif, a process likely mediated by Src family kinases, thereby initiating inhibitory signaling pathways [54–56]. Following phosphorylation, TIGIT engages the SH2 domain–containing phosphatase SHIP-1, resulting in suppression of downstream PI3K and MAPK pathways and consequent reduction in NK cell effector activity [54]. In addition, SHIP-1 interferes with the auto-ubiquitination of TNF receptor–associated factor 6 (TRAF6), thereby blocking NF-κB activation and further impairing NK cell cytotoxicity [55]. This inhibitory process generally takes place at the immune synapse and requires co-engagement of ITAM-containing activating receptors, which facilitates the spatial convergence of activating kinases and inhibitory motifs, enabling effective negative regulation [57–59]. Engagement of TIGIT with CD155 suppresses NK cell function by decreasing interferon-γ (IFN-γ) production, disrupting granule polarization, and consequently inhibiting the release of cytotoxic granules [55, 60]. Owing to the high-affinity binding between TIGIT and its ligands, blockade of this pathway has demonstrated the ability to restore NK cell cytotoxicity, highlighting its potential in NK cell–based cancer immunotherapy [61]. However, a critical unresolved issue is whether NK cells can efficiently penetrate the glioma core where CD155 expression is highest, given their limited intrinsic capacity to cross the blood–brain barrier and their sparse infiltration in GBM compared to myeloid cells. Furthermore, the abundance of TGF-β in the glioma microenvironment, which potently downregulates CD226 and NKG2D [62], raises the concern that NK cells may remain functionally impaired even if TIGIT blockade successfully relieves one inhibitory axis (Fig. 2).

Tregs are central to the establishment of an immunosuppressive environment, primarily through high-affinity IL-2 receptor signaling and the release of inhibitory cytokines. In human Tregs, immune checkpoint molecules such as TIGIT, PD-1, and CTLA-4 are expressed at high levels [63]. In tumor-bearing mice, TIGIT expression is markedly increased in Tregs relative to those in healthy controls, and TIGIT+ Tregs exhibit stronger suppressive activity, potently limiting CD8⁺ T cell activation and expansion [64]. Supporting these observations, studies in ovarian cancer models have demonstrated a significant accumulation of TIGIT-expressing immune cells within tumors, particularly in the CD4+ Treg subset. Functional blockade of TIGIT was shown to attenuate the suppressive capacity of these cells, indicating that TIGIT signaling enhances Treg-mediated immunoregulation in the TME [65]. A study by Hung et al. demonstrated that CD4+ T cells infiltrating glioma tissues in patients exhibit elevated TIGIT expression. Similarly, in the GL261 murine glioblastoma model, TIGIT levels on brain-resident Tregs were significantly higher than those observed in Tregs from peripheral lymphoid organs, including the cervical lymph nodes and spleen. These findings suggest that TIGIT may contribute to the establishment and maintenance of an immunosuppressive microenvironment within the central nervous system [15]. Furthermore, TIGIT+ Tregs exhibit a distinct transcriptional signature relative to TIGIT⁻ Tregs and demonstrate stronger suppressive function. This enhanced ability to inhibit effector immune cells underscores their critical contribution to tumor immune escape [66]. The Ccl21a-derived isoform CCL21-Ser has been shown to induce TIGIT expression on intratumoral Tregs, leading to enhanced immunosuppressive function and accelerated tumor progression in vivo [67]. Consistently, CCL21 is highly expressed in GBM cells and is associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes. In vitro evidence further demonstrates that CCL21–CCR7 signaling promotes glioblastoma cell migration and proliferation [68]. These observations suggest a potential mechanistic link in which CCL21-Ser within the GBM microenvironment may drive TIGIT upregulation on Tregs, thereby amplifying immune evasion and supporting tumor growth. A key translational consideration is that anti-TIGIT antibodies with Fc-competent domains may deplete TIGIT+ Tregs via ADCC, whereas Fc-silent antibodies may only block inhibitory signaling without affecting Treg survival; the optimal Fc format for glioma remains unresolved and warrants careful evaluation given the risk of autoimmune neuroinflammation upon systemic Treg depletion in the CNS. Notably, both Fc-silent and Fc-enabled anti-TIGIT antibodies have demonstrated efficacy through distinct mechanisms—activation of precursor exhausted T cells versus ADCC-mediated Treg depletion, respectively [69]—underscoring the need for glioma-specific optimization.

CD155 is also expressed on DCs, where it plays an important role in shaping immune responses. Notably, DCs exhibit high surface levels of CD155, which serves as a ligand for multiple immunoregulatory receptors expressed on T cells and NK cells, thereby influencing their activation and functional status [70]. Upon interaction with the inhibitory receptor TIGIT, CD155 undergoes tyrosine phosphorylation within its intracellular domain, triggering downstream signaling events. This signaling alters DC function by reducing the production of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-12p40 while promoting the release of the immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10. Consequently, DCs acquire a tolerogenic phenotype, contributing to immune suppression and supporting tumor immune escape within the TME [31]. Whether this TIGIT–CD155–mediated regulatory axis similarly controls DC function in glioma, however, remains to be fully elucidated. Given that glioma-associated DCs are known to be functionally impaired and that enhanced TIGIT/CD155 activity promotes DC tolerance and CD8+T cell dysfunction [71], elucidating this mechanism could reveal whether TIGIT blockade synergizes with DC-activating therapies. Further studies are required to characterize the distribution of CD155 on dendritic cells within the microenvironments of glioma and gynecologic malignancies, as well as to clarify the molecular mechanisms by which TIGIT signaling contributes to immunosuppressive regulation. In particular, the role of soluble CD155 isoforms in mediating TIGIT-dependent immune suppression in glioma has not yet been explored and warrants detailed investigation. Addressing these questions may identify novel targets and provide new opportunities to improve the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy.

CD155–CD226 interaction in immune activation

CD226 is a transmembrane glycoprotein widely expressed on immune cells, including T lymphocytes and NK cells, where it plays an important role in regulating immune signaling. Notably, its expression on CD8⁺ T cells has been associated with favorable clinical outcomes across multiple cancer types [35].

From a functional perspective, CD226 serves as a multifunctional immune receptor that promotes interactions between immune cells and their targets, thereby facilitating the establishment of the immune synapse [72, 73]. Beyond its role in cell adhesion, CD226 contributes to the regulation of cytotoxic activity and cytokine secretion via intracellular signaling mechanisms [74]. Following ligand engagement, CD226 recruits the adaptor protein Grb2, triggering activation of ERK and AKT pathways as well as calcium influx, all of which are essential for optimal immune function [74]. Within the TME, the activity of CD226 is often markedly impaired through multiple mechanisms. A key contributor is its competition with TIGIT for the shared ligand CD155. Owing to the higher binding affinity of TIGIT, this interaction preferentially favors TIGIT engagement, thereby weakening CD226-dependent activation signals [75]. Furthermore, direct interaction between TIGIT and CD226 disrupts CD226 dimerization, a process required for the initiation of its signaling activity [76]. In addition, decreased or absent expression of CD155 on tumor cells weakens CD226-mediated signaling, ultimately contributing to tumor immune evasion [77]. A pivotal study by Braun et al. added a critical dimension to this regulatory network by demonstrating that CD155 engagement on tumor cells actively drives the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of CD226 on CD8+ T cells, rather than merely outcompeting it for ligand binding [18]. This finding fundamentally shifts the therapeutic paradigm: simply blocking TIGIT may be insufficient to restore CD226-mediated activation if CD226 protein levels have already been depleted through CD155-induced degradation. Consequently, therapeutic strategies that simultaneously prevent CD226 degradation—for example, by blocking CD155 or stabilizing CD226 expression—may be required for optimal anti-tumor immunity. However, it remains unknown whether this degradation mechanism operates to the same extent in glioma-infiltrating T cells, as most mechanistic studies have been conducted in extracranial tumor models.

Under physiological conditions, NK cells acquire functional competence through a process known as licensing, which is associated with increased CD226 expression and concomitant downregulation of inhibitory killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs). This dynamic adjustment establishes a signaling balance that favors activation, enabling NK cells to respond efficiently to target cells. A relatively high CD226-to-KIR ratio is therefore critical for maintaining optimal NK cell responsiveness and cytotoxic potential. In the TME, however, this regulatory balance is frequently disrupted. Tumor-associated factors can impair the upregulation of CD226, thereby weakening activating signals while allowing inhibitory pathways to predominate. This shift not only limits NK cell activation but also contributes to a state of functional impairment or exhaustion, ultimately facilitating tumor escape from NK cell–mediated immune surveillance. In this context, the imbalance between CD226 and inhibitory receptors such as TIGIT and KIRs represents a key mechanism underlying suppressed NK cell activity in solid tumors [78]. A notable feature of NK cells in glioblastoma is the downregulation of CD226, an activating receptor essential for effective immune responses. This decrease is accompanied by reduced production of key effector molecules, including IFN-γ and perforin, upon PMA/ionomycin stimulation [79], indicating compromised cytotoxic function. The tumor microenvironment further amplifies this defect, as abundant TGF-β signaling suppresses CD226 expression, thereby sustaining NK cell dysfunction and limiting antitumor immunity [80]. Notably, TGF-β has also been shown to downregulate the activating receptor NKG2D on NK cells and CD8+ T cells specifically in glioma patients [62], further compounding the immunosuppressive pressure on these effector cells. TGF-β1 has been shown to impair NK cell function by activating Smad2/3 signaling, which leads to reduced expression of activating receptors, including CD226, as well as decreased levels of cytotoxic granules. This pathway contributes to the suppression of NK cell effector capacity. Notably, intervention with the IL-15 superagonist/IL-15 receptor alpha fusion complex (IL-15SA/IL-15RA) can counteract this effect by interfering with Smad2/3-mediated transcription, thereby restoring CD226 expression and reestablishing NK cell cytotoxic function [81]. Beyond cytokine-mediated regulation, tumor-derived exosomes also play a role in this process. These vesicles have been reported to reduce CD226 expression on infiltrating NK cells, further contributing to their functional impairment within the TME [82]. The convergence of multiple CD226-suppressive mechanisms in glioma—TIGIT competition, direct CD155-induced degradation, TGF-β-mediated transcriptional downregulation, and potentially exosome-mediated suppression—presents a formidable barrier to CD226-dependent immunotherapy. This raises a provocative question: is therapeutic targeting of the CD155 axis primarily about blocking inhibitory signals, or is it equally—if not more—important to preserve and restore CD226 expression and function? The current focus on TIGIT blockade may be addressing only one facet of a multi-dimensional problem, and whether these distinct suppressive mechanisms exhibit redundancy or synergy in impairing CD226 function in glioma remains an open question.

CD226 functions as a key co-stimulatory receptor not only in NK cells but also in CD8⁺ T cells, where it contributes to the amplification of activation and cytotoxic responses. Its role appears to be particularly critical under conditions in which dendritic cell–mediated antigen presentation is limited, as CD226 signaling helps sustain CD8⁺ T cell activity when conventional activation pathways are insufficient [83]. In contrast, when antigen presentation by dendritic cells is strong, the dependence on CD226 becomes less pronounced. This suggests that CD226 primarily serves to enhance the functional flexibility of CD8⁺ T cells, enabling them to maintain effective immune responses across varying immunological contexts. Given that glioma is characterized by poor DC function and limited antigen presentation [84], CD226 may play an exceptionally important role in this malignancy—yet it is precisely the receptor most vulnerable to suppression within the glioma TME. This creates a particularly detrimental scenario where the very pathway needed to compensate for defective antigen presentation is selectively targeted for disruption. In diseases such as acute myeloid leukemia, CD8⁺ T cells display a marked shift in receptor expression, characterized by increased levels of the inhibitory receptor TIGIT alongside reduced CD226 expression. This imbalance skews signaling toward inhibition, resulting in impaired cytotoxic function and diminished production of key effector cytokines, including IFN-γ and TNF-α, which is associated with poorer clinical outcomes [85]. Furthermore, tumor-derived CD155 has been shown to promote immune evasion by inducing the loss of CD226 on CD8⁺ T cells, thereby weakening co-stimulatory signaling and compromising the effectiveness of immune-based therapies [86]. Whether a similar TIGIT-high/CD226-low phenotype characterizes glioma-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and correlates with patient prognosis remains to be systematically evaluated. Furthermore, the development of CD226-agonistic agents or engineered CD226 variants resistant to CD155-induced degradation could represent alternative strategies that bypass the need to simultaneously block multiple inhibitory pathways (Fig. 3).

CD155–CD96 interaction in immune regulation

CD96 was initially characterized based on its upregulated expression during the later stages of T cell activation. In both human and murine systems, it is predominantly expressed on T cells and CD56bright NK cells [36]. Although its precise function in immune regulation remains incompletely defined, structural analyses have identified two key intracellular motifs that underpin its potential dual roles. One of these is an ITIM, which is generally associated with the transmission of inhibitory signals in immune cells [87]. In contrast, CD96 also contains a YXXM motif, which is implicated in the activation of downstream signaling pathways [88]. This structural duality—the simultaneous presence of both inhibitory and activating signaling motifs—underpins the conflicting functional data that have complicated the field’s understanding of CD96 and renders it arguably the most enigmatic component of the CD155 axis. Evidence suggests that CD96 expression is closely linked to the immune landscape of tumors, particularly with respect to the extent of immune cell infiltration. Rather than serving solely as a marker, CD96 is thought to actively participate in shaping immune responses by mediating processes such as cell–cell interaction and co-stimulatory signaling, thereby influencing immune regulation within the tumor microenvironment [89].

Bioinformatic analyses have demonstrated that CD96 is markedly upregulated in glioma. Functional enrichment and genomic profiling further suggest that CD96 is predominantly associated with immune-related pathways, particularly those involved in T cell–mediated immune responses within the glioma microenvironment [90]. In addition, CD96 expression exhibits strong positive correlations with multiple immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-1, CTLA-4, TIGIT, and TIM-3. Importantly, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses have identified CD96 as an independent prognostic factor associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes in glioma patients [91, 92]. However, these correlative studies, while suggestive, do not establish causality and should be interpreted with caution. High CD96 expression may simply reflect a generalized state of immune activation or exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment rather than indicating a direct pro-tumorigenic function of CD96 itself. Accumulating evidence suggests that CD96 overexpression in solid tumors is associated with increased chemoresistance in cancer stem cells. This process is mediated through the YXXM motif, which promotes Stat3 phosphorylation and subsequently activates the Src–Stat3–Opa1 pathway. Activation of this axis alters mitochondrial dynamics and enhances fatty acid β-oxidation, thereby sustaining the metabolic demands of cancer stem cells and promoting their survival under therapeutic pressure [93]. This discovery of a tumor cell-intrinsic, pro-survival function for CD96 adds a critical layer of complexity, suggesting that CD96-targeted therapies may exert anti-tumor effects not only by modulating immune cell function but also by directly impairing the fitness of cancer stem cells. Whether this mechanism operates in glioma stem-like cells, a population central to therapeutic resistance and recurrence in GBM, remains an important unanswered question. Emerging evidence indicates that engagement of CD96 with its ligand CD155 can negatively regulate NK cell–mediated antitumor responses. Mechanistically, CD96 competes with the activating receptor CD226 for binding to CD155, thereby limiting CD226-dependent co-stimulatory signaling. Upon ligand interaction, CD96 transmits inhibitory signals that reduce NK cell cytotoxic activity and suppress IFN-γ production [94]. However, its role in human T cells remains controversial [94, 95]. While the majority of studies suggest that CD96 expression on CD8⁺ T cells exerts an inhibitory effect on antitumor immunity, and that blockade of CD96 can reverse immune evasion in multiple cancer types—including melanoma, colorectal cancer, cervical cancer, and lung adenocarcinoma [96–99] —conflicting evidence has also been reported. Specifically, one study showed that disrupting the interaction between CD96 and its ligand CD155 expressed on tumor cells led to impaired antigen-specific CD8⁺ T cell responses [100]. Further mechanistic analysis indicated that CD96 is capable of transmitting activating signals through the MEK–ERK pathway. Activation of this pathway was associated with increased frequencies of NUR77- and T-bet-expressing CD8⁺ T cells, along with enhanced cytotoxic effector function [100]. This functional dichotomy—where CD96 acts as an inhibitor in some contexts and a co-stimulator in others—may be explained by differences in ligand density, the availability of downstream signaling adaptors, or the relative expression of other CD155 receptors such as TIGIT and CD226. In glioma, the specific conditions that dictate whether CD96 exerts inhibitory or stimulatory effects on CD8+ T cells remain completely undefined. Furthermore, it is unknown whether CD96 on Tregs, which are abundant in the glioma microenvironment, contributes to their suppressive function in a manner analogous to TIGIT. Given this uncertainty, therapeutic targeting of CD96 carries both promise and risk; therefore, future studies are needed to clarify the precise regulatory mechanisms of CD96 in glioma, and single-cell RNA sequencing analyses of glioma-infiltrating immune cells may help resolve these context-dependent functions by correlating CD96 expression with specific transcriptional programs of activation, exhaustion, or suppression.

Therapeutic strategies therapeutic strategies targeting the CD155 axis in glioma

Current studies are actively investigating therapeutic strategies targeting CD155 and its interacting receptors [89, 101]. These approaches include the use of recombinant poliovirus to selectively target tumor cells with high CD155 expression, blockade of inhibitory receptors associated with CD155 using monoclonal antibodies, and enhancement of effector cell function through CD226-based interventions. The latter can be achieved via genetic modification or ex vivo activation, such as CD226-engineered T cell therapies [92] (Table 1) (Fig. 4).

PVSRIPO as a therapeutic strategy in glioma

PVSRIPO, a genetically engineered poliovirus–rhinovirus chimera, is being explored as a novel anticancer agent. By exploiting the elevated expression of CD155 on tumor cell membranes, PVSRIPO selectively infects malignant cells and induces their destruction, while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues [102]. Following infection, the virus replicates within tumor cells and ultimately induces their lysis. In addition to its direct oncolytic effects, PVSRIPO can stimulate antitumor immune responses, thereby promoting immune-mediated recognition and clearance of malignant cells and enhancing overall therapeutic efficacy [103]. Early work by Matthias Gromeier and colleagues at Duke University in 2000 demonstrated that poliovirus possesses the ability to selectively infect and destroy brain tumor cells [104]. To improve safety and minimize the pathogenicity associated with wild-type poliovirus, the virus was genetically modified by replacing key viral elements with sequences derived from rhinovirus, thereby reducing neurovirulence while preserving tumor-targeting capability [104, 105]. This engineered construct, known as PVSRIPO, has since emerged as a promising candidate in the field of cancer immunotherapy [106].

Clinical investigations have underscored the therapeutic potential of PVSRIPO in GBM. In a phase I study conducted by Annick Desjardins, intratumoral administration of PVSRIPO in patients with recurrent WHO grade IV glioma demonstrated an absence of neurovirulence. Notably, patients receiving PVSRIPO showed improved long-term outcomes, with higher survival rates at 24 and 36 months compared with historical controls (NCT01491893) [105]. Subsequent phase III trials (NCT02967710) comparing PVSRIPO with standard chemotherapy further confirmed its clinical benefit. In this study, 21% of the 237 patients treated with PVSRIPO survived for at least three years, compared with only 11% in the chemotherapy group, indicating a significant improvement in overall survival [107, 108]. These results led to accelerated approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of recurrent GBM in 2018. Despite these encouraging results, several critical questions remain unresolved. First, the therapeutic efficacy of PVSRIPO depends on tumor CD155 expression, yet CD155 levels vary considerably among GBM patients and may be downregulated in certain tumor subclones, potentially limiting durable response. Second, the virus-induced type I interferon response can paradoxically upregulate PD-L1, suggesting that PVSRIPO monotherapy may be insufficient for sustained tumor control—a notion supported by ongoing combination trials with anti-PD-1/L1 agents (NCT03793789, NCT04479241). Third, intratumoral delivery restricts application to surgically accessible lesions and may inadequately target infiltrative tumor cells at the periphery. Whether these survival benefits extend to patients with multifocal or deep-seated tumors remains to be determined.

In summary, PVSRIPO has been extensively evaluated in both preclinical models and clinical studies, demonstrating a favorable safety profile along with promising therapeutic efficacy in glioma patients. However, key issues such as the durability of clinical responses and the identification of optimal combination strategies remain to be fully addressed. Overall, this innovative approach represents a significant step forward in cancer therapy and holds considerable potential for improving outcomes in patients with aggressive brain tumors [109, 110].

Monoclonal antibodies targeting the CD155

CD155 plays a critical role in tumor progression and invasion, making it an attractive target for therapeutic intervention [111]. Increasing efforts are being directed toward evaluating CD155 as a candidate for cancer immunotherapy. For instance, Zhao et al. developed a bispecific antibody targeting CD3 and CD155 (CD155 Bi-Ab) and assessed its ability to enhance the tumor-directed cytotoxicity of activated T cells (ATCs). Their findings showed that CD155 Bi-Ab markedly potentiated ATC-mediated killing of tumor cells. This effect was accompanied by increased secretion of effector cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2, as well as upregulation of the activation marker CD69, indicating enhanced T cell activation [112]. In addition to antibody-based strategies, small-molecule approaches have also demonstrated potential. Rediocide-A, a natural compound derived from traditional Chinese medicine, has been reported to overcome NK cell–mediated immune resistance in non-small cell lung cancer by reducing CD155 expression [113], suggesting its possible application in targeting CD155 in glioma. Furthermore, inhibition of CD155 has been shown to restore DC function [114], highlighting its potential as a therapeutic strategy, particularly in tumors that exhibit resistance to conventional ICIs. However, a fundamental challenge for direct CD155 targeting is the “double-edged” nature of this approach: blocking CD155 disrupts its inhibitory interactions with TIGIT and CD96, but simultaneously prevents its engagement with the activating receptor CD226, which is essential for optimal NK and CD8+ T cell effector function. As demonstrated by Braun et al., CD155 engagement is required to maintain CD226 surface expression, and CD155 blockade may paradoxically accelerate CD226 degradation [18]. Therefore, the net therapeutic effect of anti-CD155 antibodies may depend on the relative abundance of inhibitory versus activating receptors within the TME—a balance that likely varies across patients and tumor subtypes. A novel therapeutic approach currently under clinical evaluation involves NTX-1088, the first monoclonal antibody targeting CD155 in solid tumors, which entered a phase I clinical trial in early 2022 (NCT05378425). NTX-1088 exhibits high binding affinity for CD155 and functions by disrupting its interactions with inhibitory receptors such as TIGIT and CD96, thereby attenuating downstream suppressive signaling. Moreover, unlike conventional blocking antibodies, NTX-1088 is designed to preserve CD226-CD155 binding while selectively antagonizing TIGIT and CD96 interactions, thereby restoring CD226 expression on immune cells and boosting their antitumor activity. Whether this selective blockade strategy can achieve superior clinical efficacy compared to pan-CD155 blockade remains to be determined, and the outcome of ongoing trials will be critical in defining the optimal approach to CD155 targeting.

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic platform that combines the specificity of monoclonal antibodies with the potency of cytotoxic agents to enable targeted tumor cell killing. Clinical success with approved ADCs, such as brentuximab vedotin and trastuzumab emtansine, has validated the effectiveness of this strategy. With continuous advances in ADC design and optimization, more than 50 ADC candidates are currently being evaluated in clinical trials [115, 116]. Within this context, CD155-directed ADCs represent a potential therapeutic avenue, particularly for patients with tumors that have progressed following platinum-based chemotherapy or ICI treatment [117]. However, the development of CD155-targeted ADCs faces unique challenges. First, CD155 is also expressed on DCs and tumor-associated myeloid cells [26, 27], raising concerns about on-target, off-tumor toxicity that could deplete these immune populations and paradoxically exacerbate immunosuppression. Second, the shedding of soluble CD155 isoforms (CD155β and CD155γ) into the circulation may act as an antigen sink, sequestering ADCs before they reach the tumor bed and limiting intratumoral drug delivery. Finally, the internalization kinetics of CD155 upon antibody binding—a critical determinant of ADC payload delivery—remain poorly characterized in glioma cells. Addressing these issues will be essential for translating CD155-directed ADCs into safe and effective therapies for glioma patients.

Therapeutic targeting of CD155 receptors

CD155 engages multiple immune receptors, among which TIGIT exhibits the highest binding affinity. Upon interaction, CD155 can undergo oligomerization and subsequently associate with TIGIT to form higher-order complexes that initiate inhibitory signaling. Disruption of the TIGIT–CD155 interaction has been shown to restore both NK cell and T cell activity, highlighting its therapeutic potential, particularly in combination with other immune checkpoint inhibitors [50]. TIGIT has emerged as a key target in immunotherapy due to its strong affinity for CD155 and its elevated expression on NK and T cells. By preferentially binding CD155, TIGIT competitively limits CD226-mediated co-stimulatory signaling, thereby shifting the balance toward immune suppression and reducing NK cell activation [39]. Extensive studies have established TIGIT as a central regulator of immune inhibition, further supporting its value as a therapeutic target [60]. Functionally, engagement of TIGIT with CD155 or CD112 suppresses NK cell effector responses by reducing IFN-γ production, disrupting granule polarization, and impairing the release of cytolytic granules [118]. Consistent with these findings, blockade of TIGIT signaling has been shown to enhance NK cell function and improve the efficacy of immunotherapeutic approaches. However, clinical experience with TIGIT inhibitors has been disappointing, with several phase III trials in solid tumors failing to meet primary endpoints. Whether this reflects redundant inhibitory pathways in the TME, suboptimal patient selection, or the unique immunosuppressive barriers of glioma remains unclear.

NK cell–based therapies have gained increasing attention as a potential treatment strategy for glioma [119, 120]. To further improve therapeutic efficacy, combination approaches are being actively investigated, including the integration of NK cell therapy with immune checkpoint blockade and the use of donor-derived NK cells. These strategies are designed to overcome tumor-induced immunosuppression and enhance NK cell–mediated antitumor activity in GBM [121]. In addition, both genetically engineered and unmodified NK cells have been evaluated in clinical studies targeting tumor-associated ligands that are highly expressed on cancer cells [60]. Nevertheless, the limited infiltration and short persistence of adoptively transferred NK cells within the glioma microenvironment remain significant obstacles, and strategies to enhance their tumor-homing and resistance to TGF-β-mediated suppression are still in preclinical development. Importantly, although no clinical studies have yet evaluated NK or T cell–based therapies specifically targeting CD155, this absence likely reflects existing technical and biological challenges rather than a lack of therapeutic promise [122].

Evaluating CD155 expression across different tumor types, together with the profiling of its associated receptors—TIGIT, CD96, and CD226—on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, may facilitate the identification of appropriate patient populations for clinical investigation. Targeting CD155 indirectly by blocking inhibitory receptors such as TIGIT and CD96 has already demonstrated encouraging therapeutic potential, while preserving CD155–CD226–mediated co-stimulatory signaling [19]. Monoclonal antibodies against TIGIT have been shown to enhance immune-mediated tumor cell clearance [123]. Similarly, inhibition of CD96 has yielded favorable outcomes in metastasis models, including glioma and melanoma, through mechanisms dependent on CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells [124]. However, the dual functionality of CD96 as both an inhibitory and co-stimulatory receptor introduces uncertainty regarding the optimal therapeutic strategy—whether to block ligand binding or deplete CD96-expressing cells via ADCC. Further investigation is required to clarify the specific contributions of CD96 and TIGIT to tumor suppression and to determine whether their functions overlap or exhibit redundancy [125].

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in cancer treatment [126]. These engineered T cells are designed to recognize tumor-specific antigens, enabling targeted tumor cell elimination while overcoming mechanisms of immune evasion [127, 128]. Advances in CAR design, particularly the development of fourth-generation constructs, have further improved antitumor activity through the incorporation of cytokine secretion modules. Among these, CD226-based CAR-T cells have exhibited potent antitumor effects in both in vitro and in vivo models [129]. Despite these promising outcomes, CAR-T therapy is frequently associated with cytokine release syndrome (CRS), a significant adverse event influenced by factors such as infused cell dose and CAR-T cell activation dynamics [130]. However, because CD226 is also expressed on endogenous T and NK cells, off-target recognition and TME-mediated CD226 degradation may limit the durability of this approach in glioma. Nevertheless, CD226-based CAR-T cells continue to show considerable therapeutic promise, although additional refinement is necessary before their full clinical application can be realized [61].

Overall, therapeutic strategies aimed at the TIGIT–CD155 pathway, including monoclonal antibody–based interventions and engineered NK cell approaches, hold significant promise for cancer immunotherapy. However, the disappointing clinical performance of TIGIT inhibitors, the challenges of NK cell persistence in the CNS, and the ambiguous therapeutic value of CD96 blockade underscore the need for biomarker-driven patient selection and rational combination strategies. The integration of TIGIT–CD155 blockade with other immune checkpoint inhibitors may enhance both the magnitude and durability of antitumor responses. Considering the complexity of the tumor microenvironment, simultaneous targeting of multiple immune regulatory pathways is likely required to achieve optimal therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusion and future perspectives

In conclusion, the CD155 signaling axis, comprising CD155 and its interacting receptors TIGIT, CD96, and CD226, represents a central regulatory network governing immune responses in glioma. The dynamic balance between inhibitory and activating signals within this axis plays a pivotal role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment, ultimately determining the efficacy of antitumor immunity. Accumulating evidence indicates that dysregulation of this pathway contributes significantly to immune evasion, T cell exhaustion, NK cell dysfunction, and the establishment of an immunosuppressive niche in glioblastoma.

Therapeutic strategies targeting the CD155 axis—including oncolytic virotherapy, monoclonal antibodies, bispecific antibodies, and engineered immune cells—have shown encouraging preclinical and early clinical results. In particular, approaches that selectively disrupt inhibitory signaling while preserving or restoring CD226-mediated co-stimulation appear especially promising. However, the clinical translation of these strategies remains challenging, and several critical issues warrant further investigation.

First, the development of biomarker-driven patient selection strategies is urgently needed. The disappointing clinical performance of TIGIT inhibitors in phase III trials highlights that unselected patient populations are unlikely to derive meaningful benefit. Future studies should systematically evaluate CD155 expression levels, the TIGIT/CD96/CD226 receptor profile on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and the relative abundance of inhibitory versus activating receptors within the TME to identify patients most likely to respond to CD155-targeted therapies. Without such biomarkers, the risk of exposing patients to ineffective treatments and unnecessary toxicities remains high.

Second, tumor heterogeneity represents a fundamental challenge to durable therapeutic responses. CD155 expression varies considerably across GBM patients and may be downregulated in certain tumor subclones under therapeutic pressure, potentially driving acquired resistance. Furthermore, the functional plasticity of CD96—which can act as either an inhibitor or co-stimulator depending on ligand density and the local cytokine milieu—adds another layer of complexity that may confound therapeutic outcomes. A deeper understanding of how spatial and temporal heterogeneity within the glioma microenvironment influences CD155 axis signaling will be essential for designing effective combination regimens.

Third, the potential toxicities associated with targeting this axis must be carefully considered. Anti-TIGIT antibodies with Fc-competent domains may deplete TIGIT+ Tregs via ADCC, raising the risk of autoimmune neuroinflammation upon systemic Treg depletion in the CNS. Direct CD155 blockade or CD155-targeted ADCs may cause on-target, off-tumor toxicity, as CD155 is also expressed on DCs and tumor-associated myeloid cells, and depletion of these populations could paradoxically exacerbate immunosuppression. CD226-based CAR-T cell therapy carries the inherent risk of CRS, and off-target recognition of endogenous T and NK cells expressing CD226 may further compromise safety. Finally, disrupting the CD155 axis could disturb immune homeostasis given the involvement of this pathway in both inhibitory and stimulatory immune interactions. Comprehensive preclinical toxicology studies and careful dose-escalation strategies in clinical trials will be essential to define the therapeutic window for these agents.

Future research should focus on developing biomarker-driven strategies to identify patients most likely to benefit from CD155-targeted therapies. In addition, rational combination approaches—such as co-targeting TIGIT/CD155 with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, integrating CD155 modulation with NK or CAR-T cell therapies, or combining with metabolic and microenvironment-targeting interventions—are likely required to achieve durable clinical responses. Advances in single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and in situ immune profiling will further facilitate a more precise understanding of CD155 axis dynamics within the glioma microenvironment.

Ultimately, targeting the CD155 axis represents a promising but complex therapeutic avenue in glioma. A deeper mechanistic understanding, coupled with optimized combination strategies and improved delivery systems, will be essential for translating these approaches into meaningful clinical benefit for patients with this highly aggressive disease.
