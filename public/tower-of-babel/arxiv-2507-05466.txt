Computer-aided analyses of stochastic first-order methods, via interpolation conditions for stochastic optimization
Anne Rubbens, Sébastien Colla, Julien M. Hendrickx

Abstract
This work proposes a framework, embedded within the Performance Estimation framework (PEP), for obtaining worst-case performance guarantees on stochastic first-order methods. Given a first-order method, a function class, and a noise model with prescribed expectation and variance properties, we present a range of semidefinite programs (SDPs) of increasingly large size, whose solutions yield increasingly strong convergence guarantees on the problem. Eventually, we propose SDPs whose size depends on 2 N 2^{N} , with N N the number of iterations analyzed, that yield tight guarantees, attained by specific functions and noise distributions within these classes. On the other side of the spectrum, we propose SDPs whose size depends linearly on N N , and numerically show that, on many problems, they already provide tight guarantees. The framework accommodates a wide range of stochastic settings, with finite or infinite support, including the unstructured noise model with bounded variance, finite-sum optimization, and block-coordinate methods, in a unified manner, as guarantees apply to any setting consistent with the noise model, i.e., its expectation and variance. It covers both non-variance-reduced and variance-reduced methods. Using the framework, we analyze the stochastic gradient method under several noise models, and illustrate how the resulting numerical and analytical convergence rates connect with existing results. In particular, we provide improved convergence rates on the unstructured noise model with bounded variance and in the block-coordinate setting.

Introduction

Consider an unconstrained minimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x),

(1)

where

is a differentiable convex function. To solve

1
, consider a stochastic first-order black-box optimization method, i.e., a method that relies on evaluations of

noisy

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}(\nabla f(x_{k})+\varepsilon_{k}),

(SGD)

where
\varepsilon_{k}
is a zero-mean random variable, independent across queries.

Stochastic first-order methods, as opposed to exact first-order methods, i.e.,

f

\varepsilon_{k}
satisfies different assumptions.

Additive noise on deterministic gradients

The simplest setting arises when minimizing a deterministic function whose gradient evaluations are corrupted by additional zero-mean stochastic noise. This noise can be either
relative
, with variance depending on the gradient norm or
absolute
, with variance independent of the gradient norm. Such noise may arise in practice from limited numerical precision (e.g., floating-point computations), from simulation or measurement-based functions involving inherent randomness or error, or from intentional perturbations used to ensure data privacy, as in differentially private algorithms
[
6
]

Stochastic optimization problems

Another example is the minimization of an expectation, i.e.,

\displaystyle f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}[f_{\xi}(x)],

(2)

where samples
\xi\sim\mathcal{D}
are available, but the distribution
\mathcal{D}
itself is unknown. In such cases, gradients of

are unavailable, contrary to gradients of

, which provide an unbiased estimator of
\nabla f
. Hence, methods rely on unbiased noisy evaluations

Stochastic optimization problems are particularly relevant in statistical supervised learning. Indeed, letting

be the unknown distribution linking inputs and outputs,

\mathcal{D}

[
12

Finite-sum minimization

One of the most studied categories of problems relying on stochastic optimization consists of the minimization of a large sum of functions, i.e.,

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x),

(3)

where

is large and
f_{i}
’s are differentiable convex functions. In contrast with the first two examples, exact computation of

, an unbiaised estimator of
\nabla f(x)
, whose computation is approximately

Such minimization problems typically arise as approximations of (

Randomized block-coordinate descent methods

Finally, another example consists of minimizing large-dimensional differentiable functions, that exhibit component-wise structure. In this setting, it is again possible to compute the exact gradient, but it is computationally advantageous to evaluate the gradient only along a single coordinate, chosen uniformly at random. In this case, methods sample

Goal
. Given a function class
\mathcal{F}

Unified analysis of stochastic first-order methods

As highlighted in, e.g.,

,

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{k+1}^{2}|x_{k}]

Computer-aided analysis of stochastic first-order methods

Computer-aided techniques for automatically analyzing and designing first-order methods have recently attracted significant attention; see, e.g.,
[
68
]
for a recent survey. These include (i) the automatic computation of worst-case instances for a given method over a function class
\mathcal{F}
after
N

[

; (ii) the automated construction of Lyapunov (or potential) functions to certify (sub)linear convergence by analyzing a small number of iterations
[

,

,

]

,
26
,
45
,
70
]
. In a wide range of settings, the PEP framework provides tight

, and extracting analytical closed-form expressions from the numerical results is generally a challenging task. On the other hand, Lyapunov certificates require initial guessing on their structure and may not yield tight guarantees, but are computationally and algebraically more tractable, since the associated SDPs are small-sized. Such computer-aided analyses are mainly restricted to deterministic settings, but there exist extensions to the stochastic setting that we quickly summarize.

First, computer-aided analyses naturally extend to the analysis of
inexact
methods, where gradient errors are deterministic perturbations, as in
[
18
,
23
,
24
]
, see, e.g.,
[
21
,
31
,
40
,
72
,
77
,
78
,
79
]
for PEP analyses of inexact methods. We refer to this approach as the

approach. Indeed, conducting such analyses on settings in which perturbations are, in fact, stochastic rather than deterministic, yields convergence rates that hold for the worst realization of the noisy perturbations. Such rates are thus pessimistic in these settings.

Second, when the noise has finite support of cardinality

by enumerating all scenarios and considering the average-case performance, see, e.g.,
[

43
,

Then,
[

Finally,

1.2
Contribution

Inspired by the idea of
[
1
,
27
]
to embed expected values as variables to PEP formulations, we present a PEP-based approach for analyzing all stochastic zero-mean noises, uncorrelated across queries, and such that, for some constants
A_{1},B_{1},C_{1},E_{1},A_{2},B_{2},C_{2},D_{2},\rho\in\mathbb{R}
, and a possibly random sequence
(\sigma_{k}^{2})_{k=0,1,\ldots,N-1}
, it holds

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\|\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}|\mathcal{A}_{k}]&\leq A_{1}(f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star}))+B_{1}\|g_{k}\|^{2}+C_{1}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+D_{1}+E_{1}\sigma^{2}_{k},\\
\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{k+1}^{2}|\mathcal{A}_{k}]&\leq A_{2}(f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star}))+B_{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}+C_{2}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+D_{2}+(1-\rho)\sigma^{2}_{k},\end{split}

(5)

where
(x_{k})_{k=0,1,\ldots,N}
is the sequence of iterates of a given first-order method,
g_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k})
,
x_{\star}
is a minimizer of
f
, and
\mathcal{A}_{k}:=\sigma(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k},\varepsilon_{0},\ldots,\varepsilon_{k-1},\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
is the
\sigma
-algebra of available information up to iteration
k
, see, e.g.,
[
29
]
. This model includes, as special cases, all classical noise assumptions presented in Section
1.1
. In addition, there is no loss of generality in assuming zero-mean noise, since any stochastic distribution can always be decomposed into the sum of a deterministic term and a zero-mean random variable.

More precisely, we

•

Propose a framework, fitting the PEP methodology, for automated analysis of first-order stochastic methods with fixed stepsizes across a wide range of function classes (specified in Assumption
1
), under all zero-mean noise models satisfying (
5
) (Section
3
). Given
N
a number of iterations, a method, a function class, and a noise model (expectation and variance), we propose a spectrum of SDPs of increasingly large size, ranging from a size
\propto N
(Section
3.2
) to a size
\propto 2^{N}
(Section
3.5
), whose solutions provide increasingly stronger convergence rates on the problem. In particular, the SDP of size
\propto 2^{N}
yields tight performance guarantees, i.e., that are attained by a specific function and noise distribution. On the other side of the spectrum, the SDP of size
\propto N
yields guarantees that often happen to be, numerically, already tight (Section
4
).

•

Use the proposed framework to analyze (
SGD
) on the class of smooth (strongly) convex functions, under several noise assumptions. These assumptions cover the additive bounded noise model, the finite-sum setting with or without variance-reduction techniques, and the block-coordinate setting. We show both analytically (Theorem
4.1
, Proposition
4.4
) and numerically how the proposed framework fits in the existing first-order literature. In particular, it improves on all existing rates that only use the noise’s expectation and variance, and are hence valid for any distribution satisfying these assumptions. In addition, the framework provides overall improved guarantees, i.e., even as compared to convergence rates derived on the basis of more information on the noise distribution than its variance and expectation, (i) on unstructured noise models as the additive bounded noise model, and (ii) in certain structural settings, on sublinear convergence rates (Section
4
).

By construction, on
structural
noise models, e.g., on the finite-sum or the block-coordinate settings, the all-scenarios approach introduced in
[
69
]
, which uses this structural information on the noise’s distribution, yields stronger convergence rates than the proposed framework.
However, (i) these rates are valid for a smaller class of problems, preventing a unified analysis across different settings, (ii) the all-scenarios approach is restricted to finite-support noises, and can only improve on our framework on models for which information on the noise distribution is available, and (iii) this approach is mainly restricted, due to scalability issues, to a Lyapunov-based analysis. By contrast, while the tightness of the proposed framework also depends on solving exponentially large SDPs, it allows to trade off tightness for scalability, by solving a SDP relaxation of smaller size. Hence, in some cases of sublinear convergence, our framework, by solving small-sized relaxations, improves on the all-scenarios approach, given the conservatism introduced by guessing the Lyapunov function’s structure.

Preliminaries: computer-aided analysis of inexact methods.

This section introduces the core concepts of the Performance Estimation Problem (PEP) framework, originally proposed in
[

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in\mathbb{N},\ f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L},\\

\varepsilon_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\end{subarray}}{\max}\bigg\{f(x_{1})-f(x_{\star})\ \big|\

\displaystyle\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq 1,\ x_{1}=x_{0}-\alpha\left(\nabla f(x_{0})+\varepsilon_{0}\right),\ \nabla f(x_{\star})=0,\ \|\varepsilon_{0}\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\bigg\}.

(6)

3
Framework for computer-aided analysis of stochastic methods

This section presents a framework for automated analysis of stochastic first-order methods; via the introduction of expected values as variables to PEP formulations. We first define the classes of problems on which the framework applies, before presenting a spectrum of SDP formulations whose solutions provide convergence rates on these problems.

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{c}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,2^{p}}):=\bigg\{(F,G)\in\mathcal{G}_{c}:\

\displaystyle\exists(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})=\left(\begin{bmatrix}F\\
F\\
\vdots\\
F\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}G&G^{12}&\ldots&G^{1R}\\
G^{12}&G&\ldots&G^{1(R-1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
G^{1R}&G^{1(R-1)}&\ldots&G\end{pmatrix}\right),\text{ satisfying}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})\text{ is $\mathcal{F}$-interpolable},\mathbf{G}\succeq 0,\ \mathbf{G}\text{ is order $p$ super-symmetric},

\displaystyle\text{ and }\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,R\},\ \forall k\in[N-1],\ \mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}G^{1j}\mathbf{v}_{k}=(\Sigma^{1j}_{N,2^{p}})_{k+1,k+1}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}G\mathbf{v}_{k}\bigg\},

it holds that
\mathcal{G}_{E}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{c}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,R})\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{c}
, and

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(F,G)\in\mathcal{G}_{E}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\text{Tr }(A_{\text{obj}}G)+F^{\top}b_{\text{opt}}\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(F,G)\in\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{c}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,R})\end{subarray}}\text{Tr }(A_{\text{obj}}G)+F^{\top}b_{\text{opt}}\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(F,G)\in\mathcal{G}_{c}\end{subarray}}\text{Tr }(A_{\text{obj}}G)+F^{\top}b_{\text{opt}}.

(30)

The sets
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{c}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,R})
thus yields, for different choices of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,R}
, a spectrum of refined SDP relaxations of (
13
), starting from (

) and, as will be proven in Section

, eventually attaining (
13
).

Computational comments

When solving (
3.4
), storing the full matrix

is unnecessary. The
\mathcal{F}
-interpolability constraint only involves submatrices

Exact SDP reformulation of (

We now finally propose a SDP equivalent reformulation of (

for which (

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(F,G)\in\mathcal{G}_{E}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\text{Tr }(A_{\text{obj}}G)+F^{\top}b_{\text{opt}}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(F,G)\in\tilde{G}_{c}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,2^{N}}^{\star})\end{subarray}}\text{Tr }(A_{\text{obj}}G)+F^{\top}b_{\text{opt}}.

This equivalence holds regardless of the noise variance (

is independent of this assumption.

(Completely anti-correlated covariance matrices)

Theorem 3.20
.

Given

, consider a method

, a function class

Proof 3.21
.

The proof of Proposition
3.9
showed that
\mathcal{G}_{E}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{G}_{c}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,2^{N}}^{\star})
. In addition, the proof of Theorem
3.19
showed that
\tilde{\mathcal{G}_{c}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,2^{N}}^{\star})\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{E}
. Hence,
\mathcal{G}_{E}=\tilde{\mathcal{G}_{c}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,2^{N}}^{\star})
. Convexity of
\mathcal{G}_{E}
follows from that of
\tilde{\mathcal{G}_{c}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N,2^{N}}^{\star})
, which can be expressed as the projection of a convex set, characterized by linear constraints.

Hence, (
13
) can be equivalently formulated as (i) a maximization problem over a convex set of size
\propto 2^{N}
, as in Theorem

, or (ii) a maximization problem over a convex set
\mathcal{G}_{E}
of size
\propto N
, as in (
19
). In addition, the algorithmic constraints can be removed from
\mathcal{G}_{E}
and interpreted as additional constraints in the maximization problem. This yields a formulation of (

\propto N
, independent of algorithmic specifications, under additional algorithmic conditions. This formulation is similar to the deterministic setting, see, e.g.,
[

]
. Since there exists at the moment no tractable formulation of this set of size
\propto N
, obtaining a tight reformulation of (

) requires solving a SDP of size
\propto 2^{N}
. However, as shown in Section

, in many cases, the relaxation (

), i.e., an SDP of size
\propto N
, is sufficient to obtain tight convergent rates.

This section relies on the framework introduced in Section

) on

, under several noise models. We compare the resulting convergence rates with existing guarantees, obtained classically or in a computer-aided way.

Given, as only information on the noise distribution, its expectation, variance, and uncorrelation conditions, the proposed framework provides the best rates possible. We demonstrate both numerically (Figures

However, as compared to the all-scenarios approach introduced in

5
). On the other hand, the all-scenarios approach is restricted to the analysis of finite-support noise settings and, mainly, to a Lyapunov function approach. Hence, on (i) noise models defined only via their expectation, variance, and uncorrelation, e.g., the absolute bounded model (Figure
1
), or (ii) problems with sublinear convergence rates (Figure

In addition, the framework significantly improves on rates obtained via the worst-scenario approach, i.e., under deterministic perturbation (Figures

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
0<\mu<L
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha\in(0,\frac{2}{L+\mu}]
. Consider
N
iterations of (
SGD
) with constant stepsize
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
,
k=1,\ldots,N
, applied to a function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
, under additive bounded noise
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in[N-1]}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}|\mathcal{A}_{k}]=0
, and
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}|\mathcal{A}_{k}]^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
. Let
(x_{k})_{k\in[N]}
be the iterates of (
SGD
), and
x_{\star}
denote a minimizer of
f
. Then,

\displaystyle E[\|x_{N}-x_{\star}\|^{2}]\leq\phi^{2N}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{1-\phi^{2N}}{1-\phi^{2}}\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2},

(33)

where
\phi=(1-\mu\alpha)
.

Proof 4.2
.

The first part of the proof follows exactly
[
68
, Section 2.8.1]
, which addresses the noise-free case. Let
S=\{(x_{i},f_{i},g_{i})\}_{i\in[N]\cup\star}
denote the set of iterates (and minimizer) and associated function and gradient values. We start by analyzing a single iteration of (
SGD
), and claim the following.

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\phi^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}.

(34)

By Proposition
2.2
describing smooth strongly convex functions, and since
g_{\star}=0
, it holds

\displaystyle f_{\star}\geq f_{k}+\langle g_{k},x_{\star}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{L}{2(L-\mu)}\bigg(\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{L}+\mu\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{2\mu}{L}\langle g_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle\bigg).

\displaystyle f_{k}\geq f_{\star}+\frac{L}{2(L-\mu)}\bigg(\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{L}+\mu\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{2\mu}{L}\langle g_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle\bigg).

Summing these inequalities, multiplied by
2\alpha\phi
, yields

\displaystyle-\frac{2\alpha\phi(L+\mu)}{L-\mu}\langle g_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle+\frac{2\alpha\phi}{L-\mu}\|g_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{2\alpha\phi\mu L}{L-\mu}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\alpha\langle g_{k},x_{k}-x_{\star}\rangle+\alpha^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}\leq\phi^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha(2-\alpha(L+\mu))}{L-\mu}\|\mu(x_{k}-x_{\star})-g_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 176.407pt\leq\phi^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2},

where the second inequality follows from the domain of
\alpha
. Hence,

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\alpha\langle x_{k}-x_{\star},g_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}\rangle+\alpha^{2}\|g_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}\leq\phi^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\alpha\langle x_{k}-x_{\star},\varepsilon_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt+\alpha^{2}\big(2\langle g_{k},\varepsilon_{k}\rangle+\|\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\leq\phi^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\alpha\langle x_{k}-x_{\star},\varepsilon_{k}\rangle+\alpha^{2}\big(2\langle g_{k},\varepsilon_{k}\rangle+\|\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}\big).

Taking the expectation with respect to
\varepsilon_{k}
(conditioned on
x_{k}
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{k}}[\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\phi^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{k}}[\|\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}]\leq\phi^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2},

by assumption on
\varepsilon_{k}
. Hence, (
34
) holds. By recursion, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|x_{N}-x_{\star}\|^{2}]\leq\phi^{2N}\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\phi^{2i}\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}.

Since, for
r\neq 1
,
\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}r^{i}=\frac{1-r^{N}}{1-r}
, this concludes the proof.

5
Conclusion

We have proposed a framework for automated analysis of stochastic first-order methods, under all noise models satisfying Assumption
1
, and independently of the noise’s distribution. The framework positions itself between PEP analyses tailored for deterministic perturbation
[
72
,
21
,
78
,
79
]
(or equivalently, analyzing the worst realization of a stochastic perturbation) and those, regarding finite support settings, explicitly enumerating all scenarios
[
14
,
69
,
43
]
. Indeed, by embedding expectations as variables within the PEP framework, as in
[
1
]
, our method improves upon deterministic analyses while maintaining similar complexity via relaxations such as (
21
). On the other hand, we provide an SDP formulation achieving exact worst-case bounds, valid for all distributions satisfying the noise model, which is, in its implementation, similar to the all-scenarios approach, with
2^{N}
scenarios of a specific equiprobable
2
-realizations distribution. However, while the all-scenario approach yields convergence rates that are only valid for the setting considered, i.e., for the distribution analyzed, this exact formulation provides rates valid for all distributions, whose support may be infinite, with the same expectation and variance as the
2
-realizations distribution. This enables a unified framework applicable across diverse stochastic settings.

On structural noise models, e.g., finite-sum setting or block-coordinate settings, the proposed framework may yield looser rates than those obtained via the all-scenario approach, relying on this structural information. This is especially the case for problems with linear convergence rates. However, on problems with sublinear rates, it occasionally improves on the all-scenarios approach, since the latter is (mainly) restricted to Lyapunov-based analyses, while our framework allows trading off tightness for complexity. That is, one can solve relaxations as (
21
), tractable on a large number of iterations, and that yield stronger convergence rates than those obtained via a Lyapunov approach. Notably, the simplest relaxation often suffices to attain exact bounds.

Extensions

While this work focused on (
SGD
) over the class
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
, the proposed framework readily extends to a broader range of methods and function classes. As detailed in Section
3
, any problem satisfying Assumption
1
, including accelerated methods or those applied to non-convex or non-smooth problems, can be analyzed within this framework. Aside from analyzing existing methods, the framework can also be relied upon to design new efficient methods, as illustrated, e.g., in
[
26
]
.

The same methodology, which encodes expectations into computer-aided analysis, also opens avenues towards
averaged-case analysis
of optimization methods
[
9
]
, as compared to traditional worst-case analysis. This could yield performance guarantees closer to the practical behavior of methods.

Finally, our framework supports a reverse perspective: identifying the noise assumptions under which desirable convergence rates are achievable, and designing algorithms that satisfy them. With recent progress on solving non-convex PEPs (see, e.g.,
[
17
]
), such assumptions need not be limited to Gram-representable quantities, broadening the scope of analyzable behaviors.
