The complete mitochondrial genome of the parasitoid Microctonus aethiopoides (Loan, 1975) (Hymenoptera: Braconidae)
Authors: Meeran Hussain, Rebecca Funari, Nicolas Diercksens, Elahe Parvizi, Mark R McNeill, Angela McGaughran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract We report the first complete mitochondrial genome of Microctonus aethiopoides (Loan, 1975) (Hymenoptera: Braconidae), assembled using Oxford Nanopore and Illumina sequencing data. The circular mitogenome is 33,173 bp long and contains 37 genes (13 protein-coding genes, 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs) along with a large A + T-rich control region. It exhibits a strong AT bias (85.42%) and a unique tRNA gene arrangement, whereas protein-coding genes remain conserved across Braconidae. Phylogenetic analysis places M. aethiopoides within Euphorinae. This mitogenome offers a valuable genetic resource for future evolutionary and ecological studies and improves understanding of mitogenome evolution and gene rearrangements within Braconidae.Keywords: Biocontrol, gene order, nanopore, phylogeny

Introduction

Insect mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) typically range from 14 to 19 kb, although larger genomes (20–28 kb) occur in some lineages. This size increase is primarily attributed to expansion of the control region through tandem repeats rather than changes in gene copy number (Filipović et al. 2021; Lin et al. 2021; Morgan et al. 2022; Olli et al. 2025). However, mitogenome size variation remains underexplored due to limitations of Sanger and second-generation sequencing, which struggle to resolve repetitive regions. Their short read lengths (∼1 kb for Sanger; 100–300 bp for second-generation) often result in incomplete assemblies and underestimated genome sizes (DeSalle et al. 2017; Monnens et al. 2020; Filipović et al. 2021; Formenti et al. 2021; Morgan et al. 2022). Long-read platforms such as Oxford Nanopore Technologies now enable accurate assembly of these regions, improving understanding of mitogenome evolution (Filipović et al. 2021; Kinkar et al. 2021; Morgan et al. 2022; Olli et al. 2025).

Gene order in insect mitogenomes is generally conserved, but rearrangements are common in some lineages, particularly Hymenoptera (Wei et al. 2010; Li et al. 2016; Zheng et al. 2021). These rearrangements provide valuable phylogenetic signals for inferring evolutionary relationships, especially at subfamily and genus levels (Cameron 2014; Tang et al. 2019). Despite this, mitogenome data remain limited for the parasitoid subfamily Euphorinae (Hymenoptera: Braconidae), restricting insights into their evolution. Here, we present the first complete mitochondrial genome of Microctonus aethiopoides (Loan, 1975) (Hymenoptera: Braconidae), a parasitoid used in biocontrol, providing a resource for comparative genomics and phylogenetic studies in this lineage.

Materials and methods

Mitogenome assembly and annotation

DNA from an individual of the New Zealand-released Irish strain of M. aethiopoides (Figure 1) (Dunedin, New Zealand: 45.8570° S, 170.3916° E) was extracted as part of a previous comparative genomics study (unpublished) and sequenced using hybrid long-read Oxford Nanopore Technologies and short-read Illumina sequencing. The read coverage depth map is shown in Figure S1.

A photograph of Irish Microctonus aethiopoides (photo credit: Paige Matheson - School of science, university of Waikato, New Zealand).

Other specimens from the collection are deposited at the Invasomics Laboratory, School of Science, University of Waikato (URL: www.ang-mcgaughran.com; contact: Dr Angela McGaughran) under voucher numbers IrMaDun_12 - IrMaDun_15.

Initial assembly was performed using NOVOPlasty v4.3.5 (Dierckxsens et al. 2017), with a partial M. aethiopoides COI sequence (GenBank accession: KY845728) used as the seed. The assembly was extended with long-read data using NOVOLoci v0.4 (Dierckxsens et al. 2025), producing a circular mitochondrial genome.

The mitogenome was annotated using MITOS2 v2.1.9 (Donath et al. 2019) with the invertebrate mitochondrial genetic code. tRNA predictions were validated using ARWEN v1.2 (Laslett and Canbäck 2008) to confirm gene identity and secondary structure, and start and stop codons were manually curated. A circular mitogenome map was generated using Proksee (https://proksee.ca/) to visualize gene organization and structural features.

Phylogenetic analysis

Thirteen species were used to infer a phylogeny using mitogenomes, including M. aethiopoides, nine additional Braconidae species selected based on available annotations and distinct gene orders within their subfamilies, and three Ichneumonidae species as outgroups. Nucleotide sequences from the 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes were used for phylogenetic reconstruction. The dataset was prepared using the EZpipe tool available on the EZmito web server (Cucini et al. 2021), applying the invertebrate mitochondrial genetic code. All three codon positions were retained in the nucleotide alignment for downstream phylogenetic analysis. The resulting PHYLIP file was subjected to maximum-likelihood phylogenetic inference using IQ-TREE v3.1.1 (Minh et al. 2020). ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) was used with -m MFP+MERGE to select the best-fit substitution models and merge similar partitions. Node support was assessed with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-B 1000) and 1,000 SH-aLRT replicates (–alrt 1000).

Results

Mitogenome assembly and annotation

The complete mitogenome of M. aethiopoides was circular and 33,173 bp long (Figure 2; GenBank accession: PZ269994). It contained the typical 37 genes: 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 22 transfer RNA genes (tRNAs), and two ribosomal RNA genes (rRNAs). The nucleotide composition of the heavy strand was A = 42.00%, T = 43.42%, G = 9.75%, and C = 4.83%, giving a strong AT bias of 85.42%, as commonly observed in Hymenoptera mitogenomes (Tang et al. 2019).

The 13 PCGs totaled 11,001 bp, ranging from 150 bp (atp8) to 1,668 bp (nad5), and represented 33.16% of the genome (Table S1). Five PCGs used ATT (Ile) as the start codon (cox1, nad3, nad5, nad6, and nad4L), four used ATA (Met) (cox2, atp8, nad4, and nad1), and three used ATG (Met) (atp6, cox3, and cob). Most PCGs ended with TAA, while four genes (cox2, nad4, cob, and nad2) had truncated stop codons (T-). The 22 tRNAs were 62 to 70 bp long, averaging 65.5 bp and totaling 1,440 bp (4.34% of the genome). The two rRNAs, rrnL (1,227 bp) and rrnS (729 bp) accounted for 5.90% of the mitogenome.

Phylogenetic analysis

The maximum-likelihood phylogeny based on 13 mitochondrial PCGs recovered a well-supported topology, placing M. aethiopoides within Euphorinae. Ichneumonidae were used as the outgroup to root the phylogeny, and they formed a separate clade from the Braconidae lineages (Figure 3). Within Braconidae, the sampled Euphorinae taxa were recovered in a clade that was sister to the Microgastrinae + Cardiochilinae clade, with these then placed as sister to the remaining braconid subfamilies (Figure 3). A clade comprising Zele chlorophthalmus, M. aethiopoides, and Dinocampus coccinellae was strongly supported (100% bootstrap values; Figure 3), although the immediate sister relationship between M. aethiopoides and D. coccinellae had moderate support, indicating weaker resolution at that node.

Gene order rearrangements could be classified into translocations, local inversions (inverted in the local position), gene shuffling (local translocation), and remote inversions (translocated and inverted) (Dowton et al. 2003). When comparing the gene order of M. aethiopoides with that of Drosophila melanogaster, which follows the canonical Pancrustacea arrangement, we found no rearrangements in protein-coding genes in the mitogenome, whereas some tRNA genes exhibited changes in gene order (Figure 4). Specifically, M. aethiopoides exhibited gene shuffling of trnC-trnY and trnS1-trnN, as well as rearrangement of the control region-associated trnI–trnQ–trnM cluster. Within this latter gene cluster, the changed position and orientations of trnI and trnM are consistent with remote inversions, whereas trnQ appears to have undergone a local inversion. Across Braconidae species, the trnI-trnQ-trnM and trnW-trnC-trnY clusters were commonly rearranged relative to D. melanogaster.

Discussion and conclusion

This study presents the first complete mitochondrial genome of M. aethiopoides, providing a valuable resource for the genus Microctonus and the parasitoid subfamily Euphorinae. The mitogenome is relatively large (∼33.17 kb) and shows a distinct gene arrangement, with unique tRNA arrangements compared to other Braconidae species (Wei et al. 2010; Li et al. 2016; Jasso-Martínez et al. 2022; Yuan et al. 2022). Such rearrangements could be mediated by different scenarios, includes tandem duplication followed by random gene loss, recombination-mediated inversions or translocations, and independent lineage-specific rearrangements (Dowton et al. 2003; 2009; Song et al. 2016).

Larger insect mitogenomes are also observed in some other Braconidae species, for example, Aleiodes leptofemur (∼32.28 kb), Dinocampus coccinellae (22.8 kb), and Zele albiditarsus (23.69 kb) (Barclay and Broad 2024; Broad 2024; Crowley and Broad 2024). Long-read sequencing enables complete recovery of these repeat-rich regions (Morgan et al. 2022), which are often difficult to resolve using short-read approaches.

Phylogenetic analysis, incorporating representatives from Braconidae and Ichneumonidae, robustly placed M. aethiopoides within Braconidae under subfamily Euphorinae, supported by high bootstrap values.

Although the control region was fully recovered, its structural features remain uncharacterized. Future comparative analyses will help determine whether mitogenome expansion in M. aethiopoides has functional or evolutionary significance.
