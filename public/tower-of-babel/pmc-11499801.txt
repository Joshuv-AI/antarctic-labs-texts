Association between sleep quality and uncertainty stress among healthcare professionals in hospitals in China: a nationwide cross-sectional survey
Authors: Jianjiang Pan, Xixuan Cai, Liying Chen, Lihong Wu, Jingjing Xia, Jingjing Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesSleep quality is a critical concern among healthcare professionals, yet the role of uncertainty stress has been inadequately explored. This study aims to explore the associations between sleep quality and uncertainty stress among healthcare professionals working in Chinese hospitals.DesignCross-sectional survey.SettingData were collected via a cross-sectional survey administered to healthcare professionals across three Chinese provinces from 29 September 2022 to 18 January 2023.ParticipantsA total of 1902 participants contributed valid responses for analysis.Primary outcome measureSleep quality.ResultsOut of 1902 respondents, 26.4% reported uncertainty stress and 50.5% experienced insomnia. Binary logistic regression analysis revealed that higher uncertainty stress significantly predicted poor sleep quality (OR=3.89; 95% CI 3.06, 4.95; p<0.001) while controlling for sociodemographic characteristics. Furthermore, linear regression analysis confirmed the similar relationship between uncertainty stress and sleep quality (β=3.10; 95% CI 2.67, 3.52; p<0.001).ConclusionsThe study highlights a significant association between uncertainty stress and impaired sleep quality among Chinese healthcare professionals. These findings suggest the necessity for targeted strategies to mitigate uncertainty stress, which may potentially promote better sleep and overall well-being in healthcare settings.Keywords: sleep medicine, occupational stress, health workforce

Introduction

Sleep holds the key to overall well-being serving as a vital determinant for optimal physical and mental health.1 The quality of sleep plays a pivotal role in energising the body and facilitating its healing processes through intricate interactions with the immune system. Additionally, the regulation of sleep profoundly influences brain function and behaviour.1 This is particularly pertinent for healthcare professionals, who often endure long working hours, shift work and other challenges inherent to their demanding profession.2 Adequate sleep is indispensable for healthcare professionals to discharge their duties effectively, make informed clinical decisions, and prioritise their own health and well-being.3

Conversely, sleep problems such as insufficient duration and poor quality can have detrimental effects on healthcare professionals. These issues include diminished cognitive abilities, reduced psychomotor performance and decreased productivity.4 5 More critically, sleep problems are associated with an increased risk of medical errors, which can compromise patient health and safety.6 Healthcare professionals frequently experience high levels of stress, including uncertainty stress, which significantly impacts their sleep quality.7 Therefore, the sleep quality of healthcare professionals is not just a matter of personal well-being but also a crucial factor in ensuring patients’ safety and quality of care.

Uncertainty stress arises from stemming unpredictable and ambiguous factors such as intricate patient cases, stringent time constraints, ethical dilemmas and the perpetual need to adapt to fluctuating scenarios.8 Scholz defined it as the pressure resulting from an individual’s subjective sense of uncertainty regarding the occurrence of an event, the intensity of a condition or the correctness of a state.9 This subjective perception and experience, triggered by uncertainty, has been consistently corroborated by numerous studies as a significant contributor of stress, with psychological stress resulting from uncertainty profoundly affecting an individual’s physical and mental well-being.10 11 For instance, Yang’s survey of residents in six Chinese cities found that individuals facing high levels of uncertainty were six times more susceptible to health risks compared with those who were not.12

Such stress, stemming from uncertainty, can lead to various psychological and physiological responses, potentially influencing healthcare professionals’ functioning, including burn-out, reduced job satisfaction and compromised patient care.13,15 Understanding the impact of uncertainty stress on healthcare professionals is essential for developing strategies to mitigate its adverse effects and promote their well-being.

Although the individual impact of sleep quality and uncertainty stress on healthcare professionals has been well-documented, research investigating this relationship between these two factors is limited. Understanding the relationship is crucial for developing comprehensive interventions to support the mental health and well-being of healthcare professionals. Through an investigation of these relationships within the Chinese context, this study aims to contribute to the existing literature and provide insights that can inform targeted interventions and support systems for healthcare professionals in China.

Methods

Study population

This study was a cross-sectional survey designed to investigate the association between sleep quality and uncertainty stress among healthcare professionals in Chinese hospitals. This survey was conducted from 29 September 2022 to 18 January 2023, using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. Provinces were selected based on geographical distribution and economic development levels to ensure a representative sample. Specifically, we aimed to include provinces from different regions of China (eg, eastern, central and western) and with varying levels of economic development (eg, high, medium and low gross domestic product (GDP) per capita). Consequently, a purposive sampling strategy was adopted. The selected provinces were Zhejiang (eastern region, 2021 nominal GDP of ¥ 7351.6 billion, ranked fourth among the 31 provinces in Mainland China), Henan (central region, 2021 nominal GDP of ¥ 5888.7 billion, ranked 12th) and Guizhou (western region, 2021 nominal GDP of ¥ 1958.6 billion, ranked 22nd).16 In each province, three to five tertiary hospitals and six to eight non-tertiary hospitals were selected based on the distribution and number of hospitals. Quota sampling was then used to collect 70–90 samples from each tertiary hospital and 30–50 samples from each non-tertiary hospital. Two coordinators at each hospital were trained in ethical research practices and were specifically instructed to maintain confidentiality and impartiality throughout the study. These coordinators distributed the online survey to eligible medical staff through work WeChat groups or DingTalk groups, which are widely used communication tools in China.

Eligibility criteria for respondents included being employed at one of the selected hospitals in roles such as physicians, nurses or other hospital employees (including administrative staff, support staff and technical staff directly involved in patient care or hospital operations), being between the ages of 18 years and 65 years, expressing a willingness to participate in the survey, and providing signed informed consent within the survey period. Employees in purely logistical or non-patient-facing positions, as well as those on vacation or sick leave for more than 2 weeks at the time of the survey, were excluded from the study.

Study variables

Outcome variables

Sleep quality

The Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) was used to assess the risk of insomnia of the participants.17 This scale consists of eight items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3. The total score is calculated by summing all items, with higher scores indicating more severe insomnia symptoms. A total score greater than 6 was considered indicative of insomnia. The AIS demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.90 in previous studies and 0.88 in this study, indicating high internal consistency.

Exposure variables

Uncertainty stress

Uncertainty stress was measured using a questionnaire designed and developed by Yang,12 with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.81, which has demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability in Chinese research.18 19 This questionnaire consists of four items measuring current life uncertainty, social change uncertainty, goal achievement uncertainty and social value uncertainty, and each item was rated on a 5-point Likert Scale ranging from 0 to 4. The total stress score is obtained by summing all items, with higher score indicating greater perceived stress. A total score greater than 12 is classified as ‘High’ indicating high uncertainty stress. Cronbach’s α for uncertainty stress in this study was 0.74.

Covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics included: gender, age, years of service (total work experience), marital status, region, level of hospitals, specialisation, title, education attainment, annual income, weekly working hours and frequency of night shift.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using STATA V.16, with a significance level set at p<0.05. Missing data, accounting for less than 1%, were included in the analysis and marked as ‘.’ in STATA. We assumed that the data in this article were normally distributed and the variables were independent of each other. Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to evaluate the association between sleep quality and uncertainty stress among healthcare professionals. We hypothesised a linear relationship between uncertainty stress (independent variable) and sleep quality (dependent variable). All variables were categorical. Descriptive statistics summarised demographic characteristics and overall scores of uncertainty stress and sleep quality. The correlations between sociodemographic characteristics, uncertainty stress and sleep quality were assessed using the χ2 test. Binary logistic regression analysis was employed to determine the predictive value of uncertainty stress on sleep quality while controlling for sociodemographic characteristics. Additionally, a linear regression analysis was conducted with sleep quality as a continuous variable to further confirm the relationship between uncertainty stress and sleep quality.

Ethical considerations

All participants were informed about the study’s purpose and nature. Informed consent was obtained prior to data collection, and participants were guaranteed confidentiality and the right to withdraw at any stage without facing repercussions.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Across three provinces, out of 2761 eligible participants, 1915 (69.4%) completed the survey, of whom 1902 were identified as valid for analysis. A total of 13 participants were excluded from the final evaluation for providing responses that were inconsistent or contradictory, which compromised the reliability of their data for interpretative purposes. The incidence of high uncertainty stress and insomnia among healthcare professionals was 26.39% and 50.47%, respectively.

Discussion

To our best understanding, this is the first nationwide cross-sectional study to explore the effects of uncertainty stress on insomnia among Chinese healthcare professionals. Our research indicates a significant correlation between uncertainty stress and diminished sleep quality, with over half of the participants experiencing insomnia and a quarter reporting high levels of uncertainty stress. These results highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions to address sleep quality and uncertainty stress in this population.

Our study found that 50.47% of healthcare professionals suffer from insomnia, a rate higher than that reported in earlier surveys conducted during the initial stages of the COVID-19 pandemic.20 The data suggest that changes in epidemic prevention policies and increasing workplace pressures have exacerbated sleep issues among healthcare professionals. Nurses, in particular, showed a higher incidence of insomnia compared with physicians, echoing prior research,21 22 likely due to the greater frequency of night shifts. This finding was significant in the logistic regression analysis but not in the linear regression analysis, indicating that while nurses are more likely to experience insomnia, the severity of their symptoms may not differ significantly from those of other healthcare professionals. This underscores the importance of considering both the occurrence and severity of insomnia when evaluating its impact on different professional groups. Additionally, our research identified a correlation between lower annual income among healthcare professionals and sleep disturbances. Income, often serving as a proxy for socioeconomic status (SES), has been previously known for its association with sleep quality.23 Although education attainment is another important indicator of SES, our study didn’t find a statistically significant correlation between education level and sleep quality among healthcare professionals.

Our research unearths a startlingly high prevalence of uncertainty stress among healthcare professionals, distinguishing this group from another population.24 Previous studies have illustrated that stress, characterised by heightened psychological and physical activation in response to demands, often disrupts normal sleep patterns through the activation of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal system.25 26 Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) is a dispositional trait characterised by an individual’s tendency to perceive uncertain situations as stressful or threatening. Previous research has established an association between IU and sleep quality, with perceived stress mediating this relationship.27 28 In contrast, uncertainty stress refers to the situational stress experienced due to uncertain or unpredictable events in one’s environment. While IU is a stable trait, uncertainty stress can fluctuate based on external circumstances. Our study addresses the gap by investigating the direct impact of uncertainty stress on sleep quality among healthcare professionals. This focus allows for a more detailed understanding of how specific stressors related to uncertainty in the healthcare environment affect sleep quality, which has important implications for interventions aimed at improving the well-being of healthcare workers.

While extensive research has examined the association between occupational stress and insomnia,29 30 it is crucial to distinguish between general occupational stress and uncertainty stress. Occupational stress encompasses a wide range of stressors related to job demands, work environment and interpersonal relationships at work. In contrast, uncertainty stress specifically refers to the stress experienced due to unpredictability and lack of control over future events, which can be particularly pronounced in healthcare settings during crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic.31 32 Given the unique nature of uncertainty stress and its potential impact on risk of insomnia, our study addresses a critical gap in the existing literature. Future research should continue to explore the specific mechanisms by which uncertainty stress affects sleep quality and develop interventions to mitigate this type of stress among healthcare professionals.

This study included healthcare professionals from various roles and regions across China, coupled with a large and diverse sample size. This approach ensured that our sample captured both similarities and variations in these factors, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between uncertainty stress and sleep quality among healthcare professionals across different contexts. This comprehensive approach not only enhances the generalisability of our findings but also provides a holistic and nuanced understanding of the issue at hand. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional nature of this survey limits our ability to establish causal relationships between sleep quality and uncertainty stress. Longitudinal studies, which track individuals over time, are imperative to gain a deeper understanding. Second, our reliance on self-reported measures for sleep quality and stress levels may introduce response biases, and objective measures could provide more accurate assessments.

Third, the effective response rate is a potential limitation of this study, with respect to the potential for sampling bias. Participants who chose to respond may differ in significant ways from those who did not, potentially affecting the generalisability of our findings. Fourth, we used the AIS to assess the risk of insomnia among healthcare professionals in this study. While AIS is a validated tool for measuring insomnia symptoms, it does not encompass all components of sleep quality, such as sleep duration, efficiency, respiratory disturbances and sleep-disordered breathing. Future studies should include a broader range of metrics to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of sleep quality. Additionally, exploring the effectiveness of targeted interventions aimed at improving sleep and managing stress in hospital settings would be valuable. Such studies could inform the development of effective strategies to enhance the resilience and performance of healthcare professionals. Furthermore, understanding the role of cultural factors in shaping the experiences of healthcare professionals in China is crucial. Comparing our findings with those from other cultural contexts could provide a more comprehensive and global perspective on the issue.

Conclusion

Our study provides crucial insights into the relationship between sleep quality and uncertainty stress among healthcare professionals in China. This study may provide critical evidence-based information that can inform the development of targeted interventions. By identifying key sources of uncertainty stress and establishing their impact on sleep quality, our findings underscore the need for comprehensive strategies to support healthcare professionals. Potential interventions may include training programmes, organisational changes and mental health resources, all aimed at reducing stress and improving overall well-being.
