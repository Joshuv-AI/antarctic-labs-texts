Calculating the Probability that a Previously Susceptible Individual is Infected as a Function of Time Following Exposure to SARS-CoV-2
Authors: Alun Thomas, Karim Khader, Adam Hersh, Matthew Samore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionReliable assessment of disease state probabilities for an individual following a specific exposure event, such as an occupational exposure, is critical for managing isolation and quarantine and reducing onward transmission to susceptible individuals. Such assessments are particularly important for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), where infection, testing, and infectiousness vary substantially across individuals and time since exposure.MethodsWe present a method, accompanying software programs, and a publicly available website for calculating the probability that an individual is in each disease state immediately following an exposure event that may or may not have resulted in transmission of SARS-CoV-2. The framework integrates timing of exposure, test type and timing, and symptom status to estimate probabilities of latent infection, infectiousness, recovery, or no infection.ResultsWe illustrate the utility of this approach by calculating: (i) the time at which an exposed individual’s risk of being infectious falls below an acceptable threshold; (ii) the benefit of a second test for asymptomatic individuals with an initial negative test; (iii) the value of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and antigen testing for case counting; and (iv) the time at which the risk that an infected individual remains infectious becomes comparable to background population risk. The results demonstrate that test interpretation should not be done naively: a negative test may reflect absence of transmission, a false-negative result, rapid resolution of infection, or an unusually prolonged latent period, each with distinct implications for risk management.ConclusionsAccurate differentiation among possible disease states following exposure is essential for informed public health decision-making. Our software provides a rigorous, transparent means to assess and clearly communicate state probabilities, enabling more nuanced interpretation of test results and better-supported decisions regarding isolation, quarantine, and testing strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01371-y.Keywords: Isolation, Quarantine, Case counting, Follow-up testing

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Infection risk after a known exposure is difficult to assess because infection, testing accuracy, and infectiousness vary widely across individuals and over time, creating uncertainty around quarantine decisions.Public health agencies lack quantitative tools to integrate exposure timing, test performance, and symptoms to guide isolation/quarantine duration, especially as variants and testing modalities evolved.Existing guidelines rely on fixed durations or simplified assumptions that do not adequately account for changing pathogen dynamics or diagnostic test characteristics. What was learned from the study? Results demonstrated that negative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests provide strong evidence of no transmission, while negative antigen tests better identify individuals who have already progressed to recovery; thus, antigen tests are more informative for judging infectiousness, whereas PCR is superior for case counting.Findings revealed that test timing and test type influence quarantine decisions more than repeat testing; for example, negative PCR tests strongly reduced inferred infection probability, while antigen tests more reliably indicated progression to recovery and loss of infectiousness—insights that can refine future isolation policies and test‑use strategies.

Introduction

The devastating nature of the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic motivated an intensive debate about guidelines for duration of quarantine [1–8]. Policies changed as the pandemic progressed, reflecting the development of new options for diagnosis, prevention, and treatment, as well as the evolution of the virus itself [9]. A salient issue for policymaking was to consider the impact of quarantine on the workforce of healthcare personnel available to manage the surge in admissions of critically ill patients.

Quarantine refers to the precautionary sequestration of individuals who have been exposed to a communicable disease. The purpose is to prevent further transmission of an infectious agent in case the exposed individual becomes infectious. Quarantine is particularly important when pathogens are high threat and when infected individuals can be infectious while asymptomatic. The recommended timing of release from quarantine is a key component of public health policies.

Generally, it is not feasible to conduct randomized trials or non-experimental intervention studies to estimate the relative effectiveness of alternative policies for quarantine. However, it is increasingly recognized that mathematical models can play a valuable role in informing control. A quantitative approach is useful for identifying parameters that should be incorporated into decision-making and for integrating existing knowledge about the epidemiological characteristics of an infectious disease, such as the distribution of time from infection to infectiousness, for example, the latent period. Prior modeling studies have largely focused on population‑level transmission risk or optimal quarantine duration under assumed infectiousness curves. However, fewer approaches provide individualized, time‑dependent probabilities of latent, infectious, or recovered states conditional on symptoms and diagnostic test results following a specific exposure event.

In this paper, we describe a novel modeling method to support policymaking around termination of quarantine following a point exposure. We parameterized the model to correspond to the original strain of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) as well as the Omicron variant. However, this approach is broadly applicable for other pathogens that are appropriately represented by a Susceptible–Exposed–Infectious–Recovered (SEIR) model and which include an infectious asymptomatic or presymptomatic state.

In the Methods section, we first describe the COVID-19 disease progression model, provide sources from literature, and outline the computations. We then justify our method of computing disease-state probabilities. The implementation and code is available online and a public-facing R Shiny web application (app) is available for the reader to customize and use.

In the Results section, we consider the disease-state probabilities for following an exposure event with a 30% probability of transmission and show the effect of observing the symptomatic state of the individual and of both polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and antigen test results. We consider the added value of a second test on infection risk in the absence of symptoms and following an initial negative test. We also we evaluate the time it takes for the risk of infectiousness of an infected individual to revert to that of a general member of the population. Finally, we compare the value of PCR and antigen tests for case counting and otherwise inferring a transmission event.

Throughout this work we use ‘infected’ to mean that an individual is either in one of the active states of the disease, and hence infectious, or in the latent state and shortly to become infectious. This article is based on previously conducted studies and the development of new quantitative methods and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Methods

Disease Progression Model

Figure 1 gives a schematic of the possible paths we model through a course of infection following an exposure to SARS-CoV-2. With probability p, the exposure results in an infection and the individual enters the latent state and progresses through subsequent phases. With probability 1-p, the individual remains susceptible until they become infected through general background exposure. Note that we distinguish two recovered states depending on whether or not the individual previously experienced symptoms. This is the sole distinction between these states and is introduced only as an artifice to simplify the computations described below. Probability parameters control the path taken, and the time in state variables are all assumed to be gamma distributed.

Model for the course of infection. Letters representing the random variables specifying the time in each state and branching probabilities are shown in blue. The red numbers enumerate the possible paths

For the most part, to model the effects of the original SARS-CoV-2 virus, we chose values for the probability of asymptomatic versus presymptomatic, mild versus severe disease, and the gamma shape and rate parameters to approximately match the values provided in ref. [10]. These findings are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 (see supplementary material), with the lower and upper range values in Supplementary Table 1 corresponding with the lower and upper 2.5% quantiles of the gamma distributions, corresponding roughly to the “likely range” described in ref. [10]. The values for the time-in-susceptible state were arbitrarily chosen so that the background risk of infection is 1 per 1000 per day. During the COVID-19 pandemic, there were reports that the daily risk in the USA was approximately 1 per 10,000 per day, but we use the higher rate to better reflect the risks to higher risk individuals, allow for underreporting of cases, and model more intense stages of the epidemic. Note that the model mentioned previously [10], does not have a presymptomatic phase, so we assumed that the mean duration of the presymptomatic phase was 3 days with a likely range of (1.0, 5.0) days and reduced the times in the symptomatic phases accordingly.

To parameterize the Omicron SARS-CoV-2 variants (estimates reported that the latent period was roughly 60% that of the original strain [11]), symptomatic periods were estimated to be reduced by a factor of 90% [12], and we have assumed that both the presymptomatic and asymptomatic periods are similarly reduced. These changes were made by reducing both the means and standard deviations of the gamma distributions by the appropriate factor (Supplementary Table 3). The probabilities of asymptomatic versus presymptomatic and mild versus severe disease have been left unchanged. Since we are primarily concerned with modeling the short term following exposure, we do not model a transition from the recovered state back to susceptible.

Probabilities of Clinical Observations and Test Results Given Underlying State Probabilities

Observations about the individual following the exposure event will change the state probabilities. To account for these effects, we need only specify the probabilities of such observations for each disease state and then combine these with the above disease progression model using Bayes rule, as described below. Regarding clinical observations, if an individual is observed not to have shown any symptoms up to a particular time, the mild and severe disease states are excluded as is the recovered symptomatic state.

For test result data, we need to account for both qualitative and quantitative differences in PCR and antigen tests, and there is also evidence that the performance of both types of tests depend on viral load which can differ depending on the underlying state. For instance, it is likely that tests are more sensitive for severely symptomatic individuals than mildly symptomatic or asymptomatic individuals. The conventional two-parameter specification of test performance in terms of sensitivity and specificity is, therefore, inadequate to convey the complexity we need. Instead, we require a vector of values giving the probability of a positive test in each of the eight disease states of our model. We label these sS,sL,sA,sP,sM,sV,sRS,sRA, respectively (Supplementary Table 4). Our model does not allow for test sensitivity to vary with time within any state.

Studies consistently show that both test specificities are very high, for instance, a large meta-analysis estimates the specificity of antigen tests to be above 99.5% over a broad range of brands [13]. We therefore set sS, the probability of a true negative testing positive, at 0.5% for both antigen and PCR tests. Similarly, we set sL equal to 0.5%. On the basis of agreement between multiple independent tests on over 3500 subjects, estimates of the sensitivity of PCR tests were found to be greater than 95% with no negative results for confirmed positives until late in the recovery phase [14]. However, other estimates were found to be lower with sensitivity peaking at 83% at 3 days after onset of symptoms or 3 days after an initial positive test for asymptomatic individuals [15], although this was a smaller study. They also found a lower sensitivity of close to 20% before the symptomatic phase; however, this includes not only the presymptomatic phase for symptomatic people but also the latent phase for those who will remain asymptomatic. The dependence of sensitivity on how subjects were referred to in ref. [15] suggests that some of their results are more related to the true status of their subjects rather than on intrinsic test properties. For PCR tests, therefore, we follow the more favorable sensitivity estimates and set sA,sP,sM,sV,sRS, and sRA at 95%. Considerable variation in antigen test sensitivities by brand and phase of infection has been reported, with estimates of antigen sensitivity at just below 50% that of PCR tests [15], and somewhat higher estimates found in ref. [13]. Both studies find substantially higher sensitivity among symptomatic (65–73%) compared with asymptomatic individuals (20–55%), and even higher sensitivity (80%) was observed among individuals with fever [15]. Both studies showed sensitivity decreasing strongly after the first week of infection. While we are not able to accommodate all of these findings in our model for antigen tests, setting sA = 50%, sM = 70%, and sV = 80% is broadly in line. While PCR tests demonstrated lower sensitivity early on [15], this includes both the latent and presymptomatic states in our model, so we set sP = 50%. Moreover, while they show that PCR tests maintain sensitivity into the recovery phase, antigen test sensitivity decreases to near baseline within 1 week for asymptomatic individuals and within about 2 weeks for symptomatic, with or without fever. Hence, we set sRS = sRA = 0.5%.

There was no compelling evidence in literature suggesting different test performance for different SARS-CoV-2 strains. Supplementary Table 4 presents the test parameters that we assume on the basis of the above considerations for PCR and antigen tests for each of the underlying disease states. The relevant effects of these observational models are summarized, using the probability notation we define in the next section, and presented in Supplementary Table 6.

Implementation

We wrote programs to implement the above methods using the R statistical programming environment [16]. These are reasonably straightforward, short, and are distributed in a single R source code file called covid.R, which is available from GitHub at https://github.com/alun-thomas/covidisolation/. Also included in the GitHub repository are documentation, examples, and the code used to produce the tables and figures in this paper, which may be a useful example for those wishing to use or extend the methods described here.

An interactive R Shiny application for calculating the state probabilities following an exposure using these programs is available at https://alunthomas.shinyapps.io/covidisolation/.

The application allows the user to input parameters and change the conditioning events. A screenshot of the application is shown in Fig. 2. Our programs allow specifying arbitrary path splitting probabilities and arbitrary shape and rate parameters for the gamma time in state variables. These calculations require the pcoga() function in the coga R package [17] to calculate the density functions of sums of independent gamma random variables.

A screen shot of the Shiny application for calculating SARS-CoV-2 state probabilities

Our source distribution includes programs to calculate these probabilities by simulating infection histories for individuals over a period following exposure. This was done in the straightforward way shown in Fig. 1 using the standard R functions runif() to generate uniform random variates for the path-splitting events and rgamma() to generate the time-in-state values. We checked that the exact numerical and the simulation methods gave the same results within the sampling error of the simulation approach. The simulations are quick and the results are visually almost indistinguishable from the exact values when using 10,000 simulated individual histories. More simulations may be needed if rejecting histories to condition on specific rare events.

Results

Disease State Probabilities

First, we describe use of the tool to inform decisions regarding duration of quarantine. One approach for deciding when quarantine can end is to set a threshold probability for being infected, at or below which the person would be released from quarantine. In this context, being infected is defined as either being infectious or in the latent state but does not include individuals who reached the recovered, non-infectious state.

For demonstration purposes, we illustrate the situation where exposure carried a 30% probability of transmission, contrasting two different thresholds, 10% and 1%, which may represent different levels of acceptability to exclude infection depending on circumstances (Fig. 3). For comparison purposes, the graphs are constructed to depict how the infection state probabilities change over time following an exposure under four different monitoring policies: (a) neither symptom tracking nor testing; (b) symptom tracking alone; (c) symptom tracking plus antigen testing; (d) symptom tracking plus PCR testing. For the original strain, in the absence of symptom monitoring or testing, the 10% threshold and 1% thresholds were reached at 14.5 and 41.75 days, respectively (Fig. 3). If an individual remained symptom free, the 10% and 1% thresholds were reached at 12.25 and 23 days, respectively. If the individual remained asymptomatic, a negative antigen test on days 10.0 and 19.75 corresponded to the 10% and 1% thresholds, respectively. If the individual remained asymptomatic, a negative PCR test on days 7 and 15 corresponded to the 10% and 1% thresholds, respectively.

The vertical black lines show where the probability of the infected states decreases to 10% (solid) and 1% (dashed)

When the same analysis was performed for the Omicron variant, the length of time to reach the 10% probability threshold was about 3.5 days shorter; the amount of shortening across monitoring and testing scenarios ranged from 2.75 to 3.5 days (Fig. 4). The length of time to reach the 1% threshold was 12 days shorter with no tracking, and between 3.0 and 7.5 days with tracking.

We note that the implication of a later negative test depends on whether it is antigen or PCR. For the antigen test, negativity can be evidence for there having been no initial transmission, or that the individual has passed through the infection to the recovered phase. A negative PCR test, on the other hand, is strong evidence for no transmission.

Follow Up Testing

We now describe use of our methods to assess the impact of performing a second test following a negative initial test. As before, the original exposure was presumed to carry a 30% risk of transmission, however, in this model, individuals were assumed to have already reached the 10% probability threshold based on absence of symptoms and timing of their initial negative test. Simulation was then used to determine the point in time when the probability of being in an infectious or latent state further decreased to 1%. In the scenario of symptom monitoring alone for the original strain, this threshold was not reached until day 24, (Fig. 5). A negative antigen follow up test reduced this time to 19 days, and a PCR follow up to 14 days. These reductions were the same regardless of which test was initially carried out. For the Omicron variant, the pattern was similar with time to reaching 1%, reducing from 16.5 days for a symptom free individual to 14 days with an additional negative antigen test and 8 days with an additional negative PCR test. Again, this pattern was only minimally affected by which test was initially used.

Effect of Testing on Assessing Infectiousness

Here we consider the hypothetical situations where the probability of transmission following an exposure is either zero or one. For each of these boundary conditions, we plot the prob-abilities of being latent or infectious as a function of days following exposure. At the time that these curves cross, the risk posed by individuals who were certainly infected becomes equivalent to the general members of the population, as represented by the individuals who were certainly not infected from exposure. Within the assumptions of our model, the probability of infection among those who were certainly infected from exposure then drops below the background risk of infection for a period after the crossing point. The curves for the cases where we assume an intermediate probability of transmission will be numerical averages of the two extreme cases and, therefore, will share the same crossing points, which is why, for clarity of exposition, we consider only the extremes. Figure 6 shows plots for these probabilities, and how they are affected by observing that an individual shows no symptoms and has a negative SARS-CoV-2 test.

The probability that an individual who was certainly infected at time zero is still infectious at a given time following is shown in black. The blue line shows the probability for an individual who is asymptomatic up to the given time, the red line for an asymptomatic individual who has a negative Antigen test at the given time, the orange line for an asymptomatic individual who has a negative PCR test at the given time. The green line shows the probability of an individual who was certainly not infected at time zero but was subsequently exposed to background risk of infection. Vertical lines in the appropriate color show where the probabilities for certainly infected individuals first fall below those for certainly uninfected

The black lines in Fig. 6a, b show that it takes on average almost 5 weeks, 33.75 days for the original strain, 30 days for Omicron, for an individual to revert to presenting background levels of risk. Monitoring symptoms and testing can, however, detect those who have progressed quickly and, hence, present lower risk sooner, at a much earlier time. Symptom free individuals reach background risk at 22.75 days for the original strain and 17.75 days for Omicron as shown by the blue lines. Note that a negative PCR test does not give much additional information when no symptoms are observed, as shown by the closeness of the blue and orange vertical lines in Fig. 6a. For the Omicron variant, Fig. 6b, the vertical lines showing the crossing points are indistinguishable.

Conversely, a negative antigen test moves the crossing point 1.75 days earlier for Delta, and 1 days earlier for Omicron as shown by the red lines. This is because a negative PCR test can select for individuals with an unusually long latent period, while a negative antigen test detects those individuals who have progressed quickly through the whole course of the infection. Thus, for purposes of assessing the risk an infected individual poses to others, an antigen test is somewhat more informative.

Inferring Transmission Events

We now consider the value of testing for determining whether transmission resulted from the exposure. Curves for a range of prior probabilities were plotted, the prior being represented by the y-axis values at time 0. Bayesian analysis was used to calculate posterior probabilities as a function of time since exposure, comparing similar monitoring strategies as before (Fig. 7). We again focused on negative evidence because the tests are highly specific and the consequences of positive results are clear. Under the assumption that the antigen test is negative during the recovered state, the antigen test adds comparatively little information compared with symptom monitoring alone, for the objective of inferring transmission (Fig. 7c, d versus Fig. 7a, b). In contrast, the negative predictive value of the PCR test is much higher, as denoted by the substantial reduction in probability of transmission when the PCR test is negative (Fig. 7e, f). Thus, for case counting purposes, the PCR test is better. Performing the PCR test at least 10 days following exposure largely avoids the risk of testing during the latent period.

Discussion

We developed a Bayesian tool to estimate the impact of information regarding symptoms and test results on the probability of being in a particular state of infection at a given time after SARS-CoV-2 exposure. Coupled with an assumed probability of transmission, the tool generates an estimate of one minus the negative predictive value of symptoms being absent, alone or in combination with a negative test. Our approach accounts for the different reasons that a test may be negative following exposure: (i) transmission did not occur; (ii) the individual is in the recovered state and no longer infectious; (iii) the individual’s infection is still in the latent state; and (iv) the individual is infectious, but the test result is a false negative. In estimating the negative predictive value, we consider the first two situations to be true negatives and the second two situations to be false negatives.

[6–8]. A large body of SARS‑CoV‑2 modeling literature has focused on optimizing quarantine duration and testing strategies at the population level, typically by estimating reductions in onward transmission under alternative policy scenarios [6–8, 18, 19]. These models are well suited to inform broad public health guidance but often rely on aggregated infectiousness profiles and fixed test characteristics. Fewer approaches explicitly address individual‑level post‑exposure inference by estimating the probability that a specific exposed individual occupies a particular disease state at a given time, conditional on symptoms and test results. The present work is intended to complement population‑level models by addressing this narrower but operationally important decision context, which arises in settings such as occupational exposures, healthcare staffing decisions, and return‑to‑work or release‑from‑quarantine determinations following a known exposure event.

The simplicity of our tool enhances the ease of modifying parameters to accommodate different viral variants, new pathogens, alternative diagnostic tests, and the impacts of various interventions on disease. Our approach facilitates selecting a time for testing to achieve an acceptable probability that a person released from quarantine is infectious or latent. An important insight derived from our results is that performing repeat testing does not shorten the number of days to reach a given level of risk.

Our findings are generally consistent with epidemiological intuition. For example, we show a significant benefit of antigen tests over PCR tests for detecting resolved cases in the extreme situation where transmission definitely occurred. For the Omicron variant, a negative antigen test at 12 days indicates that there is a less than 10% chance that a symptom-free individual is latent or infectious, with the individual most likely having progressed to the recovered state. On the contrary, because the PCR test remains positive for the first portion of the recovered state, it has low utility for deciding on the duration of quarantine when the exposure certainly or almost certainly resulted in transmission. With a negative PCR test at 12 days, the probability of latent or infectious was still about 25%, which is the same level as an untested individual whether symptom free or not. Even though these qualitative differences might be anticipated, the quantitative results given here provide valuable insights that may be applied to creating public health or individualized policies for quarantine or isolation.

In summary, by differentiating disease states and calculating their probabilities, it is possible to distinguish in a quantifiable way why a test result might be negative, namely, because there was no transmission, because the test is a false negative, because the individual has passed through the course of infection, or because they are still in the latent period. Since each of these reasons has different epidemiological and clinical implications, accurately assessing their likelihood is crucial. Importantly, by altering the input parameters, the tool can be extended to apply to other existing or future emerging communicable diseases that progress from latent to infectious to recovered states.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our results ultimately depend on assumed disease‑progression parameters and test‑performance estimates drawn from literature. Although these assumptions are clear and modifiable, uncertainty remains regarding their applicability across populations, testing platforms, vaccination statuses, and emerging variants. Second, test sensitivity was assumed to be constant within each disease state and did not vary continuously with viral load or exact timing since infection, which may oversimplify real‑world testing dynamics. Third, our framework focuses on individual‑level infection state probabilities following a single exposure and does not explicitly model onward transmission, repeated exposures, or population‑level effects. Finally, while the findings are consistent with epidemiological intuition and prior modeling work, the approach has not yet been prospectively validated using longitudinal cohorts with known exposure histories.

Conclusions

We introduce a Bayesian framework for estimating time‑dependent probabilities of distinct infection states following SARS‑CoV‑2 exposure, conditional on symptoms and diagnostic test results. By explicitly distinguishing among the multiple reasons a test may be negative, including absence of transmission, false-negative testing, recovery, or persistence in the latent state, this approach provides a more accurate and interpretable assessment of post‑exposure risk than test results alone. Our results show that test timing and modality have a greater impact on residual risk than repeat testing and that antigen and PCR tests differ substantially in their usefulness for guiding quarantine duration. These quantitative insights support more informed and flexible quarantine policies while minimizing unnecessary restriction. Because the framework is simple, transparent, and easily reparameterized, it can be adapted to new variants, diagnostic technologies, and other infectious diseases characterized by latent, infectious, and recovered states, supporting both individualized decision‑making and public health policy.
