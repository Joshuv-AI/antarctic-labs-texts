High-fidelity hierarchical modeling of lithium-ion batteries: a cross-scale electrochemical-mechanical framework
Authors: Xiaoyu Li, Shen Zhao, Chiton Gwan, Zhenpo Wang, Shuqiang Jiao, Yanli Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractsMechanical stress during the cycling process notably impacts the performance of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), making it crucial to accurately monitor stress generation and propagation during battery operation. Traditional electrochemical-mechanical models are limited to the particle and electrode scales, and their parameter identification relies solely on voltage. Here, a multi-scale electrochemical-mechanical-thermal modelling framework with non-destructive parameter identification capabilities is proposed. This numerical model couples electrochemical reactions with thermal effects and links particle-scale strain to electrode-scale displacement. Diffusion-induced stress (DIS) is selected as a key indicator, combined with voltage, to analyze the sensitivity of 23 parameters. A voltage-strain multi-objective parameter identification strategy based on the Pareto front is employed to determine the key parameters. The framework demonstrates high fidelity, with the mean absolute percentage error for voltage and strain predictions below 1% and 3.6%, respectively. This work enables high-fidelity simulation of multi-physics behavior, provides an effective method for calibrating key parameters, and holds potential for establishing a reliable digital twin of LIBs.

Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have become the most crucial energy storage device in the current industry with wide applications from small portable devices to large-scale transportation and energy storage systems, mainly due to their high energy density, long cycle life and minimal self-discharge1–3. However, LIBs experience capacity decline during service due to degradation of the active material inside the battery and the occurrence of undesired side reactions. Among these factors, the phenomenon of contraction and expansion of the active particles of LIBs during charging and discharging, inducing multiscale mechanical deformations and stresses, is a major culprit in the capacity degradation and deterioration of the cycling performance of LIBs4–6.

To this end, the understanding and modeling internal mechanical behavior of LIBs are essential and have been extensively studied. The effect of mechanical stress on the voltage hysteresis of LIBs during charging-discharging cycling has been demonstrated early on7. Diffusion-induced stresses (DIS), surface effects, and stresses from inter-particle compression all impede lithium embedding, resulting in energy dissipation and stress-dependent voltage hysteresis, thereby also affecting the electrochemical impedance of the battery8. Moreover, internal stress is an important contributor to capacity degradation. This is mainly manifested in two aspects. On one hand, during the charging and discharging process, the anode particles undergo significant volume changes due to the insertion and extraction of lithium ions (Li+). The solid electrolyte interface (SEI) formation breaks as the electrode particles expand, and the side reaction occurs again in the cracks, resulting in a continuous thickening of the SEI layer, reflecting as a continuous impedance increase and a capacity decrease9,10. On the other hand, Li+ insertion and extraction in the active particles form a lithium concentration gradient leading to DIS11,12, further contributing to the accumulation of elastic deformations in the anisotropic sphere called elastic strain energy density, providing a considerable driving force for the disruption of the active particles and the loss of active material13. Quantitative mapping between the electrochemical aging and mechanical stress is hard to be determined experimentally, which is further constrained by destructive testing methods and limitations in temporal resolution and spatial coverage, thus deviating from real-world application requirements14,15. Therefore, developing an electrochemical-mechanical coupling model is essential to accurately simulate the real behavior of the battery.

Three kinds of models, namely electrochemical models (EM), equivalent circuit models (ECM) and fractional order models (FOM), are popular in battery simulation. ECM, as empirical models, employ resistor-capacitor-inductor networks to approximate electrochemical reactions16. FOM is a mathematical model based on fractional calculus theory, utilizing fractional derivatives to construct the kinetic characteristics of batteries. Compared to integer-order models, it offers superior accuracy in fitting nonlinear voltage responses and state-of-charge changes17,18. Similarly, FOM is also empirical, and its model parameters lack physical interpretability. The pseudo two-dimensional (P2D) model is one of the classical EM19. The high simulation accuracy, physical interpretability, and ability to seamlessly couple with other physical processes make it an ideal tool for studying electrochemical-mechanical behavior during lithium-ion battery operation, explaining its widespread application20,21. At the particle scale, 3-D electrochemical-mechanical models have been established to bridge the stress-strain relationship with lithium diffusion and transport processes22. Based on experimental data obtained from X-ray diffraction and swelling testers, a relationship between particle swelling and electrode thickness is established, laying the foundation for multi-scale mechanics23. Subsequent work further developed particle–electrode dual-scale mechanical models to analyze electrode mechanical mechanisms24 and safety application boundaries25. The mechanical framework was later extended to the full cell scale26,27, with thermal effects incorporated at this level to obtain one-dimensional temperature distributions28. Further refinement introduced porous electrode plasticity into a multi-scale one-dimensional thermal–mechanical model, enabling the calculation of cumulative electrode strain29. However, the internal electrochemical-mechanical parameters for these models are often derived from empirical values or simplified assumptions, potentially limiting their accuracy and predictive capability across diverse operating conditions.

EM exhibits inherent nonlinearity. The P2D model and its derivatives contain many parameters with explicit physical meaning; obtaining the accurate values of these parameters is critical to ensure the accuracy of the model30. Therefore, a large number of studies have been carried out to identify the parameters for numerical simulations and experiments. Classified by the source of algorithmic inspiration, these parameter identification methods can be broadly classified into Mathematics-inspired, Evolutionary-inspired, Biology-inspired, Swarm-inspired and Human-inspired, etc. Building on this taxonomy, numerous algorithms have been developed for battery parameter identification. Mathematics-inspired and evolutionary-inspired approaches such as the genetic algorithm (GA) and ensemble Kalman filter have been used to identify 14 highly sensitive P2D parameters, achieving a voltage prediction RMSE below 14 mV31. The whale optimization algorithm (WOA), a swarm-inspired method, attained terminal voltage errors under 15.45 mV and SOC estimation errors below 1.21% when identifying 14 electrochemical parameters32. Other swarm-inspired algorithms including multi-objective particle swarm and grey wolf optimizers have accurately identified all 23 parameters of P2D models33. In a comprehensive benchmark study, Li et al.34 extensively counted and compared 78 common meta-heuristic algorithms for electrochemical parameter identification, and comprehensively evaluated the accuracy, convergence speed, and robustness of these algorithms, among which the teaching-learning-based optimization algorithm (TLBO), Multi-objective genetic algorithm (MultiGA), Bird Swarm Algorithm (BSA), Bald Eagle Search Algorithm (BES), Search and Rescue Optimization Algorithm and Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) algorithms perform best in terms of accuracy. It is worth noting that the GA, as one of the most classical optimization algorithms, still guarantees high accuracy in most application scenarios. In recent research, GA has been used in conjunction with machine learning to optimize the initial population and prevent it from trapping into local optima, with the ultimate goal of improving the accuracy of GA or speeding up the optimization process35,36.

However, existing parameter identification frameworks for EM of LIBs primarily focus on electrochemical parameters, neglecting mechanical counterparts. Here, we propose a novel high-fidelity multiscale framework that integrates electrochemical-mechanical coupling model, sensitivity analysis and multi-objective optimization. Building upon the P2D model, the framework establishes quantitative linkages from particle-scale expansion to electrode-scale strain, and ultimately to cell-scale macroscopic displacement through multi-physics governing equations. This enables systematic characterization of DIS and its role in driving elastic strain energy density accumulation within active particles, thereby resolving mechanical-electrochemical coupling mechanisms across scales. Departing from conventional voltage-centric sensitivity analysis, hydrostatic stress is innovatively adopted as a key metric to evaluate parameter sensitivity, revealing critical mechanical parameters-including anode particle radius, Young’s modulus and electrode porosity-that dominate stress distribution and mechanical degradation. In parallel, a non-destructive multi-objective parameter identification strategy is developed by incorporating macroscopic strain measurements as an independent optimization target alongside terminal voltage. Leveraging Pareto front theory, four heuristic algorithms are comparatively evaluated. For multi-objective, MultiGA shows the fastest convergence speed and sufficiently high accuracy, with the fitness function converging to 0.0087, while the other three optimization algorithms converge to 0.0125, 0.0093, and 0.0086, respectively. The framework achieves mean absolute percentage errors (MAPE) below 1.0% for voltage and 2.9% for 1 C charging strain predictions, considerably enhancing the predictability of stress-induced capacity fade. Our method not only overcomes the limitations of destructive testing but also provides a validated tool for real-time battery management and electrode material design by correlating stress gradients with localized electrochemical activity. Results and discussion

Experimental platform and results

In this study, commercial small pouch LIBs are used for the test, with graphite anodes and LiNi0.5Co0.2Mn0.3O2 (NCM523) cathodes. The capacity of the battery is 4 Ah, the nominal voltage is 3.7 V, and the dimensions are 79 mm × 40 mm × 10 mm. The experiments were conducted under three distinct operating conditions: 1 C charging, 0.05 C discharging, and the Urban Dynamometer Driving Schedule (UDDS). These conditions were selected to isolate kinetic-dominated and diffusion-dominated behaviors, as well as to simulate real-world stochastic driving patterns, ensuring robust parameter identifiability and enhancing the generalizability of the cross-scale model. Experimental equipment can be found in the Supplementary Information. (Supplementary Fig. 9).

The experimental setup included a high-precision battery cycler manufactured by Neware (model CT-8002), a voltage sensor for real-time voltage monitoring, and a resistance strain gauge made of Constantan with a resistance of 120 Ω. The strain gauge, featuring a micron-sized foil structure, was placed at the center of the battery surface to measure surface strain caused by volume changes during cycling. A fixture was used to clamp the battery horizontally, applying an initial external pressure of 100 N to eliminate residual stresses and ensure accurate stress-strain transfer.

The strain gauge’s design, characterized by low mass-volume and non-invasive embedding, minimized additional load on the battery, making it suitable for miniaturized and flexible structures like LIBs electrodes. Its high linearity, wide frequency response, and advanced temperature compensation algorithms enabled precise measurement of micro-strain deformations, effectively suppressing ambient noise. This capability allowed for in situ strain monitoring at the electrode-electrolyte interface and internal stress field reconstruction under packaging constraints, making it an ideal tool for studying mechanical-electrochemical coupling behavior.

Multiscale electrochemical-mechanical coupling behavior

The established electrochemical-mechanical coupling model offers a comprehensive understanding of the multi-physical behaviors of LIBs, greatly enhancing the accuracy of performance prediction. This mechanism-based framework not only captures the external characteristics of the battery, such as voltage profiles, but resolves internal phenomena, including concentration gradients and polarization effects. Crucially, it quantifies the volumetric strain induced by lithium concentration gradients and the resulting stress distribution, enabling a deeper insight into stress-driven degradation mechanisms.

The current input from the pole lugs is transferred to the surface of the electrode active particles through the collector, triggering an electrochemical reaction at the electrode/electrolyte interface. Li+ is subsequently inserted/extracted within the particles via solid-phase diffusion, thereby establishing a concentration gradient between the particle surface and core.

Insertion/extraction of solute atoms changes the lattice constant. Insertion of Li+ in graphite leads to expansion of the interlayer spacing. The diffusion of Li+ is driven by a concentration gradient, resulting in local volume changes in the material. The lithium concentration on the surface of the electrode material may be higher than in the interior, causing the surface to expand more and the interior to expand less, this uneven expansion creates stresses within the particle. The radial DIS reaches its maximum compressive stress at the center of the particle during early charging, driven by the steep Li+ concentration gradient forming near the surface while the center remains less lithiated. This central compressive stress increases rapidly to a peak value of 51 MPa as charging progresses. Subsequently, as Li+ diffuses inward, reducing the concentration gradient, the central compressive stress begins to diminish. Near the end of the constant current charging phase, the central radial stress slowly rises and then begins to show a decreasing trend as lithiation progresses towards homogeneity. The tangential DIS distribution evolves significantly during charging. Initially, high compressive stress develops at the center, reaching about 50 MPa, while simultaneously, constrained surface expansion generates high tensile stress at the particle periphery. As charging continues, the radial position where tangential stress is zero gradually migrates inward toward the center. Concurrently, the magnitude of the surface tensile stress decreases from its peak. If the charging continues, as lithium concentration becomes more uniform, the surface tensile stress further decreases and the overall tangential stress distribution becomes more homogeneous. This mechanism of DIS, governed by evolving lithium concentration gradients and associated deformation constraints, is further validated under discharge conditions. During both 0.05 C and UDDS discharge tests achieving nearly full discharge, the absolute values of radial and tangential DIS consistently exhibited an initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease. While the specific stress states are reversed during delithiation compared to charging, the pattern of stress evolution, with an initial rise driven by developing concentration gradients and surface deformation constraints, followed by relaxation as gradients diminish. The local current density is regulated by overpotential. The nature of overpotential is the extra energy required to overcome the reaction energy barriers. In the charging and discharging process, overpotentials are inevitably generated due to the kinetic limitations of the electrochemical reaction, the resistance of the material and the electrolyte and the concentration gradient during the migration of Li+. In the macroscopic electrochemical characterization, overpotentials are modulated by the solid/liquid phase potential difference and the open circuit voltage, as well as affected by hydrostatic stresses. The overpotentials and the relationship with the local current density during 1 C charging are shown in Fig. 3b, c. This plot visually demonstrates the response characteristics of local current density to overpotential variation, providing profound insights into the inherent kinetics of charge transfer reactions at the electrode/electrolyte interface and their underlying limiting mechanisms. Notably, during constant-current charging, the stable Tafel slope observed in the linear regime reflects consistent charge-transfer kinetics. The electrolyte current density, determined by ionic conductivity and concentration gradients, ensures charge conservation by matching the local current density at the interface. The distribution of electrolyte current density on the electrode is shown in Fig. 3d.

The spatial heterogeneity of the local current density during charging leads to inhomogeneous lithium intercalation, which generates large concentration gradients within the active particles. These gradients induce chemical strains, which in turn lead to the accumulation of surface stresses. The current density peaks near the separator-electrode interface due to the increased ion transport efficiency. This localized electrochemical activity interacts with elevated V-M stresses in the same region. The mechanical behavior of materials is more complicated to analyze directly employing the stress tensor, especially under multi-axial stress states. Von Mises (V-M) stress, as a scalarized equivalent stress, is able to comprehensively reflect the deformation energy of materials under complex stress states, highlighting critical regions prone to failure such as particle surfaces and electrode interfaces.

Figure 3e demonstrates the V-M stress distribution on particle surfaces across the negative electrode. Throughout the initial 1770 s of charging, V-M stress exhibited an increasing trend. The stress near the separator side rose markedly faster than at the current collector side, reaching a peak value of 53 MPa. This accelerated stress accumulation stems from enhanced ion transport efficiency near the separator, which elevates local current density, accelerates Li+ intercalation, and generates steeper concentration gradients that induce severe chemical strain. After 1770 s, as active particles approached Li+ saturation near the separator and concentration gradients relaxed, V-M stress decreased abruptly. In contrast, stress at the collector side continued rising to 33 MPa due to delayed reaction kinetics and mechanical constraint from the current collector. Charging ended at this point; extended charging would likely drive the collector-side stress to peak before declining, ultimately stabilizing at a low plateau upon homogenization of concentration gradients. Figure 3f illustrates the V-M stress distribution within a particle. Stress remained zero at the core but increased rapidly near the edge where lithium intercalation initiates, creating maximal concentration gradients and heterogeneous expansion. As charging progressed, Li+ diffusion toward the particle core dynamically redistributed strain, elevating overall stress. Beyond 1950s, the inward migration of the concentration gradient peak reduced the maximum stress growth rate, culminating in a declining trend. The purely elastic model can accurately simulate low-rate charge-discharge tests over relatively short durations. However, during long-term cycling or high-rate tests, the electrodes undergo marked plastic deformation, which is a key focus of our subsequent efforts. Here, a relatively homogeneous equivalent yield stress is introduced to represent the overall plasticity of the electrode, and this equivalent yield stress value is obtained through multi-objective parameter identification, highlighting one of the advantages of parameter identification. Under both 1 C and 0.5 C charging conditions, equivalent plastic strain first appears when the half-cell SOC is around 0.55 (Fig. 4a, b). The elastoplastic-viscous model predicts 3.9% and 3.5% larger electrode swelling than the purely elastic model under 1 C and 0.5 C conditions, respectively, attributable to lithiation-induced stress exceeding the yield limit causes irreversible plastic deformation in graphite. Additionally, viscoelastic effects promote plastic flow through stress relaxation, and the two effects synergistically exacerbate strain accumulation.

During the charging and discharging process of LIBs, the insertion/extraction behavior of Li+ triggers the entropy effect through the change of lattice structure of the active material, which generates reversible heat, with its exothermic or absorptive properties determined by the direction of the reaction. Meanwhile, the current flow through the electrode and electrolyte is subject to the effect of ohmic impedance and interfacial kinetic resistance, and irreversible heat is formed through the dissipation of joule heat and polarization energy. The reversible heat is directly related to the intrinsic thermodynamic properties of the material, while the irreversible heat originates from the multiple loss mechanisms in the energy transfer process, and the synergistic effect of the two together constitutes the basis of the thermal behavior of the battery during operation. Sensitivity analysis results

Selected 20 conventional electrochemical parameters cover all electrochemical processes of the P2D model. Each physical parameter individually affects the model voltage output, i.e., the terminal voltage at the designed current input, which indicates the sensitivity of the parameter. However, these parameters are not necessarily sensitive to the mechanical behavior during charging and discharging, and more parameters are introduced when considering the mechanical behavior, ignoring material damage and plasticity for short-term operating conditions, the main mechanical parameters include Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio and partial molar volume of the particles. In this way the parameters of the whole electrochemical-mechanical process are considered comprehensively. The sensitivity analysis radar diagrams of 23 parameters are shown in Fig. 6. When terminal voltage serves as the sensitivity metric, the volume fraction and porosity of the composite electrode exhibit the highest dominance under 1 C charging and UDDS conditions, followed by the geometric dimensions of the positive and negative electrodes. In contrast, the 0.05 C discharging condition prioritizes the negative electrode particle radius as the decisive parameter, with the negative electrode diffusion coefficient ranking second in sensitivity. The parameter dominance hierarchy becomes more pronounced when hydrostatic stress functions as the evaluation indicator. The anode particle radius and Young’s modulus play a decisive role in the magnitude of stress generation. The highest normalized sensitivity coefficients of the anode particles are found in the 1 C charging and 0.05 C discharging conditions. For UDDS conditions, Young’s modulus demonstrates the highest normalized sensitivity coefficient, followed by the negative electrode particle radius, with a value of 0.482. Notably, the negative electrode diffusion coefficient retains its dominance in the 0.05 C discharging scenario, underscoring its pivotal role in low-rate diffusion-governed regimes.

Additionally, the equivalent yield stress of the electrode is also identified. Since yield stress belongs to electrode-scale mechanics, while DIS reflects particle-scale mechanics, yield stress fails to be considered in sensitivity analysis. In subsequent studies, an indicator sensitive to both multi-scale electrochemical and mechanical behavior will be explored.

Parameter identification results

Since the mechanical parameters are few and sensitive to the results of 1 C charging, accurate parametric results can be recognized from a single condition only. MultiGA explores extensively in the solution space through crossover and mutation operations, with a high initial population diversity that enables rapid discovery of multiple potential non-dominated solutions, using non-dominated ordering and crowding distances to preserve the diversity of the Pareto front and avoid premature convergence. In the early iterations, the set of non-dominated solutions expands rapidly and the fitness decreases markedly. Thus has the fastest convergence rate. the teacher phase of TLBO may repeatedly pull the population towards the local frontiers of different objectives, while the stochastic interactions in the student phase cannot effectively coordinate the multi-objective trade-offs, leading to fluctuations in fitness. the mean of the CEM may be biased towards the optimal region of Objective 1 in one iteration, and then be shifted in the next iteration due to the predominance of elite samples in Objective 2, leading to fitness oscillations. In complex multi-peak problems, WOA can adaptively adjust the search strategy to avoid falling into the local optimum prematurely, while guaranteeing high-precision convergence at a later stage, and WOA has the fastest computational efficiency. Therefore, WOA is used for parameter identification for the 0.05 C and UDDS conditions.

Methods

Model geometry and framework

As an effective electrochemical simulation tool, the P2D model is widely used to analyze the performance of electrode materials and the overall battery behavior. The P2D model can effectively describe Li+ intercalation and diffusion behavior inside the electrode, as well as the electrochemical reaction mechanisms. It focuses particularly on the interactions between the particle intercalation and the porous electrodes, which is crucial for understanding the battery charging and discharging process and optimizing its performances. Moreover, the equation structure of the P2D model can tightly couple the electrochemical, thermal and mechanical mechanisms through explicit source terms and intrinsic relationships, while retaining the independence, which facilitates numerical solution and analysis of physical mechanisms. All governing equations in this study are implemented in finite element simulations to determine the electrochemical and mechanical response of the battery. The mathematical equations are solved numerically using COMSOL 6.1. The model geometry and the multi-scale framework are shown schematically in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Particle to cell scale equations

The multi-scale electrochemical-mechanical coupling process of LIBs is a complex multi-physics problem involving electrochemical reactions, Li+ transport, electron conduction and stress-strain distribution. This process spans from microscopic particles to macroscopic electrodes and even the full cell, exhibiting markedly multi-scale characteristics and strong coupling effects. The electrochemical process primarily includes the migration and diffusion of Li+ in the electrolyte, the diffusion of Li+ in solid active particles, charge conduction and the electrochemical reactions occurring on the surface of particles. Furthermore, changes in lithium-ion concentration induce volumetric expansion and contraction of particles, leading to complex stress-strain fields, which in turn influence the electrochemical reaction kinetics and lithium diffusion processes.

The fidelity of simulation results is critically dependent on the granularity of the model. Referring to previous excellent electrochemical-mechanical related studies27,37–39, the multi-scale coupled physical model is developed. The coupled model integrates Li+ diffusion, electrochemical kinetics and stress-strain interactions across particle-to-cell scales. By bridging microscopic particle deformation with macroscopic electrode displacement, the framework resolves bidirectional stress-electrochemical coupling mechanisms. Key governing equations encompass Li+ diffusion in solid particles, stress-strain constitutive relationships and electrode-scale mechanical balance, enabling quantitative analysis of DIS and its role in capacity fade. Detailed derivations are provided in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Equation 1). This hierarchical approach ensures high-fidelity predictions of voltage-strain behavior under dynamic operating conditions.

Sensitivity analysis of electrochemical-mechanical coupling model

The P2D model of LIBs contains a large number of electrochemical, mechanical, geometric parameters with practical significance. These parameters exhibit distinct sensitivities to output responses (e.g., terminal voltage and diffusion-induced stress, i.e., DIS) under varying operating conditions, while most parameters exert negligible effects on specific outputs. To identify highly sensitive parameters across conditions and enhance identification efficiency, a local sensitivity analysis is conducted for 23 parameters prior to model parameter optimization.

Given the coupled electrochemical-mechanical interactions and the complexity introduced by multidimensional parameters, local sensitivity analysis is selected due to its suitability for high-dimensional parameter spaces and computationally intensive models. This approach enables rapid screening of parameters with high impact. Structural parameters, including anode and cathode electrode thickness, separator thickness, radius of active particles, electrode porosity, which directly affect the Li+ transport path and effective reaction area. Transmission parameters include two components: one is the liquid-phase electrolyte diffusion coefficient, electrolyte conductivity, Li+ transfer number, and the other is the solid-phase particle diffusion coefficient, electronic conductivity, etc., dominating the Li+ mass transfer process in the liquid and solid phases. Kinetic parameters include reaction rate constant, electric double-layer capacitance and charge transfer coefficient, which control the electrochemical reaction rate at the electrode-electrolyte interface. The mechanical parameters of material properties contain three aspects: the first is the density, Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio of the active particles, the second is the Young’s modulus, density and Poisson’s ratio of the composite electrode as a whole, the third is the Li+ embedding induced volumetric strain function and the partial molar volume. The specific parameters of batteries11,46,47

The terminal voltage, as an external characteristic of the battery, can be measured directly, mainly reflecting thermodynamic equilibrium and kinetic polarization. However, the response to mechanical degradation is lagging or insensitive since stress concentration may occur earlier than voltage decay. Moreover, in the electrochemical-mechanical coupling model, the mechanical response is highly nonlinearly coupled with the electrochemical process. Traditional voltage metrics cannot directly reflect the sensitivity of mechanical parameters. Therefore, a cross-scale and cross-physics metric that can reflect the mechanical characteristics is attempted to be identified.

Hydrostatic stress as a direct mechanical characterization of volume change that quantifies the hydrostatic pressure (isotropic stress component) to which the material is subjected. It is highly correlated with both the concentration gradient of the electrochemical field and the material expansion/contraction of the mechanical field. By analyzing its sensitivity to parameters, it is possible to identify key parameters that are prone to stress concentration or plastic deformation, thus providing new perspectives for the subsequent prediction of mechanical failure risk and optimization of electrode material selection or structural design.

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the simulation results, a mesh independence verification is rigorously conducted. This verification confirms that the results for both the global cell voltage and the local current density at the electrode scale are independent of the mesh size, thereby establishing confidence in the predictive capability of the model prior to the formal performance investigation. The comparison results between coarse and fine meshes can be found in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Coupled model parameter identification

Charging-discharging tests were performed to measure the strain induced in the battery due to expansion. The simulation model developed is desired to accurately predict the voltage while fitting the stress-strain during charging or discharging of the battery. The inherent conflict between voltage and strain as competing objectives creates a trade-off dilemma, where simultaneous optimization of both parameters is elusive. To resolve this multi-objective challenge, a Pareto improvement strategy is adopted, which seeks incremental adjustments to approach optimality without sacrificing either objective. Within this framework, a solution set A is defined to dominate another set B if A is non-inferior in all objectives and strictly superior in at least one, thereby establishing a hierarchy of Pareto-optimal solutions. The set of solutions that are not dominated by any other solution constitutes a Pareto frontier that characterizes the optimal situation after a trade-off between objectives.
