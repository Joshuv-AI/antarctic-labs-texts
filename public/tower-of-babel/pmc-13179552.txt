Crisis Stabilization Unit Use for Behavioral Health Visits Among Medicaid Enrollees
Authors: K. John McConnell, Ching-Wen Wendy Yang, Thomas H. A. Meath, Amy Edmonds, Benjamin F. Miller, Margaret E. Balfour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study compares primary diagnoses among Medicaid enrollees treated in crisis stabilization units and emergency departments for behavioral health–related visits in 2022 across 4 states.

Introduction

Crisis stabilization units (CSUs) are a key element of the behavioral health crisis care continuum,1 particularly within Medicaid, which covers many US individuals with serious behavioral health conditions. Although CSUs are intended as alternatives to emergency departments (EDs),2 limited evidence compares the populations they serve—a necessary step toward assessing their potential to ease ED burden. This study compares primary diagnoses among Medicaid enrollees treated in CSUs and EDs for behavioral health–related visits across 4 US states in 2022.

Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study used 2022 Medicaid claims from the Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System Analytic Files (TAF).3 Data were analyzed from April 1, 2025, to January 31, 2026. We limited the sample to 4 states (Arizona, Massachusetts, Oklahoma, Washington) where CSUs were meaningfully implemented (≥0.2% of enrollees using CSUs). The study population included Medicaid enrollees aged 11 to 64 years, excluding participants with dual eligibility and records with missing, conflicting, or low-quality demographics. CSU visits were identified from per diem claims (Common Procedural Terminology code S9485); behavioral-health ED visits were defined by primary diagnoses mapped to Clinical Classifications Software Refined codes MBD001 to MBD034. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline and was approved by the Oregon Health & Science University Institutional Review Board with consent waived because seeking informed consent from all patients was not feasible and the risk to participants was minimal. Additional methods details appear in eMethods in Supplement 1.

Results

In 144 193 CSU and ED users with 347 920 visits, the proportion of acute behavioral-health visits managed in CSUs vs EDs ranged from 55.9% (60 371 of 108 048) in Arizona to 13.2% (3811 of 28 810) in Oklahoma (Table). High use of services (≥5 visits) was generally more common in CSUs than EDs (for example, 14.0% [2899 of 20 645] for CSU and 3.6% [1093 of 30 651] for ED in Arizona and 28.0% [806 of 2883] for CSU and 4.8% [1663 of 34 674] for ED in Washington). Schizophrenia and related disorders were the most common primary diagnosis for CSU visits in 3 states (27.7% [16 700 of 60 371] in Arizona, 30.5% [1161 of 3811] in Oklahoma, and 27.8% [3472 of 12 506] in Washington) and the second most common in the fourth state (18.5% [11 136 of 60 078] in Massachusetts). In contrast, suicide-related diagnoses were rare in CSUs (from 0.02% [13 of 60 078] in Massachusetts to 0.8% [106 of 12 506] in Washington) but far more prevalent in EDs (from 17.1% [13 525 of 79 313] for Massachusetts to 30.4% [7601 of 24 999] for Oklahoma). Substance use disorders represented a major share of visits in both settings, with alcohol-related disorders most common (for example, 14.0% [8452 of 60 371] for CSU and 34.7% [16 560 of 47 677] for ED in Arizona).

Discussion

Across 4 states, CSUs and EDs served overlapping sets of Medicaid members experiencing behavioral health crises. Patterns based on use of diagnostic codes indicate that CSUs function less as suicide-focused crisis centers and more as core stabilization sites for people with serious mental illness. Although EDs remain the dominant setting for behavioral-health crises, CSUs play a larger and a more qualitatively distinct role than often recognized. Key limitations of this study include potential differences in data quality or coding across states and services, as well as a lack of consistent financing and billing policies.

Four findings stand out. First, repeat use is more common in CSUs than EDs, suggesting that CSUs often serve patients with chronic psychiatric needs requiring periodic stabilization. Second, schizophrenia and related disorders are among the most common CSU diagnoses, indicating that CSUs frequently stabilize individuals with psychosis and persistent mental illness. Third, suicide-related diagnoses are rare in CSUs but common in EDs, reflecting medical evaluation needs in the postattempt period and differences in diagnostic coding. CSUs often use psychiatric diagnoses rather than problem-oriented codes; one study identified suicidality in 14% of CSU visits using clinical documentation rather than diagnosis codes.2 Fourth, substance use disorders feature prominently in both settings, underscoring the complex co-occurring needs crisis services are absorbing.

Taken together, CSUs appear to function as frontline stabilization resources for people with serious mental illness, substance use disorders, and frequent users of the system. Yet crisis-service financing remains fragmented: only 31 state Medicaid programs cover crisis stabilization facilities,4 limiting the ability of CSUs to serve as reliable alternatives to ED boarding. Consistent with this, Medicaid billing for CSU services in this study was concentrated in a small number of states, limiting generalizability. CSUs also vary widely in admission criteria, staffing, and capacity to accept patient intake from first responders,5 although recent federal guidelines provide a framework for defining distinct levels of care.1 Strengthening the crisis continuum will require consistent and flexible financing,4 better alignment of services with clinical needs, and improved aftercare for individuals at high risk, particularly those experiencing psychosis. Without these changes, crisis systems will continue absorbing major behavioral health gaps without adequate support.
