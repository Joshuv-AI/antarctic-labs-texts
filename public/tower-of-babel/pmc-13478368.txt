Effectiveness of the SAGIP mental health mobile application for the academic community: a longitudinal study
Authors: John Robert C. Rilveria, Lorelie C. Grepo, Paola Marie Q. Lim, Joy Ann N. Malapit, Belinda Esther D. Tan, Benette P. Custodio, Stephanie Caridad D. Landicho, Iris Thiele C. Isip-Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mental health mobile applications are increasingly used to address psychological distress and promote well-being, yet evidence on how and when these tools exert their effects over time and what constitutes meaningful engagement remains mixed. This study examined whether use of the Social Activity Guardian and Intervention Project (SAGIP) mental health mobile application predicts differential growth trajectories in psychological well-being and psychological distress among members of an academic community, and whether app engagement explains individual differences in these trajectories. Using an experimental repeated-measures design, participants from a university academic community were randomly assigned to a SAGIP app group or a waitlist control group and assessed at baseline (Month 0) and monthly for three months (Months 1 to 3). Psychological well-being and psychological distress were analyzed using latent growth curve modeling and mixed-effects piecewise growth models to capture nonlinear change. Compared to waitlist controls, SAGIP users demonstrated significantly greater improvements in psychological well-being and larger reductions in psychological distress during the randomized phase. Piecewise models indicated phase-specific change, with distress reduction strongest early in the intervention and well-being gains emerging more gradually and strengthening later. Frequency and duration of app use were associated with baseline mental health status but did not predict growth trajectories. Supplementary analyses provided additional support for the robustness of the findings: descriptive post-randomized analyses suggested that the experimental group generally maintained gains in psychological well-being and stabilized psychological distress below baseline levels through Month 6, whereas the control group showed modest improvements in well-being and distress after crossover to app access. Sensitivity analyses restricted to student participants replicated the primary pattern of intervention effects, while exploratory moderation analyses found no clear evidence that intervention effects differed between students and employees. These findings suggest that the therapeutic impact of self-guided digital interventions may depend more on the timing and quality of engagement than on the quantity of use and highlight the importance of modeling nonlinear change when evaluating digital mental health interventions.

Introduction

Mental health concerns have been on the rise worldwide. This was exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic and is currently contributing to the increased need for mental health interventions. A meta-analysis of mental health research revealed that there was a 4% to 19% increase in mental health problem prevalence for the general population during the pandemic1. Likewise, in the Philippines, an exponential rise in the cases of mental health concerns was reported. However, the national information on mental health services in the Philippines reveals significant gaps and inconsistencies in the delivery of mental healthcare, leading to challenges in the provision of accessible and affordable mental healthcare2. According to the World Health Organization3, the Philippines has 1.68 mental health professionals for every 100,000 Filipinos, compared to a global median of 13 per 100,000. In addition, several cultural, social, geographic, technological, and economic factors contribute to the challenges and barriers in seeking and providing mental health services in the country. With the increasing rate of mental health issues, as well as the existing discrepancy between healthcare demand and the provision of services, there is a need to look into the potential of alternative and emerging mental health interventions4.

Over the last decade, technology-based mental health interventions have grown rapidly, making them more accessible and cost-effective in the delivery of evidence-based healthcare services5. With the increasing ownership of smartphones and accessibility over the internet, combined with the surging demand for mental health support and services, mobile mental health applications are drawing more interest from consumers and mental health providers6. Mental health applications can easily be searched, downloaded, and accessed, with more than 10,000 existing today7. Mental health applications are gaining popularity due to their “self-help” functions and features that are accessible anytime and anywhere8. Moreover, mental health applications protect the identities of users as they can be used anonymously and confidentially9, preventing an individual from being stigmatized for seeking help and support. In addition, mental health applications are gradually becoming promising early intervention and support tools that can help users at the time they need it, at their earliest convenience, until they are able to see a mental health professional. These technologies can supplement the system of care provided by a professional through the reinforcement of strategies, skills training, and information tracking.

There is an optimistic outlook regarding the potential benefits of using mental health applications10–12. A meta-analytic review of mobile mental health applications showed significant results in reductions in depression and stress scores with small to medium effect sizes13. When comparing the effectiveness of face-to-face therapy, smartphone mobile application intervention, and the combination of face-to-face therapy and smartphone mobile application intervention, there are promising results in the reduction of depression, anxiety, and stress for college students14,15. Post hoc tests showed that blended therapy had the greatest improvement in scores, suggesting the combination of face-to-face therapy and mobile platforms to support the mental health of people in their everyday lives. Aside from symptom reduction for users with mental health concerns, other mental health apps target the general population for well-being or quality of life outcomes16–18. Specific features of mental health applications like meditation exercises19,20, breathing-retraining exercises21, informational resource22, problem-solving applications23, mood monitoring24, and mental health screening25 were found to be useful in enhancing well-being, reducing stress-related experiences, and supplementing psychological interventions in improving mental health functioning.

The Asia-Pacific region is at the top when it comes to the heaviest mobile data traffic, particularly in China, India, Indonesia, Philippines, Vietnam, and Japan. The widespread ownership of mobile devices and internet access in Asia presents a significant opportunity to enhance mental health care in the digital and technological space. Mobile mental health applications can serve as a layer of mental health support for users and as a platform to connect mental health professionals with users, helping to bridge the gap in access to services6.

To further investigate the effectiveness of mental health apps, this study evaluated the Social Activity Guardian and Intervention Project (SAGIP) mental health app designed for the academic community, particularly at the University of the Philippines (UP). Representative users consisting of students, teachers, and non-teaching personnel used the SAGIP app, and mental health outcomes were measured over time. Although many evaluations of digital mental health interventions assume that benefits accumulate in a steady linear fashion, psychological change often unfolds in discontinuous and phase-specific ways. This is particularly relevant for self-guided mobile interventions, where use is voluntary, episodic, and shaped by fluctuations in stress, motivation, readiness, and contextual demands. Early changes may reflect immediate relief, novelty effects, or rapid uptake of coping tools, whereas later changes may reflect consolidation, skill integration, plateauing, or vulnerability to renewed stressors. Examining phase-specific and nonlinear trajectories is therefore important not only for statistical fit, but also for understanding the timing and nature of change that a self-guided intervention may realistically produce. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the basic effectiveness of the SAGIP mental health mobile application, estimating both overall linear change and nonlinear, phase-specific growth patterns over the course of app use. Specifically, the study examined whether use of the SAGIP mental health application predicts individual growth trajectories in psychological well-being and psychological distress from baseline to Month 3. It was hypothesized that participants assigned to the SAGIP app group would not differ from those in the waitlist control group in baseline psychological well-being (H1a) and baseline psychological distress (H2a). Over time, however, it was expected that participants who used the SAGIP mental health application would demonstrate a significantly steeper positive growth trajectory in psychological well-being (H1b) and a significantly steeper reduction in psychological distress (H2b) compared to non-users. Moreover, the study explored whether patterns of engagement with the SAGIP mental health application—operationalized as frequency and duration of use—were associated with individual growth trajectories in psychological well-being and psychological distress among app users.

Through these objectives, the present study extends the digital mental health literature by examining both temporal patterns of change and the role of user engagement in shaping the effectiveness of a self-guided mental health mobile intervention. First, unlike prior evaluations of mental health applications that rely primarily on linear longitudinal or mean-difference comparisons, this study explicitly models phase-specific and nonlinear trajectories of psychological well-being and psychological distress over time. By combining latent growth curve modeling with piecewise mixed-effects analyses, the study captures dynamic patterns of change that would be obscured under linear assumptions, including early intervention effects, later consolidation or rebound, and differential timing of change across mental health outcomes. This approach provides a more nuanced and process-sensitive understanding of how digital mental health interventions unfold longitudinally. Second, the study moves beyond binary indicators of intervention effectiveness by examining whether user engagement, operationalized through frequency and duration of app use, predicts individual differences in growth trajectories. Rather than assuming a direct dose–response relationship, the study empirically tests this assumption and demonstrates that conventional engagement metrics do not account for observed improvements. In doing so, the findings contribute to emerging critiques of quantity-based engagement models and underscore the need to distinguish between the amount and the quality of interaction in self-guided digital interventions. Third, this study contributes contextual and geographic novelty by evaluating a mental health mobile application developed for and tested within an academic community in Southeast Asia. Much of the existing evidence on digital mental health applications is derived from high-income Western contexts, often with access to extensive clinical infrastructure. By situating the evaluation within a resource-constrained university setting, the present study provides evidence on the feasibility and effectiveness of self-guided digital mental health tools in contexts where access to traditional mental health services is limited. This expands the generalizability of digital mental health research and highlights the potential role of mobile applications as scalable supports in low-resource academic environments. Taken together, these contributions position the present study not only as an evaluation of whether a mental health app works, but as an investigation of how change unfolds over time, what engagement means in practice, and where digital mental health tools may be most impactful.

Methods

This paper is part of a broader research project that primarily involved two phases: (1) the user-centered design and development of the Social Activity Guardian and Intervention Project (SAGIP) mental health mobile application and (2) the evaluation of the SAGIP app through a longitudinal and cross-sectional mixed methods approach. This paper focuses on the Phase 2 quantitative longitudinal investigation of the effectiveness of using the SAGIP app.

The research employed an experimental repeated measures design to evaluate the effectiveness of using the SAGIP app over time using latent growth curve modeling and mixed-effects piecewise growth models. It compared participants in the experimental group, who were given access to and used the SAGIP app, with those in the control group, who did not use the app during the randomized phase. The analyses examined changes in psychological distress and psychological well-being across measurement points, while also considering app usage patterns and participant status as a student or faculty/staff member. This approach allowed the study to assess both overall intervention effects and potential differences in mental health trajectories across user groups.

Intervention: SAGIP app

The SAGIP app was developed using a user-centered design framework that involved students, faculty, non-teaching staff, and mental health professionals in the University of the Philippines. User requirements were elicited through focus group discussions and in-depth interviews. Details of the app development are discussed in another paper.

SAGIP is a self-guided app with 3 main features: Directory, Resources, and Toolkits. The Directory contains contact information for mental health support providers and services within and outside the University campuses. The Resources are psychoeducational materials in the form of articles, audio, and video resources curated by clinical psychologists based on the needs identified from the interviews and focus group discussion sessions with students, faculty, and non-teaching staff. Finally, the Toolkits contain worksheets and guided exercises (i.e., problem-solving, meditation, cognitive restructuring, coping, acceptance, self-compassion, support-seeking, emotion regulation, etc.) that help enhance, manage, or tackle general mental health concerns. Other app features include Assessment of Well-being, personalization of contents and resources through the Recommended and Explore features, Daily Quotes, Notifications, Badges for completion of worksheets and exercises, and customization of the app’s settings. Study site and participants

The University of the Philippines (UP) was chosen as the research site because its population is a good representation of the academic community, with its students, faculty, and staff coming from different campuses all over the Philippines.

The study recruited two hundred twenty-four (n = 224) participants (including students, faculty, and non-teaching staff) from the UP community. This sample size is consistent with the recommendation for latent growth curve modeling for unbiased estimates26. A sample size of ~200 support longitudinal growth modeling with four repeated assessments (T0–T3) across two conditions (experimental vs waitlist control) and a time-invariant covariate (student vs faculty/staff). This target aligns with simulation-based evidence showing that moderately parameterized latent growth curve models often require sample sizes in the ~200 range for acceptable power, bias, and solution propriety, with adequacy depending on model complexity and effect magnitude27. In addition, guidance on growth curve modeling emphasizes that precision and power depend not only on N but also on the number of waves and the functional form of change, conditions addressed here by using four measurement occasions and explicitly modeling growth over time28. The final analytic sample was N = 194, reflecting a modest reduction from the initial sample size due to missing data; however, contemporary missing-data research strongly recommends maximum-likelihood/FIML approaches for longitudinal and SEM-based models because they use all available observations and generally yield less biased and more efficient estimates than case deletion under missing-at-random assumptions29. Finally, for mixed-effects growth models, simulation work indicates that the primary driver is the number of Level-2 units (participants), with substantial improvement in standard error accuracy once Level-2 sample size exceeds small-sample regimes (e.g., ≤50); thus, 194 participants is consistent with recommended ranges for standard random-intercept (and commonly used random-slope) growth models30.

Participants were randomly assigned to two groups: experimental “app” group and waitlist control “no app” group. Each group should have at least 100 participants composed of 80 students, 10 faculty, and 10 non-teaching staff. This breakdown is based on the population of about 25,000 students and 5000 faculty and staff for the entire UP system. The faculty and staff comprise about 17% of the population. This will be about 17, rounded up to 20 in every 100 participants. .

Sampling and recruitment

Participants were recruited online via convenience sampling. Emails were sent through the official mailing lists of the campuses, and online advertisements were posted on university-affiliated social media pages. Interested participants were asked to sign up online via Qualtrics. The sign-up form included a study description and a screening questionnaire. Those who signed up were screened for their eligibility for the study and contacted by the research team.

Participants qualified for the study if they are: (a) currently enrolled or working in the University (UP student or UP employee) throughout the duration of the study, (b) owning at least one smartphone with access to a minimum of 5 Mbps internet connectivity, (c) willing to commit to participating in the study by using the app at least once a week and answering the periodic assessment measures for 6 months, (d) at least 18 years old. On the other hand, participants who would fall under any of the following exclusion criteria were ineligible to participate: (a) not officially enrolled nor working in the University, (b) currently diagnosed with a mental health condition, (c) currently taking psychotropic medications, (d) currently seeing a mental health professional, (e) currently using other mental health mobile application, (f) below 18 years old.

Participants who expressed that they no longer want to continue with the study were excluded from the study. Participants who, upon periodic evaluation, had highly elevated psychological distress scores (severe to extremely severe) were also provided additional mental health resources and asked whether they still want to continue their participation. For this study, no participants were withdrawn for these aforementioned reasons. Nonetheless, data of participants were withdrawn (i.e., not included for data analysis) if there are more than 15% missing responses in each of the questionnaires they accomplished over the three-month period. From the 224 participants who were recruited and assigned to either the experimental group or control group, only the data from 194 participants were used for data analysis (with n = 93 for the experimental group and n = 101 for the control group) after data cleaning.

Study procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental “app” group or the control “no app” group. Both groups were invited to join an online orientation via Zoom webinar to learn about the nature of the study and their roles and expectations from them as participants. Participants were asked to replace their profile pictures and names to avoid identification. They were also asked to close their cameras for security purposes. Orientation of the experimental “app” group included the installation of the SAGIP mobile application with user guide. The orientation sessions were recorded and shared to those participants who were unable to attend. After the orientation sessions, participants were asked to complete the informed consent and agreement forms online via Qualtrics. The study was conducted remotely, and all interactions with the participants were virtual via email and Zoom meetings. Likewise, the assessment measures for the longitudinal study were administered online through Qualtrics.

Participants in the experimental “app” group were instructed to use the mental health application for a period of three months. Participants assigned to the waitlist control (“no app”) group were introduced to the application at baseline but were informed that they would not have access during the first three months of the study, during which time their well-being and psychological distress were continuously monitored. A baseline survey was administered at study entry, followed by periodic online assessments conducted at the end of Months 1, 2, and 3, corresponding to the randomized controlled phase of the study. After completion of the initial three-month randomized experimental period, participants in the app group were permitted to continue using the application, while participants in the waitlist control group were granted access to the application for an additional three months. Final assessments of mental well-being, psychological distress, and application engagement were collected at the end of Month 6 for both groups. However, data collected beyond Month 3 were not included in the main experimental analyses, as the randomized group comparison was no longer preserved after the control group crossed over to app access. These later data were instead examined in supplementary analyses to describe post-randomization trends, rather than to estimate causal intervention effects.

Measures and data collection

Quantitative data were collected at five time points (pre-study assessment, three monthly assessments, and post-study assessment). The following variables were measured to evaluate SAGIP app’s effectiveness: (1) Psychological Distress measured using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 short form version. The three subscales of depression, anxiety, and stress have 7 items each that are rated from 0 to 3. The general distress score is calculated by getting the sum of the ratings for all items31,32; (2) Psychological Well-being measured using the shortened 18-item Psychological Well-being Scale33 with 6 constructs: autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations with others, purpose in life, and self-acceptance; and (3) App engagement. App engagement was measured using automatically recorded in-app log data. Two indicators were derived during the randomized phase: (a) frequency of use, defined as the count of content views and feature-access events logged by the application, and (b) duration of use, defined as the cumulative time (in seconds) that the application remained active during use sessions. These indicators index behavioral exposure to the app. However, they do not distinguish between active therapeutic engagement (e.g., carefully reading a psychoeducational resource, completing an exercise, or reflectively applying content) and more passive forms of browsing or brief access. Accordingly, engagement variables in the present study should be interpreted as quantity-based usage indicators rather than direct measures of depth or quality of engagement.

Prior to participation, those who signed up were administered a questionnaire for mental health (depression, anxiety, stress, and psychological well-being) as part of a screening and to establish baseline measures. After the random assignment to experimental and control groups and their respective orientation to the study, quantitative data collection followed at the end of each month for 3 months. Links to the monthly surveys were sent to the participants through email.

Data analysis

Primary longitudinal inferences were estimated using latent growth curve modeling and mixed-effects growth models. These approaches were selected because they flexibly accommodate individual differences in change, unbalanced observations, missing data under standard assumptions, and departures from simple linear trajectories. Each of the measures was first analyzed and reported using descriptive statistics. This included the multiple time point measures of psychological well-being, psychological distress scores, and the mental health app usage. This provided an overview of how scores changed over time and how frequently the app was used. To examine change more formally, growth curve statistical models were used to track how individuals’ scores changed from baseline to Month 3. Separate models were estimated for psychological well-being and psychological distress. These models estimated (a) participants’ starting levels at baseline and (b) how quickly their scores increased or decreased over time. It was tested whether these change patterns differed between participants who used the SAGIP app and those in the waitlist control group, while also accounting for whether participants were students or faculty/staff.

In subsequent analyses focusing only on participants who used the app, patterns of app engagement, specifically how often the app was used and how long it was used, were examined to determine whether these were related to changes in well-being or distress over time. Participant status (student versus faculty/staff) was again included to account for possible group differences.

To examine the robustness of findings given the predominance of students in the sample, sensitivity analyses restricted to participants with student status were conducted. In addition, exploratory status-moderation terms in the full-sample piecewise mixed-effects models were tested to evaluate whether change trajectories differed between students and employees. Because faculty and staff comprised a relatively small subgroup, these moderation analyses were interpreted cautiously and were intended as robustness checks rather than definitive subgroup comparisons.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R34, including descriptive statistics, latent growth curve modeling, and mixed-effects growth modeling.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the University of the Philippines Manila Research Ethics Board (UPMREB 2023-0053-01).

These engagement findings suggest that commonly used indicators of engagement, such as how often or how long an app is used, may not adequately reflect the aspects of interaction that are most relevant to therapeutic change. Frequency and duration capture how much an application is accessed, but they do not capture how it is used, which specific components are engaged with, or whether the interaction meaningfully supports reflection, skill application, or emotional regulation. As a result, two users with identical usage statistics may experience substantially different psychological benefits depending on the depth, intentionality, and contextual relevance of their engagement. In self-guided mental health applications, engagement is likely to be highly selective and goal-directed rather than uniform. Users may enter the app during moments of heightened distress, uncertainty, or readiness for change; engage with a specific exercise or resource that directly addresses their immediate concern; and then disengage once their need has been met. In such cases, a brief but well-timed interaction may exert a disproportionate therapeutic effect, whereas repeated or prolonged use without psychological readiness or relevance may yield little additional benefit. This pattern would attenuate or eliminate simple dose–response relationships based solely on quantity of use. Furthermore, higher frequency or longer duration of app use may sometimes reflect need rather than benefit. Individuals experiencing greater baseline distress or lower well-being may spend more time exploring content or repeatedly accessing the app without necessarily translating that engagement into improved outcomes. Conversely, users with higher baseline functioning may use the app sparingly yet effectively, applying insights or strategies outside the app in their daily lives, effects that are not captured by in-app usage metrics.

Taken together, these considerations point to a distinction between quantity of engagement (how much the app is used) and quality of engagement (how meaningfully the app is integrated into the user’s coping, reflection, and behavior). The present findings therefore caution against interpreting frequency and duration as direct proxies for therapeutic “dose” and highlight the need for more nuanced engagement measures that better align with the psychological mechanisms through which self-guided digital interventions exert their effects.

Simulation-based power estimates indicated low power to detect the observed effects of app engagement on well-being and psychological distress trajectories. For well-being, power ranged from 0.12 to 0.34 for main effects of engagement frequency and duration, and from 0.05 to 0.25 for interaction effects with time. For distress outcomes, power ranged from 0.05 to 0.19 across engagement predictors. These results suggest that engagement-related effects were small in magnitude and that the study was unlikely to detect such effects reliably given their observed size.

Transition from linear to nonlinear (piecewise) growth modeling

Initial analyses using linear growth models provided a useful starting point for examining change over time; however, these models showed limited ability to capture the observed trajectories in psychological well-being and psychological distress. Visual inspection of observed group means (see Figs. 2 and 3) revealed clear departures from linearity, including phase-specific changes and differential acceleration or deceleration across time points. To account for this, a series of mixed-effects growth models was estimated that accommodated nonlinear trajectories with piecewise specifications, specifically differential early phase change from T0 to T2 and differential late phase change from T2 to T3. Models included group (experimental vs control), participant status (student vs faculty/staff), and their interactions with time segments. Mixed-effects modeling was selected to flexibly model individual differences in change, to accommodate unbalanced data and missing observations, and to allow direct comparison of alternative functional forms of growth. This approach allowed for a more accurate and theoretically coherent characterization of change over time and the differential effects of the intervention.

Model comparisons indicated that piecewise growth models provided a significantly better fit than linear models for both psychological well-being and psychological distress (see Table 6). Overall, the intervention signal for distress was primarily an early-phase benefit (greater symptom reduction through T2), followed by attenuation or rebound by T3. Simulation-based power analyses using the fitted piecewise mixed-effects models indicated low power to detect baseline group differences in psychological distress (power = 0.05), consistent with baseline equivalence. The study demonstrated excellent power to detect early intervention effects on distress during the T0–T2 segment (power ≈ 1.00, 95% CI [0.96, 1.0]), whereas power to detect later-segment effects from T2–T3 was only modest (power = 0.45, 95% CI [0.35, 0.55]). Consistent with the power analyses, group differences in distress trajectories were robust during the early intervention period but less conclusive during the later segment, where statistical power was limited.

Discussion

The present study provides robust evidence that the SAGIP mental health mobile application contributes to improvements in psychological well-being and reductions in psychological distress among members of the academic community, relative to a waitlist control condition. This is consistent with the significant results shown in the literature about the effectiveness of mental health applications targeting distress and well-being11,13,15 with modest effect sizes. Across analytic approaches, including latent growth curve modeling and piecewise mixed-effects modeling, the findings converge on three central findings: (1) SAGIP users exhibited significantly more favorable mental health trajectories than non-users, (2) Change processes were decidedly nonlinear and phase-specific, and (3) Conventional engagement metrics (frequency and duration of use) did not account for individual differences in mental health improvement. Together, these results advance the literature by demonstrating not only whether a mental health app “works,” but how and when its effects unfold over time. A critical contribution of this study lies in its parallel modeling of psychological well-being and psychological distress as distinct, yet interrelated, outcomes. The findings reinforce the dual-continuum model of mental health35,36, wherein reductions in distress do not necessarily mirror gains in well-being .

Psychological well-being showed consistent and cumulative gains among SAGIP users across the intervention period, with particularly pronounced improvements in the late phase (T2–T3). In contrast, psychological distress demonstrated a front-loaded intervention effect, with sharper reductions during the early phase (T0–T2) followed by attenuation and partial rebound by T3. This divergence suggests that while distress reduction may be more sensitive to immediate coping and psychoeducational inputs, sustained gains in well-being may require longer exposure and deeper integration of skills and perspectives. Importantly, the persistence of well-being gains even amid later increases in distress underscores the conceptual independence of these constructs. Users appear to retain or even strengthen their sense of purpose, mastery, or self-acceptance despite contextual stressors—an outcome that is particularly meaningful in high-demand academic environments.

Using a mental health application like the SAGIP app can be an adjunctive tool for helping users support their mental health, particularly in terms of their sense of well-being and level of general distress. The SAGIP app contains three main features: mental health directories, psychoeducational resources, and mental health toolkits (worksheets and exercises). Arguably, the self-guided nature of the SAGIP app allowed for flexibility and ease of use, making it more likely for participants to use it at their own pace whenever they need or want to37. There were also no restrictions imposed on participants regarding what contents or features of the app they should use or access. The freedom to use respects their autonomy as users, which can be related to the improved sense of well-being associated with its use38. Furthermore, the SAGIP app contains a feature that provides opportunities for users to earn points that can be accumulated to earn “badges” by completing certain tasks, exercises, and activities in the app. The badges can then be used in nourishing and growing a virtual mental health plant, symbolizing the users’ continuous engagement and personal development in the app. In this way, there is a gamification and incentivization experience of using the app that further enhances the user experience. Research has shown that gamification integrates theories of self-determination and motivational psychology39. Gamified features embedded in mental health applications enhance emotional well-being and stress management by offering virtual rewards, opportunities to track progress, and meaningful tasks40.

On the other hand, amidst the general pattern of effectiveness of using the SAGIP app is a specific pattern of sudden increase in distress of participants in both groups from the end of the second month to the end of the third month. This may be attributed to the part of the semester where students, faculty, and staff in the University experience distress due to various academic demands (i.e., examinations, checking of papers, submission of requirements, etc.) towards the end of the semester. The late-phase increase in distress observed across both experimental and control groups is best understood within the ecological context of the academic calendar. The transition from mid-semester to end-semester periods is typically marked by cumulative academic demands, evaluation pressures, and reduced recovery opportunities for students, faculty, and staff alike. Despite this increase in distress, users of the SAGIP app still experienced a continuous improvement in well-being, in contrast to non-users who showed a steady decline in well-being. That SAGIP users maintained or increased well-being during this period, despite rising distress, suggests that the app may function as a psychological buffer rather than a stress eliminator. This distinction is clinically important: interventions that preserve meaning, agency, and self-regulation during inevitable stress may be more realistic and sustainable than those aimed solely at symptom suppression.

Furthermore, one of the most theoretically and methodologically significant findings of this study is the failure of linear growth models, especially for psychological distress, and the superior fit of piecewise nonlinear models. The presence of Heywood cases and poor global fit indices in linear LGCMs underscores the risk of oversimplifying temporal change in digital mental health interventions. The piecewise analyses revealed distinct early and late phases of change, suggesting that intervention effects are not uniform over time. For distress, the early phase captured the primary benefit of app use, whereas the late phase reflected vulnerability to contextual pressures (e.g., academic workload intensification). For well-being, however, the late phase represented a period of accelerated improvement among SAGIP users, while non-users continued to decline. The nonlinearity of changes in well-being and distress outcomes reflects a more realistic view of progress in longitudinal intervention studies41. Research has shown that across various mental health interventions, discontinuous and non-linear types of changes are more likely to happen41. This is partly explained by dynamical systems theory that predicts an increase in variability and destabilization of “old patterns” or habits triggered by the introduction and perpetuation of an intervention42. With the introduction of the SAGIP app and its continued usage, users become subjected to a dynamical system where the current psychological system of the person is disturbed and reorganized because new habits and new learnings are being formed until homeostasis is achieved. The learning resources, worksheets, exercises, and activities provide opportunities for users to learn, unlearn, and re-learn mental health information. Over the six months of the study, there may be time points characterized by slow and steady movement of well-being and distress scores, while there are critical points associated with rapid and sharp changes43. Consistent with the dynamic systems theory, the psychological system will eventually stabilize when the habits of using the app and applying mental health strategies become more integrated within the user. Supplemental data were collected after Month 3, during which participants in the experimental group were allowed to continue, and participants in the control group were given the opportunity to use the app for 3 more months. From the end of the 3rd month to the end of the sixth month, associated well-being and distress scores begin to stabilize for users of the SAGIP mental health app, and participants in the control group who only began to use the SAGIP app at the end of the 3rd month experienced more observable changes in their well-being and distress scores.

From a more pragmatic standpoint, the higher rate of increase among those who just started using the app (previously part of the control group) compared to those who have already been using the app for three months (part of the experimental group) may also be influenced by factors associated with app usage. It is also possible that participants in the experimental group may have already exhausted the available content and features of the app by the end of the sixth month. Eventually, well-being gains may plateau because users have already fully utilized what the app can offer. This has potential implications for the longer-term sustainability of app usage due to content limitations. According to Alqahtani & Orji44, mobile mental health application users are more likely to be engaged and experience better outcomes when there are opportunities for continuous improvement of the app they are using (e.g., updating the app more frequently to comply with new phone features and other software requirements, offering a new variety of options, functionalities, and content).

Lastly, contrary to dominant assumptions in the digital mental health literature, frequency and duration of app use did not predict growth in well-being or reductions in distress45. While engagement metrics were associated with baseline mental health status, they failed to explain change trajectories. The commonly established dose-response effect in longitudinal intervention studies46,47 and routinely delivered interventions48, where frequency and duration of app use explain the percentage of changes in mental health outcomes, was not found. In self-guided interventions, therapeutic benefit may hinge more on what is used, when, and how meaningfully, rather than on sheer exposure. A single timely exercise, insight, or resource may exert disproportionate impact relative to repeated passive engagement. This emphasizes that users are not passive recipients of a standardized “dose,” but active agents who selectively engage with content based on salience, readiness, and contextual need.

The beneficial effects likely come from the content of the application more than the frequency and duration of usage. It is possible that users who only use the app for a few times can already find it beneficial if they are able to access certain features or content of the app useful for them at that time. Conversely, those who frequently use the app may not benefit as much if they do not access certain features beneficial for them or appropriate to their needs16. From this perspective, therapeutic change may be driven less by cumulative exposure and more by precision of engagement. Whether a specific feature, exercise, or resource is accessed at a moment when the user is cognitively, emotionally, and situationally receptive. A single well-timed cognitive reframing worksheet, grounding exercise, or psychoeducational insight may catalyze meaningful shifts in appraisal, coping, or self-understanding, whereas repeated engagement without psychological readiness may yield diminishing or negligible returns. This aligns with theories of experiential learning and self-regulation, which emphasize insight, integration, and application over repetition per se49,50. Nevertheless, user engagement may still be a critical factor in influencing changes in outcomes but from a methodological standpoint, the use of simple engagement metrics like frequency and duration may not be sufficient to capture the impact of engagement on mental health outcomes46.

Methodologically, these findings expose the limitations of commonly used engagement indicators, such as total minutes spent in-app or number of logins, which implicitly assume that all forms of use are equivalent. Such metrics obscure the qualitative and processual dimensions of engagement that are central to psychological change. In the context of mental health applications, engagement is not merely behavioral but also cognitive, emotional, and motivational. Future evaluations would benefit from adopting process-sensitive engagement metrics that more closely map onto hypothesized mechanisms of change. Feature-level usage data, for instance, can differentiate between users who primarily consume psychoeducational content, those who repeatedly engage in experiential exercises, and those who rely on resource directories or assessments. Temporal alignment with stressors, such as whether an intervention is accessed proximal to exams, deadlines, or interpersonal conflicts, may further illuminate why certain interactions exert disproportionate effects. Equally important are subjective engagement indicators, including perceived relevance, emotional resonance, and perceived usefulness of specific app components. These phenomenological dimensions may mediate the relationship between app interaction and outcome, but remain invisible in purely behavioral logs. A brief but personally meaningful encounter with a self-compassion exercise, for example, may exert stronger downstream effects on well-being than extended interaction with content that feels generic or misaligned with the user’s concerns. These considerations suggest that engagement in digital mental health interventions should be conceptualized not as a matter of quantity, but of fit, timing, and psychological uptake.

The findings have several implications for digital mental health research and practice. First, self-guided features such as psychoeducational resources and toolkits appear capable of supporting meaningful mental health outcomes in community-based settings. Second, modeling mental health change longitudinally revealed phase-specific patterns, suggesting that digital interventions may benefit from design strategies that prioritize early distress regulation and later support for meaning-making, growth, and consolidation. Third, the findings reinforce the value of conceptualizing mental health along dual continua, in which improvements in well-being and reductions in distress may unfold through distinct processes. Finally, the absence of dose–response effects suggests the need to move beyond simple metrics such as frequency and duration toward richer indicators of therapeutic interaction and engagement.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, outcomes were limited to overall psychological well-being and psychological distress, and analyses relied on composite scores from the Psychological Well-being Scale and the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale. Future studies could examine specific dimensions of well-being and distress, as well as additional outcomes such as quality of life and symptom-specific indicators. Second, the three-month intervention period represents a relatively short timeframe. Although a follow-up phase was implemented, outcomes during the final three months were assessed only in aggregate rather than monthly, limiting the ability to examine phase-specific changes during the extended period. Third, participants used the SAGIP app without standardized guidance after the initial orientation. As a result, patterns of use varied across individuals, and engagement was measured only through frequency and duration. More detailed usage metrics—such as accessed content, feature utilization, and other process-level indicators—may provide clearer insights into mechanisms of change. Fourth, technical and developmental limitations of the app may have constrained outcomes, as suggested by the plateauing of well-being toward the final month of assessment. This pattern may reflect the finite scope of available content and the need for continued refinement and expansion of app features. Fifth, the sample was predominantly composed of younger students and women and excluded individuals with diagnosable mental health conditions, which may limit generalizability to broader populations, including clinical groups and individuals less familiar with digital technologies. Future studies employing more diverse sampling strategies and targeted recruitment would help clarify the extent to which these findings generalize across populations. Sixth, a key limitation is that app engagement was operationalized using quantity-based backend metrics rather than process-sensitive indicators of meaningful therapeutic interaction. Counts of content views, feature access, and total logged time cannot determine whether participants merely opened content, briefly skimmed it, or engaged in deeper reflection and skill application. As such, the null findings for engagement should not be interpreted to mean that meaningful engagement was unimportant, but rather that the specific engagement indicators available in the present study may have inadequately captured the aspects of app use most relevant to psychological change. Finally, the inclusion of students, faculty, and staff in one analytic sample increases ecological relevance but also introduces heterogeneity in life context, role demands, and likely stress patterns. While participant status was statistically adjusted and sensitivity analyses were conducted, this strategy does not eliminate all concerns about group heterogeneity. The findings should therefore be interpreted primarily as evidence for the overall academic-community sample, with strongest confidence for the student subgroup that constituted the majority of participants. Although participant status was included as a covariate and follow-up sensitivity analyses were conducted, the sample remained heavily weighted toward students. The relatively small number of faculty and staff participants limited the precision of subgroup comparisons and constrains inference about whether the observed intervention effects generalize equally across all segments of the academic community.

Recommendations for future research include further analysis of engagement and effectiveness, particularly utilizing other user engagement metrics: not just quantity but quality of interactions, daily activity metrics, session interval, session depth, conversion rate, completion rate, perceived usability, aesthetic appeal51,52. In this regard, user experience can also be captured through qualitative methods such as interviews, focus group discussions, or open-ended survey questions to identify recommendations for further refining the SAGIP app. Future research on the effectiveness of the SAGIP mental health application should include participants with varying levels of symptom severity. It is important to investigate which specific symptoms and levels of severity allow for the use of the SAGIP mental health app to still produce clinically significant outcomes. This has implications for establishing its potential and limitations as an intervention tool. It is also interesting to compare the effectiveness of various strategies and exercises used in the SAGIP app, such as mood tracking, meditation and mindfulness, emotion regulation, problem-solving, interpersonal skills, self-compassion, etc. in promoting mental health. From a methodological perspective, an ecological momentary assessment may be warranted to improve data collection, assess the daily dynamics and experiences of users, and potentially enhance user engagement53. With the proliferation of multiple mental health mobile applications available in the Philippines, it would be best to have a comparative study on the degree of effectiveness of a select number of mental health applications. To establish incremental validity, there is a need to determine how much predictive effect the SAGIP app can still contribute over and beyond the effects of other existing mental health mobile applications on various mental health outcomes.
