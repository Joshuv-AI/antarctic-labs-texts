Bidirectional dynamic frame prediction network for total-body [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 and [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-04 PET images
Authors: Qianyi Yang, Wenbo Li, Zhenxing Huang, Zixiang Chen, Wenjie Zhao, Yunlong Gao, Xinlan Yang, Yongfeng Yang, Hairong Zheng, Dong Liang, Jianjun Liu, Ruohua Chen, Zhanli Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTotal-body dynamic positron emission tomography (PET) imaging with total-body coverage and ultrahigh sensitivity has played an important role in accurate tracer kinetic analyses in physiology, biochemistry, and pharmacology. However, dynamic PET scans typically entail prolonged durations (60 minutes), potentially causing patient discomfort and resulting in artifacts in the final images. Therefore, we propose a dynamic frame prediction method for total-body PET imaging via deep learning technology to reduce the required scanning time.MethodsOn the basis of total-body dynamic PET data acquired from 13 subjects who received [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-04 (68Ga-FAPI) and 24 subjects who received [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 (68Ga-PSMA), we propose a bidirectional dynamic frame prediction network that uses the initial and final 10 min of PET imaging data (frames 1–6 and frames 25–30, respectively) as inputs. The peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index measure (SSIM) were employed as evaluation metrics for an image quality assessment. Introduction

Positron emission tomography (PET), which is an essential functional imaging technique, has extensive applications in oncology, cardiology and neurology [1]. During the imaging process, dynamic PET scanning employs a kinetic model to measure the spatiotemporal distribution of radioactive tracers, providing significantly enhanced diagnostic, prognostic, and treatment response monitoring capabilities [2, 3]. As commonly used devices for dynamic PET imaging, PET/computed tomography (CT) systems can offer clearer and more accurate imaging data when anatomical information (CT) and metabolic activity (PET) are combined. Recently, total-body PET/CT systems with 194-cm axial fields of view (AFOVs) have been developed, achieving unprecedented image quality and offering the first opportunity to dynamically image the entire adult body [4–7]. Therefore, total-body PET/CT systems have great potential for use in dynamic PET imaging to assist with the diagnosis and treatment of diseases.

With respect to the use of radiopharmaceuticals in dynamic PET imaging, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) has played a crucial role in the detection of malignant tumors and infectious or inflammatory conditions [2, 8–11]. In recent years, novel tumor molecular probes, such as 68Ga-labeled fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (68Ga-FAPI) and 68Ga-labeled prostate-specific membrane antigen (68Ga-PSMA), have demonstrated greater imaging sensitivity in PET/CT scenarios than 18F-FDG has [12, 13]. 68Ga-PSMA primarily focuses on the diagnosis and treatment of prostate cancer [14, 15], whereas 68Ga-FAPI has garnered significant attention for its therapeutic value in cases involving various malignant tumors, including pancreatic cancer [16] and gastric cancer [17]. With the continuous introduction of different radiopharmaceutical tracers, dynamic PET imaging has become increasingly popular and can help to better understand the dynamic metabolic processes in the human body [3].

Despite the availability of improved scanners and more precise radiopharmaceuticals in the current dynamic PET process, the scanning duration remains relatively long (approximately 60 min). This extended scanning time significantly reduces patient comfort, leads to imaging artifacts, and increases the examination costs for hospitals [18]. In recent years, deep learning has been widely applied in the medical field to address tasks such as diagnostic assistance [19, 20], disease prediction [21, 22], and image enhancement [23, 24]. Many scholars have focused on finding methods for reducing the durations of dynamic PET scans. Huang et al. [25] utilized a deep learning model to uncover the potential relationships between total-body static PET images (50 ~ 60 min) and parametric images, thereby directly generating images. Wang et al. [26] achieved optimal results when converting static PET lung images (50 ~ 60 min) to images by enhancing a 3D cycle-generative adversarial network (GAN) model. Zaker et al. [27] employed a convolutional neural network (CNN) to transform SUV images from static PET images, combining them with partial frames derived from dynamic PET images to form images. Li et al. [28] employed the sine curve of long-axis dynamic PET images acquired between 40 and 65 min postinjection as inputs to generate parametric images. Miao et al. [29] generated images by averaging three 5-minute dynamic SUV frames starting at 60 min after the injection step. Liang et al. [30] utilized a parameter generator to obtain five parameters for a two-tissue, three-compartment model and generated a time‒activity curve (TAC), calculating images through dynamic PET.

However, the current research predominantly focuses on generating dynamic parametric images by utilizing short-duration PET scans (typically 10–20 min) without considering the direct prediction of imaging data at each moment in a complete dynamic PET scan. In other words, these approaches overlook all the temporal information contained in the given dynamic PET scan and the real-time metabolic processes occurring in the human body. Additionally, the radiopharmaceuticals used in the existing studies are mostly 18F-FDG, and few studies have proposed relevant dynamic frame prediction methods for novel tracers. Sanaat et al. [31] attempted to predict dynamic brain PET frames for the last 65 min based on the first 25 min of dynamic PET data. However, this method results in cumulative errors as the number of predicted frames increases, which may impose greater limitations on the ability to predict total-body dynamic PET images because of the diverse metabolic peaks associated with different organs throughout the body.

With these considerations in mind, we attempted to achieve total-body dynamic frame prediction based on deep learning technologies. Since this task involves the prediction of a PET image of the patient’s entire body, we must consider not only individual differences but also the impacts of the different metabolic rates of various organs on the prediction process of the model. Therefore, our method is trained on a large amount of complete 1-hour total-body dynamic PET data, attempting to predict the middle 40 min of imaging using only the first 10 min of dynamic PET images (the uptake phase) and the PET images from minutes 50 to 60 (the plateau phase), thereby reducing the required scanning time. As a result, patients only need to undergo scanning for the first 10 min after injection, and then, after a 40-minute break, they undergo another 10-minute scan to obtain a complete 1-hour dynamic PET sequence and corresponding parametric images. This significantly reduces the burden imposed on patients while ensuring the accuracy of the prediction results.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to predict intermediate frames in total-body dynamic PET imaging scenarios. Owing to its extensive coverage, total-body PET imaging avoids the potential omission of major lesions and provides comprehensive diagnostic information. However, in addition to interindividual metabolic differences, the total-body PET imaging prediction task must also consider the differences between organs. Therefore, traditional methods that rely solely on early frames for PET prediction may face challenges. Unlike the previous methods, our approach utilizes interpolation to mitigate frame prediction errors, and experiments were conducted on subjects who were injected with 68Ga-FAPI and 68Ga-PSMA. Both the visual and quantitative results demonstrated that our method could not only restore the complete metabolic process of the entire human body but also generate correct parametric images (68Ga-FAPI:, 68Ga-PSMA: ) through a pharmacokinetic model.

The benefits of our study can be summarized from three perspectives. (1) For the first time, we present a total-body dynamic PET frame prediction technique to complete a full 1-hour dynamic PET frame sequence. We propose a bidirectional dynamic frame prediction network by utilizing PET frame sequences acquired from the first 0–10 min and the last 50–60 min postinjection to predict intermediate frame sequences, effectively reducing the required scan time. (2) Our experiments focused on exploring multiple novel radiotracer agents. Although the novel radiotracer agents 68Ga-FAPI and 68Ga-PSMA have shown promising imaging results in clinical settings, they remain underexplored in the current research landscape. In this study, we introduced samples with these two types of tracers to investigate their potential for use in deep learning. (3) We incorporated temporal interpolation technology into the Cycle-GAN to achieve dynamic frame prediction. We generated sequence frames with equal time intervals according to the PET imaging protocol and designed a twin-subgenerator structure to bidirectionally predict sequence frames, significantly improving the resulting imaging quality and the accuracy of the SUV changes induced over time in highly metabolic regions. This enhanced the robustness and stability of the network training process. https://github.com/OPMZZZ/BDF-NET/.

Dynamic PET image acquisition

Dynamic PET images were reconstructed via the ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm equipped with a point spread function (PSF). The reconstruction process was implemented with 4 iterations and 20 subsets with time of flight (TOF). Attenuation, scatter, and randomness corrections were also included in the reconstruction process. The thickness of each PET slice was 2.9 mm, and the reconstructed image size was fixed at 192 × 192. Moreover, the dynamic protocol consisted of 92 frames, including 2 s × 30, 5 s × 12, 10 s × 6, 30 s × 4, 60 s × 25 and 120 s × 15 frames. Due to the uneven time intervals contained in the 1-hour dynamic imaging sequence, we performed interval-weighted image merging, resulting in a complete 1-hour dynamic PET acquisition sequence with a size of 120 s × 30. Pharmacokinetic parametric image acquisition

The pharmacokinetic parametric images of and were acquired via the 2-tissue compartmental model (2TCM) and the Patlak graphical model (PGM), respectively. The dynamic 68Ga-FAPI PET data were used to calculate the images, whereas the 68Ga-PSMA dynamic PET data were used to calculate the images. For both parametric analysis processes, blood input functions were extracted from the reconstructed images based on the regions of the descending aorta.

Pharmacodynamic characteristics of the investigated tracers

68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-FAPI are two receptor‒ligand-type PET imaging tracers labeled with 68Ga. Generally, we consider the process by which receptor‒ligand PET tracers bind to the cell surface to be reversible. However, for 68Ga-PSMA, during a dynamic scan lasting approximately 60 min, there is virtually no retrograde transport of the drug molecules that bind to cell surface receptors or those that enter the cells and return to the interstitium [32]. Therefore, under a 60-minute dynamic PET scanning protocol, 68Ga-PSMA can be regarded as an irreversible tracer, and kinetic parameters such as can be calculated via the Patlak model [33]. In contrast, during dynamic PET scanning with 68Ga-FAPI, the binding of the drug to cell surface receptors is fully reversible, meaning that drug molecules can detach from the receptors and return to the interstitium at any time [34]. Thus, when performing 2TCM analyses of 68Ga-FAPI kinetics, the parameter cannot be constrained to 0. Furthermore, the Patlak model is not applicable to this tracer; instead, the Logan model should be used for analysis purposes [35].

Implementation details

To train the deep learning model, we selected two patients from each tracer group as the test data, ensuring that these test data were not included in the training process. The remaining data constituted our training set, with the 68Ga-FAPI group consisting of 11 training samples and the 68Ga-PSMA group comprising 22 training samples. We utilized the 5-fold cross-validation method for training, dividing the training data into five parts. In each iteration, one part was designated the validation set, whereas the other four parts formed the training set. This process was repeated until each part had been used once as the validation set. At the end of the training process, we selected the model that demonstrated the best performance during the testing phase as the final model for our study. The network was trained for 200 epochs with a batch size of 16. The initial learning rate was set to 0.0004, and the learning rate decayed at a rate of 0.99 per epoch. The model was optimized with the adaptive moment estimation (ADAM) optimizer. All the experiments were conducted via the PyTorch framework (version 1.11.0) on a system with Ubuntu 20.04 and a 48-GB NVIDIA GeForce RTX A6000 GPU.

ROI‑based assessment

In dynamic PET imaging scenarios, regions with higher voxel intensity generally indicate higher uptake activity and are referred to as high-uptake regions. These areas are crucial to focus on, as they represent tissues with elevated drug metabolism [3]. Therefore, further validating the accuracy of the proposed dynamic PET frame prediction method in terms of predicting high-metabolism regions is essential. Different body regions with high metabolic activity were selected as ROIs, and a statistical analysis was performed on the voxel intensities within these ROIs.

As shown in Fig. 5, we identified high-uptake regions in the axial view using red boxes (spanning 5 slices in height) as ROIs. These high-uptake regions include the submandibular gland, parotid gland, kidneys, bladder, and rectum, all of which were classified as nonpathological high-uptake areas. In contrast, the prostate in the PSMA group and the pancreas in the FAPI group were classified as pathological regions. We subsequently conducted a violin plot analysis of the voxel intensity distribution within these ROIs and compared it with the ground truths. The results confirmed a high degree of similarity between the intensity distributions of the generated images and the ground truth. Specifically, the median, upper quartile, and lower quartile of the voxel intensities in the generated images closely resembled those in the ground truth. Building upon this analysis, we further examined the correlations within the ROIs and performed a t test. The Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) was utilized as the correlation metric. The results demonstrated a strong correlation between the generated images and ground truths, with PCC values exceeding 0.95. Moreover, the t test revealed no significant differences, with a p value less than 0.01.

Discussion

Total-body dynamic PET has played an important role in accurate tracer kinetic analyses in physiology, biochemistry, and pharmacology. However, dynamic PET imaging requires a scanning time of up to 1 h, potentially causing patient discomfort and resulting in artifacts in the final images. To reduce the scanning time and alleviate patient discomfort, we proposed a bidirectional dynamic frame prediction network based on a Cycle-GAN-like framework. By leveraging frames 1–6 and 25–30 of the sequence to forecast frames 7–24 in between, we achieved a significant reduction in the scanning time. The resulting images demonstrated remarkable structural similarity to the ground-truth images, and the predictions of the SUV variations occurring in high-uptake regions over time exhibited exceptional precision. Furthermore, for a predicted complete 1-hour dynamic sequence, integrating pharmacokinetic models enabled the production of high-quality parametric images. These images maintained a high standard in terms of both their overall visual aesthetics and a quantitative evaluation of their image quality.

In terms of dataset size, we collected total-body PET dynamic imaging data from 24 subjects injected with 68Ga-PSMA and 13 subjects injected with 68Ga-FAPI. As 68Ga-FAPI and 68Ga-PSMA are emerging tracers, their limited availability constrained our data collection efforts. Existing studies have indicated that FAPI and PSMA have greater imaging sensitivity than FDG does in specific PET/CT scenarios [12, 13]. In the future, more patients requiring PET scans will benefit from these advanced tracers, and we plan to collect additional patient data in subsequent studies. Static 18F-FDG PET imaging for subjects has been widely studied in the field of deep learning; however, research on subjects imaged using 68Ga-FAPI and 68Ga-PSMA is currently lacking. Consequently, our current work utilized deep learning techniques to explore the prediction potential of dynamic PET images, focusing specifically on subjects imaged using 68Ga-FAPI and 68Ga-PSMA. In future work, we will also include dynamic 18F-FDG imaging data to further validate the robustness of our methods.

With respect to image registration, in our frame prediction method, two 10-minute PET scans were conducted on patients, unavoidably introducing challenges related to image registration. This may be attributed to potential factors such as patient movements, pose changes, or organ shape variations between the two scans, leading to incomplete image alignment. As this was an exploratory study, experienced nuclear medicine technicians screened the training samples during the data preprocessing stage, excluding those samples that were misaligned due to motion. Therefore, this experiment focused solely on the ideal scenario in which all frames were perfectly aligned. Considering the importance of image registration, in future research, we plan to incorporate registration techniques such as mutual information registration [37, 38] or deformation field registration [39, 40] as preprocessing steps for input images to increase the accuracy of the model training process. The introduction of these mechanisms may contribute to ensuring the spatiotemporal consistency of dynamic PET images, ultimately improving the robustness and performance of the proposed model.

Regarding the network architecture, our network employs an architecture similar to that of the Cycle-GAN to maintain structural similarity between its inputs and outputs, utilizing a generator based on the traditional U-Net structure. The proposed network architecture still has considerable room for improvement. In future work, we plan to incorporate transformer modules into the network to enhance its performance when the input dataset has a sufficient sample size. To capture temporal information, we enhanced the sensitivity of the discriminator to temporal cues by creating samples with incorrect interframe relationships. In future iterations, we plan to incorporate structures such as long short-term memory [41] (LSTM) into the network to strengthen its understanding of the temporal information contained between the input frames.

Besides, our study shares similarities with previous research, particularly that of Liang et al. [30], as both aim to predict parametric images from dynamic PET data. While Liang et al.‘s work primarily focuses on predicting lung parametric images during the first 30 min—a domain with established methods [25–27, 29]—our approach goes further by predicting dynamic PET data over a full hour and generating the corresponding parametric images. Additionally, our experiments involve total-body PET data, enabling a more comprehensive analysis of metabolic activity across the entire body rather than being confined to a single region, such as the lungs. Furthermore, our method supports parametric image generation via pharmacokinetic models, providing dual functionality that enhances its potential clinical applications.

Finally, in our experiments, we observed significant voxel intensity differences between the high-uptake regions and other areas in the dynamic total-body PET scans. Additionally, the high-uptake regions varied significantly among different subjects. However, the high-uptake regions constituted only a small portion of the total body slices, which limited the prediction process of the network. In future work, by collecting sufficient subject data, we can fine-tune the model specifically for lesion areas, significantly improving the prediction accuracy of the network.

Our work is currently an exploratory study on the use of frame prediction methods to reduce the required total-body PET scan time. Therefore, in this study, we could not directly compare our approach with other state-of-the-art algorithms [42–44]. As mentioned above, our approach faces challenges such as the size of novel tracer datasets, image registration between two scans, and network architecture optimization. However, as an exploratory study, our method has preliminarily revealed the possibility of performing total-body PET frame prediction, offering ideas for developing new methods that can shorten the duration of total-body PET scans. In future work, we plan to refine the network architecture and experimental data by introducing comparative experiments involving advanced networks to further fine-tune the model.

Conclusion

In this study, to reduce the required scanning duration and alleviate the patient burden imposed during dynamic PET imaging, we proposed a bidirectional dynamic frame prediction network based on the Cycle-GAN framework with the initial and final 10 min of total-body PET imaging data as inputs (tracers: 68Ga-PSMA and 68Ga-FAPI). The experimental results illustrated the effectiveness of our approach. The generated dynamic PET images exhibited PSNRs of 36.056 ± 0.709 dB for the 68Ga-PSMA group and 33.779 ± 0.760 dB for the 68Ga-FAPI group. Additionally, the SSIM reached 0.922 ± 0.009 for the 68Ga-PSMA group and 0.935 ± 0.006 for the 68Ga-FAPI group. By applying a quantitative analysis to the parametric images, we obtained PSNRs of 36.155 ± 4.813 dB (68Ga-PSMA, ) and 43.150 ± 4.102 dB (68Ga-FAPI, ). The obtained SSIM values were 0.932 ± 0.041 (68Ga-PSMA, ) and 0.980 ± 0.011 (68Ga-FAPI, ), respectively. Moreover, an ROI analysis conducted on the generated entire dynamic PET sequences also illustrated that our method enables the accurate prediction of temporal voxel intensity changes and maintains overall visual consistency with the ground truth. In future works, we will attempt to validate the proposed method with more total-body PET/CT systems in clinical practice.
