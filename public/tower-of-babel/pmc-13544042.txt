David-V Procedure Combined with Zone 2 Arch Replacement for Acute Stanford Type A Dissection
Authors: István Gecse, Maria Guadalupe Lopez Trevino, Marcell Székely, Tomasso Cambiaghi, Ziyab Sarfaraz, Yuncen Atticus He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Valve-sparing aortic root replacement is increasingly used in selected patients with acute aortic dissection and preserved valve anatomy. We present the case of a 52-year-old man with acute Stanford type A aortic dissection extending from the aortic root to the iliac bifurcation with arch involvement; this was complicated by severe aortic insufficiency and right lower extremity malperfusion.The patient underwent emergent valve-sparing root replacement using the David technique combined with zone 2 arch reconstruction under hypothermic circulatory arrest and selective antegrade cerebral perfusion. Intraoperative echocardiography confirmed excellent valve function with trace residual regurgitation. The postoperative course was notable for transient vasoplegia and re-exploration without bleeding, followed by uneventful recovery and discharge on postoperative day 11.This case highlights the feasibility of combining valve-sparing root replacement with zone 2 arch reconstruction in the acute setting of Stanford type A dissection with malperfusion, allowing restoration of true lumen flow while preserving native valve function.

Introduction

Acute Stanford type A aortic dissection remains one of the most time-sensitive surgical emergencies in cardiovascular surgery, associated with high early mortality if not promptly recognized and treated. Involvement of the aortic root, arch, and branch vessels is common and frequently dictates the extent and complexity of operative repair.

Management of the aortic root in the setting of acute dissection with aortic insufficiency continues to be debated, particularly when valve morphology is preserved and regurgitation is functional in nature. While composite valve replacement has traditionally been favored in many centers, valve-sparing root replacement may offer the advantage of preserving native valve function and avoiding long-term prosthesis-related complications in appropriately selected patients.

Similarly, the optimal extent of arch replacement in Stanford type A dissection remains variable, ranging from hemiarch replacement to more extensive arch reconstruction. Increasing adoption of zone-based arch replacement strategies and selective antegrade cerebral perfusion has enabled more aggressive and durable proximal aortic repair while maintaining cerebral protection.

Case Presentation

A 52-year-old male with a history of hypertension presented following sudden onset tearing chest pain after lifting a heavy object at work. The symptoms began approximately midday the day prior to presentation and were accompanied by transient right lower extremity weakness affecting ambulation. The patient denied back, abdominal, or persistent lower extremity pain.

He initially presented to an outside hospital, where computed tomography angiography demonstrated an acute Stanford type A aortic dissection. He was hemodynamically stable on presentation and reportedly had palpable distal pulses in all extremities at that time. He was then transferred to our institution for definitive surgical management.

On arrival, physical examination demonstrated intact upper extremity pulses and palpable left lower extremity pulses; however, right distal pulses were not palpable and no Doppler signals were detectable. Motor and sensory examination of the bilateral lower extremities was grossly intact at the time of evaluation. These findings raised concern for malperfusion of the right lower extremity.

Review of computed tomography angiography demonstrated a Stanford type A aortic dissection extending from the aortic root to the iliac bifurcation, with near occlusion of the right external iliac artery and distal reconstitution (Figure 1). All major visceral vessels arose from the true lumen with the exception of the right renal artery, which demonstrated involvement of the dissection flap. The aortic root measured 4.8 cm to 5.2 cm, the ascending aorta measured 4.6 cm, and the arch demonstrated segmental dilation (zone 0: 3.9 cm; zone 2: 3.2 cm; zone 3: 3.3 cm). The mid descending thoracic aorta measured 3.0 cm.

Cardiovascular assessment demonstrated preserved biventricular systolic function. The aortic valve was trileaflet with severe aortic insufficiency secondary to prolapse of a dissection flap through the right and noncoronary sinuses, with preserved leaflet morphology and no evidence of calcification or thickening.

Given the extent of dissection involving the aortic root, arch, and branch vessels, as well as evidence of malperfusion and severe aortic insufficiency, the patient was taken emergently for operative repair after multidisciplinary discussion and informed consent.

Operative Technique

Following median sternotomy and systemic heparinization, cardiopulmonary bypass was established using central arterial cannulation of the ascending aorta under transesophageal echocardiographic guidance with confirmation of true lumen access, and right atrial multi-stage venous drainage. A left ventricular vent was placed via the right superior pulmonary vein. Cooling was initiated to a target nasopharyngeal temperature of 24°C.

Given the extent of aortic root aneurysm, a valve-sparing aortic root replacement was planned. Intraoperative inspection confirmed a trileaflet aortic valve with preserved leaflet morphology and no calcification, supporting a reimplantation (David) procedure (Video 1).

After cross-clamping and aortic transection, cardioplegic arrest was achieved via selective antegrade delivery into the coronary ostia. The aortic root was mobilized with identification and isolation of the left and right coronary buttons. Inspection demonstrated a trileaflet valve with preserved leaflet morphology and satisfactory coaptation, supporting a valve-sparing strategy. Graft sizing was determined using both annular measurements and the height of the left–noncoronary commissure, and a 30 mm Valsalva graft was selected. Subannular pledgeted sutures were placed circumferentially and secured within the graft, followed by commissural resuspension at the neosinotubular junction. A hemostatic suture line was then completed for all three sinuses. Valve competency was assessed with saline testing and found to be satisfactory without the need for cusp repair. The left and right coronary buttons were subsequently reimplanted into the graft in standard fashion.

Attention was then directed to the aortic arch. After reaching target hypothermia, circulatory arrest was initiated and the cross-clamp was removed. The aortic canula was removed, and the ascending aorta was excised to zone 2. Inspection of the arch demonstrated no intimal tear. Although the primary intimal tear was confined to the ascending aorta, a zone 2 arch replacement strategy was selected due to the patient’s young age, the presence of distal malperfusion, mild segmental arch dilatation, and to establish a durable proximal landing zone for potential future thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) should downstream intervention become necessary. Bilateral selective antegrade cerebral perfusion was instituted via the innominate and left common carotid arteries at approximately 900 mL/min with continuous cerebral oximetry monitoring.

A 28/14/8 × 10 mm Gelweave branched arch graft (Terumo Aortic) was used for reconstruction. The distal anastomosis was performed at zone 2 using a running suture technique with felt reinforcement. Following completion of the distal anastomosis, the branched graft was de-aired, and a cross-clamp was applied proximal to the first side branch of the graft. Lower body perfusion was resumed via the side branch of the graft, and systemic rewarming was initiated.

The proximal graft-to-graft anastomosis was then completed between the neo-root and the arch graft. Coronary button anastomoses were inspected and hemostasis was secured. The innominate and left common carotid arteries were sequentially reimplanted to the respective graft branches using running anastomoses. De-airing was performed prior to removal of the aortic cross-clamp.

Transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated a well-functioning reconstructed aortic valve with only trace residual aortic insufficiency, preserved biventricular function, and no evidence of regional wall motion abnormalities following revascularization.

After separation from cardiopulmonary bypass, hemostasis was achieved and mediastinal drainage was placed. The patient was transferred to the cardiothoracic intensive care unit in critical but stable condition.

Postoperative Course

On the day of the operation, the patient returned to the operating room for mediastinal exploration due to high chest tube output, with no active surgical bleeding identified. He subsequently remained hemodynamically stable but required vasoactive and ventilatory support in the setting of vasoplegia and acute postoperative critical illness.

In the early postoperative period, he demonstrated progressive hemodynamic improvement with resolution of vasoplegia and improving end-organ perfusion. He was successfully extubated following optimization of volume status with aggressive diuresis for postoperative fluid overload.

Blood pressure was initially managed with intravenous nicardipine and esmolol to provide strict anti-impulse therapy, targeting a systolic blood pressure below 130 mmHg. As the patient’s condition stabilized, antihypertensive therapy was transitioned to oral medications, including a beta-blocker, while maintaining adequate blood pressure control. The patient’s respiratory status continued to improve, with eventual discontinuation of noninvasive ventilatory support and supplemental oxygen. Temporary pacing wires were removed without incident. Neurologic examination remained intact throughout hospitalization.

With continued clinical recovery, the patient was transferred from the intensive care unit to the telemetry floor, where he underwent pulmonary rehabilitation and progressive mobilization. Renal function remained stable, and no neurologic deficits were observed. Surgical sites remained clean, dry, and intact.

Prior to discharge, follow-up computed tomography angiography demonstrated successful valve-sparing root replacement and zone 2 arch reconstruction with restoration of true lumen flow and patency of the reconstructed arch vessels (Figure 2).

The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 11 in stable condition, tolerating oral intake, ambulating independently, and demonstrating continued recovery. Outpatient follow-up with vascular surgery was arranged for evaluation of potential delayed endovascular intervention, and genetic testing for heritable aortopathy was recommended.

At 3-month outpatient follow-up, the patient remained clinically well and denied recurrent chest pain, neurologic symptoms, or lower extremity ischemic complaints. He continued to recover without evidence of recurrent malperfusion. A staged TEVAR had been planned by the vascular surgery team at an outside institution but had not been performed.

Discussion

Acute Stanford type A aortic dissection involving the aortic root and arch presents a complex surgical challenge in which operative strategy must be individualized according to the extent of disease, valve pathology, and the patient’s clinical condition. Despite substantial advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management, these procedures continue to carry considerable morbidity and mortality, emphasizing the importance of timely intervention and meticulous operative planning.1

This case demonstrates the feasibility of combining valve-sparing aortic root replacement with zone 2 arch reconstruction in the acute setting of Stanford type A dissection complicated by lower extremity malperfusion. In many emergency situations, surgeons may favor composite root replacement or a more limited hemiarch repair to minimize operative complexity and myocardial ischemic time. However, this case illustrates that a more comprehensive reconstructive strategy can be safely performed in carefully selected patients when supported by appropriate institutional expertise and cerebral protection techniques.

The decision to perform a valve-sparing root replacement was based on intraoperative findings demonstrating preserved trileaflet valve morphology without intrinsic leaflet pathology. The mechanism of aortic insufficiency was functional, resulting from prolapse of the dissected commissural support rather than structural leaflet degeneration. In this setting, preservation of the native valve avoids prosthesis-related complications, including lifelong anticoagulation or structural valve degeneration, while maintaining favorable native valve hemodynamics. Previous studies have demonstrated that valve-sparing root replacement can be safely performed in selected patients presenting with acute type A dissection, with satisfactory early and mid-term outcomes.23 Nevertheless, this approach requires careful patient selection and should be avoided in the presence of significant leaflet destruction, annular disruption, or profound hemodynamic instability, where a more expeditious composite root replacement may be more appropriate.

The extent of arch reconstruction represents another important operative decision. Hemiarch replacement remains an appropriate strategy when the primary intimal tear can be completely resected. More recently, zone 2 arch replacement has gained increasing acceptance in selected patients because it establishes a durable proximal landing zone for potential future TEVAR. Comparative data have demonstrated similar early mortality and long-term survival following hemiarch and zone 2 arch replacement. Although distal aortic reinterventions were more frequent after zone 2 repair, these were predominantly managed with TEVAR rather than open surgery, consistent with the role of zone 2 reconstruction in facilitating staged endovascular management of residual distal aortic disease.4

In the present case, the primary intimal tear was confined to the ascending aorta, and no intimal tear was identified within the aortic arch. Therefore, zone 2 arch replacement was not necessary to achieve complete tear resection. Rather, the decision reflected the patient’s relatively young age, persistent distal dissection associated with lower extremity malperfusion, and the anticipated possibility of future distal aortic intervention, for which a durable proximal landing zone would facilitate subsequent TEVAR if required. Bilateral selective antegrade cerebral perfusion provided reliable cerebral protection, allowing safe completion of the more extensive reconstruction without neurological injury.

Malperfusion further complicates the management of acute Stanford type A dissection and remains one of the strongest predictors of adverse outcomes. Data from the International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection have consistently demonstrated that malperfusion syndromes significantly increase perioperative morbidity and mortality.1 In the present patient, restoration of true lumen flow following proximal aortic repair resulted in complete resolution of lower extremity ischemia without the need for adjunctive peripheral revascularization, illustrating that correction of the proximal pathology alone may successfully resolve dynamic malperfusion.

This case underscores the importance of an individualized operative strategy in acute Stanford type A dissection. Rather than applying a uniform surgical approach, operative planning should incorporate patient age, valve morphology, aortic anatomy, malperfusion status, and the anticipated need for future distal aortic intervention. In appropriately selected patients and experienced centers, valve-sparing root replacement combined with zone 2 arch reconstruction can preserve the native aortic valve while facilitating future treatment of the residual dissected aorta without compromising early clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that in selected patients with acute Stanford type A aortic dissection, valve-sparing root replacement combined with zone 2 arch reconstruction is a feasible and effective operative strategy, even in the presence of severe aortic insufficiency and malperfusion. Careful intraoperative assessment of valve integrity and perfusion status is essential in guiding operative decision-making. A tailored, anatomy-driven approach may allow for durable repair without compromising early outcomes.
