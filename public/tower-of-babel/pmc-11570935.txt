Low prevalence of borderline oxacillin resistant Staphylococcus aureus (BORSA) in a tertiary care hospital in South Carolina
Authors: Connor Horne, Gabrielle DiMattia, Nicholas Perkins, Prerana Roth
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrompt treatment for Staphylococcus aureus bloodstream infections is often dependent on known diagnostic testing modalities to differentiate between methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). Borderline-oxacillin resistant S. aureus (BORSA), a rare, non-mecA mediated phenotype, has unclear resistance mechanisms but potentially significant consequences as it is frequently misidentified as MSSA but behaves more like MRSA. A retrospective analysis was performed of MSSA bloodstream infections to determine the prevalence of BORSA. Our institution found BORSA prevalence of 0.1%, consistent with literature. Though prevalence is low, due to unclear mechanisms and unreliable detection methods, BORSA may pose a therapeutic and epidemiological threat.Keywords: Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia, BORSA (borderline oxacillin resistant Staphylococcus aureus), Prevalence

Introduction

Ranging from simple cellulitis to fatal endovascular infections, Staphylococcus aureus is accountable for a substantial portion of nosocomial infections with high mortality rates, sometimes as high as 30% [1]. Discovered in the 1920's and introduced in 1940's, penicillin played a pivotal role in the treatment of staphylococcal infections. Unfortunately, S. aureus has adapted multiple mechanisms to ensure its survival. By 1960, 80% of strains were resistant to penicillin through penicillinase enzymes that break down the beta-lactam region of penicillin [2]. Penicillinase-resistant penicillins, such as oxacillin, nafcillin, and methicillin, are used as first line therapy to treat methicillin-sensitive staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) infections. However, alternative penicillin-binding proteins, PBP2a or PB2⸍, encoded by mecA or mecC genes, have resulted in methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) isolates which are typically treated with vancomycin or daptomycin and are felt to sometimes cause more severe infections [[2], [3], [4]].

Borderline oxacillin resistant S. aureus (BORSA) has low level penicillinase-resistant penicillin resistance. This phenotype lacks a precise definition and mechanistic understanding but is characterized by the absence of an altered PBP2a encoded by mecA or C gene which confers methicillin resistance in MRSA as stated above. BORSA is often reflected by an oxacillin MIC of ≥ 2 μg/mL but has been cited to range anywhere from 1-8 μg/mL [2,3,5]. The most common theories of resistance mechanisms include hyperproduction of beta-lactamases and different modifications in the penicillin binding proteins due to spontaneous amino acid substitutions. The clinical relevance of these mechanisms is of debate. However, ß-lactam antibiotics are not proven to be effective treatment for severe BORSA infections and reports of treatment failures exist [3,4,6,7].

This paper aims to categorise the prevalence and clinical outcomes of BORSA bacteraemia in our tertiary care healthcare system in South Carolina, Prisma Health-Upstate.

Methods

Prisma Health-Upstate is a non-profit health system comprising 1600 hospital beds spanning 8 hospitals across the northwestern third of South Carolina bordering the Appalachian Mountains. In this retrospective analysis, we included all adult (age ≥ 18 years old) patients hospitalised with MSSA bacteraemia between 1/1/2020–1/1/2023 to identify those who met criteria for BORSA infection. We defined BORSA as S. aureus isolates with an oxacillin MIC of ≥ 2 μg/mL. No isolates were excluded. During this study period, 1,355 patients with MSSA bacteraemia were identified. We reviewed the oxacillin MICs obtained via routine susceptibility testing using Vitek2® (bioMérieux Inc.) Gram-positive susceptibility card. All isolates were initially tested via PCR to assess for the presence of mecA/C genes, for which they were all absent. During the study period, cefoxitin susceptibility testing had not yet been routinely implemented and thus is not available.

Results

We identified 1,355 unique patients with bacteraemia classified as MSSA. Of these, 2 patients met our criteria for BORSA. Both had an oxacillin MIC of 2 and were negative for mecA/C genes. Cefoxitin testing was not done during the study period at our institution.

The first patient had a prolonged hospitalisation due to acute respiratory distress syndrome due to COVID-19. The source of BORSA bacteraemia was unclear but suspected to be related to either a bacterial pneumonia or a central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI). The patient's blood cultures remained positive for three days while on IV cefazolin therapy prior to a transition to comfort care by her family. The second patient had BORSA bacteraemia secondary to native hip septic arthritis. He underwent a washout for source control and more than four weeks of IV cefazolin treatment. At the end of treatment, his inflammatory markers remained elevated with ongoing pain. The antibiotic treatment was changed to oral cefadroxil and he was referred to orthopaedic surgery for evaluation for possible repeat surgical intervention. The patient has been lost to follow-up since that time.

Discussion

In this study, we analysed recent data (1/1/2020–1/1/2023) to assess the prevalence of BORSA among bloodstream infections categorised as MSSA by traditional methods in a large tertiary healthcare centre in South Carolina. Our prevalence rate of 0.1% indicates that BORSA infections are stable compared to historical values of < 1–12.5% indicating no significant increase in infection rates over time [[2], [3], [4]].

The clinical course of S. aureus bacteraemia is usually complicated by multiple factors including inadequate source control and comorbidities [1]. Thus, we cannot conclude that the two patients with BORSA infection in our health system had suboptimal outcomes due to antibiotic choice as comorbidities and source control were still factors contributing to their clinical course. Additionally, a limitation of this study was its retrospective nature and thus definitive conclusions regarding causation of outcomes cannot be determined.

This study's purpose was to identify the prevalence of BORSA in South Carolina among one of the largest healthcare systems. Fortunately, the prevalence remains low. However, it is evident that more research is needed to accurately recognise and define BORSA. At our institution we have added screening to for cefoxitin resistance as well for S. aureus isolates.

Clinicians should be aware of BORSA as a unique entity that may require alternative therapy such as higher doses of beta-lactam antibiotics or a different class of antibiotic such as vancomycin or daptomycin, similar to how MRSA is treated. Further research is needed to clearly delineate the clinical courses and optimal treatment options for BORSA infections.

Going forward, it may be prudent to assess the oxacillin MIC of S. aureus isolates and if ≥ 2μg/mL, further testing should be employed along with potential alteration of treatment regimen, especially in complicated, difficult-to-treat infections.
