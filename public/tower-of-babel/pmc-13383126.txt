Elevated CDYL expression in the peripheral blood of patients with rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Mengxue Xu, Xueli Xia, Jun Yang, Lin Zhao, Juan Xu, Shengjun Wang, Dawei Cui, Huiyong Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChromodomain Y-like protein (CDYL), a member of the CDY-related gene family, plays a pivotal role in nervous diseases, reproductive systems and tumors by chromatin remodeling and disrupting normal transcriptional regulation. However, the expression and biological functions of CDYL in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) remain unclear. This study aims to investigated the role of CDYL in the pathogenesis of RA.MethodsA cohort of 43 RA patients and 36 healthy subjects were included in the study. The transcript levels of CDYL, interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A) and IL-22 were detected by quantitative real-time PCR. Flow cytometry and Western blot were used to analyze the relationship between CDYL and Th17 cells. The GSE100191 dataset from the GEO database was downloaded to conduct a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of the potential functions of CDYL. The interaction of CDYL and EZH2 and the effects of the EZH2 antagonist on Th17 cells were measured by Western blot.ResultsThe CDYL was highly expressed in the PBMCs derived from RA patients, exhibiting positive correlations with DAS28 score (r = 0.36, p < 0.0177) and serum anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody titers (r = 0.4255, p = 0.004). Moreover, the transcript levels of CDYL were positively correlated with elevated IL-17 A levels (r = 0.5140, p < 0.001) and IL-22 (r = 0.4540, p = 0.002) levels in RA patients and the RA dataset. CDYL expression is markedly upregulated in Th17 cells, while the proportion of Th17 cells is increased in RA patients. Mechanistically, CDYL knockdown significantly inhibited Th17 differentiation in vitro, which suggests that CDYL is associated with and functionally contributes to the differentiation of Th17 cells.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that CDYL expression is associated with and promotes the differentiation of Th17 cells in RA patients and may be involved in the process of RA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12865-026-00847-3.

Introduction

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease mainly characterized by chronic, symmetrical and erosive joint inflammation [1]. The core mechanism of RA is that the immune system mistakenly attacks its own joint tissues, generating inflammatory responses targeting the synovial membrane, cartilage and bone. Specifically, it is manifested as autoantibodies, such as rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody (anti-CCP antibody), which are important markers for RA diagnosis [2, 3]. The abnormal activation of immune cells such as T cells, B cells, and macrophages is a core process of RA pathogenesis. These cells jointly drive joint inflammation and destruction by releasing inflammatory factors, generating autoantibodies, and forming immune complex [4]. As an important type of pro-inflammatory cells, Th17 cells are extensively studied in RA for their role in secreting pro-inflammatory factors IL-17 and IL-22, directly activating synovial cells, macrophages, and osteoclasts, thereby promoting pina formation and joint damage [5, 6]. To date, the regulatory mechanisms governing Th17 cells dysfunction in RA remain incompletely elucidated.

The chromodomain-containing transcriptional corepressor protein (CDYL), protein is composed of the chromodomain at the N-terminal and the enyl-coA hydrase/isomerase catalytic domain at the carboxyl terminal, which has been involved in the transcriptional repression and epigenetic modification [7]. Previous studies have shown that CDYL can collaborate with Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 (EZH2) promote the establishment and propagation of trimethylation of histone H3 at the lysine 27 (H3K27me3) site on chromatin and mediate the interaction between G9a and RE1-silenced transcription factors (REST), thereby forming a REST/CDYL/G9a complex that inhibits gene expression [8–10]. While EZH2 has been confirmed to be related to the imbalance of Th17/Treg cells [11]. Recently, CDYL plays a pivotal role in multiple biological processes, including tumorigenesis, spermatogenesis, and nervous system [12–14]. However, the relationship between CDYL and Th17 cells in RA are poorly understood.

In our study, we examined the expression of CDYL in PBMCs from RA patients and analyzed its association with cytokines secreted by Th17 cells. On this basis, the levels of CDYL in Th17 cells were detected to further verify its effect on the differentiation of Th17 cells. Using this approach, our study aims to elucidate the biological behaviors of CDYL in patients with RA and investigate its potential roles in the pathogenesis of RA.

Material and animal

Subjects and models

A total of 43 patients with RA from Jiangsu University Affiliated People’s Hospital were enrolled. All patients were hospitalized between July 2024 and October 2025 in the Department of Rheumatology. The diagnosis of RA followed the new criteria of the American College of Rheumatology and the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) in 2010. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) suffering from other systemic inflammatory autoimmune diseases (such as systemic lupus erythematosus, vasculitis, etc.), or severe extra-articular diseases secondary to RA (such as active vasculitis, interstitial lung disease); (2) had comorbid conditions including: hematological disorders, hypertension or heart disease, tumors or severe infections; (3) pregnant women, and those who required treatment with drugs containing sodium or potassium were also excluded from the study. We also recruited 36 healthy controls (HCs), age- and sex-matched to RA patients.

Seven to eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (weight 20 ± 2 g) were obtained from CAVENS (Changzhou, China) and were raised in a Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) environment, under conditions of 25 °C and a 12-hour light-dark cycle.

Laboratory indicators detection

Clinical and laboratory parameters were retrospectively collected from clinical databases. General clinical data included age and sex. Serological parameters included inflammatory indicator C-reactive protein (CRP), anti-streptolysin O (ΑSO), erythrocyte sedimentation rates (ESR), and the levels of RF, anti-CCP.

PBMCs isolation

Human PBMCs were isolated from peripheral blood by Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation. Briefly, EDTA-anticoagulated blood was centrifuged at 500 × g for 30 min and the top layer containing plasma was removed. The remaining blood was diluted with an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline, pH 7.4 (PBS), The diluted blood was layered over the same amount of the Ficoll-Paque solution (Cytiva, 75442031). Gradients were centrifuged at 500 × g for 30 min at room temperature with slow acceleration and deceleration. The PBMCs interface was carefully removed by pipetting and washed with PBS by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min. PBMCs pellets were suspended in ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysing buffer (Thermo Fisher, A1049201) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature with gentle mixing to lyse contaminating red blood cells (RBCs). This was followed by a wash with PBS.

Isolation of murine naive CD4+ T cells

Mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. Spleens were harvested aseptically and placed into cold PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 10082147) and 1 mM EDTA (hereafter referred to as sorting buffer). Spleens were gently mashed through a 70 μm nylon cell strainer (Corning, 352350) using the plunger end of a 5 mL syringe. The strainer was rinsed with 10 mL of sorting buffer. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min at 4 °C. RBCs were lysed by resuspending the pellet in 5 mL of ACK Lysis Buffer for 5 min at room temperature. The reaction was stopped by adding 5 mL of sorting buffer, and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ cells/mL in sorting buffer. Naïve CD4+ T cells were isolated by immunomagnetic negative selection using the EasySep™ Mouse Naïve CD4 + T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, 19765) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity of the isolated cells was assessed by flow cytometry. Purity was consistently > 90%.

In Vitro Th17 polarization

Purified naïve CD4+ T cells were cultured at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL in 48-well plates (500 µL per well) under Th17-polarizing conditions. Culture medium consisted of RPMI 1640 (Gibco, 11875093) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol. For Th0 conditions, cells were stimulated with plate‑bound anti‑CD3 antibody (5 µg/mL, clone 145‑2C11, BD, 553057) and soluble anti‑CD28 antibody (2 µg/mL, clone 37.51, BD #553294). For Th17‑polarizing conditions, the same anti‑CD3/CD28 stimulation was applied, and the following cytokines and neutralizing antibodies were added to the culture: recombinant human TGF‑β1 (2 ng/mL, Peprotech, 100‑21), recombinant mouse IL‑6 (30 ng/mL, Peprotech, 216‑16), recombinant mouse IL‑23 (30 ng/mL, R&D Systems, 1887‑ML), anti‑IFN‑γ antibody (10 µg/mL, clone XMG1.2, Biolegend, 505812), and anti‑IL‑4 antibody (10 µg/mL, clone 11B11, Biolegend, 504112). Cells were cultured for 3 days at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Flow cytometry

To evaluate the purity of isolated naïve CD4+ T cells, 1 × 10⁵ freshly isolated cells were suspended in PBS. Cells were washed once by centrifugation at 300×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and cells were resuspended in 50 µL of antibody cocktail containing the following antibodies: anti-mouse CD4-PE-Cyanine7 (1:200 dilution, eBioscience, clone RM4-5, 25-0042-82), anti-mouse CD62L-PE (1:200 dilution, eBioscience, clone MEL-14, 12-0621-82), Cells were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark, followed by two washes with 150 µL of FACS buffer. Stained cells were resuspended in 200 µL of FACS buffer and acquired on a flow cytometer.

For intracellular cytokine staining, cultured cells were harvested and stimulated with PMA (50 ng/mL, Sigma, P1585), ionomycin (500 ng/mL, Sigma, I0634), and Brefeldin A (BD GolgiPlug™, 555029) in complete medium for 5 h at 37 °C. Cells were collected, washed, and stained with exclusion. Surface staining was performed with anti-mouse CD4-PE-Cyanine7 or anti-human CD4-PE-Cyanine7 (1:200, eBioscience, clone OKT-4, 25-0048-42) at 4 °C for 30 min. Cells were washed with FACS buffer and then fixed and permeabilized using a fixation/permeabilization kit (eBioscience, 00-5523-00) and according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Intracellular cytokine staining was performed in permeabilization buffer with anti-mouse IL-17 A-PE (1:100, eBioscience, 12-7177-81) or anti-human IL-17 A-PE (1:100, eBioscience, 12-7178-42) at 4 °C for 45 min. For CDYL detection, we used CDYL antibody (2 µg/mL, Proteintech, 17763-1-AP) as primary antibody for 45 min, and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), APC conjugate (Invitrogen, A10931) was used as secondary antibody for 30 min. Cells were washed once with permeabilization buffer, resuspended in FACS buffer, and acquired on a flow cytometer. The gating strategies for all the data are presented in the supplementary materials.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) assay

The PBMCs were lysed by the RNA extraction buffer. The RNA was extracted and purified by RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ESscience, Shanghai, China). A total of 2 µg RNA was employed as the template for first-strand cDNA synthesis. This process was carried out using the Hiscript III Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Subsequently, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) amplification was conducted utilizing the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), following the standard protocols provided by the manufacturer. All samples were then analyzed using the CFX96 Sequence Detection System (Bio-Rad). To determine the relative mRNA expression levels of the target genes, the 2−ΔCt (cycle threshold difference) method was applied. In this approach, the Ct values of the target genes were normalized to those of the endogenous control gene, β-actin. The primer sequences used in this study are detailed as follows: mouse CDYL forward primer: GGCTGCTCTACCGTTATGTTTCCC, mouse CDYL reverse primer: GCCTCCTGTGCCGTCAACTTC; human CDYL forward primer: CAGGATGG-CTTCACCCACAT, human CDYL reverse primer: CTGAGCAGTACCAGCTTGCT; human IL-17 A forward primer: CCTCATTGGTG-TCACTGCTACTG, human IL-17 A reverse primer: TTGTCCTCAGAATTTGGGCATCC; human IL-22 forward primer: CCTATATCACCAACCGCACCTTC, human IL-22 reverse primer: CTGACTCCGTG-GAACAGTTTCTC; human β-actin forward primer: ATTGCCGACAGGATGCAGAA, human β-actin reverse primer: CGGACTCGTCATACTCCTGC; mouse β-actin forward primer: TGGAATC-CTGTGGCATCCATGAAAC, mouse β-actin reverse primer: TAAAACGCAGCTCA-GTAACAGTCCG.

Western blot analysis

T cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing PMSF (Solarbio, Beijing, China) to extract the total protein samples. The protein concentrations of cell or tumor lysates were measured using protein assay reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Protein samples were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were first blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% BSA and then incubate at 4 °C overnight in BSA in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 with anti-CDYL (1:1000, Proteintech, 17763-1-AP, 60 kDa), anti-H3K27me3 (1:1000, CST, 9733, 15 kDa) anti-EZH2 (1:1000, CST technology, 5246, 98 kDa) and anti-GAPDH (1:2000, CST, 97166, 36 kDa), followed by incubating with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The immunoreactivity was visualized with a Western HRP Substrate (Millipore, WBKLS0500) as recommended by the manufacturer using a luminescent image analyzer (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA).

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

The 1 × 107 cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Cleared lysates (500 µg total protein) were pre-incubated with Protein A/G agarose beads (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) for 1 h at 4 °C for pre-clearing. The pre-cleared supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 µg of specific primary antibody against EZH2 (CST technology, 5246) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control), and then conjugated to fresh Protein A/G beads. The beads were then washed four times with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 5× loading buffer. The immunoprecipitated complexes and input lysates were analyzed by Western blot. Of note, for the detection of CDYL, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibody horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG light chain (CST, 93702).

Small interfering (si)RNA transfection

Naive CD4⁺ T cells were transfected with either CDYL‑specific siRNA (sense 5’-3’: GCGGAGACUCAGGUGGAAATT; antisense 5’-3’:UUUCCACCUGAGUCUCC-GCTT) or negative control siRNA (50 nM, Gene Pharma) using the Lipofectamine Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, L3000150) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfected cells were then cultured under Th17‑polarizing conditions for 3 days. Knockdown efficiency was assessed by Western blot analysis. The proportion of Th17 cells was determined by flow cytometry following intracellular staining for IL‑17 A.

Dataset analysis

The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was analyzed by the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). The human CDYL gene was queried to retrieve its experimentally validated and computationally predicted functional partners based on protein‑protein interaction (PPI) networks, co‑expression, co‑occurrence, and text mining evidence. The resulting PPI network was then subjected to GO enrichment analysis using the built‑in functional annotation tool in STRING. Enrichment was calculated for GO Biological Process (BP). The significance of enrichment was assessed by the false discovery rate (FDR) based on the hypergeometric test followed by Benjamini‑Hochberg correction. GO terms with an FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. The top 10 enriched terms (ranked by FDR) were selected and visualized in Fig. 5A to illustrate the potential pathways and processes associated with CDYL. We used the RNA expression data (TPM values) for sorted immune cell populations available on the Human Protein Atlas (https://v21.proteinatlas.org) [15]. We extracted expression levels for CDYL across different immune subsets and compared median expression values. We retrieved gene expression datasets from the GEO database and compared the expression of IL-17 A, IL-22, and EZH2 between RA patients and healthy controls in the GSE100191 and GSE1919 datasets.

Statistical analysis

The values are mean ± SEM., where n indicates the number of independent sample cases. Comparisons between two groups were performed by the Mann-Whitney test and the unpaired t-test, depending on the parametricity of the data. Spearman’s rank test and Pearson’s correlation test were used to correlate non-normally and normally distributed variables, respectively. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance was denoted as ns, no significance, *p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01 and *** p < 0.001.

Results

Clinical parameters, including age, gender, ASO levels, RF levels, ESR, anti-CCP levels, CRP levels, and the Disease Activity Score 28 (DAS28) score were measured and analyzed, with the results presented in Table 1. Our analysis revealed that RA patients exhibited significantly higher ESR and anti-CCP levels compared to the HC group. However, no significant differences were observed between RA patients and HC controls in terms of age, gender, or CRP levels.

Elevated CDYL expression in the PBMCs of RA patients

To delve into the potential roles of CDYL in the pathogenesis of RA, we examined the mRNA expression levels of CDYL in PBMCs derived from RA patients. Our comparative analysis revealed a striking upregulation of CDYL expression in the PBMCs of RA patients when contrasted with those from healthy subjects (Fig. 1A). This finding suggests that CDYL may play a pivotal role in the disease process of RA. Further exploration into the clinical relevance of CDYL expression in RA patients was conducted by assessing its correlation with disease activity. Our analysis revealed that CDYL mRNA levels showed a significant positive correlation with DAS28 scores (r = 0.36, p < 0.0177, Fig. 1B). Notably, we observed a significant positive correlation between the levels of CDYL and the concentrations of anti-CCP (Fig. 1C), a well-established RA-specific autoantibody. However, no such correlations were detected between CDYL levels and other RA-related parameters, including RF (Fig. 1D), ASO (Fig. 1E), ESR (Fig. 1F), and the inflammatory marker CRP (Fig. 1G). These findings demonstrate a positive correlation between CDYL expression levels and DAS28 or anti‑CCP antibody titers in RA patients.

Correlation between CDYL expression and IL-17 A and IL-22 levels in RA patients

To investigate the relationship between CDYL and Th17 cells, we first analyzed the characteristic cytokines expression of Th17 cells (IL-17 A and IL-22) from the GEO database that contains RA patients (GSE100191). The results showed that IL-17 A and IL-22 were higher expressed in RA patients than healthy controls (Fig. 2A, B). Consistent with prior database analysis, RA patients exhibited significantly elevated IL-17 A and IL-22 levels compared to HCs (Fig. 2E, F). We subsequently leveraged the GEPIA database analysis of the correlation between CDYL and IL-17 A or IL-22. The dataset analysis demonstrated that CDYL had significant correlations with IL-17 A and IL-22 expression (Fig. 2C, D). Moreover, a strong positive correlation between CDYL expression and both IL-17 A and IL-22 expression was revealed in the PBMCs from RA patients (Fig. 2G, H). These results suggest the potential link between CDYL and Th17 cells in RA patients.

CDYL was highly expressed in Th17 cells of RA patients

For a more in-depth exploration of the cellular origin underlying the enriched expression of CDYL in RA patients, we employed flow cytometry to precisely quantify CDYL protein levels. Flow cytometric (FCM) analysis revealed significantly elevated CDYL protein expression in PBMCs from RA patients compared with healthy controls (Fig. 3A), with the gating strategy shown in Fig. S1. Concurrently, our investigation extended to scrutinize CDYL expression within distinct T cell subsets. Notably, we observed a pronounced upregulation of CDYL within the CD4+ T cell compartment in RA patients, in stark contrast to the expression levels in CD4− cells (Fig. 3B). The gating strategy is provided in Fig. S2. Meanwhile, our data further revealed a striking augmentation in the proportion of Th17 cells among RA patients relative to HC (Fig. 3C), with the gating strategy shown in Fig. S3. Delving deeper into the cellular distribution of CDYL, our subsequent analyses unveiled that CDYL expression was markedly higher in Th17 cells isolated from RA patients compared to their counterparts from HC (Fig. 3D, E). The gating strategy of Fig. 3D is provided in Fig. S4. To further elucidate the expression pattern of CDYL in Th17 cells, we leveraged data from the Human Protein Atlas database to analyze CDYL levels across distinct developmental stages of CD4+ T cells. The results demonstrated that CDYL expression was significantly upregulated in Th17 cells compared to their naive CD4+ T cell counterparts or undifferentiated Th0 cells (Fig. 3F). These findings indicate that CDYL is notably overexpressed in Th17 cells of RA patients.

The CDYL levels were enhanced during Th17 cells differentiation

The specific upregulation of CDYL in Th17 cells suggests a potential role for this protein in the pathogenesis of RA, possibly by influencing the differentiation, proliferation, or function of these pro-inflammatory Th17 cells. To evaluate CDYL expression during Th17 cell differentiation, we sorted naive CD4⁺ T cells from mouse spleens; the purity of the sorted naive CD4⁺ T cells was greater than 90% (Fig. 4A). The gating strategy is provided in Fig. S5. The Th17 cells were then induced in vitro differentiation system (Fig. 4B). The gating strategy for Th17 cells is provided in Fig. S6. During the differentiation process of murine Th17 cells, CDYL expression exhibited a marked increase at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4C, D). To further investigate the functional role of CDYL in Th17 differentiation, we performed siRNA‑mediated knockdown of CDYL in the Th17 differentiation system. The knockdown efficiency was validated as shown in Fig. 4E. Notably, CDYL knockdown significantly inhibited Th17 differentiation in vitro (Fig. 4F), with the gating strategy shown in Fig. S7. Collectively, these results indicate that CDYL is associated with and functionally contributes to the differentiation of Th17 cells.

The functional analysis of CDYL

To further investigate the potential involvement of CDYL in Th17 cells of RA patients, we analyzed the potential biological processes which CDYL was involved. The GO enrichment analysis of biological processes associated with CDYL was performed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). The GO analysis depicted that CDYL was enriched in histone H3-K9 methylation and histone modification (Fig. 5A). Notably, we discovered that EZH2, a pivotal transcriptional regulator in epigenetic modification, is capable of binding to CDYL (Fig. 5B). EZH2 is a histone lysine methyltransferase that catalyzes H3K27me3 to participate in cell fate determination and cellular function [16, 17]. Further investigation revealed a significantly elevated expression of EZH2 in RA patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 5C). Concurrently, our data demonstrated a positive correlation between the expression levels of CDYL and EZH2 (Fig. 5D, E). Furthermore, CDYL can directly interact with EZH2 in Th17 cells (Fig. 5F). Treatment of Th17 cells with GSK343, a selective inhibitor of EZH2 methyltransferase activity that does not reduce EZH2 protein levels, significantly inhibited H3K27me3 levels (Fig. 5G) (Original blots in Fig. 5F and G were listed as supplementary data). Meanwhile, GSK343 treatment also can weaken the differentiation of Th17 cells (Fig. 5H). The gating strategy of Fig. 5H is provided in Fig. S8. These collective findings suggest that CDYL may form a functional complex with EZH2, thereby participating in chromatin remodeling and epigenetic regulation processes, which can further promote Th17 cell differentiation in RA.

Discussion

In this study, we found that CDYL expression was significantly upregulated in PBMCs from RA patients and positively correlated with disease activity as well as anti-CCP concentrations. Meanwhile, CDYL levels significantly correlated with the levels of IL-17 A and IL-22 in RA patients. CDYL was highly expressed in Th17 cells of RA patients and increased during Th17 cell differentiation. Moreover, we observed that CDYL can interact with EZH2, which may affect histone H3K27me3 enrichment, however, direct evidence linking this regulatory axis to specific target genes in Th17 differentiation remains to be established.

CDYL is an epigenetic regulator that is involved in transcription repression of G9a, EZH2 and HDAC1/2 by regulating H3K9me2/3 and H3K27me3 abundance through its chromodomain [18]. Recently, the mysterious role of CDYL in representing as a crotonyl-CoA hydratase to repressively regulate histone lysine crotonylation has been disclosed [8, 19]. CDYL plays a significant role in neuronal migration, neuronal excitability, and synaptic plasticity in the central and peripheral nervous systems. Studies have found that the deficiency of CDYL leads to a significant increase in gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) -capable neurons in cortical organoids and affects the proliferation and neuronal differentiation of cortical neural stem cells [13]. In addition, CDYL has been demonstrated to play crucial roles in tumorigenesis. CDYL can indirectly inhibit PD-L1 mRNA by directly inhibiting the JAK-STAT-IRF2BP2 signaling pathway in cervical cancer cells, and CDYL overexpression can enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint anti-PD-L1/anti-PD-1 antibody [14]. However, the aberrant upregulated expression of CDYL was found in glioma tissues and cell lines and enhanced tumor cell mobility [20]. Moreover, CDYL knockdown can significantly reduce the chemokine ligands CCL2 and CXCL12 expression, as well as shift M1-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) into M2-like TAMs. In this study, we innovated to bring out the potential role of CDYL in Th17 cells and RA patients. The CDYL expression was upregulated in the Th17 cells of patients with RA and has positive correlation with anti-CCP levels. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which CDYL influences RA development and progression, as well as to explore its therapeutic potential in managing this debilitating autoimmune disorder.

The epigenome and chromatin modifications has been revealed that play crucial roles in T helper subsets differentiation and commitment [21, 22]. EZH2 is a histone methyltransferase that catalyzes H3K27me3 to facilitate heterochromatin formation, thereby silencing genes important for cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and function [16, 17]. EZH2 has been indicated that involved in the epigenetic silencing of cytokine genes through the repressive H3K27 trimethylation mark in naive CD4+ T cells, which hinders the Th1 and Th2 cells differentiation but not Th17 cells [23–25]. Another study elucidated that EHZ2 inhibition attenuates Foxp3 expression and Treg differentiation [26]. In our study, we found that EZH2 was highly expressed in RA patients. Using the EZH2 inhibitor GSK343 to treat T cells can alleviate the H3K27me3 levels and reduce the Th17 cell differentiation. The discrepancy between our findings and prior reports may stem from different models: earlier studies employed EZH2-knockout mice, which eliminate both its catalytic and non-catalytic functions, whereas we used GSK343, which reversibly binds the catalytic center of EZH2, selectively inhibiting its methyltransferase activity and lowering H3K27me3 without depleting the EZH2 protein. This suggests that the catalytic activity of EZH2 is a key driver of Th17 cell differentiation.

Moreover, a previous study showed that the levels of EZH2 and H3K27me3 were significantly decreased and negatively correlated with RORγt in patients with ankylosing spondylitis [27]. However, that study focused only on H3K27me3 in PBMCs and circulating IL-17 levels without exploring the mechanistic impact of H3K27me3 modification on Th17 cell differentiation. Indeed, H3K27me3 has been functionally linked to Th17 cell differentiation. Liu et al. have demonstrated that the histone demethylase Jmjd3 can promote Th17 cells-associated gene expression by binding to and reducing the level of H3K27me3 at Th17 cells-related gene loci [28]. The histone demethylase KDM6 inhibitor GSK-J4 can enhance genome-wide levels of the repressive H3K27me3 chromatin mark and lead to RORγt suppression during Th17 cell differentiation [29]. One possible explanation for the apparent contradictions is that EZH2 may recruit H3K27me3 to specific gene loci that normally restrain Th17 cell differentiation. In line with our work, EZH2 can also secure Tfh differentiation by reducing DNA methylation status at the Tcf7 locus, thus promoting Tcf7 and Bcl6 expression [30]. Our result revealed that CDYL interacts with EZH2 in primary CD4⁺ T cells, suggesting a physical association between these two proteins. However, whether this interaction directly influences the methyltransferase activity of EZH2 or whether CDYL primarily acts as a scaffold to recruit EZH2 to specific genomic loci remains unclear. In the absence of direct enzymatic assays or chromatin‑profiling data, the precise functional implications of the CDYL-EZH2 interaction for H3K27me3 deposition and Th17‑related gene regulation cannot be fully established. Future studies employing in vitro methylation assays, mutagenesis of interaction domains, and genome‑wide localization analyses will be necessary to determine whether and how this interaction contributes to the epigenetic control of Th17 differentiation.

It has been discovered that exon 4 of the CDYL gene can form a circular RNA, termed circCDYL. This circular RNA is generated through a back-splicing event and is predominantly located in the cytoplasm. Notably, circCDYL lacks an internal ribosome entry site, an open reading frame, and intronic sequences [31, 32]. CircCDYL typically contains multiple miRNA binding sites and can regulate miRNA functions by acting as a sponge for them [31–33]. Recently, it has been proposed that circCDYL plays a crucial role in carcinogenesis, being implicated in diseases such as multiple myeloma, breast cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer [34]. Given these findings, it is worthwhile to further explore the novel relationship between circCDYL and autoimmune diseases or the pathogenicity of Th17 cells in future research. Moreover, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we demonstrate an interaction between CDYL and EZH2 and observe changes in H3K27me3 levels, we lack direct chromatin-level evidence to confirm the enrichment of CDYL, EZH2, or H3K27me3 at key Th17-associated gene loci. Therefore, the proposed model wherein CDYL/EZH2 promotes Th17 differentiation via H3K27me3 deposition at specific target genes remains speculative and requires further validation. Second, the sample size of our clinical cohort was relatively small; future studies with larger cohorts of RA patients are needed to confirm the clinical relevance of CDYL. Third, our clinical correlation analysis was performed using total PBMCs, which represent a heterogeneous cell population comprising T cells, B cells, NK cells, and monocytes. However, our mechanistic experiments focused specifically on CD4+ T cells and Th17 subsets. Thus, the observed association between CDYL expression and clinical parameters in PBMCs may be influenced by CDYL expression in non-T cell types. Future studies should validate these correlations in purified CD4+ T cells or induced Th17 cells to exclude potential confounding effects from other cell populations. Addressing these limitations in future work will help to establish the precise mechanisms by which CDYL and EZH2 regulate Th17 differentiation and contribute to RA pathogenesis.

Conclusion

In summary, our study for the first time revealed that CDYL is highly expressed in the peripheral blood of RA patients and may be involved in the disease process of RA by promoting the differentiation of Th17 cells.
