Induction of the viable but non-culturable state by an alkyl dipropylene triamine-based disinfectant in Vibrio parahaemolyticus biofilms
Authors: Antoine Régnier, Églantine Chalivat, Cecile Dumaire, Graziella Midelet, Mélanie Gay, Thomas Brauge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVibrio parahaemolyticus is a halophilic foodborne pathogen capable of persisting throughout the seafood production chain, from aquaculture farms to processing facilities, by forming biofilms conferring increased tolerance to disinfectants. In this study, the impact of N-(3-aminopropyl)-N-dodecylpropane-1,3-diamine (APDA) was assessed by epifluorescence microscopy on the viability and structure of biofilms formed by 5 strains from a cohort of 14 V. parahaemolyticus isolated from seafood products. While with crystal violet staining most strains did not form biofilms on polystyrene microplates, structured biofilms readily formed on stainless steel surfaces, commonly used in seafood processing environments. Treatment with 300 µg·mL⁻¹ of APDA induced changes in biofilm architecture, with the emergence of cell aggregates observable by microscopy. Although a significant reduction in cultivable cells was observed, qPCR and PMA-qPCR showed that viable but non-culturable (VBNC) cells persisted across all strains, raising the risk of bacterial resuscitation. Two strains maintained low levels of cultivable cells post-treatment, suggesting potential tolerance to APDA. These results indicate that although APDA reduces bacterial cultivability, it does not ensure complete biofilm eradication and may promote persistence through structural changes and VBNC induction. This highlights the need for integrated approaches to effectively control V. parahaemolyticus in seafood processing environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27753-9.Keywords: Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Biofilm, Disinfection, VBNC, Viability qPCR, Food safetySubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Vibrio parahaemolyticus is a Gram-negative bacterium that naturally inhabits coastal marine environments. Its proliferation is closely linked to warmer sea surface temperatures (above 15 °C), making it increasingly prevalent during warmer seasons. As global temperatures rise, temperate regions are becoming new ecological niches for this pathogen1. Moreover, V. parahaemolyticus is a well-known cause of gastroenteritis in humans, often associated with the consumption of seafood. Numerous outbreaks have been documented globally, highlighting its public health relevance. For instance, recent outbreaks between September 2021 and January 2022 involving two strains of V. parahaemolyticus resulted in 268 infections linked to oyster consumption in Australia2. Similarly, between 2019 and 2023, nine outbreaks were reported in Huzhou, China, infecting over 212 individuals3. While raw and undercooked seafood remain the primary vectors, they are not the sole sources of contamination. An analysis of outbreaks in Zhejiang, China, from 2010 to 2022 revealed 29 incidents involving unexpected food vehicles such as meat, vegetables, beans, eggs, and fruit products4. Moreover, 110 outbreaks were linked to cooked seafood, which is typically considered as safe. These findings point to cross-contamination as a significant transmission route. Previous studies have highlighted such cases: one reported the infection of 100 individuals after cooked shrimp became contaminated via contact with infected ice aboard a cruise ship in Spain5. Another incident in Korea involved 237 individuals with symptoms of gastroenteritis, including 34 confirmed cases of V. parahaemolyticus infection. The outbreak was attributed to cross-contamination via contaminated inert surfaces, specifically shared kitchen utensils used between squid and other kimbap ingredients6. Cross-contamination from inert surfaces, including food preparation areas and utensils, is often driven by the presence of biofilms. These structured microbial communities are embedded within an extracellular matrix, which confers enhanced resistance to environmental stressors and allows bacteria to persist in otherwise hostile conditions7,8. V. parahaemolyticus is known to form biofilms on both living surfaces, such as mussels9, prawns, and crab shells10, as well as on non-living materials like stainless steel11 and polypropylene12 both of which are commonly found in food processing plants.

To prevent the formation of biofilms and the associated risk of cross-contamination, preventive strategies such as routine surface disinfection are critical. Food industry operators rely on a range of disinfectants to control microbial persistence. Among these, quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs) were extensively used, with benzalkonium chloride (BAC) being the most prevalent due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity13. However, their utilization has led to the emergence of resistant bacterial strains which possess antimicrobial resistance genes (ARG)14,15. Additionally, recent studies, including Mougin et al.16, and Regnier et al.17, (Submitted), have shown that exposure of V. parahaemolyticus biofilms to BAC at concentrations ranging from < 25 to 500 µg.mL− 1 can induce excessive biofilm production on polystyrene and stainless-steel surfaces, and promote the emergence of VBNC cells. In light of these limitations, alternative disinfectants are being explored, such as those based on alkyldipropylene triamine18 One of them is the N-(3-aminopropyl)-N-dodecylpropane-1,3-diamine (APDA) which could be used in combination or in alternative to QAC18. APDA is a cationic surfactant such as BAC and will integrate and disturb the bacteria cell membrane, leading to cell lysis19. Unlike BAC, the tolerance of V. parahaemolyticus biofilms to APDA remains unexplored. To date, only one study has evaluated the effect of APDA on microbial biofilms, specifically phototrophic communities that may include bacteria20. However, no data are currently available regarding its impact on bacterial biofilms, particularly those formed by the emergent human pathogen V. parahaemolyticus.

In this context, the present study aimed to fill this knowledge gap by assessing the impact of APDA on V. parahaemolyticus biofilms. Specifically, the efficiency of this treatment on the eradication of biofilms and the impact of this molecule of biofilm populations and morphology were investigated by combining microscopical and molecular approach.

Contaminated food samples originated from three countries: Bangladesh (n = 1), Ecuador (n = 1), Vietnam (n = 10), No-determined (n = 2). All isolates were cultured on saline nutrient agar (SNA, 2% NaCl; Bio-Rad, Marne-la-Coquette, France) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C.

Minimal biofilm eradicable concentration

The minimal biofilm eradicable concentration (MBEC) determination was performed in accordance with the methodology outlined by Mougin et al.16,. Individual colonies of V. parahaemolyticus grown on agar were harvested using a sterile loop and inoculated into 20 mL of Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) supplemented with 2% (w/v) NaCl (Biokar, Pantin, France; Labkem, Rungis, France). This culture was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, a subculture was prepared in fresh TSB 2% NaCl (Tryptic Soy Broth with 2% NaCl), adjusted to approximately 10⁸ CFU/mL using established OD600 correlations specific to each strain (data not shown). Aliquots of 200 µL from this suspension were dispensed into wells of a microtiter plate (REF: 353072, Falcon, Waltham, United States). To mitigate evaporation, 200 µL of sterile water were added to all edge wells. The plates were then incubated at 22 °C under static conditions for 24 h to promote biofilm development. As a sterility control, wells containing only TSB 2% NaCl without bacteria were included. Following incubation, biofilms were gently rinsed three times with 200 µL of sterile physiological water (0.85% NaCl) to eliminate planktonic cells. Control biofilm were exposed 20 min to 200µL of sterile physiological water, while treated biofilms were then exposed for 20 min to 200 µL of P3-TOPAX 990 (Ecolab, Bagneux, France), a solution containing 4% (w/v) N-(3-aminopropyl)-N-dodecylpropane-1,3-diamine (APDA), 4% (w/v) C12–C14 Alkyl dimethylamine N-oxides, 1% (w/v) Acetic acid 1% and 0.5% (w/v) Branched and linear C13–C15 ethoxylated alcohols. The disinfectant solution was diluted in sterile water to achieve final APDA concentrations of 24, 52, 100, 200, 300 and 400 µg.mL− 1. After treatment, the disinfectant was removed and the wells were incubated with 200 µL of NP2X neutralizer (Actalia, Saint-Lô, France) for 30 min. The neutralizer was then discarded, and the biofilms were rinsed three more times with sterile physiological water. To assess the residual biomass, the wells were stained with 200 µL of 0.1% (v/v) crystal violet (CV; Condalab, Madrid, Spain) for 20 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with sterile water. The plates were air-dried for approximately 2 min. Subsequently, 200 µL of 70% ethanol were added to each well to solubilize the bound dye, gently pipetting to facilitate elution. Optical density at 595 nm (OD595) was then measured using a SAFAS Xenius XC spectrofluorometer (SAFAS, Monaco, France). Each measurement was performed in biological triplicate, with blank controls. Final OD595 readings were adjusted by subtracting the mean blank values; any resulting negative values were recorded as zero.

Disinfection of stainless coupons

Rectangular coupons (40 mm × 20 mm) of AISI 316 L 2B stainless steel (Sapim Inox, Loison-sous-Lens, France) were prepared by cleaning and sterilizing them according to the procedure described by Brauge et al.22,. After sterilization, the coupons were placed in Petri dishes and left to dry overnight at room temperature within a microbiological safety cabinet. For subsequent use, two coupons were arranged in a 90 mm Petri dish (Greiner, Frickenhausen, Germany), which was then set inside a larger 145 mm Petri dish (Greiner) containing a paper towel moistened with sterile water to maintain humidity.

Biofilm formation and APDA treatment on stainless steel coupons

Biofilms were cultivated and processed largely following the method of Brauge et al.23,, with slight adjustments. Isolated colonies of V. parahaemolyticus were picked from SNA agar plates (Bio-Rad) using a sterile loop and inoculated into 20 mL of TSB 2% NaCl, then incubated overnight at 37 °C. The overnight cultures were subsequently standardized to approximately 10⁸ CFU/mL in fresh TSB 2% NaCl. A volume of 20 mL from this adjusted suspension was dispensed into 90 mm Petri dishes (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark), each containing a previously cleaned and sterilized stainless-steel coupon. These setups were incubated at 22 °C for 24 h to facilitate biofilm development. After incubation, loosely attached bacteria were removed by rinsing. The coupons placed in a 90 mm Petri dish were immersed three consecutive times in 12.5 mL of sterile physiological water (0.85% NaCl), ensuring complete submersion during each rinse.

Biofilms were then exposed to treatment by immersing the coupons, in the same way, previously described, in 12.5 mL of sterile physiological water for control or in a P3-TOPAX 990 solution prepared to achieve 300 µg.mL− 1 APDA, for 20 min at room temperature. After treatment, the disinfectant or water was discarded, and the coupons were transferred into 12.5 mL of NP2X disinfectant neutralizer, where they remained for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, the coupons were rinsed three additional times by immersion in sterile physiological water to complete the procedure.

Biofilm observations by epifluorescence and scanning electron microscopy

After treatment with either sterile water (serving as the control) or APDA (300 µg.mL− 1), the liquid was removed, and the stainless-steel coupons were immersed in 12.5 mL of NP2X disinfectant neutralizer (Actalia, Saint-Lô, France) for 30 min at room temperature. This step was followed by three rinses, each involving immersion in 12.5 mL of sterile physiological water. Next, 5 V. parahaemolyticus biofilms were subjected to staining procedures to evaluate cell viability and to visualize specific components of the extracellular matrix. Cell viability within the biofilms was assessed using the LIVE/DEAD® BacLight™ Bacterial Viability Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. To stain extracellular DNA visualization, biofilms were treated with 1 µM Dito™−1 (Bioquest) for 30 min in the dark, followed by a rinse with sterile physiological water. Fluorescence imaging of the biofilms was carried out using an Imager.Z1 epifluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with an Axiocam MRm CCD camera (Zeiss), employing filter sets appropriate for each fluorophore. For every staining condition, a minimum of three representative fields of view were captured to document the biofilm structures.

Additionally, the architecture of the biofilms was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Jeol, SM-IT210, Japan), following the procedure detailed by Brauge et al.22.

Quantification of total and viable (culturable and non-culturable) biofilm populations

Biofilms were collected by vigorously swabbing the surfaces of the stainless-steel coupons with a sterile dry cotton swab (Deltlab, Barcelona, Spain), which was then immersed in an Eppendorf tube containing 2.5 mL of TSB 2% NaCl. To enhance biofilm recovery, the coupon was swabbed a second time using the same swab. This process was then repeated with a new swab. Both swabs were subsequently broken off into the same tube and vortexed for 20 s to thoroughly disperse the biofilm cells into suspension. This homogenized suspension served as the source for quantifying total and viable cell populations, encompassing both viable culturable (VC) and VBNC cells.

Total and viable populations were assessed using a slightly modified version of the protocol established by Brauge et al.23. For assessing viable cells specifically, an aliquot of 495 µL of each sample was treated with propidium monoazide (PMA; C₂₁H₃₃Cl₂N₆; Biotium, Hayward, USA) at a final concentration of 50 µM. Following incubation in the dark, samples were exposed to intense light (100% intensity) for 10 minutes using a PhAST Blue lamp (GenIUL, Barcelona, Spain) while remaining in their tubes. Concerning quantification of total population, an aliquot of 495 µL were made and no PMA-treatment were realized. After those steps, all samples were centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 minutes, and DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s protocol for “Purification of Total DNA from Animal Tissues,” utilizing ATL buffer for lysis. Relative DNA quantification targeted the toxR gene, which encodes a membrane-associated regulatory protein. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was carried out using qPCR kit LightCyclier® 480 Probs Master (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) in 20 µL reactions containing 7.24 µL PCR-grade water, 10 µL of Roche Master Mix 2X, 900 nM each of forward (5’- GAA CCA GAA GCG CCA GTA GT −3’) and reverse (5’- AAA CAG CAG TAC GCA AAT CG −3’) primers specific to V. parahaemolyticus (Eurogentec, Seraing, Belgium), 200 nM of a FAM/TAMRA-labeled toxR probe (5’-TCA CAG CAG AAG CCA CAG GTG C-3’) (Eurogentec), and 2 µL of extracted DNA. Amplification was performed on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche) under the following cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C and 90 s at 60 °C. Each qPCR run plate included: (i) one DNA sample treated with PMA, with and without ADPA treatment; (ii) one DNA sample untreated with PMA, with or without ADPA treatment; (iii) a standard curve well; (iv) a reaction mix control well; and (v) a negative control well.

Genome equivalents (GE) were used to quantify both total and viable populations, calculated using the V. parahaemolyticusgenome size of 5.1 × 10⁶ bp determined by Witherall et al.24. Three standard curves (Supplementary Fig. 1) were generated during this study and refreshed every 14 days in order to prevent DNA degradation. The standard curve spanning from 10⁹ GE/mL to 5 × 10¹ GE/mL and prepared using V. parahaemolyticus strain 14B3PA0046. Vibrio alginolyticus CIP 71.3 genomic DNA adjusted to 50 µg.mL− 1served as a negative control.

For determining culturable populations, a 1 mL fraction from the control sample was serially diluted up to 10⁻⁴. Meanwhile, undiluted APDA-treated samples and 10⁻² and 10⁻⁴ dilutions of control samples were plated on SNA agar using spiral plating easySpiral Pro (Interscience, Saint Nom la Bretèche, France). After incubating plates for 24 h at 37 °C, colonies were counted using an automated colony counter (Scan 500®, Interscience, Saint Nom la Bretèche, France). The discrepancy between the total and viable population counts was interpreted as the proportion of dead cells. Likewise, the difference between the viable and culturable populations was used to infer the presence of VBNC cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio version 2023.12.1 + 402. Welch’s two-sample t-test was applied (α = 0.05), considering results significant when the p-value was ≤ 0.05. Data visualization related to population quantification was carried out with Excel version 2108 (Microsoft, Redmond, United-State).

Results

Determination of the minimal concentration of APDA to eradicate V. parahaemolyticus biofilms

To assess the impact of the alkyl dipropylene triamine compound APDA, the MBEC for 14 V. parahaemolyticus strains was determined (Fig. 1). In the absence of treatment (0 µg.mL− 1 APDA), 13 strains did not form detectable biofilms (OD₅₉₅ = 0). Only strain 17-B3PA-1510 showed a different biofilm-forming ability, with an OD₅₉₅ of 0.3. When treated with increasing concentrations of APDA (ranging from 24 to 400 µg.mL− 1), no significant effect was observed for most strains. However, strain 17-B3PA-1510 exhibited an increase of OD595 reaching 0.97 after treatment.

Matrix component and cell density of V. parahaemolyticus biofilms observed by epifluorescence and scanning electron microscopy

The impact of a 300 µg.mL− 1 APDA treatment was assessed by microscopy using live/dead cell staining and labelling two biofilm extracellular matrix component: extracellular DNA and polysaccharides (Fig. 2). After water treatment, strain 19-B3PA-0924 formed a uniform cells distribution biofilm, composed of viable cells (green) and dead cells (red) (Fig. 2A). APDA treatment altered the biofilm architecture, resulting in the formation of cells aggregate. These treated biofilms were predominantly composed of dead cells (red) with a few remaining viable cells (green). Dito™−1 staining revealed a well-developed extracellular matrix in untreated biofilms, confirming their uniform cells distribution (Fig. 2B). In APDA-treated biofilms, microscopy confirmed the presence of large cells aggregate rich in extracellular DNA (Fig. 2C). Similar results were obtained for the biofilms formed by strains 18-B3PA-0236, 17-B3PA-1510, 21-B3PA-1227, and 19-B3PA-2027 (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses further confirmed the presence of cells aggregate in APDA-treated biofilms for strain 19-B3PA-0924 (Fig. 3B), in contrast to the uniform cells distribution architecture with isolated cells observed in water-treated controls (Fig. 3A). Bacterial cells in treated biofilms also appeared damaged (Fig. 3D), whereas those in untreated biofilms retained their characteristic bacillary morphology (Fig. 3C).

Quantification of Vibrio parahaemolyticus populations according to their viability and culturable status in biofilms treated with APDA

Total bacterial populations (viable and dead) and viable populations (including culturable and non-culturable cells), were quantified in the biofilms formed by the 14 strains, both without treatment (water control) and with APDA treatment (Fig. 4).

Following the water treatment, the total bacterial load in the biofilms ranged from 6.14 Log GE/cm² for strain 19-B3PA-2683 to 8.50 Log GE/cm² for strain 21-B3PA-1227. According to qPCR and PMA-qPCR analyses, no dead cells were detected among the 14 tested strains. However, dead populations were still visible by microscopy (Fig. 2), suggesting that their abundance was too low to be quantified by molecular methods. Culturable populations (VC) were detected in all biofilms, ranging from 3.60 Log CFU/cm² for strain 19-B3PA-0924 to 6.74 Log CFU/cm² for strain 21-B3PA-1227. The differences between viable and culturable populations revealed the presence of VBNC cells in all untreated biofilms. The extent of the VBNC fraction was particularly high (greater than 2.67 Δ Log, corresponding to 463 fold) in biofilms formed by strains 16-B3PA-2613, 17-B3PA-3962, 19-B3PA-0924, 18-B3PA-1732, 18-B3PA-0236, 17-B3PA-1510, and 21-B3PA-0171. In the remaining strains, the magnitude of the VBNC population ranged from 0.75 Δ Log (6 fold; strain 21-B3PA-1227, the lowest value) to 1.54 Δ Log (35 fold; strain 18-B3PA-2680).

After treatment with 300 µg·mL⁻¹ of APDA, no culturable population were quantified in all biofilms, except for strain 17-B3PA-1512, where residual populations of 1.25 Log CFU/cm² were detected. For strain 21–1227, residual cells were occasionally observed, but the mean values were below the detection limit and therefore considered negative. This sharp reduction in culturable cells was accompanied by a significant increase in the VBNC fraction across all biofilms, with the highest value observed in strain 21-B3PA-0171 (7.20 Δ Log, corresponding to a 1.57 × 10⁷ fold difference). These increases were statistically significant as confirmed by Welch’s two-sample t-test (α = 0.05), with P values ranging from 3.56 × 10⁻² for strain 17-B3PA-0924 to 1.95 × 10⁻⁶ for strain 18-B3PA-0236. A dead-cell population was also detected in all treated biofilms, except for strains 18-B3PA-1732, 21-B3PA-0171, and 21-B3PA-1227. In contrast, total and viable populations remained largely unaffected by the treatment, with the exception of strain 19-B3PA-2027, which showed a significant decrease in its viable population (P = 2.28 × 10⁻²; α = 0.05).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of APDA on V. parahaemolyticus biofilms, specifically investigating whether specifically investigating whether treatment effectively eradicates biofilms and whether this molecule has an impact on biofilm populations and morphology. MBEC assays were performed on 14 V. parahaemolyticus strains isolated from seafood products. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effect of an alkyl dipropylene triamine compound on V. parahaemolyticusbiofilms. Under these experimental conditions, 13 out of the 14 strains failed to form detectable biofilms on polystyrene microplates, which contrast with findings of Mougin et al.16,. obtained using different strains. Due to the absence of biofilm formation, no MBEC values could be determined. However, for strain 17-B3PA-1510, the only strain capable to form biofilm under these conditions, biomass increased following treatment with 100 and 200 µg.mL− 1 of APDA. This phenomenon of biofilm overproduction in response to disinfectant exposure has already been described for V. parahaemolyticus biofilms treated with benzalkonium chloride (BAC)17. In a previous study, we demonstrated that strains lacking the qacEΔ1 gene (which encodes a SMR-family efflux pump conferring quaternary ammonium (QACs) tolerance) overproduced biofilm matrix when exposed to BAC17. The proposed hypothesis is that, in the absence of resistance determinant, bacteria trigger an adaptive stress response based on matrix overproduction to limit disinfectant diffusion. Conversely, strains carrying qacEΔ1 rely more on active efflux of BAC, which reduces the need for matrix production but may impose energetic cost limiting biomass accumulation. For strain 17-B3PA-1510, whole-genome sequencing confirmed the absence of the qacEΔ1 gene (accession number: SAMN48767444). This strain nevertheless harboured several efflux pump genes belonging to the Resistance-Nodulation-Division (RND) family with a complete set of vme and vpo genes (vmeA, vmeB, vmeC, vmeD, vmeE, vmeF, vmeG, vmeH, vmeI, vmeJ, vmeK, vmeP, vmeQ, vmeT, vmeU, vmeV, vmeY, vmeZ, and vpoC) associated with the RND system and its outer membrane components25,26, as well as vmrA, from the Multidrug and Toxic Compound Extrusion (MATE) system. However, since these efflux genes were also present in all other tested strains, they cannot alone explain the specific phenotype observed in strain 17-B3PA-1510. Another hypothesis is that exposure of strain 17-B3PA-1510 to the disinfectant specifically stimulated the production of components inducing directly or indirectly biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus. Two non-exclusive hypotheses may explain the observed increase in biofilm biomass. The first is supported by the work of Mougin et al. (2024)16, who demonstrated that BAC treatment could, depending on the strain, either induce or not an increase in c-di-GMP, a second messenger known to stimulate biofilm formation and matrix overproduction. Indeed, our observations with strain 17-B3PA-1510 suggest that short-term exposure to disinfectants may act as a rapid stress signal, triggering c-di-GMP–dependent regulatory pathways and promoting matrix production within minutes. Such a mechanism is consistent with previous reports showing that c-di-GMP levels can rapidly increase in response to environmental signals in other bacterial species, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa27 or Listeria monocytogenes28. The second hypothesis is that disinfectant exposure altered the biofilm architecture by promoting the formation of aggregates, as revealed by our SEM observations. These structural changes could increase the retention of crystal violet, thereby contributing to the apparent rise in measured biomass.

To better reflect the conditions encountered throughout the seafood industry, V. parahaemolyticus biofilms were cultivated and treated on stainless steel, a material widely used across the seafood and aquaculture processing chain29. The impact of APDA was assessed through microscopic observations combined with live/dead staining to visualize bacterial populations based on their viability status, as well as specific staining targeting key component of the extra cellular matrix: extra cellular DNA . Our results showed that, unlike what was observed on polystyrene, V. parahaemolyticus was able to form biofilms on stainless steel. These findings are consistent with several studies reporting that the nature of the surface, particularly its hydrophilicity, roughness, or charge can influence bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation in marine bacteria, including V. parahaemolyticus30,31. Indeed, a study of Mizan et al.32, highlighted that V. parahaemolyticus had a greater variation in biofilm formation in microtiter plate (hydrophobic surface) than on stainless steel (hydrophilic surface), and that it may vary depending on the strain and experimental conditions.

Microscopic observations further revealed that treatment with 300 µg.mL− 1 of APDA did not fully eradicate V. parahaemolyticus biofilms formed on stainless steel. The remaining biofilms were mainly composed of dead cells (red staining), but also included a few viable cells (green staining), along with an extracellular matrix primarily composed of extracellular DNA.

To pursue our investigation, the total bacterial populations and viable populations (including both culturable and non-culturable cells) in the biofilms formed by the different V. parahaemolyticus strains were quantified. This was done using enumeration on agar media, qPCR and PMA-qPCR. These methods also allowed to estimate the proportion of VBNC (Viable But Non-Culturable) and dead cells under each condition. It is important to note certain methodological limitations. As with any nucleic acid extraction method, partial DNA loss may occur during the process, which could lead to an underestimation of the total amount of DNA. Such technical constraints may influence the quantification of total and viable populations by qPCR and PMA-qPCR, respectively. In our study, this potential bias affected control and APDA-treated samples in a similar way, thereby allowing a reliable comparison between the two conditions. The results confirmed that following disinfectant treatment, VBNC populations were present in the biofilms of all 14 strains tested. Although no data are currently available on the effect of APDA on the viability of V. parahaemolyticus biofilms, these findings are consistent with previous observations using other disinfectants. For example, Mougin et al.16 and Régnier et al.17 (Submitted) demonstrated, respectively using flow cytometry and PMA-qPCR, the persistence of V. parahaemolyticus in a VBNC state after BAC treatment. It has also been shown that V. parahaemolyticus can enter a VBNC state in response to stress caused by more common cleaning agents like sodium hypochlorite and resuscitate after treatment. Indeed Wang et al.33 showed that planktonic V. parahaemolyticus cells treated with 40 mg/L of chlorine enter in VBNC state and could recover viability after 24 h. In the present study, the observed recovery time was around 7 days (data not shown), which is longer, and this difference may be due to variations in the resuscitation protocol, such as the use of sodium hypochlorite instead of APDA, and the omission in the present study of catalase and sodium pyruvate, which are known to enhance recovery34. Similarly, a study by Osland et al.35 showed that 48-hour-old E. coli biofilms exhibited a loss of cultivability, with an average log reduction of 0.95 after 15 min and 1.08 after 30 min of treatment with APDA. In contrast, here, a much higher reduction of approximately 6.34 Log (GE/cm²) was observed. It may be due E. coli intraspecies biofilms proprieties which confer higher tolerance to APDA than V. parahaemolyticus. Interestingly, VC populations were detected after treatment with 300 µg.mL− 1 of APDA in one strains: 17-B3PA-1511. The possible presence of previously described resistance genes in this strain may explain the persistence of a culturable population after APDA treatment but no direct link has been determined. Some apparently contradictory results were observed when comparing live/dead microscopy observations with molecular quantification data. In particular, red-stained populations (dead cells) were observed by microscopy but could not be quantified by molecular methods. This phenomenon was especially evident when V. parahaemolyticus biofilms were treated with water. It is therefore important to emphasize certain methodological limitations regarding the interpretation of results obtained from microscopy and molecular biology approaches. Live/dead microscopy should primarily be considered a qualitative tool: propidium iodide can penetrate stressed but still viable cells, or bind to extracellular DNA, leading to an overestimation of “dead” cells36,37. In contrast, PMA-qPCR provides a quantitative measurement at the scale of the entire sample, but may underestimate dead cells if dye penetration is limited in dense biofilms or if DNA from lysed cells is poorly recovered during extraction38–40. These methodological differences explain why microscopy revealed more red-stained cells than PMA-qPCR. Nevertheless, both approaches converge on a key finding: the persistence of viable populations after BAC exposure, confirming the induction of a VBNC state.

Eventually, treatment with APDA altered the morphology of V. parahaemolyticus biofilms, leading to the formation of large cells aggregate. A study by Mougin et al.16 demonstrated that treatment with 1.25% benzalkonium chloride for 24 h altered the architecture of V. parahaemolyticus biofilms by enhancing the presence of cells aggregates.The authors hypothesized that these architectural differences may constitute a reinforced physical barrier, limiting the diffusion of antimicrobial compounds. Therefore, the emergence of cell aggregates may partly explain why, in all biofilms of the present study, APDA treatment resulted in a complete loss of culturability rather than cell death. Similar hypotheses have been proposed in other studies, particularly in the context of Listeria monocytogenes. For instance, it has been suggested that L. monocytogenes mutants exposed to alkaline treatments modified their biofilm architecture in a way that promotes denser structures, thereby reducing disinfectant efficacy8. Study of Kocot et al.41,. showed that dual species biofilm (Staphylococcus aureus or Pseudomonas aeruginosa with Listeria innocua) formed aggregates after exposition to quaternary ammonium or tertiary alkyl amine based disinfectant.

Conclusion

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of N-(3-aminopropyl)-N-dodecylpropane-1,3-diamine (APDA) on V. parahaemolyticus biofilms, specifically investigating whether treatment effectively eradicates biofilms and whether this molecule has an impact on biofilm populations and morphology. While MBEC tests showed that most strains did not form substantial biofilms on polystyrene under the tested conditions, some biomass increases were observed following APDA exposure in one strain. However, when biofilms were grown on stainless steel, a material more representative of seafood processing environments V. parahaemolyticus developed structured biofilms that were not fully eradicated by APDA treatment at 300 µg.mL− 1. Microscopic analyses revealed significant changes in biofilm architecture after treatment, including the appearance of dense cells aggregates potentially contributing to antimicrobial tolerance. Importantly, although the treatment drastically reduced cultivable populations, VBNC cells persisted in all tested strains, representing a major concern for food safety. In two strains, residual cultivable cells were still detected after treatment, indicating a potential resistance to APDA. These findings highlight the need for further investigation into the behaviour of V. parahaemolyticus biofilms under realistic conditions and stress and the importance of evaluating disinfection strategies not only for their ability to reduce bacterial counts on agar media but also for their effects on microbial persistence and viability.
