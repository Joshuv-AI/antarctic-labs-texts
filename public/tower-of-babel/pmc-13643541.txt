Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing
Authors: Sebastian König, Kerstin Bode
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) has rapidly evolved from a niche physiological pacing technique into a cornerstone of contemporary cardiac pacing. Following accumulating observational evidence and recent consensus recommendations,1 LBBAP is increasingly being adopted for patients requiring high ventricular pacing burden because it preserves physiological ventricular activation while offering high implantation success and excellent long-term electrical performance.2 As operators gain experience and procedural indications expand, the spectrum of procedure-related complications is also becoming better recognized.

The case by Song et al3 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports provides an important contribution to this evolving field by describing septal branch perforation resulting in an interventricular septal hematoma requiring transcatheter coil embolization. Although isolated reports4, 5, 6 and an editorial7 about septal hematoma following LBBAP have previously been published with predominantly delayed presentations covered, this commentary and case advance the discussion toward intraprocedural/early recognition. This report adds a clinically relevant observation by identifying abnormal pacing parameters, ST-segment elevation, and ventricular tachycardia as potential intraprocedural/early warning signs preceding the diagnosis of coronary septal injury.3 These electrophysiological findings may allow operators to recognize this complication before significant hemodynamic deterioration occurs.

Several registries and trials have demonstrated that LBBAP is a highly successful and generally safe procedure.2,8 Nevertheless, this case report among others has also illustrated that deep septal lead implantation introduces a unique spectrum of complications that differs from conventional right ventricular pacing. Septal perforation, coronary artery injury, septal hematoma, lead migration into the left ventricle, and delayed septal perforation are complications largely specific to conduction system pacing because successful lead deployment intentionally traverses the interventricular septum. The anatomical explanation is straightforward. The target region for LBBAP lies in close proximity to septal perforator arteries arising from the left anterior descending artery. Their number, caliber, and intramyocardial course are highly variable between individuals, making prediction of a completely “safe” implantation zone difficult.9 Even when fluoroscopic lead position appears ideal, the lead helix may penetrate or traumatize a septal perforator. The growing number of published case reports suggests that these vascular complications are probably under-recognized rather than exceptionally rare. Some injuries likely remain clinically silent, whereas others present with chest pain, ST-segment changes, ventricular arrhythmias, septal hematoma, or even cardiac tamponade and are life threatening.

An important strength of the current report is the emphasis on procedural warning signs. Most operators primarily interpret sudden impedance elevation or high capture thresholds as indicators of inadequate lead fixation or excessive penetration into fibrous tissue. Otherwise, it is common in the experience of the authors that high capture thresholds decrease after several minutes with resolution of the current of injury. This case, however, reminds us that unexpected electrical measurements, particularly when combined with patient symptoms or electrocardiographic changes, should prompt consideration of vascular injury. Likewise, the occurrence of ventricular tachycardia during implantation should not automatically be attributed to mechanical irritation alone but may reflect localized myocardial ischemia resulting from septal arterial injury. Immediate multimodality assessment with echocardiography and coronary angiography, as illustrated in this case, can facilitate rapid diagnosis and timely treatment.

The successful use of coil embolization also deserves emphasis. Whereas conservative management may be appropriate for small, stable septal hematomas without ongoing bleeding, persistent angiographic extravasation warrants definitive treatment. Percutaneous coil embolization provides an elegant, minimally invasive solution that avoids surgical intervention and should be considered whenever suitable coronary anatomy permits. As experience with LBBAP increases, collaboration between electrophysiologists and interventional cardiologists will become increasingly important for the management of these uncommon but potentially catastrophic complications.

Whether selected patients—particularly those with prior coronary artery disease, hypertrophied septa, or technically challenging anatomy—could benefit from additional imaging guidance remains uncertain. Future studies evaluating intracardiac echocardiography, contrast septography, or coronary imaging during difficult LBBAP procedures may help define strategies that minimize vascular injury without compromising procedural efficiency. However, they add another level of procedural complexity and warrant a thorough cost-benefit analysis.

Importantly, this report should not discourage the adoption of LBBAP. Conduction system pacing has consistently demonstrated superior ventricular synchrony and favorable clinical outcomes, making it the preferred pacing strategy in appropriately selected patients. However, as with any evolving technology, LBBAP has a distinct complication profile that requires operator awareness, appropriate institutional infrastructure, and expertise. Early recognition of warning signs, prompt multimodality imaging, and familiarity with bailout strategies are essential for safe practice. Ultimately, the success of LBBAP depends not only on technical proficiency but also on careful patient selection and adherence to evidence-based indications, ensuring maximal benefit while minimizing procedural risk.
