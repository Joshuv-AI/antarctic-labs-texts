Assessment of bioethics education, ethical awareness, and training needs among physicians in Jordan
Authors: Hisham E. Hasan, Omar F. Khabour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTeaching bioethics contributes to promoting sound clinical decisions, but a research gap persists in Jordan regarding the ethical challenges facing physicians and their training requirements. This cross-sectional online survey assessed bioethics education, ethical awareness, perceived competence, and ethical practices among physicians practicing in Jordan. An anonymous questionnaire was distributed using convenience sampling through social media, professional networks, and medical associations. A total of 559 physicians participated. Most respondents were male (59.4%), aged 26–30 years (47.8%), and medical graduates from Jordan (83.5%). Although 64.2% reported at least 3 credit hours of ethics education, only 45.3% reported awareness of the four core bioethical principles. Most participants reported being able to identify ethical issues (83.5%) and to find answers to ethical dilemmas at least sometimes (84.4%). Ethical challenges were most often reported in relation to traditions and values (57.8%), law (53.1%), religion (52.6%), finance (51.7%), and conflict of interest (50.1%). Participants expressed strong interest in additional training, particularly treatment refusal, informed consent, diagnosis/prognosis communication, and end-of-life care. Findings suggest a need for practice-oriented, culturally grounded, and continuing bioethics education that links foundational principles to common clinical dilemmas. Education should develop case-based reasoning, reflective clinical judgment, and communication skills rather than terminology recognition alone.

Introduction

Bioethics is a cornerstone of modern clinical practice, providing healthcare professionals with a framework for identifying, analysing, and resolving ethical questions in patient care (Varkey, 2021). It includes, but is not limited to, the principles of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice, while also requiring attention to law, religion, culture, professional duties, patient rights, and health-system constraints (Gonzaga & Zahedi-Spung, 2025; Pais et al., 2025; Veatch, 2020).

Bioethics education is important because many ethical challenges in clinical practice cannot be resolved by technical knowledge alone. It should strengthen moral reasoning, communication, professionalism, and shared decision-making in complex encounters. Ethical issues such as informed consent, refusal of treatment, confidentiality, disclosure of medical errors, end-of-life decision-making, equitable access to care, and conflicts of interest are central to contemporary medical practice (Machin & Proctor, 2021; Mijaljica, 2014; Norman et al., 2011; Stoeklé et al., 2023). Knowledge of the four principles is useful as a common vocabulary, but principle recognition alone does not establish competence. Physicians must be able to apply ethical concepts to context-specific decisions and communicate effectively with patients, families, and healthcare teams (Mohamed, 2026).

Evidence from Jordan and the wider Middle East and North Africa suggests continuing gaps between formal ethics teaching and practical preparedness (Shamsi-Gooshki, 2024). In Jordan, previous studies have examined truth disclosure, patient participation in treatment decisions, confidentiality and data sharing, clinical ethics structures, and ethics-related knowledge among residents and trainees (Abdulrazeq et al., 2019; Borgan et al., 2018; Karasneh et al., 2021; Obeidat & Komesaroff, 2019; Obeidat & Khrais, 2016). Regional studies from Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Iraq, Oman, and the United Arab Emirates have similarly reported common ethical challenges and gaps in knowledge, confidence, or preparedness for handling clinical ethics issues (AbuAbah et al., 2019; Al Qadrah et al., 2021; Al-Busaidi et al., 2021; Althagafi & Alahmad, 2023; Dahiyat et al., 2025; Mohamed et al., 2012; Taher & Ahmed, 2024). Work on research ethics and informed consent in Jordan and Lebanon is related but distinct from clinical bioethics, and it reinforces the need for clear ethics governance, while avoiding the conflation of research ethics with day-to-day clinical ethical decision-making (Al Demour et al., 2019; Azakir et al., 2020).

Although ethics content is present in Jordanian medical education, available evidence does not establish that its timing, depth, teaching methods, or assessment approaches are uniform across institutions and clinical settings (Abdulrazeq et al., 2019; Obeidat & Komesaroff, 2019). The educational background of practicing physicians may therefore differ by institution, country of training, and stage of career. Jordanian physicians also practice within a social context in which religious values, family roles, legal expectations, cultural norms, institutional policies, and resource constraints may shape ethical decision-making (Hamouda et al., 2021). These factors make it important to understand not only whether physicians have studied bioethics, but also whether they feel prepared to manage ethical dilemmas in practice.

This study was designed as an educational needs assessment rather than a comprehensive measurement of the national ethical climate. The survey therefore included selected ethical-practice scenarios and training topics intended to capture common domains in clinical professionalism, confidentiality, disclosure, communication, referral practices, conflicts of interest, and culturally sensitive clinical questions.

The primary objective of this study was to assess bioethics education, ethical awareness, and self-reported ethical practices among physicians practicing in Jordan. The secondary objectives were to identify commonly encountered ethical challenges, describe preferred sources of ethical guidance and desired training topics, and examine associations between physicians' demographic and educational characteristics and selected ethical awareness and attitude outcomes.

Material and methods

Study design

This anonymous cross-sectional online survey was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cross-sectional studies. The study assessed the status of bioethics education, ethical awareness, perceived competence, selected ethical practices, and training needs among physicians practicing in Jordan.

Setting

Data were collected between January and December 2023 using Google Forms. The survey link was disseminated online through physician-facing social media channels, professional networks, and medical associations in Jordan using convenience sampling. The link was shared as an open electronic invitation rather than through individualised invitations. Because the questionnaire was distributed through open and network-based online channels, the total number of physicians who viewed or received the invitation, the number of reposts or forwarded invitations, and the number of reminders could not be determined; therefore, a response rate and a formal participant-flow diagram could not be calculated.

Participants

Eligible participants were physicians practicing in Jordan. Jordanian physicians working outside Jordan were excluded. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and electronic informed consent was obtained before the questionnaire could be completed. The final analysed dataset included 559 physicians. All submitted questionnaires included responses to the study variables. Exact duplicate rows were not identified during data screening; however, because the survey was anonymous and distributed through a shared link, technical prevention of duplicate or multiple submissions was limited.

Variables

The main outcomes were self-reported awareness of the four bioethical principles, ability to identify ethical issues, ability to find answers to ethical dilemmas, perceptions of selected ethical practice scenarios, areas in which ethical issues were encountered, perceived usefulness of bioethics, and perceived need for further training. Awareness of the four principles was treated as a limited indicator of foundational bioethics knowledge and was not interpreted as a complete measure of ethical competence. Predictor variables included gender, age group, years of clinical experience, place of obtaining the medical degree, number of ethics credit hours in medical school, perceived adequacy of bioethics resources, adequacy of ethics teaching, and awareness of the four bioethical principles. The item on adequacy of resources was a single participant-reported assessment of whether teaching resources for medical ethics at medical school were sufficient; it should therefore be interpreted as perceived resource adequacy rather than as an objective audit of curricular quality.

Data sources and measurement

The survey consisted of structured items developed from previous studies and adapted to the Jordanian clinical context (Al-Busaidi et al., 2021; Gulino et al., 2019; Singh et al., 2016). The questionnaire items were adapted rather than used as one previously validated scale. It was administered in English, which is commonly used in Jordanian medical education. It collected demographic information and educational exposure, then assessed perceptions of ethical practices, frequency and context of ethical dilemmas, sources of ethical guidance, perceived usefulness of bioethics, and interest in future training topics. Ethical-practice items were selected to cover patient-related professionalism, confidentiality, disclosure, patient communication, referral practices, and financial or institutional conflict-of-interest scenarios. These items were not intended to represent the full range of ethical issues in Jordan. Training topics were selected to cover common and sensitive areas in clinical bioethics, including informed consent, refusal of treatment, diagnosis/prognosis communication, end-of-life care, reproductive ethics, religion and patient relationships, assisted procreation, and gender identity. Gender identity was included as a sensitive bioethical topic for educational needs assessment and not because it was assumed to be common, legally unrestricted, or among the most frequent clinical dilemmas in Jordan. The questionnaire and coding scheme are provided as Supplementary File 1.

Bias

The questionnaire was anonymous to reduce social desirability pressure and encourage candid responses. Items were reviewed by experts and pilot-tested to improve clarity and reduce measurement bias. Nevertheless, selection bias, non-response bias, recall bias, and social desirability bias could not be eliminated because recruitment used convenience sampling and all measures were self-reported. In particular, younger physicians and physicians more active on digital platforms may have been more likely to participate.

Study size

The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1 for planned chi-square tests of association between categorical educational and demographic variables and selected ethical awareness or attitude outcomes. A small-to-moderate effect size of 0.15, alpha of 0.05, and power of 0.95 were used. The calculated minimum sample size was 470 participants. No formal inflation for non-response was applied because the recruitment denominator was not known; however, the final sample of 559 exceeded the minimum required sample size.

Questionnaire validation

To ensure content quality and clarity, three experts, including a bioethicist and practicing clinicians, reviewed the initial items for relevance, wording, and face/content validity. A pilot test with ten physicians was then used to identify ambiguous wording, typographical errors, and problems with item flow. Minor wording and formatting adjustments were made after pilot feedback. Pilot responses were not included in the final analysis.

Statistical methods

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27) and verified independently from the exported dataset. Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages. Pearson’s chi-square tests without continuity correction were used to examine bivariate associations between categorical participant characteristics and ethical attitudes or awareness variables. Cramer’s V was used to describe the magnitude of association. Exploratory multivariable logistic regression models were used for selected binary outcomes and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Variables were selected for the exploratory models based on conceptual relevance and bivariate findings. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

As presented in Table 1, of the 559 participating physicians, 59.4% were male. Nearly half (47.8%) were between 26–30 years old, and most (83.5%) obtained their medical degree in Jordan. The sample was relatively young, with 45.1% reporting less than 5 years of clinical practice. The level of exposure to ethics education varied, with 43.8% reporting 3 credit hours and 20.4% reporting 4 or more credit hours; therefore, 64.2% reported at least 3 credit hours of ethics education during medical school. Only 45.3% reported awareness of the four core principles of biomedical ethics, and 67.3% reported that bioethics resources at medical school were not adequate.

Discussion

This cross-sectional survey of 559 physicians in Jordan identified three main findings. First, physicians reported frequent exposure to ethical issues and strong interest in continuing bioethics education. Second, although nearly two-thirds reported at least 3 credit hours of ethics education, fewer than half reported awareness of the four core bioethical principles. Third, the most requested training areas were treatment refusal, informed consent/dissent, diagnosis and prognosis communication, and end-of-life care. Together, these findings suggest that physicians prioritised practical bedside decision-making and communication skills rather than abstract knowledge alone.

The findings support the view that formal exposure to ethics education and foundational vocabulary are necessary but not sufficient for ethical competence. Awareness of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice can help physicians organise ethical reasoning, but clinical competence requires applying these principles to concrete situations, weighing cultural and legal constraints, communicating with patients and families, and reflecting on professional duties (Alabdallat & Alsalhi, 2025). The observed association between principle awareness and selected awareness outcomes should therefore be interpreted as an indicator of foundational knowledge rather than evidence that principle recognition alone captures practical ethical competence.

Participants' preferred training topics indicate that future bioethics education in Jordan should move beyond terminology recognition toward case-based and communication-focused learning. Treatment refusal and informed consent require physicians to assess decision-making capacity, explain risks and benefits, respect patient values, and manage disagreement among patients, families, and clinicians. Diagnosis and prognosis communication requires sensitivity, clarity, and respect for patient preferences, especially when truth-telling and disclosure occur within culturally complex family-centred settings (Rosenberg et al., 2017). End-of-life care is broad and may include decisions about withholding or withdrawing treatment, palliative approaches, and requests for treatments judged unlikely to be beneficial. Studies addressing terminal care and medical error disclosure in the region further support the need for training in communication-heavy ethical domains (Mansour et al., 2020; ur Rahman et al., 2013). Future curricula and continuing professional development programmes should address these issues through locally relevant cases, supervised discussion, role-playing, bedside ethics teaching, and reflective assessment (Andersson et al., 2022; Moreira et al., 2021).

The most commonly reported areas of ethical challenge involved traditions and values, law, religion, finance, and conflicts of interest. These findings underscore the need for culturally grounded ethics education that explicitly integrates legal expectations, religious and family considerations, social norms, and professional standards. Such an approach may help physicians translate general principles into clinical decisions that are ethically defensible and sensitive to the Jordanian context, particularly when patient autonomy, family involvement, confidentiality, disclosure, and institutional policies interact in difficult clinical encounters (Borgan et al., 2018; Karasneh et al., 2021; Obeidat & Komesaroff, 2019; Obeidat & Khrais, 2016; ur Rahman et al., 2013).

The survey included selected items on gifts, referral fees, and physician-industry relationships because these practices are important professionalism and conflict-of-interest domains, not because they were assumed to be the dominant educational need. Participants' strongest training priorities were instead focused on patient communication and bedside decision-making. Accordingly, recommendations about conflicts of interest should be understood as complementary to education: professional societies and healthcare institutions in Jordan should support bioethics education with clear codes of conduct, institutional guidance, and transparent policies so that physicians can apply classroom learning within supportive practice environments (Ahmed et al., 2024; Fickweiler et al., 2017; Khan et al., 2024; Noor et al., 2024; Zarei et al., 2022).

When compared with previous literature, the findings are consistent with Jordanian and regional studies reporting gaps between formal ethics exposure and confidence in applying ethics to clinical practice. Jordanian studies have described challenges related to truth disclosure, patient participation in treatment decision-making, confidentiality and data sharing, and the development of clinical ethics structures (Borgan et al., 2018; Karasneh et al., 2021; Obeidat & Komesaroff, 2019; Obeidat & Khrais, 2016). Studies from Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Iraq, Oman, and the United Arab Emirates have also identified gaps in knowledge, confidence, or preparedness for handling common ethical issues in clinical practice (AbuAbah et al., 2019; Al Qadrah et al., 2021; Al-Busaidi et al., 2021; Althagafi & Alahmad, 2023; Dahiyat et al., 2025; Mohamed et al., 2012; Taher & Ahmed, 2024). Research from Jordan and Lebanon on research ethics and informed consent addresses a related but distinct domain; it reinforces the importance of ethics governance while also illustrating why clinical bioethics education should be considered separately from research ethics training (Al Demour et al., 2019; Azakir et al., 2020).

Several demographic and educational associations were observed, but most effect sizes were small and the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The finding that physicians trained outside Jordan differed on some ethical judgements is difficult to interpret because the survey did not collect specific countries or institutions of training. The outside-Jordan category is likely heterogeneous and should not be interpreted as representing Western, regional, or paternalistic educational systems. Future studies should collect more detailed information about undergraduate and postgraduate training environments, curricular content, teaching language, assessment methods, and the quality of previous ethics education.

The practical implications of these findings extend to medical schools, residency programmes, hospitals, professional societies, and policymakers. Medical curricula should incorporate bioethics longitudinally through case-based discussions, simulation, role-playing, bedside ethics rounds, and assessment of ethical reasoning (Bazmi et al., 2023; Machin et al., 2020). Hospitals and professional bodies should provide continuing professional development for practicing physicians, develop accessible ethics consultation or advisory services, and issue clearer guidance on confidentiality, disclosure, referrals, and physician-industry relationships. Faculty development is also needed so that qualified educators can teach, model, and assess ethical reasoning in routine clinical training.

This study has several strengths. It addresses an underexplored topic in Jordan, includes a relatively large sample of practicing physicians, examines both educational exposure and perceived training needs, and reports effect sizes and exploratory multivariable models in addition to descriptive findings. The survey also distinguishes foundational awareness from practical ethical challenges and identifies priority topics that can guide curriculum development and continuing professional education.

This study also has limitations. The online convenience sampling strategy may have introduced selection bias, and the sample was relatively young; younger physicians and those more active on digital platforms may have been more likely to participate. Because the recruitment denominator was unavailable, a response rate could not be calculated. Self-reported data may be affected by recall and social desirability bias, potentially overestimating ethical awareness and underestimating acceptance of ethically questionable practices. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. The questionnaire captured contact hours and self-reported resource adequacy, but not the detailed content, quality, teaching methods, language, or assessment approaches of prior ethics education. The study did not collect countries or institutions of medical training outside Jordan. Finally, the ethical-practice scenarios and training-topic options were selected to represent important clinical and contextual domains, but they may not capture the full range of ethical issues experienced by physicians.

Conclusion

Physicians in Jordan reported frequent exposure to ethical issues and strong interest in continuing bioethics education. Limited awareness of foundational principles, variable educational exposure, and self-reported challenges involving culture, law, religion, finance, and conflicts of interest suggest a need for practice-oriented, culturally grounded, and continuing ethics education. Educational programmes should connect principles to clinical decision-making in areas that physicians identified as most important, especially treatment refusal, informed consent, diagnosis/prognosis communication, and end-of-life care, while also addressing professional standards for confidentiality, disclosure, referral practices, and physician-industry relationships.
