Ramanujan Graphs and Interlacing Families
Nikhil Srivastava

Abstract
This survey accompanies a lecture on the paper “Interlacing Families I: Bipartite Ramanujan Graphs of All Degrees” [ 40 ] by A. Marcus, D. Spielman, and N. Srivastava which was awarded a Frontiers of Science Award at the 2024 ICBS in July, 2024. Its purpose is to explain the developments surrounding this work over the past ten or so years, with an emphasis on connections to other areas of mathematics. Earlier surveys about the interlacing families method by the same authors [ 39 , 37 ] focused on applications in functional analysis, whereas the focus here is on applications in spectral graph theory.



1
Introduction

Ramanujan graphs are sparse graphs with spectral gap as
large as possible. These graphs were introduced by Lubotzky-Phillips-Sarnak
[
36
]
and Margulis
[
44
]
and have remarkable
connections to many areas of mathematics and computer science, including
spectral graph theory, random matrices, number theory, pseudorandomness, metric
geometry, free probability, and the geometry of polynomials. In this article I
will explain some relatively recent constructions of Ramanujan graphs based on the “method of interlacing families of polynomials” and discuss the new connections they reveal.

The study of Ramanujan graphs can be motivated in at least two different ways.

Definition 1.1

A

1.2
Random Graphs

There is a second, probabilistic, context in which Ramanujan graphs appear naturally: random graphs. We will consider two models of random graphs:

(R1)

Given
d\geq 3
and
n
even, choose
G
uniformly at random from the set of all
d-
regular graphs on
n
vertices. We will also consider the related model
(R1^{\prime})

is a union of
d
random perfect matchings on
[n]

53
]
. Note that the second model produces graphs which may have multiple edges.

(R2)

Given a fixed
d-

n-

of

is a

|\lambda_{i}|\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}+\epsilon_{n}\qquad\forall i\neq 1,

(1.2)

with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
where

\epsilon_{n}=o_{n}(1)
. Alon’s conjecture was proven by Friedman
[
22
]
with

\epsilon_{n}=O_{d}((\log\log n/\log(n))^{2})
(see also a simpler proof by Bordenave

[

]
, and another proof by Puder
[

]
which obtains

). Two very recent works
significantly improve this. Huang, McKenzie, and Yau
[

(building on

]

\epsilon_{n}=n^{-2/3+o_{n}(1)}
, which
is conjectured to be sharp as it is believed that the fluctuations of

Chen et al.
[
15

obtain

by a simpler method in the more
general context of strong convergence in free probability theory (see also the influential work of Bordenave-Collins

Friedman generalized
[
21

Alon’s conjecture to the model (R2), asking whether

proved it up to a multiplicative

[

Given the above background, a natural question is the following:

Question 2.
Can we obtain
\epsilon_{n}=0
in the above bounds if we
settle for a lower probability bound?

Such a result has been conjectured by Miller, Novikov, and Sabelli
[
46
]
, based on numerical experiments. It is known that this cannot hold with probability

due
to the possibility of certain constant sized subgraphs appearing in

which can cause
\lambda_{2}(G)>2\sqrt{d-1}
. This presents an obstacle to employing the standard methods of random matrix theory, which typically yield bounds which hold with high probability, a conclusion which is too strong in this context.

2
Interlacing Families

In this section we describe a method which is able to establish
low probability

control on the eigenvalues of certain random matrices; in fact, it simply says
that a certain probability is nonzero, without providing any estimate.

Given a random
n\times n
Hermitian matrix
A
, we can consider its
expected characteristic polynomial
:

p_{A}(z):=\mathbb{E}\det(zI-A).

Expected characteristic polynomials of random unitary matrices
[
19
,
33
]
and Gaussian matrices
[
18
]
were studied in the context of number theory, where they are believed to be related to the zeros of the Riemann zeta function. Expected characteristic polynomials of certain Hermitian matrices were studied by Godsil and Gutman
[
24
]
, in the context of algebraic graph theory.

The works
[
40
,
42
]
discovered a new method relating the zeros of the polynomial
p_{A}(z)
to the eigenvalues of the random matrix
A
under certain conditions. This relationship is nontrivial since the maps relating a matrix, its characteristic polynomial, and its eigenvalues are highly nonlinear. It is useful because it is often much easier to compute
p_{A}
then to compute statistics of the eigenvalues of
A
. The following theorem follows from results in
[
40
,
42
,
28
]
.

Theorem 2.1
(Interlacing Families)

Let
A
be the adjacency matrix of a random graph chosen from model
(R1^{\prime})
or
(R2)
as described in Section
1.2
. Then
p_{A}(z)
is real-rooted and for every
i\leq n
:

\mathbb{P}[\lambda_{i}(A)\leq\lambda_{i}(p_{A})]>0,

where we use
\lambda_{i}
to denote the
i
th largest root of a real-rooted polynomial.

We refer the reader to
[
40
,
42
,
28
]
for a proof of this theorem. The key mechanism behind the theorem is encapsulated in the following lemma about a pair of real-rooted univariate polynomials, which the interested reader may prove as an exercise.

Lemma 2.2

Suppose
p_{0}(z)
and
p_{1}(z)
are monic polynomials of degree
n
such that all of the convex combinations

p_{t}:=(1-t)p_{0}+tp_{1},\quad t\in[0,1]

are real-rooted. Then, for every
i
and
t\in[0,1]
:

\min\{\lambda_{i}(p_{0}),\lambda_{i}(p_{1})\}\leq\lambda_{i}(p_{t})\leq\max\{\lambda_{i}(p_{0}),\lambda_{i}(p_{1})\}.

In particular, Lemma
2.2
allows one to relate the roots of an average of two polynomials to the roots of the individual polynomials. Theorem
2.1
is proven by writing
p_{A}
, an expectation over exponentially many polynomials, as a repeated conditional expectation over pairs of polynomials, and inductively applying Lemma
2.2
. The required real-rootedness statements are established using the theory of multivariate real stable/hyperbolic polynomials, especially the work of Borcea-Branden
[
10
,
11
]
. The term
interlacing family
refers to this inductive scheme.

Theorem
2.1
has been generalized to matrix models other than random graphs — see e.g.
[
41
,
49
,
3
,
14
]
for other
A
to which the theorem applies. The common feature of all of these models is that it is possible to generate the random matrix
A
by a sequence of independent low rank (rank
1
or
2
perturbations of a fixed matrix). It is possible Theorem
2.1
generalizes to other models which have not yet been studied. It is also easy to see that it does not hold for arbitrary random matrices
A
, even in
n=2
.

3
Random Covers of Fixed Graphs

In this section we explain how Theorem
2.1
applied to the random model
(R2)
can be used to partially answer Questions
1
and
2
. Fix a base graph
H
and let
G
be a random
n-
cover of
H
. Concretely, the adjacency matrix
A_{G}
of
G
is given by:

A_{G}=\sum_{ij\in E_{H}}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes P_{ij}+|j\rangle\langle i|\otimes P_{ij}^{*},

(3.1)

where
\{P_{ij}\}_{ij\in E_{H}}
are independent random
n\times n
permutation matrices indexed by the edges of
H
. Notice that if
v
is an eigenvector of
A_{H}
satisfying
A_{H}v=\lambda v
, then

A_{G}(v\otimes\mathbf{1})=(A_{H}v)\otimes\mathbf{1}=\lambda(v\otimes\mathbf{1}),

since
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
(the all ones vector) satisfies
P_{ij}\mathbf{1}=P_{ij}^{*}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
for all
ij\in E_{H}
. Thus, the spectrum of
A_{G}
contains the spectrum of
A_{H}
. Following Friedman
[
20
]
, we will refer to
\mathrm{Spec}(A_{G})\setminus\mathrm{Spec}(A_{H})
(counting eigenvalues with multiplicity) as the “new” eigenvalues of
A_{G}
relative to
H
.

3.1
2-
covers

Bilu and Linial
[
9
]
studied the case of
2-
covers. They observed that in this case, the new eigenvalues of
A_{G}
for a given cover (defined by permutations
P_{ij}
) are equal to the eigenvalues of the corresponding
signed adjacency matrix
of
A_{H}
:

A_{H,s}(i,j):=\det(P_{ij}),\quad ij\in E_{H}.

They showed that every
H
has a signing such that
\|A_{H,s}\|\leq O(\sqrt{d\log^{3}d})
and conjectured that this bound should be improvable to
2\sqrt{d-1}
. This conjecture would yield a way to construct infinite sequences of (bipartite and nonbipartite) Ramanujan graphs by starting with
K_{d}
or
K_{d,d}
as a base graph and repeatedly finding
2-
covers whose new eigenvalues satisfy the Ramanujan bound.

Bilu and Linial’s conjecture is still open, but
[
40
]
showed that it is true for bipartite graphs.

Theorem 3.1

If
H
is a
d-
regular bipartite graph, then there exists a signing
A_{H,s}
such that
\|A_{H,s}\|\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}
.

The proof of this theorem has three ingredients. The first is Theorem
2.1
. The second is a combinatorial expression for the expected characteristic polynomial of a random signing of a graph
G
in terms of its
matching polynomial

M_{G}
, discovered by Godsil and Gutman in 1978
[
24
]
.

Theorem 3.2
(Godsil-Gutman)

Let
H
be an undirected graph and let
A_{H,s}
be a uniformly random signing of its adjacency matrix. Then,

\mathbb{E}\det(zI-A_{H,s})=\sum_{k=0^{\lfloor}n/2\rfloor}x^{n-2k}(-1)^{k}m_{k}(H)=:M_{H}(z),

where
m_{k}(H)
is the number of matchings with
k
edges in
H
.

The third ingredient comes from statistical mechanics, in which the matching polynomial
M_{H}
was studied a decade earlier by Heilmann and Lieb
[
29
]
in the context of thermodynamic phase transitions in the monomer dimer model, where it arises as a partition function.

Theorem 3.3
(Heilmann-Lieb)

Let
H
be an undirected graph with maximum degree
d
. Then, the matching polynomial
M_{H}(z)
is real-rooted and all of its roots are contained in
[-2\sqrt{d-1},2\sqrt{d-1}]
.

The appearance of the number
2\sqrt{d-1}
in Theorem
3.3
is not an accident: it is proven by an inductive argument which deletes the vertices of
H
one at a time, whose recursion tree is a subtree of
T_{d}
. As
observed in
[
40
]
, this argument can also be generalized to universal covers of irregular graphs.

Given these three ingredients, it follows immediately that
\lambda_{1}(A_{H,s})\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}
with nonzero probability. Since bipartite graphs have symmetric spectrum, this yields that
\|A_{H,s}\|\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}
, which is the only way in which bipartiteness is used in the proof.

3.2
n-
covers

Hall, Puder, and Sawin
[
28
]
generalized the results of the previous subsection in two ways. First, they showed that the same thing is true if one considers
n-
covers (as defined in (
3.1
)) instead of
2-
covers.

Theorem 3.4
(
[
28
]
)

If
H
is a
d-
regular bipartite graph and
n\geq 2
, then there exists an
n-
cover
G
of
H
such that the new eigenvalues of
A_{G}
are bounded in absolute value by
2\sqrt{d-1}
.

Second, they considered “signings” of a base graph
H
by matrices arising from a group representation. Let
\Gamma
be a group and let
\rho:\Gamma\rightarrow GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a unitary representation of
\gamma
. Given a labeling
s:E_{H}\rightarrow\Gamma
, consider the operator

A_{H,s}=\sum_{e\in E_{H}}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes\rho(s(e))+|j\rangle\langle i|\otimes\rho(s(e))^{*},

which we will call a
signing of
H
by
\rho
.
In the special case of
\Gamma=S_{n}
with
\rho
its regular representation, the eigenvalues of
A_{H,s}
are the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of the cover defined by
s
. The ”old” eigenvalues arise from the trivial representation of
S_{n}
and the new eigenvalues arise from the remaining representations.

Theorem 3.5
(
[
28
]
)

Suppose
\Gamma
is a group and
\rho:\Gamma\rightarrow GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a unitary representation with two properties:

(P1)

All exterior powers
\bigwedge^{m}\rho
,
0\leq m\leq n
, are irreducible and non-isomorphic.

(P2)

\rho(\Gamma)
is a complex pseudo-reflection group, i.e., it is generated by matrices
A
which satisfy

rank(A-I)=1
.

Then, if
H
is a bipartite
d-
regular graph, there is a signing
A_{H,s}
of
H
by
\rho
satisfying
\|A_{H,s}\|\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.5
relies on two ingredients. The first is a generalization of Theorem
2.1
to the setting of signings by representations, showing that

\mathbb{P}[\lambda_{1}(A_{H,s})\leq\lambda_{1}(\mathbb{E}\det(zI-A_{H,s})]>0.

Here, property
(P2)
is used to produce an interlacing family. The second ingredient is a generalization of the Godsil-Gutman theorem, showing that if
\rho:\Gamma\rightarrow GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
satisfies
(P1)
then:

\mathbb{E}\det(zI-A_{H,s})=\mathbb{E}_{G\sim Cov_{n-1}}M_{G}(z),

(3.2)

where
G
is a uniformly random
n-1
-cover of
H
and
M_{G}
is the matching polynomial as in Theorem
3.2
. Interestingly, the expected characteristic polynomial “forgets” almost everything about
\rho
. The property
(P1)
is used to invoke the Peter-Weyl theorem in a way which produces the required cancellations. The identity (
3.2
) implies that
\lambda_{1}(\mathbb{E}\det(zI-A_{H,s}))\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}
since this is true for each
M_{G}
in the average by Heilmann-Lieb. Together with the generalization of Theorem
2.1
, the proof is concluded.

3.3
Beyond Trees

All of our discussion so far has been about comparing the spectrum of a finite graph with that of its universal cover
T
, which is an infinite tree. But the same kind of question makes sense even when the infinite covering graph is not a tree. Mohanty and O’Donnell
[
47
]
generalized the results of Hall-Puder-Sawin to the setting of certain infinite graphs called
additive product graphs
(see their paper for a definition) and proved the following.

Theorem 3.6

Suppose
X
is an infinite additive product graph and
H
is a finite graph covered by
X
. Then for every
n\geq 2
there is an
n-
cover
G
of
H
such that every new eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathrm{Spec}(A_{G})\setminus\mathrm{Spec}(A_{H})
satisfies
\lambda\leq\mathrm{spr}(A_{X})
.

The class of additive product graphs includes several interesting examples which are not trees, notably certain Cayley graphs of the modular group
PSL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

The proof of Theorem
3.6
requires analyzing a new family of expected characteristic polynomials which they refer to as “additive characteristic polynomials” (see also
[
49
]
), which includes all of the other expected characteristic polynomials considered in this survey as special cases. A remarkable step in the proof is to prove a root bound for all such polynomials using algebraic combinatorics, specifically Viennot’s theory of heaps of pieces
[
50
]
, which may be viewed as generalizing Heilmann and Lieb’s tree recursion proof beyond trees.

Theorem
3.6
makes progress towards a question originally asked by Clark
[
16
]
: given an infinite graph
X
with a finite quotient
H
satisfying

\lambda_{2}(A_{H})\leq\mathrm{spr}(A_{X})

(3.3)

must it have infinitely many quotients satisfying
3.3
? The condition on the existence of a finite quotient is required, as Lubotzky and Nagnibeda
[
35
]
showed that there are graphs where no finite quotient has this property.

Random Regular Graphs

In this section we consider the random matrix model
(R1^{\prime})
(random
d-
regular graphs) defined in Section
1.2
. Let
n
be even and let
A_{M}
be the adjacency matrix of a perfect matching on
n
vertices.
Then the adjacency matrix of a random graph sampled from the model
(R1^{\prime})
is given by:

A_{G}=\sum_{i\leq d}P_{i}A_{M}P_{i}^{*},

where
P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}
are independent random
n\times n
permutations. We then have:

Theorem 4.1
(
[
42
]
)

For
A_{G}
as above,

\mathbb{P}[\lambda_{2}(A_{G})\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}]>0.

i.e.,
A_{G}
is ‘‘one-sided Ramanujan’’ with nonzero probability
4
4

4

Actually,
[
42
]
showed that the same is true for random bipartite

.
By Theorem
2.1
, this conclusion follows from the bound

\lambda_{2}(\mathbb{E}\det(zI-A_{G}))\leq 2\sqrt{d-1},

(4.1)

which we will focus on for the remainder of this section.

The expected characteristic polynomial in (
4.1
) is not a matching polynomial, but it turns out to have another special structure. In particular, it can be related in a simple way to

\chi[A_{M}](z):=\det(zI-A_{M})=(z^{2}-1)^{n/2},

the characteristic polynomial of a single matching.
The relation is via the
Walsh Convolution
of two polynomials, denoted
\boxplus_{n}
, which defined as follows. Given any monic polynomial
p(z)
of degree
n
, let
\hat{p}
be the unique polynomial of degree
n
satisfying

\hat{p}(d/dz)z^{n}=p(z).

(4.2)

Given a pair of monic polynomials
p
and
q
of degree
n
, define

(p\boxplus_{n}q)(z):=\hat{p}(d/dz)\hat{q}(d/dz)z^{n}.

This convolution was originally discovered by Walsh in
[
52
]
in the context of classical invariant theory, where he showed that it preserves real-rootedness. Its connection to expected characteristic polynomials of random matrices was discovered in
[
43
]
.

The punch line is that for

5
Open Questions

The common theme in the works discussed in this survey is:

Expected characteristic polynomials allow us to
sharply
relate the spectra of finite dimensional random matrices and
the infinite dimensional operators to which they converge, but with
low probability
.

There is likely more to be said about this connection. We conclude with six questions which we hope will lead to further developments.

1.

Are there infinite sequences of non-bipartite Ramanujan graphs for every
d\geq 3
? The above theorems only handle the bipartite case.

2.

Is the probability that a random regular graph is Ramanujan bounded away from zero?

3.

Is there a polynomial time algorithm for finding the covers guaranteed by Theorems
3.1
and
3.4
? Currently, such an algorithm is only known in the setting of Theorem
4.1

[
17
]
.

4.

More speculatively, can the methods discussed above be employed in the setting of random hyperbolic surfaces? The recent breakthrough of Hide and Magee
[
30
]
shows that random covers of such surfaces are the appropriate analogue of “Ramanujan” in that setting.

5.

Can the results of this survey be extended to obtain “super-Ramanujan” bounds of type

|\lambda_{i}(A_{G})|\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}-\epsilon_{n},

for some
\epsilon_{n}
asymptotically matching the Alon-Boppana bound? The proofs above yield roughly
\epsilon_{n}=d/n
, but it is conceivable that
1/\log^{c}(n)
is achievable. This question was asked by P. Sarnak in the context of computing the “bass note spectrum” of various homogeneous spaces.

6.

Following
[
28
]
, does every
d-
regular bipartite graph have a Ramanujan signing by elements of
PSL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
?
