Cross-national comparison of the association between adult child educational attainment and older parents’ functional health
Authors: Yulin Yang, Karla Renata Flores Romero, Sirena Gutierrez, Ah-Reum Lee, Emma Nichols, Jacqueline Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMounting evidence points to the protective role of adult child education in older parents’ health, including their risk of ADL and IADL difficulties. Yet, little is known whether this association varies cross-nationally due to contextual differences in intergenerational transfers, family dynamics, and other factors. This study examines the relationship between adult child education and parents’ functional health outcomes across countries, using comparable population-based datasets.MethodsWe used harmonized data from seven population-based studies accessed through the Gateway to Global Aging Data. Our analytic sample included 88 683 adults 60 years and older from 24 countries. We estimated and meta-analyzed associations between adult child education and respondents’ (parents’) difficulty with basic and instrumental activities of daily living (ADL and IADLs), controlling for respondents’ own education and other sociodemographic characteristics. We also considered heterogeneity across parent and child demographics (eg, parent gender, child gender).ResultsAcross all studies, each additional year of education averaged across adult children was associated with a 3% lower risk of difficulty with both ADLs (95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.95, 0.98) and IADLs (95% CI: 0.95, 0.98). Statistical heterogeneity appeared to be driven by one study (eg, I2 = 78.94% when the study was included vs I2 = 0.04% when excluded). Results suggest limited heterogeneity across demographic subgroups.Conclusion“Upward” intergenerational benefits of higher adult child education for parents’ late-life ADL and IADL difficulties appear to be relatively consistent across a range of global settings. Further research is needed to uncover the mechanisms driving these cross-national associations.

Introduction

While significant gains in life expectancy have been achieved globally, emerging research is now evaluating whether these increases in life expectancy are disability-free.1 Recent evidence shows substantial cross-national variation in disability trends, with some countries experiencing increases (eg, Belgium, Czechia, and Mexico) and others declines (eg, Denmark, Greece, and Korea).1 In the United States, disability prevalence has risen, with a 1.7% annual increase in disability in activities of daily living (ADLs) between 2000 and 2014.2 As populations age and disability burdens grow, older adults increasingly depend on family support, particularly from adult children.3–5 Yet despite the importance of these intergenerational ties, little research has examined how socioeconomic resources across generations shape older adults’ health and functional outcomes.

Fundamental Cause Theory posits that socioeconomic resources, including education, shape health by providing access to flexible resources that can be used to avoid risks and minimize the consequences of disease.6,7 Consistent with this perspective, a substantial body of research has documented persistent educational gradients in late-life disability.8,9 However, most applications of this theory focus on individuals’ own socioeconomic resources, with less consideration of potential intergenerational benefits.10,11 Examining adult children’s educational attainment provides an opportunity to assess whether the knowledge, financial resources, social connections, and access to information associated with higher education can be mobilized to benefit aging parents. If so, the health advantages associated with education may extend beyond individuals themselves, helping parents avoid health risks, manage illness more effectively, and maintain functioning in later life.

An intergenerational perspective on the effects of education on late-life disability can bridge research on intergenerational social support and fundamental cause theory. Specifically, growing evidence underscores the protective role of adult children’s education across a wide range of parental health outcomes.10–17 Although some studies have documented a protective effect of adult child education on parents’ functional limitations, for example, in Mexico and Taiwan,18–20 evidence on later-life disability outcomes remains limited.

Cross-national comparisons provide a unique opportunity to examine whether the influence of adult children’s educational attainment on parental functional health is universal or shaped by broader social and institutional contexts. While evidence on adult children’s education and parents’ health has emerged from across the globe, cross-national studies that employ similar methods and measures—allowing for direct comparison of findings—remain scarce. More broadly, harmonized cross-national studies contribute to evidence triangulation21 by evaluating whether associations are consistent across diverse populations and settings with different social, cultural, and policy environments. If evidence is consistent across these contexts, it strengthens confidence that observed associations are not unique to a single country or study design and provides a stronger foundation for evidence synthesis and policy development. Such evidence also highlights the potential for investments in education to generate intergenerational health benefits, particularly in countries experiencing rapid educational expansion and economic development.

In this study, we leveraged harmonized data from seven population-based studies across 24 high-, middle-, and low-income countries (HICs and LMICs, respectively) to evaluate the association between adult children’s education and older parents’ risk of ADL and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) difficulties using consistent measurement and covariate control, which can help triangulate evidence on the mechanisms linking adult child education to the functional health of aging parents.

Adult child education and late-life health in older parents: potential mechanisms

Adult child education may serve as a “family-level resource” that may influence older parents’ functional health through a range of financial and nonfinancial mechanisms.22 In China and South Korea, older parents with more highly educated adult children are more likely to receive financial support,23,24 reflecting these children’s greater earning potential. Such support has been associated with fewer functional limitations among older adults in Mexico18 and may help cover healthcare costs and reduce the financial burden of chronic conditions that contribute to disability.

Nonfinancial support may include helping parents navigate healthcare systems, make informed medical decisions, and access timely treatment to prevent disability.25 A US-based study suggests that higher adult child education is associated with a lower risk of parental mortality, partly due to its link with parental health behaviors, such as smoking and physical activity.12 More educated children may encourage or facilitate healthy behaviors, including vigorous exercise and cultural participation, which have been linked to better cognitive and functional health and lower disability risk in later life.26–28

The mechanisms through which higher adult child education may reduce risk of ADL and IADL difficulties among older parents likely vary across contexts due to differences in intergenerational mobility, family structures, living arrangements, healthcare systems, and caregiving expectations, among others.17,20,29 In HICs, where educational gains are relatively modest across generations and families tend to be smaller, children with higher levels of education are more likely to provide time transfers, such as caregiving support,30–32 as opposed to financial support. They may advocate for preventive care, coordinate medical appointments, and arrange formal support services rather than providing hands-on care directly. By contrast, in LMICs, larger intergenerational educational gaps and limited public healthcare often necessitate that better-educated children provide both financial and caregiving support, effectively compensating for under-resourced elder care infrastructures.18,19,33 Due to cross-national contextual differences, it is necessary to use harmonized methods and measures to evaluate the relationship between adult children’s education and late-life disability outcomes.

Resources substitution versus Resource multiplication: variations by subpopulations

Although there is evidence of an association between adult children’s education and parental health, researchers continue to debate who benefits more from the educational attainment of adult children—socioeconomically disadvantaged parents in need of support or those with greater existing resources. According to the resource substitution theory, parents with limited resources, including those with lower educational levels, may benefit the most from their children’s education,34 as their children’s knowledge and financial stability help offset their own disadvantages.

Conversely, resource multiplication theory suggests that parents with greater socioeconomic resources may benefit the most, as their own knowledge and financial means enable them to leverage their children’s education more effectively—whether through shared health literacy, social capital, or access to higher-quality healthcare.34 Highly educated parents might be better positioned to translate their children’s education into well-being gains, particularly in contexts where formal caregiving services or high-quality medical care require financial investment. Together, these theories suggest that adult children’s education can either reduce or amplify existing socioeconomic disparities. However, little is known about how these patterns vary across different national contexts. Again, while scholars have tested these hypotheses in single-study analyses, direct comparisons across national datasets are scarce.

Gender differences in the cross-national contexts

The relationship between adult children’s education and parents’ ADL and IADL difficulties may also differ by the gender of the parents and vary across countries. Economic development often correlates with reduced gender inequality,35 including differences in access to education and the returns on educational investments. In LMICs, older women in current cohorts tend to have lower educational attainment, limited labor market participation due to primary caregiving roles,36 and lower accumulated wealth over their lifetime compared to their male counterparts. These factors contribute to lower yearly pensions and overall socioeconomic status (SES). Consequently, older mothers may gain greater benefits from their children’s education, which can help compensate for their relatively lower SES through both financial and nonfinancial transfers that support functional health.37

Gender differences in caregiving roles further complicate this relationship, with daughters often more involved in direct care while sons typically provide financial support.38,39 Daughters with higher educational attainment may have better health literacy and caregiving skills, which can help mitigate the risks of ADL and IADL difficulties for aging parents. In contrast, sons’ financial contributions, especially in cultures where male economic support is emphasized, can indirectly reduce the risk of ADL and IADL difficulties by facilitating access to healthcare and assistive devices. These gender differences are more pronounced in societies with traditional gender roles, where women are expected to fulfill nurturing responsibilities.

The present study

Leveraging harmonized data from seven population-based studies spanning high-, middle-, and low-income countries, this study examines the cross-national association between adult children’s education and older parents’ risk of ADL and IADL difficulties. We expect that higher levels of adult child education would be associated with lower risk across settings, but that the magnitude of this relationship might vary by setting such that respondents in higher-income countries may benefit less from higher levels of adult child education versus their counterparts in lower- or middle-income settings. We additionally examine how observed associations vary by factors such as adult child–parent coresidence status or parents’ own SES, which may either mitigate or reinforce socioeconomic disparities in late-life risk of ADL and IADL difficulties across diverse settings. Additionally, we examine how these associations differ by the gender of both the parent and the adult child across settings, recognizing the role of gender in shaping intergenerational support.

Methods

Data

We used data from seven nationally representative health and retirement longitudinal studies: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), Costa Rican Longevity and Healthy Aging Study (CRELES), Health and Retirement Study (HRS), Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), Longitudinal Aging Study in India (LASI), Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS), and the Survey of Health, Aging and Retirement in Europe (SHARE).

To ensure comparability across nations, the Gateway to Global Aging Data produced user-friendly versions of each study’s data, which included a vast array of variables specially processed to facilitate consistent cross-national comparisons.40 Most of the data used in this study comes from these harmonized data files; measures of adult child education, described below, were not part of the harmonized data files and were generated for this analysis.

Baseline data for these studies were collected between 1992 (HRS) and 2017 (LASI), with follow-ups occurring approximately every two years. The most recent waves available, as of the writing of this paper, extend to 2022 (HRS). For this study, we used data from waves collected in 2018 or the closest available year (see Supplementary Appendix Table S1 for the specific study year used in our analyses); 2018 was chosen to avoid later waves fielded during the COVID-19 pandemic and because most studies conducted data collection in 2018. Unique pandemic-era dynamics (eg, related to intergenerational coresidence) may yield estimates that do not generalize to nonpandemic waves.

Age-related inclusion criteria ranged from 45 years and older (CHARLS, CRELES, KLoSA, and LASI), 50 years and older (MHAS and SHARE), and 51 years and older (HRS). To ensure comparability and focus on older adults facing greater risk of ADL and IADL difficulties, we restricted our analytic sample to adults aged 60 years and older, with the following sample sizes: CHARLS 8012, CRELES 1518, HRS 8417, KLoSA 4994, LASI 27 026, MHAS 6700, and SHARE 32 016. Detailed information on how these sample sizes were determined can be found in Supplementary Appendix Figure S1.

Measures

Exposure

Our exposure variable was the average years of educational attainment among all children aged 25 or older. Consistent with prior research,19,41 we selected age 25 as a cutoff because most individuals have completed their formal education by that age. HRS and LASI report both the years of education and the level of education for respondents’ children, while CHARLS, CRELES, KLoSA, MHAS, and SHARE provide education levels only. To ensure consistency, we used the years of education reported by HRS and LASI and converted the education levels from the other studies into years of education following study-specific guidelines. All studies that provide education levels report them for each wave for all living children in the specified age range, except SHARE, which reports education levels at baseline and only updates them if changes occur or for children not previously listed. For SHARE, we used the most recently available education level.

In sensitivity analyses, we defined adult child education as the highest years of education attained to evaluate the robustness of findings to an alternative exposure definition.

Outcomes

Activities of daily living

We identified three activities of daily living (ADLs) that were included in all seven studies and were directly comparable across them: bathing, eating, and using the toilet. These formed the basis of our main outcome variable, as they were the only ADLs consistently fielded across all studies. Most studies asked respondents if they experienced any difficulty with these activities and provided yes/no response options. KLoSA used a slightly different approach with a combined question: “Can you do this activity, or do you need someone to help you?” with a single yes/no response. While this phrasing captures both the ability to perform the task and the need for assistance, it conceptually aligns with the concept of “experiencing difficulty” used in the other studies and was deemed sufficiently comparable for inclusion. We measured ADL difficulties using a binary indicator for difficulty with any of these three activities. We also used the count of ADLs difficulties (ranging from 0 to 3) as an alternative outcome specification.

In sensitivity analyses, we incorporated a fourth ADL, getting dressed, which was available in six of the seven studies (excluding CRELES). We generated a binary indicator for difficulty with any of the four activities.

Instrumental activities of daily living

We identified four instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs) that were included in all seven studies and were directly comparable across them: managing money, taking medication, shopping for groceries, and preparing hot meals. Like with ADLs, we based our main IADL outcome on the set of tasks that were consistently fielded in all studies. Most studies assessed difficulties with these tasks using a yes/no response to whether respondents experienced trouble. As with the ADL measures, KLoSA used a combined question: “Can you do this activity, or do you need someone to help you?” with a single yes/no response. Similarly to ADLs, we considered responses as conceptually aligned with the concept of “experiencing difficulty.” We measured IADL difficulties using a binary indicator for difficulty with any of these four activities. We also used the number of IADLs with difficulty (range from 0 to 4) as an alternative outcome specification.

In sensitivity analyses, we incorporated a fifth IADL, using the telephone, which was available in five of the seven studies (excluding CRELES and MHAS). We generated a binary indicator for difficulty with any of the five activities.

Covariates

We selected covariates that might plausibly serve as common causes of adult children’s education and older parents’ ADL and IADL difficulties. These covariates include respondents’ demographic characteristics, early-life socioeconomic status (SES), and lifecourse SES. Although some measures varied across studies, they were designed to capture equivalent constructs. Demographic characteristics included respondents’ age (all studies), gender (all studies), marital status (all studies), race and ethnicity (HRS and CRELES), nativity (HRS and SHARE), caste (LASI), speaking an indigenous language (MHAS), the total number of living children ≥25 years (all studies), the percentage of daughters among adult children (all studies), and spouses’ age (all studies, for respondents currently married/partnered, with a categorical variable for those not currently married to retain the entire analytic sample), and country of residence (SHARE).

Early-life SES variables included parental education level (all studies except CRELES, with a categorical variable included for missing data), childhood health condition (CHARLS, HRS, LASI, MHAS, and SHARE), family financial status around age 16 (CHARLS, HRS, and LASI), and rural versus urban status during grade school (HRS), and whether there was a toilet inside the home during childhood (MHAS). Lifecourse SES measures included respondents’ own years of education (all studies); their spouse’s level of education (all studies, for respondents currently married/partnered and for respondents formerly married/partnered when available, with a categorical variable for those missing on this measure to retain the entire analytic sample); current home ownership status (CRELES, HRS, and MHAS); rural versus urban status (CHARLS, CRELES, KLoSA, LASI, MHAS, and SHARE); household size (all studies); and housing characteristics such as having a toilet inside the home (CRELES and LASI), running water (CRELES, LASI, and MHAS), a kitchen (CRELES), durable housing materials (eg, roof, walls, or flooring) (LASI and MHAS), use of unclean fuel (LASI and MHAS), and the number of rooms in the house (LASI and MHAS).

Statistical approach

We fit modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors to estimate the associations between adult child education and older parents’ risk of ADL or IADL difficulties in each study.

Given the comprehensive set of covariates included in the fully adjusted models, we assessed potential multicollinearity by calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs). Mean VIF values ranged from 1.5 to 2.6 across studies, indicating little evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Although two sparse ethnicity categories in CRELES exhibited elevated VIF values, likely due to the relatively small sample size (n = 1518), VIFs for the remaining covariates were substantially lower. We then pooled these estimates using a random effects meta-analysis to generate a single estimate of the association between adult child education and the risk of ADL or IADL difficulties.

To assess heterogeneity, we conducted stratified analyses within each study by: (1) adult child coresidence status, evaluating the average education of either coresident or nonresident children; (2) parents’ own education, categorized as above versus below the country-specific median; (3) adult child gender; and (4) parent gender. We then meta-analyzed these stratified estimates across studies. In the sensitivity analysis, we also assess the robustness of our findings to alternative definitions of adult child education. We repeated the primary analyses using the highest years of education among children aged 25 years or older.

Mean age varied from 64 years (SD 2.5) in CRELES to 72 years (SD 8.2) in SHARE. Over 50% of respondents across all studies were women, and in most studies, more than 60% were married. Respondents in LASI, CHARLS, and MHAS had the lowest average years of education, ranging from 3 to 5 years (SDs: 4.1–4.5). Respondents in SHARE and HRS had the highest averages, ranging from 10 to 14 years (SDs: 2.9–4.3). One-fifth of respondents in both CHARLS and LASI had at least one ADL difficulty; 25% of those in CHARLS and 37% in LASI had at least one IADL difficulty. In the remaining studies, the percentage of older adults with at least one ADL difficulty ranged from 3.8% in KLOSA to 8.9% in MHAS, while for IADL difficulty, the range was 9% in KLOSA to 14.5% in CRELES.

Adult child education and older parents’ risk of ADL and IADL difficulty

Higher average adult child education was associated with a lower risk of ADL and IADL difficulties in all studies (Table 2 for ADLs and Table 3 for IADLs). One exception was LASI, for which the estimate for binary ADL difficulties was null (Table 2); estimates from LASI were generally smaller in magnitude and more precise than those from the other studies. Across the remaining studies, the strength of associations varied to some extent. For example, in CRELES, each additional year of education averaged among adult children was associated with a 9% lower risk of ADL difficulties (95% CI: 0.85–0.97), while in KLOSA, each additional year was associated with a 2% reduction (95% CI: 0.94–1.02), although the 95% confidence interval crossed the null. In CHARLS, HRS, MHAS, and SHARE, the reduction in ADL difficulty risk ranged from 4% to 5% (with 95% CI of 0.95–0.98, 0.92–0.99, 0.93–0.98, 0.95–0.97, respectively). For IADL difficulties, estimates ranged from 4% in CHARLS (95% CI: 0.94–0.97), CRELES (95% CI: 0.92–1.00), KLOSA (95% CI: 0.93–0.99), and SHARE (95% CI: 0.96–0.97), to 1% in LASI (95% CI: 0.99–1.00). Findings were consistent when using the number of ADL or IADL difficulties as the alternative outcome specification.

In sensitivity analyses using the highest education among adult children as the exposure measure, results for ADL difficulties were robust, with similar or smaller effect sizes observed across studies (Supplementary Appendix Table S21 and Figure S4a and b). In contrast, associations with IADL difficulties were generally attenuated, and several estimates were no longer statistically significant (Supplementary Appendix Table S22 and Figure S5a and b).

Heterogeneity by adult child and parent demographics

We generally observed stronger associations between the education of nonresident children and parents’ risk of ADL and IADL difficulties compared to coresident children (Supplementary Appendix Tables S3 and S4). For example, in CRELES, each additional year of nonresident children’s education was associated with an 8% lower risk of ADL difficulties (95% CI: 0.84–1.02), while coresident children’s education showed no association, and the 95% confidence interval crossed the null. A similar pattern was seen in KLOSA for IADLs, where nonresident children’s education was associated with a 5% lower risk, compared to 1% for coresident children, whose 95% confidence interval also included the null. Exceptions were observed in LASI and MHAS for ADLs, and in CRELES, LASI, and MHAS for IADLs, where associations were stronger or similar for coresident children, and 95% confidence intervals for nonresident children included the null.

Associations also tended to be larger among parents with at or above the median level, although 95% confidence intervals overlapped throughout and in some cases included the null (Supplementary Appendix Tables S5 and S6). In this group, IADL risk declined by up to 13% per additional year of adult child education (eg, CRELES), while reductions were smaller or absent among those with lower education (eg, 4% in CHARLS, CRELES, MHAS, and SHARE, and no association in HRS and LASI). Similar patterns were observed for IADLs, with risk reductions among higher-educated parents ranging from 8% in KLOSA to 2% in LASI. Among those with below-median education, effects were generally smaller, except in CRELES.

Across most studies, higher average education among daughters and sons was consistently associated with a lower risk of ADL and IADL difficulties (Supplementary Appendix Tables S7 and S8), with confidence intervals generally overlapping and, in some cases, including the null. In LASI and MHAS, associations between higher sons’ (vs daughters’) education and ADL difficulties were of larger magnitude, while in KLOSA, higher daughters’ (vs sons’) education was more protective. No gender difference in CHARLS, CRELES, HRS, and SHARE. For IADLs, associations were generally of larger magnitude for daughters (eg, CRELES, KLOSA, LASI, and MHAS), though in CHARLS and HRS the associations were of larger magnitude for sons; there were no differences by adult child gender in SHARE.

A similar pattern was observed by parental gender (Supplementary Appendix Tables S9 and S10). While 95% confidence intervals generally overlapped and occasionally included the null, some studies found stronger associations for mothers (eg, CHARLS and CRELES), others for fathers (eg, HRS, KLOSA, LASI, and MHAS); point estimates in SHARE were identical by parent gender. For IADLs, associations were of larger magnitude for mothers (vs fathers) in CRELES, while the opposite was true in CHARLS, KLOSA, and MHAS. Effect estimates were comparable by parent gender in LASI and SHARE.

Additionally, sensitivity analyses using the binary indicator for difficulty with the four ADLs (comparable across all studies except CRELES, see Supplementary Appendix Table S11) and the five IADLs (comparable across all studies except CRELES and MHAS, see Supplementary Appendix Table S12) yielded results largely consistent with our main analyses. This consistency was observed in the corresponding meta-analysis (Supplementary Appendix Figures S2 and S3), and in analyses stratified by coresidence status (Supplementary Appendix Tables S13 and S14), parents’ education (Supplementary Appendix Tables S15 and S16), adult child gender (Supplementary Appendix Tables S17 and S18), and parents’ gender (Supplementary Appendix Tables S19 and S20).

Discussion

Using harmonized population-based data from seven studies across 24 countries, this study provides one of the first cross-national examinations of the relationship between adult children’s educational attainment and parental functional health in later life. Extending prior research and broadening the application of Fundamental Cause Theory beyond individuals’ own SES, our primary findings suggest that the benefits of education may operate across generations. Specifically, higher adult child educational attainment was consistently associated with a lower risk of both ADL and IADL disability among older parents. The association was also remarkably consistent across countries and sociodemographic subgroups, suggesting that educational resources available through adult children may represent an important and broadly relevant determinant of healthy aging.

By leveraging harmonized, internationally comparable data with consistent measures and covariate controls that account for contextual differences, our study is useful for evidence triangulation to understand late-life risk of ADL and IADL difficulties.21 Such a comparative approach strengthens the robustness of findings by addressing the limitations inherent in any single observational study. In doing so, our analysis helps address challenges in comparing findings across prior studies, where differences in study design (eg, regression adjustment vs quasi-experimental approaches), measurement approaches—such as exposure definitions (average years of education vs degree-based measures), and outcome assessments (physical functional limitations vs years lived without functional difficulties vs ADL/IADL difficulties)—often limit comparability.

Indeed, evidence in this study based on harmonized comparable data from diverse settings demonstrates that the relationship between adult child education and parental functional health is not confined to a single country or institutional context. This suggests that intergenerational educational resources may represent a broadly relevant determinant of healthy aging, while also highlighting the need for future research to identify the social and policy conditions under which these family resources are most consequential.

While the magnitude of the association varied across studies—for example, from a 4% reduction in the risk of ADL difficulties in CHARLS, MHAS, and SHARE to a 9% reduction in CRELES, and from 1% in LASI to 5% in CHARLS, CRELES, KLOSA, and SHARE for IADLs—the overlapping confidence intervals suggest a consistent pattern. The direction of our findings is consistent with the two previous country-specific studies in Mexico19 and Taiwan20 on this topic, though direct comparisons are limited by differences in how adult child education was measured. Our findings were robust, as the associations were very similar when offspring education was measured using either the highest educational attainment or the average across children, although effect sizes were somewhat weaker for the former. This pattern suggests that parental functional health may be more strongly influenced by the overall educational resources within the family than by the resources of a single highly educated child.

Pooled estimates from our meta-analyses initially indicated some statistical heterogeneity; however, this heterogeneity disappeared when LASI was excluded. Although LASI showed the same overall direction of association, its effect estimates were consistently smaller in magnitude than those of the other studies, and its substantially larger sample size gave it greater influence on the pooled heterogeneity statistics. The weaker associations observed in LASI may reflect differences in population characteristics, including the lower average educational attainment of both adult children (7 years) and older parents (3 years), as well as broader country-specific social, economic, and institutional contexts. Future studies are needed to identify the contextual factors that may modify the relationship between offspring education and parental functional health.

Our finding of relatively consistent associations across countries was relatively surprising given contextual differences in intergenerational mobility, family structures, living arrangements, healthcare systems, and caregiving expectations, among other factors. Notably, the only other related cross-national study, which examined the health returns of adult children’s education for older parents, also reported similarly consistent associations with late-life cognitive performance in the HRS, LASI, and MHAS.42 Aligning with FCT, intergenerational educational benefits can operate through mechanisms that adapt to parents’ needs, compensating for gaps in individual socioeconomic status and country-specific welfare systems. These mechanisms could include improved health literacy, caregiving quality, and financial support, all of which provide protective effects regardless of variations in the relationship between individual education and ADL or IADL difficulties. For instance, in LMICs, highly educated adult children may provide more financial support to compensate for weaker social welfare systems,19 whereas in HICs, they may offer more support through time transfers, such as caregiving and promoting healthy lifestyles.26

We found suggestive evidence of within-country differences in the association of interest based on both parent and child socio-demographics, warranting further exploration. In stratified models, the protective effects of higher education among nonresident (vs coresident) adult children appeared stronger for ADLs in CHARLS, CRELES, HRS, KLOSA, and SHARE and for IADLs in CHARLS, KLOSA, and SHARE. However, overlapping 95% confidence intervals indicate that these differences by co-residence status were not statistically significant. This pattern suggests that even when children live apart, their education may still offer protective benefits to older parents—potentially through financial support, advice, or assistance navigating healthcare systems.

Stratification by parents’ education levels partially supported the resource multiplication hypothesis,37 as the protective associations between higher adult child education and ADL difficulties was concentrated among parents with medium or higher education in CRELES, HRS, LASI, MHAS, and SHARE, though confidence intervals again overlapped with estimates of association for those with lower levels of education. Findings for IADL difficulties were less consistent. In most contexts—China, US, Korea, India, Mexico, and European countries—higher adult child education was more strongly associated with lower IADL difficulties risk among parents with medium or higher education. However, in Costa Rica, the association was stronger among parents with lower education. The less consistent findings for IADL difficulties may reflect the greater variability in these tasks—such as managing money, shopping, and preparing meals—which are more influenced by cultural, social, and environmental contexts compared to the more universally consistent ADL tasks like bathing, eating, and using the toilet.

While prior research points to potential gender differences, we found largely similar associations by the gender of both adult children and parents, with overlapping 95% confidence intervals across comparisons. Still, some variation in effect sizes emerged. Daughters’ education was more strongly linked to lower ADL/IADL difficulties in several settings (eg, ADLs in KLOSA; IADLs in CRELES, KLOSA, LASI, and MHAS), whereas sons’ education showed stronger associations in others (eg, ADLs in LASI and MHAS; IADLs in CHARLS and HRS). For parents, effect sizes tended to be larger for mothers in CHARLS and CRELES (ADLs) and for fathers in CHARLS, KLOSA, and MHAS (IADLs), though confidence intervals consistently overlapped and often included the null.

This study has several limitations. First, although ADL and IADL difficulties are influenced by environmental factors that vary across countries (eg, living conditions affecting tasks like bathing or eating). However, previous research has validated their reliability for cross-national comparisons, and our sensitivity analyses support the robustness of our findings. Second, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to assess how the benefits of children’s education evolve over time. Third, the indicators used to construct the IADL measure, including managing money, taking medications, grocery shopping, and preparing meals, may be influenced by gendered roles. As a result, the composite IADL measure may partly reflect gendered divisions of labor rather than functional limitations alone, particularly among older cohorts and across cultural contexts. Fourth, we selected age 25 as the cutoff for adult children. Although some adult children may continue their education beyond age 25, the mean age of adult children in our sample was substantially higher than this threshold. Thus, most adult children in our analytical sample had likely completed their formal education, suggesting that our estimates were unlikely to be biased by using age 25 as the cutoff. Lastly, residual confounding remains a concern—for instance, families with higher-educated children may have longstanding health-promoting behaviors, such as better nutrition and preventive care, that influence parents’ risk of ADL or IADL difficulties independently of education. We mitigated this by adjusting for life-course socioeconomic variables. However, other factors (eg, genetic predisposition) not accounted for in our models could also confound the association, as traits influencing education may also affect parental health. Nevertheless, similar positive effects found in country- or region-specific quasi-experimental studies,15,17,26,43 reduce concerns about confounding and strengthen the case for a potential causal role of children’s education.

Despite these limitations, our study adds to the growing evidence of the protective role of adult children’s education in supporting older parents’ functional health. Our findings have important public health implications, highlighting the intergenerational benefits of investing in education to enhance family well-being worldwide. Future research should further explore the mechanisms through which adult children’s education influences parents’ functional health and examine how these associations vary across cultural and socioeconomic contexts. Additionally, investigating the broader impact of offspring education on other health-related outcomes globally could provide valuable insights for developing targeted interventions to reduce late-life ADL and IADL difficulties.
