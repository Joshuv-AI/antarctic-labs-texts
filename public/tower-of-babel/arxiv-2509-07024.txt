TGLF-SINN: Deep Learning Surrogate Model for Accelerating Turbulent Transport Modeling in Fusion
Yadi Cao, Futian Zhang, Wesley Liu, Tom Neiser, Orso Meneghini, Lawson Fuller, Sterling Smith, Raffi Nazikian, Brian Sammuli, Rose Yu

Abstract
The Trapped Gyro-Landau Fluid (TGLF) model provides fast, accurate predictions of turbulent transport in tokamaks, but whole device simulations requiring thousands of evaluations remain computationally expensive. Neural network (NN) surrogates offer accelerated inference with fully differentiable approximations that enable gradient-based coupling but typically require large training datasets to capture transport flux variations across plasma conditions, creating significant training burden and limiting applicability to expensive gyrokinetic simulations. We propose TGLF-SINN (Spectra-Informed Neural Network) with three key innovations: (1) principled feature engineering that reduces target prediction range, simplifying the learning task; (2) physics-guided regularization of transport spectra to improve generalization under sparse data; and (3) Bayesian Active Learning (BAL) to strategically select training samples based on model uncertainty, reducing data requirements while maintaining accuracy. Our approach achieves superior performance with significantly less training data. In offline settings, TGLF-SINN reduces logarithmic root mean squared error (LRMSE) by 12. 4% compared to the current baseline TGLF-NN . Using only 25% of the complete dataset with BAL, we achieve LRMSE only 0.0165 higher than TGLF-NN and 0.0248 higher than our offline model (0.0583). In downstream flux matching applications, our NN surrogate provides 45× speedup over TGLF while maintaining comparable accuracy, demonstrating potential for training efficient surrogates for higher-fidelity models where data acquisition is costly and sparse.

Preliminaries

3.1
TGLF and Saturation Rules

TGLF computes the total turbulent fluxes of particles
\Gamma
, momentum
\Pi
, and heat
Q
by summing the contributions from each linear mode at different wavenumbers
\{k_{y}^{1},\dots,k_{y}^{N_{y}}\}
:

\Gamma=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\Gamma^{j}(k_{y}^{j}),\quad\Pi=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\Pi^{j}(k_{y}^{j}),\quad Q=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}Q^{j}(k_{y}^{j}).

(1)

Here,
\Gamma^{j},\Pi^{j},Q^{j}
denote the corresponding fluxes at each wavenumber
k_{y}^{j}
. These contributions are obtained by first solving reduced-order model (ROM) equations at thermal equilibrium to compute quasilinear weights and initial intensities. The intensities are then adjusted using “saturation rules”
[
41
,
42
,
20
]
to account for nonlinear interactions between linear modes at saturation states. Finally, the spectral fluxes
\Gamma^{j},\Pi^{j},Q^{j}
are computed by multiplying the quasilinear weights by the adjusted intensities.
For conciseness, we omit the subscripts
i
or
e
in (
1
) indicating particle contributions (e.g., from ions or electrons); these are reintroduced in later result sections when metrics require distinguishing between particle types.
For a complete derivation of the TGLF model, we kindly refer the reader to
[
21
]
, and for the specific saturation rules used in this work, see
[
45
]
.

3.2
TGLF-NN

TGLF-NN

[
34
,
35
]
, the state-of-the-art (SOTA) surrogate model, is an encoder-ResNet-decoder neural network. It takes 31 physical input parameters that TGLF depends on (e.g., normalized gradients, safety factor, effective charge, shaping parameters, etc; see
[
34
,
35
]
or the General Atomics (GA) website
1
1

1

https://gafusion.github.io/doc/tglf/tglf_list.html#vexb-shear
for the full list) and predicts four outputs: electron particle fluxes
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma_{e}}
, ion momentum fluxes
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Pi_{i}}
, and electron and ion heat fluxes
Q_{e}
and
Q_{i}
.

The vanilla
TGLF-NN
adopts an uncommon loss treatment (with respect to the ML community) to account for the huge variation in flux magnitudes. Given a batch of predictions and targets, both are first transformed via an
\sinh^{-1}
function to squash the wide range of flux values locally. Then, a custom batch normalization strategy (where the batch mean adds some random shifts) is applied. Finally, an
R^{2}
-type loss between the processed predictions and targets is calculated for gradient computation. An ensemble of 30 models is required to produce stable inferences.
We note, via preliminary tests, that this approach is only stable with very small batch sizes. Combined with the ensemble requirement, this contributes to slow training, especially on large datasets. We refer interested readers to the vanilla
TGLF-NN
implementation (as of this date) at the GA repository
2
2

2

https://github.com/ProjectTorreyPines/TGLFNN.jl/tree/1f080a901ee9cacf58e0d63a65a9455b5ae76e6f
.

Methodology

In this section, we present the methodology for implementing TGLF-SINN, a surrogate model that learns the transported fluxes of TGLF. Figure
1
(a) provides an overview of TGLF-SINN: it takes the same 31 physical parameters as
TGLF-NN
, then split into 24 parallel branches, each appending the corresponding wavenumber to the input before predicting the transported fluxes contributed by this wavenumber. Figure
1
(b) illustrates the shared backbone for predicting fluxes at each wavenumber, utilizing an Encoder-Resnet-Decoder structure.

The remainder of this section is organized as follows. Section
4.1
describes the dataset generation and filtering process. Section
4.2
introduces the principled feature engineering techniques employed. Section
4.3
explains the implementation of spectral regularization in the loss term. Finally, Section
4.4
discusses the integration of Bayesian active learning (BAL) with the training pipeline to improve data efficiency.

(a) TGLF-SINN takes 31 input features (the same as

4.1
Dataset

Our dataset is generated by running the numerical solver of TGLF over a dense sample of the full 31-dimensional input parameter space, denoted as
{\bm{x}}
, as described in Section
3.1
. We generate the datasets with two levels of perturbations. The first level is the
Major perturbation
set, which consists of approximately 330,000 samples that perturb key physical parameters in a continuous, dense fashion. The second level is the
Major + Minor perturbation
set, which builds on the Major perturbationset by introducing 9 additional discrete variations, resulting in around 3 million total samples.

Filtering Outliers.

Some input combinations and output solutions are rarely encountered in real experiments (i.e., a modeling gap exists between quasilinear theory and reality). Additionally, the numerical solver, particularly the approximate eigenvalue solver using Hermite basis functions, can converge to sub-optimal solutions due to truncation or numerical errors. As a result, the generated dataset inherently contains noise or outliers that can destabilize training. To address this, we iteratively apply a Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) filtering approach. Specifically, we compute:

{\bm{m}}=\mathrm{med}\left(\left\{\left|{\bm{x}}-\tilde{{\bm{x}}}\right|,\quad\text{for }{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{X}}\right\}\right),

(2)

where
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}=\mathrm{med}({\mathbb{X}})
is the median of the dataset
{\mathbb{X}}
, and
{\bm{m}}
is the median absolute deviation (MAD). We then define a distance for each data point
{\bm{x}}
as:

{\bm{d}}=\kappa\frac{|{\bm{x}}-\tilde{{\bm{x}}}|}{{\bm{m}}},

(3)

where
\kappa=1.4826
is a scaling factor that approximates the standard deviation under normality assumptions. In each iteration, any sample with
{\bm{d}}>\epsilon
in any channel is removed, where
\epsilon=200
is a threshold hyperparameter. The iteration terminates when fewer than
1\%
of the data points are removed in a single iteration.

The original
TGLF-NN
is trained on the Major + Minor perturbationset with outliers filtered out. Unless otherwise noted, we preserve this setting in our experiments. For the ablation study on training under sparse, noisy datasets, see Section
5.2
.

4.2
Feature Tuning

Compared to the batch-wise non-standardized treatment in
[
34
,
35
]
and the mixture-of-experts (MoE) scheme in
[
36
,
37
]
, we adopt a more standard feature engineering approach from the ML community to handle the large variation in flux magnitudes. Let
{\bm{y}}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma_{e}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Pi_{i}},Q_{e},Q_{i}\}
denote the concatenated fluxes and
{\bm{y}}^{j}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma_{e}}^{j}(k_{y}^{j}),{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Pi_{i}}^{j}(k_{y}^{j}),Q_{e}^{j}(k_{y}^{j}),Q_{i}^{j}(k_{y}^{j})\}
denote the contributions at the
j
-th wavenumber for
j=1,\dots,N_{y}
. We apply
\sinh^{-1}
transformations to both the final flux predictions
{\bm{y}}{{}^{\prime}}=(\mathcal{S}\circ\sinh^{-1})({\bm{y}})
and the per-spectra contributions
{\bm{y}}{{}^{\prime}}^{j}=(\mathcal{S}\circ\sinh^{-1})({\bm{y}}^{j})
, where
\mathcal{S}
represents standardization across the training dataset. The choice of
\sinh^{-1}
over
\log
is motivated by the need to accommodate possible negative predictions in TGLF.

4.3
Spectral Regularization

We append the wavenumbers
\{k_{y}^{1},\dots,k_{y}^{N_{y}}\}
to the input parameters
{\bm{x}}
as an additional channel to enable per-wavenumber predictions. Using the same encoder-ResNet-decoder architecture as in
TGLF-NN
, the model predicts the flux at each wavenumber as
f_{\theta}({\bm{x}},k_{y}^{j})
for
j=1,\dots,N_{y}
, where
\theta
represents the trainable parameters. The prediction
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
in the original space is reconstructed by summation and then inverse transformations
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}=\left(\sinh\circ\mathcal{S}^{-1}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}f_{\theta}\left({\bm{x}},k_{y}^{j}\right)\right)
.

The supervised loss on the turbulent transport flux is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{f}=\|{\bm{y}}{{}^{\prime}}-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}f_{\theta}\left({\bm{x}},k_{y}^{j}\right)\|^{2}.

(4)

In addition, we impose a regularization term
\mathcal{L}_{s}
on the per-spectra fluxes:

\mathcal{L}_{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\|{\bm{y}}{{}^{\prime}}^{j}-f_{\theta}\left({\bm{x}},k_{y}^{j}\right)\|^{2}.

(5)

Combined, the overall objective function reads:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{f}+\mathcal{L}_{s}.

(6)

4.4
Bayesian Active Learning

To study how the model performs under sparse datasets and active learning—particularly in preparation for learning higher-fidelity models—we employ Bayesian Active Learning (BAL) in a pool-based setting. The pool-based approach is chosen because the full dataset is available in our case, though the method can be minimally modified for online settings. BAL improves data efficiency by strategically selecting the most informative data points for training the surrogate model, reducing the amount of data to be simulated.

The BAL process proceeds as follows. We start with a small initial labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{0}
. At each iteration
t
, we apply an acquisition function
g({\bm{x}})
to rank all candidates in the unlabeled input space
\mathcal{U}
. The top
k
input candidates are selected based on their acquisition scores, labeled by running the TGLF numerical simulation
{\bm{y}}=L({\bm{x}})
, and added to
\mathcal{D}_{t}
to form an augmented dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t+1}
. The model is then retrained on
\mathcal{D}_{t+1}
, and its performance is reevaluated. This iterative process continues until a stopping criterion is met. The dataset update at iteration
t
reads:

\mathcal{D}_{t+1}=\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\{({\bm{x}},L({\bm{x}}))\mid{\bm{x}}\in\text{Top-}k(g({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\text{ for all }{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U})\}.

(7)

Our primary study target is the
Expected Information Gain
(EIG) acquisition function, while random selection (i.e., sampling using the full dataset’s distribution) serves as a natural baseline for comparison. In a pool-based BAL, the chosen candidates are finally mapped to their nearest neighbor in the dataset.

Expected Information Gain.

The EIG acquisition function estimates how much information is gained—i.e., the reduction in entropy—by acquiring new data entries. For each candidate
{\bm{x}}
, we compute the
prior entropy

H[p({\bm{y}}^{\prime}\mid{\bm{x}})]
based on the variance of the model’s predictions for
{\bm{x}}
. The
posterior entropy
is obtained using an updated model, which is retrained on an intermediate-augmented dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{*}=\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\}
, i.e., taking all proposed candidates while treating their
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
as ground truth. To estimate model uncertainty given our deterministic neural network structure, we incorporate a dropout layer during inference and ensemble multiple times. The EIG formally reads:

g_{\text{EIG}}({\bm{x}})=H[p({\bm{y}}^{\prime}\mid{\bm{x}})]-\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta^{*}\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}^{*})}[H[p({\bm{y}}^{\prime*}\mid{\bm{x}},\theta^{*})]],

(8)

where
\theta^{*}
and
{\bm{y}}^{\prime*}
are the parameters of the neural network and the prediction after training on the intermediate-augmented dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{*}
.

Radius-Based Candidate Proposing.

To propose candidates for EIG, we sample randomly according to the input variables’ distribution at different minor radius locations, which helps conserve the variation of local distribution in the transport. An ablation study on the use of this prior distribution information is provided in Section
5.3
. The detailed distribution is provided in Appendix
C
.

To assess the practicality of TGLF-SINN, we integrate it into the flux matching workflow of the FUSE integrated modeling suite

\rho\in[0.1,0.85]

[

]

TGLF-NN

Convergence Performance.

Both NN surrogates exhibit faster and more stable convergence than the numerical solver. As shown in Figure
7
, TGLF-SINN converges in 129 iterations for the L-mode and 412 iterations for H-mode, while
TGLF-NN
converges in 251 and 364 iterations, respectively. The numerical solver fails to converge after 1000 iterations in both scenarios.

Examining individual transport channels reveals that the numerical solver exhibits poor convergence in the
\omega_{0}
channel for the L-mode and struggles across all channels in the H-mode. We attribute the superior NN performance to its smoother function approximation compared to discrete operations in the numerical solver, resulting in reduced oscillations during gradient-based optimization.

Reconstruction Quality.

As shown in Figure
8
, TGLF-SINN and
TGLF-NN
consistently produce lower final mismatch values between predicted and experimental transport fluxes in both L-mode and H-mode scenarios, while TJLF exhibits significantly higher flux mismatches. The reconstructed core profiles (Figure
9
) show good agreement with the experimental measurements across all channels, with deviations observed in low
\rho
regions (near the magnetic axis) for
T_{e}
in L-mode and both
T_{e}
and
T_{i}
in H-mode, consistent with higher experimental uncertainty near the magnetic axis.

Computational Efficiency Gain.

Due to the faster inference time and fewer iterations required for convergence, the flux-matching procedure with TGLF-SINN takes 20 seconds compared to 15 minutes with the numerical solver using 128 threads, representing a 45× speed-up.
TGLF-NN
achieves a 600× speed-up (1.5 seconds), likely due to its native Julia implementation being more optimized for this workflow. Nevertheless, the 45× speed-up of TGLF-SINN demonstrates substantial efficiency advantages, and its translation into a Julia implementation could enable further speed-up.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We present TGLF-SINN, a state-of-the-ar neural surrogate for TGLF that predicts both final fluxes and per-wavenumber contributions. TGLF-SINN significantly reduced training data requirements and improved robustness in noisy and sparse dataset settings. Combined with Bayesian Active Learning (BAL), TGLF-SINN achieves LRMSE only 0.0165 higher than offline
TGLF-NN
and 0.0248 higher than our offline performance using just 25% of the data, representing 12.4% error reduction compared to
TGLF-NN
.

Several promising extensions are worth exploring. First, combining our approach with Mixture-of-Experts frameworks for explicit mode classification (ITG vs. ETG) could further concentrate flux distributions and improve specialized mode predictions. Second, learning linear mode solutions directly which can analytically calculate the quasilinear weights and intensities in a more decomposed fashion, enabling zeroi-shot generation to new saturation rules without retraining. Third, TGLF-SINN spectra could serve as synthetic diagnostics for between-shot predictions of theoretically interesting wavenumber ranges for Doppler Back-Scattering diagnostics
[
58
]
. Finally, our TGLF data and active learning approach could provide warm starts for surrogating higher-fidelity models, such as full gyrokinetic simulations, where data generation is inherently expensive.

Appendix B
Hyperparameter Search Results

We conducted extensive hyperparameter searches for TGLF-SINN using the filtered Major + Minor perturbation dataset. We noted diminishing marginal benefits when the latent dimension exceeded 256 and the number of ResNet blocks exceeded 4 (Figures
13
and
15
). We also observed that the minimal loss was achieved with 4 hidden layers in the MLP (Figure
14
). Collectively, these results guided our choice of hyperparameters in the implementation, balancing accuracy and computational cost. We choose 4 total ResNet blocks, 4 hidden layers per ResNet block, and 256 neurons per hidden layer. These hyperparameters remain consistent for all L-models, including
TGLF-NN
, as to eliminate the variable of parameter count.
