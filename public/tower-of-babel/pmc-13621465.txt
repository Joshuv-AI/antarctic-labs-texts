Effects of different levels of hemp seed meal supplementation in lamb diets on fattening performance and metabolic responses
Authors: Başak Özten, Hacer Tüfekci, Hüseyin Çayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemp seed meal is being researched as an alternative feed material for ruminants. It is known that sheep perform well on a wide variety of alternative feeds due to their broad feeding preferences and ability to utilize feed sources that other animals cannot. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of adding hemp seed meal at different levels (5%, 10%, 15%) to lamb rations on fattening performance and certain blood characteristics. A total of 40 Akkaraman lambs, homogeneous in age and weight, were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10). The lambs, housed in individual pens, were fattened for 75 days after a two-week adaptation period. The lambs were fattened with four different rations: control ration, and rations supplemented with 5%, 10%, and 15% hemp seed meal (HSM).ResultsIn our study, there was no difference in the live weights of lambs fed the control or HSM-supplemented diets at different periods of the feeding period, or in their final live weights at the end of the feeding period, indicating that the overall performance of the animals was similar. Furthermore, there was no significant difference in feed conversion ratios between the groups. The biochemical characteristics in the blood serum of the lambs in this study indicate that HSM was well tolerated when supplemented to the ration and no side effects were seen during the fattening period.ConclusionsThe results suggested that HSM can be added to lamb diets without causing adverse effects on lamb fattening performance or health.

Background

Industrial hemp (Cannabis sativa L.) is considered one of the most versatile plants, with more than 25,000 different uses [1]. The industrial hemp sector has been growing rapidly worldwide, and its by-products have attracted considerable attention in recent years due to their potential as feed ingredients for farm animals and their nutritional value [2–5].

Sustainable farming practices have recently attracted significant interest from farmers around the world [6]. Rising global food consumption driven by population growth and the increasing demand for animal protein require the development of sustainable livestock production systems [7]. Within this context, hemp has emerged as a promising sustainable resource with considerable potential [8]. Dehulled or whole hemp seeds, hemp stalks and leaves, hemp oil, and hemp meal have considerable potential for use as alternative raw materials in animal feed. Current data obtained to date show that most hemp by-products are safe for use as feed ingredients for ruminants [9, 10].

Hemp seeds contain roughly 25–35% oil, 20–30% protein, with the balance made up of carbs and fiber [11]. Hemp seeds are ground to produce flour or oil for food applications. This process is carried out by pressing or solvent extraction [12]. Hemp seed meal (HSM) is a protein-rich residue obtained from the oil extraction process of hemp seeds, and studies have reported that the oil content varies depending on the meal production method [13–16]. Hemp seed meal is a by-product remaining after oil extraction from hemp seeds and can be used as an alternative feed ingredient due to its high protein content and nutritional value.

Hemp seed meal has a rich nutritional profile due to its high protein content and, owing to its favorable fatty acid composition, can partially replace common protein sources including soybean meal, canola meal, and alfalfa in ruminant animal rations [10]. Therefore, it is considered a potential alternative feed ingredient for livestock [17]. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of increasing levels of hemp seed meal inclusion in lamb rations on fattening performance and certain blood characteristics. It was hypothesized that hemp seed meal could be included in lamb rations as an alternative feed ingredient without adversely affecting fattening performance or blood characteristics.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

This study was performed with ethical approval from the Yozgat Bozok University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (No: 2025/4).

Animals, experimental design and feeding

In this study, 40 weaned Akkaraman single male lambs (aged 3.5-4 months, with a live weight of 27.63 ± 0.62 kg) purchased from a sheep farm were used. The study was conducted in the research facility (39°11′47.0″N, 35°22′32.1″E; elevation: 1050 m) between June and August 2025. Initially, all animals were vaccinated and assessed for internal and external parasites and were included in the experiment after a two-week adaptation period. The animals were weighed over three consecutive days, and the mean was calculated as the initial body weight. The lambs were randomly assigned to one of four treatment diets (n = 10 for each treatment) and housed in individual pens (1.5 m × 1.5 m). The experiment lasted 75 days in a semi-open shelter system. Live weight measurements were taken every 15 days throughout the feeding period. Feeders and waterers were removed in the evening, and the lambs were weighed the following morning before feeding (between 6:00 and 7:00 a.m.).

Four treatments were evaluated: a control diet (no inclusion of hemp seed meal) and diets containing 5%, 10%, and 15% HSM on a dry matter basis. The dry matter (DM) requirements of all animals were determined according to NRC [18], and they were fed a concentrate-to-roughage ratio of 80/20. The rations given in Table 1 were used in the feeding of the animals. The HSM to be added to the rations was obtained from a commercial company, and alfalfa was used as roughage. Daily weighing records of the feed given and leftover feed were taken every morning for all lambs to determine individual feed consumption and feed utilisation values. Clean drinking water was provided ad libitum to the lambs throughout the experiments. For the feeds used in the animals’ diet, analyses for dry matter (DM, ID 934.01), crude protein (CP, ID 984.13), crude fat (CF, ID 920.29), and ash (ID 942.05) were performed according to AOAC [19]. Crude cellulose, ADF, and NDF analyses were performed on the Ankom Fiber Analyzer 2000 device as reported by Van Soest et al. [20]. Body condition score and body measurements

In the study, the body condition score was assessed by three experienced individuals simultaneously and independently. The scoring method defined by Russel et al. [21] was used to determine the body condition score. The body condition scores and body measurements of the lambs were taken at the beginning, middle (37th day), and end (75th day) of the feeding period. Withers height, rump height, body length, chest width between the shoulder blades, and chest depth were determined with a measuring rod, and chest circumference, hindquarter circumference, and foreleg circumference were determined with a measuring tape. All measurements were taken by the same person.

Blood samples collection and analysis

Blood samples were collected twice from the animals at the beginning and end of the experiment. Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein into 10-mL anticoagulant-free biochemistry tubes before morning feeding [22] and transported to the laboratory in accordance with cold chain procedures. The samples were centrifuged (1500× g for 15 min) to obtain serum. The extracted serum was transferred to 2-mL tubes and stored at -20 °C. For biochemical analysis, a Tokyo Boeki Medical System Prestige 24i (Tokyo, Japan) autoanalyser was used, employing spectrophotometric methods with commercial kits (Biosystem). Biochemical analyses included: blood characteristics related to metabolism: glucose (code 12503), urea (code 21516), total protein (code 21500), cholesterol (code 21505), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA, code 21840), β-hydroxybutyrate (BHBA, code 12525); minerals: calcium (code 21570), magnesium (code 21797), zinc (code 12526); liver function related blood characteristics: albumin (code 21547), bilirubin (code 21510), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, code 21531), γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT, code 21520), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, code 21592).

Statistical analysis

To examine the data availability for analysis of variance, all data fitted to the Normal distribution (p > 0.05) according to Shapiro-Wilk test and variances were found equal (p > 0.05) according to Levene test. The covariance analysis was performed for all data, using the beginning values as the covariate. If the covariate was found insignificant, the one-way ANOVA was used; else, estimated marginal means and their standard error values were used, and the covariance correction values were given. Duncan’s multiple comparison test and Benferroni’s multiple comparison test were used for one-way ANOVA and covariance analysis, respectively. The orthogonal polynomials (linear, quadratic, and cubic) were applied, and the results showed that there are no quadratic or cubic effects that are statistically significant. For some measurements, only the linear effect was found statistically significant. For the blood characteristics, a paired-samples t-test was used to compare the significance between FB and FE. SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for the analysis.

Results

No statistically significant difference was found in average live weights at different stages of growth between the control and lambs fed HSM (P > 0.05). Therefore, the overall performance of the animals receiving control and HSM supplements was not different (Table 2). There was no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05) in total live weight gain at the end of the trial or during the other phases of the feeding period (Table 3). Daily live weight gains across different periods of fattening also showed a parallel change to that in total live weight gains.

No significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of blood biochemical characteristics, except for the bilirubin value. Regarding the blood characteristics examined at the beginning and end of the feeding period, differences were found to be significant in glucose, total protein, NEFA, AST, ALP, GGT, and Zn values in all groups, in BHBA values in the 10% HSM group, and in Mg and cholesterol values in all groups supplemented with HSM. Discussion

When oil is extracted from hemp seeds, a by-product is produced: HSM, which is considered nutritionally equivalent to other protein sources. It also contains a protein of high biological value, making it a valuable feed source for livestock [23, 24]. Mohamed et al. [25] noted in their study that numerous studies have evaluated by-products such as HSM as a good source of protein and energy for farm animals. Kasula et al. [26] reported in their study that HSM can be safely used as an animal feed component.

The literature reports variable dry matter contents of HSM ranging from 89.6% to 94.4%; variable crude protein ratios ranging from 21.5% to 55.12%; variable crude fat ratios ranging from 8.6% to 11.8%; and variable ash ratios ranging from 5.8% to 11.9% [15, 27–30]. Furthermore, similar to our findings, the literature documents crude cellulose content of 30%, 33.6% [28, 31], as well as a metabolic energy of 2269.67 (kcal/kg) [29]. The nutritional composition of the HSM used in this study varied when compared to values reported in other studies. The differences observed among studies may probably arise from variations in plant cultivation conditions or the selected plant species. Moreover, by-products tend to exhibit a significant variability due to different processing methods.

In this study, there was no variation in the live weights of lambs fed with either control diet or the ration enhanced with HSM at different periods and at the end of the feeding period, indicating that the overall performance of the animals was comparable. Furthermore, there was no difference in feed conversion ratios among the groups. Moreover, no statistically significant difference was found between the control and treatment groups in body measurements during the fattening period. These findings are consistent with earlier studies reporting that HSM has no adverse effects on growth performance, feed intake, or feed conversion ratio in lambs [32–34]. Although Antunović et al. [32] reported a higher average daily gain in the HSM group, they found no significant differences among the groups in overall performance, feed intake, or feed conversion ratio. Similarly, Mustafa et al. [33] and Karlsson and Martinson [34] also reported that the inclusion of HSM did not negatively impact lamb performance or feed intake.

Our findings regarding feed intake are consistent with those of Abrahamsen et al. [35], who reported that including 10%, 20%, and 30% HSM at different ratios in goat rations did not significantly affect dry matter intake throughout the feeding period. In the same study, while a linear decrease was observed in most animal performance characteristics, the performance of animals in the 30% group was still considered relatively good. It was reported that as the HSM supplementation level increased, there was a linear decrease in live weight gain, feed efficiency, and rumen fermentation. Additionally, it was emphasised that further research is needed to determine the most effective inclusion rate in the ration. Semwogerere et al. [36] compared the nutrient intake and digestibility, rumen fermentation, nitrogen balance, and microbial protein synthesis of goats fed increasing dietary HSM levels instead of SBM (soybean meal) in their study. It was concluded that HSM could replace up to 100 g/kg DM of SBM in goat finishing diets without compromising feed digestibility and utilisation. They also recommended partially or completely replacing soybean meal with HSM in goat finishing diets.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies evaluating different hemp products in ruminants. Parker et al. [22] reported that spent hemp biomass could be included in lamb rations without causing major adverse effects on lamb performance or health. Similarly, Ran et al. [37] reported that replacing alfalfa hay with hemp feed (5%, 10%, and 20%) did not affect live weight gain. Wagner et al. [4] reported reduced feed intake when maize silage was partially replaced with industrial hemp silage, whereas Krebs et al. [38] found no effect of hemp straw on dry matter intake in sheep. The variations between studies can be explained by differences in the composition and form of the hemp used. Similar studies in ruminant animals have also reported that supplementing the ration with HSM has no statistically significant effects on animal performance, including growth performance (average daily gain and body weight) and feed intake, and that animals’ performance is not affected by HSM [27, 39, 40].

No statistically significant differences were observed among the groups in body measurements throughout the fattening period. These results indicate that hemp seed meal supplementation in the ration did not affect body measurements in lambs. These findings are consistent with those reported by Antunović et al. [32], who also found no significant differences in body measurements of lambs fed rations containing hemp seed meal.

Blood characteristic profiles reflect the general health of animals. Blood characteristics other than bilirubin were not affected by the level of HSM inclusion in the diet. Bilirubin levels increased in the HSM-supplemented groups. However, the results obtained were within normal levels for sheep. The results of our study were similar to the findings reported by Parker et al. [22], who reported that sheep fed spend hemp biomass (SHB) had increased bilirubin levels, and the results of the study were within normal levels for sheep [41]. These findings indicate that the increase in bilirubin associated with HSM supplementation remained within the physiological range and did not adversely affect liver function. Albumin is one of the main protein components in the blood and constitutes 50–60% of total serum protein. Albumin synthesis occurs in the liver, and it is therefore considered a marker of the liver’s synthetic function [42]. It is also used to maintain homeostasis in the body. Values outside the normal range indicate a deficiency in protein provided by the diet [43]. As albumin is a negative inflammatory marker, albumin levels may be affected by other causes such as systemic inflammation, protein malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome, fluid overload, or protein-losing enteropathy. Albumin has many functions, including maintaining serum osmotic pressure and transporting endogenous (i.e., bilirubin) and exogenous (i.e., drugs) substances in the blood [44]. Although reported normal albumin levels in sheep are quite variable [45], the values obtained in this study were within the normal levels reported in the literature. The absence of significant differences in albumin concentrations among the groups indicates that HSM supplementation did not adversely affect protein metabolism. At the end of the feeding period, significant differences were observed in magnesium and cholesterol values in all groups supplemented with HSM. This difference in values may be attributed to the mineral and fat content of the HSM added to the ration in the present study. Sandabe and Chaudhry [46] reported in their study that the feeding period had an effect on cholesterol levels. These findings indicate that HSM supplementation at the inclusion levels used did not adversely affect the metabolic status of the lambs. The results of the biochemical characteristics obtained in the blood serum are similar to those reported in the literature [22, 32, 47]. Blood NEFA, BHBA, and glucose values are the most valuable characteristics providing information about the energy metabolism status of animals [48]. Researchers state that for a severe negative energy balance to be considered, the blood NEFA level should be above 0.8 mmol/l [49], BHBA levels above 1 mmol/l [50], and glucose levels below 40 mg/dl [51]. In the present study, none of these three characteristics reached the mentioned levels at any time point in either the control group or the HSM supplemented groups. These results show that HSM supplementation at the inclusion levels used did not adversely affect the energy metabolism of the lambs.

Biochemical and enzymological profiles provide reliable information about the health status of animals. The primary purpose of liver tests in sheep is to clarify liver functions that affect the general health of the organism. Serum levels of these enzymes are commonly used to routinely assess liver function [52]. AST activity is found in similar amounts in the liver, skeletal muscle, and heart muscle. When considering AST, caution should be exercised when evaluating abnormal levels, as it is present in other tissues [42, 53]. ALP is an enzyme found in abundance in the liver, kidneys, and bones [54]. ALP is higher in adults due to increased osteoblastic activity associated with bone growth [55]. GGT is an enzyme found in many organs throughout the body, including the pancreas, seminal vesicles, kidneys, bile ducts, and liver. GGT plays a role in glutathione metabolism and production in many tissues and is higher in adults [55, 56]. Aksoy et al. [57] conducted a study on Akkaraman sheep, comparing the activities of certain liver enzymes (ALT, AST, GGT, alkaline phosphatase, and creatine kinase) while accounting for age and gender differences. The study concluded that differences in enzyme activity may vary across animals, species, breeds, age, sex, and environment. In their study on female sheep, Yokus et al. [58] reported that differences in liver enzyme activities (ALT, AST, GGT, alkaline phosphatase, and creatine kinase) were associated with developing muscle activity and metabolic activity. The changes in liver enzymes examined in the study did not show any difference between the control group and the group supplemented with HSM in the ration. The results of the study indicate that feeding with HSM does not adversely affect liver health.

Conclusion

Due to its high protein content, hemp seed meal is a potential alternative feed material for livestock. In this study, the biochemical characteristics in the blood serum of lambs indicate that the addition of HSM to the ration was well tolerated and that there were no side effects during the feeding period. The results suggested that hemp seed meal can be added to lamb diets without adverse effects on lamb performance or health. The inclusion of hemp seed meal in lamb diets up to 15% does not affect growth performance or metabolic responses in fattening lamb. Based on these findings, further long-term feeding trials in different animal species would help evaluate the potential of hemp seed meal as a sustainable feed resource.
