Women’s empowerment and health facility utilization during childbirth in Cameroon
Authors: Armand Totouom, Gadom Djal-Gadom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low levels of women’s empowerment probably constitute a key obstacle to healthcare utilization in Africa, given the importance of gender inequalities in this region. However, evidence on the relationship between women’s empowerment and facility delivery in Cameroon is scarce. This paper examines the link between women’s empowerment and health facility childbirth in Cameroon. For empirical analysis, we used a sample of nationally representative data from the fifth Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey. The study demonstrates that women’s intra-household decision-making power, high women’s economic status and human capital, and opposing attitudes toward wife beating are associated with a higher probability of using health facilities during childbirth. This result remained when we accounted for the endogeneity of empowerment measures in our analysis. Relevant policy implications are discussed in the paper.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04365-7.

Introduction

The vast majority of maternal deaths are preventable, as the clinical knowledge and technology required to prevent them have existed for a long time [1]. However, statistics indicate that maternal mortality rates remain significant in the world. In 2020, an estimated 287 000 women globally died from a maternal cause, equivalent to almost 800 maternal deaths every day, and approximately one every two minutes. The global maternal mortality ratio in 2020 was estimated at 223 maternal deaths per 100 000 live births. Moreover, the maternal mortality rate in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is among the highest worldwide. SSA is the only region with a very high maternal mortality rate, estimated at 545 maternal deaths per 100 000 live births in 2020. This is 136 times higher than the maternal mortality rate in Australia and New Zealand, where the maternal mortality rate is lowest. SSA alone accounted for approximately 70% of global maternal deaths in 2020, followed by Central and Southern Asia which accounted for almost 17% [1].

In addition to the high maternal mortality rate, another significant challenge for promoting maternal health in low- and middle-income countries is increasing the provision and utilization of essential maternal healthcare services [2]. An overwhelming majority of women in developing countries lack access to maternal health services and about half of them choose to give birth outside health institutional settings in unsafe and unhygienic settings [3]. Health facility delivery provides skilled health attendants to better manage the outcome of pregnancy and child birth and has a positive contribution to reducing maternal and new-born mortality and morbidity [4]. Therefore, a key strategy to ensure skilled care during childbirth is that all births take place in health facilities in which obstetric complications can be treated when they arise [5].

There is a growing body of literature that seeks to understand factors that drive health facility utilization during childbirth. Understanding the determinants of health facility delivery is important to guide interventions and policies aimed at reducing maternal mortality [4]. In a literature review on the determinants of delivery service use, numerous barriers to healthcare access for women are highlighted: geographic (distance, lack of transportation), economic (poor capacity to pay for health services), and social (gender inequality, limited decision-making power) [6]. Regarding social barriers, women’s empowerment has long been hypothesized to drive maternal and child health outcomes in the developing world [7]. Low levels of women’s empowerment probably constitute a key obstacle to healthcare utilization in Africa, given the importance of gender inequalities in this region. The Human Development Report’s Gender Inequality Index computed by the United Nations Development Programme shows that Africa is the region of the world where gender inequality remains the highest [8]. The marginalization of women takes various forms and starts at home with the marginal role they play in household decision-making and their exposure to domestic violence.

Although women’s empowerment is recognized as a potential enabling factor in the utilization of health facilities during childbirth, the relationship between women’s empowerment and utilization of health facilities is poorly studied, especially in countries with high maternal mortality [9]. Evidence on the association between women’s empowerment and facility delivery in Cameroon is scarce. To our knowledge, only one study has been conducted in that context [10]. Besides, there is no consensus in the literature as there are some studies concluding either a negative effect [11] or an insignificant effect [4, 9] of women’s empowerment on health facility delivery. All in all, there is certainly a scope for more research on the effect of women’s empowerment on health facility utilization during childbirth. We fill an important gap in the literature by providing one of the first studies that examines the link between women’s empowerment and health facility childbirths in Cameroon.

Three reasons motivate our focus on Cameroon. First, although the country has achieved over the past decades a significant reduction in the maternal mortality ratio, it currently registers 438 deaths per 100 000 live births [1]. The Sustainable Development Goals aims to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio to less than 70 maternal deaths per 100 000 live births by 2030. Second, statistics show that only 67% of births take place in a health facility, meaning that 33% of births take place at home [12]. Among live births, 69% are attended by a trained health provider, 10% are assisted by a traditional birth attendant and 3% of births do not benefit from any assistance [12]. Third, the level of women’s empowerment remains low. Only 54% of women aged 15–49 participate in decision-making about their health care, 57% of women participate in decision-making concerning the major purchases for the household and 63% of women participate in decision-making on visits to their family or relatives [12]. Women seem to partially accept their marginalization. Indeed, regarding women’s perception of wife beating for example, statistics show that 22% of women think it is justified for a man to beat his wife when she neglects her children, 15% when she argues with the husband or goes out without telling him, 10% when she refuses to have sex with him and 8% when she burns the food. Overall, 28% of women aged 15–49 think that it is justified for a man to beat his wife for at least one of the five reasons cited [12]. In terms of economic status and human capital, for example, 86% of women do not own homes and 89% do not own land. Among men in the same age group, these rates are 74% and 72% respectively [12].

Using data from the fifth Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey (CDHS-V), we examine the effect of women’s empowerment on urban household health facility utilization by running standard probit and Instrumental Variable (IV) probit models. Our findings show that compared to other women, those with high decision-making power, high economic and human capital status and opposing attitudes toward wife beating, are more likely to give birth in a health facility. The marginal effects are 0.036, 0.022 and 0.010 respectively. These marginal effects grew to 0.203, 307 and 0.278 when we accounted for the problem of endogeneity arising from the analysis.

The remainder of the study is structured as follows. Section 2 provides a review of the literature. Section 3 describes data and variables. The econometric strategy is presented in Section 4. Section 5 presents the results which are discussed in Section 6. Section 7 concludes.

Literature review

The interest in the relationship between women’s empowerment and health facility utilization is not new in literature. Several studies show that health facility childbirth is a gendered phenomenon [13]. They provide evidence that women-headed households use health facilities for childbirth more than men-headed households. However, these studies are limited because they do not directly account for male–female power dynamics within the household. In women-headed households, women make decisions because they are on their own and this is not the same as saying that they are empowered. As gendered norms require women’s subordination to their partners in developing countries, women-headed households are households where either no adult men are present, owing to divorce, separation, migration, non-marriage, or widowhood; or where the men, although present, do not contribute to the household income, because of illness or disability, old age, alcoholism or similar incapacity. It is therefore important to distinguish for example between women’s decision-making power because they are empowered and women’s decision-making power because they are household heads.

In the economic literature, the household has historically been treated as a single unit with common preferences and pooled income and assets. The Samuelson’s consensus model was the first formal model to ignore intra-household differences in preferences, as seen in its assumption that all household members had the same utility function to maximize [14]. Later, Becker developed a household model, which grew to be a standard model of distribution within the household for economists [15]. In Becker’s model, an “altruistic” head of the household aggregates the preferences of each individual in the household to form one joint utility function, and sets out to maximize that [15].

Alternative models have been further proposed in order to best fit with reality. In contrast to the “unitary” version of the household, emerged a literature on household bargaining pioneered [16, 17] and the collective model [18, 19], which both consider the individual preferences inside households, and intra-household distribution of resources. Therefore, these alternative models reject the unitary model, which assumes that a household’s preferences are identical, leading to the conclusion that some sort of bargaining process exists within the household. Based on such a conclusion, several authors established the relationship between women’s empowerment and the utilization of several maternal health care services including skilled birth attendance [2, 20], antenatal and postnatal care [21, 22] and delivery in a health facility [4, 9–11, 23–26].

Regarding delivery in a health facility, there is some evidence that women’s empowerment has a negative effect [11] or an insignificant effect [4, 9] on women’s utilization of the health facility for delivery. However, several other studies conclude to a strong positive effect [10, 23–27]. For example, it has been found in Ghana that women with greater decision-making power and who perceive positive attitudes toward facility delivery (among women, men and mothers-in-law) are more likely to use health services during childbirth. Moreover, the interactions between autonomy and community-level perceptions of institutional delivery among men and mothers-in-law are significant, such that the effect of decision-making autonomy is more important for women who live in communities that are less supportive of institutional delivery compared to communities that are more supportive [25]. In a recent study conducted in Cameroon to examine the association between women’s empowerment and reproductive health care utilization, the results showed that women’s decision-making power is strongly associated with antenatal visits during pregnancy, first prenatal visit during the first 3 months of pregnancy and delivery in a hospital, although the associations change when controlling for mother and household characteristics [10].

Pregnant women are those directly subject to obstetric complications. Four obstetric complications account for most maternal deaths: postpartum hemorrhage, sepsis, unsafe induced abortion, and obstructed labor. As most obstetric complications occur around the time of delivery and cannot be predicted [6], the probability of having a safe childbirth is that birth occurs in a health facility. It seems therefore logical that women should better appreciate the full benefits of using a health facility and should have greater preference for delivery in a health facility. From this perspective, empowered women can easily direct household income towards greater consumption of reproductive health care [10]. Our study is in line with the previous ones linking women’s empowerment to health facility delivery. Our hypothesis is that women’s empowerment increases health facility utilization during childbirth.

Data and measurement of variables

Data

To analyse the relationship between women’s empowerment and health facility utilization, we use data from the fifth Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey carried out from June 2018 to January 2019 by the National Institute of Statistics of Cameroon. Data from the Individual Women’s Survey used involved 13,527 women (7,343 in urban areas and 6,184 in rural areas) aged 15–49 and 1,150 women (496 in urban areas and 654 in rural areas) aged 50–64.

The CDHS-V was designed to provide data for monitoring the population and health situation in Cameroon. The objective of the survey was to provide reliable estimates of fertility levels, marriage, sexual activity, fertility preferences, family planning methods, breastfeeding practices, nutrition, childhood and maternal mortality, maternal and child health, domestic violence, malaria, and HIV/AIDS and other sexually transmitted infections that can be used by program managers and policymakers to evaluate and improve existing programs. The CDHS-V therefore provides information on a number of variables useful for our study.

The sample used for our analysis consists of women living together with their partner as a couple (married or cohabiting). We believe that restricting the study to women living together with their partner as a couple does not create a crucial selection problem that would bias our analysis. To verify this issue, we conducted difference tests between the average characteristics of women living together with their partner as a couple and those of the other women. The results globally suggest that the two samples are on average similar in terms of these characteristics and the possibility of selection bias does not arise acutely1.

Measuring health facility utilization

The dependent variable used in the study captured the use of a health facility during delivery. It is a binary variable equal to 1 for women whose most recent birth took place in a health facility and 0 when it took place outside a health facility. Birth outside a health facility refers to home delivery. Health facilities include, among others, public hospitals/polyclinics, private hospitals/clinics/maternity homes and health centers. Our measure is widely used in previous studies [4, 9, 27].

From 1991 to 2018, the percentage of births that took place in a health facility varied irregularly in Cameroon. Statistics show that after having fallen slightly between 1991 and 2004 (from 62% to 59%), this percentage increased slightly, from 61% in 2011 to 67% in 2018. Conversely, over the same period, the percentage of births that took place at home decreased. After an increase of 3% between 1991 and 2004, it decreased from 40% in 2004 to 37% in 2011 and to 33% in 2018. Births in urban areas take place more frequently in health facilities than those in rural areas (88% versus 50%) [12].

Measuring women’s empowerment

Women’s empowerment is a complex and multi-dimensional concept. Following previous studies [9, 22, 26], we used three different composite indexes to capture women’s empowerment: women’s decision-making autonomy at home, women’s economic status and human capital, and attitude toward wife beating. Each of the composite indexes is computed, based on the Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA). The composite indexes of women’s empowerment are the first principal components of the vector of the variables used to build them. For women’s decision-making autonomy at home, women’s economic status and human capital, and attitude toward wife beating, the first principal components represent 99%, 73% and 99% of the total variance in the original data, respectively. The construction of the composite indexes has therefore reduced the dimension of women’s empowerment while preserving the essential information contained in the data.

Regarding women’s intra-household decision-making power (Women’s power), it constitutes the most common indicator used in the literature in measuring the association between women’s empowerment and maternal/child health outcomes and it originates from the DHS women’s status module [7]. The CDHS-V provides information on women’s participation in various decisions regarding what to do with the money husband earns, large household purchases, visits to family or relatives and woman say on decisions regarding her health care. Each of these four decisions is represented by a binary variable equal to 1 for women that have the final say (alone or jointly with their spouse/partner) and 0 when it is the spouse/partner alone or someone else that has the final say. The four dummies are used to construct the composite index of women’s intra-household decision-making power.

Concerning women’s economic status and human capital (Women’s economic_human capital), four binary variables are used to construct this composite index: Women’s education and three other variables on whether women are working, whether they own a piece of land (alone or jointly with someone else), and whether they own a house (alone or jointly with someone else). Women’s education takes the value of 0 for women having no-education level, and 1 for women who have at least gone through primary education.

Regarding attitudes toward wife beating (Women’s attitude), women were asked whether they agree that a husband is justified in beating his wife for one of the following reasons: she goes out without telling him, she neglects the children, she argues with him, she refuses to have sexual intercourse with him, and she does not cook food properly. For each scenario, we assigned a value of 1 to women who disagreed with the scenario and classified them as having an attitude opposed to domestic violence, meaning they have high self-esteem with respect to domestic violence. Otherwise, they are considered to have a favorable attitude towards domestic violence against women and are assigned a value of 0. The four variables defined for each scenario are used to construct the composite index of attitude toward wife beating.

Accounting for control variables

Based on the extensive literature on the drivers of health facility utilization [4, 9–11, 23–27] and the availability of data, we consider the following control variables in our study:

Access to health facilities

This variable measures women’s perception of the distance to health facilities. During the CDHS-V, women were asked whether distance to the health facility constitutes a big problem when they are ill and want medical advice or treatment. Our measure of women’s access to health facilities takes the value of 1 for those having a big problem with distance to a health facility and 0 for those not having a big problem with that distance.

Partner’s education

This variable captures the education level of women’s spouses/partners. It is also a binary variable, taking the value of 0 for spouses/partners having no education level, and 1 for spouses/partners who have at least gone through primary education.

Information exposure

We also consider a variable capturing exposure to information. Considering that individuals’ perceptions (which are a hybrid of their beliefs and attitudes) predict their behaviours, numerous theoretical models from social psychology such as the Health Belief Model [28] and the theory of planned behaviour [29, 30] allow postulating that education and information of individuals on their vulnerability to the risks associated with home birth and the strategies that can be used to reduce this vulnerability would influence their beliefs and attitudes and lead them to adopt childbirth in a health facility.

During the CDHS-V, women were asked questions about whether they used to read newspapers/magazines (less than once a week or at least once a week), listen to the radio (less than once a week or at least once a week), watch television (less than once a week or at least once a week) and whether they have used internet over the past month (less than once a week, at least once a week or almost every day). Correlation analysis

We conducted a correlation analysis between the variables used in the study. The correlation matrix presented in Appendix 1 shows that the correlation coefficients between the control variables are lower than 0.7, generally used as an empirical threshold for high correlations. Therefore, these variables are not highly correlated, which rules out the problem of multicollinearity. We also observed that Women’s power, Women’s economic_human capital and Women’s attitude are significantly correlated with the dependent variable. Based on this first evidence, we investigate in the next sections, the relationship between women’s empowerment and the utilization of health facilities, using econometric tools.

The results

Baseline results

Maximum likelihood estimates of the probit model are reported in Appendix 2. In columns 1–3, only women’s empowerment is used to explain health facility utilization. Control variables are included in the specifications reported in columns 4–6. The results show that the coefficients of Women’s power, Women’s economic_human capital and Women’s attitude are all positive and statistically significant. This suggests that women’s empowerment is associated with a higher probability of health facility utilization.

Since the variance of the model error is normalized to one, the numerical values ​​of the estimated parameters are not inherently useful, as they correspond to the parameters of the latent variable equation (Eq. 1) only up to a multiplicative constant. The marginal effects associated with the results reported in Appendix 2 are therefore calculated and displayed in Table 2. The marginal effects from the full model reported in columns 4–6 suggest that a one-unit increase in the composite indexes of women’s intra-household decision-making power, women’s economic status and human capital, and opposing attitude toward wife beating lead to an increase by 0.036, 0.022 and 0.010 points in the probability of using health facilities during childbirth, respectively.

Regarding the other covariates, the results show that the probability of giving birth in a health facility decreases when distance to the health facility constitutes a big problem. Prominent barriers to utilization of facility-based delivery highlighted in the literature include structural barriers associated with delivery-related costs and distance to health facilities. Similar findings suggest that women having no problem with distance to a health facility had higher odds of utilizing health facilities during childbirth as compared to those having a challenge with distance to access healthcare were found [31]. Table 3 also shows that the education of husband/partner is statistically significant, implying that it has a strong positive effect on the probability of giving birth in a health facility.

Regarding wealth quartiles, their coefficients are all positive and statistically significant. Overall, the wealthier a household, the more likely the women living there are to adopt health facility delivery. It is worth noting that the more we move from one wealth quartile to another higher one, the greater the marginal effect. We also controlled for the place of residence in our analysis. Our results suggest that urban residency is a significant factor positively associated with the probability of health facility utilization. In addition, age is also shown to increase the outcome variable. Its marginal effect on the probability of giving birth in a health facility is 0.001.

Finally, the results presented in Table 3 also highlight the positive effect of exposure to information on the probability of health facility utilization. It is well known since seminal works on information [32, 33] that economic agents do not always have all the necessary information when making their choices, which leads to non-optimal decisions and equilibria. Information provision can help to solve the market failure that leads many women in developing countries to give birth in a residence rather than in a health facility.

Robustness checks

In Table 3, we present robustness check results. As indicated earlier, we rely on the IV probit model to account for the potential endogeneity of women’s empowerment in the analysis. We use the age difference between women and their spouses/partners as an instrument for women’s empowerment. The positive association between spousal age difference and household male dominance is demonstrated in the literature [34, 35] and it indicates that a large spousal age difference limits women’s empowerment. The idea is that spousal age difference is usually accompanied by differences in life experience, education, finances etc. leading to unequal and detrimental relationships for women [35]. Resource theory suggests that power balance is in favour of the spouse who contributes the most resources to the marriage. In this perspective, it is argued that age is significantly related to one’s socio-economic and occupational experiences, leading to an increase in material resources [36]. Therefore, husbands who are significantly older than their wives are expected to bring more resources and authority into their marriages than spouses of the same age.

Age at marriage and educational differences between spouses are also assumed to determine women’s empowerment [37]. We also consider these two variables as instruments for women’s empowerment. Overall, our three instruments, namely age at first cohabitation, as well as differences in age and education level between spouses, have proved to be powerful determinants of women’s empowerment in the literature [38–40]. The results of the Wald test of the exogeneity of women’s empowerment measures allow us to reject the null hypothesis of their exogeneity. The use of the IV probit is therefore justified. If the women’s empowerment measures were not endogenous, the standard probit regression would be preferable.

The results displayed in Table 3 confirm the positive and statistically significant association between women’s empowerment and health facility utilization during childbirth in Cameroon. The marginal effects from robustness check results (columns 3, 6 and 9) are relatively higher in magnitude compared to those from baseline estimates. This suggests that endogeneity generated a downward bias in our baseline estimates.

Discussion and policy implications

This study deepens our understanding of the association between women’s empowerment health and facility utilization during childbirth in Cameroon. It demonstrates that women’s empowerment is a key determinant of women’s choice to give birth in a health facility. For example, women with decision-making power within household can easily influence the direction of household income expenditure with a consequent greater consumption of reproductive health care [10]. Our result is broadly consistent with several recent studies suggesting a positive association between women’s empowerment and utilization of health care for delivery [10, 23–27]. However, our analysis is a rare one linking women’s empowerment and facility delivery in Cameroon. Although there is a similar study conducted in Cameroon [10], this study, like most other existing ones, suffers from a number of limitations that we addressed in our study. Hence, our study contributes to literature in several ways. First, we used instruments for women’s empowerment to account for its potential endogeneity in the model explaining health facility utilization. This endogeneity issue has not received attention in past studies, although it can lead to biased estimates if it is not well addressed. Second, there is a paucity of studies examining the effect of women’s empowerment on the utilization of several maternal health care services in Cameroon. This is, to our knowledge, one of the first papers to investigate the relationship between the two variables in that context. Third, we applied the multiple correspondence analysis to construct composite indexes for women’s empowerment. These measures allow us to capture all the complex and multidimensional aspects of the concept.

A better understanding of the link between women’s empowerment and the utilization of health facilities provides important insights for policy-makers as to what sorts of interventions might be effective in reducing maternal mortality. Our findings speak to the importance of considering women’s intra-household decision-making power, women’s economic status and human capital, and attitude towards wife beating when trying to understand health facility utilization during childbirth. These results show that special attention should be paid to the empowerment of women, which has not often been the case in policies aimed at improving health facility childbirths.

To strengthen women’s empowerment, our results indicate that preventing child marriage and reducing age and education gaps between spouses are essential levers. They show that, regardless of the specification considered, differences in age and education level between spouses have negative coefficients, while age at first cohabitation has a positive coefficient. From this perspective, the implementation of policies enhancing the prevention of child, early and forced marriage has the potential to increase women’s empowerment, which in turn will increase health facility childbirths. In Cameroon, although the legal minimum age of marriage is 18 for boys and 15 for girls, results from the CDHS-V show that among women aged 25–49, 13% had already entered into their first union before the age of 15, 36% before the age of 18. By comparison, among men aged 30–49, these percentages are respectively less than 1% and 5%. The median age at first union for women aged 25–49 is estimated at 20 years. Among men aged 30–64, it is estimated at 27.5 years [12].

Besides, as suggested in the literature, structural gender inequalities and gender norms rooted in patriarchy should be addressed alongside efforts to economically empower women [41]. People’s sensitization campaigns and education through appropriate school curricula and mass media programmes can be used to achieve such objectives. Various incentives (such as scholarships) should also be implemented to stimulate girls’ education. Finally, empowered women should effectively use their autonomy in favour of health facility childbirth. From this perspective, it is necessary to intensify awareness campaigns for women regarding the benefits of giving birth in a health facility.

However, this study is not without limitations. Our analysis uses cross-sectional data which does not allow us to explore the trend in women’s empowerment and health facility utilization. More studies in this field should aim to collect and analyse longitudinal datasets to get at the heart of the empowerment process and its linkages with maternal and child health outcomes [7]. Moreover, the fifth Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey used in the study does not provide information on a number of factors that may affect the utilization of maternal health facilities, such as the availability of health facilities and the quality of maternal health care services. In addition, information on health data delivery collected during the CDHS-V was retrospectively collected, potentially resulting in recall bias. Future studies using more precise and complete data to address these limitations would be welcome. Finally, the use of quantitative data does not allow us to understand “why” and “how” women’s empowerment influences childbirth in healthcare facilities. Therefore, a more qualitative analysis, like specific case studies that help put the econometric estimates into context should complement our analysis.

Conclusion

This study aimed to analyse the effect of women’s empowerment on health facility utilization during childbirth in Cameroon. We used data from the fifth Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey to estimate the standard probit and recursive bivariate probit models specified. Three types of indicators of women’s empowerment are used in the study: decision-making power, economic status and human capital, and attitudes toward domestic violence. Standard probit estimates show that women’s empowerment is associated with a higher probability of giving birth in a health facility. To test this result, we used an instrumental variable approach in the analysis. The robustness check confirmed the existence of a strong relationship between women’s empowerment and health facility utilization. Therefore, women’s empowerment could be a key tool to promote health facility childbirths.
