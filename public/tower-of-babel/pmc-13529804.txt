GFPT1 as a cross-ancestry validated target for degenerative spinal disease: genetic association in a Chinese cohort and functional characterization in zebrafish
Authors: Bo Yang, Feng Li, Yongsen Zhang, Fan Zhang, Zhihao Wang, Yifeng Da, Xuejun Yang, Wenhua Xing, Jing Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Degenerative spinal disease (DSD), including spinal stenosis and spondylosis, lacks effective pharmacological treatment. To identify druggable targets and assess cross-ancestry applicability, we integrate multi-omics analyses using Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR), colocalization, and two-sample Mendelian randomization with European whole-blood, peripheral-blood, and CSF eQTL/pQTL datasets, followed by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) validation in a Chinese cohort. We identify 7 genes/proteins associated with spinal stenosis and 5 with spondylosis, with GFPT1, GPX1, and SERPINA1 shared by both. Two-sample MR further supports the causal associations of these targets with DSD. Phenome-wide MR prioritization selects GFPT1 and GPX1 as favorable candidates with no predicted adverse effects and potential beneficial effects on hypertension. In the Chinese cohort (67 lumbar spinal stenosis patients and 100 controls), WGS identifies 4 GFPT1 cis-eQTL loci (rs13016371, rs35392088, rs12997521, and rs13019789) associated with lumbar spinal stenosis risk; all risk alleles are linked to increased GFPT1 expression, and all 24 variant carriers show L4/L5 stenosis on imaging. Druggability analysis identifies IOX1 as the sole preclinical-stage compound targeting GFPT1, and molecular docking supports robust binding to GFPT1 (− 6.39 kcal/mol). Functional assays show that IOX1 directly inhibits GFPT1 enzymatic activity and induces fructose-6-phosphate accumulation. In zebrafish, IOX1 significantly rescues GFPT1-induced degenerative phenotypes. These findings establish GFPT1 as a cross-ancestry validated therapeutic target for DSD and nominate IOX1 as a promising disease-modifying candidate.

Introduction

Degenerative spine disease (DSD), such as spinal stenosis and spondylosis, is caused by the natural deterioration of spinal structures (including the vertebrae, intervertebral discs, and supportive tissues). Predominantly affecting middle-aged and elderly populations, DSD frequently manifests as chronic neck or back pain, restricted mobility, and significantly diminished quality of life1,2. With the global population aging, the prevalence and societal burden of DSD continue to escalate, posing tremendous challenges on healthcare system and patients alike. Currently, the treatment of DSD relies heavily on surgical intervention for symptom alleviation. However, these procedures carry risks including postoperative infections and accelerated spinal degeneration3,4.

Emerging evidence underscores genetic factors as pivotal contributors to DSD pathogenesis. Numerous genetic variants and susceptibility genes correlated with DSD have been identified, particularly those influencing biological processes critical to spinal integrity, osteoporosis susceptibility, and inflammatory responses. Notable examples include COL1A1, COL1A2, COL9A, COL9A2, COL9A3, ACAN, and CILP, which are associated with the composition of intervertebral discs in lumbar disc herniation, while VDR and ER are related to bone structure, and MMP3 and IL1 are involved in matrix degradation within intervertebral discs5–7. Despite these genetic insights, molecular therapeutic targets for DSD remain largely undefined. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the genetic factors associated with DSD can enhance our understanding of its etiology and establish foundations for preventive strategies, molecular therapies, and targeted drug development.

The identification of druggable targets represents the pivotal first step in drug development. While advances in multi-omics research have accelerated the discovery of disease-related biomarkers and therapeutic targets, cross-validation between proteomic and transcriptomic data significantly enhances the reliability of these findings. This is particularly evident in the expression of quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs), which exhibit strong correlated with gene and protein expression levels8,9. Currently, Mendelian randomization (MR) approaches leverage these multi-omics datasets predominantly from European populations, integrated with disease GWAS summary statistics, to prioritize causal genes/proteins as candidate therapeutic targets. However, this strategy demonstrates target feasibility exclusively within European ancestry groups, severely limiting cross-population generalizability. By conducting cross-ethnic validation using whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from a diverse population, the universality and reliability of these targets can be substantially enhanced. Furthermore, deep integration of WGS with clinical practice, incorporating individual electronic health records and quantitative clinical imaging data, enables not only analysis of multi-layered disease regulatory networks and assessment of patients’ genetic risks, but also significantly enhances the biological rationality of candidate targets and their potential as therapeutic targets across ethnic groups10.

In this study, we leveraged large-scale eQTL and pQTL data (sourced from whole blood, peripheral blood, and CSF) as exposures, combined with GWAS summary statistics for two DSD phenotypes (spinal stenosis and spondylosis), to perform SMR, colocalization, and phenome-wide MR analysis for identifying therapeutic targets with favorable safety profiles. Innovatively, we validated the cross-ancestry applicability of prioritized targets using WGS data from Chinese lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) patients. Drug prediction and molecular docking were subsequently employed to assess the druggability of targets and explore potential therapeutic agents for DSD. Further pharmacological validation through the zebrafish model was conducted to confirm the therapeutic potential of candidate compounds.

Results

Identification of candidate druggable genes for DSD through SMR and colocalization analysis

Details of the overall study design and data sources are presented in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1, respectively. Therefore, these 3 genes/proteins were expected to be used as candidate therapeutic targets for DSD.

Validation by two-sample MR

To further determine the causal relationship between the 3 genes/proteins and DSD, we conducted a two-sample MR analysis. Due to the fact that GFPT1 has more than one IVs, the IVW method was used for MR analysis, while GPX1 and SERPINA1 have only one IV, so the Wald ratio method was used for MR analysis. All the F-statistics of IVs used for analysis are greater than 10 (Supplementary Data 5). The results showed that GFPT1 (OR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.09–1.27 for spinal stenosis and OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.04–1.21 for spondylosis), GPX1 (OR: 1.70, 95% CI: 1.39–2.07 for spinal stenosis and OR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.26–1.79 for spondylosis) and SERPINA1 (OR: 0.44, 95% CI: 0.31–0.63 for spinal stenosis and OR: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.35–0.65 for spondylosis) were associated with the risk of spinal stenosis and spondylosis, and the direction of effects were consistent with the SMR results (Supplementary Data 6). In addition, sensitivity analysis was conducted on MR results using IVs exceeding 1; the sensitivity test between GFPT1 and spinal stenosis showed no horizontal pleiotropy (P_pleiotropy = 0.81) and heterogeneity (P_heterogeneity (MR Egger) = 0.06, P_heterogeneity (IVW) = 0.13). Between GFPT1 and spondylosis, there was no horizontal pleiotropy (P_pleiotropy = 0.70), although the MR Egger method detected heterogeneity (P_heterogeneity (MR Egger) = 0.03), the subsequent IVW method did not detect heterogeneity (P_heterogeneity (IVW) = 0.05), proving that the results of two-sample MR were robust (Supplementary Data 6). The two-sample MR analysis further confirmed the significant causal relationship between the 3 genes/proteins (GFPT1, GPX1, and SREPINA1) and DSD, indicating they could potentially become drug targets for treating DSD.

Phenome-wide MR analysis of DSD-associated candidate druggable genes

To evaluate whether targeting these 3 candidate druggable genes would lead to side effects and assess the safety of drug administration, we performed a phenome-wide MR analysis on 782 non-DSD diseases or traits in the UKB-SAIGE. The IVs of the 3 druggable genes used for analysis were consistent with those previously used for two-sample MR analysis. Through phenome-wide MR analysis and FDR correction (FDR_P < 0.05), we found that increased levels of GFPT1 and GPX1 could lead to an increased risk of hypertension (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.11 for GFPT1; OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.15–1.47 for GPX1) and essential hypertension (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.10 for GFPT1; OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.15–1.47 for GPX1), and the direction of effects was consistent with that of DSD (Fig. 4A, B, and Supplementary Data 7, 8). However, changes in SERPINA1 levels were associated with 35 diseases or traits, and 20 of them had opposite effects on DSD (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Data 9). This finding suggested that drug targets on GFPT1 and GPX1 for the treatment of DSD might have no side effects and could be beneficial for treating DSD, hypertension, and essential hypertension. Mutation frequency statistics of the cis-eQTL for GFPT1 and GPX1 in the Chinese LSS population

To investigate whether variations in GFPT1 expression influence LSS in the Chinese population, we analyzed 6613 cis-eQTLs of GFPT1 and 3489 cis-eQTLs of GPX1 sourced from the eQTLGen Consortium as a background reference (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). We extracted all mutation sites of GFPT1 and GPX1 from WGS data of 67 LSS patients and 100 healthy controls, excluding those known to be benign in the ClinVar database. This identified 179 GFPT1 SNPs in LSS patients versus 211 in healthy controls, and 1 GPX1 SNP in LSS patients versus 0 in controls (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Intersecting these loci in LSS patients, healthy controls, and the eQTLGen consortium’s background reference, we discovered that 82 SNPs of GFPT1 and 0 SNPs of GPX1 were common across all three datasets (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Analysis of these 82 GFPT1 SNPs in the 1000 Genomes East Asian population, healthy controls, and LSS patients revealed elevated mutation frequencies at 4 cis-eQTL loci in LSS patients (Supplementary Data 10). Notably, these 4 loci—rs13016371, rs35392088, rs12997521, and rs13019789—are derived from blood tissue eQTL data (eQTLGen consortium), where they consistently showed a significant positive effect, indicating that their risk alleles are associated with increased GFPT1 expression (Supplementary Data 11). In our cohort, their mutation frequencies were as follows: rs13016371 (0.358208955 (24/67)), rs35392088 (0.328358209 (22/67)), rs12997521 (0.313432836 (21/67)), and rs13019789 (0.313432836 (21/67)). These frequencies exceeded those in both the 1000 Genomes East Asian population (0% for all loci) and healthy controls (0% for all loci) (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Data 10). The Venn diagram indicated that 20 patients harbored all 4 mutations, 1 lacked rs35392088, 1 lacked rs12997521, and 2 patients carried only rs13016371 (Fig. 5B). All 24 patients with ≥1 of these mutations exhibited L4/L5 spinal stenosis on imaging (Fig. 5C). Druggability assessment and molecular docking

We interrogated drug databases (DGIdb, ChEMBL, and Drugbank online databases) to evaluate GFPT1 druggability, identifying IOX1 as the sole preclinical-stage compound targeting GFPT1 (Supplementary Data 12). In order to evaluate the interaction mode and affinity between the IOX1 and GFPT1, we conducted molecular docking analysis. IOX1, as an inhibitor of GFPT1, had a strong binding affinity (binding energy: −6.39 kcal/mol) with GFPT1 through the analysis of hydrophobic interaction, hydrogen bonding, π-cation interaction, and salt bridge formation (Fig. 6A). IOX1 directly suppresses the activity of GFPT1

To clarify whether IOX1 directly inhibits the enzymatic activity of GFPT1 (rather than indirectly through its histone demethylase inhibitory activity), we conducted both in vitro and intracellular enzyme function analyses. Initially, using the glutamate dehydrogenase method, we discovered that IOX1 significantly suppressed the enzymatic activity of GFPT1, which in both wild-type and GFPT1-overexpressing HEK293T cells (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Data 13, n = 3 independent biological samples). Subsequently, by monitoring the metabolic dynamics of fructose-6-phosphate (F-6-P), a substrate of GFPT1, we observed that IOX1 treatment resulted in a marked accumulation of F-6-P in both wild-type and GFPT1-overexpressing HEK293T cells (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Data 13, n = 3 independent biological samples). This indicates a direct inhibition of GFPT1’s catalytic flux within the cells. In summary, the results from both the in vitro enzyme activity assays and intracellular metabolic flux analyses were consistent, confirming that IOX1 can directly inhibit the enzymatic activity of GFPT1 at the functional level.

GFPT1 overexpression induces spine-specific defects that are rescued by IOX1 in zebrafish

Given that the four prioritized cis-eQTL loci consistently indicated increased GFPT1 expression as the risk direction in human genetic analyses, we modeled a genetically inferred “high-expression” state in vivo by overexpressing wild-type human GFPT1 mRNA (NM_002056) in zebrafish embryos. This strategy was designed to recapitulate the gain-of-function condition suggested by Mendelian randomization rather than to mimic a specific coding variant.

Western blot analysis at 3 dpf confirmed a robust increase in GFPT1 protein levels in injected embryos compared with controls, verifying effective translation of the exogenous mRNA (Supplementary Figs. 4–6 and Supplementary Data 14). Thus, the subsequent phenotypes can be attributed to bona fide GFPT1 overexpression rather than injection-related artifacts.

Because GFPT1 deficiency has previously been reported to impair neuromuscular transmission, we next assessed whether GFPT1 overexpression might induce secondary neuromuscular abnormalities that could account for altered body curvature. At 3 dpf, polarized light birefringence analysis revealed normal somite organization and muscle fiber alignment in GFPT1-overexpressing embryos. Phalloidin staining further confirmed preserved sarcomere structure and actin filament organization. Moreover, α-bungarotoxin (α-BTX) immunofluorescence demonstrated normal clustering and distribution of acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), with no detectable structural defects (Supplementary Fig. 7, n = 8 zebrafish embryos). These results indicate that GFPT1 overexpression does not disrupt muscle integrity or NMJ architecture under our experimental conditions.

In contrast, by 7 dpf, GFPT1-overexpressing zebrafish exhibited pronounced spinal curvature and reduced body length (Fig. 6C, D, n = 8 zebrafish embryos). Histological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed marked notochord vacuole developmental defects accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration at curvature sites. LysoTracker staining further confirmed impaired vacuole formation within the notochord (Fig. 6C, n = 8 zebrafish embryos). Concurrently, DSD-related marker genes were significantly dysregulated (Fig. 6E and Supplementary Data 15, n = 3 independent biological samples), with osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation markers markedly downregulated compared to wild-type controls (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Data 15, n = 3 independent biological samples). Together, these findings demonstrate that abnormally elevated GFPT1 levels disrupt spinal development, likely through impaired notochord maturation and localized inflammatory responses, thereby recapitulating key pathological features relevant to degenerative spinal disease. Importantly, treatment of GFPT1-overexpressing zebrafish with 5 mg/L IOX1 significantly ameliorated spinal curvature and restored body length (Fig. 6C, D, n = 8 zebrafish embryos). This phenotypic rescue was accompanied by improved notochord structure and normalization of DSD-, osteogenic-, and chondrogenic-related gene expression (Fig. 6E, F, and Supplementary Data 15, n = 3 independent biological samples). These results demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of GFPT1 by IOX1 reverses key structural and molecular abnormalities induced by GFPT1 overexpression, supporting its therapeutic potential in DSD.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this represents the first systematic cross-tissues and multi-omics MR study to identify therapeutic targets associated with DSD through integration of large-scale GWAS data from DSD-related phenotypes with 4463 potential druggable genes. Our analyses revealed 7 candidate druggable genes associated with spinal stenosis and 5 candidate druggable genes/proteins associated with spondylosis using SMR and colocalization. Among these, GFPT1, GPX1, and SERPINA1 emerged as shared therapeutic targets for both conditions, with phenome-wide MR analysis confirming favorable safety profiles for GFPT1 and GPX1. Critically, we validated the association between GFPT1 expression levels and LSS risk through WGS in a Chinese population cohort. Molecular docking demonstrated robust binding between IOX1 and GFPT1, further revealing the druggability of GFPT1. Subsequent zebrafish experiments established IOX1’s therapeutic efficacy in rescuing DSD phenotypes. Therefore, GFPT1 might be a promising cross-ancestry target for treating DSD.

GFPT1 serves as the primary rate-limiting enzyme in the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway. While the dysregulation of GFPT1 is established in congenital myasthenia gravis and acetylcholine deficiency, no prior studies have investigated its association with DSD11,12. Previous research indicates that the expression of GFPT1 is upregulated during osteoclast differentiation13. Our MR analysis revealed that reduced GFPT1 expression elevated the risk of spinal stenosis and spondylosis. WGS validation has further confirmed that GFPT1 expression variants correlate with LSS susceptibility in Chinese populations. Therefore, we hypothesize that elevated GFPT1 levels in the blood may potentially promote osteoclast proliferation, disrupting bone remodeling homeostasis and ultimately contributing to spinal stenosis and spondylosis development. Our study proposes for the first time the potential role of GFPT1 in the pathogenesis of DSD, which deserves further mechanistic investigation.

While GFPT1 represents a priority therapeutic target for DSD, the potential of both GPX1 and SERPINA1 warrants further investigation through experimental validation of their druggability and biological mechanisms. As a selenium-containing glutathione peroxidase, GPX1 has been implicated in cartilage homeostasis: low selenium status associates with osteoarthropathy characterized by cartilage deformity14. Additionally, GPX1 knockdown inhibits chondrogenic differentiation in ATDC5 cells, and GPX1 mutations significantly correlate with osteoarthropathy15,16. However, no in-depth study has yet explored the close relationship between GPX1 and DSD. Our MR and colocalization analysis suggest that elevated GPX1 levels in blood may increase the risk of spinal stenosis and spondylosis. Therefore, we hypothesize that overexpression of GPX1 may promote excessive chondrogenesis, potentially inducing the occurrence of DSD. SERPINA1, which encodes a serine protease inhibitor, has been shown to alleviate intervertebral disc degeneration when upregulated17. Our MR study further confirmed that a decrease in SERPINA1 levels in CSF increased the risk of spinal stenosis and spondylosis, a finding reinforced by subsequent colocalization analysis.

The GFPT1 inhibitor IOX1, sourced from the DGIdb database, demonstrated a strong binding affinity to GFPT1 in molecular docking studies, suggesting therapeutic potential for conditions involving aberrant GFPT1 overexpression, such as the elevated DSD risk identified in this study. Pharmacological experiments further confirmed that IOX1 effectively rescues spinal curvature defects in GFPT1-overexpressing zebrafish. IOX1 is a broad-spectrum 2-oxoglutarate (2OG) oxygenase inhibitor capable of suppressing multiple oxygenase activities18. It has been extensively utilized in the research and development of drugs for treating various diseases. Existing evidence indicates potent anti-inflammatory properties of IOX1, highlighting its promise for inflammatory disorders19. Additionally, it has shown efficacy in treating cancers like colorectal cancer, yet its relevance in the treatment of DSD has not been reported20. Given the established role of inflammation in DSD pathogenesis, we hypothesize that IOX1 may mitigate DSD progression through dual mechanisms: by direct inhibition of GFPT1 (validated in our docking and zebrafish models) or by suppression of the inflammatory cascade.

The strengths and innovations of our study are as follows. Firstly, this represents the first large-scale screening of drug targets for DSD, integrating multiple tissue data for SMR and colocalization analysis to identify several genes that may provide potential targets for treating DSD. Secondly, these candidate targets were evaluated for potential side effects, beneficial effects, and druggability through phenome-wide MR. Thirdly, the clinical relevance and cross-ancestry applicability of GFPT1 was further validated through WGS in the Chinese population, confirming its association with LSS risk and reinforcing its candidacy as a therapeutic target for DSD. Finally, we conducted in vivo experiments to further enhance the biological plausibility of IOX1 as a therapeutic drug for DSD.

This study is subject to certain limitations. First, while our results suggest that GFPT1 may influence spinal development through mechanisms involving osteoclasts, osteoblasts, and chondrogenesis, direct cellular and mechanistic evidence in mammalian systems—such as osteoclastogenesis assays, chondrocyte differentiation models, or nucleus pulposus cell experiments—is currently lacking. Future studies should prioritize these functional validations to clarify the precise cellular pathways involved. Second, our functional interpretations are primarily based on blood or CSF-derived QTL data, and the regulatory effects of the four prioritized genetic variants in spine-relevant cell types—such as cartilage, intervertebral disc, or bone tissue—have not been systematically validated. Further investigation using disease-relevant cellular models or tissue-specific QTL resources will be necessary to confirm their local regulatory roles. Third, the integration of QTL data from diverse analytical platforms may introduce heterogeneity into our analysis. Additionally, although our study examined a large set of genes, we may have overlooked certain important genes that lack suitable instrumental variables in the eQTLGen, CAGE, and NIAGADS datasets. Finally, the predictive value of the identified genes for the risk of developmental spinal disorders requires further investigation in independent clinical cohorts. We plan to address these gaps through a combination of in vitro mammalian cell models, organoid systems, and co-culture assays in subsequent work, with the aim of translating these genetic insights into biologically and clinically actionable mechanisms.

In conclusion, we identified 3 potential drug targets (GFPT1, GPX1, and SERPINA1) associated with 2 DSD phenotypes (spinal stenosis and spondylosis) in blood and CSF. Among them, targeting GFPT1 and GPX1 for DSD treatment showed no potential side effects. WGS analysis in a Chinese cohort indicates that GFPT1 is associated with the risk of LSS, highlighting its cross-ancestry applicability. Molecular docking demonstrated favorable druggability for GFPT1, suggesting that IOX1 might be a potential therapeutic agent for DSD caused by abnormal expression of GFPT1. The potential of IOX1 in treating DSD has been substantiated by in vivo pharmacological experiments. These findings provide new insights into the genetic basis and treatments for DSD. However, it is worth noting that these findings are preliminary and require further validation through clinical and experimental studies.

Methods

Study design

The MR study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of using Mendelian randomization (STROBE-MR) to strengthen epidemiological observation research reports.

The schematic representation of our research design is presented in Fig. 1. The requisite eQTL and pQTL data for exposures were procured from 3 sources: eQTLGen Consortium, CAGE, and NIAGADS (Supplementary Data 1)21–23. Phenotypic data related to DSD, serving as the outcomes, were obtained from the FinnGen R9 study and the UK Biobank study (Supplementary Data 1)23,24. Publicly available GWAS, eQTL, and pQTL summary statistics used in this study were obtained from studies with existing ethics approval and participant consent, as reported in the original publications. This study involving human participants was approved by the medical ethics committee of Inner Mongolia Medical University (approval no. YKD202301033). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to blood sample collection and WGS analysis. No software was used for data collection. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Druggable genes list

The druggable genes discussed in this study were derived from a review by Finan et al., which identified 4479 druggable genes out of 20,300 protein-coding genes. Excluding 16 pseudogenes, the remaining 4463 genes with unique gene symbols were used in our study25.

Data source for QTLs

The eQTLGen consortium conducted a large-scale meta-analysis on 31,684 blood samples from 37 cohorts, and identified cis-eQTLs of 16,989 genes20. After intersecting with 4463 potential druggable genes, 2235 druggable genes were obtained for this study. The cis-eQTLs of 1593 peripheral blood druggable genes used in this study were obtained from CAGE, which includes 8080 peripheral blood genes of 2765 individuals through genome-wide association analysis21. The cis-pQTLs of 151 cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) druggable genes utilized in this study were derived from the NIAGADS database, which contained summary statistical data obtained from genome-wide association analysis of 181 CSF proteins involving 835 individuals22. The window size of the above cis-QTLs was all 1 Mb, and cis-QTLs at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) were included in SMR analysis. Clump performed during the SMR process using the 1000 Genomes European reference.

Data sources for two phenotypes of DSD

The GWAS summary statistics for the two phenotypes of DSD, including spinal stenosis and spondylosis, were available from the FinnGen R9 study and the UK Biobank study. All participants with the two phenotypes of DSD from FinnGen and UK Biobank were of European ancestry23,24, and the underlying datasets included both female and male participants. The FinnGen R9 study represents a comprehensive genomics investigation that examined over 500,000 samples from the Finnish Biobank. In this study, the R9 study comprised 18,989 cases of spinal stenosis and 19,892 cases of spondylosis, with a total of 270,964 controls23. We also obtained summary data on two phenotypes of DSD from the UK Biobank, including 3733 cases of spinal stenosis and 5077 cases of spondylosis, with a total of 391,917 controls24. The UK Biobank data were analyzed using SAIGE, based on generalized linear mixed models (GLMM). GLMM was employed to scrutinize approximately 1400 phecode binary phenotypes in the UK Biobank, with an estimated 28 million variations, encompassing 400,000 samples from white individuals in Britain. This effectively alleviated the multiple false positives and complex population structure issues caused by imbalanced case and control samples in GWAS24. The GWAS meta-analysis results of the two phenotypes of DSD in FinnGen and UKB were used as the outcomes for SMR analysis.

GWAS meta-analysis

Before meta-analysis, quality control was performed on the two phenotypes of DSD (spinal stenosis and spondylosis) in FinnGen and UKB, including removing missing data, meaningless values (P > 1 and β equal to infinity or 0), duplicate SNPs, and SNPs with a MAF < 0.5% to obtain cleaned data26. METAL was used for further GWAS meta-analysis on cleaned data. In this study, the GWAS meta-analysis method based on standard error was executed, simultaneously performing genomic control, sample overlap correction, and allele frequency tracking on all datasets27. The CMplot package was used to draw a Manhattan plot (Supplementary Fig. 3) to display GWAS meta-analysis results for the two phenotypes of DSD28.

SMR analysis

We conducted SMR analysis using the SMR software tool (version 1.3.1) on cis-QTLs at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) and two phenotypes of DSD, including spinal stenosis and spondylosis29. Further false discovery rate (FDR) correction was carried out to remove false positive results (FDR_P > 0.05). This correction was performed using the “p.adjust” function in R software (version 4.3.1). The R package ggplot2 (version 3.4.4) was utilized to generate volcano and forest plots to visually represent the FDR correction results.

Two-sample MR analysis

We performed two-sample MR analysis using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.7) on druggable genes and two phenotypes of DSD (spinal stenosis and spondylosis) to validate SMR results. To meet the prerequisites for effective instrumental variables (IVs) in MR for the included SNPs, PLINK (version 1.9) and ieugwasr (version 0.1.5) were employed to include SNPs with the following criteria: SNPs significantly associated with druggable genes were selected (P < 5 × 10−8); the LD clumping was then carried out to identify SNPs (r2 < 0.001) within a 10,000 kb range30. Finally, those independently significant SNPs were extracted as IVs, and further quantified through F-statistics, with F-statistics > 10 providing robust evidence of the power of the IVs30. These IVs were also used for subsequent phenome-wide MR analysis. We performed inverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR for IVs that contain more than one variant and Wald ratio for only one IV. The forest plot drawn by ggplot2 was used to display the results of the two-sample MR analysis.

Sensitivity analysis

To enhance the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. For SMR analysis, we conducted a HEIDI test using the SMR software tool. We selected cis-QTL with P < 1.57 × 10−3 for HEIDI testing29. The minimum number of cis-QTLs required for each HEIDI test was 3, and the maximum was 2029. If P_HEIDI > 0.05, it indicates there was no heterogeneity29. For two-sample MR analysis, we performed the horizontal pleiotropy test and the heterogeneity test. These tests were executed using the “mr_pleiotropy_test” and “mr_heterogeneity” functions in the TwoSampleMR package, respectively. The MR Egger and IVW method were employed to conduct the horizontal pleiotropy test. If P_pleiotropy > 0.05, it suggests that the SNPs incorporated in the analysis were not influenced by horizontal pleiotropy. The Cochran Q test was utilized to ascertain whether there was heterogeneity among the SNPs in the analysis. If P_heterogeneity > 0.05, it indicates an absence of heterogeneity.

Colocalization analysis

The Coloc R package (version 5.2.3) and Bayesian method were employed for colocalization analysis31. The Bayesian method yielded a posterior probability for the following hypotheses: (i) H0: there was no causal variant in druggable gene level (trait1) and two phenotypes of DSD (spinal stenosis and spondylosis) within a specific genomic region; (ii) H1: there was one causal variant for trait1 only within a specific genomic region; (iii) H2: there was one causal variant only for trait2 within a specific genomic region; (iv) H3: there were two distinct causal variants for trait1 and trait2 within a specific genomic region; (v) H4: there was a shared causal variant for trait1 and trait2 within a specific genomic region. In this study, we set the prior probabilities of the SNP being associated with trait 1 only (p1) at 1 × 10−4; the probability of the SNP being associated with trait 2 only (p2) at 1 × 10−4; and the probability of the SNP being associated with both traits (p12) at 1 × 10−5, respectively. If the posterior probability of shared causal variants (PH4) between two signals was ≥0.8, it was considered to provide strong support for colocalization. The indication of medium support for colocalization was defined as 0.5 < PH4 < 0.831. The R package ggplot2 was utilized to generate bar plots to visually represent the results of colocalization analysis. This rigorous approach ensured the validity of our research findings and contributed to the understanding of the genetic basis of DSD.

Phenome-wide MR

To predict drug safety and the risk of targeted adverse reactions, a phenome-wide MR analysis was performed to systematically infer the causal effects of previously druggable gene expression on 782 non-DSD diseases or traits with a sample size of more than 500 in the European population from the UKB-SAIGE24. The summary statistical data for disease-related SNPs were downloaded from SAIGE GWAS (https://www.leelabsg.org/resources).

Peripheral blood sample collection from LSS patients

Peripheral blood samples were collected from Chinese LSS patients meeting the following inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosis confirmed by clinical symptoms and physical signs, (2) radiological evidence (X-ray, CT, or MRI), and (3) consensus by ≥2 senior orthopedic specialists. The study cohort included both female and male participants. Using standard phlebotomy protocols, blood was collected into EDTA-Na anticoagulation tubes. A minimum volume of 5 mL was drawn from each adult participant and aliquoted equally into two pre-chilled tubes. Tubes were immediately inverted 8–10 times to ensure proper anticoagulant mixing. Samples underwent visual inspection to confirm the absence of hemolysis or coagulation before initial storage at −80 °C. For intermediate storage (<2 years), samples were maintained at −20 °C with ≤5 freeze-thaw cycles. All samples were transported on dry ice with continuous temperature monitoring throughout the chain of custody32.

WGS analysis

WGS sequencing was conducted using the DNBSEQ platform. After filtering out reads with low quality, adapter contamination, and a high proportion of N bases, an average of 650,678,982 clean reads (97,601,847,381 bps) were obtained per sample. The clean reads from each sample exhibited high Q20 and Q30 scores, indicating good sequencing quality. The average GC content was 40.93%. The Burrows–Wheeler Aligner was utilized to align each sample to the reference genome (GRCh38) sequence. On average, 99.99% of the reads aligned to the reference genome, with 95.13% aligning uniquely to the reference genome. The average proportion of duplicate reads was 0.87%. After removing duplicate reads, the average sequencing depth was 32.66X, with the whole genome region of each sample being covered by at least one read on average33. Based on the alignment results, the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) was used to analyze SNPs and InDels in the BAM files, and ANNOVAR was employed to annotate the results for subsequent analysis34,35.

The cis-eQTL of GFPT1 from the eQTLGen consortium was extracted and utilized as a background ref. 20 All mutation sites of GFPT1 in WGS data from LSS patients and healthy controls were extracted, with the exclusion of all known benign or likely benign sites listed in the ClinVar database36. The intersection of the filtered loci in LSS patients, healthy controls, and the background reference from the eQTLGen consortium was visualized using a Venn diagram. Subsequently, the mutation frequencies of these cis-eQTLs in the 1000 Genomes East Asian population, healthy controls, and LSS patients within the intersection were analyzed to identify any cis-eQTLs with significantly altered mutation frequencies in LSS. The raw sequence data reported in this paper have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive in the National Genomics Data Center, China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, under accession number GSA-Human: HRA017983. These data are available under controlled access through GSA-Human at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa-human37,38.

Druggability

In order to determine whether the identified genes contain targeted drugs, the DGIdb (version 4.0), ChEMBL, and Drugbank online (version 6.0) databases were used to search for known drugs39–41. To more finely evaluate the medicinal feasibility, we classified the identified targeted genes and drugs according to regulatory approvals as follows: (i) approved (targeting drugs have been approved); (ii) in clinical trials (targeting drugs are currently being studied in clinical trials); (iii) preclinical (targeting drugs are in preclinical pipelines); (iv) druggable (the max phase of this targeted drug is unknown).

Molecular docking

To further experimental validation and optimize potential drug designs, we conducted molecular docking between candidate drugs and their targets to assess the binding energy and interaction modes, thereby identifying drugs with strong interactions with the DSD targets. The small molecule structure data used in this study were sourced from the PubChem database, and the protein structures of GFPT1 were derived from 6R4E in the Protein Data Bank (PDB)42. Initially, all water molecules were removed from the protein and ligand structures, and polar hydrogen atoms were added. The grid box was centered to encompass each protein’s structural domains and to permit unrestricted molecular flexibility. Subsequently, ligand and protein molecular docking was performed using a semi-flexible docking method, all implemented with AutoDock4 (version 4.2.6)43. Finally, the protein–ligand interaction profiler website was used to visualize the interaction patterns between ligands and proteins44.

Cell culture and plasmid transfection

HEK293T cells (Procell, China, CL-0005) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% streptomycin-penicillin (Gibco, USA), and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. GFPT1 (NM_002056)-pCMV6-Myc-DDK plasmid (RC207225) was purchased from OriGene (USA). Cells were transfected with the use of Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions45.

GFPT1 enzyme activity assay

To directly evaluate the enzymatic activity of GFPT1, wild-type HEK293T cells and HEK293T cells transfected with the GFPT1 (NM_002056)-pCMV6-Myc-DDK plasmid were treated with 100 μM IOX1 for 12 h before being collected19. Cell lysis was performed using a specific lysis buffer, which consisted of 50 mM KH2PO₄, 10 mM EDTA, 5 mM reduced glutathione, 12 mM sodium glucose-6-phosphate, and 1 mM PMSF, with the pH adjusted to 7.6. Take 100 μL of cell lysate and add an equal volume of reaction buffer to bring the total volume to 200 μL. The reaction buffer contained 100 mM KH₂PO₄, 10 mM fructose-6-phosphate, 6.0 mM glutamine, 0.3 mM 3-acetylpyridine adenine dinucleotide, 50 mM KCl, and 6 units of bovine liver-derived glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), with the pH adjusted to 7.6. The reaction system was incubated at 37 °C for 45 min, and then absorbance changes were monitored in real time at a wavelength of 370 nm using a microplate reader (Bioeko, Japan)46,47. Additionally, to further confirm the inhibitory effect of IOX1 on GFPT1 enzyme activity, we used a fructose-6-phosphate (F-6-P) content assay kit (G0878F, Geruisi-bio) to measure the content of its direct substrate, F-6-P. The detection process (sample preparation, reaction mixture preparation, and detection) was carried out according to the kit’s instructions.

Zebrafish maintenance and embryo collection

AB strains were purchased from the China Zebrafish Resource Center. All adult wild-type zebrafish (AB strain, ZDB-GENO-960809-7) were maintained at 28.5 °C in a recirculating water system with a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle. Embryos were obtained by natural crosses and staged as described previously. To ensure biological reproducibility and minimize clutch-specific bias, all experiments were performed using embryos collected from multiple independent clutches (at least 3 clutches per experimental group). Each treatment and control group included embryos from different parental pairs and was repeated across multiple breeding rounds. To inhibit pigmentation, embryos were treated with 0.03% 1-phenyl-2-thiourea (Sigma, USA)48. All zebrafish embryos were euthanized by hypothermic shock in ice-cold water. All zebrafish experiments in this study were approved by the Experimental Animal Management and Ethics Committee of Northwest University (NWU-AWC-20241104Z), and we complied with all ethical regulations related to animal experimentation.

Synthesis and injection of mRNA

Full-length human GFPT1 (NM_002056) cDNA was cloned into the pCS2+ plasmid, and the capped human GFPT1 mRNA was synthesized by using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE SP6 Transcription kit (Invitrogen, USA). Two hundred picograms of human GFPT1 mRNA were injected into each embryo at the 1–2 cell stage, using PLI-100A Plus Pico Injector (Harvard, USA)45.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from zebrafish embryos using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 1% NP-40, 1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA, and 150 mM NaCl) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Pierce, 88666). A total of 50–80 embryos were collected at 3 days post-fertilization (dpf) and snap-frozen on ice. After centrifugation, supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (10–20 μg) were separated on 12% SDS–polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Flag (HT201-01, 1:1000, ProteinFind) and β-actin (HC201-01, 1:1000, ProteinFind). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and visualized with a ChemiDoc imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to β-actin48.

Analysis of skeletal muscle structure by birefringence and phalloidin staining

At 3 dpf, embryos were anesthetized with 0.02% tricaine (MS-222) and mounted in 2% low-melting-point agarose. Birefringence images of skeletal muscle structure were acquired using a Leica DM2500 microscope equipped with a polarizing filter48. For phalloidin staining, larvae at 3 dpf were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C overnight, followed by permeabilization with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20–30 min at room temperature. Samples were incubated with phalloidin working solution (1:200 dilution in PBS) for 120 min at room temperature in the dark. After washing with PBS, fluorescence images were captured using a SMZ25 microscope (Nikon, Japan) under 488 nm excitation to visualize F-actin48.

α-bungarotoxin staining

Zebrafish larvae at 3 dpf were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight. Samples were washed with PBS and permeabilized with pre-cooled acetone at −20 °C for 15 min. Larvae were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated α-bungarotoxin (α-BTX; 1:200 dilution in PBS). After incubation, samples were washed three times with PBSTx (PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100). Fluorescence images were acquired under 488 nm excitation using an SMZ25 microscope (Nikon, Japan)48.

Pharmacological rescue experiment

IOX1 (T6545; TargetMol) is a GFPT1 inhibitor, whose stock solution is dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 40 mg/mL and stored at −20 °C49,50. The experiment was divided into three groups: the control group (treated solely with DMSO), the GFPT1 overexpression group, and the intervention group with GFPT1 overexpression and IOX1 treatment. IOX1 treatment of GFPT1-overexpressing zebrafish commenced at 3 days post fertilization (dpf) by immersing the embryos in an egg water solution containing 5 mg/L IOX1; embryos in the control group were treated with egg water containing the same final concentration of DMSO. All embryos were incubated at 28.5 °C, with the solution being changed every 24 h51. At 7 dpf, zebrafish embryos in the control group, GFPT1 overexpression group, and IOX1 treatment group were anesthetized with 0.02% tricaine (MS-222), fixed in 2% low-melting-point agarose, and observed and photographed using an SMZ25 microscope (Nikon, Japan). The body lengths of 7 dpf embryos were analyzed and recorded using ImageJ software52.

H&E staining

To assess the histopathological changes in the zebrafish spinal cord, 7 dpf embryos were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C. Following fixation, the samples underwent decalcification, were embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 µm slices. The sections were then stained with H&E and examined under an SMZ25 microscope (Nikon, Japan) for histopathological analysis.

LysoTraker staining

To assess the development of notochord vesicles in zebrafish, zebrafish embryos at 7 dpf were collected and thoroughly washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, the samples were stained with LysoTracker Green DND-26 (40738ES50, Yeasen) at a concentration of 10 μM for 1 to 2 h. After staining, the samples were washed three more times with PBS to eliminate any residual dye. Finally, the samples were examined under a multi-mode spinning disk confocal microscope (Dragonfly 200, UK) to observe and document the morphology and distribution of the notochord vesicles.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 30 intact zebrafish larvae per group using TRIzol™ reagent (Ambion, USA), and 1 μg of RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed using the SuperScript™ system (Invitrogen, USA). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Kapa Biosystems, USA) on three independent biological replicates derived from distinct zebrafish batches, as previously described. For each biological replicate, PCR reactions were run in technical triplicate, and the technical replicates were averaged before statistical analysis53. The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 20 s. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 16. All primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST to span exon-exon junctions, generating amplicons of 80–200 bp without significant secondary structure or primer-dimer potential. Product specificity was confirmed by single-peak melt curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis. The housekeeping gene gapdh was used as the reference for normalization.

Statistics and reproducibility

Quantitative PCR data were analyzed using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method. For each biological replicate, values from technical triplicates were averaged and used as a single data point. Data are presented as mean fold change ± SEM from at least three independent biological replicates. The normality of all data sets was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Unless otherwise specified, all statistical tests were two-sided. Based on the test results, data conforming to a normal distribution were analyzed using parametric tests: a two-tailed Student’s t test for comparisons between two groups, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for comparisons among multiple groups. For data that did not meet the assumption of normality, corresponding nonparametric tests were employed: a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons, or a two-sided Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multi-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2.263). Randomization was not applicable to the human genetic analyses. For the experimental analyses, samples and embryos were allocated according to the predefined study design, and no formal randomization procedure was used. Blinding was not applicable to the human genetic analyses, and outcome assessment in the experimental analyses was not performed under blinded conditions.
