The role of circulating metabolomics in linking metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, and risk of diverticulitis outcomes
Authors: Yilun Wu, Mengxi Du, Alaina M Bever, Mingyang Song, Edward Giovannucci, Lisa Strate, Andrew T Chan, Wenjie Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundObesity and chronic inflammation are associated with an elevated risk of diverticulitis. However, the underlying mechanisms, particularly the role of circulating metabolites are not well understood.MethodsWe derived metabolomic signatures of metabolic dysfunction (body mass index, waist circumference, C-peptide, and adiponectin) and inflammation (C-reactive protein, interleukin 6, and tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily 1B). Research in context

Evidence before this study

Diverticulitis is the most common complication of colonic diverticulosis, pouches in the alimentary tract. Clinical management and prevention of diverticulitis has been limited due to its undetermined pathogenesis. While obesity and chronic inflammation have been associated with an increased risk of diverticulitis, specific patterns of circulating metabolomics that may link these factors to diverticulitis have not been studied. We conducted a literature search in PubMed up to August 25, 2025, with terms ("diverticulitis" OR "diverticular disease" OR "diverticulosis") AND ("metabolic" OR "obesity" OR "body mass index" OR "inflammation" OR "inflammatory" OR "metabolomics" OR "metabolite" OR "metabolome") and no language restrictions. We did not find any articles directly linking metabolomics of metabolic dysregulation or inflammation to diverticulitis, highlighting the need for a better understanding of the disease's pathogenesis through the lens of metabolomics.

Added value of this study

Using data from three nationwide prospective cohorts, we derived metabolomic signatures for metabolic dysfunction and chronic inflammation and linked the metabolomic signatures to diverticulitis outcomes. We found that both signatures were significantly associated with diverticulitis incidence. The metabolic dysfunction signature was further associated with diverticulitis recurrence and surgery requirement.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings support that circulating metabolites pertaining to metabolic disturbance and inflammation are associated with diverticulitis, suggesting a novel direction to clarify the disease etiology. The robust association between the metabolic dysfunction signature and diverticulitis incidence and recurrence highlights the potential of dietary and lifestyle intervention in the management of diverticulitis.

Introduction

Diverticulitis is inflammation of one or a few adjacent colonic diverticula, which are pouches in the lining of the gastrointestinal tract. It is highly prevalent in the Western population with incidence and mortality rates increasing over time.1, 2, 3 The admission rate due to complicated diverticulitis, a more severe form of the condition often requiring colectomy, has risen by 43% over the last decade.4 In the United States, diverticulitis was one of the top 10 most common gastrointestinal diagnoses for hospital admissions, with an economic burden of approximately $9 billion.5

Previous studies have shown that obesity, particularly central adiposity, is associated with an increased risk of diverticulitis.6, 7, 8 Other lifestyle and dietary patterns that are related to metabolic dysfunction and chronic inflammation, such as smoking,9,10 physical inactivity, a Western diet high in red meat,11,12 and low fiber intake13,14 are also associated with an elevated risk of diverticulitis. In a prior study, circulating levels of pre-diagnostic inflammatory markers including C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin 6 (IL-6) were associated with an increased risk of diverticulitis.15

The risk of recurrent diverticulitis is estimated to be 22% after the first episode and 55% after the second episode, and is significantly higher in younger people and women.2 Evidence for risk factors of diverticulitis recurrence remains sparse, but these likely include younger age, obesity, smoking, number of previous episodes, severity of diverticulitis, and conservative treatment of complicated disease.16,17

Despite the epidemiological evidence linking lifestyle and diet to the risk of diverticulitis, the underlying biological mechanisms remain poorly understood. Metabolomics, the identification and quantification of circulating metabolites, offers promising insights into the systemic metabolic and inflammatory perturbations that may contribute to the pathophysiology and progression of the disease.18 Furthermore, metabolomic signatures may serve as biomarkers for disease severity and facilitate the prediction of recurrent episodes or the need for surgical intervention, therefore aiding in personalized treatment strategies.

Therefore, in this study, by leveraging profiling of circulating metabolomics and biomarkers, combined with detailed and repeated assessments of disease outcomes over decades of follow-up across three nationwide prospective cohort studies, Nurses’ Health Studies (NHS) I and II and Health Professional Follow-up Study (HPFS), we derived metabolomic signatures of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation and evaluated their roles in diverticulitis incidence, severity, and recurrence.

Methods

Study population and blood collection

Established in 1976, the NHS recruited 121,700 female registered nurses aged between 30 years and 55 years.19 In 1989, the NHSII enrolled 116,429 female registered nurses aged between 25 years and 42 years.19 The HPFS was established in 1986 and recruited 51,529 male veterinarians dentists, pharmacists, optometrists, osteopath physicians, and podiatrists aged between 40 years and 75 years.20 Participants received biennial questionnaires asking for information about demographics, disease outcomes, and lifestyle factors. Diet information was collected through food frequency questionnaires every four years. Blood specimens were collected in a subsample of 32,826 participants in NHS from 1989 to 1990, 29,611 participants in NHSII from 1996 to 1999, and 18,225 participants in HPFS from 1993 to 1995. Specimens were shipped back on ice packs (95% arrived within 24 h of blood collection) overnight, immediately centrifuged and aliquoted into cryotubes upon arrival, and stored in liquid nitrogen under −130 °C.21

Ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Mass General Brigham and the Harvard T. H. Chan School of Public Health, and the IRBs allowed participants’ completion of questionnaires to be considered as implied consent (IRB protocol number: 2014P000837).

Metabolite profiling

A total of 13,707 individuals across all three cohorts had metabolomics data measured as part of 19 nested case–control studies of various conditions. Metabolomic profiling data were obtained through the targeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) at the Broad Institute (Cambridge, Massachusetts).22 Hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography with positive ionization mode (HILC-pos) was used to separate cationic polar metabolites.23 C8 chromatography with positive MS ionization mode (C8-pos) and C18 chromatography with negative MS ionization mode (C18-neg) were used to detect lipids and moderate polarity.24 Detailed descriptions of LC-MS methodology have been previously published.25,26 Missing values of metabolites with <10% missingness were imputed with half of the minimum measurement for each metabolite.27 Metabolites with ≥10% missingness were excluded.

Markers of metabolomic signatures

The markers of the metabolomic signature of metabolic dysfunction contain body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, C-peptide, and adiponectin. BMI was calculated as the cumulative average of self-reported weight divided by height squared. Waist circumference was self-reported in 1986 for NHS participants and in 1988 for HPFS participants. Both weight and waist circumference have been validated in the cohorts with high correlations with technician-measured values.28 C-peptide was measured using an alcohol precipitation nonequilibrium assay (Diagnostic Systems Laboratories/Beckman Coulter, Webster, TX), and adiponectin was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; ALPCO Diagnostics, Salem, NH).29,30 The markers of the metabolomic signature of inflammation contain CRP, IL-6, and tumor necrosis superfamily 1B (TNFR1B). CRP was measured using a highly sensitive immunoturbidimetric assay (Denka Seiken Co.) and IL-6 and TNFR1B were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA; R&D Systems).31

Metabolomic signature derivation

In the signature derivation, we adapted a robust method that derived and validated metabolomic signatures published previously.24 Similarly, We used data from 1269 colorectal cancer cases and controls from NHS and HPFS with available metabolomics and biomarkers data to derive our metabolomic signatures of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation based on 192 metabolites after excluding missingness. We first separated our data into 10 sets, each of which was used as the test set in a total of the 10 iterations, with the remaining 9 sets as training sets. In each iteration, we applied reduced rank regression (RRR) on the signature components as the outcome and all metabolites as predictors in the training sets. We then extracted the first factor, a linear combination of the components, as the summary score for metabolic dysfunction or inflammation. We then applied elastic net regression (ENR) with a 10-fold cross-validation on that summary score as the outcome with all 192 metabolites as predictors, adjusting for age, date of blood collection, fasting status, and sex as the covariates. From the 10 ENR models obtained, we selected the model with the least mean squared error on the test set for metabolomic signature predictions of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation. Spearman correlation was used to examine the correlation between the derived signatures and their corresponding components and adjusted R squared was used to assess the proportion of explained variation. The predictability of metabolites was assessed based on the magnitude of their coefficients in the selected ENR model. Only metabolites with nonzero coefficients were selected as predictive metabolites.

Ascertainment of diverticulitis

The primary outcome was diverticulitis incidence. Secondary outcomes included diverticulitis recurrence and diverticulitis requiring surgery, with the latter of which serving as a surrogate for severity. The NHS participants were asked whether they had diverticulitis requiring antibiotics or hospitalization on biennial questionnaires (in 2008, 2012, 2014, 2016, and 2020). Participants who reported diverticulitis were asked for the year of diagnosis back until 1990 as well as number of episodes and whether surgery was required. The NHSII participants were similarly asked in 2015, 2017, and 2019, with diagnoses dating back to 2003. The HPFS participants were asked biennially from 1990 to 2020 whether they had been newly diagnosed with diverticulitis. Participants who reported diverticulitis were further followed up with supplementary questionnaires inquiring about the date of diagnosis, clinical symptoms, diagnosis methods, and treatments received during their episodes. Self-reporting of diverticulitis was validated through medical records review based on evidence from computed tomography, surgical pathology, or clinical presentations consistent with diverticulitis (e.g., abdominal pain, elevated white blood cells, fever), with a confirmation rate of 88%, 92%, and 84% in NHS, NHSII, and HPFS, respectively.32, 33, 34

Statistical analysis

After deriving the metabolomic signatures of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation, we applied it to the larger population within NHS, NHSII, and HPFS who had metabolomics data. We excluded participants with incomplete metabolite data for the signature prediction (N = 5487) as well as those who had missing diverticulitis reports on their questionnaire or a diagnosis of diverticulitis before their blood collection date (N = 332), leaving 7888 participants for the prospective evaluation of the signatures and diverticulitis risk (Fig. 1). The follow-up time was defined in person years from blood collection to the earliest among each participant's event date, death date, and last questionnaire response. We used a Cox proportional hazard ratio model to evaluate the associations of metabolomics signatures of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation with risk of incident diverticulitis, recurrent diverticulitis, and diverticulitis requiring surgery, adjusting for age at blood collection (years), date of blood collection (months), fasting status (fasting ≥8hrs, non-fasting), sex (male, female), smoking status (current smoker, past smoker, never smoked), menopausal status and hormone use in women (premenopausal, postmenopausal but not on hormone use, postmenopausal and on hormone use), physical activity (metabolic equivalent of task score [MET] hours/week), alcohol intake (g/day), red meat intake (servings/day), total fiber intake (g/day), aspirin use (yes, no), NSAID use (yes, no), and acetaminophen use (yes, no). All covariates were obtained from the questionnaire that was closest to the date of blood collection. The missing covariates were imputed using the last observation carried forward method from the previous questionnaires with non-missing values. The missing indicator method was further applied on the missing values of physical activity (n = 64) and intake of alcohol/red meat/total fiber (n = 329). The proportional hazards assumption was verified using martingale residuals and log cumulative hazard plots. The metabolomic signatures were examined in both quartiles and per standard deviation (SD) increase. The p-trend was assessed with 1-SD increase. We performed stratified analyses for each metabolomic signature by age (<55 vs. ≥55 years old), smoking (never smoked, current smoker, past smoker), median level of physical activity (<11 vs. ≥11 MET-h/week), alcohol intake (<5 vs. ≥5 g/day), median follow-up time (<23.8 vs. ≥23.8 years), and BMI (<25, 25–29.99, vs. ≥30 kg/m2). We computed the P-value for interaction using the likelihood ratio test for BMI and smoking status and Wald test for all other subgroups. We further conducted sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of our metabolomic signatures by adjusting for each participant's case control status in their individual case control study in the association analysis. We also derived the signatures only in the controls in the colorectal cancer study and adjusted for each participant's case control status in their individual case control study in the association analysis. All significant tests were two-sided with a significance level of 0.05. All analyses were performed using statistical language R version 4.3.2.

Study overview. We derived metabolomic signatures of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation using metabolomics and biomarker data from 1269 participants in Nurses' Health Study (NHS) and Health Professional Follow-up Study (HPFS) using reduced rank regression and elastic net regression. We subsequently predicted both metabolomic signatures in 7888 participants from the NHS, NHSII, and HPFS. Finally, we evaluated the associations between the metabolomic signatures with risk of diverticulitis outcomes, including diverticulitis incidence, recurrence, and surgery requirement using Cox proportional hazards model.

Role of the funding source

The funders had no role in study design, data collection and analysis, interpretation of data, writing of the report, and decision to submit the paper for publication.

Results

Metabolomic signatures of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation showed modest to strong correlations with the individual components used to derive each signature, with Spearman correlation coefficients ranging from 0.43 (inflammation signature and IL-6) to 0.61 (metabolic dysfunction signature and BMI) (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2). The proportion of variation in each individual marker explained by the metabolomic signatures ranged from 0.22 in IL-6 to 0.39 in TNFRSF1B (Supplemental Figure 1). On average, the metabolic dysfunction and inflammation signatures explained 32% of the variation in the metabolic dysfunction biomarkers and 29% of the variation in inflammation biomarkers. Among the 70 metabolites selected for the derivation of the metabolomic signature of metabolic dysfunction (Supplemental Table 1), several amino acids, including l-tyrosine, l-phenylalanine, and l-valine were among the strongest positive predictors, while l-asparagine and glycine were the most negatively predictive. Among the 113 metabolites selected for the metabolomic signature of inflammation (Supplemental Table 2), the most predictive metabolites included ribothymidine, cis-5-tetradecenoylcarnitine, dodecanoylcarnitine, PE(16:0/P-18:1(9Z)), N2,N2-dimethylguanosine, PC(P-16:0/18:0), and pseudouridine. The two metabolomic signatures were modestly correlated (Spearman correlation coefficient: 0.46).

There were 548 diverticulitis cases documented over 190,514 person-years of follow-up (median, 23.8 years). Metabolomic signatures of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation were both associated with an increased risk of diverticulitis (Table 2). The multivariable-adjusted hazard ratio (HR) comparing participants in the highest quartile to those in the lowest quartile was 1.97 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.52–2.54; P-trend <0.0001) for the metabolic dysfunction signature and 1.40 (95% CI: 1.08–1.81; P-trend = 0.02) for the inflammation signature. The stratified analyses showed that inflammation signature was associated with an increased risk of diverticulitis incidence among younger participants (extreme-quartile HR: 1.65; 95% CI: 1.20–2.27), but not among the older participants (HR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.68–1.60; P for interaction = 0.02; Supplemental Figure 2). The sensitivity analyses of further adjusting for case control status of individual studies (Supplemental Table 3) or deriving the signatures using controls only (Supplemental Table 4) showed similar results. We further adjusted diabetes at baseline which might affect the metabolome, and the results remained largely the same for both metabolomic signatures (data not shown).

Discussion

In this study, we derived signatures of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation using circulating metabolomics data from two nationwide cohorts. Both signatures explain a substantial amount of variation in their corresponding composing markers. Both signatures were associated with an increased risk of diverticulitis incidence. The signature of metabolic dysfunction was additionally associated with an increased risk of diverticulitis recurrence and surgery requirement.

Previous evidence suggests that obesity is a risk factor for diverticulitis.6,34 In particular, visceral and subcutaneous adiposity is independently associated with an increased risk of diverticulitis.8 Excessive adipose tissues are not only related to elevated risk of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, joint disorders, and cancer,35,36 but are involved in metabolic disturbance and relevant inflammatory disorders by releasing surplus amounts of fatty acids and increasing adipokine secretion.37 Consistently, our results showed that metabolic dysregulation was significantly associated with a higher risk of incident diverticulitis, implying that metabolic disturbance also plays a role in the development of gastrointestinal disorders. We additionally documented a positive association between metabolomic signature of metabolic dysfunction and diverticulitis recurrence and severity. This further supports the potential use of metabolomics, especially those related to metabolic pathways, as a risk prediction tool for future outcomes at the time of initial episode.

Multiple studies have detected increased levels and gene expressions of various pro-inflammatory proteins among patients with diverticulitis,38, 39, 40, 41, 42 suggesting a potential role of inflammation in diverticular disease development, though the role of chronic inflammation in uncomplicated diverticulosis remains controversial.43,44 It has also been found that two enzymes, cyclooxygenases-2 and 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase, have been deregulated in patients with acute diverticular disease. The resulting decrease in prostaglandin E2, the major colonic prostaglandin, was hypothesized to be associated with the onset of diverticular disease.45 On the other hand, patients with acute diverticulitis have displayed an overexpression of TNF-α.46 Our metabolomic signature of inflammation contained TNFRSF1B, a receptor of TNF-α,47 and showed a significant association with diverticulitis incidence and recurrence. Patients with severe diverticulitis had higher levels of inflammatory biomarkers including CRP and IL-6 compared to non-severe cases,48 while another prospective cohort study found associations between prediagnostic CRP and IL-6 and incident diverticulitis.15 These studies align with our findings supporting a role of chronic inflammation, potentially reflected by changes in metabolites, in the development of diverticulitis.

We found that l-tyrosine and l-valine are amongst the metabolites that are most positively correlated with the metabolic dysfunction signature. In a previous chronic toxicity study, increases in triglycerides, cholesterol, and phospholipids were found in rats with accumulation of l-tyrosine.49 In the colon, higher amounts of l-tyrosine lead to excessive production of p-cresol produced by the anaerobic microbiota in the colon, which is genotoxic to colonic epithelial cells.50 Accumulative l-valine modifies polyamine metabolism, retards cell growth, and stimulates apoptosis via multiple molecular pathways.51,52 On the other hand, glycine and l-asparagine are strongly negatively correlated with metabolic dysfunction signature. Essential as an antioxidant and immunomodulatory agent, glycine protects human intestinal epithelial cell lines from injuries and oxidative challenges.53 l-asparagine plays an important role in colonic cell proliferation, regulates the corticotropin-releasing factor signaling pathway, and thus attenuates intestinal barrier dysfunction from stress.54 Given the unrevealed mechanism of diverticulitis etiology, further investigation into the role of individual metabolites is warranted.

One of the main strengths of our study is that we derived metabolomic signatures based on integrated biomarker and metabolomic data, and we prospectively evaluated the association with the risk of diverticulitis. We evaluated incident diverticulitis, as well as the severity and recurrence of the disease. We applied our signature derivation to a larger population, improving the generalizability of our method. Other strengths of our study include the prospective cohort design that allows us to longitudinally collect detailed information about participants’ lifestyles and outcomes. The biennial questionnaires during follow-up reduce recall bias and measurement error.

The study also has several limitations. First, most of our study population was White and female. As a consequence, the generalizability of our results to men and other races and ethnicities remains to be validated. Second, we only included known metabolites in the signature derivation. Thus, the potential role of unannotated metabolites is unclear. The intended selection of metabolites based on their missingness in the sample population also raised such possibility of exclusion. Third, the metabolomic data were obtained from a single blood collection, restricting us from taking intra-subject variability into account. Fourth, physical measurements (BMI, waist circumference) and diverticulitis outcomes were self-reported in our studies. However, each has been validated and shows high correlations with measured values or medical records.6,33,55,56 In addition, any misclassification of the outcome would be likely to be non-differential and would have attenuated our model results towards the null hypothesis.57 Finally, information on clinical characteristics and treatment of diverticulitis is lacking, which might have influenced metabolomic profiles and recurrence of diverticulitis. Future studies examining the dynamic and resolution of diverticulitis, intervention after the first episode, and alteration in the gut microbiota and metabolic activity are needed.

In conclusion, we derived metabolomic signatures of metabolic dysfunction and inflammation and found significant associations between both signatures and the risk of diverticulitis incidence and recurrence. The metabolic dysfunction signature was additionally associated with an increased risk of complications requiring surgery. Certain individual metabolites that are highly predictive of the metabolic dysfunction signature may explain the association from a biological perspective. While further studies to explore the underlying mechanisms of diverticulitis pathogenesis are needed, our findings unveil the importance of metabolic disturbance in diverticulitis development and shed light on the etiology of diverticulitis. Our study also highlights metabolomics as a meaningful approach to risk stratification, predicting long-term outcomes of diverticulitis, and dietary and lifestyle intervention for diverticulitis prevention.
