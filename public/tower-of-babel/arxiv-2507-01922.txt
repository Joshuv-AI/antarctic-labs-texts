Efficient stochastic simulation of gene regulatory networks using hybrid models of transcriptional bursting
Mathilde Gaillard, Ulysse Herbach

Abstract




Abstract.

Single-cell data reveal the presence of biological stochasticity between cells of identical genome and environment, in particular highlighting the transcriptional bursting phenomenon. To account for this property, gene expression may be modeled as a continuous-time Markov chain where biochemical species are described in a discrete way, leading to Gillespie’s stochastic simulation algorithm (SSA) which turns out to be computationally expensive for realistic mRNA and protein copy numbers. Alternatively, hybrid models based on piecewise-deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs) offer an effective compromise for capturing cell-to-cell variability, but their simulation remains limited to specialized mathematical communities. With a view to making them more accessible, we present here a simple simulation method that is reminiscent of SSA, while allowing for much lower computational cost. We detail the algorithm for a bursty PDMP describing an arbitrary number of interacting genes, and prove that it simulates exact trajectories of the model. As an illustration, we use the algorithm to simulate a two-gene toggle switch: this example highlights the fact that bimodal distributions as observed in real data are not explained by transcriptional bursting per se, but rather by distinct burst frequencies that may emerge from interactions between genes.

Keywords:
Gene expression, Regulatory networks, Stochastic simulation, Piecewise-deterministic Markov processes, Single-cell transcriptomics

1
Introduction

A common simplification of the gene expression mechanism considers that a gene is transcribed into mRNA, which in turn is translated into proteins. With the advent of technologies that can measure this process at the level of individual cells and individual molecules, it has become clear that the first step is not as smooth as previously expected from bulk data
[
7
]
. In particular, mRNA synthesis typically occurs in bursts, i.e., short but intense periods of transcription during which many mRNA copies are produced
[
34
,
35
]
(corresponding to convoys of closely spaced polymerases
[
44
,
46
]
), interspersed with inactive periods: these transcriptional bursts generate intrinsic stochasticity
[
34
,
29
]
with functional implications for the cell
[
33
]
.

In the context of gene regulatory networks, this fundamental property should therefore not be ignored, especially when considering single-cell data
[
14
,
45
]
. It is worth noticing that single-cell RNA-seq data, which measures the expression of many genes simultaneously but is usually seen as very noisy, turns out to be largely consistent with much more precise techniques such as RT-ddPCR
[
1
]
and smFISH
[
34
,
42
]
. In previous work, we introduced a mechanistic approach to gene regulatory network inference
[
14
]
that was later implemented as a Python package
[
13
]
. This approach can be interpreted as calibration of a particular dynamical model describing an arbitrary number of interacting genes, formulated as a piecewise-deterministic Markov process (PDMP) driven by transcriptional bursting: once calibrated, the model can be used to simulate single-cell data and is able to reproduce the biological variability observed experimentally
[
45
]
.

Here we focus on exact simulation of this model. Indeed, while PDMPs are being used increasingly as a powerful hybrid framework for modeling various biological phenomena
[
37
]
, their simulation is not yet as widespread as their fully discrete counterpart, the well-known Doob–Gillespie algorithm, also known as stochastic simulation algorithm (SSA, see e.g.
[
40
]
for an introduction). The aim of this article is to present a simple algorithm capable of generating exact trajectories: it is reminiscent of SSA but introduces an acceptance-rejection step which, although sub-optimal in general, is straightforward to implement and considerably reduces the computational cost. As an underlying message, we argue that transcriptional bursting represents an ideal situation for this type of hybrid model, where abundant chemical species are described using ordinary differential equations (ODEs) corresponding to mass action kinetics, but affected by discrete events related to rare species.

The algorithm we propose is not new per se: it can be seen as a particular case of a more general framework for simulating PDMPs
[
21
]
. Nevertheless, the present paper is motivated by three main reasons.

First, we provide a particular instance that is dedicated to modeling gene regulatory networks, aiming at a wider adoption of this framework to simulate large networks efficiently, as an alternative to exact but inefficient SSA, or faster but approximate SSA variants. We emphasise that the bursty PDMP is the simplest model capable of correctly capturing the specific variability (namely, distribution tails) observed in current single-cell data.

Second, we adopt a simplified viewpoint compared to more theoretically oriented papers
[
3
,
21
]
, in order to highlight the feasibility and modularity of this framework in the context of systems biology. In particular, the proposed procedure can be easily extended beyond the two-stage mRNA-protein model, for example by distinguishing mature mRNA and pre-mRNA
[
36
]
.

Finally, we could not find a detailed proof of the algorithm in the literature, that is, a proof that the construction procedure simulates exact trajectories of the model. In more mathematical terms, one needs to show that the constructed Markov process actually has the correct infinitesimal generator.
The procedure is similar to the more common continuous-time Markov chains but needs a specific treatment, due to the fact that the state of the system between jumps is not constant. We therefore provide a proof here for completeness.

Section

2
we introduce the model, and then detail the simulation algorithm in
Section

3
. The interest of this model is discussed in
Section

4
, and a proof of the algorithm is given in
Section

5
.

2
Mathematical model

In the following, we consider a network of
n
genes possibly in interaction. We start with the well-known ‘two-state model’ of gene expression, which corresponds to the following set of elementary chemical reactions for each gene
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}G_{i}\xrightleftharpoons[k_{\text{off},i}]{k_{\text{on},i}}{G_{i}}^{*},&{G_{i}}^{*}\xrightarrow{s_{0,i}}{G_{i}}^{*}+M_{i},&M_{i}\xrightarrow{d_{0,i}}\varnothing,\\
&M_{i}\xrightarrow{s_{1,i}}M_{i}+P_{i},&P_{i}\xrightarrow{d_{1,i}}\varnothing,\end{array}

(1)

where
G_{i}
,
{G_{i}}^{*}
,
M_{i}
and
P_{i}
denote inactive promoter (off), active promoter (on), mRNA and protein for gene
i
.
These reactions describe the main two stages of gene expression, namely
transcription
(rate
s_{0,i}
) and
translation
(rate
s_{1,i}
), along with degradation of mRNA (rate
d_{0,i}
) and protein (rate
d_{1,i}
) molecules. Note that
G_{i}
and
{G_{i}}^{*}
satisfy a conservation relation and only one molecule of
G_{i}
or
{G_{i}}^{*}
is present at a time: for convenience we define
E_{i}\in\{0,1\}
based on
{G_{i}}^{*}
.
The particularity of the two-state model is that transcription can only occur when the promoter is active, i.e.,
E_{i}=1
(
Fig.

1
A). The bursty regime corresponds to
k_{\text{off},i}\gg k_{\text{on},i}
, meaning that promoter active periods are short compared to inactive periods (with
s_{0,i}\gg d_{0,i}
such that
k_{\text{on},i}s_{0,i}/(k_{\text{off},i}d_{0,i})
is fixed). In the limiting case, active periods become infinitely short so the promoter state is not needed anymore and the gene activity is characterized by its
burst frequency

k_{\text{on},i}
(
Fig.

1
B).

Although regulation can take different forms
[
27
,
38
]
, modulation of burst frequency appears as a fundamental mechanism for possible interactions between genes
[
20
,
30
,
46
,
17
]
. We therefore assume that
k_{\text{off},i}
is constant, while
k_{\text{on},i}
is a function of proteins levels
P=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
. In our paradigm, the burst themselves are thus not coordinated directly (which would be relevant for genes in the same genomic location
[
34
]
), but interactions arise through functions
k_{\text{on},i}
which introduce correlation of burst frequencies.
For example, in
[
13
,
45
]
these functions take the following form:

k_{\text{on},i}(P)=\frac{k_{0,i}+k_{1,i}\exp(\beta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{ji}P_{j})}{1+\exp(\beta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{ji}P_{j})}\qquad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

with
k_{0,i}\ll k_{1,i}
so that
\beta_{i}
encodes basal activity of gene
i
and
(\theta_{ij})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}
can be interpreted as a network interaction matrix.

It is also similar to that of

In the next section, we focus on exact simulation of model (
3
) as the simplest compromise for describing the transcriptional bursting property (
Fig.

). For mathematical completeness and with a view to proving the algorithm in

, we mention that the model is characterized by its infinitesimal generator:

\hskip-5.69054pt\begin{array}[]{rll}Lf(y,z)&\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-d_{0,i}y_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}f(y,z)+(s_{1,i}y_{i}-d_{1,i}z_{i})\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}f(y,z)\right.&\\
&\qquad\displaystyle+\left.k_{\text{on},i}(z)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f(y+he_{i},z)-f(y,z))b_{i}e^{-b_{i}h}\mathrm{d}h\right]\end{array}

(4)

acting on smooth test functions

, where
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
and
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
respectively correspond to values of
M(t)=(M_{1}(t),\dots,M_{n}(t))
and
P(t)=(P_{1}(t),\dots,P_{n}(t))

.
See
[

Notably, this form highlights the fact that all genes follow the same individual dynamical model with no direct coupling between bursts, but rather frequency-modulated interactions coming from the dependence of burst frequency
k_{\text{on},i}(z)
on possibly all proteins
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
.

3
Simulation algorithm

In this section, we present a simple algorithm to simulate exact trajectories from the bursty PDMP model (
3
) with corresponding infinitesimal generator (
4
). Here the process is denoted by
(Y(t),Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
where
Y(t)
is the vector of mRNA levels and
Z(t)
is the vector of protein levels.
Formally, the dynamics of this PDMP can be summarized as follows. Given a starting point, the process is driven by a deterministic flow until the first jump time
T_{1}
. Let
\varphi
denote the flow related to the ODE system (
3
). Namely, starting from
(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
, the solution at time
t\geqslant 0
is

\varphi(y,z,t)=(\varphi_{\text{M}}(y,z,t),\varphi_{\text{P}}(y,z,t))\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}

(5)

where for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} {}_{i}&=e^{-d_{0,i}t}y_{i}\\
[\varphi_{\text{P}}(y,z,t)]_{i}&=e^{-d_{1,i}t}z_{i}+\big(e^{-d_{1,i}t}-e^{-d_{0,i}t}\big)\frac{s_{1,i}}{d_{0,i}-d_{1,i}}y_{i}\end{aligned}\right.

gives the solution for the mRNA component
y
and the protein component
z
(note that we consider
d_{0,i}\neq d_{1,i}
, as derived from the biologically relevant regime
d_{0,i}>d_{1,i}

[
41
]
, but the special case
d_{0,i}=d_{1,i}
is also analytically solvable).
The distribution of
T_{1}
is characterized by

\mathbb{P}_{y,z}(T_{1}>t)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{\text{on},i}(\varphi_{\text{P}}(y,z,\tau))\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

(6)

where
\mathbb{P}_{y,z}
denotes the probability conditionally to
\{Y(0)=y,Z(0)=z\}
. A new (random) position for the process is drawn, according to the current one. The motion then restarts from this new point, exactly as before.

Presented this way, the dynamics seems straightforward. However, trying to directly simulate the next waiting time according to (
6
) requires numerical integration, as the corresponding distribution generally does not have a closed form: the algorithm presented here is a natural solution to bypass such integration step. The main underlying assumption is that
k_{\text{on},i}
is continuous and bounded for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, which is biologically relevant (note that the bounding assumption is consistent, since the bursty regime already assumes
k_{\text{on},i}\ll k_{\text{off},i}
). We can therefore consider a fixed parameter
\lambda
such that

\lambda\geqslant\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{\text{on},i}(z)\Big\}

(7)

and that will serve as a global
“exponential clock”
for computing jump times. From a computational perspective, the optimal value for lambda is the lowest possible one, which might not be available analytically. In practice, one can simply use
\lambda=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}
where
k_{\text{on},i}(z)\leqslant\lambda_{i}
are individual bounds. The interest of the simulation method also lies in the fact that the ODE system in (
3
) can be solved analytically.

Let
t\geqslant 0
be a fixed time. In order to obtain a realisation of a (random) cell state
(Y(t),Z(t))
, we first need to simulate every random event that occurred before time
t
. The bounding assumption allows us to simulate event occurrences according to a homogeneous Poisson process with intensity
\lambda
and reject some jumps, as in the classical
“thinning”
method for simulating non-homogeneous Poisson processes. Given an initial state, a simulation step consists in drawing a waiting time
U
, apply the deterministic flow during a time
U
and then decide which type of jump occurs then according to the current position of the process: a
“phantom”
or a
“true”
jump. If it is a phantom jump, the process stays at the same position. On the other hand, if it is a true jump, a new random position is chosen. Namely, we select which gene is impacted by a jump, according to the probability distribution
\mathcal{P}(z)
on
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
defined by

p_{i}(z)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{\text{on},i}(z)&\text{if $i=0$}\\
\displaystyle\frac{k_{\text{on},i}(z)}{\lambda}&\text{if $1\leqslant i\leqslant n$}\end{cases}

(8)

and add an exponential random variable of parameter
b_{i}
to the
i
-th component of the mRNA vector
Y(t)
. The simulation step is iterated with this new starting point. When the simulation time is beyond
t
,
(Y(t),Z(t))
follows only the flow applied from the last jump until
t
.

This algorithm is computationally efficient since for the same final time required by the user, it requires the generation of much fewer random variables than the SSA model. Although a proper benchmark is out of scope, we mention that for a particular instance of realistic parameter values, the PDMP simulation was found to be approximately 200 times faster than SSA
[
14
]
.
Besides, it is also efficient among other PDMP simulation algorithms, as it does not use the distribution (
6
) to simulate inter-arrival times: this would require numerically integrating the jump rate along trajectories, invert the cumulative distribution function and evaluate the inverse at a uniform random variable
[
47
]
.

In some circumstances, computational cost can still be improved. For example, we used the bound
\lambda
over
\sum_{i}k_{\text{on},i}
, but a bound over
\sum_{i}k_{\text{on},i}\circ\varphi_{\text{P}}
may be sharper, making it possible to reduce the number of phantom jumps. Note that the flow (
5

[
13
]
.
Furthermore, we made a distinction here between
Y(t)
, which is impacted by jumps, and
Z(t)
, which is involved in interactions, but
Algorithm

1
can be directly adapted to cases with more variables and other role combinations.

4
Discussion

Contrary to the usual SSA, which becomes costly when simulating single-cell gene expression data
[
5
]
, the PDMP model presented here is much faster to simulate (see, e.g., a factor of 200 in
[
14
]
). Mathematically, it can be justified as a rigorous hybrid limit of the discrete formalism when mRNA and protein levels span an important range
[
8
,
9
,
6
]
, consistently with biological observations (see
Fig.

2
and
[
41
,
42
,
1
]
).
We also mention another interesting link: using the formalism of Poisson representation
[
11
]
, it is possible to show that in absence of interactions, the discrete and hybrid models are in fact related by an exact intertwining relation, with no need for considering a limit regime. Namely, the distribution from the SSA model can then be written as a mixture of Poisson distributions, whose time-dependent mixing distribution is that of the PDMP counterpart
[
16
,
12
]
. This type of result is of major interest when distinguishing measurement and expression models for single-cell data analysis
[
39
]
.

Considering the two-state PDMP model, it is possible to adapt the algorithm presented here to this case, without changing the computational cost. However, we emphasize that only the bursty regime is observed in practice. Note that there is also a basic,
“Poissonian”
mode of expression
[
48
]
but it is described by the
“1 state”
model (not 2) with in this case the PDMP being trivial (linear input/output ODE). Basically, the biological phenomenon of transcriptional bursting can be described correctly with the bursty model, not the 2-state model, except when precisely in the bursty regime.

Switching from 2-state PDMP to the bursty PDMP has two advantages: (1) we remove the description of the promoter, which frees up computational memory space, especially when simulating complete trajectories for many cells; (2) we force the correct biological regime, preventing users from accidentally simulating the unrealistic
“saturated”
regime (
k_{\text{off},i}<d_{0,i}
, inducing a peak of mRNA at
s_{0,i}/d_{0,i}
with incorrect tail). In particular, we aggregate the parameters
k_{\text{off},i}
and
s_{0,i}
, which are not identifiable in any case in the bursty regime.

Considering the simulation of PDMP models, previous authors
[
47
]
proposed a natural algorithm where the waiting time before the next jump is obtained by numerical integration to calculate the survival function of the waiting time, followed by inversion of this function (combined with uniform random variable). We prefer the acceptance-rejection approach because: (1) we are guaranteed exact simulations (unlike numerical integration, no time discretisation is required), and (2) it is easy to code. It is also potentially much faster, since the biologically interesting cases are precisely those where numerical integration is difficult (i.e. requires fine discretisation) because the
k_{\text{on}}
function is
“steep”
at certain points (i.e., large Lipschitz constant).

As an illustration, we show in
Fig.

4
an example of basic toggle switch network, which generates a bistable pattern emerging from interactions.
We believe this example is interesting because a new
“two-state”
layer appears, which seems to be consistent with previous diffusion modeling
[
15
]
while the authors were interpreting the two states as promoter states, possibly from a confusion of time scales and the fact that they had no single-cell data. The interpretation here is that bursts are transcriptional, with proteins tending to buffer these bursts, making the pattern robust.

22
]
: this model can still be rigorously justified as a limit of very stable proteins (i.e., small degradation rate) compared to mRNA, but one must be careful about its interpretation, as it is often stated that in this regime, only 0 or 1 mRNA molecule can be present at a time (so-called
“translational bursts”
), which is contradicted by gene expression data showing many mRNA copies at a time.

Another efficient option is to use a diffusion model
[
32
]
, but then there is no biological foundation and in practice such models do not match the bursty model correctly
[
22
]
and need extra
“ad hoc”
noise in order to match real data.

Some more precise data indicate that the
“off”
time, i.e. waiting time between bursts, is sometimes non-exponential
[
43
]
, but
“on”
time is well described by exponential: the bursty regime would correspond to a non-exponential waiting time between two bursts, which would be a natural extension of the algorithm presented here.

Finally, thanks to its efficiency, this model can be implemented as an internal component of agent-based frameworks describing a population of spatially interacting cells
[
26
]
, as already done for example within the Simuscale software
[
28
]
.

5
Proof

In this section we present an explicit construction of the process corresponding to
Algorithm

1
and illustrated in
Fig.

3
. We then prove that the infinitesimal generator of the resulting process is that of the PDMP model (
4
). This is sufficient to conclude that obtained trajectories are exact. The reader can refer to
[
2
]
for additional details on the underling theory.

In all this section we consider the space of real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
that are continuous and vanish at infinity, equipped with the uniform norm. Namely,

\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}|f(y,z)|.

Algorithm

1
leads to the following construction. We first define a discrete-time Markov chain
(Y_{k},Z_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
which corresponds to the right limit of the process
(Y(t),Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
at specific (random) time points. These time points are given by a homogeneous Poisson process
(N(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
with intensity
\lambda
. Let
(T_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
denote its jump time sequence and
(U_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
its inter-arrival times. Let
Y_{0}
and
Z_{0}
be random variables independent of
(N(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. For
k\geqslant 0
, we recursively define

\left\{\begin{aligned} Y_{k}&=\varphi_{\text{M}}(Y_{k-1},Z_{k-1},U_{k})+S_{k}\\
Z_{k}&=\varphi_{\text{P}}(Y_{k-1},Z_{k-1},U_{k})\end{aligned}\right.

where
S_{k}
is a random vector constructed as follows: at each step
k
, we draw a random index
I\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
according to the probability distribution
\mathcal{P}(Z_{k})
defined at (
8
). When
I\neq 0
, we consider an additional variable
E
from exponential distrobution
\mathcal{E}(b_{I})
. We set

S_{k}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $I=0$}\\
Ee_{i}&\text{if $1\leqslant i\leqslant n$}\end{cases}

where
e_{i}
is the
i
-th vector of the canonical base of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Finally, we define
(Y(t),Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
for every
t\geqslant 0
by applying the flow between jump times. Namely,

\forall t\in[T_{k},T_{k+1}),\quad(Y(t),Z(t))=\varphi(y_{k-1},z_{k-1},t-T_{k}).

The following result states that the constructed process
(Y(t),Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
corresponds to the bursty model.

Theorem 1
.

The infinitesimal generator of the semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
induced by
(Y(t),Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is the operator defined at (
4
). Moreover the semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is strongly continuous.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{E}_{y,z}
denote the conditional expectation given initial data
\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\,|Y_{0}=y,Z_{0}=z]
. The process
(Y(t),Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is characterized by its semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
where for every
t\geqslant 0
the operator
T(t)
is defined by

T(t)f(y,z)=\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[f(Y(t),Z(t))\right].

The infinitesimal generator of
(T(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is defined by

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{T(t)f-f}{t},

for
f
such that the limit exists. To calculate this limit, we first obtain an explicit form for
(T(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. In order to do that we first need the transition operator
P
of the Markov chain
(Y_{k},Z_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
.
Let us define the operators
J
and
K_{t}
by

Jf(y,z)=f(y,z)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{\text{on},i}(z)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f(y+he_{i},z)-f(y,z))b_{i}e^{-b_{i}h}\mathrm{d}h

and

K_{t}f(y,z)=f(\varphi(y,z,t)).

By definition, the transition operator
P
of
(Y_{k},Z_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
is

Pf(y,z)=\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[f(Y_{1},Z_{1})\right].

Using the recursive definition of
Y_{1}
and
Z_{1}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[f(Y_{1},Z_{1})|U_{1}=u\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[f(\varphi_{\text{M}}(y,z,u)+S_{1},\varphi_{\text{P}}(y,z,u))\right]

\displaystyle=K_{u}Jf(y,z).

Thus the operator
P
is given by

Pf(y,z)=\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[K_{U_{1}}Jf(y,z)\right]=\int_{0}^{+\infty}K_{s}Jf(y,z)\lambda e^{-\lambda s}\mathrm{d}s.

Thanks to the previous construction, we have

T(t)f=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{N(t)=n\}}K_{U_{1}}J\circ\cdots\circ K_{U_{n}}J\circ K_{t-T_{n}}f\right].

(9)

Indeed, conditionally to having
n
events of the underlying Poisson process
(N(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
,
f(Y(t),Z(t))
is only the flow operator applied from the last jump until
t
. Namely

\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[f(Y(t),Z(t))|N(t)=n\right]=\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[K_{t-T_{n}}f(Y_{n},Z_{n})\right].

Furthermore, for every
k\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[f(Y_{k},Z_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[Pf(Y_{k-1},Z_{k-1})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y,z}\left[K_{U_{k-1}}Jf(Y_{k-1},Z_{k-1})\right].

Integrating this relation, backwards from the
n
-th jump to 0, leads to the formula at (
9
). Let us now calculate the infinitesimal generator of
(T(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. Note that jumps (including phantom ones) arrived according to the Poisson process
(N(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. Thus the probability of having two jumps in the interval
[0,t]
decreases faster than
t
as
t\rightarrow 0
. This is why we isolate the first two terms in (
9
) and get

\displaystyle T(t)f

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda t}K_{t}f+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{U_{1}\leqslant t}\mathds{1}_{U_{2}\geqslant t-U_{1}}K_{U_{1}}JK_{t-U_{1}}f\right]+R(t,f)

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda t}K_{t}f+\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}K_{u}JK_{t-u}\mathrm{d}u+R(t,f).

(10)

As stated,

\|R(t,f)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}(N(t)>1)=\|f\|_{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda t}(1+\lambda t))

so

\frac{\|R(t,f)\|_{\infty}}{t}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{when}\quad t\rightarrow 0.

We then use Taylor expansion on the term
e^{-\lambda t}
and simplify to get

\frac{T(t)f-f}{t}=\frac{K_{t}f-f}{t}-\lambda K_{t}f+\frac{\lambda}{t}\int_{0}^{t}K_{u}JK_{t-u}f\mathrm{d}u+o(1).

To conclude the proof, we need to show that

\frac{K_{t}f-f}{t}\to\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle F_{i},\nabla_{i}f\rangle,\quad K_{t}f\to f,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}K_{u}JK_{t-u}f\mathrm{d}u\to Jf

uniformly when
t\rightarrow 0
. We are exactly in the setting of
[
2
, Chapter 16, assumptions page 253]
. The convergence of the first term (usually called Koopman semigroup) is done at
[
2
, pages 255-256]
. The two other convergences can be proved using the same two ingredients: the uniform continuity of
f
and the Lipschitz continuity of the flow in its first variable. The uniform continuity of
f
is immediate given the chosen space of test functions, and the property on the flow is a consequence of the vector field regularity.
Finally, after simplification, we obtain

\frac{T(t)f-f}{t}\to Lf,

where
L
is the operator defined at (
4
). In order to show that
(T(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is strongly continuous, it is sufficient to show that for every
t\geqslant 0
and for every function
f
,
T(t)f
is continuous and that
\|T(t)f-f\|
tends to 0 as
t\rightarrow 0
. The regularity of the flow
\varphi
to the initial condition gives the continuity of
Kf
. Combined with the continuity of
Jf
, we obtain the continuity of
T(t)f
. The second part is the result of (
10
) and the fact that
K_{t}f\rightarrow f
uniformly when
t\rightarrow 0
. Note that the strong continuity of the semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
implies that it is characterized by its infinitesimal generator
L
(see
[
2
, page 122]
).
∎
