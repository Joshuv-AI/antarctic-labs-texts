Advance Care Planning in structurally vulnerable populations: A scoping review of barriers, facilitators, and interventions
Authors: Andrew Jeong, Brian C. M. Li, Rachel Z. Carter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Individuals experiencing structural vulnerabilities experience greater rates of serious illness and face barriers to accessing healthcare, including advance care planning. However, evidence to understand relevant barriers, facilitators, and effective interventions for advance care planning engagement among different structurally vulnerable populations remains fragmented.Aim:To inform advance care planning interventions for structurally vulnerable patients by reviewing: barriers and facilitators to advance care planning; and advance care planning interventions.Design:Scoping review.Data sources:We searched MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, and PsycINFO for studies published between January 2003 and October 2024. Eligible studies examined advance care planning for those experiencing housing instability, poverty, racialization of Black or Indigenous race, serious mental illness, or substance use disorders.Results:From 70 included articles, 13 barriers, 13 facilitators, and 6 targets of intervention were identified. Barriers and facilitators were commonly experienced between multiple structurally vulnerable populations. The most common barriers include low advance care planning literacy, discomfort with conversations around mortality, personal, cultural and/or spiritual beliefs, and familial and social isolation. Common facilitators include trusted provider relationships, provider training and empowerment, social support, and tailored advance care planning content and delivery. We identified 27 interventions, most commonly involving advance care planning education and outreach or guided advance care planning.Conclusions:Structurally vulnerable patients share many barriers and facilitators to advance care planning, which can inform interventions for underserved populations. Interventions targeting advance care planning education and outreach or guided advance care planning show promise if providers can incorporate advance care planning facilitators.

Introduction

Advance Care Planning is an important aspect in the provision of patient-centered care for people with serious illness that includes preparation for both in-the-moment and advance decisions. 1 This requires individuals understanding and sharing their goals, values and preferences for future medical care.2,3 What this looks like, and who’s involved, shifts throughout the life course as individuals face changes in health status and different types of decisions, but is embedded in the concept of preparing people for future decision-making that allows for the consideration of what quality of life means to the person at that time.1,4 It can also include how decisions would be made if an individual is not capable of making them, through identification and preparation of substitute decision maker(s). 2 Healthcare providers can facilitate the process through conversations such as Serious Illness Conversations 5 and Goals of Care conversations. 6 While systematic reviews have revealed mixed findings,7–9 results for outcomes such as behavior change, treatment preference congruence, satisfaction with communication, decisional conflict, and patient mental health were predominantly positive. 9

People experiencing structural vulnerabilities, defined as a disadvantaged position in societal power hierarchies, 10 face greater rates of life-limiting illness as well as barriers to healthcare access and advance care planning. 11 One example that illustrates this is that ill-housed patients have greater rates of emergency department utilization leading to in-hospital death.12,13 While numerous factors contribute to this outcome, advance care planning represents an opportunity for many structurally vulnerable patients to decide an alternative palliative care trajectory. By empowering marginalized groups to receive high-quality goal-concordant care, defined as care that aligns with patients’ values and expressed preferences, advance care planning can prevent harms from goal-discordant care. 14 This includes resource-intensive and invasive hospital-based interventions of potentially minimal benefit at time of death. Advance care planning conversations can also help patients transition to a palliative care approach and access specialized services such as hospice, instead of otherwise facing end-of-life in hostels, recovery-focused shelters, and other facilities. While these sites may provide familiarity, they may also be insufficiently resourced or have inflexible policies to adequately provide person-centered care at end of life. 12 Pursuing a “good death” and dignified end-of-life care has been shown to still be important for patients with structural vulnerabilities, even if they have other pressing concerns such as survival and shelter. 15 Research supporting advance care planning for structurally vulnerable populations has therefore been of particular interest to mitigate historical discrimination, provide greater quality care, and better leverage health resources. 16

An intersectional understanding of these disadvantages shows that they are often compounded on top of one another in a dynamic fashion that creates uniquely vulnerable states, affecting access to healthcare and social resources, including engagement in advance care planning.10,17 Major examples of compounded disadvantages include the racialization and poorer healthcare outcomes of Black and Indigenous populations. With a history of receiving unethical medical practices and ongoing disproportionately poor healthcare, Black populations often have systemic medical mistrust and face discriminatory racialization as well as adverse health consequences.18,19 Indigenous populations also face health disparities, ranging from systemic unavailability of culturally safe services, to individual-level interactions with racism in healthcare, which also contributes to medical mistrust and healthcare reluctance. 20 This development and reinforcement of medical mistrust by racism and other structural inequalities is well-documented, and it is known to further widen health disparities. 21 Improving advance care planning for structurally vulnerable populations requires interventions that address this intersectional experience.

There are several reviews that examine palliative care and advance care planning for disadvantaged populations, such as patients who are ill-housed,22–28 racialized,29–32 Indigenous, 33 experiencing poverty or low socioeconomic status, 34 or have serious mental illness.35,36 However, existing literature is largely fragmented with respect these structural vulnerabilities, even though individuals within these populations commonly experience more than one structural vulnerability, which further compounds the effect of disadvantages and health inequity. 37 One article does address this broader approach, 38 examining experiences of advance care planning interventions for several vulnerable populations, and stresses the importance of personalized communication. However, their scope is limited to qualitative data and does not examine provider perspectives. 38 In this paper, we define providers as any member of the patient’s care team who may be involved in advance care planning discussions, which includes healthcare workers but also can include other roles such as community case managers. A holistic approach to advance care planning interventions, as examined in this paper, requires consideration of patient, systemic, and provider-level factors.

Therefore, in this scoping review, we aim to understand factors affecting advance care planning engagement across a broad range of structurally vulnerable populations (barriers and facilitators), as well as to identify the evidence base of interventions developed for these populations, including how the interventions target these factors. By examining factors and interventions in multiple structurally vulnerable contexts (as opposed to individually), we hope to identify similarities and differences between these populations. The understanding of inter-group generalizability and differences will be valuable in the development and implementation of interventions targeting these populations. For example, identification of shared experiences within multiple structurally vulnerable populations may indicate that a heterogeneous population would be more responsive to community-level interventions targeting those common factors. We shall also identify the common interventional targets for these populations, and their relationships to the identified barriers and facilitators. Through this we hope to identify commonalities between populations, and which targets may have gaps. This information may inform further research in this area, and support the selection, implementation and development of interventions seeking to improve advance care planning engagement among heterogeneous structurally vulnerable populations.

Methods

Study design

We selected scoping review methodology as it allows us to: identify and map available evidence on a topic; identify key factors relating to a concept; and identify and analyze knowledge gaps. 39 The included evidence base can be heterogeneous with respect to study type, structural vulnerability context, and target of study (patients, providers, caregivers, etc.). This holistic approach supports the widely encompassing nature of our study. Therefore, we followed the Scoping Reviews chapter of the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis. 39

Study team

The study team included one white female academic researcher (RC), and two Asian male physicians (AJ, BL). None of the team identify with the structural vulnerabilities examined in this study. The authors recognize the potential for biased colonial perspectives within this study without lived experience, and have attempted to the best of our ability to mitigate this via consensus-based decision making and seeking advice from an expert advisory committee.

Eligibility criteria & search strategy

The structurally vulnerable populations we identified for inclusion in this review were: homelessness and housing instability (ill-housed); substance use and addiction; poverty; severe mental illness; and racialized groups with historic and systemic reasons to mistrust the medical system, including Black/African-American and Indigenous (North American, and also Australian Aboriginals and New Zealand Māori, who have similar historical colonization contexts).

This review was conducted as part of establishing an equitable palliative care research program within Providence Health Care, located in Vancouver, British Columbia. As such, vulnerabilities were selected based on prevalence within urban centers in high-income countries, such as the Downtown Eastside of Vancouver, with input from an expert advisory committee with experience working with structurally vulnerable individuals in palliative care. The vulnerabilities are also associated with increased rates of serious illness.40–44 As such, we deemed it appropriate to exclude rural and incarcerated contexts due to the different experiences and resources in these locations.

We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, and PsycINFO for studies published in English between January 2003 and 7 October 2024. This approximately 20-year period was chosen to capture historical trends in research on this topic. Search terms for [“vulnerable population,” “poverty,” “unhoused,” “racialized,” “mental health,” “substance use”], and [“advance care planning,” “serious illness conversation,” and “goals of care”] were used. Our full search strategy for MEDLINE is provided in Supplemental Table I.

Study selection

Titles/abstracts and full texts were screened using Covidence, a web-based screening and data extraction tool for conducting reviews. Screening involved independent assessment by two of three reviewers (AJ, BL, RC) according to inclusion and exclusion criteria determined according to the PICOS framework, detailed in Table 1. Any discrepancies in screening were discussed amongst all three reviewers to reach unanimous agreement.

Data items

Two reviewers (AJ, BL) used a standardized extraction template (Supplemental Table II) to extract data from full texts into a Microsoft Excel Spreadsheet. 45 Data was extracted by one reviewer, and confirmed by the second, with any discrepancies resolved by discussion among the two reviewers. Extracted data included: study and publication details; study design and characteristics; participant characteristics; study intervention (where applicable); results pertaining to barriers, facilitators or interventions for advance care planning; and conclusions from the study pertaining to advance care planning.

Data analysis

To support the interpretation of the extracted data, we conducted basic content analysis to categorize the barriers, facilitators and intent and scope of interventions.39,45 Two reviewers (AJ, BL) coded the extracted data by consensus. Barriers and facilitators were identified and descriptive codes added to group them. Interventional studies were coded based on the intent and scope of the intervention. The reviewers worked together iteratively to review the codes, to remove redundancy, and subsequently categorize as patient, systemic or provider related.

Conceptual mapping of the barriers and facilitators to create a graphic representation was conducted by RC through iterative review of the extracted data and synthesized tables reported in this review, with reference back to the studies on occasion. The conceptual map was reviewed by AJ, and any discrepancies discussed to reach consensus.

We used the 2020 PRISMA-Scr statement to guide reporting. 46

Included Studies.

USA: United States of America, UK: United Kingdom, SES: socio-economic status.

While most studies included only one structural vulnerability, eight studies explicitly included multiple vulnerabilities. The vulnerability included in the most studies was Black racialization (n = 25), followed by housing instability (n = 20), poverty (n = 19), Indigenous racialization (n = 8), Serious Mental Illness (n = 5), and Substance use (n = 5). Almost all the studies (n = 65) were from the USA. The remainder were from New Zealand (n = 2), Canada (n = 1), the Netherlands (n = 1), and the UK (n = 1). Study designs included qualitative interviews, case studies, cohort studies, and randomized controlled trials. Many studies explicitly outlined advance care planning in their objectives, but numerous others had relevant findings despite having broader objectives such as exploring end-of-life experiences and palliative care. Relevant findings from full-text review were divided into barriers, facilitators, and interventional targets for advance care planning, which were each further subdivided into patient, systemic and provider factors. These factors were categorized according to how an intervention for each factor would be carried out. For example, any factor relating to an interpersonal relationship between patient and provider would be intervened on through the provider considering their responsibility to the patient, thus would be categorized under provider-related factors.

Barriers

Forty-four studies contained barriers to advance care planning, from which we identified 13 distinct categories, with patient barriers being the most numerous (Table 3 – superscript numbers indicate study citations). Low advance care planning literacy was identified as a patient barrier amongst all populations examined. This included general health literacy 47 as well as advance care planning specific literacy, such as low ACP awareness, 68 confusion around terminology, 68 not understanding the medico-legal significance of documenting advance care planning in writing, 59 and not understanding how to designate a healthcare proxy. 64 Diverse personal beliefs were also identified as patient barriers. These ranged from cultural discomfort discussing death,58,65 religious beliefs to leave it in “God’s hands,”78,98,100 and personal beliefs. For example, in one qualitative study, ill-housed adults described how discussing end of life can create “negative energy” and cause “undesirable life events.” 82 General discomfort about end-of-life conversations was common among all populations examined. Negative experiences witnessing others’ end-of-life care exacerbated fears with advance care planning engagement. This included experiences witnessing denial of nutrition and spiritual leadership involvement in Black interview participants, 51 and witnessing insensitive discussions about end-of-life topics in ill-housed interview participants. 82 Lack of a suitable healthcare proxy was also common, 55 but patients with family members also experienced barriers. For example, they would defer to family decisions instead of engaging with advance care planning, or have hesitant decision-making due to family members’ conflicting opinions.86,100

Systemic and provider-related barriers were also identified. Medical mistrust was a common barrier for almost all groups, with some ill-housed patients seeing advance care planning as not worth engaging in as their wishes would not be respected. 94 Patients also had significant resource limitations preventing them from engaging with advance care planning, including lacking food and shelter, or numerous other medical appointments. 94 Limited time for advance care planning was also shared from a provider perspective, especially if the outcome was considered “unlikely to be used by hospitals” due to poor transfer of documents across the healthcare system. 75 Providers also stated barriers in emotional burnout and low confidence in advance care planning with marginalized populations, citing fears of lacking cultural knowledge or speaking inappropriately about end-of-life issues.93,102

Facilitators

We found facilitators to advance care planning within 33 studies, from which we identified 13 distinct categories (Table 4). Recognizing advance care planning as a tool for autonomy was a facilitator, such as for patients who knew their healthcare wishes or who knew their wishes for after death.88,106 Several positive patient attitudes facilitated advance care planning, such as feeling that advance care planning is important to complete or being open to documenting end-of-life wishes.61,110 Patients also had various facilitative experiences and perspectives toward mortality. These included prior ICU admissions, 81 desires for a peaceful death, 61 and motivation from prior conflicts in family members’ end-of-life care. 69 Spiritual beliefs were found to be facilitators instead of barriers in some contexts, such as advance care planning helping overcome fears of death. 58 Relationships were also potential facilitators, such as if patients had families that “talks about problems” or even became friends with supportive housing staff.88,94

Discussion

Main findings

This scoping review examined advance care planning in structurally vulnerable populations to explore barriers, facilitators, and targets for intervention. Advance care planning interventions aiming to better support vulnerable populations follow a range of approaches, which our review followed by dividing our analysis into patient, systemic, and provider factors. Divisions between these categories or factors are not very well-defined, although it is clear that a comprehensive approach is needed for structurally vulnerable populations.38,117 For example, medical mistrust can originate from both personal and historical experiences, and interventions may have several different approaches to address mistrust. In addition, medical mistrust could lead to disengagement with the healthcare system and lead to lower health and advance care planning literacy. Nonetheless, the categorized dataset provides a scoping overview in a delineated fashion between different structurally vulnerable groups, which allows for some broader analysis.

Barriers and facilitators were often conceptually related to each other in various ways and combinations. Depending on the context and permutability of these factors for an individual, this suggests that these barriers could potentially be intervenable to instead act as facilitators (See Supplemental Figure I). While most patient-level facilitators align with patient-level barriers, and system-level facilitators predominantly address system-level barriers, provider-level facilitators have the potential to address all levels of barriers.

Barriers and facilitators were commonly shared among multiple structurally vulnerable populations. Low advance care planning literacy, discomfort with conversations around mortality, trusted provider relationships, and provider training and empowerment were important factors in all vulnerability contexts examined. Several others were present among four to five of the six vulnerable contexts examined. The presence of shared experiences and attitudes is consistent with previous literature, which suggests that positionality within social and structural hierarchies shapes healthcare experiences at end-of-life. 10 A trusted relationship between patient and provider that enables individualized understanding of patient needs around advance care planning appears to be important to address many of the barriers and facilitators we identified.

This positionality results in cumulative disadvantages experienced throughout the life course, such as through systemic stressors like healthcare coverage and public benefit gaps, which are known to limit advance care planning. 78 Notably, these commonalities must be interpreted with consideration to the groups or individuals to which they apply, as they originate from distinctly different and non-monolithic structural vulnerability contexts that shape their experiences further beyond what can be described in this scoping review. Although strong conclusions cannot be drawn about inter-group differences as they could simply be due to lack of evidence, some differences could be rationalized in the context of the vulnerability. For example, individuals experiencing poverty more often reported barriers stemming from family relationships compared to ill-housed individuals who were often socially isolated.

Serious mental illness and substance use disorders were relatively underrepresented in our review, with five studies each. As such there were knowledge gaps in understanding multi-level barriers and facilitators for these groups. Notably, the most predominant barrier for people living with serious mental illness was a discomfort with conversations around mortality, which could be in part due to low insight or a disconnect between recognition of their psychiatric illness and other illnesses. Several of the barriers and facilitators that were identified in most or all other groups but not identified in these understudied populations may be applicable to these populations. For instance, personal, cultural and/or spiritual beliefs were not identified as a barrier for those experiencing serious mental illness, but it seems reasonable to hypothesize that this barrier may also apply to members of this population. This represents a gap in current research for these understudied populations, which is important to address considering that issues around (perceived) capacity and decision-making are common issues for patients with these structural vulnerabilities.

All structurally vulnerable patient groups examined had studies reporting advance care planning interventions for their population. With few exceptions,48,62,66,68,72 most interventions seemed to have positive effects on advance care planning engagement and documentation, although methodological approach was variable, with many interventions described but not tested. This presents an opportunity for future work testing these promising interventions.

Interventions most commonly targeted patient education and outreach or provider-guided advance care planning. Both targets have the potential to address the common barrier of low advance care planning literacy. Guided advance care planning, in particular, represents an opportunity to fully engage vulnerable patients in advance care planning and comprehensively overcome complex barriers together. Our analysis suggests that confident, trusted providers with tailored advance care planning content in flexible settings could best support engagement. Other studied interventions, such as adaptation of advance care planning materials or provider training, could also improve this patient-provider interaction, such as by ameliorating negative cultural/personal beliefs around advance care planning or improving provider confidence.

Systemic interventions were fewer in number, possibly due to a higher logistical and resource burden. This is consistent with known challenges in targeting social determinants of health, such as housing or employment, for intervention despite their role in inequitable healthcare. 118 When implemented, advance care planning documentation systems seem to offer systemic improvements to advance care planning consistency and utilization. Many patient-level factors, such as relationships, spirituality, attitudes to advance care planning, and relevant experiences with serious illness were not explicitly targeted by any of the interventions we identified. Considering these topics could represent patient-level barriers or facilitators, this represents a potential interventional gap that could be explored. Interventions addressing these areas may best be delivered by those closest to the patient including social, familial, cultural, or religious supports.

What this study adds

To our knowledge, this is the first study that integrates the literature of advance care planning barriers, facilitators and interventions for multiple structurally vulnerable populations. Many of the barriers and facilitators we identified are well-known and present in the general population, such as low advance care planning literacy and low provider confidence. 119 Here we highlight that themes previously identified in individual vulnerable groups are applicable to many other vulnerability contexts: social isolation, competing priorities, and medical mistrust in those experiencing housing instability 79 ; illness experience, social connections, and provider relationships in Black patients 32,120; tailored communication and trusted relationships in Indigenous patients 33 ; provider education in those living with serious mental illness 35 ; and social supports in those living with substance use disorder. 121 These shared experiences further validate previous knowledge and also point toward broadly applicable factors stemming from systemic disadvantages, which may be prime targets for intervention. This approach may facilitate filling of knowledge gaps, such as when advance care planning interventions for patients with serious mental illness and substance use disorder for which there is relatively limited advance care planning literature, especially in an interventional context. When contextual differences between vulnerable populations are important, such as when targeting a group with a shared structural vulnerability, future researchers, and policymakers can more directly explore these differences through interrogation of the discrete factors and linkages synthesized in this review.

This study also extends current knowledge of effective advance care planning interventions for structurally vulnerable groups. Several previous reviews examining advance care planning interventions have focused on the ill-housed population,22,24,28 as these patients face extreme structural disadvantages and poor health outcomes. Our findings correlate with their recommendations, including a focus on patient education and outreach. Similarly, our findings agree with previous reviews of advance care planning interventions for Black patients, which recommend incorporating education, spirituality, and trusted relationships.29,30 In one review examining multiple vulnerabilities, providers’ approaches are considered just as important as the intervention itself. 38 Our study highlights the barriers and facilitators that providers should consider in their approach to delivering any advance care planning intervention.

Strengths and limitations

This study broadly examines advance care planning in structurally vulnerable populations and has several strengths. A large sample size of 70 studies enabled a wide-ranging analysis of patient, systemic, and provider-level factors from both patient and provider perspectives. Several different vulnerable patient groups were examined at once, enabling recognition of similarities and identification of knowledge gaps. Diverse study types were integrated in our review, allowing for both qualitative and quantitative data to be incorporated into the analysis. Examining both barriers and facilitators together with interventions showed how barriers and facilitators could be considered in advance care planning interventions.

There are several limitations to this study. While broad, the range of vulnerabilities examined was finite and did not examine other groups who may also face structural disadvantages, including LGBT+, incarcerated, rural, or patients of other cultures. Vulnerabilities documented in the studies may not be comprehensive either, as patients involved in the studies may have been experiencing other vulnerabilities that were not documented. We are thus unable to conclude that any of the factors distinguished in this article do not apply to a different group, nor explore or distinguish between unique intersectional experiences that are formed from specific interactions between structural vulnerabilities. Our analysis was qualitative to broadly capture the literature and thus we could not quantitatively define any intervention as superior. Our study explores advance care planning engagement but does not examine downstream outcomes such as in-hospital advance care planning documentation use, care quality, or resource utilization. Finally, while we did a broad database search of four databases, there are potentially studies that are not indexed by these databases but may otherwise have met our inclusion criteria.

Conclusion

This scoping review presents a broad overview of barriers, facilitators, and targets of intervention for advance care planning in structurally vulnerable populations. These populations were found to share many barriers and facilitators to advance care planning, suggesting the potential for common advance care planning interventions. Patient education and outreach, as well as provider-guided advance care planning, offer interventional approaches with the greatest quantity of evidence. Future advance care planning interventions may benefit from these approaches, especially by providers who embody facilitators such as confidence in advance care planning, trusted relationships, and tailored advance care planning delivery.
