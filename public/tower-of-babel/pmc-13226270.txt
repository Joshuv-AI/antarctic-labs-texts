Nanotechnology for efficient delivery of TCM active ingredients in treating brain diseases
Authors: Yuting Yang, Yufei Gao, Junping Li, Junbo Zou, Fei Luan, Yajun Shi, Bingtao Zhai, Qianqian Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain diseases, including Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), brain tumors, and stroke, remain difficult to treat due to the restrictive nature of the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) bioactive compounds offer multitarget therapeutic potential, but their clinical translation is limited by poor solubility, instability, rapid clearance, and insufficient brain exposure. Nanodelivery systems can enhance pharmacokinetics and enable brain-targeted delivery via engineered nanocarriers that exploit multiple BBB-crossing routes. This review summarizes nanotechnology-based strategies that facilitate efficient BBB transport, targeted brain accumulation, and microenvironment-responsive release of TCM-derived bioactives. Emphasis is placed on rational nanocarrier design, including biomimetic surface modification, physicochemical optimization, multicomponent integration, and stimulus-responsive systems that improve spatiotemporal control and therapeutic precision. Collectively, these strategy-oriented principles provide a translational framework for advancing nano-enabled TCM therapeutics in complex brain disorders.Keywords: Nanomedicine, Nanodelivery systems, BBB, TCM, Transcytosis

Introduction

Brain diseases, including Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), brain tumors, and stroke, are becoming increasingly prevalent and currently represent the second leading category of life-threatening illnesses worldwide [1,2]. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has a long history of application in the treatment of brain disorders, with documented use spanning thousands of years. To date, both classical TCM formulas and their bioactive constituents have been clinically employed to manage various brain-related diseases [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Accumulating evidence from modern pharmacological studies further demonstrates that these active components can improve cerebral microcirculation and exert protective effects on the brain [7]. Despite these therapeutic advantages, the primary obstacle remains the blood–brain barrier (BBB), whose highly selective permeability severely restricts the delivery of most therapeutic agents to the brain [8]. The ability of a drug to penetrate the BBB is largely determined by its physicochemical properties, such as molecular size, lipophilicity, and degree of ionization [9,10]. Many water-soluble TCM components exhibit insufficient lipid solubility and suboptimal molecular size, resulting in poor BBB traverse and low bioavailability [11,12]. This failure to achieve therapeutic concentrations within brain tissue substantially limits their application in treating brain disorders. Thus, advanced drug delivery systems that enhance brain penetration are essential for managing neurological diseases.

Brain diseases discussed in this review mainly include neurodegenerative disorders, brain tumors, and cerebrovascular injury, represented by AD, PD, glioblastoma, and stroke. Although these disorders differ in etiology and clinical manifestation, they share several pathological features that critically contribute to disease progression, including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuronal loss, abnormal protein aggregation, and, in some cases, BBB disruption [13]. In particular, AD and PD are characterized by chronic and progressive neurodegeneration, whereas glioblastoma and stroke are associated with rapid progression, high mortality, and limited therapeutic windows [14]. These features highlight the complexity of brain diseases and underscore the need for therapeutic strategies capable of simultaneously addressing multifactorial pathology and achieving effective brain exposure.

TCMs and their bioactive constituents have shown broad therapeutic potential in these disorders because of their multitarget pharmacological activities. Existing studies indicate that various TCM-derived compounds, including flavonoids, alkaloids, phenolic acids, terpenoids, and saponins, can exert antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, neuroprotective, anti-amyloid, and anti-tumor effects in models of AD, PD, stroke, and other CNS disorders. However, the current research status remains largely preclinical, and the therapeutic translation of these agents is still limited by poor water solubility, instability, rapid systemic clearance, low bioavailability, and especially insufficient BBB penetration. Therefore, although TCM-derived bioactives possess promising pharmacological activities, their in vivo efficacy in brain diseases is often constrained by inadequate delivery to the lesion site [15].

Over the past several decades, a variety of BBB-crossing drug delivery strategies have been extensively investigated and progressively developed for clinical and preclinical applications [16]. Among these approaches, nanodelivery systems have emerged as one of the most promising platforms owing to their favorable characteristics, including nanoscale size, high structural stability, tunable surface properties, and versatile functionalization potential [17]. Integration of nanodelivery systems with TCM effectively addresses key limitations associated with conventional TCM delivery, such as poor solubility, instability, low bioavailability, and rapid systemic clearance [18]. As an evolving therapeutic modality, nanomedicine can facilitate transport across the BBB and subsequently enable more selective delivery to specific brain cell populations, while also supporting sustained and controlled drug release within the brain. Together, these capabilities highlight the strong potential of nanodelivery systems for the treatment of brain disorders [19,20].

In this review, we focus on recent advances in nanodelivery systems of bioactive compounds derived from TCM for the treatment of brain disorders. We first outline the structural and physiological features of the BBB and analyze the inherent challenges faced by TCM components in crossing this highly selective barrier. We then summarize the major nanocarrier platforms used for brain delivery of TCM-derived actives, followed by the principal BBB-crossing pathways exploited by these systems. Next, we discuss design-oriented optimization strategies that further improve delivery performance and therapeutic precision. Finally, we highlight the major therapeutic consequences after brain entry, together with the current limitations and future perspectives of nanodelivery systems in brain disorder treatment.

The key barrier for brain drug delivery - the BBB

Structure and physiological characteristics of the BBB

The BBB is a semi-permeable, highly selective interface that segregates the bloodstream from the brain parenchyma. Structurally, this critical barrier is composed of the foot processes of brain microvascular capillary endothelial cells (BMECs), pericytes, astrocytes, and the basement membrane [21]. The BBB is characterized by high transendothelial electrical resistance and low paracellular and transcellular permeability. These structural and functional traits endow it with a high degree of selectivity in substance exchange between the brain and the external environment, which in turn limits the entry of most therapeutic drugs and toxic substances into the brain [22,23].

BMECs constitute the principal gatekeeping element of the BBB. Compared with peripheral endothelial cells, BMECs form continuous tight junctions (TJs) together with adherens junctions, which generate high transendothelial electrical resistance and restrict paracellular leakage, thereby maintaining barrier tightness and central nervous system (CNS) homeostasis [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Pericytes reside on the abluminal side of BMECs and are embedded within the basement membrane. Through direct contacts and paracrine signaling, they regulate endothelial phenotype and junctional organization, stabilize microvessels, modulate capillary tone and cerebral blood flow, and shape immune surveillance at the neurovascular interface [28,29]. Astrocytic endfeet ensheath the microvascular wall and couple vascular elements with neurons, forming a signaling network that supports BBB maintenance and neurovascular communication [24]. BMECs, pericytes, astrocytes, and the basement membrane together constitute the core neurovascular unit, while additional cellular components such as neurons, microglia, vascular smooth muscle cells, oligodendrocytes, and infiltrating immune cells can further modulate BBB permeability and structural stability under physiological and pathological conditions [[30], [31], [32]]. Collectively, the highly organized cellular architecture and stringent regulatory mechanisms of the BBB ensure precise control over molecular exchange between the bloodstream and the brain. However, this high degree of selectivity also imposes substantial constraints on the brain entry of many exogenous compounds.

Inherent barriers for TCM active ingredients to cross the BBB

The penetration of bioactive components derived from TCM across the BBB remains a major bottleneck for their development as brain therapeutics [33,34]. The complex structural organization of the BBB, the presence of active efflux transport systems, and the unfavorable physicochemical characteristics of many TCM-derived constituents constitute formidable barriers to effective brain delivery. These multifaceted obstacles critically limit the brain exposure, regional accumulation, and therapeutic efficacy of TCMs, thereby representing a major challenge for their development and clinical translation in the treatment of brain disorders.

Under physiological conditions, TJs formed between brain capillary endothelial cells establish a continuous, pore-free barrier that effectively suppresses paracellular diffusion. This structural feature prevents the free passage of macromolecules and polar substances, thereby posing a substantial challenge for the delivery of many TCM-derived compounds [35]. In addition to the physical barrier imposed by tight junctions, endothelial cells express multiple ATP-binding cassette efflux transporters, including P-glycoprotein (P-gp), breast cancer resistance protein, and multidrug resistance-associated proteins. These transporters actively extrude xenobiotics from endothelial cells back into the systemic circulation, markedly reducing intracellular retention and subsequent brain accumulation of therapeutic agents [33]. The barrier function of the BBB is further reinforced by the basement membrane and astrocytic endfeet, which together with pericytes constitute the neurovascular unit. This multicellular architecture enhances the structural integrity of the BBB and limits available transcellular and paracellular transport pathways, thereby imposing additional constraints on drug permeation [23,27].

Beyond anatomical and transporter-mediated restrictions, the intrinsic physicochemical properties of most TCM-derived molecules further limit their BBB permeability. Key determinants such as molecular size, polarity, charge, lipophilicity, and hydrogen bonding capacity play decisive roles in governing BBB penetration. Many TCM compounds exhibit high polarity, large molecular weights, or insufficient lipid solubility, which collectively hinder passive diffusion across the endothelial lipid bilayer and reduce overall brain exposure [28,29]. Macromolecular constituents such as glycosides, polysaccharides, and saponins generally fail to meet the physicochemical criteria required for efficient BBB transport [29].

Under pathological conditions, alterations in BBB structure and function may variably influence drug delivery. Disruption of TJs and barrier integrity can lead to increased permeability, as exemplified by the blood–brain tumor barrier, which exhibits higher permeability than the intact BBB [28]. Nevertheless, even under such conditions, many TCM components continue to face substantial delivery challenges. High polarity, excessive molecular size, and poor lipophilicity still limit effective transport, while the lack of intrinsic targeting capability and generally low bioavailability further restrict accumulation in diseased brain regions and the maintenance of therapeutically effective concentrations [23,28,29]. Taken together, the delivery obstacles of TCM-derived therapeutics are not limited to generic BBB restriction. Relative to many conventional single-molecule drugs, TCM-derived cargos more often combine several concurrent challenges, including unfavorable and sometimes heterogeneous physicochemical properties, inadequate solubility-permeability balance, rapid systemic clearance, insufficient lesion selectivity, and, in some cases, dependence of therapeutic efficacy on coordinated multicomponent exposure rather than on a single isolated constituent. These characteristics impose corresponding design criteria for nano-TCM, namely, improving solubility and membrane accessibility, protecting labile cargos and prolonging circulation, enhancing BBB transport or bypass efficiency while reducing off-target loss, and, where relevant, supporting multicomponent-compatible loading and controlled release.

Nanotechnology facilitates delivery of TCM to the brain

The emergence of nanodelivery systems offer a rational response to these TCM-specific delivery challenges. By integrating TCM-derived cargos with tunable nanocarriers, nano-TCM can simultaneously improve solubility–permeability mismatch, protect unstable constituents, prolong systemic circulation, enhance BBB transport or bypass efficiency, and, when necessary, preserve coordinated delivery of multicomponent cargos [20,36]. In this sense, the design logic of nano-TCM is not merely to increase brain entry, but to match formulation architecture with the compositional and pharmacological characteristics of the TCM payload.

Nanodelivery systems offer a multifunctional toolkit to address biological and pharmacokinetic barriers faced by TCM formulations, including poor water solubility, rapid systemic clearance, enzymatic degradation, and limited BBB permeability [37,38]. Through rational design of nanocarriers with tunable particle size, surface charge, and functional ligands, nanodelivery systems enable precise modulation of drug pharmacokinetic properties. This extends circulation time, enhances brain accumulation, and facilitates stimulus-responsive or controlled drug release [39,40]. Furthermore, engineered nanocarriers can integrate targeting moieties to interact with pathological BBB or disease-associated microenvironments, enabling site-specific delivery while minimizing off-target distribution and systemic toxicity [41].

Notably, distinct categories of nanomaterials, including organic, inorganic, hybrid, and biomimetic nanocarriers, exhibit unique physicochemical properties and biological behaviors. These platforms can be tailored to meet therapeutic needs across diverse brain disorders [42,43]. These advances not only broaden the application scope of nanomedicines in brain diseases but also elucidate mechanisms by which nanodelivery systems modulate drug-brain interactions and enhance therapeutic outcomes. The following sections systematically review representative nanomaterials used for the delivery of TCM-derived medicine and explore their specific application advantages in treating various brain disorders.

Nanomaterials for brain delivery of active TCM components

Nano-TCM is typically prepared through two main routes. One route directly nanosized the active ingredients, most often via ultrafine milling, to generate particles in the nanometer range. This size reduction increases the specific surface area and usually accelerates dissolution, which can translate into improved apparent efficacy. In practice, however, this “straight nanosizing” approach offers limited control over bioavailability and provides little targeting capacity. The other, and more widely used, route integrates TCM actives into nanocarriers. These delivery systems generally fall in the nano size range and can reshape drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics [44]. Within this route, formulations may be constructed using an exogenous carrier matrix or arise from carrier-minimized assemblies driven by drug–drug or drug–excipient interactions, yet both are ultimately designed to achieve predictable transport, retention, and spatiotemporal control in the brain [45]. Because of these practical advantages, nanocarrier-based formulations have become a major focus in nano-TCM research and have been explored from molecular and cellular studies through to in vivo models for the diagnosis and treatment of brain disorders. By chemical composition, nanocarriers are commonly grouped into inorganic, organic, biomimetic, and hybrid systems (Table 1). Importantly, the TCM payloads incorporated into these nanosystems may exist at different compositional levels, ranging from single active monomers to multicomponent combinations, extracts, polysaccharide fractions, and formula-related preparations.

Inorganic nanocarriers

Inorganic nanocarriers, comprising metals, metal oxides, carbon-based materials, or silica, are versatile nanoscale platforms for drug delivery. Owing to their high chemical stability, tunable surface chemistry, and high drug-loading capacity, these nanomaterials have attracted considerable interest in biomedical applications [75,76]. Current studies mainly focus on gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), carbon quantum dots (CQDs), and mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs), all of which show promise for improving the efficacy of TCM-derived therapeutics in brain diseases [77].

AuNPs exhibit good biocompatibility, high physicochemical stability, distinctive optical properties, and strong affinity for biomolecules [78]. Paeonia moutan-derived AuNPs reduced inflammatory responses in BV2 microglia and improved neuroinflammation and motor coordination in PD models [46], while piperine-coated AuNPs reduced oxidative stress, preserved mitochondrial function, and alleviated neurotoxicity in PD-like models [47]. These findings suggest that AuNPs can both stabilize TCM actives and enhance their neuroprotective effects.

CQDs are fluorescent carbon-based nanomaterials with ultrasmall particle size and favorable biocompatibility [79]. Natural product-derived CQDs can retain bioactive features from their precursors. Quercetin-derived CQDs and ginsenoside Rb1–derived CQDs alleviated secondary injury after intracerebral hemorrhage by scavenging ROS and chelating excess iron [48,49]. Their ultrasmall size, typically 2–11 nm, also helps from a delivery standpoint. After intrathecal administration, CQDs can accumulate in the subarachnoid space and access the meningeal system, which supports local therapeutic action while limiting systemic exposure and reducing the impact of BBB restriction [48,49]. In addition, CQDs derived from Semen pruni persicae and Carthamus tinctorius L. increased claudin-5 and ZO-1 expression and attenuated BBB disruption after traumatic brain injury [50]. These studies indicate that CQDs can function not only as carriers but also as active nanotherapeutics.

MSNs possess high specific surface area, tunable pore size, good biocompatibility, and facile surface functionalization [[80], [81], [82]], enabling efficient drug loading and controlled delivery [83]. Berberine-loaded lipid-coated MCM-41 MSNs showed therapeutic potential in AD by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase activity, suppressing amyloid fibrillation, and improving cognitive dysfunction [51]. Quercetin-loaded MSNs likewise improved solubility and exerted neuroprotective effects against copper-induced oxidative injury in primary rat hippocampal neurons [52]. Overall, MSNs represent a versatile platform for brain-targeted delivery of TCM-derived compounds.

Beyond AuNPs, CQDs, and MSNs, other inorganic platforms such as pure metals (e.g., silver), metal oxides (e.g., γ-Fe2O3/Fe3O4), semiconductor materials, and calcium phosphate have also been explored [84]. These materials add complementary functions and physicochemical traits, broadening the options for nano-enabled TCM delivery and opening additional routes to improve efficacy in brain disorders.

Organic nanocarriers

Organic nanocarriers are primarily constructed from lipids or polymers. Typically ranging from tens to several hundred nanometers in size, these carriers can facilitate transport across biological barriers and improve accumulation at pathological sites, for example within tumor tissues. Encapsulation within organic nanocarriers can protect labile drugs from enzymatic degradation and improve the stability of compounds with poor physicochemical properties. Common organic nanocarriers include lipid-based nanocarriers, emulsions, and polymeric nanocarriers. Owing to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and structural tunability, these systems have been widely explored for TCM delivery.

Lipid nanocarriers

Lipid-based nanocarriers are widely used for brain delivery of TCM-derived bioactives because their composition and interfacial properties can be readily tuned to improve solubility, stability, and in vivo exposure. Among them, liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) are the most representative platforms in nano-TCM. Although all three systems share favorable biocompatibility and formulation flexibility, they differ in structural organization, drug-loading behavior, and release characteristics, which in turn influence their suitability for different neurological disorders and administration routes.

Liposomes are spherical vesicles composed of one or more phospholipid bilayers, allowing hydrophilic drugs to be loaded into the aqueous core and lipophilic drugs into the lipid membrane [85,86]. Because of their good biocompatibility and biodegradability, liposomes have been widely used to improve drug solubility and stability [87], controlling drug release kinetics [88], prolonging systemic circulation, enhancing brain targeting and bioavailability [89,90], and enabling the co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents [91]. Representative examples include angiopep-2 peptide-modified PEGylated liposomes for echinacoside delivery in PD [53] and quercetin-loaded liposomes for AD, which improved cognitive deficits and exerted antioxidant neuroprotection [54].

SLNs are a class of nanocarriers composed of a solid lipid core stabilized by a surrounding surfactant layer [89]. Owing to their good biocompatibility, physical stability, and capacity for controlled drug release, SLNs have attracted considerable attention for brain-targeted drug delivery. For example, chitosan-coated SLNs significantly enhanced the brain bioavailability of salvianolic acid A (SAc), achieving a 3.7-fold increase compared with free SAc. In addition, these SLNs also prolonged nasal residence and promoted cellular uptake [55]. Quercetin-loaded SLNs exhibited particle sizes below 200 nm, high drug entrapment efficiency, and improved memory retention in AD models [56]. Furthermore, curcumin-encapsulated SLNs with a mean particle size of 148 nm effectively attenuated 3-nitropropionic acid–induced Huntington's disease in rats by restoring mitochondrial complex activities, reducing oxidative stress, and ameliorating neurobehavioral deficits [57].These studies highlight SLNs as versatile carriers for brain-targeted delivery of TCM-derived compounds.

NLCs were developed as second-generation lipid nanoparticles to overcome limitations of SLNs, such as low hydrophilic drug loading and the risk of drug expulsion during storage [92,93]. Intranasal quercetin-loaded NLCs improved solubility, brain bioavailability, and sustained release, leading to better cognitive performance in an AD model [58]. Likewise, sialic acid–modified cationic NLCs encapsulating myricetin showed improved entrapment efficiency, enhanced cellular uptake, and inhibition of amyloid-β aggregation [59]. Overall, by improving drug loading, stability, and bioavailability, NLCs represent an advanced lipid nanocarrier platform for brain-targeted delivery in neurodegenerative diseases.

Emulsions

Owing to their thermodynamic or kinetic stability, small droplet size, and high solubilization capacity, microemulsions (MEs) and nanoemulsions (NEs) have emerged as versatile platforms for enhancing drug solubility and improving therapeutic efficacy in complex disease settings [94,95]. For example, a food-grade quercetin-loaded NE improved quercetin solubility and enhanced neuroprotection in a transgenic Caenorhabditis elegans model of PD [60]. In glioblastoma research, a honokiol-loaded NE formulated from a commercial intravenous lipid emulsion achieved high drug-loading efficiency (>95 %) with good physical stability and cytotoxicity against glioma cells [61]. A pterostilbene-loaded NE further provided sustained release and activated the Nrf2 pathway in an AD-related setting [62]. MEs have shown similar promise, as a turmeric oil-based curcumin ME with sub-30 nm globules increased oral bioavailability and brain exposure and improved neurobehavioral and oxidative stress readouts in a trimethyltin-induced zebrafish model [64].

Polymer nanocarriers

Polymer-based nanocarriers offer flexible architectures and chemical tunability for brain delivery of TCM-derived bioactives. Representative platforms mainly include polymeric micelles (PMs) and polymeric nanoparticles (PNs). PMs are self-assembled nanostructures of amphiphilic block copolymers with a hydrophilic corona and a hydrophobic core [[96], [97], [98]], which enable efficient loading of lipophilic drugs, improved aqueous dispersibility, and controllable surface functionalization and release [99]. For example, a borneol-modified PM system encapsulating BAPTA-AM achieved an encapsulation efficiency of approximately 86.5 %, enhanced BBB penetration to 12.7 % ID/g in injured brain regions, and showed ROS-responsive release with good in vivo stability [65]. Likewise, SS-31 peptide- and hyaluronic acid–modified PMs improved rutin solubility, promoted preferential accumulation in ischemic brain tissue, and exhibited favorable biocompatibility (LD50 56.25 mg/kg) [66]. These studies illustrate the significant therapeutic potential of polymeric micelles for targeted intervention in cerebral ischemic stroke (CIS) and other brain injuries.

PNs are solid nanostructures prepared from biodegradable polymers through polymerization, dispersion, or self-assembly [96]. Their solid-core architecture allows encapsulation or adsorption of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic compounds, while biodegradability helps reduce systemic toxicity [100,101]. Common matrices include polylactic acid, polyglycolic acid, and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), which have been widely studied for brain disorders [102,103]. Representative examples include intranasal pterostilbene-loaded nanoparticles, which improved neurological outcomes and reduced neuroinflammation and BBB disruption in intracerebral hemorrhage models [67], and a brain-targeting nanoparticle system carrying biatractylolide, which effectively crossed the BBB and delivered therapeutic agents into the brain [68]. These findings highlight biodegradable polymeric nanoparticles as versatile and effective nanocarriers for targeted drug delivery in brain injury therapy.

Hybrid nanocarriers

In recent years, hybrid nanocarriers formed by the integration of two or more organic and/or inorganic nanomaterials have emerged as a promising strategy for advanced drug delivery. These systems often adopt core-shell architectures generated through the self-assembly of amphiphilic components. By combining the complementary properties of different nanocarriers, hybrid nanodelivery systems exhibit enhanced drug-loading capacity, improved physicochemical stability, and greater structural tunability, which collectively facilitates the development of efficient BBB-targeting delivery platforms for TCM [104].

For CIS therapy, a hybrid nanocarrier based on Yulangsan polysaccharide was shown to improve the water solubility and in vivo stability of encapsulated drugs. This system efficiently crossed the BBB, achieved targeted accumulation in ischemic brain tissue, and exerted pronounced anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects. As a result, treatment reduced cerebral infarct volume and improved neurological function [69]. Similarly, in PD therapy, a tannic acid–loaded hybrid nanocarrier enhanced oral bioavailability and prolonged systemic circulation. This formulation exhibited effective BBB penetration and preferential accumulation in the substantia nigra, where it scavenged ROS, promoted anti-inflammatory microglial polarization, and protected dopaminergic neurons, while maintaining favorable biocompatibility [70]. These studies demonstrate that hybrid nanocarriers effectively integrate the advantages of multiple material components to overcome the delivery limitations associated with TCM-derived active ingredients. Such systems therefore hold considerable promise for improving therapeutic outcomes in the treatment of brain diseases.

Biomimetic nanocarriers

Biomimetic nanocarriers, engineered from natural biomaterials such as proteins, cell membranes, and extracellular vesicles, have emerged as a particularly attractive platform for brain delivery because they combine nanoscale delivery advantages with endogenous biological functions. Compared with conventional synthetic nanocarriers, these systems generally exhibit better biocompatibility, reduced immune clearance, prolonged circulation, and improved brain-targeting potential [105]. Representative examples include albumin-based nanoparticles, which have been used to enhance the nose-to-brain delivery of resveratrol and the BBB penetration of procyanidin C-1 in AD-related models [71,72]. Cell-derived and cell membrane–mimetic nanocarriers provide another important biomimetic route. Neutrophils, for example, can cross the BBB under inflammatory conditions [106], and their lesion-directed migration is promoted by inflammatory cues during brain injury [107]. Based on this property, neutrophil-like cell membrane vesicles carrying ginsenoside Rg1 improved ischemic-region targeting and therapeutic outcomes in stroke [108], while microglia-mimetic nanodelivery systems co-delivering berberine and euonymusine improved cognitive performance in AD mice [109].

Exosomes and exosome-like vesicles constitute another important class of biomimetic nanocarriers because of their lipid bilayer structure and natural role in intercellular communication [110,111]. Owing to their low toxicity, excellent biocompatibility, modifiable surfaces, and intrinsic targeting capability, they have attracted increasing interest as carriers for TCM-derived therapeutics in brain disorders [[112], [113], [114]]. For instance, Pueraria lobata-derived exosomes improved brain delivery and therapeutic efficacy in PD models [73], whereas Panax notoginseng-derived exosome-like nanoparticles showed beneficial effects in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury [74]. Overall, by integrating biological compatibility with delivery functionality, biomimetic nanocarriers represent a promising strategy for enhancing the brain delivery and efficacy of TCM-derived compounds.

Strategy-oriented selection of nanocarriers for TCM delivery in brain diseases

From a strategy-oriented perspective, no single nanocarrier is universally optimal for all TCM-derived therapeutics, and carrier selection should be matched to both the compositional features of the TCM cargo and the pathophysiological demands of the target brain disease [42,43]. In general, lipid-based carriers are more suitable for lipophilic or poorly water-soluble small-molecule TCM actives when the main goals are biocompatibility, high loading efficiency, and repeated administration in chronic disorders such as AD and PD [[53], [54], [55]]. Polymeric systems are advantageous when prolonged circulation, controlled release, or intranasal delivery is required, and are particularly useful in disease settings where exposure stability and spatiotemporal release control are important, such as ischemic injury or neurodegeneration [[65], [66], [67], [68]]. Biomimetic carriers are better suited to applications requiring immune evasion, inflammation-homing, or preservation of biointerface compatibility, including multicomponent or biologically complex TCM-derived cargos, especially in stroke, PD, and glioblastoma-related contexts [73,74,108]. Hybrid carriers are most appropriate when no single material can simultaneously meet the delivery requirements, for example when brain delivery must be integrated with multifunctionality or enhanced penetration in complex disease settings [69,70]. Therefore, the optimal carrier should be determined by the intersection of cargo characteristics, disease context, therapeutic objective, and translational feasibility rather than by carrier class alone (Table 2).

Delivery mechanisms mediating the transport of TCM active ingredients across the BBB

The bioavailability of TCM actives in the brain is more often limited by their transport across the BBB than by their intrinsic pharmacological potency. The following sections therefore summarize four major transport paradigms, including passive and active BBB pathways, externally assisted strategies that enhance permeability into the brain, and intranasal nose-to-brain entry that bypass the BBB for brain entry (Fig. 1). Importantly, these mechanisms should not be interpreted as purely categorical routes. Their delivery performance is jointly determined by quantitative parameters such as molecular size, lipophilicity, surface charge, ligand density, endothelial uptake, transcytosis efficiency, BBB integrity-related readouts, and ultimate brain accumulation. Moreover, the relative contribution and interpretation of each pathway may differ substantially between the intact physiological BBB and dynamically remodeled pathological BBB states.

Passive transport

Passive BBB transport mainly involves two mechanisms: transcellular diffusion, through which small lipophilic molecules cross endothelial cells into the brain parenchyma, and paracellular transport, through which hydrophilic compounds pass between adjacent endothelial cells [38].

Transcellular diffusion depends primarily on molecular weight and lipophilicity. Highly lipophilic small molecules with molecular weights of approximately 400-500 Da and fewer than nine hydrogen bonds can cross the BBB along concentration gradients [115]. In TCM delivery, nanodelivery systems may enhance passive diffusion by modifying physicochemical properties. Lipid-oriented modifications such as cyclization, acylation, halogenation, esterification, and methylation can increase lipophilicity [19], while nanolipid carriers, suspensions, and nanocrystals can improve solubility and diffusion in lipid membranes. For example, a rutin NE system facilitated brain entry and alleviated oxidative stress–related pathology in PD [116]. Xu et al. employed a modified direct hydration method to encapsulate the anticancer drug paclitaxel (PTX) within a core–satellite structure composed of PEG-b-poly (ε-caprolactone). To track cellular internalization and intracellular fate, they further prepared coumarin-labeled polymersomes and quantified the uptake of coumarin-loaded polymersomes by flow cytometry (Fig. 2A). This formulation, used for glioma treatment, enhanced passive transcellular diffusion and improved brain penetration. It also downregulated the efflux transporter P-gp, helping to mitigate drug resistance [117]. However, increased lipophilicity alone is insufficient once molecular weight exceeds 400-500 Da [119], and CNS entry is further limited by a polar surface area above 80 Å2 and a tendency to form more than six hydrogen bonds [33]. This limitation restricts the application of passive diffusion for delivering active components from TCM, necessitating alternative delivery mechanisms to achieve more effective brain targeting. More importantly, passive BBB entry should be understood as a quantitatively constrained process rather than a purely qualitative one, because molecular weight, lipophilicity, polar surface area, and hydrogen-bonding capacity act together to determine whether improved membrane affinity can be translated into meaningful brain exposure.

Paracellular transport refers to movement through intercellular gaps rather than across the plasma membrane. Under certain conditions, borneol can enhance BBB permeability by modulating efflux proteins, tight junctions, vasodilatory neurotransmitters, and ion channel-related transport, suggesting its potential as an adjuvant for brain drug delivery [120]. Consistent with this, borneol-modified SLNs exhibited low cytotoxicity, high cellular uptake, strong BBB penetration, and improved brain targeting [121]. Wang et al. further encapsulated borneol with BAPTA-AM (a cell membrane-permeable calcium chelator) into nanoparticles, enabling more efficient brain delivery and sequential treatment of cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury (Fig. 2B) [65]. Further, borneol-modified doxorubicin nanomicelles developed by Meng et al. exhibited strong BBB penetration and significant anti-tumor and anti-metastatic effects (Fig. 2C) [118].

Active transport

Carrier-mediated transport

Carrier-mediated transport (CMT) is a major route for transmembrane trafficking of endogenous nutrients and small molecules. At the BBB, CMT is mediated by multiple carrier systems, including glucose, amino acid, choline, peptide, and ion transporters [33]. These carriers exhibit substrate selectivity and operate through alternating-access conformational changes that translocate bound solutes across endothelial membranes, typically driven by concentration gradients and electrochemical forces [122]. Building on this physiology, nanocarriers can be engineered with substrate-mimicking motifs or transporter-engaging ligands to access selected CMT pathways, thereby promoting the brain delivery of TCM-derived actives.

Glucose transporters and GSH transporters represent two extensively investigated carrier-mediated pathways for nanoparticle transport across the BBB [123]. Glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), which is highly expressed in brain capillary endothelial cells and glioblastoma tissue, plays a key role in regulating BBB permeability. Nanocarriers modified with glucose or glucose derivatives can exploit GLUT1-mediated transport to enhance BBB penetration and brain accumulation, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy [124,125]. Duan et al. developed a glucose and polydopamine (GPDA)-coated nanodelivery systems surface-modified with Glu-PEG and polydopamine, which enabled targeting of cerebral endothelial cells and enhanced cellular uptake (Fig. 3A and C) [126]. Additionally, cholesterol undecanoate-glucose conjugate-based artemether-chuanxiongzine liposomes exhibited improved brain targeting and anti-cerebral malaria efficacy [[127], [128], [129], [130], [131], [132], [133]]. Tian et al. presented the glucose moiety outward for effective GLUT1 recognition, the formulation achieved significantly higher intracerebral accumulation than non-targeted liposomes after administration (Fig. 3B) [127]. This strategy strengthens brain targeting and improves anti-cerebral malaria outcomes, providing a promising approach for lowering CM mortality. In parallel, GSH-mediated transport has emerged as another promising carrier-mediated strategy. GSH is an endogenous antioxidant with higher concentrations in the brain than in peripheral tissues, and its transporter, multidrug resistance-associated protein 1 (MRP1), is highly expressed on BBB and contributes to the maintenance of cerebral redox homeostasis. Leveraging this pathway, GSH-conjugated nanocarriers have been developed to enhance brain delivery of therapeutic agents via transporter-mediated uptake [134]. Yang et al. reported GSH-modified gold nanocages that exhibited improved BBB transport compared with unmodified nanocarriers in vitro. In vivo imaging further demonstrated a brain delivery efficiency of 62.48 %, with peak fluorescence observed at 24 h post-injection, highlighting the effectiveness of GSH-mediated BBB transport for nanocarrier delivery [135].

In addition to glucose transporters and GSH transporters, several other endogenous carriers expressed at the BBB have also been explored for carrier-mediated nanocarrier transport, including amino acid transporters, ion transporters, peptide transporters, choline transporters and so on [33]. However, the efficiency of CMT is strongly context-dependent. In addition to endogenous substrate competition, transporter abundance, regional distribution, substrate specificity, and the quantitative balance between uptake and efflux processes may all influence the actual transport outcome [136]. Therefore, this pathway may be particularly relevant in brain diseases accompanied by impaired nutrient or energy metabolism, but its performance should be interpreted in both mechanistic and quantitative terms [124].

Receptor-mediated transcytosis

Receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) is a highly specific BBB transport pathway that exploits receptor-ligand interactions to achieve intracerebral delivery of nanoparticles [36]. Multiple BBB-expressed receptors have been explored for this purpose, including transferrin receptors (TfR), lactoferrin receptors, insulin and insulin-like growth factor receptors, low-density lipoprotein receptors, leptin receptors, and heparin-binding epidermal growth factor-like receptors [137]. Among these, TfR is one of the most extensively studied targets because of its central role in iron transport and its high expression in cerebral capillary endothelial cells [138]. TfR-targeted systems, such as T7 peptide–modified PLGA nanoparticles and PTX-loaded SLNs, have shown improved cellular uptake and brain delivery efficiency in tumor models [[139], [140], [141]]. However, TfR-mediated delivery is influenced by endogenous transferrin competition, ligand density, antibody characteristics, and intracellular trafficking behavior [123,[142], [143], [144]]. Thus, stronger receptor binding does not necessarily translate into better BBB transport, because excessive avidity may increase endothelial retention rather than productive transcytosis into the brain parenchyma.

Low-density lipoprotein receptors (LDLRs) and low-density lipoprotein receptor–related proteins (LRPs), including LRP1, LRP2, and LRP8, constitute another important receptor class involved in RMT-mediated transport across the BBB [123]. Among them, LRP1 is highly expressed in brain endothelial cells and has been widely investigated as a target for brain drug delivery [145]. Angiopep-2–functionalized nanocarriers, including liposomal, polymeric, and dendritic systems, have demonstrated enhanced brain penetration and therapeutic efficacy, particularly in glioblastoma [128,146]. In parallel, classical LDL receptors recognize apolipoprotein E and apolipoprotein B and mediate the endocytosis of cholesterol-rich lipoproteins, thereby contributing to neuronal signaling and lipid homeostasis in the brain [[147], [148], [149], [150], [151], [152]]. Nanoparticles and liposomes functionalized with LDL or apolipoprotein-derived ligands have shown improved BBB transport in both in vitro and in vivo models [149,[153], [154], [155], [156], [157], [158]]. Moreover, engineered therapeutic proteins or peptides incorporating LDL receptor–binding domains have been reported to cross the BBB and exert neuroprotective or disease-modifying effects in animal models of neurodegenerative and lysosomal storage disorders [159]. Together, these findings highlight LDLR- and LRP-based receptor-mediated pathways as robust and versatile strategies for enhancing brain delivery of nanocarriers.

Beyond TfR and LDLR/LRP family members, several additional receptors have also been exploited for RMT-based delivery. Representative examples include rabies virus glycoprotein 29 targeting nicotinic acetylcholine receptors for quercetin delivery in ischemic stroke [160], Angiopep-2-functionalized mesoporous selenium nanoparticles for PTX delivery [161], ionizable lipids derived from berberine and vincristine for brain-directed gene transport [162], and lactoferrin receptor–targeted systems for glioma therapy [163]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that diverse BBB receptors can be rationally utilized to expand the applicability of receptor-mediated nanodelivery systems in brain disease treatment.

Adsorption-mediated transcytosis

Adsorption-mediated transcytosis (AMT) exhibits low affinity but high binding capacity, with transcellular transport efficiency comparable to RMT [164,165]. This mechanism primarily relies on the surface charge properties of nanoparticles to achieve transport across the BBB. The endothelial cell membranes exhibit specific charge distributions. Positively charged nanoparticles can undergo electrostatic attraction with negatively charged endothelial cell membranes, thereby triggering AMT mediated BBB crossing. During this process, nanoparticles are progressively enveloped by the cell membrane, forming endocytic vesicles that enter the cell. Subsequently, they traverse the BBB into brain tissue via intracellular transport mechanisms [123].

Drug-loaded nanoparticles can enhance endocytosis by conjugating to positively charged groups, thereby improving brain-targeting efficiency. Cationic bovine serum albumin and cell-penetration peptide (CPP) are among the most frequently used moieties conjugated on NP to trigger NPs across the BBB through AMT. Kamalinia et al. utilized cationized human serum albumin to deliver drugs into the brain via AMT, which effectively prevented apoptotic cell death in AD and ameliorated amyloid-beta (Aβ)-induced learning and memory impairments [166]. CPPs, typically consisting of 5–30 amino acids, are generally positively charged and amphipathic, which enables them to penetrate cell membranes and transport into the cytoplasm. Thus, CPPs are also often employed for brain drug delivery via the AMT pathway. Li et al. modified liposomes with the CPP dNP2 to enhance brain accumulation of PTX in glioblastoma models, demonstrating improved BBB penetration and intracerebral distribution [167]. In conclusion, AMT is a promising strategy for the targeted brain delivery of nano-TCM. However, because this pathway relies heavily on electrostatic interaction, its efficiency must be balanced against the potential risks of nonspecific adsorption, serum protein interaction, endothelial stress, and reduced biocompatibility.

Cell-mediated transport and cell membrane-based biomimetic strategies

Cell-mediated transport exploits the intrinsic or engineered properties of specific cells to facilitate the delivery of nanocarriers across the BBB, particularly under pathological conditions associated with neuroinflammation. During the inflammatory phase of brain disorders, cells of the myeloid lineage, including monocytes, neutrophils, macrophages, and microglia, are recruited from the circulation and migrate into the brain [37]. Unlike conventional drug delivery strategies that aim to avoid immune cell uptake, cell-mediated transport adopts an inverse design principle by deliberately loading therapeutic agents or nanocarriers into cells with inherent BBB tropism. Acting as “Trojan horses”, these carrier cells traverse the BBB, home to lesion sites, and subsequently release their payload within the diseased brain microenvironment. Owing to the high loading capacity, active targeting capability, and preserved biological functionality of endogenous carrier cells, cell-mediated delivery systems generally exhibit lower cytotoxicity and immunogenicity than fully synthetic nanocarriers, thereby offering a safer and more precise approach for brain-targeted therapy [168]. Wang et al. constructed a cell-mediated delivery system by encapsulating PTX-loaded PLGA nanoparticles into mesenchymal stem cells, leveraging their tumor tropism and low immunogenicity to enhance glioma targeting (Fig. 4A) [169]. Similarly, Du et al. exploited the strong BBB penetration ability of engineered microglia cells and served them as carriers for PTX-loaded liposomes. Through extracellular vesicle secretion and tunneling nanotube-mediated intercellular transport, this system achieved efficient drug delivery to glioma cells (Fig. 4B) [170].

Building on this cell-mediated delivery concept, cell membrane-mediated biomimetic nanocarriers represent a simplified yet robust strategy that retains the targeting and immune-evasive properties of carrier cells without the use of living cells. By coating nanoparticles with membranes derived from specific cells, such systems inherit key functional proteins and surface markers from source cells, enabling prolonged circulation and disease-site targeting. In particular, neutrophil membrane–coated nanoparticles have demonstrated strong potential for ischemic stroke therapy due to their innate inflammation-homing capability [171]. Hu et al. developed a neutrophil membrane–derived nanovesicle system loaded with ginsenoside Rg1 (Rg1@NCM), which enhanced BBB penetration and targeted ischemic brain regions via PSGL-1 and integrin β2 expressed on the membrane (Fig. 4C) [108]. Apart from immune cell–derived systems, stem cell membrane-based biomimetic carriers have also been explored for brain-targeted delivery. Wu et al. reported stem cell membrane-modified liposomes that preferentially accumulated in ischemic brain regions, which was attributed to their affinity for brain endothelial cells and activated microglia [172]. Notably, curcumin delivery via this biomimetic system increased the survival rate of ischemic stroke mice from approximately 30% to over 90%, supporting its pronounced therapeutic benefit.

Together, cell-mediated transport and cell membrane–based biomimetic strategies represent complementary approaches that exploit endogenous cellular behaviors to overcome the BBB, either through active cellular migration or through membrane-derived targeting functions integrated into nanodelivery systems.

External stimulus assisted Delivery Across the BBB

Beyond transport category alone, BBB delivery performance should also be interpreted in light of particle size, surface properties, ligand design, endothelial uptake, and final brain accumulation. Moreover, enhanced brain entry under pathological or transiently permeabilized BBB conditions should be distinguished from transport across an intact physiological BBB. External physical stimuli, such as focused ultrasound, can induce localized and reversible BBB opening, thereby increasing brain exposure of compounds with limited permeability. Gastrodin, a phenolic glycoside derived from Gastrodia elata, provides a representative example. In a kainic acid–induced seizure model, the combination of focused ultrasound and gastrodin reduced seizure frequency and duration more effectively than either treatment alone. Imaging and histological analyses suggested a delivery-related mechanism, as ultrasound-enhanced membrane permeability was associated with elevated brain levels of gastrodin [173]. Similar findings were reported in an Aβ1–42–induced AD-like mouse model, where ultrasound-mediated BBB opening potentiated the therapeutic effects of gastrodin [174]. Collectively, these studies indicate that focused ultrasound can function as an external adjunct to transiently reduce barrier constraints and improve local brain delivery of TCM-derived actives.

Other external modalities provide complementary routes to guide carriers or enhance local transport. Near infrared irradiation (NIR) can supply spatial control through photothermal effects, which may facilitate BBB passage and support deeper access to vulnerable neuronal compartments. In PD oriented designs, a quercetin loaded nanocomposite showed BBB passage and preferential accumulation at sites of mitochondrial damage under near infrared guidance, which coincided with improved mitochondrial function and neuroprotection in vivo [175]. Another platform combined a polydopamine coated MgO core with PEGylation and surface functionalization using lactoferrin and puerarin, and the system entered the brain under photothermal guidance and was taken up by neuronal cells in PD settings [176]. These reports indicate that near infrared input can enhance brain exposure while also improving intracellular access in relevant disease contexts. In addition, magnetic guidance represents a third external strategy that uses applied fields to bias the distribution of magnetic carriers and increase their local exposure in selected regions. This approach is particularly attractive for highly vascularized tumors, where local enrichment can raise the probability of transvascular delivery. In glioblastoma, gallic acid coated magnetic nanoclovers based on cobalt doped iron oxide were reported to support tumor vessel association and to improve delivery performance under magnetic actuation [177]. Overall, external stimuli can complement ligand based and materials-based approaches by adding time and location control over brain exposure, which can be valuable when barrier properties and lesion access vary across diseases and stages.

BBB bypass strategies

In contrast to strategies that promote drug transport across the BBB, intranasal administration enables direct or partial nose-to-brain delivery via the olfactory and trigeminal pathways, thereby reducing dependence on BBB translocation. This route is therefore more appropriately classified as a BBB bypass strategy rather than as a BBB permeability-modulating approach. Mechanistically, this delivery route depends on drug deposition in the nasal cavity, followed by transport across the mucus layer and nasal epithelium, and subsequent access to the brain through olfactory and trigeminal pathways. Intranasal administration represents a noninvasive strategy that can partially bypass the BBB via the olfactory and trigeminal pathways, enabling direct nose-to-brain delivery. Following deposition in the nasal cavity, drugs must traverse the mucus layer and the nasal epithelium, where mucociliary clearance and tight junction–regulated permeability limit absorption. Transport occurs primarily through transcellular and paracellular routes, after which molecules can access the brain along olfactory pathways through the cribriform plate [178]. This route avoids hepatic first-pass metabolism and is particularly advantageous for compounds with poor oral bioavailability, a common limitation of many TCM actives. The highly vascularized nasal mucosa and relatively large absorptive surface further support drug uptake, while formulation strategies such as mucoadhesive systems and in situ gels can prolong residence time and enhance nose-to-brain transport efficiency [179,180].

The feasibility of intranasal delivery has been demonstrated across several disease models. Huang et al. formulated a thermosensitive in situ gel containing a microemulsion of Rhizoma Chuanxiong volatile oil. This design prolonged nasal residence and improved nose-to-brain transport, resulting in higher bioavailability of volatile components [180]. Along the same line, Fonseca et al. [181] used intranasal in situ gelling liquid crystals to deliver resveratrol and observed attenuated neuroinflammation with improved memory performance in AD models. These formulation gains have also paved the way for more “smart” intranasal systems that add biological targeting or microenvironment-triggered release. Boric acid ester-grafted dextran loaded with the drug baicalin, an M2 microglial membrane–coated nanoparticle carrying baicalin, entered through the nasal mucosa, enriched in ischemic regions, and released the drug under ROS-rich conditions, which increased brain exposure and improved functional outcomes in global cerebral ischemia [182]. A similar delivery logic has been applied to PD, where TPGS-stabilized paeoniflorin nanocrystals enhanced nasal permeation and achieved higher brain exposure than free paeoniflorin after intranasal administration [183].

Optimization strategies for nanodelivery systems capable of crossing the BBB

Optimization of carrier physicochemical properties

The ability of nanocarriers to deliver drugs to the brain depends strongly on physicochemical compatibility with the BBB. Particle size, surface charge, material composition, and morphology jointly determine circulation stability, endothelial engagement, and translocation efficiency [173]. More generally, recent analyses of vesicular delivery systems have emphasized that carrier composition, bilayer architecture, and surface modification collectively shape stability, barrier interaction, and ultimate delivery performance across multiple sequential biological barriers [184]. Importantly, tuning these parameters extends beyond enhancing colloidal stability to redirecting the in vivo fate of nnaocarriers, which ultimately reshapes its brain exposure and therapeutic efficacy.

The particle size and the structure of nanomaterials significantly affect their bioactivities [174,175]. Studies indicate that particles below 10 nm are preferentially cleared by renal filtration, whereas particles above 300 nm are more readily removed by the reticuloendothelial system [176,177]. Colloidal stability is strongly influenced by surface charge, and particles exhibiting sufficiently high absolute zeta potentials (commonly above approximately ±30 mV) are generally considered electrostatically stabilized due to interparticle repulsion rather than aggregation [185]. However, excessive surface charge—particularly strong cationic potential—may increase plasma protein adsorption and rapid clearance in vivo. Thus, physicochemical optimization typically requires balancing colloidal stability with biological compatibility. Ghoreyshi et al. reduced the particle size of PLGA-chitosan nanocarriers to approximately 77 nm and tuned the surface charge to a weakly positive level(11.72 mV), which mitigated rapid clearance while enhancing endothelial adhesion, ultimately leading to increased curcumin accumulation in glioblastoma tissue [186]. Using a different optimization strategy, Qian et al. improved curcumin delivery by stabilizing nano-vesicles at around 168 nm with a narrow size distribution and introducing a moderately negative surface potential, a change that enhanced colloidal stability and reduced reticuloendothelial uptake, resulting in improved BBB penetration and glioma targeting [187]. Alterations in material composition can further modify biological behavior. Hou et al. replaced conventional polymer matrices with an amphiphilic Poloxamer 188 system, which not only improved blood compatibility and micellar stability but also suppressed P-gp–mediated efflux at the BBB, thereby increasing curcumin retention within the brain. [188]. Morphology can add another layer of control. Saini et al. reported that converting vanillic acid formulations into uniformly spherical, chitosan-coated SLNs enabled sustained release and increased brain bioavailability by 3.7-fold relative to free drug [189]. This exposure gain was accompanied by improved cognitive outcomes and reduced oxidative stress in AD models.

Collectively, these studies indicate that physicochemical optimization is often transformative rather than incremental. Deliberate control of size, charge, composition, and morphology can redirect biodistribution, relieve BBB related constraints, and materially improve therapeutic performance in brain disease models.

Surface chemical modification

Surface chemical modification is a practical strategy to strengthen interactions between nanocarriers and the BBB or diseased brain tissue [190]. This approach primarily enhances localization and retention in the brain rather than fundamentally changing intrinsic drug pharmacokinetics. Common implementations include chemical surface modification, receptor ligand decoration, cell penetrating peptide functionalization, and biomembrane coating. Surface chemical modification is widely used to reduce nonspecific clearance and increase brain exposure [191]. Methods such as PEGylation, PSAylation, and lipidation help extend blood residence time while preserving the bioactivity of the drug. A typical example is the Tween 80 coating on mPEG-PLGA nanoparticles, which were used to carry willow leaf alkaloids and facilitated brain entry, providing neuroprotection in an AD model [[192], [193], [194]]. Modifying the structure of small bioactive molecules can also improve their brain delivery. For instance, oxidation and indole conjugation of atractylenolide from Atractylodes macrocephala generated LSD1 inhibitors with enhanced BBB penetration, leading to reduced Aβpathology in APP/PS1 mice [195].

Receptor-mediated targeting aims to enhance BBB transport through specific ligand–receptor interactions [196]. Modification of carriers with specific ligands bind to target receptor and enter the brain via receptor-mediated endocytosis. This increases the brain accumulation of drugs [197]. This strategy typically targets receptors such as TfR and insulin receptors [198,199]. Furthermore, the delivery efficiency of this targeting strategy can be further enhanced by carefully tuning the ligand type and density. Cell-Penetrating Peptides (CPPs) are another powerful tool for enhancing cellular entry and supporting the delivery of various therapeutic cargos [200,201]. For example, the RMMR1 peptide was conjugated to polymeric micelles carrying quercetin and etoposide. This modification increased nasal epithelial and glioblastoma cell uptake, and improved nose-to-brain delivery [202]. Biomembrane coating is another approach to improving circulation stability and reducing immune clearance [203]. It also confers lesion-specific targeting through membrane-inherited functions [204]. For example, RBC membrane-coated nanoparticles loaded with curcumin were further functionalized with a targeting peptide, enhancing BBB transport and therapeutic outcomes in an AD model [205]. Similarly, a NK cell membrane-mimetic nanocomplex was developed to improve brain delivery and therapeutic efficacy in PD models [206].

In conclusion, surface modification strategies significantly enhance BBB-oriented delivery by promoting stronger engagement with the BBB and improving drug retention in diseased regions. Future research should focus on standardizing metrics for targeting efficiency and safety while optimizing ligand density and membrane functionality to minimize off-target accumulation and immune-related risks.

Optimization of multicomponent synergistic delivery

Optimization of multicomponent delivery is particularly relevant to TCM because its therapeutic effects often depend on the coordinated actions of multiple constituents acting on multiple pathological targets. In brain disorders, this feature is especially important, as oxidative stress, inflammation, vascular dysfunction, mitochondrial injury, and neuronal loss frequently coexist within the same disease context. Therefore, nano-formulation strategies should not only improve the delivery of single TCM-derived compounds, but also support the controlled co-delivery of multicomponent systems, including active compound combinations, extract- or polysaccharide-based preparations, and formula-inspired therapeutic designs.

Optimization of multicomponent strategies serve distinct therapeutic purposes. Sensitization-type combinations are exemplified by nanomicellar curcumin paired with temozolomide or erlotinib in glioblastoma, which enhanced growth inhibition and coordinated Wnt and NF-κB signaling pathway modulation compared with monotherapy [207,208]. Synergistic dual-drug encapsulation is illustrated by PTX–curcumin lipid nanocapsules, which improved exposure and cytotoxic potency relative to free-drug suspensions [209]. Detoxification-oriented optimization is reflected in the CLA–PTX microemulsion, which retained antitumor efficacy while improving tolerability [63]. Beyond oncology, liposomes co-delivering Panax notoginseng saponins with acetylsalicylic acid improved BBB transport and outcomes in ischemia–reperfusion models [210], and responsive antioxidant nanoparticles combined with curcumin accumulated in injured regions and mitigated oxidative–inflammatory injury in traumatic brain injury [211]. These examples demonstrate sensitization, synergy, toxicity reduction, and multi-pathway coordination as practical goals of multicomponent design.

Optimization therefore focuses on enforceable formulation parameters. Ratio precision during co-encapsulation preserves synergy fidelity. Controlled release kinetics, whether synchronized or sequential, maintain temporal overlap of complementary mechanisms. Coordinated targeting strategies strengthen co-localization within pathological niches. Across disease categories, endothelial efflux represents an additional constraint. The BBB expresses ATP-binding cassette transporters such as P-gp, breast cancer resistance protein, and multidrug resistance-associated proteins [212,213], which can limit brain exposure [214]. Classical pharmacological evidence, including enhanced PTX permeability with PSC833 and increased brain levels of vincristine and ivermectin in P-gp-deficient animals [215], underscores the need to address efflux in preserving intracerebral ratio control.

Overall, effective multicomponent nanodelivery requires stable composition, regulated release dynamics, reinforced target-site convergence, and mitigation of barrier-related exposure loss to ensure reproducible synergy in brain therapy.

Stimulus-responsive “smart” drug delivery

Stimulus-responsive delivery aims to control drug release in a time- and site-dependent manner. This design reduces premature leakage during circulation and increases drug availability at diseased brain regions. The trigger can come from the pathological microenvironment, such as acidic pH, elevated ROS, or enzyme upregulation. The trigger can also be applied externally, such as electrical stimulation, light stimulation, ultrasonic stimulation, magnetic stimulation. In nano-TCM systems, these triggers are often integrated with BBB-crossing designs to improve brain exposure while limiting off-target toxicity.

Endogenous stimuli

Endogenous stimuli-responsive delivery relies on nanocarriers that can change their structure in the diseased brain microenvironment. Common designs include ROS-responsive carriers, pH-responsive carriers, hypoxia-responsive carriers, redox-responsive carriers, and enzyme-responsive carriers. These carriers are usually stable in blood. These carriers then undergo controlled disassembly or bond cleavage after they reach lesions or enter intracellular compartments. This response promotes local release and reduces premature leakage.

Oxidative stress is a recurring feature in many brain disorders. The ROS-rich lesion milieu can act as an internal trigger for controlled release. ROS-responsive systems aim to keep cargo stable during circulation but accelerate release once the carrier reaches oxidative sites [216]. In ischemic injury, PUELipo/R-R uses a thioketal-based component to destabilize the liposomal structure under high ROS, while RVG29 supports BBB transport and enrichment in the penumbra, which improves Pueraria delivery to the target region [217]. A related design adds intracellular precision. SPNPs reach the brain through a nose-to-brain route and use SS-31 to bias distribution toward neuronal mitochondria. Oxidative conditions then disrupt the responsive framework and promote puerarin release, together with improvements in redox balance, inflammation, and apoptosis markers (Fig. 5A). ROS-triggered release can also be combined with ROS scavenging. PPCeO2/Cur integrates ApoA-I-assisted brain entry with an ROS-degradable polydopamine shell to liberate curcumin (Fig. 5B), while the cerium-based component provides sustained antioxidant activity that supports microglial function and facilitates Aβ clearance [219]. The same trigger principle has also been adapted to a noninvasive format. An intranasal polysaccharide nanogel co-loading glycyrrhizic acid and paeoniflorin uses disulfide chemistry for ROS-sensitive release after transferrin-assisted brain delivery (Fig. 5C), and the formulation reduces oxidative and inflammatory signals with behavioral benefit and low toxicity [220]. A recent study also reported a ROS-responsive quercetin nanoplatform based on polydopamine nanoparticles (Fig. 5D). The system combined RVG29-assisted BBB transport with ROS-triggered carrier destabilization to promote lesion-side quercetin release, and it reduced oxidative stress and neuroinflammation while improving neurological outcomes in an ischemic stroke model [160].

pH-responsive delivery follows a similar trigger-and-release logic. Many formulations introduce acid-labile bonds or ionizable segments that remain relatively stable at physiological pH but promote faster release in acidic endosomes and lysosomes. This design can bias drug release toward diseased regions and reduce unnecessary exposure in normal tissue. A stepwise pH-responsive micelle platform combines pH activation with targeting. The micelles were built from PEG-acetaldehyde–PCL–PEG and loaded with resveratrol. The surface carried cRGD to support BBB interaction and transport. After cellular uptake, lysosomal acidity detached the shielding PEG layer and exposed TPP, which increased mitochondrial targeting in microglia and improved intracellular delivery [221]. pH-triggered release has also been used in hemorrhagic injury, where the peri-hematomal niche tends to acidify during inflammation. Neutrophil-like pH-responsive nanoparticles (D&G@NPEOz) used inflammation-associated targeting to access injured regions and traverse the compromised BBB. The carrier then disassembled under acidic conditions and released its payload locally, which increased lesion enrichment and reduced distribution to normal tissue [222].

In addition to ROS and pH cues, other endogenous triggers have been incorporated into brain delivery designs. Hypoxia-activatable linkers, redox-cleavable bonds, and enzyme-responsive substrates can further restrict release to pathological niches and add another layer of control for site-specific therapy [218].

Exogenous stimuli

Exogenous stimulus-controlled delivery systems utilize external physical signals to regulate the delivery of drugs in the brain, controlling when and where the drug is released. Common external signals include electrical stimulation, light stimulation, ultrasonic stimulation, magnetic stimulation [223] and, to a lesser extent, temperature control and radiation. Under the guidance of these signals, drug release can be precisely regulated in both time and location, enhancing brain-specific accumulation while limiting systemic exposure. Ultrasound, light, and magnetic fields are frequently used in brain delivery studies due to their practical applications and promising potential for future clinical use.

Ultrasound acts as an effective external trigger to activate stimulus-responsive carriers once they reach the brain [203]. By enabling on-demand release at the target site, this approach allows nanocarriers to remain structurally stable during systemic circulation while achieving localized drug release within the lesion. In a subarachnoid hemorrhage model, a nanoparticle system was developed for ultrasound-triggered release of astaxanthin, a potent antioxidant. A perfluorocarbon-cored nanoparticle protected by a polydopamine shell enabled ultrasound-responsive astaxanthin liberation, demonstrating lesion-specific activation following focused stimulation. Ultrasound activation disrupted the carrier and accelerated local payload liberation, which was supported by ultrasound-dependent release profiles and imaging signal changes (Fig. 6A), together with antioxidant and anti-apoptotic benefits and acceptable in vivo safety. [224]. This example illustrates how ultrasound can be used not only to trigger localized drug release but also to improve the precision of smart intracerebral delivery. More recently, this concept has been extended to cerebral ischemia, where transcranial low-intensity ultrasound was combined with a curcumin-loaded liposomal platform to enhance lesion-site drug release and improve the neuroinflammatory microenvironment [227].

NIR irradiation offers another method for external control over drug delivery [228]. NIR-responsive systems use light as a switch to regulate therapeutic activation and to intensify treatment at the target site. In PD models, a nanocomposite based on quercetin and zeolitic imidazolate framework 8-coated Prussian blue nanoparticles were developed to respond to NIR. This system combined NIR responsiveness with therapeutic action, releasing quercetin to increase ATP levels, reduce oxidative stress, and alleviate dopaminergic neuronal injury. Mechanistic studies indicated the involvement of PI3K/Akt signaling [229]. Similarly, a polydopamine-coated MgO nanoplatform (Fig. 6B), functionalized with lactoferrin and puerarin, combined gene therapy with antioxidant support. NIR irradiation activated the system, enabling both gene and antioxidant therapy after neuronal uptake [225]. Beyond neurodegeneration, NIR phototheranostic approaches have been applied in brain tumor treatment. A macrophage-mimetic curcumin-based platform demonstrated enhanced photothermal conversion and singlet oxygen production, while inhibiting heat shock protein 70 (HSP-70)-mediated thermal defense. This multi-modal strategy, guided by NIR, prolonged survival in glioblastoma models [230]. Collectively, these studies show that light-responsive systems can integrate imaging guidance with externally enhanced therapy, thereby improving the precision and efficacy of brain-targeted delivery. Consistent with this trend, a recent glioblastoma study further reported a luteolin-coordinated ferric ion nanoplatform that functioned as a photothermal-enabled system under light irradiation while simultaneously amplifying ferroptosis [231].

Magnetic nanoparticles provide another externally controllable strategy for brain drug delivery. External magnetic fields can be applied to direct magnetic carriers to specific brain regions, thereby increasing drug concentration at the target site. In addition, some systems combine alternating magnetic fields with heat generation, allowing remote activation in deep tissues. In a glioblastoma model, a gallic acid-coated magnetic nanoclovers platform used a clover-shaped magnetic core that generated efficient magnetic heating under an alternating magnetic field (Fig. 6C). This process inhibited brain tumors progression, disrupted tumor vasculature, and enhanced the effectiveness of combined systemic chemotherapy [226]. More recently, this strategy has also been extended to magnetic/magnetothermal systems involving TCM-derived components. For example, a pH-responsive magnetic nanomicelle system co-delivering doxorubicin and curcumin was reported for astrocytoma treatment, further highlighting the potential of magnetic or magnetothermal designs in externally controlled brain tumor therapy [232].

Incorporating external stimuli-responsive systems into TCM drug delivery has the potential to significantly improve brain delivery efficiency. These systems protect the drug during circulation and BBB passage while enabling localized and timely release at the lesion site. By combining both endogenous and exogenous signals, these systems can further enhance drug availability in the brain, ultimately improving therapeutic outcomes.

Nano-delivery systems unlock the therapeutic potential of TCM

TCM offers a rich source of bioactive compounds with broad therapeutic relevance, particularly for complex and multifactorial diseases. However, the clinical translation of many TCM-derived phytochemicals remains limited by unfavorable pharmacokinetics, insufficient targeting efficiency, and unclear mechanisms of action. These challenges are especially pronounced in neurological disorders, where stringent biological barriers and complex pathophysiology further restrict therapeutic efficacy. Nanodelivery systems provide a versatile platform to address these limitations by enabling precise regulation of drug disposition, spatial distribution, and biological interactions. Through rational nanocarrier design, TCM-based therapies can be systematically optimized to improve systemic exposure, achieve coordinated multi-component delivery, and facilitate mechanistic understanding at cellular and molecular levels.

Improving pharmacokinetics of TCM

The clinical translation of TCM is frequently constrained by the unfavorable pharmacokinetic profiles of its active constituents. A substantial proportion of TCM-derived bioactive compounds are hydrophobic, resulting in poor aqueous solubility and limited absorption following oral administration. Consequently, insufficient systemic exposure remains a major bottleneck that compromises therapeutic efficacy. In addition, many representative phytochemicals, such as curcumin, tetrandrine, and baicalin, exhibit low chemical stability and are susceptible to rapid metabolic degradation in vivo, further reducing their bioavailability and pharmacological activity [233,234]. Nanocarrier-based delivery systems have therefore been extensively investigated as an effective strategy to improve the pharmacokinetic behavior of TCM components by enhancing solubility, protecting labile molecules from premature degradation, and prolonging systemic circulation.

Among the various platforms explored, lipid-based nanocarriers are the most widely studied due to their favorable biocompatibility, low toxicity, and suitability for encapsulating hydrophobic compounds. Liposomes and plant-derived liposomal formulations can accommodate lipophilic TCM constituents within their lipid bilayers, thereby improving aqueous dispersibility and increasing systemic exposure [[235], [236], [237]]. For example, co-encapsulation of curcumin and tetrandrine into liposomes significantly enhanced the solubility and bioavailability of both compounds compared with their free forms [238]. In addition to improving solubility, rational formulation design can further modulate drug distribution and stability. Baicalin, a flavonoid with therapeutic potential in cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, suffers from limited clinical applicability due to its poor solubility and unfavorable pharmacokinetics. Incorporation of borneol into baicalin-loaded liposomes provided an effective formulation strategy to enhance systemic exposure and tissue distribution. Borneol–baicalin liposomes exhibited markedly increased transmittance in vitro and achieved higher plasma concentrations and area under the curve values in vivo compared with non-modified formulations, indicating improved absorption and retention of baicalin [239].

Beyond lipid-based systems, inorganic nanocarriers such as MSNs have also been employed to improve the pharmacokinetic performance of poorly soluble TCM compounds. Owing to their high surface area, tunable pore size, and chemical stability, MSNs can efficiently encapsulate hydrophobic molecules such as curcumin and protect them from degradation. Encapsulation efficiencies exceeding 85 % have been reported, accompanied by improved dispersibility and sustained release profiles. In vivo studies further demonstrated enhanced systemic exposure and improved therapeutic outcomes in disease models, underscoring the contribution of optimized nanocarriers to improving the pharmacokinetics of TCM-derived compounds [240]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that nanodelivery systems can substantially improve the pharmacokinetic limitations of TCM by increasing solubility, enhancing stability, and prolonging systemic exposure. Such improvements provide a necessary foundation for subsequent enhancement of brain delivery and therapeutic efficacy, which are further addressed through advanced targeting and exposure-enhancing strategies.

Toward synergistic therapy through rational Co-delivery strategies

Single TCM constituents used for complex brain disorders often face efficacy ceilings, adaptive resistance, or dose-limiting toxicity. Although combination therapy is conceptually attractive, conventional co-administration or simple nanoformulation cannot ensure that multiple agents cross the BBB at defined ratios or accumulate within the same pathological cells. As a result, the anticipated synergy is often constrained by inadequate and unsynchronized intracerebral exposure.

Rational co-delivery addresses this bottleneck by converting combination therapy into a controlled intracerebral exposure program. By integrating agents with distinct physicochemical properties into a single carrier, nanoplatforms synchronize pharmacokinetics, enforce co-localization, and stabilize therapeutic ratios within lesion sites. In glioblastoma, curcumin-based nanomicelles combined with standard agents enhanced suppression of proliferation and invasion alongside coordinated inhibition of Wnt-related and NF-κB-associated signaling, demonstrating that shared delivery conditions promote convergent mechanism engagement [241,242]. Dual-drug lipid nanocapsules further showed that co-encapsulation can simultaneously improve exposure and apparent potency, underscoring delivery architecture as a determinant of combination performance [243].

In cerebrovascular injury, liposomes co-delivering Panax notoginseng saponins and acetylsalicylic acid improved BBB transport and therapeutic efficacy in ischemia–reperfusion models, illustrating how synchronized delivery enhances multi-target coverage of thrombo-inflammatory cascades [244]. Borneol–baicalin liposomes similarly coupled transport facilitation with neuroprotection, increasing brain exposure while improving functional outcomes and BBB integrity [239,245]. Co-delivery also bridges molecular regulation and physiological endpoints. A lipid nanoparticle system carrying resveratrol and SPAK siRNA reduced ventriculomegaly and inflammatory indices at the blood–CSF barrier interface [246], while ROS-scavenging materials combined with curcumin interrupted coupled oxidative–inflammatory injury in traumatic brain injury models [211].

Overall, rational co-delivery transforms synergy from a theoretical pharmacological interaction into a delivery-controlled phenomenon, enabling coordinated BBB penetration, lesion-site co-localization, and reproducible multi-target engagement beyond the limits of single-agent therapy.

Elucidation of mechanisms

TCM active components often exhibit multi-target regulatory potential in complex brain disorders but are hampered by poor bioavailability and limited BBB penetration, obscuring mechanistic interpretation at the molecular level. Nanodelivery systems not only enhance delivery efficiency but also provide a controllable experimental interface that couples intracerebral exposure with molecular response, enabling spatiotemporal tracking and quantitative elucidation of biological mechanisms.

In brain tumors such as glioblastoma, nanocarriers encapsulating TCM constituents (e.g., curcumin or baicalin) have been shown to improve BBB crossing and tumor accumulation, facilitating downstream mechanistic analysis. For example, baicalin lipid nanocapsules enhanced brain delivery and sustained release, leading to increased cellular uptake and modulation of oxidative stress–related genes such as Nrf2 and HO-1 and decreased expression of VEGF in glioma cells, thereby linking exposure to antiproliferative and anti-angiogenic pathways [247]. Engineered exosomes co-delivering TCM monomers have also demonstrated synergistic anti-tumor effects by regulating STAT3 and VEGF signaling in glioma models, illustrating how nano–TCM delivery can reveal convergent pathway modulation [248].

In ischemic stroke models, nanocarrier systems loaded with antioxidants or anti-inflammatory herbal extracts have enabled quantitative assessment of neuroprotective pathways. TCM monomers such as curcumin and quercetin encapsulated in nanoparticles enhance BBB penetration and attenuate ROS and cytokine release, implicating modulation of PI3K/Akt and Nrf2/HO-1 signaling in lesion sites and allowing mechanistic dissection of anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory effects [249,250]. Nanodelivery systems also clarify mechanisms in neurodegenerative diseases by stabilizing brain exposure and supporting exposure–effect linkage. In AD models, microglia-targeting nanodelivery systems co-delivering berberine and palmatine crossed the BBB and specifically targeted microglia, reducing amyloid-β aggregation and modulating neuroinflammatory environments, thereby linking cellular targeting to cognitive improvement [109].

In PD, nanodelivery systems have facilitated the mechanistic study of oxidative stress and proteostasis regulation. For instance, integration of nanodelivery systems with NIR–responsive systems increased the brain uptake of quercetin-loaded nanoparticles, which corresponded to improved motor function, increased tyrosine hydroxylase–positive neurons, and normalization of mitochondrial and inflammatory markers in PD mice, providing a quantifiable link between intracerebral exposure and pathway modulation [229]. Biomimetic nanodelivery systems targeting neural inflammation have further highlighted mechanisms involving microglial phenotype reprogramming and mitochondrial function recovery in PD models [251].

Collectively, these examples illustrate how nanodelivery systems transform complex, network-based TCM actions from empirical observations into experimentally traceable processes. By enabling delivery optimization, exposure stabilization, and targeted accumulation, nanoplatforms support mechanistic elucidation through quantitative association between intracerebral exposure and modulation of signaling pathways relevant to proliferation, oxidative stress, inflammation, and protein aggregation across diverse brain disorders.

Nano-enabled microenvironment modulation

Beyond improving pharmacokinetics and enabling rational co-delivery, nanodelivery systems fundamentally expand the therapeutic scope of TCM by actively reprogramming disease-associated microenvironments at molecular, cellular, and tissue levels. Rather than serving solely as passive carriers, these nanosystems function as microenvironmental modulators that reshape inflammatory signaling, vascular remodeling, immune activation, and barrier integrity within pathological brain niches.

In ischemic stroke, ROS-responsive and neuron-targeted liposomes (PUE-Lipo/R-R) delivering puerarin not only achieve efficient BBB penetration but also contribute to microenvironmental repair. These systems promote the polarization of microglia from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, upregulate angiogenesis-related factors such as VEGF-A and VEGFR-2 to enhance endothelial cell proliferation and tube formation, and protect the endothelial–astrocyte–pericyte unit by increasing the expression of tight junction proteins (e.g., claudin-5) (Fig. 7A) [217]. Through these coordinated effects, cerebral perfusion and microcirculatory function are significantly improved [109]. In AD, transferring-modified hybrid vesicles co-loading berberine and palmatine preferentially target microglia and suppress neuroinflammation by inhibiting the NF-κB and p38 MAPK signaling pathways. This modulation reduces the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and TNF-α, while increasing anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-4 and IL-10. In parallel, these vesicles attenuate amyloid-β deposition and tau hyperphosphorylation. Complementarily, berberine-inspired ionizable lipid nanoparticles enable efficient delivery of siRNA to downregulate BACE1 expression, suppress GSK-3β activity, preserve neuronal integrity, and alleviate oxidative stress through regulation of antioxidant markers such as SOD and MDA [253]. In glioblastoma, genetically engineered biomimetic nanoparticles (SIRPα@BSA/PTX) exploit melanoma cell membrane tropism to cross the BBB and accumulate within tumor tissue. By blocking the CD47–SIRPα “don't eat me” signaling axis, these nanocarriers reprogram tumor-associated macrophages toward an M1 phenotype while simultaneously delivering PTX (Fig. 7B) [252]. This immune modulation promotes the recruitment of CD8+ T cells and reshapes the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity.

Overall, nano-enabled TCM therapies extend beyond delivery enhancement to reshape pathological microenvironments. Through coordinated regulation of inflammation, vascular remodeling, immune responses, and barrier integrity, these systems enabling multi-target and multi-cellular regulation within the neurovascular unit, expanding its mechanistic and therapeutic scope in brain disorders [254].

Disease-indication-oriented translational synthesis and clinical outlook

From a disease-indication perspective, the translational relevance of nano-enabled delivery of TCM-derived therapeutics can be understood more clearly when key cargos, their mechanistic rationale, and representative in vivo formulations are considered together rather than by carrier class alone (Table 3). In AD, TCM-derived bioactives such as quercetin [54]and myricetin [58] are mechanistically relevant because they target core pathological processes including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, amyloid-β deposition, tau-related injury, and synaptic dysfunction. Representative in vivo nanoformulations with relatively strong evidence include quercetin-loaded liposomes and nanostructured lipid carriers that improved cognitive performance and oxidative stress-related outcomes [71], as well as glucose-modified bovine serum albumin nanoparticles delivering procyanidin C1 [72], which enhanced BBB transport and attenuated Aβ and tau pathology in AD models. In PD, TCM-derived actives such as echinacoside and quercetin are mainly used to counter oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and dopaminergic neuronal loss. Representative in vivo systems include Angiopep-2–modified echinacoside liposomes, which enhanced BBB transport and neuroprotective efficacy in MPTP-induced models, and Pueraria lobata-derived exosome systems, which improved neuronal uptake and mitochondrial homeostasis in PD-related settings [53,73]. In stroke, particularly ischemia/reperfusion injury, ginsenoside Rg1, borneol-based combinations, and Panax notoginseng-derived systems are mechanistically attractive because they address BBB damage, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and microvascular remodeling. Representative in vivo examples include neutrophil membrane–derived vesicles carrying ginsenoside Rg1, which improved ischemic-region targeting and stroke outcomes [108], Panax notoginseng-derived exosome-like nanoparticles, which entered brain tissue [74] and reduced cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury, and borneol/BAPTA-AM nanoparticles developed for sequential intervention in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury [65]. In brain tumors, TCM-derived actives such as curcumin and honokiol, as well as paclitaxel/curcumin or doxorubicin/borneol-based systems, are mechanistically relevant because they combine cytotoxic, anti-migratory, anti-resistance, or microenvironment-modulating actions with BBB-oriented delivery. Representative in vivo nanoformulations include PTX-loaded polymersomes that enhanced passive transcellular penetration and glioma delivery [63], borneol-modified doxorubicin nanomicelles with strong BBB penetration and anti-tumor/anti-metastatic activity, and honokiol nanoemulsions showing antiglioma potential [121]. Taken together, these studies indicate that the translational significance of nano-TCM lies not merely in increasing brain exposure, but in matching disease-specific pathological demands with mechanistically relevant TCM-derived cargos and formulation strategies supported by in vivo efficacy evidence.

Importantly, the currently available clinical evidence for nano-TCM in brain disorders remains extremely limited. According to the registry screening summarized in this review, only two ClinicalTrials.gov records met the working definition of nano-TCM for brain disorders, namely liposomal curcumin used in combination with standard therapy for high-grade glioma (NCT05768919) and curcumin nanoparticles evaluated as an adjunct intervention for schizophrenia-related cognitive outcomes (NCT02104752). This point should be stated explicitly, because it indicates that the field is still dominated by preclinical proof-of-concept studies rather than mature clinical translation. The scarcity of trial-stage nano-TCM systems likely reflects several unresolved barriers, including the compositional complexity of TCM-derived cargos, insufficient formulation standardization and batch-to-batch consistency, the process sensitivity of nanomedicine manufacturing, limited human pharmacokinetic and brain-exposure data, and the difficulty of aligning complex nanosystems with disease-specific clinical endpoints and therapeutic windows. In brain disorders, these challenges are further amplified by the need to simultaneously satisfy BBB delivery efficiency, central nervous system safety, route-dependent tolerability, and practical feasibility for repeated or indication-specific administration.

Conclusion and perspectives

Nanodelivery systems have emerged as powerful tools for improving the transport of active components from TCM across the BBB and for enhancing therapeutic outcomes in brain disorders. Substantial progress has been achieved in the design of nanocarriers, the understanding of BBB transport pathways, and the optimization of delivery performance. By integrating rational carrier design with appropriate BBB-crossing strategies, nanodelivery systems has markedly improved brain exposure, pharmacokinetic behavior, and therapeutic efficacy of TCM-derived agents, thereby expanding the potential for both single-compound delivery and multi-component formulations.

Despite these advances, the clinical translation of nano-based TCM delivery systems remains challenging. Nanomaterials frequently accumulate in non-target organs such as the liver, spleen, and kidneys, which may lead to off-target toxicity and long-term safety concerns. In addition, cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, and immunotoxicity are strongly influenced by physicochemical characteristics including particle size, morphology, surface properties, and core composition, making it difficult to balance therapeutic efficacy with biosafety. Moreover, although several studies have incorporated pathological BBB conditions into delivery design, standardized and quantitative evaluation of BBB-crossing efficiency remains inconsistent. In particular, stage-specific dynamic alterations in BBB structure and function under physiological and pathological conditions are not always systematically characterized, which may influence delivery performance and therapeutic reproducibility. The compositional complexity of TCM formulations further increases the demands on carrier compatibility, stability, and controllable synergistic interactions.

Future research should focus on several key directions. First, improving the biocompatibility and targeting precision of nanocarriers through surface engineering and stimulus-responsive designs will be essential to reduce off-target accumulation and enhance safety profiles. Second, a deeper understanding of BBB dynamics under disease conditions is needed, supported by quantitative evaluation methods and real-time monitoring technologies, to guide rational delivery system design. Third, greater emphasis should be placed on the systematic nano-formulation of TCM multicomponent systems, including compound combinations, extract-derived fractions, and classical formula-related preparations, so that the intrinsic multi-component and multi-target characteristics of TCM can be more faithfully translated into adaptable and multifunctional therapeutic platforms. With continued integration of mechanistic insights into BBB structure, transport pathways, and pathophysiological alterations with advances in nanodelivery systems, these efforts are expected to accelerate the clinical translation of TCM nanodelivery systems and facilitate the development of safer and more effective therapeutic strategies for brain disorders.
