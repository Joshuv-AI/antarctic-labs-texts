Segmentation regularized training for multi-domain deep learning registration applied to magnetic resonance-guided prostate cancer radiotherapy

Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) guided adaptive radiotherapy (MRgART) combines an onboard MR imager with a linear accelerator, enabling daily treatment adaptation through superior soft-tissue contrast [1], [2]. Daily adaptation requires fast, accurate organ of interest segmentation, commonly achieved through deformable image registration (DIR) for contour propagation [3], [4], [5]. DIR also enables dose accumulation for treatment quality assessment [6], [7], [8]. However, multi-institutional analysis demonstrated considerable variability in existing DIR implementations, with algorithm-dependent differences causing clinically relevant discrepancies in accumulated dose metrics [6]. Robust DIR generalizing across imaging platforms remains an unmet need.

Variational as well as deep learning DIR (DL-DIR) methods have been developed for MR-to-MR registration applied to MRgART for pelvis and abdominal cancers [6], [9], [10]. Unlike variational methods, DL-DIR methods directly compute the transformation between a pair of images, obviating the need to iteratively refine image alignment during inference. DL-DIR methods using unsupervised training such as VoxelMorph [11] and others [10], [12] learn to directly compute dense deformation fields by maximizing similarity of deformed and target images, eliminating the need for ground truth deformation supervision. However, DL methods are susceptible to MR imaging variations when applied to datasets that differ from training datasets [6], [13].

Data augmentation through generative adversarial networks (GAN) to create examples with realistic image variations have shown to improve generalizability [14], [15]. However, GAN requires additional datasets and increases complexity of the training framework overall. A second set of approaches uses the organ geometry to constrain DIR. The latter strategy has shown to be effective in both iterative or variational [16], [17], [18], [19] and DL-DIR methods [20], [21], [22]. Organ contours can be included explicitly as input masks or conditioning information [16], [23] or to optimize the cost function [20] for computing DIR. Hence, we adopted a joint registration-segmentation approach called ProRSeg previously shown to be effective for aligning MR-MR scans of patients with pancreatic cancers [10] for aligning pelvic MRI scans of patients with prostate cancers. Geometry regularization is applied as a loss to train the registration and segmentation network. The segmentation network trained in parallel provides additional regularization to the registration network as a multi-task objective. Following training, only the registration network is kept for testing.

This study evaluated ProRSeg for multi-domain MR-MR registration in prostate cancer radiotherapy with three objectives: (1) train ProRSeg with weighted segmentation regularization loss using longitudinal 3 T MRSim data, (2) evaluate generalization of ProRSeg on same-domain (MRSim-MRSim), cross-domain (MR-Linac-MR-Linac), and mixed-domain (MRSim-MR-Linac) datasets, and (3) assess feasibility of dose accumulation for treatment quality evaluation using ProRSeg generated DIR.

Materials and methods

Datasets and experiments

This retrospective analysis was approved by the MSKCC institutional review board (IRB protocol #21-129). Discovery dataset consisted of 3 Tesla T2-weighted MRI (TR: 4740 ms, TE: 100 ms) acquired for treatment planning simulation (MRSim) and at follow-up intervals up to 24 months post-RT from 34 patients. Follow-up frequency varied across patients and not all patients completed imaging at every scheduled interval. A total of 320 image volume pairs were available for model development (n = 262) and testing (n = 58). Data partitioning was performed at the patient level: 27 patients (79%) were assigned to model development and 7 patients (21%) to testing, with no patient overlap between sets. Hyperparameter optimization used 5-fold cross-validation on development data with patient-level fold assignment. The final model was trained on all 262 pairs from 27 development patients. All 58 pairs from 7 testing patients were reserved for final evaluation and never used during model development.

Cross-domain testing was performed using 1.5 Tesla MR-Linac images (TR: 1300 ms, TE: 87 ms) from 8 patients undergoing 5-fraction radiotherapy, generating 72 test pairs from inter-fraction 3D T2WI MRI scans. Mixed-domain testing evaluated registration between different scanner types using 7 patients who underwent both longitudinal MRSim follow-up and MR-Linac treatment. Each available MRSim timepoint for these patients was aligned to their corresponding first MR-Linac treatment fraction, generating 42 mixed-domain test pairs (Fig. 1B). All evaluation contours were obtained independent of the registration methods: MR-Linac fractions used treatment-approved contours from a clinically validated auto-segmentation system [24], approved by radiation oncologists. No contours were propagated using the registration methods under evaluation. Dose accumulation was performed on an independent cohort of 42 patients undergoing 5-fraction MRgART on MR-Linac, reserved exclusively for this analysis. Dose mapping combined warped doses from consecutive MR-Linac fractions with daily adaptive treatment plans [25].

Original MRSim images (480 × 480 × 52 voxels, 0.5 × 0.5 × 3 mm3) and MR-Linac images (448 × 448 × 125 voxels, 1.0 × 1.0 × 2 mm3) were resampled to 256 × 256 × 128 voxels using trilinear (images) and nearest-neighbor (masks) interpolation. For mixed-domain pairs, MRSim images were rigidly aligned to the MR-Linac first fraction. Segmentation masks were generated using a clinically validated auto-segmentation system [24] and approved by radiation oncologists. Intensities were clipped at the 10th and 90th percentiles and scaled to [0,1].

ProRSeg network architecture and training details

Fig. 1A shows ProRSeg architecture: a recurrent registration network (RRN) and recurrent segmentation network (RSN) optimized simultaneously. RRN refines deformation fields through 8 CLSTM iterations; RSN uses 9 CLSTM steps for segmentation.

RRN was optimized using a combination of image similarity (Lsim, patch-based mean square error), deformation smoothness (Lsmooth=‖∇u‖2), and weighted segmentation regularization (Lcons). Our segmentation regularization loss modified the original ProRSeg approach by using a weighted Dice (DSC) loss to give greater importance to highly deforming organs, such as the bladder, over the rectum and clinical target volume (CTV).

The RSN network is used for multi-task training and is removed following training, keeping only the RRN of ProRSeg, providing a single-backbone network during testing.

A grid search was performed to optimize the loss weights, evaluating λsmooth ∈ {1, 10, 30, 50} and λcons ∈ {1, 5, 10}. The best model, selected on the validation set, used λsmooth of 30 and λcons of 5.

Geometric metrics

ProRSeg was evaluated on the held-out same-, cross-, and mixed-domain datasets. On the same-domain set, ProRSeg was compared against a rigid registration baseline and two iterative methods: SyN [26], [27] and EVolution [28]. Geometric accuracy was quantified for the bladder, rectum, CTV, and urethra using the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), 95th Percentile Hausdorff Distance (HD95), and Mean Distance to Agreement (MDA).

VoxelMorph was implemented using its original single-backbone architecture, where one U-Net predicts the DVF, and a spatial transformer warps the moving image and segmentation masks to the target space. For VoxelMorph-Seg, organ-specific Dice loss was applied to the warped masks during training and backpropagated through the spatial transformer to regularize the DVF prediction.

Dose distributions from each fraction were warped to first-fraction anatomy using model-generated deformation vector fields and trilinear interpolation, then summed to generate the accumulated dose for the entire treatment course.

To compare model performance across the independent same-, cross-, and mixed-domain datasets, the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used as shown in Table S1. Segmentation differences between cross-domain and benchmark model DIRs were measured using paired, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Table S2). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Results

Imaging differences between 3T MRSim and 1.5T MR-Linac platforms (Supplementary Fig. S1) present challenges for cross-dataset model application, which we addressed using segmentation-regularized ProRSeg.

On same-domain (MRSim-MRSim) data (Fig. 2), all non-rigid methods achieved mean CTV DSC exceeding 0.80 with ProRSeg of 0.85 ± 0.04. For highly deforming organs, ProRSeg was the most accurate (bladder: DSC 0.88, HD95 2.99 mm; rectum: DSC 0.86, HD95 2.50 mm), followed by EVolution (bladder: DSC 0.81, p < 0.001; rectum: DSC 0.83, p < 0.001). Discussion

This study evaluated ProRSeg, a segmentation-regularized DIR model, for multi-domain MR-guided prostate cancer radiotherapy. ProRSeg generated robust DIR for both cross-domain and same-domain datasets and was applied to dose accumulation for assessing clinical compliance. Dose accumulation analysis demonstrated the feasibility of using ProRSeg for clinical compliance evaluation. Accumulated doses showed 100% compliance for mean CTV dose target, and 83.3% compliance for CTV D95 coverage and bladder sparing constraints, providing feedback for treatment protocol refinement.

Our study demonstrated the impact of segmentation regularized training with organ-specific weighting (bladder: 0.40, rectum: 0.30, prostate: 0.30) essential for achieving reliable performance for highly deforming organs. This provides the geometric accuracy necessary to map daily dose distributions back to reference anatomy for cumulative constraint evaluation. These results align with segmentation-guided registration principles demonstrated in cardiac imaging [14] and pelvic CT-CBCT [29], with direct validation for prostate MRI provided using segmentation regularized training for VoxelMorph, which improved performance. Segmentation regularization used in our work differs from input conditioning or masking approaches used for constraining registration that always requires a target segmentation mask as input [30], [31], [32] to compute the cost function for registration [23], [33], [34]. Hence, following training, our method does not require target segmentations during inference.

Whereas our work demonstrated similar or better performance on cross and same-domain datasets, prior work by [35] showed poor generalization across different domain datasets; however, our setting differed in that we focused on cross-domain DIR of same patients scanned at different field strengths (3 T MRSim vs. 1.5 T MR-Linac) during a 5-day treatment course with minimal anatomical changes. In contrast, Chaudhari et al. [35], combined multiple factors such as disease stage, labeling protocols, and scanner differences in the evaluated domains. Our longitudinal DIR scans acquired at 3-month follow-up intervals showed substantial performance drop despite same-domain imaging (MRSim-MRSim), which aligns with their findings on anatomical variability.

ProRSeg shares the original construction of VoxelMorph for the registration network but includes CLSTM layers for progressive deformation and a segmentation subnetwork, used only during training serving as multi-task learning objective and removed for testing. Hence, only the single-backbone registration network is used for testing as is the case for VoxelMorph and VoxelMorph-Seg methods. On the in-distribution MRSim test set, VoxelMorph-Seg improved substantially over standard VoxelMorph (e.g., rectum DSC from 0.65 to 0.84), confirming that segmentation regularization benefits registration accuracy. ProRSeg achieved further improvement (rectum DSC 0.86), with statistically significant DSC differences for all structures compared to VoxelMorph-Seg (p < 0.05).

For cross-domain generalization (MR-Linac-MR-Linac), VoxelMorph-Seg showed a performance drop on cross-domain data compared to in-distribution results. On the other hand, ProRSeg maintained or improved its performance relative to the training domain, indicating that multi-task learning provided via the segmentation subnetwork enhanced testing performance, even when the segmentation network is no longer used in testing. This result is consistent with prior works showing multi-task training improves generalizability [36], [37], [38]. The single-task training approach is more parameter-efficient and may be preferable when training and test domains match, whereas the multi-task training approach demonstrated advantages for multi-domain applicability.

Despite good cross-domain performance, ProRSeg (DSC: 0.51, HD95: 3.97 mm) underperformed for urethra compared to the EVolution method (DSC: 0.61, HD95: 3.17 mm), indicating that direct DIR computation is ineffective to account for small tubular structures.

This study has a few limitations. First, domain generalization analysis only considered two different acquisition platforms. Future analysis would focus on extension to multi-institutional data. Second, our dose compliance analysis is a preliminary feasibility study and large-scale analysis is work for the future. Additionally, our dose accumulation analysis used only ProRSeg-derived deformations since comparisons to additional methods would only show variations between the methods but is insufficient to establish accuracy with respect to a ground truth. It is also possible to combine the surrogate ground truth digital twin or physical phantoms with known deformations to evaluate accuracy of deformation vector fields. Evaluation using digital twins or physical phantoms with known deformations is planned for future work. Last, consistent with prior works, we used contour-based metrics for assessing registration accuracy [39], [40], [41]. Target registration error (TRE) as suggested by prior works [3], [42], are difficult to identify for highly deformable organs including bladder, rectum and prostate as used in this work. Nevertheless, this study for the first time, to our best knowledge, presents a near complete analysis starting from the evaluation of generalization performance of a DL-DIR method to dose accumulation based clinical compliance evaluation for MR-Guided Prostate Cancer Radiotherapy. Furthermore, the cross-domain generalization addresses a critical challenge in domain adaptation for medical imaging [43], where maintaining performance across imaging protocols remains a barrier to clinical deployment.

In conclusion, segmentation-regularized training improved registration accuracy for both ProRSeg and VoxelMorph. ProRSeg achieved the highest accuracy for highly deforming organs and generalized from 3 T MRSim training data to 1.5 T MR-Linac test data. Dose accumulation using ProRSeg-generated deformations showed 83.3% compliance with institutional CTV coverage and bladder sparing constraints. These findings support the feasibility of segmentation-regularized DIR for multi-domain registration and treatment quality assessment in MR-guided prostate radiotherapy.
