Nonlinear dose–response evaluation of RenalMax supplementation in broiler chickens

Introduction

The increasing restriction on the use of antibiotic growth promoters has stimulated interest in phytogenic additives and functional nutrients as sustainable strategies to improve poultry health and productivity. Phytogenic compounds contain numerous bioactive constituents with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties that may enhance growth performance, nutrient utilization, and physiological homeostasis in poultry (Flees et al., 2021b; Wang et al., 2021; Biswas & Kim, 2025). Their efficacy may be further enhanced when combined with complementary nutrients that target multiple physiological pathways involved in metabolism and oxidative balance.

Among phytogenic ingredients, Urtica dioica (stinging nettle) has received considerable attention because of its high concentration of polyphenols and flavonoids with antioxidant activity. Experimental studies have shown that nettle extract can reduce oxidative stress and modify serum biochemical variables associated with liver and kidney function under different physiological challenges (Adejuwon et al., 2014; Buchko et al., 2019, 2023; Muceniece et al., 2025). Likewise, vitamins C and B-complex participate in antioxidant defense, energy metabolism, and cellular biochemical reactions, while sorbitol and ammonium chloride have traditionally been incorporated into poultry formulations to support metabolic and urinary functions. Collectively, these components may exert complementary physiological effects, although their combined responses in broiler chickens have not been adequately investigated.

Most previous studies have evaluated individual phytogenic extracts or single nutrients, making it difficult to determine whether combining phytogenic and nutritional components provides additional biological benefits. Consequently, information remains limited regarding the integrated effects of multi-component formulations on growth performance, serum biochemical variables, antioxidant status, lipid metabolism, and mineral metabolism in broiler chickens. Evaluating the complete formulation rather than its individual ingredients is particularly relevant because commercial products are administered as mixtures in which interactions among components may influence the overall biological response.

Therefore, the objective of the present study was to evaluate the effects of RenalMax, a phytogenic formulation containing nettle extract, sorbitol, ammonium chloride, and vitamins B1, B2, B6, and C, on growth performance, serum biochemical variables, antioxidant status, lipid metabolism, and mineral metabolism in broiler chickens. In addition, nonlinear dose-response analyses were performed to characterize the response patterns across supplementation levels and to estimate the supplementation range associated with the most favorable biological responses under the experimental conditions.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study protocol adheres to the guidelines established by the Iranian Council of Animal Care and it has been approved by the Research Animal Ethics Committee (AEUOZ-2012|UP-2020-BR-21) at the University of Zabol.

Birds, housing, and experimental design

A total of 400 one-day-old male broiler chicks (Ross 308) were obtained from a local hatchery (initial body weight: 39.4 ± 2.15 g). Birds were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments with five replicate pens per treatment and 20 birds per pen. Each pen measured 2.0 × 1.5 m (3.0 m²), resulting in an initial stocking density of approximately 6.7 birds/m². Birds were housed on a concrete floor covered with fresh wood shavings, and each pen was equipped with one bell-shaped drinker and one feeder. Environmental conditions, including temperature and lighting programs, were managed according to commercial Ross 308 recommendations. Drinking water was the method of treatment administration; control (no RenalMax), and RenalMax concentrations of 0.25, 0.50, and 1.00 mL/L were added on alternating days, beginning on day 10, as per the manufacturer's guidelines. The initiation of supplementation on day 10 was selected because birds enter a period of accelerated growth and increasing metabolic demand after the early brooding phase. On each supplementation day, RenalMax was freshly diluted in drinking water and provided continuously throughout the day. Fresh treated water was prepared and supplied according to bird age and water consumption to maintain uniform exposure. The birds had continuous access to food and water, receiving a commercial diet (Table 2) for the duration of the experiment, which was formulated accoding to Ross 308 breeder guidelines (Aviagen, 2022). A 33 °C temperature was used for a week, after which it was lowered by 2 °C per week to 22 °C. A continuous lighting protocol was used for 3 days, then changed to a 16L:8D cycle.

Performance parameters

To calculate body weight gain, feed intake, and feed conversion ratio, birds were weighed on a pen basis at hatch and 7, 14, 21, 35, and 42 days old. Mortality was recorded daily throughout the experimental period, including the date of occurrence and suspected cause of death. The corrected feed conversion ratio was calculated considering mortality according to standard broiler performance evaluation procedures. On day 42, two birds per pen (n = 10 birds per treatment) were selected, euthanized, and dissected to assess the relative weights of their internal organs. The breast, thigh, abdominal fat, liver, and heart were excised and weighed. The relative weights of tissues (breast meat yield: BMY and thigh meat yield: TMY) and organs were expressed as a percentage of live body weight.

Blood collection

At 21 and 42 days of age, two birds from each pen were randomly selected for blood collection. To minimize the influence of circadian variation on blood biochemical parameters, all blood samples were collected at the same time of day (08:00–10:00 h) before routine feeding and management activities. Blood samples were collected from the brachial vein into sterile tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature before serum separation by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min. The obtained serum samples were stored at −20 °C until biochemical analyses.

Biochemical analyses

Total protein (TP) concentration was quantified by the biuret method, whereas serum albumin was measured using the bromocresol green (BCG) method with commercial kits (Pars Azmun, Tehran, Iran). Absorbance was recorded at 630 nm with a spectrophotometer, and serum globulin was determined as the difference between total protein and albumin concentrations (Kumar et al., 2005). Serum concentrations of cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), triglycerides (TG), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were determined spectrophotometrically using a microplate reader (Stat Fax 3200; Awareness Technology Inc., Palm City, FL, USA) with Pars Azmun diagnostic kits. Hepatic enzyme activity

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activities were assessed according to the procedure of Reitman and Frankel (1957), in which 0.3 ml of serum was combined with 300 ml of reagent from Pars Azmun. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was analyzed by the DEA method, using 20 µl of serum mixed with 1 ml of reagent and incubated at 37 °C (Sun et al., 2018). The absorbance changes for ALT, AST, and ALP were monitored at 340 nm.

Antioxidant assay

Plasma antioxidant indices, including total antioxidant capacity (TAC), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPX), were determined colorimetrically with a Biomate 5 spectrophotometer (Thermo Electron Corporation, Rochester, NY, USA) using commercial assay kits (Randox Laboratories Ltd., Crumlin, UK) (Baser et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2017). Lipid peroxidation was evaluated as malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration via the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method. For this assay, 0.5 ml of serum was mixed with 1 ml of trichloroacetic acid, centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, and 0.5 ml of the supernatant was subsequently reacted with 0.5 ml of 2-thiobarbituric acid solution. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 10 min, cooled, and the absorbance was read at 532 nm (Wang et al., 2008).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The pen was considered the experimental unit for all statistical analyses (n = 5 pens per treatment). For carcass traits and all serum biochemical, hepatic enzyme, antioxidant, and mineral variables, two birds were sampled from each pen, and the mean of the two birds was used as a single pen observation for statistical analysis. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed with RenalMax inclusion level as the main fixed effect. Polynomial contrasts were applied to assess linear and quadratic dose–response trends. Model assumptions (normality and homogeneity of variance) were verified using residual diagnostics. To characterize the dose–response relationships and estimate the supplementation level associated with the most favorable responses, parabolic polynomial (PP), two-slope linear (LL), and two-slope quadratic–linear (QL) regression models were fitted following Khosravi et al. (2016). Model performance was compared using the coefficient of determination (R²), residual standard deviation (Sy.x), root mean square error (RMSE), and corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc), and the model with the best overall fit was selected for each response variable.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to explore multivariate patterns among serum biochemical, hepatic enzyme, antioxidant, and performance variables. The first two principal components were retained to visualize clustering and interpret variable contributions to the observed variance. All results are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM), where SEM reflects variation among pen means. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) and SAS (2002).

Results

Growth performance and carcass characteristics

Average daily water consumption was recorded at the pen level throughout the experimental period. Mean water consumption was approximately 7, 14, 16–18, 20–22, 25–28, and 32–35 L/pen/day during weeks 1 to 6, respectively. Based on these values, estimated RenalMax intake increased proportionally with age and supplementation level. For example, at 0.50 mL/L, estimated daily intake increased from approximately 3.5 mL/pen/day during week 1 to approximately 16.8 mL/pen/day during week 6.

RenalMax supplementation affected growth performance (Table 3). Feed intake increased significantly (ANOVA P < 0.0001) and exhibited both significant linear (P-linear < 0.0001) and quadratic (P-quadratic < 0.0001) responses, with the highest mean value observed at 0.50 mL/L. Body weight gain also differed among treatments (ANOVA P < 0.0001) and showed significant linear (P-linear = 0.019) and quadratic (P-quadratic < 0.0001) responses. Feed conversion ratio differed among treatments (ANOVA P = 0.018), although neither the linear (P = 0.127) nor quadratic (P = 0.206) contrast was significant. Dressing percentage differed among treatments (ANOVA P < 0.0001) with significant linear and quadratic responses (both P < 0.0001), although the numerical differences among treatments were small. Thigh meat yield (P = 0.206) and breast meat yield (P = 0.543) were unaffected by supplementation.

Serum lipid profile and glucose

RenalMax supplementation affected all lipid variables and serum glucose (Table 5). Triglycerides, VLDL, total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and glucose all differed among treatments (all ANOVA P < 0.001). Dose–response modeling

Dose–response analyses demonstrated nonlinear responses for most measured variables (Table 10). Model comparison using R², Sy.x, and AIC showed that LL model provided the best overall fit for most serum biochemical, lipid, hepatic enzyme, antioxidant, and oxidative stress variables. The PP model provided the best fit for calcium, phosphorus, and HDL, whereas QL and LL performed similarly for uric acid, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine. Across response variables, the estimated optima generally occurred within the range of approximately 0.4 to 0.7 mL/L RenalMax. Because only four supplementation levels were experimentally evaluated, these estimates should be interpreted as model-derived predictions within the tested range rather than definitive biological optima.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the effects of RenalMax, a blend of phytogenics and essential nutrients, including nettle extract, sorbitol, ammonium chloride, and vitamins B1, B2, B6, and C, on broiler performance, renal function, lipid metabolism, hepatic enzymes, antioxidant status, and mineral metabolism. However, it is important to distinguish statistical significance from biological relevance. Although some variables, such as dressing percentage, showed statistically significant treatment effects, the numerical differences among treatments were small and are unlikely to have practical importance under commercial production conditions. Therefore, the biological interpretation of the present study was based primarily on the magnitude and consistency of the dose-response relationships described by the regression models rather than on statistical significance alone.

Our findings revealed that RenalMax supplementation significantly enhanced growth performance, modified serum renal- and hepatic-related biochemical markers, altered lipid and glucose profiles, and enhanced antioxidant-related variables. Because RenalMax is a multi-component formulation, the present experiment cannot distinguish the contribution of individual ingredients. Nevertheless, based on previous literature, the observed responses may be associated with the combined biological activities of its phytogenic compounds, vitamins, sorbitol, and ammonium chloride. The nettle extract (Urtica dioica L.) is rich in polyphenols such as chlorogenic acid (301 μg/g), caffeic acid (171 μg/g), kaempferol (301 μg/g), and quercetin (303 μg/g), which possess strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunostimulatory properties. Sorbitol has been reported to act as an osmotic agent that improves nutrient absorption and reduces early inflammatory responses, contributing to better growth performance and reduced serum glucose and lipid levels (Takahashi et al., 2002). These compounds may contribute to reducing oxidative stress and enhance immune function, contributing to favorable changes in serum biochemical markers associated with renal and hepatic function in broilers (Hashemi & Davoodi, 2010; Ahmadipour & Khajali, 2019).

The results demonstrate significant enhancements in body weight gain and feed intake, indicative of improved growth efficiency and nutrient utilization, aligning with previous findings on phytogenic-nutrient blends (Wang et al., 2021; Al-Garadi et al., 2025). While dressing percentage and liver weight were significantly affected by RenalMax supplementation, the numerical differences in dressing yield were relatively small, suggesting a limited biological impact. Notably, abdominal fat deposition was markedly reduced at the optimal inclusion level, indicating improved lipid metabolism and energy utilization. In contrast, thigh and breast meat yields were not significantly influenced, indicating that RenalMax did not alter muscle deposition patterns. Similarly, heart weight remained unchanged across treatments, suggesting no adverse or stimulatory effects on cardiac development. The reduction in fat pad alongside stable muscle yield suggests a favorable shift in body composition without compromising lean tissue accretion. Notably, RenalMax supplementation reduced serum uric acid, creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen concentrations compared with the control treatment. These changes are consistent with alterations in nitrogen metabolism and serum biochemical markers commonly associated with renal function. Likewise, reductions in AST, ALT, and ALP activities indicate favorable changes in serum biochemical markers associated with hepatic function. However, because the present study did not include histopathological examination, molecular analyses, or functional organ assessments, these findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of nephroprotection or hepatoprotection (Salih, 2015; Siouda & Abdennour, 2015; Dhouibi et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022). Liver enzyme activity reductions (AST, ALT, ALP) reflect hepatoprotective effects and reduced oxidative damage, coherent with phytogenics’ antioxidative capacities documented in poultry. Enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase, alongside reduced malondialdehyde, signify strengthened antioxidant defense and diminished oxidative stress, aligning with established roles of supplementation in augmenting cellular redox balance (Surai et al., 2019; Bacou et al., 2021; Abubakar et al., 2023). Finally, marked increases in serum calcium and phosphorus indicate improved mineral absorption and regulation critical for skeletal health and metabolic functions, corroborating findings on mineral metabolism interplay in poultry (Yang et al., 2025). Phytogenics such as nettle extract are rich in bioactive compounds like flavonoids and phenolics, which have been shown to enhance digestive enzyme activity and nutrient absorption (Hashemi and, Davoodi2010). The lack of change in thigh meat yield suggests that RenalMax did not significantly influence protein accretion in muscle tissues. The stable FCR combined with increased intake and gain implies that RenalMax optimizes nutrient utilization without compromising feed efficiency. These results corroborate findings by Rahimi et al. (2011), who reported enhanced broiler growth with phytogenic blends, and reinforce the notion that phytogenic compounds can act synergistically with vitamins to promote growth.

The reductions observed in serum uric acid, creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen suggest altered nitrogen metabolism and changes in serum biochemical variables commonly associated with renal function. These changes may partly reflect biological activities previously reported for nettle extract and other phytogenic compounds (Chrubasik et al., 2007), although the contribution of individual ingredients cannot be distinguished in the present experiment. Ammonium chloride’s role in acidifying urine may have further enhanced renal clearance of nitrogenous wastes, improved nitrogen utilization efficiency and reducing metabolic burden (Horst & Jorgensen, 1974). Lower circulating nitrogenous wastes also likely contribute to improved metabolic status and reduced renal stress, consistent with studies by Abubakar et al. (2023), who demonstrated that phytogenic compounds can ameliorate renal dysfunction markers in poultry.

RenalMax supplementation markedly improved lipid profiles, with substantial decreases in triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL, and VLDL, alongside a dramatic increase in HDL levels. These findings underscore the lipid-lowering potential of the phytogenic components, particularly flavonoids and vitamin C, which exert hypolipidemic and antioxidant effects by modulating lipid metabolism and preventing oxidative modification of lipoproteins (Kotze, 1975; Domjanic Drozdek et al., 2022). The significant reduction in LDL-cholesterol coupled with HDL elevation is particularly important for cardiovascular health and mirrors outcomes reported by Tehseen et al. (2016) Jang et al. (2008) in broilers supplemented with herbal extracts. Lower glucose concentrations highlight improved carbohydrate metabolism, possibly mediated by vitamin B complex and C, which enhance enzymatic activities critical for glucose utilization (Öztürk Özkan, 2018). Chlorogenic acid, in particular, has been shown to improve growth performance, alleviate oxidative stress, and support hepatic function in broilers by elevating antioxidant enzyme activities and reducing lipid peroxidation (Liang & Kitts, 2015). Both quercetin and kaempferol have been shown to possess strong antioxidant properties. For instance, quercetin oxidation leads to the formation of metabolites that exhibit enhanced antioxidant potency compared to their precursors, indicating that these compounds can effectively protect cells from oxidative stress (Speisky et al., 2023). Additionally, flavonol glucuronide conjugates of quercetin and kaempferol have been identified as contributing to antioxidant effects, which may play a role in reducing lipid peroxidation in cellular membranes (Fernández‐Arroyo et al., 2012).

The pronounced reductions in AST, ALT, and ALP enzyme activities indicate favorable changes in serum enzyme activities commonly associated with hepatic function, likely resulting from its antioxidant-rich phytogenic and vitamin components. These enzymes are sensitive markers of liver injury and oxidative damage; their normalization suggests reduced hepatic stress and improved liver integrity (Samih & Ahami, 2019). The significant decrease in ALP, an enzyme linked to biliary function and liver metabolism, further supports improved hepatic health, consistent with previous studies demonstrating the ability of nettle extract and vitamin supplementation to alleviate liver toxicity and enhance hepatic antioxidant defense (Ramírez-Marroquín & Jiménez-Arellanes, 2019).

RenalMax significantly enhanced antioxidant capacity, demonstrated by increased TAC and elevated activities of GPX and SOD, along with a marked reduction in MDA levels, a key biomarker for lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage. These antioxidant improvements may reflect the combined biological activity of the phytogenic compounds and vitamins present in RenalMax (Sies, 2015; Mnisi et al., 2022). Such data reinforce existing literature on phytogenic antioxidants improving poultry oxidative status and reducing oxidative stress during intensive growth periods (Mohamed & Hassan, 2023). By reducing oxidative damage, RenalMax may contribute to better cellular integrity, immune function, and physiological status.

The significant increase in serum calcium and phosphorus concentrations implies enhanced mineral absorption and metabolic utilization. Vitamins B and C are known to facilitate calcium and phosphorus metabolism through improved intestinal uptake and bone mineralization processes (NRC, 1994). Additionally, ammonium chloride’s metabolic acidifying effect can increase calcium mobilization from bones, optimizing mineral balance (Pearlin et al., 2020). Improved mineral status supports skeletal development and metabolic functions essential for growth, paralleling findings by Flees et al. (2021a) who reported augmented calcium and phosphorus levels following phytogenic supplementation in broilers. Ammonium chloride also plays a role in mineral metabolism by promoting the mobilization of calcium and phosphorus. Research indicates that NH4Cl can lead to increased serum ionized calcium concentrations and higher urinary calcium excretion, which are essential for bone health (Ching et al., 1989). This mobilization is vital for the development and maintenance of strong bones in broilers, which is critical for their growth and overall metabolic function. The acidifying effect of ammonium chloride leads to a decrease in urinary pH, which promotes the excretion of hydrogen ions and enhances the clearance of nitrogenous wastes (Edelmann et al., 1967). This mechanism is crucial for maintaining acid-base balance in the body, especially in broilers that may experience metabolic stress.

Dose-response models generally predicted maximal responses within approximately 0.5–0.7 mL/L RenalMax. However, because these estimates were derived from regression analyses based on only four supplementation levels, they should be considered model-based predictions within the experimental range rather than definitive biological optima. This aligns with previous phytogenic research highlighting dose-dependent efficacy with potential plateauing at higher levels (Windisch et al., 2008). The PCA distinguished treatment groups based on metabolic, renal, and growth markers. PC1 was primarily characterized by high positive loadings for lipid-related variables, hepatic enzymes, and MDA together with negative loadings for antioxidant variables and growth traits, whereas PC2 was mainly associated with nitrogen metabolism variables. These multivariate analyses support the observed patterns among measured variables but should not be interpreted as direct evidence of specific physiological processes.

From a practical production perspective, the estimated optimal supplementation range of 0.5–0.6 mL/L should be interpreted as a model-derived response point within the tested range rather than a fixed commercial recommendation. The application value of RenalMax should be further evaluated considering additive cost, farm management conditions, and production objectives. Because RenalMax was administered through drinking water, this approach may provide operational flexibility in commercial broiler production, particularly during growing and finishing periods characterized by rapid growth and increased metabolic demands. However, further studies are required to evaluate its long-term safety, economic return, and applicability during different production phases, including the starter and brooding periods.

Conclusion

RenalMax supplementation modified growth performance, serum biochemical variables, antioxidant indices, and mineral-related variables in broiler chickens. Collectively, these responses indicate that the formulation may favorably influence several physiological processes associated with growth, lipid metabolism, oxidative status, and nitrogen metabolism. Dose-response modeling generally identified maximal predicted responses between approximately 0.5 and 0.6 mL/L; however, these values should be interpreted cautiously because they were derived from regression models based on only four supplementation levels. Additional studies incorporating more dose levels together with histopathological and molecular assessments are warranted to validate these findings and confirm the underlying biological mechanisms.
