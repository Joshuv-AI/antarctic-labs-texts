Protocol for identification of intronic lariat RNAs by RNA deep sequencing in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Haoran Ge, Xiaotuo Zhang, Qi Tang, Binglian Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLariat RNAs formed from excised introns during RNA splicing typically undergo DBR1-mediated debranching and degradation; however, many lariat RNAs accumulate stably in various organisms. Here, we present a protocol for constructing RNA sequencing libraries and a bioinformatic pipeline to identify intronic lariat RNAs in Arabidopsis thaliana. We describe steps for extracting and enriching RNA, library preparation, and capturing reads flanking branchpoint-to-5′ splice site junctions through sequencing. We then detail procedures to identify lariat RNAs derived from excised introns using computational screening.For details on the original application and validation of this protocol, please refer to Ge et al.1Subject areas: Bioinformatics, Sequence analysis, Genetics, Genomics, RNAseq, Model Organisms, Plant sciences, Molecular Biology

Before you begin

Intronic lariat RNAs are circular splicing byproducts formed during pre-mRNA splicing through a 2′–5′ phosphodiester bond at the branch point. Their unique lariat structure makes them resistant to exonuclease degradation and often more stable than linear intronic intermediates. This protocol presents a comprehensive and broadly applicable workflow for the genome-wide identification and quantification of lariat RNAs. The protocol was optimized and experimentally validated using Arabidopsis seedlings in our associated study. In principle, the computational and experimental framework may be extended to other eukaryotic species, including plants, animals, and other model organisms. However, its performance is expected to depend on the quality of genome annotation and the availability of branch-point annotations or accurate branch-point prediction tools. Adaptation to other species certainly requires high-quality genome and branch-point annotation which may not be available for many non-model organisms.

The workflow integrates both biochemical enrichment and a customized computational pipeline to improve the sensitivity and specificity of lariat RNA detection. Total RNA is first subjected to sequential DNase I and RNase R treatment, followed by rRNA depletion and controlled RNA fragmentation. This process enriches stable circular lariat structures while substantially reducing linear RNA background, making it suitable for complex tissues with high transcript diversity.

By combining optimized sample preparation with rigorous in silico filtering, this protocol provides a robust and scalable method for lariatome profiling and overcomes common limitations associated with low sequencing depth, transient intermediate capture, and species-specific transcriptome complexity. Before starting, ensure that all reagents, consumables, and equipment are prepared under RNase-free conditions to prevent RNA degradation.

Innovation

This protocol presents a comprehensive, integrated workflow optimized specifically for the genome-wide identification and quantification of lariat RNAs in plant models. While standard transcriptomic approaches struggle to capture these transient, low-abundance splicing intermediates, our method introduces significant advancements by coupling stringent biochemical enrichment with a customized computational pipeline.

First, the biochemical workflow is highly optimized for complex plant tissues. By employing a sequential DNase I and RNase R enzymatic treatment, followed by efficient rRNA depletion and strictly controlled thermal fragmentation, the protocol maximizes the retention of stable circular lariat structures while heavily depleting the linear RNA background.

The major innovation, however, lies in the specialized, dual-layered bioinformatics approach. Rather than relying solely on standard linear alignment, we implemented a customized, branch-point-guided quantification module that mechanically splits introns into distinct loop and tail regions. By enforcing a rigorous empirical threshold (FPKM ≥ 5 and a loop/tail ratio > 2), the pipeline reliably discriminates genuine lariat RNAs from co-transcribed linear artifacts. Furthermore, we integrated an independent back-splicing junction (BSJ) validation step with strict sequence-overlap and genomic-distance filtering criteria (≤2 nt gap, ≥20 nt overlap, ≤50 kb distance). By merging these sample-specific biochemical optimizations and rigorous in silico filtering modules into a single, cohesive framework, this protocol provides a highly robust and reproducible method for profiling the lariatome, effectively overcoming previous limitations related to high false-positive rates and insufficient sequencing depth.

Expected outcomes

Following this protocol, researchers should obtain high-quality, strand-specific RNA-seq libraries that are strongly enriched for lariat RNA species after RNase R treatment and rRNA depletion. Successful library preparation typically yields sufficient material for paired-end sequencing (150 bp), with library size distributions centered around ∼250–400 bp (including adaptors), as assessed by Bioanalyzer or similar platforms. Efficient rRNA removal should result in a low proportion of rRNA-derived reads (generally <5–10%), thereby enabling deeper coverage of intronic and lariat-derived sequences. High-quality input RNA is expected to preserve library complexity and improve representation of intact lariat RNA structures. After sequencing, libraries should exhibit high base-calling quality (typically Q30 ≥85%) and minimal residual adapter contamination following read preprocessing, reflecting successful library construction and sequencing performance.

After read filtering and alignment, a high mapping rate to corresponding reference genome of the target species (typically >80%) is expected when using consistent parameters. Duplicate removal is included to minimize potential PCR amplification bias. Because duplicate reads in RNA-seq data may also arise from highly abundant transcripts, users may evaluate duplication levels before applying this step. In our datasets, duplicate removal had only a limited impact on lariat RNA detection and overall library complexity. Quantification of intronic regions, including loop and tail segments defined by branch point annotation, should produce reproducible read count distributions across biological replicates. Principal component analysis (PCA) is expected to show tight clustering of biological replicates within the same biological condition or sample group, indicating good experimental reproducibility and low technical variability.

Using the defined criteria (FPKM ≥ 5 and loop/tail ratio > 2), a robust set of candidate lariat RNAs should be identified. The number of identified lariat RNAs will depend on the species, tissue type, sequencing depth, branch-point annotation quality, and experimental conditions. Therefore, no universal expectation regarding the total number of detected candidates should be assumed. The protocol has also been successfully applied to publicly available datasets from additional species using species-specific genome annotations and branch-point resources. These lariat RNAs should display a characteristic enrichment of reads in loop regions relative to tail regions, reflecting resistance to RNase R digestion. Genome browser visualization (e.g., IGV) should reveal distinct coverage patterns over introns, with clear accumulation near branch point–associated regions.

For back-splicing junction (BSJ) detection, the pipeline is expected to yield a high-confidence set of split reads supporting head-to-tail junctions. After stringent filtering (gap ≤2 nt, overlap ≥20 nt, genomic distance ≤50 kb, same chromosome and strand), the resulting BSJ events should represent a refined subset with low false-positive rates. These junctions can be exported in BED format for downstream analysis, and corresponding BAM files containing supporting reads should allow direct visualization and manual validation. In genome browsers, true BSJ events are expected to appear as split-read alignments spanning circularized junctions with consistent orientation.

Overall, this protocol should generate three primary data outputs: (1) quantified lariat RNA profiles with loop/tail enrichment metrics, (2) normalized expression values (e.g., FPKM) for intronic regions, and (3) a curated list of BSJ sites with supporting reads. These datasets enable downstream analyses such as differential lariat RNA identification, correlation with gene expression changes, and structural validation of lariat RNAs and branch-point-associated BSJ-like intermediates. When properly executed, the workflow provides a reproducible and sensitive framework for studying lariat RNA metabolism and back-splicing events at transcriptome scale.

Limitations

The success of this protocol is highly dependent on the initial quality of the RNA and the strict control of biochemical steps. Environmental RNase contamination or the use of partially degraded RNA prior to RNase R treatment can severely compromise the library, leading to the loss and underrepresentation of lariat RNAs. Additionally, the protocol is highly sensitive to the efficiency of the rRNA depletion step; incomplete rRNA removal will reduce effective sequencing depth for lariat-derived reads, resulting in insufficient read depth to reliably detect rare lariat species. Furthermore, the RNA fragmentation step is highly sensitive to temperature and time deviations. Even minor over-incubation at 80°C can cause excessive fragmentation, potentially destroying the structural integrity of the RNA loops and hindering downstream bioinformatic mapping.

This protocol focuses on the enrichment and identification of lariat RNAs and does not require dedicated control samples for routine implementation. However, inclusion of untreated RNA samples and no-RNase R controls can facilitate assessment of enrichment efficiency and help distinguish bona fide lariat-derived signals from background reads. For experiments involving quantitative comparisons between biological conditions, external RNA spike-in controls may also be considered to monitor technical variability and support normalization. These controls were not required for the workflow described here but may improve data interpretation in specific experimental settings.

Computationally, the protocol possesses inherent limitations related to alignment and filtering parameters. The mechanical reliance on short-read sequencing (150 bp paired-end) restricts the ability to unambiguously align split reads across repetitive, complex, or highly homologous genomic regions, which can lead to mapping biases. To mitigate false positives, the pipeline employs strict filtering criteria, such as limiting the maximum genomic distance to 50 kb and enforcing rigid gap and overlap constraints (≤2 nt gap, ≥20 nt overlap). While these stringent parameters improve overall accuracy, they create a limitation where the protocol may artificially exclude genuine, long-range lariat RNAs or those with non-canonical structures (false negatives). Finally, high transcriptomic variability between biological replicates, a common biological and environmental factor in RNA experiments, can disproportionately reduce the statistical power of differential expression tools like edgeR and DESeq2, making the identification of differential lariat RNAs unreliable in datasets with high baseline biological noise.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Variability in lariat RNA detection and quantification due to differences in RNA quality, library preparation, and sequencing depth (related to Steps 1–6, 16).

Potential solution

•RNA quality and integrity: Before RNase R treatment, RNA integrity should be assessed using a Bioanalyzer, TapeStation, or equivalent platform. Samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) values below 7 are more likely to produce inconsistent lariat RNA detection, whereas RIN values ≥7 (preferably ≥8) generally provide reliable results. Electropherograms showing extensive degradation or loss of distinct rRNA peaks indicate poor RNA quality and may necessitate RNA re-isolation. To minimize degradation, RNA samples should be stored at −80°C and repeated freeze–thaw cycles should be avoided.

Problem 2

Inconsistent lariat RNA identification due to variability in intron annotation and branch point definition (related to Steps 9–12).

Potential solution

Accurate lariat RNA identification depends on the use of consistent genome and transcript annotations. All samples should be analyzed using the same genome assembly and the corresponding gene annotation release for the species under investigation. Mixing annotation versions can alter intron coordinates and transcript structures, leading to inconsistent loop/tail definitions and biased quantification. For species with well-curated annotations, users should obtain genome and annotation files from the same source and release (e.g., Ensembl, Ensembl Plants, RefSeq, GENCODE, Araport, or other species-specific databases) to ensure coordinate compatibility.

Branch-point annotation is another important source of variability. Whenever available, experimentally validated branch-point datasets should be used. Such resources have been reported for several model organisms, including Arabidopsis,13 human14 and yeast.15 For species lacking published branch-point annotations, branch points may be predicted computationally using established branch-point prediction tools or by identifying characteristic branch-point sequence motifs within annotated introns.16,17,18 To minimize technical variability, the same prediction method, software version, and parameter settings should be applied consistently across all samples.

To evaluate annotation quality, users should inspect a subset of representative introns using genome browsers such as IGV. Consistent enrichment within predicted loop regions and correct positioning of loop and tail segments relative to intron boundaries provide evidence that branch-point annotations and intron segmentation are functioning as expected. Systematic discrepancies may indicate annotation incompatibilities or inaccurate branch-point predictions and should be resolved before downstream lariat RNA identification.

Problem 3

Reduced accuracy in BSJ detection due to improper handling of split reads and alignment artifacts (related to Steps 16–20).

Back-splicing junction (BSJ) identification relies heavily on correct alignment of split reads. Misalignment, multi-mapping reads, or improper BAM file processing can introduce false positives or lead to missed BSJ events, reducing confidence in detected circularization signals.

Potential solution

•Read sorting and preprocessing: Ensure BAM/SAM files are properly sorted by read name before split-read analysis. Incorrect sorting can disrupt read pairing and affect BSJ detection accuracy.

Read sorting and preprocessing: Ensure BAM/SAM files are properly sorted by read name before split-read analysis. Incorrect sorting can disrupt read pairing and affect BSJ detection accuracy.

•Stringent filtering criteria: Apply consistent and strict thresholds for gap size, overlap length, and genomic distance. This reduces spurious alignments and improves the reliability of detected BSJ events.

Stringent filtering criteria: Apply consistent and strict thresholds for gap size, overlap length, and genomic distance. This reduces spurious alignments and improves the reliability of detected BSJ events.

•Handling multi-mapping reads: Ambiguously mapped reads can generate false-positive BSJ calls, particularly in repetitive genomic regions. In this workflow, BWA-MEM alignments are processed using a custom BSJ detection script. Secondary alignments are not automatically discarded; instead, only secondary alignments that cluster with the corresponding primary alignment on the same chromosome, have a compatible strand orientation, and occur within 50 kb of the primary alignment are retained. Candidate BSJ reads are further required to have all mapped segments located within the predicted BSJ boundaries. Secondary alignments failing these criteria are excluded from downstream analysis. This strategy reduces spurious BSJ calls while preserving informative split-read evidence supporting genuine lariat-derived junctions.
