On the Value of Cross-Modal Misalignment in Multimodal Representation Learning
Yichao CaiYuhang Liu Erdun GaoTianjiao Jiang Zhen Zhang Anton van den Hengel Javen Qinfeng Shi Australian Institute for Machine LearningThe University of Adelaide, SA 5000, Australia

Abstract
Multimodal representation learning, exemplified by multimodal contrastive learning (MMCL) using image-text pairs, aims to learn powerful representations by aligning cues across modalities. This approach relies on the core assumption that the exemplar image-text pairs constitute two representations of an identical concept. However, recent research has revealed that real-world datasets often exhibit cross-modal misalignment . There are two distinct viewpoints on how to address this issue: one suggests mitigating the misalignment, and the other leveraging it. We seek here to reconcile these seemingly opposing perspectives and to provide a practical guide for practitioners. Using latent variable models, we thus formalize cross-modal misalignment by introducing two specific mechanisms: selection bias , where some semantic variables are absent in the text, and perturbation bias , where semantic variables are altered---both leading to misalignment in data pairs. Our theoretical analysis shows that, under suitable assumptions, population global minimizers of an alignment--entropy surrogate yield representations that depend only on the semantic variables unaffected by selection and perturbation biases and locally identify this subset almost surely. Extensive empirical studies on synthetic data and real image-text datasets further examine how cross-modal misalignment shapes semantic predictability and downstream performance, shedding light on its nuanced impact on multimodal representation learning. 1 1 1 Code available at https://github.com/YichaoCai1/crossmodal_misalignment

Introduction

Modern multimodal learning has achieved remarkable success in jointly modeling information from heterogeneous sources such as vision, language, and audio. Multimodal contrastive learning (MMCL) on paired data has emerged as a dominant strategy for aligning modalities
[

. This approach is exemplified by vision-and-language models like CLIP, which attempt to identify a common representation space that maximizes the similarity of real image-text pairs while minimizing that of incorrect pairs
[

One of the assumptions underpinning this approach is that the training pairs are aligned across modalities, meaning that they convey the same semantic information
[

[
46
,
47

. More critically, text taken from image captions typically only partially describes the paired image content, and often contains descriptive elements that are irrelevant or misleading, a situation referred to as
cross-modal misalignment

[
46
]
. It is worth noting that the impact of cross-modal misalignment is mainly concentrated in the text modality, as text captions, often derived from images, are more susceptible to semantic incompleteness and interpretive variability than the typically consistent visual semantics.

In light of this, we offer a theoretical perspective that not only facilitates the understanding of misalignment but also provides guidance for real-world applications. Specifically, we formulate the problem using a latent variable model (LVM), which depicts the underlying generative process of image-text data with misalignment, as shown in
Figure

1

In it, the latent space consists of latent semantic variables representing factors common to both modalities (e.g., object shapes and colors), along with modality-specific subspaces that capture unique variations in images and text. To make the LVM more adaptable to diverse real-world scenarios, we allow for an arbitrary causal structure among the latent semantic variables, providing flexibility in multimodal contexts. To model misalignment, we introduce two mechanisms: selection bias and perturbation bias. Both act on latent semantic information but differ in their effects. Selection bias determines which semantic information is preserved in the text. For example, when describing an object, the text might preserve information about its color (“black”) but omit its texture details or shape. On the other hand, perturbation bias introduces errors, such as misannotating “black” (correct color) to “red” (incorrect color). Moreover, given their heterogeneity, we model the two modalities with separate generative processes.

Building on the proposed LVM (i.e., the
generative model
), we present a theoretical identifiability analysis within the MMCL framework (i.e., the
inference model
). We show, under the stated assumptions, that the subset of semantic variables unaffected by selection and perturbation biases is locally block-identifiable almost surely (
Definition

4.1
) at population global minima of the alignment–entropy surrogate. Specifically, the learned representations depend only on this unaffected subset, and the corresponding maps from this subset to the representations are locally invertible around almost every realization. The remaining misaligned semantic variables affected by either bias are functionally excluded from the learned representations, irrespective of the latent causal structure among the semantic variables. This result provides a unified perspective on the seemingly opposing views discussed above. While misalignment can be problematic in tasks that rely on fully preserving semantic information to maximize downstream utility, it may paradoxically become beneficial in scenarios where robustness to distribution shifts is desired. In such cases, cross-modal misalignment naturally acts as a regularizer, encouraging models to focus on stable, invariant semantic factors shared across modalities.

Preliminaries: Multimodal Contrastive Learning

Multimodal contrastive learning (MMCL)
[
51
,
24
,
78
]
aims to learn joint representations by aligning paired samples from different modalities, i.e.,
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{T}
for text and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
for images, while pushing apart unpaired (negative) samples. In practice, MMCL typically employs two modality-specific encoders, i.e.,
f_{t}(\mathbf{t})
for text and
f_{x}(\mathbf{x})
for images which project observed paired data into a shared representation space. The learning objective is generally formulated as the following contrastive loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny MMCL}}(f_{x},f_{t})=-\frac{1}{2K}\left[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{K}\log\frac{e^{\kappa\left(f_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),f_{t}(\mathbf{t}_{i})\right)/\tau}}{\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{K}e^{\kappa\left(f_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),f_{t}(\mathbf{t}_{j})\right)/\tau}}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{K}\log\frac{e^{\kappa\left(f_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),f_{t}(\mathbf{t}_{i})\right)/\tau}}{\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{K}e^{\kappa\left(f_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{j}),f_{t}(\mathbf{t}_{i})\right)/\tau}}\right],

(1)

where
\{\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
are sampled paired data,
K
denotes the number of training pairs,
\tau
is a temperature hyperparameter controlling the concentration of the similarity distribution, and
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes a similarity measure. Motivated by the alignment and uniformity perspective on contrastive learning
[
65
]
, and following prior identifiability analyses
[
45
,
11
,
74
]
, we study the following population alignment–entropy analytical surrogate:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny SymAlignMaxEnt}}(f_{x},f_{t})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\nolimits_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})\sim p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}}}\Bigl[\|f_{x}(\mathbf{x})-f_{t}(\mathbf{t})\|_{2}\Bigr]-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(H\bigl(f_{x}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)+H\bigl(f_{t}(\mathbf{t})\bigr)\Bigr),

(2)

Here,
H(\cdot)
denotes differential entropy. The first term aligns paired representations, while the second maximizes the entropy of their marginal distributions. We adopt this objective for theoretical analysis, replacing the log kernel averages that arise in the normalized (
\text{}-\log K
), large-batch limit of
Equation

1
with the marginal differential entropies in
Equation

2
. Since these two objectives do not necessarily share the same minimizers, our formal guarantees hold for the population global minimizers of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny SymAlignMaxEnt}}
, while we evaluate practical loss empirically. See
Section

D.1
for further details.

Prior work uses alignment–entropy objectives to study how shared latent factors are recovered in multimodal and partially observed multi-view settings
[
11
,
74
]
. We instead study how semantic omission and perturbation in text supervision shape the population optima of this surrogate. To formalize these effects, we introduce a latent variable model (
Section

3
) that captures selection and perturbation biases without imposing a fixed causal ordering among semantic variables. Our analysis (
Section

4
) characterizes which semantic coordinates are functionally excluded and proves almost-sure local recovery of the retained block. This connects the modeled misalignment mechanisms to the distinction between preserving semantic coverage and inducing invariance to selected attributes.

and are locally invertible with respect to this block almost surely. This excludes local dimensional collapse of the retained block, but distinct, separated latent states may still have the same representation. Thus unique global recovery is not guaranteed. Exclusion of
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbb{I}_{\rho}}
,
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbb{I}_{\theta}^{c}}
,
\mathbf{m}_{x}
and
\mathbf{m}_{t}
means the absence of additional functional dependence. Misaligned semantic variables may remain statistically predictable through their dependence on the retained block. A detailed proof is provided in
§

C.2
.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we present a formal analysis of cross-modal misalignment in multimodal data pairs within the MMCL framework, examining its impact on the learned representations. Under the stated assumptions, population global minimizers of the alignment–entropy surrogate yield representations that depend only on the unbiased shared semantic variables and locally identify this subset almost surely. Misaligned variables are functionally excluded, so selection and perturbation biases determine which semantic block the representations depend on. This characterization highlights a task-dependent trade-off: detailed and consistent captions support broader semantic retention, whereas selective or perturbed text may promote invariance to distribution-sensitive factors. Our experiments across simulations and image-text datasets examine these effects on semantic predictability and downstream performance. These insights highlight the need for multimodal learning frameworks that mitigate harmful misalignment or exploit beneficial biases to enhance representation learning in real-world settings. More discussion of these implications is provided in
Appendix

J
.

Appendix B
Related Work

Theoretical multimodal (multi-view) contrastive learning.

Recent work has sought to formalize the theoretical foundations of multimodal and multi-view representation learning, particularly under contrastive objectives. Wang et al.
[
65
]
decompose the InfoNCE loss
[
48
]
into an alignment term, which pulls positive pairs together, and a uniformity term, which encourages dispersion over a hypersphere—laying the groundwork for subsequent analysis. Zimmermann et al.
[
79
]
show that contrastive objectives can invert the data-generating process, while Liu et al.
[
42
]
extend this result to multimodal settings. However, these approaches often rely on strong assumptions about latent distributions or manifold structures, which limits their practical applicability. A complementary line of work, such as
[
64
]
, demonstrates that contrastive learning with data augmentation can recover shared content without requiring such strong parametric assumptions. This has been extended to MMCL by
[
45
,
11
]
, and Yao et al.
[
74
]
further investigate identifiability under partial observability in multi-view settings. Distinct from prior work, we do not assume fixed content-style decompositions, causal directions, or perfectly aligned pairs. Instead, we analyze an alignment–max-entropy surrogate under cross-modal misalignment and empirically examine the resulting predictions in MMCL settings.

Vision-language models and perspectives on misalignment.

Multimodal contrastive learning (MMCL) has achieved significant empirical success, particularly in aligning visual and textual modalities using models such as CLIP
[
51
]
and ALIGN
[
24
]
. These successes are partly attributed to the use of massive training corpora, e.g., LAION-5B
[
58
]
, which are substantially larger than those used for vision-only foundation models
[
20
,
49
]
. However, real-world multimodal datasets are often imperfectly aligned and noisy
[
46
]
. Existing empirical methods
[
1
,
31
]
typically treat such misalignment as label noise, employing strategies such as multiple-instance learning or dataset refinement
[
1
]
to mitigate its impact. While some recent work suggests that contrastive models are robust to certain forms of structured misalignment
[
47
]
, Others suggest augmenting text as a proxy for changes in visual content
[
5
]
. Our work suggests that cross-modal misalignment can act as either a barrier or a bridge, depending on the application. Unlike
[
47
]
, which assumes linear representations without modeling the generative process, our analysis is grounded in a realistic latent variable model that provides a deeper understanding of misalignment from a data-generating perspective.

Identifiability in latent variable models.

Identifiability analysis addresses the fundamental question of whether the learning process can uniquely recover the latent generative structure or distribution underlying the observed data. This problem has been extensively studied in the context of nonlinear independent component analysis (ICA)
[
21
,
43
,
25
,
60
]
and causal representation learning (CRL)
[
38
,
40
,
76
,
73
,
41
]
. In either setting, full identifiability, typically up to permutation, is rarely achievable in practice without strong assumptions, such as access to interventional data.
Consequently, recent works have focused on partial identifiability
[
19
,
28
,
17
]
or relaxed equivalence classes, such as identifiability up to linear transformations
[
79
,
42
]
or up to group-wise/block-wise indeterminacy
[
64
,
74
]
, which can offer sufficient guarantees for specific tasks or applications. In the context of multimodal representation learning, several recent studies have explored identifiability results
[
45
,
11
,
42
]
, but largely neglect the presence of cross-modal misalignment. Our work explicitly models misalignment and establishes local block recovery almost surely for population global minimizers of an alignment–max-entropy surrogate.

Invariant representation learning.

Invariant representation learning (IRL) seeks to learn representations that remain robust under distributional shifts between environments
[
2
,
13
,
17
]
, particularly in settings where empirical risk minimization (ERM)
[
63
]
fails to generalize out of distribution. In the absence of such challenge, ERM is sufficient for in-distribution prediction, rendering the objective of IRL unnecessary. From a causal perspective, learning invariant representations, or more ambitiously, autonomous mechanisms
[
57
]
, requires observing sufficient variations in the latent factors underlying data
[
38
]
. Such variability can be introduced through interventional data
[
36
,
30
]
, exchangeability assumptions
[
52
]
, or the use of auxiliary variables such as domain indices
[
76
,
39
]
. However, direct interventions on latent variables are typically infeasible in real-world observational data, and auxiliary variable methods often require access to a large number of diverse environments to ensure identifiability—an assumption that is often challenging to satisfy in practice. Our work sheds light on an alternative approach: by leveraging the inherent flexibility of text supervision, we show that manipulating biases, specifically through selective omission or semantic perturbation in text, can serve as a controllable proxy for environmental variation.

Lemmas

Before proceeding with the proof, we first establish the following lemmas.

Entropy convention.

For a random vector
\mathbf{z}
taking values in
(0,1)^{n}
, we use the extended-entropy convention

H(\mathbf{z}):=-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{\mathbf{z}}\,\middle\|\,\operatorname{Unif}((0,1)^{n})\right)
,
where
p_{\mathbf{z}}
denotes its probability law. This agrees with differential entropy for absolutely continuous laws on the unit cube, including the value
-\infty
, and equals
-\infty
whenever the law is not absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Under this convention,
Equation

2
is well defined with values in
[0,+\infty]
. It equals
+\infty
whenever either output marginal is not absolutely continuous, and equals zero if and only if the paired representations agree a.s. and both marginals are uniform on
(0,1)^{n}
.

Lemma C.1

(Global Minimum of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny SymAlignMaxEnt}}
)
.

Under the assumptions of

, the global minimum of

\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny SymAlignMaxEnt}}(f_{x},f_{t})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\theta)})\sim p_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\theta)}}}\left[\|f_{x}(\mathbf{x})-f_{t}(\mathbf{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\theta)})\|_{2}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(H\bigl(f_{x}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)+H\bigl(f_{t}(\mathbf{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\theta)})\bigr)\Bigr),

(6)

over the smooth encoders considered in that theorem is

. This minimum is attained by the following pair of smooth functions:

\displaystyle f_{x}^{*}=\mathbf{d}\circ(g^{-1}_{x})_{\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho}}:\mathcal{X}\to(0,1)^{n},

(7)

\displaystyle f_{t}^{*}=\mathbf{d}\circ(g^{-1}_{t^{\scriptscriptstyle(\theta)}})_{\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho}}:\mathcal{T}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\theta)}\to(0,1)^{n},

Lemma C.2

(Local invertibility a.s.)
.

Let
h:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be smooth, where
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
are open, and let
\mathbf{u}
be a random vector taking values in
\mathcal{U}
. If the law of
\mathbf{v}=h(\mathbf{u})
is absolutely continuous with respect to
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure, then

\Pr\!\left(\det J(h)(\mathbf{u})=0\right)=0.

Thus
h
has a smooth local inverse around almost every realization of
\mathbf{u}
. In particular, this holds when
h(\mathbf{u})\sim\operatorname{Unif}((0,1)^{n})
. If, additionally,
\mathbf{u}
has a density strictly positive a.e. on
\mathcal{U}
, then the critical set of
h
is also Lebesgue-null.

Proof of
Lemma

C.2
.

Let
K=\{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{U}:\det J(h)(\mathbf{a})=0\}
be the critical set of
h
. By Sard’s theorem
[
56
]
, its image
h(K)
has Lebesgue measure zero. Absolute continuity of the law of
\mathbf{v}
therefore gives

\Pr(\mathbf{u}\in K)\leq\Pr\bigl(h(\mathbf{u})\in h(K)\bigr)=0.

At every point outside
K
, the inverse function theorem gives open neighborhoods on which
h
restricts to a diffeomorphism.

Finally, if the density of
\mathbf{u}
is strictly positive a.e. on
\mathcal{U}
, every measurable subset of
\mathcal{U}
with positive Lebesgue measure has positive probability. Since
\Pr(\mathbf{u}\in K)=0
, the set
K
is Lebesgue-null.
∎

Remark C.1

(Causal interpretations)
.

The exclusion established above is functional: both representations depend on the latent variables only through
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbb{I}_{\rho}^{c}}
. This does not imply statistical independence from omitted or perturbed semantic variables, which may remain correlated with the retained block. The proof permits dependence among semantic variables and uses variation within the support of the observational pairing law, without requiring identification of the underlying causal structure.

Definition C.1

(Graded lexicographic order)
.

Let
S
be a finite set with a total order (e.g.,
S=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
). The
graded lexicographic order
on the non-empty subsets of
S
is defined as follows:
(i)
Subsets are ordered first by increasing cardinality (i.e., all subsets of size
k
precede those of size
k+1
).
(ii)
Within each group of equal cardinality, subsets are ordered lexicographically: that is,
\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}\}<\{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{k}\}
if there exists an
\ell
such that
i_{m}=j_{m}
for all
m<\ell
and
i_{\ell}<j_{\ell}
.

Remark C.2

(Fixed graded lexicographic order)
.

The
graded lexicographic order
over subsets of semantic indices is fixed solely for notational consistency and clarity. For example, given semantic variables
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
with index set
\mathbb{I}_{\mathbf{s}}=\{1,2,3\}
, the graded lexicographic order over the non-empty powerset
\mathcal{P}^{+}(\mathbb{I}_{\mathbf{s}})
yields:

\mathcal{P}^{+}(\mathbb{I}_{\mathbf{s}})=\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\},\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{2,3\},\{1,2,3\}\}.

This ordering imposes no constraints on the latent structure and is adopted without loss of generality. Since the true permutation of latent variables is unidentifiable, any consistent order can be used to index subset-valued parameters such as
\theta
(selection) and
\rho
(perturbation). Importantly, both
\theta
and
\rho

Appendix D
Scope of the Recovery and Objective Claims

D.1
Large-Batch Contrastive Objective

For fixed encoders with bounded outputs, i.i.d. paired batches,
\tau=1
, and
\kappa(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=-\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}
, the expected objective in
Equation

1
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny MMCL}}^{(K)}(f_{x},f_{t})]-\log K\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})}\bigl[\|f_{x}(\mathbf{x})-f_{t}(\mathbf{t})\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}}\log\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\sim p_{\mathbf{t}}}\exp\!\left\{-\|f_{x}(\mathbf{x})-f_{t}(\mathbf{t}^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{t}}\log\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim p_{\mathbf{x}}}\exp\!\left\{-\|f_{x}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-f_{t}(\mathbf{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(19)

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny MMCL}}^{(K)}
denotes the objective evaluated on a batch of
K
pairs. The expectation on the left is over batches, and primes denote independent draws from the indicated marginals. Subtracting
\log K
replaces each denominator sum by its average. Conditional on the anchor, the law of large numbers gives the corresponding kernel average, with the paired positive contributing a term of order
K^{-1}
. Because the encoder outputs are bounded, the exponential kernels remain bounded away from zero, justifying passage of the limit through the logarithm and outer expectation.

Unlike the marginal entropies in
Equation

2
, the last two terms involve fixed-bandwidth kernel averages. Further, the limiting alignment term uses a squared Euclidean distance rather than the unsquared norm in
Equation

2
—a distinction consistent with the contrastive limit and kernel-density perspective analyzed by
[
65
]
. Because neither objective equality nor minimizer equivalence follows from this large-batch limit, we adopt
Equation

2
as a dedicated population surrogate for our theoretical analysis.

D.2
Local versus Global Recovery

Our proof adapts the entropy-based strategy developed in prior identifiability analyses
[
79
,
64
,
45
,
11
,
14
,
74
]
. We note a distinction regarding the scope of this technique: without additional topological or global assumptions, smoothness of the induced map on the retained block and a uniform pushforward distribution alone do not guarantee global invertibility (nor injectivity on a set of full probability). Instead, as established in the revised
Lemma

C.2
, these conditions certify local invertibility around almost every latent point. We therefore state the recovery guarantees in
Theorem

4.1
and
4.1
and
4.2
in terms of almost-sure local block recovery, updating an earlier version of this manuscript that adopted the global-recovery formulation used in this line of identifiability research.

The distinction between local and global recovery persists even for smooth maps with everywhere nonzero Jacobian determinants and continuous input and output variables. Let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})
be uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{Q}=(0,1)^{2}
and, for an integer
k\geq 2
, define

w_{k}(\mathbf{u})=\bigl(\sqrt{u_{1}}\cos(2\pi ku_{2}),\sqrt{u_{1}}\sin(2\pi ku_{2})\bigr).

This map is smooth on
\mathcal{Q}
, satisfies
\det J(w_{k})(\mathbf{u})=\pi k
everywhere, and maps
\mathcal{Q}
onto the punctured open unit disk
\mathcal{D}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, where
\mathcal{D}=\left\{\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}<1\right\}
. On each open strip

(0,1)\times(j/k,(j+1)/k),\qquad j=0,\ldots,k-1,

w_{k}
is a diffeomorphism onto
\mathcal{D}
minus the nonnegative horizontal radius. Each branch contributes density
1/(\pi k)
, so the
k
branches sum to
1/\pi
a.e. on
\mathcal{D}
. The disk center and the branch seams are null sets that do not affect the pushforward distribution. Thus,
w_{k}(\mathbf{u})
is uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{D}
.

Next, apply the Rosenblatt transformation
[
54
]
to the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{D}
. In this case, the conditional-CDF diffeomorphism
r:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{Q}
is given by

F(v_{1})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{v_{1}\sqrt{1-v_{1}^{2}}+\arcsin v_{1}}{\pi},\qquad r(\mathbf{v})=\left(F(v_{1}),\frac{1}{2}+\frac{v_{2}}{2\sqrt{1-v_{1}^{2}}}\right).

The map
r
is smooth, satisfies
\det J(r)(\mathbf{v})=1/\pi
everywhere, and pushes the uniform disk law forward to the uniform square law. Consequently, the composite map

h_{k}=r\circ w_{k}:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}

is smooth on all of
\mathcal{Q}
, preserves the uniform distribution, and satisfies
\det J(h_{k})(\mathbf{u})=k
everywhere. Although

h_{k}(\mathcal{Q})=\mathcal{Q}\setminus\{(1/2,1/2)\},

the omitted point has zero measure and does not affect the uniform pushforward law. Nevertheless,

h_{k}(u_{1},u_{2})=h_{k}(u_{1},u_{2}+1/k)\qquad\text{whenever }0<u_{2}<u_{2}+1/k<1,

and
h_{k}
is
k
-to-one a.e. Moreover,
h_{k}
cannot be injective on any full-measure subset of
\mathcal{Q}
: translation by
1/k
in the second coordinate preserves null sets, so almost every point
(u_{1},u_{2})
in the first strip and its translated partner
(u_{1},u_{2}+1/k)
must both belong to that subset.

To instantiate this counterexample in our LVM, take
n_{s}=n=2
,
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{Q}
, and
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{u}
. Select all semantic coordinates and introduce no perturbation:

\mathbb{I}_{\theta}=\mathbb{I}_{\mathbf{s}}=[2],\qquad\mathbb{I}_{\rho}=\emptyset,\qquad\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho}=\mathbb{I}_{\mathbf{s}}.

Choose independent, continuous modality-specific variables
\mathbf{m}_{x}
and
\mathbf{m}_{t}
with smooth, strictly positive densities, independent of
\mathbf{s}
. Let
g_{x}
and
g_{t^{\scriptscriptstyle(\theta)}}
be the identity maps, so that

\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{m}_{x}),\qquad\mathbf{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\theta)}=(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{m}_{t}).

Define the encoders to apply
h_{k}
to the semantic coordinates while ignoring the modality-specific variables:

f_{x}(\mathbf{x})=h_{k}(\mathbf{s}),\qquad f_{t}(\mathbf{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\theta)})=h_{k}(\mathbf{s}).

Because the paired representations agree and both output marginals are uniform on
\mathcal{Q}
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{\text{\tiny SymAlignMaxEnt}}(f_{x},f_{t})=0.

These encoders therefore attain the population global minimum of
Equation

2
without globally identifying the retained block
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho}}
. All latent variables remain continuous, and the uniform distribution on the retained block satisfies the conditional-CDF regularity condition in
Theorem

4.1
. For
n>2
, apply
h_{k}
to the first two coordinates of a uniform vector on
(0,1)^{n}
and the identity map to the remaining coordinates. Accordingly, our revised results establish functional exclusion alongside almost-sure local recovery. Upgrading to global recovery requires additional structural restrictions to rule out multiple preimages. Uniformity and simple connectedness alone do not provide such restrictions.

Remark D.1

(A sufficient condition for global recovery)
.

Let
h:\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho}}\to(0,1)^{n}
denote the smooth retained-block map, and suppose that
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho}}
is a connected open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
h
is proper and its Jacobian determinant is nonzero everywhere, then
h
is a global diffeomorphism, since
(0,1)^{n}
is simply connected
[
55
, Theorem 2.1]
. Here, properness means that
h^{-1}(A)
is compact for every compact set
A\subset(0,1)^{n}
. These conditions provide one route to global block recovery.

Remark D.2

(Compactness and the choice of latent space)
.

A continuous map from a compact domain into a Hausdorff space is proper. However, compactness of the retained latent domain is incompatible with the exact uniformity required in our formulation. Indeed, if
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho}}
were compact, then its image under the continuous map
h:\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho}}\to(0,1)^{n}
would also be compact. Hence, there would exist
\varepsilon>0
such that

h\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho}}\bigr)\subseteq[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]^{n}.

The pushforward distribution could therefore not be uniform on the entire open cube. Assuming only that the latent domain is bounded, or that its closure is compact, does not ensure properness of a map defined on that open domain. Changing the latent space to a closed cube would require a separate treatment of boundary behavior, since the global inverse theorem above concerns open manifolds.

Remark D.3

(Relation to hyperspherical results)
.

In the hyperspherical setting of
[
79
]
, both the latent variable and its representation lie on spheres. Compactness of the source sphere ensures properness of any continuous latent-to-representation map. If the spheres have equal intrinsic dimension of at least two, a smooth map with an everywhere nonsingular differential is therefore globally invertible, since the target is simply connected
[
55
, Theorem 2.1]
. Their main recovery result
[
79
, Theorem 2]
uses a more specific argument: in the infinite-negative-sample limit, the specified positive-pair conditional distribution and matching model family yield inner-product preservation up to a fixed scale, and hence orthogonal recovery after rescaling. This relies on structure beyond marginal uniformity and does not establish global invertibility from smoothness and marginal uniformity alone in our open-cube setting.

independent

\Sigma_{\mathbf{s}}=I_{10}

[
25
,
60
]

dependent
semantics, we sample
\Sigma_{\mathbf{s}}
from Wishart distribution
\mathcal{W}_{10}(I,10)
(identity scale, 10 degrees of freedom), introducing latent dependencies as in
[
64
,
11
,
74
]

\Sigma_{\mathbf{m}_{x}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathbf{m}_{t}}
are sampled similarly from
\mathcal{W}_{5}(I,5)

Parameter settings.

We parameterize the generative functions

and

using randomly initialized 3-layer invertible MLPs, following prior work
[

. Invertibility is enforced by maintaining a condition number threshold of

for each layer.

The encoding functions
f_{x}
and
f_{t}
are implemented as 7-layer MLPs and optimized using the Adam optimizer. For MMCL training, we use a batch size of
6144
, a learning rate of
1\mathrm{e}{-}4
, and train for
100{,}000

, with squared Euclidean distances, cross-modal positive pairs, independently sampled same-modality negatives, and temperature

Downstream tasks design.

To evaluate pretrained representations under various bias conditions, we construct several downstream tasks. Specifically, four regression tasks are created by generating labels using complex nonlinear functions
f_{y_{i}}
, each applied to different subsets of the true semantic variables:

y_{1}=f_{y_{1}}(\mathbf{s}_{[3]}),\quad y_{2}=f_{y_{2}}(\mathbf{s}_{[5]}),\quad y_{3}=f_{y_{3}}(\mathbf{s}_{[7]}),\quad y_{4}=f_{y_{4}}(\mathbf{s}_{[9]}).

Each function
f_{y_{(\cdot)}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
includes quadratic, cubic, pairwise, and triple-wise interaction terms, as well as sinusoidal, logarithmic, and exponential transformations. The full formulation for a semantic vector
\mathbf{s}_{[d]}
is:

\displaystyle f_{y_{(\cdot)}}(\mathbf{s}_{[d]})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}^{2}+0.3\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}^{3}+0.5\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}s_{i}s_{j}+0.2\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq d}s_{i}s_{j}s_{k}

\displaystyle\quad+0.7\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sin(s_{i})+\cos(s_{i})\right)+0.4\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log(1+|s_{i}|)+0.6\sum_{i=1}^{d}e^{-|s_{i}|}.

For the classification, binary labels are obtained by fitting two-cluster k-means to
y_{2}
separately within each generated batch, with labels ordered by the cluster centers. To simulate distribution shifts in the observations
\mathbf{x}
, we apply a skewed, heavy-tailed transformation to semantic dimensions
9
and
10
:

s_{i}^{\text{ood}}=2\,\mathrm{sign}(s_{i}^{\text{id}})\cdot|s_{i}^{\text{id}}|^{2},\quad\text{for }i\in\{9,10\}.

After this transformation, each affected coordinate is independently replaced, with probability
0.1
, by
10
times its sign. For both downstream tasks, we
fix
the pretrained encoders and evaluate the quality of the learned representations using a two-layer MLP as a probing model. We generate
20{,}480
samples as the evaluation set for training the regressors and classifiers, along with an additional
20{,}480
samples as the in-distribution test set. To assess OOD generalization, we generate another
20{,}480
samples from the shifted latent space as the OOD test set.

The regressors are trained using Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss, and the classifiers use Cross-Entropy loss, both with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
and trained for
10{,}000
steps. We report classification performance using the average Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). Importantly, in the OOD setting, we perform
no adaptation or fine-tuning
; the classifier is evaluated directly to test the generalization capability of the pretrained representations.

Training details.

For each setting, the dataset is partitioned into training, evaluation, and test subsets in a fixed ratio of
44{,}720:23{,}040:23{,}040
. Across all configurations, we train the image and text encoders for
200{,}000
steps using the training subset, averaging results over three random seeds. The training objective is a symmetric InfoNCE loss with cosine similarity and both within-modality and cross-modal negatives, excluding same-modality self-pairs, and optimized using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of
1\times 10^{-5}
, a batch size of 256, and a temperature parameter
\tau=1.0
.

For image encoding, we use a ResNet18 backbone followed by a fully connected layer with a fixed input dimensionality of 100. The output dimensionality is adjusted according to the number of unbiased semantic factors under each bias setting.

For text encoding, we tokenize text using the
nltk.PunktTokenizer
, following the procedure in
[
11
]
. Tokenized sequences are transformed into two-dimensional one-hot embeddings and processed using a convolutional neural network (CNN) with a variable number of layers, determined by the shape of the tokenized input. The output dimensionality of the CNN is configured to match that of the image encoder, ensuring compatibility in the joint representation space.

The encoding size is generally set to match the number of unbiased semantic dimensions, i.e.,
\mathrm{dim}(\mathbb{I}^{c}_{\rho})
. However, when this number is less than 3, we set the encoding size to 3 to ensure minimal representational capacity. An ablation study on this design choice is provided in the following section.

Evaluation metrics.

After training the representations, we freeze the encoders and train both a linear classifier (logistic regression) and a nonlinear classifier (a two-layer MLP with ReLU activation) for each setting and each image latent factor. Classifiers are trained on the evaluation subset for
10{,}000
steps.

Performance is assessed on the test set using the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), computed separately for each latent factor. MCC is chosen for its robustness in evaluating binary classification performance under class imbalance.

Evaluation metrics.

After training the representations, we freeze the encoders and, for each bias setting, train both a linear classifier (logistic regression) and a nonlinear classifier (a two-layer MLP with ReLU activation) for each discrete latent factor. Similarly, for continuous latent factors, we train both a linear regressor and a nonlinear regressor (a two-layer MLP with ReLU activation). All classifiers and regressors are trained for
10{,}000
steps using the evaluation subset.

We assess the predictive performance of the learned representations by evaluating their ability to recover the ground-truth latent factors corresponding to their respective modalities. This evaluation accounts for the fact that some semantic variables may appear in discrete or continuous form, depending on the modality and rendering process.

Prediction performance is measured on the test set using the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) for discrete factors and the coefficient of determination (
R^{2}
) for continuous factors. Metrics are computed separately for each latent factor.

G.2
Additional Results

Results of text representations.

We now turn to the analysis of the text representations learned by MMCL. As shown in
Figure

19
, we observe patterns similar to those found in the image modality. In particular, discrete latent semantic variables—such as
shape
,
x_pos
, and
y_pos
—are accurately predicted by the evaluated decoders, provided they are unbiased and consistently aligned across modalities. A notable case is
s_pos
, which is a continuous latent factor in both modalities but is mapped to only five discrete tokens in the text observations, rendering the text generation process non-invertible. Despite this lossy transformation, the model achieves a relatively high
R^{2}
, suggesting that the learned text representations remain strongly influenced by the alignment objective, even when semantic information is partially lost.

For other latent semantic variables that are continuous in the image modality but discretized in the text modality—such as
color
,
s_color
, and
b_color
—we observe varying degrees of performance degradation. This drop in performance is likely attributable not only to the quantization of continuous values but also to semantic ambiguity introduced during text generation. Notably, all three attributes correspond to different aspects of color, yet they may be described using overlapping vocabulary drawn from distinct color palettes. For instance,
tab:cyan
from
TABLEAU_COLORS
refers to object color (
color
),
cyan
from
CSS4_COLORS
describes spotlight color (
s_color
), and
xkcd:cyan
from
XKCD_COLORS
indicates background color (
b_color
). Despite referencing different latent variables, these tokens all contain the word
cyan
, which is tokenized identically by
nltk.PunktTokenizer
, resulting in ambiguity in the text observations. These findings highlight the importance of using distinct and unambiguous content words when representing semantically different concepts in multimodal learning—particularly when the text modality is not treated merely as an auxiliary input for visual representation learning.

Interestingly, the identification of
x_pos
and
y_pos
in the text representations does not lead to improved predictability of
color
, in contrast to what is often observed in the image modality. The difference in predictability between modalities is an empirical observation; the model’s text-side perturbation mechanism does not imply that the underlying image latent causal structure changes.

Regarding the text-specific factor
phrase
, we find it to be partially encoded in the learned representations. This contrasts with the image-specific continuous factors, which are poorly predicted by the evaluated decoders. The partial predictability of
phrase
is likely attributable to its discrete nature, which violates the conditions typically required for modality-specific factors to be excluded—consistent with findings reported in
[
11
]
. Moreover, this effect appears more pronounced under selection bias settings, particularly when the encoding dimensionality exceeds the true dimensionality of the unbiased semantic subspace.

Overall, the behavior of the learned text representations provides empirical support for our theoretical analysis, even under conditions where certain modeling assumptions are relaxed or violated.

H.3
Zero-Shot Evaluation Details

To probe the representations of pretrained OpenCLIP models, we evaluate two variants trained on the LAION-400M dataset: ViT-B-32 and ViT-L-14. Using the collected image samples, we perform zero-shot analysis by employing the concept names as text prompts. Each concept group is treated as a distinct solution space. To mitigate the impact of sampling bias in the test dataset, we report macro-averaged F1 scores at the group level.

As shown in
Figure

10
, concept groups that are frequently captioned—defined here as those with coverage rates exceeding 0.15%, are generally well represented in the multimodal contrastive learning (MMCL) models, with F1 scores above 65%. In contrast, concept groups that are severely under-captioned, often due to selection bias or ethical omission, exhibit poor representation, with F1 scores falling below 40%. The results are consistent across different model scales, further reinforcing our theoretical findings, particularly the impact of selection bias on the learned representations.

Figure

23
presents the intra-group confusion matrix from the zero-shot evaluation using the OpenCLIP ViT-B-32 model trained on the LAION-400M dataset. As clearly shown, well-captioned concept groups exhibit strong diagonal patterns, indicating accurate predictions—whereas concept groups affected by selection bias suffer from substantial misclassification.

This misclassification has multifaceted implications. For certain under-captioned yet potentially valuable concepts, such as
Texture
(texture) and
Emot.
(mental state), the poor performance suggests a need for more comprehensive and semantically rich captioning pipelines in multimodal contrastive learning (MMCL) pretraining. Conversely, for sensitive concepts like
Trait
(moral judgement) and
Stere.
(stereotypes), which ideally should not be encoded, this under-representation may be desirable. In these cases, the inherent biases in the captioning process may function as epistemic filters that implicitly align model representations with human ethical standards.

Appendix I
Computation Resources

All experiments were conducted on a high-performance computing cluster equipped with 4×NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40 GB each), running CUDA 12.2 and driver version 535.161.07. The system also included an AMD EPYC 7313 16-core processor and 503 GB of RAM. For the numerical simulations, we trained over 120 models in total, requiring approximately 70 GPU-hours across 4 GPUs. On the MPI3D-Complex dataset, we trained 36 models, consuming approximately 27 GPU-hours. For the Causal3DIdent dataset, we trained 42 models, which required roughly 25 GPU-hours across 4 GPUs. Additionally, we generated 100,000 synthetic images for the Causal3DIdent dataset using Blender. Rendering was performed over four days on a separate workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 7700X 8-core processor (4.50 GHz) and a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB).

Appendix J
Discussions

In this section, we reflect on the limitations of the current study, propose future research directions informed by our findings, and discuss broader implications.

J.1
Limitations and Future Directions

Estimating dataset biases.

Our study focuses on formalizing the effects of semantic misalignment, but does not directly address the empirical estimation of dataset-specific biases such as selection and perturbation bias. These biases are often latent and difficult to observe, particularly in large-scale, web-curated datasets. Yet, identifying and quantifying them is essential for improving interpretability, data quality, and downstream robustness. This task involves challenges in defining concept taxonomies, determining annotation granularity, and choosing evaluation metrics. Our framework suggests practical strategies: selection bias may be investigated through concept coverage conditioned on concept presence in the corresponding images, while comparisons with captions generated by strong image-to-text models can flag potential perturbations for image-grounded verification. We view the development of robust bias identification pipelines, such as those explored by
[
75
]
, as a valuable direction for future work.

Expanding the taxonomy of misalignment.

Multimodal data presents a wide range of potential misalignments across modalities. In this work, we deliberately focus on semantic misalignment in image-text pairs, a particularly prevalent and influential form of bias in vision-language learning. This type of misalignment is central to current multimodal research due to its relevance in large-scale pretraining paradigms and real-world applications (e.g., CLIP, ALIGN). By explicitly modeling selection and perturbation biases, our framework captures common semantic omissions and distortions that significantly affect representational quality. Extending the analysis to other forms of misalignment, such as temporal lag in video-language data, modality-specific dropout, or ambiguity arising from multi-entity co-occurrence, poses additional challenges and may require substantially different modeling assumptions and techniques. We view our proposed abstraction as a tractable and principled foundation for understanding semantic misalignment and believe it can work as a building-block for future explorations on this direction.

Modeling cross-modal semantic synergy.

Multimodal representations often exhibit semantic synergies arising not only from the alignment of shared content but also from emergent meaning generated through cross-modal integration. At a theoretical level, this aligns with ideas in grounded cognition: cognition (and by extension, semantic representation) is rooted in the integration of perceptual, motor, and introspective modalities via
simulation

[
3
]
. This motivates a conceptual distinction between two forms of semantic information: 1) shared information available from either modality, such as object identity in an image and its description, which our shared-latent formulation accommodates; and 2) information available only through joint observation of the modalities, as in some instances of sarcasm, irony, or humor. While our current theory focuses on shared semantics, extending the framework to capture such joint information remains a direction for future research.

Formulating missing data in the latent space.

Our current framework addresses fixed semantic selection patterns and random perturbations within a prescribed subset of semantic variables in textual annotations. However, real-world datasets, particularly large-scale, user-generated corpora such as LAION-5B
[
58
]
, often display random and unstructured semantic omissions. Extending our latent variable model to accommodate such random missingness (e.g., missing completely at random or missing not at random) poses both theoretical and practical challenges. Future research should examine the identifiability consequences of randomly missing semantic variables and establish conditions that ensure partial or probabilistic recovery of latent factors remains feasible.

Linearity of multimodal representations.

Our theoretical analysis establishes almost-sure local identifiability of unbiased semantic factors at population global minima of the alignment–entropy surrogate, while empirical observations show that these factors can often be predicted accurately using linear probes. This motivates an important open question: under what conditions can identifiability up to a
linear
transformation be rigorously established, without imposing overly restrictive assumptions on data-generating processes or latent structures? Clarifying this issue is not only theoretically significant but also practically beneficial, as linear representations enhance interpretability and simplify downstream applications. Future studies might investigate training objectives, regularization strategies, or architectural inductive biases explicitly designed to encourage linearity. Bridging this empirical regularity with robust theoretical guarantees represents a promising research frontier.

J.2
Broader Implications

Linguistic relativity and epistemic constraints in multimodal AI.

Our findings offer a computational perspective on how linguistic supervision shapes multimodal representations, drawing an analogy with linguistic relativity
[
66
]
. In multimodal AI, linguistic supervision implicitly determines which aspects of the visual world are foregrounded, and which are omitted. Within our theoretical setting, selection and perturbation biases in textual annotations act as
epistemic filters
, determining which semantic variables the representations functionally depend on. This reframes dataset design as both an epistemic and normative act: inclusion or omission in annotations encodes a stance on salience, relevance, or appropriateness. Two practical insights follow. First, supporting diverse downstream tasks motivates supervision that faithfully captures the intended semantic scope without systematic omissions of relevant information. Second, curating the treatment of ethically sensitive or socially salient content offers a way to shape supervision in accordance with explicitly stated values
[
4
,
9
]
. Since omitted attributes may remain predictable through correlations with retained factors, their omission from captions alone does not ensure their exclusion from downstream predictions. When annotation is treated as an epistemic commitment, dataset design becomes a central tool for shaping both semantic fidelity and ethical alignment.

Human annotation biases as signals of implicit value judgments.

Beyond explicit annotation content, systematic omissions and mislabels may provide clues to annotators’ attention and priorities
[
23
]
. Such patterns invite a complementary perspective: annotation errors can be studied not only as noise, but also as possible reflections of which semantic factors annotators consider salient. Interpreting these patterns requires accounting for annotation instructions, perceptual difficulty, ambiguity, and collection practices. Studying annotation biases alongside explicit human judgments may help distinguish these influences and inform models that better reflect human preferences and priorities
[
16
]
.
