A Tight Quantum Algorithm for Multiple Collision Search
Xavier Bonnetain, Johanna Loyer, André Schrottenloher, Yixin Shen

Abstract
Searching for collisions in random functions is a fundamental computational problem, with many applications in symmetric and asymmetric cryptanalysis. When one searches for a single collision, the known quantum algorithms match the query lower bound. This is not the case for the problem of finding multiple collisions, despite its regular appearance as a sub-component in sieving-type algorithms. At EUROCRYPT 2019, Liu and Zhandry gave a query lower bound Ω ⁡ ( 2 m / 3 + 2 ​ k / 3 ) \Omega{}(2^{m/3+2k/3}) for finding 2 k 2^{k} collisions in a random function with m m -bit output. At EUROCRYPT 2023, Bonnetain et al. gave a quantum algorithm matching this bound for a large range of m m and k k , but not all admissible values. Like many previous collision-finding algorithms, theirs is based on the MNRS quantum walk framework, but it chains the walks by reusing the state after outputting a collision. In this paper, we give a new algorithm that tackles the remaining non-optimal range, closing the problem. Our algorithm is tight (up to a polynomial factor) in queries, and also in time under a quantum RAM assumption. The idea is to extend the chained walk to a regime in which several collisions are returned at each step, and the “walks” themselves only perform a single diffusion layer.

Introduction

The collision search problem asks to find pairs of (distinct) inputs of a function that have the same output. It is a fundamental problem in quantum algorithms and cryptography, arguably only second to unstructured search. Many applications require to find many such collisions, which we can formulate as follows.

Problem 1
(Multiple collision search)

Given access to a (random) function
f~:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
, where
n\leq m\leq 2n

k\leq 2n-m

2^{k}
collisions of

, i.e., pairs of distinct
x,y
such that
f(x)=f(y)
.

The constraints on the parameters
n,m,k
ensure that such collisions exist and that we are not asking for more collisions than the expected number of them (
\Theta(2^{2n-m})
for a random function
f
of this form).

Tightness of Existing Algorithms.

Classically, it is well known that one can find
2^{k}
collisions in
\mathcal{O}(2^{(m+k)/2})

\mathcal{O}(2^{m/2})
for a single collision. Indeed, a list of
2^{(m+k)/2}
random queries to

For a general

Contribution.

In this paper, we give a new algorithm that covers this remaining range. Like in
[
3
]
, we use a
chained
version of the MNRS quantum walk framework
[
15
]
. The idea of the chained quantum walk is to reuse the final state of a walk as starting state for a new one. Indeed, the state of a walk is a superposition of subsets of
\{0,1\}^{n}
. At the end of a walk, one expects the subsets to always contain a collision: one can
extract
and measure this collision, collapsing the vertex on a superposition of subsets which is still close to uniform – and sufficient to carry on.

In this new version, we increase the vertex size so much that in fact, the quantum state always contains (exponentially many) collisions on average. Now, there is no need to properly
walk
during the quantum walks, and they are reduced to a single diffusion layer. The effect of the diffusion is to re-randomize the vertex, making new collisions appear.

This allows us to prove the following:

Theorem 1.1

For all
n\leq m\leq 2n
,
k\leq 2n-m
, there exists a quantum algorithm solving the multiple collision search problem in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(2^{2k/3+m/3}\right)
queries, memory and total gate count (assuming qRAM gates). Furthermore, for any
\ell\leq 2k/3+m/3
, there exists a quantum algorithm using memory
2^{\ell}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(2^{k+m/2-\ell/2}\right)
queries and total gate count (assuming qRAM gates).

With the lower bound from
[
14
]
this implies, as a direct corollary:

Corollary 1
(Complexity of Multiple collision search)

The query complexity of
1
is, up to a factor polynomial in
n
,
2^{m/3+2k/3}
.

Applications in Symmetric Cryptography.

In symmetric cryptography, the
limited birthday
problem asks, given black-box oracle access to a permutation
E
of
\{0,1\}^{n}
(a block cipher), to find many pairs
P,P^{\prime}
such that
E(P)\oplus E(P^{\prime})
and

both belong to a given vector subspace of
\{0,1\}^{n}
. It is an important part of impossible differential attacks
[
4
]
, and also serves as a distinguisher of random permutations
[
12
]
. The limited birthday problem is solved by restricting the inputs of
E
(or outputs) to
structures
and solving a multiple collision search problem on such structures. Depending on the sizes of the restricting vector subspaces, one can encounter both cases with
few
collisions to output, or all of them, making this a very versatile application
[
8
]
.

Applications in Asymmetric Cryptography.

Sieving algorithms are currently the fastest at solving the shortest vector problem on integer lattices, and are used to estimate the security of post-quantum lattice-based cryptosystems. They also exist in the cryptanalysis of code-based schemes
[
9
,
10
]
. Sieving algorithms construct several lists of elements (e.g., vectors) where the next ones are obtained by finding partial collisions between the previous ones. Previously, the generic improvement of multiple collision-finding in
[
3
]
allowed to improve (slightly) the previous best quantum algorithm for lattice sieving given in
[
7
]
. In this paper, we do not improve lattice sieving (since we only improve multiple-collision search in an adjacent regime), but we believe that our new technique could likely be used inside similar algorithms.

Finding multicollisions.

The problem of multicollision-finding in the quantum setting has only been studied in the case
n=m

[
14
]
. As the known algorithms in this case start by computing a large number of collisions, a tight algorithm to find many collisions is likely a first step towards a tight quantum algorithm for multicollision-finding in the case
m>n
.

Preliminaries

We assume knowledge of the basics of quantum computing in the circuit model
[
16
]
such as qubits, quantum states, unitaries,
etc.
In order to solve
1
in the quantum setting, the function
f
is accessed as a unitary oracle:

\left|x\right\rangle\left|y\right\rangle\xmapsto{O_{f}}\left|x\right\rangle\left|y\oplus f(x)\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt,

where
x
is an
n
-bit input and
y
an
m
-bit input.

We estimate the complexities of our algorithms asymptotically in
n,m
and
k
, as follows. The
query complexity
is the number of queries to
O_{f}
(which also includes classical queries to
f
as a special case). The
time complexity
(or gate count) is the number of elementary quantum gates; the
memory complexity
is the number of qubits or classical bits of memory. Since we are only interested in asymptotic complexities, any set of universal quantum gates is sufficient; one can take the Clifford+T gate set.

In order to reach optimal time complexities, we need
quantum RAM
(qRAM). Different variants of qRAM are used in collision search algorithms: for example, the BHT algorithm requires only
quantum-accessible classical memory

[
6
]
. In our case, we need
quantum-accessible quantum memory
. Like previous works
[
2
]
, we formalize our qRAM assumption by introducing the so-called qRAM gate:

\left|y_{1},\ldots,y_{M}\right\rangle\left|x\right\rangle\left|i\right\rangle\xmapsto{\mathsf{qRAM}}\left|y_{1},\ldots,y_{i-1},x,y_{i+1},\ldots,y_{M}\right\rangle\left|y_{i}\right\rangle\left|i\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

We give cost 1 to the qRAM gate (like other elementary gates) even if
M
is exponential-size; the qRAM gate can even span the entire quantum circuit. While this is obviously a very powerful model, it has no incidence on the query complexity, since a qRAM gate can always be simulated by an exponential-size Clifford+T circuit.

2.1
Quantum Search

Let
\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle
be a quantum state (the “uniform superposition”) produced by some
Setup
algorithm, which is a superposition of two orthogonal components
\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle
(the “good subspace”) and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
(the “bad subspace”):

\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle=\beta\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle+\alpha\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle

for some parameters
\alpha,\beta
. Let
Ref_{\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle}
be the reflection over
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
, that performs:

Ref_{\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle,\quad Ref_{\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle}\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle=-\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

We assume that this reflection can be performed by an oracle
O_{f}
; that is, there is a Boolean function
f
defined on the outputs of
Setup
that returns 1 iff an output belongs to the good subspace. We can either consider a
phase oracle
for
f
that immediately implements
Ref_{\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle}
, or a computational oracle.

Grover’s algorithm
[
11
]
and more generally amplitude amplification
[
5
]
, consist in alternating reflections over
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
, which are implemented using a
checking oracle
that recognizes the components of
\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle
, and reflections over
\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle
, which can be implemented by calling the
Setup
algorithm. Indeed, it can be shown that
Ref_{\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle}Ref_{\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle}
is a rotation of angle
2\arcsin\alpha
in the plane spanned by
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle,\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle
.

We view amplitude amplification as a way to transform a linear combination of
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle,\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle
into another.

Lemma 1

Assume that
\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle=\beta\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle+\alpha\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle
. Suppose that
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
.
Starting from
\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle
or
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
, there exists
k=\mathcal{O}\!\left(1/\alpha\right)
such that, after
k
iterations of
Ref_{\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle}Ref_{\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle}
, the resulting state is a linear combination
\beta^{\prime}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle+\alpha^{\prime}\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle
where
\alpha^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(1\right)
.

Proof

We have:

\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle=\sin\theta\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle+\cos\theta\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle

where
\theta=\arcsin\alpha
, so after
k
iterations we obtain:

\cos((\arcsin\alpha)+2k(\arcsin\alpha))\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle+\sin((\arcsin\alpha)+2k(\arcsin\alpha))\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

We have:

\displaystyle\sin((\arcsin\alpha)+2k(\arcsin\alpha))

\displaystyle\geq(2k+1)\arcsin\alpha-((2k+1)\arcsin\alpha)^{2}/6

\displaystyle\geq(2k+1)\arcsin\alpha-((2k+1)\alpha\pi)^{2}/24\kern 5.0pt.

This can be made a constant by choosing
k=\mathcal{O}\!\left(1/\alpha\right)
. If we start from
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
, the initial angle is 0 instead, but the asymptotic behavior is the same. ∎

One can be more precise with the number of iterations required to achieve
\alpha^{\prime}
close to 1, but this is not necessary for us since we focus on asymptotic complexities only.

Lemma 2

Suppose that
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
. Starting from
\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle
, there exists
k=\mathcal{O}\!\left(1/\alpha\right)
such that after
k

Ref_{\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle}Ref_{\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle}
, the resulting state is a linear combination
\beta^{\prime}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle+\alpha^{\prime}\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle
where
\beta^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(1\right)
.

Proof

The initial angle in the plane spanned by
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle,\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle
is

in that case, so after
k

\cos(\pi/2+2k(\arcsin\alpha))\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle+\sin(\pi/2+2k(\arcsin\alpha))\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle\\
=\sin(2k(\arcsin\alpha))\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle-\cos(2k(\arcsin\alpha))\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

Thus, by choosing
k=\mathcal{O}\!\left(1/\alpha\right)
we get a constant amplitude on
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
.∎

In both cases, we navigate in the plane spanned by
\left|\psi_{G}\right\rangle,\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
. If we assume that one of them (their roles can be exchanged) represents a “typical” case and the other an “atypical” case (with an amplitude

After performing the iterations, we will project the result and see if we obtain

or

2.2
MNRS Quantum Walks

The MNRS quantum walk framework
[
15
]
can be seen as an extension of quantum amplitude amplification where the implementation of
Ref_{\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle}
differs from the
Setup
algorithm. We recall here some essential features, but for a more comprehensive account, one can refer to
[
3
]
.

One considers a regular undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with degree
d
, vertex set
V
and edge set
E
, where a proportion
\varepsilon
of the vertices are
marked
(noted
M\subseteq V
). The goal of the quantum walk is to recover a marked vertex. Assume that an encoding of vertices as orthogonal quantum states is given, denoted as
\left|\hat{x}\right\rangle
for
x\in V
. For
x\in V
, let
N_{x}
be the set of its neighbors. The MNRS quantum walk is analogous to a random walk on
edges
of the graph
G
. One defines:

\displaystyle\left|p_{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{y\in N_{x}}\left|\hat{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{x\in V}\left|\hat{x}\right\rangle\left|p_{x}\right\rangle,\quad

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{M}\right\rangle

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{x\in M}\left|\hat{x}\right\rangle\left|p_{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{span}(\left|\hat{x}\right\rangle\left|p_{x}\right\rangle)_{x\in V},\quad

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{span}(\left|p_{x}\right\rangle\left|\hat{x}\right\rangle)_{x\in V}

The MNRS framework emulates
Ref_{\left|\psi_{U}\right\rangle}
by a
phase estimation
of the unitary
Ref_{B}Ref_{A}
. This phase estimation requires
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\delta})
calls to both
Ref_{B}
and
Ref_{A}
, where
\delta
is the
spectral gap
of the graph
G
. The spectral gap is related to classical random walks:
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta)
is the number of random walk steps to take, starting from any vertex, before the current vertex becomes uniformly distributed.

Both
Ref_{B}
and
Ref_{A}
can be implemented from a unitary that, on input
\left|\hat{x}\right\rangle
, produces the superposition of neighbors
\left|p_{x}\right\rangle
. This is what is commonly known as the
Update
unitary. Finally, one needs a
Check
unitary that flips the phase of bad vertices. The algorithm is often constructed so that
Check
has a trivial implementation. By applying
Algorithm

1
on the output of
Setup
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 2.1
(MNRS (informal))

There is a quantum algorithm that, using 1 call to
Setup
, and on expectation
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\right)
calls to
Update
and
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)
calls to
Check
, returns
\left|\psi_{M}\right\rangle
.

While this point of view suffices for query complexity, the state of a vertex is a very large one. Thus, keeping two entire vertices in memory is wasteful, and having to reconstruct an entire vertex during the
Update
is too costly.

Johnson Graph and Radix Trees.

Most MNRS quantum walks in cryptanalysis use
Johnson graphs
, for which this problem can be solved generically: the vertex-vertex pair can be compressed to a single vertex and a
coin
indicating the next one. This is detailed in
[
3
]
.

A Johnson graph
J(X,R)
is the graph where the vertices are the subsets of
X
containing
R
elements. Two sets are adjacent if they have exactly one differing element. In collision search and similar algorithms, the vertex
x
is encoded into
\left|\hat{x}\right\rangle
by using a specific set data structure, and possibly computing additional information. In our case we store pairs
(z,f(z))
as
(f(z)|z)
using a concatenation of bit-strings.

The quantum radix trees
[
13
]
allow both to represent efficiently an ordered set and to perform complex operations efficiently (assuming qRAM), such as adding an element, removing an element and finding an element. We can also augment the radix trees with useful information such as the number of collisions that each branch contains (in order to quickly find collisions, without having to write them down). All of this side information is part of the encoding
\left|\hat{x}\right\rangle
, and also makes trivial any implementation of
Check
that would rely on the number of collisions in a vertex.

2.3
Statistics on the Number of Collisions

In the remainder of this paper, we will work with
multicollision tuples
rather than collision pairs. By multicollision, we mean a unique tuple of elements which all have the same image by
f
(for example, a 3-collision is not counted as a pair of collisions). Our algorithm will return such tuples.

In a random function on
n
bits, the largest size of a multicollision is expected to be
\mathcal{O}\!\left(n\right)
. This means that up to a polynomial factor, the number of multicollisions and the number of collisions returned by our algorithm is the same; which is why we can focus on the former. This is more practical and equivalent to
1
up to a polynomial factor. The exceptional case (a function having a larger multicollision than expected by this bound) is taken as a case of failure of our algorithm.

Lemma 3
(
[
17
, Theorem 2]
)

Let be
n,m,\ell
with
n\leq m
. Then the probability
P(n,m,\ell)
that a random function
f:X\rightarrow Y
where
|X|=2^{n}
and
|Y|=2^{m}
has a
\ell
-multicollision satisfies
P(n,m,\ell)\leq 2^{-m(\ell-1)}\cdot\binom{2^{n}}{\ell}.

Corollary 3

There exist constants
\varepsilon
and
c
such that with probability
1-\varepsilon
, all multicollisions in a random function are tuples of size
\leq cn
.

Proof

Let
\ell=cn
. By applying the previous lemma and the standard bound
\binom{2^{n}}{\ell}\leq(e2^{n}/\ell)^{\ell}
, we get
P(n,m,\ell)\leq\left(2^{-n(1-1/\ell)}\frac{e2^{n}}{\ell}\right)^{cn}
as
n\leq m
. For large
n
, the union bound over all
\ell\geq cn
shows that a multicollision of size larger than
cn
exists with negligible probability
\varepsilon\leq 2^{-\Omega(n)}
. ∎

Let us denote
X=\{0,1\}^{n}
and
Y=\{0,1\}^{m}
. As our main algorithm considers mutiple walks, we will need to define reduced sets
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
and a corresponding function
f^{\prime}~:X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
which is simply a restriction of
f
.

We are interested in the number of multicollisions that can appear in a vertex. The vertex is a set
S^{\prime}
of elements taken uniformly at random from
X^{\prime}
, with
|S^{\prime}|<|X^{\prime}|
. All three of
|S^{\prime}|
,
|X^{\prime}|
and
|Y^{\prime}|
can be varying parameters, but we will ensure that they do not vary too much throughout the algorithm, so that our statistics remain valid from one walk to another.

We use the following heuristic.

Heuristic 1

The number of multicollisions in a set follows a Poisson distribution. When its expectation is large, the number of multicollisions follows a normal distribution.

Under this heuristic, we obtain the following result, which is our main tool to handle the statistics of collisions in vertices.

Lemma 4

Let
Z(S^{\prime})
be the number of multicollisions (of
f^{\prime}
) in the set
S^{\prime}
, with elements selected uniformly at random from
X^{\prime}
. Assume that
R/2\leq|S^{\prime}|\leq R
and
M/2\leq|Y^{\prime}|\leq M
for some global parameters
R
and
M
, where
8R<M
. There is a constant
c
such that, for any pair of constants
c_{1},c_{2}
, the probability:

\Pr_{S^{\prime}}\left[c\frac{|S^{\prime}|^{2}}{|Y^{\prime}|}-c_{1}\frac{R}{\sqrt{M}}\leq Z(S^{\prime})\leq c\frac{|S^{\prime}|^{2}}{|Y^{\prime}|}+c_{2}\frac{R}{\sqrt{M}}\right]

(1)

is also constant.

Proof

Let us write
Z:=Z(S^{\prime})
for simplicity.
By definition, we have
Z=\#\{y\in Y^{\prime}:N_{y}\geq 2\}
with
N_{y}:=\#\{x\in S^{\prime}:f^{\prime}(x)=y\}
.
By
1
, we can approximate
p:=\Pr(N_{y}\geq 2)=1-e^{-\lambda}-\lambda e^{-\lambda}
with mean
\lambda=|S^{\prime}|/|Y^{\prime}|
.
For
\lambda\in(0,1/4)
, we have
\lambda^{2}/2\leq 1-e^{-\lambda}(1+\lambda)\leq 2\lambda^{2}/3
. Note that
\lambda<1/4
follows from
8R<M
, and so
4|S^{\prime}|<|Y^{\prime}|
for any values of
S^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
.
We have
\mathbb{E}[Z]=|Y^{\prime}|\cdot p
, hence

\frac{|S^{\prime}|^{2}}{2|Y^{\prime}|}\leq\mathbb{E}[Z]\leq\frac{2|S^{\prime}|^{2}}{3|Y^{\prime}|}.

(2)

Now we aim to bound the variance.
For distinct
y,z\in Y^{\prime}
, the events
(N_{y}\geq 2)
and
(N_{z}\geq 2)
are negatively correlated, since having more elements
x\in S^{\prime}
with images
f^{\prime}(x)=y
decreases the chance that another
z\in Y^{\prime}
admits at least two preimages in
S^{\prime}
. So the covariance of these two events is negative, and thus

\mathrm{Var}(Z)=\sum_{y}\mathrm{Var}(\mathds{1}_{(N_{y}\geq 2)})+2\sum_{y<z}\mathrm{Cov}(\mathds{1}_{(N_{y}\geq 2)},\mathds{1}_{(N_{z}\geq 2)})\leq\sum_{y}\mathrm{Var}(\mathds{1}_{(N_{y}\geq 2)})

We have
\mathrm{Var}(\mathds{1}_{(N_{y}\geq 2)})=p(1-p)\leq p
, then
\mathrm{Var}(Z)\leq\mathbb{E}[Z]
.
By
Equation

2
, the standard deviation then satisfies
\sigma_{Z}\leq\sqrt{2/3}\frac{|S^{\prime}|}{\sqrt{|Y^{\prime}|}}\leq C\frac{R}{\sqrt{M}}
for constant
C>0
, as
|S^{\prime}|\leq R
and
|Y^{\prime}|\geq M/2
.

For any fixed constant
c>0
, we deduce

\Pr\left(\frac{|S^{\prime}|^{2}}{2|Y^{\prime}|}-cC\frac{R}{\sqrt{M}}\leq Z\leq\frac{2|S^{\prime}|^{2}}{3|Y^{\prime}|}+cC\frac{R}{\sqrt{M}}\right)\leq\Pr(|Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]|\leq c\sigma_{Z})

and by applying Chebyshev’s inequality, we know this probability is at least
1-1/c^{2}
, which is constant.
∎

When we take a random subset of size
R
with codomain of size
M
, with many multicollisions, the average number of multicollisions is
E=\mathcal{O}(R^{2}/M)
, and the standard deviation is
T=\mathcal{O}(R/\sqrt{M})
. As a consequence of
Lemma

4
we can say that a constant proportion of subsets have a number of multicollisions in the interval
[E;E+T]
; and also, a constant proportion fall in the interval
[E-T;E]
. Note that the technical condition
8R<M
in
Lemma

4
is also obviously validated in our applications, as the vertex size is at most of order
\mathcal{O}\!\left(2^{n}\right)
, and the domain size at least
\mathcal{O}\!\left(2^{n}\right)
, so we can always leave a constant factor of difference between these two.

We can even go further. Because of the evolution of
R
and
M
throughout our algorithm, the average of the number of multicollisions will vary as well. But if we don’t change
R
and
M
“too much”, then we still have constant probability to fall into the intervals with the new value of
E^{\prime}
.

Lemma 5

Fix
T=\mathcal{O}\!\left(R/\sqrt{M}\right)
and assume
R\ll M
. Let
R^{\prime}\in[R-T;R]
and
M^{\prime}\in[M-T;M]
. Let
E^{\prime}=c\frac{(R^{\prime})^{2}}{M^{\prime}}
where
c
is the universal constant of
Lemma

4
. Then the number of collisions in a random vertex of size
R
falls in the intervals
[E^{\prime};E^{\prime}+T]
and
[E^{\prime}-T;E^{\prime}]
with constant probability.

Proof

We simply prove that the difference between
E^{\prime}
and
E
is smaller than the standard deviation. Indeed, we have:

E^{\prime}=\frac{(R^{\prime})^{2}}{M^{\prime}}=\frac{(R-x)^{2}}{M-y}

(3)

for some
x,y\in[0;R/\sqrt{M}]
, which is much smaller than both
R
and
M
. Thus:

\displaystyle E^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}}{M}\left(1-\frac{x}{R}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{y}{M}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{R^{2}}{M}\left(1-\frac{2x}{R}+\frac{y}{M}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

We notice then that:
\frac{R^{2}}{M}\frac{x}{R}=\frac{Rx}{M}\leq\frac{R^{2}}{M^{3/2}}\ll\frac{R}{\sqrt{M}}
as we have
R\ll M
. Besides:
\frac{R^{2}}{M}\frac{y}{M}\ll y=R/\sqrt{M}
.∎

In summary, if the parameters of the set are modified up to an amount equal to the standard deviation of the number of collisions; then our reasoning on intervals still holds.

2.4
Main Ideas

In order to explain our main idea, we first briefly recall the algorithms of
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
(with slight modifications).

Ambainis’ element distinctness algorithm
[
2
]
can be reframed as an MNRS quantum walk on a Johnson graph
J(\{0,1\}^{n},R)
, where vertices are subsets of
\{0,1\}^{n}
of size
R
. A vertex is marked if it contains a collision. Therefore, the probability for a vertex to be marked is
\mathcal{O}\!\left(R^{2}/2^{m}\right)
. The spectral gap is
\delta=1/R
. The cost of
Setup
is
\mathcal{O}\!\left(R\right)
, the cost of
Update
is negligible (using a quantum radix tree), and the total gate count of the algorithm is:
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(R+\frac{2^{m/2}}{R}\sqrt{R}\right)
which is optimized by setting
R=2^{m/3}
and obtaining
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(2^{m/3}\right)
.

Chained Walk.

In the
chained
quantum walk, the goal is to obtain many collisions. One starts with a vertex of size
R
, and performs a first quantum walk. At the end of this walk, one obtains the uniform superposition of marked vertices, i.e., subsets of
\{0,1\}^{n}
containing at least one (multi)collision. These (multi)collisions are
removed
from the subset, and measured. Crucially, the rest of the vertex is not measured. The remaining elements form a uniform superposition over:

\bullet

Subsets of size
R-t
where
t
is the number of elements measured;

\bullet

Excluding the elements of
\{0,1\}^{n}
which were measured;

\bullet

Excluding all other elements of
\{0,1\}^{n}
which would have an image equal to the images measured;

\bullet

Which
do not
contain a (multi)collision.

This is the
extraction
step of the walk. After outputting this collision, the remaining state can be reused as the starting state in a new walk, with a restricted function
f
and a reduced vertex size. Note that this starting state is actually a superposition of
unmarked
vertices (that do not contain a multicollision), but this is enough for quantum search.

The gate count is of order:

R+2^{k}\frac{2^{m/2}}{R}\sqrt{R}\kern 5.0pt.

(4)

One can use any vertex size
R
as long as
R\leq 2^{m/2}
: afterwards, vertices contain on average one collision or more, and this formula is not valid anymore.

Therefore, in order to reach the optimal complexity
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{\frac{m}{3}+\frac{2k}{3}})
(obtained for
R=2^{\frac{m}{3}+\frac{2k}{3}}
), one needs to have
k\leq\frac{m}{4}
. In other words, the algorithm fails when
the number of collisions to output is too high
with respect to
m
, and is not optimal in a regime with intermediate
m
(e.g,
m=1.5n
) where we would want to output all collisions.

New Regime.

Our new algorithm follows the principle of the chained quantum walk (the final state of a walk is reused as the initial state of the next one), but fits in the regime where a vertex contains more than one (multi)collision on average.

As a simplified view, let us neglect the fact that extracting multicollisions post-selects the remaining elements, modifying their distribution (and the domain of the quantum walk). Indeed, as long as the number of extracted elements remains at most a constant proportion of the entire domain, these modifications do not significantly impact the algorithm, although we will handle them carefully.

Following
Lemma

4
, a vertex of size
R
contains
\mathcal{O}(R^{2}/2^{m})
multicollisions on average, and the standard deviation is
\mathcal{O}(R/2^{m/2})
. This means that if we extract
\mathcal{O}(R/2^{m/2})
multicollisions and measure them, we collapse on a superposition of vertices which are still
typical
: they only contain slightly less multicollisions than average. Using
Corollary

2
, we will show that we can always change a
typical
superposition of vertices into another typical one after
\mathcal{O}\!\left(1\right)
steps of quantum search, and in our case, of quantum walk.
We can thus transform our superposition of vertices into vertices having slightly
more
multicollisions than average, repeat the extraction, and so on. Therefore, at the cost of a total time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\max(\sqrt{R},R/2^{m/2}))
, we can extract
\mathcal{O}(R/2^{m/2})
multicollisions. As we have
\sqrt{R}<2^{m/2}\implies R/2^{m/2}<\sqrt{R}
, the time is dominated by
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{R})
.

We find that
2^{k}
is always larger than
(R/2^{m/2})
and the total complexity is:

R+\frac{2^{k}}{(R/2^{m/2})}\times\sqrt{R}=R+\frac{2^{k}2^{m/2}}{\sqrt{R}}\kern 5.0pt.

Optimizing
R
, we recover the time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(2^{2k/3+m/3}\right)
.

This new regime, together with the previous one of
[
3
]
(where the vertex contains less than one multicollision on average) covers all possible parameters for
k
and
m
. It also completes the time-memory trade-off curve, where for any
\ell\leq 2k/3+m/3
, there exists an algorithm with memory
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(2^{\ell}\right)
and time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(2^{k+m/2-\ell/2}\right)
: for small values of
\ell
the algorithm is the one of
[
3
]
, and for large values of
\ell
it is ours.

3
New Algorithm

This section is dedicated to the details of our new algorithm. We will rely mostly on:

\bullet

Corollary

2
to
flip
a superposition of vertices into its complementary, using a constant number of walk steps, as long as the proportion of such vertices is constant;

\bullet

Corollary

2
to
correct
a superposition of
atypical vertices
back into a superposition of typical ones;

\bullet

Lemma

4
for the statistics on the number of collisions.

3.1
Preliminaries: Restricted Functions

Throughout this section we write
X=\{0,1\}^{n}
and
Y=\{0,1\}^{m}
.

The multicollision tuples returned by our algorithm are stored in a classical, quantum-accessible table
C
with entries of the form:
u:\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}
where
f(x_{1})=\ldots=f(x_{r})=u
. The set of
images
of
C
is defined as:
I_{C}=\{u\in Y,\exists t\subseteq X\text{ s.t. }(u:t)\in C\}
. We have
I_{C}\subseteq Y
.
The set of
preimages
of
C
is defined as:
P_{C}=\{x\in X,\exists u\in I_{C},f(x)=u\}\subseteq X
. We caution the reader here that
P_{C}
is in general bigger than the multicollision tuples stored in
C
, because
C
does not necessarily contain all preimages.

We can ensure that throughout the algorithm,
|P_{C}|<2^{n-1}
. Indeed, even in the corner case where
n=m
and we want
\mathcal{O}\!\left(2^{n}\right)
collisions, we can stop the algorithm after returning
2^{n}/\mathcal{O}\!\left(n\right)
multicollision tuples, and repeat at most
\mathcal{O}\!\left(n\right)
times afterwards (ultimately, a polynomial factor in the time complexity).

Given a table
C
, we define a restricted function
f_{C}
that excludes all preimages of
C
, and all corresponding images:

\begin{cases}f_{C}~:X\backslash P_{C}\mapsto Y\backslash I_{C}\\
f_{C}(x)=f(x)\kern 5.0pt.\end{cases}

(5)

Vertices and Data Structure.

Given the table
C
, given a size parameter
R
(which varies during our algorithm), we define a set of vertices for one of our walks:

V^{R,C}:=\{S\subseteq(X\backslash P_{C}),|S|=R\}

(6)

and furthermore, we define
V^{R,C}_{x,y}\subseteq V^{R,C}
as the subset of vertices
containing between
x
and
y
multicollisions
. We authorize
y=\infty
in this notation, where
V^{R,C}_{x,\infty}
means all vertices containing more than
x
multicollisions. By definition of
P_{C}
, the corresponding images of these multicollisions are ensured to not appear in
I_{C}
.

Like in previous work
[
3
]
, subsets of
X
are identified with (orthogonal) quantum states thanks to the quantum radix tree data structure
[
13
]
, and we can operate efficiently on them. Therefore, we can introduce the notation
\left|V^{R,C}_{x,y}\right\rangle:=\sum_{S\in V^{R,C}_{x,y}}\left|S\right\rangle
as a uniform superposition of such sets. The set data structure also encodes additional data:

\bullet

The outputs of
f
;

\bullet

The number of multicollisions and their locations. This can be added to the radix tree by storing at each node the number of multicollisions that occur in its sub-tree.

Polynomial-time algorithms exist for:

\bullet

Inserting an element into the set;

\bullet

Checking if an element belongs to the set;

\bullet

Creating a uniform superposition of (multi)collisions in the vertex.

3.2
Superposition of Elements

During our algorithm, we populate the table
C
of multicollisions. However we cannot compute
P_{C}
, the set of all their preimages (we only know the preimages which belong to the table
C
). Despite this limitation, we can still create a uniform superposition over the set
X\backslash P_{C}
: intuitively, we just have to perform rejection sampling.

Lemma 6

Given
C
stored in quantum-accessible memory such that
|C|\leq 2^{n-1}
, there exists a unitary to construct a uniform superposition over
X\backslash P_{C}
:

\left|0\right\rangle\mapsto\sum_{x\in(X\backslash P_{C})}\left|x\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

(7)

with negligible error and polynomial time.

Proof

The idea is to perform a (quantum) rejection sampling, starting from:

\sum_{x\in X}\left|x\right\rangle

and testing whether
x\in P_{C}
efficiently thanks to the quantum RAM access. We obtain:

\sqrt{\frac{|X|-|P_{C}|}{|X|}}\sum_{x\in(X\backslash P_{C})}\left|x\right\rangle\left|0\right\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{|P_{C}|}{|X|}}\sum_{x\in P_{C}}\left|x\right\rangle\left|1\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

We amplify the part 0 using robust amplitude amplification techniques. In particular, while we do not know the size of
P_{C}
, an upper bound of
|P_{C}|<|X|/2
is sufficient to succeed in polynomial time. ∎

By using the same algorithm with an additional set of forbidden elements, we obtain a polynomial-time algorithm that creates a uniform superposition of neighboring vertices, which is sufficient to implement the
Update
unitary of our walks.

3.3

We consider quantum walks on Johnson graphs. The set of vertices for each walk is given by

as defined above, for

the current table of multicollisions, and

the current vertex size. As explained above,

remains similar to the initial vertex size.

Our algorithm (

and

, which are detailed later in this section. An extraction step starts from a superposition of typical vertices with more collisions than average,
\left|V^{R,C}_{E,E+T}\right\rangle
, and produces a superposition
\left|V^{R,C}_{E-T,E}\right\rangle
with less collisions. A walk step starts from
\left|V^{R,C}_{E-T,E}\right\rangle
and restores a superposition of vertices
\left|V^{R,C}_{E,E+T}\right\rangle
suitable for the next extraction step.

3.4
Extraction Steps

We detail the
extraction
routine which transforms
\left|V^{R,C}_{E,E+T}\right\rangle
into
\left|V^{R^{\prime},C^{\prime}}_{E-T,E}\right\rangle
for modified parameters
R^{\prime},C^{\prime}
, and returns
T
multicollision tuples (classical values). Actually, we give a more general routine to extract a single multicollision tuple from a state
\left|V^{R,C}_{x,y}\right\rangle
, where
x,y
are input parameters.

Basic Idea.

Given a vertex
V
, we know thanks to the tree data structure how many multicollisions appear and where they are located. The basic idea of the extraction routine is to create a uniform superposition of these collisions, outside of the tree, into a separate register, and to measure:

\bullet

the size of the register, which projects on multicollisions having the same number of elements (and determines the new parameter
R^{\prime}
);

\bullet

the actual elements of the multicollisions, which gives the new
C^{\prime}
.

This transforms the state
\left|V^{R,C}_{x,y}\right\rangle
into a superposition
\left|V^{R^{\prime},C^{\prime}}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\right\rangle
of vertices which exclude any preimage of the newly measured multicollision, and contain between
x-1
and
y-1
collisions.

An Issue with post-Selection.

There is a subtlety due to the fact that the vertices in
\left|V^{R,C}_{x,y}\right\rangle
do not contain the same number of multicollisions. Consider the following example of a superposition with a vertex having 3 collisions
\left|c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle
and a vertex having two collisions
\left|c,c^{\prime}\right\rangle
. Let us simplify the writing of these vertices by considering only the collisions, furthermore we write
\left|\{c,c^{\prime}\}\right\rangle
for an unordered
set
of elements. We will select one of the collisions and move it to a fixed position in the quantum memory, which we measure. Before measurement, the quantum state has the form:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left|c\right\rangle\left|\{c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left|c^{\prime}\right\rangle\left|\{c,c^{\prime\prime}\}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left|c^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle\left|\{c,c^{\prime}\}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left|c\right\rangle\left|\{c^{\prime}\}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left|c^{\prime}\right\rangle\left|\{c\}\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

(8)

Now, we measure. Suppose that we obtain
c
, then the state becomes proportional to the following, non-uniform superposition:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left|\{c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left|\{c^{\prime}\}\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

(9)

The issue here is that there is a post-selection depending on the number of collisions in the vertices, which is not constant in our algorithm. As we need the superposition to remain uniform, we need to correct this deviation.

Procedure.

Let us start from the superposition
\left|V_{x,y}^{R,C}\right\rangle
. In a given vertex
V
with
z
multicollisions, let
(t_{i})_{1\leq i\leq z}
be the multicollision tuples. When we create the uniform superposition of multicollisions, we obtain the state:

\sum_{x\leq z\leq y}\sum_{V\in V^{R,C}_{z}}\left|V\right\rangle\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq z}\left|t_{i}\right\rangle\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(10)

The problem here is that the amplitude over a given
t_{i}
depends on
z
(the number of multicollisions in the vertex). So we make it constant by adding new “dummy” values
d_{i}
:

\sum_{x\leq z\leq y}\sum_{V\in V^{R,C}_{z}}\left|V\right\rangle\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq z}\left|t_{i}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}\sum_{z+1\leq i\leq y}\left|d_{i}\right\rangle\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(11)

Now, we measure the last register:

\bullet

With probability
\frac{1}{|V^{R,C}_{x,y}|}\sum_{x\leq z\leq y}|V^{R,C}_{z}|\frac{z}{y}
, we obtain a multicollision. We then collapse on a uniform superposition of vertices
V^{R^{\prime},C^{\prime}}_{x-1,y-1}
where
R^{\prime}
is reduced by the size of the multicollision, and
C^{\prime}
contains this new multicollision (as a result of post-selection, the vertex excludes any preimage of the new value). This is what we wanted.

\bullet

For all
x+1\leq i\leq y
, we measure
d_{i}
with probability:

\frac{1}{|V^{R,C}_{x,y}|}\sum_{x\leq z\leq i-1}|V_{z}^{R,C}|\frac{1}{y}=\frac{1}{y}\frac{|V_{x,i-1}^{R,C}|}{|V^{R,C}_{x,y}|}

In that case we project the vertex on:

\sum_{x\leq z\leq y|z+1\leq i}\sum_{V\in V^{R,C}_{z}}\left|V\right\rangle=\left|V^{R,C}_{x,i-1}\right\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

(12)

Indeed, we still have a uniform superposition of sets, and a set belongs in this superposition if and only if it has less than
i-1
multicollisions (in which case, the dummy
\left|d_{i}\right\rangle
is created).
There are no new multicollisions extracted; we have simply changed the bounds on the number of multicollisions in the superposition of vertices.

The latter case is problematic for us, as we have refined the superposition: this makes the vertex possibly more “atypical”. Fortunately, we can always go back to the typical case, at a cost depending on the exact proportion of vertices that we fell upon, using
Lemma

2
.

Lemma 7

Assume that
V^{R,C}_{x,y}
,
V^{R,C}_{0,x}
,
V^{R,C}_{y,\infty}
contain a constant proportion of all vertices
V^{R,C}
, and
x/y=\mathcal{O}\!\left(1\right)
. Then there is a quantum algorithm with
average time

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{R}(y-x)}{y}\right)
that, on input
\left|V^{R,C}_{x,y}\right\rangle
extracts
on average
a constant number of multicollisions and returns an updated
\left|V^{R^{\prime},C^{\prime}}_{x-1,y-1}\right\rangle
.

Proof

The probability to obtain a multicollision immediately is bigger than
x/y
, which is constant. If we do not obtain a multicollision, we need to revert back to
\left|V^{R,C}_{x,y}\right\rangle
.

For all
i\geq x
, we project on
\left|V_{x,i}^{R,C}\right\rangle
with probability
\frac{1}{y}\frac{|V_{x,i}^{R,C}|}{|V^{R,C}_{x,y}|}
. Via a series of a constant number (on expectation) of manipulations, we will transform the interval
[x;i]
into
[0;x[
, then
[x;y]
.

\bullet

Starting from
[x;i]
, we use
Corollary

2
to obtain
[0;x]\cup[i;\infty[
using an expected number of
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{|V^{R,C}|}{|V^{R,C}_{x,i}|}}\right)
iterations. With constant probability we project on
[0;x]
or
[i;\infty[
.

\bullet

Starting from
[i;\infty[
, we use
Corollary

2
to obtain
[0;i[
with
\mathcal{O}\!\left(1\right)
iterations. With constant probability we project on
[0;x]
or
[x;i]
. (If
i

x

[x;i]
is actually low). In the latter case, we go back to the previous step.

As a consequence, for all
i\geq x
, we succeed later with
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{|V^{R,C}|}{|V^{R,C}_{x,i}|}}\right)
iterations on average. By taking the average over all
i
, the number of iterations that we need is, up to a constant:

\sum_{i=x}^{y}\frac{1}{y}\frac{|V_{x,i}^{R,C}|}{|V^{R,C}_{x,y}|}\sqrt{\frac{|V^{R,C}|}{|V^{R,C}_{x,i}|}}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{y}\sum_{i=x}^{y}\sqrt{\frac{|V^{R,C}_{x,i}|}{|V^{R,C}_{x,y}|}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{y-x}{y}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(13)

Here we use the fact that
|V^{R,C}|
and
|V^{R,C}_{x,y}|
are the same up a constant, and then, that
|V^{R,C}_{x,i}|\leq|V^{R,C}_{x,y}|

\sqrt{R}

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\sqrt{R}\right)
time, giving the result.∎

We use this algorithm for
\left|V^{R,C}_{E,E+T}\right\rangle
where
x=E

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{R}T/E\right)

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{R}T^{2}/E\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{R}\right)
, the same as the walk step in
Algorithm

2
.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we proved that the multiple collision search problem quantum complexity in queries (and time) is
2^{2k/3+m/3}
where
m
is the output bit-size and
2^{k}
the number of collisions, closing the remaining gap in some range of
k
and
m
. In the new targeted regime, we use MNRS quantum walks as a tool for
diffusion
in the current state rather than
search
, which allows to correct the expected number of collisions in this state – allowing to extract collisions in subsequent steps. This peculiar use of the diffusion operator in the MNRS walk could perhaps have further applications.
