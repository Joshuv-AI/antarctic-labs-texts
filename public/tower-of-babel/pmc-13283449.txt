What matters beyond model choice for wearable sleep staging? How personalization, evaluation choices, and easy-to-classify wake impact performance†
Authors: Eric Canton, Franco Tavella, Christopher Drake, Olivia Walch, Philip Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesMethods to improve sleep classification of wearable data often emphasize model choice. However, without widely used benchmark datasets, it is difficult to determine factors driving performance gains (model architecture, dataset selection, or evaluation decisions). This study examines how dataset characteristics influence sleep classification performance beyond model choice.MethodsWe collected SleepAccel-Clinical, a dataset of Apple Watch acceleration and polysomnography from 28 individuals with sleep apnea. Neural network models were trained on this dataset alongside SleepAccel (31 healthy individuals) and DREAMT (100 individuals with suspected sleep disorders) to evaluate how training and testing set choices impact performance. All models were compared against an Easy To Classify (ETC) wake model, which estimates wake probability by smoothing and scaling activity in a brief time window. The ETC model’s average area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) on each dataset was used to quantify intrinsic ease of classification.ResultsThe ETC model accounted for substantial amount of the models’ performance across the architectures, datasets, and training conditions, with significant correlations (p≪ .01) between ETC performance and all other scenarios. Including individuals with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) in the training data improved performance when testing on datasets in which sleep disorders were suspected.ConclusionsSleep classification performance depends heavily on training and testing dataset characteristics, not solely on model choice. The presence of ETC wake epochs can markedly inflate performance metrics. Researchers should benchmark models on widely available datasets and quantify intrinsic class separability when introducing new datasets. This article is part of the Consumer Sleep Technology Special Collection. Statement of SignificanceThis work addresses a critical reproducibility challenge in sleep research by demonstrating that performance differences in wearable-based sleep classifiers are dominated by dataset characteristics rather than model architecture alone. By analyzing three datasets including a newly introduced cohort with sleep apnea, we reveal how the presence of ETC wake periods and training set choice substantially affect reported metrics. These findings challenge the field’s focus on architectural innovations while highlighting systematic biases in performance evaluation. We advocate for standardized benchmark datasets and transparent data sharing practices to enable meaningful comparisons across studies. This work provides essential guidance for researchers developing and evaluating wearable sleep classification systems, ultimately advancing toward more reliable and generalizable solutions. Keywords: actigraphy, sleep classification, wearables, machine learning, sleep apnea, personalized medicine, benchmark datasets, polysomnography, consumer sleep technology

Introduction

In the last twenty years, wrist worn wearable sleep monitoring has transitioned from a research utility to a highly sought-after consumer offering. Classical “research grade” actigraphy devices, which emphasize long-term battery life and continuous capture of light and activity data, have been increasingly placed into competition with consumer sleep technologies (CSTs) that offer new sensors, stronger user-focused design and usability, and improved accuracy over older methods through proprietary black box algorithms [1, 2]. Along the way, researchers have tapped into the raw data streams offered by CSTs to develop open source models which leverage more modern machine learning approaches than classic algorithms in the sleep field (i.e. Cole-Kripke and Sadeh [3, 4]), and offer insight into what could be running under the hood of devices like the Apple Watch.

The most common inputs into models of this kind are acceleration (either raw, or processed into activity counts) and heart rate metrics derived from photoplethysmography (e.g. HRV or beats per minute), with models generally performing well at identifying sleep, struggling to identify periods of motionless wake (or low motion wake), and capturing sleep stages with an accuracy of about 60%–70% [5–17]. This aligns well with the observed performance of consumer wearable devices, which have been reported to have sleep accuracies (or sensitivity; fraction of true sleep correctly labeled as sleep by the classifier) above 90%, wake accuracies (specificity; fraction of true wake correctly labeled as wake) ranging from 20%–70%, and 4-class sleep staging accuracies from 60% to 86% [1, 2, 18].

Yet while many exciting new models have been explored for the problem of sleep classification (e.g. [5, 19]), it is difficult to compare results from paper to paper, as each model is typically trained and evaluated on a dataset unique to that publication. This stands in stark contrast to standard practice in other fields [20–24], where algorithms are evaluated against the same openly available benchmark datasets, such as MNIST for handwritten digits [20]. This standard has not yet been adopted for wearable sleep classification algorithms.

One notable exception is [25], in which the authors use the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) [26] dataset as a benchmark for comparing the performances of a wide variety of models trained on activity counts. Activity counts, usually provided in 30 second bins over the course of the night, represent the presence or absence of motion during that 30 second period, with higher numbers indicating more motion and lower numbers indicating less motion. Despite forming the foundation of many older sleep classification models, activity counts are a very coarse approximation of motion over the night and cannot pick up on subtle patterns in acceleration that happen at the level of fractions of a second, limiting the amount of information they can encode. Indeed, the authors of [25], find minimal improvement from using a convolutional neural network (wake accuracy: 67.6%, sleep accuracy 92%) trained on activity counts over the much simpler logistic regression (wake accuracy: 67.2%, sleep accuracy 90.7%) trained on the same data, suggesting that activity counts may introduce a hard ceiling on how good the performance of any classifier can be, the same way an image classification algorithm can only perform so well if the inputs it is provided are downscaled and pixelated. In the absence of using the same benchmark to evaluate both simple and more complex algorithms, such an important result may have been slow to come into focus.

As the MESA data collection was carried out with the research grade devices available at the time (Actiwatch Spectrum), it does not contain the raw acceleration data needed for models seeking to push past the apparent performance ceiling of activity counts. This limits its utility as a benchmark dataset for algorithms that require high resolution (e.g. 32 Hz or higher) triaxial acceleration. Unfortunately, no datasets of MESA’s size with high resolution triaxial acceleration and co-recorded polysomnography (PSG) have been put into publicly available repositories like PhysioNet and the National Sleep Research Repository. Two smaller datasets, however, are available open access: SleepAccel [6, 27] (N = 31, healthy volunteers) and DREAMT [28, 29] (N = 100, disordered sleeper population), both of which provide high resolution acceleration alongside polysomnography. An additional dataset comes from Weaver et al. [19, 30], which consists of acceleration data from children with suspected sleep disorders and was shared with us on request by the authors.

Here, we introduce a fourth dataset, SleepAccel-Clinical, which adds another 28 people with sleep apnea to the pool of datasets of co-recorded polysomnography and raw acceleration available for benchmarking performance. We use this dataset, as well as SleepAccel, DREAMT, and the data set from Weaver et al. to compare models trained on wearable data from healthy individuals to models trained on people with sleep disorders in order to assess how choice of training dataset affects performance in individuals with sleep apnea or other sleep disorders. We also examine how choices about the testing dataset can affect performance metrics. Based on our findings, we hypothesize that performance can be increased with a precision medicine approach where training and evaluation are conducted on datasets with matching characteristics. We also demonstrate that performance metrics are highly correlated with easy-to-classify (ETC) wake periods.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Four datasets were used in this work. First, SleepAccel, an open access dataset of Apple Watch triaxial acceleration and polysomnography consisting of 31 individuals without a sleep disorder was used as the benchmark for healthy controls; we refer to this set as the “healthy” population below. Demographics information and other details for this dataset are discussed in [6]. For a large, diverse set of individuals with sleep disorders, the DREAMT dataset (64 Hz version 2.0.0), featuring acceleration from the Empatica wristband and polysomnography, was used; full details for that dataset are reported on PhysioNet [28] and in the accompanying report [29]. We also include the subjects with Apple Watch data from [19, 30], providing N = 142 additional training and testing recordings from a pediatric population (ages 5–12 years) with suspected sleep disruptions. Details of the new dataset introduced in this manuscript are provided below.

SleepAccel-Clinical. 40 subjects were recruited for participation in the study from Henry Ford Health Sleep Disorders and Research Center. Participants were sampled from the clinic, and only included patients who were referred by their physician for an in-lab polysomnography screening for suspected obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Exclusion criteria included clinical patients that did not stay for a full night or engaged in a titration night during which a portion of the overnight was dedicated to adjusting CPAP parameters. One participant requested to abstain from completion of the study, 4 participants’ data were excluded due to incomplete recordings or less than 6 hours of recorded data, and 7 participants did not meet the requirements of having OSA, with an Apnea Hypopnea Index (AHI) < 5 (i.e. they were given another sleep disorder diagnosis, such as narcolepsy, or were found not to have a sleep disorder). Data from the remaining 28 subjects were eligible for inclusion.

Participants underwent an 8-hour sleep opportunity (i.e. an 8-hour window of time, from lights out to lights on) monitored with PSG with lights out at the self-reported habitual bedtime. The PSG screening followed clinic protocol for a full-body hookup conducted by trained sleep technicians on participants prior to lights off. Participants were recruited upon arrival, and those that provided written consent were given the Apple Watch [31]. The Apple Watch was applied to the non-dominant wrist with a mobile application that recorded raw acceleration data (open source; available at https://www.github.com/ojwalch/sleep_accel). The phone synced to the Apple Watch was placed in the room on a charger, face down, and out-of-reach of the participant. The recording began when the subject was in bed, approximately 5–10 minutes before lights out.

Eight hours after lights out, the Apple Watch and associated phone were collected by a member of the research team who stopped the recording. After the subject was compensated, the data was processed and stored on the Henry Ford Health System server.

Data preprocessing, feature selection, and model choice

All details of the preprocessing, feature extraction, and machine learning code can all be found on the pisces2 repository on GitHub (https://www.github.com/arcascope/pisces2).

To explore the limits of battery life, a range of sampling rates were collected in SleepAccel-Clinical, ranging in mean sampling rate from 9 to 57 Hz. The set of mean sampling rates across all subjects has a median of 50 Hz. We detected a timing alignment issue in several DREAMT recordings between 64 Hz accelerometer and PSG, and used the cosine distance between the activity proxy feature (see below) and PSG wake to determine an optimal time offset correction.

All polysomnography data was scored in 30 second epochs. In the different models explored, acceleration data was processed in different ways to generate input features aligned with the time interval of the PSG. In some cases, there was a 1:1 correspondence between acceleration input features to the model and PSG epochs (e.g. a single numeric measure of activity in that 30 second bin); in other cases, the acceleration input was mapped by the model to line up with the PSG values (e.g. a spectrogram of acceleration being passed as input to a neural net, with the FFT window step chosen to align with the PSG binned into 30 second epochs). Full details for each model are included in the accompanying code.

Missing data in the accelerometer signal was handled by excluding epochs with fewer than 32 datapoints during the 30 second interval, as well as the 1.5 minutes surrounding the epoch. Unscorable PSG epochs were also excluded from the analysis.

The only inputs to our models are derived from triaxial accelerometer recordings, scored against PSG reported with a 30 second epoch length; in particular, we do not use smartwatch reported heart rate from photoplethysmography (PPG). Recent literature finds that in many cases these signals can be recovered directly from frequency analysis of accelerometer recordings, see [19, 32] for example. For simplicity, we do not include explicit heart rate proxies like PPG and limit our inputs to spectrograms and binned activity levels. Since we focus on sleep–wake evaluation, not including PPG is expected to give a representative measure of model performance. For example, [19] does not report a significant difference in sleep–wake performance when including heart rate.

We began by normalizing the accelerometer so that Earth’s gravity reads as value 1.0 when aligned with one of the accelerometer’s x, y, or z axes (or -1.0, for the reversed orientation). This step is important because while the Apple Watch natively exports accelerometer samples this way, the Empatica device used by DREAMT uses 64.0 for the value of Earth’s gravity. We then mask low sampling rate regions, as described above. Finally, we resample the three axes to 32.0 Hz (dt = 31.25 ms). Once the recordings are normalized and resampled, we compute the (magnitude of) jerk, i.e. jerk[i] = √((x[i + 1] – x[i])² + (y[i + 1] – y[i])² + (z[i + 1] – z[i])²).

Finally, we compute the spectrogram of this jerk feature using a Fourier transform window corresponding to 32 seconds (so 32 • 32 = 1024 points; chosen to be a power of 2), with a 30 second step size between windows. We then process these spectrograms into features that are aligned in time for models as follows to generate two features for use in analysis:

Spectrogram Feature: filtering the spectrograms to just frequencies 0.0 to 3.0 Hz, which focuses on sleep-relevant bands such as breathing (9–25 BPM) and heart rate (30–120 BPM).

Activity Proxy Feature: sum all the frequency powers per Fourier window.

The Spectrogram feature was used for the LSTM model; all other models used the Activity Proxy.

After per-recording processing and feature extraction is complete, we normalize the features in a way that avoids skewing by high activity (feature clipping). We do this by centering features around 0.0 and rescaling based on values where activity is low, applying the same centering and scaling to the high activity times using the scale and center from the low-activity only.

Finally, we pad each recording to exactly 1500 PSG time stamps by extending PSG by the masking value. We extend input features by padding with 0.0 to match the 1500 epoch length.

To evaluate the ability of a variety of models to score the sleep in these features, we compare the performance of several models:

1. ETC model: As a baseline for performance, not updated with training data, we use a trivial classifier that smooths/blurs the activity proxy feature in a narrow window around each PSG epoch using a Gaussian filter. This model captures the simple heuristic that activity and the wake class are strongly correlated.

2. Logistic regression (in the style of Cole-Kripke)

3. Random Forests

4. LSTM, based on the code from [19].

We selected these models to represent a range of methods that have been used over the years, including the recent LSTM. We do not aim to be exhaustive, nor propose a superior model type to those developed previously. Aside from ETC, these model types are exactly those considered in [19].

The models are implemented using Scikit-Learn for Logistic Regression and Random Forests, TensorFlow for ETC and LSTM models.

For the LSTM model, we use the 0-3 Hz spectrogram band as the input to the model. For the ETC, logistic regression, and random forest models, we use only the activity proxy as input to the classifiers.

Cross-validation and analytical approach

In every scenario, no data from an individual in the testing set was used in the training set or process. LSTM models used a model checkpoint that monitored loss on the 3 subject validation set from SleepAccel-Clinical who did not have a diagnosed sleep disorder, and selected weights with the lowest loss on the healthy validation set from all training epochs at the end of 200 epochs.

The models are cross-validated by training on all data from one of the SleepAccel sets and evaluating on recordings from the other data sets.

When the training or initialization of models includes a random seed, we noticed a small but significant variation between initial seeds and so chose to use 3 seeds for each trained model’s initialization. Metrics we report are averages across initial seeds.

Results

Models trained exclusively on people with a sleep disorder yields best performance for people across ages with suspected sleep disorders

To understand how the choice of training dataset could influence results, we trained models on a healthy population without sleep apnea (SleepAccel), which we label as “Healthy”, and a clinical population with a sleep apnea diagnosis (SleepAccel-Clinical), labeled “Clinical”. These models were then evaluated on sleepers from the DREAMT data set with a clinical sleep disorder, the data from Weaver et al. [19] as well as the SleepAccel subpopulation (healthy or clinical) that was not in the training set.

We found a consistent improvement in area under the ROC curve (AUROC) for subjects with a suspected sleep disorder (DREAMT and Weaver) when evaluated using models trained on SleepAccel-Clinical, versus a model trained only on SleepAccel. The DREAMT set revealed a consistent and significant improvement of 0.02 - 0.035 AUROC when models were trained on SleepAccel-Clinical versus SleepAccel. Of the models and training sets considered, only LSTM performed significantly better than ETC when pooled across all DREAMT subjects. We see the same improvement of clinical over healthy training data when evaluating models with Weaver et al. data, albeit in the 0.01 - 0.02 range. See Figure 1, cf. The fraction of “easy-to-classify” wake in a testing dataset can dominate model performance

The DREAMT dataset consists of individuals with a sleep disorder (not exclusively apnea) and many of them are diagnosed with more severe sleep apnea than the SleepAccel-Clinical set. The observed failure of LSTM to improve significantly over the ETC model led us to investigate how much the AUROC of the ETC model dominates performance seen by any model. Essentially, the wake found by ETC can be found by any of these models, by simply modeling high wake probability when activity is high. True wake correctly predicted by ETC we define here to be ETC wake. A simple illustration of ETC wake is provided in Figure 2A and B. In it, synthetic data (Figure 2A) is used to show how increasing the amount of ETC wake pulls the wake accuracy (fraction of wake epochs correctly identified as wake) towards 1, increasing the AUROC despite no change in the performance of the model during the night, when it is most relevant. See also Supplementary Figure 2 for an example hypnogram and activity proxy of a DREAMT subject with about 2.5 hours of easily classified wake at the beginning and a corresponding ETC model AUROC of 98%.

The simple illustration in Figure 2A and B is validated experimentally in Figure 2C. In 2C, the AUROC for a single individual is shown when evaluated using a non-ETC model trained on all (other) individuals in either the healthy or clinical populations of SleepAccel. All models and training data are combined in this scatterplot. As the AUROC of ETC wake increases in that night of sleep, the AUROC for other models increases as well, with a strongly significant correlation (p ≪ 0.01). That the median AUROC of ETC wake is 3.3% higher in DREAMT versus SleepAccel-Clinical (Figure 2C), with many participants moving significantly before falling asleep (Supplementary Figure 2), suggests that the performance differences observed with DREAMT as the dataset in question (Supplementary Figure 3.D) may be attributable to this higher concentration of ETC wake in that population.

Discussion

This study sought to inform the evaluation and improvement of sleep classification methods, with particular focus on how we can determine if novel methods are truly an improvement compared to models put forth in the past. Without a benchmark dataset, it’s hard to say. Here, we use four different datasets to understand the extent to which training set matters in reporting performance for a fixed model choice. We find that for healthy individuals, the use of individuals with sleep apnea in a training set does not hurt performance so long as sufficiently many individuals without a sleep disorder are included in training. For a population of sleep apnea patients, however, model performance may benefit from training solely on data from apnea patients. Across different training/testing sets, we see very different ROC curves (Figure 1D), varying in mean AUROC by up to 5.5%, despite a fixed model choice.

These results have two major implications. First, that personalization of a model—training exclusively on a population with sleep apnea or heavily weighting apnea samples in training—can improve performance for people with sleep apnea. As models are trained on larger and larger datasets, our results highlight the importance of intentionally including diverse samples (e.g. apnea patients) in the training data if said model is expected to work well for patients across the board.

While it may be tempting to suggest that this can be controlled for by reporting in a manuscript whether the population used for training and testing had a sleep disorder, the markedly different results yielded when using the DREAMT and Weaver datasets of individuals with a suspected sleep disorder versus our own SleepAccel-Clinical dataset suggests this disordered/not-disordered binary is too simplistic a way of characterizing datasets. The participants contributing data to DREAMT generally had more severe sleep problems than those in SleepAccel-Clinical, which frequently caused them to move more during the night (Supplementary Figure 2). Even very simple classifiers can learn that obvious movement almost always corresponds to wake. This, in turn, makes it so the floor on wake accuracy performance when DREAMT or Weaver is used as the dataset of choice is higher than for other datasets with fewer ETC wake epochs.

Indeed, while the DREAMT or Weaver dataset’s many ETC wake epochs almost certainly are due to the serious sleep disorders of the population enrolled in that data collection, the ability of ETC wake to significantly impact performance presents an opportunity for “metric hacking” in future data collections. To improve apparent sleep and wake accuracy numbers, one could simply define the period of time being evaluated to be wider than the traditional lights-off to lights-on period; e.g. extending from one hour before PSG start to one hour after and padding with wake on either side. Such an evaluation choice could easily go unnoticed, especially if the code and data are never shared. In the same vein, a protocol that includes more obvious motion after the start of PSG (e.g. a calibration test to make sure the watch is recording successfully) could be innocuously implemented but have similar impacts on the reported performance numbers, muddying the narrative in the literature around which models perform best in much the same way. While it is natural to look for ways to control for the amount of ETC wake in interpreting results in new datasets, such a correction factor is likely to be challenging to arrive at given the inability to quantify the amount ETC wake without access to the data itself.

There is one solution to this problem: Using benchmark datasets to report performance. Through the use of benchmarks, factors such a sleep disorder status, protocol choices, and anything else that could matter for performance are controlled for. Without benchmarking, there is no way to distinguish between a model that truly does better and a testing dataset with a lot of easy dunks. While we propose SleepAccel, SleepAccel-Clinical, DREAMT, and Weaver dataset as benchmark datasets for any sleep classification algorithm based in raw acceleration (or the other data types available in these datasets), we strongly encourage the sharing of other datasets, on PhysioNet or the National Sleep Research Resource, to broaden and expand the pool of available benchmarks for future work. Said future work could include analyzing the effects of CPAP usage on how models perform in individuals with a sleep apnea diagnosis, as well as stress-testing personalized models to look for signs of overfitting and poor generalizability.

Our work has a number of limitations. We are relying on using acceleration signals from a consumer device which, while more likely to be free from bias than proprietary signals, may still be influenced by device hardware (see, e.g. the potential motion artifact in ActiGraph reported in [30]). Our number of participants is lower than other datasets that have been reported in the past, and the same results may not hold as the number of participants increases. The SleepAccel-Clinical dataset is restricted only to apnea patients, while the DREAMT dataset has a larger number of participants with a wider range of sleep disorders, and our hypothesis of ETC wake may not be the only reason for the differences in performance when the two datasets are used for testing. Moreover, the DREAMT dataset takes raw acceleration data from the Empatica wristband as opposed to the Apple Watch, which could introduce an additional variable influencing results.

Nonetheless, the results presented here strongly indicate that wearable sleep classifiers need to follow the example of the rest of the machine learning world in anchoring results to benchmark datasets or risk running in circles, unable to track which modeling decisions best improve performance and which are needlessly introducing complexity. Without reporting results on a benchmark dataset, a new sleep classifier model’s performance cannot be understood. Moreover, increased data sharing, as done by the authors of [19] for this manuscript, is needed to increase the pool of available benchmarks. There are many reasons why sharing datasets with health data included can be challenging—for instance, the labor involved in removing identifying timestamps—but the benefits of benchmark datasets to the field are clear and more are urgently needed.

Conclusion

Raw acceleration collected from a consumer device can be used to distinguish sleep from wake. Performance of models trained on this data depends not only on the choice of model employed but also properties of the data used to train and evaluate its performance. Models trained only on people with sleep apnea outperform models trained on healthy controls when applied to sleep apnea populations, suggesting the potential value in personalization. The same model trained and tested on two different populations of disordered sleepers can yield very different results, with some of the difference apparently due to the presence of more or fewer ETC wake epochs. Benchmark datasets are the key to controlling for factors such as these and ensuring future models and results are able to be compared.
