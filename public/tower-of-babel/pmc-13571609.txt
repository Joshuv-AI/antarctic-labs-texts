Nurse and Patient Perspectives on Self-Management and mHealth Service Requirements of Patients with Neurogenic Bladder Following Spinal Cord Injury: A Qualitative Study Based on the Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change
Authors: Lulu Zheng, Weiyue Cong, Li Li, Yuantong Zang, Na Li, Guanzhong Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGuided by the Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change (ITHBC), this study aimed to explore self-management barriers and mobile health (mHealth) service requirements among patients with spinal cord injury-related neurogenic bladder from patient and nurse perspectives, providing evidence for developing a nurse-patient collaborative home-based self-management platform. The ITHBC highlights the synergistic interaction among knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation, and social support, fitting this study’s goal of identifying multidimensional self-management barriers. Dual-perspective exploration also compensates for limitations of single-perspective studies and more comprehensively identifies clinical barriers and practical requirements.Patients and MethodsThis study adopted a descriptive qualitative design. Participants including 15 patients with neurogenic bladder following spinal cord injury and 11 nurses were recruited via purposive sampling from the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine and the Department of Spinal Surgery at a tertiary hospital in Inner Mongolia from August to December 2025. Semi-structured interviews were conducted, with telephone interviews for patients and face-to-face interviews for nurses. The ITHBC served as the conceptual framework; interview data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method.ResultsFour themes were extracted: (1) knowledge and belief barriers; (2) self-regulation barriers; (3) social support barriers; (4) mHealth service requirements.ConclusionPatients with neurogenic bladder following spinal cord injury suffer multidimensional self-management barriers, and conventional follow-up fails to meet their long-term home rehabilitation requirements. Guided by ITHBC, a mobile home-based self-management platform developed by integrating the requirements elicited from patients and nurses, may help address these self-management challenges and strengthen the social support chain, offering a new avenue for optimizing neurogenic bladder care. Nevertheless, implementation challenges including increased workload and legal liability warrant careful consideration. It should be noted that this is only a qualitative exploratory study. The conceptual mobile health platform proposed in this paper requires iterative development and effectiveness verification in future research before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe neuropathological condition that causes motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction, leading to loss of self-care ability, high incidence of complications, aggravated psychological stress, and heavy economic burden.1 Neurogenic bladder (NB) is one of the common lower urinary tract dysfunctions following SCI, with an incidence of 70%–84%.2 Due to detrusor‑sphincter dyssynergia, patients are vulnerable to complications including urinary tract infections, urinary calculi, hydronephrosis, urosepsis, renal impairment, and even urinary system tumors.3 These complications not only severely reduce quality of life, but also constitute the primary cause of readmission among patients with SCI.4

The 2023 China Spinal Cord Injury Patient Quality of Life and Disease Burden Survey Report showed that 74% of patients with SCI experienced urinary incontinence within 3 months post-injury. Each patient suffered approximately 10 urological complications annually, and the risk of upper urinary tract damage within 5 years after injury was 30 times higher than that of the general population.5 This challenge is not unique to China. Loftus et al6 conducted a survey of 1461 patients with spinal cord injury in the United States and found that bladder management was their top daily health concern, which exerted a greater impact than pain and bowel management. Taking Australia as an example, urinary complications constitute one of the leading causes of readmission within the first two years after injury, and the cumulative expenditure for emergency department visits and inpatient admissions solely attributable to urinary tract infections exceeded 3.3 million Australian dollars.7 Notably, even in regions with relatively abundant medical resources, a 2025 Brazilian study reported that among 903 patients with SCI and comorbid neurogenic bladder, 58.4% suffered from urinary incontinence, and clean intermittent catheterization remained their mainstay of bladder management.8 Inconvenience and financial burdens resulting from frequent hospital visits, coupled with limited medical resources, prevent numerous patients from accessing continuous professional in-hospital and outpatient support, forcing them to rely on home-based self-management. Nevertheless, self-management carried out without professional supervision often yields unsatisfactory outcomes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that effective post-discharge self-management reduces complications and improves quality of life and self-efficacy in patients with SCI and NB.9,10 However, Zlatev et al11 found, through a 5-year follow-up, that patients’ self-management efficacy gradually declined after hospital discharge, with up to 50% of patients abandoning intermittent catheterization. A telephone survey carried out by Zacharia et al12 involving 121 community-dwelling SCI patients further indicated that although 73.6% of the subjects continued intermittent catheterization after hospital discharge, only 15% maintained full adherence, 59% exhibited partial adherence, and 26% ultimately discontinued the procedure. Chinese studies indicate that most patients with SCI and NB lack awareness of rehabilitation training and evidence-based self-management methods.13 When these patients are removed from professional supervision, interrupted continuity of care, inadequate guidance on bladder function training, and poor adherence hinder long-term effective management, and predispose them to vesicoureteral reflux and upper tract damage.14 In summary, the self-management capacity of patients with SCI and NB remains generally weak, and patients face multiple barriers.

Currently, support for self-management among SCI patients with NB predominantly takes the form of traditional approaches, such as structured verbal health education, distribution of patient education brochures, and follow-up via telephone or WeChat groups. Although these methods can enhance patients’ self-management capacity to a certain extent,15 they are constrained by limited manpower, time, and space, hindering real-time monitoring and continuous guidance, often resulting in fragmented care.16 With the rapid advancement of information technology and growing demand for health information services among the general population, mHealth has emerged as a promising solution. Defined as the delivery of medical services and information via mobile communication technologies—including personal digital assistants (PDAs), mobile phones, and satellite communications—mHealth is efficient, convenient, and highly informative, gradually becoming a mainstream modality for home-based chronic disease rehabilitation.17

In recent years, domestic and international studies have begun to explore the application of mobile health tools in spinal cord injury rehabilitation. A questionnaire survey conducted by Iranian scholar Azad et al involving 20 SCI patients and 20 spinal cord injury specialists identified three major modules of core demands for mobile applications: educational information, disease management, and technical functions.18 Turkish researcher Aras et al verified through a randomized controlled trial that Clean Intermittent Catheterization (CIC) training delivered via mobile applications could significantly improve catheter-related quality of life and treatment adherence among patients.19 New Zealand scholar Parittotokkaporn et al completed a 4-week telehealth-delivered home-based transcutaneous electrical sacral nerve stimulation (TES) intervention trial, in which 15 SCI patients received 15-minute daily treatments at home with courier-delivered equipment and remote supervision. The trial achieved a 100% adherence rate with 80% of participants expressing confidence in device operation, and 66% perceived symptomatic improvements, which confirmed the feasibility and high acceptability of telehealth interventions for home-based SCI management.20 Chinese scholar Liu et al further confirmed via a multicenter randomized controlled trial that App-based transitional care could effectively enhance the self-efficacy of patients with spinal cord injury, whereas its beneficial effect on quality of life required longer-term follow-up for further verification.21

The above studies have provided valuable preliminary evidence for the adoption of mHealth tools in SCI rehabilitation. Nevertheless, existing investigations predominantly address two questions: what types of tools patients require and whether these tools exert beneficial effects. Sufficient attention has rarely been paid to a more fundamental issue: what underlying barriers patients encounter during self-management and how these barriers are formed.

Specifically, on the one hand, most existing studies have collected data solely through questionnaires, and lack in-depth qualitative interviews to capture divergent perceptions of the same self-management behaviors between patients and nurses. On the other hand, no prior research has systematically analyzed the three dimensions of knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation, and social support under the analytical framework of the Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change. This limitation results in weak explanatory power of current evidence regarding barriers to self-management.

The Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change (ITHBC), proposed by Ryan22 in 2009, is an emerging theoretical framework for disease management. It integrates three core components: knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation, and social support. The theory posits that health behavior change can be facilitated by advancing knowledge and beliefs, enhancing self-regulation skills and capabilities, and strengthening social support. To date, it has been applied and yielded positive results in self-management studies for multiple chronic conditions including diabetes, tuberculosis, and stroke,23–25 yet it remains rarely adopted in qualitative studies concerning self-management among patients with SCI-related neurogenic bladder (NB). Moreover, the ITHBC emphasizes the synergistic interaction among knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation, and social support, which is highly consistent with the exploratory objectives of the present study; hence, it was selected as the analytical framework for this investigation.

Given the above research gaps, this study adopted the Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change as the overarching theoretical framework and conducted in-depth interviews from the dual perspectives of patients and nurses to systematically analyze the existing barriers to self-management among patients with neurogenic bladder following spinal cord injury. Meanwhile, we summarized and analyzed the mHealth requirements emerging from interview data. The findings are expected to provide empirical evidence for the development of a standardized mHealth platform for bladder self-management that is theoretically underpinned, clinically practical, and patient-nurse collaborative. Subjects and Methods

Research Design

To guarantee methodological rigor and reliable results, this study strictly followed the 32-item checklist of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) to standardize the research design.26

Participants

Purposive sampling was adopted. To ensure sample heterogeneity, patient recruitment took into account age, sex, education, injury level, and ASIA grade; nurse recruitment considered working years and professional title.

Patient inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Clinically diagnosed with spinal cord injury (SCI) in accordance with the 2019 revised International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI) issued by the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA);27 (2) Presenting at least one bladder dysfunction: urinary incontinence, urinary retention, or post-void residual urine volume>100 mL; (3) Stable condition during SCI recovery; (4) Disease duration ≥3 months and consistent implementation of standardized bladder self-management, including fixed fluid intake plans, catheterization/voiding schedules, and bladder function training; (5) Clear consciousness without cognitive, mental or communication disorders, with basic verbal capacity to understand and respond to interview questions; (6) Voluntary participation with written informed consent.

Patient exclusion criteria were: (1) Receiving cystostomy; (2) Complicated with severe comorbidities, including spinal tumors, pulmonary infection, cardiac insufficiency, etc.; (3) Unwilling to share personal thoughts or withdraw from the interview halfway.

Nurse inclusion criteria were: (1) Full-time registered nurses working in the rehabilitation department; (2) Clinical experience ≥3 years; (3) Rich experience in nursing patients with neurogenic bladder; (4) Voluntary participation with informed consent.

The sample size was determined by data saturation, which meant recruitment stopped once no new themes emerged in data analysis.28 This research was launched in August 2025 at the Department of Spinal Surgery and Rehabilitation Medicine of a tertiary hospital in Inner Mongolia. Patient interviews were scheduled one week after discharge, a critical high-risk window for bladder management complications;29 while nurse interviews took place in the department demonstration room. Recruitment and data analysis proceeded concurrently. By December 2025, no new thematic information was generated after additional interviews, so participant recruitment was terminated. Relevant literature predicted that 12–30 patients and 8–12 nurses were required to reach data saturation.30,31 During the study period, 23 patients were contacted, of whom 5 declined due to persistent depression, 2 declined due to physical discomfort, and 1 dropped out mid-interview and was excluded. Among the 13 contacted nurses, 2 declined due to work conflicts. Finally, 15 patients and 11 nurses signed informed consent and completed the interviews.

Research Methods

Interview Guide Development

This study adopted ITHBC as the conceptual framework. The theory identifies three interrelated core dimensions—knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation, and social support—that jointly shape health behavior outcomes.22 Within this framework, knowledge refers to context-specific factual information, while beliefs represent individuals’ perceptions of a given health condition or health behavior. Self-regulation describes the cognitive and behavioral processes people use to integrate health behavior changes into daily routines. Social facilitation covers two components: social influence and social support. Social influence means guidance from authoritative or credible sources reshapes individuals’ thoughts and motivation to engage in healthy behaviors, while social support includes emotional, instrumental, or informational assistance, which together promote sustained health behavior adherence.22

Drawing on domestic and international clinical guidelines,32,33 this study defines self-management as a set of standardized behaviors for patients with SCI-related neurogenic bladder to preserve bladder function and avoid complications, including clean intermittent catheterization (CIC), structured fluid intake regimens, voiding log documentation, pelvic floor muscle training, and regular urinalysis surveillance. mHealth service needs refer to patients’ comprehensive demands for professional support and services delivered via mobile terminals such as smartphones or tablets, alongside associated applications, mini-programs, or WeChat official accounts during home-based self-management.34

The research team drafted an initial interview guide based on relevant literature and the ITHBC framework. Two rehabilitation nursing specialists (holding a master’s degree or above with associate senior professional titles or higher) reviewed and revised the draft. Pilot semi-structured interviews with two patients and two nurses refined the guide.

Interview questions for patients: (1) What is your understanding of neurogenic bladder, and how do you manage your voiding routine in daily life? (2) Do you think consistent bladder self-management matters for your rehabilitation? (3) Please describe your daily schedule for fluid intake, voiding, and catheterization. Can you fully follow this plan? (4) How do you cope with complications such as urinary tract infection, fever, or urine leakage? (5) Does your family provide assistance with bladder management? Do you receive sufficient professional guidance from medical staff? (6) Do you communicate bladder self-management experiences with other patients who have similar spinal cord injuries? (7) Would you use a mobile application that assists with fluid tracking, voiding schedules, catheterization reminders, and provider communication? What core functions would you expect this app to contain? (8) What are your main concerns or barriers preventing you from using such a mobile tool?

Interview questions for nurses: (1) Based on your clinical observations, what is the general level of disease and self-management knowledge among patients with neurogenic bladder after spinal cord injury? (2) How would you characterize patients’ beliefs and long-term willingness to persist in bladder self-management? (3) What major challenges and misconceptions do you commonly observe when patients implement self-management behaviors? (4) In your experience, how does family support affect patients’ adherence to bladder self-management? (5) What limitations exist within the current professional nursing support system for these patients? (6) From a clinical perspective, what essential functions should mHealth tools be equipped with to support patients’ bladder management? (7) What is your greatest concern regarding the application of such mHealth tools in clinical practice?

Data Collection

This descriptive qualitative study used semi-structured interviews. Given patients’ limited mobility and scattered residential locations, telephone interviews were arranged approximately one week after hospital discharge. Novick proposed that telephone interviews should not be treated as a secondary data collection option for grounded theory and other qualitative designs; instead, they can serve as a credible primary approach.35 This mode has also been proven feasible for SCI populations in prior investigations.36

Before participants’ discharge, researchers conducted ward-based recruitment, explained the study purpose, reviewed the interview guide, clarified confidentiality rules, obtained written informed consent, and scheduled follow-up telephone interviews. One week before the interview, researchers confirmed the appointment time with participants. Patient telephone interviews lasted 30–40 minutes, while face-to-face nurse interviews (conducted in the department demonstration room) took 20–30 minutes on average. Two postgraduate nursing students with systematic training in qualitative research jointly completed all interviews: one led the conversation, and the other took field notes and operated audio recording equipment. During interviews, researchers maintained a neutral attitude, listened actively, adjusted questioning sequences and probing depth flexibly according to responses, and strictly avoided leading questions. All interviews were fully audio-recorded. Within 24 hours post-interview, two researchers independently transcribed recordings verbatim and marked nonverbal cues with reference to field notes. Detailed field notes on nonverbal behaviors were recorded during in-person nurse interviews. Since bodily nonverbal signals could not be observed in telephone interviews with patients, researchers documented paralinguistic characteristics including pauses, tone shifts, and emotional reactions, and supplemented contextual notes immediately after each call. Data saturation was achieved after interviewing 12 patients and 9 nurses, with no new themes emerging from subsequent conversations.; The remaining 3 patients and 2 nurses had already signed up and confirmed their interviews before saturation was identified. To respect participants’ consent and maximize sample demographic diversity, all scheduled interviews were completed as planned. No new themes emerged after including these additional participants, which further validated full data saturation. Finally, interview data from all 15 patients and 11 nurses were analyzed.

Data Analysis

Within 24 hours post-interview, two researchers transcribed audio recordings, supplemented transcripts with field notes, and cross-validated all textual data. Data were managed with NVivo 12 software and analyzed via qualitative content analysis.37 Ambiguous or confusing segments were clarified by replaying audio files or group discussion. Two researchers independently coded the data. Specifically, both analysts separately coded the first five transcripts, then held meetings to compare coding outputs and resolve inconsistent labels through discussion. Subsequent transcripts were coded by the primary coder, with regular team conferences organized to polish the coding framework and sustain analytical consistency. Intercoder reliability statistics were not calculated in this study; this practice aligns with the principles of Colaizzi’s seven-step method, where coding consistency is established via mutual consensus rather than statistical indicators.

Data analysis followed six standardized procedures: (1) An a priori coding framework was established grounded in the Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change (ITHBC), covering three core dimensions: knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation, and social support; (2) Researchers repeatedly read full transcripts and reviewed audio recordings to achieve thorough familiarity with all raw data; (3) Open coding was performed to extract discrete meaning units including words, phrases, sentences and paragraphs; (4) Similar and interrelated codes were aggregated to generate subthemes and overarching themes; (5) Preliminary analytical outcomes were sent to four participants for member checking to verify whether the results truly reflected their experiences. The four participants were selected to cover typical demographic features: one less-educated elderly rural patient, one well-educated young urban patient, one junior-title nurse and one senior-title nurse, with their willingness to take part in validation also considered. Although only four participants were involved in member checking, this step aimed to identify resonance between interpretations and participants’ original experiences rather than pursuing statistical representativeness, and it is a widely used method to boost qualitative trustworthiness.38 All four participants confirmed alignment between findings and their personal experiences, with no major inconsistencies found after verification. (6) Inductive analysis was conducted on content unaccounted for by the initial framework, such as participants’ mHealth service requirements, which formed an independent supplementary theme.

Rigor

All researchers received systematic training in qualitative methodologies prior to data collection. An experienced professor specializing in qualitative research supervised the interview guided interview design, interviewing skills, and data analysis. The research team strictly protected all interview records and medical information against unauthorized access. The interview pace was adjusted flexibly during conversations, and all ambiguous statements were clarified on site. To ensure reliable and valid data analysis, team members held collective discussions and cross-checked all codes, subthemes, and core themes. Audio recordings and field notes supported accurate interpretation of participants’ narratives.

To further secure methodological rigor, this study adopted the four trustworthiness criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba:39 (1) Credibility was strengthened through prolonged engagement with the research topic, member checking, and internal peer debriefing to ensure findings faithfully reflect participants’ real experiences and perspectives.(2) For transferability, thick descriptions of participants’ demographic profiles and research contexts were offered, enabling readers to judge whether these findings could be applied to their own clinical environments.(3) As for dependability, a complete audit trail that documented all research decisions, analytical workflows, and methodological rationales was maintained throughout the investigation.(4) Regarding confirmability, researchers consistently kept reflexive journals and built clear logical links between raw data, codes, themes, and analytical interpretations. This ensured that findings were grounded in participants’ accounts rather than the researchers’ subjective preconceptions.

Reflexivity Statement

The research team has long committed to advancing knowledge and clinical practice concerning self-management for neurogenic bladder rehabilitation. The first author, Lulu Zheng, is a postgraduate student majoring in rehabilitation nursing. Fully recognizing gaps in disease experience and cognitive levels between herself and participants, she maintained a learner mindset, listened to interviewees without judgment, and built a relaxed, open and respectful interview atmosphere. To systematically document and reflect on subjective biases throughout the research, the researcher regularly kept reflexive journals during data collection and analysis, recording personal assumptions, emotional reactions, and preconceived viewpoints that might skew data interpretation. The corresponding author, Yuantong Zang, holds a Master of Nursing degree, serves as ward head nurse and possesses years of clinical experience in rehabilitation nursing with profound expertise in neurogenic bladder management. Long-standing clinical trust between her and participants effectively removed communication barriers. Li Li works as the Director of Nursing in the study hospital. Other team members including Weiyue Cong, Na Li and Guanzhong Yan are current or soon-to-graduate master’s students who played vital roles in participant recruitment, data transcription and preliminary coding. All participant recruitment was completed by Lulu Zheng, who had no clinical diagnosis or treatment relationships with interviewees; this setup eased participants’ worries and encouraged sincere expression. All interviews complied with naturalistic inquiry principles,39 with no leading prompts or preset research outcomes. Clarification and summary probing techniques were adopted during interviews, supplemented by post-interview team discussion and reflection to guarantee accurate interpretation of participants’ narratives. The research team held regular review seminars to conduct critical evaluation of preliminary analytical results, identify and discuss potential biases, and improve the trustworthiness of study findings.

Data Management and Audit Trail

All interview transcripts and analytical files were saved on password-locked computers, accessible solely to research team members. To ensure research transparency and replicability, a complete audit trail was preserved, covering: (1) de-identified raw interview transcripts; (2) coded documents labeling meaning units and corresponding assigned codes; (3) thematic development logs recording the whole process of aggregating codes into subthemes and core themes; (4) meeting minutes documenting team discussions and analytical decisions; (5) full entries of reflexive journals.

Ethical Approval

This study strictly abided by the Declaration of Helsinki and obtained ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University (Approval No.: EF20260112(05)). Before study initiation, researchers thoroughly explained the study purpose, procedures, confidentiality rules, and voluntary participation requirements to all participants. Every interviewee joined the research voluntarily after adequate informed consent and signed written consent forms, authorizing the use of anonymized research data for academic publication. Participants were clearly informed that they could withdraw from the study unconditionally at any time without negative consequences to their routine treatment and nursing care. All audio recordings and transcribed texts were encrypted for storage, with access limited exclusively to the research team.

Barriers to Home-Based Self-Management and mHealth Requirements Among Patients with Neurogenic Bladder Following Spinal Cord Injury

This study adopted the ITHBC as the deductive analytical framework. The theory contains three core domains: knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation, and social support. The first three themes correspond to these domains and reflect barriers to patients’ self-management. Since ITHBC has no pre-defined analytical dimensions for mHealth-related content, specific questions regarding mHealth requirements were added to the interview guide. In Step ⑥ of data analysis, inductive analysis was performed on content not covered by the initial framework, allowing this theme to emerge independently from the data. Separately presenting “mHealth requirements” as the fourth theme acts as a necessary complement to the ITHBC framework and fully addresses the original intention of the study design: to systematically identify patients’ barriers and requirements. Accordingly, the interview data revealed multiple barriers to home-based self-management among patients with neurogenic bladder following spinal cord injury, alongside consensus-based requirements for mHealth support shared by patients and nurses. Discussion

Guided by the Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change (ITHBC), this study examined home-based self-management barriers and mHealth-related demands among patients with neurogenic bladder following spinal cord injury from both patients’ and nurses’ perspectives. Our data confirmed that ITHBC fits well for analysing self-management within this patient group, while also bringing to critical elements insufficiently addressed in the original theoretical framework. Specifically, many patients exhibited fragile “affective beliefs”: they understood intellectually why self-management mattered, yet struggled to sustain desired behaviours without ongoing positive reinforcement. This indicates that the belief construct within ITHBC covers both cognitive and affective sub-dimensions, each exerting distinct influences on behavior maintenance. Ryan’s original description of ITHBC did not explicitly draw this distinction.

Present findings further show that insufficient professional support represents a distinct gap in social support, distinct from family and peer-based resources. For clinical application, the social support dimension of ITHBC therefore requires further refinement. Apart from conventional family and peer support, structured professional healthcare services should also be incorporated into the framework, given their critical influence on patients’ self-management outcomes. Meanwhile, functional requirements for mHealth tools emerged as an inductive theme derived from empirical data, providing empirical evidence for adapting ITHBC for digital-health contexts.

All participants were recruited in Inner Mongolia, China. These results provide unique empirical insights into self-management hardships for this vulnerable population across under-resourced regions of northern China. Building on these study outcomes, this study further refines the implications for nursing education, rehabilitation practice, policy formulation and interdisciplinary cooperation. In the following sections, these findings are discussed in depth by integrating the ITHBC lens with existing domestic and international literature.

Knowledge and Belief Barriers: From Cognitive Stratification to Belief Fragility

This study revealed that most patients lacked basic understanding of the neurogenic bladder pathogenesis, self-management protocols, and complication prevention, alongside multiple common cognitive misconceptions. Pan et al29 reported similar patterns in a cross-sectional survey of people living with SCI in Southwest China, where poor disease-related knowledge predicted sub-optimal post-discharge follow-up adherence. This challenge is not confined to Chinese clinical settings. In a qualitative study of United States veterans, Fitzpatrick et al40 documented substantial knowledge gaps concerning urinary tract infection prevention and symptom recognition.

Collectively, these findings indicate that knowledge deficit constitutes a common barrier to self-management. One contributing factor may be fractured health education provision after discharge: information delivered during admission fails to meet patients’ long-term ongoing needs, and some individuals consequently miss opportunities for health improvement.41 Accordingly, one-off intensive education delivered during hospitalization cannot meet clinical demands. Healthcare systems need continuous health-education pathways combining “in-hospital intensive teaching, transitional discharge guidance, and regular community-led knowledge updates.”

Noticeable stratification in cognitive levels was observed. Younger, urban patients with higher educational attainment demonstrated relatively better understanding, whereas older, rural, and less-educated patients held poorer disease knowledge. Zhang et al42 demonstrated that residential location is an important factor influencing which intermittent-catheterization regimens patients adopt. One multicenter study further showed that age is an independent negative predictor of self-management capacity (β = −0.204, P < 0.001), with older age associated with poorer self-management.43 Edwards et al44 in a systematic review also noted that individuals with high health literacy in the SCI population were more likely to have higher education and socioeconomic advantages.

These patterns also reflect real-world hardships for rural northern Chinese communities. Rural Inner Mongolia faces multiple overlapping pressures. Population ageing proceeds faster in rural areas compared with cities;45 young working-age adults migrate away from home, expanding the size of the left-behind elderly population. Primary care services across the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region operate under conditions characterised by “low health service utilization, low health resource allocation, and high health service need”46 Combined, these circumstances drive rapid erosion of disease knowledge and deterioration of self-management behaviors among rural patients after discharge.

Consequently, interventions targeting older, rural, and less-educated patients should prioritise highly simplified, voice-enabled and offline-accessible educational tools, with face-to-face reinforcement delivered by village doctors or community volunteers. Meanwhile, clinicians and programme designers should treat older, less-educated and rural patients as high-priority intervention groups, and develop tailored health education strategies aligned with their cognitive capacities and living contexts.

Furthermore, although patients generally acknowledged the importance of self-management, their adherence often remained passive. Many reported urges to discontinue self-management triggered by guilt over burdening family members, catheter-related pain, or a lack of perceived therapeutic progress. This finding corroborates the conceptual distinction proposed by Janssen et al47 between “cognitive beliefs” (knowing an action is important) and “affective beliefs” (feeling that it matters). Cognitive beliefs rely on rational appraisal of health risks and can be built relatively quickly via knowledge transfer. Affective beliefs depend on subjective emotional response to risk and require sustained self-efficacy and positive outcome expectations to maintain. Qama et al48 in a qualitative study on the integration of self-management among SCI patients, found that physical-emotional dynamics as core influencing factors, reinforcing how subjective emotional experience shapes behavioural change. Moving from “knowing one should do it” to “being able to persistently do it” lies in whether patients’ affective beliefs are firmly established. Nursing health education should therefore move beyond knowledge delivery alone. Behavior change techniques such as motivational interviewing, small goal setting, and immediate feedback can help patients gain a sense of accomplishment through incremental successes, gradually solidifying affective beliefs.

However, this study found that patients only received informal emotional support from nurses and family caregivers during hospitalization; formal structured psychological interventions were unavailable. Almost no structured psychological support exists within community settings after discharge. Future self-management programmes for this population should embed standardized psychological support, working synergistically alongside established behavior change strategies.

Self-Regulation Barriers: Multifaceted Constraints Involving Physical, Psychological, Environmental, and Cognitive Factors

This study found that patients’ self-regulation was subject to the interactive constraints imposed by multiple factors: physical, psychological, environmental, and cognitive-habitual factors. Physical dysfunction constituted the most fundamental hard constraint. Pan et al29 reported that clean intermittent catheterization was more prevalent among patients with thoracic and lumbar SCI, whereas those with cervical SCI were more dependent on indwelling catheters, indicating a clear association between injury level and voiding method selection. Zacharia et al12 showed that 3.1% of patients who discontinued intermittent catheterization did so due to positioning difficulties. Together, these findings suggest that intervention plans must be tailored to the type and severity of patients’ functional impairments. The interplay between psychological factors and stigma further aggravated implementation difficulties. Hearn et al49 revealed that individuals experiencing recurrent urinary tract infections following spinal cord injury might delay help-seeking, conceal their condition, or adopt alternative treatments to avoid disclosure. Chinese scholar Zhang Huiying,50 demonstrated in clinical practice that comprehensive continence management could effectively reduce patients’ sense of stigma. This conversely indicates that psychological intervention and stigma management should become integral components of self-management programs. Therefore, alongside technical support, close attention must be paid to patients’ psychological experiences. Burdens can be alleviated through privacy-preserving design, peer-support services, and psychological counseling.

In this study, environmental constraints exhibited obvious regional characteristics. The Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region covers a vast territory, where SCI patients are scattered across remote areas. Poor transportation infrastructure leads to long travel distances and prolonged commutes for medical treatment. In addition, the long, frigid winters of northern China discourage patients from getting out of bed at night for catheterization, leading to a seasonal exacerbation of execution barriers. As early as 1979, Ogata et al51 found that SCI patients have weakened environmental adaptability in winter. Their reduced basal metabolic rate and impaired thermoregulation, expose them to greater physiological and behavioral challenges during the cold winter. Hence, for rural northern populations, targeted intervention strategies need to simultaneously address geographic access barriers and poor climate adaptability. Hospitals can expand telemedicine services to cut down patients’ travel distance for medical consultations, and arrange more frequent on-site professional guidance during severe cold months to offset adverse environmental impacts.

More notably, patients’ insufficient self-monitoring capacity was prominently manifested in low completion rates of urinary voiding diaries, an inability to recognize signs of infection, and a tendency toward self-management rather than timely clinical help. Fitzpatrick et al40 reached similar conclusions: most patients struggle to recognize UTI symptoms. Distrust of unfamiliar medical staff often makes them postpone medical visits and handle discomfort by themselves. Goldstine et al52 in interviews with long-term intermittent catheterization users across five countries, also revealed that UTI, physical pain, and the daily burden of performing catheterization dominate patients’ negative experiences. Many participants described catheterization as a lifelong burden.

These findings indicate that insufficient self-monitoring is not merely a matter of lacking tools or operational skills. It is closely related to patients’ vague perception of infection symptoms, low trust in the healthcare system, and the perceived daily burden of catheterization. Compared with traditional paper voiding records, digital monitoring tools may better match this group’s needs. However, tool design must fully consider two local realities: insufficient network coverage in rural areas and low digital literacy among older patients. Qualified digital tools should be lightweight, voice-enabled, and capable of offline use.

The multiple constraining factors mentioned above, including physical, psychological, environmental and cognitive factors, are consistent with the findings from a longitudinal qualitative study conducted by Qama et al48 involving 32 patients with SCI in Switzerland for three months after hospital discharge. Their research also concluded that SCI survivors face comprehensive barriers integrating self-management into daily life, which fall into three categories: physical and psychological conditions, living environment, plus information access and social cognitive factors. This cross-study consistency further verifies that barriers to self-regulation arise from the combined, overlapping effects of multiple interrelated factors.

Social Support Barriers: Family-Care Reliance, Shortfalls in Professional Services, and Shared Responsibilities Between Nurses and Patients

In this study, both nurses and patients acknowledged family support as a crucial pillar for patients’ self-management. This finding is consistent with Fitzpatrick et al40 who reported that caregivers can facilitate patients’ health-seeking behaviors and enhance self-efficacy. However, this study found that, over the course of long-term home care tended to make family care move away from “standardized execution” to “experience-based practice.” Farmahini-Farahani et al53 revealed that the transition of SCI patients from hospital to home transfers care responsibilities from healthcare institutions to family caregivers. These caregivers face numerous challenges during the care transition, directly affecting the care quality and the continuity of patients’ self-management. Therefore, caregiver training should be a core component of discharge planning. Training ought not to be limited to pre-discharge technical guidance. Instead, it should start in the early stage of hospitalization and run through inpatient rehabilitation all the way to the post-discharge transition period. Training content should include practical skills such as catheterization operation and infection prevention, as well as emotional support strategies and the ability to identify patients’ psychological distress, so as to build sustainable family care partnerships.

Insufficient professional support was another important finding of this study. This is highly consistent with the findings of Bryden and Gran:54 curtailed and prematurely terminated rehabilitation services after discharge, barriers to resource access, and the challenge of managing multiple issues simultaneously during the early post-discharge phase constitute the core difficulties in transitioning from hospital to community. Among American patients, an average of four institutional transfers occurred within the first three months after post-discharge. Half of these patients were admitted to skilled nursing facilities, and difficulties navigating resources together with disrupted support were commonly observed. Chinese scholar Wang Luyao et al55 also pointed out that spinal cord injury continuing care in China still faces systemic problems including inadequate follow-up mechanisms. This suggests that follow-up work should be incorporated into departmental performance evaluation, and dedicated follow-up positions should be established. Combined with mHealth platforms, a multi-level continuing care system should be established that integrates “online data monitoring + telephone follow-up + regular home visits.”

This study found that patients perceive nurses’ health education as less authoritative than physicians’. Within China’s healthcare system, physicians hold substantially higher authoritative status than nurses, creating a credibility discrepancy between physicians and nurses in patient trust. In a randomized controlled study by Yang et al56 patients were divided into a nurse-led education group and a physician-led health education group. Results showed significantly higher medication adherence in the physician-led group (high adherence: 64.52% vs 29.03%), This suggests that patients tend to give greater weight to authoritative advice from physicians. Thus, although nurses take primary responsibility for health education, their professional recommendations are often viewed as supplementary. This bears important implications for platform design. A “physician-nurse collaborative education” model is recommended: physicians endorse educational materials or participate in explaining key content to enhance perceived authority, while nurses play a central role in ongoing follow-up and procedural guidance.

Younger patients attached great value to peer exchange as a form of informal support. However, such peer-support resources remain inaccessible to rural, elderly, and non-smartphone users. This finding suggests that peer support should adopt a “dual-track” approach: an online formal community moderated by professionals, and offline regular peer gatherings organized through township health centers or village clinics. This ensures that patients of all ages and varying levels of digital literacy can gain access to peer support resources.

Mobile Health Requirements: From Barrier Analysis to Functional Mapping

Effective patient self-management is built upon accessible tangible support resources. This study found that patients generally suffer from insufficient physical support in community-home settings, including the lack of necessary home barrier-free adaptations, limited access to assistive devices and bladder management supplies, and inadequate routine complication monitoring and timely medical guidance. Faced with these practical difficulties, patients also encounter three-fold barriers: deficient knowledge and beliefs, poor self-regulation capacity, and insufficient social support, which further heighten their demand for professional guidance and ongoing supervision. The Hospital at Home model provides a theoretical framework integrating these fragmented care resources by extending hospital-level multidisciplinary care into patients’ homes. Within this framework, mHealth tools serve as an information bridge connecting patients, family caregivers and healthcare professionals, instead of replacing the tangible support resources mentioned above. On this basis, the following section explores the functional design of the mHealth platform corresponding to the three categories of self-management barriers.

To tackle knowledge-and-belief barriers, the home-based self-management platform is recommended to deliver stratified, voice-enabled health-education modules. This study shows that older, rural, and less-educated patients had markedly lower cognitive levels alongside knowledge misconceptions. Particularly in low-resource rural settings, scarce conventional offline health-education resources and monotonous delivery formats further aggravate patients’ cognitive biases and poor adherence. Moreover, patients’ affective beliefs remain fragile, and they tend to give up when perceiving no therapeutic effects. Domestic and international studies have similarly confirmed that health-education content should be adapted for users with low digital literacy,57 and that immediate feedback mechanisms help strengthen patients’ affective beliefs.58 The platform should support low-threshold educational formats such as voice narrations and short videos. It should also generate visual progress charts based on patients’ recorded data, enabling patients to intuitively perceive the improvements brought by consistent self-management.

For self-regulation barriers, core platform functions can focus on reminders, voice-enabled recording, and risk alerts. This study identified common patient‑side problems including irregular fluid intake and catheterization, extremely low voiding-diary completion rates, and inability to recognize infection warning signs. International studies validate the necessity of these functions: Wilde et al10 confirmed that a scheduled reminder mechanism can effectively improve regular catheterization rates; Kriz et al59 found that patients with upper-limb motor impairment secondary to cervical spinal cord injury have substantial demands for voice assistants, consistent with participants’ expectation of an operation that “just talk and it works.” While the value of alert functions is recognized, their accuracy remains controversial. Fluid-intake and catheterization data alone cannot precisely predict infection risk, and more comprehensive clinical parameters need to be incorporated. A qualitative study by Hose et al60 involving 30 individuals with SCI similarly found that patients held high trust in ChatGPT-generated advice for urinary tract symptoms, yet widely worried about missing reference citations and insufficient integration of personal health data. This finding aligns with the shared judgment of nurses and patients in the present study: warning functions are “necessary yet questionable in terms of accuracy.” Therefore, risk alerts generated purely by general algorithms can hardly satisfy real-world clinical needs. Moreover, practical constraints including inadequate rural network coverage and low digital literacy among older patients must be considered. These functions need to support offline operation, large-font display modes, and vibration reminders.

To mitigate social support barriers, the platform should construct a tripartite collaborative system linking patients, family members, and nurses to realize data sharing and remote guidance. Family support serves as a critical guarantee for patients’ self-management. Nevertheless, family caregivers are prone to reduced engagement over long-term care, and professional follow-up is severely deficient. International practice has demonstrated that mHealth can effectively bridge this gap. Stothers et al61 showed in a prospective cohort study of patients with neurogenic bladder after spinal cord injury that telehealth combined with home-based monitoring achieved a 91% visit adherence rate. In low-resource rural environments, weak grassroots follow-up capacity, long travel distances for medical appointments, and poor accessibility to professional guidance constitute major implementation obstacles restricting patients’ long-term self-management. Given these findings, the platform should be equipped with family-oriented functions that enable family members to receive reminders and view records synchronously. Meanwhile, nurses should be provided with a data dashboard to support remote patient condition monitoring and individualized feedback delivery. Such an online collaborative model can compensate for the shortage of rural medical resources and resolve implementation dilemmas of mHealth in low-resource settings.

At the implementation level, both nurses and patients expressed reasonable concerns regarding operational complexity, cost, increased workload, and legal liability. Bertolazzi et al62 in an integrative systematic review of health technology adoption among older adults with chronic diseases, noted that cost and ease of use are dual determinants influencing acceptance among the older population. China’s Administrative Measures for Internet-Based Diagnosis and Treatment (Trial)63 clarifies liability allocation for telemedicine and offers a policy foundation to address nurses’ legal concerns. However, further institutional exploration is still required to incorporate online follow-up into routine workflows without substantially increasing workload. Furthermore, mHealth implementation dilemmas in rural low-resource settings are reflected in multiple constraints: limited infrastructure, inadequate user digital literacy, insufficient allocation of medical resources, and imbalance between technical costs and clinical workload. These represent core practical challenges for scaling up mHealth services at the grassroots level.

Implications for Nursing Education, Rehabilitation Practice, and Policy Development

At the knowledge and belief level, this study identifies disparities between patients’ cognitive beliefs and affective beliefs. The findings indicate that the current nursing education model requires further optimization to move beyond the traditional teacher-centered knowledge indoctrination mode. Current nursing curricula lack sufficient training for nurses on skills to facilitate patients’ self-management, particularly motivational interviewing, identification of affective beliefs, and behavioral change techniques, for which systematic professional training remains inadequate. Accordingly, this study recommends integrating the incorporating the Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change (ITHBC) into compulsory rehabilitation nursing coursework. Nurses can be trained to apply behavioral change techniques including small-goal formulation and immediate feedback. These approaches help patients build a sense of achievement by accomplishing staged rehabilitation tasks, consolidate positive affective beliefs gradually, and ultimately enhance patients’ intrinsic motivation for health self-management across both cognitive and affective dimensions.

At the self-regulation level, the physical, psychological, environmental, and cognitive constraints uncovered in this study indicate that clinical rehabilitation practice should abandon the conventional evaluation framework that solely centers on patient compliance. Instead, clinical work ought to shift toward systematic identification, dynamic assessment, and targeted hierarchical intervention for multidimensional barriers. As core practitioners of continuing care, nurses should prioritize the dynamic fluctuations and individual differences in patients’ self-regulation abilities during follow-up management, rather than merely focusing on patients’ mastery of health-related knowledge.

At the social support level, this study verified that patients commonly suffer from three major social support deficits: insufficient family support, inadequate professional follow-up services, and lack of peer support. Such deficiencies stem fundamentally from incomplete institutional construction of the supporting system. Family caregivers receive no standardized professional training, professional follow-up services lack dedicated posts and performance appraisal incentives, and interdisciplinary collaboration cannot be effectively implemented in home-based rehabilitation management.

To address these issues, standardized training for family caregivers should be integrated into core discharge nursing plans, with regular sessions covering care skills, communication approaches and psychological support to solidify the foundation of family care. Meanwhile, dedicated nursing positions for continuing follow-up should be established with clear job specifications. Workloads for online and offline continuing nursing services should be incorporated into nurses’ performance salary calculation systems to motivate nurses to engage actively in follow-up work. Frontline nurses reported that interdisciplinary collaborative mechanisms have not been fully integrated into home rehabilitation management. Therefore, policymakers need to refine interdisciplinary collaborative diagnosis and treatment systems, build a unified remote multidisciplinary consultation and management platform. This platform enables rehabilitation physicians, physical therapists, psychological counselors, social workers and other professionals to participate collaboratively throughout patients’ home-based rehabilitation. This transition from independent single-discipline care to integrated multidisciplinary team-based care can effectively remedy the flaws within the social support system.

Regarding mHealth implementation, patients’ and nurses’ shared concerns over economic costs, workload burdens and legal liabilities indicate that relevant telemedicine policies need further elaboration. Clear definitions should be stipulated concerning the practice scope, legal status and liability boundaries for nurses delivering online rehabilitation guidance. Institutional safeguards and technical upgrades should also be explored to sustain the long-term operation of online follow-up services, so that such services no longer rely on voluntary personal efforts from nurses.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study was carried out in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine of a tertiary Grade A hospital in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. Patient interviews were completed within one week after hospital discharge, and nurse interviews were conducted in the department’s demonstration classroom. Therefore, the transferability of the present findings needs to be assessed prudently in consideration of the research setting.

All enrolled patients were from rural northern China, characterized by advanced age, low educational level, and farming as the main occupation. These demographic traits differ greatly from those of patients in eastern coastal areas and first-tier cities in China. Accordingly, the findings are primarily applicable to rural northern China and underdeveloped western regions with scarce medical resources. Nevertheless, the core barriers identified—insufficient knowledge and beliefs, poor self-regulation capacity, and inadequate social support systems—demonstrate cross-regional and cross-cultural commonalities, which align with qualitative research results reported in Switzerland, the United States and other countries.

The conceptual framework and analytical methods of this study can be applied to other populations living with spinal cord injury with similar characteristics. Still, specific intervention strategies require localized adaptation according to local medical resource allocation, cultural backgrounds and digital infrastructure.

Based on the above analysis of transferability, future research should expand the sampling scope to recruit patients and nurses from diverse regions and medical institutions at different levels. Longitudinal follow-up is recommended to track changes in patients’ self-management status from hospitalization to various post-discharge stages, so as to obtain more comprehensive and generalizable evidence. Furthermore, interview data were merely derived from self-reports of patients and nurses without independent perspectives from family caregivers. Hence, future studies should incorporate family members’ viewpoints and adopt longitudinal research designs.

Conclusion

Patients with neurogenic bladder after spinal cord injury face three intertwined barriers to home-based self-management: inadequate knowledge and beliefs, insufficient self-regulation ability, and limited social support. From the nurses’ perspective, the professional support system has obvious drawbacks, including imperfect follow-up mechanisms, lack of multidisciplinary collaboration, and economic constraints. Patients and nurses hold highly consistent expectations for the functions of mHealth tools, while both raise reasonable concerns regarding operational difficulty, costs, increased workload, and legal liabilities.

These findings provide empirical evidence for establishing an integrated support system centered on families, supplemented by mHealth services, and guaranteed by professional follow-up. Nevertheless, mHealth tools only serve an auxiliary role within the holistic care system and cannot replace fundamental supportive measures, such as home environment adaptation, assistive device provision, and comorbidity monitoring. Future research could further focus on identifying high-risk groups and developing individualized intervention strategies. Randomized controlled trials are also required to verify the efficacy and sustainability of mHealth tools embedded in this integrated support system.
