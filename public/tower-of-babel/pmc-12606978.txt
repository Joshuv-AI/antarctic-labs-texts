Inducing macrophage M2-Type polarization via anodized anisotropic TiO2 nanoporous topography on titanium surfaces
Authors: Siyu Xie, Wei Wei, Bin Luo, Jiangqi Hu, Yunping Lu, Lin He, Xiaoyu Wang, Xu Sun, Qingsong Jiang, Tianxiao Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental implant success critically depends on soft tissue integration (STI) to prevent peri-implantitis. Macrophages, key immune regulators, exhibit distinct pro-inflammatory (M1) or anti-inflammatory/pro-healing (M2) phenotypes, significantly impacting peri-implant tissue responses. While implant surface nanostructures modulate macrophage polarization, the effects of specific topographies and underlying mechanisms require further elucidation. This study investigated the ability of an anodized anisotropic titanium dioxide nanoporous (TNP) surface to direct human macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and explored the associated mechanisms.MethodsAn anisotropic nanoporous titanium topography was fabricated via electrochemical anodization followed by annealing. Surface characterization included morphological analysis (FE-SEM, AFM), wettability assessment (contact angle measurement), and evaluation of mechanical properties (nanoindentation, nanoscratch testing). THP-1-derived macrophages were utilized for in vitro evaluation. Cell viability and adhesion were measured using the CCK-8 assay. Macrophage morphology was observed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Polarization status was assessed by flow cytometry (CD68, CD86, CD206) and RT-qPCR (CD86, CD206, TNF-α, IL-1β, iNOS, TGF-β). Subsequently, transcriptomic profiling through RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) was conducted to analyze underlying molecular pathways.ResultsThe TNP surface featured uniform 30 nm diameter nanopores, significantly increased hydrophilicity and surface energy, and adequate mechanical properties. Macrophage early adhesion and spreading (enhanced pseudopodia formation) were significantly increased on TNP compared to control. Flow cytometry and RT-qPCR revealed that TNP induced M2 polarization, as evidenced by significantly upregulated CD206 and TGF-β expression, while downregulating pro-inflammatory markers (CD86, TNF-α, IL-1β, and iNOS). RNA-Seq analysis further identified significant alterations in genes associated with cell projection membranes, collagen fiber organization, and actin binding.ConclusionsAnodization followed by annealing successfully created a mechanically stable, hydrophilic titanium nanoporous surface. The in vitro study demonstrated that the specific nano-topographical structure enhanced macrophage adhesion and spreading, inducing a shift towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype characterized. The observed polarization is potentially associated with cytoskeletal reorganization and the activation of mechanotransduction pathways triggered by the nanostructure. This surface modification strategy offers significant potential for enhancing dental implant abutment soft tissue integration by favorably modulating the local immune response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07131-7.

Introduction

Dental implants are a critical therapeutic modality for the restoration of missing teeth. However, peri-implantitis, one of the most severe biological complications, has been reported to have a prevalence as high as 19.53%, significantly compromising the long-term success of implants [1]. It is imperative to recognize that, beyond achieving osseointegration with bone tissue, the healing and stabilization of the surrounding peri-implant soft tissues are equally critical, particularly in patients with compromised systemic or local conditions [2–4]. Ideal soft tissue integration (STI) is crucial for establishing a hermetic seal that prevents bacterial ingress, thereby providing a stable and protected environment conducive to bone tissue integration and preventing subsequent bone resorption [5–7].

Following the implantation of a titanium implant, tissue damage and bleeding inevitably occur, resulting in direct contact between the material surface and blood components. This interaction leads to protein adsorption and triggers an immune response [4, 8, 9]. Macrophages and other immune-inflammatory cells are recruited to the implant site, where they mediate debris clearance and secrete numerous cytokines and growth factors [5, 10, 11]. These secreted factors subsequently stimulate the recruitment and proliferation of mesenchymal cells, angiogenesis, and collagen matrix deposition [9]. Throughout this process, macrophages undergo a phenotypic transition from a pro-inflammatory state within an initial inflammatory microenvironment to an anti-inflammatory, pro-healing state within a subsequent reparative microenvironment [12]. This transition is pivotal for coordinating wound healing and peri-implant tissue integration [13]. Therefore, macrophages play a pivotal role in orchestrating both the initial tissue integration and the long-term stability of dental implants.

Macrophages exhibit two primary polarization states: the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and the anti-inflammatory, pro-healing M2 phenotype. The M1 phenotype, which is classically activated by stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), is characterized by the expression of the surface markers CD86 and MHC-II (including HLA-DR) [3, 14]. M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and TNF-α, which are implicated in the pathogenesis of peri-implantitis [3, 14]. Conversely, the M2 phenotype, which is alternatively activated by cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13, expresses CD206 and secretes factors including arginase-1 (Arg-1), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which are essential for tissue repair and angiogenesis [10, 15, 16].

To mitigate bacterial adhesion, titanium abutment surfaces are typically polished to achieve a high degree of smoothness. However, this smooth surface topography compromises the integration between the abutment and the surrounding soft tissues. Furthermore, a native oxide layer (typically 2–5 nm thick) is inherently present on titanium surfaces, contributing to their biological inertness [17, 18]. The surface modification of titanium abutments can modulate cellular behavior by altering their physical, chemical, and biological properties. Techniques such as acid etching, sandblasting, plasma spraying, and anodization can be employed to modify surface topography, enhance wettability, and introduce bioactive coatings [4]. Among these techniques, anodization enables the creation of highly ordered and anisotropic nanostructures on titanium surfaces, which can significantly modulate macrophage polarization and function [17]. For instance, surfaces featuring nanotube structures with diameters of 60–70 nm have been shown to inhibit macrophage adhesion and proliferation compared with smooth titanium surfaces, with adherent macrophages exhibiting an oval-shaped morphology indicative of low activity [19]. However, a study reported that macrophages cultured on nanotubes with a diameter of 78 nm exhibited comparable proliferation but reduced inflammatory activity relative to control, which contrasts with findings of other studies, where macrophages cultured on 80 nm diameter nanotubes displayed a pronounced pro-inflammatory functional state [15, 20]. Meanwhile, studies demonstrate that titanium dioxide nanotubes with smaller diameters (ཞ30 nm) exhibit significantly greater capacity to suppress inflammation or promote macrophage M2 polarization compared to larger-diameter counterparts [20, 21]. Consequently, despite numerous investigations into the effects of nanostructures on macrophage polarization and function, a consensus remains elusive. This discrepancy likely stems from variations in experimental parameters (e.g., nanotube/nanopore dimensions), cell types and sources, and other material surface characteristics [20–23]. Furthermore, the underlying mechanisms through which nanotube/nanopore morphology regulates macrophage function and polarization require further elucidation.

In this study, we employed anodization to fabricate an anisotropic titanium dioxide nanoporous (TNP) structure on titanium surfaces, thereby modifying surface roughness, hydrophilicity, and morphological characteristics. We investigated the polarization state and functional regulation of macrophages cultured on this modified surface and utilized RNA-Seq to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and signaling pathways involved in macrophage polarization.

By optimizing the surface properties of titanium abutments, we aim to modulate the host immune response and enhance the integration between titanium abutments and surrounding soft tissues, thereby improving the long-term stability of dental implants. This research contributes to understanding how surface nanostructures can be tailored to direct macrophage behavior toward a phenotype conducive to STI.

Materials and methods

Titanium sample Preparation

Pure Ti foils (1 cm × 1 cm × 0.2 mm) were ultrasonically cleaned sequentially with acetone, ethanol, and deionized water (all analytical reagents, Sinopharm, Beijing, China) to remove organic contaminants. The electrolyte mixture consisted of ethylene glycol (Analytical Reagent, Sinopharm, Beijing, China) containing 2 vol% DI water and 0.3 wt% ammonium fluoride (NH₄F, Analytical Reagent, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), with a carbon rod serving as the cathode. Anodization was performed under direct current at 20 V for 30 min using a DC power supply (model CZ3457, Shenzhen Guce Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Subsequently, the anodized Ti foils were annealed in the presence of air at 500 °C for 2 h, employing a preheating rate of 4 °C/min, followed by furnace cooling. Following annealing, all the samples were ultrasonically cleaned sequentially in acetone, ethanol, and DI water. Non-anodized Ti foils served as the control group. Prior to cell culture, all the samples were sterilized with 75% ethanol and subsequently exposed to UV light for 1 h.

Surface characterization

The surface topography and cross-sectional features were characterized via field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; JSM-6700 F, JEOL, Japan). The diameter and length of the nanopores were measured and statistically analyzed.

The surface roughness and three-dimensional (3D) topography of the samples were measured using an atomic force microscopy (AFM; ICON4-System, Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA), and the roughness average (Ra) value was determined. The AFM data were analyzed via Nanoscope Analysis software (version 7.20).

Wettability and surface free energy were assessed using an optical contact angle goniometer (OCA20, DataPhysics Instruments, Germany). Measurements were performed using the sessile drop method. A 1 mL droplet of distilled water or ethylene glycol was dispensed from a microliter syringe positioned above the sample. The sample stage was then raised to contact the droplet. Images were captured immediately upon contact, and the contact angles between the droplet and the substrate were measured. At least six measurements were performed per group. The surface energy was then calculated via the Owens-Wendt two-liquid method.

The hardness and Young’s modulus of the Ti substrate and the TiO₂ nanoporous layer were measured via nanoindentation testing using a nanoindenter (NanoTest Vantage, Micro Materials Ltd., Tampa, FL, USA). A Berkovich diamond indenter was used with a maximum load of 10 mN, and each measurement set was repeated five times.

The adhesion strength of the TiO₂ nanoporous layer was evaluated via nano-scratch testing using the nanoindenter (NanoTest Vantage, Micro Materials Ltd., Tampa, FL, USA). The applied load was linearly increased from 0 to 100 mN at a constant speed of 25 μm/s over a scratch length of 500 μm. Three nano-scratch tests were conducted per sample, with test positions separated by at least 100 μm.

Cell acquisition and culture

The human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 (JNO-H0561) was obtained from GuangZhou Jennio Biotech Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (P-S), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells between passages 3 and 8 exhibiting optimal viability were utilized for subsequent differentiation into M0 macrophages. In brief, cells were induced by stimulation with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Sigma-Aldrich Corp, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 72 h, followed by a 48-hour rest period in PMA-free medium.

Cell viability and adhesion assays

To assess cell viability and early adhesion, macrophages were seeded onto sample surfaces within 24-well plates at 1 × 105/well and incubated for 1 h, 2 h, 4 h and 24 h, respectively. Following three rinses with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol for the CCK-8 assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax Paradigm, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The absorbance values measured at 1 h, 2 h and 4 h were aggregated to evaluate and compare the early-stage cell adhesion capacity across the different sample groups. Cell morphology

Macrophages were seeded onto sample surfaces in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. After incubation, the samples were rinsed gently with PBS and fixed overnight at 4 °C with 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution. Subsequently, the samples were rinsed again with PBS and subjected to sequential dehydration in graded ethanol solutions (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) for 10 min at each concentration. Following critical point drying and sputter coating with gold, the cell morphology was examined using FE-SEM (JSM-6700 F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The semi-quantitative analysis of cell area was conducted using the “Analyze Particles” function in the ImageJ software (Fiji, v1.53q).

Flow cytometry

Macrophages were seeded onto sample surfaces in 24-well plates at 1 × 105/well and incubated for 72 h. The cells were harvested and stained with the following fluorescent conjugated antibodies: fFITC-conjugated anti-human CD68 (clone Y1/82A, Biolegend, #333805), APC-conjugated anti-human CD86 (clone BU63, Biolegend, #374207), and PE-conjugated anti-human CD206 (clone 15 − 2, Biolegend, #321105). Following washing and centrifugation steps with PBS, the cells were resuspended in an appropriate buffer and analyzed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The acquired data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1, Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA). The percentage of cells positive for each macrophage surface marker was quantified.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was employed to evaluate the expression of inflammatory and anti-inflammatory genes in macrophages cultured on the different sample surfaces. Macrophage culture conditions mirrored those described in the flow cytometry section. Total RNA was isolated from macrophages using the RNAsimple Total RNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China), which strictly adhered to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the FastKing RT Kit (with gDNase) (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The synthesized cDNA was amplified using a real-time PCR system with the MagicSYBR Mixture (Cowin Biotech, Jiangsu, China). Relative mRNA expression levels of the target genes were quantified on a CFX Connect™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). GAPDH served as the endogenous reference gene for normalization. The primer sequences utilized in this study are detailed in Table 1.

RNA-Seq analysis

Macrophage culture conditions were identical to those described for flow cytometry. Total RNA was extracted from macrophages cultured on different sample surfaces (n = 3 biological replicates per group) using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The integrity, purity, and concentration of the RNA samples were assessed using multiple methods: agarose gel electrophoresis, a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (Implen GmbH, Munich, Germany), and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Library construction and paired-end sequencing (150-bp read length) were conducted by Shanghai WeiHuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. via the Illumina platform. High-quality reads (clean reads) were aligned to the reference human genome (GRCh38/hg38) using the HISAT2 aligner. Differential gene expression analysis between the control and TNP groups was performed using the DESeq2 package in R. Genes exhibiting an adjusted p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg) < 0.05 and an absolute fold change > 1.5 (log2 fold change >|0.585|) were considered significantly differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, was conducted using the clusterProfiler package.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences between groups were assessed using either one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post-hoc tests or unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, as applicable. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of specimens

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) was employed to characterize the surface morphology of the specimens, as presented in Fig. 1. Under low magnification (10,000×), the surfaces of both the control group (Ti) and the TNP group appeared relatively smooth and flat. At higher magnification (100,000×), the control surface lacked discernible nanoscale features, while the TNP surface displayed uniformly distributed TiO2 nanopore arrays, with the top-view image revealing nanopores possessing an inner diameter of approximately 30 nm. A cross-sectional view (Fig. 1C) demonstrated that the nanoporous layer had a thickness of approximately 2 μm.

Macrophage identification, cell viability and adhesion assays

M0 macrophages were differentiated from THP-1 cells through PMA stimulation. Phase-contrast microscopy revealed that THP-1 cells maintained a suspended, rounded morphology, whereas M0 macrophages exhibited adherent characteristics with flattened or branched morphology (Fig. 4A). Successful differentiation was confirmed via flow cytometric analysis of the pan-macrophage marker CD68, demonstrating > 95% positivity in both groups without statistically significant differences (Fig. 4B). No significant reduction in macrophage viability was observed on the TNP surfaces compared with the control (Ti), indicating negligible cytotoxicity (Fig. 5A). Macrophage adhesion increased temporally on both surfaces, with no significant difference at 1 h and 2 h. However, TNP surfaces demonstrated significantly enhanced adhesion at 4 h compared to control (p < 0.0001, Fig. 5B).

Following 24-hour culture, macrophages on TNP surfaces displayed enhanced spreading with spindle-shaped or polygonal morphology and extensive pseudopodia formation. In contrast, the macrophages on smooth Ti surfaces retained a rounded morphology with restricted spreading and minimal pseudopodia development (Fig. 6A). Semi-quantitative analysis verified a significantly larger cell spread area on TNP surfaces (p < 0.001, Fig. 6B).

Macrophage polarization and gene expression on different surfaces

Flow cytometric analysis revealed significantly elevated expression of the M2 marker CD206 on TNP surfaces, whereas M1 marker (CD86) expression showed no significant intergroup difference. Subsequent RT-qPCR analysis confirmed comparable CD68 (M0 marker) expression between the groups (Fig. 7A). Notably, TNP surfaces significantly downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators including CD86 (M1 marker), TNF-α, iNOS, and IL-1β (p < 0.05, Fig. 7B). Conversely, the anti-inflammatory mediators CD206 (M2 marker) and TGF-β were significantly upregulated on TNP surfaces (p < 0.05, Fig. 7C).

RNA-seq

RNA sequencing was performed to elucidate nanotopography-mediated regulatory mechanisms involved in macrophage polarization (Fig. 8). Compared to the Ti group, the TNP group upregulated the expression of 469 genes and downregulated the expression of 975 genes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis also revealed that cell projection membrane, postsynaptic membrane, actin binding, collagen trimer, banded collagen fibril and fibrillar collagen trimer genes were significantly upregulated. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes using the clusterProfiler software revealed that macrophages in the TNP group upregulated the expression of genes related to cell adhesion, such as Focal adhesion and ECM-receptor interaction, and the expression of genes associated with the PI3K-Akt-signaling pathway and MAPK pathway was also significantly upregulated.

Discussion

In this study, anodization was employed to modify the titanium surface, generating an anisotropic TiO2 nanoporous structure with a pore diameter of approximately 30 nm. This nanostructured surface exhibits enhanced hydrophilicity and surface energy, alongside increased surface roughness, consistent with prior research findings [24–26]. Significantly, this specific surface morphology induces macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, concomitant with the downregulation of pro-inflammatory genes (CD86, TNF-α, IL-1β, iNOS) and the upregulation of anti-inflammatory genes (CD206, TGF-β). The immunomodulatory effect is mechanistically linked to enhanced cell adhesion capacity and cytoskeletal reorganization, as corroborated by morphological expansion and elevated pseudopodia formation observed in cellular assays.

Furthermore, the mechanical integrity of the nanoporous layer, including its strength, Young’s modulus, and adhesion strength to the underlying titanium substrate, is a critical consideration. Superior mechanical properties and resistance to delamination are essential prerequisites for clinical application on implant abutment surfaces to effectively modulate the peri-implant immune microenvironment [27]. Previous investigations have demonstrated that, for equivalent diameters, nanoporous structures possess higher hardness compared to nanotube structures (Supplementary Table S1). This enhanced mechanical robustness stems from the interconnected nature of nanopores, in contrast to the discrete tubular configuration of nanotubes, which are more susceptible to collapse under mechanical stress [24, 28]. Consequently, the nanoporous structure, offering superior mechanical properties, was selected for this study. Additionally, annealing was applied to crystallize the initially amorphous TiO2 nanoporous layer formed by anodization, resulting in more stable and mechanically stronger anatase and rutile crystalline phases [29]. This thermal treatment also eliminates residual fluorides, carbon, and hydroxides from the nanopore surfaces, thereby improving antibacterial efficacy, enhancing biocompatibility, and promoting favorable protein adsorption [2, 29–31]. Thus, annealing was incorporated to optimize the mechanical and biological performance of the fabricated TiO2 nanoporous structure.

Upon implantation, the host immune response is triggered, representing a foreign body reaction to the implanted material. Macrophages, as pivotal immune cells, are recruited to the implant site and undergo phenotypic modulation [10]. Our findings demonstrate that the anisotropic nanoporous structure fabricated on titanium via anodization effectively induces macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype. Material characterization confirmed that anodization significantly increased both surface hydrophilicity and roughness. These observations align with previous studies indicating that heightened surface hydrophilicity and roughness can promote a stronger anti-inflammatory macrophage response, with hydrophilicity exerting a more pronounced immunomodulatory effect, and the combination of wettability and roughness exhibiting synergistic potential [32]. However, it is noteworthy that some studies report increased surface roughness promoting pro-inflammatory macrophage responses [33, 34]. For instance, ultraviolet irradiation of TiO2 nanotube surfaces enhances hydrophilicity, facilitating macrophage proliferation while reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine secretion [35]. Similarly, oxygen plasma treatment of SLA-modified surfaces increases hydrophilicity and induces a stronger anti-inflammatory macrophage response [32]. The significant influence of nanoscale feature dimensions, particularly diameter, on macrophage polarization and function has been extensively documented [20, 21]. Specifically, nanotube structures with smaller diameters (30 nm) tend to induce M2 polarization, whereas larger diameters (100 nm) favor M1 polarization [21]. Research has confirmed that macrophages on small-diameter (30 nm) nanoporous surfaces exhibit reduced inflammatory activation compared to both smooth surfaces and larger-diameter (80 nm, 150 nm) nanoporous surfaces [20]. Nevertheless, conflicting results exist, with other studies reporting M2 polarization and anti-inflammatory gene expression on nanotubes with diameters of 50 nm and 100 nm [22]. This apparent discrepancy underscores that macrophage polarization and function are modulated by a complex interplay of confounding factors, including the cell source/model (e.g., THP-1 [36], J774A.1 [37], and RAW264.7 [38]), specific surface morphology, hydrophilicity, roughness, and resultant cellular morphology. Elucidating the underlying regulatory mechanisms is therefore paramount to understanding and controlling macrophage behavior.

To identify potential mechanisms driving M2 polarization, RNA sequencing was performed to screen for differentially expressed genes, followed by enrichment analysis. Notably, biological processes and cellular components such as the cell projection membrane, postsynaptic membrane, focal adhesion, actin binding, and collagen trimer were significantly enriched (Fig. 7D), suggesting a crucial role for interactions between the extracellular matrix (ECM), cell membrane, and cytoskeleton. This aligns with our experimental observation of enhanced macrophage spreading and pseudopodia formation on nanoporous surfaces. Prior studies have indicated that topographically induced macrophage elongation is correlated with M2 polarization and is actin cytoskeleton contraction-dependent [39]. Importantly, inhibition of actin and associated signaling pathways did not affect cytokine-induced (IL-4/IL-13) elongation or arginase-1 expression, suggesting distinct pathways for cytokine-mediated versus topography-mediated macrophage polarization [39]. Mechanotransduction has been proposed as a key mechanism underlying topographical sensing [40]. Research suggested that strontium-loaded nanotubes promote osteogenesis in rBMMSCs via the upregulation of cytoskeleton-related genes, hypothesizing that surface topography influences integrin arrangement along nanotube walls [41]. The local stress generated by integrin alignment is transmitted to the actin cytoskeleton, directly stretching chromatin via the nucleoskeleton-cytoskeleton complex, thereby regulating gene transcription [42]. Furthermore, the 30-nm nanoporous structure, closely matching the size of fibronectin (28 nm), has been shown to produce a “size-confined effect“ [20]. This effect exposes the RGD sequence of fibronectin, facilitating anti-inflammatory macrophage differentiation through the FAK-PI3Kγ signaling pathway [20]. Similarly, our transcriptomic data revealed significant alterations in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MAPK pathway, and ECM-receptor interaction pathway (Fig. 7E), implicating these pathways in the observed induction of M2 polarization.

This study acknowledges certain limitations. Firstly, while the nanoporous morphology enhances abutment surface hydrophilicity, it concurrently increases surface roughness, potentially raising concerns regarding bacterial adhesion, as bacteria often favor rougher surfaces. However, evidence indicates that when the surface roughness (Ra) remains below a threshold of approximately 0.2 μm, variations in roughness exert minimal influence on bacterial adhesion [43]. The surface roughness of our material (20–30 nm Ra) is substantially below this threshold. Moreover, numerous studies confirm that nanotube/nanoporous surfaces possess inherent antibacterial properties [44, 45]. Secondly, when applying this surface morphology to implant abutments, in addition to mechanical properties, the corrosion resistance under the complex conditions of the oral environment must also be considered [18]. Further evaluation of corrosion resistance in simulated oral environments and long-term wear testing under cyclic loading are essential prior to clinical application. Conclusively, while this research presents RNA sequencing results and preliminary analyses, it has not fully elucidated the underlying mechanisms. Future studies will focus on deeper mining of the data, particularly focusing on ECM-cell membrane-cytoskeleton interactions.

Conclusion and clinical implications

In summary, an anisotropic TiO2 nanoporous structure exhibiting enhanced mechanical properties was successfully fabricated on titanium surfaces via anodization followed by annealing. The macrophages cultured on this modified surface displayed an extended morphology with prominent pseudopodia formation and were polarized toward the M2 phenotype, concomitant with upregulated anti-inflammatory gene expression and downregulated pro-inflammatory gene expression. Our findings suggest that this immunomodulatory effect is likely to be related to the alterations in cellular morphology and cytoskeletal organization. Further exploration and validation of these mechanisms are warranted. This surface modification strategy holds significant potential to enhance the integration of titanium implant abutments with surrounding soft tissues by modulating the peri-implant immune microenvironment, thereby providing a robust theoretical foundation for future mechanistic investigations.
