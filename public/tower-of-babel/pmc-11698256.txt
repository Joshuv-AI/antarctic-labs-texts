Treatment of adult class II division 2 with deep bite using Forsus appliance and intrusion with TADs
Authors: Amani Alkhamees
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This case report details the treatment of a 23-year-old female patient with Class II division 2 malocclusion with a Class II skeletal base due to a retrognathic mandible. The condition was further complicated by a complete overbite, absence of tooth #36, and a gummy smile. The Forsus appliance has shown to be effective in correcting Class II malocclusion in adult patients, with significant improvements in bite and facial esthetics. The use of temporary anchorage devices for intrusion resulted in lower overbite and increased actual intrusion compared with alternative methods for intruding upper incisors.

Introduction

Class II division 2 is characterized by the retroclination of the upper central incisors (typically the lower incisors), causing the lower incisors to occlude palatally to the cingulum of the upper incisors and potentially leading to trauma to the palatal tissues.[1] The molar relationship is commonly Class II, but it may shift to Class I if there is premature loss of a lower deciduous molar, causing forward drift of the first molar.[2]

Etiologically multifactorial, Class II division 2 malocclusion often involves skeletal factors such as a retrognathic mandible. Patients may also exhibit prognathic maxilla, vertical and size discrepancies between the maxilla and mandible, dental factors, and early loss of primary molars. Soft tissue factors such as habits (thumb sucking, tongue thrusting), sleep apnea, and adenoids can further contribute to Class II malocclusion.[2]

According to a 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis comprising 26 studies, the prevalence of malocclusion among children in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia was 72%, with Class I, Class II, and Class III malocclusions at 66.51%, 17.70%, and 15.79%, respectively. The prevalence of Class I malocclusion was the highest, followed by that of Class II and Class III malocclusion.[3]

A study by Almalky et al.[4] found a 10.7% prevalence of Class II division 2 among 289 adolescents aged 14–17 years in Makkah governorate school. Treating Class II division 2 in nongrowing patients is more complicated as the growth of the jaws has already been completed.[5] In addition, the treatment options are more limited than those for growing patients[5] and include fixed orthodontic appliances, clear aligners, tooth extractions, and Class II corrector appliances, which can still be used to modify the position of the teeth and jaws. However, the effects may be more limited than those for growing patients.[5] Orthognathic surgery may be considered for severe Class II division 2 malocclusion in nongrowing patients.[5]

Correcting a deep bite in adult patients with orthodontic mechanics is challenging due to bone and tooth maturity.[2] However, several methods have been found to be effective,[67] including the intrusion of anterior teeth using TADs, anterior bite planes, correction of the posterior occlusal plane, and the use of lingual orthodontics.[56]

The TAD system offers potential advantages, allowing the application of corrective forces to reposition and incline the upper/lower incisors without destructive forces or patient cooperation.[8] Nevertheless, TADs may cause minimal protrusion, achieve higher intrusion speeds, and avoid affecting posterior teeth. Using a miniscrew can provide the most stable force and maintain the magnitude of applied force.[91011]

This case report aims to present the effects of both the Forsus appliance and TADs in treating a Class II division 2 patient with mandibular retrognathism and a severe deep bite.

Case Presentation

The patient was 23 years and 3 months old, who was referred to the orthodontic clinic with the complaint, “I can’t bite properly.” Extraoral examination revealed a mesiofacial face type, equal facial proportions, a fairly symmetrical face with competent lips, and a slightly convex facial profile with an average chin size and an average mentolabial fold. A smile analysis indicated a high smile line with a canted anterior occlusal plane and a nonconsonant smile arc with increased gingival display at the upper anterior 2-2.

Intraoral examination revealed fair oral hygiene with slightly visible plaque, generalized plaque-induced mild gingivitis, a thin band of attached gingiva around the lower canines, and gingival enlargement around the lower incisors labially and around tooth #12. Permanent dentition was present in the upper and lower 7-7, except for the missing tooth #36. Class V clinical caries were observed on tooth #13; enamel hypoplasia was present in tooth #11, 21, and 16; and attrition of the incisal cusp was noted on tooth #23. The canine relationship was full unit Class II on the right side and 1/4 unit Class II on the left. The molar relationship on the right side was full unit Class II. Overjet measured 8 mm in reference to tooth #21. The patient presented with a complete traumatic overbite and a deep curve of Spee, and the upper dental midline was shifted 2 mm to the left in reference to the facial midline. Arch-length deficiencies were noted, with 4-mm spacing in the maxilla and 3-mm crowding in the mandible [Figure 1]. Examination of the lateral cephalometric radiograph [Figure 2] indicated a normally positioned maxilla (SNA: 80°), a retrognathic mandible (SNB: 75.3°), skeletal Class II malocclusion (ANB: 4.7°), and a favorable vertical growth pattern (ManplSN: 32.5°). Both the upper incisors (U1-SN: 95.4°) and lower incisors (IMPA: 86.9°) were retroclined [Table 1]. A panoramic radiograph showed a permanent dentition with all teeth present except tooth #36, root dilaceration in tooth #12 and 22, mild lower anterior horizontal bone loss, and a vertical bone defect mesial to tooth #37 [Figure 3].

Treatment objectives

(1) Correct the inclination and anteroposterior/vertical position of incisors, (2) correct severe deep bite, (3) establish Class I canine and molar relationships, (4) achieve normal overjet and overbite, and (5) enhance the patient’s facial esthetics.

Treatment plan

Comprehensive nonsurgical, nonextraction fixed orthodontic treatment utilizing a 0.018” straight wire appliance with MBT prescription. The Forsus appliance was employed as a Class II corrector, and TADs were used for intrusion and correction of the deep bite.

Treatment progress

The orthodontic treatment commenced with bonding and leveling of the upper and lower arches using a 0.018” straight wire appliance with MBT prescription. The upper arch was segmentally bonded, with laterals bonded passively to prevent root movement into the area where the miniscrew would later be inserted after reaching U/L 0.016 × 0.022 SS. Two 6-mm miniscrews were inserted between the upper laterals and canines just above the mucogingival junction. Upper 2-2 were ligated together, and a power chain was attached from the wire to the TADs for intrusion. Activation was performed at 4-week intervals until the desired intrusion was achieved [Figure 4]. A continuous archwire was inserted once the upper anterior segment reached the same level as the posterior. On the mandibular arch, a reverse COS was added on the 0.016 × 0.022 SS archwire to improve the inclination of the lower incisors. After progressing to upper and lower 0.017 × 0.025 SS archwires, FRD appliances with a 32-mm rod length were inserted bilaterally [Figure 5]. Stainless-steel wires were cinched back in the lower arch once the FRD was installed. Continuous ligation was applied to the lower arch to limit the proclination of the lower incisors, and the FRD appliance was placed on the distal side of the mandibular canines. FRD activation was performed monthly until overcorrection (superclass I canine and molar) and normal overjet were achieved. The active intrusion period lasted 4 months, and the active FRD application took 8 months. The total active orthodontic treatment period spanned 24 months. After achieving the ideal overjet, overbite, and functional interdigitation, the brackets were removed, marking the start of the retention period [Figure 6]. Significant improvement in the smile line was evident in the post-treatment smile photographs [Figure 7]. During the retention period, the protocol included an upper modified Hawley retainer with an anterior bite plane and a lower Essix retainer, incorporating a pontic in the tooth #36 space [Figure 8]. Additionally, fixed mandibular and maxillary lingual retainers were used.

Treatment results

The final cephalometric radiograph is presented in Figure 9, and cephalometric measurements at pretreatment and post-treatment are detailed in Table 1. The results indicate improvements in both skeletal and dental parameters. By the end of treatment, the overjet had been reduced from 8 mm to 2 mm, and the overbite decreased from 100% to 10%. Cephalometric superimposition [Figure 10] revealed downward and forward movement of the mandibular dentoalveolar arch and backward movement of the maxillary dentoalveolar arch. The ANB angle decreased from 4.7° to 3.9° and convexity reduced, with diminished prominence of the labiomental fold. The post-treatment panoramic radiograph showed no evidence of alveolar bone loss or apical root resorption [Figure 11]. Post-treatment intraoral photographs demonstrated well-aligned teeth, with a stable occlusion presenting Class I molars and canines bilaterally. The arch form of both arches was coordinated, the lower midline was centered to the upper midline, and the Spee and compensating curves were within the normal range. Good marginal ridge relations were observed in the upper and lower arches. The third molars were well positioned, as confirmed by the post-treatment panoramic radiograph, and the patient was subsequently referred for implant placement of tooth #36.

Discussion

The treatment approach for a Class II skeletal relationship in adult patients typically involves a choice between camouflage or orthognathic surgery, depending on the severity of the maxillomandibular discrepancy.[2] The range of camouflage treatments has expanded with the advancement of orthodontic mechanotherapy,[12] offering options such as extraction, distalization, or segmental mechanics. The Forsus device, serving as an alternative to class II elastics, has demonstrated improved treatment outcomes by eliminating the need for patient cooperation.[13] A systematic review and meta-analysis comparing the dental and skeletal effects of the Herbst appliance, Forsus Fatigue Resistance Device, and Class II elastics found that Forsus was more effective in molar correction, overjet reduction, and upper incisor control than Class II elastics.[13] The patient in this study exhibited both skeletal and dentoalveolar improvements [Table 1]. Numerous studies have highlighted that the mechanism of skeletal Class II correction with Forsus FRD may involve mandibular growth, dentoalveolar effects, or a combination of both.[1415] Deep bite is a common issue associated with Class II division 2 malocclusion and is considered a complex orthodontic problem requiring early correction.[1] Treatment approaches for deep bite vary depending on factors such as the etiology (skeletal, dental, or soft tissue).[16] Various orthodontic mechanics have been introduced to address deep bites, including intrusive arches (such as Rickett’s utility arch, segmental intrusion arches, Connecticut sion arch, and three-piece intrusion arch).[171819]

Miniscrews are utilized as TADs to facilitate various dental movements. The key advantages of the TAD system include the ability to apply force for tooth displacement in any direction, avoiding destructive forces, and eliminating the need for patient cooperation. Additional benefits encompass the ability to alter the position and inclination of incisors and achieve higher intrusion speed, without affecting posterior teeth.[91011]

The position of the maxillary incisors, particularly in relation to the upper lip, plays a crucial role in determining the appropriate treatment approach. Correcting a deep bite through maxillary incisor intrusion in individuals with limited incisor display can lead to a flattening of the smile arc and diminished smile attractiveness.[20] In case of this patient, the upper incisors were retroclined and extruded, and the anterior occlusal plane was canted. Using TADs for intrusion facilitated the reduction of the deep bite while concurrently correcting the inclination of the incisors [Figure 12].

TADs for intrusion

The stability of deep bite correction in nongrowing patients with average to low mandibular plane angles is questionable.[21]

A systematic review and meta-analysis focusing on the effects of intrusion of anterior teeth using skeletal anchorage in deep bite patients indicated that the use of miniscrews resulted in reduced overbite and increased actual intrusion (approximately 0.45 and 0.62 mm, respectively) compared with alternative intrusion methods.[22] Achieving a favorable interincisal angle is crucial for stable overbite reduction, necessitating the lower incisor to be positioned 0–2 mm ahead of the upper incisor centroid. A well-interdigitated occlusion helps prevent tooth migration, and maintaining a Class I molar relationship can contribute to stability.[23]

Conclusion

Intrusion using TADs proves to be an effective method for correcting deep bites and ensures precise anchorage control. The Forsus appliance emerges as an excellent alternative for correcting mild to moderate skeletal Class II discrepancies.
