Introduction

Hyperkalemia is a potentially life-threatening electrolyte abnormality that can induce fatal ventricular arrhythmias by altering myocardial membrane excitability and conduction velocity [1,2]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common cause of severe hyperkalemia, and postrenal AKI due to urinary tract obstruction is a reversible but often underrecognized etiology [3,4]. Urinary retention can cause obstructive nephropathy, resulting in impaired renal potassium excretion and potentially severe hyperkalemia, particularly when obstruction is prolonged or accompanied by marked renal dysfunction [3,5]. Hyperkalemia classically produces progressive electrocardiographic changes, including tall, symmetric peaked T waves, PR prolongation, loss of P waves, progressive QRS widening, and ultimately a sine-wave pattern that can precede ventricular fibrillation or asystole [6,7]. Although hyperkalemia classically presents with bradyarrhythmias and conduction disturbances, malignant ventricular tachyarrhythmias, including ventricular tachycardia (VT) and ventricular fibrillation (VF), can occur in severe cases [1,2,6]. Brugada syndrome is an inherited cardiac channelopathy characterized by coved-type ST-segment elevation in the right precordial leads (V1–V3) and an increased risk of ventricular fibrillation and sudden cardiac death [8,9]. Brugada phenocopy refers to a Brugada-like electrocardiographic pattern caused by an identifiable and reversible clinical condition rather than congenital Brugada syndrome [10]. Several cases of hyperkalemia-associated Brugada phenocopy have been reported, including presentations associated with severe metabolic disturbances such as renal dysfunction and diabetic ketoacidosis [11–14].

The purpose of this report is to illustrate that severe hyperkalemia caused by reversible postrenal acute kidney injury can present with malignant ventricular tachyarrhythmia and transient electrocardiographic abnormalities involving the right precordial leads that resolve after potassium-directed treatment. This case highlights the importance of recognizing reversible metabolic electrocardiographic abnormalities that can mimic primary arrhythmic disorders in emergency clinical settings.

Case Report

A 55-year-old man with no known cardiovascular history was brought to the emergency department with acute palpitations, dyspnea, diaphoresis, and a sensation of heat. He lived alone and had not received regular medical care. There was no known family history of sudden cardiac death, unexplained syncope, or inherited arrhythmia syndrome. He was not taking sodium-channel-blocking drugs or other medications known to induce a Brugada-like electrocardiographic pattern, and there was no clinical history suggesting drug intoxication. On arrival, he was conscious but in respiratory distress. Electrocardiography revealed sustained wide-QRS tachycardia consistent with monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (Figure 1). His level of consciousness rapidly deteriorated, and electrical cardioversion at 150 J was performed, resulting in transient asystole followed by recurrent VT/VF. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, repeated defibrillation, epinephrine administration, and intravenous amiodarone were required. Endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation were initiated. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated severe metabolic acidosis and marked hyperkalemia, with profound renal dysfunction (Table 1). Following intravenous calcium gluconate and sodium bicarbonate administration, the electrocardiographic abnormalities, including marked QRS widening, terminal conduction delay, and ST-segment abnormalities in leads V1 and V2, resolved promptly (Figure 2). Emergency coronary angiography was deferred because no persistent ischemic electrocardiographic changes or focal left ventricular wall-motion abnormalities were identified on transthoracic echocardiography. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after approximately 12 minutes of total cardiac arrest time, with bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation having been initiated prior to hospital arrival. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit, treated with targeted temperature management according to contemporary post-cardiac arrest care recommendations [15], and started on continuous hemodiafiltration. Subsequent evaluation revealed urinary retention due to suspected prostatic obstruction, which was considered to be the cause of the postrenal acute kidney injury. After bladder catheterization and renal replacement therapy, serum potassium levels normalized, and no further ventricular arrhythmias occurred. The patient recovered without neurological sequelae and was transferred for rehabilitation and urological evaluation. No recurrent ventricular arrhythmias were observed during hospitalization, and no antiarrhythmic therapy was prescribed at discharge. The overall clinical course is summarized in Figure 3.

Discussion

The principal educational value of this case lies in the uncommon combination of severe hyperkalemia, sustained ventricular tachyarrhythmia with hemodynamic collapse, marked transient conduction abnormalities involving the right precordial leads, and reversible postrenal acute kidney injury. Previous reports have described hyperkalemia-associated Brugada phenocopy with a more typical type 1 Brugada electrocardiographic pattern [12,13]. Brugada phenocopy has also been described in diabetic ketoacidosis accompanied by hyperkalemia [14]. In contrast, our patient predominantly exhibited marked QRS widening and terminal conduction delay, and the underlying reversible cause was postrenal acute kidney injury due to urinary retention. There have been few reports describing the simultaneous occurrence of severe hyperkalemia, sustained ventricular tachyarrhythmia causing hemodynamic collapse, transient right precordial conduction abnormalities, and reversible postrenal acute kidney injury. Therefore, the present case provides additional clinical evidence supporting recognition of this uncommon but potentially reversible presentation.

The present findings were most consistent with severe hyperkalemia as the principal reversible trigger. However, concomitant metabolic acidosis, uremia, and post-cardiac arrest physiological changes may also have contributed to the observed conduction abnormalities. Hyperkalemia is a well-recognized cause of life-threatening arrhythmias, typically presenting with bradyarrhythmias, conduction disturbances, and eventual asystole. However, severe hyperkalemia can also provoke malignant ventricular tachyarrhythmias, including ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation, although this presentation is less commonly recognized. The observed electrocardiographic changes may reflect sodium channel inactivation caused predominantly by hyperkalemia, potentially augmented by severe metabolic acidosis [2,16]. Such conduction slowing can become heterogeneous, creating regions of functional conduction block and facilitating reentrant ventricular arrhythmias [2].

Hyperkalemia-induced Brugada phenocopy has been previously reported [11,16] and has been proposed to result from accentuation of transmural voltage gradients in the right ventricular outflow tract due to sodium channel inactivation [2,16]. This mechanism is similar to that observed with sodium channel blocker drugs, which are known to unmask Brugada syndrome [8,9]. Unlike previously reported cases demonstrating a definite type 1 Brugada electrocardiographic pattern, the predominant findings in our patient were marked QRS widening and terminal conduction delay. Importantly, this electrocardiographic pattern resolved rapidly after administration of intravenous calcium, strongly suggesting that the electrocardiographic abnormalities were secondary to severe hyperkalemia rather than a primary inherited arrhythmia syndrome.

Although hyperkalemia is classically associated with bradyarrhythmias, ventricular tachycardia can occur in severe cases due to marked conduction slowing and heterogeneous refractoriness, which promote reentry [2,6]. Severe metabolic acidosis, which was also present in this patient, can further exacerbate sodium channel inactivation and conduction slowing, thereby increasing arrhythmogenicity [2]. Hyperkalemia can also produce a variety of systemic manifestations affecting neuromuscular and cardiovascular function [17].

Another important clinical issue in this case was the differential diagnosis of irregular or wide-QRS tachycardia in the emergency setting. Severe hyperkalemia can mimic ventricular tachycardia, sodium channel blocker toxicity, Brugada syndrome, or acute myocardial ischemia [1,6,11,16]. Recognition of hyperkalemia is critical because the treatment strategy differs fundamentally from that for primary ventricular tachyarrhythmias, and early potassium-directed therapy remains the cornerstone of emergency management [1]. The rapid improvement after intravenous calcium strongly supports membrane stabilization as an important therapeutic mechanism, although a causal relationship cannot be established from a single case [1].

In this patient, the underlying cause of hyperkalemia was postrenal acute kidney injury due to urinary retention. Postrenal acute kidney injury is a reversible but frequently underdiagnosed cause of severe hyperkalemia, particularly in elderly men with prostatic disease. Early recognition and relief of urinary obstruction are essential for definitive management [3].

Although pharmacological provocation testing with a sodium channel blocker such as ajmaline can be useful when Brugada syndrome remains clinically suspected [9], it was not performed in the present case because the electrocardiographic abnormalities resolved completely after correction of hyperkalemia, and no spontaneous type 1 Brugada pattern was observed during hospitalization. Such testing could be considered during follow-up if future clinical suspicion for Brugada syndrome were to arise. Finally, the indication for implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) therapy was carefully considered. Current ESC and AHA/ACC/HRS guidelines recommend ICD implantation for secondary prevention in patients with ventricular fibrillation or hemodynamically unstable ventricular tachycardia in the absence of a reversible cause [18,19]. Because the ventricular arrhythmia in the present patient was most consistent with a correctable metabolic trigger and did not recur after normalization of serum potassium, ICD implantation was not pursued. No recurrent ventricular arrhythmias were documented during the index hospitalization, further supporting this management strategy.

This report has several limitations. As a single case report, a causal relationship between severe hyperkalemia and the observed electrocardiographic abnormalities cannot be established definitively. Furthermore, serial potassium measurements obtained before treatment initiation and previous electrocardiograms were not available. Pharmacological provocation testing and genetic testing for Brugada syndrome were not performed because the electrocardiographic abnormalities resolved completely after correction of the electrolyte disturbance and no further clinical suspicion remained. Finally, because the patient was transferred to a hospital closer to his residence shortly after completion of acute treatment, only limited follow-up data were available, precluding a comprehensive assessment of long-term clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

This case suggests that severe hyperkalemia caused by reversible postrenal acute kidney injury can present with both malignant ventricular tachyarrhythmia and transient electrocardiographic abnormalities involving the right precordial leads that can mimic inherited arrhythmia syndromes. Although hyperkalemia is more commonly associated with bradyarrhythmias and conduction disturbances, clinicians should recognize that life-threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmias can also occur. Early identification and treatment of hyperkalemia are critical because prompt correction can completely reverse both the electrocardiographic abnormalities and the arrhythmic substrate.
