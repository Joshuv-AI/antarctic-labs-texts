I, Robot? Socio-Technical Implications of Ultra-Personalized AI-Powered AAC; an Autoethnographic Account CCS: Human-centered computing Accessibility systems and tools
Tobias Weinberg, Ricardo E. Gonzalez Penuela, Stephanie Valencia, Thijs Roumen

Abstract
. Generic AI auto-complete for message composition often fails to capture the nuance of personal identity, requiring significant editing. While harmless in low-stakes settings, for users of Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) devices, who rely on such systems for everyday communication, this editing burden is particularly acute. Intuitively, the need for edits would be lower if language models were personalized to the communication of the specific user. While technically feasible, such personalization raises socio-technical questions: what are the implications of logging one’s own conversations, and how does personalization affect privacy, authorship, and control? We explore these questions through an autoethnographic study in three phases: (1) seven months of collecting all the lead author’s AAC communication data, (2) fine-tuning a model on this dataset, and (3) three months of daily use of personalized AI suggestions. We reflect on these phases through continuous diary entries and interaction logs. Our findings highlight the value of personalization as well as implications on privacy, authorship, and blurring the boundaries of self-expression.

1.
Introduction

For people with speech impairments who type to speak, composing messages with just a few clicks is not a luxury but a necessity
(
Waller [n. d.]
;
Seale et al. 2020
)
. Yet, generic auto-complete systems for composing messages often fall short: they rarely capture the nuance of personal identity, forcing users to spend significant effort editing suggestions
(
Valencia et al. [n. d.]
;
Valencia et al. 2024
)
. For users of Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) devices, who rely on such systems for their everyday speech, this becomes a heavy burden
(
Kane et al. 2017
;
Waller et al. 2025
)
. Intuitively, the need for text editing would be significantly lower if the model was trained on the specific user’s communication as opposed to a generic model.

While personalization has been shown to be technically feasible
(
Salemi et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Cai et al. 2023
)
, we set out to explore the socio-technical implications of such “fix” for AAC users: does it indeed support better communication, what is the impact of logging your own communication, how does one train such models, and what are implications of using AAC tools which auto-complete to support your communication in daily live? Given the potentially large social and ethical implications, it is crucially important to study this before deploying such technology in the wild
(
Dash et al. 2025
;
Shneiderman 2020
;
Reisman et al. 2018
)
.

In this paper, we approach these questions through an autoethnographic lens in three phases: (1) the lead author (who has a speech impairment and is a full-time AAC user) collected 7 months of all his personal speech data, while reflecting on this experience, (2) we filtered the data, and trained a language model to build a simple AAC app implementing the trained model (3) finally, the lead author used that app as his main AAC device for 3 months, again reflecting on this experience through diary logs and app usage data.

Within the field of AAC research, several AI-based techniques have been proposed to predict or expand on users’ typed messages
(
Valencia et al. 2024
;
Valencia et al. [n. d.]
)
.
Kwickchat

(
Shen et al. [n. d.]
)
is a sentence-based text entry system that allows AAC users to generate replies with minimal input by predicting sentences based on keyword entry.
Cai et al. [n. d.]
demonstrated the potential of LLMs to abbreviate input for eye-gaze, and further showed that incorporating conversational context significantly improved the efficiency
(
Cai et al. 2022
)
.
Weinberg et al. 2025a
found that in time-pressured scenarios, AAC users were willing to trade a degree of authorship to the LLM for speed gain, while others expressed concerns about authenticity and the risk of AI altering their self-perception
(
Valencia et al. [n. d.]
)
. However, the integration of AI into AAC is not without controversy. A recent community consultation highlighted concerns about bias, ownership of output, and data protection, with calls for a code of practice to guide responsible adoption
(
Griffiths et al. 2024
)
.

Kane et al. 2017
emphasizes that AAC users often go to great lengths to preserve self-expression, using humor, timing, and personalization to construct a sense of identity. Recent AAC ethics work cautions that LLM-enabled aids risk muddying authorship and reducing opportunities for co-construction if adopted uncritically
(
Griffiths et al. 2025
)
. More personalized LLMs for AAC users thus seem to hold promise.

Outside of AAC, personalization has been shown to enhance relevance and fluency, but also raises concerns around authorship, tone, and control
(
Peng et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Salemi et al. 2024
;
Yeh et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2023
)
. These systems increasingly treat personalization as a sociotechnical question, less about optimizing output, and more about who is being represented, when, and with what degree of user agency.

Our reflections reveal dynamics around personalization, usability, and control. When the ultra-personalized AI-powered app performed well, it adapted closely to the user’s style, producing contextually accurate and personally aligned suggestions that enhanced fluency and expressivity. In this work, we discuss the integrity implications of both data collection and usage by drawing on the notion of
Contextual Integrity

(
Nissenbaum 2004
)
, considering appropriateness of information flow relative to the value it provides. Ultra-personalization risks breaching the tacit social contracts that guide how we express ourselves across contexts. Furthermore, in our case, building an ultra-personalized model was not just a technical endeavor, but an ongoing negotiation of effort, authorship, and control. Collecting training data meant re-purposing intimate conversations, drawing boundaries between what should and shouldn’t be included. The autoethnographic lens we use in this work provides an extra layer of insight regarding
perceived
privacy implications, such as self-censoring speech due to the acute awareness of knowing that all the data would be later used to train a model.

Our findings highlight both the potential and the risks of ultra-personalized AI in AAC: it requires careful attention to how such systems respond in real-time to evolving conversations (responsiveness), whether their suggestions align with the norms and expectations of a given interaction (social context), and how much control users retain over what is said and how it is said (user agency). Without this alignment, even highly personalized outputs may feel intrusive, inauthentic, or misrepresentative, undermining the very identity they aim to support.

2.
Contributions, Benefits, and Limitations

This paper makes the following contributions:

•

Empirical insight into ultra-personalized AAC:
Through an autoethnographic study grounded in a multi-month deployment, we provide a nuanced account of how a large language model (LLM) trained on a single AAC user’s communication data shaped their conversational fluency, perceived authorship, and privacy boundaries.

•

Identification of socio-technical implications of personalized AI:
We surface trade-offs unique to ultra-personalized systems, including the impact of personalization on self-censorship, the social discomfort of visible AI suggestions, and the potential for models to overstep contextual boundaries by inserting culturally specific content at inappropriate moments.

•

Positioning lived experience in AAC research:
By documenting the evolving relationship between user and model over three months of daily use of personalized AI suggestions, we highlight real-world tensions in personalization, authorship, and data use that may not emerge in lab-based or short-term studies. We hope this work inspires further use of experiential, longitudinal methods to surface the complexities of AI adoption in assistive technologies, before deployment at scale.

This study contributes early, experience-driven insight into how ultra-personalized AI might be used to enhance expressivity, efficiency, and cultural relevance for AAC users. By documenting the socio-technical implications of system use over time, we offer design implications for systems that aim to preserve user intent while accelerating message composition. Our findings highlight contexts where personalized LLMs are most effective, such as academic self-presentation or culturally grounded small talk, and propose mechanisms to support agency in these moments.

This work is grounded in the lived experience of a single AAC user-researcher who only experiences speech production and minor motor disabilities. We do not claim generalizability across the wider population of AAC users. While this first-person perspective offers deep insight into underexplored dynamics, it also has important limitations. Many of the phenomena we describe, such as self-censorship or shifts in authorship, are difficult to access through external observation, making autoethnography especially valuable. At the same time, the account necessarily reflects a single trajectory, shaped by the author’s individual communication patterns, cultural background, and research motivations.

We also note that the technical implementation of our prototype, such as model architecture, training method, or system performance, is not a core contribution of this work. We took a pragmatic approach to building a functioning personalized system for long-term use, rather than optimizing for state-of-the-art AI performance. Environmental factors (e.g., interface lag or network issues) occasionally limited the system’s responsiveness, and future offline-capable or more robust implementations may behave differently. Our conclusions are therefore not tied to the specific accuracy of the model, but instead focus on the broader experiential and socio-technical implications of living with a personalized AI over time.

3.
Positionality Statement and user profile

The lead author of this work has been a full-time AAC user for over a decade, since a neuro-motor disease manifested when he was a teenager, affecting his ability to speak. He brings his lived experience navigating the complexities of communication asymmetry, authorship, and social presence in both personal and professional contexts. This dual perspective, as both researcher/developer and daily AAC user, deeply informs the framing, design, and analysis of this study. We disclose his experiences, as they played a significant role in shaping the research process, and contextualize the autoethnographic methodology.

, the author uses a “text-showing” style for most of his communication, typing in large fonts on his phone and showing the screen to interlocutors. This practice allows him to leverage the interlocutor’s imagination and his own non-verbal cues (e.g., facial expressions) to preserve tone and nuance, sidestepping the flat, unexpressive qualities of synthetic voices.

These preferences also shaped the interface design. The phase 1 data collection app was built around minimal friction in note-taking, mirroring his everyday use of the iOS Notes app, before layering in personalized LLM suggestions. Thus, the design of the interface was not generic, but tuned to his own style, and comfort with showing text. Beyond design, this perspective informed the analysis: his diary entries describe subtle experiential dynamics such as self-censorship when knowing conversations were logged, the difficulty of labeling interactions without disrupting flow, and the blurred boundary between system output and personal voice.

The role of user and developer brings a rather uncommon perspective where you are designing for yourself. We believe this adds to the experience of building an ultra-personalized system since it’s not only the personalized LLM that enhanced the experience, but the exclusive design of the interface based on his communication style and needs.

We emphasize that the primary contribution of this work is not the technical system, but the socio-technical implications surfaced through this lived experience. The author’s positionality as both user and researcher reveals how ultra-personalized AI can enhance fluency while also introducing new risks around privacy, authorship, and identity, where this first-person lens is essential for surfacing these tensions before naively deploying such technology in the wild.

4.
Related Work

We build on work examining the challenges of expressivity and authorship in AAC, the trade-offs in AI writing support, and the socio-technical implications of personalization, privacy, and agency in AI-mediated communication.

4.1.
Expressivity and Authorship in AAC

AAC users typically compose messages at a rate (12–18 WPM), compared to typical speech (125–185 WPM)
(
Waller [n. d.]
)
, creating what has been termed “communication asymmetry”
(
Seale et al. 2020
)
. This asymmetry can disrupt conversational rhythm, reduce opportunities for spontaneous expression, and create perceptions of disengagement
(
Higginbotham et al. 2016
)
. Yet timing alone does not determine communicative success. Research in AAC has shifted toward understanding
conversational agency
, the capacity to express intent and interact in dynamic contexts
(
Valencia et al. 2020
;
Barnlund 1970
)
.

To address this asymmetry, researchers have explored tools such as word prediction
(
Trnka et al. [n. d.]
)
and the use of visuals
(
Fontana De Vargas et al. [n. d.]
;
Fontana de Vargas and Moffatt [n. d.]
)
. More recent systems move beyond these strategies by inferring suggestions directly from context, drawing on cues like location, conversation transcripts, or surrounding images
(
Kane and Morris [n. d.]
;
Kane et al. [n. d.]
;
Valencia et al. 2024
)
. As AAC systems are increasingly integrated with generative AI to support faster communication, generic models often lack the nuance to reflect a user’s personal tone or identity
(
Valencia et al. [n. d.]
;
Shen et al. [n. d.]
;
Weinberg et al. 2025a
)
. Concerns about authorship and control have also been raised, in a community consultation, AAC users and practitioners worried that AI systems could homogenize voices, misattribute authorship, and breach privacy through context-aware sensing, underscoring the need for safeguards and codes of practice
(
Griffiths et al. 2024
)
.

While supporting faster communication rates can be beneficial, personal expression remains a critical factor for AAC users.
Kane et al. 2017
have shown the complex dynamics that AAC users go through to be able to express themselves and retain their identity.
Weinberg et al. 2025a
showed that in time-sensitive contexts, AAC users accept reduced agency in favor of delivering the comment faster.
Furthermore, by studying the backchanneling practices of AAC users,
Weinberg et al. 2025b
demonstrated that these unique cues form part of a broader AAC micro-culture, supporting self-expression and turn-taking. Together, these studies suggest that expressivity in AAC involves trade-offs negotiated in real time, shaped by social norms, contextual pressures, and individual communication goals.

Our work builds on these insights by investigating ultra-personalized AI systems trained on an AAC user’s own prior speech data instead of generic data. Unlike prior systems that offer speed at the cost of personal nuance, we examine whether an LLM fine-tuned on personal communication patterns can increase fluency without eroding the user’s identity.

4.2.
AI Writing Support: Co-Authorship and Authenticity

Advances in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated the ability to generate human-like text
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, adapt to individual writing styles
(
Toshevska and Gievska 2025
)
, and assist in text composition
(
Yuan et al. 2022
)
.
Early work focused on predictive text entry
(
Bi et al. 2014
;
Vertanen et al. 2015
)
. More recent systems move beyond token-level prediction toward sentence- and paragraph-level assistance, but with distinct aims: tools for ideation generate prompts for the user to inspire new directions for science writing
(
Gero et al. 2022
)
, while post-hoc revision interfaces support correction and editing
(
Cui et al. 2020
)
. Work on human–AI co-writing examines how model contributions interact with writers and writing outcomes:
CoAuthor

(
Lee et al. 2022
)
introduces a dataset of assisted writing sessions to study collaboration dynamics in creative and argumentative writing. While other domain-specific systems,
Dramatron

(
Mirowski et al. 2023
)
, explore hierarchical prompting for long-form co-creativity screenplay writing with industry evaluations. Furthermore, controlled experiments show that opinionated assistants can shape both what users write and what they subsequently believe
(
Jakesch et al. 2023
;
Fu et al. 2023
;
Agarwal et al. 2025
)
.

Researchers have explored how AI tools can scaffold writing by visual prompts
(
Chung et al. 2022
)
and by text summarization
(
Dang et al. 2022
)
. Others investigate AI’s role in collaboration and social dynamics, showing how suggestions affect group processes or perceptions of ownership
(
Gero et al. 2023
;
Stark et al. 2023
)
. A different line of work focuses on stylistic and genre-specific support, such as generating metaphors
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
. More recent systems, like
WordFlow

(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, highlight how collaborative prompt design can enhance the writing process. Finally, systematic reviews synthesize these developments, mapping how AI writing tools are used across diverse tasks and user groups
(
Fang et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2024
)
.

While these systems promise increased productivity and fluency, they may also reshape a user’s tone, intent, or perceived authorship
(
Kadoma et al. 2024
)
. Recent empirical work shows that people often do not perceive themselves as owners/authors of AI-generated text, yet still self-declare authorship, what Draxler et al. term the “AI Ghostwriter Effect,”
(
Draxler et al. 2024
)
, and that personalization alone does not resolve this effect; perceived ownership increases mainly with greater human influence over the output. In contrast,
Mishra et al. 2025
found that LLMs in collaborative writing can produce a sense of chat-ghosting, where system contributions overshadow human intent and leave participants feeling displaced from authorship. As LLMs become more powerful, the use of AI writing assistants raises longstanding questions about co-authorship, intent, and authenticity. At the same time, such systems often operate as opaque, one-directional suggesters offering completions or rewrites without revealing how they interpret the user’s intent. The complexity of articulating a prompt can elicit feelings of limited agency when working with LLMs
(
Ippolito et al. 2022
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
subtly steering expression, masking alternatives, or requiring users to “write against” the system.

This problem is magnified for users with limited physical input or slower composition rates: accepting a suggestion may be faster, but rejecting or editing it often requires disproportionate effort
(
Valencia et al. [n. d.]
)
.
Valencia et al. 2020
found that AAC users were wary of being perceived as lazy or inauthentic when relying on AI-generated responses. Similarly, in
Why So Serious?
, some users described discomfort when AI responses blurred the line between their own voice and system-generated output
(
Weinberg et al. 2025a
)
. Complementing design-centric work, ethics analyses warn that LLMs in VOCAs can blur authorship and remove co-construction opportunities, arguing against technocentric adoption and for explicit attention to humanness and user-centred design
(
Griffiths et al. 2025
)
. These findings call for tools that support user authorship without compromising responsiveness.

Our work extends this conversation by exploring what happens when the AI model is trained not just on general data, but on a user’s own communication history. We show how deep personalization can enhance fluency and relevance, while also introducing new forms of entanglement between user identity and system output.

4.3.
Privacy and Personalization in AI-Mediated Communication

Personalization in AI-mediated communication promises fluency and relevance, but also raises concerns about authorship, privacy, and control.
Chen et al. 2019
demonstrates large-scale AI-assisted writing and discusses deployment and fairness considerations in a personalized feature, while
Hermann 2022
emphasizes the need for AI literacy and multi-stakeholder oversight to mitigate ethical risks.
Frameworks like Contextual Integrity
(
Nissenbaum 2004
)
define privacy as the preservation of appropriate information flows between stakeholders, where violations occur when information shared in one setting is reused in another without regard for the original norms and expectations. In AAC, this manifests when ultra-personalized systems resurface private expressions in public or professional settings, even without external data sharing.

Recent systems frame personalization as a dynamic process rather than a fixed model state. One system supports user control through adjustable stylistic boundaries, showing that interpretability and real-time negotiation can help preserve authorship
(
Yeh et al. 2024
)
. Others highlight how design decisions shape the user’s voice, through scaffolded generation pipelines
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, explicit cues to preserve tone
(
Peng et al. 2024
)
, or lightweight embeddings that inject style while avoiding full retraining
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
, which outperform existing methods on the LaMP benchmark
(
Salemi et al. 2024
)
without requiring parameter tuning. These approaches point to a shift: personalization is no longer just a tool for fluency; it is a sociotechnical question about identity, authorship, and control. In AAC, where generated text becomes the user’s literal voice, this negotiation plays out word by word.

Personalization doesn’t just affect output quality; it shapes how users relate to AI over time. As systems become more responsive, users begin to ascribe trust, intent, and even social presence to them, with these perceptions shifting based on context and relational closeness
(
Araujo and Bol 2024
;
Lim and Shim 2022
)
. These dynamics raise ethical concerns around transparency, control, and the limits of consent, especially when personalization becomes invisible or automatic
(
King and Meinhardt 2024
)
. In AAC contexts, where AI-generated text functions as a user’s voice, these stakes are amplified: trust and privacy fundamental for expressing identity safely and authentically.

While our focus is not on securing data pipelines, privacy-preserving personalization has been widely explored in parallel fields. Techniques such as federated learning, on-device adaptation, differential privacy, and secure aggregation offer important infrastructural safeguards for real-world deployment
(
McMahan et al. 2017
;
Bonawitz et al. 2017
;
Hard et al. 2018
;
Abadi et al. 2016
)
. These methods address data protection at the system level, but they do not resolve the lived, moment-to-moment tensions around visibility, consent, and contextual misuse that emerge when personalized AI becomes part of everyday communication. Our work complements these efforts by examining how such tensions unfold experientially, particularly when the system functions as the user’s voice.

Work in value-sensitive design
(
Friedman et al. 2013
)
and user-controlled privacy
(
Erlingsson et al. 2014
)
has argued for local differential privacy, but rarely in the context of everyday conversation. In AAC, where authored text is not only logged but literally becomes the user’s spoken voice, the stakes are higher.

Our autoethnographic study exposes these stakes in lived experience. We examine privacy–personalization tensions not in abstract or technical terms, but in the moment-to-moment dynamics of AAC speech. This perspective reveals that ultra-personalization must account for context, relationship, and the shifting boundary between the user’s and the AI’s voice.

5.1.
Methodology

Over a period of seven months (November 2024 – May 2025), the lead author used a custom-built iOS app as his primary in-person communication tool. Every face-to-face communication was typed using the app, allowing it to act as both an AAC interface and a data collection platform.
During this time, the lead author maintained reflective notes in the form of open-ended entries to document his experiences during the data collection period. The app continuously logged only the lead author’s side of face-to-face communication, operating 24/7.

We experimented with several ways of delineating conversations, grouping by physical context, grouping by conversation topic, but ultimately, the only trackable approach was segmenting by day, which provided a consistent and manageable unit of analysis, though at the cost of topical granularity. These methodological choices, and the reflections they prompted, shaped the dataset into a form that was both authentic to lived AAC use and tractable for subsequent analysis.

Conversations data was accompanied by optional annotations describing the context and communicative intent (entered by the lead author). These annotations included selecting an emoji representing communicative intent for individual messages (e.g., smiling, frowning, etc.), and a short textual description of the topic and setting for conversations.

5.2.
Interface Design

The data collection interface was designed to be similar to the iOS Notes app (the author’s previous mode of communication) to be minimally invasive in his communication workflow, while providing a systematic way of collecting the data. The app was developed in Swift and deployed on the author’s iPhone 16 Pro Max.

As shown in Fig.
3
a, the first screen presents a list of conversation threads. Tapping the
+
button starts a new conversation and opens the text editor for the lead author to type. Selecting an existing conversation opens its message history for that conversation Fig.
3
b which has a title and a context description that the lead author can edit to add relevant information to that particular conversation, and also the lead author can review past messages or tap
+
to add a new message to that conversation, which again opens the text editor.

We initially planned to use the emoji to annotate the sentiment of each message(bottom of Fig.

5.3.
Raw Data

The Phase 1 data collection app structured data into conversation threads, where each thread acted as a parent node containing multiple messages. Every thread included a title and a short description entered by the lead author to capture contextual information (e.g., setting, activity, or interlocutor). Each message stored within a thread contained the text content, its parent thread ID, a timestamp, and an optional label of communicative intent (
messageType
).

5.4.
Results and Reflections

Over the course of seven months, we collected 247 conversation threads spanning 19,764 messages. These threads captured a wide range of communicative contexts, ranging from professional discussions to casual conversations in bars and even brief interactions such as ordering drinks on a plane. As we can see in Fig.
4
, most messages are relatively short (mean 8.5 words) responses in conversations with some outliers where the lead author tried to explain more of a monologue at once, for example, a lab meeting update with 377 words. We summarized and categorized the diary entries into 3 themes: (1) privacy and self-censorship, (2) Online Labeling: Trade-offs in Real-Time Annotation, and (3) delineation of conversational boundaries. To reflect the personal nature of these observations, we report these from a first-person perspective.

5.4.1.
Privacy and Self-Censorship

One of the earliest and most striking observations during the data collection process was the profound impact that constant surveillance had on my communication habits. In the first two weeks, I noticed a gradual but noticeable increase in self-censorship, where I consciously avoided certain words, phrases, or tonal choices that I would otherwise use freely in everyday conversations. For example, using “F**k” in a sentence, or gossiping about someone in the building with the doorman, or even making some dark humor jokes that I would normally do. This change was not a deliberate effort to alter my communication style; rather, it was an automatic, subconscious response to the knowledge that my words were being logged and analyzed. I became acutely aware of how I phrased things, much like a person who refrains from swearing in front of children or adjusts their speech when they know they are being recorded. This heightened awareness led to a more cautious approach in communication, ultimately limiting my freedom of expression. There was one time when I reverted back to my old IOS notes app because I needed to complain about a neighbor with a friend, and since I was going to say "not very nice things" about this person with whom I had an ongoing dispute, I didn’t want to have any records of what I said.

This issue touches on two interrelated themes:

(1)

The Ephemeral vs. Permanent Nature of Speech: Spoken language is inherently transient; it exists momentarily before fading away, leaving no permanent record. In contrast, AAC messages, particularly typed ones, endure, both in the effort required for their construction and in their potential storage. During data collection, this permanence became particularly salient: I sometimes noted avoiding certain words or jokes with close friends because I thought, "I don’t want the LLM to learn this from me.” What would normally have been an ephemeral remark in spoken conversation instead felt like leaving a permanent trace to be replayed, analyzed, or resurfaced later. This awareness created a form of self-monitoring not typically present in spoken interaction. Routine exchanges, like talking to a girl at a bar, hanging out with my colleagues in the office, or late-night existential talks with a close friend, took on a different weight when preserved as text logs, raising questions about how knowing that speech is being logged shapes what users choose to express. The durability of AAC speech in this context thus complicates the spontaneity that often defines everyday talk, making self-censorship and shifts in communication style more likely over time.

(2)

Context Sensitivity and Privacy: The need for vast amounts of data to effectively train ultra-personalized LLMs creates another layer of concern. Speech is highly context-dependent, meaning that what is appropriate or acceptable in one setting may be entirely inappropriate in another. A casual inside joke with a close friend, for example, “We basically get paid to f**k around” humorously referring to our job as researchers, which might be humorous in the context of friends/co-workers, but would be totally inappropriate or offensive in a different setting, e.g., a talk with your advisor about research funding.
Capturing the context and/or the intent with which the message was conveyed is crucial in this aspect. For example, the above scenario would be labeled as joke-sarcasm or in-joke, which suggests a level of comfort or familiarity with the speaking partner. Additionally, capturing the context in the conversation description as “talk with a friend” can further reinforce this delineation. Drawing from the
contextual integrity

(
Nissenbaum 2004
)
concept, the flow of information in a different context is only appropriate if the usefulness of that information outweighs the integrity violation, in the case of humor or playful language like teasing up a friend because of their choice of outfit the value (informational-wise) is so low that the reusing is unlikely a good practice.

5.4.2.
Online Labeling: Trade-offs in Real-Time Annotation

We experimented with attaching labels such as “humor” or “anger” to each utterance during data collection, but quickly abandoned this approach. Any attempt to label speech in real time—whether before, during, or after display—proved too invasive, interrupting the flow of conversation and drawing attention away from the speaker. While such labels might enrich training data, the cost to natural interaction was simply too high. We therefore dropped real-time labeling in favor of lightweight interaction.

5.4.3.
Delineating When a Conversation Ends

One of the challenges I faced during data collection was determining when a conversation truly ends and when a new one begins. This issue became particularly apparent when trying to split conversations into discrete units for analysis. For instance, if I am having a conversation with one of my labmates in the lab and then transition to a discussion with another, the question arises: Is this a continuation of the same conversation, or does it represent the start of a new one? Does the topic change affect this distinction, or is it more about the social context in which the interaction occurs?

This dilemma was further complicated when I attempted to group conversations based on location. For example, when I went to a bar to play music, I found myself speaking with several different people in the same setting, yet the topics of discussion varied widely. One moment, I might be talking about the chords we were playing, and the next, I could be asking about a friend’s father’s health. These exchanges might be followed by a philosophical debate with yet another friend. This approach to grouping conversations by location created an overly large thread, containing diverse topics ranging from mundane to deeply personal. In this case, the contextual setting (the bar) did not provide sufficient cohesion between the topics, resulting in a jumble of unrelated discussions.

We therefore adopted a simpler and more reliable approach: conversations were delineated by day, more akin to diary entries than discrete sessions. This ensured that all face-to-face communication within a given day was preserved without the burden of making fine-grained segmentation decisions in real time. While this method sacrificed topical granularity, it provided a consistent, low-overhead way of structuring the data while still allowing for later reflective analysis.

Looking forward, however, delineation remains an open methodological challenge. Future work could explore clustering methods: for example, grouping conversations by both location (context) and topic, with perhaps a separate category for interactions that don’t fully qualify as conversations. Some exchanges may be too brief or transactional to be considered full conversations. For example, a simple interaction with the doorman inquiring about a package, where I might ask, “Packages?” followed by “7A” and “Thank you”—feels more like a brief, contextually insignificant exchange than a meaningful conversation. These kinds of interactions, while valuable in their own right, do not hold the same conversational weight and should be distinguished from more substantial discussions. One intriguing possibility would be to train separate, lightweight models for different contexts and dynamically switch between them, though this was beyond the scope of our present study.

6.
Phase 2: Curation and Model Training

In this section, we describe how we prepared the training data for our ultra-personalized model and the steps we followed to train the model. This process included four major parts: (1) filtering data, (2) structuring the data for training, (3) training a Personalized Model (trials with different models, and testing different training hyperparameters), and (4) augmenting the interface with the trained model.

6.1.
Filtering Data

We curated a subset of the collected data to train a personalized language model. We first started by filtering messages that the lead author did not want to publicly disclose. We applied a standard toxicity filtering pipeline
(
Logacheva et al. 2022
)
to exclude any messages containing potentially offensive or inappropriate content. While this filtering step inevitably reduced the “truthfulness” or fidelity of the raw dataset, it removed utterances that may have reflected authentic but messy moments of interaction.
This was especially important given that the model was trained using OpenAI’s fine-tuning API, which required uploading data to external servers.

Beyond privacy, this filtering also reflected a deliberate design choice about authorship. We did not want the AI to be responsible for producing utterances such as profanity, curse words, or dark humor. Although these play a role in everyday communication, we did not trust the model to generate them in a way that was contextually appropriate. By excluding them from training, the user retained control over when and how these more sensitive or risky expressions occur. In practice, this meant that the model was shaped to produce only the kinds of utterances the user was comfortable delegating to it, while reserving sensitive forms of speech for his own discretion. In this sense, filtering was not just a technical preprocessing step, but a way of aligning the model with the lead author’s communicative boundaries.

Out of 19,764 messages, 269 were filtered out automatically by the toxicity pipeline. Examples of such filtered utterances include: “It’s not just bull***t I wrote.” These removals illustrate the kinds of expressions that, while authentic to everyday communication, were judged unsuitable for inclusion in the training data, both because of privacy concerns and because the author did not want the model to reproduce them automatically.

6.2.
Structuring Data for Training

To fine-tune LLMs, training data must be formatted to follow the model’s specific input–output conventions. Beyond formatting, the data also needs to be structured so that the model can “learn” when and what tokens to generate given an input (i.e., modeling the probabilistic distribution of word sequences encoded in the training data). For example, our final model was a fine-tuned version of GPT-4.1-mini. Preparing a training corpus for this model required reformatting each conversational entry into paired training examples consisting of a user message and an assistant message, which in normal instruction LLM training would be pairs of questions and answers data. In our case, the user message corresponds to text written by the lead author, while the assistant message represents the expected continuation that the model should attempt to predict. We applied this reformatting consistently across all models we evaluated, adjusting to each model’s specific data requirements.

We adopted a simple incremental chunking approach: splitting each message by word, predicting the remaining portion, and sequentially adding more words until the

6.3.
Training a Personalized Model

The personalized model was fine-tuned on the author’s conversational corpus collected during Phase 1. We evaluated several candidate LLMs, ranging from smaller offline models that can run on-device, such as Gemma 3 and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, to larger models accessed online, such as Llama 3.5, Cohere’s Command-R-08-2024, and GPT-4.1-mini. In conventional machine learning practice, the primary metric for model selection is accuracy. However, for ultra-personalized systems, accuracy alone proved insufficient. What mattered most was whether the model’s output felt representative of the author’s voice, style, and cultural-linguistic background.

This criterion of alignment with self-representation is inherently subjective, but it reflects the central goal of personalization: to preserve the user’s sense of authorship and identity. For example, even when smaller models performed reasonably well on standard benchmarks, their completions often felt generic or failed to capture the author’s bilingual (English/Spanish) communication style. Multilingual capability thus became a second decisive factor, narrowing the viable candidates to Gemma, Llama, Cohere, and GPT.
Through direct testing with comparable completion prompts like "hello, I’m " or "my research is " across several regenerations, the lead author voted on which one was similar to what he would write. Gemma was discarded because, after fine-tuning, the coherence of the output sentences significantly degraded, and Llama 3.5 gave too generic answers; the final decision boiled down to Cohere vs GPT-4.1-mini, where GPT-4.1-mini emerged as the model that best reflected the author’s communicative voice across languages and contexts. It was therefore selected and implemented in the app.

GPT-4.1-mini was fine-tuned using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) within the OpenAI platform service, which enables training with custom examples tailored to specific use cases. The resulting model more reliably produced text in the author’s desired style and tone. Training was performed on 22,415,365 tokens over one epoch, with a batch size of 64 and a learning rate multiplier of 2. This configuration resulted in a training loss of 1.087 and a validation loss of 4.889, corresponding to an accuracy of 81.2% (Fig.

). The total cost of training using OpenAI services was approximately $120.

7.
Phase 3: Deployment and Evaluation

In this last phase, we deploy the application for 3 months, we describe the methodology, and report both usage analytics and qualitative autoethnographic reflection of the lead author.

7.1.
Methodology

After training, we deployed the app on the lead author’s iPhone 16 Pro Max for daily use over 3 months (June – August 2025). The app integrated the personalized model to provide AI-generated in-line writing suggestions in real time as the lead author composes messages.

During this period, the lead author completed structured diary entries regularly (at least once every three days) using a survey that combined Likert-scale ratings and open-ended reflections. The lead author first indicated whether AI-generated suggestions were used that day (frequently, occasionally, or not at all). They then rated the helpfulness of suggestions on a 5-point scale (1 = not helpful, 5 = very helpful) and described one concrete moment where a suggestion worked well or poorly. Additional items asked whether the LLM reflected or adapted to the author’s personality, whether more or less personalization would have been preferred, and whether any surprising or inappropriate phrases or tones appeared.

Privacy and authorship were explicitly addressed through targeted items: he rated his feeling of control of how the app represented his "voice" that day (1 = rarely, 5 = always), and whether any suggestions felt too personal or unexpected. Finally, he reflected on any discomfort about how his data might be used or stored. Each diary entry concluded with an open-ended field for any additional reflections. We analyzed the diary entries using affinity diagramming.

In parallel, the app logged interaction data, tracking the following metrics: duration of the interaction, the number of words typed by the user, how many suggestions were shown, how many suggestions were accepted, how many words were accepted from the accepted suggestions, and how many words the accepted suggestions had, as well as keystrokes and touch logs, to measure performance and adaptation over time. These logs were analyzed to evaluate how personalization affected communication efficiency, while diary reflections offered insights into expressivity, identity, and authorship in practice.

Diary Reflections

Personalization & Identity.
One of the most striking capabilities of the ultra-personalized LLM was its ability to capture my identity and cultural background. I usually have encounters with Spanish-speaking people where I would naturally want to speak Spanish, but Argentine Spanish and/or Spanglish. The LLM handled this seamlessly, for example, when I met with 2 high school classmates that I haven’t seen in over 10 years, the LLM generated casual Argentine slang like “che boludo” in its predictions as well as an instance where it popped up without any additional context “no soy boludo”, an extremely casual way to say “I understand”. Although this instance was sprinkled with some good suggestions, in reality, the LLM only contributed only 1.7% of my speech during this day. This kind of code-switching is very natural and frequently happens in my day-to-day face-to-face communication.

Another instance was when I was explaining my research to a Venezuelan person I just met, the LLM understood that I was using Spanglish and completed the my sentences “Mi research es ’ with ’en human computer interaction”, to which I added “I’m looking into how” and further completed with “to ayudar a people with speech impairment” and I finished it with “tener tech q sea más expresiva” which is indeed what I do. In this conversation, I accepted 100% of the suggestions, inputting 11 out of 26 words of the message. The system mimics my Spanglish balance, showing how the model progressively complements my speech. The LLM was also surprisingly good at explaining stuff about my cultural background. An outstanding example of this was with one of my labmates and when it gave the suggestion that we should go out to eat knishes (traditional Jewish food), but she did not know what it was, so, as I was typing “knish is…” with the model, it completed “a Jewish food that’s basically a pastry filled with stuff like potatoes” contributing the 53.3% of the sentence. Again, this type of support was correct and mimicked my style of explaining, as I often use “basically” and “stuff” in my vocabulary. Similarly, when talking with friends at an empanada shop, the system helped me describe elements of Argentine culture. For instance, while describing the doneness of pizza crust, the model suggested the word “doradito” in Argentine Spanish, a term very characteristic of how young Argentines talk, and exactly how I would say it myself, and it was a word that was not in the training corpus.

These examples showcase how ultra-personalization helped me express and amplify my identity and culture.

Usefulness and Contextual Fit.
One of the most noticeable dynamics of the ultra-personalized LLM was how strongly its usefulness depended on context. In some conversations, the suggestions amplified my communication, helping me explain my research more fluently or add details I would not have bothered to type on my own due to the length and effort it requires in some contexts. For example, when I was describing an autoethnography of a person with Cerebral palsy, the LLM completed my sentence with “and later analyzed the themes in the responses.” I had not planned to add that level of detail, but it was accurate and helpful, so I accepted it. Similarly, during a group lab meeting and later when I visited a big tech research lab for a meeting, the suggestions supported me in explaining my work smoothly and even made me “shine” in front of colleagues and new acquaintances, in this case I had 9 instances where I accepted 100% of the suggestions which ranged from as much as adding 29 words to my composition to as little as just 1-2 words suggestions which in total sum up to 74 words during the course of that meeting. In these cases, even though the model did not exactly match the words I would have typed, it was close enough, and the end result still felt authentic.

At other times, when discussing niche technical topics or having casual conversations, the same system became distracting. For example, when helping a friend debug code, the model often hallucinated or proposed irrelevant steps in 66 out of 68 messages composed during that period of time, leading to 738 suggestions rejected, and the only 2 suggestions that I accepted contributed 2 and 3 words, which slowed down the conversation. In casual settings with rapid topic shifts, such as conversations at a bar, it struggled to anticipate where the talk was heading. On routine days (paper deadlines, quick work check-ins), nothing particularly stood out: suggestions rarely added value and sometimes nudged in new ideas I was not trying to introduce. In mixed conversations that jumped between research and everyday topics, occasional work-related completions were useful, but most steered toward content I did not intend to bring up. Over time, I noticed that the model was subtly steering dialogue in specific directions; once a topic was mentioned, the AI often pulled toward familiar patterns or vocabulary, narrowing the expressive space rather than expanding it.

These examples show a central tension: ultra-personalized LLMs can amplify communication when the conversational goal benefits from elaboration, recall, or polished phrasing, yet they can distract when speed, precision, or fluid topic shifts matter most.

Privacy and Intrusion.
Personalization often felt uncanny when the model surfaced highly specific details from my past. For instance, while recounting my family history, the LLM completed the story after just a couple of words, inserting accurate details that I had only mentioned once or twice in the training data. On one hand, this was impressive—proof that the system could retain and retrieve rare biographical information—but simultaneously felt invasive.

Intrusiveness depended heavily on context. In environments such as airports, suggestions felt more disruptive than helpful. For instance, when I was at the airport late for my flight, in such scene, the AI just gets in the way, for utterances like “Hi, where is the priority line”, or “I need to take the laptop out”. During the 2+ hours I was at the airport, there was a sense of rush. In that timeframe, I received 130 suggestions which I all rejected, also my word output during that period was rather low with only 134 words typed in total. The constant flow of irrelevant or mistimed completions, coupled with the pressure of moving quickly through a public space, made the presence of AI more annoying than supportive. Instead of accelerating communication, the system became a source of friction.

In other contexts, the model tended to surface aspects of my religious background in moments where they were not contextually relevant. While sometimes these completions reflected accurate knowledge about me, their timing often felt inappropriate, shifting the tone of the exchange and risking awkwardness with partners who might not share the same background. For example, when introducing myself to someone in an academic environment or when chit-chatting with a labmate about what they did on the weekend (which has nothing to do with religion). Such moments illustrate a breakdown of contextual integrity: content that may be entirely appropriate in one relationship setting (e.g., with close friends) became misplaced in others (e.g., casual or professional interactions).

Finally, privacy concerns were not just about what the system suggested, but about the visibility of the suggestions on the screen. In social settings like bars, where people could see my phone screen as I typed, suggestions appeared and disappeared in plain sight. On several occasions, since people usually read my screen as I type to increase the flow of conversation, interlocutors mistook suggestions for things I was about to say, or became distracted by the flickering text, interfering with the flow of human conversation by drawing attention to the AI itself rather than to me as the speaker. This exposure made me feel uncomfortable, as I felt that I was not in control of what the AI might reveal.

These experiences highlight a critical tension: ultra-personalization can enhance fluency by recalling rare details and aligning with identity, but it also risks intruding on privacy, resurfacing sensitive content in the wrong moments, and even undermining social presence by making the AI’s role too visible.

Fragility of Access:

While personalization showed promise in supporting my daily conversations, technical reliability often determined whether the system was usable at all. Because the model required constant connectivity, poor reception or lack of Wi-Fi frequently rendered suggestions unusable. During a weekend camping trip, the absence of cellular signal forced me to turn off suggestions entirely, since the delay made them impractical for real-time interaction.

The subway provided an interesting counterexample: while riding home from the bar with a new acquaintance, although there was no reception underground, knowing the model excelled at providing polished research summaries, I delayed my typing of the response. As soon as we reached the next station (a few seconds later) and my phone reconnected to 5G, the system delivered the research explanation I needed. This moment highlighted both the fragility of network dependency and the occasional payoff of waiting through lag.

These challenges reveal that access to ultra-personalized AAC is not only about the quality of the suggestions, but also about stable infrastructure and dependable software performance. Without both, the promise of personalization is quickly overshadowed by the frustration of unreliability.

8.
Discussion

Our work presents an autoethnographic account of using an ultra-personalized AAC system, examining how agency, privacy, and identity are negotiated. The data collection process revealed how self-censorship shapes model behavior. Our implementation highlighted how design decisions, such as partial suggestions and user-developed tooling, can reclaim agency across data, engineering, and use. Finally, our reflections on usage raised questions about contextual integrity, authorship boundaries, and what it means to sound like oneself.

8.1.
Agency across the board

Each phase of our deployment revealed trade-offs in how agency (the capacity to express intent and achieve communicative goals in constrained contexts
(
Valencia et al. 2020
)
) was negotiated. We observed implications on forms of agency in each phase of this work: self-censorship during data collection (agency over data), the dual role of user and developer during development (agency over technology), and negotiation of shared authorship between AI and user during deployment (agency in authoring). We expand on each below.

Phase 1: self-censorship (agency over data)
Self-censorship leads to filtering of certain parts of speech, disproportionately implicating the registers where personalization is most needed (humor, stance, code-switching across styles of conversation). This shrinks linguistic diversity, skews context coverage, biases the data toward “good” utterances, and creates feedback loops that narrow the expressivity of the model’s voice. Even when users have control over what they share, the act of speech being recorded alters how the resulting speech is composed. AAC speech is already marked by deliberation and permanence, factors amplified when messages are stored for training. This shift raises important questions: How might the knowledge of being recorded shape what users choose to express? How do these dynamics interact with a user’s sense of authorship and control? Does perceived self-censorship fade with time?

We collected data for seven months, yet self-censorship impacted the experience from the get-go, highlighting the importance of this challenge. The key question over longer periods of time is whether the AI’s influence becomes so embedded that it inadvertently stifles the user’s genuine communication. Or even shifts the style towards an “ideal” based on the self-image of users.

Phase 2: Dual role of user and developer/designer (agency over technology)
This project was based on an interesting preposition: the end-user was also the engineer/designer of the software we developed. Especially in AAC, where personalization is often limited to customizing icons on vocabulary boards
(
Waller et al. 2025
;
Beukelman et al. 1998
)
, this level of agency is unique. It did allow us to develop a user-centric interface specifically optimized for real-world AAC use and the specific communication style of our user. Technical nit-bits are the partial suggestions and primary focus on large visible text at all times, adapting both to his preferences and functional abilities, in line with the principles of
Ability-Based Design

(
Wobbrock et al. 2011
)
. This deep integration of lived experience into design went beyond personalization of language; it shaped how the system felt in everyday use, preserving authorship and identity.

By allowing control at the word level, partial suggestions supported a nuanced negotiation between speed and authorship, critical for AAC users who may otherwise be forced to choose between expressive fidelity and conversational timing
(
Weinberg et al. 2025a
)
. The broader implication here is that ultra-personalization is about giving users fluid control over that trade-off is essential to preserving agency, not just about tailoring what the model says, but also how much it says. In doing so, it extended personalization beyond surface-level customization toward what
Preece et al. 2024
describes as ownership as an ongoing process, where users actively shape how technology mediates their voice.

Our implementation of partial suggestions aligns perfectly with the high dexterity of motor skills of the lead author. This allowed us to study such suggestions with its best fidelity. However, this also flags the cost of having the user and engineer being the same person; for others to experience similar agency, it will be important to develop partial suggestions or generally AI-mediated AAC with their skills in mind. By no means does the application developed in this paper translate to a general population of AAC users. But we believe that the notion that this interface is highly personalized, thus giving the end-user agency over the technology, serves as a crucial requirement for future developers.

Phase 3: AI and user negotiate shared authorship (agency in authoring)

While AI suggestions accelerated writing, they were not always appropriate or welcome. We observed two kinds of intent when composing a message, which align with Flower and Hayes’ cognitive process model of writing
(
Flower and Hayes 1981
)
:
planned writing
and
exploratory writing
.

Planned writing
occurs when the user already knows exactly what they want to say; they are effectively “translating” a formulated thought into text. For example, in a heated discussion about politics with a friend. The user has a clear picture of what and how they want to say, and improper formulation may undermine their argument. Slightly mis-formulated suggestions are distracting and may implicitly influence the argument
(
Jakesch et al. 2023
;
Fu et al. 2023
;
Agarwal et al. 2025
)

Exploratory writing
, on the other hand, happens when the user is still developing their thought while typing, pausing, and revising. In these moments, ultra-personalized suggestions can help scaffold the process, for example, when explaining what they want to make for dinner. Here, the user can benefit from the model’s “rambling on” and selectively adopt phrasing that fits what they want to say.

These observations suggest that supporting agency over authorship in ultra-personalized AAC requires sensitivity to when and how much the AI should speak. In our work, this was pre-configured; future work could explore a more automatic negotiation of agency over authorship between user and AI. The simple solution would be a simple on/off toggle of AI suggestions. In phase 3, we actually had such toggle (only used in poor connectivity scenarios to not slow down the app). The problem with such solutions is that it is hard to predict when exactly ultra-personalization is useful, and thus there is a good chance that the toggle remains on or off indefinitely (like the silent mode on phones), adding 0 value at the cost of interface complexity. A more subtle technique would be to monitor low-level interaction patterns, such as word-by-word timing, pause length, or typing speed, to infer the user’s intent (e.g., planned writing vs. exploratory writing). Because these may shift mid-sentence, the system could toggle visibility or adjust suggestion frequency in real time. Authorship agency here becomes a matter of tempo, not just content. A truly responsive system would know when to step back, preserving user authorship, and when to step in, offering expressive acceleration, without needing the user to explicitly configure those boundaries.

8.2.
Contextual Integrity and Privacy

Another crucial theme in our reflections is the focus on privacy and contextual integrity. To expand on our findings, we draw on Nissenbaum’s framework of contextual integrity
(
Nissenbaum 2004
)
, which defines integrity as the preservation of appropriate information flows between stakeholders, where violations occur when information shared in one setting is reused in another without regard for the original norms and expectations. Information flow is subject to norms of flow that are agreed upon between stakeholders. Imagine a conversation between two friends; there are norms as to what you do/do not talk about. Such norms deviate from the norms of a conversation with your employer or a government official. An ultra-personal AI, trained on a mixture of such data, is blind to these norms. And it similarly resurfaces content largely ignorant of norms of flow (to whom it shares) or appropriateness (contextual agreement between interlocutors). In the meantime, there is no norm of appropriateness between the AI and any of the stakeholders. In our reflections, this frequently caused friction.

In our context of use, where the AAC user is a stakeholder and an interlocutor who looks at the screen in real-time, the user is not given any opportunity to align what is visible on the screen to the contextual norms of appropriateness. The suggestions of the AI are directly read by the interlocutor. Arguably, the worst part of this violation of contextual integrity was its unpredictability, when exercising speech, users of ultra-personal AAC are continuously at risk of exposure of their private information. Similar to patterns of “forced intimacy”
(
Alharbi et al. 2023
)
, which unfortunately are common problems in accessibility technology. Community consultation likewise flags privacy, authorship authenticity, and data governance as first-order risks for AI-powered AAC, recommending a Code of Practice for deployment
(
Griffiths et al. 2024
)
.

In line with our observations around agency over technology, one potential solution could be to provide contextual norms to the AI model. However, this will require very careful implementation and evaluation. Ideally, AAC users can discretely configure this without the interlocutor seeing them do it. For example, if you are upset with your partner, you do not first want to set the AAC to “upset” and “interlocutor profile–partner” before even saying anything. Similarly, when collecting data, it might be valuable to label speech with contextual information.

Based on that idea, we hypothesized that the more context we could include in each recorded message, the better our ultra-personalized model would perform. We therefore experimented with labeling every message in real time with metadata such as emotional weight or intent (e.g., sarcasm, frustration, playfulness). This approach proved impractical. Labeling messages before they were displayed breached the tacit performance of intent, e.g., tagging a message as “sarcasm” before showing it completely undermined the subtlety that sarcasm relies on. Post-hoc labeling was equally disruptive, as it caused the user to miss their communication partner’s response or appear disengaged
(
Higginbotham and Wilkins 2013
)
. While we initially believed this context would be essential for improving model quality, we found that our model still performed "well" without it. We conclude that only if automatic (at 0 user effort) such contextual labeling outweighs its cost.

An automatic labeling tool could pre-label messages on the fly, allowing the user to review and correct as needed. This would reduce the labeling burden while still preserving user agency. Similarly, collecting contextual data could enable the creation of multiple ultra-personalized agents, each attuned to their own contextual norms. This may reduce the contextual integrity violation.

We suggest reframing ultra-personalization not as a singular model that knows everything, but as an ecosystem of modular, context-aware agents that defer to user control and adapt fluidly to contextual norms. Such systems may preserve contextual integrity (or reduce its violation), ensuring that what the AAC user says not only sounds like them, but fits where, how, and to whom they are speaking.

8.3.
Identity and Authorship

The last theme ties into the essence of ultra-personalization: the role of identity. One of the most powerful reflections was when the model captured deeply personal nuances: Argentine slang like
doradito
for pizza crust, bilingual code-switching in Spanglish, and subtle stylistic tics like explaining with “basically” and “stuff.” These moments suggested the model reflected the lead author’s identity. This underscored the potential of personalization to go beyond functionality, offering a fuller, more situated, and expressive sense of voice.

Aligned with our discussion of contextual integrity, at times, the model surfaced details the lead author had not intended to share, like religious references in professional exchanges, creating moments of awkwardness. Beyond the violation of privacy/integrity, this also poorly reflects the lead author’s identity. Because the lead author often uses a “show text” communication style, suggestions flicker publicly, sometimes leading interlocutors to mistake system-generated suggestions for his own. As one friend put it: “You know the funny thing is, since you started using your new app, I am always thinking if you are talking to me or AI is talking to me.”

These aren’t just interface quirks; they are authorship ruptures. Prior work shows that heavy reliance on AI obscures authorship; people often do not perceive themselves as owners/authors of AI-generated text, yet still self-declare authorship, which came to be known as
“AI Ghostwriter Effect,”

(
Draxler et al. 2024
)
. Echoing
Griffiths et al. 2025
, while personalization amplifies identity, it can also destabilize it when suggestions overstep authorship boundaries, so adoption should privilege co-construction and user-centred design over technocentric speed gains.

Ultimately, identity support in AAC is not about sounding like oneself in every utterance, but about preserving the capacity to decide how one shows up. This includes deciding what is voiced, how, and in front of whom. A system that learns from the user must also know when to step back, to defer, and to adapt. Personalization, in this light, becomes less about perfect mimicry and more about co-authorship: a partnership that helps users express themselves on their own terms, across the varied textures of everyday life.

9.
Conclusion

This work contributes to the ongoing discourse on AI-assisted AAC. While the promise of ultra-personalized LLMs trained on a user’s own speech data—offers exciting possibilities for enhancing expressivity and speed, it also introduces complex socio-technical implications that are difficult to anticipate in controlled settings. This study lays bare those implications and provides early insight into how ultra-personalized AI might affect AAC users before broader deployments or multi-user evaluations.

Reflections are grounded in the first-hand lived experience of the lead author, an AAC user and researcher who has navigated the complexities of technology-mediated communication for over a decade. By turning the lens inward, we examine the long-term effects of ultra-personalization while minimizing harm and maintaining user autonomy. This approach allowed us to surface the nuanced interplay between personalization and agency in AI-mediated AAC, and to reflect on broader questions about AI’s role in shaping self-expression, the ethics of ultra-personalization, and the need for AAC systems that preserve individuality while avoiding unintended bias.
