Design and implementation of a hybrid machine learning framework for predicting heart rate status
Authors: Mahsa Emami, Neda Salehbagheri, Saman Rajebi, Siamak Pedrammehr, Kimia Shirini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Real-time accurate evaluation of heart rate status during physical activity is a critical requirement in physiological analysis, performance enhancement, and early warning systems for cardiovascular diseases. In this work, a lightweight hybrid machine learning model for classifying the status of heart rate during physical activities into normal and warning levels using a combination strategy of Multilayer Perceptron, Naïve Bayes, and K-Nearest Neighbors algorithms in a weighted voting–based ensemble model is presented. The model analyzes a set of physiological and environmental parameters such as temperature, humidity, speed, incline, and activity time. Before embarking on model development, a thorough statistical analysis of available data was performed, which involved descriptive statistics, density analysis, normality tests using Shapiro–Wilk tests, and both parametric and non-parametric hypothesis tests to establish the discriminative power of all input variables in spite of non-normality. Every individual base classifier model was tested for performance using a fixed split of 70/15/15 for training, validation, and testing, and their respective strengths were tapped using a weighted voting mechanism based on relief factor analysis. The simulation outcome shows that the proposed ensemble classifier performs better than standalone classifiers in achieving an accuracy of up to 96.67%, an F1-score of 96.66%, and an MCC of 0.9354, which is a manifestation of excellent classification balance and statistical significance. The proposed system was also tested on an embedded system developed using an Arduino platform for real-time processing, and it achieved an accuracy of 90.83% and an MCC of 0.8167. The performance degradation is due to sensor noises and limited numerical precision in an embedded system. The proposed framework contributes to improving real-time heart rate monitoring by addressing computational constraints and enabling efficient deployment on embedded platforms.

Introduction

Heart rate variability (HRV), as temporal fluctuation among consecutive heartbeats, is a recognized index of autonomic activity and general physiological status. Endurance-trained individuals typically display low resting heart rate with higher HRV, reflecting a more efficient cardiac regulating mechanism1. Therefore, accurate tracking of heart rate and HRV plays a significant role in performance assessment, optimization of training guidelines, and compensation against adverse health effects2,3. Abnormalities of heart rate dynamics are associated with severe clinical consequences. An abnormally elevated or poorly recovered post-exercise heart rate has been associated with arrhythmias, high blood pressure, and cases of sudden cardiac mortality. For instance, Guasch and Mont have shown that prolonged endurance exercise induces atrial remodeling, thus elevating atrial fibrillation risk4. Similarly, research conducted by Cole et al.5 and Jouven et al.6 showed that slow heart rate recovery is predictive of enhanced cardiovascular mortality.

As Fig. 1 shows, one study describes that there is a mainly negative correlation such that average resting heart rate decreases with age, with younger ages having higher heart rates that decrease with age. However, data from two age groups, namely 10–20 years and 60–70 years, differ in this general pattern, and more studies are needed in order to understand specific physiological characteristics of both adolescence as well as older age7. This depiction not only highlights the decline in the mean heart rate with age but also emphasizes its heterogeneity, critical in the development of machine learning models that are to be used in anticipating cardiac conditions. Such variations, attributed to reduced autonomic nervous function, highlight the importance of closely following heart rate as a critical physiological index.

Besides explaining the inverse link among age and heart rate, Fig. 1 also makes clear why traditional threshold-based methods are insufficient. The erratic fluctuations observed during late adolescence and in older adults highlight the presence of non-linear dynamics that could not be explained by basic statistical rules. In this regard, this figure helps support that argument in favor of machine learning algorithms, as such algorithms also have the potential to adapt to such irregularities in terms of heart rate status predication. As shown in Fig. 1, although a general decreasing trend in resting heart rate with age is observed, significant variability exists in certain age groups. This nonlinearity highlights the limitation of simple threshold-based approaches and supports the use of machine learning models.

Machine learning offers good tools for this task. KNN and Bayesian classifiers have been used in disease diagnosis8–10, SVM in dermatology and epilepsy8,11, and neural networks in cardiovascular and behavior monitoring12–17. Genetic algorithms and Hopfield networks also aided optimization and pattern recognition work13,14,18. MLPs describe nonlinear relations, though at the expense of needing extensive data and tuning19–22, while Naïve Bayes is efficient computationally at small data sizes, though limited by its independence assumption23–25.

This research introduces an advanced hybrid framework that incorporates three classifiers Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Naïve Bayes (NB), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) to predict heart rate status during physical activity. As depicted in Fig. 2, physiological and environmental parameters (including temperature, humidity, speed, and duration) are gathered using wearable sensors and fed in parallel into all three models. The probability outputs of these classifiers will be further fused by means of a weighted voting ensemble approach to obtain a single final class decision, labeled as ‘normal’ or ‘warning’. Having a KNN classifier along with MLP and Naïve Bayes increases learning diversity, and the use of a weight fusion technique significantly increases the robustness of the proposed system.

This approach increases the accuracy of predictions and the robustness of the model’s operation while being fully functional for implementation in real-time on wearable devices with limited computational capabilities. Based on existing research in machine learning for medical predictions26,27, the new extended model provides a full-fledged solution for athletes’ tracking, medical support, and future hybrid-deep learning predictive frameworks in medical monitoring.

Figure 2 shows the overall architecture of the proposed framework for detection of heart rate status, which uses a two-level ensemble learning approach with adaptive weighted voting. The proposed framework uses a approach paradigm where the predictions of different base classifiers are treated as meta-features at the second level. Unlike traditional approach ensembles that use a meta-classifier, the final result is obtained through a data-driven weighted voting scheme in the proposed method. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the proposed framework combines the outputs of three base classifiers using a weighted voting mechanism. Unlike traditional stacking, no meta-classifier is used, and the final decision is obtained based on weighted contributions derived from validation performance.

Under this approach, multi-sensor physiological as well as environmental signals are subjected to a preprocessing phase before they can be fed jointly to a set of different base classifiers. Their results are further combined using a two-level learning architecture to provide a resultant decision with improved accuracy.

The first stage entails the gathering of the raw data acquired through the use of environmental and physiological sensors. The data acquired comprises the individual’s physiological particulars and the environmental setting within which the individual is found, playing a very important role in the process of heart rate status identification, especially in the case of physical activity. The data acquired through sensors often faces the problem of noise, variation, and size difference, hence the need for operations like noise removal and normalization before the stage of learning takes place.

After processing, the datasets are simultaneously processed by three diverse base classifiers, namely Naïve Bayes, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural network. The Naïve Bayes classifier uses probabilistic representation in terms of prior probability and likelihood function to predict whether an input example is classified as belonging to the Normal or Warning class of heart rate. The KNN classifier predicts the output class based on the K closest training samples in feature space and chooses the major class represented among them, hence being sensitive to data structures. On the other hand, the MLP network learns intricate and nonlinear relationships between physiological variables and heart rate classes using its hidden layers and nonlinear activation function. This is in contrast to the other two methods. The combination of diverse models improves generalization.

Training the base classifiers represents the initial phase of the proposed two-level meta-learning approach. The subsequent phase represents the primary phase of the proposed method. The predictions made by each base classifier on the validation examples are identified and considered meta-features at level two. The meta-features are accordingly formulated for each example by aggregating the output predictions of Naïve Bayes, KNN, and MLP classifiers. A meta-matrix summarizing level one decision information for all examples in the validation set can then be formulated by aggregating these meta-feature vectors. The proposed method differs from traditional ensemble learning in that it does not employ meta-classifier learning.

Rather, the weights representing the relative importance of each individual base classifier are computed using the ReliefF approach. In this respect, the outputs attributed to individual base classifiers are considered as features in the meta-space, and the ground truth class labels are used as the reference for assessment. The criterion used in the ReliefF approach involves analyzing the similarity of the classifier outputs for samples belonging to the same class (near hits) and their differing counterparts for distinct samples (near misses). The classifiers demonstrating high degrees of discriminability are assigned larger weights, whereas those demonstrating lower degrees of discriminability are given lower weights. This enables the acquisition of a final weight vector following normalization procedures. Finally, the system results are derived from the inference performed with an adaptive voting weight approach.

Each base classifier makes separate predictions for the test data regarding the class of the heart rate. The final system output for each class is determined as the sum of the voting weights for the classifiers that predict the class. The class with the highest score is chosen as the final system output. This approach automatically adjusts the influence of base classifiers with stronger validation performance compared to others and reduces the relative impact of the underperforming classifiers.

It is important to note that the selected feature set (temperature, humidity, speed, incline, and duration) primarily reflects external conditions and exercise intensity rather than direct physiological measurements of the cardiovascular system.

Therefore, the proposed model is designed to evaluate exercise-induced cardiovascular response and physiological stress, rather than to diagnose cardiac diseases. This design choice is motivated by the goal of developing a low-cost, real-time monitoring system suitable for embedded wearable devices, where acquiring clinical-grade signals such as ECG or HRV may not be feasible.

Novelty and contributions

Although a considerable amount of research work has been conducted in connection with heart rate monitoring and machine learning algorithms for physiological parameter analysis, some basic issues have not yet received adequate attention. A major part of existing literature trails a single classifier or is limited to simulated environments for offline analysis, thus failing to address the realities of real-time implementation in embedded systems and wearables26,27. Additionally, in a majority of such studies, attention remains more on accuracy in classification, with less focus on other parameters such as stability and feasibility related to computation resources.

The main originality of this work is related to the implementation and validation of a lightweight weighted ensemble learning strategy incorporating three different heterogeneous classifiers: Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Naïve Bayes (NB), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifiers for physical activity HR status. Unlike other stacked ensemble learning methods based on a heavy composite meta-classifier or computationally expensive fusion algorithms28–31 for class combination, our strategy utilizes a simple decision-level weighted voting technique where all classifier weights are calculated using validation set performance and analysis of feature discriminability using the ReliefF algorithm.

A major strength of this research is the simultaneous assessment of the proposed framework in both simulation and real-life settings. While many existing studies assess their proposed solutions solely in simulation-based environments21,26, in this research, an ensemble approach to image segmentation is developed and evaluated in a real-life embedded system setup based on an Arduino microcontroller operating in real-time settings. Test outcomes have proved that in real life, some elements, such as sensor noises, imprecision, and heterogeneity in performance, may affect classifications, which are not considered in simulations.

Moreover, this work offers a comparative analysis among individual models and the proposed ensemble model using a wide range of evaluation criteria, such as Accuracy, Precision, Sensitivity, F1-score, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). The consideration of MCC is especially important in classifying physiological signals because it provides a well-rounded assessment with a good level of accuracy despite class imbalance issues28. At last, with a collective integration of physiological and environmental factors, such as temperature, humidity, speed, angle, and duration of activity, into a single framework of ensemble learning, this paper provides a feasible model and architecture for wearable health tracking devices. The model fills a significant gap in using machine learning in practical medical solutions and can serve as a sound basis for developing future medical systems for intelligent physiological observation in real time21,26,32.

Materials and methods

Data employed in classification networks in this research are of 4000 samples, whose every sample holds five input features along with one output target. Classification techniques that have been implemented in this work include a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural network, a Naïve Bayes classifier, and a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm. All three models require training data to learn and use this data as a foundation to identify patterns within the features and accurately predict heart rate status. The data set adhered to conditions as defined below. Although the proposed architecture is inspired by stacking concepts, it does not employ a meta-classifier. Therefore, the method is more accurately described as a weighted voting ensemble with decision-level fusion.

According to information disseminated by the American Heart Association (AHA), the optimal heart rate zone for aerobic activities diminishes with advancing age. An examination of the mean values for this zone across various age categories reveals that individuals aged between 20 and 40 exhibit an average target heart rate ranging from approximately 120 to 140 beats per minute (bpm). This range indicates the typical cardiovascular system’s response to moderate to vigorous exercise within this demographic. The comparative chart illustrated in Fig. 3 further indicates that this range falls within the core of the ideal target heart rate zone for these age groups33. Consequently, in the context of this study, the target output data, which pertains to heart rate following physical exertion, is classified into two distinct levels: normal and warning.

Target heart rate by age (based on American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines).

In this study, the definition of “Normal” and “Warning” heart rate states is based on age-adjusted target heart rate zones recommended by the American Heart Association (AHA). Specifically, for individuals aged 20–40 years, a heart rate range of approximately 120–140 bpm during moderate to vigorous exercise is considered physiologically normal. Values exceeding this range, particularly above 140 bpm under similar conditions, are labeled as “Warning”, indicating potential cardiovascular stress or abnormal physiological response.

It should be noted that this classification is not intended as a clinical diagnosis but rather as a risk-indication framework for real-time monitoring during physical activity.

It should be noted that these thresholds are simplified approximations based on general AHA guidelines and may vary depending on individual fitness level, maximum heart rate, and physiological conditions. Figure 3 not only verifies the medical relevance of the selected categories but also illustrates the importance of age-adjusted thresholds in predictive modeling. Through linkage of the classification framework with established clinical guidelines, the figure ensures that the proposed structure is clear to practitioners in healthcare and easy to apply in real-world monitoring cases. The clinical grounding of this work facilitates greater credibility in subsequent machine learning predictions.

To provide a precise and repeatable measure of physiological and environmental parameters during exercise, a data acquisition system was designed and developed using an embedded system. The system was implemented on the treadmill and allowed measurements to be taken and recorded simultaneously for temperature, humidity, speed, angle of elevation, and duration of exercise. The data acquisition system developed was employed to obtain all data used in this study, as shown in Fig. 4.

The classification problem in this study is formulated as a binary task with two classes: “Normal” and “Warning”.

The labeling is based on heart rate ranges during physical activity. Specifically, heart rate values within the expected range for moderate exercise (approximately 120–140 bpm for the studied age group) are labeled as “Normal”. Values exceeding this range are labeled as “Warning”, indicating elevated physiological stress.

Initially, three heart rate zones were considered (low, moderate, high). However, for the purpose of simplifying the classification and improving model robustness, the upper two zones were merged into a single “Warning” class.

Data analysis

The selected input features (temperature, humidity, speed, incline, and exercise duration) are indirectly related to cardiovascular response during physical activity. Exercise intensity (represented by speed, incline, and duration) is a primary determinant of heart rate increase due to metabolic demand. Environmental factors such as temperature and humidity influence thermoregulation and cardiovascular strain, leading to variations in heart rate.

Although direct physiological signals such as ECG or heart rate variability (HRV) provide more precise cardiac information, the present study focuses on a lightweight and low-cost sensing framework suitable for embedded wearable systems.

In this topic, the data used in the current research will first be examined through statistical analysis and description. In order to understand the relations between the environment and physiological variables, and the variable of interest, which is the level of heart rate, a variety of analysis methods such as descriptive statistics, normality tests, variance analysis, and binary comparison analysis will be used.

First, the exercise duration itself is considered the reference variable, and the variation of the four environmental and physiological attributes of temperature, humidity, treadmill inclination, and speed with respect to training time is studied. This helps in the study of the dynamic processes in the given data and in the inspection of the transitions of the samples from the Normal to the Warning heart rate state through physical exercise. Another point worth highlighting in this analysis regards the fact that, despite the reference attribute in this part of the study, the statistical investigation of the attribute itself, given the input attributes, takes place in the following parts of the section.

These graphs in the figure are direct visual representations of the original dataset employed in this work, as opposed to derived presentations resulting from modeling or other forms of dataset processing. Descriptive analysis and density patterns

A descriptive analysis using the discriminant function is also performed to assess the nature of data distribution for environmental and physiologic variables across the two Heart Rate Intensity levels (Normal and Warning). The summary of four essential descriptive measures, namely Sample Size, Mean, Standard Deviation, and Standard Error, is explored in Table 2. From this, it is noticeable that most variables’ means, such as speed, slope, temperature, and duration, follow an increasing trend for higher Heart Rate Intensity, signifying uniformity in most attributes, whereas humidity is more irregular in its distribution, which could influence classification as an attribute of uncertainty.

These observations highlight that while many variables provide clear patterns, some, like humidity, are less consistent. A hybrid modeling approach is therefore beneficial, leveraging highly separable features while accounting for variability in irregular attributes to enhance classification accuracy and robustness.

The two heart rate classes’ distributional characteristics and the extent of overlap were examined through the calculation of probability density functions for each variable (Fig. 6). The result shows that, to a larger extent, temperature, speed, and exercise duration serve to better distinguish the two classes, and that humidity and slope have a considerable overlap, potentially posing a challenge to models that strictly depend on linear assumptions. This irregular behavior is reinforced as a justification for an MLP-KNN-Naïve Bayes combined model, where MLP is efficient for handling irregular patterns, KNN can improve local separability of irregular feature areas, and Naïve Bayes can serve for efficient probabilistic reasoning.

A p-value of less than either of 0.05 or 0.01 implies violation of normality assumption in data. Multilayer perceptron classification (MLP)

Since MLP, KNN, and Naïve Bayes are well-established methods, only a brief description is provided here, focusing on their role within the proposed framework. The selection of MLP, KNN, and Naïve Bayes was motivated by their complementary characteristics. MLP is capable of modeling complex nonlinear relationships, KNN captures local data structures, and Naïve Bayes provides efficient probabilistic inference. This diversity improves the robustness of the ensemble model. Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural network, being a canonical model in the field of deep learning, is used in this work to predict the status of heart rate. The architecture consists of two fully connected hidden layers with 10 and 5 neurons, respectively, along with an input layer and an output layer, all interconnected. The model’s training was performed using the error backpropagation algorithm in combination with the gradient descent optimization method, such that the network weights are continually updated in order to reduce error in prediction and improve model accuracy. Naïve Bayes classification

In the present investigation, the Naive Bayes classification algorithm was employed as a fundamental technique in statistical learning to forecast heart rate status. Utilizing Bayes’ theorem and presuming conditional independence among features, this model determines the likelihood of each sample being categorized into the target classes and subsequently predicts the class exhibiting the highest posterior probability as its outcome. To develop the model, initial frequency tables corresponding to features across various classes were constructed, followed by the establishment of the final model through probability estimation. The foundation of this methodology is encapsulated by Bayes’ theorem, which can be articulated through the following relation:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left( {C_{k} {|}x} \right) = \frac{{P\left( {C_{k} } \right) \cdot P\left( {x{|}C_{k} } \right)}}{P\left( x \right)}$$\end{document}

In this regard, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{k}$$\end{document} is one of the possible classes and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x = \left( {x_{1} ,x_{2} , \ldots ,x_{n} } \right)$$\end{document} referred to as the vector of features. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left( {C_{k} } \right)$$\end{document} is used to refer to the prior probability in terms of occurrence of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{k}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left( {x{|}C_{k} } \right)$$\end{document} shows the possibility of observing the features in case they are categorized under \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{k}$$\end{document}, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left( x \right)$$\end{document} shows the total possibility of seeing the features, which is omitted in terms of final decision-making because it remains constant in all cases. The conditional independence of features in the naive Bayes model is seen to mean that features are treated as being unrelated under the condition of knowing about the class. The total possibility of the features, therefore, is computed by multiplying the probabilities of each feature individually:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left( {x{|}C_{k} } \right) = \mathop \prod \limits_{i = 1}^{n} P\left( {x_{i} {|}C_{k} } \right)$$\end{document}

Thus, the posterior probability in simplified form will be the following:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left( {C_{k} } \right)\cdot \mathop \prod \limits_{i = 1}^{n} P\left( {x_{i} {|}C_{k} } \right) \propto P\left( {C_{k} {|}x} \right)$$\end{document}

The classifier then chooses the class with the highest posterior probability as the final output.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{C} = \arg \mathop {\max }\limits_{{C_{k} }} \left[ {P\left( {C_{k} } \right)\cdot \mathop \prod \limits_{i = 1}^{n} P\left( {x_{i} {|}C_{k} } \right)} \right]$$\end{document}

Frequency counting has been applied to pull probabilistic values from the training dataset. Prior probability in each class is represented by the ratio of the samples in the class to the overall number of samples:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left( {C_{k} } \right) = \frac{{Count\left( {C_{k} } \right)}}{{\mathrm{N}}}$$\end{document}

Conditional probability of features was then obtained by dividing the ratio of the number of occurrences of a certain feature value in a given class to the total number of instances in the class:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left( {x_{i} = v|C_{k} } \right) = \frac{{Count\left( {x_{i} = v,C_{k} } \right)}}{{Count\left( {C_{k} } \right)}}$$\end{document}

To effectively implement this model, a data structure in the form of a 3D frequency table [feature] [class] [feature value] was developed to facilitate easy accessibility and accurate probability computation. K-Nearest neighbors (KNN) classification

The present research uses the K-Nearest Neighbors algorithm as an instance-based learning algorithm for making predictions on heart rate status. The algorithm works on the principle that instances with similar feature set characteristics have a greater tendency to belong to a common category. To classify an instance, the algorithm calculates the K nearest instances with similar feature sets based on an appropriate distance measure, and then assigns it to a category with which the greatest number of nearest instances are associated. Since there are no parametric learning procedures involved, the classification task can be achieved solely based on a comparison with preexisting instances. Because all input features are continuous variables, the Euclidean distance measure is considered.

The decision rule for KNN classification can be written as follows: Given some new input \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x = \left( {x1,x2, \ldots ,xn} \right)$$\end{document}, let NK​(x) be the set of its K nearest neighbors. The predicted class label will then be determined via majority voting as follows:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{C} = arg \mathop {{\mathrm{max}}}\limits_{{C_{k} }} \mathop \sum \limits_{xi \in NK\left( x \right)}^{ } {\mathbb{I}}\left( {{\text{yi }} = { }C_{k} } \right)$$\end{document}

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbb{I}}$$\end{document}(·) be the indicator function, taking on 1 if the nearest sample occurs in category \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{k}$$\end{document} and 0 otherwise. The value of the number of nearest samples, K, was set to 3 in this research. It should be noted that a very small value of K can sometimes make the model very vulnerable to noise, while large numbers have an over smoothing effect on the classification boundary. From experience, it was ascertained that K = 3 offered optimal performance on the given data.

To effectively evaluate different models, a structure dividing 70% of the data for the modeling process, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing purposes was adopted. The result obtained from the validation process was incorporated into an ensemble method, making KNN a complementary model within an MLP and Naïve Bayes classification system. Tests conducted on the model showed that it added value to the collective classification process.

Ensemble learning with a stacking-based architecture using weighted voting

A strong paradigm within machine learning is that of ensemble learning wherein multiple base models are blended together to form a predictive model with better accuracy, stability, and generalizability. The basic motivation for ensemble learning models will be that different models will focus on learning different aspects of a given set of data, and these models, when pooled together, will be able to overcome some weaknesses associated with a single model. According to Dietterich, ensembles will be very useful in either reducing the variance, bias, or generalization error based on a strategy adopted. Among various methods associated with ensembles, there are three main methods within which bagging decreases the variance using bootstrapping samples and boosting decreases bias based on an iterative technique involving incorrectly labeled instances with stacking involving combining forecasts generated from multiple base models with a multi-level hierarchical structure involving multiple models33–36.

Within the scope of this research, a stacking approach with weights and a lightweight stacking algorithm were used. During stage one, three diverse base classifiers—K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and Naïve Bayes (NB)—were trained on 70% of the data. Next, predictions from these three classifiers were made on the validation set (15%), and a level-one output matrix similar to the preliminary stage of traditional stacking methods was obtained. Rather than learning a level-two meta-classifier, the ReliefF method on the predictions made on the validation set provided a discriminative weight for each base classifier. ReliefF ranks importance on feature values (output values within this research) based on their descriptive capability for separating classes based on analyses involving near-hit and near-miss neighbors. The weights issued were normalized and were used as a foundation for defining a decision rule. During the final stage, the weighted voting ensemble method was used on the predictions made by the three classifiers on the test data (15%). A weighted sum of votes from base classifiers for each class was calculated, with votes being multiplied by ReliefF weights. The class with the maximum weighted sum obtained the highest votes. It should be noted that this configuration helps reduce the effect of weaker classifiers and highlights the expertise of better ones. As a result, it leads to better classification accuracy and sensitivity compared to single base classifiers. Based on its architecture, it can be formally said that the proposed technique belongs to the family of “Weighted Voting–based Stacking Ensembles”.

After the training phase, the weights and biases obtained from the MLP were used in a real-time environment based on the implementation of these weights and biases by utilizing the Neurona library within the IDE of the Arduino platform. Classification accuracy on the tested dataset proved excellent, with a Correct Classification Rate of 96.67%, establishing the effectiveness of such a configuration in an MLP for classifying HR status.

Practical implementation of a Naïve Bayes classifier

As per the experimental protocol, the dataset was split into 70% for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing purposes. After completing the training process, the Naïve Bayes classifier was tested using the test dataset. The obtained results show the effectiveness of the NB classifier with simple probabilistic models in classifying the status of the heart rate, achieving a high level of accuracy and reliability with a CCR of 95%. Results of the ensemble consensus structure based on KNN, MLP, and Naïve Bayes models

This work will analyze and evaluate the performance of an ensemble consensus framework based on a combination of three different machine learning models using K-Nearest Neighbors, Multilayer Perceptron, and Naïve Bayes algorithms in a simulated setting in addition to a real-world setting implementation. Unlike other models based on a standalone classifier using a single learning algorithm, this consensus framework will make use of a stacking algorithm based on weights to blend decisions drawn from different models.

The ensemble architecture is based on a two-step hierarchical processing system. During stage one, the three individual classifiers are all trained independently on 70% of available information, with a subsequent 15% used for validation. In this case, it is important to include validation sets because they are critical in determining a level of confidence in any given model, given that accuracy within the validation set is considered a leading factor in setting weights. Additionally, in stage one, the ReliefF algorithm is used to assess the discriminating capability of each model’s output.

In stage two, the ultimate weight for each base classifier is calculated as a normalized linear combination of the two aforementioned criteria above (Validation Accuracy & ReliefF weights). The weights control how each model affects the ultimate output. To make predictions, each classifier computes an independent output for an input instance. For each class label, an ensemble output is calculated based on a weighted combination of the classifier outputs assigned to each class. The class with the highest ensemble output is chosen as the ultimate output, which incorporates a weighted voting system with increased flexibility and accuracy over majority voting rules.

Discussion and limitations

The results should be interpreted in the context of exercise monitoring rather than clinical diagnosis. The model effectively captures the relationship between physical activity conditions and heart rate response, making it suitable for real-time risk indication during exercise. This study demonstrates that the weighted ensemble approach based on MLP, Naïve Bayes, and KNN classifiers is a good and reliable way for classifying the status of heart rate in real time. The performance of this ensemble is better than each individual classifier in both simulation and real-world environments, which demonstrates that individual strengths of the classifiers complement each other nicely. Here, MLP classifies complicated nonlinear relationships, Naïve Bayes classifies quickly with less computation, and KNN assists in identifying local relationships in the observed data.

A small margin of error in simulation performance relative to embedded hardware performance can be attributed to sensor noise, a loss of numerical precision, and a hardware margin for embedded systems. However, the ensemble performed with stable decisions and good performance. To evaluate the robustness of the embedded system under realistic conditions, additional analysis was conducted considering both physiological and environmental variability.

Physiological variability was simulated by introducing variations in resting heart rate (± 10 bpm), age-dependent response differences, and inter-individual fitness levels. Environmental variability included fluctuations in temperature (18–32 °C) and humidity (30–80%), as well as sensor noise and motion artifacts.

The results indicate that the model maintains stable performance under moderate variability, with accuracy decreasing slightly from 91.8 to 89.9%. Sensitivity remained relatively high (from 93.6 to 91.2%), demonstrating robustness in detecting warning conditions.

However, specificity showed a larger reduction (from 89.7 to 86.5%), primarily due to increased false positives caused by environmental noise and motion artifacts.

These findings suggest that while the embedded system is robust to moderate variability, further optimization (e.g., adaptive filtering and personalization) is required for deployment in highly dynamic real-world conditions. In real-world health monitoring systems, variability due to user behavior, sensor placement, and environmental conditions is inevitable; therefore, designing models that maintain high sensitivity under such conditions is a key requirement.

The variability ranges were selected based on commonly reported physiological and environmental conditions in exercise studies; however, validation using real-world uncontrolled environments remains part of future work.

Some limitations exist in this study. Firstly, Gaussian Naïve Bayes considers normality in the input variables. However, in this case, where variables include environmental temperature, humidity, surface slope, speed, and activity duration, obvious non-normality can be observed. Some common transformations in those variables such as log, square root, Box-Cox, and Yeo-Johnson did not completely transform them into a normal distribution. Although in most cases, where a degree of non-normality is present, Gaussian Naïve Bayes can be considered a robust algorithm, in this study, a weighted voting ensemble assisted in overcoming this problem.

Conclusion

Although the proposed model demonstrates promising performance in controlled experimental settings, it has not yet been validated across diverse populations or real-world healthcare environments. Therefore, the current system should be considered as a proof-of-concept or an engineering prototype for real-time cardiovascular monitoring rather than a clinically validated diagnostic tool. Future work will focus on large-scale validation across different age groups, fitness levels, and clinical conditions to assess generalizability and clinical reliability. The lack of clinical validation across heterogeneous populations remains a key limitation of this study.

The proposed model should be considered as an engineering prototype for real-time heart rate monitoring during physical activity.

While the results are promising, the absence of clinical-grade signals such as ECG or HRV limits its applicability as a diagnostic tool. Therefore, further validation in clinical environments is required.

Furthermore, the feasibility of the proposed ensemble framework for implementation on an embedded system using an Arduino platform has justified its applicability in real-time processing. Although affected by sensor noise and resource constraints, the implementation achieved a satisfactory performance with an accuracy level above 90% in the experimental setup, thereby establishing its effectiveness for application in real-world situations. Apart from this, a combination of low-cost sensors, embedded systems, and an efficient ensemble learning approach provides a heterogeneous strategy with immense scalability and portability for physiological sensing in sports and healthcare domains. Future research can focus on scaling up the approach using larger datasets, including more physiological signals, and improving adaptive learning elements for enhanced applicability in health monitoring systems.
