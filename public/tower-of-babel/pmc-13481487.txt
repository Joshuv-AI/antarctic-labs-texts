Making Sense of Implementation Science Planning Tools: Addressing Implementation Challenges in Substance Use Disorder and Mental Health Treatment
Authors: Mei Elansary, Divya A. Shankar, Katherine Rizzolo, Cristina Gago, Rebecca K. Rudel, Shana A.B. Burrowes, Erika G. Cordova-Ramos, Nicholas A. Bosch, Kayla C. Jones, Kelli Finn, Mari-Lynn Drainoni, Kathryn L. Fantasia, Kirsten Austad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Implementation planning tools provide structured approaches for integrating evidence-based interventions into real-world settings, helping navigate the complexities of adopting, adapting, and sustaining interventions. We examine four prominent implementation planning tools: (1) implementation mapping, a theory-driven, participatory approach to tailor implementation strategies to contexts through participant engagement and barrier assessment; (2) implementation blueprint, a plan documenting goals, strategies, and performance measures; (3) causal pathway diagrams, which illustrate the mechanisms through which implementation strategies operate; and (4) implementation research logic model, which links key implementation components—determinants, strategies, mechanisms, and outcomes across planning, execution, and evaluation stages. Each tool serves distinct purposes that align with different implementation phases and needs. Implementation mapping provides a methodical approach for implementation strategy design, generating approaches through theory-informed, iterative processes that heavily involve participants. Implementation blueprint translates plans into actions to drive day-to-day implementation—serving as a plan with timelines and accountability measures. Causal pathway diagrams illuminate the mechanistic understanding of how and why implementation strategies work, making them valuable for predicting implementation success; and the implementation research logic model excels at organizing concepts to promote theoretical coherence. These tools differ primarily in focus: implementation mapping in creating context-specific strategies, implementation blueprint in orchestrating action, implementation research logic model in organizing theoretical concepts, and causal pathway diagrams in proposing theories on how implementation strategies function. The trade-offs typically involve depth versus simplicity and theory-oriented versus practice-oriented approaches. By understanding these distinctions, implementation teams can select the most appropriate tool for their specific needs and contexts or combine implementation planning tools to promote successful and sustainable implementation of evidence-based interventions.

Despite the existence of numerous evidence-based interventions for the prevention and treatment of substance use disorders, these interventions often fail to achieve population-level impact due to implementation challenges (Institute of Medicine (US) Committee on Quality of Health Care in America, 2001; Shelton et al., 2018). Globally, an estimated 64 million people were living with a substance use disorder in 2023 with critically large treatment gaps across all regions, including high-income contexts (United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, 2025). For example, among the 48.5 million people in the United States with a substance use disorder in 2023, only 4.5% received any form of treatment (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024), and many of those who did encountered services misaligned with evidence-based recommendations (Jones et al., 2023; Kazdin & Blase, 2011). Implementation science has emerged to address the evidence-practice gap by studying how to promote the adoption, integration, and sustainment of evidence-based interventions in real-world settings. Central to the field is the recognition that implementation is an active, deliberate process that must be planned, managed, and adapted to local contexts. To support this work, implementation science process models, e.g., Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS), provide conceptual guidance by outlining the key phases of implementation efforts (Nilsen, 2015; Meyers et al., 2012; Aarons et al., 2011). While these models are useful for describing what should occur across the implementation continuum, they lack guidance on how to operationalize each phase in practice. This gap is particularly problematic in substance use disorder treatment settings, where stigma, resource constraints and system fragmentation are common, and must be considered for effective evidence-based intervention implementation (Priester et al., 2015; Bouchery, 2018; Adeniran et al., 2023; Mojtabai et al., 2014). The current opioid epidemic, which has resulted in over 100,000 overdose deaths annually in recent years in the United States (National Center for Health Statistics, 2025), exemplifies the need to ensure that evidence-based interventions reach at risk populations effectively.

Bridging the gap between conceptual guidance and practical application is essential to scale evidence-based interventions in substance use disorder treatment settings. A range of practical, structured approaches have emerged to help implementers systematically plan, coordinate, and evaluate implementation activities. Because no standardized terminology currently exists to describe these approaches, we propose the term Implementation Planning Tools (IPTs) to refer to resources that provide structured guidance across the continuum of implementation phases —from planning to execution and evaluation. Unlike conceptual frameworks that identify determinants or define stages of implementation, IPTs offer actionable step-by-step methods for designing, tailoring, and assessing implementation strategies in real-world settings. Comprehensive IPTs span at least two phases of the implementation process and help operationalize implementation science principles in ways that improve feasibility, enhance fidelity, and support the scalability of evidence-based interventions.

During the planning phase, IPTs facilitate the identification and prioritization of context-specific determinants, the selection of strategies grounded in theory and evidence, the definition of implementation outcomes, and the specification of mechanisms of change. During execution, IPTs support logistical planning—such as establishing timelines—and enable ongoing monitoring of progress. In the evaluation phase, they also standardize reporting and documentation with the goal of improving transparency, reproducibility, and cross-site learning. IPTs are practical extensions of process frameworks, which describe the sequence of stages required to move research evidence into practice (Nilsen, 2015). By functioning as structured implementation guides, IPTs translate abstract concepts into actionable steps through concrete tools and decision supports. They clarify the logical connections between implementation constructs and provide a roadmap for operationalizing process frameworks in real-world settings.

Despite their growing use, implementation science literature lacks systematic guidance for selecting the most appropriate IPT for specific implementation needs and contexts. This gap leaves teams without clear criteria for choosing among available tools, potentially limiting their ability to maximize successful and sustainable implementation of evidence-based interventions. To address this gap, we examine four IPTs: Implementation Mapping, the Implementation Blueprint, Causal Pathway Diagrams, and the Implementation Research Logic Model. We did not conduct a systematic review of all available IPTs, but selected these four to represent both established and emerging approaches in the implementation science literature. Our selection criteria prioritized IPTs that offer distinct guidance for implementation planning and are either widely adopted or show strong potential for advancing the field. Implementation mapping, implementation blueprinting, and the implementation research logic model are well-established tools frequently applied in recent implementation studies (Hyzak et al., 2023; Ike et al., 2024; Lewis et al., 2018; Lovero et al., 2022; Merle et al., 2024; National Institutes of Health, 2023; Pérez Jolles et al., 2022; Valentine et al., 2022) with PubMed citations of their sentinel publications reflecting widespread use: 814 for implementation mapping (Fernandez et al., 2019), 4,801 for implementation blueprinting (Powell et al., 2015), and 723 citations for IRLM (Smith et al., 2020). While causal pathway diagrams are less cited (Lewis et al. 2018a; 330 citations), this approach is newer and rapidly garnering interest (Causal Pathway Diagrams Toolkit for Selecting, Designing, Tailoring, and Optimizing Implementation Strategies, 2023). For each tool, we provide an overview of its purpose, trace its historical development, summarize its core strengths (Table 1), and review applications of the tool in implementation science literature, highlighting examples from substance use disorder and mental health research when available. We then compare the four IPTs, with particular emphasis on their relevance to addiction and mental health contexts. Finally, we provide example applications of each IPT. Our goal is to define the emerging concept of IPTs and provide an accessible synthesis of tools most relevant to substance use and mental health services.

Implementation Planning Tools

Implementation Mapping

Implementation Mapping is a structured, theory-based approach for prospectively designing implementation strategies that fit an intervention and the context in which that intervention will be used. Implementation mapping comprises five steps: (1) conduct a needs assessment and identify adopters and implementers, (2) specify relevant implementation outcomes and factors influencing them, (3) apply theory-informed approaches to link determinants with appropriate implementation strategies, (4) create implementation plans, tools, and supporting materials, and (5) assess implementation outcomes (Fernandez et al., 2019).

Origin and Development

Implementation mapping arose from intervention mapping, a well-established six-step protocol developed in the 1990 s to guide the systematic design of theory-informed, evidence-based multi-level health interventions (Bartholomew Eldredge et al., 1998). Intervention mapping’s fifth and sixth steps—specifying the planning for adoption and implementation, and planning for evaluation—aligned with what would become the field of implementation science, but lacked the methodology needed for systematic implementation strategy selection. Recognizing this gap and the limited use of theory in most implementation studies, Fernandez et al. introduced “Implementation Mapping” in a (Fernandez, et al., 2019) methodological paper that expanded and adapted implementation mapping’s fifth step to create a dedicated framework for implementation science. Fernandez (2019) reconceptualized this approach as “Implementation Science + Intervention Mapping = Implementation Mapping,” (p. 3) with the goal of advancing theory-based strategy design and selection that addresses specific contextual needs.

Applications in Implementation Science

In one of the earliest applications of implementation mapping in a mental health context, Powell and colleagues (2020) integrated multiple theoretical approaches—including an implementation science process model, an outcomes framework, and behavioral theory—to help community mental health organizations select and tailor implementation strategies. Likely due to the close timing of their work with the publication of Fernandez et al. (2019) that formally defined implementation mapping, they primarily refer to their approach as “intervention mapping” and combined the first two implementation mapping steps into a single phase. The process was facilitated by coaches with expertise in the fundamentals of implementation mapping. Coaches guided collaborators from the community mental health organizations in selecting implementation strategies that would address identified barriers and facilitators and in refining and specifying them using prompts and behavior change taxonomies.

Following Powell et al. (2020), two other notable mental health studies applied implementation mapping to design tailored strategies for implementing evidence-based interventions —integrating adolescent depression services in primary care setting in Mozambique and screening children for Adverse Childhood Experiences in a system of health centers in the United States (Lovero et al., 2022; Pérez Jolles et al., 2022). These studies identified and engaged potential adopters, implementers, and maintainers but differed in operationalizing determinant selection. Both studies embedded the EPIS framework within implementation mapping, though Lovero et al. (2022) used EPIS only for strategy temporality while Pérez Jolles et al. (2022) leveraged EPIS as the foundation for all implementation mapping steps. Neither Lovero et al. (2022) nor Pérez Jolles et al. (2022), however, describe how implementation strategy selection was informed by behavior change theory, which Fernandez et al. (2019) originally emphasized as important to the implementation mapping process. Once implementation strategies were selected, Lovero et al. (2022) uniquely leveraged a simplified version of another IPT, the implementation research logic model, to synthesize and guide discussion with workshop participants (Smith et al., 2020).

Summary

Implementation mapping is a systematic method for identifying and tailoring strategies that align with specific contextual needs and resources. Implementation mapping encourages the use of theory to guide the selection of methods and to strengthen the connection between implementation strategies and their underlying mechanisms to achieve the desired outcome. Implementation mapping is particularly useful when introducing, adapting, or scaling programs in complex or diverse settings where participant engagement and context-specific strategies are essential. These applications (Powell et al., 2020; Lovero et al., 2022; Pérez Jolles et al., 2022) highlight implementation mapping’s strength in balancing structure with flexibility while maintaining a strong theoretical grounding.

Implementation Blueprint

An Implementation Blueprint is a detailed logistical plan that summarizes the goals and strategies of an implementation effort to guide its execution over time (Powell et al., 2015). According to the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC), an implementation blueprint includes: (1) aim/purpose of implementation, (2) scope of change (organizational units affected), (3) timeframe and milestones, and (4) appropriate performance/progress measures (Powell et al., 2015). Implementation blueprints are typically created collaboratively between researchers and implementers (e.g., community partners), used to monitor progress, and iteratively revised as implementation evolves.

Origin and Development

The concept of an implementation blueprint emerged from early efforts to bring evidence-based mental health treatments into real-world settings, before the formal development of implementation science as a distinct field. Drawing on theories of organizational change and diffusion of innovation, researchers anticipated the challenges of implementing complex, phased, multi-component, multi-level interventions that are context sensitive. This theory-informed approach emerged from early efforts to plan and test interventions, such as the Availability, Responsiveness and Continuity initiative, which aimed to improve evidence-based intervention implementation for children’s mental health by addressing organizational and community-level barriers (Glisson & Schoenwald, 2005). The Availability, Responsiveness and Continuity initiative integrated 12 organizational intervention components bringing together researchers, community participants, and mental health agency staff to systematically plan implementation activities.

While these foundational concepts existed in practice, Powell et al. (2012) first coined the term “Implementation Blueprint” in a review article summarizing implementation strategies, initially defining it as a plan that “integrates multiple strategies from multiple levels using multiple theories or explicit theoretical frameworks (p. 22).” This emphasis on theoretical frameworks in creating an implementation blueprint reflected the implementation science field’s recognition that implementation efforts required robust conceptual foundations. However, when the more widely adopted definition emerged in Powell et al.’s (2015) article defining the ERIC strategies, the explicit requirement for theoretical foundations was not retained. The 2015 ERIC definition emphasized implementation blueprint’s practical function, describing it as a tool that includes the aim/purpose of implementation, scope of change, timeframe and milestones, and performance measures. Importantly, this definition emphasized implementation blueprint’s dynamic nature, instructing users to “use and update this plan to guide implementation effort over time,” reflecting the iterative and adaptive nature of implementation processes (Powell et al., 2015, p. 8).

Applications in Implementation Science

Lewis and colleagues (2018b) published the first study focusing on implementation blueprint development steps, illustrating their methodology through implementing cognitive behavioral therapy in a youth residential center. Their description—“engage in a structured, stakeholder-driven approach to prospectively build a tailored plan for implementation” (p. 2)—aligned closely with the Powell et al., 2015 definition. Lewis et al. (2018b) offered a theory-driven approach that expanded beyond creating the blueprint document itself, combining implementation blueprint development with four preparatory steps to identify and prioritize implementation barriers and match them to implementation strategies. This process integrated systematic planning activities with blueprint creation, encompassing the entire process from initial assessment through strategy selection, to team organization and execution.

Two additional examples (Hyzak et al., 2023; Valentine et al., 2022) operationalized implementation blueprints as collaborative, systematic planning approaches that generate dynamic documents. These implementation blueprints synthesize key elements including components, scope, outcomes, and logistical details. Consistent with Lewis et al.’s approach, both implementation efforts paired implementation blueprint with additional methods to identify barriers and select implementation strategies while providing details about how implementation blueprints were used and refined across implementation phases. Whereas Lewis and Hyzak focused on organizational-level participant engagement, Valentine et al. (2022) uniquely engaged intervention recipients in implementation blueprint creation.

Summary

Implementation blueprints focus on creating logistical plans for implementation efforts that may include numerous components guided by multiple theories. Implementation blueprints do not prescribe methods for identifying determinants or selecting implementation strategies, though others have demonstrated how to combine it with other approaches that do (Lewis et al., 2018b). Although implementation blueprints are categorized as implementation strategies within existing taxonomies (Powell et al., 2012, 2015), they function at a higher level than discrete strategies. Specifically, implementation blueprints serve as structured planning tools that organize, specify, and operationalize other implementation strategies, detailing what strategies will be used, by whom, and at what time points. Implementation blueprints are most useful during execution phases given their focus on detailed logistics designed for implementers to achieve successful outcomes.

Causal Pathway Diagrams

Causal Pathway Diagrams are visualization tools illustrating theorized mechanisms by which implementation strategies work. Causal pathway diagrams are often generated by expert groups, drawn from existing theories, and used to improve implementation strategy design and generate evidence about which strategies work in different settings (Lewis et al., 2022; Weiner et al., 2024). Components include: (1) a “stem” describing the mechanisms through which a strategy addresses determinants and yields an outcome; and (2) “leaves” representing moderators (factors that influence the strength or direction of the relationship between an implementation strategy and its outcome) and preconditions (factors that must be in place for the implementation strategy to succeed) influencing pathway success.

Origin and Development

The development of causal pathway diagrams emerged from a critical gap in implementation science: while researchers frequently employed numerous implementation strategies, there was limited understanding of how these strategies influenced the adoption of evidence-based interventions (Lewis et al. 2018a). To address this challenge, Lewis et al. (2018a) introduced causal pathway diagrams as a systematic approach for mapping the mechanisms through which implementation strategies affect desired outcomes.

The conceptual foundation for causal pathway diagrams draws from two key sources: Kazdin’s research on therapeutic change mechanisms (2008) and Agile Science methodology (Hekler et al., 2016), which provides a framework for developing and studying behavioral interventions (Lewis et al. 2018a). Causal pathway diagrams function by specifying precise relationships between mediators, moderators, preconditions, and outcomes within implementation strategy action. Causal pathway diagrams are informed by theories beyond implementation science—including social, cognitive, and organizational theories—serving varying purposes from improving implementation efforts to developing “micro-theories” of strategy mechanism (Klasnja et al., 2024).

Lewis et al. (2018a) first demonstrated the practical application of causal pathway diagrams through a case study involving a community mental health center implementing patient-reported questionnaires. Their methodology involved a systematic four-step process: (1) specifying the implementation strategies to be used, (2) generating clear linkages between strategies and their proposed mechanisms, (3) identifying both distal and proximal outcomes, and (4) articulating the effect modifiers that might influence these relationships.

Applications in Implementation Science

The original developers of causal pathway diagrams received federal funding to generate plausible theories about how, when, and under what conditions an implementation strategy functions (Lewis et al., 2022). They began by selecting 30 strategies from the ERIC compilation and followed a structured five-step process to develop causal pathway diagrams for each. This process included literature reviews, a multi-day Deep Dive meeting, and iterative group discussions. Draft causal pathway diagrams were then reviewed by expert panels composed of implementation researchers and practitioners, who rated the plausibility of each component and provided suggestions for refinement. The result was a set of theory-informed diagrams that clarified the causal logic underlying each strategy and supported future mechanism-focused implementation research. Weiner et al. (2024) detail this process using three example strategies: opinion leadership, educational outreach visits, and innovation champions.

Causal pathway diagrams have not yet been applied to substance use disorder treatment and mental health in the literature. Other examples include using causal pathway diagrams to choose quality improvement strategies addressing colorectal cancer screening disparities (Ike et al., 2024) and developing causal pathway diagrams for chatbots addressing racial inequities in breast cancer screening (Marcotte et al., 2024). In 2024, the creators of causal pathway diagrams further detailed their multifaceted application, illustrating how to account for complexity by layering interdependent strategies and integrated causal pathway diagrams with other tools, such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)-ERIC Implementation Strategy Matching Tool, to link strategies to determinants (Klasnja et al., 2024; Waltz et al., 2019).

Summary

Causal pathway diagrams improve understanding of mechanistic pathways through which implementation strategies work and visually specify factors influencing strategy success or failure in a given context. Creating causal pathway diagrams is relevant both for logistical planning of implementation efforts and for providing an overview of how strategies function across settings and conditions. Strengths include their ability to identify moderators and preconditions, explain contextual variation in strategy effectiveness, and focus on proximal outcomes, enabling rapid learning cycles.

Implementation Research Logic Model

The implementation Research Logic Model is a structured framework designed to enhance the planning, execution, reporting, and synthesis of implementation research. By integrating multiple implementation science models into a single logic model, the implementation research logic model provides a clear, visual framework to map relationships between core implementation components—determinants, strategies, mechanisms, and outcomes—and real-world contexts. This flexibility makes it applicable across various types and stages of implementation research.

Origin and Development

Smith et al. (2020) developed the implementation research logic model to address implementation science’s identified need for greater transparency and reproducibility in research (Slaughter et al., 2015). The developers sought to create a practical tool to guide theory-informed strategy applications while enhancing rigor. The implementation research logic model’s underlying theory proposes that implementation strategies are selected based on context-specific determinants and operate through mechanisms that influence either context or behaviors, producing implementation outcomes that serve as proximal effects to clinical outcomes. This approach integrates both Theory of Change and Theory of Action into a single explanatory model that can be adapted to include the evidence-based intervention’s own Theory of Action. The framework synthesizes multiple established models, including the CFIR, ERIC strategies, and Proctor’s implementation outcomes framework (Smith et al., 2020).

The implementation research logic model’s utility was demonstrated when it served as the foundation for a training delivered to 65 planning projects under the national Ending the HIV Epidemic initiative (Fauci et al., 2019). A two-day summit in 2019 indicated strong participant engagement with 132 attendees representing 63 projects, with 100% of the 66 confirmed training attendees completing all implementation research logic model-related components (Smith et al., 2020; Proceedings from the 11th Annual Conference on the Science of Dissemination and Implementation, 2019).

Applications in Implementation Science

Since 2020, implementation research logic model has gained widespread adoption and is now required in funding opportunities by major agencies including National Institutes of Health, Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (Merle et al., 2024; National Institutes of Health, 2023). The framework has been applied across all stages of implementation research, from initial planning to outcome evaluation and reporting. While studies documenting the use of the implementation research logic model in substance use disorder treatment and mental health are currently limited, other applications span diverse contexts, such as planning community-based bacterial infection treatment in Ethiopia to reduce neonatal mortality (Tiruneh et al., 2022), where implementation research logic models helped establish linkages between known determinants, informed adaptive strategies, and facilitated participant engagement.

Summary

The implementation research logic model represents a flexible and pragmatic tool that enhances the clarity, rigor, and utility of implementation research. It provides structure for visualizing and planning implementation while illustrating multi-level and interdisciplinary approaches to examining factors that inform implementation, sustainment, and spread of evidence-based interventions. Key strengths include its integration of multiple theoretical frameworks into a single visual structure—making complex intervention processes more accessible to diverse audiences—and its comprehensive documentation of causal pathways between strategies and outcomes, thereby improving the potential for replication and knowledge synthesis.

Example Applications of IPTs to Substance Use Disorder Treatment

The fields of substance use disorder treatment and mental health face unique implementation challenges when deploying evidence-based intervention. These settings are often characterized by under-resourced and fragmented systems, where practitioners must navigate multifaceted barriers spanning individual, organizational, and systemic levels. The complexity increases when addressing health disparities and stigma, as effective implementation requires strategies that are equity-informed, participatory, and adapted to local contexts.

Recognizing these challenges, the National Institute on Drug Abuse’s (NIDA) 2022–2026 Strategic Plan emphasizes the critical role of implementation research informed by community input to improve the real-world deployment of evidence-based interventions (National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2025). The plan identifies key barriers that implementation science can address in substance use disorder treatment settings. To illustrate how IPTs can systematically tackle these barriers, we present four hypothetical cases below. Each case addresses one of the common implementation challenges highlighted in the NIDA Strategic Plan, demonstrating how different IPTs can be strategically applied to overcome specific obstacles in delivering evidence-based care to individuals impacted by substance use disorder. Cases focus on addressing treatment gaps for medication for opioid use disorder, an underused, yet effective, evidence-based intervention (Jones et al., 2023) and illustrate the need for different IPTs to address distinct implementation challenges. While we present only one IPT per implementation challenge for illustrative purposes, it should be noted that IPTs are highly complementary and that different IPTs may be applied to these challenges.

Implementation Challenge 1: Promote Coordination Across Fragmented Systems

Evidence-based interventions for substance use disorder, including medication for opioid use disorder treatment, are often delivered across siloed systems, including primary care, specialty addiction and psychiatry care, and criminal justice. This fragmentation creates significant barriers to care continuity, with individuals often falling through gaps between systems. The lack of standardized communication protocols, misaligned incentives, and varied organizational cultures further complicate coordination efforts (Buck, 2011; Friedmann et al., 2012). Implementation mapping (Fig. 1) could guide the selection and development of care coordination as a strategy to improve medication for opioid use disorder continuation among those transitioning from incarceration to community settings. Through its systematic, participatory approach, implementation mapping would engage key participants across systems—correctional health staff, community treatment providers, probation officers, and formerly incarcerated individuals—to identify determinants of successful care transitions. The implementation mapping process could reveal barriers such as lack of communication protocols between correctional and community providers, limited medication access during transition periods, and mistrust of healthcare systems among justice-involved populations. Based on these determinants, implementation mapping would guide the selection of implementation strategies such as developing standardized care transition protocols, establishing medication bridge programs, and training peer support specialists. If used as intended, implementation mapping would incorporate input from key participants, theory, and empirical evidence to support selection and design of implementation strategies. Taking the example of training peer specialists as an implementation strategy, implementation mapping offers a structured process for incorporating partner input, theory, and existing evidence to develop a tailored training approach with a mechanism of action. Implementation Challenge 2: Integrate Medication for Opioid Use Disorder treatment into Existing Care Structures

The integration of medication for opioid use disorder treatment into emergency department care represents a critical opportunity to expand care access. Emergency departments serve as a crucial healthcare access point for individuals with substance use disorders, who frequently seek emergency care despite limited engagement with specialty addiction services (Hawk et al., 2019). This integration challenge is compounded by providers’ concerns about scope of practice, time constraints, and workflow disruption, necessitating systematic approaches that address both clinical and operational considerations (D’Onofrio et al., 2017; Hawk et al., 2020).

An implementation blueprint (Fig. 2) could guide integration efforts of medication for opioid use disorder within emergency department settings (Saitz et al., 2014; Williams et al., 2018). Researchers would design an implementation blueprint to detail specific goals (e.g., medication for opioid use disorder initiation and linkage to ongoing care for eligible adult emergency department patients presenting with opioid use disorder), scope of change (emergency department staff and workflows), timeline (6-month implementation period with monthly milestones), and performance measures (proportion of eligible patients screened, medication for opioid use disorder initiation and referrals completed). The blueprint would specify roles and responsibilities, such as nurses conducting initial screening, physicians providing psychoeducation and prescribing, and social workers referring to outpatient care. Progress monitoring would track both implementation outcomes (staff adherence to protocols) and clinical outcomes (patient engagement with follow-up services).

Implementation Challenge 3: Scale Up Evidence-Based Practices

Expanding substance use evidence-based interventions from pilot implementations to broader deployment requires systematic approaches that maintain fidelity while adapting to diverse contexts (Chambers et al., 2013; Milat et al., 2011). Scale-up efforts often fail due to insufficient attention to the mechanisms through which implementation strategies work, leading to ineffective strategy selection or inappropriate application across varied contexts. Understanding not just whether strategies work, but how and under what conditions they work, is critical for successful scale-up.

Causal pathway diagrams (Fig. 3) could provide mechanistic insights when scaling up emergency department-initiated medication for opioid use disorder across multiple hospital systems. A causal pathway diagram would map how the use of a champion (the implementation strategy) operates through specific mechanisms, such as creating a positive climate for emergency department medication for opioid use disorder initiation, helping emergency department providers buy into medication for opioid use disorder implementation, and facilitating a feeling of greater collective efficacy among emergency department providers. The diagram would illustrate the pathway from the implementation strategy through mechanism activated to proximal outcomes (providers participating in medication for opioid use disorder prescription) and ultimately to intermediate and distal implementation outcomes (increased skill and use of medication for opioid use disorder prescriptions, implementation of medication for opioid use disorder, organizational policy for emergency department-initiated medication for opioid use disorder, and widespread uptake of emergency department initiated medication for opioid use disorder). The causal pathway diagram would also identify key moderators and preconditions that must be considered when the strategy is deployed to new contexts. This tool helps implementation teams predict which sites are likely to succeed with champion approaches versus those that might require more intensive external support, enabling strategic resource allocation during scale-up efforts.

Implementation Challenge 4: Address Multi-Level Barriers

Substance use disorder and mental health services often face barriers at multiple levels: patient (e.g., stigma and trauma), provider (e.g., limited training, high caseloads), organizational (e.g., resource constraints, misaligned workflows), and policy/system levels (e.g., regulations, funding). The complexity of these interconnected barriers requires implementation approaches that systematically identify, prioritize, and address determinants across multiple levels simultaneously.

An implementation research logic model (Fig. 4) could systematically organize the complex, multilevel approach needed to implement medication for opioid use disorders in rural primary care settings (Damschroder et al., 2009; McLellan & Woodworth, 2014). The implementation research logic model would map key determinants across multiple levels. The logic model would then link these determinants to specific implementation strategies—such as provider education and consultation support, clinical decision support tools, and community education campaigns. Researchers can use implementation research logic model to articulate mechanisms through which these strategies operate and connect these to both implementation outcomes (adoption of medication for opioid use disorder prescribing protocols) and clinical outcomes (increased patient access to treatment, reduced overdose deaths). This visual framework could facilitate communication among diverse rural healthcare participants and provides a clear roadmap for implementation.

Discussion

IPTs can be defined as resources used to guide implementation efforts across the continuum of planning, execution, and evaluation. This manuscript has two primary goals. First, we described four common IPTs—Implementation Mapping, Implementation Blueprint, Causal Pathway Diagrams, and implementation research logic model—examining their historical development, applications within implementation science, and notable features and unique strengths. Second, we demonstrated how these tools could address common implementation challenges encountered in the substance use disorder treatment and mental health fields. Building on this analysis, below we synthesize our findings to provide guidance on selecting the most appropriate IPTs for specific implementation projects and unique circumstances.

Considerations for Selecting an IPT

To choose an IPT, start by determining whether there is a need to select and/or tailor implementation strategies (Fig. 5). For strategy selection as well as design of implementation strategies, implementation mapping serves as an excellent foundation through comprehensive participant engagement and needs assessment. Implementation research logic model provides theory-informed guidance on implementation strategy selection with a coherent framework that encompasses planning, execution, and evaluation, which is particularly helpful for research contexts. If the primary focus is examining the mechanisms by which implementation strategies achieve success, causal pathway diagrams are a logical choice. When strategies have already been identified and the project is transitioning into execution, an implementation blueprint becomes a practical choice given its focus on execution logistics which IPTs such as causal pathway diagrams lack.

Assessing the team’s skills and available resources is essential, as IPTs vary in the extent to which they require engagement with theoretical foundations. Teams that include implementation scientists are well-positioned to undertake implementation mapping, which involves a detailed, theory-informed process and emphasizes structured stakeholder engagement. Teams familiar with logic models may find the implementation research logic model more accessible to apply. In contrast, practitioner-led teams without formal implementation science training may find implementation mapping challenging to execute without support. In such cases, involving an expert to guide the application of behavioral taxonomies and implementation theory—similar to the coaching approach used by Powell et al. (2020)—could help bridge this gap.

IPTs differ substantially in their engagement with theoretical foundations. The level of theoretical underpinnings desired may influence choice of IPT. In implementation mapping, integration of behavioral change theories is highly recommended and central to the methodology. Employing theory in an implementation blueprint is recommended for best results, but blueprints can operate in a ‘theory-light’ way if desired. Causal pathway diagrams encourage but don’t require theory use. A key feature of implementation research logic models is the integration of multiple theories, models, and frameworks. One must consider how key partners will be involved in implementation planning, as collaborative processes are often key to success. If there are plans to engage partners heavily in defining implementation strategies, implementation mapping provides a structured way to do that during strategy development. Implementation blueprint can also be created as collaborative processes with community partners. Implementation research logic model may be harder to use as a participatory tool and may be better for the core project team to create and then share with partners for feedback. Causal pathway diagrams visual nature may be well suited to engage participants in understanding how an implementation strategy will work. Projects driven by implementation research that require high rigor benefit from the implementation research logic model or implementation mapping. The implementation research logic model can simplify complexity by distilling the project to key logical components, while implementation mapping can handle complexity by systematically addressing each facet of implementation. An implementation blueprint will manage complexity by scheduling and delegating tasks with clear timelines. For projects that are small in scope with fewer logistical details, a full implementation mapping exercise might not be necessary. If the context prioritizes quick action or a pragmatic approach, a well-scoped implementation blueprint paired with another approach to select implementation strategies might be more immediately useful.

Ability to Combine IPTs

IPTs are highly complementary and often synergistic, allowing implementation teams to leverage the distinct strengths of each tool to address different phases or components of complex implementation efforts. For example, although implementation blueprints do not prescribe how to select implementation strategies, researchers have shown how they can be combined with more structured approaches to guide strategy selection in a systematic and theory-informed way. IPTs can also be used sequentially, aligned with the stages of an implementation timeline, or nested in one another; e.g., beginning with implementation mapping to identify contextual determinants and design tailored strategies, followed by the development of an implementation research logic model to map those strategies to relevant implementation frameworks and outcomes (Lovero et al., 2022). Alternatively, IPTs can be used in parallel to address different facets of an initiative simultaneously. For instance, a substance use disorder treatment program might use causal pathway diagrams to explore how peer support strategies influence outcomes, while using an implementation blueprint to coordinate implementation logistics across multiple sites.

IPTs can also inform each other through iterative refinement. Initial strategy selection through implementation mapping might reveal gaps in understanding mechanisms, prompting causal pathway diagram development. Different IPTs may serve participant-specific needs within the same project. Researchers may rely on the implementation research logic model for grant applications, while community partners focus on implementation blueprint development for practical execution, and both groups use causal pathway diagrams to build shared understanding.

Implications for Research and Practice

The practical utility of IPTs extends beyond their individual applications to their potential for standardizing implementation planning processes. In substance use and mental health fields, where implementation challenges are particularly complex due to stigma, fragmented systems, and multilevel barriers, IPTs offer structured approaches to improve both the rigor and reproducibility of implementation efforts.

However, several important limitations remain in the current evidence base. There is a lack of empirical data comparing structured implementation planning using IPTs to unstructured or ad hoc approaches. Additionally, IPTs have not been rigorously compared to one another in head-to-head trials, limiting the empirical basis for guiding tool selection. Future research should prioritize several key areas. First, comparative effectiveness studies across varied implementation contexts are needed to generate evidence-based guidance on IPT selection. Second, research examining how IPTs can be adapted for specific populations or service settings could enhance their utility. Third, although IPTs are often described as flexible, there is no standardized classification indicating which phase(s) of implementation—planning, execution, or evaluation—each tool is best suited to support. Establishing classifications would build on work summarizing approaches to implementation strategy selection (Powell et al., 2017). Formal mapping of IPTs to specific stages of the implementation process would support intentional selection, improve usability, and clarify opportunities for integration or sequencing across tools. Lastly, an important conceptual issue warrants further examination: certain IPTs (e.g., implementation blueprints) are labeled as implementation strategies, yet operate at a higher level by structuring the selection, timing, and actors of other strategies. This dual designation highlights a mismatch between taxonomy and function, and suggests that IPTs may be more appropriately conceptualized as meta-strategies or strategy-specification tools rather than discrete strategies themselves. Clarifying the vocabulary and conceptual boundaries around these dual roles would enhance theoretical coherence and practical application.

Finally, attention to implementation science training and capacity building is essential. As IPTs gain wider adoption, ensuring that users are adequately prepared to apply them with fidelity and flexibility will be critical. This includes developing concise training resources, decision aids to guide tool selection, and communities of practice to support ongoing learning and refinement.

Limitations

This is the first article in the literature that has defined IPTs and compared their uses. While this article has multiple strengths, our analysis of IPTs has several limitations. We focused on four commonly used IPTs but did not conduct a systematic review of all available tools. Other existing and emerging approaches may offer additional benefits or be better suited to specific contexts. Additionally, our examples, while grounded in implementation science principles, are hypothetical and may not capture the full complexity of real-world applications.

While our review highlighted the utility of IPTs for addressing common implementation challenges, we did not evaluate the tools by criteria such as usability, acceptability among end-users, or contextual adaptability. These factors are critical for real-world uptake and warrant further empirical investigation. Future research should examine experiences with different IPTs, particularly among diverse participant groups including community partners, frontline providers, and organizational leaders.

Conclusion

IPTs represent valuable resources for systematic implementation planning, particularly in complex service delivery contexts like substance use disorder treatment and mental health. By understanding the unique strengths and applications of different tools, implementation teams can make informed decisions about which approaches best fit their specific needs, contexts, and goals. The continued development and evaluation of these tools will strengthen the field’s capacity to translate evidence-based interventions into improved outcomes for the populations most in need of effective services.
