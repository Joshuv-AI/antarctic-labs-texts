Differential expression of cell death-associated markers may reflect distinct renal lesion patterns in effusive and non-effusive feline infectious peritonitis
Authors: Mustafa Usta, Musa Karaman, Fatma İlhan, Hasan Özen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFeline infectious peritonitis (FIP) is a fatal, immune-mediated disease caused by the mutagenic variants of feline coronavirus and manifests as effusive (wet) and non-effusive (dry) clinical forms with distinct pathological features. Although necrosis is a common histopathological finding in FIP, the contribution of specific regulated cell death (RCD) pathway-associated protein expression patterns to tissue injury across different clinical forms remains poorly understood. This study aimed to concurrently and comparatively evaluate apoptosis, necroptosis, autophagy-associated cell death, and ferroptosis in the renal tissues of cats with effusive and non-effusive FIP.MethodsArchival kidney samples from cats diagnosed with effusive (n = 18) and non-effusive (n = 18) FIP were included in the study. Immunohistochemistry on tissue sections was performed to assess markers associated with apoptosis by Caspase-3 and Caspase-8, necroptosis-associated signaling by RIP3, autophagy-associated activity by LC3-II, and ferroptosis-associated lipid metabolic susceptibility by ACSL-4. Necrosis was evaluated histomorphologically using a semi-quantitative scoring system, and immunohistochemical expression levels were digitally quantified and statistically compared between the groups.ResultsHistopathological evaluation revealed widespread tubular degeneration and necrosis in both clinical forms, with no significant difference in necrosis scores between the effusive and the non-effusive FIP. In contrast, quantitative differences in RCD-associated marker expression were identified. The non-effusive form exhibited significantly higher Caspase-3, Caspase-8, and LC3-II immunoreactivity, suggesting a higher prevalence of markers associated with caspase-dependent apoptosis and autophagy-associated activity. Conversely, RIP3 and ACSL-4 immunoreactivity were significantly increased in the effusive form, indicating increased expression of markers associated with necroptosis-related and ferroptosis-related processes. All evaluated markers were detected in both disease forms, indicating overlapping expression profiles rather than mutually exclusive pathway involvement.ConclusionCollectively, these findings indicate that RCD-associated proteins are differentially expressed in the effusive and the non-effusive FIP, reflecting quantitative differences in marker expression rather than definitive evidence of pathway-specific activation. While the non-effusive form showed higher expression of markers associated with caspase-dependent apoptosis and autophagy-associated activity, the effusive form exhibited increased expression of proteins related to proinflammatory and oxidative cell death-associated pathways. These findings provide a descriptive immunohistochemical profile of cell death-associated markers in renal lesions of FIP and highlight the need for future studies using complementary molecular and protein-based approaches to further clarify their functional significance.Keywords: Feline infectious peritonitis (FIP); Apoptosis, Necroptosis, Ferroptosis, Necrosis

Introduction

Cell death is essential for development, tissue remodeling, immune regulation, and the elimination of damaged or infected cells. It is widely accepted that cell death occurs either as accidental cell death, resulting from overwhelming physicochemical injury, or as regulated cell death (RCD), which proceeds through genetically encoded and evolutionarily conserved programs [1, 2]. The latter category encompasses several distinct modes of RCD, each characterized by specific morphological and biochemical features but often overlapping in physiological contexts [3]. Among these regulated processes, apoptosis is the best-characterized form and is considered an immunologically silent mechanism of cell removal. It is marked by controlled dismantling of cellular components, allowing efficient clearance without provoking inflammation. Apoptosis is central to normal morphogenesis, tissue turnover, and the resolution of immune responses [4]. Necroptosis represents a form of regulated necrotic cell death in which cell lysis leads to the release of intracellular contents, thereby promoting inflammation. As a lytic and pro-inflammatory death pathway, necroptosis serves as a host defense strategy when apoptosis is inhibited or subverted, particularly during infection. Unlike accidental necrosis, necroptosis follows a defined signaling program, distinguishing it as an orchestrated form of RCD [3]. Autophagy-associated cell death occurs when autophagic activity exceeds adaptive or cytoprotective functions. Although autophagy primarily maintains cellular homeostasis, it may contribute to cell death under conditions of persistent stress or metabolic imbalance. This mode of death is associated with extensive degradation of intracellular components through the autophagic machinery [1]. Ferroptosis is a metabolically driven, iron-dependent form of RCD. It is characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products and is distinct from apoptosis and necroptosis in both morphology and execution. Ferroptosis is closely linked to cellular metabolic state, oxidative load, and redox homeostasis. As a regulated program, it contributes to disease settings where lipid peroxidation and iron dysregulation are prominent [5].

The RCD pathways are activated in diverse pathological and physiological circumstances and may interact with or compensate for one another depending on cellular context [4]. Importantly, viral infections are known to engage multiple forms of RCD. Depending on the viral strategy and host immune status, apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, or other programmed pathways may participate in controlling infection or shaping inflammatory outcomes [3, 4].

Feline infectious peritonitis (FIP) is a fatal systemic and immune-mediated disease that arises when Feline Coronavirus (FCoV) acquires mutations that confer monocyte and macrophage tropism. The disease manifests in two clinical pathological forms, an effusive (wet) form characterized by serous effusions and a non-effusive (dry) form marked by organ localized granulomatous lesions. Histopathologically, FIP is characterized by fibrinous to pyogranulomatous inflammation, vasculitis, serositis, and necrotic lesions affecting multiple organs in both forms [6–8]. Although necrosis is a prominent feature of FIP-associated tissue damage, the specific RCD pathways contributing to these necrotic changes have not been elucidated.

Although FIP is a systemic disease, the kidney represents a relevant target organ because renal involvement is commonly observed in both effusive and non-effusive forms and may include granulomatous lesions, tubular degeneration, and necrosis. Therefore, renal tissue provides a suitable organ-based context for comparing cell death-associated protein expression in relation to FIP-associated inflammatory and parenchymal lesions. We hypothesized that renal lesions in effusive and non-effusive FIP would exhibit overlapping but quantitatively different immunohistochemical expression patterns of proteins associated with regulated cell death pathways. Accordingly, the present study aimed to comparatively evaluate the renal immunohistochemical expression of markers associated with apoptosis, necroptosis, autophagy-associated activity, and ferroptosis in cats with effusive and non-effusive FIP.

Materials and methods

Animals and tissue samples

This study utilized archival paraffin-embedded kidney tissue samples collected from cats submitted for necropsy to the Department of Pathology, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Balıkesir University, between 2016 and 2024. A total of 36 samples were included: 18 cats with the effusive form and 18 with the non-effusive form of FIP. The cats included in the study ranged in age from 6 months to 1.5 years. Cases were included when preservation was adequate for histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation, and the diagnosis of FIP was supported by compatible gross necropsy findings, histopathological lesions, and positive FCoV antigen immunoreactivity in inflammatory foci. The differentiation between effusive and non-effusive FIP was based on predefined gross necropsy criteria. Cases were assigned to either group when at least four of five characteristic macroscopic lesions specific to the respective form were present. To maintain clear separation between disease forms, cats exhibiting mixed or transitional pathological features were excluded from the study. Additional exclusion criteria included severe autolysis, insufficient renal tissue, poor tissue preservation, absence of renal lesions suitable for evaluation, or equivocal/negative FCoV immunolabeling. Consequently, cases displaying overlapping gross necropsy findings attributable to the opposite form were not considered for analysis (Table 1) [7, 9].

Histopathology

The kidney tissue samples were sectioned at 4 μm thickness and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) for histopathological evaluation. The tissue sections were viewed under a light microscope for the evaluation of necrotic changes. Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining was also performed to assess the changes in glomerular basement membranes. The severity of necrosis was assessed using a semi-quantitative scoring system based on the distribution of lesions. Scoring was performed on a 4-point scale, where 0 indicated no necrosis, 1 represented mild focal necrosis involving less than 5% of the examined tissue, 2 corresponded to moderate multifocal necrosis affecting 6–20% of the area, and 3 denoted severe extensive or confluent necrosis occupying more than 20% of the section. All assessments were independently conducted by two blinded veterinary pathologists, and the mean score was used for statistical analysis [10, 11].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue sections of 4 μm thickness were cut from each paraffin blocks for immunohistochemical analysis. The avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex method was employed for immunostaining. Sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated through graded alcohols. Antigen retrieval was performed using a microwave-based method in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for all markers investigated. Briefly, tissue sections were heated in citrate buffer in a microwave oven for 15 min and then allowed to cool at room temperature for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min at room temperature. After endogenous peroxidase blocking, the sections were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). To reduce non-specific background staining, the sections were incubated with 5% non-immune goat serum before primary antibody application. The primary antibodies used in this study were FCoV antibody (diluted in PBS 1:300, clone FIPV3-70, MCA2194, Bio-Rad); LC3-II (diluted in PBS 1:100, 14600-1-AP, Proteintech) as an autophagy-associated marker; RIP-3 (diluted in PBS 1:100, 17563-1-AP, Proteintech) as a necroptosis-associated marker; Caspase-3 (diluted in PBS 1:200, ab13847, Abcam) and Caspase-8 (diluted in PBS 1:200, ab4052, Abcam) as apoptosis-associated markers; and ACSL-4 (diluted in PBS 1:100, 66617-1-Ig, Proteintech) as a ferroptosis-associated marker. For each antibody used in the study, a negative control was performed by applying PBS instead of the primary antibody. Sections were incubated with the primary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After primary antibody incubation, the sections were washed three times in PBS for 5 min each and then incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. Following another PBS washing step, the sections were incubated with streptavidin conjugated to peroxidase for 30 min at room temperature. The chromogen 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) was used as the substrate. Counterstaining was performed with Mayer’s hematoxylin. The stained slides were mounted with entellan and examined under a light microscope. Digital images were captured using a camera integrated into the microscope. For each case and antibody, five non-overlapping microscopic fields were captured at 200× magnification from renal cortical areas. Field selection was performed blindly and randomly, while areas with severe tissue detachment, section artefacts, extensive necrotic debris, or nonspecific background staining were avoided. Immunohistochemical reactivity was evaluated using QuPath software (version 0.4.3). Quantification was performed using a digital positive-cell detection approach. DAB-positive immunoreactive cells were identified and counted separately in each field. For each case, the arithmetic mean of the five analyzed fields was calculated and accepted as the final positive cell count for that case.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version X.0, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The study population was divided into two groups based on the clinical pathological presentation: effusive (n = 18) and non-effusive (n = 18) forms of FIP. The normal distribution of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All variables that did not meet the assumption of normality were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The results were presented as graph displaying the median (line) and individual values.

Results

Histopathological examination of the kidneys in FIP cases revealed a broad spectrum of renal lesions extending from the cortex to the medulla, with no marked differences between the effusive and non-effusive forms. The most prominent finding was widespread tubular epithelial degeneration and necrosis. Necrotic tubular epithelial cells exhibited intensely eosinophilic cytoplasm, while their nuclei showed features of pyknosis and karyorrhexis. In some tubules, cell borders appeared indistinct, and the nuclei were totally lytic. These alterations were frequently accompanied by intraluminal proteinaceous desquamation and varying degrees of interstitial edema (Fig. 1). Consistent with these observations, semi-quantitative necrosis scores did not differ significantly between the effusive and non-effusive forms, indicating a comparable degree of histomorphological necrosis in both groups (Fig. 2).

Other renal histopathological lesions, excluding necrosis and degeneration, exhibited subtle and largely overlapping differences between the two clinical forms. In the effusive form, vascular alterations were more apparent and were mainly characterized by endothelial swelling and perivascular fibrin deposition. Glomerular exudation was relatively more evident in this form (Fig. 1b). In addition, fibrin deposits accompanied by scattered neutrophils and mononuclear cell infiltrations were occasionally observed, particularly in subcapsular regions, forming mild vasculocentric to pyogranulomatous inflammatory foci in some areas.

Kidneys of the cats with the non-effusive form generally displayed more localized lesions, including focal areas of coagulative necrosis associated with pyogranulomatous inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells, and scattered neutrophils (Fig. 1c). These infiltrates were observed either surrounding the necrotic foci or forming discrete perivascular aggregates. In some areas, inflammatory cells showed a tendency to organize into granulomatous structures extending from perivascular regions into the adjacent parenchyma.

The statistical evaluation of immunohistochemical scores is presented in Fig. 2. Intracytoplasmic FCoV antigen immunoreactivity was detected in large mononuclear inflammatory cells morphologically compatible with macrophages in both the effusive and the non-effusive forms (Fig. 3b, c). All evaluated RCD-associated markers were detected in both disease forms; therefore, the observed differences were interpreted as quantitative differences in marker expression rather than mutually exclusive pathway-specific patterns. The presence of apoptosis-associated markers was further evaluated through Caspase-3 and Caspase-8 expression. In the effusive form, immunoreactivity of both markers was lower in intensity and predominantly confined to the cytoplasm, whereas the non-effusive form exhibited stronger expression (Fig. 3d–i). Evaluation of autophagy-associated marker expression demonstrated increased LC3-II immunoreactivity in the non-effusive form, with a higher proportion of LC3-II-positive cells compared to the effusive form (Fig. 3j–l). Potential necroptosis-associated marker expression was assessed through RIP-3 expression, revealing a greater number of RIP-3-immunopositive cells in the effusive form than in the non-effusive form (Fig. 3m–o). Ferroptosis-associated marker expression was evaluated by ACSL-4 immunostaining, which revealed increased immunoreactivity in the effusive form relative to the non-effusive form (Fig. 3p–r).

Discussion

Cell death is a fundamental biological process, as integral to life as cell proliferation itself. As succinctly captured by Francis Bacon’s observation, “It is as natural to die as it is to be born,” underscoring the intrinsic role of this phenomenon in biological systems [2]. During organismal development, programmed elimination of superfluous cells is crucial for proper morphogenesis and organogenesis. In adult organisms, tightly regulated elimination mechanisms are essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis by removing damaged, autoreactive, or transformed cells, including those harboring oncogenic mutations [3, 4].

Cell death may occur through non-lytic and largely immunologically silent mechanisms, including apoptosis, necroptosis, autophagy-associated cell death, and ferroptosis, or via lytic and pro-inflammatory pathways such as necrosis [12]. In viral infections, RCD represents a central component of host defense. Viral nucleic acids are particularly recognized by host pattern recognition receptors, initiating antiviral signaling cascades that may be associated with multiple RCD-related processes. This process can contribute to the elimination of infected cells, thereby limiting viral spread and promoting the development of effective antiviral immunity. Accordingly, the balance among distinct RCD modalities emerges as a critical determinant of viral pathogenesis and host immune responses [13].

Apoptosis is now recognized as one of the earliest and best-characterized non-immunogenic forms of RCD, and is commonly evaluated by the expression of caspase-related proteins in tissue-based studies [14, 15]. Caspases are broadly classified into initiator and executioner groups [16]. Upon detection of cellular damage, initiator caspases primarily Caspase-8 and Caspase-9 are activated from their inactive procaspase forms and subsequently trigger the activation of executioner caspases, including Caspase-3, Caspase-6, and Caspase-7 [17, 18]. Apoptosis, mediated largely by caspase pathways, is a hallmark of FIP pathogenesis, although its precise role remains unclear [7, 19]. In the present study, Caspase-8 and Caspase-3 were immunohistochemically evaluated in the renal tissues of cats with FIP as apoptosis-associated markers representing initiator- and executioner-caspase-related protein expression, respectively. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the number of immunopositive cells for both caspases was significantly higher in the non-effusive form than in the effusive form. This finding points toward higher immunoreactivity of proteins involved in caspase-mediated apoptosis in the non-effusive form of FIP, potentially reflecting a disease phenotype characterized by sustained cellular immune activity and relatively controlled elimination of damaged renal cells. In contrast, the comparatively lower Caspase-3 and Caspase-8 immunoreactivity observed in the effusive form may reflect differences in the inflammatory microenvironment and the relative balance of cell death-associated processes between the two disease forms. Collectively, these results indicate that caspase-dependent apoptosis-associated marker expression is more pronounced in renal lesions of non-effusive FIP and may be linked to the more localized and chronic pathological pattern of this form.

Necroptosis is a genetically regulated form of cell death that proceeds independently of caspase activation and is characterized by necrotic-like morphology and loss of plasma membrane integrity, ultimately resulting in a highly proinflammatory outcome. This pathway is commonly initiated by death receptor ligands, such as TNF-α, or by innate immune pattern recognition receptors, leading to the formation of the RIPK1–RIPK3 necrosome complex [20]. Activated RIPK3 subsequently phosphorylates mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL), promoting its oligomerization and translocation to the plasma membrane, where membrane permeabilization and cell lysis ensue [21]. In viral infections, necroptosis has been implicated as an important component of innate host defense by limiting viral replication and dissemination, particularly through the sensing of viral nucleic acids by receptors such as ZBP1, which may promote RIPK3-dependent necroptosis-associated responses. The subsequent release of damage-associated molecular patterns and induction of inflammatory cytokines may further amplify antiviral immune responses, albeit at the cost of exacerbated tissue injury [20, 22]. Given its pivotal role in necroptotic signaling, RIPK3 (RIP3) is widely regarded as a central molecular mediator and commonly used marker associated with necroptotic processes in both infectious and inflammatory disease models [23, 24]. In the present study, RIP3 expression was significantly higher in the effusive form than in the non-effusive form. This finding is consistent with a higher RIP3-associated immunoreactive profile in the effusive phenotype. Previous experimental studies have shown that viral nucleic acid sensing can be linked to RIP3-dependent necroptosis-associated signaling [25, 26], in this context, and considering the exudative, vasculocentric, and fibrin-rich inflammatory features of effusive FIP, increased RIP3 immunopositivity may be associated with a renal microenvironment characterized by stronger proinflammatory and lytic cell death-associated processes. Collectively, these findings suggest that RIP3 expression is more pronounced in the effusive form and may contribute to the distinct inflammatory profile of this phenotype.

Autophagy, long regarded as a cellular recycling mechanism that promotes cell survival under stress conditions, may also be associated with cell death-related processes when it becomes dysregulated or excessive. When excessively activated or dysregulated, autophagy may lead to irreversible degradation of essential cellular components, culminating in autophagy-dependent cell death [3]. In this context, autophagy represents a stress-response mechanism that may interact with apoptosis, necrosis, and other cell death pathways under specific pathological conditions [27]. Macroautophagy is initiated by the formation of a phagophore, followed by sequestration of cytoplasmic cargo into a double-membraned autophagosome, which subsequently fuses with the lysosome to form an autolysosome [28]. This multistep process is tightly regulated by the ULK1/2 complex, the class III PI3K complex containing Beclin-1, and the ATG12–ATG5–ATG16L1 conjugation system. A critical event in autophagosome maturation is the conjugation of LC3 to phosphatidylethanolamine, resulting in the conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II, which serves as a widely used marker associated with autophagic activity [27]. LC3-II is incorporated into the autophagosomal membrane and plays a central role in cargo recognition and delivery to lysosomes for degradation [29, 30]. In viral infection models, increased LC3-II immunoreactivity has been associated with infection-related autophagic responses [31]. In the present study, LC3-II expression was significantly higher in the non-effusive form compared to the effusive form of FIP. This finding suggests increased expression of an autophagy-associated marker in the non-effusive disease course, which is characterized by a more localized, chronic, and granulomatous inflammatory milieu.

Autophagy plays a dual role in viral infections: while it may contribute to innate immune activation, interferon responses, and viral clearance during the early phases of infection, it is frequently subverted by viruses during persistent or latent infections to promote immune evasion, cellular adaptation, and long-term viral persistence. In chronic infections caused by viruses such as EBV, HCMV, HCV, and HIV, autophagic pathways are often dysregulated or selectively manipulated. Notably, in chronic HCV infection, increased autophagy-associated signaling has been reported together with impaired lysosomal degradation, a mechanism thought to favor viral persistence rather than effective clearance [32]. Accordingly, the increased LC3-II expression observed in the non-effusive form of FIP may be consistent with an altered host–virus interaction involving autophagy-associated responses during the chronic disease course. This increased LC3-II immunoreactivity may reflect cellular adaptation and persistence-associated stress responses, rather than acute tissue destruction, thereby potentially contributing to the distinct pathological profile of non-effusive FIP.

Another programmed cell death pathway, ferroptosis, is an iron-dependent form of cell death characterized by the lethal accumulation of lipid peroxides within cellular membranes [33]. In viral infections, has been associated with host cell fate and inflammatory tissue injury, as virus-induced oxidative stress, dysregulated iron metabolism, and impaired antioxidant defenses may create a cellular environment permissive to lipid peroxidation-driven cell death. While ferroptosis may restrict viral replication under certain conditions, increased ferroptosis-associated responses can exacerbate inflammation and tissue injury, underscoring its dual role in viral pathogenesis [5, 34]. Among lipid metabolism enzymes, acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL-4) is a key determinant of ferroptosis sensitivity. ACSL-4 preferentially catalyzes the activation of PUFAs such as arachidonic and adrenic acids, facilitating their incorporation into membrane phospholipids that are particularly vulnerable to peroxidation during ferroptosis [35, 36]. Importantly, ACSL-4 is closely associated with ferroptosis sensitivity by shaping the phospholipid landscape susceptible to lipid peroxidation. Accordingly, ACSL-4 was selected in the present study as a ferroptosis-associated marker rather than a direct indicator of pathway execution [35, 37]. In the present study, ACSL-4 immunoreactivity was significantly higher in the effusive form compared with the non-effusive form of FIP, indicating a distinct upregulation of markers associated with ferroptotic sensitivity in the effusive phenotype. This finding suggests that ferroptosis-associated lipid metabolic alterations may be linked to the severe inflammatory milieu and vascular pathology characteristic of effusive FIP. Consistent with this interpretation, ferroptosis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of several coronaviruses, including SARS-CoV-2 and murine hepatitis virus, primarily through disruption of iron homeostasis, redox balance, and antioxidant defense mechanisms [34]. Collectively, these findings suggest that ACSL-4-associated immunoreactivity in FIP differs according to clinical disease manifestation. Therefore, future studies incorporating complementary molecular and protein-based approaches may further clarify the functional relevance of ferroptosis-associated pathways in FIP.

In the present study, no histopathological difference in the extent of necrosis was observed between the two clinical forms of FIP. Unlike other cell death modalities whose involvement can be detected at the molecular level, necrosis primarily represents a histomorphological outcome rather than a pathway-specific, genetically regulated process. Although advances in molecular and immunohistochemical techniques allow the identification of distinct regulated cell death mechanisms, these pathways do not exhibit unique or definitive histomorphological signatures that would permit reliable discrimination by routine histopathology alone [38]. Accordingly, necrosis is best interpreted as the terminal morphological manifestation of multiple converging cell death pathways, rather than as an independent, molecularly defined mode of cell death.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comparative analysis of selected markers associated with apoptosis, necroptosis, autophagy-associated cell death, and ferroptosis in the renal tissues of cats with effusive and non-effusive FIP. The findings indicate that the non-effusive form shows higher Caspase-3, Caspase-8, and LC3-II immunoreactivity, consistent with a greater expression of proteins associated with caspase-dependent apoptosis and autophagy-related processes in this disease form. In contrast, the effusive form is characterized by higher RIP3 and ACSL-4 immunoreactivity, suggesting a stronger association with necroptosis- and ferroptosis-related molecular responses. Importantly, the detection of these markers in both disease forms indicates that regulated cell death-associated processes in FIP are likely overlapping rather than completely form-specific. Overall, these findings support the view that the pattern of cell death-associated protein expression may differ between the effusive and non-effusive forms of FIP and may contribute to the distinct pathological features of these forms. Further studies using additional pathway-related markers together with complementary molecular and protein-based methods are needed to clarify the functional roles of these pathways in FIP-associated renal injury.
