Image defined risk factor(s) and outcomes for abdominal neuroblastoma: a surgical perspective from a Thailand National Cancer Centre
Authors: Bhavita Thamsopitt, Thitiporn Junhasavasdikul, Suwadee Eng-Chaun, Paul D Losty, Pornsri Thanachatchairattana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the impact of IDRFs on surgical outcomes, complications, and survival in abdominal neuroblastoma (ANB).MethodRetrospective study of patients (< 15 years) with ANB stage L2 or higher treated over two decades at a university centre in Thailand.ResultsAmong fifty patients, 94% had high-risk neuroblastoma. Preoperative tumour encasement of the celiac axis (CA) or origin of SMA was more frequent in incomplete resection (33% vs. 9%, p = 0.04). Complications were greater in children with abdominal aorta/IVC tumour encasement (58% vs. 27%, p < 0.03) and adjacent organ infiltrative tumours (42% vs. 7%, p < 0.04). Visceral organ injuries correlated with vascular/infiltrating IDRFs. Tumours invading CA/SMA carried less risk (%) of vascular injury (r =- 0.28, p = 0.04). Only tumour encasement of the SMA at the mesenteric root predicted disease progression (r = 0.35, p = 0.01). Incomplete resection was associated with higher risks (%) of disease progression. Two- and five-year survival rates were 80.6% and 52.1%, respectively.ConclusionVascular IDRFs especially mesenteric root encasement were linked to risk of visceral organ injury and disease progression. Awareness of risks may reduce vascular injury during operation though incomplete resection influenced disease progression. Survival outcomes are comparable to Southeast Asia Regions.Keywords: Neuroblastoma, Image defined risk factors (IDRF), Surgery, Survival, Outcome metrics

Introduction

The International Neuroblastoma Risk Group Staging System (INRGSS) originating from a study that analysed in a survival tree regression model over 8000 neuroblastoma (NB) patients is widely deployed for disease risk stratification before initiating treatment of neuroblastoma. It is based on age at diagnosis, histology grading, MYCN amplification, oncogene status, chromosomal aberrations, tumour cell ploidy, and radiological imaging [1]. The Thai Pediatric Oncology Group (ThaiPOG) has adapted the INRG system into the Thai national neuroblastoma therapy protocol(s) which tailored for the setting and the non-availability of immunotherapy (anti-GD2 antibody) for neuroblastoma patients in Thailand. The system is now firmly established as a standard of care for Thailand’s staging, risk assessment, and treatment of neuroblastoma.

SIOPEN has classified neuroblastoma patients with locoregional tumours as characterized by specific imaging features, now termed Image Defined Risk Factors (IDRFs). As surgical risk factors are based on certain radiographic images, IDRFs are deployed in assessment of neuroblastoma [2]. The IDRF defines four stages in INRGSS: L1—absence of IDRFs; L2—presence of IDRFs; M—widely disseminated disease; and MS—metastatic NB limited to skin, liver, and bone marrow in children aged below 18 months with L1 or L2 primary tumours [1]. Abdominal neuroblastoma (ANB) often infiltrates vital organs, encases and entangles major abdominal vessels. Several studies on IDRFs have highlighted the significant operative challenges facing surgeons in their efforts to achieve gross tumor resection (GTR) [3]. It has been shown that an increase in the number of IDRFs is correlated with more aggressive tumour biology, more demanding surgeries, and poorer oncologic outcomes [4–7]. Although IDRFs are known to influence tumour resectability, it remains unclear which specific IDRFs significantly affect surgical outcomes and risk(s) of complications. The current study aimed to investigate the relationship between tumour resectability/surgical complications and specific IDRFs, and how these factors may impact overall survival (OS) and event-free survival (EFS) in children with ANB treated at Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand – a designated Thai National Pediatric Cancer Centre.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective review of medical records of pediatric patients with ANB who underwent surgical management at Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand, from January 2003 to August 2023.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients younger than 15 years of age at the time of diagnosis; (2) diagnosis confirmed by pathology studies showing ganglioneuroma (GN), ganglioneuroblastoma (GNB), and neuroblastoma (NB), or from positive bone marrow in conjunction with elevated urinary catecholamine metabolites or serum neuron-specific enolase (NSE); (3) disease stage L2 (IDRF-positive) or higher (M or MS); and (4) all patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery undertaken at Ramathibodi Hospital.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) incomplete medical records and/or missing surgical documentation; (2) inadequate studies and radiology images for a full assessment of IDRFs; and (3) patients who did not undergo resection of their tumour at our centre.

Data collection

We collected data according to age at primary diagnosis, sex, presenting signs and symptoms, tumour staging (INRGSS), and re-classified the INRG status for patients that were originally grouped in the International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS). Patients with standard-risk disease, categorized in the ThaiPOG system, align with the INRGSS intermediate risk groups. According to the ThaiPOG neuroblastoma study protocol, MYCN is the only biological marker apart from histological reporting that is used to define pre-treatment disease risk stratification. Therefore, in this current study, only MYCN is reported when it became routinely available to our unit. The timing of surgery in this study report followed the ThaiPOG study protocols. For standard-risk patients, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is administered initially, and then surgical resection is considered after an assessment of response after four cycles of treatment. If the tumor is then assessed and considered unresectable, surgery is deferred at this point and reconsidered after completion of a further eighth cycle of chemotherapy. In high-risk neuroblastoma patients, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is given first, and surgical resection is then typically performed after the fifth or sixth cycle of induction therapy. As the study was conducted during the era(s) January 2003—August 2023, some variations in treatment protocols and timing of surgery will have occurred over time. Additionally, deviations from protocol were occasionally necessary due to individual treatment responses, tumour resectability, and surgical judgment.

The extent of resection was extracted from the detailed surgical operative records. Gross total resection (GTR) or complete surgery refers to the gross absence of residual tumour and involved lymph nodes after operation. Incomplete resection refers to circumstances where a visible macroscopic tumour or involved lymph nodes were evident following operation. Perioperative complications listed and included documentation of injuries to vessels and visceral organs that required surgical repair. Perioperative morbidity was further recorded – (i) chyle leakage, (ii) pancreatitis, (iii) surgical site infection (SSI), and (iv) any other associated infections following operative surgery that led to a prolonged postoperative hospital stay. Data on disease progression, tumour recurrence, and death were collected for further detailed analysis. For patients and families who discontinued follow-up care at our hospital facility, the date of their last hospital visit and clinical outcome(s) were accurately recorded.

IDRF detection

IDRFs at primary diagnosis were determined by CT scan imaging. Preoperative IDRFs were then assessed from the most recent CT scan prior to operative surgery. Two board-certified paediatric radiologists—blinded to clinical status of patients—independently reviewed all imaging and evaluated patient IDRFs. A joint review meeting by radiology specialists resolved any discrepancy. Consensus findings were later used for further data analysis.

All IDRFs were strictly classified according to the ‘Guidelines for Staging of Neuroblastic Tumours’ INRGSS criteria published in 2011 [10]. We further grouped IDRFs into four distinct categories to facilitate analyses: (i) extensive IDRFs, (ii) vascular IDRFs, (iii) neurological IDRFs, and (iv) infiltrative IDRFs. Extensive IDRFs referred to tumours involving multiple anatomical compartments. Vascular IDRFs included tumours encasing—(a) aorta and/or vena cava in the thoracoabdominal region, (b) branches of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) at the mesenteric root, (c) origins of the celiac axis (CA) and/or SMA, (d) one or both renal pedicles, (e) abdominal aorta and/or inferior vena cava (IVC), and (f) major iliac vessels. Neurological IDRFs involved – (a) tumour invasion of more than one-third of the spinal canal in the axial plane, (b) loss of visualization of the peri-medullary leptomeningeal spaces or abnormal spinal cord signal, or (c) pelvic tumours crossing the sciatic notch. Infiltrative IDRFs included (a) infiltration of the porta hepatis and/or hepatoduodenal ligament (HDL), as well as (b) infiltration of the pericardium, diaphragm, kidney, liver, duodenopancreatic block, and mesentery, which we will herein refer to as adjacent organs thereafter. This grouping system was designed to facilitate correlations (if any) with operative surgery and was not intended to replace INRGSS standards.

Statistics

Categorical variables were analyzed as percentages (%), while continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges for non-parametric distributed data and means ± SD for normally distributed data. Differences in outcomes were assessed using appropriate statistical tests: Fisher’s exact or Chi-square test for categorical variables, and independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables. Inter-observer reliability for radiologists was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) within a two-way random-effects model. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis estimated survival and event-free survival (EFS) rates; log-rank tests further compared survival curves between groups. Cramer’s V Pearson correlation analyzed the relation of specific IDRFs to any specific concerned events. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and figures were performed using STATA version 18.

Ethics approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University Ethics Committee (Approval No. MURA2022/587). The Institutional Review Board at Mahidol University is fully compliant with International Guidelines for Human Research Protection, including the Declaration of Helsinki, The Belmont Report, CIOMS Guidelines, and the International Conference on Harmonization in Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP). The requirement for informed consent was waived because the study design was retrospective and involved the analysis of de-identified medical records.

Results

Patient characteristics

As shown in Table 1, a total of 50 index patients were eligible for the study: 24 (48%) males and 26 (52%) females. The median age at diagnosis was 34 months (IQR: 20–63 months). Fever was the most frequent presenting clinical symptom (56%), followed by evidence of an abdominal mass (47%). Most patients were diagnosed with advanced-stage NB or stage M disease (88%). Metastatic disease was primarily in the bone marrow (70%), including widespread skeletal dissemination (70%). Twenty-five patients following presentation had percutaneous tumour biopsy and all revealed diagnosis of NB. Among 19 patients with available MYCN biology, thirteen cases (68%) showed amplification. According to the ThaiPOG NB risk stratification system, 94% of index patients were considered high risk and 6% of cases were in the standard risk group category. All patients received several cycles of chemotherapy ranging from 4 to 21 cycles with a mean of 6 cycles. GTR was documented in 35 patients (70%) and incomplete resection in the remaining 15 cases (30%). Median operative blood loss was estimated at 190 ml (IQR: 70–400) with a mean operative time duration of 244 ± 123 min. Nearly half of all patients (48%) experienced a complication(s) accounting for 38 recorded events. The most common complication recorded was vascular injury (30%) which included renal vein, aorta, IVC and/or hepatic vein injury(s). Twenty-eight percent of patients encountered visceral organ injuries primarily involving the duodenum and/or kidney. Less common but noteworthy were diaphragmatic injury, splenic injury requiring splenectomy and a single patient who sustained a very minor liver injury. Other post-operative morbidity documented included chyle leaks (6%), pancreatitis (2%), and SSI (2%). Other infections that prolonged postoperative hospital stay (8%) were urinary tract infections, postoperative intra-abdominal hematomas, which required reoperation to eliminate the source of infection, and infectious diarrhea.

IDRF assessment

From the primary diagnostic CT scan, an agreement between the radiologist raters demonstrated a strong correlation (r = 0.839, p < 0.001), and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) indicated good average agreement (ICC = 0.872). Similarly, preoperative CT imaging findings showed a trend towards a strong correlation (r = 0.705, p < 0.001) and an ICC indicating good average agreement (ICC = 0.835, p < 0.001). Examining the ICC for each individual consultant radiologist reporting all had good and moderate agreement on the imaging at primary diagnosis and the preoperative CT scans before elective operation with ICC = 0.773 and 0.716 (p < 0.001), respectively. These results indicated a high level of reliability in IDRF scoring between the two consultant radiologists across both primary diagnostic and preoperative CT imaging evaluation(s). Any discrepancy of IDRFs encountered was resolved through consensus between radiologists resulting in an ‘agreement IDRF’ for further statistical analysis in each timeframe.

All NB patients after receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy subsequently underwent a preoperative CT scan before their scheduled elective operation(s) which revealed a mean total IDRF count of 1.6 ± 1.7 significantly reduced from the initial mean of 4.1 ± 2.7 at primary diagnosis as shown in Table 2. The distribution of patients in the study across all relevant IDRFs reported for ANB showed a statistically significant decrease after chemotherapy, except for the neurological IDRFs. Tumour infiltration of the porta hepatis or HDL markedly decreased after chemotherapy with the number of affected patients reducing from 16 to only 2 index cases.

We analysed and reviewed CT scans at diagnosis to comprehend risk factors for residual tumour or if the IDRFs at that time point could predict future risk(s) of injury. Patients with preoperative tumour encasing abdominal aorta or IVC (58% vs. 27%, p = 0.025) or infiltrating adjacent organs (42% vs. 7%, p < 0.04) significantly differed in terms of those groups without complication and with complications—Table 5. Preoperative tumours encasing the abdominal aorta or IVC were associated with a greater likelihood of visceral organ injury than vascular injury (r = 0.37, p = 0.01 vs. r = -0.12, p = 0.42). Tumours encasing branches of the SMA at the mesenteric root or infiltrating the porta hepatis or HDL also significantly correlated with visceral organ injury (r = 0.31, p = 0.03 and r = 0.33, p = 0.02, respectively). Tumours encasing origins of the CA or SMA were associated with less significant risk (%) for vascular injury (r = -0.28, p = 0.04).

IDRF and operative time

Since operative time may be considered an indirect indicator/marker of the complexity of surgery, specific IDRFs may thus potentially show correlation(s) to prolonged operative times. Table 5 illustrates that encasement of the abdominal aorta or IVC at the time of diagnosis significantly correlated with much longer operative times (r = 0.37, p = 0.01). Prolonged operative times were associated with the preoperative IDRFs in such patients as those found to have tumour encasing branches of SMA at the mesenteric root, involving major iliac vessels, and infiltrating the porta hepatis or HDL (r = 0.40, 0.42, and 0.37, respectively; p < 0.05).

Clinical outcomes and mortality

Among fifty patients, twelve (24%) cases experienced disease progression: five had GTR and seven had incomplete tumour resection, which differed significantly (14.3% vs. 46.7%, p < 0.02). Notably, only tumour encasement of the SMA at the mesenteric root was associated with disease progression, demonstrating a statistically significant correlation (r = 0.35, p = 0.01) as shown in Table 5. Eight (16%) patients developed recurrent disease with no significant differences observed between those patients who had GTR versus incomplete resection (17.1% vs. 13.3%, p = 0.74). Most patients developing recurrent disease had distant metastases. One patient with incomplete resection had an isolated local recurrence. Two patients having GTR were later found to have local and distant disease relapse.

Figure 1 shows the overall survival rate. The median time until death in the study population was 24 months (IQR: 15, 30). Overall 2-year and 5-year survival rates (OS) were 80.6% (95%CI: 64.7%-89.5%) and 52.1% (95%CI: 33%-68.1%), respectively. These were not adjusted for disease risk or completeness of surgery. No patient died in the perioperative period from a tumour-related operative event. However, a single child sustained an iatrogenic SVC injury during a percutaneous Hickman catheter line insertion of the left subclavian vein, leading to massive blood loss and subsequent cardiac arrest. The patient later suffered hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) before death from septicemia.

At a median follow-up time of 25 months (IQR, 15–47 months), no case(s) of a secondary new malignancy(s), ovarian failure, or growth hormone deficiency were recorded in our study population. Non-malignancy related events in this study included a single patient developing scoliosis; five patients having sensory neural hearing loss as confirmed by audiometry testing; ten children acquired hypothyroidism following MIBG treatment and three children developed impairment in renal function without any history of sustaining operative injury to the kidney or having nephrectomy to achieve gross resection of neuroblastoma tumour.

Discussion

This study has evaluated the role of IDRFs at primary diagnosis and later at preoperative imaging in relation to surgical outcomes and complications in children with abdominal neuroblastoma. Our findings confirm their importance for risk stratification and surgical planning, while also revealing specific associations between IDRFs and intraoperative morbidity. We found that specific IDRFs—infiltration of the porta hepatis or HDL at diagnosis and preoperative encasement of the celiac axis—were significantly associated with incomplete tumour resection. Regarding complications, tumour encasing the abdominal aorta or IVC at diagnosis correlated with both vascular and visceral organ injury whereas tumour encasement of SMA at the mesenteric root correlated with only visceral organ injury in this study. Preoperative IDRFs—particularly encasement of vessels at the mesenteric root, aorta, IVC, and disease that infiltrated the porta hepatis or HDL were all associated with increased risk (%) of visceral organ injuries.

Interestingly, preoperative encasement of the CA/SMA was paradoxically associated with a lower incidence of vascular injury. Knowledge of such challenges reflected greater surgical caution when operating in these critical vital regions, rather than the IDRF itself acting as a surrogate index or protective factor. When surgeons were therefore cognizant that tumours encased these critical anatomical regions such as the CA/SMA where injury could result in major complications, including bowel ischemia, massive hemorrhage or death on table, aggressive dissection was avoided in these vital regions. These factors are supported further by our operative findings with neuroblastoma tumours, in which encasement of the mesenteric and major iliac vessels was also associated with prolonged operative time, confirming a more meticulous and cautious surgical approach. Preoperative CA/SMA encasement was notably more frequently associated with incomplete resection reflecting deliberate surgical avoidance in high-risk regions. Our study aligns with previous published reports that certain IDRFs such as tumours infiltrating the porta hepatis or HDL [8], tumours encasing the branches of the SMA at the mesenteric root and at the origins of the celiac axis or SMA [9], and tumours encasing the aorta and its midline branches [10] were more frequently associated with incomplete surgery. We concur and strongly agree with Avanzini et al., who cautiously emphasized in their detailed study of the challenges of resection in high-risk anatomical areas. Due to the inherently higher risk(s) of complications (%), surgeons may therefore opt for incomplete resection as a safer strategy approach as previously discussed to avoid on-table fatality during the operation [9].

Despite recognition of specific IDRFs, our results further lend support to the noteworthy reports emphasizing that groups of IDRFs—particularly vascular and infiltrative—may predict tumour resectability and intraoperative risks of complications [6, 11–17]. Of interest, several studies now emphasize that vascular IDRFs are more likely associated with surgical risks/complications than other IDRFs [7, 11, 12].

Importantly, findings from this current study also support key observation that IDRFs alone do not always fully capture surgical complexity; that is, not all IDRFs may equally impact the feasibility of complete tumour resection. The Surgical Complexity Index (SCI) 2023 based on surgeon expert consensus [18] and the subsequent IDRF Score represents a novel tailored approach to better refine surgical risk stratification by assigning weighted values to specific IDRFs based on their impact on operative morbidity and complexity. For instance, infiltration of the duodenopancreatic block and tumour encasement of the CA or SMA are assigned the highest risk score of three reflecting their significant impact with regard to surgical challenges and technical operative difficulty [19].

We have found both time points of IDRF assessment to be very useful. CT imaging at time of diagnosis defines risk stratification, treatment planning, and initial surgical resectability prediction. Preoperative CT imaging is then essential for later operative planning since chemotherapy can reduce tumour size and modify the IDRF pattern. Nevertheless, reviewing CT scans at primary diagnosis remains important for identifying specific areas at risk for residual tumour or persistent dysplastic tissue changes which may later affect the surgical outcomes such as incomplete tumour removal, perioperative complications and increased operative blood loss. In the current study, we therefore focused on both IDRFs at the time of diagnosis and then at preoperative imaging. IDRFs at the primary diagnosis are considered the most useful for risk classification and predicting the surgical completeness of resection. Preoperative IDRFs may later guide operative strategy and better anticipate perioperative complications. These study findings are reassuringly supported by several publications [9, 14, 15, 17, 19]. Parhar et al. [20] examined the prediction of NB resectability on IDRFs at diagnosis and preoperative IDRFs, and Simon et al. [21] also found that higher IDRFs at diagnosis correlated with lower rates of complete NB resection. We therefore wish to underscore the key importance of a comprehensive, time-sensitive assessment of IDRFs throughout treatment course planning in NB patients, as shown and highlighted by this study.

Although incomplete tumour resection in this study was associated with a higher risk of disease progression, we found no significant difference in disease recurrence rates (%) between GTR and incomplete resection, likely due to the predominance of metastatic recurrences in this patient cohort. These findings strongly suggest that NB tumour biology may have greater influence than local surgical control in determining recurrence patterns also supported by Vo et al. who clearly stated that tumour biology may predict relapse patterns and prognosis rather than resection alone [22]. GTR tends to improve the event-free or disease-free survival rate (DFS) compared to overall survival (OS), as previously emphasized by Mullassery et al. [23] and von Allmen et al. [24].

In examining chemotherapy response, we noted that most IDRFs were reduced in more than half of all our NB patients, except the IDRFs involving abdominal aorta or IVC, adjacent organs, and in the neurological category subgroup, which is again consistent with previous studies [5, 14, 15]. Irtan et al. in their study reported that the greater decline in numbers of IDRFs after chemotherapy were observed in those patients harboring metastatic disease and linked with neuroblastoma histology [15]. These observations correlated with our current study despite having a smaller sample size. However, Avanzini et al. have recently commented to be aware that new IDRFs may also emerge at the time of preoperative CT imaging in nearly 20% NB patients [9].

Two- and five-year overall survival rates (80.6% and 52.1%) in this current study are comparable to those reported within the Southeast Asia Region [25] and have shown progress with now better patient outcomes experienced compared to a 33.6% survival rate recorded in 2011 [26] and 65.6% survival in 2022 [27], despite lack of accessibility to anti-GD2 based immunotherapy. Risk group clearly also affects overall survival (OS), though it should be noted that the majority of patients in this current study were designated high-risk neuroblastoma, with only a small number in the standard-risk group, with no cases in the low-risk group with respect to survival analyses. We had no surgical-related deaths, which we believe, reflected the high standard of care practiced at Ramathibodi Hospital over the 20-year study period. The shorter median survival time (%) we observed in event-free patients further showed that non-malignant causes may also contribute to early mortality compared to the malignancy itself. Attention on detailing surgical complications, together with vigilant post-operative management, should always be emphasized and prioritized alongside protocol-driven treatments for NB.

CT imaging was undertaken at both primary diagnosis and in the preoperative period, with all patients classified as having INRGSS stage L2 disease or higher. The study design sought to minimize limitations associated with the inherent variability encountered with NB patients and the time-based evolution of advanced NB management care strategies. We acknowledge certain limitations of the current study, including its retrospective design and relatively small sample size. In addition, as the study spanned a prolonged time period, heterogeneity in treatment strategies, including variations in chemotherapy regimens and timing of surgery, was inevitable. Advances in chemotherapy for neuroblastoma over time may also have affected outcomes. A Thai multicentre survey would greatly facilitate a wider nationwide study report examining specific IDRFs on NB surgical complexity. However, we acknowledge at the time of writing that a surgeon skill mixed workforce with variable operator experience may still exist, potentially confounding practice variation outcomes across Thailand centres in the definitive operative management of NB.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study has demonstrated that specific vascular and infiltrative IDRFs, particularly those involving the porta hepatis, hepatoduodenal ligament, and mesenteric root, were key predictors of incomplete NB resection and intraoperative morbidity (%). Vascular IDRFs, as defined by mesenteric root encasement, were associated with visceral organ injury(s) rather than direct vascular injury and were also associated with NB disease progression. Surgical awareness of risks may help prevent or reduce vascular injury. However, balancing the goals of minimizing complications with the need to achieve complete or gross tumour resection is critical as incomplete surgery leads to disease progression. Survival outcomes from this report are comparable to the wider Southeast Asia Region, with reassuringly no surgical-related deaths in a 20-year study period.
