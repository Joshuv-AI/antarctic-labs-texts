Early C-reactive protein and ferritin kinetics after tocilizumab initiation predict macrophage activation syndrome in Still disease: a single-centre retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kohei Yo, Takumi Nagamoto, Yuri Nishida, Mari Hara, Yudai Koshida, Kazuki Orita, Hiroyuki Murabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of tocilizumab (TCZ) as induction therapy in Still disease and identify early dynamic biomarkers, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and ferritin changes, for macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) risk stratification.MethodsThis single-centre retrospective cohort study included patients with Still disease treated from 2004 to 2024. Patients were classified by induction therapy (TCZ vs non-TCZ). MAS was defined using the 2016 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology/American College of Rheumatology/Paediatric Rheumatology International Trials Organisation criteria. Early biomarker changes after prednisolone initiation were evaluated. Primary outcomes were week-4 CRP normalisation and clinically inactive disease (CID) at month 3. Associations with induction therapy were analysed using Firth’s penalised logistic regression. In TCZ-treated patients, receiver operating characteristic analyses assessed laboratory biomarkers for MAS prediction.ResultsAmong 54 patients, 12 received TCZ as initial induction therapy and 7 for relapse (19 episodes). MAS occurred in 5 episodes (26.3%). No significant differences were observed in week-4 CRP normalisation (75.0% vs 51.2%, P = .193) or month-3 CID (40.0% vs 14.6%, P = .090). After adjusting for the higher initial prednisolone dose, early TCZ exposure was independently associated with month-3 CID (odds ratio: 9.73, 95% CI: 1.25-77.50, P = .030). Week-1/baseline CRP ratio and ferritin ratios predicted MAS after TCZ induction; the week-1/baseline ferritin ratio showed the highest area under the curve (0.914), followed by the CRP ratio (0.877).ConclusionsEarly TCZ exposure was independently associated with CID at month 3. Insufficient early suppression of inflammatory markers, particularly higher ferritin and CRP ratios, may identify patients at higher risk of subsequent MAS.

INTRODUCTION

Immunologic basis of clinical heterogeneity in Still disease

Still disease is a systemic inflammatory disorder driven by dysregulated innate immunity, characterised by increased proinflammatory cytokines including interleukin (IL)-18, IL-1β, and IL-6 [[1], [2], [3]]. The disease encompasses a spectrum from severe systemic inflammation with quotidian fever, characteristic rashes, serositis, hepatosplenomegaly, and hyperferritinaemia to chronic courses dominated by persistent erosive arthritis [[1], [2], [3]]. Recent findings indicate that combined innate and adaptive immune pathway involvement contributes to pathogenesis across age groups [4].

Treat-to-target strategy and unmet needs in early disease control

In 2024, the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) and Paediatric Rheumatology European Society (PReS) referred to systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) and adult-onset Still disease (AOSD) collectively as ‘Still disease’ and proposed a treat-to-target strategy focused on rapid systemic inflammation control [3]. These recommendations define intermediate treatment targets at day 7, week 4, month 3, and month 6, emphasising early IL-1 or IL-6 inhibition combined with short-term glucocorticoids [3]. In a Japanese observational study, clinically inactive disease (CID) at month 6 was associated with reduced relapse risk, and successful glucocorticoid withdrawal correlated with early CID achievement and timely biologic initiation [5]. Despite their prognostic importance, CID attainment rates remain low, with prolonged glucocorticoid exposure as a major barrier [5].

Evidence gaps regarding early biologic induction

IL-1 and IL-6 inhibitors have demonstrated substantial efficacy in randomised controlled trials and real-world studies [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. In Japan, tocilizumab (TCZ) is frequently selected owing to its availability and accumulated clinical experience, underscoring the need to clarify its optimal timing and safety as remission-induction therapy. Nevertheless, important evidence gaps persist. The pivotal randomised trial of TCZ in AOSD enrolled patients with persistent disease activity despite high-dose glucocorticoids, but excluded those with severe organ involvement or life-threatening systemic manifestations [7]. Therefore, although current EULAR/PReS recommendations support the use of TCZ in Still disease, the optimal timing, patient selection, and safety considerations for TCZ during the early induction phase remain incompletely characterised.

Macrophage activation syndrome as a critical complication

Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) is a severe and potentially fatal hyperinflammatory complication of Still disease, affecting approximately 7% to 15% of patients [10,11]. MAS is characterised by uncontrolled activation of macrophages and cytotoxic T cells, leading to cytokine storm and multiorgan dysfunction [10,11]. Observational data indicate that MAS may occur during biologic therapy, and its incidence appears to vary by treatment regimen [12,13]. The 2024 EULAR/PReS recommendations therefore emphasise rapid recognition of suspected MAS and prompt escalation of immunosuppressive therapy [3]. Case series and observational studies have reported MAS under TCZ treatment in both paediatric and adult Still disease, sometimes with attenuated clinical or laboratory features [[14], [15], [16]]. These observations highlight the need for better tools to identify at-risk patients and monitor disease activity during cytokine-targeted therapy.

Rationale for biomarker-based risk stratification

Although biomarkers such as IL-18 and S100 proteins have demonstrated diagnostic and prognostic utility in Still disease and MAS [17,18], they are not routinely available in clinical practice. The absence of validated indicators to guide the timing and safety of early TCZ initiation contributes to reluctance in biologic use and may impede treat-to-target objectives [3,18].

Study objectives

This study aimed to: (i) evaluate the effectiveness and safety of TCZ as induction therapy in Still disease; (ii) compare early treatment responses and short-term clinical outcomes between TCZ and non-TCZ induction strategies; and (iii) explore early biomarkers—particularly C-reactive protein (CRP) and ferritin dynamics, together with cytokine-related parameters including baseline IL-18 and IL-6 levels and derived ratios—for predicting MAS risk after TCZ initiation.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This single-centre retrospective cohort study was conducted at Kurashiki Central Hospital (Kurashiki, Japan). Consecutive patients with Still disease, including sJIA and AOSD, between January 2004 and October 2024 were reviewed. Clinical, laboratory, and treatment data were extracted from electronic medical records.

Participants

Patients were included if they met the definition of Still disease, fulfilling the Yamaguchi criteria for AOSD [19], the International League of Associations for Rheumatology criteria for sJIA [20], or the PRINTO provisional classification [21], and had available baseline clinical and laboratory data before biologic therapy. Patients with active infection, malignancy, alternative causes of hyperinflammation (including other forms of secondary haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis), or insufficient outcome data were excluded.

Treatment exposure

Exposure definition and group classification

Patients were classified based on initial remission-induction therapy:

•TCZ induction group: Patients who received TCZ as initial remission-induction therapy at our institution.

TCZ induction group: Patients who received TCZ as initial remission-induction therapy at our institution.

•Non-TCZ induction group: Patients who did not receive TCZ as first-line induction therapy.

Non-TCZ induction group: Patients who did not receive TCZ as first-line induction therapy.

For the MAS prediction analysis, all TCZ induction episodes (including both initial induction and re-induction at relapse) were included to maximise statistical power for biomarker evaluation.

Definition of MAS and risk assessment

MAS was defined according to the 2016 EULAR/ACR/PRINTO classification criteria [22] for MAS complicating sJIA, previously applied to AOSD-related MAS [10].

Baseline MAS risk was assessed using the following 2 validated scoring systems:

1.H-score: A validated diagnostic score for haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis incorporating clinical and laboratory parameters [23].

H-score: A validated diagnostic score for haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis incorporating clinical and laboratory parameters [23].

2.M-score: A MAS prediction score proposed by Eloseily et al [24], calculated using ferritin, platelet count, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and fibrinogen.

M-score: A MAS prediction score proposed by Eloseily et al [24], calculated using ferritin, platelet count, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and fibrinogen.

Both scores were calculated retrospectively from available baseline data. For the M-score, baseline fibrinogen values were unavailable in a small number of patients, precluding M-score calculation in those cases; these were treated as missing values.

Data collection and Biomarker assessment

Collected variables included age, sex, disease phenotype, clinical manifestations, and laboratory parameters were assessed at baseline (week 0), week 1, week 4, and month 3. Week 0 was defined as the start of remission-induction therapy, which usually corresponded to glucocorticoid initiation at our institution. TCZ exposure was defined as the addition of TCZ during this induction phase.

Evaluated parameters included:

•Inflammatory markers: CRP and erythrocyte sedimentation rate

Inflammatory markers: CRP and erythrocyte sedimentation rate

•Haematologic parameters: white blood cell count, neutrophil count, haemoglobin, and platelet count

Haematologic parameters: white blood cell count, neutrophil count, haemoglobin, and platelet count

•Liver function: AST, alanine aminotransferase

Liver function: AST, alanine aminotransferase

•MAS-related markers: ferritin, fibrinogen, triglycerides, lactate dehydrogenase, and D-dimer

MAS-related markers: ferritin, fibrinogen, triglycerides, lactate dehydrogenase, and D-dimer

•Cytokines: IL-6, IL-18, and IL-2 receptor

Cytokines: IL-6, IL-18, and IL-2 receptor

Derived indices included the week-1/baseline CRP ratio, week-1/baseline ferritin ratio, week-4/baseline ferritin ratio, the IL-18/CRP ratio, and the IL-18/IL-6 ratio.

Baseline IL-18 levels were measured using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, and IL-6 levels were measured using a chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay before biologic therapy [18,[25], [26], [27]]. Treatment-related variables included timing of TCZ initiation, early CRP response, CID achievement at month 3, and MAS development after TCZ initiation. CID was defined according to the 2024 EULAR/PReS recommendations [3].

Treatment protocols

Treatment strategies followed institutional clinical practice and were individualised according to disease severity, organ involvement, and treatment response. Initial treatment typically consisted of high-dose glucocorticoids (prednisolone 0.5-1.0 mg/kg/d or equivalent), with or without intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (500-1000 mg/d for 3 days). Additional therapies included immunosuppressive agents (calcineurin inhibitors [cyclosporine or tacrolimus] and methotrexate), biologic agents (TCZ and infliximab), and cytotoxic agents (intravenous cyclophosphamide and etoposide), introduced according to disease activity and treatment response. Canakinumab was administered in 1 patient for disease relapse.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were as follows: (i) Week-4 CRP normalisation: Defined as CRP <0.3 mg/dL at week 4 after treatment initiation. (ii) Month-3 CID: Defined as CRP <0.3 mg/dL while receiving prednisolone ≤10 mg/d or ≤0.2 mg/kg/d at month 3.

Secondary outcomes: post-TCZ MAS events included new-onset MAS or clinically significant MAS-related deterioration requiring treatment escalation/discontinuation after TCZ initiation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarised as medians (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test; categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed for MAS prediction, and area under the curves (AUCs) with 95% CIs were calculated. Multivariable logistic regression using Firth’s penalised method was applied for primary outcomes, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Given the limited number of outcome events, model covariates were restricted to key baseline variables to avoid overfitting and preserve model stability. The primary multivariable models adjusted for early TCZ exposure, initial prednisolone dose, and baseline CRP; sensitivity analyses additionally examined models including either the modified Pouchot score or baseline MAS status. Analyses were conducted using complete cases. All tests were 2-sided, with P < .05 considered significant. Analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2) and Python (version 3.10).

Ethical considerations

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of Kurashiki Central Hospital (approval number 4805). Informed consent was obtained using an opt-out approach.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics, baseline clinical features, and treatment characteristics

During the study period, 69 patients with Still disease were identified. Fifteen were excluded mainly because initial treatment at referring institutions resulted in missing week-0 CRP or early follow-up laboratory data. The final analytic cohort comprised 54 patients who initiated remission-induction therapy at our institution, although some variables remained missing. Twelve patients (22.2%) received TCZ as initial induction therapy (TCZ induction group), whereas the remaining 42 patients (77.8%) comprised the non-TCZ induction group (Fig 1). Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics were broadly comparable between groups (Table 1). Median age (50.0 vs 67.5 years, P = .261) and sex distribution (male 28.6% vs 8.3%, P = .254) did not differ significantly. Baseline disease activity, assessed by systemic score and modified Pouchot score (median 5 and 6 in both groups, respectively), MAS/sJIA (MS) score (median −0.640 vs −0.775, P = .582), and H-score (median 148.5 vs 151.0, P = .901), was similar between groups (all P > .05). At baseline, 66.7% of patients in the TCZ group and 69.0% in the non-TCZ group fulfilled the 2016 EULAR/ACR/PRINTO criteria for MAS (P = 1.000). Regarding treatment characteristics, patients in the TCZ induction group received significantly higher initial prednisolone doses than those in the non-TCZ group (median 57.5 vs 40.0 mg/d, P = .024; 1.015 vs 0.706 mg/kg/d, P = .002), and intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy was administered more frequently (91.7% vs 52.4%, P = .020). The use of concomitant immunosuppressive agents, including cyclosporine, tacrolimus, and methotrexate, did not differ significantly between groups (all P > .05). Methotrexate was initiated earlier in the TCZ group (median day 9 vs day 21, P = .008), whereas cyclosporine and tacrolimus initiation timing did not differ significantly (P = .590 and P = .333). Among the 12 patients who initiated TCZ, 10 continued for at least 3 months.

In multivariable analyses using Firth’s penalised logistic regression (Table 3), the primary models adjusted for initial prednisolone dose and baseline CRP, and sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for either the modified Pouchot score or baseline MAS status. The association of early TCZ exposure with short-term outcomes differed by time point.

Week 4 CRP Negativity

In the primary model adjusting for initial prednisolone dose and baseline CRP, early TCZ exposure was not significantly associated with CRP normalisation at week 4 (OR: 4.00, 95% CI: 0.76-18.86; P = .104). This finding remained consistent in sensitivity analyses adjusting for disease severity or baseline MAS status (all P > .05). No other covariates were significantly associated with week 4 CRP normalisation.

Month 3 CID

Early TCZ exposure was significantly and robustly associated with CID achievement at month 3 across all models. In the primary model, early TCZ exposure showed a strong positive association (OR: 9.73, 95% CI: 1.25-77.50; P = .030), although CIs were wide. This association remained statistically significant after additional adjustment for disease severity (OR: 10.21, 95% CI: 1.20-86.91; P = .034) and baseline MAS status (OR: 9.50, 95% CI: 1.20-75.19; P = .033). Initial prednisolone dose showed a trend towards lower odds of CID achievement (primary model: OR: 0.09, 95% CI: 0.00-1.37; P = .080). Neither baseline CRP, modified Pouchot score, nor MAS at baseline were independently associated with month 3 CID.

Baseline characteristics of TCZ-treated patients with and without MAS (Supplementary Table S1)

TCZ was administered as remission-induction therapy in 19 episodes: 12 as initial induction and 7 as re-induction at relapse (1 patient received TCZ at both time points). Among these, 9 of 12 initial inductions (75.0%) and 5 of 7 reinductions (71.4%) resulted in continued therapy, whereas 3 (25.0%) and 2 (28.6%), respectively, discontinued TCZ within 14 days of the first infusion due to clinical and laboratory features suggestive of MAS, including worsening cytopenia, marked hyperferritinaemia, and coagulopathy. In most episodes, TCZ was initiated after clinical stabilisation of baseline MAS; 1 patient received TCZ during active, uncontrolled MAS before complete stabilisation. Baseline characteristics of the 19 TCZ induction episodes are summarised in Supplementary Table S1; longitudinal changes following TCZ initiation are presented in Supplementary Table S2, and overall treatment patterns in Supplementary Table S3.

Early dynamic biomarkers predicting MAS after TCZ induction

At baseline, patients who subsequently developed MAS had numerically higher CRP levels (median 14.96 vs 7.33 mg/dL, P = .500), and ferritin levels (median 4171.0 vs 4008.5 ng/mL, P = .754), although these differences were not statistically significant. Baseline ferritin, IL-18, IL-6, AST, fibrinogen, triglycerides, and derived cytokine ratios were also comparable between groups (all P > .05). At week 1 after TCZ initiation, patients who subsequently developed MAS showed significantly higher CRP ratios (median 62.21% vs 9.79%, P = .014) and ferritin ratios (median 65.25 vs 30.14, P = .00499). The week-4/baseline ferritin ratio also differed significantly between groups (237.08 vs 16.17, P = .0194). ROC analyses (Table 4, Fig 2) showed that the week-1/baseline ferritin ratio had the highest discriminative performance for subsequent MAS (AUC: 0.914, 95% CI: 0.745-1.000), followed by the week-1/baseline CRP ratio (AUC: 0.877, 95% CI: 0.629-1.000). The week-4/baseline ferritin ratio also showed good discriminative ability (AUC: 0.862, 95% CI: 0.533-1.000). The week-1 CRP level showed comparatively lower performance (AUC: 0.769, 95% CI: 0.414-1.000). The optimal cut-off for the week-1/baseline ferritin ratio was 41.19, yielding 100% sensitivity and 78.6% specificity. For the week-4/baseline ferritin ratio, an optimal cut-off of 115.25 yielded 80% sensitivity and 100% specificity. For the week-1/baseline CRP ratio, an optimal cut-off of 61.97% achieved 80% sensitivity and 100% specificity. For week-1 CRP level, a cut-off of 3.94 mg/dL yielded 80% sensitivity and 76.9% specificity. Baseline cytokine-related parameters did not show discriminative ability in this cohort.

DISCUSSION

Overall interpretation

In this single-centre cohort, which included a high proportion of patients with MAS at treatment initiation, TCZ induction was associated with effective control of systemic inflammation and CID achievement in a subset of patients. These findings suggest that TCZ may be a useful component of induction therapy in severe systemic disease, although careful patient selection and close monitoring remain essential.

Principal findings

Our findings suggest that TCZ induction may facilitate early disease control compared with non-TCZ strategies. In multivariable analysis, early TCZ exposure was independently associated with CID at month 3. However, CIs were wide, reflecting the limited sample size and statistical uncertainty; these results should therefore be interpreted cautiously. The most clinically actionable finding was the prognostic value of early inflammatory marker dynamics. Both the week-1/baseline ferritin ratio and the week-1/baseline CRP ratio differentiated patients who subsequently developed MAS from those who did not. These findings suggest that insufficient early suppression of CRP and ferritin reflects inadequate control of systemic inflammation within the first week and identifies patients at increased risk of MAS, whereas baseline cytokine-related parameters did not show early discriminative ability.

Comparison with prior studies: predictors of MAS, TCZ, and disease heterogeneity

Previous cohort studies have consistently shown that MAS risk in Still disease is closely associated with markers of heightened systemic inflammation. Elevated ferritin, cytopenias, coagulopathy, and organ involvement—including neurologic manifestations, lymphadenopathy, and abdominal pain—have been repeatedly reported as features associated with MAS and adverse outcomes [11,28]. These studies support the concept that MAS arises preferentially in patients with high inflammatory burden.

Although specific predictors vary across cohorts, a unifying theme is that persistent and insufficiently controlled systemic inflammation underlies MAS risk [11,[28], [29], [30]]. Our findings extend prior observations by showing that early CRP and ferritin kinetics after initiation of systemic therapy can help identify patients at increased risk of subsequent MAS. This observation is consistent with reports highlighting the prognostic value of dynamic inflammatory markers [30]. Importantly, our results should not be interpreted as suggesting that ferritin or CRP alone is sufficient for MAS prediction. Current recommendations emphasise that MAS risk should be assessed using a combination of clinical features and laboratory parameters, together with their evolution over time [3]. In our cohort, early dynamic changes in ferritin and CRP were more informative than baseline biomarker levels for identifying patients at risk of subsequent MAS. These findings support the importance of serial monitoring rather than reliance on single baseline measurements alone. In particular, the rate of ferritin and CRP decline after treatment initiation showed a stronger association with later MAS development than the early dynamics of other inflammatory parameters. These findings suggest that, within the context of comprehensive clinical and laboratory monitoring, early ferritin and CRP kinetics may represent a practical indicator of insufficient inflammatory control.

An important framework for interpreting these findings is the growing recognition that Still disease is clinically and biologically heterogeneous. Multicentre cohort studies using hierarchical clustering and multidimensional analyses have identified distinct phenotypes with differences in systemic manifestations, inflammatory marker profiles, prognosis, and susceptibility to severe complications, including MAS [31,32]. Differences in cytokine dominance—most notably between IL-6–weighted articular phenotypes and IL-1/IL-18–driven systemic phenotypes—appear to influence disease expression, treatment response, and MAS risk [1,33]. Patients with predominantly systemic inflammatory features are more likely to develop MAS, whereas those with arthritis-dominant disease tend to follow a different course [34]. In our cohort, however, the limited number of patients with available baseline IL-18 and IL-6 measurements may have constrained our ability to fully characterise cytokine-based phenotypes. Nevertheless, the observation that early ferritin and CRP dynamics—rather than baseline cytokine levels—were associated with subsequent MAS suggests that treatment response kinetics may provide complementary prognostic information beyond static baseline phenotyping.

In addition, cytokine profiling analyses from a subanalysis of a randomised controlled trial of TCZ suggested that patients with higher baseline interferon-γ and IL-1β levels, as well as persistently elevated interferon-γ and IL-18 during follow-up, are less likely to respond to IL-6 blockade [35]. These observations further support the concept of cytokine-informed stratification in Still disease. Our findings complement this by suggesting that early treatment response, as reflected by ferritin and CRP kinetics, may help identify patients in whom systemic inflammation remains insufficiently controlled despite TCZ therapy. In such patients, careful clinical reassessment and close monitoring of multiple inflammatory parameters may be warranted to facilitate earlier recognition of evolving MAS.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include detailed longitudinal biomarker assessment, systematic application of validated MAS criteria, and multivariable adjustment for potential confounders. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the retrospective single-centre design and clinician-driven treatment selection limit causal inference and introduce potential treatment-selection bias. Second, the small sample size (54 patients, 19 TCZ induction episodes, and 5 MAS events) limits statistical power and precision of effect estimates. Third, IL-18 and IL-6 measurements were unavailable for all patients, restricting cytokine-based analyses. Fourth, current MAS classification criteria may have reduced sensitivity under IL-6 inhibition due to suppression of acute-phase reactants, potentially delaying recognition of MAS in TCZ-treated patients [14,22]. Finally, validation in larger prospective cohorts is required before clinical implementation.

Clinical implications and future directions

Within a treat-to-target framework, early TCZ induction can be effective for rapid systemic inflammatory control and may facilitate earlier glucocorticoid tapering in selected patients. However, this potential benefit must be balanced against the need for vigilant monitoring for MAS. Early ferritin and CRP kinetics provide clinically accessible signals during TCZ induction. Marked early suppression of ferritin and CRP supports continuation of TCZ with cautious steroid tapering, whereas persistent elevation or an inadequate decline should prompt urgent reassessment for evolving MAS and consideration of treatment escalation, including IL-1 blockade or calcineurin inhibition, in line with current guideline recommendations [3]. Future prospective studies integrating early CRP dynamics with cytokine profiling, including IL-18, and developing MAS criteria adapted to IL-6–inhibited states are needed to validate risk thresholds and guide safer biologic sequencing in Still disease.

CONCLUSIONS

TCZ is an effective induction therapy for Still disease in routine clinical practice, facilitating rapid suppression of systemic inflammation and higher rates of CID at month 3 under low-dose glucocorticoid therapy. Importantly, higher week-1 ferritin and CRP ratios after TCZ initiation were associated with subsequent MAS, with the ferritin ratio showing the highest discriminative performance. These findings highlight early inflammatory marker kinetics as clinically accessible indicators to support safer timing of TCZ induction and timely escalation of therapy when indicated. Larger prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and refine risk-stratified treat-to-target management.
