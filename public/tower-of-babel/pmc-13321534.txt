Current status and influencing factors of digital literacy among clinical pediatric nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xianghui Yu, Shaoxue Zhan, Cheng Yang, Yanbo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the in-depth advancement of digital medical transformation, the digital literacy of clinical pediatric nurses has become a key factor affecting the quality and efficiency of pediatric nursing services. Given the unique vulnerabilities of pediatric patients—including limited expressive ability, rapid clinical deterioration, and the need for family-integrated care—understanding and enhancing digital literacy in this specialty is particularly critical. This study aims to investigate the current status of digital literacy among clinical pediatric nurses and identify its influencing factors.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted at a tertiary Grade A children’s hospital in China using convenience sampling. Of 300 questionnaires distributed, 284 were included in the final analysis (response rate: 94.67%). The self-designed “Digital Literacy Scale for Clinical Pediatric Nurses” (with good reliability and validity, including exploratory factor analysis confirming a four-factor structure) was used for data collection.ResultsA total of 284 clinical pediatric nurses were included. The total digital literacy score was 68.95 ± 9.02 points, with an overall moderate level. Age, educational level, professional title and work experience were moderately positively correlated with digital literacy scores (r = 0.426 ~ 0.478, all P < 0.001), and recent digital technology training was weakly positively correlated (r = 0.395, P < 0.001). Given observed collinearity between professional title and work experience (r = 0.782), a composite “Career Seniority” variable was constructed for the regression model. Multiple linear regression analysis identified four independent positive predictors of digital literacy: age (β’ = 0.159, P < 0.001), educational level (β’ = 0.281, P < 0.001), Career Seniority (β’ = 0.215, P < 0.001), and recent digital technology training (β’ = 0.370, P < 0.001). The final regression model explained 37.0% of the variance in digital literacy scores (adjusted R² = 0.370, F = 34.892, P < 0.001), with recent digital technology training emerging as the strongest predictor.ConclusionThe overall level of digital literacy among clinical pediatric nurses is moderate. These findings highlight the importance of both experiential factors (age, career seniority) and modifiable factors (educational level, recent training) in shaping digital literacy. The strong predictive role of recent training suggests that training may be an important modifiable correlate of digital literacy, warranting evaluation in future interventional studies. Healthcare institutions should consider developing structured, scenario-based training programs and personalized learning pathways to support the continuous development of digital literacy in pediatric nursing practice.

Background

With the in-depth integration of the new generation of information technology and the healthcare field, digital transformation has become the core driving force for promoting the high-quality development of medical services [1]. As a key component of medical services, the process of digital development in clinical nursing is directly related to patients’ diagnosis and treatment experience as well as medical safety [2]. Pediatric patients have unique clinical characteristics such as rapid changes in condition, limited expression ability, and poor treatment compliance, which impose greater demands on the accuracy, timeliness, and individualization of nursing services [3]. The application of digital technologies in clinical pediatric nursing, including electronic medical record systems, intelligent monitoring devices, remote nursing platforms, and pediatric-specific health management applications, can effectively break through the limitations of traditional nursing models [4]. By real-time collection of children’s vital sign data, accurate tracing of diagnosis and treatment information, and convenient implementation of health education and follow-up, these technologies significantly improve the efficiency and quality of nursing work, and provide full-cycle, refined nursing services for children [5]. Against this backdrop, clinical pediatric nurses, as the direct practitioners of digital technologies, their digital literacy has become an important indicator for measuring the level of pediatric nursing services, and even a core element for adapting to the development of digital healthcare and ensuring the safety of pediatric nursing.

Despite the broad application prospects of digital technologies in pediatric nursing, challenges persist in clinical practice, including the uneven distribution of nurses’ digital competencies and the non-standardized application of these technologies. Such issues, to some extent, impede the full realization of the potential benefits of digital nursing [6]. The unique nature of pediatric care—characterized by patients’ limited expressive abilities, rapid changes in clinical condition, and the need for family-integrated care—necessitates that nurses possess not only fundamental operational skills but also advanced digital literacy. This includes the ability to make clinical judgments based on digital data, safeguard children’s privacy, and provide digital health guidance tailored to pediatric populations [7, 8]. Moreover, inadequate digital literacy in this setting may lead to delayed recognition of critical events, misinterpretation of monitored data, and breaches of confidentiality, directly impacting pediatric patient safety.

As noted in recent study [9], research on the digital literacy of clinical pediatric nurses remains in its infancy. A comprehensive understanding of the current state of their digital literacy is lacking. More importantly, there has been insufficient in-depth analysis to identify the key factors influencing the development and enhancement of this digital literacy—including individual nurse characteristics, organizational training systems, the technological practice environment, and management incentive structures. To date, no previous study has systematically investigated the current status and modifiable correlates of digital literacy specifically among clinical pediatric nurses, making this work both timely and necessary. Accurately identifying these determinants is essential for the precise design of effective educational interventions and the optimization of digital nursing practice frameworks. Without a clear understanding of these core influences, improvement strategies are likely to lack specificity, thereby limiting their effectiveness in enhancing the digital literacy of pediatric nurses.

Therefore, a systematic investigation into the current status and influencing factors of digital literacy among clinical pediatric nurses is warranted. By clarifying these dimensions, this study aims to address existing gaps in the literature, thereby providing both a theoretical foundation for the digital advancement of pediatric nursing and practical evidence base for healthcare institutions. The findings are intended to inform the development of targeted, personalized training programs and to guide the optimization of digital nursing management strategies. Ultimately, this research seeks to offer actionable insights to support ongoing digital transformation and contribute to the high-quality development of pediatric nursing services.

Methods

Ethical approval and study design

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (approval number: 202602053-1) and was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [10]. Prior to the study, all potential participants were fully informed of key information including the study purpose, research content, data collection methods, privacy protection measures, and research significance. The questionnaire survey was only conducted after obtaining written or electronic informed consent from the participants. A cross-sectional survey design was adopted in this study, and relevant data on the digital literacy of clinical pediatric nurses in digital technology application were systematically collected through questionnaires. The main study was conducted from March to May 2025.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using the sample size formula for cross-sectional surveys [11]: n = Z²α/2×P(1-P)/d², where Z²α/2 was 1.96 (95% confidence level) and the allowable error d was 0.05. Based on the results of a preliminary pre-survey and relevant literature reports [12, 13], the expected proportion P of nurses with moderate or higher levels of digital literacy was estimated to be 0.6. This estimate was primarily derived from a preliminary pre-survey (n = 35) conducted three months prior to the main study, in which approximately 62% of participating pediatric nurses scored above the midpoint on the preliminary version of the digital literacy scale. Substituting these values into the formula yielded a minimum required sample size of 231 participants. The calculation was based on estimating the proportion of nurses with a moderate or higher level of digital literacy, which was a key descriptive aim of this study, as a basis for ensuring an adequate sample to support the subsequent regression analyses. To account for an anticipated 10% rate of invalid or incomplete questionnaires, the target sample size was adjusted upward to 255 cases. Accordingly, a minimum of 255 clinical pediatric nurses was determined necessary to ensure adequate representativeness and to support robust statistical conclusions.

Study population

Convenience sampling was used to select clinical pediatric nurses from a tertiary children’s hospital in China (classified as “Grade A” in the Chinese hospital grading system, indicating the highest level of medical care, teaching responsibilities, and research capabilities) as the research subjects. Inclusion criteria: (1) Holding a nurse’s practicing qualification certificate and being registered and on duty; (2) Engaging in clinical pediatric nursing work. Exclusion criteria: (1) Pediatric nurses in the period of further study, internship, or standardized training; (2) Having not directly participated in clinical nursing work in the past 6 months (e.g., long-term sick leave, maternity leave, administrative secondment, etc.); (3) Refusing to participate or being unable to complete the questionnaire. By clarifying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the study population was focused on the core research objects, and the interference of irrelevant variables on the research results was reduced.

Questionnaire

The research instrument was a self‑designed “Digital Literacy Scale for Clinical Pediatric Nurses”. The development of the scale followed a structured process comprising literature review, framework construction, item generation, expert consultation, and a pre‑survey.

First, a systematic review of the literature on digital nursing, health informatics competencies, and pediatric nursing was conducted to identify relevant domains and potential items [14–16]. Based on this review and the contextual considerations of clinical pediatric nursing practice, an initial conceptual framework was established, encompassing five dimensions: (1) basic demographic and occupational information; (2) perception of digital technology and learning willingness; (3) digital literacy in technology application; (4) perceived problems and barriers; and (5) preferences for countermeasure needs.

Subsequently, an expert panel was convened to assess content validity. The panel comprised five specialists: two senior pediatric nursing managers (each with over 20 years of clinical and administrative experience), one pediatric nursing educator responsible for the hospital’s nursing curriculum, and two digital health specialists (one specializing in clinical informatics and the other in medical software development). These experts participated in two rounds of review. They were asked to rate the relevance and clarity of each item and to provide qualitative feedback. The criteria for item revision were as follows: any item with an Item-Level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) below 0.78 was automatically revised or removed, and items were modified based on consolidated expert feedback to enhance their clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness. The expert panel demonstrated strong authority, with a mean authority coefficient (Cr) of 0.92, calculated based on the experts’ self-rated familiarity with the topic and the judgment criteria used in their evaluations, a common indicator in Delphi studies.

Finally, a pre-survey was conducted with 20 pediatric nurses who met the inclusion criteria. The primary purpose of this pre-survey was to assess the face validity, clarity, and practical applicability of the questionnaire items, leading to minor wording refinements. Item analysis was performed, and reliability was assessed. The final version of the questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.912 for the overall scale and Cronbach’s α coefficients for the individual dimensions ranging from 0.823 to 0.896. The Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) was 0.93, and the I-CVI values ranged from 0.83 to 1.00. According to established criteria, an S-CVI of 0.90 or above is considered indicative of excellent content validity [17].

Psychometric validation

To further assess structural validity, we conducted exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on the 15 items of the digital literacy dimension (i.e., excluding demographic items) using the main study sample (n = 284). Principal axis factoring with promax rotation was performed. The Kaiser‑Meyer‑Olkin measure was 0.887, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 1892.4, df = 105, P < 0.001). EFA confirmed a four‑factor structure corresponding to the four pre‑specified sub‑dimensions: (a) operational competence of digital nursing tools (5 items, factor loadings 0.68–0.85), (b) digital information processing and analysis competence (4 items, loadings 0.62–0.79), (c) innovation competence in digital technology application (3 items, loadings 0.71–0.88), and (d) digital safety and ethical literacy (3 items, loadings 0.65–0.82). The four factors collectively explained 68.4% of the total variance. Convergent validity was assessed using average variance extracted (AVE) for each factor: operational competence (AVE = 0.62), information processing (AVE = 0.58), innovation competence (AVE = 0.65), and safety/ethical literacy (AVE = 0.60). All AVE values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.50, indicating adequate convergent validity. These results support the structural validity of the scale.

Specific scoring rules and content of the questionnaire

The questionnaire consisted of items covering five dimensions, with specific scoring rules as follows:

Scoring Rules: Items in the dimensions of perception of digital technology and learning willingness (7 items), operational competence with digital nursing tools (5 items), digital information processing and analysis competence (4 items), innovation competence in digital technology application (3 items), and digital safety and ethical literacy (3 items) were scored using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely inconsistent, 2 = somewhat inconsistent, 3 = average, 4 = somewhat consistent, 5 = completely consistent). The total score for each dimension was calculated as the sum of its constituent items, with higher scores indicating a higher level of the corresponding ability or perception. Items related to problems, barriers, and countermeasure needs were treated as categorical variables and used only for descriptive statistics; they were not included in the dimension scoring. The total questionnaire score ranged from 22 to 110. Based on the distribution of possible scores, digital literacy levels were categorized as low (≤ 44 points), medium (45–88 points), or high (> 88 points).

Specific Content: The basic information dimension comprised 8 items covering demographic and occupational characteristics (e.g., gender, age, educational background, professional title). The perception of digital technology and learning willingness dimension contained 7 items. The digital literacy in digital technology application dimension contained 15 items, subdivided into four sub-dimensions as described above. All items in these dimensions employed the 5-point Likert scale.

Data collection

Prior to the survey, all investigators received standardized training on the research purpose, questionnaire administration, informed consent procedures, and quality control protocols. Data were collected using a combination of online and offline methods to maximize participation. For online collection, a questionnaire link was generated via the Wenjuanxing platform. The first page of the link presented complete informed consent information; clicking “Agree to Participate” constituted electronic informed consent, after which participants could access the questionnaire. For offline collection, investigators distributed paper questionnaires and written informed consent forms on-site to nurses less familiar with online platforms. After obtaining signed informed consent, investigators provided standardized instructions and remained available to answer questions without offering suggestive guidance. All responses were collected anonymously, and participants were assured that data would be used solely for academic research, with personal information protected through encrypted storage and restricted access.

Before completion, participants received detailed instructions regarding single-choice and multiple-choice questions, scale scoring criteria, and the importance of complete responses. Upon questionnaire retrieval, two investigators independently reviewed each submission for quality. Questionnaires were excluded if they had missing items (≥ 3 items), exhibited logical inconsistencies, or showed patterns of consecutive identical responses. For questionnaires with fewer than three missing items (n = 9), missing values were addressed using person-mean imputation, whereby each missing item was replaced with the mean score of the respondent’s completed items within the same dimension. This approach was considered appropriate given the small number of missing cases and the pattern of missingness, which appeared to be completely at random (MCAR), likely due to inadvertent skipping.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a two-tailed significance level set at α = 0.05. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median with interquartile range [M (Q1, Q3)]. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages [n (%)]. Descriptive statistics were first employed to characterize participant demographics, dimension scores, overall digital literacy levels, and the distribution of reported problems and countermeasure preferences. For univariate comparisons, participants were grouped according to demographic and occupational characteristics (e.g., age, educational level, professional title); group differences were examined using independent samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data with homogeneous variance, and Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-normally distributed data. Bivariate correlations between digital literacy scores and continuous or ordinal variables (e.g., age, work experience, training frequency) were assessed using Pearson or Spearman rank correlation coefficients as appropriate. To identify independent predictors of digital literacy, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with total digital literacy score as the dependent variable. Independent variables included those that demonstrated statistical significance (P < 0.05) in univariate analyses. Given the conceptual overlap and observed correlation between professional title and work experience (r = 0.782), multicollinearity was formally assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF). Initial VIF values for these two variables were 3.85 and 3.92, indicating moderate multicollinearity; therefore, a composite variable termed “Career Seniority” was created by standardizing and summing work experience (in years) and professional title (coded ordinally) to retain the conceptual contribution of both constructs while mitigating collinearity. This composite variable was entered into the final regression model alongside age, educational level, and recent digital technology training using the enter method. Model assumptions were verified through examination of residual plots (which confirmed approximate normality and homoscedasticity) and collinearity diagnostics, with all VIF values in the final model below 2.5, indicating no significant multicollinearity.

Results

A total of 300 questionnaires were distributed, with 284 valid responses recovered (effective recovery rate: 94.67%). As shown in Table 1, the 284 participating pediatric nurses exhibited distinct demographic and occupational characteristics. Females predominated (95.77%), with males accounting for 4.23%. The largest age group was 26–30 years (33.45%), followed by 20–25 years (27.46%). Regarding education, bachelor’s degree holders (54.93%) slightly outnumbered junior college graduates (45.07%). Professionally, senior nurses (39.44%) and nurses (34.51%) formed the majority, with smaller proportions of charge nurses (22.89%) and associate chief nurses and above (3.17%). In terms of work experience, the 6–10 years (33.80%) and 1–5 years (28.87%) groups were most represented. Department-wise, internal medicine (51.06%) had the highest proportion, followed by surgery (35.92%), and other departments (13.03%). Over one-third (36.97%) had not received digital technology training in the past three months, while 63.03% had.

Univariate analysis revealed no significant differences in digital literacy scores by gender or department type (P > 0.05). In contrast, age, educational level, professional title, work experience, and recent digital technology training were significantly associated with digital literacy scores (all P < 0.001). Specifically, scores increased with advancing age, higher educational level, elevated professional title, and longer work experience. Additionally, nurses who had received recent digital technology training achieved significantly higher digital literacy scores than those who had not.

As presented in Table 2, the total digital literacy score among the participating pediatric nurses was 68.95 ± 9.02 points, with an average score per item of 3.13 ± 0.41 points, indicating an overall moderate level of digital literacy. Based on the predefined cutoffs, 26 nurses (9.2%) scored in the low level, 236 (83.1%) in the medium level, and 22 (7.7%) in the high level, confirming that the overall level is predominantly moderate. Among the five dimensions, the operational competence of digital nursing tools had the highest average score per item (3.58 ± 0.57 points), followed by the perception of digital technology and learning willingness (3.48 ± 0.46 points) and digital security and ethical literacy (3.15 ± 0.59 points). The digital information processing and analysis competence had a relatively lower average score (3.14 ± 0.53 points), while the innovation competence in digital technology application had the lowest average score (2.92 ± 0.66 points), falling below the moderate level.

To identify the independent factors influencing digital literacy among pediatric nurses, multiple linear regression analysis was performed. Variables that demonstrated statistical significance (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis were entered as independent variables, with the total digital literacy score serving as the dependent variable. Given the conceptual overlap and observed correlation between professional title and work experience (r = 0.782, P < 0.001), multicollinearity was formally assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF). Initial VIF values of 3.85 and 3.92 for these two variables indicated moderate multicollinearity; therefore, a composite variable termed “Career Seniority” was constructed by standardizing and summing work experience and professional title to retain the conceptual contribution of both constructs while addressing collinearity concerns (Table 4).

The regression model was statistically significant (F = 34.892, P < 0.001) and explained 37.0% of the variance in digital literacy scores (adjusted R² = 0.370), indicating a moderate level of explanatory power (Table 5). Four variables emerged as significant independent positive predictors of digital literacy: age, educational level, Career Seniority, and receipt of digital technology training within the past three months (all P < 0.001). These findings suggest that while formal education and accumulated professional experience contribute meaningfully to digital literacy, targeted training interventions may play a particularly important role in enhancing nurses’ capabilities in this domain.

Discussion

This study found that the overall digital literacy score of clinical pediatric nurses was 68.95 ± 9.02 points, with an average item score of 3.13 ± 0.41 points, indicating a moderate level of digital literacy. This result is generally consistent with the findings of relevant studies [18–20] on nurses’ digital literacy, reflecting that with the popularization of digital medical construction in hospitals, pediatric nurses have initially mastered the basic digital nursing skills required for clinical work. However, the moderate overall level also indicates that there is significant room for improvement in their digital literacy. From the perspective of clinical practice, the moderate competency level may be related to the dual characteristics of pediatric nursing and digital technology: on the one hand, pediatric patients have special needs such as poor expression ability and rapid condition changes, which require nurses to not only master digital tool operation but also integrate digital data with pediatric clinical characteristics for accurate judgment [21]; on the other hand, the update speed of digital medical technology is fast, and some nurses may have difficulty keeping up with the pace of technological development, resulting in insufficient overall competency [22].

Among the five dimensions, operational competence with digital nursing tools achieved the highest mean item score (3.58 ± 0.57), likely due to the widespread use of electronic medical record systems, intelligent monitoring devices, and other digital tools in pediatric clinical settings. Hospitals typically provide systematic training on these common tools, resulting in relatively proficient operational skills among nurses [23]. The dimension of perception of digital technology and learning willingness also scored relatively high (3.48 ± 0.46), indicating that most pediatric nurses recognize the value of digital technology in enhancing nursing efficiency and quality and maintain a positive attitude toward learning—an important prerequisite for improving digital competencies. In contrast, innovation competence in digital technology application yielded the lowest mean item score (2.92 ± 0.66), falling below the moderate level. This may be attributed to current training programs that emphasize basic operational skills while providing limited guidance on innovative applications of digital technology [9, 24]. Furthermore, nurses are often occupied with heavy clinical workloads, leaving insufficient time and energy to explore new scenarios for integrating digital technology into pediatric nursing practice [15, 25]. The lack of role models or champions who demonstrate innovative use of digital tools in pediatric care may further discourage experimentation. Similarly, digital information processing and analysis competence scored relatively low (3.14 ± 0.53), reflecting difficulties in extracting key information from large volumes of digital data, performing in-depth analysis, and making clinical judgments—weaknesses that may constrain the decision-support function of digital technology in clinical nursing [16, 26]. Notably, the digital safety and ethical literacy dimension scored at a moderate level (3.15 ± 0.59 points). In the pediatric context, this finding carries particular clinical significance, as issues such as obtaining parental consent for digital health applications, safeguarding children’s privacy in electronic medical records, and ensuring secure data sharing across family-integrated care settings are paramount. This underscores the need for ethics-focused digital training modules that address the unique vulnerabilities of pediatric patients.

Mechanisms underlying the observed associations

Multiple linear regression analysis showed that age, educational level, career seniority (a composite measure of work experience and professional title), and recent digital technology training were independent positive predictors of digital literacy, and the model explained 37.0% of the variation in digital literacy scores.

Age and career seniority showed positive but relatively weaker predictive effects (β’ = 0.159 and 0.215, respectively). These findings may reflect that cumulative clinical exposure fosters pattern recognition and data-integration skills, which are core components of digital literacy. However, seniority alone does not guarantee digital proficiency without ongoing updates; older nurses who lack recent training opportunities may rely on outdated workflows, which could attenuate the observed association. The moderate correlation (r = 0.782) between professional title and work experience justified their combination into the composite Career Seniority variable, as these two factors are closely interrelated in clinical practice and have overlapping explanatory effects on digital literacy.

Educational level ranked second in predictive strength (β’ = 0.281). This is likely because baccalaureate programs increasingly embed health informatics competencies, critical appraisal skills, and evidence-based practice into their curricula, whereas diploma-level training may focus more on basic tool operation. Nurses with higher educational degrees may also have greater exposure to digital learning resources and be more accustomed to self-directed skill acquisition.

Recent digital technology training demonstrated the strongest predictive power (β’ = 0.370). This finding emphasizes that targeted, short-term interventions can yield measurable benefits, possibly by filling immediate knowledge gaps, boosting self-efficacy, and reinforcing safe and efficient use of digital tools. Notably, the positive correlation (r = 0.395) was weak-to-moderate, suggesting that while training is important, its quality, content, and delivery mode likely matter as much as its mere receipt. Current training may still overemphasize basic operations and underemphasize analytical and innovative skills, which may explain why the overall explained variance (adjusted R² = 0.370) remained moderate.

Concrete recommendations for practice and future research

The findings of this study have important implications for promoting the digital transformation of pediatric nursing and improving the quality of digital nursing services. The moderate overall level of digital literacy and the obvious differences among dimensions remind us that the improvement of pediatric nurses’ digital literacy should adopt a targeted and hierarchical approach, focusing on addressing identified weaknesses while building on existing strengths [27, 28]. For the dimensions with low scores such as digital technology application innovation competence and digital information processing and analysis competence, it is necessary to optimize the training content and form; for the key influencing factors identified by regression analysis such as training, educational level, career seniority and age, corresponding intervention measures should be formulated [29, 30]. In addition, the positive learning willingness of nurses reflected in the study provides a good foundation for the implementation of competency improvement strategies [31, 32]. By giving full play to the subjective initiative of nurses and combining with scientific external support measures, the overall level of pediatric nurses’ digital technology application digital literacy may be effectively improved, thereby promoting the in-depth integration of digital technology and pediatric nursing, and better serving the health of children.

Based on our mechanistic interpretations, we propose several concrete, actionable recommendations. For training programs, scenario-based modules should be developed to target the weakest dimensions: anonymized electronic health record data from real pediatric cases (e.g., sepsis trends, respiratory deterioration) can be used to teach digital information processing and analysis (pattern recognition, alert generation, clinical judgment), while protected time for “digital innovation huddles” or simulation-based workshops may foster innovation competence by allowing nurses to propose and test small-scale improvements to existing digital workflows, and ethics-focused sessions should address pediatric-specific issues such as parental consent for digital applications and secure data sharing in family-integrated care settings. For educational curricula, health informatics competencies should be integrated into both undergraduate nursing education and continuing professional development, moving beyond basic tool operation to include critical appraisal of digital data, privacy protection, and the use of digital tools for family-centered care. For clinical management, designating “digital literacy champions” on each pediatric ward—who receive advanced training and provide just-in-time peer support—may be beneficial; structured, recurring training (e.g., quarterly workshops) is preferable to one-time sessions, and linking training completion to professional development portfolios, along with age- and seniority-tailored learning pathways (advanced analytical modules for younger nurses, foundational updates on new technologies for more senior nurses), could further enhance outcomes. For future research, the moderate adjusted R² (37.0%) suggests that a substantial proportion of variance in digital literacy remains unexplained; therefore, future studies should incorporate unmeasured determinants such as organizational climate (e.g., administrative support for digital innovation), prior technology exposure (e.g., personal digital device use), specific training program characteristics (content, duration, delivery mode, interactivity), and individual psychological factors (e.g., technology readiness, self-efficacy). Longitudinal designs are needed to establish temporal relationships and causal pathways, multi-center sampling across different levels of healthcare institutions (primary, secondary, tertiary) would enhance generalizability, and given that exploratory factor analysis was performed on the same sample used for the main analyses, future independent samples should conduct confirmatory factor analysis to further validate the scale’s structural validity.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, regarding sample selection, the use of convenience sampling from a single tertiary children’s hospital in China (classified as “Grade A,” representing the highest level of care) limits the generalizability of our findings. The study population may not be representative of pediatric nurses working in primary hospitals, community health centers, or other healthcare settings where resources, training opportunities, and patient populations differ substantially. This selection bias may have resulted in an overestimation of the average level of digital literacy among pediatric nurses generally. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting the digital literacy status among nurses in similar tertiary hospital settings, and caution is warranted when extrapolating to broader populations. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences regarding the relationships between identified factors and digital literacy. While we identified several significant correlates, the directionality of these associations cannot be determined, and unmeasured confounding variables may have influenced the observed relationships. Third, although we performed exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to assess structural validity, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) could not be conducted on the same sample. Future studies with independent samples should perform CFA to confirm the four-factor structure. Additionally, while the scale demonstrated good content validity (S-CVI = 0.93) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.912), other forms of validity (e.g., criterion-related validity, test-retest reliability) were not assessed and should be examined in future research. Fourth, regarding statistical considerations, the final regression model explained only 37.0% of the variance in digital literacy scores (adjusted R² = 0.370), indicating that a substantial proportion (63.0%) of the variance remains unexplained by the factors examined in this study. This suggests the presence of important unmeasured determinants, such as organizational support for digital initiatives, prior technology exposure, personal innovativeness, or specific characteristics of training programs (e.g., content, duration, delivery mode), which should be explored in future research. Additionally, while missing data were minimal (n = 9, < 3% of questionnaires) and addressed using person-mean imputation under the assumption of missing completely at random, this approach may still introduce some degree of bias, and more sophisticated imputation methods could be considered in future studies with larger amounts of missing data. Future research should address these limitations by employing multi-center sampling strategies that include diverse levels of healthcare institutions to enhance representativeness and generalizability. Longitudinal study designs are needed to establish temporal relationships and explore causal pathways between potential determinants and digital literacy outcomes. Further refinement of the measurement instrument through CFA and cognitive interviewing would strengthen its validity, and inclusion of a broader range of potential predictors—such as organizational climate, technology access, and individual psychological factors—may improve the explanatory power of future models. Importantly, given the descriptive nature of this study, the implications drawn from our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating, and the potential interventions suggested require rigorous evaluation in well-designed experimental studies before definitive recommendations can be made.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found that the overall level of digital literacy among clinical pediatric nurses was moderate, with notable variation across dimensions. While operational competence with digital nursing tools and perceptions of digital technology and learning willingness were relatively strong, innovation competence in digital technology application and digital information processing and analysis competence emerged as weaker areas requiring attention. Age, educational level, career seniority (a composite measure of work experience and professional title), and recent digital technology training were identified as independent positive correlates of digital literacy. Among these, recent digital technology training demonstrated the strongest relative predictive strength within the context of the factors examined, although its contribution, like that of the other significant predictors, was modest. Based on our mechanistic interpretations, we propose concrete, actionable strategies: scenario-based training targeting analytical and innovative skills, integration of informatics competencies into nursing curricula, and tailored learning pathways for different age and seniority groups. These findings offer foundational insights that may inform future strategies aimed at enhancing digital literacy among pediatric nurses. The observed associations suggest several potential directions for future interventional research, including the development and evaluation of structured, scenario-based training programs targeting identified weak dimensions, as well as personalized learning and peer support mechanisms. Such hypothesis-generating implications, however, require rigorous testing in well-designed experimental studies before definitive recommendations can be made. Future research should also expand to multi-center samples encompassing diverse levels of healthcare institutions to enhance generalizability, employ longitudinal designs to explore causal relationships between identified factors and digital literacy, and integrate qualitative methods to further refine understanding of this multifaceted construct. Such efforts would contribute to robust evidence base for supporting the ongoing digital evolution of pediatric nursing services and, ultimately, improving the quality of pediatric healthcare delivery.
