Prospective Evaluation of Thiotepa Pharmacokinetics in Children and Young Adults Undergoing Allogeneic or Autologous Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation
Authors: Jordan Brooks, Shams Ismaeil, Maria‐Stephanie Hughes, Beth Apsel‐Winger, Ying Lu, Liusheng Huang, Christine Higham, Kristina Nelson, Susie Long, Ashish Gupta, Nahal Rose Lalefar, Eric Anderson, Michael A Pulsipher, Sung‐Yun Pai, Ron J Keizer, Christopher C Dvorak, Janel Long‐Boyle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThiotepa (TT) is often included in high‐dose chemotherapy prior to hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT); however, exposure–response relationships of TT and its primary metabolite, N,N',N''‐triethylenethiophosphoramide (TEPA), remain undefined. This was a prospective study enrolling children and young adults undergoing autologous and allogeneic HCT from multiple centers. Time–concentration data were modeled using nonlinear mixed effects modeling along with clinical covariates potentially impacting pharmacokinetics. Cumulative area‐under‐the‐curve (cAUC) for TT and TEPA were derived using post hoc individual estimates for clearance. The combined cAUCTT and cAUCTEPA were also evaluated, reflecting total alkylating exposure (cAUCtotal). The ratio of cAUCTT/cAUCTEPA was used to evaluate the efficiency of metabolic conversion of TT to TEPA. A total of 91 subjects were included with a median (range) actual body weight and age of 16.7 kg (4.6–110) and 4.5 years (0.23–22.5), respectively. The best fit model was a two‐compartment structure for TT, and a one‐compartment structure for TEPA. The conversion of TT to TEPA was found to be saturable at higher doses and may have important implications for optimizing exposure. Model‐based dosing, which accounts for age and body size, results in more predictive exposure as compared to conventional mg/kg dosing, particularly in the very young and obese. Next steps include evaluation of pharmacogenomics and exposure–response relationships to optimize dosing of TT in children.Keywords: hematopoietic cell transplantation, pediatric, population pharmacokinetics, thiotepa

Introduction

Thiotepa (TT) is an alkylating agent with potent immunosuppressive properties routinely incorporated into high‐dose combination conditioning prior to autologous and allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) to support stem cell engraftment. Despite increasing interest and application of TT in both the pediatric and allogeneic haploidentical HCT setting, the pharmacokinetics (PK) of TT remain largely uncharacterized, particularly in infants and young children. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Understanding the relationship between dose–exposure–response is critical for the optimal use of therapeutics and particularly impactful for drugs like TT with a narrow therapeutic index and high inter‐subject variability. Unfortunately, response to TT therapy is highly variable, and the therapeutic index remains poorly defined, allowing acute and long‐term drug‐related toxicity to persist as a prominent clinical problem.

In the setting of high‐dose combination chemotherapy and HCT, TT‐containing regimens have been associated with sinusoidal obstruction syndrome of the liver, pulmonary complications, and severe mucositis. 3 More recently it has been demonstrated that when included as part of pre‐transplant conditioning prior to HCT, TT increased the incidence of transplant associated thrombotic microangiopathy (TA‐TMA). 5 Studies in mice evaluating neurological effects of TT demonstrate that long‐term cognitive deficits may also result. 6 Work by our group and others have shown PK‐guided dosing of pre‐transplant conditioning to target optimal exposure pre‐transplant conditioning agents can significantly improve clinical outcomes in the setting of pediatric HCT including the alkylators busulfan and melphalan, along with the lymphodepleting agents fludarabine and anti‐thymocyte globulin. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13

TT undergoes metabolic conversion in the liver by the cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, CYP2B6 and CYP3A4, to the primary active metabolite N,N',N'' ‐triethylenethiophosphoramide (TEPA); both TT and TEPA possess alkylating activity. 14 TT and TEPA are reportedly equivalent in alkylating potential and the resulting immunosuppressive effects on the bone marrow and circulating T and NK cells. 14 Further detoxification of thiotepa and TEPA is mediated by glutathione conjugation. 14 Although inconsistent, dose‐dependent kinetics of thiotepa and TEPA have been described over doses ranging from 180 to 900 mg/m2, suggesting saturable clearance. 14 Renal elimination of TEPA is limited, accounting for less than 10% of an administered dose, with a single case report describing altered PK in the presence of moderate renal insufficiency. 14 , 15 , 16

The overarching goal of this work is to develop personalized dosing strategies for the use of TT in pediatric HCT to ensure safe and efficacious drug therapy in all patients. Patient‐specific covariates are expected to contribute substantially to inter‐individual variability in TT exposure in pediatric HCT recipients and thus clinical response and drug‐related toxicity. The primary objective of this work was to characterize the PK of systemic TT and TEPA in pediatric patients receiving TT as part of their preparative regimens prior to autologous and allogeneic HCT.

Methods

Study Population

This was a prospective PK study of thiotepa in children and young adults who underwent autologous or allogeneic HCT for a variety of malignant and nonmalignant pediatric disorders. Patients were eligible to participate in PK analysis if they were between 0 and 26 years of age, met protocol‐specific eligibility criteria for transplantation, and were to undergo an allogeneic or autologous HCT that included intravenous thiotepa as part of the conditioning regimen, irrespective of the dose or dosing regimen. Patients who received thiotepa given as a one time single dose or as several doses throughout conditioning were eligible to participate.Thiotepa PK data were collected between 2017 and 2023 at the University of California San Francisco Benioff Children's Hospital (n = 54), the University of Minnesota Masonic Children's Hospital (n = 12), UCSF Benioff Children's Hospital Oakland (n = 7), and Rady Children's Hospital of San Diego (n = 9). Additionally, nine infants were included from the multi‐center study evaluating thiotepa as part of pre‐transplant conditioning in infants newly diagnosed with severe combined immunodeficiency (PTCTC NMD 1801, CSIDE, NCT01346150). All local institutional review boards approved this study and written informed consent/assent was obtained from all patients. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT03609840.

PK Sampling

All patients underwent PK sampling following a single dose of TT. For patients undergoing double or triple tandem autologous transplants, PK was performed following a single occasion only. Thiotepa was administered intravenously over 1 to 2 h in all patients depending on the type of transplant (autologous or allogeneic). For the first 17 subjects that consented to PK analysis, intensive PK was performed. For a 1‐h infusion (allogeneic conditioning), blood samples for the quantification of TT and TEPA were collected at 1, 2, 4, and 24 h after the start of TT infusion. For a 2‐h infusion (autologous conditioning) blood samples were collected at 2, 3, 4, 6, and 24 h. In the remaining subjects (n = 74) a limited sampling strategy was implemented with one PK collection 0–2 and 2–24 h post end of infusion. All blood samples for each plasma PK time point were collected in a 0.5 mL spray‐dried K2EDTA microtainer tube, spun at 3000–4000 rpm for 10 min in a refrigerated centrifuge at 4°C, and frozen at −70°C within 30 min of drawing.

Bioanalysis of TT and TEPA

Plasma samples were analyzed for TT and TEPA using a validated liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry method, as previously described. 17 The TT and TEPA assay was linear in the range of 2.5 to 2000 ng/mL. During sample analysis, 17 sets of low‐, medium‐, and high‐quality control samples and 19 extra‐high‐quality control samples (for dilution) were analyzed. The mean accuracies (mean ± coefficient of variation) for TT were 98.9% ± 13.3%, 102.3% ± 7.6%, 94.9% ± 8.6%, and 99.9% ± 8.3% at low‐, medium‐, high‐, and extra‐high‐quality control levels, respectively. For TEPA the mean accuracies (mean ± coefficient of variation) were 104.0% ± 10.9%, 105.3% ± 8.6%, 94.3% ± 10.0%, and 105.6% ± 5.9% at low‐, medium‐, and high‐quality control levels, respectively.

Population PK Analysis

A nonlinear mixed effects modeling approach was used to describe the time course of TT and TEPA plasma concentrations utilizing NONMEM (v7.6) using the first‐order conditional estimation with interaction method to estimate PK parameters and variability. Data visualization and manipulation were performed in R (v4.3), and Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM was also used for tools including vpc and scm. For TT and TEPA concentrations which fell below the lower limit of quantification, the actual values were reported by the lab and utilized in model development. 18 Model selection was informed by objective function values, goodness‐of‐fit plots, a visual predictive check (VPC), and model estimation stability. Both TT and TEPA concentrations were modeled on log‐transformed concentration values and residual unexplained variability proportional to the concentration measurement was captured (additive on log‐scale).

Covariate Modeling

Clinical data were collected on the day of PK sampling prior to administration of TT to assess the influence of patient‐specific factors on TT and TEPA PK parameters. Continuous covariates included age, height, total body weight, body surface area, fat‐free mass (FFM) as estimated by the Al‐Sallami method, 19 creatinine clearance (CLcr), and markers of liver function including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and total bilirubin. In patients 0–16.99 years of age, CLcr was estimated by the modified bedside Schwartz equation (mL/min/1.73 m2) and, in young adults (17 years and older), by the Cockcroft–Gault equation using actual body weight (mL/min). Categorical covariates included biological sex, race, and ethnicity. Race was evaluated as indicated by the clinical site as either Asian, Black, Native American or Alaska Native, White, Other, or Not Recorded. Ethnicity was evaluated as either Hispanic or Latino, Not Hispanic or Latino, or unknown. Covariate analysis was performed using a stepwise forward additive approach followed by a stepwise backward elimination approach with an alpha of 0.05 and 0.001, respectively.

Simulations for AUC‐Specific Dosing

Based on the final population PK model, individual doses of TT were simulated to achieve the median cumulative area‐under‐the‐curve cAUCtotal (cAUCTT × number of doses) + cAUCTEPA × number of doses) of the observed data for the corresponding total number of doses administered over the course of pre‐transplant conditioning and reflecting total alkylating potential of both parent and metabolite. Two complementary simulation analyses were conducted: (1) population‐level dose curves characterizing the model‐predicted dose as a function of patient body weight across the clinically relevant weight spectrum, and (2) a stochastic clinical simulation comparing conventional flat‐dose regimens to therapeutic drug monitoring‐guided model‐informed precision dosing (TDM‐MIPD) using the mipdeval R package. 20 Run evaluation of iterative predictive performance based on historical datasets. R package version 0.1.0.9000. https://github.com/InsightRX/mipdeval).

Final Population PK Model

The final dataset included a total of 210 and 235 quantifiable concentrations for TT and TEPA, respectively. The final model parameters are presented in Table 2. The final model found to best fit these data was a two‐compartment structural model describing TT, a nonlinear conversion from TT to TEPA, and a one‐compartment structural model describing TEPA. Covariates retained in the final model were age (via maturation functions applied to clearance and volume of distribution parameters) and FFM (allometric scaling with exponents of 0.75 for clearance and 1.0 for volume terms). To account for age, two separate maturation function terms were evaluated: one applied to PK parameters related to CL (CLTT, CLTEPA, and Vmax) and one applied to the volume of distribution of TT. The maturation function for CL resulted in a dOFV of −14.71 (P < .001), and the maturation function for volume of distribution of the TT further improved fit by dOFV −17.80 (P < .001). For allometric scaling based on FFM, exponents 0.75 for CLTT and CLTEPA, and 1.0 for Vc, TT and Vp, TT. The volume of TEPA (Vm) was fixed to 1 L as it was not independently estimable from the available data; cAUCTEPA was derived from the cumulative metabolite compartment amount directly. The VPC of both parent and metabolite as seen in Figure 1 shows the distribution of the observed data is well captured across the majority of the PK time curve. There was some misspecification with the TT peak concentrations as demonstrated by the median raw data line and median prediction interval in the first bin, likely driven by the somewhat limited sampling strategy employed for this study. Exposure by Regimen

The median (IQR) observed cAUCtotal for study patients receiving conventional dosing was 51.0 mg h/L (IQR: 42.0–60.1) for the 5 mg/kg every 12 h × 2 doses and 140.6 mg h/L (112.5–164.6) for the 300 mg/m2 every 24 h × 3 doses, with substantial interpatient variability in both groups (Table 3). The ratio of TT‐to‐TEPA cAUC differed by regimen decreasing with dose from 1.0 to 0.5 in the 5 mg/kg every 12 h × 2 doses to 300 mg/m2 every 24 h × 3 doses, respectively.

Conventional Versus Exposure Targeted Dosing

In conventional weight‐ or BSA‐based dosing groups, cAUCtotal demonstrated substantial interpatient variability across all three regimens, with a coefficient of variation (CV) of 30%. Median cAUCtotal with conventional dosing was 54.8 mg h/L (IQR: 44.2–66.6) for 5 mg/kg every 12 h × 2 doses, 149 mg h/L (IQR: 120–183) for 10 mg/kg every 24 h × 3 doses, and 145 mg h/L (IQR: 119–175) for 300 mg/m2 every 24 h × 3 doses. Target attainment within ±20% of the pre‐specified cumulative cAUCtotal goal was 47% and 51% for conventional 5 mg/kg and 300 mg/m2 based regimens, respectively.

The model‐predicted dose group reduced cAUCtotal variability and improved target attainment across all regimens. The CV of cAUCtotal was reduced to approximately 13%, and 12% for the 5 mg/kg and 300 mg/m2 based regimens, respectively. Median cAUCtotal for the model‐predicted doses was 53.1 mg h/L (IQR: 48.6–57.3) and 142 mg h/L (IQR: 130–153). Target attainment within ±20% of the target improved to 83% and 90% for the 5 mg/kg and 300 mg/m2 based regimens, respectively (Table 4; Figure 2).

Discussion

The study population included in this analysis was purposely heterogeneous in order to provide a broad range of TT dose levels and regimens for evaluation. Our analysis aligns well with several prior studies describing the nonlinear kinetics of TT at higher doses in contrast those smaller studies evaluating a single dose level within a population. 21 , 22 The final structural model characterized TEPA disposition using a one‐compartment model, in contrast to the two‐compartment structure employed for TT. Although this configuration provided an adequate description of the available concentration–time data, identification of a two‐compartment model for TEPA may have been precluded by limited sampling density in the post‐distributive elimination phase. It is possible the current structural model overestimates cAUCTEPA in some patients, which would have implications for AUC‐based dose recommendations given TEPA contributes substantially to total alkylating potential. To assess this, additional PK samples collected in the elimination phase of drug clearance with futures studies will help to confirm the TEPA compartmental structure and improve the accuracy of cAUCTEPA estimations.

Beyond structural considerations, the covariate relationships retained in the final model also warrant discussion in the context of prior work. In an earlier population PK analysis of TT and TEPA, Huitema et al identified ALP and serum albumin as significant covariates influencing TT and TEPA clearance, respectively. In this study, inclusion of these covariates did not result in a statistically significant improvement in model fit as assessed by objective function value or goodness‐of‐fit diagnostics and were therefore excluded from the final model. While there may be little or no effect driven by these covariates, this discrepancy is more likely attributable to the limited variability within the current study population with most patients within or near the normal reference range for age at the time of PK sampling. These findings do not exclude the possibility that hepatic function or nutritional status may influence TT or TEPA disposition in patients with more markedly abnormal biochemical profiles, and this relationship warrants further evaluation in larger and more clinically heterogeneous populations.

The covariates retained in the final model, specifically FFM‐based allometric scaling and age‐dependent maturation, have direct and significant implications for considering dosing in vulnerable subpopulations. Particularly for children less than 2 years of age, the age‐maturation functions reduces clearance to approximately 15%–20% of mature values in the first few weeks of life. Conventional flat weight‐ or BSA‐based dosing, cannot account for this developmental ontogeny, and therefore may substantially overshoot the goal cAUCtotal and increase the risk of regimen‐related toxicity in the youngest patients. In individuals classified as overweight or obese, the divergence between total body weight and FFM produces proportional increases in cAUCtotal that go unaccounted for with conventional dosing strategies. The prospective integration of patient‐specific FFM and maturation‐informed clearance estimates into dose selection represents a clinically actionable strategy to reduce both over‐ and under‐exposure in these high‐risk subgroups and supports the broader application of model‐based dosing frameworks for TT individualization in pediatric HCT.

Several limitations of this analysis should be noted for this analysis. First, the final population PK model underlying these simulations was developed without the inclusion of pharmacogenomic data, which is a notable gap given substantial genetic variability which has been shown to contribute to the metabolism and distribution of thiotepa. Thiotepa is metabolized by CYP2B6 and CYP3A4/5 into the active metabolite TEPA and with further detoxified by glutathione S‐transferase, all of which have variants associated with intermediate to poor function that might alter the ratio of these agents. 23 Prospective integration of these genotypic information for the variants responsible for the activation and detoxification of both TT and TEPA may reduce unexplained variability and strengthen the foundation for model‐based dosing implementation.

Second, the current conventional dosing strategy of administering thiotepa over 1–3 days was not initially derived from PK‐guided data but from early phase I studies correlating CNS toxicity with higher single dose administration. 24 This clinically observed effect has not been prospectively evaluated and may be partially explained by increased dose and metabolic saturation. As concentrations approach Km, the conversion of parent to metabolite becomes saturable, producing disproportionately larger increases in the TT/TEPA ratio. This divergence was visible across the evaluated dosing simulations and consistent with our model structure incorporating Michaelis–Menten kinetics. This mechanism was described by Ekhart et al, demonstrating that consolidating a 4‐day fractionated regimen into a 2‐day unfractionated schedule results in a 13% increase in cAUCTT and a 21% reduction in cAUCTEPA. 16 Given IIV on both Vmax and CLTT further increased the TT/TEPA ratio at higher doses, the current empiric schedule may be introducing PK heterogeneity that covariate‐informed dosing alone cannot resolve. A more frequent, lower per dose strategy will maintain concentrations closer to the linear range, reduce variability in the TT/TEPA ratio distribution, and create natural inter‐dose sampling windows that are currently absent from standard regimens yet essential for the potential of future adaptive dosing strategies. Prospective, PK‐driven evaluation of the total number and size of each dose is warranted.

Third, the simulations presented in this analysis reflect achievement of the median cAUCtotal observed in the data for each dosing regimen. Defining the optimal cAUCtotal target for thiotepa to optimize clinical outcomes was not within the scope of the current analysis with goal targets presumably variable among different subgroups of patients. Exposure–response analysis is therefore a next critical next step of a subsequent planned analysis for our group. Additionally, the extent of between‐patient variability captured by the current model may limit how precisely a single covariate‐informed dose can achieve potentially narrow exposure targets, suggesting that therapeutic drug monitoring may be necessary to fully optimize dosing. However, the potential advantage of TDM is limited by practical feasibility, including assay availability among clinical sites for drug quantification and the temporal proximity of doses in conventional TT dosing schedules. Standard 12‐ or 24‐h dosing intervals of thiotepa schedules leave little opportunity for real‐time dose adjustment.

In summary, this work highlights key PK properties of thiotepa that can be employed to prospectively evaluate exposure–response relationships and optimize dosing across a variety of patient populations. The conversion of TT to TEPA was found to be saturable at higher doses and may have important implications for exposure optimization. Model‐based dosing, which accounts for age and body size, results in more predictive exposure compared with conventional mg/kg dosing, particularly in the very young and obese. Next steps include evaluation of genetic variants for their impact on thiotepa PK and subgroup analysis of exposure–response relationships to optimize dosing in both the allogeneic and autologous HCT setting.
