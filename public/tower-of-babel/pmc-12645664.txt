Patterns and characteristics of cranial nerve neuropathy in West Nile virus infection: a systematic review of cases reported globally
Authors: Abdalla Abu-Hammad, Leen Alshamasneh, Osama Abu-Hammad, Batool Albdour, Ibraheem Maraqa, Mustafa Alkhader, Douleh Al-Fatafta, Najla Dar-Odeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to review case reports/series on West Nile virus (WNV) infection-associated cranial nerve (CN) neuropathy to explore the associated patterns and characteristics.DesignA systematic review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses criteria.Data sourcesWe reviewed the literature in PubMed/Medline, Google Scholar and ScienceDirect databases to retrieve relevant case reports.Eligibility criteriaCase reports published in the past 25 years on CN neuropathy in WNV-infected patients, with no language or geographic restrictions.Data extraction and synthesisRetrieved data included patient demographics, disease presentation and treatment outcomes. Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to describe frequency and characteristics of CN neuropathies. Cross tabulation was performed to calculate statistical significance of association between patient characteristics, disease factors and treatment outcomes. Risk of bias was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool for case reports.ResultsA total of 30 case reports satisfied inclusion criteria. These studies described 42 cases that developed CN neuropathies as an outcome of WNV infection. Patients were in the main males (54.8%) and had a mean age of 52.5±15.5 years. The most frequently affected CNs were, in descending order, optic (n=21, 50.0%), facial (n=14, 33.3%) and abducent nerves (n=8, 19.0%). Age was significantly associated with optic nerve neuropathy (≥51 years) and facial palsy (<51 years) (p=0.005 and p=0.008, respectively). Regression analysis of patient and disease variables did not reveal any significant predictor for delay of CN neuropathy following the onset of illness. As for treatment outcomes, complete recovery was significantly associated with absence of comorbidities and administration of systemic antivirals (p=0.029 and p=0.041, respectively).ConclusionsCN neuropathy represents an important subset of neuroinvasive disease caused by WNV. There is a slight male preponderance among the patient population who are mostly middle aged. Optic and facial nerves are mostly affected. Most patients show complete recovery, particularly in the absence of chronic diseases. The role of systemic antivirals in improving disease outcomes should be further investigated. Early detection of CN neuropathies is recommended by routine CN screening and use of specific tools such as MRI and neurophysiological tests.Keywords: Adult intensive & critical care, Diagnostic microbiology, Head & neck surgery, INFECTIOUS DISEASES

Introduction

West Nile virus (WNV), a mosquito-borne flavivirus, was initially considered a relatively minor pathogen causing self-limiting viral infection. However, it emerged as a significant cause of viral encephalitis and other neuroinvasive diseases worldwide.1 While mosquito bites represent the major transmission route, other rare modes of transmission to humans include blood transfusions, organ transplants, breast feeding and intra-uterine exposure.2 Humans and other mammals are considered incidental or ‘dead-end’ hosts because they do not develop sufficient viral load to contribute to further transmission.

The first major outbreaks occurred in Romania and North America in the 1990s, where it rapidly became endemic. Since then, WNV has been reported in Africa, Europe, the Middle East, Asia and the Americas, causing periodic outbreaks.3 Clinical manifestations of WNV infection are variable. Patients are asymptomatic in 80% of cases; however, 20% develop West Nile fever, a self-limiting illness characterised by fever, headache, fatigue, myalgia and maculopapular rash.4 Neuroinvasive WNV disease affects <1% of cases and may manifest as encephalitis, meningitis and poliomyelitis.5 Patients who develop neuroinvasive disease may exhibit a mortality rate of up to 30%, depending on age and immunity.6 Although WNV is classically associated with encephalitis and meningitis, growing evidence suggests that it can directly or indirectly affect CNs, leading to a spectrum of significant neurological sensory and motor deficits.

The virus’s neurotropic nature allows it to invade and damage neurons, causing cranial neuropathies that may present as vision disturbances, facial paralysis and other manifestations. Understanding these manifestations is critical for early diagnosis, management and prevention of long-term neurological sequelae.

Given the potential for severe neurological impairment, understanding WNV’s neurotropic mechanisms is crucial for developing targeted treatments and neuroprotective strategies. This is particularly important when CN neuropathy is concerned. Several significant clinical implications are identified, affecting both acute disease management and long-term neurological outcomes. Despite the increasing recognition of these complications, knowledge gaps remain in understanding the exact mechanisms, prevalence and long-term effects of WNV-induced cranial neuropathies.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically and thoroughly review all case reports and series that described CN neuropathy in WNV infection around the world. We hope by this review to draw attention to diagnostic features and inform clinical decision-making, as early diagnosis and effective treatment are certainly required for improving prognosis and mitigating adverse disease outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This study is a systematic review of case reports and case series describing CN neuropathy in patients infected with WNV. The research question was developed using PEO statement (Patient, Exposure, Outcome) and formulated in the following research question: What are the characteristics of CN neuropathy (Outcome) in adult patients (Patients) affected with WNV infection (Exposure). The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (ID #CRD42024592778), and it was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.7

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted independently by two reviewers (ND-O and AA-H) across three electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar and ScienceDirect. The search was performed in November 2024 with a primary objective of identifying all relevant case reports and case series describing one or more CN neuropathies in association with WNV infection. The search terms included a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings related to WNV and CN neuropathy, as follows: “West Nile Virus”, “West Nile encephalitis”, “Cranial nerve “, “neuropathy”, “neuroinvasive disease”, “case report” and “case series”. Further, individual CN names were entered as keywords. Boolean operators (AND/OR/NOT) were used to refine the search queries, and filters were applied to include only articles published in peer-reviewed journals during the period 2000–2024 with no language restrictions. A detailed search strategy throughout the three databases is presented as online supplemental material.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers (ND-O and DA) conducted independently an initial screening of 1846 articles identified through the database searches. Titles and abstracts were reviewed to determine relevance. Full-text articles were then assessed for eligibility. Those that did not address CN neuropathy were excluded. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion.

After applying the eligibility criteria, 30 scholarly articles were selected, reporting on 42 patients with documented WNV-associated CN neuropathy.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included case reports and case series was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports and Case Series.8 Studies were assessed for clarity in reporting patient history, clinical presentation, diagnostic workup, interventions and outcomes. Studies missing some clinical data were discussed among reviewers before making the decision for inclusion.

Data synthesis

Results were synthesised using descriptive statistical analysis by SPSS V.25 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.25.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics were used to summarise key findings, including patient demographics, CNs involved, clinical manifestations, treatment modalities and outcomes. Linear regression analysis was employed to explore possible predictor variables for onset of CN neuropathy following viral disease onset. Statistical significance was calculated for two main associations; the first was the association between patient factors and types of CNs involved, and the second was the association between patient factors/treatment modalities and treatment outcomes. A p value of ≤0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical considerations

This study retrieved previously published case reports/case series. Further, it did not collect direct patient data. Therefore, institutional review board approval and patient consent were not required. However, all efforts were made to ensure accurate reporting and adherence to ethical guidelines for systematic reviews.9

None.

No studies reported neuropathy of CN I and CN VIII.

In a total of four (9.5%) cases, the diagnosis was not confirmed as laboratory testing was carried out after the resolution of the infection; however, the course of the disease was described by the authors as consistent with the clinical picture of WNV infection.15 17 18

Duration in days from viral illness onset to cranial nerve neuropathy

This duration was reported for a total of 36 cases. The mean time until onset of signs and symptoms of CN neuropathy was 17.58±14.41 days (range=1–40 days). CN II showed the longest range (n=27 cases, 1–40 days), followed by CN VII (n=6, 1–16 days), while others showed shorter durations as follows: CN V (n=2 cases, 2–4 days), CN VI (n=3 cases, 1–9 days), CN IX (n=1 case, 2 days), CN X (n=1 case, 2 days), CN XI (n=3 cases, 1–6 days) and CN XII (n=2 cases, 1–2 days).

Linear regression analysis explored possible predictor variables for this delay. It showed that all predictor variables were not significant except for the administration of intravenous acyclovir (p=0.025) (table 3). On the other hand, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was non-significant at p=0.207.

Clinical findings and association with existing co-morbidities

Reported cases had the following signs and symptoms (table 1): viral illness (n=33, 78.6%), vision problems (n=21, 50.0%), tinnitus (n=1, 2.4%), facial palsy (n=13, 31.0%), weakness of extremities (n=13, 31.0%), dysphagia (n=2, 4.8%), ophthalmoplegia/diplopia (n=8, 19.0%), bulbar palsy (n=2, 4.8%), decreased shoulder shrug (n=1, 2.4%) and dysarthria (n=1, 2.4%). Cross tabulation showed no significant association between clinical presentation and co-morbidities (p>0.05), indicating that clinical features are primary, specific and initiated by the viral infection itself, not by the associated co-morbidities.

Treatment

Treatment was described for 35 (83.3%) cases; however, 7 (16.7%) cases did not include the treatment modalities.1314 17,20 Patients were treated by one or more of the following modalities: topical steroids (n=2, 4.8%), systemic antivirals (n=11, 26.2%), systemic steroids (n=10, 23.8%), systemic antibiotics (n=14, 33.3%), supportive therapy (n=10, 23.8%) and immunoglobulins (n=3, 7.1%). Treatment outcomes were not reported for one case (2.4%).10 For the remainder of cases, a total of 22 (52.4%) cases showed recovery, while 9 (21.4%) cases showed partial recovery and 10 (23.8%) cases showed no recovery. For the purpose of statistical analysis, the unreported outcome was considered an indication of improvement and total recovery.

Statistical analysis of significance (table 4) indicated a significant association between recovery and absence of co-morbidities (p=0.029). Patients who showed no recovery were significantly associated with lack of administration of systemic antivirals (p=0.04). Finally, cases who received antibiotics demonstrated a significant association with partial recovery (p=0.008) (table 4).

Discussion

In the past two decades, several emerging zoonotic viral infections have caused global concern due to the associated serious outcomes involving humans.21,23 Among these, WNV has demonstrated wide geographic distribution and evolving epidemiological trends.24 Cranial nerve neuropathies are an under-recognised subgroup of neuroinvasive WNV infection. Up to our best knowledge, this is the first study that critically tackles CN neuropathy in WNV infection. It is established that WNV demonstrates a strong neurotropism. Within the context of CNs, it seems that WNV affects CNs via certain mechanisms such as blood-brain barrier disruption allowing viral particles and immune cells to infiltrate CN nuclei and cause inflammation of CN roots.4 Further, it may initiate motor neuron death in the spinal cord and CN pathways, causing paralysis, dysphagia and facial weakness.25 Less than 1% of WNV infections involve neuroinvasive disease,26 with a lower proportion involving CNs. It is estimated that CN neuropathy affects approximately 10%–20% of patients with neuroinvasive WNV infection.6 However, the severity of associated disease outcomes necessitates adopting the essential measures for achieving early diagnosis, initiating appropriate treatment and mitigating adverse outcomes.

This review identified North America, the USA in particular, as the most frequently affected geographic area with neuroinvasive disease manifested as CN neuropathy. Most of these cases were reported during the first decade of the twenty-first century, aligning with data from the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention that indicated a surge in WNV infections in the USA in 2003. During that year, a total of 9862 cases were documented.3 On the other hand, there are other geographic areas that are known for the appearance of WNV infection among humans such as Africa and the Middle East.27 In 2007, Khairallah et al described what appears to be the largest documented case series of WNV-associated chorioretinitis in the Middle East, originating from Tunisia.28 However, this study was not included in this review because key information was lacking.

There was a slight male preponderance, probably reflecting the higher male prevalence of WNV infection.3 Previous studies concluded that WNV-infected males have a higher incidence of neuroinvasive disease than WNV-infected females.29 Jean et al investigated a total of 305 WNV-infected patients with neuroinvasive disease and concluded that male sex was an independent risk factor for the development of neuroinvasive disease.30 Further, Hoffman et al concluded that sex differences in interferon-α production may manifest in neuroinvasive WNV infection.31 Therefore, some scientists recommend taking these differences into consideration and adopting a different course of action based on sex of patients.32 On the other hand, no sex significant associations were identified in our study for types of CNs involved, onset of CN neuropathy or treatment outcomes.

The ages of patients in our sample ranged from the second to the ninth decade of life, with a mean age of 52.5 years. There was a highly significant association between younger patients (<51 years) and facial palsy occurrence, while older patients were significantly more susceptible to optic palsy. It is well-established that older patients are more susceptible to severe forms of neuroinvasive disease such as optic neuritis.6 A case series describing WNV-infected patients showed that patients older than 45 years were significantly more prone to chorioretinitis.28 Moreover, another case series concluded that patients presenting with WNV-associated ocular manifestations had a mean age of 58 years.33 This link could be explained by the potential susceptibility of ocular tissues to chronic systemic diseases that are expectedly more prevalent in older individuals. On the other hand, several studies have reported that patients with facial palsy were in their 30s or 40s.34 35 This may have prognostic implications for treatment, highlighting the urgency required from clinicians to establish early diagnosis and prompt treatment, as delays can lead to irreversible vision loss. It is particularly important for older populations who may have other confounders and co-morbidities to get attention in a timely manner. The medical history of the patient sample in this study indicates that most of them were medically compromised, having at least one chronic disease, a fact that makes them more susceptible to ocular problems. Currently, there is no specific treatment or vaccine for WNV infections; however, certain measures have been recommended for ocular symptoms such as topical steroids, intravitreal injection of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor agents or even surgical management.36

This study reports the frequency of specific CN neuropathy in WNV infection. The most frequently affected CNs were CN II, CN VII and CN VI, in contrast to the CN I and CN VIII which seemed to be spared. Only one case report described CN V neuropathy. In this report, Sejvar et al did not explicitly mention CN V neuropathy; however, they reported jaw tremor and increased T2 signal in trigeminal motor nuclei in a 39-year-old man, which are indicative of CN V neuropathy.12 One in two patients had CN II neuropathy, while one in three patients manifested with CN VII neuropathy. Some authors argued that CN VII is the most frequently affected CN in WNV.37 On the other hand, a large retrospective study concluded that the most frequently identified neurological manifestations of CN neuropathy were diplopia/ophthalmoplegia, facial weakness and blurred vision.4 Most cases in this study presented with solitary CN neuropathy. Only eight cases presented with multiple CN neuropathy, where ophthalmoplegia (CN III, IV, VI) or bulbar palsy (CN IX, X, XI, XII) was identified. Neuropathies of the CN II and CN VII exhibited a longer latency from the onset of viral illness compared with other CNs. This may be explained by the larger number of cases presenting with these neuropathies as they affected 21 and 14 cases, respectively. This highlights the importance of following patients with WNV infection for periods that may extend to weeks or months. Further, it has been estimated that approximately 60% of hospitalised patients continue to show clinical signs for durations exceeding 3 months after diagnosis.6

At present, no specific antiviral therapy exists for WNV infection. The disease is generally self-limiting and confers long-lasting immunity, although persistent infections have occasionally been reported.27 It is also expected that WNV seroprevalence will increase with time, resulting in increasing immunity in populations and a reduction in outbreaks.38 Nonetheless, viral mutations can be identified particularly for enhanced neuroinvasive nature from newly emerging virulent isolates, which may have an impact on the pattern of CN neuropathy in WNV infection.27 Therefore, antivirals remain a valid treatment option. This study showed that none of the investigated factors of demographics or treatment modalities was significant in predicting delay of CN neuropathy after onset of viral illness. Despite the lack of effective antiviral drugs for flaviviruses such as WNV, there are continuous efforts to develop effective agents. Current attention and recommendations are primarily directed toward nucleotide- and nucleoside-based drugs.39 On the other hand, several flexible analogues of acyclovir (fleximers) were shown by the in vitro studies of Thames et al to have moderate effectiveness against various RNA viruses including flavivirus,40 such as galidisivir and favipiravir. Although acyclovir has been included in successful treatment protocols for some patients with WNV infection,41 our study found that it does not significantly predict the delay in onset of CN neuropathy following viral illness.

This study showed that only 47% of cases identified in North America have shown complete recovery compared with 83% in other parts of the world. One of the possible explanations for this finding is the severe neuroinvasive viral nature, or virulence of the virus genotype isolates identified in North America. Studies demonstrated that WNV continues to evolve in North America,42 which highlights the importance of understanding virus spread, and viral and host factors contributing to virulence.43 Previous research concluded that WNV strains exhibit considerable genetic variation and postulated that envelope glycosylation significantly contributes to the enhanced virulence of the North American strains in mammals.44

This review also showed that patients who did not receive antibiotics were more likely to show recovery. Antibiotic administration in viral infection is not justified;45 however, antibiotics are sometimes administered on an empiric basis to patients with undiagnosed viral illness. The possible diagnostic delay in WNV may be attributed to difficulty in the interpretation of serological results which may be related to the taxonomic complexity of the genus Flavivirus and the presence of cross-reactive antigens.46

Finally, early detection of WNV-related cranial neuropathies is critical. Suggested strategies include routine CN screening and use of efficient diagnostic methods such as MRI and neurophysiological tests. It is recommended that more prospective studies are conducted to document prevalence and severity of CN neuropathy in WNV infection. Further, exploring the potential pathogenesis is warranted and whether neuropathy is due to inflammatory response of direct viral injury. Other studies may involve cohort studies that track the long-term outcomes of CN neuropathy in terms of recovery or residual deficits. Therapeutic aspects are of paramount importance, as well, highlighting the need for developing potential neuroprotective agents and designing specific rehabilitation techniques for WNV-related nerve and motor dysfunction.

This review has limitations. We included only case reports because they provided sufficient details that satisfy the aims of this review including patient demographics (age, gender, country) and clinical profile (clinical findings, treatment and outcome). We could not find any experimental studies that address CN neuropathy in WNV infection in a detailed manner. On the other hand, we followed the rigorous methodology of the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and used strict criteria for selecting the eligible studies. Literature search and screening were done by two authors to ensure broad and systematic coverage of relevant studies, while data extraction was done by four authors working independently. The retrieved data items were further revised by the corresponding author to ensure consistency, accuracy and completeness of information, particularly that some studies did not explicitly note specific CN neuropathy as an outcome of WNV infection.

Conclusions

CN neuropathy in WNV infection is a significant but often overlooked complication with important diagnostic, therapeutic and public health implications. Increasing awareness among clinicians, improving diagnostic tools and conducting long-term studies on WNV-induced neuropathies are crucial steps toward better management of this condition. Future research should focus on understanding the mechanisms of nerve injury, developing targeted treatments and optimising rehabilitation strategies for affected patients. Moreover, it should cover questions such as the severity of CN neuropathy in patients with WNV neuroinvasive disease, disease outcomes and duration of WNV-induced cranial neuropathies, particularly when neurorehabilitation is employed.
