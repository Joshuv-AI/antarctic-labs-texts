Flow Matching for Probabilistic Learning of Dynamical Systems from Missing or Noisy Data
Siddharth Rout, Eldad Haber, Stéphane Gaudreault

Abstract
Learning dynamical systems is crucial across many fields, yet applying machine learning techniques remains challenging due to missing variables and noisy data. Classical mathematical models often struggle in these scenarios due to the arose ill-posedness of the physical systems. Stochastic machine learning techniques address this challenge by enabling the modeling of such ill-posed problems. Thus, a single known input to the trained machine learning model may yield multiple plausible outputs, and all of the outputs are correct. In such scenarios, probabilistic forecasting is inherently meaningful. In this study, we introduce a variant of flow matching for probabilistic forecasting which estimates possible future states as a distribution over possible outcomes rather than a single-point prediction. Perturbation of complex dynamical states is not trivial. Community uses typical Gaussian or uniform perturbations to crucial variables to model uncertainty. However, not all variables behave in a Gaussian fashion. So, we also propose a generative machine learning approach to physically and logically perturb the states of complex high-dimensional dynamical systems. Finally, we establish the mathematical foundations of our method and demonstrate its effectiveness on several challenging dynamical systems, including a variant of the high-dimensional WeatherBench dataset, which models the global weather at a 5.625° meridional resolution.



Introduction

The modelling of dynamical systems is of paramount importance across numerous scientific and engineering disciplines, including weather prediction, finance, and biology
(
Brin and Stuck 2002
;
Tu 2012
)
. These methods can be limited by their underlying assumptions and the complexity of the dynamical system
(
Guckenheimer and Worfolk 1993
;
Guckenheimer and Holmes 2013
)
. Furthermore, classical approaches often demand significant computational resources
(
Moin and Mahesh 1998
;
Lynch 2008
;
Wedi et al. 2015
)
. Traditional methods for predicting the behaviour of these systems often rely on explicit mathematical models derived from known physical laws. Mathematically, these modelling methods fall prey to missing data, noise or chaoticity. Hence, probabilistic prediction makes more sense. For probabilistic approaches, we typically employ a combination of numerical methods, Bayesian inference, and sensitivity analysis. Such classical methods are well summarised by
Reich and Cotter 2015
.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful tool for predicting dynamical systems, leveraging its ability to learn patterns and relationships from data without requiring explicit knowledge of the underlying physical laws
(
Ghadami and Epureanu 2022
)
. This is especially beneficial when complete physical models are unknown or intractable. In particular, these models can be used for prediction over a very large time step compared to classical methods that are limited by the accuracy and stability of numerical time integration.

We acknowledge traditional probabilistic ML techniques like autoregressive models (ARIMA), vector autoregression (VAR), Hidden Markov Models (HMM), Kalman filter based models, Gaussian processes, etc. They are well summarised by
Murphy 2012
. However, these techniques rely on statistical interpretations and numerous assumptions, such as linearity, Gaussianity, stationarity, and the Markov property. As a result, they are less generalizable and lack the capability to capture high-dimensional nonlinearities and complex patterns. Our primary motivation is the recent success of ML techniques, particularly those based on deep learning, for weather and climate prediction, where vast amounts of high-fidelity multiphysics meteorological data have been analysed to forecast weather patterns and climate changes
(
Pathak et al. 2022
;
Lam et al. 2023
;
Bodnar et al. 2024
)
. Deep learning based ML approaches are well-suited to model complex dynamical systems, as they can automatically uncover patterns in high-dimensional data. Hence, we have rather focused on the probabilistic approaches that rely on deterministic models
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Price et al. 2025
;
Oskarsson et al. 2024
)
.

We would like to focus our attention on the class of deep learning techniques typically used for time series modelling. Among the most effective models are Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), and Transformers
(
Chattopadhyay, Hassanzadeh, and Subramanian 2020
;
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
. These models are specifically designed for sequential data and retain memory of previous inputs, making them well-suited for time series prediction in dynamical systems. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have also been adapted for time series prediction, particularly in spatiotemporal problems
(
Shi et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Zakariaei et al. 2024
)
. The recent state-of-the-art models are transformers
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
augmented for timeseries predictions
(
Zhou et al. 2021
)
. Similarly, vision transformers (ViTs)
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
)
are augmented for spatiotemporal predictions
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
.

Motivation:

Most deep learning models for dynamical systems are designed for deterministic forecasting, producing a single output for each input. However, in the presence of incomplete or noisy data, a single input may correspond to multiple plausible outcomes. To capture this uncertainty, models must map a point input to a distribution over possible outputs. The density of this output distribution reflects the likelihood of each outcome, making uncertainty quantification (UQ) essential, especially in high-stakes applications such as cyclone tracking, where small variations in initial conditions can lead to widely divergent forecasts.

Recently,
Price et al. 2025
employed diffusion models on graph neural networks to learn stochastic mappings from a single point to a distribution. Similarly,
Chen et al. 2024
used stochastic interpolants (SI)
(
Albergo, Boffi, and Vanden-Eijnden 2023
)
with Föllmer’s constraint for probabilistic forecasting. These approaches are built upon flow matching (FM)
(
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden 2022
;
Lipman et al. 2022
)
, a homotopy-based framework that includes normalizing flows and score-based diffusion models
(
Song et al. 2020
;
Albergo, Boffi, and Vanden-Eijnden 2023
)
, and enables learning transformations either between two distributions or from a point to a distribution. So far, mappings from a point to a distribution have been realized only through stochastic differential equations (SDEs)
(
Price et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
, as they naturally encode stochasticity. However, SDE-based models are computationally expensive and hard to scale.

One common approach to address this issue is ensemble forecasting
(
Kalnay 2003
)
, widely used in numerical weather prediction (NWP) to account for uncertainty in initial conditions. This involves perturbing a single initial state to generate a set of plausible initial conditions, which are then propagated through a deterministic model to produce an ensemble of possible outcomes, representing the range of valid forecasts for a single input.

We can hence reformulate the typical problem of probabilistic forecasting of learning transformation from a single point to a distribution, to learning a transformation from one distribution to another distribution. The benefit is that we can use Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs) instead of SDEs, facilitating easier and more accurate integration
(
Liu, Gong, and Liu 2022
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
. See Appendix
C
to see how ODEs are faster and more accurate for numerical integration of a trivial case that is relevant to our proposition.

Again, the set of all possible states of a dynamical system defines a complex, high-dimensional vector space. Perturbing such a space is non-trivial, as it is often infeasible to adjust each degree of freedom while respecting its physical constraints. In practice, Gaussian perturbations with finite variance are commonly used, even though the underlying space is non-Gaussian
(
Kalnay 2003
)
. This often results in non-physical perturbations, which means the perturbed variables fall outside the valid vector space. To address this, we propose a generative FM approach that produces physically meaningful perturbations of dynamical states, enabling the construction of realistic input distributions for ensemble forecasting.

Specifically, we employ a variant of deterministic FM using ODEs. This approach has never been explored for UQ of dynamical systems. Our method uses deterministic FM both for time evolution and for sampling the initial distribution. Uncertainty is introduced solely through sampling the input distribution. We demonstrate its effectiveness across a range of benchmark problems.

Mathematical Foundation

Assume that there is a dynamical system of the form

\displaystyle\dot{\bf y}=f({\bf y},t,{\bf p}).

(1)

Here,
{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state vector and
f
is a function that depends on
{\bf y}
, the time
t
and some parameters
{\bf p}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
We assume that
f
is smooth and differentiable so that
given an initial condition
{\bf y}(0)
one could compute
{\bf y}(T)
using some numerical integration method
(
Stuart and Humphries 1998
)
. In this case, our goal is to learn the function
f
given the observations
{\bf y}(t)
. There are several approaches by which this can be done
(
Reich and Cotter 2015
;
Chattopadhyay, Hassanzadeh, and Subramanian 2020
;
Ghadami and Epureanu 2022
)
.

We assume that the data on
{\bf y}
are measured in intervals of time
T
which, for simplicity, we assume to be constant. In such cases, we have

\displaystyle{\bf y}(t+T)=F({\bf y}(t),t,{\bf p})=\int_{t}^{t+T}f({\bf y}(\tau),\tau,{\bf p})d\tau.

(2)

Here,
F
is the function that integrates the ODE from time
t
to
t+T
. We can then approximate
F
using a sufficiently expressive functional architecture
g
and solving the optimisation problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\ \sum_{j}\left\|g\left({\bf y}_{j},t_{j},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)-{\bf y}_{j+1}\right\|_{2},

(3)

where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are learnable parameters in
g
. It should be noted that
g
is only a surrogate of
F
and the parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are very different from
{\bf p}
. The above scenario falls under the case where we can predict the future, regardless of the size of the time interval unlike finite, based on the current value of
{\bf y}
.

Definition 1
.

Closed System.

Let
{\cal Y}
be the space of some observed data
{\bf y}
from a dynamical system
{\cal D}
. We say the system is closed if given
{\bf y}(t)
we can uniquely recover
{\bf y}(t+T)
for all
t
and finite bounded
T\leq\tau
, where
\tau
is some constant.
Practically, given the data,
{\bf y}(t)
and a constant
\epsilon
we can estimate a function
F
such that

\displaystyle\|{\bf y}(t+T)-F({\bf y}(t),t,{\bf p})\|^{2}\leq\epsilon~.

(4)

Definition
1
implies that we can learn the function
F
in equation
2
assuming that we have a sufficient amount of data in sufficient accuracy and an expressive enough architecture to approximates
F(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
. In this case, the focus of any ML based method should be given to the appropriate architecture that can express
F
, perhaps using some of the known structure of the problem.

Definition 2
.

Open System.

Let
{\cal Y}
be the space of some observed data
{\bf y}
on a dynamical system
{\cal D}
. We say the system is open if given
{\bf y}(t)
we
cannot
uniquely recover
{\bf y}(t+T)
for all
t
and finite
T\leq\tau
, where
\tau
is some constant. That is, there is a constant
\epsilon
such that

\displaystyle\|{\bf y}(t+T)-F({\bf y}(t),t,{\bf p})\|\geq\epsilon~,

(5)

independent of the amount of data and the complexity of
F
.

This concept and its limitations are illustrated below using the predator-prey model. This example demonstrates how a seemingly simple dynamical system can exhibit complex behaviour through the interaction of just two variables. While theoretically deterministic, the system can becomes practically unpredictable if measured in regions where the predator-prey cycle trajectories are very close, in other words states are very sensitive.

Example 1
.

Predator-Prey Model
(
Murray 2007
, Chapter 3)
:

The predator-prey model is

\displaystyle{\frac{d{\bf y}_{1}}{dt}}={\bf p}_{1}{\bf y}_{1}-{\bf p}_{2}{\bf y}_{1}{\bf y}_{2}\quad{\frac{d{\bf y}_{2}}{dt}}={\bf p}_{3}{\bf y}_{1}{\bf y}_{2}-{\bf p}_{4}{\bf y}_{2}

(6)

:

The temporal trajectories for different initial condition
{\bf y}(0)
and a given physical parameter
{\bf p}
are shown in

, where the possible states for a particular initial condition follows the cyclic trajectory shown in a single colour. Assuming that we record data in sufficient accuracy, the trajectory at any time can be determined by the measurement point at earlier times, thus, justifying the model in equation

Note that the trajectories cluster at the lower left corner. This implies that even though the trajectories never meet in theory, they may be very close, so numerically, if the data is noisy, the corner can be thought of as a bifurcation point. The system is therefore closed if the data

.

.

Consider again the predator-prey model, but this time assume that we record

only. Now assume that we known that at

,

and our goal is to predict the solution at time

, which is very far into the future. Clearly, this is impossible to do. However, we can perform many simulations where
{\bf y}_{1}=1

in equation

.

The second case is when the data is noisy. Consider the case that
{\bf y}_{0}^{\rm obs}={\bf y}(0)+\epsilon
. In this case, we consider
{\bf y}_{0}=[0.1,0.3]^{\top}
and we contaminate it with Gaussian noise with

The predator-prey model draws our attention to a different scenario that is much more common in practice. Example
2
represents a much more realistic scenario, since in reality data is almost always sampled only partially or on low resolution and includes noise. For example, in weather prediction, where the primitive equations are integrated to approximate global atmospheric flow, it is common that we do not observe important variables like pressure, temperature or wind speed in sufficient resolution. In this case, having information about the past is, in general, insufficient to predict the future. While we could use classical ML techniques to solve closed systems it is unreasonable to attempt and solve open systems with similar techniques. This is because the system does not have a unique solution, given partial or noisy data. Motivated by the two examples above, we now form the learning problem of probabilistic forecasting (see, e.g.
(
Reich and Cotter 2015
)
).

Definition 3
.

Probabilistic Forecasting.

Probabilistic forecasting predicts future outcomes by providing a range of possible scenarios along with their associated probabilities, rather than a single point estimate.

To be specific, let the initial vector
{\bf y}(0)\sim\pi_{0}({\bf y})
and assume that
{\bf y}(T)
is obtained from integrating the dynamical system
{\cal D}
. Probabilistic forecasting refers to estimating and sampling from the distribution
\pi_{T}({\bf y})
.

This approach acknowledges that a unique prediction may not be attainable but allows for generating samples of future outcomes. In contrast, deterministic prediction assumes a closed system and highly accurate data, making it often inapplicable.

Probabilistic Forecasting and Flow Matching

In this section, we discuss a version of FM. FM is the technique that we use to propagate the uncertainty and also to generate the initial distribution. For many systems, it is impossible to observe the exact complete state. The inability to obtain information about the full state implies that it is most likely impossible to deliver a deterministic prediction, and thus stochastic prediction is the natural choice. Let us consider the case that we observe a noisy or partial system. In particular, let
{\bf y}(t)
be a vector that is obtained from an unknown dynamical system
{\cal D}
and let

\displaystyle{\bf q}(t)={\bf S}{\bf y}(t)+{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},

(7)

where
{\bf S}
is some sampling operator that typically reduces the dimension of
{\bf y}
and
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}{\bf I})
.
The distinction between
{\bf y}
and
{\bf q}
is important. The vector
{\bf y}
represents the full state of the system, while
{\bf q}
represents only partial knowledge of the system.

Our goal is to predict a sample from the distribution of observed partial state
{\bf q}_{T}={\bf q}(t+T)\sim\pi_{T}({\bf q})
, given a sample from the distribution on the data at
{\bf q}_{0}={\bf q}(t)\sim\pi_{0}({\bf q})
.
Clearly, we do not have a functional expression for neither
\pi_{0}
nor
\pi_{T}
, however, if we are able to obtain many samples from both distributions, we can use them to build a probability density. This assumption is unrealistic without further assumptions in the general case since we typically have only a single time series.
A few assumptions are common and we use them in our experiments. The most common assumptions are, i) The time dependent process is autoregressive. This implies that we can treat every instance (or instances) of the time series as
{\bf q}_{0}
and use the future time series as
{\bf q}_{T}
. Such an assumption is very common, although it may be unrealistic. ii) Periodicity and clustering. For many problems, especially problems that relate to earth science, there is a natural periodicity, and data can be clustered using the natural cycle of the year.

Given our ability to obtain data that represents samples from the probability at time
0
and at time
T
, we are able to use the data and provide a stochastic prediction. To this end, we note that stochastic prediction is nothing but a problem of probability transformation, which is in the base of mass transport
(
Benamou and Brenier 2003
)
. While efficient solutions for low dimensions problems have been addressed
(
Fisher 1970
;
Villani et al. 2009
)
, solutions for problems in high dimensions are only recently being developed. One recent and highly effective technique to solve such a problem is stochastic interpolation (SI)
(
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden 2022
)
, which is a technique that belongs to a family of FM methods. The basic idea of stochastic interpolation is to generate a simple interpolant for all possible points in
{\bf q}_{0}
and
{\bf q}_{T}
. A simple interpolant of this kind is linear and reads

\displaystyle{\bf q}_{t}=t{\bf q}_{T}+(1-t){\bf q}_{0}

(8)

where
{\bf q}_{T}\sim\pi_{T}
,
{\bf q}_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
, and
t\in[0,1]
is a parameter. The points
{\bf q}_{t}
are associated from a distribution
\pi_{t}({\bf q})
that converges to
\pi_{T}({\bf q})
at
t=1
and to
\pi_{0}({\bf q})
at
t=0
.
In FM, one learns (estimates) the velocity

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{t}({\bf q}_{t})=\dot{\bf q}_{t}={\bf q}_{T}-{\bf q}_{0}~,

(9)

by solving the stochastic optimisation problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf q}_{0},{\bf q}_{1}}\int_{0}^{1}\|{\bf v}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf q}_{t},t)-({\bf q}_{T}-{\bf q}_{0})\|^{2}\,dt~.

(10)

Here
{\bf v}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf q}_{t},t)
is a function approximator for the velocity
{\bf v}_{t}
that is given at points
{\bf q}_{t}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the trainable parameters. A common function approximator that is used for
{\bf v}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf q}_{t},t)
is a deep neural network.

For inference, we assume that the velocity model
{\bf v}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf q}_{t},t)
is trained and we use that to integrate
{\bf q}
from time
0
to
T
. That is, we solve for
{\bf q}_{T}={\bf q}(T)
given that

\displaystyle{\frac{d{\bf q}}{dt}}={\bf v}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf q},t)\quad\text{and},\quad{\bf q}(0)={\bf q}_{0}~.

(11)

Note that we use a deterministic framework rather than a stochastic framework. This allows to incorporate high accuracy numerical integrators that can take larger step sizes. However, this implies that the solution of the ODE given a single initial condition
{\bf q}_{0}
gives a single prediction. In order to sample from the target distribution
\pi_{T}
, we sample
{\bf q}_{0}
from
\pi_{0}
and then use the ODE (equation
11
) to push many samples forward. We thus obtain many samples for
{\bf q}_{0}
from
\pi_{0}
(see next section) and use them to sample from
\pi_{T}
.

Comment 1
.

Note that the ODE obtained for
{\bf q}
is not physical. It merely used to interpolate the density from time
0
to
T
. To demonstrate we continue with the predator-prey model.

Sampling the Perturbed States

Sampling the initial distribution
\pi_{0}
in a meaningful way is not trivial. There are a number of approaches to achieve this goal. A brute force approach can search through the dataset for so-called similar states
(
Delle Monache et al. 2013
)
, for example, given a particular state
{\bf q}_{0}
, we can search other states in the dataset such that
\|{\bf q}_{0}-{\bf q}_{i}\|^{2}\leq\epsilon
. While this approach is possible in low dimension it is difficult if not impossible in very high dimensions. To this end, we turn to a generative ML approach that we use to generate realistic perturbations for a single input state.

We turn our attention to Variational Autoencoders (VAE’s). In particular, we use a flow-based VAE
(
Dai and Wipf 2019
;
Vahdat, Kreis, and Kautz 2021
)
. In the encoding stage, they push the data
{\bf q}_{0}
from the original distribution
\pi_{0}({\bf q})
to a tensor
{\bf z}
that is sampled from a Gaussian noise,
N(0,{\bf I})
. Next, in the decoding stage, the model pushes the tensor
{\bf z}
back to
{\bf q}_{0}
. Since the Gaussian space is convex, small perturbations can be applied to the latent tensor
{\bf z}
, which lead to samples from
\pi_{0}({\bf q})
that are perturbed around
{\bf q}_{0}
.

The difference between standard VAEs and flow-based VAEs is that give
{\bf q}_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
, VAEs attempt to learn a transformation to a Gaussian space
N(0,{\bf I})
directly. However, as have been demonstrated in
(
Ruthotto and Haber 2021
)
, traditional VAEs are only able to create maps to a latent state that is similar to but not exactly Gaussian, making it difficult to perturb and sample from them.
VAEs that are flow-based can generate a Gaussian map with a much higher accuracy. Furthermore, such flows can learn the decoding from a Gaussian back to the original distribution as the learned transformation is invertible, for proof see the Appendix
A
and continuous time normalising flow
(
Chen et al. 2018
;
Grathwohl et al. 2018
;
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden 2022
)
.

Figure 3:
Diagram to show the process for obtaining a physical perturbed state.

Such flows can be considered as a special case of FM, a deterministic scheme for learning a transformation from a physical state
{\bf q}_{0}
to a latent Gaussian state
{\bf z}
. Since the transformation is invertible, we can easily reverse integrate from the latent space back to the physical space.

To this end, let us define the interpolant as

{\bf q}_{t}=(1-t){\bf q}_{0}+t{\bf z}~,\text{ if }t\in[0,1].

(12)

The velocities associated with the above interpolant as

{\bf u}_{t}={\frac{d{\bf q}_{t}}{dt}}={\bf z}-{\bf q}_{0}~,\text{ if }t\in[0,1].

(13)

Note that the flow starts at
{\bf q}_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
towards
{\bf z}\sim N(0,{\bf I})
at
t=0
and arrive to
{\bf z}
at
t=1
. This is the encoding state. Training these models is straightforward and is done in a similar way to the training of our flow matching model, namely we solve a stochastic optimisation problem of the form

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf q}_{0},{\bf z}}\int_{0}^{1}\|{\bf u}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf q}_{t},t)-({\bf z}-{\bf q}_{0})\|^{2}dt.

(14)

Given the velocity
{\bf u}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
we now generate perturb samples in the following way. First, we integrate the ODE from
t=0
to
t=1
, that is, we solve the ODE

\displaystyle{\frac{d{\bf q}}{dt}}={\bf u}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf q},t),\quad\text{given }\quad{\bf q}(0)={\bf q}_{0}.

(15)

This yields the latent state
{\bf q}(1)={\bf z}\sim N(0,{\bf I})
.
We then perturb the encoded latent state as

\displaystyle\widehat{\bf q}(1)={\bf q}(1)+\sigma\boldsymbol{\omega},

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}\sim N(0,{\bf I})
and
\sigma
is a hyper-parameter.

Note that we can use the same velocity for the reverse process of decoding. The decoding map pushes the points
\widehat{\bf q}(1)
back to
\widehat{\bf q}_{0}
by solving for
\widehat{\bf q}_{0}={\bf q}(t=0)
such that

\displaystyle{\frac{d{\bf q}}{dt}}=-{\bf u}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}({\bf q},t)\quad\text{and},\quad{\bf q}(1)=\widehat{\bf q}(1)~.

(17)

We then integrate the ODE equation
17
from
1
to
0
starting from
\widehat{\bf q}(1)
, obtaining a perturb state
\widehat{\bf q}_{0}
for an initial state
{\bf q}_{0}
. The magnitude of perturbation is controlled by the hyperparameter
\sigma
. We decode
M
perturbed latent states simultaneously for a single initial state to obtain
M
perturbed samples that define the input distribution
\pi_{0}
for our ensemble prediction in
\pi_{T}
as explained in the previous section
Probabilistic Forecasting and Flow Matching
.

Experiments

In this section, we apply the proposed framework to a variety of temporal datasets to demonstrate its ability to estimate forecast uncertainty. We conduct experiments on two synthetic datasets, which allow for full analytical evaluation, as well as a real dataset for weather prediction.

Experiment and Results

We evaluate our proposed framework on the aforementioned datasets using the test cases that are obtained by classically introducing uncertainty. Hence, for each dataset, we have access to ground truth mappings between initial and final distributions, allowing us to assess the statistical properties of the distribution of predictions.

To model uncertainty in the initial state, we use the generative flow matching method described in the previous section
Sampling the Perturbed States
to generate an ensemble of initial states. These are propagated forward using a deterministic flow matching model to produce an ensemble of final states, thereby capturing and propagating uncertainty to the final states. Finally, we compare the predicted distributions with the ground truth and standard ensemble forecasting methods using evaluation metrics detailed below and in the Appendix.

Metrics:

We evaluate our method’s performance using a set of statistical and image-based metrics. For each predicted ensemble, a corresponding ground truth ensemble is available. The objective is to ensure that the statistical properties of the predicted ensemble closely match those of the target.

We begin with basic statistical metrics: the mean score and standard deviation score, which measure the average and spread of pixel values across the state space. Ideally, these scores should closely align between the predicted and target ensembles—indicating similar distributions.

Inspired by techniques in turbulent flow field analysis
(
Pope 2001
)
, we also compare the ensemble mean state and the ensemble standard deviation (SD) state. To quantify similarity, we apply standard image comparison metrics: Mean Squared Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM). Lower MSE and MAE values indicate better alignment with the ground truth, while higher SSIM reflects better structural similarity and better alignment with the ground truth. Full metric definitions are provided in Appendix
D
.

Conclusion

Probabilistic forecasting is an important topic for many scientific problems. It expands traditional ML techniques beyond paired input and outputs, to mapping between distributions. While generative AI has been focused on mapping Gaussians to data, similar methodologies can be applied for the generation of predictions given current states, which may not be Gaussian.
In this work, we have coupled FM based normalising flow for generating perturbations for a given dynamical state and quantify uncertainty. Such an approach of perturbation of states using generative ML has not been explicitly explored before. We then use another FM based deterministic forward map between distributions to propagate the uncertainty over a long time in the dynamical system.
Existing algorithms rely on stochasticity during training, thus, the trained model infers with the same stochasticity
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Price et al. 2025
)
. In contrast, our algorithm uses ODE instead of SDE, making it quicker to train and infer. Again, our inference stochasticity is independent of our training process. Our approach avoids these constraints, offering greater flexibility.

ODE vs SDE

ODE Problem

We are given a trivial linear initial value problem like FM:

\frac{dy}{dx}=1,\quad y(0)=0

We want to approximate
y(1)
using Euler’s method.

Euler’s Method Formula

Euler’s method updates the solution using:

y_{n+1}=y_{n}+h\cdot f(x_{n},y_{n})

where
f(x,y)=\frac{dy}{dx}=1
, and
h
is the step size.

Step-by-Step Approximation

Let the interval
[0,1]
be divided into
N
steps with step size:

h=\frac{1}{N}

Set
y_{0}=0
, and iterate:

\text{For }n=0,1,2,\dots,N-1:\quad y_{n+1}=y_{n}+h

Since the slope is constant, each step adds
h
to the previous value.

After
N
steps:

y_{N}=y_{0}+N\cdot h=0+N\cdot\frac{1}{N}=1

ODE solution for different step sizes.

SDE Problem

Consider the stochastic differential equation (SDE):

dy=\,dt+0.2\,dW_{t},\quad y(0)=0

We want to simulate a numerical solution using the Euler-Maruyama method.

Figure 8:
SDE solution for different step sizes.

Euler–Maruyama Method

Given a time interval
[0,1]
and number of steps
N
, define step size:

h=\frac{1}{N}

Let
W_{t}
denote the Wiener process. Then the Euler-Maruyama approximation is given by:

y_{n+1}=y_{n}+h+0.2\Delta W_{n}

where
\Delta W_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h)
is a normally distributed random variable with mean 0 and variance
h
.

V_{ES}=\frac{1}{N\cdot C\cdot H\cdot W}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{C}\sum_{k=1}^{H}\sum_{l=1}^{W}(P_{i}^{j,k,l}-V_{EM})^{2},

(54)

where
P_{i}^{j,k,l}\in I_{i}
.

MSE, MAE, SSIM

\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{N\cdot C\cdot H\cdot W}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{w=1}^{W}\sum_{c=1}^{C}(y-\hat{y})^{2}

(55)

\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{N\cdot C\cdot H\cdot W}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{w=1}^{W}\sum_{c=1}^{C}|y-\hat{y}|

(56)

\text{SSIM(x,y)}=\frac{(2\mu_{x}\mu_{y}+C_{1})(2\sigma_{xy}+C_{2})}{(\mu_{x}^{2}+\mu_{y}^{2}+C_{1})(\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2}+C_{2})}

(57)

\overline{\text{SSIM}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{SSIM}(x,y)

(58)

where:

is the number of images in the dataset,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle C

is the number of channels (e.g., 3 for RGB images),

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\text{ is the true pixel value at position }(i,h,w,c),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\hat{y}

\displaystyle\text{ is the predicted pixel value at position }(i,h,w,c).

MAX

is the maximum possible pixel value of the image,

(e.g., 255 for an 8-bit image),

MSE

is the Mean Squared Error between the original

and compressed image.

\displaystyle\mu_{x}

\displaystyle\text{ is the average of }x,

\displaystyle\mu_{y}

\displaystyle\text{ is the average of }y,

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{ is the variance of }x,

\displaystyle\sigma_{y}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{ is the variance of }y,

\displaystyle\sigma_{xy}

\displaystyle\text{ is the covariance of }x\text{ and }y,

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=(K_{1}L)^{2}\text{ and }C_{2}=(K_{2}L)^{2}\text{ are two variable}

to stabilise the division with weak denominator,

\displaystyle L

is the dynamic range of the pixel values

Datasets

Vancouver 93 Temperature Trend (Vancouver93)
: This is a real nonlinear chaotic high dimensional dynamical system, in which only a single physical variable (temperature) is recorded. The daily average temperatures at 93 different weather stations in and around Vancouver, BC, Canada are captured for the past 34 years from sources such as National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), the Government of Canada, and Germany’s national meteorological service (DWD) through Meteostat’s python library
(
Lamprecht
)
. Essentially, it is a time series of 12,419 records at 93 stations. The complex urban coastal area of Vancouver with coasts, mountains, valleys, and islands makes it an interesting location where forecasting is very difficult than in general
(
Vannini et al. 2012
;
Leroyer et al. 2014
)
. The historical temperature alone insufficient to predict the temperature in the future, as it requires additional variables like precipitation, pressure, and many more variables at higher resolution.
This makes the dataset fit the proposed framework.

(

)

\times

\times
15 km).
