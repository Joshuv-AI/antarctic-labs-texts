Integrated Molecular Risk Stratification and Measurable Residual Disease‐Guided Consolidation Improve Outcomes in Pediatric Non‐Down Syndrome Acute Megakaryoblastic Leukemia
Authors: Chunxia Cai, Yiqiao Chen, Chunping Wu, Xiaoqin Feng, Chunfu Li, Mincui Zheng, Huirong Mai, Lihua Yang, Hua Jiang, Xiangling He, Hong Wen, Honggui Xu, Chun Chen, Shaohua Le, Nainong Li, Yongzhi Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTManagement of pediatric acute megakaryoblastic leukemia (AMKL) without Down syndrome (non‐DS‐AMKL) remains challenging due to suboptimal induction responses and poor survival. This study assessed the efficacy of fludarabine, cytarabine, granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor, and idarubicin (FLAG‐IDA) in non‐DS‐AMKL and evaluated the role of risk‐adapted consolidation strategies, including hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) during first complete remission (CR1), guided by genetic profiles and measurable residual disease (MRD) dynamics. In this multicenter retrospective study from China, we analyzed 58 non‐DS‐AMKL patients treated with FLAG‐IDA (n = 50) or daunorubicin, cytarabine, etoposide (DAE) (n = 8) induction, followed by risk‐adapted consolidation. FLAG‐IDA demonstrated CR rates comparable to those with DAE (first‐course: 70.0% vs. 87.5%, p = 0.423; cumulative: 82.0% vs. 87.5%, p = 1.000). Compared with other AML subtypes, non‐DS‐AMKL showed inferior 5‐year overall survival (OS) (61.7% vs. 78.2%, p = 0.001) and event‐free survival (EFS) (58.5% vs. 68.8%, p = 0.045), attributable to a higher relapse rate (34.8% vs. 19.5%, p = 0.002). High‐risk genetics predicted worse outcomes. HSCT in CR1 significantly improved survival for high‐risk or poor responders. CR with MRD negativity after second induction predicted superior OS and EFS (p < 0.001). Risk‐adapted HSCT and MRD‐guided stratification are critical for non‐DS‐AMKL management.Keywords: acute myeloid leukemia, chemotherapy, clinical observations, cytogenetics

Introduction

Acute megakaryoblastic leukemia (AMKL), a heterogeneous subtype of acute myeloid leukemia (AML), is subdivided pediatrics into Down syndrome associated (DS‐AMKL) and non‐DS‐AMKL [1, 2]. Non‐DS‐AMKL often involves chromosomal translocations causing oncogenic fusion proteins and has a poor prognosis, as survival rates across studies vary widely from 10% to 70% [3, 4]. This subgroup's prognostic indicators and treatment suggestions are still debated.

Genomic studies have highlighted the heterogeneity of pediatric non‐DS‐AMKL characterized by recurrent and mutually exclusive genetic aberrations [1, 3, 5, 6, 7]. Achieving complete remission (CR) at early treatment assessment correlates with favorable prognosis in pediatric AML [8, 9, 10]. Nevertheless, the CR rate for non‐DS‐AMKL remains suboptimal (50%–80%) with standard “3 + 7” induction [4, 9, 11, 12, 13]. We hypothesized that strengthening induction chemotherapy may improve CR rates and long‐term survival.

While the FLAG‐IDA (fludarabine, cytarabine, granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor, and idarubicin) regimen demonstrates superior CR rates and reduced relapse versus daunorubicin‐based regimens in AML, its efficacy as frontline therapy for pediatric non‐DS‐AMKL remains unexplored [14, 15, 16]. Furthermore, while the prognostic value of molecular risk stratification has been increasingly recognized [5, 7, 17], its clinical utility in guiding post‐remission strategies—particularly the timing and indications for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) in first complete remission (CR1)—remains undefined in this population. Therefore, this multicenter retrospective study aimed to assess the efficacy of FLAG‐IDA in non‐DS‐AMKL and to evaluate the role of risk‐adapted consolidation strategies, including CR1 HSCT guided by genetic profiles and measurable residual disease (MRD) dynamics.

Materials and Methods

Patients

Pediatric patients with newly diagnosed non‐DS‐AMKL aged < 14 years and managed per the C‐HUANAN‐AML‐2015 protocol [18] across 11 tertiary centers (Southern China, 2015–2023) were included in this study. Patients with DS or prior cytotoxic chemotherapy exposure were excluded. Diagnosis adhered to the World Health Organization (WHO) classifications [19], with confirmation requiring one of follow: ≥ 20% blasts in bone marrow (BM), with ≥ 50% demonstrating megakaryoblastic morphology; ≥ 10% blasts express platelet‐specific markers (CD41, CD42b, CD61) as determined by multiparametric flow cytometry (MFC). Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (2025KY266), with a waiver of informed consent granted in accordance with national regulations and Declaration of Helsinki.

C‐HUANAN‐AML 2015 Protocol

The C‐HUANAN‐AML‐15 protocol [18], adapted from the UK MRC AML15 trial [14], is a four‐cycle regimen consisting of: (1) two sequential FLAG‐IDA or DAE induction courses; (2) homoharringtonine cytarabine consolidation; and (3) mitoxantrone‐cytarabine consolidation. Patients were non‐randomly assigned to the FLAG‐IDA (Group A) or DAE (Group B) induction arms. The choice of induction regimen (FLAG‐IDA vs. DAE) was primarily based on physician discretion and protocol preferences at the participating centers during the study period. The treatment schematics are shown in Figure S1.

Risk Stratification

Non‐DS‐AMKL cases were stratified into two risk categories based on molecular/cytogenetic features with established prognostic relevance in prior studies [3, 5, 8, 17, 20]. High risk (HR), defined by CBFA2T3::GLIS2, NUP98::KDM5A, KMT2A rearrangements (KMT2A‐r), FUS::ERG, monosomy 7 (−7), or FLT3‐ITD, and non‐HR, which accounted for all remaining cases that did not meet the HR criteria. This classification schema was prospectively applied to analyze associations between risk‐adapted consolidation therapies (chemotherapy alone vs. HSCT) and survival outcomes.

Definitions

CR required < 5% marrow blasts and hematologic recovery. MRD negativity was defined as < 0.1% leukemic cells by MFC. Events included: relapse, death, therapy abandonment, or secondary malignancy. Serious adverse events (SAEs) comprised grade ≥ 3 toxicities following the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Treatment‐related mortality (TRM) denoted deaths from therapy complications. The overall survival (OS) was measured from diagnosis until death or last follow‐up. The event‐free survival (EFS) was calculated from diagnosis to the first event or last follow‐up. TRM was considered a competing risk for relapse cumulative incidence (CIR), and relapse was a competing risk for treatment‐related mortality cumulative incidence (CIDTRM). Patients were followed until death, last contact, or censoring at the study cutoff (1 April 2024).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 28.0 statistical software (IBM, Chicago, Illinois, USA) and graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism version 7 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, California, USA). Continuous variables, presented as median (range), were compared using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Categorical variables were analyzed by the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Survival endpoints (OS, EFS) were estimated via the Kaplan–Meier method with log‐rank comparisons. Competing risks analysis (Gray's test) evaluated the CIR and CIDTRM. Prognostic factors were identified through univariate Cox regression (variables with p < 0.05), followed by multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling. All statistical tests were bilateral, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

In the C‐HUANAN‐AML cohort of 875 patients with pediatric de novo AML (excluding acute promyelocytic leukemia), 58 (6.6%) had non‐DS‐AMKL. With regard to baseline characteristics, patients with non‐DS‐AMKL were significantly younger (median 23 vs. 79 months; p < 0.001), were more frequently female (55.2% vs. 41.5%; p = 0.043), and had a lower median WBC count (11.5 × 109/L vs. 22.2 × 109/L; p < 0.001) than did those with other AML subtypes (summarized in Table 1). Compared with other AML subtypes, non‐DS‐AMKL was characterized by higher frequencies of adverse features, including EVI1 overexpression (EVI1 high), NUP98 rearrangements (NUP98‐r), and complex karyotypes (≥ 3 abnormalities). Non‐DS‐AMKL was not found in the favorable subtypes. Notably, our cohort showed that genomic heterogeneity of pediatric non‐DS‐AMKL was characterized by recurrent genetic lesions (Figure S2).

Evaluation of Induction

Figure 1 presents the flowchart of newly diagnosed patients with non‐DS‐AMKL. Fifty patients were assigned to FLAG‐IDA (Group A) and eight were assigned to DAE (Group B) as first‐course induction. Comparisons of baseline characteristics between the two groups demonstrated no significant differences in key clinical and demographic features (Table S1). The CR rate after first‐course induction was 74.1% (43/58) in non‐DS‐AMKL cases, lower than that in other AML subtypes (84.7% [692/817], p = 0.034). Of the 14 patients with non‐CR, 7 (50.0%) achieved CR after a second induction, leading to a cumulative CR rate of 86.2% (50/58), aligning with other AML subtypes (91.9% [751/817]; p = 0.140). Notably, FLAG‐IDA demonstrated no significant CR advantage over DAE in either first‐course (70.0% [35/50] vs. 87.5% [7/8], p = 0.423) or dual courses induction (82.0% [41/50] vs. 87.5% [7/8], p = 1.000).

Selected Toxicities of FLAG‐IDA Induction

Selected treatment‐related SAEs among 50 patients receiving the FLAG‐IDA regimen induction (Group A) are shown in Table S1. The median neutropenia duration after the first and second FLAG‐IDA induction was 26 (range, 14–42) days and 25 (range, 10–40) days, respectively. All the patients in Group A (50/50, 100%) developed infectious SAEs after the first induction. Notably, two patients died from SAEs after the first induction; one died from a bloodstream infection and the other died from cardiotoxicity. Forty‐three patients (43/50, 86%) developed SAEs after the second induction, but none of the patients died from them.

Overall Long‐Term Outcomes

Among 58 patients with non‐DS‐AMKL, 24 (41.4%) experienced events. Three patients withdrew from treatment post‐induction, three (5.2%) died from TRM during induction, and three succumbed to refractory disease progression. Relapse occurred in 15 patients (25.9% of the total cohort), with a median time to relapse of 8.1 months (range, 3.5–25.0 months). With a median follow‐up of 39.9 months (range, 1–103.4 months), the 5‐year OS and EFS of the patients with non‐DS‐AMKL were significantly lower than those of children with other AML subtypes (OS: 61.7% ± 6.4% vs. 78.2% ± 1.6%, p = 0.001; EFS: 58.5% ± 6.5% vs. 68.8% ± 1.7%, p = 0.045), which was caused by the comparable CIDTRM but significantly higher CIR in children with non‐DS‐AMKL (CIDTRM: 8.0% ± 4.4% vs. 10.3% ± 1.2%, p = 0.702; CIR: 34.8% ± 6.6% vs. 19.5% ± 1.6%, p = 0.002) (Figure 2). Compared with patients who received DAE induction (Group B), those who received FLAG‐IDA induction did not have superior outcomes (Figure S3).

Prognostic Impact of Clinical and Genetic Factors

Risk factors, including age, sex, WBC count at diagnosis, genetic lesions, and karyotypes were evaluated in 58 patients with non‐DS‐AMKL using univariate Cox regression analysis. Only the NUP98::KDM5A fusion retained prognostic significance for inferior EFS and OS (hazard ratio > 1 and p < 0.05 for all) (Table S2). To mitigate potential statistical power limitations arising from small subgroup sizes, we aggregated molecular subtypes with analogous prognoses into risk‐stratified cohorts as predefined (Materials and Methods). The HR group demonstrated significantly inferior 5‐year OS (42.1% ± 10.2% vs. 75.8% ± 7.5%; p = 0.023) and EFS (EFS: 39.0% ± 9.9% vs. 72.7% ± 7.7%; p = 0.023) compared with those in the non‐HR group, coupled with a 2.8‐fold increased CIR (55.6% ± 10.8% vs. 20.0% ± 7.3%; p = 0.011) (Figure 3).

Comparative outcomes of high‐risk (HR) versus non‐HR in pediatric acute megakaryoblastic leukemia without Down syndrome (non‐DS‐AMKL). Kaplan–Meier curves for (A) overall survival and (B) event‐free survival, and competing risks analysis for (C) the cumulative incidence of relapse.

Prognostic Impact of the Induction Chemotherapy Response

We evaluated the prognostic significance of treatment response in patients with paired morphologic and MFC‐MRD assessments after the first‐course (time point 1, TP1) and second course (time point 2, TP2) induction. Assessment data were missing for some patients who could not be evaluated at specific time points (one because of TRM before TP1, two because of TRM, and one because of treatment discontinuation before TP2); these patients were excluded from the respective landmark analyses. Morphologic response (achievement of CR) could significantly stratify OS and EFS at both TP1 (Figure S4A,B) and TP2 (OS: 70.6% ± 6.6% vs. 33.3% ± 19.2%, p = 0.024; EFS: 66.5% ± 6.8% vs. 33.3% ± 19.2%, p = 0.040) (Figure 4A,B). MFC‐MRD assessments (< 0.1%) at TP1 could not significantly stratify OS and EFS (Figure S4C,D), whereas MFC‐MRD at TP2 independently stratified both OS and EFS (OS: 73.5% ± 6.6% vs. 25.0% ± 15.3%, p < 0.001; EFS: 69.2% ± 6.9% vs. 25.0% ± 15.3%, p < 0.001) (Figure 4C,D). Patients achieving CR after induction were stratified by MFC‐MRD status at both TP1 and TP2. The results showed no prognostic impact on OS or EFS at TP1 (Figure S4E,F). Notably, MRD status at TP2 emerged as a critical discriminator: those achieving CR with MRD‐negative status demonstrated significantly superior OS and EFS compared to those for CR patients with MRD‐positive status at TP2 (OS: 74.7% ± 6.6% vs. 25.0% ± 21.7%, p = 0.007; EFS: 70.2% ± 6.9% vs. 25.0% ± 21.7%, p = 0.019) (Figure 4E,F).

Prognostic Impact of Post‐Induction Treatment

Among the initial cohort of 58 patients, nine (15.5%) were unable to proceed to consolidation therapy because of treatment‐related mortality (n = 3), dropout (n = 3), death from disease progression (n = 2), or a switch to an alternative regimen (n = 1). The remaining 49 patients who commenced consolidation therapy were all uniformly recommended to undergo HSCT in CR1. However, only 26 of these patients (consolidation therapy: 53.1%, total cohort: 44.8%) ultimately received HSCT. The predominant reasons for declining or deferring HSCT were familial financial constraints and parental apprehension regarding transplant‐associated morbidity and mortality. In the overall cohort, HSCT in CR1 (26/49, 53.1%) significantly improved outcomes compared to those with consolidation chemotherapy alone (23/49, 46.9%) (OS: 84.8% ± 7.0% vs. 53.8% ± 10.2%, p = 0.012; EFS: 84.8% ± 7.0% vs. 45.8% ± 10.2%, p = 0.002) (Figure 5A,B). In the predefined HR genetic subgroup, HSCT in CR1 (11/19, 57.9%) conferred significant survival advantages over chemotherapy alone (8/19, 42.1%): 5‐year OS (74.1% ± 12.9% vs. 16.7% ± 14.2%, p = 0.006) and EFS (74.1% ± 12.9% vs. 11.1% ± 10.5%, p < 0.001) (Figure 5C,D). In contrast, this benefit was not observed in the non‐HR genetic subgroup (HSCT: 15/30, 50%; chemotherapy alone: 15/30, 50%) (Figure S5). Notably, patients who failed to achieve morphologic CR and/or had persistent MRD positivity at TP1 demonstrated significantly improved 5‐year OS (71.6% ± 14.0% vs. 33.3% ± 19.2%, p = 0.034) and EFS (71.6% ± 14.0% vs. 16.7% ± 15.2%, p = 0.004) with HSCT during CR1 (7/13, 53.8%) compared to chemotherapy alone (6/13, 46.2%) as consolidation (Figure 5E,F). Among seven patients with persistent treatment resistance after two induction courses who achieved delayed remission through salvage regimens, five underwent HSCT bridging (three survivors; two non‐relapse deaths) and two continued chemotherapy (both relapsed and died). This limited cohort (n = 7) showed a suggestive trend favoring HSCT, though the sample size precluded formal statistical analysis.

Discussion

Our multicenter cohort reveals that non‐DS‐AMKL exhibits distinct clinicogenomic features and significantly inferior survival versus other pediatric AML subtypes. Contrary to expectations, FLAG‐IDA showed no superiority over DAE, with similar CR and 5‐year survival rates. Molecular risk stratification indicated worse long‐term outcomes for the high‐risk genetic subgroup. Early treatment response, especially MFC‐MRD post‐second induction, was a key prognostic factor. Importantly, HSCT in CR1 provided a 3.5‐fold survival advantage for the high‐risk molecular subgroup and poor early responders, suggesting the need for integrated genomic risk assessment, MRD‐guided stratification, and risk‐adapted consolidation.

In the C‐HUANAN‐AML 15 study cohort, non‐DS‐AMKL accounted for 6.6% of pediatric AML cases, aligning with the 5%–10% range reported in previous studies [3, 21]. Non‐DS‐AMKL demonstrated distinct clinical characteristics versus other pediatric AML subtypes—including significant female predominance, younger median age, and lower baseline WBC count—consistent with prior reports [4, 9, 11, 13, 21]. Furthermore, this study highlights the significantly inferior long‐term survival in non‐DS‐AMKL compared to that in other pediatric AML subtypes, a disparity attributable to its distinct molecular landscape. Non‐DS‐AMKL is characterized by mutually exclusive fusion drivers such as CBFA2T3::GLIS2 and NUP98::KDM5A. These findings align with recent genomic studies implicating CBFA2T3::GLIS2‐mediated stemness programs and NUP98‐rearranged epigenetic dysregulation as key mediators of chemorefractory status in this cohort [3, 5, 7, 17].

Our findings validate the prognostic significance of genetic risk stratification in pediatric AML and demonstrate a robust correlation between early treatment response and survival outcomes, aligning with prior evidence from the German AML‐BFM consortium [9]. In our study, both morphologic and MFC‐MRD assessments at two timepoints significantly discriminated EFS and OS. Notably, among patients achieving morphologic CR, those with MRD positivity at TP2 exhibited markedly inferior outcomes compared to those shown by their MRD‐negative counterparts. These data suggest that MFC‐MRD assessment provides superior prognostic precision to conventional morphologic evaluation, with TP2 emerging as a critical decision point for risk‐adapted therapy. This observation corroborates findings from the St. Jude Children's Research Hospital (SJCRH) AML02 trial, where MRD persistence after induction 2 predicted a dismal prognosis [8]. Therefore, incorporating MRD status into CR definitions—as proposed in the recent European LeukemiaNet guidelines [22]—could facilitate earlier transition to salvage or experimental therapies for MRD‐persistent disease.

Despite the persistently poor prognosis of pediatric non‐DS‐AMKL, standardized treatment algorithms remain elusive. While intensive chemotherapy forms the therapeutic backbone for this subtype, extensive efforts have focused on refining induction strategies—notably through cytarabine dose escalation and triple‐agent combinations (anthracycline/cytarabine/etoposide), with CR rates plateauing at 75%–85% across major trials (Japan‐cohort, AML‐BFM, SJCRH, CAMS) [4, 8, 9, 11, 12]. Moreover, the optimal consolidation strategy post‐induction for pediatric non‐DS‐AMKL remains contentious, with conflicting evidence regarding the roles of chemotherapy intensification and HSCT in CR1 [4, 9, 11, 23, 24].

Our study identified distinct response kinetics in pediatric non‐DS‐AMKL, with a first induction CR1 rate of 74.1%—significantly lower than that with other AML subtypes—suggesting inherent chemoresistance and delayed blast clearance kinetics. The cumulative CR rate after dual inductions reached 83.3%, aligning with prior reports from the Japanese [11], AML‐BFM [9], SJCRH [8], and Chinese CAMS‐2016 [4] cohorts. Notably, while FLAG‐IDA demonstrated a better CR rate in younger adults [25, 26, 27] and a select pediatric AML study [15], our findings revealed comparable early response rates between FLAG‐IDA and DAE induction, challenging its presumed superiority in non‐DS‐AMKL. This discrepancy likely stems from the unique biology of non‐DS‐AMKL, characterized by fusion‐driven transcriptional dysregulation, coupled with developmental pharmacokinetic differences, potentially subverting the efficacy of FLAG‐IDA.

Contemporary studies suggest that augmenting chemotherapy intensity alone fails to confer significant survival benefits, whereas HSCT in CR1 may substantially improve long‐term outcomes. Data from the SJCRH cohort illustrate this dichotomy: patients undergoing HSCT in CR1 achieved a 2‐year OS of 30%, compared to 0% with chemotherapy alone (p < 0.001), prompting their recommendation for HSCT as the preferred curative approach [23]. This strategy was further validated in the AML02 trial, where patients with non‐t(1;22) AMKL receiving intensive chemotherapy exhibited inferior survival to historical controls treated with conventional regimens. Consequently, the AML08 protocol continues to classify non‐t(1;22) AMKL as high‐risk, advocating HSCT in CR1 [8]. Supporting evidence has emerged from the European Group for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EBMT), which reported a 3‐year OS of 82% in children undergoing CR1 HSCT [24]. Notably, a multinational study (n = 411) revealed that OS post‐HSCT was superior when performed in CR1 (58.0% ± 5.3%) versus second remission (CR2: 27.6% ± 6.1%, p < 0.001), underscoring the prognostic impact of transplantation timing [7].

However, emerging studies suggest that intensified chemotherapy regimens may achieve favorable outcomes without mandating HSCT in CR1. In a Japanese cohort (n = 21), survival did not differ between CR1 patients undergoing HSCT (5‐year OS: 79%) and those receiving chemotherapy alone (80%, p = 0.98) [11]. Similarly, the German AML‐BFM 87/93/98 trials revealed survival outcomes of pediatric patients with non‐DS‐AMKL were comparable between patients receiving HSCT in CR1 and those undergoing chemotherapy alone (5‐year OS: 43% ± 15% vs. 54% ± 7%, p = 0.37) among [12]. In AML‐BFM 98/04 trials, dose‐escalated cytarabine during second induction significantly improved outcomes, achieving 83.6% complete remission (CR) and 70% ± 6% long‐term survival. Multivariable analysis confirmed chemotherapy intensification—not HSCT—as the primary contributor to these gains [9]. Recently, a Chinese study by Zhang et al. [4] replicated this approach with augmented cytarabine consolidation, reporting comparable 2‐year OS (chemotherapy: 73.9% ± 4.6% vs. HSCT: 75.0% ± 4.7%) and EFS (65.2% ± 4.9% vs. 75.0% ± 5.4%, p > 0.05). Collectively, these findings challenge the necessity of routine HSCT in CR1, proposing that modern chemotherapy intensification may obviate transplantation for pediatric non‐DS‐AMKL without compromising survival outcomes.

HSCT's survival benefit in CR1 for high‐risk patients aligns with emerging evidence that it can overcome the adverse impact of certain genetic lesions. For example, CBFA2T3::GLIS2‐positive AML retains sensitivity to graft‐versus‐leukemia effects [28]. However, the absence of HSCT benefit in non‐HR subgroups suggests that indiscriminate transplant strategies may subject patients without high‐risk genetic lesions to unnecessary toxicity without survival gain. Notably, we found that patients with a suboptimal early treatment response—defined as failure to achieve morphologic CR or MFC‐MRD negativity after the first induction course—derived significant survival benefit from HSCT. These collective findings highlight the nuanced role of HSCT in non‐DS‐AMKL. While CR1 transplantation does not universally guarantee survival advantage over chemotherapy, its benefit appears most pronounced in high‐risk subgroups and when implemented early in the disease course. Therefore, we recommend prioritizing allo‐HSCT in CR1 for patients with non‐DS‐AMKL, particularly those with adverse molecular features or suboptimal early treatment response. Future protocols must integrate molecular profiling and MRD monitoring to refine patient selection, ensuring that transplantation's benefits outweigh its inherent risks in this biologically diverse malignancy.

This study's limitations, including its retrospective design, non‐randomized induction allocation, and small DAE subgroup, preclude definitive conclusions about the role of FLAG‐IDA. Selection bias may have influenced regimen assignment, as clinicians might have preferentially used FLAG‐IDA for higher‐risk cases. Nevertheless, the consistency of our MRD and HSCT findings with other non‐DS‐AMKL cohorts strengthens their validity.

In conclusion, while induction intensification with FLAG‐IDA offers no survival advantage over DAE, risk‐stratified HSCT and MRD‐guided consolidation are indispensable for mitigating relapse. Future protocols must prioritize molecular characterization at diagnosis and accelerate the translation of novel agents targeting disease‐specific vulnerabilities.
