Perceptions of the decriminalisation of suicidal attempts among mental health service users in Ghana: a qualitative study
Authors: Cynthia Quarshie, Dennis Bomansang Daliri, Richard Dei-Asamoa, Jeduthun Osei-Sekyereh, Daniel Adjei, Akwasi Osei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide remains a major global public health concern, with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) disproportionately affected. In Ghana, attempted suicide had been criminalised under Section 57 (2) of the Criminal and Other Offences Act, 1960 (Act 29) until March 2023 when it was decriminalised. Despite this major reform, little is known about how mental health service users, the group most directly affected perceive the decriminalisation of attempted suicide and its potential impact on health-seeking behaviours. This study explored the perspectives of mental health service users on the decriminalisation of atempted suicide in Ghana, and anticipated effects on help-seeking behaviours.MethodsA qualitative phenomenological design was employed. Thirty adult mental health service users with lived experience of suicidal ideation or attempts were purposively recruited from the Psychiatry department of the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, Accra. Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews and analyzed thematically using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach.ResultsParticipants’ perspectives revealed two key themes regarding perceptions of suicide: (1) perception of suicide as an escape route, a means of vindication, and a stress reaction and (2) perceptions about its criminality (suicidal attempt as a crime and suicide attempt not a crime). On the impact of decriminalisation of on health seeking behaviours, two key themes were developed. These were: (1) Positive Impact and (2) Negative impact. Their corresponding subthemes are discussed in the paper.ConclusionIn this study, participants perceived suicide to be a escape from the challenges of life, a means of vindication, or a stress reaction. They further identified that decriminalisation had both positive and negative impacts on the health-seeking behaviours of service users. The findings point to the fact that decriminalization alone may not suffice in improving suicide prevention outcomes in Ghana. Complementary interventions such as stigma reduction campaigns, and community sensitization in terms of education are essential to maximize the benefits of the legal reform.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07720-4.Keywords: Suicide, Suicidal attempt, Decriminalisation, Mental health, Ghana, Qualitative study, Service users

Introduction

Suicide continues to pose a major global public health challenge, with more than 700,000 deaths reported each year across the world [1]. It is generally defined as death arising from self-inflicted harm undertaken with the intention to die [2]. When such self-directed behavior occurs with similar intent but does not result in death, it is termed a suicide attempt [3]. These behaviors remain leading contributors to mortality globally, particularly among younger populations [4]. For instance, in the United States, suicide is the second leading cause of death among individuals aged 10 to 29 years and the third among those aged 15 to 45 [4]. The burden intensified during the COVID-19 pandemic, amid widespread disruptions that heightened psychological distress, social isolation, and financial insecurity [5].

Sub-Saharan Africa continues to register disproportionately high suicide rates relative to many other regions, suggesting the need for contextualized prevention strategies that account for unique sociocultural and systemic realities [6]. Ghana reflects this regional pattern, with growing evidence of significant suicidal behaviors among its adolescent and young adult populations. Azasu et al. [7], for example, found that 25% of Ghanaian middle and high school students reported suicidal ideation, while 19% had attempted suicide and 22% had made plans to do so. These figures highlight the considerable scale of suicidality among young people and underscore the urgency of strengthening preventive responses.

The heightened risk of suicide among individuals with mental health conditions is well-documented in global literature. People living with psychiatric illnesses, particularly depressive disorders, face markedly elevated suicide risk, with some studies reporting rates as high as 534 per 100,000 person-years among diagnosed depressive patients [8]. Meta-analyses further affirm strong associations between suicide and a broad range of psychiatric disorders, including mood, substance use, and psychotic disorders [9–11]. These findings emphasize the need for integrated mental health interventions as central components of suicide prevention efforts.

Despite this, suicide and attempted suicide remain criminalised in more than 20 countries [12]. Justifications for criminalisation often rest on assumptions that punitive approaches deter suicidal behavior or prevent moral permissiveness [13]. However, these arguments consistently lack empirical support. Evidence suggests that countries retaining punitive laws frequently have higher suicide rates than global averages, whereas those that have decriminalised attempts tend to experience more substantial declines in suicide rates over time [14]. The argument for decriminalisation, however, becomes stronger when examined comparatively across different health systems. Countries that have successfully reduced suicide rates following decriminalisation, such as India and Sri Lanka, typically paired legal reform with investments in crisis services, community mental health infrastructure, and improved pathways to care [15]. In contrast, countries with limited mental health resources may struggle to realize the full impact of decriminalisation unless health systems are strengthened. This comparison highlights that legal reform alone is insufficient; the broader healthcare ecosystem must be capable of responding effectively. Bringing this into Ghana’s context, gaps in financing, workforce availability, and mental health literacy remain relevant structural concerns that must be addressed to ensure the benefits of decriminalisation are fully realized [16]. Incorporating this nuance supports a more balanced understanding of why punitive laws were ineffective and how systemic factors interact with legislative change.

In Ghana, attempted suicide was criminalized under Sect. 57 [2] of the Criminal and Other Offences Act, 1960 (Act 29) [17], categorizing it as a misdemeanor punishable by fines or imprisonment. After years of advocacy from mental health professionals, civil society, and human rights groups, Parliament repealed the law in March 2023 [18]. This repeal marked a shift toward a more compassionate, public health–oriented approach to suicide prevention.

Yet, there remains limited empirical evidence on how Ghanaians, particularly mental health service users, perceive this policy change. Understanding the views of individuals who live with mental health difficulties and have heightened vulnerability to suicidal behavior is essential for determining whether decriminalization has reduced stigma, improved help-seeking, or altered the way suicide is discussed within communities. As Lew et al. [14] emphasize, decriminalisation can be impactful only when accompanied by comprehensive preventive efforts, awareness programs, and improved access to care.

This study aims to fill this critical gap by exploring the perceptions of mental health service users in Ghana regarding the decriminalisation of attempted suicide. Using a qualitative approach, it provides an in-depth understanding of how individuals who are most affected by the policy change interpret its intentions, benefits, and limitations. The findings will contribute to the ongoing national and regional discussions on suicide prevention and offer evidence to guide future policy development, advocacy efforts, and culturally grounded mental health strategies across Sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative design grounded in a descriptive phenomenological approach to explore how mental health service users in Ghana perceive the decriminalisation of attempted suicide. Descriptive phenomenology was deemed appropriate because it enables the researcher to access and describe participants’ lived experiences as faithfully as possible, privileging their own meanings while deliberately suspending theoretical assumptions or prior interpretations through the practice of bracketing [19]. In keeping with this philosophical orientation, the development of the interview guide involved careful attention to openness and neutrality. Although the guide was drafted by the research team, expert consultation was sought to refine its cultural sensitivity and ensure that questions encouraged participants to describe their experiences in their own terms rather than steering them toward predetermined conceptual categories. The guide served only as a flexible framework to support the conversation and ensure coverage of the phenomenon of interest. During the interviews, participants’ perspectives and experiential accounts were prioritised, enabling the study to remain open to the emergence of unanticipated meanings and to capture their lived realities with minimal intrusion from researcher presuppositions.

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Psychiatric Department of the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, Ghana’s main referral center for psychiatric services. The hospital is a tertiary facility with all the medical specialties. It serves as a training centre for doctors, nurses, medical laboratory technicians, and several other cadres of healthcare workers. It has departments of medicine, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, and pediatrics, with their associated sub-specialties. Among the specialties is the department of Psychiatry, which has various units including general adult, child and adolescent, addiction, old age, forensic, and neuropsychiatry. It receives referrals from all over the country but predominantly within the Accra Metropolis. The hospital was purposively selected because it serves both urban and rural populations, thereby providing access to a diverse group of service users shaped by differing socio-cultural backgrounds.

Study population

The study population consisted of adult mental health service users aged 18 years and above who had experienced suicidal ideation or attempts and who were currently receiving or had previously received psychiatric care at the facility.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were those with sufficient cognitive ability to engage meaningfully in interviews, who provided informed consent, and who demonstrated a reasonable understanding of the study topic.

Exclusion criteria

Service users experiencing acute psychiatric distress at the time of data collection, where participation could exacerbate symptoms or hinder meaningful engagement, were excluded.

Sampling procedure and sample size

A purposive sampling technique was employed to recruit a diverse range of participants who met the inclusion criteria. This method was selected to ensure variation by actively seeking out mental health service users with differing demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, diagnosis, and treatment histories) and lived experiences with suicidal ideation or attempts. Such diversity allowed the study to maximize the richness and breadth of the data, ensuring that multiple perspectives were captured.

In line with established qualitative research standards, the final sample size was determined by the principle of data saturation rather than a fixed numerical target at the outset. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently in an iterative process, enabling the research team to assess emerging patterns. In total, 30 participants were interviewed. This sample size aligns with recommendations for qualitative inquiry, where 20–30 interviews are generally sufficient to achieve thematic saturation while still allowing for diversity and depth of perspectives [19, 20].

Data collection instrument

The study was conducted using a semi-structured interview guide developed by the researchers through extensive literature search and consultations with psychiatrists and mental health advocate groups to ensure that the questions are suitable and address the topic under study. It was made up of three sections. The first section was made up of the sociodemographic characteristics (sex, age, occupation, and religion. The second section was made up of questions on the perception about suicide and the decriminalisation of attempted suicide. The third section enquires about the impact of attempted suicide decriminalisation on health seeking behaviours among service users. Details about the questionnaire can be found in supplementary file 1. Prior to the use of the tool for the study, it was pretested among five service users at the Accra Psychiatric hospital which is about 5 km from the study site. This was done to ensure that the tool is easy to understand and also addresses what it seeks to address.

Data collection procedure

Permission was obtained from the hospital’s research department before scheduling face-to-face interview sessions with study participants. At the start of each session, the first author, a female medical student with a BSc in Medical Sciences, fluent in English and Twi with experience in qualitative interviews, introduced herself, explained the study’s aims, and sought participants’ informed consent. Participants were also asked for permission to record the interviews using an audio recorder. All interviews were conducted in a private and conducive room at the Psychiatry department. This room ensured confidentiality since only the participant and the interviewer were present during each session. Follow-up and probing questions were employed whenever clarifications were needed. Each interview lasted an average of forty-five (45) minutes. In addition to the audio recordings, the researcher maintained detailed field notes and documented nonverbal cues, such as body language and posture, to enrich contextual understanding of the data. To ensure credibility and trustworthiness, transcripts were returned to participants for member checking, allowing them to review and correct any misrepresentations. On average, five participants were interviewed weekly, and data collection took place between 1st May and 30th June 2025. A total of 30 participants were interviewed. To safeguard anonymity, pseudonyms were assigned. There was no prior relationship with participants, thereby minimising the risk of social desirability bias. Data collection was discontinued after the thirtieth interview, at which point no new information or concepts were emerging, confirming that data saturation had been achieved. No repeat interviews were conducted.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in strict accordance with ethical principles for research involving human participants, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, Accra, Ghana (KBTH-ADM/000247/2025). Prior to participation, all respondents were provided with detailed information regarding the purpose, procedures, potential risks, and anticipated benefits of the study. Written informed consent was obtained from participants who were able to sign, while those unable to do so indicated consent through thumb-printing in the presence of a witness. Considering the sensitive nature of the topic understudy, arrangements were made with the head of department at the psychiatry department to ensure that participants who felt distressed in the course of the study (during interview process) were referred to the department for management. Confidentiality and anonymity were rigorously maintained by de-identifying data and ensuring that information obtained was used solely for research purposes. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without any consequences to their ongoing care.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed thematically following the six-phase approach outlined by Braun and Clarke [21]. Data analysis was done manually through the following steps: firstly, the first and Second authors familiarized themselves with the data by reading and re-reading the interview transcripts alongside field notes, while making reflective notes to capture initial impressions. Second, initial codes were generated manually and independently by the two authors to identify meaningful features of the data relevant to the research objectives. Areas of disagreements in the codings were settled by the fifth author (supervisor). In the third phase, these codes were collated into potential themes, which captured broader patterns of meaning across the dataset. During the fourth phase, themes were reviewed and refined by comparing them against the coded extracts and the entire dataset to ensure coherence, internal consistency, and distinctiveness. In the fifth phase, the themes were defined and named to clearly capture their essence and scope. Finally, in the sixth phase, the findings were organized into a coherent narrative account, supported by verbatim excerpts from participants to illustrate key points. The reporting adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [22] (see Supplementary file 1), ensuring methodological transparency and completeness. Collected materials, including audio recordings, transcripts, and field notes, were stored securely and anonymized. Unique identification codes were assigned to each participant ensuring that no personally identifiable information was retained in any data files. Soft copies of data were stored on a password-protected computer, and physical materials were kept in a locked storage accessible only to the researchers, in as dictated by ethical research guidelines.

Trustworthiness

The study adhered to Lincoln and Guba’s criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability to ensure trustworthiness [23]. Credibility was enhanced through peer debriefing with an experienced qualitative research supervisor, who provided external insights that helped challenge assumptions and refine interpretations. Member checking was also undertaken by sharing a summary of preliminary themes with selected participants to validate the accuracy and resonance of the findings. Transferability was addressed by providing thick descriptions of the study setting, participant characteristics, recruitment strategies, and the interview process. Such detail enables readers to judge the applicability of the findings to other contexts. Additionally, direct quotations from participants are presented throughout the results section to illustrate themes and authentically represent participants’ perspectives. Dependability was maintained by establishing a clear audit trail that documented all methodological decisions, coding processes, and theme development steps. This transparent record allows others to follow the research process and assess its consistency over time. Confirmability was achieved through reflexive journaling, in which the researcher documented personal assumptions.

Results

The study included 30 participants, of whom 60% were female and 40% were male. In terms of age distribution, the majority fell within the 21–30 years age group (50%), followed by those aged 31–40 years (20%), 41–50 years (16.7%), and smaller proportions in the 51–60 years (6.6%) and 61–70 years (6.7%). With respect to religion, two-thirds of participants (66.7%) identified as Christian, while 16.7% were Muslim and 16.7% were Atheist. Regarding occupation, students and the unemployed made up 30% each forming the majority. Regarding engagement with services, the number of visits varied. The largest proportions of participants had made either two visits (33.3%) or three visits (33.3%), while 16.7% had four visits, 6.7% had five visits, and 6.7% had six visits. Perceptions of mental health service users towards the decriminalization of suicidal attempts

The first objective explored how mental health service users conceptualised suicide and how they perceived the criminalisation of suicidal attempts in Ghana. Two overarching themes emerged: (1) Understanding of suicide and (2) Perceptions about the criminality of suicidal attempts.

Theme 1: Understanding of suicide

Participants’ understanding of suicide was varied, shaped by personal experiences, cultural interpretations, and psycho-social contexts. Their perspectives were grouped into three subthemes: suicide as an escape route, suicide as a means of vindication, and suicide as a stress reaction.

Subtheme 1.1: Suicide as an escape route

A dominant view was that suicide serves as a deliberate means of escaping persistent suffering, hopelessness, or unbearable life circumstances. For these participants, suicide was seen less as impulsive and more as a reasoned decision in the face of distress.

In my opinion as far as suicide is concerned, suicide means many things such as suicide is willingly ending your life. Suicide is bad but in some people it is understandable. It is putting an end to your suffering (26 year-old female).

Hmm, you want to know my understanding about suicide right? I think suicide is like a necessary evil… an easy way out when life becomes too difficult to live (51 year-old female).

Subtheme 1.2: Suicide as a means of vindication

For some, suicide was interpreted as a way of sending a message to others about the seriousness of one’s struggles or to restore dignity. It was seen as a form of protest or self-justification against misunderstanding or unfair treatment.

It is actually something everybody has to consider. Sometimes no body understands you, the difficulties you are going through and all that. When things become this difficult but no one seem to understand you or you are going through a problem and people don’t see the seriousness, then suicide may be the option. It is a way to show family members that your problem is a serious one (47 year-old male).

Sometimes, suicide is the only way to show people that I was right when they wrongly accused me (21 year old female).

Subtheme 1.3: Suicide as a stress reaction

Other participants viewed suicide primarily as an emotional reaction to overwhelming stressors and the exhaustion of coping resources. They associated suicidal behaviour with the inability to manage emotions or share problems.

People get stressed and they are not able to control their emotions… it happens when people are not able to share their problems so they have to finish themselves (28 year-old male).

Suicide is killing your own self with poison. sometimes you feel so stressed and without thinking at all, you just do it like taking any chemical close by. When it happens, it means you are tired (55 year-old female).

Theme 2: Perceptions about the criminality of suicidal attempt

This theme examined participants’ awareness of and attitudes toward the legal status of suicide in Ghana. Two contrasting subthemes emerged: “suicidal attempt is a crime” and “suicidal attempt is not a crime”.

Subtheme 2.1: Suicidal attempt is a crime

The majority of participants endorsed the view that suicide should be considered a criminal offence. Their reasoning was rooted in moral, legal, and religious principles, particularly the sanctity of life.

As far as the criminality of suicidal attempt is concerned, I think there is no excuse for anyone to attempt suicide, I believe irrespective of the reason, it is a crime. Even if the person has a mental health issue, it is still taking life so yes, it is a criminal act (29 year-old male).

The criminality or otherwise of attempted suicide depends on some factors; I believe that if they are trying to run away from a bad thing they have done, like committing a crime and wanting to use suicide as a hide-out to avoid punishment, then yes, it is a crime (22 year-old male).

Subtheme 2.2: Suicide is not a crime

A smaller but notable group believed suicide should not be criminalised. They argued it was either a personal right or a mental health issue that required care, not punishment.

Are you asking if attempted suicide is a crime or not?….No, attempted suicide is not a crime. For me, it is rather a good way to end things, no life, no troubles (51-year-old female).

I believe that people with mental illness who attempt suicide should not be punished, so if the person has a mental health issue, then no, it is not a crime. What they need is help, not jail (28-year-old male).

Perceived impact of suicidal attempt decriminalisation on help-seeking behaviours

The second objective examined participants’ perceptions of how removing legal penalties for suicide might influence help-seeking behaviour. Two broad themes were identified: positive impact and negative impact.

Theme 1: Positive impact

Most participants anticipated that decriminalisation would reduce fear and stigma, thereby encouraging individuals experiencing suicidal ideation to seek professional help earlier and more openly.

Subtheme 1.1: Improvement in mental health service use

Participants reported that knowing suicide is no longer criminalised would make them more willing to seek care without fear of legal consequences.

I am one of the people who believe suicide should not be criminalised. I believed that it will create a situation where some of us I mean we the mental health service users may find it difficult to come out when we face difficulties So when I heard that suicide had been decrminalised, I was comfortable and now that I know it is not a crime, yes, I will gladly seek for help at any time (36 year-old male).

Now that I know it is not a crime, I will comfortably come to a mental health facility whenever I am having suicidal thought (55 year-old female).

Subtheme 1.2: Openness about mental health challenges

Decriminalisation was also seen as enabling openness, allowing people to discuss their challenges with friends, family, or professionals without fear of punishment.

…With the decrminalisation of suicidal attempts, it means now I can talk to my friends and family, even strangers without fearing that I will be sent to prison (31 year-old male).

When you know it is not a crime, you can speak your mind more freely and seek help before it gets worse (42 year-old female).

Theme 2: Negative impact

Subtheme 2.1: Decriminalisation may encourage suicide

A minority of participants expressed concern that decriminalisation might remove a deterrent effect and inadvertently encourage suicidal behaviour.

…then freedom of death oo, people will feel too free to commit suicide if they know that there is no law against killing yourself (28 year-old female).

If they say it is not a crime, then people will think it is normal, and more people may do it (29 year-old male).

Subtheme 2.2: Stigma may persist despite decriminalization

Some participants observed that even if criminal penalties were abolished, communities might continue to gossip about and marginalize individuals who attempt suicide.

Even if the law changes, people will still gossip and look down on those who attempt suicide (33 year-old female).

Whether the law changes or not, people will still treat survivors of suicide attempts with shame and discrimination (23 year-old female).

Discussion

The study aimed to explore the perspectives of mental health service users regarding the decriminalisation of attempted suicide in Ghana. Specifically, it addressed two objectives: (1) to examine service users’ perceptions of the decriminalisation of attempted suicide, and (2) to explore their views on the potential impact of decriminalisation on help-seeking behaviours. In relation to the first objective, two overarching themes were identified: (1) perception of suicide and (2) perceptions of the criminality of attempted suicide, each with their associated subthemes. With respect to the second objective, two further themes emerged: (1) perceived positive impacts of decriminalisation and (2) perceived negative impacts of decriminalisation.

Participants demonstrated varied perceptions of suicide, represented by three subthemes: suicide as an escape route, suicide as a means of vindication, and suicide as a stress reaction.

Firstly, the perception of suicide as an escape route aligns with global evidence suggesting that individuals often regard suicide as a means of escaping unbearable psychological pain or life difficulties. Shneidman’s [24] theory of “psychache” underscores this perspective, arguing that intolerable mental pain is a central motivator for suicide. Similar findings have been reported in both high- and low-income countries, where suicide is frequently described as a rationalized choice to end suffering [25]. Within sub-Saharan Africa, Osafo et al. [26] observed that Ghanaians frequently interpret suicidal behaviour as an attempt to flee unmanageable social or financial burdens, which mirrors the narratives expressed in this study. Secondly, the interpretation of suicide as a means of vindication introduces a more socially embedded understanding. Here, suicide is framed as a communicative act intended to demonstrate the seriousness of one’s struggles, seek validation, or restore dignity in the face of stigma or unfair treatment. This corresponds with Durkheim’s sociological perspectives, which view some suicidal acts as protest against social disintegration or injustice [27]. Similar patterns have been identified in Asia, where suicide notes often reflect an intent to communicate grievances or to influence the behaviour of others [28]. In Ghana, Osafo et al. [29] have also highlighted the communicative function of suicidal behaviour, particularly in family contexts where individuals feel misunderstood. Thus, this subtheme reinforces the social dimension of suicide as more than a purely individual act. Thirdly, the view that suicide is a stress reaction reflects participants’ recognition of the link between stress, emotional regulation, and suicidal behaviour. This interpretation is consistent with evidence from psychological autopsy studies that highlight the role of acute stressors such as interpersonal conflict, financial hardship, or chronic illness in triggering suicidal crises [30]. The emphasis on emotional exhaustion and lack of support also resonates with research from Ghana and Nigeria, where stigma and limited avenues for emotional expression often exacerbate distress [31]. Importantly, this framing positions suicide less as a deliberate choice and more as a consequence of depleted coping mechanisms, underscoring the need for strengthening psychosocial support systems.

The study also identified varied views on the legality or otherwise of attempted suicide. Participants who regarded attempted suicide as a crime drew on moral, religious, and legal frameworks. This echoes longstanding influences of both colonial law and Christian doctrine, which historically criminalised suicide as self-murder [32]. In Ghana, the legal framework continued to criminalise attempted suicide under Sect. 57 of the Criminal Code until recently, reflecting the persistence of these moralistic interpretations. Religious underpinnings, particularly the view of life as sacred and divinely given, further reinforce the perception of suicidal acts as morally and legally condemnable [33]. This aligns with findings from Kenya and Uganda, where attempted suicide is similarly stigmatised as a violation of divine or communal laws [34]. Conversely, participants who argued that attempted suicide is not a crime construed it either as a personal right or as a manifestation of mental illness. This aligns with contemporary global mental health discourse, which views suicide and attempted suicide through a public health and human rights lens rather than as a criminal act [35]. In particular, the recognition that individuals with mental health conditions require help rather than punishment parallels advocacy efforts in Ghana and elsewhere to decriminalise attempted suicide and shift toward prevention and care [18]. Similar findings have been reported in India, where service users and families increasingly reject punitive responses and call for compassionate care-based approaches [32]. The divergence of perspectives in this study highlights the transitional stage in Ghana’s discourse on suicide. On one hand, criminalising views remain deeply entrenched in moral and cultural frameworks. On the other hand, a growing recognition of suicide/attempted suicide as a mental health issue reflects the gradual uptake of global health perspectives among service users. This duality may partly explain why suicide remains highly stigmatized in Ghana, even though it has been decriminalised. It further beckons on policy makers and advocates to intensify destigmatisation educational campaigns to consolidate the gains so far.

Perceived impact of suicide decriminalisation on help-seeking behaviours.

The most dominant perspective was that removing criminal penalties for suicide would encourage greater willingness to seek mental health care. Participants suggested that the fear of arrest, imprisonment, or public exposure currently deters individuals from approaching healthcare professionals when experiencing suicidal thoughts. Also, the fear of legal repercussions, combined with the profound societal shame attached to a non-fatal suicide attempt, may paradoxically compel individuals to engage in more deliberate and lethal planning. Consequently, individuals may feel pressure to ensure that the act is fatal in order to avoid the social and legal consequences of survival. This troubling dynamic has been documented in several contexts where suicide attempts remain criminalised, showing that punitive laws can inadvertently increase suicide lethality while simultaneously suppressing help-seeking [7, 32]. Recognising this paradox reinforces the urgent need to decriminalise suicide attempts, as legal reform can help remove the fear-driven secrecy that heightens risk and instead promote early disclosure, compassionate intervention, and access to mental health care. This finding is consistent with previous research in Ghana, which highlights that criminalisation acts as a significant barrier to timely help-seeking and instead fosters secrecy, shame, and isolation [29]. Decriminalisation, therefore, was perceived as a step toward dismantling these barriers by reducing the immediate fear of legal punishment. This finding resonates with global evidence. In India, where suicide was decriminalised in 2017, early evaluations indicate increased openness in public discourse about suicide and a shift toward viewing suicidal behaviour as a health rather than criminal issue [32]. Similarly, in countries such as Sri Lanka and Guyana, both of which have considered decriminalisation, advocates argue that legal reform facilitates earlier intervention by legitimising help-seeking and reducing the stigma attached to suicidal behaviour [36].

Participants also highlighted that decriminalisation could foster greater openness about mental health challenges beyond clinical settings, including within families and communities. This is particularly significant in Ghana, where strong cultural taboos against discussing suicide persist, often leading to concealment of distress and underutilisation of support systems [37]. By removing the association with criminality, decriminalisation could create safer spaces for conversations, enabling individuals to disclose suicidal thoughts to trusted networks before crises escalate. This aligns with evidence from high-income contexts, where reducing stigma has been shown to enhance disclosure and help-seeking behaviour [38]. Importantly, this shift could strengthen both formal and informal pathways of care, recognising that in Ghana, social and family networks remain a primary resource for emotional support. In sharp contrast, some service users opined that decriminalisation could normalise or encourage suicidal behaviour. They feared that removing legal sanctions would eliminate an important deterrent and potentially lead to increased suicide attempts. This perception is not unique to Ghana; similar concerns have been raised in jurisdictions debating decriminalisation of suicidal attempts, particularly in societies where moral and religious frameworks strongly shape public attitudes toward attempted suicide [39]. However, empirical evidence does not support the claim that criminalisation reduces suicide rates. A review by Mishara and Weisstub [40] found no credible evidence that punitive laws deter suicidal behaviour, and in some cases, criminalisation may worsen outcomes by pushing individuals away from care. Nevertheless, the persistence of these concerns highlights the importance of coupling legal reform with public education, to clarify that decriminalisation does not mean condoning or encouraging suicide but rather recognising it as a health issue requiring compassionate intervention.

Another important concern raised by participants was that stigma may persist despite legal reforms. While decriminalisation may reduce fear of punishment, participants doubted its ability to address entrenched cultural beliefs and community gossip surrounding suicide attempts. The perception that survivors would continue to face shame, discrimination, and marginalisation, regardless of legal changes, is well documented in the literature. In Uganda, for instance, Rukundo et al. [34] found that suicide attempters remained highly stigmatised even though criminal prosecutions were rare in practice. Similarly, studies from Nigeria [41] and Kenya [42] indicate that stigma surrounding suicide is deeply rooted in religious and cultural narratives that may not shift automatically with legislative changes. This highlights the risk that decriminalisation, while necessary, may not be sufficient to transform community attitudes without parallel efforts in stigma reduction and mental health literacy.

Implications for practice and research

The study highlights that criminalisation deters help-seeking and reinforces stigma, thereby supporting Ghana’s action at decriminalising attempted suicide. Decriminalisation should serve as a foundation for broader public health–oriented suicide prevention, supported by accessible, compassionate, and culturally sensitive mental health services. Interventions must address not only individual psychological needs but also family and community stressors, reflecting the social and communicative dimensions of suicidal behaviour. Given the persistence of stigma despite legal reforms, targeted anti-stigma campaigns, especially involving health workers, religious leaders, and community gatekeepers, are essential to reshape public perceptions. In line with these established gaps and recommendations, future research in Ghana should adopt mixed-methods designs that quantify public knowledge and attitudes toward suicide laws while also examining the nuanced social and psychological meanings that shape behaviour. Such integrated evidence would provide a stronger empirical foundation for policy discussions on suicide decriminalisation and its potential public health implications [31].

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. The sample comprised mental health service users from one facility which may have limited the information gathered. Another notable limitation is the study’s limited engagement with the contextual differences between urban and rural populations. Although both groups were included, their distinct social environments, service access, and cultural norms may have shaped participants’ reflections in ways not fully captured by the analysis. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable, in-depth insights into an underexplored area in Ghana especially after the recent decriminalisation of suicide.

Conclusion

This study explored the perspectives of mental health service users on the decriminalisation of suicide in Ghana, focusing on both perceptions of suicide criminality and the anticipated impact of legal reform on help-seeking behaviours. The findings reveal that while many participants continue to view suicide through moral and legal frameworks that support criminalisation, there is also a growing recognition of suicide as a health and human rights issue requiring care rather than punishment. Participants anticipated that decriminalisation would encourage greater openness and earlier engagement with mental health services, though concerns about normalisation of suicide and persistent stigma remain.

Taken together, these results highlight that decriminalisation, though essential, will not be sufficient to reduce the burden of suicide in Ghana. It must be accompanied by broader structural reforms, including investment in mental health care, anti-stigma campaigns, and culturally sensitive suicide prevention strategies. Future research and practice should therefore adopt a comprehensive public health and human rights approach, positioning suicide not as a crime but as a pressing health issue requiring empathy, intervention, and societal support.
