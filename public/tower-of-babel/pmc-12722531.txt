Training Medical Students in Deaf, DeafBlind, and Hard-of-Hearing Patient Care Through Interactive Lecture and Simulation
Authors: Shannon Y. Zhou, Morgan McBride, Marcus Milani, Jonathan Penny, Heath Focken, Kathy Manlapas, Joseph M. Miller, Tseganesh Selameab, Hope Pogemiller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionApproximately one in seven individuals in the United States has hearing loss, yet many medical students have limited exposure to Deaf culture, accessibility practices, and health disparities faced by Deaf, DeafBlind, and Hard-of-Hearing (DDBHH) patients. To address this, an educational session was introduced that later expanded to include simulated scenarios.MethodsWe developed a two-part initiative incorporating community narratives, lecture, and case-based learning. First, we implemented a 2-hour elective interactive lecture co-designed with Deaf collaborators, which later expanded into a required session for third-year medical students. We developed a 2-hour simulation session featuring four scenarios with community-recruited and trained DDBHH patients, andvarious interpreters, including American Sign Language, Certified Deaf, Video Remote, and DeafBlind Interpreters. Presession and postsession surveys assessed student knowledge and confidence.ResultsAmong 236 third-year medical students, approximately 80% self-identified as having minimal or no knowledge of Deaf culture or disparities. Postlecture, the proportion of students who felt equipped to provide equitable care to DDBHH patients increased from 7% to 84% (35% response rate). Eight students participated in the simulated session. Postsimulation, knowledge scores increased by 75%, and mean confidence scores (5-point Likert scale; 1 = not confident, 5 = confident) in caring for Deaf and DeafBlind patients increased by 1.3 and 1.7 points, respectively (100% response rate). Both sessions received strong positive feedback that emphasized applicability of the experiences.DiscussionIntegrating education on DDBHH health disparities and culture into medical training is needed and feasible. Next steps include program expansion and supporting adaptation at other institutions.

Introduction

Effective health care communication is critical to equitable patient care, yet Deaf, DeafBlind, and Hard-of-Hearing (DDBHH) patients encounter significant health care communication challenges, contributing to restricted access to quality care, lower health literacy, and poorer health outcomes.1 An estimated 50 million Americans (one in seven) have some hearing loss, and approximately 12 million Americans consider themselves deaf.2,3 In Minnesota, where this initiative took place, approximately one in five residents is deaf or hard-of-hearing, yet medical students often receive no training in Deaf culture, accessibility practices, or the unique health disparities affecting this population.4 Health care providers often lack skills to effectively communicate with DDBHH patients, contributing to disparities in health care access, miscommunication, and suboptimal patient experiences.5–8

These challenges, compounded by socioeconomic disparities, mean that Deaf patients are less likely to have a primary care provider, less likely to receive a thorough workup (eg, labs, imaging) in the emergency department, and more likely to experience longer hospitalizations than hearing individuals.9–11 Deaf individuals also have higher rates of chronic disease, maternal morbidity, and mental health concerns.12–15 Alarmingly, Deaf patients are four times more likely to be restrained during hospitalization, attributed to lack of cultural awareness and misinterpretation of threat signals (ie, expressive signing).16

Several medical schools have addressed these disparities by integrating Deaf cultural awareness and accessibility training into curricula via lectures, panel discussions with Deaf and Hard-of-Hearing (DHH) individuals, American Sign Language (ASL) courses, and role-reversal exercises where Deaf volunteers act as health care providers and students navigate health care without full communication access.17–20 Our initiative combines an interactive lecture on Deaf culture and health disparities with simulations involving community-recruited/trained DDBHH patients and various interpreters (including ASL Interpreters, Certified Deaf Interpreters [CDIs], Video Remote Interpreters [VRIs], and DeafBlind Interpreters [DBIs]). Interactive methods were employed because ASL is inherently visual, providing a more authentic and effective learning experience.21

To further contextualize our approach, it is pertinent to understand the functions of different interpreters. CDIs work alongside hearing ASL interpreters to facilitate clearer communication when patients have limited language access, often due to early language deprivation, concomitant diagnoses like autism, or fluency in a non-ASL sign language. CDIs use universal gestures, props, and sometimes multiple signed languages, rendering them indispensable in complex communication encounters. DBIs use protactile ASL, which conveys visuospatial information through touch, and have Clear Speech training, which involves rephrasing and speaking clearly into a frequency modulation system that is relayed wirelessly via Bluetooth to the DeafBlind individual's hearing aids. Notably, many hearing providers are unfamiliar with how to use these systems or how to adapt their speech for DDBHH individuals. To our knowledge, this is the first program to provide simulated interactions with DeafBlind patients.

Our initiative addressed these gaps by delivering didactic and simulation-based learning to advance clinical communication skills, equip students with accessibility tools, and foster cultural humility to promote equitable care for DDBHH patients.

Methods

Overview

Our two-part educational initiative blended Deaf culture and accessibility practices using community narratives, lectures, think-pair-share exercises, case-based learning, and simulated learning. We implemented the initiative within the context of the University of Minnesota (UMN) Medical School's Becoming a Doctor (BaDr) Week, a longitudinal course for medical students focused on advancing technical and soft clinical skills. Part 1 began with an elective interactive lecture in January 2024 for third-year medical students. Course directors later approved the session as a required lecture for third-year medical students, delivered in August 2024 and March 2025. Part 2 of the initiative included an elective simulated session for third-year medical students in March 2025.

The UMN Institutional Review Board deemed this initiative not human research (No. STUDY00024908). No prior experience with ASL or Deaf culture was required. Efficacy was assessed using presession surveys, postsession feedback forms, and exit tickets—a knowledge assessment required by the BaDr course. We designed surveys based on existing literature on disability and cultural humility education. UMN and the Minnesota Medical Association provided grant funding, which allowed us to compensate community partners for their time and training.

Interactive Lecture

A hearing student initiated this project after witnessing inequitable care for DDBHH patients during clinical rotations. To recruit community partners, the student reached out to UMN's ASL department and a local Deaf community organization. Together with an ASL teacher and Deaf community health worker, the lecture was co-created and co-delivered.

The presentation consisted of three sections, including (1) foundations (terminology, interpreter etiquette, accessibility tools), (2) historical context and health disparities, and (3) application of knowledge (cases, best practices; Appendix A). Narratives in each section illustrated disparities. Interactive elements included think-pair-share activities, case-based learning, live instruction of basic ASL phrases, and Menti, a real-time digital word cloud generation software (slide 11, Appendix A).

A prelecture survey (Appendix B) assessed students’ self-perceived baseline knowledge of Deaf culture and health disparities and whether they felt they had tools to provide equitable care to DDBHH patients. A postsession feedback survey and knowledge assessment/exit ticket (Appendix C) were also administered. Question types included multiple choice, Likert scale, multi-select, and free-response feedback. Survey items were developed in partnership with DHH community collaborators. Questions were created to align with the educational objectives, ensuring that items reflected the experiences and priorities emphasized by collaborators. Following overwhelmingly positive feedback, the lecture became a required part of BaDr Week, with two DHH medical students and a Deaf guest presenter joining as collaborators.

Simulation Design and Execution

We gauged student interest in a simulated session using a lecture feedback form. Given substantial interest, we pursued session development, aiming to reinforce lecture content and offer hands-on experience working with DDBHH patients and various interpreter types—ASL interpreters, CDIs, VRIs, and DBIs. We conducted the simulation at the UMN M Simulation Center, which provided outpatient-style clinic rooms, debrief classrooms, and a tablet for remote interpretation.

Four simulated scenarios were developed. (1) A Deaf patient in the emergency room presents with appendicitis but no interpreter is initially present. (2) A Deaf patient receives a routine diabetes checkup with an ASL interpreter and CDI. (3) A DeafBlind patient receives contraception counseling. (4) A Hard-of-Hearing parent with a Deaf child is concerned about the child's fatigue and delayed growth. Cases were designed by the student team, then reviewed by the Deaf collaborators and simulated patients (SPs) (Appendices D–H). Appendices D–G describe the individual cases, and Appendix H is a combined version of all four cases used for SP training. Door instructions for each simulated clinical encounter are provided in Appendix I. Scenarios were 11 minutes long, followed by 4 minutes of immediate feedback from SPs and interpreters, and a 2-minute transition period. Overhead announcements kept time and signaled transitions. No postencounter activity (eg, note writing) was required.

SP recruitment began 6 months prior to the simulation event via a flyer (Appendix J), electronically distributed to local Deaf community organizations recommended by collaborators. SPs were not recruited in clinical settings. SP training was designed in conjunction with Deaf collaborators and occurred via Zoom with ASL Interpreters to review scenarios, logistics, and expectations (Appendix K). The same student team that delivered the lecture (students with personal and professional ties to the Deaf community) led the SP training. To support SPs, we explicitly explained that they could leave the simulation at any point, recognizing that these scenarios can evoke emotions related to adverse health care experiences. Organizers remained available throughout the training and simulation for support. Interpreters were requested through the UMN Disability Resource Center.

On simulation day, SPs and interpreters arrived 40 minutes prior to the start of the simulation to orient and practice. The simulation event included: (1) prebriefing of SPs and interpreters, (2) prebriefing of students, (3) four rounds of simulations (SPs played their role with four sets of students), (4) group debrief, and (5) feedback and exit ticket. Slides outlining the prebriefing and postbriefing details are provided in Appendix L. Students completed a presession knowledge assessment survey that also measured self-reported confidence level caring for DDBHH patients, which was repeated postsession (Appendix M). To support preparation, students received access to the previously attended interactive lecture slides several days in advance. Materials included paper and pens for optional note-taking, and contraceptive model items borrowed from the OB/GYN department as a tactile teaching tool to support communication with a DeafBlind SP.

The SPs and interpreter prebrief included a schedule overview and scenario practice. They then moved to clinic rooms, and students arrived for their prebrief. Students were given a schedule overview and rapid review on accessibility, including optimal placement of a VRI, use of frequency modulation systems, and purpose of CDIs. Student pairs then rotated through four scenarios, with instructions to take turns asking questions within each scenario. After the final scenario, we transitioned to the group debrief to discuss successes, challenges, and takeaways. This included 7 minutes of small-group discussion—students in one group, interpreters and SPs in another—followed by a 13-minute full-group debrief. The student team led the debrief. Emphasizing constructive, open discussion, the debrief encouraged students, interpreters, and DDBHH SPs to develop a shared consensus on delivering equitable care to DDBHH patients. Finally, students completed the knowledge assessment and feedback form (Appendix M).

Analysis

Analysis of presession and postsession survey data included descriptive statistics. Open-ended student feedback was reviewed by two independent reviewers, who analyzed responses, identified recurring concepts, and grouped them into themes. Comments from individual students could be categorized under multiple themes. Differences in coding were discussed until consensus was reached.

Results

Interactive Lecture

Interactive lecture facilitators included three medical students (one Deaf, one Hard-of-Hearing, and one hearing), a Deaf ASL professor, and a Deaf community health worker. Two hundred thirty-six third-year medical students attended the interactive lecture; nearly 80% reported baseline minimal or no knowledge of Deaf culture or disparities (Table 1). Presession, only 7% of respondents felt they had tools to provide equitable care to DHH patients, compared to 84% postsession. However, the lower response rate on the postsession survey (35% [83/236] vs 100% [236/236] presession) limits the ability to make direct comparisons.

Written feedback was consistently positive, though there was a low response rate (28%; 73/256). Students valued the perspectives and experiences shared by Deaf collaborators, as well as the tangible skills, including basic ASL phrases, accessibility techniques like flickering the lights instead of knocking, and strategies to partner with interpreters. Representative comments included: “This session was by far the most important and impactful of BaDr week. . .truly phenomenal and irreplaceable. . .one of the top 10 experiences in med school overall”; “lots of very practical knowledge”; and “personal stories were very impactful” (Table 2).

Simulated Session

Eight students attended the simulated session, rotating in pairs. Five participants completed the presession knowledge assessment, all eight participants completed the postsession knowledge assessment, and seven participants completed the feedback form. The mean presession knowledge score was 17%, while the mean postsession (exit ticket) knowledge score increased notably to 92% (Table 3). The mean self-reported score of student confidence in caring for Deaf patients, measured on a 5-point Likert scale, increased by 1.3 points following the session (Table 3). Although the confidence scores for caring for DeafBlind patients were lower overall, the mean score demonstrated a larger increase postsession, increasing by 1.7 points.

When asked if students found the lecture or simulation experience more helpful, five of seven respondents (72%) preferred the simulation, while two respondents (29%) found both formats equally beneficial in different ways. Positive written feedback showed appreciation for the diversity of DDBHH SPs and the opportunity to practice practical communication strategies (Table 2). Suggestions for improvement included offering more preparation for working with interpreters and DDBHH patients (eg, presession asynchronous review), providing more time for feedback, and providing the session earlier in medical school. All respondents would recommend this session to their peers and believe it should be a required part of the curriculum.

Key takeaways from the group debrief included asking patients for their communication preferences, announcing one's name before speaking (especially in multi-speaker scenarios) to assist interpreters, requesting permission before touching, and using tactile tools for DeafBlind individuals. Additionally, students learned how to communicate in emergent settings without interpreters. In that scenario, writing and typing on a phone was helpful. Using a speech-to-text modality (eg, via Notes app) was preferred because it reduced the mental strain of reading handwriting when in pain. Additional communication tips included maintaining eye contact, being mindful of body language, and ensuring comprehension is registered by the patient before proceeding.

Discussion

The implementation of our educational innovation demonstrated that interactive lecture-based education and simulated training can improve medical students’ self-reported preparedness and confidence in caring for DDBHH patients. Postlecture, the percentage of students who felt equipped to provide equitable care rose by 77% (from 7% to 84%). Average simulation knowledge scores increased 74.5% (from 17% to 92%). Qualitative feedback further supported the session's efficacy in addressing educational objectives, with students valuing the diversity of perspectives, real-life narratives, and hands-on communication practice with ASL interpreters, CDIs, DBIs, and VRIs.

The simulation not only reinforced lecture content but also provided an opportunity to practice communication skills and obtain feedback in a low-stakes environment. The experiential format also fostered a deeper understanding of Deaf sociocultural considerations, which is critical for reducing health disparities. Notably, this initiative included DeafBlind patients and DBIs—a grossly underrepresented area in medical education.

While successful at our institution, we recognize replicability challenges related to personnel, time/space, logistics, and funding. We share several adaptations below.

Personnel

Leveraging partnerships with local DDBHH organizations to identify community educators and SPs

A key strength of this initiative was its early and close collaboration with DDBHH community members. Their lived experiences and leadership ensured the initiative was built with intention and authenticity. For institutions without DDBHH-identifying students/faculty, partnerships with DDBHH community organizations can help identify collaborators or SPs. Importantly, these partnerships should be grounded in bidirectional capacity building, supporting not only student learning but also community leadership via training, compensation, resource sharing, and mutually defined goals that advance both educational and community health objectives.22,23

Time/Space Constraints

Virtual options

Coordinating collaborators can present logistical challenges. A virtual format can improve feasibility. This approach can work, evidenced by successful virtual SP training, remote guest presenters, and since the original initiative, a fully virtual lecture reaching multiple campuses. Virtual sessions also offer students insight into communication challenges DDBHH patients may face when using VRI or small screens. Nonetheless, in-person participation remains ideal, given the visuospatial nature of ASL.

Resources

Reach out to institutional departments

Securing materials or technology may be a challenge. Departments can serve as valuable resources. For example, practice contraceptive items for the simulation were provided by the UMN OB/GYN department.

Cost

Seek funding across departments

Grants enabled us to compensate collaborators and secure simulation space. Funding can be sought from student group grants; diversity, equity, and inclusion departments; community engagement offices; or institutional/state education grants. As an alternative to paid simulation centers, reserving classrooms (typically free) is a feasible cost-saving method.

Finally, additional adaptations may include pursuing only the lecture component, limiting students to one SP interaction followed by an extended group debrief, and simplifying the simulation medical scenarios (eg, common cold, annual physical with minimal medical history).

While outcomes were encouraging, our evaluation strategy had notable limitations. Data access restrictions within our institution's grading system prevented us from comparing lecture presession and postsession data. Suboptimal survey completion rates introduce potential bias. We recommend that future versions use a single, accessible platform (eg, Google Forms) for all surveys to streamline data collection and analyses. For a more standardized assessment approach, one can consider piloting surveys with students to test their understanding of questions before administration. In addition, while being student-led fueled the initiative's momentum, it also posed challenges with respect to time and coordination. That said, with most materials now developed (see Appendices), future iterations will require less intensive preparation. Continued institutional support and thoughtful delegation of responsibilities will be key to scaling and sustaining this work.

Moving forward, we plan to consolidate the Fall and Spring lecture sessions into a single session. Given strong engagement and adequate classroom capacity, consolidation is feasible and will help conserve resources. For the simulation, next steps include scaling up to reach more students. We are currently recruiting additional student and faculty support and designating leads to oversee key areas, such as SP recruitment and communications, simulation center coordination, faculty engagement, administrative tasks, and scenario development. We also aim to enhance the realism of scenarios by providing SPs with targeted follow-up questions to ask when prompted by students. More time will also be allotted for the group debrief (>20 minutes) to allow for deeper reflection.

Overall, this initiative addressed a critical gap in medical education. The combination of positive reception alongside improved learner confidence and knowledge signifies a cultural shift toward more inclusive, culturally sensitive care. Student and faculty preference for making this session a required part of the curriculum further emphasizes the value of this training. Expanding this program could help standardize the preparation of health care professionals in delivering equitable care to DDBHH patients and ultimately contribute to a reduction of health disparities for this population nationwide.

Conclusion

Our initiative demonstrates the feasibility and impact of integrating DDBHH education into undergraduate medical training. Through community-partnered interactive lectures and simulated clinical encounters, medical students gained foundational knowledge, practical tools, and increased confidence caring for DDBHH patients. The overwhelmingly positive student feedback and considerable improvements in perceived confidence highlight the need for such training in medical curricula. The interactive lecture has proven to be replicable and scalable across three semesters. Expanding and standardizing this curriculum beyond UMN will help equip future physicians with the tools and cultural humility necessary to provide accessible care that ultimately works to reduce DDBHH health disparities.
