About testing Bell locality at colliders

Abstract
. Quantum tomography at colliders makes it possible to measure the amount of entanglement and test the violation of the Bell inequality. We explain in detail how this is possible and why it offers the possibility of examining from a new angle the feasibility of local hidden variable models.



1.
Introduction

One of the most characteristic properties of a quantum system is the possible presence of
entanglement
, that is of quantum correlations among
observables that cannot be accounted for by classical physics

[
1
,

As entanglement often leads to conflict with common sense, which is grounded on local realism, classical models encompassing standard quantum mechanics have been proposed in order to explain the phenomena in terms of underlying hidden variables. These variables can be deterministic or stochastic but, to mimic the probabilistic nature of quantum mechanics, only their average over a characteristic distribution enters the result of measurements in either case.

Investigations about these hypothetical variables gave rise to lively debates—often touching intricate philosophical considerations. A first outcome of these discussions—after von Neumann’s rather abrupt dismissal
[
5
]
was shown to be premature
[
6
,
7
]
—is that hidden variables cannot be agnostic about the experimental setup through which they are being measured because simultaneous values to all observables of a quantum mechanical system cannot be assigned in a way that respects their algebraic structure. This
contextuality
requirement comes from a theorem
[
8
,
7
,
9
]
that severely restricts which models of hidden variables are possible. The theorem holds for Hilbert spaces of dimension larger than 2. For a two-dimensional Hilbert space, there exists a simple non-contextual model for the spin of a particle
[
7
]
. Quantum contextuality has been experimentally tested at low energies by means of photons and other devices
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
and more recently also at high energies
[
16
]
.

The discussion was given new scope by J. Bell’s seminal paper
[
17
]
, in which it is shown that a large class of these contextual models of hidden variables, those obeying a locality condition (
Bell locality
), satisfy specific constraints (the
Bell inequalities
)
that can be violated by quantum mechanics
[
18
,
19
]
. This result makes it possible to discriminate between classical models and quantum mechanics
by performing actual experiments
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24

.
Many tests of this kind have been performed with various systems, both at low energies within atomic and solid-state physics
[

and, more recently, at high energies in colliders experiments (for a review, see
[

).

At particle colliders it is possible to reconstruct
the spin state of the products of a scattering process by using the angular distributions of the momenta—as recorded by a detector—of the related final-state particles. The complete knowledge of the state, obtained through this
quantum state tomography
process, should be compared to the traditional setting of a Bell inequality test where only joint probabilities along given directions in space are measured. For the latter, since only these probabilities are measured, the final state is only partially determined and this lack of knowledge can be exploited to devise
loopholes
in the test. At colliders, the complete knowledge of the state closes all loopholes and the Bell test becomes a simple determination of suitable observables on the reconstructed state. Because, sometimes, in physics the equations are not enough---and some explaining is required lest their meaning remains unclear---we discuss in detail the testing of nonlocality at colliders and compare it to the traditional low-energy experiments.

Before embarking on such a discussion, a methodological premise is perhaps necessary: Experiments always compare models against the data. The data are the “real world” and, given a model, we check how well the data fit it. There is no such a things as testing directly the “real world”. The Bell test therefore simply checks the factorization of probabilities (Bell locality) against the data; its results can be used to exclude local theories (if the inequality is violated), as well as quantum mechanics (if the violation exceed a critical value known as Tsirelson’s bound
[
34
]
). The same holds true for measurements of entanglement.

Let us stress an important point that perhaps makes clearer the complete similarity between Bell tests at colliders and those in atomic physics with photons. All current experiments—be them at low or high energy—must necessarily assume some model about the working of the polarimeter utilized in the measurements. It is the
polarimeter
that transforms a measured data (light or darkness in the case of photons, angular distribution of momenta in the case of the decay of a particle) into a polarization measurement. In the case of the atomic-physics tests, this equipment consists of the photon polarimeter, while in the case of the high-energy tests, in the polarimetric vector and spin-analyzing power of a particle emitted in a decay. In both cases, its actual operation has to be determined independently of the Bell test or a measurement of entanglement, in separate experiments providing the characteristic properties of the photon polarimeter or of the polarimetric vector. It is only after this step that the experimentally collected data—be them in the form of frequencies, probabilities or correlations—can be presented as those giving photon or elementary particle polarizations. On the other hand, in a analytic computation or a Monte Carlo simulation, the operation of the polarimeter can be obtained through the model itself instead of being derived from some other data. For example, in the case of tests at colliders the polarimetric vector in the fully leptonic decay of the top quark is found to be one because of the chiral coupling between elementary fermions and the gauge bosons in the Standard Model.

We are aware that the ideal configuration to test nonlocality against hidden variable models would be, like in quantum information, a
black box
in which the inputs can be varied at will and the outputs recorded
[
35
,
36
]
. Unfortunately, no present or planned collider experiment satisfies these conditions. Nor do the low-energy experiments in which Bell inequality has been tested.

2.
Quantum entanglement

Consider a pair of charged particles originating from the decay of a neutral state. They fly apart. If at a successive time and somewhere in space you look and find a charged particle, you know for sure that somewhere else there is going to be a particle with the opposite charge. By a measurement now and here, we learn something about somewhere else that can be very far away and space-like separated. Still, this is not a sign that the two particles are entangled: their charge is an intrinsic property that they have independently of whether we measure it or not. For classical properties, such as all the conserved ones, the knowledge of the distant particle which we instantaneously acquire by measuring the one close to us simply testifies the presence of classical correlations enforced by the neutrality of the progenitor particle.

Instead, genuine quantum correlations originate because of the linearity of the theory, which allows for superpositions of states. Consider the spin of a spin-1/2 particle in a direction determined by the unit vector
\vec{n}
. Before the spin is measured, the particle can be in a superposition of the two possible measure outcomes, for instance:

|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\big(|\!\uparrow_{\vec{n}}\rangle+|\!\downarrow_{\vec{n}}\rangle\big)\,,

where
|\!\uparrow_{\vec{n}}\rangle
and
|\downarrow_{\vec{n}}\rangle
indicate states with the spin aligned or anti-aligned with respect to the direction indicated by
\vec{n}
, respectively. This means that the component of the spin along a given direction is not an intrinsic property of the fermion.

A paradigmatic case in which entanglement can be easily studied is precisely that of a bipartite system formed by two spin-1/2 particles, yielding a joint spin state with quantum correlations that can be analyzed. For instance, take a the two-particle state

(2.1)

|\Psi\rangle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(|\!\uparrow_{\vec{n}}\rangle|\!\downarrow_{\vec{n}}\rangle+|\!\downarrow_{\vec{n}}\rangle|\!\uparrow_{\vec{n}}\rangle\Big)\ ,

and repeat the test described before for the charged particles: neither of the two particles carries a definite value for its spin
along the vector
\vec{n}
, but as soon as one of the two particle is subject to a spin measurement—the result of which is completely random—we learn that the spin of the other lies on the opposite direction.

By taking spin measurements along different directions, the state is not defined by the conservation of the angular momentum and one detects correlations not amenable to a classical explanation. In fact, perfect knowledge of the joint spin state of the particles,
given in (
2.1
), results in a total indetermination of the state of each of the constituent particles. Since this would occurs even if the two particles were separated at an arbitrarily large distance at the time of the measurement, one speaks of quantum nonlocality.

This short discussion emphasizes how the central feature of the quantum mechanical description is that particles, in general, do not have definite properties before these are measured. It is not that we lack information about them, it is the quirkiness proper of the superposition principle which allows quantum systems to exist in states which are incompatible with fixed values of these properties prior to measurements. This is in stark contrast with
local realism
, which uses hidden variables to define each and every property of a particle so as to represent an element of reality that is locally defined at all times
[
37
]
.

3.
Bell nonlocality

Let us focus again on the bipartite system made of two spin-1/2 particles, where quantum correlations among spin observables can be analyzed and entanglement easily studied. In the center of mass frame of the bipartite system, the two spin-1/2 particles are moving apart in opposite directions. Then, upon boosting to the rest frames of each particle, one can there measure the projections of the spin along chosen spatial directions. From these data, one can then extract, for instance, the probability
\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};-)

of finding the spin of one particle in the state
\ket{\uparrow_{\vec n}}
, that is, aligned to the unit vector
\vec{n}
, disregarding the spin of the companion particle. Experimentally, the probability can be obtained by looking at the ratio of partial
(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};-)
event
counts over the total number of particle pairs. Similarly, one can also consider double probabilities, like
\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};\downarrow_{\vec{m}})
,
that is the probabilities of finding the projection of the spin of one particle aligned with the unit vector
\vec{n}
and the companion with spin
anti-aligned to a different unit vector
\vec{m}
. This is indeed what has been measured in all Bell tests so far performed at low-energy using photons, atoms and quantum dots as qubits.

Let us assume now that the state of the bipartite system can be described by a set of variables that we globally denote with
\lambda
, assumed for simplicity to take continuous values. This description of the system
is also assumed to provide the best characterization of the system.
In particular, the variables
\lambda
can set the probability
p_{\lambda}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};-)

of finding a particle in the state
\ket{\uparrow_{\vec n}}
regardless of the spin state of the companion particle. Similarly, these variables can set the probability
p_{\lambda}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};\downarrow_{\vec{m}})
of finding the
first particle spin in the state
\ket{\uparrow_{\vec n}}
and the companion particle spin in the state
\ket{\downarrow_{\vec m}}
.

The corresponding average probabilities
\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};-)
and
\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};\downarrow_{\vec{m}})
,
the only ones accessible to experiments, are then obtained as

(3.1)

\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};-)=\int d\lambda\ \eta(\lambda)\ p_{\lambda}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};-)\ ,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};\downarrow_{\vec{m}})=\int d\lambda\ \eta(\lambda)\ p_{\lambda}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};\downarrow_{\vec{m}})\ ,

with the probability density distribution
\eta(\lambda)
, normalized as
\int d\lambda\ \eta(\lambda)=1
, which characterizes the ensemble of bipartite systems observed in the experiment. It is important to stress that this description is very general and can be made to agree with the prediction of quantum mechanics.

We shall now focus on the situation, common in classical probability theory,
for which the two probabilities
p_{\lambda}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};-)

and
p_{\lambda}(-;\downarrow_{\vec{m}})
are assumed to be independent.
In other terms, we shall consider a description of the two spin-1/2 system for which:

(3.2)

\boxed{p_{\lambda}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};\downarrow_{\vec{m}})=p_{\lambda}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};-)\ p_{\lambda}(-;\downarrow_{\vec{m}})\ .}

This assumption is known as
Bell locality condition

[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
.
Measurable consequences of this assumption can be easily derived
[
38
]
.

First of all, note that for any set of four non-negative numbers
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
x_{3}
and
x_{4}
,
less or equal than 1, the following inequality holds:

(3.3)

x_{1}x_{2}-x_{1}x_{4}+x_{3}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}\leq x_{3}+x_{2}\ .

Replacing
x_{i}
with suitable probabilities
p_{\lambda}
and integrating over
\lambda
with the weight

\eta(\lambda)
, from (
3.3
) one can deduce the following relation:

(3.4)

\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{1}};\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{2}})-\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{1}};\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{4}})+\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{3}};\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{2}})+\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{3}};\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{4}})\leq\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{3}};-)+\mathcal{P}(-;\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{2}})\ ,

where
{\vec{n}}_{1}
,
{\vec{n}}_{2}
,
{\vec{n}}_{3}
and
{\vec{n}}_{4}

are four independent three-dimensional unit vectors determining four spatial directions
along which the spins of the two particles can be measured.
This condition involving double probabilities
\mathcal{P}
is a particular instance of a Bell inequality.
It is worth stressing that due to the great generality used in its derivation, the relation (
3.4
) must be satisfied by all local deterministic or stochastic alternatives to quantum mechanics—that is,
models in which the condition (
3.2
) is assumed to hold.

The spin state of the bipartite system can be encoded in a density operator
\rho
represented by a
4\times 4
matrix
that must be semi-positive and of unit trace in order to assure the correct interpretation of its eigenvalues
\rho_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
, as probabilities:
\rho_{i}\geq 0
,
{\rm Tr}[\rho]=\sum_{i}\rho_{i}=1
. It can be expanded as

(3.5)

\rho=\frac{1}{4}\Big[{\bf 1}\otimes{\bf 1}+\sum_{i}B_{i}^{(+)}(\sigma_{i}\otimes{\bf 1})+\sum_{j}B_{j}^{(-)}({\bf 1}\otimes\sigma_{j})+\sum_{ij}C_{ij}(\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j})\Big]\ ,

where
\sigma_{i}
are the Pauli matrices,
\bf 1
is the unit
2\times 2
matrix, and where the sums involving the indices
i
and
j
run over the labels
x
,
y
and
z
, representing any orthonormal spatial reference frame. The real coefficients
B_{i}^{(+)}={\rm Tr}[\rho\,(\sigma_{i}\otimes{\bf 1})]
and

B_{j}^{(-)}={\rm Tr}[\rho\,({\bf 1}\otimes\sigma_{j})]
give the spin averages (
i.e.
the polarizations) of the corresponding particles, while the real matrix
C_{ij}={\rm Tr}[\rho\,(\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j})]
contains the spin correlations. Note that while the density matrix in (
3.5
) is normalized to
{\rm Tr}[\rho]=1
, extra constraints on
B_{i}^{(+)}
,
B_{j}^{(-)}
and
C_{ij}
are needed to guarantee non-negative eigenvalues. These extra conditions are in general non-trivial, requiring
all principal minors of
\rho
to be non-negative. Knowledge of
\rho
allows to compute the average of any two-spin observable
\mathcal{O}

as
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle={\rm Tr}[\rho\,\mathcal{O}]
, including
the probabilities appearing in (
3.4
).

The use of a density matrix does not entails that quantum mechanics is necessarily being used, but simply that the utilized quantities have no fixed values and follow instead a probabilistic distribution—the same as in statistical mechanics or classic optics, in which density matrices are often used. Also the Pauli matrices appearing in Eq. (
3.5
)—which seems to bring in quantum mechanics—are just a convenient basis in the
2\times 2
space of the polarizations.

The probability for a single spin to be in a state
\ket{\uparrow_{\vec n}}
or
\ket{\downarrow_{\vec n}}
along the direction
\vec{n}
can be obtained by computing the average over the spin state of the following two
projection operators, respectively:

(3.6)

P_{\uparrow_{\vec{n}}}=\frac{1}{2}\big({\bf 1}+{\vec{n}}\cdot{\vec{\sigma}}\big)\ ,\qquad P_{\downarrow_{\vec{n}}}=\frac{1}{2}\big({\bf 1}-{\vec{n}}\cdot{\vec{\sigma}}\big)\ ,\quad\text{and}\quad{\vec{n}}\cdot{\vec{\sigma}}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{3}n_{i}\,\sigma_{i}\ .

Then, in the case of the two-spin system, one similarly has:

(3.7)

\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};-)={\rm Tr}\Big[\rho\,\Big(P_{\uparrow_{\vec{n}}}\otimes{\bf 1}\Big)\Big]\ ,\qquad\mathcal{P}(-;\uparrow_{\vec{m}})={\rm Tr}\Big[\rho\,\Big({\bf 1}\otimes P_{\uparrow_{\vec{m}}}\Big)\Big]\ ,

as we here disregard the spin state of the companion particle. Analogously, in the case of the double probability

\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};\uparrow_{\vec{m}})
, one has:

\mathcal{P}(\uparrow_{\vec{n}};\uparrow_{\vec{m}})=\ {\rm Tr}\Big[\rho\,\Big(P_{\uparrow_{\vec{n}}}\otimes P_{\uparrow_{\vec{m}}}\Big)\Big]\ .

Inserting these results into the inequality (
3.4
) leads to the following algebraic relation:

(3.8)

{\vec{n}}_{1}\cdot C\cdot\big({\vec{n}}_{2}-{\vec{n}}_{4}\big)+{\vec{n}}_{3}\cdot C\cdot\big({\vec{n}}_{2}+{\vec{n}}_{4}\big)\leq 2\ .

involving only the correlation matrix
C
. Combining this condition with the analogous one obtained by replacing
\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{1}}
with
\downarrow_{{\vec{n}}_{1}}

and
\uparrow_{{\vec{n}}_{3}}
with
\downarrow_{{\vec{n}}_{3}}
in Eq. (
3.4
), we finally obtain the following inequality:

(3.9)

\Big|{\vec{n}}_{1}\cdot C\cdot\big({\vec{n}}_{2}-{\vec{n}}_{4}\big)+{\vec{n}}_{3}\cdot C\cdot\big({\vec{n}}_{2}+{\vec{n}}_{4}\big)\Big|\leq 2\,.

In order to put under test this Bell inequality, one needs to experimentally determine the matrix
C
and then suitably choose four independent spatial directions
{\vec{n}}_{1}
,
{\vec{n}}_{2}
,
{\vec{n}}_{3}
and
{\vec{n}}_{4}

that maximize the left-hand side of Eq. (
3.9
). Fortunately, this maximization process can be easily performed analytically, for a generic spin correlation matrix
[
39
]
. Indeed, consider the matrix
C
and its transpose
C^{T}
and form the symmetric, positive,
3\times 3
matrix

M=C^{T}C
; its three eigenvalues
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
,
m_{3}
can be ordered in decreasing order:
m_{1}\geq m_{2}\geq m_{3}
. Then, the following result holds:
the two-spin state
\rho
in Eq. (
3.5
) violates the inequality (
3.9
), or equivalently (
3.4
),
if and only if the sum of the two largest eigenvalues of
M
is strictly larger than 1, that is

(3.10)

\mathfrak{m}_{12}\equiv m_{1}+m_{2}>1\,.

In other terms, given a spin correlation matrix
C
of the state
\rho
that satisfies
\mathfrak{m}_{12}>1
, then there is at least a choice of the four independent vectors
{\vec{n}}_{1}
,
{\vec{n}}_{2}
,
{\vec{n}}_{3}
,
{\vec{n}}_{4}

for which the left-hand side of Eq. (
3.9
) is larger than 2.

It should be stressed that the above formulation departs from the more standard approaches adopted in testing Bell inequalities, in particular within quantum optics. In fact, while in the standard tests the probabilities entering
the inequality (
3.4
) are experimentally determined by measuring frequencies of events for every choice of the directions
{\vec{n}}_{i}
,
in the above approach actual measures of probabilities are avoided. In their place, we have the experimental determination of the spin correlation matrix
C
; its entries are typically reconstructed via suitable angular distributions observed at collider experiments, without any reference to specific sets of prescribed directions
{\vec{n}}_{i}
.

4.
Quantum information observables in the
S
-matrix framework

A superficial comparison with the low-energy tests of quantum mechanics based on polarized photons and polarimeters might suggest that, in order to explore quantum entanglement and Bell inequality violation at colliders, one must necessarily use polarized beams and polarized targets. Yet this is not the way high-energy physics has developed and there are essentially no experiments using such beams and targets. Almost all experiments are, instead, based on (mostly) unpolarized beams and detectors that do not measure directly polarizations. Is this situation a show stopper
2
2

2

See, for example,
[
40
]
.
for testing quantum mechanics—be it Bell nonlocality or entanglement—at colliders? Not at all—as the rich physics program on polarization effects at colliders shows. Polarizations are reconstructed from the angular distributions of particles in the final state. As explained in detail below, the momentum angular distribution is used to estimate correlations, thereby bringing us from the cross section to a density matrix. It is in the space in which this matrix is defined that entanglement and Bell inequality violation must be studied—not in the cross section. This is a powerful way of studying polarizations and it allows for their measurement also of those particles that are not directly seen in the detector because they decay.

Consider the simplest process at colliders, namely the production of a pair of
\tau
leptons from electron-positron annihilation. We take
\tau
leptons because their polarizations can be reconstructed by analyzing the angular distributions of suitable decay products. The final state, in general, is given by a superposition of bipartite
\tau
lepton states that span the four possible
R
,
L
-helicity combinations:

(4.1)

|\Psi\rangle=\zeta_{1}|\tau^{-}_{L}\rangle|\tau^{+}_{L}\rangle+\zeta_{2}|\tau^{-}_{R}\rangle|\tau^{+}_{L}\rangle+\zeta_{3}|\tau^{-}_{L}\rangle|\tau^{+}_{R}\rangle+\zeta_{4}|\tau^{-}_{R}\rangle|\tau^{+}_{R}\rangle\,,\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{i}|\zeta_{i}|^{2}=1\,.

Denoting with
\Theta
the
\tau^{+}
scattering angle in the centre-of-mass frame of reference, a direct computation within the Standard Model gives

(4.2)

\boxed{|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1+\cos\Theta}{\sqrt{1+\cos^{2}\Theta}}|\,\tau^{-}_{R}\rangle|\tau^{+}_{L}\rangle+\frac{1-\cos\Theta}{\sqrt{1+\cos^{2}\Theta}}|\,\tau^{-}_{L}\rangle|\tau^{+}_{R}\rangle\ ,}

because of the vector-like photon coupling.

The angular distribution of the charged pions produced in the decays
\tau^{\pm}\to\nu_{\tau}\pi^{\pm}
can be used to reconstruct the polarizations and spin correlation of the tau pairs. The connection is provided by the conservation of the angular momentum in the rest frame of the decaying lepton.

For a single
\tau
lepton, the polarization density matrix is given by

(4.3)

\rho[s_{i}]=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}+\langle s_{z}\rangle&\langle s_{x}+is_{y}\rangle\\
\langle s_{x}-is_{y}\rangle&\frac{1}{2}-\langle s_{z}\rangle\end{pmatrix}\ ,

in which
\expectationvalue{s_i}
are the polarization projections onto a given basis.

As already discussed, the use of the density matrix does not imply that quantum mechanics is being used. For example, in optics
the same density matrix is often used to describe a beam of light as

(4.4)

\rho_{\text{light}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1+\xi_{3}&\xi_{1}-i\,\xi_{2}\\
\xi_{1}+i\,\xi_{2}&1-\xi_{3}\end{pmatrix}\ ,

in terms of the Stokes parameters
\xi_{i}
—each of them characterizing linear and circular polarizations along given directions.

The expectation values of the polarization components in Eq. (
4.3
) are reconstructed from the events that give rise to the differential cross section

(4.5)

\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{\mbox{d}\sigma}{\mbox{d}\cos\theta_{i}^{\pm}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\langle s_{i}\rangle\,\alpha_{i}\,\cos\theta_{i}\Big)\ ,

with
\theta_{i}
the angles described by the momenta of the charged pions in the decay of the
\tau
lepton.
This reconstruction, in which the decaying particle acts as its own polarimeter, is based on the independent experimental determination of the polarimetric vector, the coefficient
\alpha_{i}
in Eq. (
4.5
), which turns out to be
\alpha_{i}=1
for the single-pion
\tau
decay. This determination is mirrored in low-energy experiments with photons by the measurement of the properties of the polarimeter used in the experimental setup. Both experiment types require an external input which however comes from experiments that are independent of those testing the Bell inequality. Equation (
4.5
), apart for the polarimetric vector, is nothing but the result of the conservation of angular momentum in the decay—no quantum mechanics is required.

For the final state with two
\tau
leptons, the differential cross section is given by

(4.6)

\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{\mbox{d}\sigma}{\mbox{d}\cos\theta^{+}_{i}\mbox{d}\cos\theta^{-}_{j}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\alpha_{i}B^{+}_{i}\cos\theta^{+}_{i}+\alpha_{i}B^{-}_{i}\cos\theta^{-}_{i}+C_{ij}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\cos\theta^{+}_{i}\cos\theta^{-}_{j}\Big)\ ,

where the angles
\theta_{i}^{\pm}
give the spatial orientation of the charged pion momenta in the rest frames of the progenitor
\tau
leptons with respect to some basis with components
i
and

. The coefficients
B^{\pm}_{i}
and
C_{ij}
can be included
into the polarization density matrix
\rho[C_{ij}\,,B_{i}]
, as in (
3.5
). The logical steps leading to the determination of the average
of any system observable
\mathcal{O}
is then as follows:

(4.7)

\boxed{\biggl\langle\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\cos\theta_{i}\cos\theta_{j}\biggr\rangle\quad\Rightarrow\quad C_{ij}\,,B_{i}^{\pm}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\rho[C_{ij}\,,B_{i}^{\pm}]\quad\Rightarrow\quad\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle={\rm Tr}[\rho\,\mathcal{O}]\ .}

The measurement provides the angular distributions of interest, in the first term above, averaged over the overall distribution of the events. From there, one extracts polarization and correlation coefficients,
B_{i}
,
C_{ij}
, that can be organized in the density matrix
\rho
, then used to determine averages of physical quantities.
The polarization state of the two
\tau

Once the state is given, its properties can be obtained by means of averages of suitable observables,like the concurrence
{\mathcal{C}}[\rho]
for quantifying its entanglement content

\overline{\rho}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\differential\Omega\frac{\differential\sigma}{\differential\Omega}\rho(\Omega)}{\int_{\Omega}\differential\Omega\frac{\differential\sigma}{\differential\Omega}}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\int_{\Omega}\differential\Omega\frac{\differential\sigma}{\differential\Omega}\rho(\Omega)

To understand why, notice that the quantum tomography procedure relies on a triad of orthonormal vectors, explicitly used in the summation appearing in Eq. (

\hat{x}
,
\hat{y}

5.
Quantum tomography: a new take on loopholes

Even though loopholes to the Bell test are often invoked without providing a detailed model, their potential presence highlights several ways that theories built on local realism could exploit to bypass the bound posed by the Bell inequality. Saving these theories, however, requires to give up assumptions we cherish and usually make in our experiments: free will, relativistic invariance or fair sampling, depending on the invoked loophole.

Regardless on whether all of the loopholes are closed or not, establishing the violation of the Bell inequality in new settings—like those provided by collider experiments—is of interest because, at the vey least, it makes the construction of local hidden-variable models alternative to quantum mechanics harder.

All the loopholes so far discussed in the literature exploit the incomplete experimental determination of the bipartite state that is provided in the traditional setup of a Bell test, notably in the two photons system. Two directions, arbitrary but fixed, are defined for each of the two polarization measurements performed on each subsystem; correlations among these polarization measurements are then compared and the Bell inequality evaluated. These four directions are the context of the measurement; correlations are computed on some statistical distributions of the variables.

Loopholes bypass nonlocality by encoding information on these directions in some extra variables, in such a way that the correlations entering the Bell inequality are altered once this information has been passed across. This is achieved with an exchange of information that makes the direction
\vec{n}_{i}
, along which the first particle spin is measured, known to the second particle whose spin is measured along the direction
\vec{n}_{j}
. This is the key observation that provides a new angle on all loopholes affecting Bell tests. It is crucial for the existence of loopholes that the directions used in the measurements are set at some point because, otherwise, no information could be passed across to manipulate the correlations and consequently satisfy the inequality.

Quantum tomography provides us with the state of the bipartite system under exam, a system that we could then subject to a Bell test to assert the potential inequality violation. However, if the state is given with sufficient precision, there is no way to bypass the mathematics that connects operators, states, and expectation values. The presence of entanglement and the potential violation of Bell inequalities can then be straightforwardly established through the mentioned observables, within the uncertainties proper of the tomographic measurements. There is no room for loopholes to act: loopholes do not affect states, quantum tomography nor quantum mechanics, they exploit the fact that only partial information is made available in a concrete Bell test.

In the following we briefly review the different loopholes affecting Bell tests one at a time, showing how the tomographic procedure made available by collider experiments allows to disregard them.

5.1.
Space-time locality

This loophole exploits the space-time arrangement of the events involved in a Bell test. Communication pertaining to the direction settings used in the polarization measurements performed on the two subsystems is possible in a local manner if these settings are fixed before the source emits the entangled particles, or if one setting and measurement is performed in the past cone of the second one. In both cases, the Bell inequality is violated because of the local exchange of information
[
17
]
.

Local hidden variables theories can bypass the non-separability by using interactions to transmit information, provided the measurements are taken at time-like intervals and the hidden variables carry information about the direction setting of one of the two photon measurements. In the traditional Bell test setting, this loophole has been closed by first arranging the polarization measurement to be space-like separated and by letting the polarization directions be determined by a quantum random number generator
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
.

At collider experiments, the condition of space-like separation between the polarization measurements requires, at first blush, particles with the same lifetime and mass. Yet this restriction is actually not necessary because the time-like separation between production and measurement points is only an issue for experiments with the traditional settings. At colliders, in fact, no specific setting is decided beforehand as the whole density matrix is fully reconstructed. The directions involved in a Bell test are decided only afterwards, when the reconstructed state is pulled through the machinery of quantum mechanics to compute the required correlation values. There is no way for the state at the production point, nor for the two subsystem composing the final state, to know this choice in advance. For this reason, even for two decays taking place one inside the future cone of the other—as in the case of two particles with very different masses and decay lengths—the loophole cannot be invoked.

5.2.
Detection

It is possible that the correlations, as detected in the experiment, are only due to the particular subset of recorded events: although these events show a violation of a Bell inequality, if all events were detected the Bell inequality would actually be respected. If some events are not recorded, extra correlations could be hiding there
[
26
]
. In
[
48
]
it was shown that the efficiency for the detection of the photons should be at least 83%.

The loophole has been closed in low-energy tests in

This loophole is partially closed in collider experiments by the very high efficiency rate for the detection of most charged particles as performed by modern detectors (see, for example,

Fair sampling is necessary in high-energy physics also in ordinary cross section measurements—otherwise you might think that, for instance, supersymmetry is hiding in the many events that have not been detected. Be that as it may, a close look at the conditions under which the loophole might work shows that also this loophole is closed by the collider settings. The missing events, on which the loophole is based, are not generic but those corresponding to measurement directions that can actually modify the Bell inequality. Again, since no such directions are chosen when performing the tomographic reconstruction of the quantum state, the loophole cannot even be formulated. A low efficiency detection at colliders will only result in a lower significance of the Bell test performed under these conditions.

At collider experiments, no detector settings are chosen. There is therefore no information that the hidden variables can carry to alter the reconstruction of the state which proceeds always in the same manner. Likewise, the loophole cannot alter the subsequent probing of correlations in the Bell test, which is solely regulated by quantum mechanics.

5.4.
Free will

There exists a stronger form of the previous loophole that consists in supposing that the hidden variable distribution contains the outcome of the experiment about to be performed. For particle accelerators, this would mean that the hidden variables regulate the angular distribution of the momenta involved in the tomographic procedure. This leaves us with the choice between living with a nonlocal theory, such as quantum mechanics, or with the absence of free will, at least, in physics research. For the purposes of the present work, we will not entertain the latter option.

5.5.
Memory

If the measurements are repeatedly made, a local hidden variable theory could exploit the memory of past measurement settings and outcomes to increase the correlations and yield a violation of the Bell inequality in the upcoming tests

The loophole was identified in

At collider experiments, the measurements are intrinsically random because the events originate from particles decaying at different times and positions in the beam-pipe or the detector. Moreover, the memory that is passed on is about the directions of the measurements and, again, these are not set by the tomographic procedure. Finally, the number of events collected at colliders is large enough for the loophole to be closed.

5.6.
Coincidence

In many experiments, especially those based on photon polarizations, pairs of events in the two sides of the experiment are only identified as belonging to a single pair after the experiment is performed, by judging whether or not their detection times are close enough one to another. This generates a new possibility for a local hidden variables theory to fake quantum correlations: altering the detection time of each of the two particles according to some relationship between hidden variables carried by the particles and the detector settings encountered at the measurement station

60

The loophole was identified and closed at low-energy in the same work

60
]

Coincidence is well verified in collider experiments by the kinematic reconstruction performed event by event. Moreover, the amount of misidentifications is routinely included in the uncertainty of the measurements.

6.
If they exist, hidden variables are new physics

As we contemplate the future of quantum tomography at colliders, two unexpected things can happen. If the experimental data yield a density matrix that exceeds the Tsirelson’s bound
[
34
]
, this result will imply that nonlocality is stronger than what is predicted by quantum mechanics. All the tests at collider have so far confirmed quantum mechanics. It could also happen that the state being reconstructed is not an acceptable state in the Hilbert space of quantum mechanics. This is because the experimental values of some cross section could, for instance, imply a violation of unitarity. In this case, the problem will be addressed by looking for new physics, rather than as a signal of the failure of quantum mechanics.

Coming to hidden variables,
these have been originally postulated to exist for theories like atomic physics that were not fundamental. Particle physics, however, describes the most fundamental level of Nature. Though one cannot exclude that some clever hidden variable model could reproduce the states identified by the tomographic procedure, whatever this model may be, it is bound to be contextual and nonlocal---barring the super-deterministic choice. If we set out to construct such a model
5

The hidden variables must manifest themselves as these new particles or interactions with special properties and, so-far, elusive interactions. From the point of view of particle physics, the search for hidden-variable models is simply the search for physics beyond the Standard Model.
