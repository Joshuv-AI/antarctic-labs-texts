Development and Internal Validation of a Nomogram for Predicting Acute Moderate-to-Severe Postoperative Pain After Achilles Tendon Surgery: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Wenwen Zhai, Zhuoying Yu, Ning Wang, Jiahong Zhou, Yanhan Lv, Min Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute moderate-to-severe postoperative pain is common after Achilles tendon surgery and may hinder early mobilization and the implementation of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a perioperative nomogram for predicting acute moderate-to-severe pain within the first 24 hours after Achilles tendon surgery using routinely available clinical variables.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 489 patients who underwent Achilles tendon surgery at a single center. Demographic, surgical, and perioperative analgesic data were extracted from electronic medical records. The primary outcome was moderate-to-severe postoperative pain, defined as any Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) score ≥ 4 within the first 24 postoperative hours. Candidate predictors were screened using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, followed by multivariable logistic regression to construct a parsimonious predictive model. The model was intended for perioperative use at or near the end of surgery, when the surgical approach and peripheral nerve block status are known. Model performance was evaluated using the Area Under the Curve (AUC), calibration plots, bootstrap internal validation, temporal split validation, and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA).ResultsAmong the 489 patients, 208 (42.5%) experienced moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. The final model included open surgery (OR 4.457, 95% CI 2.109–9.417), higher BMI (OR 1.059 per 1 kg/m2, 95% CI 1.006–1.115), and no peripheral nerve block (OR 2.022, 95% CI 1.369–2.988). The apparent C-statistic was 0.652 (95% CI 0.603–0.700), and the bootstrap-corrected C-statistic was 0.648. Calibration was acceptable (optimism-corrected calibration slope 0.950; optimism-corrected calibration intercept −0.017; Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.162). In temporal split validation, the model achieved an AUC of 0.634 (95% CI 0.565–0.703). Decision curve analysis suggested potential net benefit across threshold probabilities from 0.11 to 0.56.ConclusionOpen surgery and higher BMI were associated with increased odds of acute moderate-to-severe postoperative pain, whereas perioperative peripheral nerve block was associated with lower odds. The proposed 3-variable nomogram showed modest discrimination and acceptable internal calibration. It may serve as an adjunctive tool for perioperative risk stratification, but external validation and prospective evaluation are required before routine clinical use.Keywords: achilles tendon surgery, postoperative pain, nomogram, prediction model, peripheral nerve block, ERAS

Introduction

Acute postoperative pain is one of the most common clinical problems in perioperative care.1 Its development involves multiple pathophysiological processes, including tissue damage directly caused by surgical trauma, the release of inflammatory mediators, and sensitization of the peripheral and central nervous systems.2,3 It is also influenced by a combination of patient-specific factors, surgical details, and perioperative management strategies. Inadequately controlled acute postoperative pain may restrict early mobilization, delay functional recovery,4,5 and be associated with adverse outcomes such as Postoperative Nausea and Vomiting (PONV),6 sleep disturbances, anxiety, and depression.7 These consequences may prolong hospital stays, increase medical costs, and reduce patient satisfaction and quality of recovery.8 Within the concept of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS), postoperative pain management is a core component.9

Extensive research has examined risk factors for acute postoperative pain across various surgical specialties, including orthopedics, urology, general and lung surgery.10,11 These studies have identified several associated factors such as patient age, sex, preoperative pain history, anxiety and depression status, surgical approach, anesthesia techniques, and analgesic medication use.12–14 However, Achilles tendon surgery is a common orthopedic procedure primarily performed for conditions such as Achilles tendon rupture, insertional Achilles tendinitis,15 and other sports-related disorders.16 Such tissue trauma may result in a high incidence and considerable intensity of acute postoperative pain. Inadequate pain control can limit early postoperative ankle joint mobility and hinder early rehabilitation exercises.17

Nevertheless, systematic research on acute postoperative pain following Achilles tendon surgery remains insufficient, and clinical pain management often relies on institutional protocols and clinician judgment. In addition, identifying isolated risk factors is not by itself sufficient for perioperative decision-making; clinicians may benefit from a simple tool that combines routinely available variables into an individualized probability of moderate-to-severe pain. Previous prediction models related to postsurgical pain or orthopedic recovery have mainly been developed in mixed surgical populations or other orthopedic settings,10,17 whereas evidence specific to acute postoperative pain after Achilles tendon surgery remains limited.

Because acute postoperative pain is multidimensional, candidate predictors were considered across demographic and anthropometric characteristics, pre-existing pain and psychological status, injury- and diagnosis-related features, surgical invasiveness, and anesthetic or analgesic management. Since surgical approach and peripheral nerve block status are perioperative variables rather than purely baseline characteristics, the model was designed as a perioperative risk-stratification tool for use at or near the end of surgery, when these factors are known. It was not intended as a purely preoperative model to determine whether a nerve block should be performed. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and internally validate a pragmatic perioperative nomogram for predicting acute moderate-to-severe pain within the first 24 hours after Achilles tendon surgery.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study, approved by the Medical Science Research Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: M20260277, Approval Date: 2026–04-15). Ethics approval was obtained before data extraction/analysis of de-identified records. The retrospective study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07558512, Date: 2026–04-23). The study protocol complied with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As it involved retrospective analysis using historical clinical data, without additional patient intervention or disclosure of identifiable personal information, the ethics committee waived the requirement for written informed consent. This study was reported in accordance with the TRIPOD statement for multivariable prediction model development and validation.

Study Subjects

Patients who underwent Achilles tendon surgery at the Department of Sports Medicine of our hospital from January 1, 2025, to March 15, 2026, were selected for eligibility. Inclusion Criteria: age ≥ 18 years, with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification I~III; clinically diagnosed with Achilles tendon rupture (acute, old, partial, insertional, or re-rupture), Achilles tendinopathy, insertional Achilles tendinitis, Haglund’s deformity, or other Achilles tendon disorders, undergoing open or arthroscopic Achilles tendon surgery, including elective and emergency surgeries; conscious and able to cooperate with postoperative pain assessment; complete and accessible medical records related to surgery, anesthesia, and postoperative pain assessment; completion of at least 24 hours of standardized postoperative pain monitoring and clinical follow-up. Exclusion Criteria: missing/incomplete key clinical data such as pain assessment, surgery and anesthesia, or failure to complete 24-hour standardized pain monitoring after surgery; perioperative unplanned secondary surgery or transfer to the intensive care unit; patients with mental illness, cognitive dysfunction, or language communication barrier, unable to cooperate with pain assessment; patients with a history of alcohol or drug addiction; patients with acute or chronic pain conditions unrelated to Achilles tendon disease; bilateral Achilles tendon surgery; concomitant surgery for other lower extremity injuries (for example, ankle fracture and ankle ligament tear).

Perioperative Anesthetic and Analgesic Management

All patients were managed in accordance with an institutional perioperative anesthetic and analgesic protocol. Neuraxial anesthesia was adopted as the default anesthetic technique, with the selection of either combined spinal-epidural anesthesia or single-shot spinal anesthesia determined based on the estimated surgical duration. Spinal anesthesia was performed using 10–15 mg of 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine. For patients receiving an adjunctive peripheral nerve block in addition to neuraxial anesthesia, ultrasound-guided popliteal sciatic nerve block was administered before spinal anesthesia with ropivacaine (0.2%-0.3%, 15–20 mL). Because this was a retrospective study, detailed local anesthetic regimens were not uniformly standardized and were extracted from anesthesia records when available. In a small number of patients, nerve block was recorded as the primary anesthetic technique and was coded separately from adjunctive peripheral nerve block analgesia. The decision to perform a peripheral nerve block was made by the attending anesthesiologist rather than according to a fixed protocol, which was recognized as a potential source of confounding by indication. Preoperatively, oral NSAIDs, primarily etoricoxib and other conventional nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, were routinely administered in the absence of explicit contraindications. No standardized institutional protocol was applied for the intraoperative and postoperative administration of NSAIDs, dexmedetomidine, or local anesthetic agents, and their use was determined by clinical judgment.

Postoperative pain intensity was assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), which ranges from 0 to 10. Scores were interpreted as follows: 0 for no pain; 1–3, mild pain; 4–6, moderate pain; and 7–10, severe pain. Trained nurses and anesthesiologists conducted scheduled pain assessments immediately after return to the ward, and at 8 hours, 12 hours, and 24 hours postoperatively. These time points corresponded to the routine nursing pain assessment schedule at our institution, ensuring standardized documentation across all included patients. The NRS scores at each time point were recorded, and the highest NRS score within 24 hours postoperatively was calculated. Rescue analgesia was initiated whenever the NRS pain score was ≥4 on scheduled assessments or upon patient complaint, with oral oxycodone or intravenous tramadol used as the first-line analgesic regimen. All opioid doses were subsequently converted to Morphine Milligram Equivalents (MME) using published equianalgesic conversion references and institutional opioid conversion guidance (oral morphine = 1.0, oral oxycodone = 1.5, intravenous morphine = 3.0, oral tramadol = 0.2, in intravenous tramadol = 0.3).18 Given the retrospective nature of data collection from routine clinical medical records, the nursing and anesthesia staff who recorded postoperative NRS scores were not blinded to the surgical approach and intraoperative analgesic management of the enrolled patients.

Data Collection

Data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and the anesthesia information management system. Two researchers who had received standardized training independently extracted data using a pre-designed data extraction form. The extracted data were cross-checked, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus or by consulting the original records. The following variables were collected:

Preoperative indicators: demographic characteristics (sex, age, height, weight, BMI), smoking history, baseline health status (ASA classification, comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and hyperlipidemia), and functional status (Barthel Index recorded on admission, reflecting temporary injury-related limitation). Pain and psychological-related variables included chronic pain history, history of preoperative analgesic use, and preoperative anxiety and depression status. Surgical diagnosis and injury chronicity (acute/chronic) were also recorded. For patients with Achilles tendon rupture, rupture chronicity was further classified as fresh (<3 weeks) or old (≥ 3 weeks). The distance of the rupture site from the calcaneal insertion was additionally recorded.

Grouping

Patients were grouped according to the occurrence of moderate-to-severe pain within the first 24 hours after surgery. Moderate-to-severe pain group: Patients with an NRS score ≥ 4 at any time point within 24 hours postoperatively; No-to-mild pain group: Patients with NRS scores ≤3 at all time points within 24 hours postoperatively.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The primary outcome was moderate-to-severe pain as defined above. The secondary outcomes included the severity of postoperative acute pain (highest NRS score within 24 hours postoperatively), rescue analgesia administration (time to first rescue analgesia, total opioid consumption, and types of rescue analgesic drugs), incidence of PONV, postoperative hospital stay and total hospital stay. All outcomes in the present analysis were restricted to the first 24 postoperative hours or the index hospitalization. Longer-term outcomes, including tendon healing, rehabilitation quality, long-term function, persistent pain, and chronic postsurgical pain, were not assessed.

Sample Size and Missing Data

Because this was a retrospective study, no formal sample-size calculation was performed a priori, and all eligible patients during the study period were included. To contextualize the available dataset, we performed a post hoc assessment using the prediction-model-specific criteria of Riley et al,19 assuming a shrinkage factor of 0.90 and a margin of error of 0.05 for the intercept, with the observed prevalence (208/489, 42.5%) and the apparent C-statistic of the final model (0.652). For the broad candidate set of 25 variables (26 predictor parameters), approximately 3260 participants with 1380 events would have been required. Because variable selection was performed within the broad candidate set, the model-development process as a whole did not satisfy the criterion for unrestricted development, and the analysis was therefore considered exploratory. LASSO penalization was applied to limit overfitting but cannot fully compensate for this limitation. After verification against the source records, no missing data remained, and neither imputation nor listwise deletion was required (Supplementary Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation (normal distribution) or median (interquartile range, IQR) (skewed distribution). Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t test or Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and compared using the Pearson’s χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test (when expected cell count was < 5).

Candidate predictors were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, previous literature, and availability in the electronic medical record system. LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was performed to screen potential predictors of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain from all candidate variables. Variables with non-zero coefficients at λ_1-SE were retained and subsequently entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. To improve model parsimony and clinical usability, a reduced model was further developed by retaining variables with stable associations and minimal loss of predictive performance. The initial 5-variable model and the final 3-variable model were compared using the AUC with DeLong test, likelihood-ratio test, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). (Supplementary Table S2).

Internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism-corrected model performance. Specifically, bootstrap resampling on the original cohort was used to obtain the bias-corrected AUC and to assess calibration performance. In addition to bootstrap internal validation, temporal split validation was performed according to the median operation date. The model was fitted in the earlier subset and evaluated in the later subset. Discrimination was quantified using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). Calibration was evaluated using the calibration plot, calibration slope, calibration intercept, Hosmer-Lemeshow test, mean absolute calibration error, and Brier score.

For the construction of the predictive model, variables were coded as follows: open surgery = 1, arthroscopic surgery = 0 (reference); no nerve block = 1, nerve block used = 0 (reference); and BMI was treated as a continuous variable (kg/m2). Logit(P) represented the log‑odds of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. The assumption of linearity in the logit for the continuous predictor BMI was assessed using restricted cubic splines. As no substantial departure from linearity was observed, BMI was retained as a continuous linear term in the final logistic regression model.

The nomogram was constructed from the final logistic model and internally validated using the rms package; LASSO regression was performed with glmnet, receiver operating characteristic analysis with pROC, and decision curve analysis with rmda. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and R software (version 4.4.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient Characteristics and Perioperative Predictors

A total of 489 patients who underwent Achilles tendon surgery were included in the final analysis. Among them, 208 patients (42.5%) developed moderate-to-severe postoperative pain (NRS score ≥ 4), and 281 patients (57.5%) had no or mild pain (NRS score < 4).

Compared with the no-to-mild pain group, patients in the moderate-to-severe pain group had significantly greater body weight, higher BMI, and longer tourniquet time; intraoperative fluid volume also differed statistically between groups (all P < 0.05). Additionally, patients with moderate-to-severe pain were more likely to be male, have an acute injury, Achilles tendon rupture, undergo open surgery, have preoperative anxiety or depression, and not receive a peripheral nerve block (all P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in age, height, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, smoking history, chronic pain, preoperative analgesic use, comorbidities, PCA use, or anesthesia type between the two groups (Table 1; the full list of baseline and perioperative variables, including subgroup variables, is provided in Supplementary Table S3).

Secondary Postoperative Outcomes

The incidence of PONV was significantly higher in the moderate-to-severe pain group compared to the no-to-mild pain group (13.0% vs 4.6%, P < 0.001). The moderate-to-severe pain group also experienced a slightly longer postoperative hospital stay [2 (1, 2) vs 1 (1, 2) days, P = 0.026]. There were no significant differences between the two groups regarding total hospital stay [3 (2, 3) vs 2 (2, 3) days, P = 0.390] or the postoperative use of NSAIDs (99.5% vs 97.5%, P = 0.146) (Supplementary Table S4).

Among the 208 patients who experienced moderate-to-severe pain, the median highest NRS score within 24 hours postoperatively was 5 (4, 6). A total of 204 patients received rescue analgesia within 24 hours postoperatively. The median time to first rescue analgesia was 7.5 (5.5, 11.5) hours. Oxycodone and tramadol were the most commonly used rescue agents, with a median opioid consumption of 15 (8, 18) mg, calculated as MME.

Multivariable Logistic Regression Analysis

The candidate predictors entered into LASSO regression are listed in Supplementary Table S5. LASSO regression identified five candidate predictors with non-zero coefficients, including open surgery, BMI, absence of peripheral nerve block, Achilles tendon rupture, and preoperative anxiety or depression (Figure 1). These variables were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to estimate their independent associations with moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. In the initial 5-variable model, open surgery (OR 2.657, 95% CI 1.092–6.461, P = 0.031), higher BMI (OR 1.063 per 1 kg/m2, 95% CI 1.009–1.120, P = 0.021), and no peripheral nerve block (OR 1.975, 95% CI 1.329–2.937, P < 0.001) were associated with moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. Achilles tendon rupture reached nominal statistical significance with a confidence interval close to unity (OR 2.146, 95% CI 1.003–4.588, P = 0.049), whereas preoperative anxiety or depression was not statistically significant (OR 2.233, 95% CI 0.790–6.309, P = 0.130) (Table 2).

To improve simplicity and clinical applicability, a reduced 3-variable model including open surgery, BMI, and peripheral nerve block status was further constructed. The final 3-variable model included open surgery (OR 4.457, 95% CI 2.109–9.417, P < 0.001), higher BMI (OR 1.059 per 1 kg/m2, 95% CI 1.006–1.115, P = 0.028), and no peripheral nerve block (OR 2.022, 95% CI 1.369–2.988, P < 0.001) (Table 3). Although the initial 5-variable model had a slightly higher apparent AUC than the 3-variable model (0.666 vs 0.652), the difference was not statistically significant by DeLong test (P = 0.138). The likelihood-ratio test and AIC favored the 5-variable model, whereas BIC favored the 3-variable model. The apparent Brier score differed only marginally between the two models (0.224 vs 0.227). Considering parsimony, model usability, the modest discriminative gain, and the potential collinearity or ascertainment issues related to the two additional variables, the 3-variable model was selected for nomogram construction (Supplementary Table S2). Although Achilles tendon rupture reached nominal significance in the 5-variable model, the lower bound of its confidence interval was close to unity and its inclusion markedly altered the coefficient for open surgery, indicating unstable estimation; preoperative psychological status was ascertained only from documented medical history. Both variables were therefore considered less robust for a pragmatic perioperative tool, and the 5-variable model is reported in full in Table 2 as a supporting analysis. The predictive equation was as follows: Logit(P) = −3.422 + 1.494 × Open_surgery + 0.058 × BMI + 0.704 × No_peripheral_nerve_block, where P represented the probability of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. Restricted cubic spline analysis showed no significant evidence of nonlinearity for BMI (P for nonlinearity = 0.416), supporting its inclusion as a continuous linear predictor.

Development and Validation of the Predictive Nomogram

A nomogram was constructed based on the three predictors retained in the final multivariable model (Figure 2). Each variable was assigned a weighted score according to the regression coefficient. The point scale reflected the relative contribution of each predictor according to its regression coefficient, with open surgery contributing the largest increase in predicted risk among the categorical predictors.

Nomogram for predicting the risk of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain in patients undergoing Achilles tendon surgery. The nomogram integrated three perioperative predictors retained in the final model: surgical approach, Body Mass Index (BMI), and peripheral nerve block status. To use the nomogram, locate the patient’s status on each variable axis and draw a vertical line upwards to the “Points” axis to determine the score for that variable (for example, a continuous variable like a BMI of 30 kg/m2 corresponds to approximately 44 points). Sum the points from all three variables and locate this total on the “Total Points” axis. Finally, draw a vertical line downwards to the “Risk of Moderate-to-Severe Pain” axis to determine the individual’s predicted probability of experiencing moderate-to-severe pain.

Discrimination Performance

The discrimination power of the nomogram was measured using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve (Figure 3). The final 3-variable model showed modest discrimination, with an apparent C-statistic of 0.652 (95% CI 0.603–0.700). The optimism-corrected C-statistic after 1000 bootstrap resamples was 0.648. The optimal cutoff probability was 0.370, with a sensitivity of 84.6% and a specificity of 39.1%. This cutoff favored sensitivity over specificity, consistent with the use of the model as a screening aid rather than a definitive decision rule.

Apparent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve of the final predictive model for moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. The apparent AUC was 0.652 (95% CI 0.603–0.700). The optimal cutoff probability was 0.370, corresponding to a sensitivity of 84.6% and a specificity of 39.1%. Because specificity was low, this cutoff should be interpreted as favoring sensitivity for screening rather than as a definitive decision threshold.

Calibration Performance

Model calibration was assessed using a calibration curve with 1000 bootstrap resamples (Figure 4). The bias-corrected curve was close to the 45° ideal reference line, indicating acceptable agreement between the predicted probability of moderate-to-severe pain and the actual observed proportion. Because apparent calibration slope and intercept are 1.000 and 0.000 by construction in the development sample, calibration was quantified after bootstrap correction: the optimism-corrected calibration slope was 0.950 and the optimism-corrected calibration intercept was −0.017. The apparent Brier score was 0.227, and the optimism-corrected Brier score was 0.230. The mean absolute calibration error was 0.008. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test suggested no significant lack of fit (χ2 = 11.774, df = 8, P = 0.162).

Clinical Utility (Decision Curve Analysis)

DCA was performed to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the nomogram (Figure 5). The three-variable model showed a positive standardized net benefit across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities (0.11–0.56). Within this interval, the model showed a greater net benefit than the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies, suggesting that the nomogram may have potential value as an adjunctive risk-stratification aid.

Temporal Split Validation

Temporal split validation was performed using the median operation date. A total of 244 patients were assigned to the earlier development subset and 245 patients to the later validation subset. In the temporal validation subset, the model achieved an AUC of 0.634 (95% CI 0.565–0.703), indicating modest discrimination consistent with the bootstrap-corrected estimate (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

Moderate-to-severe postoperative pain is common after foot and ankle surgery.20,21 Suboptimal pain management is associated with delayed mobilization, prolonged hospital stays and impaired functional recovery, impeding the implementation of ERAS pathways.22 In this retrospective study of 489 patients undergoing Achilles tendon surgery, we identified open surgery, higher BMI, and absence of perioperative peripheral nerve block as three variables independently associated with moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. We combined these variables into a parsimonious nomogram. The model showed modest discrimination, acceptable calibration, and potential net benefit across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities.

Our results showed that open surgery was the strongest predictor retained in the final model and was associated with more than fourfold higher odds of moderate-to-severe pain compared with arthroscopic surgery. Open repair might require extensive dissection of the paratenon and surrounding soft tissues, leading to the local release of inflammatory mediators such as substance P, prostaglandins, and bradykinin.23,24 This inflammatory cascade may lower the activation threshold of peripheral nociceptors and contribute to peripheral sensitization. In contrast, an arthroscopic approach may better preserve the integrity of the paratenon and local blood supply, thereby reducing the inflammatory burden and subsequent peripheral sensitization. These findings are hypothesis-generating and suggest that surgical invasiveness may be relevant to early postoperative pain when minimally invasive strategies are clinically feasible.25,26 However, surgical approach was not randomly allocated, and confounding by surgical indication, rupture pattern, and injury chronicity cannot be excluded.

Higher BMI was also associated with moderate-to-severe postoperative pain in our cohort, with a 5.9% increase in the odds for every 1 kg/m2 increase. The association between obesity and postoperative pain was multifactorial. Mechanistically, adipose tissue is a highly active endocrine organ that secretes pro-inflammatory adipokines, contributing to a chronic low-grade systemic inflammation that may lower baseline pain thresholds.27,28 Surgically, surgery in patients with obesity is often technically more challenging, requiring more vigorous tissue retraction and dissection.29 In the context of Achilles tendon surgery, although early weight-bearing was usually restricted in our cohort within the first 24 hours postoperatively, greater limb mass and surrounding adipose tissue may increase the resting tension on the surgical site. Furthermore, higher BMI made patients more susceptible to severe localized postoperative edema, which may compromise local microcirculation, aggravate tissue ischemia, and further activate peripheral nociceptors. Consequently, patients with higher BMI may represent a more vulnerable subgroup in whom closer monitoring and multimodal analgesic strategies could be considered.

Perioperative peripheral nerve block was associated with lower odds of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain. Regional anesthesia, such as popliteal sciatic block, can reduce the transmission of afferent nociceptive signals to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. By preventing this massive sensory influx during the surgical incision and tissue manipulation, nerve blocks may play an important role in reducing central sensitization and the wind-up phenomenon.30 Consistent with previous clinical studies in foot and ankle surgery, popliteal sciatic blocks and ankle blocks were shown to provide effective analgesia and improve postoperative pain control.31,32 Because peripheral nerve blocks were not randomly allocated, this association may be influenced by patient selection, contraindications, surgical indication, anesthesiologist preference, and institutional practice patterns. Therefore, the coefficient for peripheral nerve block status should be interpreted as an association within our perioperative practice pattern rather than as evidence of a causal analgesic effect. Consistent with this interpretation, the model is intended for risk stratification at or near the end of surgery rather than for deciding whether a nerve block should be performed.

In the initial 5-variable model, Achilles tendon rupture reached nominal statistical significance with a confidence interval close to unity, whereas preoperative anxiety or depression was not statistically significant. Neither variable was retained in the final parsimonious model, and this decision was based on collinearity, estimation stability, and clinical usability rather than on statistical significance alone. First, Achilles tendon rupture and open surgery overlapped substantially in our cohort, because patients with rupture were predominantly managed by open repair; this overlap makes their separate contributions difficult to estimate reliably and may attenuate the apparent independent value of rupture status itself. The odds ratio for open surgery increased from 2.657 in the initial 5-variable model to 4.457 in the final model. This change is consistent with the association between Achilles tendon rupture and open repair in our cohort: when tendon rupture was removed, part of its effect was absorbed by the surgical approach variable. The magnitude of the open-surgery coefficient should therefore be interpreted with this collinearity in mind. Second, although recent literature highlighted the importance of psychosocial factors, such as anxiety, depression, and Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) scores, in predicting postoperative pain,33,34 these variables did not contribute sufficient additional predictive value to justify inclusion in the final parsimonious model. This discrepancy was probably due to our study population, which consisted mostly of young, physically active individuals with a lower baseline prevalence of psychological comorbidities. Furthermore, owing to the retrospective design, psychological distress was identified only through documented medical history rather than routine screening, potentially leading to under-ascertainment of mild or subclinical cases.

The final nomogram developed in this study yielded an apparent AUC of 0.652 and an optimism-corrected C-statistic of 0.648. Although some predictive models in other surgical fields reported higher AUCs (eg, >0.80),35,36 pain is a subjective, multidimensional experience influenced by complex neurobiological and psychosocial factors. Previous studies have shown that models predicting acute pain often yielded AUCs around 0.65.10,37 Despite modest discrimination, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and calibration curve suggested acceptable agreement between predicted and observed probabilities in the internal validation. Furthermore, DCA suggested that use of this nomogram may provide greater net benefit compared to “treat-all” or “treat-none” approaches. Given its modest discrimination and the absence of external validation, the nomogram should be regarded as an exploratory, internally validated screening aid that complements clinical judgment, rather than a diagnostic instrument or a validated decision rule.

With respect to analytical approach, we used LASSO for variable selection and bootstrap resampling for internal validation, which is preferable to stepwise selection with a single data split in a cohort of this size; nevertheless, penalization does not remove the instability arising from the limited sample size. The primary model was developed using the whole cohort and internally validated using 1000 bootstrap resamples. In addition, temporal split validation was performed as a supplementary internal validation, with the model fitted in the earlier subset and evaluated in the later subset. Both validation procedures were conducted within the same single-center cohort and therefore cannot replace external validation in an independent cohort.

A potential practical advantage of this study is the simplicity of the final model. The proposed nomogram relies on only three easily obtainable variables, without requiring specialized tests or complex laboratory measurements. This may facilitate preliminary risk stratification in routine perioperative settings. Once high-risk patients (eg, those with elevated BMI, scheduled for open surgery, or with contraindications for peripheral nerve blocks) are identified, the multidisciplinary ERAS team could consider closer monitoring and individualized multimodal analgesic strategies. This might include closer postoperative pain reassessment, optimization of multimodal non-opioid analgesia, and non-pharmacological measures such as cryotherapy and limb elevation, depending on institutional protocols and patient-specific contraindications. This proposed workflow remains hypothetical and requires prospective evaluation. In addition, to our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to develop a perioperative prediction model for moderate-to-severe acute postoperative pain specifically after Achilles tendon surgery. The candidate variables covered demographic, injury-related, surgical, anesthetic, and perioperative analgesic domains, allowing exploratory perioperative risk stratification within our clinical dataset.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study and is therefore subject to selection bias and limited generalizability. Second, peripheral nerve block is a modifiable perioperative intervention and was not randomly allocated, so the observed association may reflect confounding by indication rather than a treatment effect. Third, some subgroups were small, which limited the precision of estimates for certain variables. Consequently, although LASSO penalization was used to reduce overfitting, the selected predictors, regression coefficients, and performance estimates should be regarded as exploratory and potentially unstable Fourth, psychological status was obtained from documented medical history rather than prospectively administered standardized instruments, which may have led to under-ascertainment of anxiety, depression, or pain catastrophizing. Fifth, pain documentation was performed by clinical staff who were not blinded to surgical approach or analgesic exposure, and routinely recorded NRS scores did not distinguish resting pain from movement-evoked pain. Sixth, the model was internally validated by bootstrap resampling and temporal split validation only; true external validation in independent cohorts is still lacking. Finally, the model predicts acute pain within the first 24 postoperative hours and should not be extrapolated to longer-term recovery, rehabilitation quality, tendon healing, long-term function, or chronic postsurgical pain. Future work should therefore focus on prospective multicenter external validation, incorporation of standardized psychological instruments (eg, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale and the Pain Catastrophizing Scale), and assessment of dynamic or movement-evoked pain, which may improve discrimination beyond the modest performance observed here.

Conclusion

In this retrospective single-center cohort, open surgery and higher BMI were associated with increased odds of acute moderate-to-severe postoperative pain after Achilles tendon surgery, whereas perioperative peripheral nerve block was associated with lower odds. The final 3-variable nomogram showed modest discrimination and acceptable internal calibration. This model may assist perioperative risk stratification as an adjunctive tool, but external validation and prospective evaluation are required before routine clinical use.
