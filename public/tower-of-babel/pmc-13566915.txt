Identifying the neural mechanisms associated with older adults’ dynamic theory of mind judgments during a movie-watching task
Authors: Anne C Krendl, Roberto French, Colleen Hughes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging is associated with widespread deficits in theory of mind – the ability to infer others’ mental and emotional states. Theory of mind is a complex construct, but age deficits are generally measured in a single domain, overlooking how older adults engage multiple theory of mind processes concurrently and spontaneously. The current work used neuroimaging to characterize the neural mechanisms associated with older adults’ deficits in concurrent theory of mind judgments (awkwardness, deception) during a movie-watching task. 102 older adults (MAge = 73.5, SD = 6.6) and 110 young adults (MAge = 21.75, SD = 4.2) passively viewed a mockumentary television show while undergoing functional magnetic resonance imaging. Continuous ratings of awkwardness and deception were collected from an independent sample of young and older adults and used as parametric regressors. Across both judgment types, young adults had stronger activation in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, a region broadly implicated in theory of mind. Moreover, higher activation in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex in response to deception and awkwardness was associated with older adults’ higher theory of mind performance on an unrelated task. The brain activity involved in decoding concurrent mental states may provide unique insight into older adults’ theory of mind deficits.Keywords: Theory of mind, older adults, fMRI, spontaneous mentalizing

Theory of mind – the ability to infer what others are thinking and feeling – plays a critical role in older adults’ social relationships and cognitive well-being (Krendl et al., 2024, 2025), but declines in healthy aging (Grainger et al., 2023; Henry et al., 2013). An important limitation in the extant work is that it generally only assesses a single domain of theory of mind (e.g., belief inference, understanding emotions) Fernandes et al., 2021) (e.g., pictures, vignettes, but see Krendl et al., 2024, 2025; Laillier et al., 2019). Because every day social interactions are dynamic, spontaneous, and engage multiple theory of mind processes concurrently (e.g. Tamir & Thornton, 2024), such approaches may not fully capture the nature and magnitude of older adults’ real-world theory of mind deficits (Grainger et al., 2019; Hamilton et al., 2022).

A challenge to identifying the mechanisms underlying dynamic, spontaneous, and concurrent theory of mind processes is that, on their own, behavioral tasks lack sufficient specificity to disentangle them. Specifically, behavioral measures are typically explicit assessments of discrete domains of theory of mind. Thus, they are not well-suited to uniquely characterizing mechanisms underlying spontaneous and concurrent theory of mind processes. Neuroimaging complements behavioral approaches because different types of theory of mind processes (e.g., inferring beliefs versus understanding emotions) are associated with distinct patterns of neural activity (Schurz et al., 2014, 2021). Moreover, spontaneous mentalizing can be characterized dynamically using passive-viewing tasks paired with analytical approaches such as parametric regressors (Pantelis et al., 2015). Neuroimaging can thus provide unique insight into whether older adults can engage in spontaneous and concurrent theory of mind, as well as whether different mechanisms underlie these processes for young and older adults. Thus, the current investigation used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to identify the brain mechanisms associated with age-related deficits in navigating dynamic, spontaneous, and concurrent theory of mind processes.

Theory of mind is a complex construct (Apperly, 2012) that includes cognitive (e.g., inferring beliefs or intentions, detecting deception) and affective (identifying social faux pas, and understanding emotions) domains (Baron-Cohen, 2001; Quesque & Rossetti, 2020). Behavioral work has generated mixed findings related to age deficits across these two domains, with some work suggesting that age-related theory of mind deficits are more pronounced on cognitive than affective domains (Bottiroli et al., 2016; Henry et al., 2013; Wang & Su, 2013) – and other work suggesting that they persist across cognitive and affective domains (Duval et al., 2011; Fischer et al., 2017; Krendl, 2025). An important limitation in this work is that it is generally based on static tasks that look at single channels, such as the Reading the Mind in the Eyes task (Fischer et al., 2017; Otsuka et al., 2021; Yıldırım et al., 2020), which asks participants to decode emotions from static images of eyes (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001). Because real-world social interactions unfold over time and involve continuous updating (Ferguson et al., 2019), theory of mind processes often occur dynamically and concurrently (e.g. Tamir & Thornton, 2024). In such cases, performance in one domain (e.g., understanding how someone is feeling) could affect performance in the other (e.g., inferring their intentions), thus obfuscating the mechanisms underlying older adults’ deficits. Neuroimaging approaches may be uniquely suited to disentangle the mechanisms underlying older adults’ performance on cognitive and affective theory of mind tasks. The current study used a passive movie-watching task alongside two independent measures that captured cognitive and affective theory of mind concurrently.

Passive movie-watching is well-suited to assess dynamic, spontaneous, and concurrent social cognitive processes (for review see Jääskeläinen et al., 2021). Because the neural correlates associated with theory of mind have been well-characterized (Schurz et al., 2014, 2021), passive movie-watching provides an ideal framework for the current work. Neuroimaging work has shown that different types of theory of mind processes (e.g., inferring beliefs versus understanding emotions) are associated with disparate patterns of neural activity (Schurz et al., 2014, 2021). By using off-line measures of theory of mind (e.g., coding events in each frame, using ratings collected offline), neuroimaging approaches can be leveraged to dissociate the brain mechanisms associated with specific theory of mind processes (e.g. Jääskeläinen et al., 2021; Nanni-Zepeda et al., 2024; Pantelis et al., 2015). Moreover, analytical approaches such as parametric regressors can assess how these processes unfold over time (Meyer et al., 2012; Pantelis et al., 2015). For example, Pantelis et al. (2015) had a group of participants passively view an episode of the U.S.-based version of The Office while undergoing fMRI. The researchers also collected continuous ratings of awkwardness from an independent sample of participants. These ratings were then used as a parametric regressor to identify brain regions that were associated with dynamically decoding awkwardness during passive viewing. A benefit of this approach was that it allowed scanned participants to watch the show without explicitly attending to its awkwardness, which may be a better proxy of their everyday theory of mind engagement. Results revealed that awkwardness was associated with increased activation in brain regions commonly associated with theory of mind, including the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal junction. In the current study, we leveraged this approach to characterize the neural mechanisms engaged by young and older adults when they spontaneously and concurrently decoded two dynamic theory of mind judgments – deception and awkwardness – during a passive movie-watching task.

We focused on deception and awkwardness for several reasons. First, both are important aspects of complex theory of mind (Baron-Cohen et al., 1999; Quesque & Rossetti, 2020; Stone et al., 1998). Specifically, awkwardness judgments require individuals to infer beliefs and understand emotions (French et al., 2025; Heavey et al., 2000), whereas deception requires individuals to decode false beliefs (Frith & Frith, 2005) and emotions (Stanley & Blanchard-Fields, 2008). Second, prior work has shown that older adults are impaired at detecting deception and awkwardness, respectively (Baksh et al., 2018; Ebner et al., 2023; Halberstadt et al., 2011; Ruffman et al., 2012; von Hippel & Dunlop, 2005). Specifically, older adults are less accurate than young adults in detecting deception (Ebner et al., 2023; Ruffman et al., 2012), including in real-world settings (Ebner et al., 2020). They are also less sensitive to detecting key components of awkwardness, notably social norm violations (Baksh et al., 2018; Halberstadt et al., 2011) and socially inappropriate behavior (von Hippel & Dunlop, 2005).

The neural mechanisms underlying decoding deception and awkwardness have been well-characterized in prior work using dynamic stimuli (Hughes et al., 2025; Pantelis et al., 2015; Przysinda et al., 2024), including episodes from the U.S.-based version of The Office (Pantelis et al., 2015; Przysinda et al., 2024). One of the few such studies with older adults found that older adults’ sensitivity to deception was related to higher activation in neural regions associated with theory of mind (e.g., dorsal and ventral medial prefrontal cortex), as well as in cognitive control regions (e.g., dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, inferior frontal gyrus, cingulate cortex) (Hughes et al., 2025). In this study, participants viewed 20-second new clips in which the featured individual was later found to have been lying or telling the truth. For each video, participants indicated whether the featured individual was being truthful. Though prior work has not examined the neural correlates underlying awkwardness detection with older adults, neuroimaging research with young adults has found that detecting social faux pas is associated with increased activity in brain regions associated with vicarious embarrassment (Krach et al., 2011) and empathy (Melchers et al., 2015), including the insula, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, and somatosensory cortex (Fan et al., 2011; Piretti et al., 2023). However, an important limitation of this work is that it examined single domains of theory of mind asynchronously, raising the question of whether aging disrupts overlapping or distinct mechanisms associated with dynamic and concurrent theory of mind engagement.

In the current study, young and older adults passively viewed a mockumentary style television show, Nathan for You® while undergoing fMRI. Separate groups of young and older adults watched the same episodes outside of the scanner and made separate, continuous judgments of the episodes’ awkwardness and deceptiveness to use as parametric regressors (see Pantelis et al., 2015) for similar approach. We predicted that both awkwardness and deception would engage brain regions associated with theory of mind (e.g., dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, temporoparietal junction) (Schurz et al., 2014) as shown in prior work using dynamic stimuli (Hypothesis 1) (Hughes et al., 2025; Pantelis et al., 2015; Przysinda et al., 2024), as well as distinct neural activations (Hypothesis 2). Specifically, we predicted that awkwardness, but not deception, would engage activity in brain regions associated with decoding empathy and embarrassment, e.g., the anterior cingulate cortex, insula (Fan et al., 2011; Piretti et al., 2023), whereas detecting deception would be uniquely associated with higher activation in cognitive control brain regions (e.g., inferior frontal gyrus) (Hughes et al., 2025). Hypothesis 3 predicted that older adults’ age differences would emerge in brain regions that were engaged in both tasks (e.g., dorsomedial prefrontal cortex). To further assess whether older adults’ theory of mind deficits were associated with deficits in a mechanism shared across multiple theory of mind processes, young and older adults also completed an additional out-of-scanner theory of mind task that interrogated their general theory of mind ability. We predicted that higher activation in brain regions associated with decoding both awkwardness and deception would predict better performance on the unrelated task.

Methods

Participants & procedure

Older adults (OA; N = 108, MAge = 73.39, SD = 6.48, 71 female, 37 male) and young adults (YA; N = 123, MAge = 21.99, SD = 4.31, 74 female, 45 male, 4 non-binary) were recruited for the current study. Participants received monetary compensation for participating. For older adults, exclusion criteria included being under the age of 60 and/or an inability to pass a six-item cognitive screener administered over the phone (Callahan et al., 2002). For young adults, exclusion criteria included being over the age of 35. For both age groups, participants were also excluded if they were unwilling or unable to undergo fMRI (e.g., due to being claustrophobic, having non-MR compatible implants, being pregnant).

Power analyses conducted in G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) with five regressors and a small effect size (ƒ = .15) targeted an N of 92 for 80% power at α = .05. Older adults were predominantly White (N = 105; 97.22%) and well-educated (college degree or higher: N = 87; 83.65%). Young adults were also predominantly White (N = 86; 69.92%) and mostly undergraduates. Six older adults and 13 young adults were excluded from analyses because the relevant scans were not acquired (4 older, 12 young) or deemed poor quality (2 older, 1 young; e.g., due to high motion, see MRI data acquisition section). Data collection was approved by the Indiana University Institutional Review Board (#11801). Young and older adults completed the tasks across two sessions. In the first session, participants completed a screener to ensure they were eligible to undergo MRI. If they had not been previously scanned, they also were placed in a mock scanner to ensure they would be comfortable completing the scanning tasks. During this session, young and older adults also completed the neuropsychological battery from the Uniform Data Set 3.0 (UDS) (Weintraub et al., 2018), and measures on social connectedness that are outside the scope of the current investigation. See Supplemental Methods for a subset of the UDS tasks.

In the second session, they underwent MRI. The order of scans was: 15-minute eyes open resting-state scan, followed by the naturalistic movie-watching task (~15 minutes total), a T1w anatomical scan, and two social cognitive tasks (~20 minutes total). At the conclusion of the neuroimaging session, participants completed several social cognitive tasks outside of the scanner, including the unrelated theory of mind task (discussed below). The data for this article will be shared on request to the corresponding author.

Passive movie-watching task

Young and older adults watched two episodes of the mockumentary-style television show Nathan for You® while undergoing functional magnetic resonance imaging. The two episodes were taken from Season 1, Episode 2 (“Petting Zoo”) and Season 2, Episode 2 (“Souvenir Shop”). Show order was consistent across participants. Recent work with a lifespan sample found that the show engages theory of mind (French et al., 2025; Krendl et al., 2024), familiarity is relatively low (Krendl et al., 2024), and older adults’ explicit theory of mind engagement in response to these television shows predicts real-world outcomes, notably the structure of their social networks (Krendl et al., 2024, 2025).

The two episodes were displayed in MATLAB (version R2020a). Participants were fitted with MR-compatible earbuds so they could hear the show’s audio. An additional set of MR-compatible over-ear headphones were also used to minimize scanner noise. Prior to beginning the full scan, participants watched a 13-second introductory clip from the television show while undergoing an alignment scan which approximated the noise patterns and level of the full scanning sequence. If participants reported they had not been able to hear the introductory clip, the audio was adjusted and the sound check was repeated until participants reported that they could comfortably hear and see the video.

Post-scan movie comprehension measure

Upon completion of the scan, 100 of the scanned young adults and 84 of the scanned older adults viewed both Nathan for You episodes again while making continuous ratings of the show’s awkwardness using a Logitech Extreme 3D Pro joystick. Participants were instructed to move the joystick forward or backward in proportion to how socially awkward they thought the plotline was at any given moment, similar to previous work (French et al., 2025; Pantelis et al., 2015). There are several benefits to the joystick ratings: they provided a dynamic, continuous measure of comprehension, they allowed us to assess whether age differences emerged in comprehension of the videos, and this approach has been used in prior work as a proxy for theory of mind engagement (Pantelis et al., 2015).

Joystick ratings were sampled at approximately 30 Hz to extract a unique awkwardness timeseries spanning the length of both videos for each individual. Timeseries were z-scored within individual. We then generated a single comprehension score for each participant via a leave-one-out correlation approach in which a higher correlation indicated a greater similarity in rating between the removed participant and the group (i.e., comprehension).

Out-of-scanner theory of mind task

At the conclusion of the MRI session, young and older adults completed a novel theory of mind task that was based on a separate episode of Nathan for You that they had not seen in the scanner (Season 3, Episode 3: “The “Antique Shop”). The episode was broken down into 18 sequential clips ranging from 15 to 45 seconds (MLength = 23 seconds, SD = 4 seconds), each followed by 2 to 6 multiple-choice questions (for a total of 63 questions). To generate a comprehensive measure of theory of mind, these questions assessed five subcomponents of theory of mind – inferring beliefs, detecting deception, understanding emotions, inferring motivations, and detecting faux pas. Control questions were also included that did not rely on theory of mind. For analyses using these data, three OA and two YA were excluded for low performance (more than 3 SD below the mean), which suggested poor attention to the task. Two additional older adults did not complete this task due to time constraints.

There were several benefits to including this task. First, this task has been used in prior work with lifespan samples (Krendl et al., 2023), and validated in prior work (Krendl et al., 2024). Second, by assessing multiple domains of theory of mind, we could provide a general assessment of theory of mind ability. Finally, using the same television show (but novel episode) as was used in the scanner allowed for more direct comparisons between brain activation and out-of-scanner performance without introducing confounds associated with prior familiarity with the episode. The details of both tasks, including all questions and response options, are reported in full elsewhere (see Krendl et al., 2023), but see Supplementary Materials for example items.

MRI data acquisition

Whole-brain imaging was performed on a Siemens 3.0T Prisma MRI Scanner using a 20-channel phase arrayed head coil at the Indiana University Imaging Research Facility in Bloomington, Indiana. Stimuli were presented using a back projector (Sony WUXGA VPL-FH30) and behavioral data were collected on a Dell laptop running Windows 10. The television episodes were presented using MATLAB version 2022b. Anatomical scans were acquired with a high-resolution 3-D magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo sequence (sagittal rotation; 160 slices, TE = 2.7 ms, TR = 1800 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, 1.0 mm isotropic voxels; with no fat suppression). Functional scans were collected using an echo-planar image (EPI) sequence sensitive to blood oxygen level-dependent contrast (T2*; 54 slices with 2.2 mm thickness and no gap, TE = 30 ms, TR = 2000 ms, flip angle = 70°, 2.2 mm isotropic voxels, FOV = 242×211.2 mm, in-plane matrix size = 110 × 96, A/P phase encoding direction). Slices were collected in an interleaved order (multi-band acceleration factor = 2). Prior to all functional imaging, phase-encoding polarity reversed (PEPOLAR) single echo EPI image pairs were acquired for distortion correction (54 slices with 2.2 mm thickness and no gap, TE = 58 ms, TR = 7700 ms, flip angle = 90°, 2.2 mm isotropic voxels, FOV = 242×211.2 mm, in-plane matrix size = 110 × 96; no multi-band acceleration). At the beginning of each functional run, the scanner acquired and discarded two dummy scans.

Initial anatomical and functional image preprocessing was performed using fMRIPrep version 23.2.0 (Esteban et al., 2019) (RRID: SCR_016216) which is based on Nipype 1.8.6 (Esteban et al., 2022; Gorgolewski et al., 2011) (RRID: SCR_002502). Functional images underwent distortion correction based on two echo-planar imaging references, were realigned to correct for motion, and were normalized to the MNI152NLin6Asym (Evans et al., 2012) template space. All resamplings were performed with a single interpolation step by composing all the pertinent transformations. Two participants (1 older and 1 young) failed segmentation, and 1 older adult was excluded from analysis due to high motion, defined as >25% of volumes with framewise displacement > 0.9 mm (Siegel et al., 2014), in both movie-watching runs.

Consensus ratings to create parametric regressors

We created parametric regressors based on awkwardness and deception consensus ratings collected from independent samples of young and older adults who had not been scanned. For the awkwardness consensus ratings, we recruited 80 older adults (MAge = 75.55 years, SD = 7.14, 48 female; 96.25% White) and 80 gender-matched young adults (MAge = 18.73 years, SD = 0.95, 49 female, 30 male, 1 non-binary; 73.75% White). For the deception consensus ratings, we recruited a separate sample of 79 older adults (MAge = 75.23 years, SD = 5.88, 48 female; 96.2% White) and 79 gender-matched young adults (MAge = 18.68 years, SD = 0.81, 48 female, 27 male, 3 non-binary, 1 NA; 73.41% White). See Table 1. These participants made continuous ratings of awkwardness or deception using a Logitech Extreme 3D Pro joystick while viewing the Nathan for You episodes. Participants were instructed to move the joystick forward or backward in proportion to how socially awkward or deceptive they thought the plotline was at any given moment, similar to previous work (French et al., 2025; Pantelis et al., 2015). Joystick ratings were sampled at approximately 30 Hz to extract two unique timeseries concatenated across both videos for each individual: one for awkwardness, and one for deception. Timeseries were z-scored within individual.

To assess the inter-rater reliability of the awkwardness and deception consensus scores, we employed a calculation of the intra-class correlation coefficient ICC(3, k) (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979). The ICC value for the set of consensus raters was 0.985 for the awkwardness ratings, and 0.986 for the deception ratings, suggesting good reliability of each measure. For the main analyses, two averaged baseline consensus scores were constructed: one for awkwardness and one for deception. In Figure 1, we provide a graphical depiction of the time series for awkwardness and deception, respectively. Comparing across the two depictions, the awkwardness and deception joystick consensus ratings were moderately correlated with each other, r = 0.57, p < 0.001. Though the analyses reported in the main manuscript use the averaged baseline consensus scores, we also report in the Supplemental Materials the results using age-specific regressors (e.g., the independent YA ratings for the young adults and the independent OA ratings for the older adults). These supplemental analyses account for potential group differences in how awkwardness and deception are perceived.

fMRI analyses

After undergoing initial preprocessing, images were smoothed with an 8 mm FWHM isotropic Gaussian kernel and then underwent first and second level analyses in SPM12 (Wellcome Trust Center for Neuroimaging, London, UK; www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). We convolved the awkwardness and deception ratings separately with a Glover (Glover, 1999) hemodynamic response function via the “compute_regressor” function (Nilearn) (RRID:SCR_001362) and included these convolved continuous regressors together in a general linear model as variables of interest. We did not orthogonalize the awkwardness and deception regressors in our first level analyses (Mumford et al., 2015), but confirmed that, as expected, results are the same whether awkwardness or deception is entered first in the model. Models also included covariates of no interest (a session mean and, from the fMRIPrep confounds file: six movement parameters derived from realignment corrections, mean cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signal, mean white matter signal, a dummy coded run covariate, and spike regressors for volumes with high motion as quantified by framewise displacement > 0.9 mm) (Siegel et al., 2014). Of the analyzed sample, young adults had an average of 2.32 (SD = 7.05; range: 0–51) and older adults had an average of 4.03 (SD = 7.89; range: 0–47) volumes flagged as motion outliers. For each set of first level analyses, we then computed parameter estimates (β) at each voxel and for each participant. Second-level analyses were conducted using a whole-brain ANOVA.

To identify overlapping brain regions for both awkwardness and deception (Hypothesis 1), we conducted conjunction analyses using the masking function in SPM12 on the activation maps for the awkwardness and deception models, respectively. Each mask was thresholded at FWE p < .05, 20-voxels. To identify activation that was unique to each judgment type (Hypothesis 2) and each age group (Hypothesis 3), we conducted a 2 (judgment type: deception, awkwardness) × 2 (age group: young, older adult) whole brain ANOVA. We examined results that emerged for the main effect of judgment type for Hypothesis 2, and results that emerged for the main effect of age group (young adult, older adult) and the interaction for Hypothesis 3.

Results

Older adults had lower post-scan comprehension scores (joystick ratings) (M = .75, SD = .28) than young adults (M = .96, SD = .24), t(182) = 5.52, p < .001, Cohen’s d = .82, 95% CI [.51, 1.12], as well as lower performance overall on the unrelated theory of mind task. See Supplemental Table 1 for descriptive statistics by age group and question type, and Supplemental Materials for details.

Hypothesis 1:

Deception and awkwardness share some overlapping patterns of neural activation in theory of mind brain regions.

Across all participants, higher awkwardness was associated with increased activation in brain regions associated with attention and cognitive control – including right extrastriate cortex, bilateral anterior cingulate cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex – and emotional salience – including bilateral insula. There was also increased activation in a subset of brain regions associated with theory of mind, notably bilateral precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex, and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex. See Supplemental Table 2; Figure 2A. Deception was associated with more widespread activation in brain regions associated with theory of mind, including bilateral dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, precuneus, temporoparietal junction, and temporal pole. It was also associated with brain regions associated with cognitive control (e.g., inferior frontal gyrus). See Supplemental Table 2; Figure 2A.

Maps generated using FWE-corrected p < .05 with a 20-voxel threshold.

The conjunction analysis (see fMRI analyses for details) identified significant clusters associated with theory of mind – including right dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, bilateral precuneus, and bilateral temporal pole – as well brain regions associated with visual and perceptual processing (e.g., the bilateral cuneus, right fusiform gyrus, and right extrastriate cortex) and control and attention (e.g., bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, supramarginal gyrus, and frontal eye field) (Supplemental Table 2; Figure 2B).

Hypothesis 2:

Deception and awkwardness are associated with distinct patterns of neural activation in theory of mind brain regions.

The main effect of judgment type from whole brain ANOVA (see fMRI analyses for details) identified brain regions that were uniquely or more active for awkwardness and deception. For awkwardness > deception, there was increased activation in neural regions associated with sensory processing and attention, including right supramarginal gyrus, bilateral anterior cingulate cortex, right superior parietal lobule, and bilateral posterior insula (Figure 3, Table 2). However, for deception > awkwardness, there was greater activation in neural regions associated with theory of mind, including right precuneus, bilateral temporoparietal junction, and right dorsomedial cortex (Figure 3, Table 2).

Hypothesis 3:

Young adults have higher neural activation in brain regions underlying theory of mind than older adults (3A); activity in these regions will predict out-of-scanner theory of mind performance (3B).

The main effect of age group from the whole brain ANOVA yielded activation in two brain regions – the right dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and the posterior cingulate cortex. A closer examination revealed that activation was higher in both regions for young versus older adults, irrespective of the judgment type (Figure 4, Table 2). Older adults did not have heightened activation in any brain region relative to young adults (but see Supplemental Table 4). However, the main effect was qualified by an age group × judgment type interaction in numerous brain regions associated with theory of mind, including the bilateral dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, bilateral temporoparietal junction, and right precuneus, as well as in the bilateral superior temporal sulcus, and regions associated with sensory and emotional processing (e.g., the right auditory cortex, right orbitofrontal cortex, and the left insula, see Figure 5, Table 3).

Because the right dorsomedial prefrontal cortex was the only region to emerge both in the main effect of age and the age × judgment type interaction, we conducted a region-of-interest (ROI) analysis (6 mm sphere around peak: 2, 60, 14) using the marsbar tool in SPM12 (Brett et al., 2002) to interrogate the nature of this interaction. Subsequent analyses demonstrated that older adults also had weaker activation than young adults in the deception condition (p < .001), but the two age groups did not differ in the awkwardness condition (p = .121). See Figure 5.

Finally, we tested our prediction that parametric modulation in the dmpFC in response to awkwardness and deception would predict worse out-of-scanner theory of mind performance. Further, we examined whether this relationship was moderated by age group. To do this, we identified an independently defined ROI using the meta-analysis tool Neurosynth (Yarkoni et al., 2011). We used the search term “mentalizing” to identify relevant brain regions based on the extant neuroimaging research. Given our interest in dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, we isolated the strongest peak within that region (peak 6, 54, 26; z-score = 11.5), and extracted parameter estimates for a 6 mm sphere for each participant at that peak in response to awkwardness and deception, specifically. Because dmPFC activation in response to both awkwardness and deception uniquely predicted theory of mind performance when the two were modeled together in a regression (Supplemental Table 5), we conducted two separate hierarchical regressions (for awkwardness, deception, respectively) to examine their respective interactions with age group. For both regressions, the first step included control variables – age group (0 = older adults, 1 = young adults) and performance on the non-theory of mind questions – and the main effect of dmPFC activation (for either awkwardness or deception). The second step included the interaction between age group and the respective dmPFC engagement.

When modeled on its own, higher activation in the right dmPFC during movie-watching did not predict higher subsequent theory of mind performance for awkwardness (ß = .09, p = .169), but it did for deception (ß = .143, p = .050). However, the interaction between age group and activation in the right dmPFC predicted theory of mind performance for awkwardness (ß = .285, p = .016), but not deception (ß = −.091, p = .489). See Table 4 for full list of results. The interaction emerged for awkwardness because dmPFC activation and performance were positively related for young adults (ß = .232, p = .015), but were unrelated for older adults (ß = −.101, p = .286); see Figure 6, also Supplemental Figure 3.

Discussion

There were several key results in the current study. First, using a large sample of more than 100 young and 100 older adults, we found that the neural mechanisms underlying different aspects of theory of mind (awkwardness, deception) were overlapping, but distinct. Moreover, this was the case for both young and older adults. Specifically, though both judgments were associated with increased activation in brain regions previously implicated in theory of mind (e.g., dorsomedial prefrontal cortex), awkwardness was also associated with increased activation in brain regions associated with sensory processing and emotional salience (e.g., insula), whereas deception was associated with increased activation in brain regions associated with cognitive control (e.g., inferior frontal gyrus). Second, a common mechanism – the right dorsomedial prefrontal cortex – was associated with age differences in detecting both awkwardness and deception. Specifically, we found that young adults had higher activation in this region than older adults, regardless of the theory of mind judgment. Finally, the magnitude of activation in this region predicted performance on an out-of-scanner theory of mind measure. Together, this study provided the first evidence of the neural mechanisms associated with older adults’ concurrent theory of mind judgments during a dynamic task.

The results of this study align with prior work examining the neural correlates associated with deception and awkwardness, respectively, and extend them to an older adult sample. For example, we found that dynamic deception detection was associated with increased activation in brain regions underlying theory of mind (e.g., dorsomedial prefrontal cortex), and cognitive control (e.g., dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, inferior frontal gyrus), similar to Hughes et al., (2025). We also found that awkwardness was associated with increased activity in brain regions in the salience network, including the insula and anterior cingulate cortex. Prior work has related structural connectivity within this network to better theory of mind performance among cognitively normal and cognitively impaired older adults (Rijpma et al., 2021). Moreover, activation in this network is associated with vicarious embarrassment and empathy (Krach et al., 2011; Melchers et al., 2015), suggesting that decoding awkwardness may also involve the integration of sensory, emotional and cognitive information. Interestingly, the conjunction analyses revealed that both deception and awkwardness were associated with increased activation in brain regions commonly associated with theory of mind, including the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal junction. This finding is consistent with the results from a study by Pantelis et al. (2015) that also found that decoding awkwardness from a mockumentary-style television show was associated with heightened activation in brain regions associated with theory of mind.

The current study also extends the findings from one prior study examining the neural correlates underlying age deficits in dynamic theory of mind judgments (Laillier et al., 2019). That study demonstrated that cognitive and affective theory of mind performance were associated with higher volume in the right temporoparietal junction and medial prefrontal cortex – brain regions widely implicated in theory of mind. However, an important caveat that this study is it did not measure theory of mind during scanning, raising questions about the functional mechanisms underlying older adults’ theory of mind performance. This is important because functional neuroimaging can elucidate whether age differences emerged in brain regions that are unique to one task (e.g., deception), but not the other (e.g., awkwardness). Indeed, our finding that age differences emerged in brain regions engaged in both tasks (e.g., dorsomedial prefrontal cortex) suggests that there may be a common mechanism underlying older adults’ theory of mind deficits. The finding aligns with prior work showing that activity in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex was associated with age deficits in theory of mind performance across multiple theory of mind tasks (Moran et al., 2012).

An unexpected finding from the current study was the fact that age group moderated the relationship between dorsomedial prefrontal cortex activity and out-of-scanner theory of mind performance, but only for awkwardness. Specifically, we found that increased activity in dorsomedial prefrontal cortex predicted better out-of-scanner performance for young adults, but was not related to older adults’ performance. Conversely, increased dorsomedial prefrontal cortex activity in response to deception predicted out-of-scanner theory of mind performance for both young and older adults. One possibility for these findings is that the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex may play different roles in decoding awkwardness in young versus older adults. Indeed, the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex has been implicated in several different aspects of mentalizing. For example, a study using transcranial direct current stimulation found that the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex was causally involved in distinguishing self from others and integrating social information about others (Martin et al., 2017). Other studies, however, have implicated the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex in higher-level episodic memory, especially for emotional information (Ford & Kensinger, 2019). This is not to suggest, however, that different mechanisms underlie young and older adults’ theory of mind altogether. Rather, the fact that activation in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex in response to deception was positively associated with out-of-scanner theory of mind performance for both young and older adults suggests that the manner in which this brain region is engaged to decode deception likely relates to how young and older adults make other theory of mind judgments. Future work should investigate this possibility.

Another important consideration in the current study is what role, if any, age-related cognitive decline might have played in the results. Age-related cognitive decline, particularly in executive function and episodic memory, has frequently been implicated in older adults’ theory of mind deficits (for review see Fernandes et al., 2021). A recent study found that episodic memory accounted for unique, albeit if not all, variance in older adults’ performance on dynamic and static theory of mind tasks (Krendl, 2025). Thus, a limitation of this study is that it did not directly explore potential confounds related cognitive decline. However, it is worth noting that neither older adults’ executive function nor episodic memory performance was significantly related to activation in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex in response to awkwardness or deception (see Supplemental Table 6). This suggests that the interaction in this region was not driven specifically by age-related cognitive decline. Rather, this finding aligns with a growing literature suggesting that cognitive decline does not fully explain age deficits on social cognitive tasks (Grainger et al., 2023; Krendl, 2025). Thus, one possibility is that older adults’ theory of mind deficits may be related to unique social cognitive processes associated with the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex.

A unique contribution of the current work was that it identified distinct, but overlapping, mechanisms associated with concurrent theory of mind judgments. Moreover, an intriguing possibility that rises from these findings is that different aspects of spontaneous theory of mind may unfold in different manners and along different timeframes. Consistent with these assertions, the joystick consensus ratings revealed that awkwardness was relatively salient and detected quickly (as denoted by multiple transient moments), whereas deception generally seemed to unfold over time (as denoted by more sustained deceptive moments). With respect to the former, these transient moments for awkwardness were observed in another neuroimaging study that used a similar approach (Pantelis et al., 2015). Together, these findings suggest that some brain regions underlying theory of mind may be engaged over prolonged periods during theory of mind. Given that social interactions in everyday life are dynamic and complex (Tamir & Thornton, 2024), future work should more closely explore this possibility.

There are several limitations in the current study. First, using the age-group combined regressors could have obfuscated differences in how young and older adults perceive awkwardness and deception. To address this concern, we repeated the analyses using age-specific regressors only (e.g., young adult regressors for the young adult sample, older adult regressors from the older adult sample). Results are largely consistent across the two analyses, with the main differences being that the main effect of age group yielded more widespread activation patterns in response to the age-specific versus the combined regressors. Notably, in addition to heightened activation in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate, young adults also had heightened activation in the bilateral temporal pole, bilateral temporal gyrus, and left temporoparietal junction compared to older adults. Conversely, older adults had heightened activation in bilateral cuneus compared to young adults. Though speculative, these discrepancies could reflect differences in how young and older adults perceive awkwardness and deception. Future work should explore this possibility. A second limitation of the current study is that the older adults’ were relatively homogeneous (e.g., mostly White, well-educated). Recent work has shown that older adults’ theory of mind deficits may interact with education and/or their cultural background (Yong et al., 2022). Thus, future work should replicate and extend these findings in sociodemographically diverse older adult samples.

Together, the results of the current study suggest that age-related deficits in theory of mind performance are related to older adults’ ability to spontaneously navigate concurrent aspects of theory of mind. Moreover, the current study provides methodological approaches for measuring multiple components during a spontaneous theory of mind task. Specifically, by using independently acquired continuous ratings of deception and awkwardness, respectively, the current study identified the neural regions that were similar and distinct to each theory of mind process. A benefit of this approach is that it provides an opportunity to collect a wide range of ratings that could inform how different aspects of theory of mind rely on shared and non-shared neural correlates during spontaneous theory of mind.
