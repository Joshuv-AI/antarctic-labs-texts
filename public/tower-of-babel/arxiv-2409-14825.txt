∂ ¯ \bar{\partial} -problem for focusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation and soliton shielding
Marco Bertola, Tamara Grava, Giuseppe Orsatti

Abstract
We consider soliton gas solutions of the Focusing Nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation, where the point spectrum of the Zakharov-Shabat linear operator condensate in a bounded domain 𝒟 \mathcal{D} in the upper half-plane. We show that the corresponding inverse scattering problem can be formulated as a ∂ ¯ \overline{\partial} -problem on the domain. We prove the existence of the solution of this ∂ ¯ \overline{\partial} -problem by showing that the τ \tau -function of the problem (a Fredholm determinant) does not vanish. We then represent the solution of the NLS equation via the τ \tau of the ∂ ¯ \overline{\partial} - problem. Finally we show that, when the domain 𝒟 \mathcal{D} is an ellipse and the density of solitons is analytic, the initial datum of the Cauchy problem is asymptotically step-like oscillatory, and it is described by a periodic elliptic function as x → − ∞ x\to-\infty while it vanishes exponentially fast as x → + ∞ x\to+\infty .



1
Introduction

In this manuscript we consider the focusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS)

i\psi_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{xx}+|\psi|^{2}\psi=0,

(1.1)

with nonstandard initial data that originate from the infinite soliton limit.
The NLS equation is an integrable equation and the Cauchy problem can be solved via the inverse scattering transform of the Zakharov-Shabat linear operator
[
45
]
when the initial data satisfies zero boundary conditions at infinity, it is periodic
[
23
]
or the initial data has asymmetric boundary conditions
[
11
,
21
]
, or also when one considers an initial boundary value problem instead of a Cauchy problem (see e.g.
[
26
]
,
[
36
]
).
In all these cases the inverse problem can be formulated as a Riemann-Hilbert Problem
for a
2\times 2
matrix function defined on the complex plane with discontinuities along paths and with a certain number of poles. These paths and poles correspond to the support of the spectral scattering data.
Here we consider instead the case in which the inverse problem is necessarily cast as a
\overline{\partial}
-problem because the spectral scattering data has a two-dimensional support.

The
\overline{\partial}
-method in inverse scattering was developed by Fokas-Ablowitz
[
25
,
24
]
and Beals-Coifman
[
3
,
4
]
to study the Cauchy problem of integrable, dispersive nonlinear equations in two or more space dimensions. Its application to
inverse scattering has been studied by many authors (see for example
the monograph
[
1
]
for references up to 1990, and Grinevich, Grinevich-Novikov
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
).

To formulate the
\overline{\partial}
-problem for the NLS equation let
\mathcal{D}
denote a compact domain with smooth boundary in the upper half–plane
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{+}
and let
\beta:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{C}
be a smooth bounded function continuous up to the boundary of
\mathcal{D}
, that describes the spectral data (soliton density) of the problem. The
\overline{\partial}
-problem is to find a
2\times 2
matrix function
\Gamma(z;x,t)
depending on the complex variable
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
that satisfies the conditions

\left\{\begin{split}\bar{\partial}\Gamma(z;x,t)&=\Gamma(z;x,t)M(z;x,t)\\
\Gamma(z;x,t)&=\mathbb{I}+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1}),\quad\mbox{as $z\to\infty$}.\end{split}\right.

(1.2)

Here

M(z;x,t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\beta^{*}(z)^{2}e^{-2\theta(z;x,t)}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\text{ for }z\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\beta(z)^{2}e^{2\theta(z;x,t)}&0\end{pmatrix}\text{ for }z\in\mathcal{{D}},\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

\theta(z;x,t)=i(z^{2}t+zx),

(1.4)

where
\beta^{*}(z):=\overline{\beta(\bar{z})}
and
\overline{\mathcal{D}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\overline{z}\in\mathcal{D}\}
.

If the solution
\Gamma(z;x,t)
of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.2
) exists, the solution
\psi
, of the NLS equation, is recovered from the solution
\Gamma(z;x,t)
by the formula

\psi(x,t)=2i\lim_{z\to\infty}z(\Gamma(z;x,t))_{12}.

(1.5)

The
\overline{\partial}
-problem for non zero boundary condition has recently been considered in
[
46
]
.
The
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.2
) was formulated in
[
5
]
in the setting of integrable operators (see section below).
However the existence of solutions of such family of
\overline{\partial}
-problems has remained an open problem.
Furthermore, for general
\mathcal{D}
and smooth
\beta
the class of initial data
\psi(x,0)
described by the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.2
)
is in general unknown and requires the asymptotic analysis of
\overline{\partial}
-problems
and the extension of the
\overline{\partial}
-steepest descent method developed by McLaughlin and Miller and Dieng

[
40
]
,
[
22
]
.

If
\beta
is analytic in
\mathcal{D}
and the domain is a “generalized quadrature domain”
[
34
]
, the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.2
) can be reduced to a classical Riemann-Hilbert problem with discontinuities along a collection

\mathcal{L}
of arcs. We call this reduction
soliton shielding
because the effective soliton charge can be reduced from a two-dimensional domain to a collection of arcs.

An example of this case, first spotted in
[
5
]
, is the ellipse where
\mathcal{L}
is the segment joining the foci.
The goals of this manuscript are:

•

to show the existence of the solution of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.3
) and to show that such solution is obtained as an infinite soliton limit (see Theorem
3.2
and formula (
3.29
)); on the way we represent
|\psi(x,t)|^{2}
by the second
x\penalty\
derivative of the logarithmic of the
\tau
-function of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem;

•

to show that when
\mathcal{D}
is an ellipse and
\beta(z)
is analytic, the solution of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.3
) produces an initial datum that is step-like oscillatory.
In particular we show in Theorem
4.1
that

\psi(x,0)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}&-i{\rm e}^{2i(g_{\infty}x+\phi_{\infty})}(\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1})\dn\left((\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1})(x-x_{0});m\right)+\mathcal{O}({\rm e}^{c_{-}x}),\quad\mbox{as $x\to-\infty$},\\
&\mathcal{O}({\rm e}^{-c_{+}x}),\quad\mbox{as $x\to+\infty$}\end{array}\right.

(1.6)

where
c_{\pm}
are positive constants,
\dn(z;m)
is the Jacobi elliptic function of modulus
m=\frac{4\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}}{(\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1})^{2}}
and
x_{0}

is a constant that depends on
\beta(z)
and the geometry of the problem,
g_{\infty}
and
\phi_{\infty}
are real constants, and
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are the location of the foci of the ellipse
\mathcal{D}
on the positive imaginary axis.

). Such an initial datum

2
Infinite soliton limit and
\overline{\partial}
-problem

The
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.2
) appears when considering the limit of infinitely many solitons of the NLS initial data.
The one–soliton solution is given by

\psi(x,t)=2b\,\mbox{sech}[2b(x+2at-x_{0})]{\rm e}^{-2i[ax+(a^{2}-b^{2})t+\frac{\phi_{0}}{2}]}

(2.1)

where
x_{0}
is the initial peak position of the soliton,
\phi_{0}
is the initial phase,
2b
is the modulus of the wave maximal amplitude and
-2a
is the soliton velocity.
The
N
soliton solution can be obtained from the inverse scattering problem for the Zhakarov-Shabat linear spectral problem with only discrete spectrum
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}

and norming constants
\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
.
When the solitons are far apart, each spectral point
z_{j}=a_{j}+ib_{j}
describes a soliton with speed
-2a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and amplitude
2b_{j}>0
.
The phase space of the
N
-soliton solution is
\mathbb{C}_{+}^{N}\times\{\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus\{0\}\}
where
\mathbb{C}_{+}
is the upper half space.

The inverse scattering problem recovers the
N
-soliton solution from the spectral data
\{z_{j},c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
.
This goal is accomplished by formulating the inverse scattering problem as a Riemann-Hilbert Problem for a
2\times 2
matrix
Y^{N}(z;x,t)
where
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Here we follows the presentation of the
N
-soliton solution implemented in
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
, to obtain the infinite soliton limit.

Suppose that the points
z_{j}=a_{j}+ib_{j}
are contained within a counterclockwise close curve
\gamma_{+}
in the upper half space and similarly the points
\overline{z}_{j}=a_{j}-ib_{j}
are contained in
\gamma_{-}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\;|\;\overline{z}\in\gamma_{+}\}
that is oriented counterclockwise.
The matrix
Y^{N}(z;x,t)
is required to be analytic for
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{\gamma_{+}\cup\gamma_{-}\}
and
with boundary values satisfying

\begin{split}&Y^{N}(z_{+};x,t)=Y^{N}(z_{-};x,t)\begin{pmatrix}1&\sum\limits^{N}_{j=1}\frac{\bar{c}_{j}e^{-2\theta(z,x,t)}}{z-\bar{z}_{j}}{\bf 1}_{\gamma_{-}}(z)\\
-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\frac{c_{j}e^{2\theta(z,x,t)}}{z-z_{j}}{\bf 1}_{\gamma_{+}}(z)&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\end{split}

(2.2)

for
z\in\gamma_{+}\cup\gamma_{-}
where
{\bf 1}_{\gamma_{\pm}}
are the characteristic function of the curves
\gamma_{\pm}
.
The normalization of the problem is fixed by the behaviour at infinity

\begin{split}&Y^{N}(z;x,t)\underset{z\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{I}\,.\end{split}

(2.3)

From the inverse scattering problem one finds that

Y^{N}(z;x,t)=I+\frac{1}{2iz}\begin{pmatrix}-\int_{x}^{\infty}|\psi(s,t)|^{2}ds&\psi(x,t)\\
\psi(x,t)^{*}&\int_{x}^{\infty}|\psi(s,t)|^{2}ds\end{pmatrix}+O(z^{-1}).

(2.4)

From (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) the matrix
Y^{N}(z;x,t)
can be reconstructed by the formula

Y^{N}(z;x,t)=\left[\mathbb{I}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\dfrac{\begin{pmatrix}l_{j}(x,t)&0\\
m_{j}(x,t)&0\end{pmatrix}}{z-z_{j}}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\dfrac{\begin{pmatrix}0&-\overline{m_{j}(x,t)}\\
0&\overline{l_{j}(x,t)}\end{pmatrix}}{z-\overline{z_{j}}}\right]J_{Y}(z)\,,

(2.5)

where

J_{Y}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\sum\limits^{N}_{j=1}\frac{\bar{c}_{j}e^{-2\theta(z,x,t)}}{z-\bar{z}_{j}}{\bf 1}_{D_{\gamma_{-}}}(z)\\
-\par\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\frac{c_{j}e^{2\theta(z,x,t)}}{z-z_{j}}{\bf 1}_{D_{\gamma_{+}}}(z)&1\end{pmatrix},\quad

and
D_{\gamma_{\pm}}
are the domains bounded by
\gamma_{\pm}
, respectively. The request that
Y^{N}(z;x,t)
is analytic for
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{\gamma_{+}\cup\gamma_{-}\}
leads to a determined linear system of equations for the coefficients
l_{j}
and
m_{j}
.
Then, the reconstruction formula of the
N
-soliton solution
\psi_{N}(x,t)
is obtained from (
2.4
) by

\psi_{N}(x,t)=2i\lim_{z\to\infty}z(Y^{N}(z;x,t))_{12}=-2i\sum_{j=1}^{N}\overline{m_{j}(x,t)}.

A direct expression of the amplitude of the solution can be obtained from (
2.4
) using the Kay-Moses formula
[
37
]
,

|\psi_{N}(x,t)|^{2}=2i\partial_{x}\sum_{j=1}^{N}l_{j}(x,t)=\partial^{2}_{x}\log({\rm det}(\mathbb{I}_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}+\Phi_{N}(x,t)\,\overline{\Phi_{N}(x,t)})),

(2.6)

where
\Phi_{N}
is an
N\times N
matrix with elements

(\Phi_{N}(x,t))_{jk}:=\frac{\sqrt{c_{j}\overline{c_{k}}}e^{\theta(z_{j},x,t)-\theta(\overline{z_{k}},x,t)}}{i(z_{j}-\overline{z_{k}})},\quad\Phi_{N}(x,t)^{T}=\overline{\Phi_{N}(x,t)}.

(2.7)

We observe that
\Phi_{N}
is a Hermitian matrix. The determinant expression (
2.6
) was derived in
[
13
]
.

We consider the limit
N\to\infty
as in
[
5
]
. Let
\mathcal{D}
be the simply connected domain bounded by
\gamma_{+}
(and
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
bounded by
\gamma_{-}
).
We assume that the poles
z_{j}
accumulate uniformly in
\mathcal{D}
as
N
grows. We choose the norming constants
c_{j}
to be interpolated by a smooth function
\beta^{2}(z,\overline{z})
in
\mathcal{D}
(we write for brevity
\beta^{2}(z)
where it is understood that the function is smooth and not necessarily analytic)

c_{j}=\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\pi N}\beta^{2}(z_{j}){\bf 1}_{\mathcal{D}}(z_{j}),

(2.8)

where
{\bf 1}_{\mathcal{D}}
is the characteristic function of the domain
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{A}
is the area of
\mathcal{D}
.
Upon taking the limit
N\to+\infty
, we get

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-z_{j})}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\pi N}\frac{\beta^{2}(z_{j})}{(z-z_{j})}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\iint_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{\beta^{2}(w)}{z-w}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi}

(2.9)

where
{\rm d}^{2}w=\frac{{\rm d}\bar{w}\wedge{\rm d}w}{2i}={\rm d}x{\rm d}y
is the usual area element.

Consequently, the RHP (
2.2
),(
2.3
), for
(x,t)
in compact sets in the limit
N\to\infty
, is the following.

Problem 2.1
.

We are looking for a
2\times 2
matrix
Y^{\infty}(z;x,t)
analytic in
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{\gamma_{+}\cup\gamma_{-}\}
such that

\begin{split}Y^{\infty}_{+}(z;x,t)&=Y^{\infty}_{-}(z;x,t)J_{\infty}(z;x,t),\\
Y^{\infty}_{+}(z;x,t)&=\mathbb{I}+\mathcal{O}\left(z^{-1}\right),\text{ as }z\to\infty\end{split}

(2.10)

with jump matrix

J_{\infty}(z;x,t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\iint\limits_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\frac{e^{-2\theta(z,x,t)}\beta^{*}(w)^{2}{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi(z-w)}{\bf 1}_{\gamma_{-}}(z)\\
-\iint\limits_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{e^{2\theta(z,x,t)}\beta(w)^{2}{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi(z-w)}{\bf 1}_{\gamma_{+}}(z)&1\end{pmatrix},

(2.11)

where we have introduced the notation
\beta^{*}(z):=\overline{\beta(\overline{z})}
.

We can relate this RHP to a
\overline{\partial}
-problem as we now explain.
Consider the transformation

\Gamma(z;x,t)=Y^{\infty}(z;x,t)A(z;x,t)

(2.12)

where the matrix
A(z;x,t)
is defined by

A(z;x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}&\begin{pmatrix}1&-\iint\limits_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\frac{e^{-2\theta(z,x,t)}\beta^{*}(w)^{2}{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi(z-w)}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\text{ for }z\text{ inside the loop }\gamma_{-}\\
&\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\iint\limits_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{e^{2\theta(z,x,t)}\beta(w)^{2}{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi(z-w)}&1\end{pmatrix}\text{ for }z\text{ inside the loop }\gamma_{+}\\
&\mathbb{I}\text{ otherwise }\end{array}\right.

We apply the
\overline{\partial}
operator to (
2.12
)

\overline{\partial}\Gamma(z;x,t)=Y^{\infty}(z;x,t)\overline{\partial}A(z;x,t)=\Gamma(z;x,t)M(z;x,t)

where

M(z;x,t)=A^{-1}(z;x,t)\overline{\partial}A(z;x,t)\,.

(2.13)

A simple computation shows that

\begin{split}M(z;x,t)&=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\beta^{*}(z)^{2}e^{-2\theta(z,x,t)}{\bf 1}_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}(z)\\
\beta(z)^{2}e^{2\theta(z,x,t)}{\bf 1}_{\mathcal{{D}}}(z)&0\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

so that
\Gamma(z;x,t)
defined in
(
2.12
) satisfies the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.2
).
The NLS solution is still recovered by the formula

\psi(x,t)=2i\lim_{z\to\infty}z(\Gamma(z;x,t))_{12}.

3
Fredholm determinant for the soliton gas

In
[
6
]
, it was shown that the
\overline{\partial}
-problems of the form (
2.13
) extend the theory of integrable operators of the Its-Izergin-Korepin-Slavnov (IIKS) framework
[
35
]
. The gist of
[
6
]
is to consider a Hilbert-Schmidt operator of integrable type acting on
L^{2}(\mathscr{D},{\rm d}^{2}w)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
to itself:

\mathcal{K}[v](z,\overline{z})=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}(z,\overline{z},w,\overline{w})v(w){\rm d}^{2}w,\;\;z\in\mathscr{D},

(3.1)

with kernel of the form

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}(z,\overline{z},w,\overline{w})=\frac{p(z,\overline{z})^{T}\,q(w,\overline{w})}{(z-w)},

(3.2)

\displaystyle p(z,\overline{z})^{T}\,q(z,\overline{z})\equiv 0\equiv(\overline{\partial}p(z,\overline{z}))^{T}\,q(z,\overline{z})

\displaystyle\qquad p,q\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D},{\rm Mat}(r\times n,\mathbb{C})).

(3.3)

Here
\mathcal{D}
is just an arbitrary compact set in the complex plane (consisting possibly of several connected components): as mentioned earlier, we will drop explicit notation of the
\overline{z}
dependence when indicating a smooth function.
The result of
[
6
]
is that the resolvent operator
\mathcal{R}
of
\mathcal{K}
exists if and only if the following
\overline{\partial}
-problem for the matrix
\Gamma(z)\in GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})
admits a solution:

\overline{\partial}\Gamma(z)=\pi\Gamma(z)p(z)\,q(z)^{T}{\bf 1}_{\mathcal{D}}(z),\qquad\Gamma(z)\underset{z\to\infty}{\to}\mathbb{I}.

(3.4)

The resolvent operator
\mathcal{R}
has kernel
\hat{\mathcal{R}}(z,w)
expressed via
\Gamma
,
p
and
q
by the relation

\hat{\mathcal{R}}(z,w)=\frac{p(z)^{T}\Gamma^{T}(z)\Gamma^{-T}(w)q(w)}{z-w}\,.

(3.5)

In
[
6
]
we also showed
that the
\tau
-function of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem identified by
the Hilbert–Carleman determinant (see e.g.
[
29
]
)

\tau:=\text{det}_{2}\big[{\rm Id}-\mathcal{K}\big]=\text{{\rm det}}\left[(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}){\rm e}^{\mathcal{K}}\right],

(3.6)

defined on the space
L^{2}(\mathscr{D},{\rm d}^{2}z)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
. The Hilbert–Carleman determinant is well defined for Hilbert-Schmidt operators. We then showed the following

Theorem 3.1
.

[
6
]

The operator
\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}
, with
\mathcal{K}
as in (
3.1
) and kernel
\hat{\mathcal{K}}(z,w)
of the form (
3.2
) is invertible in
L^{2}(\mathscr{D},{\rm d}^{2}z)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
if and only if the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
3.4
admits a solution.

The goal of this section is to identify the integrable operator
\mathcal{K}
for the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
2.13
) and show that the corresponding
\tau
-function is positive, thus proving the solvability of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
2.13
).

In our case, we can factorize the matrix
M(z,\overline{z})
, defined in (
2.13
), in the matrix product of
2\times 1
vectors
p(z,\overline{z})
and
q(z,\overline{z})

p(z)=\frac{e^{-\theta(z,x,t)\sigma_{3}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\begin{bmatrix}-{\beta^{*}(z){\bf 1}_{{}_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}}(z)}\\
{\beta(z){\bf 1}_{{}_{\mathcal{D}}}(z)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad q(z,\overline{z})=\frac{{\rm e}^{\theta(z,x,t)\sigma_{3}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\begin{bmatrix}\beta(z){\bf 1}_{{}_{\mathcal{D}}}(z)\\
\beta^{*}(z){\bf 1}_{{}_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}}(z)\end{bmatrix}.

(3.7)

From (
3.2
), the corresponding operator
\mathcal{K}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\cup\overline{\mathcal{D}})
has the following kernel:

\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(z,w)=\frac{{\beta(z)\beta^{*}(w)}{\rm e}^{\theta(z,x,t)-\theta(w,x,t)}{\bf 1}_{{}_{\mathcal{D}}}(z){\bf 1}_{{}_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}}(w)}{\pi(z-w)}-\frac{{\beta^{*}(z)\beta(w)}{\rm e}^{\theta(w,x,t)-\theta(z,x,t)}{\bf 1}_{{}_{\mathcal{D}}}(w){\bf 1}_{{}_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}}(z)}{\pi(z-w)}.

(3.8)

The goal is to show that

\text{det}_{2}\big[{\rm Id}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\cup\overline{\mathcal{D}})}-\mathcal{K}\big]
is non vanishing for all
x,t\in\mathbb{R}
thus proving that the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.2
) has always a solution.

In order to proceed with our analysis, we first revisit the theory of
N
-soliton solutions.

Review of the
N
-soliton solution.

The
N
-soliton solution of the NLS equation (
1.1
) with scattering data
\{z_{j},c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
,
\,{\rm Im}(z_{j})>0
,
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
can be recovered by the Kay-Moses formula (
2.6
)
where
\Phi
is an
N\times N
matrix with elements

(\Phi_{N}(x,t))_{jk}:=\frac{\sqrt{c_{j}\overline{c_{k}}}e^{\theta(z_{j})-\theta(\overline{z_{k}})}}{i(z_{j}-\overline{z_{k}})},\quad\Phi_{N}(x,t)^{T}=\overline{\Phi_{N}(x,t)}.

We observe that
\Phi_{N}
is a Hermitian matrix.
The determinant appearing in the Kay-Moses formula is precisely the finite–dimensional counterpart of the
\tau
-function and thus we set

\tau_{N}(x,t)={\rm det}(\mathbb{I}_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}+\Phi_{N}(x,t)\,\overline{\Phi_{N}(x,t)}).

(3.9)

This expression can be derived from the IIKS theory starting from the RHP (
2.2
) as we show in Appendix
A
for the benefit of the reader.

We can represent the matrix
\Phi_{N}
as a composition of an operator with its adjoint: define
\mathcal{M}_{N}:L^{2}([x,+\infty))\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
and its adjoint
\mathcal{M}_{N}^{{\dagger}}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\to L^{2}([x,+\infty))
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{N}[u]_{j}:=\int^{+\infty}_{x}\sqrt{c_{j}}e^{i(z_{j}s+z_{j}^{2}t)}u(s){\rm d}s,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{{\dagger}}_{N}[\vec{v}](y):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sqrt{\overline{c_{k}}}e^{-i(\overline{z_{k}}y+\overline{z_{k}}^{2}t)}v_{k},\ \ \ y\in[x,\infty).

A direct computation shows that

.

(no transposition), is also a Hermitian, non-positive definite matrix and hence the determinant (

) is necessarily strictly positive.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let

be the spectral data of the

Let us assume that

are interpolated by a smooth function as in (

.

Remark 3.1
.

The operator

is identically zero on the diagonal.

The corresponding convolution operators

In order to connect this Fredholm determinant with the Hilbert-Carleman determinant (

) for the operator

defined in (

), we decompose the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\varphi](s):=\iint_{\mathcal{D}}{\beta(w)}{\rm e}^{i(ws+w^{2}t)}\varphi(w)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi},\ \ \ \ \varphi(w)\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D}),\,\ s\in\left[\right.x,+\infty\left.\right)

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[f](w):={\beta(w)}\int^{+\infty}_{x}{\rm e}^{i(ws+w^{2}t)}f(s){\rm d}s,\ \ \ \ \ \ w\in\mathcal{D},\ f(s)\in L^{2}(\left[\right.x,+\infty\left.\right)).

(3.20)

The same is valid also for the operator
{\overline{\mathcal{B}}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\dagger}
. Now using cyclicity of the determinant,
we can rewrite (

) in the form

\displaystyle\tau(x,t)={\rm det}(\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}([x,+\infty))}+\mathcal{B}\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}})={\rm det}(\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}+\overline{\mathcal{P}}\circ{\mathcal{P}}),

where
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{L}^{{\dagger}}\circ\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}[\varphi](z)

\displaystyle=i\iint_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{{\overline{\beta(z)}\beta(w)}{\rm e}^{-\theta(\overline{z};x,t)+\theta(w;x,t)}}{(w-\overline{z})}\varphi(w)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi}

(3.21)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{P}}[\varphi](z)

\displaystyle=-i\iint_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{{\beta(z)\overline{\beta(w)}}{\rm e}^{\theta(z;x,t)-\theta(\overline{w};x,t)}}{(\overline{w}-z)}\varphi(w)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi}.

(3.22)

Both the operators
\mathcal{P}
and
\overline{\mathcal{P}}

The spaces

as maps
\mathcal{P}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to L^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})
and
\overline{\mathcal{P}}:L^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}[\varphi](z)

\displaystyle=i\iint_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{{{\beta^{*}(z)}\beta(w)}{\rm e}^{-\theta(z;x,t)+\theta(w;x,t)}}{(w-{z})}\varphi(w)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi},\ \ \ \ z\in\overline{\mathcal{D}},\ \varphi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{P}}[\psi](z)

\displaystyle=-i\iint_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\frac{{\beta(z){\beta^{*}(w)}}{\rm e}^{\theta(z;x,t)-\theta(w;x,t)}}{({w}-z)}\psi(w)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}w}{\pi},\ \ \ \ \ z\in\mathcal{D},\ \psi\in L^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{D}}).

We thus have

\begin{split}\tau(x,t)&=\text{\rm det}(\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}+\overline{\mathcal{P}}\circ{\mathcal{P}})={\rm det}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}+\overline{\mathcal{P}}\circ\mathcal{P}&i\overline{\mathcal{P}}\\
0&\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})}\end{bmatrix}\\

i\mathcal{P}&\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}&0\\
-i\mathcal{P}&\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})}\end{bmatrix}\right)={\rm det}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}&i\overline{\mathcal{P}}\\
i\mathcal{P}&\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})}\end{bmatrix}\,.\end{split}

(3.25)

With this new understanding, we define the trace class operator
\mathcal{K}
on

as:

\mathcal{K}:=-i\mathcal{P}-i\overline{\mathcal{P}}

(3.26)

that has kernel

\displaystyle={\rm det}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}\,_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}&i\overline{\mathcal{P}}\\

\psi_{0}(x)=\mathcal{O}({\rm e}^{-c_{+}x}).

We now focus on the asymptotic behaviour for
x\to-\infty
.
From the knowledge of
X(z;x)
(exponentially close to the model problem
T^{(2)}(z)
) we can find out the asymptotic behaviour of the initial datum
\psi_{0}(x)
for the NLS equation.
Indeed from (
4.23
) and (
4.39
) we have

\begin{split}\psi_{0}(x)&=2i{\rm e}^{i(g_{\infty}x+\phi_{\infty})}\lim_{z\to\infty}\left[zT^{(2)}_{12}(z;x)e^{ig(z)x}f(z)\right]\\
&=2i{\rm e}^{i(g_{\infty}x+\phi_{\infty})}\lim_{z\to\infty}\left[z(\mathcal{E}(z;x,t)X(z;x))_{12}e^{ig(z)x}f(z)\right]\\
&=2i{\rm e}^{i(g_{\infty}x+\phi_{\infty})}\lim_{z\to\infty}\left[zX_{12}(z;x)e^{ig(z)x}f(z)\right]+\mathcal{O}({\rm e}^{c_{-}x}),\text{ for }x\to-\infty\\
\end{split}

(4.40)

From (
4.15
) we obtain as
z\to\infty

{\rm e}^{ig(z)x}={\rm e}^{ig_{\infty}x}(1+\mathcal{O}\left(z^{-1}\right)),\quad

while for
f(z)
:

f(z)=e^{i\phi_{\infty}}+\mathcal{O}\left(z^{-1}\right).

Next we need to expand
X_{12}(z)
:

\begin{split}X_{12}(z)&=-i\frac{\vartheta(0)\vartheta(u(z^{(2)},\infty_{1})-\epsilon)}{2\vartheta(\epsilon)\vartheta(u(z^{(2)},\infty_{1}))}\left(\phi(z)-\phi(z)^{-1}\right)\\
&=-i\frac{\vartheta(0)\vartheta(\epsilon+\frac{1}{2})}{2\vartheta(\epsilon)\vartheta(\frac{1}{2})}\left(-i\frac{(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}{z}+\mathcal{O}\left(z^{-2}\right)\right)\\
&=-\frac{\vartheta(0)\vartheta(\epsilon+\frac{1}{2})(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})}{2\vartheta(\epsilon)\vartheta(\frac{1}{2})z}+\mathcal{O}\left(z^{-2}\right)\end{split}

where
u(\infty^{2},\infty^{1})=-\frac{1}{2}
by the symmetry of the problem. Inserting the above three expansions into (
4.40
) we obtain (
4.8
).

\square

Remark 4.1
.

The formula for the elliptic solution (
4.8
) can be rewritten in terms of the Jacobi elliptic function
\dn(z;m)
.
Let us introduce the Jacobi theta-functions

\displaystyle\theta_{3}(z;\tau):=\vartheta(z;\tau),

(4.41)

\displaystyle\theta_{4}(z;\tau):=\vartheta\left(z+\frac{1}{2};\tau\right)

(4.42)

and Jacobi elliptic functions

\displaystyle\dn(2K(m)z;m)=\dfrac{\theta_{4}(0;\tau)}{\theta_{3}(0;\tau)}\dfrac{\theta_{3}(z;\tau)}{\theta_{4}(z;\tau)}

(4.43)

Then, using the above relation, we can rewrite the initial datum as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \psi_{0}(x)&=-i{\rm e}^{2i(g_{\infty}x+\phi_{\infty})}(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})\frac{\theta_{3}(0;\tau)}{\theta_{3}(\frac{1}{2};\tau)}\frac{\theta_{3}(\tfrac{1}{2}+\frac{\Omega x-i\Delta}{2\pi};\tau)}{\theta_{3}(\frac{\Omega x-i\Delta}{2\pi};\tau)}\\
&=-i{\rm e}^{2i(g_{\infty}x+\phi_{\infty})}(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})\dfrac{1}{\dn\left(\frac{K(m)}{\pi}({\Omega}x-i\Delta);m\right)}\\
&=-i{\rm e}^{2i(g_{\infty}x+\phi_{\infty})}\frac{\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}}{\sqrt{1-m}}\dn\left(\frac{K(m)}{\pi}({\Omega}x-i\Delta+\pi);m\right)\\
&=-i{\rm e}^{2i(g_{\infty}x+\phi_{\infty})}(\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1})\dn\left((\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1})(x-x_{0});m\right),\end{aligned}

(4.44)

where in the second relation we used the identity
\dn(u+K(m);m)=\dfrac{\sqrt{1-m}}{\dn(u;m)}
and in the last relation we use the parity of
\dn(-u;m)=\dn(u;m)
and plug in the explicit values of
\Omega
and
\Delta
as in (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) and

x_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\int_{i\alpha_{1}}^{i\alpha_{2}}\frac{\log{r}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{R_{+}(\zeta)}}d\zeta-\int_{-i\alpha_{2}}^{-i\alpha_{1}}\frac{\log{r}^{*}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{R_{+}(\zeta)}}d\zeta\right]-\frac{K(m)}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}

In the limit
m\to 1
, or
\alpha_{2}\to\alpha_{1}
, we have that
\dn(u;m)\sim\mbox{sech}(u)
and
K(m)\sim\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{8}{(1-m)}
so that
\psi_{0}(x)
tends to the initial datum of the one soliton solution (
2.1
), with
x_{0}\to-\infty
.
In the limit
\alpha_{1}\to 0
we have
m\to 0
and the elliptic function
\dn(z,m)\to 1
so that the NLS initial datum
\psi_{0}(x)
is a plane wave at
x\to-\infty
.
