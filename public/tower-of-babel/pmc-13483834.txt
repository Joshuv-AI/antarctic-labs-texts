Linking childhood interstitial lung disease to IARS-related disorder: clinical and preliminary functional studies in four new cases
Authors: Dan Dai, Weitao Zhou, Gaoli Jiang, Zhuoyao Guo, Huixian Wang, Jinglong Chen, Mei Mei, Yi Lu, Libo Wang, Liling Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBiallelic mutations in aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase genes have been implicated in a variety of multisystem disorders. Nonetheless, the correlation between cytosolic IARS (which encodes isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase) and childhood interstitial lung disease (chILD) as well as pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) has yet to be established.MethodsWe characterized the clinical features of four pediatric patients exhibiting progressive chILD/PAP alongside extrapulmonary manifestations (growth retardation, intellectual impairment, hypotonia, and liver disease). Whole exome sequencing was performed to confirm that biallelic IARS mutations are the etiological cause of the disease, and protein structural prediction was conducted for the identified pathogenic mutations via AlphaFold modeling. Periodic-Acid Schiff (PAS) and Oil-Red O (ORO) staining, along with western blotting and transmission electron microscopy of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were used to characterize the disease phenotype and explore potential pathogenic mechanisms.ResultsAll four patients were confirmed to possess compound heterozygous IARS variants, inherited in trans from unaffected parents. Analysis of BALF revealed characteristic lipoprotein accumulation associated with PAP, evidenced by positive PAS and ORO staining. Additionally, surfactant proteins exhibited abnormal expression, accompanied by notable secondary lysosome formation and vacuolization within alveolar macrophages. scRNA-seq of PBMCs identified a specific depletion of CD14 + CD16+ intermediate monocytes and a transcriptional downregulation of phagosome/lysosome pathways in CD14 + classic monocytes. These findings suggest a potential intrinsic defect in the monocyte/macrophage lineage, potentially contributing to surfactant clearance dysfunction.ConclusionOur findings expand the phenotypic spectrum of IARS-related disorder by identifying chILD/PAP as a core and clinically significant pulmonary manifestation of biallelic IARS mutations. This study establishes IARS as a novel pathogenic gene for chILD/PAP and provides preliminary insights into the immune-related pathogenic mechanisms, highlighting the need for genetic screening of IARS variants in pediatric patients with unexplained respiratory failure and multisystem involvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-026-00998-9.Keywords: Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase, Childhood interstitial lung disease, Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, Whole lung lavage, Single cell RNA sequencing

Introduction

Cytosolic isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (IARS) belongs to the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (ARSs) family, and their primary function is to catalyze the esterification of the corresponding tRNA to its cognate amino acid according to the principles of genetic codon pairing, i.e., it catalyzes the aminoacylation reaction between specific tRNA and its homologous amino acids, which plays a critical role in protein synthesis and cellular homeostasis [1–3]. Mutations in ARSs have been implicated in various human diseases, including multisystem developmental disorders [4, 5]. Although IARS mutations have been linked to growth retardation, hypotonia, and liver dysfunction, their involvement in pulmonary disease remains sketchy and lacking in detail [6–9]. This study aims to develop a thorough understanding of the relationship between biallelic IARS mutations and childhood interstitial lung disease (chILD) as well as pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP).

The entire ARSs gene family, comprising 37 members, has been reported to be associated with over 60 human diseases to date and novel phenotypes and diseases associated with ARSs gene mutations continue to be identified [10, 11]. Other ARSs family genes have been implicated in chILD, supporting a possible link between IARS and pulmonary pathology. Mutations in MARS (encoding methionyl-tRNA synthetase) and LARS (encoding leucyl-tRNA synthetase) have been reported in cases of chILD, hepatopathy, and multi-organ dysfunction. Specifically, MARS mutations have been associated with progressive lung fibrosis, while LARS mutations contribute to interstitial lung disease and metabolic abnormalities [12–14]. Given these findings, it is plausible that IARS mutations could similarly affect lung homeostasis, particularly surfactant metabolism and macrophage function.

To date, fifteen patients with biallelic IARS mutations have been reported globally, primarily presenting with growth retardation, impaired intellectual development, hypotonia, and hepatopathy (GRIDHH; OMIM #617093). However, there has been little focus on pulmonary manifestations in these cases. Only three cases of infants with concomitant PAP have been documented, yet there remains a paucity of comprehensive research data on the subject [4, 15]. This study fills this critical gap by providing the first systematic characterization of chILD/PAP as a core pulmonary manifestation of IARS mutations, integrating clinical, genetic, and cytological evidence, alongside preliminary insights from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) to explore potential pathogenic links to monocyte differentiation and surfactant metabolism.

Materials and methods

Participants

Four patients were admitted to the Division of Pulmonary Medicine at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University between July 2018 and November 2021. They were all admitted to our department with respiratory dyspnea requiring oxygen support. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest revealed bilateral diffuse lesions and consolidation, characterized by multiple ground-glass opacities accompanied by fibrous streak shadows or a “crazy-paving” appearance. Additionally, they all had multisystem involvements(including growth retardation, intellectual impairment, hypotonia, and liver disease) and were undiagnosed following clinical procedures. All the patients were followed up by outpatient or telephone every three months, and the contents of follow-up mainly included physical examination, imaging of liver, spleen and renal, and chest X-ray and/or HRCT, laboratory examinations (liver and kidney function, coagulation function and hormonal profiles).

Ethical and informed consent

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and Chinese law, with parental written consent obtained. It was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (Ethics No. 2021 − 172).

Whole exome sequencing and data analysis

Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed on DNA extracted from peripheral blood using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with an average read depth of 100×. Raw sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using BWA-MEM. Variants were called using GATK HaplotypeCaller and filtered based on the following criteria: minimum read depth ≥ 30, allele frequency < 1% in gnomAD, and pathogenicity predictions using MutationTaster, PolyPhen-2, and SIFT. Variants were validated via Sanger sequencing in probands and parents to confirm segregation [16]. These methods have been previously detailed and are available in the Supplementary Material [17].

Protein structural modeling for variants in IARS gene

Protein structural modeling of IARS variants was performed using AlphaFold DB (version 2.2.0). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2022.3 with the CHARMM36m force field. The system was solvated in a TIP3P water box with 150 mM NaCl and energy minimized using the steepest descent algorithm. Equilibration was performed for 100 ps (NVT) followed by 500 ps (NPT), and production runs were carried out for 50 ns at 310 K using the Nose-Hoover thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analyses were performed to assess protein stability and flexibility.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples collection and analysis

The bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples from Patient 1 and Patient 3 were obtained through whole lung lavage (WLL) and subsequently placed on ice. Patient 2, however, succumbed to respiratory failure as a result of exacerbated interstitial lung disease before WLL could be performed. The BALF of Patient 2 and 4 was collected from routine bronchoscopy. Each sample was divided into four aliquots: one aliquot was used for smear preparation and staining, one was concentrated using a 3 kDa ultrafiltration tube (Millipore, USA) and the total protein of which was quantified using the BCA assay (Vazyme, China), one was prepared for transmission electron microscopy, and one was centrifuged (3000 rpm for 10 min) and the supernatant was saved for further analysis. The protein levels of IARS and pulmonary surfactant (PS) protein including SP-A, proSP-B, proSP-C, and SP-D of concentrated BALF were detected by western blot analysis. Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was conducted utilizing a 10% polyacrylamide gel. Concentrated BALF, at a concentration of 28 µg per lane, was subjected to electrophoresis on the SDS-PAGE gel, followed by transfer onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. Primary antibodies used as follows: anti-IARS (ab229643, Abcam, UK), SP-A (PA5-79987, Invitrogen, USA), proSP-B (WRAB-55522, Seven Hills Bioreagents, USA), proSP-C (WRAB-9337, Seven Hills Bioreagents, USA), and SP-D (ab17781, Abcam, UK).

BALF smear staining and electron-microscopic examination

BALF smears for microscopy were stained by the HE staining and special staining including periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining and Oil Red O (ORO) staining following the manufacturer’s instructions (Solaibao, China). BALF samples were fixed with electron microscope fixation liquid (Solarbio, China) overnight and were used for transmission electron microscopy.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

ScRNA-seq was performed on PBMCs (collected in a clinically stable, infection-free state) from Patient 4 and his fraternal twin brother (healthy control), processed using the Cell Ranger pipeline (version 3.0.1, 10x Genomics). To ensure high data quality, cells with > 20% mitochondrial gene expression and genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were filtered out. Batch effect correction was performed using Harmony integration to account for technical variability between patient and control samples. Normalization and clustering were conducted using Seurat (version 4.0), and differential gene expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was generated based on the top 30 dimensions derived from principal component analysis (PCA). Cell clusters were identified from the PCA-reduced data and subsequently determined using SingleR (version 1.0.6) with automatic assignment. Finally, the annotations of the cell clusters were refined by examining the expression profiles of canonical marker genes. Ultimately 11,999 cells from Patient 4 and 10,526 cells from health control for bioinformatics analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism and R (version 4.1.2). Given the small patient cohort (n = 4), power calculations were not feasible, but effect size estimation was performed for key comparisons. For scRNA-seq analysis, differential gene expression was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. Western blot quantifications were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test. Given the rarity of IARS mutations, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, warranting validation in larger cohorts.

Results

Genetic analysis

Biallelic IARS variants were found in four patients through WES, and further validated by Sanger sequencing in the probands and their parents that suggesting compound heterozygous variants: c.1330 C > T (exon14), p.R444X and c.443 A > G (exon5), p.Y148C (NM_002161) in Patient 1 (this case was recently documented by a colleague of mine [18]); c.1497_1498delCA (exon15), p.H499Qfs*5 and c.1310 C > T (exon14), p.P437L in Patient 2; c.1556G > A (exon16), p.R519H and c.241 A > G (exon3), p.R81G for Patient 3; c.443 A > G (exon5), p.Y148C and c.963delT (exon10), p.V321Vfs*42 in Patient 4 (Fig. 1). Most of the allele frequencies for these variants are rare in ExAC, 1000 Genome and GNOMAD. The predicted results of the missense variants were shown in Fig. 1 and Table S1.

Protein structural modeling of variants within the IARS gene

All seven identified variants in the IARS gene induce modifications in the protein structure. Notably, four of these variants—R444X, H499Qfs5, V321Vfs42, and R785Rfs*3—lead to the truncation of substantial portions of the protein by altering the position of the stop codon. For instance, the deletion of a base at P4 is predicted to cause a frameshift, thereby modifying 42 C-terminal amino acids starting from residue 321. This modification generates a stop codon, leading to the formation of a truncated protein (363 amino acids), which is 900 amino acids shorter than the wild type (1262 amino acids). A representative IARS structure (Alpha-Fold DB P41252) and the comparative structural alignments of the mutant structures modeled by AlphaFold are presented in Figure S1. Within these, the original amino acid side chain hydrogen bonds to surrounding amino acids were lost in the three mutants (Y148C, R81G, R519H) in IARS (shown in Figure S1 A-2, C-2 and C-4). However, no significant changes were exhibited in the mutant of P437L. Additionally, the MD simulation reveals that the mutation of R444X leads to protein instability due to its larger RMSD fluctuation of the protein backbone (Figure S1 E-F). In contrast, the wild-type form maintains within a narrow RMSD range, indicating its stability during the simulation. The RMSF analysis demonstrates a consistent trend in structural flexibility between the mutated and wild-type forms. The N-terminal structure of the protein shows less flexibility than the C-terminal structure. The mutation seemingly allosterically alters the flexibility of the C-terminal structure based on its position in the protein sequence.

Clinical characteristics of four patients

Four individuals with biallelic variants in IARS gene from four unrelated families. They all demonstrated a complex phenotype affecting multiple organ systems (Fig. 2), and were hospitalized with recurrent predominant respiratory insufficiency and complicated respiratory infection as well as other systems symptoms including growth retardation, muscular hypotonia, thrombocytopenia, hepatopathy, and abnormal brain development. The three boys (Patient 1, 2 and 4) were onset as early as infancy and all required with oxygen support due to respiratory insufficiency. They progressively developed intolerance of feeding and poor weight gain begin from birth. However, the other girl (Patient 3) only had a transient respiratory distress in the neonatal period. And she had several upper respiratory tract infections and mild exercise intolerance until she suddenly developed rapidly progressive shortness of breath and dyspnea at 8 years of age. Moreover, she had hardly undergone health checkups prior to this time. Tandem mass spectrometry screening for inborn errors of metabolism yielded negative results in all subjects.

And his bone mineral density testing revealed reduced bone density (Z=-2.88, Figure S2). Moreover, during this two-year period, he underwent repeated respiratory-related hospitalizations due to severe pneumonia and exacerbations of PAP.

Patient 2, a male infant, was delivered via cesarean section at 38 weeks and 2 days of gestation due to intrauterine growth restriction, with oligohydramnios as the only other complication. Birth weight and height were low (weight 2,150 g, -3.2SDS; height 45.0 cm, -3 SDS, see Table S2). On the 5th day after birth, gradually deepening skin and sclera yellow dye, and pottery clay-like stool appeared, later hepatosplenomegaly was detected. Meanwhile, he had elevated serum bile acids (220.3 µmol/L, reference range 0–10 µmol/L), indicative of cholestasis. Pulmonary function tests showed abnormal lung function, presenting mixed ventilation dysfunction (FVC: 1.1 L, moderate decrease; FEV1: 1.04 L, moderate decrease; FEV1/FVC, 110%, normal).

Patient 4, a male infant, was delivered via cesarean section at 31 weeks of gestation due to premature membrane rupture in a twin pregnancy. Birth weight and height were slightly low (weight 1,700 g, -2 SDS; height 38 cm, -3 SDS, see Table S2), and he suffered from severe growth retardation but his twins’ older brother didn’t. He developed recurrent pneumonia and clubbing. X-ray and HRCT revealed chILD (diffuse grid shadows and GGO in both lungs). The multi-organ syndrome caused by biallelic variants in IARS was summarized in Fig. 2; Tables 1 and S2.

Identification of PAP

BALF from Patient 1–4 were both milky, and was stratified after standing (Fig. 3). As for BALF cell smear staining in the two patients (Patient 1 and 3), positive staining for PAS staining was used to confirm the presence of amounts of PS-like substances and increased numbers of macrophages in the alveolar lumina. It showed a large amount of purplish red deep staining inside and outside the macrophages without structural substances, and it is lumpy. ORO staining showed that the number of dark/purplish red positive staining like substances in alveolar macrophages increased significantly, suggesting that intracellular lipid droplets (mainly neutral fat) were over aggregated and deposited (Fig. 4). Additionally, BALF cell smear staining in these two patients showed that HE staining presenting a large number of histiocytes, including more epithelial cells and a small number of neutrophils, lymphocytes and other histiocytes, and there were multifocal eosinophilic unstructured substances inside and outside the cells (Figure S3).

The detection of expression levels of PS related proteins in concentrated BALF total protein in Patient 3 showed that the expression of each component of PS related protein was abnormal. There was no expression level of proSP-B protein in concentrated BALF, and abnormal proSP-C cleavage precursors appeared, what’s more, the expression of each precursor was abnormal. Furthermore, the protein expression levels of SP-A and SP-D were elevated.

Regrettably, the Patient 2 died from exacerbations of respiratory failure caused by chILD before WLL performed.

Single-cell RNA sequencing of PBMCs

ScRNA-seq was performed on PBMCs from Patient 4 with IARSY148C/V321Vfs*42 variants and a healthy control (fraternal twin brother) to examine transcriptional changes (Fig. 4A). We analyzed 22,525 cells and identified 8 clusters using UMAP with graph-based clustering (Fig. 4B). Dimensionality reduction indicated that monocytes were relatively increased (Fig. 4C), and top 10 upregulated signal pathways included phagosome, lysosome and Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis (Fig. 4J). We performed a re-clustering analysis of monocytes, T cells, and B cells, and their respective proportions shown in Fig. 4D-I. We identified three distinct monocyte cell types: CD14+classic monocytes, CD16+ non-classic monocytes, and CD14+CD16+ intermediate monocytes, with the latter showing a significantly reduced proportion and lower odds ratio (OR = 0.292, Fig. 4G). Velocities obtained from the dynamic model for monocytes from Patient 4 and his dizygotic twin brother serving as health control indicated a sequential development from classic monocytes to intermediate monocytes and then to non-classic monocytes (Fig. 4K). To understand the functions of three distinct monocyte cell subsets, we conducted differential expression and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses between Patient 4 and healthy control. The top 20 significantly downregulated pathways in Patient 4 compared to healthy controls were associated with phagocytosis, lysosome and infection signaling pathway, and the difference was significant in classical monocytes (Fig. 4L). ScRNA-seq analysis of Patient 4 revealed an increase in CD14+ monocytes and a reduction in CD14+CD16+ intermediate monocytes, accompanied by monocyte dysfunction. These findings suggest a potential role for IARS in immune regulation, particularly in macrophage function. Given the known role of alveolar macrophages in surfactant homeostasis and immune defense, our data provide preliminary mechanistic clues into how IARS dysfunction may contribute to pulmonary pathology. However, these observations are currently correlative, and additional functional studies are necessary to establish causality.

Treatments and outcome

The Patient 1 primarily received combined anti-infective, respiratory support (non-invasive mechanical ventilation and nasal cannula), and symptomatic treatment including nasogastric tube feeding, liver protection therapy, component transfusion, oral methylprednisolone, and whole lung lavage therapy about every half a year. It was partial improvement by comparison of HRCT on chest before and after each WLL therapy each 6 months (Figure S5). However, he had recurrent hospitalizations for severe pneumonia, and eventually died due to exacerbation at the age of three.

For Patient 2, he received combined anti-infective, respiratory support (high-flow nasal cannula), conservative management of abnormal liver function and cholestasis, nasogastric tube feeding, vitamin K1 therapy and folic acid supplementation for treatment of haematological disorders. However, he died due to respiratory failure caused by severe pneumonia complicated with severe sepsis at 4 months of age.

The Patient 3 received combined anti-infective, anti-inflammatory (methylprednisolone and hydroxychloroquine), respiratory support (invasive and non-invasive mechanical ventilation) and liver protection therapy. She kept relatively stable with regular WLL companied with subcutaneous and aerosolized granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor treatment with regular follow-up. Regardless of respiratory infections, her HRCT always indicated the presence of bilateral diffuse pulmonary interstitial infiltrates and PAP. And she suffered from tachypnoea in the night sometimes and would have had shortness of breath and dyspnea requiring nasal cannula oxygen therapy during respiratory infection.

A scheduled follow-up HRCT of the chest was obtained at 3- to 6-month intervals for Patient 4. From first presentation, the HRCT consistently exhibited diffuse grid shadows and GGO. However, during episodes of respiratory infection, new patchy exudative changes were observed. And he survived with exercise intolerance, clubbing, and severe malnutrition with 2 years follow-up.

Discussion

In this study, we report four unrelated pediatric patients diagnosed with chILD/PAP accompanied by multisystem extrapulmonary manifestations, and a definitive genetic diagnosis was established for all cases following our standardized variant screening pipeline. Through this systematic process, biallelic IARS variants with high pathogenicity potential were identified as the sole genetic cause in each patient; WES further excluded any additional pathogenic variants that could account for the observed full phenotypic spectrum. All identified IARS variants were validated by Sanger sequencing and confirmed to be inherited in trans from the probands’ unaffected parents, consistent with a compound heterozygous genotype and an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. Notably, each patient harbored one truncating variant (including stop-gain, frameshift, and splice acceptor variants) in combination with one missense variant in IARS, and the clinical phenotypes of all patients were highly consistent with the phenotypic spectrum associated with IARS gene mutations. All eight variants identified across the four patients exhibited low allele frequencies in public population databases, and the missense variants were predicted to be damaging by at least two in silico pathogenicity prediction tools. With the exception of the P437L variant, all other variants are novel and have not been documented in public databases including HGMD, ClinVar, and UniProt. AlphaFold DB (version 2.2.0)-based protein structural modeling of these IARS variants further supported the probable loss or impairment of IARS protein function, as all variants were shown to either disrupt the intrinsic structural integrity of the IARS protein or alter the intermolecular interactions of adjacent amino acid residues.

The four patients were repeatedly hospitalized due to recurrent respiratory failure secondary to chILD/PAP especially during respiratory infections caused by bacteria, viruses, and even fungi. They all had multisystem involvements with dysfunctional lung, liver, hematopoiesis, gastrointestinal representations, and a part with brain/development, urological system and/or adrenal representations. The situation of multisystem involvements is similar to the reported patients with IARS mutations in previous studies. At present, there was a total of 15 children with mutations in IARS gene have been reported internationally, and most of them had multisystem involvements, including postnatal growth retardation, intellectual disability and liver dysfunction as the most frequent clinical presentations [4, 7, 8, 15, 18]. However, there was only a very brief description of their respiratory involvement, of whom 1 had recurrent respiratory tract infection [19], 3 deaths occurred early life with PAP changes of lung pathology [4, 15], and pulmonary phenotype was not referred to the others. In spite of the brief descriptions about pulmonary phenotype, clear establishment of the relationship between IARS mutations and pulmonary phenotype still remained elusive. Therefore, elucidating the pulmonary phenotype associated with IARS mutations is crucial, as it represents the most significant factor influencing the prognosis of infants and children. This is underscored by the significant correlation between PAP and mortality prognosis [18]. In our study, the pulmonary phenotype was the main manifestation of the four children reported, and one of them died of respiratory failure in infancy. Three patients (Patient 1, 2 and 4) had early infantile onset multisystem disease and two (Patient 1 and 2) died within 3 years due to the aggravation of PAP despite of aggressive treatment, the other is currently alive with chILD. But, interestingly, another girl (Patient 3) had childhood-onset PAP and was in relatively stable condition under active supportive treatment, which may be related to the two missense variants in IARS that she carried. It suggests clinical phenotype differences may be associated with genetic heterogeneity as well as different effect of different mutation sites and types on aminoacylation activity of IARS, which indicates a different prognosis. Therefore, further works remain necessary to assay the aminoacylation activity of IARS of the four patients, despite the decreased protein expression of IARS in BALF of the Patient 3 were described.

The appearance of BALF of patients with IARS mutations showed milk-like changes. And PAS staining showed a significant increase in mass like purplish red staining positive substances inside and outside the cells, suggesting a large amount of abnormal deposition of phospholipid rich proteins in the alveolar cavity and alveolar macrophage (AM) cells. At the same time, combined with the positive-ORO staining of cells, it suggested the excessive deposition of triglycerides and other neutral lipids in AM. It suggested that a large number of PS protein and lipid components are abnormally deposited in the alveolar cavity. It is consistent with the BALF pathological staining results of children, including congenital PAP caused by gene mutations such as ABCA3, SFTPB and SFTPC [20–24]. Electron microscopy showed that a large number of secondary lysosome formation, vacuolization and myeloid body formation could be seen in macrophages, and a large number of digesting proteins like substances could be seen in cells, and lamellar body like structural substances released from disintegrated type II epithelial cells could be seen outside cells [25], which was consistent with the performance of PAP.

The detection of PS related proteins in the concentrated BALF of Patient 3 showed that the expression of each component was abnormal, in which the expression of SP-B precursor protein was significantly reduced, while the SP-C precursor protein showed abnormal shear precursor bands with molecular weights of about 33KD and 18kD, and the 15kD precursor was not expressed, suggesting that the shear and processing of SP-C may be abnormal, and the expression level of SP-A and SP-D proteins increased.

SP-C needs to undergo a series of rigorous proteolysis processes through 197kd precursor protein to form mature SP-C protein to function. A variety of proSP-C shear precursors can be detected in BALF of normal people, mainly 21kD precursor protein, and 15kD and 25kd precursor protein [26]. The detection results of children with IARS gene mutations in this study suggest that SP-C precursor protein has abnormal shearing and processing, which is similar to the abnormal processing of SP-C precursor protein in most children with SFTPC gene mutations reported in clinic [22, 27], while SFTPB gene mutations usually lead to the maturation and processing obstacles and reduced expression of SP-B protein, resulting in fatal respiratory failure immediately after birth, and also affect the processing of SP-C precursor protein [28, 29], It may be related to their close interaction as hydrophobic proteins in the process of PS synthesis and extracellular participation in maintaining the stability of monolayer phospholipid membrane. This study makes a significant contribution to the understanding of monogenic causes of chILD. The discovery of IARS as a novel disease-associated gene not only expands the genetic landscape of chILD/PAP but also provides potential diagnostic and therapeutic implications. Genetic screening for IARS variants should be considered in pediatric patients with unexplained respiratory distress, especially those with concurrent multisystem involvement. Furthermore, our findings open avenues for future therapeutic research, including targeted interventions to correct surfactant dysregulation.

The scRNA-seq data from Patient 4 indicates a disruption in lysosomal pathways within monocytes and a specific depletion of CD14 + CD16+ intermediate monocytes, which are critical precursors for alveolar macrophage replenishment. Macrophages are associated with lipid storage, removal of apoptotic, damaged or unfit cells, wrapped pathogen, and recycling factories that maintain tissue function [30–32]. In addition, monocyte derived alveolar macrophages are involved in lung fibrosis, injury and inflammation [33, 34]. These processes closely linked to the pathogenesis of chILD/PAP. In our study, alveolar macrophages from patients exhibited abnormal lipid accumulation, secondary lysosome formation, and vacuolization, paired with surfactant protein dysregulation in BALF. These clinical and cytological observations, combined with the transcriptional alterations in PBMC-derived monocytes, suggest a potential association between IARS deficiency and monocyte/macrophage lineage dysfunction. However, these findings remain correlative at present, and the direct causal relationship between IARS mutations, monocyte/macrophage dysfunction, and surfactant metabolism disorders has not yet been fully established.

Notably, the observed immune abnormalities could be either intrinsic to macrophages or secondary to broader cellular disruptions induced by IARS deficiency. As ARSs play essential roles in multiple cellular processes—including protein synthesis, metabolism, and signaling—it is plausible that the transcriptional changes in monocytes reflect indirect effects of global cellular dysfunction rather than direct impairment of immune cell-specific pathways. To resolve this uncertainty and validate the proposed mechanistic links, additional functional studies are imperative. Specifically, the establishment of patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) models, differentiated into functional alveolar macrophages in vitro, will enable direct assessment of whether IARS mutations intrinsically impair macrophage phagocytic capacity, surfactant clearance, and cytokine secretion. Complementarily, the construction of IARS transgenic mouse models (knockout or knock-in) will allow for in vivo verification of monocyte differentiation defects, alveolar macrophage dysfunction, and resultant surfactant accumulation, while controlling for confounding factors inherent to clinical samples. These models will also facilitate investigation of whether IARS deficiency alters systemic metabolic pathways, mitochondrial function, or inflammatory responses—alternative explanations for the observed immune phenotypes.

A key limitation of our study is the lack of such functional validation models, which restricts the definitive interpretation of the scRNA-seq findings. While our data provide preliminary clues into potential immune-related pathogenic mechanisms, the absence of direct experimental evidence linking IARS deficiency to macrophage dysfunction limits the causal inferences that can be drawn. Future work should integrate these model systems with transcriptomic, proteomic, and functional assays (e.g., phagocytosis assays, surfactant clearance tests, cytokine profiling) to confirm whether IARS mutations directly disrupt monocyte/macrophage function or exert indirect effects through broader cellular perturbations. Such approaches will be crucial for confirming the proposed disease mechanism and identifying potential therapeutic targets for IARS-related chILD/PAP.

Conclusions

Our study characterizes four pediatric patients with compound heterozygous IARS variants, establishing chILD/PAP as core pulmonary manifestations of IARS-related disorder and broadening the phenotypic spectrum of biallelic IARS mutations. Our findings indicate an association between IARS variants and immune dysfunction, yet it remains uncertain whether this dysfunction is a direct consequence of IARS mutations or a secondary effect of broader cellular dysregulation, necessitating further experimental validation in cell and animal models to confirm causality. Collectively, our results underscore the need for IARS genetic screening in pediatric patients with unexplained respiratory failure and multisystem involvement, providing critical insights into the phenotypic diversity of IARS-related disorder and laying the groundwork for future targeted therapies to improve clinical outcomes.
