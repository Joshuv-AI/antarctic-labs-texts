Influence of ultrasonic pre-treatment on drying behaviour, energy performance, and quality characteristics of capia pepper cubes under various drying methods
Authors: Ahmet Polat, Nazmi Izli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Capia pepper, Colour, Drying methods, Energy analysis, Ultrasound pre-treatment

Introduction

Capia pepper, belonging to the genus Capsicum, is widely cultivated worldwide, with Capsicum annuum L. being the most commercially important species. Owing to its intense red pigmentation and rich nutritional composition, including provitamin A and vitamins C, E, B1, B2, and B3, it is widely used in the food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries. Despite its nutritional value, fresh pepper contains approximately 90 % moisture, making it highly susceptible to post-harvest deterioration. Therefore, drying is widely employed to extend shelf life while preserving product quality and ensuring year-round availability. Dried peppers are extensively used as natural colourants and flavouring agents in soups, bakery products, spice blends, condiments, and sauces [1], [2].

Various drying technologies have been applied to reduce the moisture content of agricultural products while maintaining product quality. Freeze drying effectively preserves product structure and quality through low-temperature sublimation but is one of the most energy-intensive drying methods [3]. Electrohydrodynamic (EHD) drying is a non-thermal technique in which ionic wind enhances mass transfer and accelerates evaporation, making it particularly suitable for heat-sensitive products. However, its effectiveness decreases as moisture content declines; therefore, hybrid EHD systems have been proposed to improve overall drying performance [4], [5]. Conventional hot-air drying is widely used because of its simple design and low cost, but is associated with long drying times, high energy consumption, and quality deterioration [6]. Microwave drying offers rapid heating, shorter drying times, and improved energy efficiency, although non-uniform heating may occur. Consequently, microwave energy is often combined with hot-air drying to improve heating uniformity and drying performance [7].

In addition to selecting appropriate drying techniques, the application of pre-treatments has become an important strategy to improve drying efficiency and product quality. Pre-treatments can modify the internal structure of biological materials and thereby facilitate moisture migration during drying. Among these techniques, ultrasound technology has attracted considerable attention. The effectiveness of ultrasonic pre-treatment is primarily attributed to acoustic cavitation, which involves the formation, growth, and collapse of microscopic bubbles in a liquid medium. The collapse of these bubbles generates localized microjets, shock waves, and shear forces that disrupt cell walls and increase membrane permeability. In addition, acoustic streaming and microstreaming enhance liquid circulation around the sample surface, facilitating mass transfer between the surrounding medium and the plant tissue. These physical phenomena promote the formation of microscopic channels within the cellular structure, reduce internal moisture diffusion resistance, and facilitate water migration during subsequent drying [8]. Since cavitation behaviour depends strongly on ultrasonic operating conditions, particularly frequency, power, and treatment duration, different ultrasonic frequencies may induce different degrees of structural modification and consequently influence the drying performance and quality attributes of agricultural products [9], [10]. Ultrasound can be applied either continuously during drying or as a pre-treatment prior to the drying process. In ultrasound-assisted drying, mechanical waves propagate through the air and the product, generating micro-agitation at the solid–fluid interface and reducing external mass transfer resistance. When ultrasound is applied as a pre-treatment, the product is immersed in a liquid medium, typically distilled water or an osmotic solution, and exposed to ultrasonic waves before drying. This treatment can induce structural modifications such as the formation of microscopic channels within the material, which reduces internal resistance and enhances moisture diffusion during subsequent drying processes [11]. Recent studies have demonstrated that ultrasonic pre-treatment can substantially improve drying performance by modifying the internal microstructure, promoting microchannel formation, reducing moisture diffusion resistance, and facilitating water migration. These structural modifications have been reported to shorten drying time and enhance product quality in various fruits and vegetables, including apple, pineapple, and blueberry, confirming the broad applicability of ultrasound as an effective non-thermal pre-treatment for drying processes [12], [13], [14].

Although numerous studies have investigated the drying characteristics of peppers using different drying technologies, most of these studies have focused on single drying methods or have examined limited quality parameters [15], [16]. Furthermore, comprehensive studies comparing multiple drying techniques together with ultrasound pre-treatment in terms of drying kinetics, energy performance, and product quality characteristics remain limited. In particular, there is a lack of systematic investigations evaluating the combined effects of ultrasound pre-treatment and advanced drying techniques such as electrohydrodynamic drying and hybrid microwave–hot air drying on the drying behaviour and quality attributes of capia pepper.

Therefore, the present study aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the drying performance of capia pepper cubes subjected to ultrasound pre-treatment and dried using electrohydrodynamic (EHD), hot-air (HA), freeze-drying (FD), microwave (MW), and microwave–hot air (MW–HA) methods. In this study, the effects of two different ultrasound frequencies (35 and 53 kHz) on drying kinetics, energy consumption, colour characteristics, rehydration capacity, microstructure and thin-layer drying behaviour were investigated. The novelty of this study lies in the systematic comparison of ultrasonic pre-treatment across five fundamentally different drying technologies under identical experimental conditions. Furthermore, drying kinetics, effective moisture diffusivity, energy performance, physical quality characteristics, and quantitative ImageJ-based microstructural analysis were integrated into a unified evaluation framework, providing a comprehensive understanding of the relationships among structural modification, mass transfer, product quality, and energy efficiency.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The capia pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) samples used in the experiments were purchased from a local retail supermarket in Bursa, Türkiye. According to the product label, the peppers were produced in the Kemalpaşa district of Bursa Province. Only commercially mature capia peppers with uniform size, colour, and without visible mechanical damage or physiological defects were selected for the experiments. The products were transferred into vacuum bags without damaging their structure using a manually adjusted vacuum packaging device. The products placed in vacuum bags were stored at 4 ± 0.5 °C until the experiments were completed. Capia pepper samples free from microbial spoilage and physical damage were selected for the experiments. After removing the seeds and internal tissues, the peppers were cut into cubes with approximate dimensions of 11.08 ± 0.11 mm × 11.11 ± 0.63 mm. For each drying experiment, 45 ± 0.01 g of sample was used. The initial moisture content of the samples was found to be 10.81 (d.b.) following oven drying at 70 °C for 24 h.

Experimental procedure

Drying experiments on products subjected to ultrasonic pre-treatment at different frequencies were conducted under laboratory conditions. A laboratory-type freeze dryer with an operating temperature of −50 °C and a constant pressure of 52 Pa in the drying chamber (Alpha 1–2 LD Plus, Osterode am Harz, Germany), A laboratory-scale microwave–hot air drying oven (Arçelik, Türkiye) was used for the microwave, microwave–hot air, and hot-air drying experiments with an air velocity of 1 m/s, whereas a laboratory-scale electrohydrodynamic-assisted hot-air drying system developed in the laboratory was used for the EHD–HA experiments. The EHD module consisted of six parallel wire electrodes (0.4 mm diameter) with an inter-electrode spacing of 50 mm. The distance between the wire electrodes and the grounded counter-electrode was 30 mm, corresponding to an average electric field strength of approximately 10 kV/cm at the applied voltage of 30 kV. The system was supplied from a 220 V AC mains input through a high-voltage power unit providing an adjustable negative DC output (−15 to − 30 kV) and was operated in a negative corona configuration, in which the wire electrodes were connected to the negative high-voltage output and the lower counter-electrode was grounded. During EHD drying, the air velocity and relative humidity were maintained at 1 m/s and approximately 50 %, respectively. For the drying processes, hot air at 60 °C, microwave power at 300 W, and EHD voltage at 30 kV were selected. Thus, the following five different experimental protocols were applied for the drying process:

(1) Freeze Drying (FD, −50 °C, 52 Pa)

(2) Electrohydrodynamic-Hot Air Drying (EHD-HA, 30 kV-60 °C)

(3) Hot Air Drying (HA, 60 °C)

(4) Microwave Drying (MW, 300 W)

(5) Microwave-Hot Air Drying (MW-HA, 300 W-60 °C).

Ultrasonic pre-treatment was applied to the products to be dried in the experiments and compared with control samples. Ultrasonic pre-treatment was performed using a dual-frequency ultrasonic bath (KUDOS, Shanghai Kudos Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., China), operating at switchable frequencies of 35 and 53 kHz with a nominal ultrasonic power of 90 W, and the bath was operated at 100 % power throughout all experiments. The selected ultrasonic frequencies (35 and 53 kHz) were chosen based on previous studies reporting effective ultrasonic pre-treatment within this frequency range for food materials [17], [18]. The objective of this study was not to optimize the ultrasonic operating conditions but to evaluate the isolated effect of ultrasonic frequency on the subsequent drying behaviour of capia pepper cubes. The ultrasonic bath, with a total capacity of 3 L, was filled with approximately 1.35 ± 0.01 L of distilled water at an initial temperature of 20 ± 1 °C. No active temperature control or external cooling was applied during sonication because of the relatively short treatment time (10 min). Samples (45 g) were treated for 10 min using a liquid-to-solid ratio of 1:3 (w/v). No active temperature control or external cooling was applied during sonication because of the relatively short treatment time (10 min). The water temperature was recorded before treatment; however, the temperature increase during sonication was not measured and is acknowledged as a limitation of the present study. To ensure that ultrasonic frequency remained the only independent pre-treatment variable, ultrasonic power (90 W), treatment duration, initial water temperature, sample loading, and liquid-to-solid ratio were kept constant throughout the experiments. Control samples were immersed in distilled water under identical conditions, but without ultrasound exposure. After pre-treatment, all samples were gently blotted with absorbent paper to remove excess surface water, weighed, and their initial moisture content was determined prior to drying. These procedures were performed in triplicate. For each drying test, the moisture loss in the sample was measured with a precision of ± 0.01 g during the drying process until a final moisture content of 0.1 g water/g dry matter (dry basis) was reached: 60 min for freeze-drying, 10 min for the electrohydrodynamic method, 20 min for the hot air method, and 3 min for the microwave and microwave-hot air methods. The experiments were performed in triplicate. The drying kinetics of the capia pepper products were compared. The drying parameters applied were determined based on the results of the experiments performed for each product. The experiments performed are given in Fig. 1.

Determination of colour parameters

Rehydration properties

In the studies to determine rehydration capacity, dried capia pepper samples were left to stand for 14 h at room temperature in a beaker filled with distilled water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:50. To avoid any ambiguity, we clarified in the manuscript that 14 h was a fixed experimental period used for comparison across treatments. [32]. At the end of this process, the rehydrated samples were removed from the beaker, excess water was removed using filter paper, and they were weighed on a precision balance. The experiment was conducted on both the control group and the dried capia samples that had undergone ultrasonic pre-treatment. Each experiment was carried out in triplicate. Microstructure evaluation

The microstructural changes in capia pepper cubes subjected to different drying methods, with and without ultrasonic pre-treatment, were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (EVO 40, Germany). For SEM analysis, dried samples were carefully sectioned from the same region using a scalpel and mounted on double-sided adhesive stubs. The prepared samples were subsequently coated with a gold–palladium alloy (60/40) under vacuum conditions and then placed in the SEM chamber to observe and evaluate their microstructural characteristics. Quantitative image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). SEM images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, and identical threshold settings were applied to all images before binary conversion. Apparent porosity (%) was determined using the Area Fraction function as the ratio of pore area to the total analysed area after excluding the scale bar and image annotation regions.

Determination of energy consumption and drying efficiency

The energy consumption (EC) values of capia pepper samples dried using different drying methods, both with and without ultrasonic pre-treatment, were determined using a digital power meter (EU TS-836A; Floureon, China; accuracy: ±1 %) connected between the power supply and each drying system [34]. For ultrasonically pre-treated samples, the total energy consumption included both the electrical energy used during ultrasonic pre-treatment and the energy consumed by the subsequent drying process. Energy measurements were based on the operational power consumption of each drying system and did not include preheating or standby energy consumption. For the freeze-drying system, the measured energy consumption included the operation of the vacuum pump and condenser. Experimental data were recorded using Microsoft Excel 2016. Statistical analyses of the obtained data, except for modelling procedures, were performed using JMP Statistical Discovery Software (Version 7.0, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Results and discussion

Drying curves of capia pepper cubes

Fig. 2 shows the time-dependent changes in moisture content during the drying process for capia pepper samples that have undergone pre-treatment at two different ultrasonic frequencies (35 and 53 kHz). In all drying trials, the longest drying time was observed in the untreated (control) samples. A reduction in drying times was observed as a result of the pre-treatment. In the EHD-HA method, whilst the drying time under untreated conditions was 140 min, the application of ultrasonic pre-treatment reduced the drying time in the US35 and US53 samples by 14.29 % and 28.57 %, respectively. Under other drying conditions—freeze-drying (FD), hot air (HA), microwave (MW) and microwave-hot air (MW-HA)—drying times for untreated samples were determined to be 480, 360, 39 and 33 min, respectively, with the application of US35 and US53 ultrasonic pre-treatment, these times were reduced to 360–300, 300–260, 33–30 and 30–27 min, respectively. Consequently, a reduction in drying time was achieved at rates of 25.00–37.50 %, 16.67–27.78 %, 15.38–23.08 %, and 9.09–18.18 %, respectively. No constant-rate drying period was observed under any drying condition, and moisture removal occurred predominantly during the falling-rate period, indicating that internal moisture diffusion governed the drying process. As can be seen from the results, the longest drying time was obtained with the freeze-drying method; this was followed, in order, by the hot air, EHD–hot air, microwave, and microwave–hot air methods. This trend was consistently observed not only in the control samples but also in the samples treated with US35 and US53 ultrasonic pre-treatment. The freeze-drying process consists of four main stages: freezing, vacuum, sublimation, and condensation; in particular, the considerable duration of the sublimation stage has resulted in this method having a longer total drying time compared to other drying techniques [36]. In contrast, in the microwave–hot air method, the microwaves induce a bipolar state in the water molecules within the product, leading to volumetric heating; this causes the internal temperature and internal vapour pressure to rise rapidly, resulting in the removal of moisture in a shorter time and a significant reduction in drying time compared to other methods [37]. Ultrasonic pre-treatment has significantly reduced drying time in both freeze-drying and thermal drying methods. This effect can be attributed to the microstructural changes caused by ultrasonic cavitation. The fine pores and microchannels formed as a result of cavitation have facilitated moisture removal by reducing mass transfer resistance within the product. In freeze-drying, this structural improvement has contributed to a shorter primary drying time by accelerating vapour diffusion during the sublimation stage [38]. In thermal drying methods, ultrasonic pre-treatment accelerated moisture transport by increasing molecular mobility and internal vapour pressure, thereby enabling higher mass transfer rates to be achieved. Accordingly, ultrasonic pre-treatment not only shortened the overall drying time but also enhanced moisture removal by promoting mass transfer and increasing the effective moisture diffusivity. This enhancement is commonly associated with cavitation-induced microchannels, the development of a sponge-like microstructure, and improved internal moisture migration. The influence of ultrasonic pre-treatment is particularly evident during the falling-rate period, where internal moisture diffusion becomes the dominant resistance to drying. Moreover, the extent of this improvement varied among the drying methods, reflecting the different heat and mass transfer mechanisms governing each drying technique [39]. These results demonstrate that ultrasonic pre-treatment is an effective pre-treatment technique that positively influences drying kinetics even in processes with different drying mechanisms [40]. Numerous studies in the literature have shown that ultrasonic pre-treatment significantly reduces drying time in various drying methods. Rybak et al. [41], in their study examining the freeze-drying of red bell peppers using both traditional and innovative pre-treatments, reported that ultrasonic pre-treatment significantly shortened the drying time. Similarly, Ni et al. [42] applied different pre-treatments to raisin samples and carried out drying using the electrohydrodynamic (EHD) method, finding that ultrasonic pre-treatment markedly reduced the EHD drying time. Abbaspour-Gilandeh et al. [37], in their study where they dried samples subjected to ultrasonic pre-treatment using hot air, microwave–hot air, infrared–hot air, and freeze-drying methods, reported that the longest drying time was observed in freeze-drying, whilst the shortest was achieved with the microwave–hot air method. Furthermore, they emphasised that the use of ultrasonic pre-treatment reduced drying times across all drying methods. Similarly, studies examining the effects of ultrasonic parameters have observed comparable trends. Yue et al. [43] demonstrated that increasing the frequency of ultrasonic pre-treatment reduced the drying time during the drying of Codonopsis pilosula (Franch.) Nannf slices. Similarly, Xu et al. [38] reported that samples processed at 40 kHz in single-frequency ultrasonic applications dried in a shorter time compared to those treated at 20 kHz. All these studies clearly demonstrate the accelerating effect of ultrasonic pre-treatment on drying kinetics, and the findings obtained for different products and drying methods show strong consistency with the results observed in our study.

In this regard, the experimental MR–time data were fitted to thin-layer drying models commonly used in the literature using non-linear regression analysis. In all drying techniques examined, statistical indicators revealed a strong correlation between experimental and predicted moisture content values. The variation of R2, χ2, and RMSE values within narrow ranges indicates that the applied models are generally adequate in representing the drying behaviour of the samples. However, when comparing the overall fit performance of the models under different drying conditions, significant differences were observed between the models. Across all drying methods, the Midilli et al. model yielded the highest R2 (0.9983–0.9998) and lowest χ2 (3.5988 × 10−4–0.0865 × 10−4) and RMSE (0.0043–0.0159) values, independent of the ultrasonic pre-treatment frequency. These results demonstrate that the Midilli model successfully represents the internal moisture diffusion and external mass transfer mechanisms governing the drying process. The superior performance of the Midilli model can be attributed to its semi-empirical structure, which includes both exponential and linear terms. This structure allows the model to adapt flexibly to different drying regimes. This feature is particularly important in this study, where drying technologies with different heat and mass transfer mechanisms are used. Furthermore, the fact that the Midilli model yields consistent results across all drying methods indicates that ultrasonic pre-treatment does not alter the fundamental drying mechanism; instead, it increases moisture transport rates, and this effect is successfully reflected in the model parameters.

A comparison of the moisture content values obtained experimentally with those predicted by the Midilli model as a function of drying time is presented in Fig. 3. It can be seen that the predicted MR curves closely follow the experimental data throughout the drying time for all drying methods and ultrasonic pre-treatment conditions. This high agreement between experimental and simulation results confirms the adequacy of the Midilli model in representing the thin-layer drying behaviour of the samples. These findings are also consistent with previous studies in the literature. Yilmaz et al. [44] reported that the Midilli model best represented the moisture content data in apricot samples dried using tray, microwave, and freeze-drying methods with different pre-treatments. Similarly, Akhoundzadeh Yamchi et al. [45] stated that the Midilli model provided the highest agreement with experimental data in peach slices pre-treated with ultrasound and dried in a cabinet-type dryer at 65 °C. Krzykowski et al. [46] showed that the Midilli model successfully represented the drying kinetics throughout the entire experimental range in pepper samples dried using convective, convective-microwave, vacuum, and freeze-drying methods. Furthermore, Arslan et al. [47] identified the Midilli model as the most suitable model for drying organically and conventionally produced sweet red peppers with hot air.

Statistical performance indicators and graphical validation results consistently indicated that the Midilli et al. model was among the most suitable thin-layer drying models for ultrasonically pre-treated samples dried under EHD-HA, FD, HA, MW, and MW-HA conditions, as it repeatedly provided superior agreement with experimental moisture-ratio data and maintained low prediction error across diverse drying environments. Effective moisture diffusivity of capia pepper cubes

The effective moisture diffusivity (Deff) values obtained under different drying methods and ultrasonic pre-treatment conditions are presented in Fig. 4. In the control samples, the effective diffusion coefficients were, in order, FD-C (1.865 x 10-8), HA-C (2.613 x 10-8), EHD-HA-C (5.976 x 10-8), MW-HA-C (2.016 x 10-7) and MW-C (2.405 x 10-7), respectively. Among these values, the lowest effective diffusion coefficient was observed in the FD group, whilst the highest value was found in the MW group. This result demonstrates that microwave-based drying accelerates internal moisture transfer. The findings indicate that the drying method applied and the pre-treatment conditions have a significant effect on the effective diffusion coefficient. In general, higher diffusion coefficients were obtained in the microwave (MW) and microwave-hot air (MW-HA) treatments, whilst the lowest values were observed in the freeze-drying (FD) group. This suggests that moisture transport occurs more efficiently during drying in the MW and MW-HA treatments [50], [51], [52]. It is observed that ultrasonic pre-treatment (US35 and US53) increases the diffusion coefficient in all drying methods. For example, in the FD group, the values rose to 2.323 x 10-8 and 2.809 x 10-8; in the HA group, to 2.937 x 10-8 and 3.260 x 10-8; and in the MW group to 2.707 x 10-7 and 2.948 x 10-7. A similar trend was observed in the MW-HA and EHD-HA groups. This increase suggests that the changes in tissue structure caused by the ultrasonic pre-treatment may have facilitated moisture diffusion [53], [54]. Consequently, the MW and MW-HA treatments yielded the highest effective diffusion coefficients, whilst the FD treatment yielded the lowest values. Ultrasonic pre-treatment, meanwhile, positively influenced the drying process by increasing the diffusion coefficient in all groups. This behaviour is in agreement with the quantitative ImageJ porosity analysis (Fig. 4), which demonstrated that ultrasonic pre-treatment generally promoted the formation of a more porous microstructure, thereby facilitating internal moisture transport during drying. These findings demonstrate that both the choice of method and pre-treatments are important in improving drying performance [55]. The enhanced effective moisture diffusivity observed in ultrasonically pre-treated samples was further supported by SEM micrographs, which demonstrated microstructural modifications characterized by increased porosity and the development of moisture transport pathways. These structural alterations likely contributed to the reduction of internal mass transfer resistance during drying [56].

Colour evaluation

One of the quality criteria consumers first evaluate in a product is surface colour, and this parameter plays a decisive role in the product's acceptance or rejection. Consumer decisions are largely based on colour, as colour can be directly associated with other quality elements such as sensory and nutritional characteristics, as well as visual or non-visual defects.

The colour values of dried capia peppers, dried using various methods by applying different control and ultrasonic pre-treatment frequencies, are shown in Table 7. When the highest L* values were examined, they were determined to be 35.466 ± 0.198 in fresh samples, and a general decrease in L* values occurred due to the effects of drying and ultrasonic application. The L* values closest to the fresh product were obtained in the FD-C and FD-US53 applications, while the lowest L* values were detected in the MW-C and MW-HA-US35 samples. While the ultrasonic application in the EHD method had no statistically significant effect on the L* value, it was determined that the L* values were higher in the microwave method. When compared to the a* value of fresh capia peppers, a significant decrease (P < 0.05) in the red colour component (a*) was observed in all drying methods. However, the level of this decrease varied depending on the drying method used. The a* values of samples dried using the EHD-HA and hot air methods were found to be closest to the fresh product. While no statistically significant effect of ultrasonic pre-treatment on the a* value was detected in samples dried with EHD-HA (P > 0.05), ultrasonic application in other drying methods caused increases or decreases in a* values depending on the drying technique. Furthermore, while an increase in ultrasonic frequency did not cause a significant change in the red colour component in EHD-HA and hot air methods, it contributed to the preservation of red colour in freeze-drying and microwave methods; conversely, it caused a noticeable colour loss in the microwave–hot air combination.

The Chroma value, which indicates colour saturation, increases as colour intensity increases [57]. The highest Chroma value was determined in the fresh sample, and a general decrease was observed due to the effects of drying and ultrasonic treatment. However, ultrasonic pre-treatment in microwave, hot air, and freeze-drying methods resulted in higher Chroma values. The Chroma values of samples dried using microwave and microwave-hot air methods were found to be lower compared to EHD-HA, freeze-drying, and hot air methods. The hue angle, which characterises the tone of the colour, was highest in the MW-US35 application and lowest in the samples dried using the hot air method. No statistically significant difference in hue value was found between the fresh sample and the HA-US35, HA-US53, and EHD-HA-US35 applications (P < 0.05). In contrast, the hue angles of samples dried using microwave, microwave-hot air, and freeze-drying methods were found to be higher than those of the fresh product.

Total colour difference (ΔE) is an important parameter that reveals the overall colour change in dried samples compared to fresh capia, and a low ΔE value is expected in high-quality dried products [58]. ΔE values were found to be higher in the microwave and microwave-hot air methods compared to other methods, indicating that colour change was more pronounced in these methods. The lowest ΔE values were determined in the ultrasonically pre-treated and untreated EHD-HA, HA-US53, and FD-US53 applications.

The colour results should also be interpreted together with the drying kinetics and microstructural findings. Drying methods that produced shorter drying times and higher porosity, particularly after ultrasonic pre-treatment, generally exhibited better colour retention. The enhanced moisture transport associated with the formation of microchannels reduced the exposure time of the samples to thermal degradation, thereby limiting pigment deterioration. These observations are consistent with the effective moisture diffusivity, rehydration, and ImageJ porosity analyses, demonstrating that the structural modifications induced by ultrasonic pre-treatment contributed simultaneously to improved drying efficiency and preservation of product quality [54], [59].

When reviewing the literature findings, consistent with our results, a study by Sharangi et al. [60] found that drying red pepper samples by sun, solar, and oven drying at different temperatures resulted in significant reductions in the L*, a*, and b* colour values. Furthermore, Krzykowski et al. [46] stated that samples obtained by freeze-drying and vacuum drying methods had higher brightness values compared to convective and convective-microwave methods. This finding supports the notion that drying applications carried out under low-temperature conditions are more advantageous in terms of colour preservation. Similar findings were reported by Pinar et al. [61] in a study examining the effects of different drying methods on colour parameters in two different pepper varieties.

Rehydration capacity

The rehydration process cannot be considered a simple reversal of the drying process. Irreversible structural changes occurring in the food material during drying limit the samples' ability to reabsorb water and may prevent the dried product from fully reaching the moisture content of its fresh state. Therefore, rehydration capacity is directly related to the degree of cellular and structural damage caused during the drying process [62]. The rehydration values of capia pepper samples dried using different drying methods, with and without ultrasonic pre-treatment, are presented in Fig. 5. The highest rehydration rates were determined in samples dried with the FD-US35 and FD-US53 applications, while the lowest rehydration value was obtained in the control (HA-C) samples dried with hot air. The rehydration capacities of samples dried using the EHD-HA and FD methods were generally higher than those of samples dried using the HA, MW-HA, and MW methods under the same conditions. The highly porous structure formed as a result of the sublimation of moisture present in the food during freeze-drying, as clearly seen in the scanning electron microscope images given in Fig. 6, ensured high rehydration rates in the capia. In contrast, the lowest rehydration rate was observed in hot air drying due to the significant structural changes in the food structure and low porosity associated with the long drying time. Similarly, Abbaspour-Gilandeh et al. [37] reported in their study, where they dried hawthorn fruit using different drying methods after ultrasonic pre-treatment, that the highest rehydration values were obtained in freeze-dried samples, followed by microwave-hot air and hot air methods. Zhang et al. [63], in their study where they dried golden pompano using different methods, showed that the highest rehydration values were obtained in freeze-drying, while the lowest were obtained in hot air drying. Although conducted on different product groups, these findings are consistent with the results of the present study. Ultrasonic pre-treatment generally increased the rehydration capacity of capia pepper samples dried using methods other than the EHD-HA method. Ricce et al. [11] reported that the rehydration capacity of samples subjected to ultrasonic pre-treatment for different durations was higher than that of untreated samples. This can be attributed to the increased porosity and formation of microscopic channels due to ultrasonic application, allowing water to penetrate the product structure more easily during rehydration. Similar results have been reported by Salehi et al. [64] for microwave drying, Su et al. [65] for microwave-hot air drying, and Zhang et al. [66] for freeze-drying methods. The effect of increasing ultrasonic frequency on rehydration capacity varied depending on the drying method applied. While no statistically significant change in rehydration values was observed with increasing ultrasonic frequency in the FD and MW methods (P > 0.05), a decrease in rehydration capacity occurred in samples dried with the EHD-HA and MW-HA methods, and an increase occurred in samples dried with the HA method (P < 0.05). The different response of rehydration capacity to ultrasonic frequency among the drying methods may be attributed to the interaction between ultrasonic pre-treatment and the specific heat and mass transfer characteristics of each drying technique. Ultrasonic pre-treatment has been reported to enhance water diffusion and mass transfer through cavitation-induced microstructural modifications, thereby influencing the internal structure of the product. Accordingly, the improvement in rehydration observed with increasing ultrasonic frequency during hot-air drying may be associated with the formation of a more favourable porous structure that facilitates water uptake. In contrast, under high-energy drying methods such as EHD-HA and MW-HA, the combined effects of ultrasonic pre-treatment and intensive energy input may have promoted greater structural collapse or cellular disruption, thereby reducing the water absorption capacity during rehydration. These findings indicate that the influence of ultrasonic frequency on rehydration is highly dependent on the drying mechanism employed and highlight the importance of optimizing ultrasonic pre-treatment conditions for each drying method [38], [67] Contrary to these findings, Fijalkowska et al. [68] reported that increasing the frequency had no statistically significant effect on rehydration capacity in apple samples dried with hot air after 21 and 35 kHz ultrasonic pre-treatment (P > 0.05). This discrepancy is thought to stem from processing parameters such as the applied ultrasonic frequency and pre-treatment duration.

Microstructure of dried capia pepper cubes

Fig. 6 presents scanning electron microscope (SEM) images, while the quantitative porosity values obtained by ImageJ analysis are presented in Fig. 4, for dried capia samples with and without ultrasonic pre-treatment using different drying methods. It was observed that the internal structure of the capia pepper samples dried using the EHD-HA and freeze-drying methods contained more voids compared to other drying methods and exhibited a more uniform morphology. In contrast, it was determined that the cellular voids in the control samples dried with hot air were largely closed, while the intracellular voids in the samples obtained with other drying methods remained at a limited level. The quantitative porosity analysis supported these observations. The highest porosity value was obtained in the FD-US35 treatment (30.898 %), followed by FD-US53 (28.430 %), EHD-HA-C (25.874 %), and FD-C (25.874 %). In contrast, the lowest porosity was observed in the HA-C samples (2.144 %), confirming the compact microstructure produced by conventional hot-air drying. It was found that samples dried with US35 and US53 ultrasonic pre-treatments largely retained the porous and regular structure obtained with the freeze-drying method, while the porosity of samples dried with microwaves was lower compared to other methods. Compared with their respective control samples, ultrasonic pre-treatment generally increased the porosity of the dried samples. The increase was particularly evident in the HA method, where porosity increased from 2.144 % in the control to 12.007 % and 16.900 % after US35 and US53 pre-treatments, respectively. Similarly, porosity increased from 10.895 % to 20.763 % and 18.063 % in MW-HA samples, from 25.874 % to 30.898 % and 28.430 % in freeze-dried samples, and from 15.169 % to 16.405 % and 16.908 % in microwave-dried samples. In contrast, ultrasonic pre-treatment caused only minor changes in the EHD-HA samples, with porosity values of 25.874 %, 22.242 %, and 20.763 % for the control, US35, and US53 treatments, respectively. These findings are consistent with the results reported in the literature. Deng et al. [69], in their study examining the microstructure of red pepper dried using different drying methods, reported that samples dried with hot air exhibited a less porous structure. Similarly, Hawlader et al. [70] reported that freeze-dried papaya samples had the highest porosity in their study, where papaya and guava samples were dried using hot air heat pump drying, vacuum drying, and freeze-drying methods. In a study conducted by An et al. [71] on edamame samples, it was reported that samples dried with hot air exhibited a more compact structure. The results of these studies support the findings of the present study. In samples dried using the EHD-HA method, it was determined that the effect of ultrasonic pre-treatment on the microstructure was limited and that they exhibited similar structural characteristics to the control samples. In contrast, in samples dried using drying methods other than EHD-HA, it was found that the amount of cellular voids in samples subjected to ultrasonic pre-treatment was higher than in the control samples. The quantitative ImageJ analysis further confirmed that ultrasonic pre-treatment generally promoted the development of a more porous microstructure, although the magnitude of this effect depended on the drying method employed. The sponging and cavitation effects of ultrasound contribute to the formation of intercellular microchannels and the development of a more porous structure [72]. Nian et al. [73] reported similar microstructural changes in their studies on hot peppers, Li et al. [74] on kidney beans, and Lučić et al. [75] on peppers. Furthermore, Nowacka and Wedzik [76], in their study examining the effects of ultrasonic treatment on carrot tissue, noted that samples sonicated at high frequencies had larger cellular voids compared to samples treated at low frequencies. This finding is consistent with the results of our study. The increase in porosity observed after ultrasonic pre-treatment is also consistent with the enhanced rehydration behaviour and shorter drying times reported in the present study, indicating that the formation of interconnected pore networks reduced internal moisture transfer resistance during drying and facilitated water absorption during rehydration. In conclusion, SEM analyses together with quantitative porosity measurements reveal that ultrasonic pre-treatment facilitates moisture transport by creating well-developed microchannels and a more porous structure; as a result, rehydration capacity increases and drying time is reduced.

Energy consumption and drying efficiency

The parameters relating to the energy efficiency of the equipment and processes used in the drying of capia peppers are presented in Table 8. According to the results obtained, the highest energy consumption was observed in the freeze-drying method. The primary reason for the high energy consumption in the freeze-drying method is that the process consists of a multi-stage procedure requiring significant energy, such as the freezing of the food, the removal of the ice phase via sublimation, the condensation of the resulting water vapour, and the maintenance of vacuum conditions within the system [77]. The lowest energy consumption values, however, were observed in the microwave and microwave–hot air methods. This can be explained by the fact that hot air is effective in removing free water from the surface, whilst microwaves, through their internal heating mechanism, facilitate the removal of bound water from the inner layers, thereby accelerating the transport of moisture from the interior to the surface [78]. Within the scope of this study, the energy consumption values of samples dried using the hot air method were found to be lower than those of samples dried using the EHD-HA method. In the literature, it is expected that the EHD-HA method would exhibit lower energy consumption under the same temperature conditions [79]. However, the fact that the EHD-HA drying system used in this study is an open system and has a different design from the hot-air drying apparatus has led to this difference. Therefore, a direct comparison of these two methods should be regarded as a limited assessment. As expected, the energy consumption values of samples dried using hot air were found to be higher compared to the microwave and microwave–hot air methods. It was determined that ultrasonic pre-treatment had a statistically significant effect on energy consumption in freeze-drying and the EHD-HA methods (P < 0.05). In these methods, the application of ultrasound reduced energy consumption values. Whilst no statistically significant difference was observed between the energy consumption values of control samples and those dried using the US35 application for the hot-air method, it was determined that the US53 application reduced energy consumption values. The reduction in total energy consumption after ultrasonic pre-treatment is consistent with the enhanced effective moisture diffusivity and the more porous microstructure identified by ImageJ analysis, which collectively accelerated moisture removal and shortened the subsequent drying process.

Specific Energy Consumption (SEC) is a parameter expressing the amount of thermal energy used to dry 1 kg of product, and is employed to assess the energy efficiency of drying systems. Consequently, drying methods with high SEC values indicate low energy efficiency, suggesting that drying operations require improvement or a shift towards more efficient drying technologies [80]. In the study, the highest SEC value was obtained in the freeze-drying method for the control samples, whilst the lowest values were determined in both the control and ultrasonic pre-treated samples for the microwave and microwave–hot air methods. It was determined that ultrasonic pre-treatment reduced the SEC values of samples obtained using the EHD-HA and freeze-drying methods. Although the application of ultrasonic treatment in the microwave and microwave–hot air methods showed a tendency towards a decrease in SEC values, this change was not found to be statistically significant (P > 0.05).

The Specific Moisture Evaporation Rate (SMER) is another indicator used to assess the energy efficiency of the drying process, similar to the SEC parameter, and high SMER values indicate more efficient drying performance. It was determined that SMER values were at their lowest levels in capia pepper samples dried using the EHD-HA and freeze-drying methods with control and ultrasonic pre-treatment, followed by the hot-air drying method. The highest SMER values, however, were obtained using the microwave and microwave–hot air methods. No statistically significant effect of ultrasonic pre-treatment was observed on the SMER values of samples dried using the EHD-HA and freeze-drying methods (P > 0.05). In contrast, it was found that the application of US53 resulted in a significant increase in SMER values compared to the control samples in the hot air and microwave–hot air methods. The inverse relationship between SEC and SMER indicates that drying systems that remove moisture more rapidly consume less energy per unit of water evaporated; indeed, low SEC and high SMER have been reported simultaneously under microwave and microwave-hybrid drying conditions [81].

The moisture removal rate (MER) indicates the amount of water removed per unit time and is directly related to the drying time. Upon examination of the MER values, it was determined that the MER values of the control samples dried using the microwave and microwave–hot air methods were lower than those of the samples subjected to ultrasonic pre-treatment. In the EHD-HA and freeze-drying methods, no significant difference was observed between the MER values of the control samples and those treated with US35; however, it was determined that the MER values of samples treated with US53 were higher than those of the control samples. It was found that ultrasonic pre-treatment had no statistically significant effect on the MER values of samples dried using the hot-air drying method. Furthermore, it was determined that the MER values of samples dried using the microwave and microwave–hot-air methods were higher compared to those of other methods.

A review of studies in the literature reveals that the findings are generally consistent with the present study. Kusuma et al. [82], in their studies on the drying of moringa leaves, and Motevali et al. [83], in their studies on mushroom drying, reported that hot-air drying results in higher energy consumption than microwave drying. Jia et al. [84] noted in their studies that freeze-drying has a higher energy consumption compared to hot-air and microwave–hot-air methods. Rybak et al. [41] reported in their study on the freeze-drying of red peppers that ultrasonic pre-treatment reduced energy consumption. Baysal et al. [85], in their studies on the drying of apple slices using different methods, determined that the freeze-drying method had a higher SEC value than the microwave method, but lower SMER and MER values. Similarly, Altay et al. [86], in their studies on the drying of purple basil leaves using different drying methods, established the following relationships: for SEC values, freeze-drying > hot air > microwave; and for SMER and MER values, freeze-drying < hot air < microwave. The results of these studies are generally consistent with the findings obtained in the present study.

From an industrial perspective, the results indicate that the selection of the drying method should consider not only product quality but also processing time and energy efficiency. Microwave and microwave–hot air drying demonstrated the greatest potential for large-scale production owing to their short drying times and low energy requirements, whereas freeze-drying, despite producing superior product quality, may be less suitable for high-throughput industrial applications because of its long processing time and high energy consumption. Ultrasonic pre-treatment represents a promising strategy for industrial drying operations, as it can be readily integrated into existing processing lines to improve drying efficiency without substantial modifications to conventional drying systems.

Conclusions

In this study, the effects of ultrasonic pre-treatment applied to cubed capia peppers on drying behaviour, energy performance, physical quality characteristics, and drying kinetics under different drying methods were comprehensively evaluated. A major strength of this study is the systematic comparison of ultrasonic pre-treatment across five different drying methods under identical experimental conditions, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of drying performance, energy efficiency, physical quality characteristics, and microstructural changes. The results demonstrated that ultrasonic pre-treatment significantly reduced drying time by promoting microchannel and pore formation, thereby increasing effective moisture diffusivity and facilitating moisture transport. Among the drying methods, freeze drying exhibited the longest drying time, the highest energy consumption, and the highest SEC, whereas microwave and microwave–hot air drying achieved the shortest drying times, the highest SMER and MER values, and the best overall energy performance owing to volumetric heating. Drying generally reduced colour parameters; however, EHD–HA, freeze drying, and hot air drying provided better colour preservation than microwave-based methods. Freeze-dried samples also exhibited the highest rehydration capacity because of their highly porous structure. Quantitative ImageJ and SEM analyses confirmed that ultrasonic pre-treatment increased porosity and microchannel formation, supporting the improvements in effective moisture diffusivity, drying behaviour, and rehydration capacity. In addition, the Midilli et al. model successfully described the thin-layer drying behaviour under all drying methods. Overall, it was concluded that ultrasonic pre-treatment is an effective pre-treatment technique that improves drying performance and increases energy efficiency in different drying methods. The combined evaluation of drying kinetics, effective moisture diffusivity, energy performance, colour characteristics, rehydration behaviour, and microstructural analysis demonstrated that ultrasonic pre-treatment enhanced mass transfer by modifying the internal structure of capia pepper, resulting in improved overall drying performance. The findings provide useful information for selecting suitable pre-treatment–drying combinations and may serve as a basis for future studies addressing industrial-scale applications. Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. Only two ultrasonic frequencies were investigated under fixed pre-treatment conditions, and the effects of other ultrasonic parameters, such as treatment duration, power, and temperature, were not evaluated. In addition, product quality assessment was limited to colour, rehydration, and microstructural characteristics. Future studies should investigate a wider range of ultrasonic operating conditions, including bioactive and nutritional quality attributes, and validate the findings under industrial-scale drying conditions.
