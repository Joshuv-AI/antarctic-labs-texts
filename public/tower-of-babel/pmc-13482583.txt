Development of complex technology for pyrite-cobalt concentrate processing by sodium carbonate-carbon reductive smelting
Authors: T A Chepushtanova, A Altmyshbayeva, Y Merkibayev, K K Mamyrbayeva, Ata Akcil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn integrated pyro–hydrometallurgical technology for the processing of pyrite-cobalt concentrates has been developed based on sodium carbonate–carbon reductive smelting followed by aqueous treatment of the slag–matte melt. Thermodynamic modeling of the Fe–Ni–Co–Cu–O–S system demonstrated that at 1450–1500 °C the Na₂CO₃–C system forms a strongly reducing environment due to CO generation and sulfur fixation as Na₂S. It was established that this mechanism simultaneously decreases oxygen and sulfur potentials, shifting sulfide systems into the metallic stability region of Me–O–S diagrams. New experimental data were obtained on the distribution of Ni, Co, Fe, Cu, and Zn between metallic alloy, slag–matte melt, and volatilized phases during soda-reductive smelting of synthetic and industrial sulfide materials. The optimal smelting parameters were determined as 7–8 wt% carbon and 130–140% Na₂CO₃ of the theoretical requirement for Fe, Ni, and Co (180% for Cu). Under these conditions, recoveries reached up to 96.5% Fe, 94–96% Ni, 97.5–99% Co, and 93.2% Cu. For Zn-bearing sulfide cakes, selective separation was observed: Ni, Co, and Fe were concentrated in a metallic alloy, whereas Zn was transferred predominantly to dust (up to 82.3%). The resulting alloy (Ni ≈ 26%, Co ≈ 42%, Fe ≈ 30%, S ≈ 0.074%) forms a homogeneous γ-(Ni, Co, Fe) solid solution suitable as an intermediate material for alloyed steels and functional alloys.Keywords: Critical raw materials, Mining waste, Cobalt, Zinc, Hydrometallurgy, PyrometallurgySubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

The growing demand for nickel and cobalt, driven by the expansion of electric vehicles, energy storage systems, and high-performance alloys, necessitates the development of efficient technologies for processing unconventional and low-grade raw materials. Pyrite-cobalt concentrates represent a complex but promising feedstock containing iron sulfides with industrially significant amounts of Ni and Co. However, their utilization remains limited due to the difficulty of selective extraction of non-ferrous metals from the Fe-S matrix1–4.

Traditional processing routes for pyrite-based materials are primarily focused on iron and sulfur recovery and do not ensure efficient concentration of nickel and cobalt into high-value products2–4. The development of technologies enabling direct recovery of Ni and Co into metallic or easily processable forms is therefore of considerable technological and economic interest. Improved process efficiency can be achieved through high metal recovery, recycling of intermediate products, and minimization of waste streams, which is consistent with modern requirements for sustainable metallurgy5,6.

Cobalt remains a critical raw material due to the concentration of its production in a limited number of countries and the growing demand from battery technologies and high-temperature alloys7–10. Pyrite-cobalt concentrates may serve as an alternative feedstock, offering the possibility of controlled phase transformation and selective recovery of valuable components while managing iron and sulfur content11.

Cobalt maintains its status as a critical metal due to the concentration of production in a limited number of countries, the asymmetry of the “mining-refining-cathode materials” chain, and limited interchangeability without loss of performance7,11. Key demand areas include lithium-ion batteries (NMC/NCA cathode materials), where Co improves thermal stability and cyclic stability; superalloys for aircraft and gas turbine power plants; hard alloys, magnetic materials, and chemical catalysis12–14.

Technologically, the processing of pyrite-cobalt concentrates is developing in two main directions: pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical15–19. Pyrometallurgical routes (oxidative roasting, smelting to matte) provide decomposition of the sulfide matrix and partial removal of sulfur, but require careful control of phase equilibria and often lead to losses of valuable metals into slag phases18,20–22. Hydrometallurgical methods (including atmospheric and pressure leaching) offer higher selectivity but are sensitive to mineralogical variability, involve high reagent consumption, and may result in the formation of poorly filterable iron-containing residues16,17.

At the industrial level, an important and widely applied approach for processing pyrite and pyrite-derived materials is sulfating roasting, which enables selective conversion of non-ferrous metals into water-soluble sulfates16–18. In such processes, sulfide minerals are oxidized under controlled conditions (typically 500–700 °C), leading to the formation of CoSO₄, NiSO₄, and CuSO₄, while iron is predominantly converted into Fe₂O₃. This difference in phase stability enables subsequent selective leaching of valuable metals, leaving iron in the solid residue. Sulphation roasting has been widely applied in the treatment of pyrite cinders and copper–cobalt sulfide materials and remains a benchmark technology for selective metal recovery.

The thermodynamic basis of sulfating roasting is well established, with the stability of metal sulfates being strongly dependent on temperature and oxygen partial pressure. Previous studies have shown that controlled sulphation allows high selectivity of Co, Ni, and Cu extraction due to preferential sulfate formation and suppression of iron dissolution16–18.

In addition, ammonium sulfate-assisted roasting has been reported as an effective route for enhancing sulfation reactions and improving leaching efficiency of non-ferrous metals from complex sulfide materials.

In contrast to sulfating roasting, pyrometallurgical approaches based on reductive smelting enable direct transformation of sulfide phases into metallic products. In particular, soda-reductive smelting in Na₂CO₃-C systems provides a fundamentally different reaction pathway, in which sulfur is transferred into a sodium-based melt (Na₂S-Na₂SO₄), while Ni, Co, Fe, and Cu are reduced to a metallic alloy. This approach eliminates the need for intermediate matte formation and allows direct recovery of metals in a concentrated form23–26.

Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of combined pyro-hydrometallurgical routes, in which thermal treatment is used to modify phase composition, followed by selective leaching to recover valuable components27–31. Compared to conventional routes, such integrated approaches may provide improved metal recovery, reduced waste generation, and better process controllability32–35.

Soda-reductive smelting represents a promising alternative to sulfating roasting and conventional matte smelting. It enables direct formation of Ni-Co-Fe-Cu metallic alloys, incorporation of iron into a useful product, regeneration of sodium-based reagents during subsequent hydrometallurgical treatment, and effective separation of zinc via volatilization. These features contribute to improved resource efficiency and environmental performance of the process36.

Based on this analysis, the present study is aimed at developing and experimentally validating an integrated pyro-hydrometallurgical technology for processing pyrite-cobalt concentrates via soda-reductive smelting followed by leaching of the solidified products37–40. Particular attention is given to metal recovery efficiency, phase transformations, and the overall sustainability of the proposed process.

Experimental technique for melting metal sulfides with sodium carbonate

The melting experiments were carried out in a modified laboratory silite electric resistance furnace based on a CHY-T1260A horizontal tube furnace platform, Fig. 1. The experimental setup included a customized quartz tube assembly, gas exhaust line, and dust/sublimates collection system. The furnace provided programmable temperature control up to 1600 °C.

Experimental setup for smelting experiments.

The temperature inside the furnace was monitored using a Pt–PtRh (type S) thermocouple, and controlled by a digital PID controller. The temperature measurement system was calibrated prior to the experiments using standard reference materials, ensuring an accuracy of ± 5 °C in the operating range around 1450 °C.

Dust and sublimates were transported by the gas flow into a dust collection chamber equipped with a fiberglass filter. The temperature in the dust chamber was maintained in the range of 60–100 °C, preventing condensation of volatile species inside the reactor zone.

The charge materials were preliminarily ground to a particle size of 200 mesh. (~ 74 μm), thoroughly homogenized, and slightly moistened to improve granulation and reduce dusting during loading. The prepared charge was placed into alundum (Al₂O₃) crucibles, which were selected due to their high thermal and chemical stability under experimental conditions.

The crucible containing the charge was introduced into a preheated furnace at 1450 °C. The melts were conducted in graphite crucibles with screw-on lids containing a small hole for the release of gaseous products. After complete melting of the charge, the system was held isothermally for 30 min to ensure completion of reduction and phase separation processes. The temperature was maintained constant throughout the experiment.

After smelting, three products were obtained: metallic alloy (Ni–Co–Fe–Cu), slag–matte (sodium-based melt), dust (volatile fraction).

The products were cooled under ambient conditions, carefully separated, weighed using an analytical balance (accuracy ± 0.01 g), and subjected to chemical analysis.

Elemental composition of solid and liquid products was determined using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) after appropriate sample preparation. The phase composition was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD). Calibration of analytical instruments was performed using certified reference materials.

To investigate the effect of carbon content on the smelting process, a series of experiments was conducted using iron sulfide as a model system. The charge consisted of 100 g of sulfide material and a stoichiometric amount of sodium carbonate, with the addition of carbonaceous reductant (charcoal) in the range of 0–15 wt% relative to sulfide mass.

Design of experiments and experimental setup

The experimental program was designed to evaluate the effect of reductant (charcoal) dosage and sodium carbonate addition on metal recovery and phase distribution. A series of smelting tests was performed at a fixed temperature of 1450 °C with varying carbon content (0–15 wt% relative to sulfide mass) and Na₂CO₃ addition (expressed as % of the theoretical requirement). All experiments were conducted in a horizontal tube furnace under an air atmosphere, without external gas control, with volatilized products removed to a dust collection system. The charge materials were preliminarily ground to 200 mesh, homogenized, and slightly moistened prior to loading to ensure reproducible packing and reaction conditions. After smelting, products (metal alloy, slag–matte phase, and dust) were separated, weighed, and characterized by XRF, ICP-OES, and XRD using calibrated instruments and standard sample preparation procedures.

Special attention was paid to the choice of crucible material. Alumina (Al₂O₃) crucibles were used in experiments with lower carbon content or near-stoichiometric conditions, where the system remained relatively less reducing and chemical interaction with the crucible was minimal. In contrast, graphite crucibles were employed in experiments with elevated charcoal additions, where strongly reducing conditions prevail. The use of graphite in these cases minimizes the risk of parasitic oxidation reactions, improves thermal stability, and provides a chemically compatible environment for reduction processes. Additionally, graphite crucibles help maintain a reducing micro-atmosphere at the melt–crucible interface, whereas alumina crucibles prevent unintended carbon input in experiments where the reductant level is a controlled variable. This approach ensured that the influence of carbon addition on the process could be accurately evaluated without interference from the crucible material.

Physicochemical methods of sample analysis

The initial samples and melt products were analyzed on a JXA-8230 electron probe microanalyzer (JEOL) at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, a beam current of 100 pA–5 nA, and a dead time of up to 17%. These parameters enabled the recording of structural features from fractions of a micron to hundreds of microns at magnifications of 40–2500 x. Scanning electron microscopy was performed primarily in backscattered electron mode (COMPO), which provides a more informative contrast compared to secondary electron intensity (SEI) due to the increased brightness of phases with higher atomic numbers. Micrographs were obtained in SEI and BSE modes in the magnification range of 40–2500 x.

X-ray diffraction analysis was performed on an X-ray D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker) with a Cu Kα source (40 kV, 40 mA). Processing of diffraction patterns and calculation of interplanar distances were carried out in the EVA software package.

Quantitative electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) was performed using the wavelength-dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) mode with calibration against certified external reference materials. Pure element and oxide standards were used for calibration, including Ni (metal), Co (metal), Fe (metal), Cu (metal), Zn (metal), and Fe₂O₃ and NiO for oxide phases. Matrix corrections were applied using the ZAF (atomic number–absorption–fluorescence) method implemented in the JEOL software.

The analytical accuracy was verified by repeated measurements of reference standards under identical operating conditions. The relative standard deviation (RSD) for major elements (Ni, Fe, Co) did not exceed ± 2–3%, while for minor elements (Zn, Cu) it was within ± 5%. The detection limits for WDS analysis were approximately 0.01–0.05 wt% depending on the element and measurement conditions.

Uncertainty analysis was carried out based on counting statistics, calibration accuracy, and instrumental stability. The combined measurement uncertainty for major elements is estimated to be within ± 3 wt% (relative), and for trace components within ± 5 wt%. To ensure reproducibility, multiple point analyses (n ≥ 5) were performed for each phase, and the reported compositions represent averaged values.

Scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDS) was performed using a JEOL JSM-7000 scanning electron microscope equipped with an EDS detector. The analyses were carried out at an accelerating voltage of 15–20 kV and a working distance of 10–15 mm.

The EDS system was calibrated using certified reference materials and pure element standards (Ni, Co, Fe, Cu, Zn) prior to measurements. Quantitative analysis was performed using standardless ZAF correction with software-based matrix correction. The accuracy of EDS analysis for major elements was within ± 3–5 wt%, while for minor elements it was within ± 5–10 wt%. EDS analysis was primarily used for rapid compositional screening and phase identification, while quantitative compositional data were obtained using WDS (EPMA) for higher accuracy.

Thermodynamic justification of soda-reduction smelting

Using the HSC thermodynamic calculation program, the thermodynamics of the soda-reduction smelting process was studied (Table 4). Studying the thermodynamics of the main reactions involving iron, nickel, cobalt, and copper sulfides interacting with sodium carbonate in the presence of carbon reveals their potential for practical applications28–30.

Analysis of the smelting products and thermodynamic studies demonstrate that at temperatures of 1400–1500 °C, nickel, cobalt, iron, and copper are released in significant quantities as metallic elements when their sulfides interact with soda and carbon (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Equilibrium state diagrams of systems at 1500 °C.

Analyzing the thermodynamic data, it was established that the reaction (CO₂ + C → 2CO, ΔG° = -129.2) is very efficient, since CO₂ formed from soda is quickly converted into CO. The reaction Na₂CO₃ + C → Na₂O + 2CO, ΔG° = -36.7 is explained as follows: in the presence of carbon, soda becomes a source of Na₂O and CO. That is, the system itself creates: a strongly reducing gas phase (CO); the main reagent Na₂O, which converts sulfides into Na₂S and metal oxides. At 1450–1500 °C, the combination Na₂CO₃ + C is thermodynamically efficient and defines a “two-step” scheme sulfide → oxide → metal/sublimation.

Thus, at 1450 °C, the sodium carbonate system thermodynamically forms Na₂O and CO (ΔG° < 0), ensures the binding of sulfur to Na₂S and the conversion of Fe/Co/Zn sulfides to oxides (ΔG° ≈ -100…-120 kJ/mol), after which the oxides are reduced to metals, and ZnO is reduced to form Zn(g), which explains the presence of dust/sublimates. The overall reactions R14-R18 have strongly negative ΔG° (up to -529 kJ/mol for nickel), which is consistent with the observed stable formation and good separation of the metallic phase.

For all metals studied, the overall reactions are characterized by negative ΔG° values at 1450 °C. After normalization to one mole of sulfur, the Ni₃S₂ reaction also remains strongly thermodynamically favorable (ΔG∘=−264.4 kJ/mol S) for R18 reaction.

It was found that at 1450–1500 °C the Na₂CO₃–C system generates a strongly reducing CO-rich atmosphere (low pO₂) and simultaneously lowers the sulfur potential (pS₂) due to the formation of Na₂S. As a result, the equilibrium in the Fe–O–S, Ni–O–S, Co–O–S, and Cu–O–S systems shifts toward the stability region of metallic phases.

The overall reactions are characterized by negative ΔG° values (up to -528 kJ/mol for Ni), confirming their thermodynamic feasibility under the studied conditions.

This behavior can be described as a simultaneous decrease in both oxygen and sulfur potentials, which drives the system from sulfide stability fields into the metal stability regions on the predominance diagrams. The consistency between the calculated ΔG° values and the predicted phase stability fields supports this interpretation.

To determine optimal conditions, large-scale crucible melts with soda were performed on synthetic Ni, Co, Fe, and Cu sulfides, as well as industrial sulfide materials, including cobalt-pyrite concentrate from the processing of Sokolov-Sarbai Mining Production Association (SSGPO) JSC raw materials. The results of the laboratory and large-scale soda-reduction melts are presented below.

The effect of excess soda on the results of Ni, Co, Fe, and Cu extraction into metal form was tested at a constant rate of charcoal and the corresponding sulfide. The smelting results established that increasing the soda dosage beyond 140–160% does not lead to a significant increase in recovery for Fe, Co, and Ni. For Cu, a maximum recovery of 93.2% is achieved at a Na₂CO₃ dosage of 180%. The quality of the produced metallic phases is characterized by Ni, Co, and Cu contents of 98–99% with minimal sulfur levels. Iron forms a high-carbon iron alloy containing 94–96% Fe and 0.06–0.4% S.

Dust loss (dust yield) amounts to 4–5% of the charge mass, with the following composition: total alkalinity 85–90%, Na₂S – 1–2%, Na₂SO₄ – 3–4%, and metal content 0,5–4% (in the form of the initial sulfide). The dust can be recycled back into the smelting process, thereby increasing the total metal recovery.

The achieved metal recoveries (Fe – up to 96.5%, Co – up to 97.5%, Ni – up to 94.3%, Cu – up to 93.2%) are determined by the thermodynamic and kinetic conditions of the smelting stage. The regeneration of 20–25% of sodium compounds during subsequent hydrometallurgical treatment improves the overall process efficiency by reducing reagent consumption, but does not influence the metal extraction achieved during smelting.

Metal content and recovery into the metallic alloy.

It was established that the recovery of Ni and Co into the metallic alloy is consistently high, typically exceeding 95% (Table 8). Copper recovery increases with its initial concentration in the starting material: from 16.2% in the alloy at a 9.86% Cu charge content (smelting experiment No. 62) to 78.6% at a 55.4% Cu content (No. 63). For pure Cu2S at 1450 °C, the recovery exceeds 90% (Table 6).

In the presence of both FeS and Cu-sulfides (matte and converter matte No. 2 and 3), the Fe recovery into the alloy is higher compared to copper-free systems (No. 1), as confirmed by Tables 7 and 8. This phenomenon is attributed to the formation of a liquid Cu-phase acting as a collector. This phase intensifies metal coalescence and reduces sulfur activity by sequestering it into Na2S, which shifts the FeS reduction equilibrium toward the metallic phase.

A similar effect is observed for nickel: while the recovery from pure Ni₃S₂ reaches 94.6% (Table 5), it increases up to 97.8% in sulfide mixtures (smelting experiment No. 44, Table 8). The enhanced recovery is explained by the alteration of thermodynamic activity within the «Na₂CO₃ -Na₂O-Na₂S / sulfide melt / metal” system, the reduction of Ni2S activity, and the collector role of the Cu/Fe-bearing metallic phase. These factors shift the reduction reaction equilibrium to the right and minimize Ni losses. Based on the smelting experiments, the thermodynamic efficiency limit for soda consumption was identified. It was experimentally demonstrated that increasing the Na₂CO₃ dosage beyond 130–140% (for Ni, Co, and Fe) and 180% (for Cu) does not lead to a significant increase in metal recovery. This indicates that the process is not limited by the amount of alkali but by the attainment of thermodynamic equilibrium in the metallic phase region; further increases in soda dosage do not shift the operating point on the phase diagram. Thus, an optimal alkaline potential range was established, corresponding to the transition into the metallic field without excessive reagent consumption.

The high selectivity of the reduction process was confirmed. The data show high recovery rates into the metallic alloy for nickel and cobalt: 96% Ni and 98–99% Co from the matte, and 93–94% Ni and 97.8–98.5% Co from the converter matte.

A decrease in iron recovery into the alloy was observed as its concentration in the sulfide feed decreased. Copper present in the industrial products predominantly remains (85%) in the slag-matte melt. The metallic alloy produced from the matte contains 0.1–0.12% sulfur, while the alloy from the converter matte contains 0.4–0.5% S.

The dissolution of small amounts of cobalt – produced during the soda smelting of the studied sulfide mixtures – into the metallic alloy leads to enhanced cobalt recovery (97–99%). This exceeds the recovery rates achieved during the smelting of pure cobalt sulfide with soda and carbon under similar conditions.

Smelting of sulfide mixtures corresponding to the composition of nickel concentrate (obtained from copper-nickel converter matte flotation) was conducted at three different soda dosages: 125, 135, and 150% of the stoichiometric requirement. The resulting data (Table 8, Runs No. 70, 71, 72) indicate the possibility of reducing the soda dosage to 125% without a noticeable decrease in the recovery of nickel, cobalt, and iron into the metallic alloy. In all sulfide smelting cases, the resulting slag–matte melt is composed predominantly of sodium compounds, with Na₂S as the major phase and minor amounts of oxidized sodium species and excess alkali. Iron, nickel, cobalt, and zinc are present in minor quantities, mainly in the form of oxide and sulfide species.

The dust, which contains only minor amounts of metals, is composed of 75–85% total alkali, Na2S – 0.5–2.5%, Na2SО4 – 2–6%. Due to its high alkalinity, this dust can be utilized as a recycled material in the smelting process.

The metallic alloys of varying compositions produced during the soda smelting of sulfide materials – containing nickel, cobalt, and iron, and in some cases copper and precious metals – can serve as feedstock for the production of alloyed steels or specialized alloys. Smelting of Ni-Co-Fe-Cu sulfide mixtures with soda and carbon at 1450 °C demonstrated that copper is onlypartially recovered into the metallic alloy, while a significant fraction remains in the slag-matte melt. During subsequent processing of this melt, copper is concentrated into a separate product.

The distribution of Ni and Co between phases is influenced by the iron content in the system. An increase in iron content promotes the transfer of Ni and Co into the metallic phase, which can be attributed to changes in phase equilibria and mutual solubility in the Fe-containing melt. This behavior is consistent with the experimental results obtained in this study, where higher iron levels are associated with increased recovery of Ni and Co into the metallic alloy. These findings support the feasibility of conducting smelting under conditions that favor the formation of an Fe-rich intermediate, followed by its conversion into a metallic alloy, enabling incorporation of iron into a value-added product19,36.

Smelting nickel matte with soda yields a nickel-bearing alloy with lower impurity levels compared to ferronickel produced by conventional ore electric smelting, making it suitable for specialized alloy production. Meanwhile, up to 85% of the copper remains in the slag-matte melt, ensuring its separation into an independent product. During subsequent hydrometallurgical treatment (water leaching) of the slag–matte melt, sodium compounds are selectively dissolved, while copper remains in the solid residue (cake) due to the low solubility of copper sulfides. As a result, copper is concentrated in this solid phase, which can be further processed to obtain a separate copper-containing product.

The melt composition (predominantly Na₂S and Na₂O with minor Na₂SO₄) allows for the regeneration of 80–85% of the soda - the sole flux used in the process – via hot carbonization reactions.

A closed-loop circulation of the alkaline component was demonstrated: the dust (4–5% of the charge mass) contains 85–90% total alkali and is recycled back into the smelting stage. Additionally, 20–25% of the soda is regenerated during the hydrometallurgical processing of the melt. The majority of the Ni, Co, and Fe in the melt exists as sulfides and remains in the cake after water leaching. This cake, together with the dust and regenerated soda, is returned to the smelting process, thereby increasing the cumulative metal recovery.

The proposed soda-reduction smelting approach was compared with conventional sulfating roasting processes used for the treatment of sulfide concentrates. In sulfating roasting, selective oxidation of sulfides leads to the formation of water-soluble sulfates of Ni, Co, and Cu, followed by hydrometallurgical recovery. This process is typically carried out at moderate temperatures (500–700 °C) under controlled oxidizing conditions38–40.

In contrast, the proposed method operates at higher temperatures (≈ 1450 °C) under strongly reducing conditions, promoting direct conversion of sulfides into metallic phases and sodium sulfide. This results in a different distribution of elements: Ni and Co are recovered into a metallic alloy, while sodium compounds are concentrated in the slag–matte melt and subsequently recovered during leaching.

Compared to sulfating roasting, the soda-reduction route reduces the number of process stages and enables incorporation of iron into a value-added product, while also allowing partial regeneration of the flux.

Sodium in the melt is mainly present in the form of Na₂S (up to 50 wt%), Na₂SO₄ (1–2 wt%), and Na₂O, while the remaining fraction consists of other sodium-containing species and an amorphous alkaline matrix, as well as minor amounts of Ni, Co, Fe, and Zn.

Considering that the alkaline compounds of the slag-matte melt are readily soluble during aqueous leaching, the melts were subjected to hydrometallurgical treatment in order to transfer sodium compounds into solution and separate Ni, Co, Fe, and Zn into a cake as a separate product. The resulting alkaline solution can subsequently be processed to regenerate sodium carbonate or used for sodium sulfide production. The cake containing valuable metals is recycled back to the smelting stage to recover nickel, cobalt, and iron into the metallic alloy and zinc into the volatilized phase. This approach increases the overall recovery of metals into the metallic alloy and dust during smelting. Conclusions

The present study experimentally confirms the feasibility of efficient extraction of Ni, Co, Fe, and Cu from their sulfides by soda-reductive smelting at 1450 °C. The optimal process parameters are 7–8% carbon and 130–140% Na₂CO₃ of the theoretically required amount (180% for Cu), ensuring metal recoveries of up to: Fe – 96.5%, Ni – 94–96%, Co – 97.5–99%, and Cu – 93.2%. The resulting metallic phases are characterized by high purity (98–99% for Ni, Co, and Cu; Fe in the form of pig iron at 94–96% with 0.06–0.4% S).

No classical slag–matte separation was observed due to the formation of a sodium sulfide-based melt and direct reduction of sulfides to metallic phases under strongly reducing conditions.

The applicability of the technology to a wide range of sulfide materials (mattes, fine mattes, synthetic mixtures, cobalt-bearing pyrite cakes), including products from the processing of SSMPC materials, has been demonstrated, with the formation of Ni-Co-Fe-Cu metallic alloys and the capture of Zn in the volatilized phase.

Compared to conventional sulfating roasting, the proposed soda-reduction smelting approach demonstrates several process-level advantages. The method enables direct recovery of Ni and Co into a metallic alloy, eliminating intermediate hydrometallurgical extraction steps. Sulfur is effectively fixed in the form of Na₂S, reducing the formation of sulfur-containing gaseous emissions.

In addition, iron is incorporated into a value-added metallic phase rather than being removed as waste, and partial regeneration of sodium compounds is achieved through recycling of dust and leaching solutions. The process also provides phase-based separation of elements, with Ni and Co concentrated in the alloy, Zn in the dust, and Cu in the solid residue.

The proposed soda-reduction smelting route was compared with established processing technologies for Ni–Co–Cu sulfide materials, including sulfating roasting, pressure/atmospheric leaching, and conventional smelting–converting.

Process configuration. Sulfating roasting operates at 500–700 °C in oxidizing conditions and requires multiple downstream hydrometallurgical steps (leaching, purification, recovery). In contrast, the present process operates at ~ 1450 °C under strongly reducing conditions and produces a metallic Ni–Co alloy directly, reducing the number of downstream separation stages.

Metal distribution and selectivity. In this study, Ni and Co are recovered predominantly into the metallic alloy (≈ 94–99%), while Zn is volatilized into dust and Cu is retained in the solid residue after leaching. This phase-based separation differs from sulfating roasting, where metals are transferred into solution and require subsequent separation by SX/IX or precipitation.

Sulfur management. Sulfur is fixed mainly as Na₂S in the slag–matte melt (> 90–95% based on mass balance), minimizing sulfur-bearing gaseous emissions. In sulfating roasting, sulfur is oxidized to SO₂/SO₃ and requires gas handling and scrubbing systems.

Iron utilization. Iron is incorporated into a metallic Fe-rich alloy (~ 94–96 wt% Fe) rather than being discarded as hydrometallurgical residue, improving resource utilization.

Reagent circulation. Partial regeneration of sodium compounds (≈ 20–25% in leaching) and recycling of alkali-rich dust (≈ 85–90% Na-bearing) enable a semi-closed alkali loop, whereas sulphate routes rely on continuous acid consumption.

The scientific novelty lies in the substantiation of a thermodynamically controlled mechanism of soda-reductive conversion of the Me-S system into the stability domain of metallic phases, combined with the regeneration of 80–85% of the flux during subsequent hydrometallurgical treatment of the melt. An innovative integrated pyro-hydrometallurgical flowsheet is proposed, ensuring the incorporation of iron into a commercial product, reduction of waste generation, and improvement of the economic sustainability of pyrite-cobalt raw material processing.
