A sustainability framework and application pathway for AI-assisted restoration and iterative interaction of MR digital heritage based on stakeholders
Authors: Wei Yuan, Miao Wang, Xiaodong Huangfu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focuses on the limitations of enhancing the sustainability of AI-assisted restoration and iterative interaction through the two paths of ‘assisted restoration’ and ‘artistic visual identity’ based on stakeholder perspectives and using heritage as a case study for application scenarios. This study adopts a mixed qualitative and quantitative approach to collect primary data. Through a systematic analysis of the in-depth requirements of heritage content, four strategic pathways were reconstructed in this research: AI-assisted restoration, cultural heritage characterisation, digitalisation pathways, and strategic design. Furthermore, integrating the pre, mid and post stages of project strategy design with the six processes of IDEO design thinking, the DTH-AI framework model was constructed. The sustainability strategy model DTH-AI and its application pathway framework for the integration and application of digital intelligence is finally constructed, and evaluated by a focus group composed of relevant stakeholders, which verifies the innovation of the research framework, effectively solves the sustainability problem in the process of AI-assisted restoration and iterative interaction, It provides solid theoretical support and methodological guidance for the sustainable digital intelligence development of other similar cultural heritages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54543-8.Keywords: AI-assisted, MR, Virtual interaction, Digital and intelligent iteration pathways, Stakeholders, SustainabilitySubject terms: Cultural and media studies; Cultural and media studies; Development studies; Science, technology and society

Introduction

With the continuous penetration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in the field of cultural heritage preservation and communication, the application of cultural heritage is currently undergoing a transitional phase from ‘digitalisation’ to ‘digital intelligence.’ Due to its rich heritage content, unique spatial structure, and diverse communication needs, grotto heritage urgently needs to leverage intelligent technologies to achieve higher quality and more effective digital display and communication. In addition, from a sub-research perspective, the digitalisation of Grotto Cultural Heritage can be divided into two parts: digital preservation and digital display. Currently, most research focuses on digital preservation, especially the technical optimisation of specific 3D scanning software and hardware. However, the application path and sustainability-driven strategy at the digital display level are still relatively weak, especially the lack of support from a systematic theoretical framework and method model embedded with AI. Moreover, the scope of digital cultural heritage has expanded beyond mere display technologies to encompass comprehensive challenges such as ‘virtual exhibition ecosystems, sustained user engagement, offline visit conversion, trustworthy AI governance, and long-term sustainable operations.’1–7.

In particular, AI-assisted restoration, which forms the core of this study, is typically based on machine learning and computer vision technologies. It enables the predictive reconstruction of missing information in cultural heritage and has been widely applied in the fields of virtual restoration and digital reconstruction. Relevant research indicates that this method primarily serves as an auxiliary tool to support expert decision-making rather than as a substitute for human intervention and is typically applied in non-invasive digital restoration processes8,9. In this regard, this study systematically reviews and analyses the current state of digital display for grotto heritage, potential integration points and feasibility of AI, as well as the theoretical foundations for sustainable operational development, thereby laying a solid research foundation for the construction of a final pathway model to support the digital and intelligent transformation of high-density cultural heritage.

Differences in grotto heritage forms and regional characteristics

As an important carrier of Chinese civilisation and art, the Grotto Cultural Heritage is an irreplaceable cultural and artistic treasure of world cultural heritage. In particular, the internationally renowned Mogao Caves have a rich and diverse heritage of murals and painted sculptures, mainly with Buddhist themes, such as Buddha statues, murals depicting Buddhist stories, traditional mythological themes, and portraits of donors, as well as many fragments of murals10,11. In the grottoes of the Central Plains and northern regions (within China), those in the Hexi and Gansu regions east of the Yellow River are mainly characterised by sculptures and murals, while those in Henan, Shanxi and Shaanxi provinces and eastward are mainly characterised by carvings, most of which are in a state of disrepair due to weathering12. In addition, the diversity of the interior spatial structure of the grottoes with a central tower pillar and temple-type alcove layout, as well as the richness of the decorations, such as the patterns on the cave ceilings and the statues on the front walls13. However, the murals and sculptures in the grottoes face various threats from time, nature, and human activities. The passage of time, erosion by the natural environment, and inappropriate behaviour by tourists have all caused irreversible damage to the murals14. In summary, although the grottoes contain a large amount of faded and fragmented murals, as well as painted sculptures and stone carvings dam-aged by weathering and historical human factors, the heritage content is vast and extremely valuable, the form and composition of the grotto heritage varies significantly and there are notable regional differences. This will be an important objective factor in the de-sign of the digital display strategy, to adapt to the current situation and characteristics of different regions and types of grottoes, and to achieve scalability and replicability, which will be one of the significant challenges in this study.

Status of digitalisation applications

The Dunhuang Academy, in collaboration with technology companies, has launched comprehensive online digitalisation projects such as ‘Digital Dunhuang’, ‘Digital Cave of the Thousand Buddha’, ‘3D Animation of Cave 61 in the Mogao Caves’, and ‘Cloud Tour of Dunhuang’. The Longmen Research Institute has launched the ‘Longmen in the Cloud’ display project, which provides visitors with a personalised browsing experience15,16. The above-mentioned online digital displays, including PC-based digital resource libraries and mobile applications, not only help to increase public participation, but also help the public to understand and access heritage content, breaking the limitations of viewing within scenic spots and enabling wider online and re-mote communication. In addition, the ‘Spiritual Journey to Dunhuang’ project, which re-constructs Cave 61 of the Mogao Caves based on a VR platform, and the VR system for the replica of Cave 18 of the Yungang Grottoes, as well as the virtual space created through 3D reconstruction of the Longmen Wanfo Cave, integrate contextualised virtual lighting functions and enable remote immersive browsing for visitors, who can explore details and enjoy a comprehensive experience. The ‘Colourful World’ immersive exhibition allows visitors to enjoy a multi-sensory interactive experience through giant digital installations17–19. Moreover, virtual exhibitions of cultural heritage have evolved from a singular display technique into a comprehensive research domain encompassing platform ecosystems, educational dissemination, user experience, and global communication1. VR cultural heritage research has progressively shifted its focus from evaluating ‘the feasibility of immersion’ towards assessing ‘long-term viability and sustained utilisation’2. In summary, the digitalisation of grotto heritage has gradually presented comprehensive characteristics such as diversified application technologies, platform-based communication channels, non-fixed application scenarios, and interactive and immersive experiences. This trend not only reflects the deep integration of digital media and heritage content but also poses a high challenge for the scientific embedding of AI characteristics to adapt to the rationality and effectiveness of different platforms and scenario conditions. However, many digital heritage projects continue to prioritise technical presentation and access expansion, whilst failing to sufficiently demonstrate their effectiveness in terms of authentic sensory perception, sustained engagement, and long-term usability across diverse scenarios.

Pathways for artificial intelligence integration, ethical risks and sustainability constraints

Recently, AI-based approaches (including large language models, generative AI, and deep learning-based restoration models) have opened new avenues for the preservation and dissemination of cultural heritage. In the field of integrated artificial intelligence applications, the generation technology of Large Language Models (LLMs) provides a new path for cultural heritage preservation, with the core means being the digitalisation of content through Chinese text and images20. However, AI has both positive impacts and potential risks in cultural heritage preservation and dissemination. For example, training data bias can lead to a lack of authenticity and accuracy, and image generation involves issues such as copyright ownership and user privacy21.In addition, in terms of automation, the Apple AFM uses data decontamination methods such as deleting low-quality content and personalisation, and filters high-quality data using classifiers. Its training data includes authorised and public sources, such as Apple Bot web page collection, and the mixed use of these sources has improved the stable performance of multiple benchmark tests22. Further-more, the Auto-GPT intelligent agents can complete tasks autonomously and are more automated than ChatGPT, which requires step-by-step guidance23. A novel quantitative computational framework that integrates machine learning models with explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques, achieving the dual improvement of accuracy and interpretability in material property prediction. The framework establishes a structured pipeline, which includes key links such as data processing, feature selection, model training, performance evaluation, explainability analysis, and practical deployment24. In addition, in the field of grotto-type cultural heritage, the ‘Dunhuang Artificial Intelligence’ dataset (v1.0) by combining deep learning models and using deep networks for automatic restoration, which supports functions such as mural style transfer, local damage restoration, and image tag retrieval15. However, there are still limitations in terms of complex element recognition, large-area damage restoration, technical integration, adaptability to complex problems, and style realism in murals. In response, a new KAN neural network architecture to address accuracy and complexity issues25. Their research confirmed that KAN has strong fitting capabilities and flexible non-linear performance, enabling it to be used for complex image restoration tasks. In particular, it demonstrated higher interpretability and model computing efficiency in small-scale AI + science tasks. In addition, a method based on generative adversarial networks (GANs) and UNet architecture for the restoration of ancient murals to improve restoration efficiency and consistency. In order to better evaluate the performance of large language models (LLMs), a reference framework for the integration of cultural heritage and AI that embodies AI ethics and guidelines and is applicable to various cultural heritage contexts to ensure the preservation of cultural heritage and its value26,27. Finally, by focused on actual cases and prototypes to systematically review the integrated application of VR, AI, and IoT, indicating the potential of digital twins, AIoT, and immersive XR in multiple fields and promoting interdisciplinary research28. The limitations lie in the insufficient research in some fields, and data interoperability, user experience, and scalability issues still limit its widespread application. Furthermore, trustworthy AI within cultural heritage increasingly emphasises transparency, interpretability, evidence chains and governance accountability to prevent AI-generated content from misleading public perceptions of authenticity5. Furthermore, recent case studies for high-fidelity reconstruction scenarios demonstrate that while AIGC enhances reconstruction efficiency, verification of its geometric and semantic authenticity remains dependent on high-quality reference models and archival evidence29.

In summary, in the field of grotto-type cultural heritage with rich information density, image AI generation systems based on Large Language Models (LLMs) are reshaping the preservation and communication of cultural heritage. Through image restoration accuracy optimisation, deep learning-driven automated restoration, neural network structure expansion, and intelligent agent task execution capabilities, AI has significantly improved the efficiency potential and positive performance of digitalisation applications. Furthermore, during the research process, challenges such as data bias, copyright ownership, ethical norms, privacy protection, and adaptability to complex tasks have gradually emerged as areas of ongoing concern. The above research further emphasises that interpretability, transparency, evidence traceability, and community-sensitive governance are core conditions for trustworthy AI in the cultural heritage sector, particularly when AI-generated or restored content may reshape public perceptions of authenticity. Furthermore, recent studies indicate that AI sustainability in cultural heritage should encompass technological deployment, case operability, governance standards, and long-term maintenance, rather than being confined to model efficiency alone4 AI-driven digital cultural heritage platforms not only transform online experiences but also further influence users’ willingness to engage with offline cultural activities6. However, much of the research has focused on technological breakthroughs in large language models, and there is currently a lack of systematic guidelines for integration to form a comprehensive strategic framework. Although there has been research on ethical frameworks and interdisciplinary integration, based on the above research findings, breakthroughs are still needed in terms of scalability, user needs and experience, and standardisation of integration pathways.

Moreover, regarding the sustainability architecture and evaluation of artificial intelligence, GreenAuto serves as an end-to-end construction platform for sustainable AI research. However, it lacks practical cross-hardware support, particularly concerning the energy efficiency assessment requirements for LLaMA models in edge device scenarios30. To achieve systematic quantitative analysis of model supply chains and cross-stage strategic trade-offs, a unified AI sustainability assessment framework has been proposed. However, its methodology remains unvalidated in large-scale real-world applications regarding feasibility and data adaptability31. Furthermore, the Artificial Intelligence and Sustainability Perception Inventory (AISPI), as the first standardised tool, enables systematic quantification of public perceptions regarding AI’s role in sustainable development. However, its limited sample size and applicability across multicultural contexts require further validation and expansion32.

In summary, current research on the sustainability dimension of AI, while featuring innovative platforms and systematic quantitative evaluations with some efficacy assessment for large language models, suffers from limitations in its singular architecture and its scalability and adaptability across multicultural contexts. Specifically, there remains a lack of explicit, concrete research on constructing AI sustainability within the culturally dense heritage sector and on Iterative Interaction systems within the specific pathways of virtual restoration.

Sustainability value orientation

Version 2.1 of the London Charter for Computer Visualisation of Cultural Heritage proposes improving the consistency and accuracy of computer visualisation in restoring the history and content of heritage and ensuring that the process and results of digital visualisation can be accurately understood and evaluated by users or viewers. Further-more, stakeholders should be able to universally accept and recognise basic standards. Finally, accessibility and availability should be strengthened33. In addition, the heritage restoration should be based on local knowledge and global standards, promoting the integration of bottom-up and top-down strategies to enhance the sustainability and legitimacy of restoration actions. Once again, the principles of ‘multidisciplinary collaboration’ and ‘holistic governance’ are emphasised as particularly important34. The above provides a solid theoretical foundation and goal-oriented approach for the design of sustainability-driven strategies in this study.

Strategy design

The design strategy is a scientific and analytical approach to design that requires in-depth analysis and testing during the design process35. Furthermore, design is a process of research and research methods, and to a certain extent, it is also a quantitative analysis method and inductive research method. It can effectively promote knowledge acquisition36–38. The universal design concept and win-win strategy for designing for the public rather than generalising design. This design thinking incorporates the diverse needs of specific groups (including people with disabilities)39. Additionally, in the context of specific design applications for complex scenarios, design thinking is a cross-disciplinary innovative method centred on user experience, featuring standardisation and usability, and can be widely applied to complex products and scenario-based services to effectively enhance innovation efficiency. By adopting a multidisciplinary approach and design pro-cess (pre-design, design, and post-design), projects are implemented through an iterative process and mindset comprising six stages: empathy, definition, ideation, prototyping, testing, and implementation. This principle-based strategy and methodological framework is applicable to all design domains, from cultural spaces and exhibitions to virtual spaces and their underlying technologies40–42.

The design thinking approach proposed by IDEO, which consists of six iterative stages: empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test, and implement, emphasizes user-centeredness and interdisciplinary collaboration, providing a theoretical method for solving complex problems43 With the development of information technology, design thinking has become an important tool for stimulating innovation. It introduces the concept of universal design that takes into account the diverse needs of specific groups, including people with disabilities, and enhances the universality of products, making it applicable to various design fields40,44. Furthermore, integrating IDEO design thinking with “wicked problems” demonstrates its value in addressing complex social issues, highlighting interdisciplinary collaboration and contextualized implementation, which represents a cross-context expansion and refinement of the six-stage model45.

In summary, design thinking constitutes the underlying logic of strategic design. With its people-oriented, interdisciplinary nature and continuously iterative design path, it emphasizes a systematic design process, sustained user experience, and a rigorous yet creative implementation process. As such, it can effectively address grotto heritage characterized by dense information and complex attributes and offers significant guidance for improving the implementation effectiveness and iterative sustainability of existing AI-driven digital integration pathways.

Research methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee, (All experimental protocols in this study have been approved by the Authorized Committee of the School of Fashion and Textiles, De Montfort University, UK, on 25 January 2024 at 14:10; the approval number is 524,708. Furthermore, all research methods have been conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations; meanwhile, informed consent has been obtained from all experts participating in the focus groups and all questionnaire respondents.) and adopts a mixed-methods approach combining data from both qualitative and quantitative perspectives, innovatively employing a cross-progressive process to gather primary data from both the researcher’s and the viewer’s perspectives. The entire study was conducted from January 2024 to September 2025, spanning 21 months: field observation was carried out from January 2024 to May 2024, including SOP development and on-site data assessment; the questionnaire survey was implemented from July to September 2024, covering questionnaire design, data collection and data screening; and focus group evaluation was conducted from March 2025 to September 2025, involving the recruitment of expert evaluators from five countries and the formulation of evaluation SOPs.

(1) Qualitative inductive analysis method combining literature and case studies. It approaches the complex causal relationship between the two specific conditions of ‘AI integration point’ and ‘path integration sustainability’ from two dimensions and uses a questionnaire survey of the needs of a wide range of users to systematically identify the key factors and entry points that affect the application path of digital intelligence integration in grotto cultural heritage. It then explores a set of diverse conditions and a co-constructed non-linear integration path and summarise and construct a combination of necessary and sufficient factors for sustainable application results. On this basis and propose a strategic framework model for the sustainability of digital intelligence applications that covers AI assistance, the characteristics of grotto heritage, digitalisation paths, and strategy design.

(2) Questionnaire, in response, a total of 1,200 questionnaires were distributed, and 1,168 were returned. To ensure data quality, the research team screened the questionnaires based on three criteria: contradictory answers, abnormally short response times, and incorrect respondent location. Forty-nine invalid questionnaires were discarded, leaving 1,119 valid questionnaires, with an efficiency rate of 95.8%.In order to ensure that the questionnaire participants were sufficiently broad and representative, they were mainly residents who had lived for a long time in China, and the sample covered two types of people who had and had not visited grotto scenic spots. The data came from Part C (Question 18) and Part F (Question 30) of the questionnaire (Appendix A). See Appendix B-TQ18 and B-TQ30 for details.

(3) Field Observation. the digital application status of six representative large, medium and small grotto sites was systematically observed, including the Mogao Grottoes and its small Satellite grotto groups (Yulin Grottoes), as well as the Longmen Grottoes and its Satellite grotto groups (Gongyi Grottoes, Shuiquan Grottoes and Wanfo Mountain Grottoes), all located in China. An evaluation criterion was established to ensure the extensiveness and practical relevance of the study. Details are provided in Appendix B.

(4) Focus Group. The evaluation panel for this method was designed in accordance with the stakeholder principle of multi-agent collaboration as emphasized in the London Charter33. This study centres on grotto heritage, XR, AI and strategic design. Stakeholders are selected based on their professional background, occupational field, functional role and research relevance, and classified into academic research, management and policymaking, heritage practice, public participation and industrial technology groups, ensuring the evaluation covers key participants across the full chain of grotto heritage presentation, technological application and social engagement. It mainly comprises academic and professional groups, management and policymakers, creators and heritage practitioners, and members of the public. Specifically, academic and professional groups ensure the academic rigor and knowledge validation of content; management and policymakers are responsible for establishing standards and allocating resources; creators and heritage practitioners lead the translation and representation of cultural content; and public participants engage in value co-construction through experience and feedback. Existing studies indicate that the optimal size of a focus group is typically 6–8 participants, while a range of 4–12 participants is also considered methodologically reasonable46. Accordingly, this study recruited a total of 26 experts from relevant fields for the evaluation, a scale notably larger than that of conventional focus group research, which further enhances the professionalism, reliability and interdisciplinary representativeness of the research findings. A total of 26 representative experts were invited for the study, who were assigned to four groups (6–7 participants in each group) in the evaluation of this method: the visitor group, the grotto research and management group, the interdisciplinary expert group, and the technology enterprise group. The participants represented both China and other countries (including the United Kingdom, Ukraine, India, and Nigeria) and came from diverse yet relevant domains—digital technology development, human–computer interaction design, grotto cultural heritage, design research, and grotto management. All experts and visitors had at least five years of professional experience in their respective fields. The experts’ organisational affiliations and positions (anonymous) and the visitors’ countries (anonymous) are listed in Appendix D. To enhance reliability and innovation, each expert completed the evaluation independently and anonymously, accompanied by open-ended feedback. This approach effectively avoided group influence and improved the validity, diversity, and authenticity of the data.

It should be noted that although this study focuses on representative grotto heritage sites within China, these sites possess global significance and civilisational value47. Therefore, taking grotto heritage within this region as the research subject provides important universal and instructive implications on a global scale, rather than being limited to regional research relevance.

Results

This study employs a mixed qualitative and quantitative research methodology and utilises multiple analysis methods to elaborate on the research framework from the progressive design logic of the model, the setting of content modules, and the design of application pathways.

Content design of DTH-AI model

The content module design of this framework is based on the results of data analysis obtained through quantitative and inductive analysis methods (derived from Sect. "Literature and case studies", “Questionnaire analysis”, and “Field observation”), corresponding to four modules: AI assistance, grotto heritage characteristics, digitalisation path, and strategy design. The four modules correspond respectively to technical support, heritage content identification, digital exhibition implementation and overall system governance, and together form the core operational logic of DTH-AI. Specifically, the AI-assisted module primarily refers to functions such as mural restoration, content generation and intelligent interaction, achieved through data analysis and intelligent recognition and generation based on large language models; the cave heritage characteristics module primarily refers to visual cultural information—such as artistic styles, historical semantics, compositional patterns, material deterioration and regional culture—contained within cave murals, coloured sculptures and the spatial environment; the Digital Pathways module primarily refers to the realisation of heritage digital display and public engagement through Mixed Reality (MR), cross-platform dissemination, intelligent interactive devices, and online-offline integration mechanisms; whilst the Strategy Design module emphasises design thinking as its core, ensuring the framework’s implement ability and sustainable development through stakeholder collaboration, ethical standards, and mechanisms for continuous iteration and optimisation. Furthermore, through design thinking theory, it scientifically presents the digital intelligent application framework for grotto cultural heritage, accurately focusing on the limitations, trends, and optimisation of the four existing modules and their corresponding deep connections. The DTH-AI model establishes a four-dimensional integrated framework for the sustainable presentation of cave heritage. This framework encompasses four key modules: AI-enabled technology, heritage content characteristics, digital integration pathways, and strategic design. Design thinking serves as the core driving mechanism, underpinning the entire process of framework development and operation. The core functions and roles of the interaction areas within each module are as follows:

(1) The intersection of AI-assisted functions and digital integration pathways: Through agent automation, visualisation technologies and ethical guidelines, this area effectively enhances the system’s operational efficiency and scalability, providing technical support for the framework’s implementation.

(2) The intersection of digital integration pathways and the characteristics of cave heritage: This area emphasises regional adaptability, cross-platform dissemination capabilities and interactive devices to ensure the accuracy of cultural expression and public engagement, thereby highlighting the core value of the heritage.

(3) The intersection between strategic design and the characteristics of cave heritage: Guided by the principle of deep audience accessibility and adhering to visual communication principles, this approach integrates collaborative design involving multiple stakeholders and achieves long-term sustainable operation through an integrated online and offline mechanism.

(4) The intersection between AI-assisted approaches and strategic design: As an independent and critical dimension, its primary functions are to construct end-to-end processes, formulate implementation standards and define evaluation criteria, thereby ensuring the smooth implementation and long-term sustainable operation of the system.

Application pathway design

The application pathway of the DTH-AI framework is based on a project strategy design methodology and is divided into three iterative collaborative phases: the initial, mid-term, and final stages. Specifically, By adopting the IDEO six-stage design thinking process consisting of Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test, and Implement, this study adopts an embedded strategic design approach to realize the functional modules from (a) to (h) and their internal logical relationships in the application pathway of the framework shown in Fig. 5. Specifically, Empathize and Define jointly correspond to (a) Cross-Platform & Cross-Scenario, which meets the dual demands of online platforms and offline in-grotto visiting scenarios. Ideate corresponds to (b) Regionalization Type Adaptation, (c) Design Engineers, (d) Principles of LLMs Applications, and (e) Principles of Visual Applications. With the participation of the design and development team, pre-processing of mural digital resources is conducted in advance. The phased training mechanism of large language models and visual artistic feature rules are collaboratively formulated to jointly complete the contents numbered ①, ② and ③ corresponding to the Prototype stage (see the simulated application section in Sect. “Simulated application” for details). Test corresponds to (f) Stakeholders and (g) Public Engagement. An evaluation and review team composed of stakeholders is established, and iterative feedback is collected from the public during their actual visiting and participation process. Implement corresponds to (h) Interactive Devices, represented by smart wearable display devices such as MR equipment.

(2) Mid-Stage: Model training (d) follows a four-step procedure22: pre-processing (cleaning, annotation, classification of online digital content); pre-training (general modelling of Buddhist scriptures, artistic styles and historical contexts); post-training (optimisation of security and ethical issues regarding copyright and privacy); and iterative optimisation (automation of agents and adaptation to public and diverse application scenarios). It should be noted that, in accordance with the principles of visual art feature recognition (e), the design team will establish detailed content generation specifications at an early stage. These include the visual characteristics of mineral pigments and signs of ageing; the compositional and decorative patterns of mural imagery; the line-drawing outlines and colouring techniques used in mural painting; the colour characteristics of heritage from different dynasties; and the pathological features resulting from natural weathering, such as fading, fissures, flaking and powdering. This is based on the artistic characteristics (such as the ‘Lan Ye’ line-drawing technique) and missing content identified in the dataset and by historians (one of the stakeholders) as belonging to the 1351 Yuan Dynasty restoration. By extracting these mural art features and pre-embedding the aforementioned pre-training specifications into the language model, on the one hand, maximising the stability and effectiveness of large language model (LLM) training at the algorithmic level; on the other hand, enhancing the authenticity and accuracy of the generated content for the incomplete murals in terms of sustainability performance33. It should be noted that the term “artistic characteristics” refers to the patterns of visual cultural expression in cave murals that have emerged within specific historical contexts and can be digitally identified and extracted. It is defined as a comprehensive visual information system that combines historical chronological value, cultural semantic attributes, and AI learnability. In this study, this specifically includes the composition and aging characteristics of pigments, compositional rules, painting techniques, cultural background, and traces of natural weathering. Among these, pigment composition and weathering conditions reflect the material properties and chronological patterns of the murals; compositional forms and the arrangement of elements such as Buddha figures and donors embody religious narrative logic and the aesthetics of the era; painting techniques such as line drawing and colour application reflect the characteristics of the artistic language of a specific historical period; and natural weathering phenomena—such as cracks, fading, flaking, and powdering—provide crucial evidence of authenticity for digital restoration and intelligent generation. Consequently, it serves as a crucial foundation within the DTH-AI framework for AI-assisted identification of digital heritage, enabling intelligent generation, style recognition, and sustainable digital presentation.

(3) Final stage: Once the above stage has been completed, stakeholders (academic and professional communities, managers, and application developers) will provide reviews and feedback (f). Furthermore, public participants (g) not only take part in the initial collaborative creation but also use terminal MR interactive devices (including other smart wearable devices) as viewers (h) to engage in a blended virtual-physical viewing experience within Cave 61. On this basis, on the one hand, to meet viewers’ needs for convenient and user-friendly navigation (as indicated by questionnaire survey results) and, on the other hand, to provide real-time, iterative and bidirectional feedback. Within this framework, MR technology primarily utilises spatial positioning, 3D environmental mapping and visual recognition to overlay AI-generated mural restoration content, historical information and interactive digital layers onto the actual cave space, enabling users to access digitally enhanced content whilst retaining a sense of the physical heritage space. Through MR headset devices, users can interact in real time with mural details, historical scene reconstructions and multi-dimensional guided tour information via gesture recognition, eye tracking, spatial anchoring and voice interaction. Simultaneously, the system captures users’ interactions, dwell times and areas of focus in real time, feeding this data back to the AI module for continuous iterative optimisation. This enables dynamic adjustments to guided routes, information presentation and interactive content, creating a cyclical mechanism of ‘physical space – digital augmentation – user feedback – system optimisation’ to enhance the immersion, interactive efficiency and sustainable application capabilities of the grotto’s digital exhibition.

In comparison with recent frameworks related to digital heritage, such as the dual-path model of perceived benefits and risks that explains the impact of AI-driven immersive experiences on user behaviour and willingness, and the generative AI framework for the reconstruction and dissemination of cultural heritage that focuses on technological processes and the expansion of application scenarios6,49. existing research has largely concentrated on technological applications or a single user-centric approach, lacking systematic support at the strategic design level. In contrast, the DTH-AI framework developed in this study centres on ‘stage embedding + dual-pathway integration’. By synergistically integrating the six processes of design thinking with the three stages of strategic design, it combines AI-assisted restoration, the identification of visual artistic features in heritage, and dynamic interactive iteration mechanisms. Consequently, it demonstrates greater theoretical coherence and practical adaptability in the sustainable development of digital heritage and its application in real-world projects.

Due to restrictions set by the Dunhuang Academy’s internal management policies regarding the caves, this study conducted a simulated application case based on the visual brightness conditions of the caves as recorded in the official Digital Dunhuang online platform dataset50. However, issues such as AI scene recognition and user experience on MR devices still require ongoing, in-depth collaboration with application development teams and cave management authorities to address. For instance, when conducting simulated MR demonstrations within the physical cave space, the system is susceptible to objective factors such as lighting conditions and spatial structures within the cave. Additionally, potential challenges in practical applications include noise generated by visitors, interference from human figure recognition, and regulations regarding visitor route design.

Discussion

This section is divided into two parts: inter-discussion and intra-discussion and outlines the limitations of this study.

Intra-discussion

(1) In terms of terminal interaction and user understanding, the standardisation of digital intelligence integration can be improved. In the era of digital intelligence, the partial extension of AI based on computer visualisation and the London Charter principles in this study is reflected in the specific integration path level. However, in the future, the sustainability of data recording and the security of sharing and transmission need to be strengthened to further expand the depth of theoretical principles and their practical guidance value.

(2) Although this study has incorporated a team of design engineers into the early stages of the project, supported by large language models, to optimise and mitigate AI-related issues such as ethics and copyright in advance, whilst standardising visual criteria such as the ageing characteristics of murals and compositional principles to ensure cultural authenticity from the outset and has undergone a secondary review by a stakeholder team during the final stages to reinforce the strategy of addressing ethical and cultural authenticity issues proactively. However, ethical issues surrounding the application of artificial intelligence within the DTH-AI framework remain highly complex and uncertain. Firstly, as generative AI is driven by the Dunhuang dataset, its outputs may be influenced by biases in the training data, gaps in historical records, and algorithmic mechanisms. This can lead to errors arising from the incorrect application or misuse of artistic features during the training phase, thereby compromising the authenticity and academic credibility of the mural heritage content. Secondly, there are marked differences in how various stakeholders interpret ‘authenticity’, ‘restoration boundaries’ and ‘digital reproduction’. Consequently, even if uniform standards are established, significant discrepancies in evaluation may still arise among experts due to differences in disciplinary backgrounds and cognitive perspectives. Furthermore, AI-assisted restoration must address the issue of homogenisation in cultural expression. Future research should establish a framework for the ‘human-AI’ partnership to avoid excessive homogenisation or excessive variation in digital content, whilst also enhancing the scientific rigour and effectiveness of both preliminary planning and post-project evaluation. Consequently, this study posits that, as a collaborative tool for restoration, artificial intelligence should fundamentally be grounded in the leading role of human experts, the joint participation of stakeholders, and continuous ethical oversight. The future application of artificial intelligence in digital heritage will require the establishment of a more comprehensive governance system, encompassing aspects such as algorithmic transparency, cultural interpretative authority, and mechanisms for authenticity assessment.

(3) In the framework path, real-time iterative feedback from public participations highlighted as an important part of the final stages, emphasising the user-centred thinking and methods of design thinking, and paying more attention to the intangible contribution of the public who visit the grottoes, rather than a point-to-point, one-way audience. Instead, it focuses more on the iterative feedback of data flows such as user behaviour and emotional perception embedded through terminal AI interaction, as a source of continuous optimisation in digital intelligence integration. Although the evaluation expert group did not provide further feedback on this dimension, its dimension score and overall recognition received a high level of positive affirmation.

(4) The content module of the DTH-AI model is set in a specific integration application path. Although it has been highly recognised by the expert group com-posed of stakeholders in grotto heritage, it still needs to be implemented in practice through multi-party collaboration and feedback on actual problems in order to further optimise the model path of this study. In addition, future research could introduce sensitivity analysis of key influencing factors within the specific application pathways of the framework, such as the quality of datasets and generated information, as well as the prioritisation of stakeholder needs. This would demonstrate significant application potential for the present study and ultimately enhance the robustness of the proposed framework.

(5) Based on the overall mean feedback from all expert groups in Table 5, the dimensions of Integration Standards and Theoretical Principles Expansion, although meeting the criteria of effectiveness and success, remain the two relatively weaker dimensions. These areas should be given priority for continued in-depth research in the future.

(6) Research on Digital Dunhuang has begun shifting from the display itself towards empirical analysis of public aesthetic perception and the sustainability of cultural dissemination7. While this study focuses on stakeholders, there remains a lack of in-depth research concerning sustainable public perception.

(7) This study selected two large grotto complexes and four associated satellite grottoes as case samples, which are representative in terms of scale and heritage type, whilst also taking into account the generalisability of the framework. The results of the focus group evaluation showed that the DTH-AI framework achieved a composite score of 4.27 across all generalisability dimensions, exceeding the validity threshold of 2.5 and demonstrating good overall performance. However, there are marked differences in heritage forms and artistic systems among grottoes across different regions of China. For instance, the Longmen Grottoes, which primarily feature stone carvings, differ in their display and restoration logic from the Mogao and Yulin Grottoes, which centre on murals and polychrome sculptures12. Consequently, although this study relies on artistic feature recognition and AI-assisted restoration mechanisms to ensure the framework possesses a certain degree of generality and transferability in the contexts of murals, polychrome sculptures and some stone carving heritage sites, its adaptability in cross-regional cultural contexts, sites with specific types of damage, and other non-grotto cultural heritage scenarios remains to be further verified. Future research could conduct empirical applications across different types of cultural heritage to further enhance the framework’s generalisation capabilities and practical value.

Inter-discussion

(1) Although the AI-assisted generation in this study is a theoretical strategy within the framework, its specific technical implementation requires AI development professionals to refer to this research framework and integrate algorithm efficiency-related technologies in the actual application development process. The application case of Cave 61 developed through joint simulation in Sect. “Simulated application”, the following preliminary qualitative conclusions based on a process-oriented preliminary assessment51: First, in low-light environments, visitors are prone to cognitive disorientation regarding the cave’s actual spatial layout, posing potential safety risks such as collisions and personal injury. Therefore, it is necessary to regulate the presentation of virtual content on MR headsets by setting a mandatory threshold for maximum immersion, enabling visitors to perceive the location and distance of other visitors within the cave, thereby ensuring safety during the tour. Second, during AI-assisted interactive demonstrations, voice interactions are susceptible to interference from background noise generated by multiple visitors inside the grotto. This may lead to AI recognition errors and cause crosstalk from similar voice commands issued by other visitors, thereby degrading the visitor experience. Therefore, it is essential to prioritize optimizing the noise reduction capabilities and recognition accuracy of the MR devices’ voice input channels. Furthermore, the question of whether AI should fully accommodate visitors’ diverse personalized needs (such as real-time content generation) may pose a dual challenge to the operational performance of MR devices and visitors’ wait times. Additionally, the real-time generation of virtual mural content and its application effectiveness may face limitations in computing power and generation mechanisms. To address this issue, The Variable Generation Machine (VCM) and its extended models (such as SVCM, MSVCM, and DSM)52, it can provide potential feature generation support for the framework. By automatically constructing highly correlated and highly discriminative features through variable relationship learning, they can identify the implicit correlations between mural textures, colors, forms, and historical styles under the condition of limited samples, thereby reducing the problem of “visually reasonable but historically distorted” results caused by data missing in traditional generation models. This mechanism is particularly applicable to digital heritage scenarios with characteristics of damage, aging, and incomplete information, such as Dunhuang murals and colored sculptures.

At the same time, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)53 can provide support at the level of physical constraints for the framework. By embedding partial differential equations (PDEs), material mechanics relationships, and surface degradation laws into the neural network training process, the generated results not only meet visual similarity but also conform to real physical structures and material change rules. Compared with traditional data-driven models, PINNs help reduce the impact of AI hallucination on heritage authenticity and improve the stability and credibility of crack simulation, surface weathering prediction, and three-dimensional spatial reconstruction. Above methods can provide support for the modelling work of this module, helping to achieve higher modelling accuracy and interpretability. Specifically, the pre-training phase of the language model in the initial stage of the DTH-AI framework can be applied to the extraction, intelligent detection and generation of visual art features of murals, effectively improving the prediction effect and accuracy of the model; this phase can not only be used for the intelligent restoration of mutilated murals and damaged coloured sculptures, but also serve the iterative review work of stakeholders in the later stage, thereby improving the operational efficiency of the entire framework and the accuracy of the public’s interpretation of heritage.

(2) Building on this foundation and incorporating digital twin technology, it is possible to achieve dynamic interaction and continuous optimisation between virtual and physical systems, thereby providing new technical approaches for the monitoring, management and presentation of cultural heritage.

(3) Furthermore, although the initial stages of the DHT-AI framework’s implementation path specify that the framework operates on the basis of a digital dataset which integrates the existing Digital Dunhuang data platform (including copyright)15,48, the later stages of the AI-assisted restoration process still require the involvement of stakeholders such as historians and cultural experts for review. This is a key foundation for ensuring data usability and quality during the final stages of the MR interactive iterative demonstration. Furthermore, to ensure the scalability of this framework, it remains necessary for cave heritage sites of large, medium and small scales to establish databases similar to ‘Digital Dunhuang’, a matter requiring the continued attention of the respective cave management authorities.

(4) Furthermore, the DTH-AI framework has been designed, in parts (a) and (b) of the application pathway shown in Fig. 5, to integrate with existing digital heritage databases, 3D scanning platforms and smart museum systems, thereby enhancing the efficiency of digital resource integration and public engagement. On the other hand, sections (e), (f), (g) and (h) of the framework’s design incorporate mechanisms for stakeholder collaboration and sustainable iteration; their logic aligns closely with current industry priorities in the field of digital cultural heritage, such as the preservation of authenticity, digital archiving, ethical standards and long-term sustainable management. Consequently, DTH-AI not only serves as a strategic framework for the digital and intelligent display of cave heritage but also offers valuable reference for the intelligent management and display of future digital museums, historic buildings, murals and sculptural cultural heritage. Therefore, the DTH-AI framework is not only applicable to digital display scenarios for cave heritage but also possesses the potential for integration with existing digital heritage platforms and industry standards.

Limitation

The following limitations exist in this study: (1) At the level of terminal interaction and user understanding, the process standardisation of digital intelligence integration needs to be improved, and the sustainability of data recording and the security of sharing and transmission still need to be strengthened in order to expand the depth of theoretical principles and the value of practical guidance. (2) Despite the early integration of design engineers, the adoption of visual art feature standards, and the use of large language models to avoid AI ethical and copyright risks in advance, the task of iterative review by stakeholders at a later stage is large. (3) Although real-time iterative feedback and user-centred public participation are emphasised, the study on the sustainability of public perception is not in-depth enough. (4) Although Mogao Caves, as a case study, is representative and the framework is scalable in its design, the framework has limitations in adapting to cross-cultural scenarios and special heritage (e.g., heavily damaged rock carvings) due to the differences in the morphology of the regional grottoes. (5) The two dimensions of integration criteria and theoretical principle expansion meet the criteria but are relatively weak dimensions that need to be focused on in subsequent research. (6) Although the content module of the DTH-AI model is highly recognised, it still needs to rely on multi-party collaboration to put it into practice and continue to optimise the model path in combination with the feedback from practical applications.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study constructs the DTH-AI model, a sustainability strategy framework and implementation pathway for digital-intelligent integrated applications exemplified by cave temple cultural heritage. The innovation and application pathways of the framework have been evaluated and verified. Project-based strategy design from a stakeholder perspective proposes dual pathways for digital heritage: “auxiliary restoration” and “artistic visual characteristics”. It provides an effective strategic framework of AI-assisted restoration and iterative interaction for the digitally intelligent sustainable development of grotto mural heritage.

Future research should not only address the sub-fields identified in the discussion section, but also further investigate the practical effectiveness of digitally intelligent technologies in heritage interpretation and public engagement, as well as the institutional frameworks governing the iteration, security, and sustainable transmission of research data. At the same time, pilot implementation applications at the Mogao Caves, together with cross-cultural scenario validation across other comparable grotto heritage sites, should be explored to further examine the adaptability, scalability, and long-term sustainability of the framework within different cultural contexts and application environments, thereby continuously enhancing the theoretical contribution and practical significance of the research. In addition, collaboration among stakeholders and practical projects should be strengthened, and an iterative feedback mechanism capable of improving public engagement should be established in order to promote a sustainable and public-centred participation model.
