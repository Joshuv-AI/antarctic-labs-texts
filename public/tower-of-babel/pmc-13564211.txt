Telehealth use among US adults with type 2 diabetes: differences in medication fill patterns and care processes
Authors: Terrence T Liu, John Thomas Menchaca, Alexander Chaitoff, Boomer Olsen, Julia E Szymczak, Jorie Butler, Kensaku Kawamoto, Renuka Tipirneni, Molly B Conroy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe expansion of telehealth following the COVID-19 pandemic has enhanced access to care, but questions remain about how well telehealth supports the delivery of comprehensive, guideline-concordant care for type 2 diabetes.MethodsWe conducted an observational cross-sectional study using nationally representative survey data from the 2021-2023 Medical Expenditures Panel Survey to make unadjusted descriptive comparisons between adults with any diabetes-related telehealth use and those receiving only in-person diabetes care.ResultsOf 4348 respondents who had at least one visit for type 2 diabetes from 2021 to 2023, 90.7% had only in-person visits, whereas 9.3% had ≥1 telehealth visits. Insulin prescriptions were more common among telehealth users compared to nonusers (47.5% [95% CI, 41.0%-54.1%] vs 34.4% [95%CI, 32.1%-36.7%]).ConclusionOverall, this study provides current national data on patterns of telehealth use among US adults with type 2 diabetes. Differences in insulin prescription fill patterns may reflect greater clinical complexity and care needs among patients who use telehealth. These findings offer important context for policy-makers and health systems as they evaluate the role of telehealth in managing chronic disease and consider how virtual care models can be implemented while maintaining high-quality diabetes care.Keywords: telehealth, diabetes

Introduction

Telehealth care for management of type 2 diabetes can expand access while maintaining quality comparable to in-person visits.1,2 Despite the growth of telehealth services, little is known about how telehealth is used for diabetes care nationally or how care experiences differ between telehealth and in-person care beyond single health systems.3-5 As telehealth-based care models expand and evolve, understanding the current landscape of diabetes care delivery in this context is essential to inform how emerging virtual and hybrid care models may influence diabetes care.6 Without this knowledge, growing adoption of telehealth-based care models may risk unintended consequences in care quality if the limitations of virtual care are not fully reconciled with its advantages.

To address this gap, we used nationally representative survey data to examine differences among US adults using telehealth vs in-person care for type 2 diabetes across 3 domains: (1) patient demographics and health characteristics, (2) prescription fills and diabetes care processes, and (3) self-reported experiences with care delivery.

Study data and methods

Data source

We used pooled 2021-2023 data from the Household Component and Prescribed Medicines files of the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS), which includes demographic, healthcare utilization, and prescription medication data for a nationally representative sample of community-dwelling US adults.7 Starting with the 2021 MEPS survey, data were collected on whether each outpatient visit was conducted through telehealth (“Was your visit to your provider over the phone, through real-time video, or some other way?”) or in-person.8 For each outpatient visit, respondents were asked whether the visit was related to a specific medical condition and only visits linked with an International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision diagnosis of type 2 diabetes were included in our study.9 Individuals with ≥1 telehealth diabetes visit were classified as telehealth users; others were classified as nonusers. We analyzed data from January 2021 through December 2023.

Diabetes medication prescription fills

We examined individual diabetes medication prescription fills during the 3-year study period, comparing the prevalence of receiving diabetes medication prescriptions by class between telehealth users and nonusers. MEPS collects information from survey participants about their prescription drugs and for permission to collect more detailed information from their pharmacies.10 Pharmacy data include the type, dosage, and payment for each filled prescription. Respondents were classified as having received a prescription if they had ≥1 fill or refill of a diabetes medication for the following medication classes: 1) metformin, 2) insulin, 3) sulfonylurea, 4) glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, 5) sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitor, 6) dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP-4) inhibitor, and 7) Thiazolidinedione.

Diabetes care processes and patient experiences of care

We assessed diabetes quality of care processes available in the Diabetes Care Survey (DCS), including measures of confidence with diabetes self-management and self-reported screening. We assessed patient experiences of care and affordability of care using the MEPS Self-Administered Questionnaire (SAQ). Both SAQ and DCS are supplemental surveys administered to a subgroup of the respondents in the Household Component.

Statistical analysis

For all analyses, we compared outcomes between telehealth users and nonusers. We pooled annual observations from 2021 through 2023. The unit of analysis was the person-year. Within each person-year, respondents were classified as telehealth users if they had at least one diabetes-related telehealth visit and as nonusers if all diabetes-related visits were in person. Medication outcomes indicated at least one fill or refill within that survey year. We used MEPS person weights to produce nationally representative estimates.11,12 All analyses were descriptive, unadjusted, and conducted using Stata 18. This study was exempted from institutional board review because of the use of deidentified, publicly available data.

Study results

Patient demographics and health characteristics

Of 4348 respondents who had at least 1 visit for type 2 diabetes from 2021 to 2023, 90.7% had only in-person visits, whereas 9.3% had ≥1 telehealth visits (Table 1). Over the study period, the proportion of telehealth users was highest in 2021 (11.1% [95% CI, 9.5%-13.1%]), followed by 2022 (8.6% [95% CI, 6.9%-10.7%]), and 2023 (8.1% [95% CI, 6.2%-10.4%]). Respectively, more telehealth users than nonusers reported the following: any physical or cognitive limitation (50.9% vs 47.3%), fair or poor health (39.4% vs 32.1%), and higher mean total number of outpatient visits for diabetes (7 [95% CI, 5-8] vs 4 [95% CI, 3-4]). Telehealth users and nonusers were otherwise similar in age, sex, race and ethnicity, education level, and income.

Diabetes medication prescription fills and care processes

Metformin was the most commonly filled medication class, followed by insulin (Table 1). Insulin prescription fills were more common among telehealth users (47.5% [95% CI, 41.0%-54.1%]) than among nonusers (34.4% [95%CI, 32.1%-36.7%]). 75.4% [95% CI, 56.3%-83.4%] of telehealth users reported using diet modification for diabetes treatment compared to 77.0% [95% CI, 73.7%-79.9%] of nonusers. Telehealth users had their hemoglobin A1c checked 4 [95% CI, 1-8] times per year, compared to 3 [95% CI, 2-4] times per year for nonusers. There were similar rates of self-reported nephropathy and retinopathy complications between telehealth users and nonusers, while fewer telehealth users reported ever receiving an in-person diabetic foot exam (Table 2). Fewer telehealth users reported being very confident or confident in taking care of their diabetes (62% [95% CI, 51%-72%]) compared to nonusers (77% [95% CI, 74% to 80%]).

Patient experiences of care

Respectively, telehealth users and nonusers reported similar prevalences of the following: delaying prescription medications due to worry about cost (7.9% [95% CI, 4.3%-14.1%] vs 8.0% [95% CI, 6.5%-9.8%]) and inability to receive needed prescription medications due to cost (6.4% [95% CI, 3.3%-12.0%] vs 6.8% [95% CI, 5.4%-8.5%]). For patient-centered care measures, telehealth users and nonusers reported similar levels of information sharing (“Does your usual source of care present and explain all options to you?”), care coordination (“Does your usual source of care usually ask about prescription medications and treatments other doctors may give?”), and shared decision-making (“If there were a choice between treatments, how often would your usual source of care ask you to help make the decision?”) with their clinician (Table 2).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional analysis of a nationally representative sample of US adults with an outpatient visit for type 2 diabetes from 2021 to 2023, 1 in 10 individuals used telehealth for their visit. Telehealth users reported poorer overall health and more functional limitations, and had greater insulin prescription fills, compared to telehealth nonusers. Perceptions of patient-centered communication were similar between groups, whereas lower confidence levels in the ability to self-manage diabetes were observed among telehealth users.

Our finding that telehealth users reported poorer health status and greater functional limitations is consistent with prior studies of telehealth use in other populations, which presents an opportunity for telehealth to potentially improve access to care for individuals with greater health needs.13-15

Prior studies that examined telehealth use specifically for type 2 diabetes care at a national population level are limited. One study using data from Epic Cosmos, a large US electronic health record (EHR) vendor, found that 0.04% of US adults with type 2 diabetes had a telehealth visit specific for diabetes care between March 2019 and February 2020, which increased to 18% during the period from March 2020 to February 2021.16 Given these findings were during the early COVID-19 pandemic and focused on health systems with Epic EHRs, our study provides new insights into how diabetes care is delivered in the current era of hybrid in-person and telehealth care models following the pandemic.

Insulin prescription fills were more common among telehealth users. Because insulin is often prescribed for more advanced or poorly controlled diabetes,17 this finding may reflect differences in underlying disease severity, an important confounder in this study. As adults with type 2 diabetes continue to receive care via telehealth, future studies should closely monitor prescribing pattern trends and investigate whether observed differences reflect disease severity or whether telehealth can facilitate care for patients using insulin.

Although newer higher-cost agents, including GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT-2 inhibitors, have expanded treatment options for type 2 diabetes, lower-cost metformin remained the most commonly prescribed medication in this study. Given that approximately 6%-8% of individuals reported delaying or not obtaining medications because of cost, the availability of lower-cost therapies can be critical for patients with affordability concerns.

Dietary modification was reported by only 3 out of 4 individuals as part of their diabetes management, with similar rates among telehealth users and nonusers. This finding suggests an opportunity to strengthen non-pharmacologic management, a foundational component of diabetes care, regardless of in-person vs telehealth care settings.17

Rates of diabetes-related complications were similar between groups; however, ever receiving an in-person diabetic foot examination was less common among telehealth users. While these outcomes are self-reported and do not correspond to guideline-concordant annual screening intervals, these findings highlight potential challenges for telehealth-based diabetes care. Prior studies have also found that telehealth use is associated with lower rates of guideline-concordant diabetes care, including reduced rates of diabetic eye examinations and hemoglobin A1c testing.18,19 This may reflect inherent limitations of telehealth, including absence of physical examination and the need for additional coordination to obtain laboratory testing outside of the clinical encounter, which will be important to recognize as patients receive both in-person and telehealth-based diabetes care.

When more than 1 treatment option was available, patients reported being involved in decision-making only about half of the time with their clinicians, with similar rates among telehealth users and nonusers. This finding highlights an opportunity to improve shared decision-making in diabetes care, particularly given the growing number of treatment options with different cost considerations.20,21 Engaging patients in shared decision-making to balance the effectiveness, tolerability, and cost of both nonpharmacologic and pharmacologic options will play an increasingly important role in delivering high-quality, patient-centered diabetes care.

Limitations

This study had limitations. First, all comparisons were unadjusted and descriptive. Telehealth users had poorer self-reported health, more functional limitations, and more diabetes-related visits. Differences in insulin fills, foot examinations, and self-management confidence may reflect disease severity, care intensity, or other unmeasured characteristics rather than telehealth use. Second, the SAQ and DCS surveys were only administered in years 2021-2022, and the DCS does not differentiate between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Third, DCS data does not contain hemoglobin A1c laboratory values, a widely used clinical measure to assess well-controlled vs poorly controlled diabetes. Fourth, the DCS questions used in this study ask if diabetic foot exam or retinopathy screening were “ever” performed and do not necessarily correlate to guideline-concordant annual screening intervals.

Conclusion

Overall, this study describes national-level findings on patterns of telehealth use among US adults with type 2 diabetes, showing that telehealth users self-reported worse health and had higher insulin prescription fills. Although the descriptive, observational, and cross-sectional study design precludes causal inference, these differences in prescription fill patterns may reflect greater clinical complexity and care needs among telehealth users. These findings offer important context for policy-makers and health systems as they evaluate the role of telehealth in managing chronic disease and consider how virtual care models can be implemented while maintaining high-quality diabetes care.
