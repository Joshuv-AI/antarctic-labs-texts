Serum calcium levels are independently associated with severe coronary artery stenosis combined with severe cerebral artery stenosis
Authors: Yi Wang, Xinyu Qiu, Yichen Wang, Yawei Gu, Lan Zhao, Xuemei Liang, Yinhua Dong, Yun Shi, Lijun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCardiovascular and cerebrovascular illnesses typically coexist clinically, resulting in increased rates of disability and fatality. Current clinical research on the link between albumin-corrected calcium (Ca) and the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disorders is contentious. To address a void in this study field, we intend to investigate the relationship between Ca and severe coronary artery stenosis (Degree of vascular stenosis ≥ 70%) coexisting with severe cerebral artery stenosis (Degree of vascular stenosis ≥ 70%) (SCSC).MethodsThis single-center retrospective study enrolled 197 symptomatic patients with ischemic Cardio-CerebroVascular comorbidities. After preprocessing the data, we employed elastic net regression to automatically select the most informative variables from the large dataset. Stable variables were incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model to determine their statistical significance and effect size. Variables demonstrating statistical significance were chosen as core variables for incremental value analysis based on traditional risk factor models. Finally, to assess potential nonlinear relationships, generalized correlation models were applied to all continuous variables, and dose–response curves were fitted for statistically significant variables.ResultsSCSC is commonly observed in patients with symptomatic ischemic cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications (36.04%). Significant associations exist between Ca, international normalized ratio, monocytes, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol with SCSC. Among these, Ca exhibits the strongest and most stable association with SCSC (selection frequency = 99.51%; OR = 2.21; 95% CI: 1.34–3.64; p = 0.002). Ca also contributes the greatest incremental value to the baseline model (∆AUC = -0.040, ∆Birer = -0.017). After controlling for traditional risk factors and serum phosphorus, renal function, and lipid profile indicators that may influence Ca levels, the relationship between Ca and SCSC remained statistically significant (OR = 2.12; 95% CI: 1.27–3.56; p = 0.005).ConclusionThere may be a close relationship between Ca and SCSC, but further research is needed to confirm whether a causal association exists.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04828-2.

Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (CCVD) are a major global disease burden, constantly ranking high in disability-adjusted life years. Their rates of disability and mortality remain persistently high [1]. Atherosclerosis is the leading cause of coronary heart disease and strokes. Early detection of vascular lesions is critical for patients satisfying the criteria for revascularization. Although the coronary and cerebral arteries differ anatomically and functionally, their stenoses show a strong correlation [2, 3]. Hypertension, diabetes, and advanced age all increase the risk of simultaneous stenosis in both the cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems [4]. Patients with severe coronary artery stenosis coexisting with severe cerebral artery stenosis (SCSC) exhibit poorer clinical outcomes, making the identification of their specific risk factors crucial for clinical decision-making [5].

Calcium is an essential mineral for the human body, crucial for bone health, nerve transmission, and muscle contraction. Calcium ions are important second messengers in various activities, including contraction-relaxation coupling in cardiomyocytes, tension modulation in vascular smooth muscle cells, and neural activity [6]. Although numerous mechanisms link calcium intake and serum calcium levels to the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, conclusions about this relationship are still debated [7]. On the one hand, studies have shown that calcium supplements are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, but this association varies across different population subgroups [8–10]. On the other hand, a recent meta-analysis found no significant association between calcium supplementation and myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, or all-cause/cardiovascular mortality [11]. Regarding the relationship between blood calcium levels and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, some studies suggest that elevated serum calcium levels may be an independent risk factor for these conditions [12–14]. Some research suggests that low serum calcium levels are associated with poor cardiovascular outcomes [15, 16], whereas another study found no significant association between serum calcium and acute coronary syndrome [17]. Recent prospective studies suggest that the relationship between serum calcium levels and cardiovascular disease risk may not follow a simple linear pattern but instead exhibits a U-shaped curve. This means that both excessively high and excessively low serum calcium levels may raise cardiovascular risk [18]. This nonlinear association is more compelling, suggesting that abnormalities in calcium homeostasis may enhance cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risks.

The aforementioned studies have a fundamental disadvantage: they primarily focus on clinical endpoints of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular illnesses, whereas the direct pathophysiological relationship between serum calcium and stenotic lesions of the cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems has received minimal attention. We hypothesize that serum calcium is independently associated with SCSC. As a result, based on personal history, past medical history, and venous blood test signs, we use angiography to diagnose vascular stenosis and support our theory. This approach allows us to gain more direct pathophysiological insights into cardiac and cerebral vascular stenosis, offering new evidence for the diagnosis, treatment, or screening of high-risk CCVD populations and exposing possible therapeutic research avenues.

Methods

Subjects

This study selected eligible symptomatic patients who underwent cardiac and cerebral angiography from the angiography registry of Tianjin Medical University Fourth Central Hospital, from November 2019 until the pre-specified data lock date (August 2025). All patients underwent coronary angiography and cerebral angiography either concurrently or separately within a two-month period. It should be noted that the retrospective methodology and single-center data source may introduce selection bias, a significant weakness of this study. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Central Hospital of Tianjin Medical University (Approval No. SZXLL-2025-K023).

All enrolled patients were hospitalized individuals who either: (a) presented with highly clinically suggestive cardiac and/or cerebral ischemic symptoms after excluding other organic pathologies, or (b) had well-documented prior cardiac and/or cerebral ischemic events. We define this cohort as symptomatic patients with ischemic cardio-cerebrovascular comorbidities. Therefore, this study specifically selected symptomatic patients with advanced disease and did not assess risk at the population level. The degree of cardiac and cerebral vascular stenosis in all patients was determined by digital subtraction angiography. At least two specialists at the associate director level or higher assessed the severity of vascular stenosis using visual estimates. In cases requiring semi-quantitative confirmation, the most severe visual estimation angle guided quantitative measurements of stenosis severity, with final results documented in the medical record system. Final analysis results were derived from independent review and analysis of angiographic data by one neurologist and one interventional cardiologist, with cross-validation of outcomes between both specialists. Both specialists were kept unaware of the patient's background, previous medical records, and blood test results. Analysis compared the two specialists' assessments against the medical record system results, demonstrating consistency in evaluation outcomes. Other clinical and laboratory data of the patients were reviewed by a designated person unrelated to this study, and the clinical and laboratory data of patients hospitalized twice were based on the first results. All blood specimens for laboratory data were collected 0–8 days before the angiogram (2.77 ± 1.81). This retrospective study collected data from the electronic medical record system of Tianjin Medical University Fourth Affiliated Hospital. Definition of endings

Our study analyzed the presence of SCSC in patients with combined cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. The objective was to identify factors associated with severe concomitant disease. In coronary and cerebral arteries, "stenosis" and "severe stenosis" are defined as stenosis of ≥ 50% and ≥ 70%, respectively [19–21]. This study evaluated only the major arteries within and outside the cranium, excluding small artery lesions. For specific definitions of other variables, see the "Clinical Definitions" section of the supplementary materials.

Data preprocessing

We aimed to include as many pathophysiological processes as possible to avoid overlooking potential associations. Subsequently, we examined the covariance between variables and confirmed that the variance inflation factor (VIF) was less than 10. Ultimately, after considering the clinical relevance of variables based on prior knowledge, we included 41 variables. Following the exclusion of variables with a missing rate exceeding 24%, the number of variables was reduced to 37 [22]. The variables included in our analysis encompassed traditional risk factors, comprehensive blood and biochemical indicators (reflecting inflammation, lipid metabolism, red blood cell and platelet function, hepatic and renal function, electrolytes, coagulation function, myocardial injury status, etc.). Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the missing patterns in the remaining data, confirming that the missing data were most likely random. Subsequently, we standardized continuous variables and used multiple imputation to fill missing data, yielding 40 imputed datasets [23]. The imputation model included all analysis variables and auxiliary variables related to missingness. Outliers in continuous variables were identified using Tukey's fences method (exceeding 1.5 times the interquartile range). To mitigate the impact of outliers on multiple imputation, we trimmed continuous variables to the 5th and 95th percentiles after multiple imputation for subsequent analysis.

Elastic net regression

Given the relatively large number of variables (n = 37) compared to the sample size (n = 197), elastic net regression demonstrates superior performance and stability when handling high-dimensional data and strongly correlated variables compared to lasso regression and stepwise regression methods [24, 25]. Therefore, we established an elastic net regression model. We used bootstrapping to ensure the reliability of the elastic network regression results. Each resampling iteration required re-estimating all coefficients. This method measures a variable's robustness to data ambiguity, with higher selection frequencies indicating a better chance of retaining predictive performance in future clinical applications [26, 27]. We used 85% as the unified stability threshold. Through inflection-point analysis of variable counts across several thresholds, we determined that 85% is the optimal stability threshold within the 70%−95% range. At the 85% threshold, 6 variables were retained, or 16.22% of all candidate variables. The average selection frequency of the chosen variables was 91.40%, showing strong stability.

Nonlinear analysis

We used generalized additive models (GAMs) to examine nonlinear interactions among continuous variables across several interpolated datasets, combining results from 40 datasets. We controlled for all laboratory variables, as well as age, gender, smoking history, drinking history, history of stroke, history of old myocardial infarction, history of atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and diabetes duration. Because the huge number of variables controlled for in the GAMs made it difficult to apply Rubin's principle to result integration, we eventually settled on the mean p-value plus standard deviation as a metric for analyzing nonlinear connections. When the mean p-value plus standard deviation remained less than 0.05, we concluded that the variable and the result had a stable nonlinear connection. Finally, for variables identified as stable using GAMs, we used restricted spline regression to investigate the dose–response associations between variables and outcomes. The extrema of the second derivative of the anticipated probability curve were calculated to detect inflection points.

Multivariate logistic regression

We integrated the final variables selected through elastic net regression. Subsequently, we incorporated the integrated variables to construct a multivariate logistic regression model and calculated odds ratios (OR) to compare the relative effects of different variables. The final multivariate logistic regression findings were combined using Rubin's method. For the core variables identified in the model, we further fitted four models. Model 1: No control variables; Model 2: Controlled for demographic factors; Model 3: Controlled for demographic factors + traditional cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk factors; Model 4: Model 3 plus potential confounding factors affecting the variables. Finally, we conducted an incremental value analysis using Model 3 to validate the association between these variables and the outcome.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted consistency testing by screening factors through Lasso regression fitting to evaluate the sensitivity of the Elastic Net regression. For the aforementioned Bootstrap process, we simultaneously fitted Elastic Net (α = 0.5) and LASSO (α = 1) models to obtain the variable selection frequencies. All other settings remained consistent. Finally, we computed two consistency statistics: the Jaccard Index and Cohen's Kappa Coefficient. Based on our previous experience, we interpret Kappa values and Jaccard indices as follows: Kappa values > 0.8 and Jaccard indices ≥ 0.75 indicate near-perfect agreement.

Statistical analysis

A p-value of < 0.05 for all tests in our study was considered statistically significant, and continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (Q1, Q3). In contrast, categorical variables were expressed as percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The t-test was used for normally distributed variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables. We performed albumin-corrected serum calcium measurements, defining Ca as albumin-corrected serum calcium. The correction formula is: Serum calcium (mmol/L) + (40—Albumin (g/L)) × 0.02. All analysis results were obtained using the R language (version 4.4.1). Set the random seed to 42. The 'mice' package (version 3.17.0) performs multiple imputation for missing data, whereas the 'glmnet' program (version 4.1–8) performs elastic net regression. The 'mgcv' package (version 1.9–1) performs GAMs testing, whereas the 'rms' program (version 8.0–0) conducts restricted cubic spline analysis. A detailed explanation of the aforementioned research methods can be found in the “Supplementary Methods” section of the supplementary materials.

Results

Epidemiology and general features

We enrolled 224 patients and excluded 27, yielding data on 197 individuals, 177 of whom received simultaneous cardiac and cerebral interventional angiography. The bulk of the symptomatic patients with ischemic cardio-cerebrovascular comorbidities cohort (62.44%) consisted of men, with ages ranging from 48 to 86 years (65.98 ± 7.57 years). Of them, 153 had coronary artery stenosis (77.66%), 112 had cerebral artery stenosis (56.85%), and 71 had SCSC (36.04%). The median calcium level in this symptomatic, late-stage patient population was 2.29 mmol/L (2.25, 2.35), while the median calcium level in SCSC patients was 2.31 mmol/L (2.28, 2.39). This difference was statistically significant compared to patients without SCSC (Z = −3.85; p < 0.001). Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of all variables in the study population and displays the findings of the differential analysis between those with and without SCSC.

Core variable screening

Table 1. presents the variables retained following elastic net regression analysis, their respective selection frequencies, and the outcomes after these variables were incorporated into multivariate logistic regression. The four variables with the highest selection frequencies identified via elastic net regression also exhibited statistically significant associations when included in multivariate logistic regression (p < 0.05). Among the four variables, calcium exhibited the highest stability (99.51%) and the largest OR value (OR = 2.21; 95% CI: 1.34–3.64), indicating a reliable association between calcium and SCSC across all data attribution and sampling schemes. Elevated monocyte (MON) levels also showed correlation with SCSC. In contrast, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and international normalized ratio (INR) exhibited comparable OR values and negative associations with SCSC. As depicted in Fig. 2, Ca, MON, HDL-C, and INR may represent core associated factors for SCSC, a finding warranting further validation.

Ca maintains a robust and consistent positive correlation with SCSC risk. This association remains statistically significant and stable across all four models. Model 4, which incorporates renal function indicators, serum phosphorus, and lipid profile—factors that may influence Ca levels—still demonstrates a strong relationship between Ca and SCSC risk. INR also showed a statistically significant association with SCSC in all models. In Model 4, which included hematologic, hepatic, renal, lipid, and other coagulation-related markers, the OR reached 0.51 (95% CI: 0.30–0.86, p = 0.012). After adjusting for all hematologic, hepatic, renal, metabolic, and coagulation biomarkers, MON lost statistical significance in the final model (p = 0.242). This may be due to Model 4 including too many variables (n = 29) within a limited sample size (n = 197), leading to model overfitting, increased parameter standard errors, and reduced overall statistical power. Ultimately, this resulted in the MON-SCSC association being obscured by noise. Of course, the association between MON and SCSC was most likely diluted by confounding from the variables ultimately included. Finally, in the analysis of HDL-C, statistical significance was only observed in Model 3, which accounted for major traditional cardiovascular risk factors (p = 0.038). The relationship between HDL-C and SCSC is complex: while it may be independent of other traditional risk factors, this association loses statistical significance in Model 4 after incorporating hematological indicators, liver and kidney function, serum iron, and other lipid parameters. This suggests the association may be masked or diluted by these additional indicators.

As shown in Table 3(a), the Ca model outperformed the univariate logistic regression models for the other three variables. This observation is consistent with earlier stability findings and the effect size of the OR values. Figure 3(a) and (b) display the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and calibration curves of the Ca model, providing a more intuitive assessment of the model's predictive performance. Table 3(b) details the incremental information provided by the four core variables compared to traditional risk factors. The AUC for the Ca incremental model was 0.748 (95% CI: 0.718–0.775), with an absolute gain of 0.040. Although the absolute gain in AUC was 0.001 lower than that of the MON incremental model, the Ca incremental model had the lowest Birer score among the four incremental models at 0.188. Furthermore, the Ca incremental model exhibited the highest Net Receiver Operating Characteristic Index (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement Index (IDI) among the four variable sets. Figure 3(c), (d) show the ROC curves and calibration curves comparing the Ca incremental model with the baseline model, respectively. The ROC and calibration curves for the other three variables are presented in Supplementary Figs. 2, 3, and 4.

Discussion

Calcium and vascular stenosis

Calcium ions are closely associated with the pathophysiological processes of vascular lesions, yet results from relevant clinical studies remain inconsistent. This may be due to differences in study populations, adjustment for confounding factors, and endpoint definitions. Our study defined the outcome as SCSC, providing a more direct reflection of the association between calcium and vascular stenotic lesions. Our findings indicate that calcium plays a crucial role in SCSC.

SCSC results reflect a high-risk phenotype defined by advanced cardiovascular stenotic burden, but do not represent the entire atherosclerotic burden. Since we excluded small- and medium-sized vessel lesions, this association between Ca and SCSC primarily supports the hypothesis that Ca may serve as a potential biomarker or associated factor for advanced, high-risk cardiovascular stenotic phenotypes. We validated the Ca-SCSC association through multiple dimensions: elastic net regression screening for SCSC, logistic regression adjusting for confounders, incremental information of Ca in SCSC discrimination, and nonlinear relationships between Ca and SCSC. Although a consistent and robust association persisted across these dimensions, our study cannot establish a causal relationship between Ca and SCSC.

Previous basic research has largely focused on calcium's influence on the initiation of vascular atherosclerosis [28–32]. It remains unclear whether calcium promotes or catalyzes this late-stage phenotype or merely accompanies it as a concomitant manifestation. Future studies should conduct basic or prospective research to determine whether a causal relationship exists between calcium and SCSC.

Monocytes, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, international normalized ratio

It has been extensively demonstrated that MON and HDL-C, as key players in the atherosclerotic plaque formation process, serve as independent predictors of CCVD incidence, mortality, and prognosis [33–36]. However, in this study, the association between these two factors and SCSC was no longer statistically significant after adjusting for all confounding variables. Our study's cohort did not include individuals on long-term, regular anticoagulant medication; consequently, the INR may better reflect the overall performance of the body's extrinsic coagulation pathway[37]. The negative association between INR and SCSC remained relatively stable and consistent across all analyses in this study, but its discriminatory ability for SCSC was poor, and its incremental value was also relatively limited.

In this small retrospective study, all laboratory values were single measurements, whereas vascular stenosis data were based on expert visual estimates, thereby inevitably introducing nondifferential measurement errors. Most importantly, observational associations do not imply causation. Therefore, we must acknowledge that this study aims to present an exploratory scientific hypothesis regarding late-stage phenotypes associated with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular stenosis and cannot be directly applied clinically. Notably, due to their lower stability, effect size, and incremental information value compared to the Ca variable, the aforementioned three variables are more susceptible to the study's limitations.

Exploratory discovery of nonlinear correlations

Our study reveals a nonlinear relationship between Ca and HDL-C levels and SCSC risk, with the association becoming more pronounced beyond a certain threshold. However, we must recognize that the dose–response curve, particularly its inflection point, is entirely dependent on data analysis. Our study merely proposes a scientific hypothesis suggesting a potential nonlinear relationship between these factors and the high-risk phenotype in patients with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular stenosis. This finding underscores the importance of considering possible nonlinear associations in future research.

Limitations and outlook

This study has limitations: 1. The retrospective, single-center design inherently introduces selection bias and cannot establish causality. We only included symptomatic patients who underwent repeated coronary angiography and cerebral angiography within two months. This cohort likely represents a high-risk subgroup with advanced disease. Additionally, we excluded small-to-medium artery disease, limiting generalizability. Our findings cannot be extrapolated to asymptomatic patients or those with mild-to-moderate stenosis. Furthermore, sequential angiography within two months may introduce temporal misclassification and survivor bias. 2. We measured serum calcium levels only once prior to angiography and did not account for potential residual confounders such as parathyroid hormone, vitamin D, dietary intake, or chronic kidney disease. Consequently, our study cannot determine whether these factors confound or mediate this association. 3. Our research on vascular stenosis assessment methods primarily relies on visual estimation. Although the evaluation results demonstrate high consistency, unavoidable biases may still exist. Our limited sample size introduces the possibility of Type II errors—meaning we may overlook factors with minor or moderate impact. 4. The study lacks external cohort validation for incremental value analysis, making it impossible to ensure the clinical applicability of the findings. Prospective studies are still needed to further confirm these results.

Future research should undertake extensive prospective or Mendelian randomization studies that include genes associated with calcium metabolism or constantly monitored serum calcium levels to elucidate a causal association between calcium and SCSC. Moreover, confounding variables such as parathyroid hormone and vitamin D must be considered to ascertain their potential mediating role in this association. Lastly, future research should further examine the actual effectiveness of MON, HDL-C, and INR on SCSC and explore the influence of calcium on small-vessel disease.

Conclusion

This study identified a significant and stable association between calcium and SCSC. Although calcium may serve as a potential biomarker for high-risk cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease phenotypes, its clinical significance remains to be confirmed.
