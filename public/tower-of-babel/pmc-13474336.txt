Transient ultrasonographic findings suggestive of vesicoureteral reflux associated with cystitis in pediatric and adolescent patients: Case reports and pathophysiological insights
Authors: Riu Homma, Toshihiro Yanai, Keiichi Motoyama, Hirota Saito, Isho Izumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough the clinical progression from lower to upper urinary tract infection is well recognized, the underlying pathophysiology remains understudied. Although vesicoureteral reflux is considered an important factor in pyelonephritis development, the mechanism underlying cases without reflux remains unclear. We report 2 pediatric and adolescent cases in which transient steepening of the ureteral jet angulation and bladder wall thickening were observed on ultrasonography during episodes of cystitis, with subsequent normalization after the resolution of inflammation. These findings suggest that cystitis-induced bladder wall thickening may transiently impair the antireflux mechanism and can provide a possible explanation for some cases of pyelonephritis without documented vesicoureteral reflux.Keywords: Vesicoureteral reflux, Cystitis, Pediatric pyelonephritis, Transient reflux, Bladder wall thickening, Urinary tract infection

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections, and pyelonephritis is a severe upper tract manifestation [1]. Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is defined as the retrograde flow of urine from the bladder to the upper urinary tract [2,3] and has traditionally been regarded as an uncommon condition that affects approximately 1% of children. UTIs in the setting of VUR are often associated with pyelonephritis, as reflux allows direct communication of infected urine between the bladder and kidney, thereby permitting cystitis to rapidly progress to acute pyelonephritis [4]. Clinically, pyelonephritis frequently develops after episodes of cystitis across a wide age range, even in the absence of documented VUR; the underlying mechanism remains unclear [5]. Several nonreflux mechanisms, including voiding dysfunction and intrarenal reflux, have been proposed as potential contributors to pyelonephritis. Some reports have suggested that reversible VUR may occur during active urinary tract infection as a result of infection-related changes at the ureterovesical junction; however, the precise mechanism has not been clarified [6,7].

Recent advances in ultrasonography have enabled the dynamic and physiological assessment of urinary tract function [8]. In this context, color Doppler observation of ureteral jets has been described as a potential imaging finding associated with VUR [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Previous reports have noted that ureters entering the bladder at a steeper angle can reduce the effectiveness of the antireflux mechanism during voiding.

This report details 2 cases of pyelonephritis in patients under 18 years, in which ultrasonography revealed findings suggestive of transient VUR associated with bladder wall thickening during cystitis. These observations suggest a previously underappreciated reversible mechanism by which lower urinary tract inflammation can temporarily impair the antireflux barrier and contribute to the development of UTIs. This report focuses on ultrasonographic findings observed during acute cystitis and discusses their potential pathophysiological implications rather than proposing a diagnostic method for VUR.

Case report

Case 1

A 10-year-old girl presented with dysuria and lower abdominal pain. Her body temperature was 38.2°C. Urinalysis revealed pyuria and elevated leukocyte esterase levels. On day 2, the urine culture revealed Escherichia coli (105 CFU/mL), and treatment with cefmetazole (CMZ), a second-generation cephalosporin, was initiated at a dose of 4000 mg/d. Ultrasonography revealed echogenic debris in both the bladder and the ureter. Evaluation of stored transverse color Doppler images obtained with moderate bladder filling revealed a steep ureteral jet angle (right, 83°; left, 87°; Figs. 1 and 2, Video 1), defined as the angle between the direction of the ureteral jet and the adjacent bladder wall at the ureterovesical junction, on both sides. Superb Microvascular Imaging (SMI) showed retrograde flow extending toward the renal pelvis (Video 2). Follow-up transabdominal ultrasonography on day 5 demonstrated resolution of bladder wall thickening, clearance of intravesical debris, and normalization of the ureteral jet angles (54° right and 43° left; Figs. 3 and 4). Following resolution of the acute inflammatory findings, voiding-phase ultrasonography revealed no ureteral or renal pelvic dilatation during urination, further supporting the transient nature of the observed abnormalities. Voiding Cystourethrography (VCUG) was not performed, given the normalization of findings and the favorable clinical course. No recurrence was observed during the 4-year follow-up period.

Case 2

An 18-year-old man with a history of horseshoe kidney, grade 3 left hydronephrosis, and left ureteropelvic junction obstruction, and no prior UTI, presented with left flank pain and a fever of 38.9°C. Urinalysis revealed elevated pyuria and elevated nitrite levels. Serratia marcescens (105 CFU/mL) was isolated from the urine culture, and CMZ was administered at a dose of 4000 mg/d. On day 1, ultrasonography revealed increased echogenicity in the left kidney, ureteral and bladder wall thickening, and echogenic debris in the renal pelvis. Color Doppler imaging revealed a steep left ureteral jet angle of 88° (Fig. 5) and ureteral wall edema. By day 2, bladder wall thickening had resolved, and the previously steep ureteral jet angle normalized from 88°to 45° (Fig. 6). Voiding-phase ultrasonography demonstrated no ultrasonographic findings suggestive of reflux (Video 3). Given the resolution of clinical symptoms and ultrasonographic findings, VCUG was not performed. No recurrence was observed during the 3-year follow-up period.

Discussion

In both the present cases, directly observed ultrasonographic findings during acute cystitis included bladder wall thickening, echogenic debris, and transient steepening of the ureteral jet angle. In Case 1, SMI also showed retrograde flow extending toward the renal pelvis. Following antibiotic treatment, bladder wall thickening resolved, echogenic debris cleared, and the ureteral jet angles returned to values observed in normal controls. These serial observations demonstrate a temporal association between acute cystitis and reversible changes in bladder morphology and ureteral jet configuration.

Although VUR was not confirmed by VCUG, these findings suggest a possible physiological mechanism. Acute cystitis may have caused bladder wall thickening and edema around the ureterovesical junction, altering the intramural ureteral course and producing a steeper ureteral jet angle. This altered configuration may have transiently reduced the effectiveness of the antireflux mechanism. Normalization of both the bladder wall appearance and ureteral jet angle after treatment supports the transient and reversible nature of these inflammation-related changes.

Recent studies have reported that steeper ureteral or ureteric jet angles, typically exceeding 50°-70°, are associated with congenital VUR, supporting the concept that jet angulation may reflect an impairment of the antireflux mechanism [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. This may be explained by the fact that a steep intramural ureteral angle compromises the normal antireflux mechanism, in which increased intravesical pressure during voiding compresses the intramural ureter against the bladder wall to prevent retrograde urine flow [6,7]. In the present study, similar steep jet angles were observed only during cystitis episodes (Fig. 7), suggesting that inflammation-related bladder wall thickening acutely altered the intramural ureteral angle and may have transiently impaired the antireflux mechanism.

Comparison of normal and acute cystitis findings. Representative images demonstrate bladder wall thickening and a steep intramural ureteral angle during acute cystitis compared with that in cases with a normal bladder.

Follow-up voiding-phase ultrasonography in both patients demonstrated no residual ureteral or pelvic dilatation after clinical improvement, supporting the transient nature of the observed ultrasonographic abnormalities. Although voiding-phase ultrasonography may miss intermittent episodes, its noninvasive and repeatable nature makes it a practical adjunct for confirming the resolution of inflammation-related reflux.

Ultrasonography offers several advantages in evaluating VUR and associated upper UTIs. Its radiation-free and repeatable nature allows dynamic assessment of ultrasonographic findings potentially associated with transient impairment of the antireflux mechanism during the acute inflammatory phase, including steep ureteral jet angles, bladder wall thickening, ureteral wall edema, and Doppler-detected retrograde flow.

Moreover, when ultrasonographic findings suggest transient impairment of the antireflux mechanism, ultrasonography enables the simultaneous evaluation of renal manifestations suggestive of acute pyelonephritis, such as renal enlargement, pelvic debris, and pelvic wall thickening. This combined noninvasive assessment may facilitate the early recognition of upper urinary tract involvement and confirmation of resolution during follow-up.

Careful interpretation of serial imaging findings may provide important insights into transient pathophysiological changes in pediatric diseases, even when the underlying mechanism cannot be directly confirmed, as illustrated in previous pediatric imaging case reports [13].

Limitations

First, the diagnosis of VUR was not confirmed using VCUG, which remains the gold standard [14]. In both cases, VCUG was not performed because invasive imaging was considered inappropriate during the acute inflammatory phase. Following antibiotic treatment, clinical symptoms and ultrasonographic abnormalities resolved, and further invasive evaluation was deemed unnecessary. Second, this report included only 2 cases, one of which had horseshoe kidney, hydronephrosis, and ureteropelvic junction obstruction. These anatomical abnormalities may independently influence urinary tract dynamics, consequently limiting the generalizability of the findings. Third, ultrasonography with Doppler cannot directly diagnose upper urinary tract infection, and the association between cystitis, transient ultrasonographic changes suggestive of impaired antireflux function, and pyelonephritis remains inferential rather than confirmatory. In both cases, pyelonephritis was clinically diagnosed on the basis of fever, flank pain, urinalysis, urine culture results, and laboratory findings, rather than definitive imaging findings. Furthermore, ultrasonographic findings may be influenced by operator experience, patient characteristics, and equipment availability, which can vary across institutions. Because this was a retrospective case report, standardized quantitative measurements of bladder wall and ureteral wall thicknesses were not obtained during the original examinations. Accordingly, the aim of this report was to describe transient ultrasonographic changes that may reflect temporary impairment of the antireflux mechanism during acute cystitis, not to describe the diagnosis of VUR. In addition, although SMI enabled visualization of slow retrograde flow, it is not a validated diagnostic modality for VUR, and its findings should be considered supportive imaging findings rather than validated diagnostic findings or definitive evidence of reflux.

Conclusions

The findings from these cases suggest a previously under-recognized mechanism by which bladder inflammation can transiently impair the antireflux mechanism. The observed ultrasonographic findings may provide a possible explanation for the development of pyelonephritis in some patients without documented vesicoureteral reflux. Further studies should determine whether inflammation-related, transient impairment of the antireflux mechanism contributes to upper urinary tract infection and validate the present observations in larger patient populations.
