EBUS‐Guided Incidental Finding of Pulmonary Vein Thrombosis: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Mohammed A. Alqawasma, Yunus E. Yesilkaya, Tatiana A. Rojas‐Zapata, Regina Murillo‐Torres, Alejandra Yu Lee‐Mateus, Linh H. Vu, Zachary J. Fleissner, Bryan C. Husta, David Abia‐Trujillo, Sebastian Fernandez‐Bussy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary vein thrombosis (PVT) is an uncommon but potentially fatal condition that is often underdiagnosed due to nonspecific clinical presentation. We report a case of a 47‐year‐old woman with a thrombus in the right inferior pulmonary vein incidentally found during a robotic‐assisted bronchoscopy biopsy (RAB) and endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) performed for mediastinal evaluation and lymph node sampling. PVT was confirmed with computed tomography pulmonary angiography, and the patient was successfully treated with anticoagulation therapy. A review of the literature revealed that reports of PVT detected via EBUS are exceedingly rare and usually malignant in nature. To our knowledge, this is the first peer‐reviewed case report describing PVT identified incidentally during EBUS, underscoring the potential of this technique to reveal clinically significant vascular abnormalities.

Introduction

Pulmonary vein thrombosis (PVT) is a rare and often underrecognised thrombotic entity whose true incidence remains unknown. It is most frequently associated with lung transplantation, lobectomy, malignancy and radiofrequency catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation [1], while a small number of cases occur without an identifiable precipitating factor [1, 2]. Clinical manifestations for PVT are usually nonspecific and vary widely, including cough, dyspnea and hemoptysis or may be absent, often resulting in delayed or missed diagnosis [1, 3, 4].

Consequently, a high index of suspicion is essential, and identifying PVT requires a comprehensive approach involving conventional imaging modalities. While transthoracic echocardiography is often part of the initial assessment, a combination of more advanced techniques, including transesophageal echocardiography, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging, is typically used to characterise the thrombus and adjacent anatomy definitively [3, 5]. Potential complications can significantly alter patients' hemodynamic stability and may comprise hypoxic respiratory failure, stroke, systemic embolisation or death [4]. Treatment plans typically involve anticoagulation therapy and may also involve thrombectomy or resection in some cases [1, 3].

Endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS), primarily used for transbronchial needle aspiration (TBNA) in mediastinal and hilar lymph node evaluation, has demonstrated diagnostic capabilities beyond its conventional role and can incidentally identify pulmonary vascular abnormalities such as pulmonary embolism (PE). Literature reports describe incidental PE detection during EBUS in up to 0.7% of procedures, with approximately 96% diagnostic concordance with CT angiography [6, 7]. However, reports of PVT detected via EBUS are exceedingly rare and mostly describe malignant thrombi. We present a case report of a 47‐year‐old female with a bland thrombus found incidentally during robotic‐assisted bronchoscopy (RAB) biopsy and EBUS for diagnostic evaluation of lung nodules and lymph node sampling. This finding is discussed within the context of current literature, emphasising its significance and clinical implications. A review of the relevant literature is also presented.

Case Report

A 47‐year‐old female nonsmoker with a history of a right lower lobe (RLL) mass and cutaneous mycobacterial infection underwent RAB biopsy of a RLL nodule, EBUS‐TBNA and bronchoalveolar lavage. During EBUS assessment, an unexpected intraluminal filling defect was visualised within a distal branch of the right inferior pulmonary vein, raising concern for PVT (Figure 1 and Video 1). The procedure was completed without complications, and the patient was directly admitted for further examination of a PVT on postprocedure imaging.

A postbronchoscopy CT angiogram performed on the same day revealed a persistent thin linear filling defect within the right inferior pulmonary vein, suggestive of a pedunculated thrombus at the pulmonary venous ostium (Figure 2). No pulmonary emboli were identified in the pulmonary arteries (Figure 3).

The patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout hospitalisation and did not require supplemental oxygen, maintaining adequate oxygenation in room air. She reported mild wheezing and shortness of breath, without associated chest pain, hemoptysis or extremity swelling. Anticoagulation therapy was initiated prior to discharge with apixaban 10 mg twice daily for 7 days, followed by a planned transition to 5 mg twice daily for a minimum duration of 3 months, based on established PE and venous thromboembolism (PE/VTE) treatment guidelines [8, 9]. She was discharged home in stable condition the following day, with outpatient follow‐up arranged for ongoing management of her underlying conditions. No complications related to the PVT were observed during hospitalisation or early follow‐up.

Results from the biopsy of the RLL mass yielded suspicious but nondiagnostic findings, describing lung tissue with areas of organisation and several fragments containing lymphoid infiltrates concerning for involvement by low‐grade extranodal marginal zone lymphoma. Two weeks after the initial procedure, a positron emission tomography (PET) scan was performed, which demonstrated scattered hypermetabolic pulmonary lesions, findings suspicious for low‐grade marginal zone lymphoma. No additional abnormalities or associated vascular involvement were identified.

The patient remains under active evaluation and follow‐up for her underlying diagnosis suspicious of extranodal lymphoma. No follow‐up imaging specific to PVT was performed.

Discussion

The clinical manifestations of PVT vary from asymptomatic presentations to life‐threatening embolic events [1, 3, 10], and the known etiologies of PVT typically involve important disruption to the anatomy or physiology of pulmonary veins such as surgery, active malignancy, direct extension of malignant processes or extrinsic vein compression, which can produce tumour thrombi within the pulmonary veins [1, 2, 3]. Hypercoagulable states and mechanical compression of veins, leading to stasis and endothelial damage, are major contributors to the development of this condition [11, 12]. In this case, the RLL mass likely exerted mechanical compression on a pulmonary vein branch, resulting in impaired blood flow and subsequent PVT.

Due to the nonspecific or absent nature of symptoms, diagnosis is often delayed or overlooked, highlighting the importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion, particularly in patients with risk factors. Accurate identification relies on comprehensive imaging, as clinical findings alone are rarely diagnostic [2]. Conventional modalities such as pulmonary angiography, transthoracic echocardiography and transesophageal echocardiography are commonly used and can help differentiate between a benign or bland thrombus and a malignant thrombus or tumour invasion [1, 3]. To reduce flow‐related artefacts, late‐phase CT with intravenous contrast material is often employed, and magnetic resonance imaging has also emerged as a valuable diagnostic tool [3].

In the present case, the initial suspicion was not raised by any of these conventional modalities, but rather incidentally by EBUS and subsequently confirmed with CT angiography. Although EBUS is not a primary tool for identifying venous thrombosis, it may serve as a valuable adjunctive technique for specialists, enabling visualisation of vascular abnormalities during intrathoracic ultrasound procedures [5, 7, 13]. Prompt recognition can allow earlier confirmation with cross‐sectional imaging and initiation of appropriate management.

A review of the literature indicates that sonographic identification of pulmonary vein pathology with EBUS is rare. Of note, four cases involved malignant thrombi, in which EBUS enabled direct visualisation and diagnostic sampling of tumour thrombus within the pulmonary vein via EBUS‐TBNA, allowing confirmation of malignancy on histology [14, 15, 16, 17], while only one previous EBUS‐guided evidence of a benign thrombus within the pulmonary vein documented after detection by CT [18] (Table 1). Although the thrombus did not appear to represent direct tumour invasion, it was classified as a bland thrombus. To our knowledge, this case provides the first comprehensive account of a bland thrombus PVT incidentally identified by EBUS in the absence of prior imaging or clinical suspicion.

To date, no specific guidelines have been established for the diagnosis of PE or PVT during EBUS, and the available literature remains limited to isolated case reports. CT pulmonary angiogram can be used to confirm suspected PVT, while EBUS should be used adjunctively when CT pulmonary angiogram is contraindicated or not immediately available. This case report underscores the potential for EBUS to detect clinically significant vascular findings that extend beyond its conventional role in mediastinal lymph node staging. It serves to expand the awareness of the spectrum of pulmonary venous pathology that may be encountered during routine interventional pulmonology procedures and highlights the critical importance of distinguishing between benign and malignant intravascular findings to ensure appropriate and safe patient management.

This rare case of incidentally found PVT further illustrates the versatility of EBUS beyond nodal and parenchymal assessment and emphasises the value of thoroughly examining adjacent vascular structures during EBUS techniques, particularly in patients with a history of or high risk of malignancy and other prothrombotic risk factors.
