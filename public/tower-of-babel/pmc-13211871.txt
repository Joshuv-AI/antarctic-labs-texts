Sensory Impairment and Risk of Elder Mistreatment in Community‐Dwelling Older Adults
Authors: Mara W Rosenberg, Evelyn Li, Wei‐Lin Xue, Elizabeth Eckstrom, Melissa Howe, Alexander K. Smith, Ashwin A. Kotwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEmotional and financial mistreatment among older adults is often under‐recognized among clinicians. Exploring whether sensory impairment is linked to an elevated risk of elder financial or emotional mistreatment could help inform targeted screening strategies.MethodsWe used nationally‐representative, cross‐sectional data from the National Social Life, Health, and Aging Project, including interview rounds with available assessments of recent elder mistreatment (Round 1 (R1): 2005–06 and Round 4 (R4): 2021–23). Elder mistreatment included reported emotional mistreatment (being insulted or feeling controlled) and financial mistreatment (money or belongings taken without permission) within the last 12 months. Assessments of sensory impairment differed slightly by round of data collection and included self‐reported overall vision, olfaction, and hearing, self‐reported hearing loss that interferes with communication, and objective vision (visual acuity) and olfaction (sniff test). We used multivariable logistic regressions to determine the association of self‐reported and objective individual and multi‐sensory impairments with mistreatment risk. Analyses for multisensory impairment were conducted using the full sample, while analyses for individual impairments were stratified by round of data collection.ResultsThe total sample included 5231 participants; R1 included 2461 participants who were on average 70 years old (SD 7) and 53% female, and R4 included 2770 participants who were on average 71 years old (SD 9) and 55% female. Multisensory impairment was linked to a higher adjusted probability of experiencing either emotional or financial mistreatment (0 impairments: 20% vs. 1 impairment: 23% vs. 2+ impairments: 28%, p < 0.01). Respondents with self‐reported vision impairment had a higher adjusted probability of financial mistreatment (8% vs. 3%, p < 0.001) whereas objective vision impairment had no association. Self‐reported hearing impairment that interfered with communication was associated with financial mistreatment (12% vs. 7%, p = 0.03).ConclusionIntegrating assessments for multisensory impairment, vision, and hearing loss may add to elder mistreatment prevention and detection efforts.

Introduction

Older adults are at risk of mistreatment from others stemming from emotional, physical, and financial abuse, and through self‐neglect. While recent data is limited, as of 2015, approximately one in 10 older adults in the United States experience mistreatment annually: 5%–12% emotional, 2%–3% physical, 5%–7% financial, and 5% self‐neglect [1, 2]. The effects of mistreatment can extend beyond immediate harms, contributing to worse mental health, mortality, and cognitive decline [3, 4]. Yet, mistreatment remains poorly recognized by clinicians.

Prior research suggests that sensory impairments may be a risk factor for mistreatment. For example, those with poor eyesight are more likely to report discrimination than sighted adults [5], while those with frailty and poor sensory function have a greater risk of financial exploitation [6]. Sensory loss may be related to elder mistreatment in several ways. Hearing or vision impairment could limit one's ability to self‐advocate, while poor odor identification may prevent recognition of neglect (ex: unable to identify poor self‐hygiene). In addition, those with sensory losses in vision, hearing, or olfaction are at risk for social isolation, loneliness, and poorer mental health, all of which increase vulnerability to mistreatment [7, 8, 9].

While these individual mechanisms may be relevant to situations of multisensory impairments, a phenomenon common in older adults [10], the compounding effects of multisensory loss should be uniquely considered. Multisensory impairment has demonstrated associations with faster cognitive decline among older adults when considering compounding sensory loss across four measures (vision, hearing, smell, and touch) [11]. As a result, multisensory impairment could increase the vulnerability of older adults to exploitation by increasing the risk of dementia and cognitive impairment. In addition, individuals may be unable to compensate for multiple sensory impairments compared with single sensory loss, therefore heightening dependency and, in turn, mistreatment risk. Moreover, multisensory losses increase the risk of social withdrawal thereby increasing vulnerability [11]. However, despite these potential mechanisms, the relationship between sensory impairments and mistreatment risk has been largely unexplored, particularly using national samples.

Sensory impairments are modifiable and common, with around 30% of US adults aged 65+ with vision impairment and 70% with hearing impairment—30% of whom use hearing aids [12, 13]. Consequently, a better understanding of the link between sensory impairment and elder mistreatment could inform both mistreatment screening and management strategies. In the present study, we therefore explore this association using the National Social Life Health and Aging Project (NSHAP). NSHAP, to our knowledge, is the largest nationally representative study to include both comprehensive measures of sensory impairment (multiple sensory impairments as well as subjective and objective measures) and self‐reported elder mistreatment, a well‐established measure of mistreatment that is strongly associated with poor health outcomes [14, 15]. Our objectives were to characterize (1) the association between multisensory impairment and elder mistreatment risk and (2) the relationship between individually assessed sensory impairments (vision, hearing, and/or olfaction) and mistreatment. The second objective leverages the different assessment strategies, including self‐report versus objective measures, used for measurement of sensory impairment in NSHAP.

Methods

Data and Study Population

The National Social Life, Health, and Aging Project (NSHAP) is a longitudinal, population‐based cohort developed to identify health and social factors affecting community‐dwelling older adults in the United States. NSHAP includes in‐person interviews and leave‐behind questionnaires. This analysis focused on the rounds that assessed recent mistreatment (i.e., in the last year): Round 1 (R1) and Round 4 (R4). Round 2 (2010) and Round 3 (2015) did not have assessments of recent mistreatment [16]. R1 data collection (2005–2006) involved multistage probability sampling of U.S. adults aged 57–85 years; R4 (2021–2023) included all surviving respondents, cohabiting spouses/partners, and additional participants to maintain a nationally‐representative sample. Focusing on participants aged 60 or older led to a cohort of 2461 in R1; 2770 in R4 (Figure S1).

Elder Mistreatment

Mistreatment was measured as the presence or absence of emotional and/or financial mistreatment within the past 12 months (Table S1). Physical mistreatment was excluded due to low prevalence (< 1%). If a respondent answered yes to either mistreatment question, they were labeled as experiencing “any mistreatment.”

Sensory Impairment

Sensory impairment in vision, hearing, or sense of smell was assessed through self‐report or objectively. The availability of assessments differed by round, with question wording provided in Table S1. Multisensory impairments were determined by a count of sensory impairments (Range: 0, 1, or 2+). In R1, multisensory impairment was defined using the three self‐reported sensory impairment measures (hearing, vision, or olfaction) for consistency and to maximize sample size since objective vision assessments were conducted on a sub‐sample. In R4, multisensory impairment included objective vision impairment, objective olfactory impairment, and self‐reported hearing impairment since there was no R4 data on self‐reported vision or olfactory impairment. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding olfaction from the multisensory impairment measure, focusing on the composite measure of hearing and/or vision loss.

Sociodemographic and Health Measures

Participants self‐reported age, ethnicity, education, household assets, and difficulty with activities of daily living (ADLs), including dressing, bathing/showering, eating, getting in/out of bed, and toileting. Multimorbidity was defined as those that had three or more co‐morbidities including: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, emphysema/asthma, heart attack/heart failure, or stroke. Dementia was determined by asking (yes/no): “Has a doctor ever told you that you have dementia?”

Statistical Analysis

We used multivariable logistic regressions to determine if multisensory loss (0, 1, 2+) was associated with mistreatment. We adjusted for covariates based on prior literature identifying factors contributing to sensory loss as well as the Abuse Intervention Model which identified factors contributing to mistreatment [17]. Final covariates included: age, sex, ethnicity (Non‐Hispanic White; Non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Other), education, difficulty in one or more ADLs, multimorbidity, and dementia [18, 19]. Inclusion of age, sex, education, and ethnicity also adjusted for potential reporting bias derived from differences in subjective vs. objective reporting rates between these groups [20]. Analyses were conducted using the combined R1 and R4 samples and stratified by round given differences in the multisensory impairment measure across rounds.

We next assessed the relationship of each measure of sensory impairment and mistreatment using multivariable logistic regression models, adjusting for the same covariates as in the primary model. R1 and R4 cohorts were analyzed separately due to differences in sensory impairment assessments. We report adjusted probabilities of mistreatment from each. All comparisons were hypothesis‐driven; therefore, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons. Statistical associations were significant for a threshold p < 0.05. All analyses included NSHAP survey design weights to account for complex sampling and non‐responses and were performed with R v4.4.1.

Results

In R1, average age was 70 years (SD 7 years), 53% were women, 81% self‐identified as non‐Hispanic White, and 20% reported emotional mistreatment, 5% financial mistreatment, and 22% any mistreatment. In R4, average age was 71 years (SD 9 years), 55% were women, 77% identified as non‐Hispanic White. Approximately 17% reported emotional mistreatment, 9% financial mistreatment, and 23% any mistreatment (Table 1). One percent had sensory impairments in all domains (hearing, vision, and olfaction) and 8% had sensory impairments in two domains (Table S2).

Discussion

In this nationally representative study of older adults, we identified a significant association between multisensory impairments, particularly vision and hearing impairment, and the risk of elder mistreatment. Notably, our study includes the most up‐to‐date U.S. national prevalence estimates of self‐reported mistreatment, which suggests an overall high burden of financial and emotional mistreatment and a general need for clinical awareness around this important issue. This study suggests that sensory impairment may provide an additional clue for clinicians to assess for mistreatment assessments and that addressing sensory impairment may be relevant to mistreatment prevention and management efforts.

Current literature suggests that single and dual sensory impairments relate to several poor health outcomes: depression, poor self‐rated health, and decreased functional ability [18, 21]. Our findings suggest that elder mistreatment may be an additional negative sequela of multisensory impairment. Based on prior literature [11], we hypothesize that while an individual might compensate for single sensory loss (i.e., those with hearing impairment can rely on written communication), there is less ability to compensate for compounding impairments, which leads to greater dependence and vulnerability. Alternatively, multisensory loss may be a marker of general health decline and increased dependence and frailty, which can increase the risk for mistreatment [6, 19]. This association may also reflect response bias where certain respondents are more likely to report self‐report negative responses across all survey markers [22]. Ultimately, clinicians who identify and/or treat vision or hearing impairment might proactively assess for and educate around mistreatment risk.

In addition, these results suggest that individual impairments in self‐reported vision or hearing may associate with a higher risk of financial mistreatment. This may be due, in part, to the social consequences of vision and hearing loss, notably increased social isolation and loneliness compared to the general population [23]. Loneliness could lead to misplaced trust and reliance on others, increasing the risk of exploitation. Additionally, it may be difficult for those with vision impairment to access resources to prevent or resolve ongoing mistreatment (e.g., a limited ability to handle cash, manage a bank account, or ask for assistance in returning funds).

Further, we found that the method of assessment for hearing and vision loss impacted the association with mistreatment. For hearing impairment, financial mistreatment was only seen among those who reported their hearing impairment led to trouble hearing during conversations (R4). It was not seen with self‐reported overall hearing (R1). We hypothesize that the functional impact of hearing loss on communication and social participation is part of the mechanism which links hearing impairment to mistreatment [24]. However, careful interpretation of this is necessary as underreporting is common, with prior studies suggesting self‐reported hearing impairment decreases with age—as opposed to objective measures of hearing which increase with age [25]. Future studies would benefit from comparing subjective measures to objective assessments to address underreporting and examine how this may influence the association with mistreatment [26]. For vision impairment, self‐reported vision loss was associated with financial mistreatment whereas measured vision loss was not. This may be in part attributed to the self‐reported measure capturing both near and far‐sightedness, while measured sensory loss only captured distance visual acuity. Alternatively, self‐report may better reflect the functional and psychological aspects of vision loss [27]. Additionally, self‐reporting of impairments may capture feelings of dependence on others and reflect a sense of guilt about this dependence, all of which amplify vulnerability to exploitation [26, 28]. Taken together, clinicians should consider assessing hearing and vision impairment through a functional lens to better identify mistreatment vulnerability.

Our study has several strengths including drawing from a large nationally representative cohort and detailed data on multisensory impairments and elder mistreatment. Several limitations should be considered as well. First, while the NSHAP dataset includes complex sampling to approximate the U.S. population [29], those with severe sensory losses may be under‐represented due to difficulty reaching this population. Additional studies targeting this population would help inform generalizability of our findings. Second, the analyzed dataset only assessed hearing subjectively, an often underrepresentation of true hearing loss compared to objective screens. Third, each sensory assessment has its own sensitivity/specificity leading to variability in the count for multisensory loss. Future studies would benefit from exploring multisensory impairment using similar assessments across all domains. Fourth, the cross‐sectional design limits the ability to assess causal relationships between single and multi‐ sensory impairments and mistreatment. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how the progression of sensory impairments may influence the risk of mistreatment, and to identify potential opportunities for intervention.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates an association between self‐reported vision and hearing impairment and the risk of mistreatment, particularly financial mistreatment, among older adults. Multisensory impairments may compound this risk. Notably, our study demonstrates an overall high prevalence of self‐reported mistreatment, which is relevant to the recent 2025 Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Age Friendly Hospital Measure [30] which requires hospitals to screen for elder abuse and intervene to reduce risk. Sensory impairment may be relevant to both clinical efforts to improve detection and management of elder mistreatment within this and other broad initiatives.
