Public Awareness of Stroke Risk Factors, Warning Symptoms, Prevention Measures, and Stroke Management Among Adults in Two Major Cities in Yemen: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Najmaddin A H Hatem, Mohamed Izham Mohamed Ibrahim, Ahmed Salem Al-Jarba’a
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke remains a major global health challenge and a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide. In low-income, conflict-affected countries such as Yemen, the burden is amplified by fragile healthcare systems and limited access to preventive and acute care services. Hence, this study addresses a critical awareness gap by assessing awareness of stroke risk factors, symptom recognition, prevention strategies, and stroke management among adult community members in two major urban cities in Yemen.MethodologyA cross-sectional survey design was employed using paper-based questionnaire. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling. Data analysis included descriptive statistics, non-parametric tests, correlation analysis, and bivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsOur study revealed a high level of overall recognition-based awareness was observed among surveyed participants (87.9%; 95% CI: 84.7–91.1%). Specific areas of high recognition included obesity or overweight (89.4%) and high blood pressure (86.2%) as risk factors, dizziness/loss of balance (79.1%) as a symptom, and taking prescribed medications (87.2%) and regular medical checkups (86.9%) for prevention. Awareness of immediate medical care (87.7%) and future prevention medications (85.9%) also stood out in stroke management domain. A personal or family history of chronic diseases was strongly associated with greater overall stroke awareness score (p<0.001), as was a history of stroke itself (p=0.03). Bivariate regression analysis highlighted significant associations between high awareness and education level for symptoms, prevention, and stroke management domains scores (p <0.001).ConclusionDespite the encouraging overall awareness, the findings highlight areas where targeted public health interventions may be warranted, particularly for less recognized aspects of stroke awareness. These should focus on reinforcing awareness, particularly concerning less recognized symptoms, and addressing disparities linked to socio-economic factors. These findings highlight the need for targeted stroke awareness interventions in urban settings, while underscoring the importance of further nationally representative studies. This study sheds light on the current level of stroke awareness and offers vital insights for policymakers and medical professionals to create culturally appropriate and successful educational campaigns.Keywords: stroke awareness, stroke risk factors, symptom recognition, stroke prevention, stroke management, health literacy, urban populations, low-resource settings, Yemen

Introduction

Stroke is the second most common cause of death globally, following ischemic heart disease. Each year, approximately 15 million people experience strokes, leading to 5.8 million deaths related to stroke, with over 65% of these fatalities occurring in developing nations.1–3 Moreover, stroke is the third most frequent cause of disability, rendering victims dependent on society and impacting their personal, social, and economic well-being. Stroke presents a significant risk not only to public health but also to the socioeconomic advancement of communities. The estimated annual cost of treating stroke patients in the United States is around $34.3 billion.4,5 While lifestyle behaviors that influence stroke risk may vary across countries, the core biomedical risk factors for stroke such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and smoking are generally consistent worldwide. However, the prevalence, distribution, and lifestyle related contributors to these risk factors can differ by region, underscoring the importance of examining country-specific patterns to guide effective educational initiatives, reduce the stroke burden, and minimize the costs of rehabilitation and long-term disability.6,7 In low-income nations like Yemen, stroke is increasingly becoming a major cause of premature death and disability. According to the most recent data from the World Health Organization (WHO), Yemen recorded 13,570 stroke-related deaths in 2020, which represents 8.72% of total deaths, with an age-adjusted death rate of 127.49 per 100,000 population, placing Yemen 32nd worldwide.8 One contributing factor is the ongoing civil war, which has severely disrupted Yemen’s health system by damaging infrastructure, limiting the functionality of healthcare facilities, and reducing access to essential medical services. This systemic instability has hindered routine care, health promotion activities, and early detection of non-communicable diseases such as stroke.9 Additionally, while mortality data exists, there is a significant lack of local, population-based epidemiological data on stroke risk factors and potential outcomes in Yemen, with only a limited number of studies addressing these topics.10–13

Research on stroke awareness in Yemen is crucial given the rising prevalence of non-communicable diseases, making stroke a leading cause of illness and mortality. Evaluating the public’s awareness is vital for designing targeted health interventions and educational campaigns.14,15 Recognizing awareness deficiencies regarding stroke risks, symptoms, prevention, and treatment will aid in developing culturally appropriate health programs. This investigation may inform efforts to promote timely symptom recognition, preventive measures, and access to prompt treatment.15,16 By emphasizing particular challenges and prospects within Yemen’s healthcare system, this study can assist policymakers and healthcare practitioners in executing effective strategies to diminish the effects of stroke.

The goal of this study is to provide a comprehensive understanding of stroke awareness among adult community members from two major urban cities in Yemen. It evaluates baseline awareness of stroke risk factors, early symptoms, prevention measures, and stroke management. The study also identifies common misconceptions and areas where awareness is limited, while examining how socio-demographic characteristics are associated with levels of stroke awareness. By clarifying these key aspects of public awareness, the findings can support the development of targeted health education initiatives and policy strategies aimed at improving stroke literacy and strengthening public health outcomes in Yemen.

Methodology

Study Design and Settings

This study employed a cross-sectional observational design and convenience sampling conducted among adult community members in two major urban cities in Yemen. The research utilized an anonymous survey approach to gather data. Participants were individuals aged 18 years or older, who were invited to take part in the study in various general public spaces, including hospitals. To enhance diversity within the sampled population, we decided to distribute the survey in two major cities one in the northern region and one in the southern region of Yemen to enhance participant diversity. This approach helps account for regional differences in healthcare access, socio-economic conditions, and population characteristics, but does not provide nationally representative estimates.

Sample Size Determination

The determination of the minimal sample size for this study was based on a lack of comparable prior research conducted in Yemen, necessitating a robust calculation to ensure statistical validity. A target sample size of approximately 385 individuals was established. This figure was derived using an online Raosoft software online sample size calculator, applying a 95% confidence interval, a standard deviation of 0.5, and a 5% margin of error. The calculation was based on an estimated 50% prevalence of stroke awareness to maximize the sample size for a population survey. This calculation was performed assuming unlimited population size, a common practice when the exact population size is either unknown or very large. A total of 400 survey copies were distributed equally between the two cities, Aden and Sana’a, with 200 copies allocated to each city using convenience sampling. Non-response rate was not incorporated into the initial calculation, and the distributed 400 copies were an attempt to exceed the minimum required sample size. This balanced distribution is designed to ensure adequate geographic representation of the target population within the two cities. This approach is aimed at capturing diverse urban perspectives but does not provide nationally representative estimates.

Instrument

The primary data collection instrument was a self-administered, paper-based questionnaire. Participants typically completed the questionnaire within approximately 10 minutes. To ensure cultural relevance and accessibility, the questionnaire was developed and administered in local Arabic. The development process involved a rigorous review by the authors of this study and five additional academic members, who provided expert feedback on its content and structure. Following this initial review, the questionnaire underwent a pilot test with five students. This pilot phase was crucial for assessing the clarity, comprehensibility, and overall flow of the questions, and modifications were implemented based on the feedback received to enhance its effectiveness. The questionnaire’s design and content were adapted from instruments used in previously published studies assessing stroke awareness,14–16 ensuring its scientific rigor and comparability with existing research. The final version of the questionnaire was structured into five distinct parts:

The first section provided essential information to participants, including a clear explanation of the study’s aims and objectives, a detailed outline of participants’ rights, and assurances regarding the confidentiality of their responses. The second section was dedicated to collecting demographic information and other relevant characteristics of the participants, which were later used to analyze potential associations with stroke awareness levels. The third section comprised four subsections that were specifically designed to gather comprehensive information regarding participants’ awareness of stroke. They systematically addressed four key domains: stroke risk factors, recognition of symptoms, preventive measures, and stroke management. The management domain assessed awareness of acute treatment options, rehabilitation, and long-term stroke management. With 39-items participants were required to respond to each question using a forced-choice format: “Yes”, “No”, or “I do not Know”. This structured response format facilitated quantitative analysis of awareness levels across different aspects of stroke. The questionnaire assessed recognition-based awareness (the ability to correctly identify stroke-related risk factors, symptoms, prevention and management) rather than depth of understanding, decision making capacity, or actual preventive or care-seeking behaviors.

Data Collection

Data were collected using the structured, self-administered questionnaire in public spaces chosen to reach a diverse cross-section of the population. Data collection was conducted between September to October 2025. Trained data collectors approached individuals, explained the study’s objectives, and invited voluntary participation. After obtaining verbally informed consent, participants were given adequate time to complete the questionnaire on-site. For those with limited literacy, data collectors read the questions aloud and recorded responses as indicated, while ensuring confidentiality.

Ethical Considerations and Consent

Prior to participation, verbal informed consent was meticulously obtained from all individuals included in the study. This process involved clearly informing participants about the overarching proposal of the study, their fundamental rights as participants, and the stringent measures taken to ensure the confidentiality of their collected data. Emphasis was placed on the voluntary nature of their participation, and explicit assurances were provided that their identities would remain anonymous throughout the entire duration of the study and in any subsequent dissemination of findings. The comprehensive study design and all associated procedures were submitted for review and subsequently received approval from the Institutional Review Board at Aden University, Yemen (REC-246-2025). This ethical approval process confirmed the study’s adherence to established ethical guidelines, specifically referencing compliance with the principles outlined by the International Conference of Harmonization, thereby upholding the highest standards of ethical research conduct.

Data Analysis and Management

Data were coded and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 26 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages were used to summarize respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics. A structured questionnaire was used to assess awareness of stroke risk factors, recognition of stroke symptoms, preventive measures, and stroke management. Each correct response was assigned a score of one, while incorrect or “I do not know” responses were scored as zero. A total awareness score was calculated for each participant, then converted into a percentage by dividing the obtained score by the total possible score and multiplying by 100. Participants scoring ≥70% were categorized as having high awareness, while those scoring below 70% were categorized as having low reported awareness. This value was used as an operational benchmark rather than a diagnostic standard. Although awareness was categorized for descriptive purposes, statistical analyses were primarily based on continuous awareness scores. Normality testing (Shapiro–Wilk) indicated non-normal distribution of awareness scores; therefore, non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis) were applied to assess significance between variables and overall awareness. Correlation analysis (Spearman’ rho test) was conducted to examine the strength and direction of relationships between awareness domains (risk factors, symptoms, prevention, and stroke management). Associations with awareness status (high vs low) were examined using binary logistic regression: crude odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for each characteristic, and a multivariable logistic regression model was then fitted for overall awareness, entering variables selected a priori for clinical relevance and bivariate association (age, education, income, and family/personal history of chronic disease); adjusted ORs (aORs) with 95% CIs are reported. Because 48 participants were classified as having low awareness, the number of covariates was kept small to maintain an adequate events-per-variable ratio. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 398 participants were included in this cross-sectional study. The demographic profile of the study population revealed a near-even distribution between genders, with males constituting 52.3% (n=208) and females 47.8% (n=190) of the sample, most of participants were married, accounting for 63.5% (n=252). In terms of occupation, employees represented the largest group at 47.7% (n=190), followed by housewives at 18.1% (n=72), students at 11.8% (n=47). Educational attainment was notably high, with 63.6% (n=253) of participants having a university education or higher. Those with a high school education or less comprised 28.89% (n=115), while 7.29% (n=29) were illiterate. The study population was predominantly urban, with 88.2% (n=351) residing in urban areas compared to 11.8% (n=47) in rural settings. A significant majority of participants, 81.7% (n=325), reported that their income was not sufficient, while 18.3% (n=73) considered their income sufficient.

Regarding lifestyle factors, 36.2% (n=144) of the participants reported being smokers, and a substantial 77.1% (n=307) engaged in Khat chewing. (For additional details see Table 1).

Overall Awareness

A high level of overall recognition-based awareness was observed among surveyed participants (87.9%; 95% CI: 84.7–91.1%). Most respondents correctly identified nine or ten risk factors (49.8%), while over half (49.2%) recognized at least eight early symptoms. Similarly, awareness of prevention measures was strong, with 54.3% providing nine or more correct answers. Treatment awareness was also highly reported, as 63.5% identified at least nine available treatment options. These findings suggest generally high awareness among participants, particularly regarding treatment and prevention strategies (Supplementary Table 1). This awareness reflects recognition of correct responses rather than deeper, actionable knowledge or behavioral readiness.

Awareness of Stroke Risk Factors

Participants demonstrated a generally high recognition level of awareness regarding various stroke risk factors (82.4%; 95% CI: 78.7–86.2%). The most recognized risk factor was obesity or overweight, with 89.4% (n=356) of respondents identifying it. This was closely followed by high blood pressure, recognized by 86.2% (n=343), and family history of stroke, identified by 85.7% (n=341). Awareness regarding age as a risk factor was 77.4% (n=308). Sex as a risk factor had the lowest recognition among the listed factors, with 69.1% (n=275) of participants aware. Khat chewing, a prevalent local practice, was recognized as a risk factor by 71.4% (n=284) of the respondents (For additional details see Figure 1).

Symptom Recognition

Recognition of stroke symptoms was also generally high among the participants (79.4%; 95% CI: 75.4–83.4%). The most recognized symptom was dizziness or loss of balance, identified by 79.1% (n=315) of respondents. This was closely followed by sudden difficulty swallowing (78.9%, n=314) and sudden change in coordination or difficulty walking (78.4%, n=312). Difficulty speaking or understanding speech was recognized by 78.1% (n=311). Sudden severe headache and sudden vision problems or loss of vision in one or both eyes were both recognized by 76.6% (n=305) of participants. Sudden confusion or difficulty understanding others was recognized by 75.6% (n=301), and sudden numbness or weakness on one side of the body by 75.1% (n=299). Sudden loss of consciousness was the least recognized symptom, with 73.1% (n=291) awareness (Figure 1).

Prevention Awareness

Participants reported high awareness of stroke prevention measures with (86.4%; 95% CI: 83–89.8%). The highest awareness was observed for taking prescribed medications (87.2%, n=347) and regular medical checkups (86.9%, n=346). Reducing stress was recognized by 84.7% (n=337), followed by regular exercise (82.9%, n=330), managing high blood pressure (82.7%, n=329), and quitting smoking (82.7%, n=329). Maintaining a healthy weight was recognized by 82.2% (n=327), and a healthy diet by 82.4% (n=328). Controlling diabetes was recognized by 80.4% (n=320), and reducing alcohol consumption by 79.6% (n=317) (Figure 1).

Stroke Management

Awareness regarding stroke management was also generally high recognition (87.2%; 95% CI: 83.9–90%). The necessity of immediate medical care was recognized by 87.7% (n=349) of participants, followed by medications for future prevention (85.9%, n=342). Blood pressure medication for treatment was recognized by 84.7% (n=337), and lifestyle changes for recovery by 84.2% (n=335). Medical devices like stents for treatment were recognized by 83.4% (n=332), and surgery for blood vessel malformations by 83.2% (n=331). Rehabilitation therapies for recovery and the importance of a long-term treatment plan were both recognized by 82.9% (n=330). Surgical removal of blood clots by 81.4% (n=324) (Figure 1).

Significant Associations

Overall stroke awareness (Table 1) was significantly associated with employment (p = 0.002), with self-employed participants showing the highest median scores; with a family or personal history of chronic disease (p < 0.001); and with a history of stroke (p = 0.03). Participants residing in rural areas showed a higher median overall awareness score than urban participants (p = 0.017); however, this subgroup was small (n = 47, 11.8%) and the difference was not significant in the domain-level analyses.

Domain-level associations are shown in Table 2. University education was associated with higher awareness of symptoms, prevention, and management (all p < 0.001). Insufficient income was associated with higher awareness of symptoms (p = 0.02), prevention (p = 0.009), and management (p < 0.001) than sufficient income. A family or personal history of chronic disease was associated with higher awareness of risk factors, prevention, and management (all p < 0.001), and a family history of stroke with symptom recognition (p = 0.026). Behavioral factors (smoking, khat chewing) and residency were not significantly associated with any domain.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of recognition-based awareness of stroke risk factors, symptom recognition, preventive measures, and stroke management among 398 adults in two major urban cities in Yemen. Overall recognition-based awareness was high (87.9%; 95% CI 84.7–91.1%), with domain-level awareness of 82.4% for risk factors, 79.4% for symptoms, 86.4% for prevention, and 87.2% for management. The most recognized items were obesity/overweight (89.4%) and high blood pressure (86.2%) as risk factors, dizziness/loss of balance (79.1%) as a symptom, prescribed medications (87.2%) and regular checkups (86.9%) for prevention, and the need for immediate medical care (87.7%) in management. In both bivariate and multivariable analyses, higher overall awareness was independently associated with university-level education (adjusted OR 2.64, 95% CI 1.26–5.53) and with a family or personal history of chronic disease (adjusted OR 5.07, 95% CI 2.03–12.67). Notably, participants reporting insufficient income also had higher odds of high awareness than those reporting sufficient income (adjusted OR 2.47, 95% CI 1.21–5.06). After mutual adjustment, education, income, and history of chronic disease remained independent predictors, whereas associations with age and sex were attenuated and no longer significant. All associations are correlational, and the high awareness levels should be interpreted considering convenience sampling, a predominantly urban and highly educated sample, and a recognition-based questionnaire, all of which may overestimate awareness and limit external validity.

A study by Alassar et al (2025) in Sana’a City, Yemen, focused on the clinico-epidemiological and neuroimaging findings of cerebrovascular accidents.17 While not directly assessing public awareness, their findings on prevalent risk factors among stroke patients offer an important comparison. Alassar et al reported that khat chewing was the most frequent potential risk factor among 64.2% of stroke patients, followed by hypertension (46.8%), smoking (39.8%), and diabetes mellitus (33.3%).17 This highlights a significant discrepancy: while the current study found high awareness of general risk factors like obesity and high blood pressure, the Alassar et al study indicates that Khat chewing, a prevalent local practice, is a highly frequent risk factor among actual stroke patients. Although 71.4% of participants in the current study recognized Khat chewing as a risk factor, its high prevalence among stroke patients in Alassar et al’s study suggests that awareness might not always translate into behavioral change or that the perceived importance of this risk factor might be lower than its actual impact.

Furthermore, the Alassar et al study noted that dysarthria (49.3%) and left-sided hemiplegia (39.8%) were the most frequent clinical presentations among CVA patients.17 The current study found dizziness or loss of balance (79.1%) and sudden difficulty swallowing (78.9%) as the most recognized symptoms by the general population. While there is some overlap in symptom recognition, the specific prevalence of dysarthria and hemiplegia in actual stroke cases, as reported by Alassar et al, suggests that public awareness campaigns could further emphasize these highly prevalent clinical signs.

The current study’s findings align with some regional studies but contrast with others, underscoring the diverse landscape of health literacy across different populations. For instance, a study assessing stroke awareness among Arabic-speaking adults in seven MENA countries found varying levels of public awareness, indicating that while some populations exhibit high general awareness, significant gaps often remain, particularly in recognizing all stroke symptoms.18 Another study in the United Arab Emirates reported that 70.8% of participants recognized the brain as the primary organ affected by a stroke, yet only about 20% could recognize all symptoms.16 Similarly, a study in Saudi Arabia indicated high levels of health literacy regarding stroke risk factors, symptoms, and consequences among the general population.19 These findings resonate with the current study’s observation of high awareness in Yemen, suggesting a regional trend towards increasing stroke literacy, possibly due to enhanced public health initiatives or increased access to information.

However, some studies from developing countries reveal lower awareness levels. For example, a 2018 study in Egypt highlighted a poor level of stroke awareness among a significant portion of the population.20 This disparity could be attributed to differences in socioeconomic factors, educational attainment, healthcare infrastructure, and the effectiveness of public health campaigns across various developing nations. The current study’s emphasis on the high educational attainment of its participants (63.6% with university education or higher) might explain the relatively high awareness levels observed, as education is consistently linked to better health awareness.

Regarding specific risk factors, hypertension and diabetes mellitus are frequently cited as major contributors to stroke in Arab nations and developing countries.1,3,21 The current study’s participants recognized obesity or overweight and high blood pressure as key risk factors (89.4%), (86.2%) respectively, which is consistent with regional and global data. However, the unique cultural practice of Khat chewing, identified as a highly prevalent risk factor in Yemeni stroke patients,17 presents a localized challenge that may not be as prominent in other Middle Eastern or developing country contexts. This highlights the importance of culturally sensitive public health interventions tailored to specific regional risk factors.1,3,21

In terms of symptom recognition, while the current study found high awareness for several symptoms, other regional studies have pointed to specific gaps. For example, some research indicates that while general awareness of stroke symptoms might be present, the ability to recognize all critical symptoms, particularly those requiring immediate action (eg, FAST acronym components), can be limited.16 The current study’s high recognition of symptoms like dizziness/loss of balance and difficulty swallowing is encouraging, but continuous efforts are needed to ensure comprehensive symptom recognition, especially for less commonly perceived signs.

Stroke management awareness data presents a crucial distinction between recognizing the need for action and understanding the nature of intervention. The highest ranked item, the necessity of immediate medical care is the most vital component of the acute stroke response, confirming that the public understands the urgency of the event. However, the awareness of advanced, interventional treatments shows a distinct relative decline. The lowest-ranked item in the management category was the surgical removal of blood clots, a clear proxy for mechanical thrombectomy. While the participants know to call for help, they may not understand the full spectrum of modern, time-critical care available. Increasing public literacy regarding the availability and efficacy of thrombectomy is essential for empowering patients and families to prioritize rapid transport to comprehensive stroke centers, thereby translating high awareness into optimal clinical outcomes.

The current study has several important implications for public health policy and clinical practice in urban settings in Yemen. The strong recognition of key risk factors such as obesity, high blood pressure, and physical inactivity provides a solid foundation for targeted health promotion campaigns. Public health initiatives could leverage this reported awareness as a foundation for interventions aimed at supporting behavioral change.22 For example, campaigns could emphasize practical steps for maintaining a healthy weight, managing blood pressure through diet and exercise, and incorporating regular physical activity into daily routines. The high awareness of prevention measures like regular medical checkups and adherence to prescribed medications suggests that the population is receptive to medical guidance, which can be capitalized upon to improve chronic disease management and reduce stroke incidence.22

However, the discrepancy between the general awareness of risk factors and the high prevalence of specific behaviors like Khat chewing among stroke patients, as highlighted by Alassar et al,17 indicates a critical gap between awareness and practice. This suggests that while general health messages are being received, culturally specific risk factors may require more intensive and tailored interventions. Public health strategies must address the social and cultural dimensions of Khat use, moving beyond simple awareness campaigns to implement comprehensive programs that include cessation support, policy changes, and community-based initiatives to challenge social norms associated with this practice.

The observed associations between higher education and income levels and greater stroke awareness suggest the presence of health disparities among the participants. Individuals with lower socioeconomic status may have limited access to health information and educational resources, which may contribute to lower levels of stroke awareness. This highlights the need for targeted outreach programs that use accessible language and communication channels to reach underserved communities. Health literacy programs tailored for individuals with lower educational attainment could be particularly effective in bridging this awareness gap. It is important to note that these associations are correlations, not causal relationships, but they strongly suggest that socioeconomic factors are a critical determinant of health awareness in this context. Furthermore, the finding that a family history of chronic diseases is strongly associated with higher stroke awareness suggests that personal experience plays a crucial role in health awareness. This association highlights a potential opportunity for healthcare providers to engage families in discussions about stroke prevention, using a family-centered approach to education and risk reduction.23 When a family member is diagnosed with a chronic condition like hypertension or diabetes, it can serve as a teachable moment to educate the entire family about their shared risks and the importance of preventive measures.23 The high awareness observed among rural respondents should be interpreted cautiously given their small proportion in the sample and possible exposure to health campaigns related to chronic disease care.

In the clinical setting, the high perception of the need for immediate medical care for stroke is a positive finding that can be reinforced by healthcare professionals.14,20,22 However, the study also reveals that specific, highly prevalent stroke symptoms like dysarthria and hemiplegia may not be as widely recognized by the participants as other symptoms. Clinicians should take every opportunity to educate patients and their families about the full range of stroke symptoms, emphasizing the importance of seeking immediate medical attention for any sudden neurological changes. The FAST (Face, Arms, Speech, Time) acronym, while a useful tool, may need to be supplemented with information about other common stroke signs to ensure comprehensive public understanding.3,17

The observed moderate positive correlations between the different domains of stroke awareness indicate that high awareness in one domain tends to co-occur with high awareness in others. While these associations do not imply causality, they suggest that awareness components are interrelated. From a public health perspective, this finding supports the potential value of integrated health education campaigns that address stroke risk factors, symptom recognition, prevention strategies, and treatment options simultaneously, rather than in isolation. Delivering a comprehensive and coherent message may enhance overall stroke literacy and improve the public’s ability to recognize and respond promptly to stroke events.

Limitations of the study

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design does not allow for causal conclusions, and the use of self-reported questionnaires may have introduced social desirability bias, with participants potentially overstating their awareness level. Moreover, the use of convenience sampling and data collection limited to two major urban cities restricts the generalizability of the findings. The sample was predominantly urban and highly educated, which may not reflect awareness levels in rural or less-educated populations across Yemen. The ≥70% cutoff was used as an operational benchmark for descriptive purposes and does not represent a validated or diagnostic threshold. Awareness was assessed using a forced-choice response format (Yes/No/I do not know), which may have contributed to overestimation of awareness due to recognition bias or guessing. Consequently, the reported awareness levels reflect recognition-based awareness rather than depth of understanding, decision-making competence, or actual preventive and health-seeking behaviors. These factors should be considered when interpreting the relatively high awareness levels observed in this study. Finally, the study did not explore the sources from which participants obtained their awareness, an omission that limits understanding of how health messages are being disseminated in Yemen.

Conclusion

This study offers insightful information on public awareness of stroke risk factors, symptoms, prevention, and management among adults in two major urban cities in Yemen and suggests a potentially receptive environment for public health initiatives. Because the study used convenience sampling in an urban, highly educated sample, the findings apply to the surveyed population rather than to the entire Yemeni population, and the high awareness observed being recognition-based should not be equated with behavior or outcomes. The study nonetheless highlights areas needing attention: translating awareness into behavioral change, addressing culturally specific risk factors such as khat chewing, and clarifying the less-recognized aspects of advanced stroke treatment. We call for a comprehensive action plan informed by urban population data and involving national health authorities and international partners to support stroke awareness and prevention. Future studies using nationally representative sampling and validated measures of stroke knowledge and health-seeking behavior are warranted to characterize awareness across diverse regions and to evaluate whether increased awareness translates into timely, appropriate action.
