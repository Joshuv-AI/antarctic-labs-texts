Consent beyond the written form: Implications for ketamine trials in the Philippines
Authors: Abegail A Pagallaman, John Noel E Fermin
Published: 2026

Dear Editors,

Swieczkowski et al. 1 provide an important analysis of 14 English informed consent forms for ketamine trials involving mental health conditions. Thirteen forms exceeded an eighth grade reading level, the mean length was about 5,000 words, and estimated reading time exceeded 20 minutes. These findings expose a substantial barrier to meaningful participation and appropriately position readability as an ethical concern rather than a cosmetic feature of research documentation.

However, a grade level threshold should not become the endpoint of consent quality. Readability formulas estimate linguistic difficulty, not whether participants understand randomization, alternatives, uncertainty, or intervention specific risks. Evidence consistently shows that comprehension may remain incomplete despite formal consent procedures.2,3 For psychoactive interventions, simplification may also create semantic compression: language becomes easier while clinically consequential uncertainty or unusual subjective effects become less precise. 4 The unresolved issue is therefore consenting fidelity, defined as correspondence among the protocol, every language version, the consent conversation, and the participant’s demonstrated understanding.

This distinction is especially consequential in the Philippines. The first national survey found limited health literacy among 51.5% of Filipinos, with substantial variation across populations and health literacy domains. 5 An English readability score cannot establish accessibility after translation into Filipino or a regional language. Translation itself may be underfunded and can restrict trial inclusion. 6 A form may satisfy an English grade target yet become conceptually harder when technical terms are transliterated, code switched, or explained orally. Thus, readability parity can coexist with informational inequity.

A further limitation is the assumption that consent is an exclusively individual transaction. Relational autonomy research shows that supportive relationships can strengthen decision making, whereas family pressure and dominant professional voices can weaken it. 7 Similar tensions have been documented in culturally collectivist clinical settings. 8 The appropriate counterargument is not family authorization, but participant controlled relational consent: individuals may invite a trusted person, while researchers separately confirm comprehension, voluntariness, and freedom to decline.

Philippine research ethics committees should require a consent fidelity dossier rather than a readability certificate alone. This dossier should document parallel English, Filipino, and locally relevant versions; independent translation review; cognitive interviews; and teach back assessment of study purpose, randomization, alternatives, rescue care, and ketamine related risks. Consent should be treated as continuous communication, not a single signature. 9 Layered summaries can address overload, 10 while electronic formats should supplement, not automatically replace, paper because evidence for superior comprehension remains mixed. 11 Reassessment is also warranted before repeated dosing or after material protocol changes.

Swieczkowski et al. establish a necessary benchmark, but future Philippine studies must test whether readability predicts actual, language specific comprehension and voluntary choice. Regulation should require demonstrated consent fidelity, because a simpler form is ethically insufficient when the participant cannot accurately explain the decision being made. That is the essential ethical standard meaningful consent requires.
