Correlation study of infrapatellar fat pad EI in assessing clinical symptoms of knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Guo Wenjing, Geng Fengqin, Fu Zhuo, Qi Weihua, Zhang Yaoli, Fu Jiahao, Xu Pai, Jiao Yaxue, Li Yaowei, Chen Miao, Li Geng, Feng Bilin, Shen Suhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) is considered a marker for disease progression in knee osteoarthritis (KOA), but currently no ultrasound parameters effectively quantify abnormal changes in IPFP. To investigate the correlation between clinical symptoms of KOA and semi-quantitative ultrasound scores as well as the echo intensity (EI) of the IPFP, and investigate the correlation between the IPFP EI and knee joint effusion and synovium. This study included 75 patients with primary KOA. Ultrasound semi-quantitative scoring of the knee joint and measurement of IPFP EI were performed. Patients’ Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) scores and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores were assessed. Spearman correlation analysis was used to compare the correlations between these two symptom scores and the ultrasound semi-quantitative score and IPFP EI. The correlation between IPFP EI and joint effusion and synovium was also compared. Ordered logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the factors influencing clinical symptoms of KOA. Correlation analysis reveals that VAS scores, WOMAC pain scores, stiffness scores, and functional scores all show a positive correlation with IPFP EI. Synovial thickening also exhibits a positive correlation with IPFP EI. Ordered logistic regression analysis demonstrates that IPFP EI is an independent and significant predictor of KOA symptom severity. In summary, synovial thickening shows a significant correlation with IPFP’s EI. The stronger the echo of the IPFP, the more severe the clinical symptoms of KOA. This may offer a new perspective for ultrasound in assessing the severity of KOA patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46880-5.

Introduction

KOA is a degenerative joint disease affecting the entire joint, primarily characterized by degradation of articular cartilage, abnormal hypertrophy of chondrocytes, abnormal bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation1,2, These symptoms diminish patients’ quality of life and impose a heavy economic burden on society3. According to statistics, Up to 25% of people aged 50 and older suffer from KOA4. However, by the time patients exhibit typical symptoms of joint pain, stiffness, deformity, and functional impairment, the disease has progressed to an advanced stage where symptom relief and functional recovery become challenging5. Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains the only effective treatment for end-stage OA. Therefore, elucidating the pathogenesis of KOA and identifying novel therapeutic targets are crucial for slowing its progression. Changes in the intra-articular micro -environment significantly influence the progression of KOA, and any tissue within the joint may contribute to its onset and development6. Previously established histological and molecular evidence demonstrates that inflammation is an active component in the OA process7. New evidence indicates that tissue inflammation and fibrosis in the IPFP play a significant role in the onset and progression of KOA8. However, there are currently no effective ultrasound parameters to quantify abnormal changes in IPFP.

IPFP is a fatty tissue cushioning the patella, patellar ligament, anterior aspect of the femoral condyle, and superior border of the tibial condyle. It was first identified and described by German physician HOFFA in 19049. This adipose tissue is rich in blood vessels, nerve fibers, and diverse immune cell populations, playing a crucial role in knee joint health10. Its primary functions include load-bearing, expanding the synovial cavity, supplying blood to the patellar tendon, and secreting lubricants within the joint11. As the knee’s local adipose tissue, IPFP serves as a primary source of inflammatory mediators—including cytokines, interleukins, and adipokines—which permeate the synovium and promote cartilage and meniscus degeneration12. Studies indicate that in some specimens, the IPFP surface adjacent to the synovium exhibits severe fibrosis, and synovitis may be the primary cause of IPFP fibrosis13. EI is defined as the quantitative value representing the average pixel intensity within the region of interest. Its numerical value is closely correlated with the pixel density of the image and can sensitively reflect changes in tissue water content, Brighter pixels have higher values, while darker pixels have lower values14,15. In muscle diseases, EI has been recognized as a sensitive indicator for assessing muscle mass and strength by reflecting the ratio of fat cells to fibrous tissue within muscle tissue, particularly changes in intramuscular fat16,17. This study aims to measure the EI of IPFP in patients with KOA and to compare whether changes in its EI correlate with clinical symptoms of KOA.

Knee X-rays are the most commonly used diagnostic method for KOA, frequently employed to assess its severity18, but they cannot reveal changes in surrounding soft tissue composition. MRI clearly displays all structural features of KOA lesions. Parameters of MR signal intensity, shape, and volumein the IPFP have been considered as biomarkers for structural abnormalities and inflammation severity in KOA19. While ultrasound demonstrates 83% sensitivity and 75% specificity for detecting osteophytes20. Compared to X-rays and clinical examinations, ultrasound can identify more osteophytes and detect medium-to-large osteophytes more readily than MRI. Furthermore, ultrasound findings show significant correlation with multiple MRI features21. Clinical studies suggest that early cartilage changes detectable by musculoskeletal ultrasound may be one source of KOA pain22.Ultrasound assessment of KOA severity is a multiparametric, systematic process primarily focused on evaluating cartilage condition, osteophyte formation, synovial inflammation;effusion, and meniscal degeneration. However, Ultrasound shear wave elastography assessment of IPFP stiffness revealed increased IPFP hardness with increasing OA severity in patients with KOA13,23. Despite these findings, the organizational characteristics of IPFP have not been fully studied. To investigate the clinical utility of ultrasound parameters in assessing KOA symptoms, this study explored the correlation between US parameters and KOA symptom severity by evaluating semi-quantitative US parameters of the knee joint and measuring the EI of the IPFP.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study included patients who visited Luoyang Orthopedic Hospital in Henan Province between May 2025 and November 2025 due to knee pain. Seventy-five patients with primary KOA were included in the study based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards and was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Luoyang Orthopedic Hospital of Henan Province (Henan Orthopedic Hospital) (Approval No.: 2024KYK0006-02). All enrolled participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. This research was conducted following the relevant guidelines and regulations including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnostic criteria

All enrolled patients met the 2001 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) diagnostic criteria for KOA; ① Recurrent knee pain over the past month; ② Radiographs (standing or weight-bearing position) showing joint space narrowing, subchondral bone sclerosis, and/or cystic changes, along with marginal osteophytes; ③ Age ≥ 50 years; ④ Morning stiffness lasting ≤ 30 min; ⑤ Crepitus during movement. Inclusion criteria include:(1) Meeting diagnostic criteria for KOA; (2) Age > 50 years; (3) No history of knee surgery or trauma within the past 3 months; (4) No recent history of knee infection or non-infectious inflammatory disease。The following conditions are excluded: (1) Patients with systemic inflammatory diseases (e. g. , rheumatoid arthritis); (2) Patients with intellectual disabilities or psychiatric disorders; (3) Patients with a history of surgery or infection in the affected knee joint.

Musculoskeletal ultrasound examination

Prior to performing musculoskeletal ultrasound (MSKUS) examinations, Clinicians at the Knee Joint Center assess patients’ baseline data (gender, age, height, and weight) along with VAS scores and WOMAC scores for the affected knee joint. The Canon Aplio 500 ultrasound diagnostic system with a 10 MHz linear array probe was used. For the examination, all patients were positioned supine with the knee flexed at 20 degrees, using a towel rolled beneath the knee. Effusion and synovial thickening in the suprapatellar region were assessed; the medial and lateral menisci were evaluated; the medial and lateral collateral ligaments and the IPFP were examined. Articular cartilage damage was assessed at maximum knee flexion. In the standard section, longitudinal section images of the IPFP were collected. The criteria for lesion assessment and scoring were based on the semi-quantitative ultrasound scoring system for KOA established by the Organization for Methodology and Research in Rheumatology (OMERACT) in 201624. This included synovial thickening (0–1 points), joint effusion (0–1 points), articular cartilage (0–3 points), meniscal extrusion (0–2 points), and osteophytes (0–3 points). The sonographer performed a semi-quantitative ultrasound grading and measured the EI for each patient’s knee joint.

Since EI is influenced by ultrasound parameters, each patient underwent examination and imaging of the IPFP by the same musculoskeletal sonographer with ten years of experience, using identical ultrasound settings (depth, gain, and frequency). The ultrasound technician was unaware of the patient’s VAS and WOMAC scores during the scan.

Measure the EI of the IPFP

EI measurements were performed using ImageJ 1. 54f. software. Depth adjustment was performed to ensure the tibial plateau appeared in the deep plane before image acquisition (Fig. 1). First, the Ultrasound images were converted from RGB format to 8-bit format. Polygon functions were used to outline the boundaries of the IPFP, Carefully excluded the structures surrounding the fat pad. Then, using grayscale analysis and histogram functions, the average voxel intensity of the selected region was calculated. The original EI value was represented by any unit between 0 (black) and 255 (white) (Fig. 2). The EI value was measured three times at different time intervals and was averaged. VAS score and WOMAC score25,26

Pain and knee function were assessed using the VAS and WOMAC, respectively. Clinical knee symptoms were assessed using VAS scores and WOMAC scores over the past week. The VAS ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe pain. The WOMAC index comprises three Sects. (24 items total): pain (5 items), stiffness (2 items), and function (17 items). Each item is scored from 0 (“none”) to 4 (“extremely severe”). The total score is 96 points, distributed as follows: pain (20 points), stiffness (8 points), and function (68 points). Higher scores indicate more severe knee joint dysfunction. A WOMAC total score < 21 indicates mild severity, 21–48 indicates moderate severity, and > 48 indicates severe severity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27. 0 and R 3. 4. 1 software, with P < 0. 05 indicating statistically significant differences. The normality of measurement data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test: data meeting normality requirements were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (25th–75th percentile). Spearman correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the relationship between VAS scores, WOMAC scores, and ultrasonographic semi-quantitative parameters, as well as the association between IPFP EI and joint effusion/synovial hyperplasia. Correlation coefficients r with absolute values < 0. 3 were considered weak correlations, 0. 3–0. 6 indicated moderate correlations, and > 0. 6 represented strong correlations. Patients were categorized into mild, moderate, and severe groups based on WOMAC total scores, serving as an ordered multinomial dependent variable. An ordered multinomial logistic regression model was employed to analyze the impact of ultrasound semi-quantitative scores and IPFP EI on knee symptom severity. A parallelism test was conducted prior to model fitting to validate the proportional advantage hypothesis. If P > 0. 05, the proportional advantage was considered established. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Correlation analysis revealed that both osteophytes and meniscal extrusion showed significant positive correlations with patients’ VAS scores (r = 0. 290, P = 0. 012; r = 0. 273, P = 0. 018). However, neither of these ultrasound parameters demonstrated significant correlations with pain, stiffness, functional impairment, or total scores on the WOMAC scale (P > 0. 05). Articular cartilage showed no statistically significant correlations with any clinical symptom scores (P > 0. 05). The IPFP EI exhibited the strongest correlations. It showed significant positive correlations with all clinical symptom scores, including VAS score (r = 0. 421, P < 0. 010), WOMAC pain (r = 0. 309, P = 0. 007), stiffness (r = 0. 240, P = 0. 038), function (r = 0. 271, P = 0. 018), and total score (r = 0. 300, P = 0. 009). Additionally, synovial thickening correlated with VAS score (r = 0. 405, P < 0. 01), WOMAC stiffness (r = 0. 304, P = 0. 008), function (r = 0. 235, P = 0. 043), and total score (r = 0. 262, P = 0. 023), but showed no significant association with WOMAC pain scores (P > 0. 05). In contrast, joint effusion showed no significant correlation with any clinical symptom score (p > 0. 05). (Table 2). To further investigate the relationship between IPFP EI and local inflammatory changes in the knee joint, this study analyzed the correlation between IPFP EI and joint effusion as well as synovial thickening(Table 3) . Analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between IPFP EI and synovial thickening (r = 0. 258, P = 0. 026), whereas no significant correlation was observed between IPFP EI and joint effusion (r = 0. 024, P = 0. 841). Discussion

In this study, VAS scores showed significant positive correlations with osteophyte scores (r = 0. 290) and meniscal extrusion (r = 0. 273). However, the WOMAC pain score was significantly correlated only with IPFP, showing no significant association with other intra-articular structural indicators (osteophytes, meniscal extrusion). This suggests that the source of KOA pain may be more likely to originate from inflammation (synovium, IPFP) rather than purely mechanical structural damage (osteophytes, meniscal extrusion). For stiffness and functional impairment, the impact of structural lesions (such as meniscal extrusion) is not significant, further supporting that aseptic inflammatory activity is the key driver influencing patients’ daily experiences. This finding is consistent with the results from MRI studies showing that increased IPFP signal intensity (indicating inflammation) is positively correlated with knee pain27,28. IPFP EI showed significant positive correlations with all clinical symptom scores in this study (VAS scores, WOMAC pain, stiffness, function, and total scores), indicating that enhanced IPFP echo plays a relatively important role in reflecting the overall pathology of KOA. In this study, synovial thickening was also associated with all clinical symptom scores. Therefore, in this cohort, soft tissue factors such as synovial inflammation and the IPPF exert a direct influence as the primary drivers of pain, while the role of bony factors appears limited. This conclusion is inconsistent with recent studies examining the association between osteophytes, articular cartilage, and pain29. The discrepancy between our findings and previous studies may be attributed to our smaller sample size and the inclusion of only symptomatic primary KOA patients.

In muscle diseases, muscle EI is increasingly used as a marker of skeletal muscle mass. Studies indicate that in older adults, higher EI values (brighter images) correlate with increased interstitial fibrous tissue within skeletal muscle30. This study aims to measure the EI of the IPFP and investigate its relationship with the progression of KOA symptoms. Correlation analysis revealed a positive association between IPFP EI and clinical symptoms of KOA. Ordered logistic regression analysis indicated that ultrasound-measured EI is a primary factor influencing KOA clinical symptoms, suggesting that changes in IPFP composition occur during the progression of KOA. Recent studies have revealed that as KOA progresses, adipocytes in the IPFP are progressively replaced by inflammatory cells and fibrous tissue, leading to a reduction in fat content. This transformation is closely associated with the inflammatory response and metabolic dysfunction observed in IPFP31,32. Previous studies indicate that inflammatory hyperplasia and hypertrophy of the IPFP initially manifest as fibrosis and calcification, accompanied by clinical features of knee pain, restricted mobility, and swelling. This represents the outcome of recurrent microtrauma to the IPFP. Among various pathological manifestations, fibrosis is one of the most characteristic changes in patients with OA. Histological examination reveals that the degree of fibrosis in the IPFP is more severe than in the subcutaneous soft tissue10. Satake and Okada et al13,23. investigated the relationship between IPPF ultrasound elastography and KOA severity. Results showed that as OA severity increased, IPFP shear wave velocity increased, and pathological biopsy confirmed a higher proportion of fibrosis detected in IPFP. This suggests that the elevated EI value of IPFP in KOA patients may result from fibrosis.

The correlation analysis results of this study indicate that the EI values of IPPF show a positive correlation with synovial thickening, but no correlation with effusion. Increased infiltration of immune cells and secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 in the IPFP can alter the phenotype of synovial fibroblasts, promoting fibrosis throughout the joint33. Conversely, cytokines secreted by the synovium can induce an inflammatory response in the IPFP10. Oo et al34 demonstrated a statistically significant correlation between synovial hypertrophy and pain severity, but no association with effusion, consistent with the findings of this study. This suggests that synovial lesions and IPFP fibrosis likely exert synergistic effects: inflammatory mediators originating from the synovium drive pathological changes in the IPFP, while the fibrotic, functionally compromised IPFP further exacerbates symptoms through mechanical imbalance and neural stimulation. Targeting the IPFP-synovium pathological axis in KOA treatment may offer a novel approach to alleviate pain and slow disease progression in patients. Overall, musculoskeletal ultrasound provides new insights into assessing KOA severity through its high soft tissue resolution and quantitative analysis of IPFP. However, it is worth noting that this study did not include joint synovial fluid analysis or peripheral blood test indicators, Therefore, the precise inflammatory pathways underlying the correlation between US parameters and symptoms remain incompletely elucidated.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the semi-quantitative ultrasound parameters used may be subject to operator dependence and lack the precision of quantitative measurements. Second, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings. Third, while we used WOMAC symptom severity scores for group classification in the ordered logistic regression analysis, future studies incorporating standardized radiographic grading systems—such as Kellgren-Lawrence (K-L) grading or MRI-based classification—would provide more robust and compelling evidence. Fourth, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes causal inferences and can only demonstrate associations between variables. Specifically, it remains unclear whether increased IPFP EI contributes to symptom severity or, conversely, whether severe symptoms exacerbate pathological changes within the fat pad. Future research should prioritize validating the inflammatory nature of IPFP EI through biochemical markers and adopt longitudinal study designs to elucidate the temporal dynamics and causal relationships underlying these observations.

Conclusion

In summary, despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the correlations between ultrasonographic semi-quantitative parameters and IPFP EI with clinical symptoms in KOA.
