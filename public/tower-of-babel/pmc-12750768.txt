Implementation strategies for the WHO Safe Childbirth Checklist: a scoping review
Authors: Zenewton André da Silva Gama, Katherine E A Semrau, Tatyana Maria Silva de Souza Rosendo, Marise Reis de Freitas, Cecília Olívia Paraguai de Oliveira Saraiva, Christopher Michael Westgard, Carol Mita, Danielle E Tuller, Kelienny de Meneses Sousa Freitas, Rose L Molina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundThe WHO Safe Childbirth Checklist (SCC) has been implemented in diverse settings to improve the quality and safety of intrapartum care, but implementation strategies and their relationship with adoption and fidelity remain heterogeneous and incompletely described.ObjectivesTo describe the landscape of SCC implementation, map the implementation strategies used and explore how these strategies were reported in relation to adoption and fidelity.Eligibility criteriaWe included primary studies reporting SCC implementation in healthcare settings that described at least one implementation strategy, with no restrictions on country or language. Studies that did not report implementation strategies or did not involve SCC use in real-world care settings were excluded.Sources of evidenceWe searched PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Global Health and Global Index Medicus (June 2024), screened reference lists and consulted grey literature for the period 2009–2024.Charting methodsThis scoping review followed JBI methodology (Peters et al) and was reported according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews. We extracted study characteristics and implementation findings, coded strategies using the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy and grouped them by clusters. Adoption (initial uptake) and fidelity (adherence to core components) were categorised following Proctor’s implementation outcomes. We created a descriptive implementation intensity score and conducted exploratory analyses (tertiles, boxplot).Results34 studies described 19 SCC implementation projects across 16 countries. We identified 24 distinct ERIC strategies, with most projects using 5–11 strategies. Frequently reported strategies included educational meetings, audit and feedback, supervision, contextual adaptation and leadership or champions. Exploratory analyses did not show consistent associations between implementation intensity and adoption or fidelity. ‘Change infrastructure’ strategies (such as record system or equipment changes) were variably defined and warrant cautious interpretation. Adaptations (eg, translation and alignment with national guidelines) were common and aimed at improving local fit, but heterogeneous reporting limited cross-study comparability.ConclusionsSCC implementation has relied on diverse, multicomponent strategies, yet reporting—especially of strategy content and adaptations—remains insufficient, constraining comparison and synthesis across settings. As a pragmatic bundle, implementers may prioritise brief team training, unit-level champions and leadership signals, point-of-care audit and feedback, light-touch SCC adaptation that preserves core content and structured supervision or peer coaching, combined with systematic inclusion of women and families through codesign and companion-mediated prompting. Using theory-informed frameworks (such as Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment and Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [CFIR]) and standardised reporting tools (eg, Proctor’s outcomes; Template for Intervention Description and Replication / Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies [TIDieR/StaRI]) can make SCC implementation strategies more transparent, comparable and scalable.RegistrationOpen Science Framework: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/RWY27.Keywords: Implementation Science, Safety, Quality in health care

Introduction

Despite recent gains, maternal and neonatal mortality remains unacceptably high and inequitable, with approximately 287 000 maternal deaths, 1.9 million stillbirths and 2.3 million neonatal deaths each year, reinforcing the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) targets to accelerate reductions worldwide.1,3

The WHO developed the Safe Childbirth Checklist (SCC) as a tool to improve the quality of facility-based childbirth care and support 29 essential evidence-based practices at four critical time points: on admission, before pushing or caesarean delivery, within 1 hour after birth and before discharge.4 The SCC addresses key clinical causes of maternal (eg, haemorrhage, hypertensive disorders, infections) and neonatal (eg, asphyxia, prematurity, sepsis) mortality. Beyond promoting technical quality, the SCC encourages team communication, clinical accountability and person-centred care, aligning with broader frameworks of health systems strengthening and the right to health.5

Evidence supports the SCC’s potential to improve adherence to essential birth practices and reduce adverse outcomes.6 7 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that SCC implementation increased adherence by 65% and showed moderate-quality evidence of reducing stillbirths and early neonatal mortality.7 Implementation outcomes vary widely; there is limited documentation on how implementation strategies have been used across diverse settings, including how they have been reported in relation to key implementation outcomes such as adoption and fidelity.8

In 2015, the WHO published a global implementation guide for the SCC.9 Since then, countries and health systems have developed diverse strategies to implement and adapt the SCC to their institutional realities, often involving different levels of leadership engagement, provider training, audit systems and contextual adaptations.10 11 Importantly, the SCC was designed to be adapted to local contexts, allowing flexibility in content and implementation to align with context-specific protocols and workflows.9 While this flexibility is essential for contextual relevance and local ownership, it also poses challenges for cross-study comparisons and for generating generalisable evidence on implementation processes. There is no comprehensive synthesis examining how these strategies have been operationalised across settings, which strategies have been used, in what contexts, and their associations with implementation outcomes.

This scoping review was conducted to: (1) describe the landscape of organisations and facilities that have implemented the WHO SCC; (2) map the range of strategies used to implement the SCC; and (3) explore how these strategies have been reported in relation to implementation outcomes, particularly adoption and fidelity. These outcomes were prioritised due to their relevance in early-stage implementation and their relatively consistent measurement across studies.

By situating implementation evidence within the broader agenda of health systems strengthening and maternal health, this review contributes actionable insights for scaling context-sensitive, evidence-informed strategies to improve childbirth care.

Methods

This scoping review followed the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis and adhered to the six-step framework originally proposed by Arksey and O’Malley,12 later refined by Levac et al.13 We reported the review according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Checklist.14 We registered our protocol on the Open Science Framework15 and later published it.16 This approach enabled us to map how the WHO SCC has been implemented across diverse settings—identifying the strategies used, the contexts in which they were applied and the implementation outcomes reported.17 Detailed eligibility criteria were defined a priori in the protocol and applied consistently throughout study selection.

Step 1: Identifying the research questions

We structured our review using the Population, Concept, Context framework. The population included pregnant individuals and health professionals involved in childbirth. The concept focused on implementation strategies for the WHO SCC. The context included any health facility providing childbirth care. We used controlled vocabularies, such as Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Health Sciences Descriptors, for consistent terminology.

We aimed to answer the following research questions:

What types of organisations and facilities have implemented the WHO SCC?

What implementation strategies have been used across different contexts?

How have these strategies been reported in relation to implementation outcomes, particularly adoption and fidelity?

How have these strategies been reported in relation to implementation outcomes, particularly adoption and fidelity?

We drew the conceptual definitions of implementation outcomes from established implementation science frameworks, particularly those proposed by Proctor et al.18 Adoption was defined as the initiation or documented use of the SCC in clinical settings (eg, use recorded in patient charts or completion of at least one item at pause points). Fidelity was defined as the extent to which the SCC was implemented as intended, typically measured as the proportion of checklist items completed. These outcomes were selected due to their relevance to early-stage implementation and their relative frequency in the literature. Our analysis was descriptive in nature, as appropriate for a scoping review, and did not infer causal relationships.

Eligibility criteria

We included primary, empirical studies that: (1) reported implementation of the WHO SCC in real-world health facilities providing childbirth care and (2) described at least one implementation strategy used to introduce, adapt, scale up or sustain the SCC. We considered any study design (experimental, quasiexperimental, observational or mixed methods) and any publication status (peer-reviewed articles, theses or reports), with no language restrictions. We restricted the timeframe to 2009–2024 to correspond with the development and dissemination of the WHO SCC.

We excluded: (1) non-empirical publications such as reviews, editorials, letters, commentaries, protocols without implementation data and conference abstracts; (2) studies that did not focus on the WHO SCC (eg, other checklists or quality bundles); (3) simulation-only studies where the SCC was not used in routine clinical care; and (4) studies that evaluated clinical outcomes of the SCC but did not describe any implementation strategies. These criteria were defined a priori in the published protocol and guided all screening and selection decisions.

Step 2: Identifying relevant studies

To identify studies addressing our research questions, we conducted a systematic literature search in PubMed (National Center for Biotechnology Information), Embase (Elsevier, embase.com), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL Complete, EBSCOhost), Global Health (C.A.B. International, EBSCOhost) and Global Index Medicus (WHO, www.globalindexmedicus.net) on 17 June 2024. Controlled vocabulary terms (ie, MeSH, Emtree, CINAHL Subject Headings, Centre for Agriculture and Bioscience International (CABI) Thesaurus) were included when available and appropriate. The search strategy was developed and executed by a health science librarian (CM) in consultation with the research team. No language limit was applied. We restricted the search to studies published from 2009 onward to correspond with the development of the WHO SCC. The exact search terms used for each of the databases are provided in the online supplemental document 1.

Additionally, we searched grey literature in the following sources: Google Scholar, the CAPES Theses and Dissertations Portal, the National Library of Australia’s Trobe (Trove), DART-Europe E-Theses Portal, Electronic Theses Online Service and Latin American Theses and Dissertations. We also conducted citation tracking by reviewing the reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews.

Step 3: Study selection

Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts and assessed full texts using Covidence software.19 In the first stage, duplicate records and clearly ineligible references (eg, non-SCC interventions, non-empirical publications, simulation-only studies or articles published before 2009) were excluded based on titles and abstracts. In the second stage, full-text articles of potentially eligible records were retrieved and assessed against the eligibility criteria described above. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, without the need for third-party adjudication. The selection process was summarised in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

When studies did not report fidelity directly, we used proxies such as adherence to essential practices or direct observation, as reported by the authors. To harmonise outcome data, we calculated arithmetic means across time points, facilities or observation rounds when needed and treated these as continuous variables.

For descriptive subgroup analysis, we categorised adoption and fidelity into low, moderate or high levels using tertile cut-offs. These categorisations were used for descriptive purposes, to support visual summaries and exploratory comparisons across studies.

We also calculated a composite standardised implementation intensity score (Z-score: Intensity), based on two variables: (1) the number of ERIC strategies applied and (2) the number of ERIC clusters represented, as classified by Waltz et al.21 We standardised both (mean=0, SD=1) and computed the average as the intensity score. This metric enabled cross-study comparisons of implementation intensity (online supplemental note 1). This metric was developed to describe the breadth and complexity of implementation efforts, not to infer effectiveness.

To support transparency and replicability in how strategies were classified, we grouped each identified ERIC strategy into one of nine conceptual clusters, following the classification by Waltz et al.21 A complete list of identified ERIC strategies and their cluster classification is provided in table 1.

To further synthesise and contextualise the reported implementation strategies, we mapped them against the phases of the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework. This mapping enabled us to identify when specific strategies were used along the implementation continuum and to explore potential gaps in planning for long-term integration. The EPIS framework was used post hoc as a conceptual lens to complement our ERIC-based classification and support the interpretation of implementation patterns across phases.

Step 5: Collating, summarising and reporting the results

We produced descriptive and thematic summaries aligned with the review objectives. We described the global landscape of SCC implementation, synthesised strategies and their relation to implementation outcomes (as described by the authors) and identified gaps in implementation knowledge. We presented results narratively and supported them with tables and figures.

Step 6: Consulting stakeholders

To enhance the interpretation and applicability of the findings, we conducted a virtual expert panel with five professionals from Brazil and four from the USA. The panel included researchers and implementers affiliated with Ariadne Labs (a joint centre for health system innovation at Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and Brigham and Women’s Hospital) and the QualiSaúde research group (Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte), all with practical experience in SCC implementation. The consultation aimed to review preliminary results, validate interpretations and discuss contextual factors influencing SCC use. A brief presentation of key findings was followed by a structured discussion about implementation strategies, definitions of adoption and fidelity, contextual influences and the potential contribution of the SCC to maternal care. Insights from this consultation were used to refine the interpretation and reporting of results.

Results

Selection of evidence sources

Of the 2105 records identified (2070 from databases and 35 from citation tracking and grey literature), 691 duplicates were removed, resulting in 1411 records screened by title and abstract. After assessing 85 full-text articles for eligibility, 34 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis (figure 1).

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews flow diagram of the study selection process for the scoping review on the implementation of the WHO Safe Childbirth Checklist (SSC).

Study characteristics and landscape of SCC implementation across countries, organisations and levels of care

The included studies correspond to 19 distinct implementation projects of the WHO SCC, conducted across 16 countries. The studies spanned diverse institutional settings and levels of care, including primary, secondary and tertiary hospitals; public or private institutions; and facilities affiliated with government, academic and non-governmental organisations, located in both rural and urban areas. Several projects were described in multiple publications. SCC implementation efforts have ranged from small-scale feasibility pilot components (eg, baseline assessment, process evaluation or outcome analysis) to national and multinational programmes, demonstrating its adaptability across contexts. As summarised in online supplemental table 1 and illustrated in the global map (figure 2), SCC has been predominantly adopted in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), where maternal and neonatal mortality remain high. The BetterBirth Trial in India represents the most intensively evaluated implementation to date, covering 120 public facilities and over 157 000 births, supported by structured peer coaching and direct observations.22,27 The Dakshata programme, led by the Indian Ministry of Health, reached over 200 facilities,28,30 though with less standardised monitoring structures. Similarly, large-scale initiatives, such as the Preterm Birth Initiative (PTBi) in East Africa (Kenya and Uganda)31,33 and the Maternal and Newborn Health Quality of Care Initiative in Ethiopia,34 35 integrated SCC into broader quality improvement platforms.

Global sites of WHO Safe Childbirth Checklist implementation projects.

Implementations spanned all levels of care. Level 3 hospitals, including tertiary maternity hospitals, were implementation sites in Sri Lanka,36 Cameroon37 and China,38 while primary and secondary facilities were implementation sites in Bangladesh,39 Pakistan40 and Indonesia.41 42 Multilevel implementations (eg, BetterBirth, Dakshata, MNH Ethiopia) highlight the SCC’s adaptability across referral networks.

A wide range of stakeholders supported SCC implementation. Ministries of Health led several national efforts (eg, Ethiopia, India), while academic institutions such as Ariadne Labs, University of California, San Francisco, BRAC (formerly Bangladesh Rural Advancement Committee) University and the University of Göttingen led research and evaluation. International organisations including WHO, Johns Hopkins Program for International Education in Gynecology and Obstetrics (Jhpiego), Partners in Health, Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit and Clinton Health Access Initiative provided technical and financial support. Many projects embedded SCC within broader maternal and newborn health initiatives, such as the US Agency for International Development’s Transform project in Ethiopia and the East Africa PTBi.

While some studies mentioned adaptations to the SCC (eg, integration into local tools or workflows), detailed descriptions of such adaptations were limited.

Methodological features of included studies

While we did not perform a formal critical appraisal, we examined study designs and reporting features to contextualise the implementation findings. The 34 studies included in this review represent a heterogeneous body of evidence on SCC implementation, encompassing a variety of study designs and reporting approaches. Study designs ranged from pragmatic cluster-randomised trials to quasiexperimental pre–post studies and mixed-methods evaluations, reflecting the complexity of real-world implementation.

The BetterBirth trial in India22,27 and the SCC trial in Pakistan40 stand out for their scale and methodological structure, including use of cluster-randomised designs, predefined protocols and comprehensive data collection systems. However, despite their strengths, both trials faced important limitations in the measurement and reporting of implementation outcomes—particularly fidelity. For example, BetterBirth documented substantial variation in checklist adherence across facilities and over time23 25; post hoc analyses underscored the challenges of sustaining high fidelity even with intensive coaching.23 27 By contrast, most other studies used quasiexperimental pre–post designs without control groups (eg, Brazil,43 44 Zambia,45 Ethiopia34 35), which, while useful for assessing real-world feasibility, limit causal inference due to potential confounding. The study from Mexico,5 however, explicitly adopted a convergent mixed-methods design, integrating quantitative adherence data with qualitative findings on feasibility and acceptability.

The scale and scope of implementation also influenced methodological depth. Larger, multisite initiatives such as BetterBirth,22,27 Dakshata,28,30 the PTBi in Kenya and Uganda,31,33 and the Maternal and Newborn Health Quality of Care Initiative in Ethiopia34 35 were more likely to incorporate structured evaluation components, including routine data monitoring and fidelity tracking. In contrast, smaller or single-site studies, such as those in Namibia,46 Rwanda47 and Tanzania,48 often lacked standardised outcome measures, formal frameworks or longitudinal follow-up.

Across the included studies, methodological quality and reporting consistency varied widely. While some used robust designs and structured outcome measures, many relied on simpler approaches with limited application of implementation frameworks or reporting of system-level outcomes. These inconsistencies limited cross-study comparability and precluded meta-analysis. A total of 24 distinct ERIC strategies were reported. Most studies applied between 5 and 11 strategies, with educational meetings (n=17), adaptability promotion (n=17), audit-and-feedback (n=11), distributing educational materials (n=13) and clinical supervision (n=11) being the most frequently used. Other common strategies included promoting champions or coalitions (n=9) and assessing readiness (n=8).

Most implementation efforts combined strategies from at least three ERIC clusters. The most commonly represented clusters were: ‘Train and educate stakeholders’ (eg, educational meetings, educational materials, ongoing training) (n=18); ‘Use evaluative and iterative strategies’ (eg, audit-and-feedback, PDSA cycles, quality monitoring systems) (n=17); and ‘Adapt and tailor to context’ (eg, promoting adaptability, using local knowledge) (n=16). Less frequently used clusters included ‘Change infrastructure’ (eg, change record systems or equipment) (n=6) and ‘Use financial strategies’ (eg, financial incentives) (n=1). The number and combination of strategies varied substantially, a pattern reflected in the implementation intensity scores presented in table 2. To support interpretation, ERIC strategies were grouped into nine clusters following Waltz et al, and the corresponding strategies for each cluster are illustrated in table 1.

Across the 19 implementation projects, strategy descriptions were most often pragmatic and quality-improvement oriented (eg, brief or multisession training, on-the-job supervision or coaching, regular audit-and-feedback meetings and adaptation of SCC forms into existing charts or tools), with SCC frequently integrated into ongoing quality initiatives. Only a minority of projects reported formal implementation frameworks: five projects described at least one framework (eg, IHI (Institute for Healthcare Improvement) Breakthrough Series, Opportunity–Ability–Motivation, Opportunity–Ability–Motivation-Supplies, PDCA (Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle)/PDSA (Plan–Do–Study–Act cycle) cycles, WHO Implementation Guide for the SCS, Program Impact Pathway). In our synthesis, we therefore applied the ERIC taxonomy and mapped strategies to the phases of the EPIS framework to provide a common lens across heterogeneous descriptions.

Figure 4 maps the implementation strategies used according to the phases of the EPIS framework. Assessing readiness and identifying champions aligned with the exploration and preparation phases, while educational meetings, audit-and-feedback and supervision were categorised in the implementation phase. Few strategies were associated with the sustainment phase. These findings highlight the breadth of strategies used and highlight EPIS phases where fewer strategies were reported in support of long-term integration.

Reported relationship between the implementation strategies and adoption and fidelity outcomes

Among the 19 implementation projects, outcomes related to adoption and fidelity were reported with substantial heterogeneity. Adoption and fidelity were assessed according to operational definitions detailed in the Methods section. However, definitions, measurement formats and data sources varied across studies, as detailed in online supplemental note 1. This heterogeneity in implementation outcomes mirrored the diversity of implementation approaches and the limited, uneven use of formal frameworks described above, further constraining cross-study comparability.

We explored how implementation strategies were reported in relation to adoption and fidelity outcomes using descriptive comparisons and the implementation intensity score; these analyses were exploratory and not designed to infer causality. Exploratory analysis did not identify consistent associations between higher implementation intensity and reported adoption or fidelity outcomes; accordingly, no causal inferences should be drawn. Similarly, applying a greater number of strategies did not consistently predict improved implementation outcomes.

Boxplot comparisons by ERIC cluster category showed that studies reporting ‘Change infrastructure’ strategies—such as modifying record systems or equipment—often had lower reported fidelity scores. However, this pattern was not examined with inferential purpose; it is hypothesis-generating only, and no conclusions about effectiveness can be drawn (figure 3).

Some studies, such as Kumar et al and Kabongo et al,28 46 applied multiple strategies across clusters and reported moderate-to-high adoption and fidelity. Others, such as Nababan et al,39 used fewer strategies and reported only partial outcome data. Due to the diverse configurations and reporting standards, no single strategy or combination was consistently described as being associated with superior adoption or fidelity outcomes.

Contextual factors such as leadership engagement, staff capacity and baseline infrastructure were reported inconsistently and often without analytical depth, limiting our ability to describe how these elements may have influenced implementation strategies or outcomes across settings.

Synthesis of results

This scoping review synthesises implementation experiences of the WHO SCC across diverse health systems contexts and countries. Most implementations occurred in public-sector maternity hospitals, often supported by ministries of health, academic institutions or international organisations (online supplemental table 1 and figure 2).

Implementation strategies varied widely, with most studies employing multicomponent approaches that combined educational, feedback, supervision and adaptive elements (figure 3 and table 2). While we noted some descriptive patterns (eg, lower fidelity in studies emphasising infrastructure changes), our exploratory analyses did not identify consistent, generalisable associations between strategy intensity or type and adoption or fidelity (figures4 5).

We observed heterogeneity in outcome definitions, strategy descriptions and use of implementation frameworks, which limited cross-study comparability. In particular, the predominance of pragmatically described strategies, the small number of projects using formal frameworks and the absence of dedicated evaluation frameworks reduced the possibility of drawing consistent inferences about which strategies were associated with better implementation performance.

Discussion

This scoping review examined how the WHO SCC has been implemented across diverse countries and settings, primarily in low-income and middle-income contexts. Since its publication, studies have documented substantial heterogeneity in facility types, implementation models and institutional support, consistent with the SCC’s adaptability.

Taken together, our findings provide a descriptive answer to the three review questions that guided this scoping review. First, the SCC has been implemented in at least 19 projects across 16 countries, predominantly in public-sector maternity hospitals and district-level facilities, often embedded in broader maternal and newborn quality improvement initiatives. Second, these projects used 24 distinct ERIC strategies, most frequently combined into multicomponent bundles drawing on the ‘Train and educate stakeholders’, ‘Use evaluative and iterative strategies’ and ‘Adapt and tailor to context’ clusters, with additional but less frequent use of ‘Develop stakeholder inter-relationships’, ‘Provide interactive assistance’ and ‘Change infrastructure’. Third, although adoption and fidelity were defined and measured heterogeneously across studies, our synthesis suggests that higher implementation intensity—defined as using more strategies and clusters—was not consistently associated with higher adoption or fidelity, highlighting the importance of contextual fit and coherent design of strategy bundles rather than the sheer number of strategies.

We identified 24 distinct ERIC strategies, spanning eight of the nine conceptual clusters. Notably, the ‘Engage consumers’ cluster was not reported in any included study. Rather than indicating infeasibility, this gap suggests a missed opportunity—and may partly reflect reporting limitations—to strengthen adoption and fidelity. Concretely, future implementations can: codesign SCC adaptations and workflows with pregnant women; enable companions/doulas to act as real-time checklist prompters; integrate brief, structured postbirth debriefs that capture women’s feedback; and provide plain-language materials that mirror SCC items. These actions operationalise ‘Engage consumers’ within routine care and are compatible with the pragmatic bundle above.

Most strategies were applied in multicomponent bundles, with frequent use of clusters such as ‘Train and educate stakeholders’, ‘Adapt and tailor to context’ and ‘Use evaluative and iterative strategies’. These clusters appeared in nearly all projects and accounted for the largest share of strategies. This predominance of bundled, context-sensitive approaches reflects current implementation practice and aligns with prior experiences of SCC scale-up.10 11

Adoption and fidelity were the most frequently reported outcomes. However, definitions, measurement methods and reporting formats varied widely, limiting comparability. Our exploratory analyses did not reveal consistent associations between the number or type of strategies and higher implementation performance. This pattern is consistent with the notion that contextual alignment and coherence may matter more than strategy count, but our exploratory synthesis does not support causal inference.

Strategies within the ‘Change infrastructure’ cluster—primarily record-system modifications—were less commonly reported and, in some studies, co-occurred with lower fidelity, aligning with ERIC expert-consensus ratings that have classified this cluster as lower on importance and feasibility since 2015.21 Pragmatically, teams can leverage existing record systems with minimal, reversible adjustments (eg, templates, prompts) and sequence any unavoidable system changes alongside workflow redesign, brief user training and near-term audit-and-feedback to minimise disruption and resistance.21

Adaptation to the SCC—such as translation, formatting changes, digital integration or alignment with national protocols—was frequently described. These adaptations may have enhanced local feasibility but introduced variability that complicates cross-site comparisons. Moreover, reporting on adaptation was inconsistent and rarely followed the standard framework. Future research should distinguish between core and adaptable elements to preserve intervention fidelity while allowing contextual flexibility.10 Clear reporting that distinguishes core versus adaptable elements would help maintain fidelity while allowing contextual flexibility, while also improving comparability across settings.

Some projects with higher reported adoption or fidelity described practices such as leadership engagement, audit and feedback, and structured supervision. However, given inconsistent reporting and heterogeneous measurement, no specific strategy or combination can be linked to superior outcomes in this review.

What has been widely studied and what remains underexplored

This scoping review of SCC implementation helps distinguish areas where implementation knowledge is relatively mature from those where evidence remains sparse. Most published work has focused on facility-level SCC implementation in public hospitals in LMICs, using multicomponent bundles centred on training, supervision, audit-and-feedback and contextual adaptation, often embedded in broader quality improvement initiatives led by ministries of health or international partners. In contrast, we found far fewer studies that: (1) engaged women, families or community actors as explicit implementation partners; (2) evaluated organisational, financing or policy-level strategies to support SCC scale-up; (3) explicitly planned for sustainment beyond short-term project cycles; or (4) examined SCC implementation in high-income settings or primary-level birth centres. Reporting on the dose, sequencing, mechanisms of action and costs of implementation strategies was also limited. These gaps indicate that, despite the growing number of SCC implementation studies, important questions remain about people-centred and system-level strategies, as well as long-term sustainment and scale-up.

Practice implications (promising strategies)

Although effect estimates were beyond the scope of this scoping review, convergent descriptions across settings suggest a pragmatic implementation bundle that is feasible and actionable: across countries and health systems, projects that reported higher or sustained SCC use tended to combine: (1) brief practical team training that rehearsed SCC use within existing workflows (eg, at the bedside or during pre-briefs); (2) unit-level champions plus visible leadership signals that normalised SCC use as routine practice; (3) simple, point-of-care audit-and-feedback cycles (rapid cycles, run charts or regular team meetings) focused on SCC use and essential practices; (4) light-touch contextual adaptation of the SCC (templates, prompts, integration into existing forms) while preserving core content; and (5) structured supervision or peer coaching to sustain routines and help teams problem-solve barriers over time. Where feasible, these bundles can be strengthened by systematically including women and families—for example, through codesign of SCC use and companion-mediated prompting. These practice-oriented suggestions are grounded in convergent descriptive evidence and implementation logic, not in causal inference, but they offer concrete guidance for implementers while allowing local tailoring.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, heterogeneity and incomplete reporting may have introduced measurement and reporting bias; ERIC strategies could have been misclassified within broad clusters, and details on dose, duration, sequencing and execution quality were rarely available, affecting cross-study comparability. Second, adoption and fidelity were defined and measured inconsistently, sometimes requiring proxy indicators. Third, strategies were frequently reported as multicomponent bundles, limiting our ability to isolate specific effects. Fourth, our standardised implementation intensity score was a descriptive index of breadth (number of strategies) and was not intended for causal interpretation. Fifth, contextual factors—such as leadership, team capacity, organisational culture and infrastructure—were unevenly reported, limiting interpretation of between-site variability. Lastly, as a scoping review, we did not assess methodological quality or risk of bias.

Conclusions

This scoping review shows that the WHO SCC has been implemented in 19 projects across 16 countries, mainly in public maternity hospitals and district-level facilities in low-income and middle-income settings, using multicomponent bundles dominated by training, supervision, audit-and-feedback and contextual adaptation, with little attention to strategies that engage women and families or operate at organisational, financing or policy levels. We found no consistent association between higher implementation intensity (more ERIC strategies and clusters) and better adoption or fidelity, supporting the use of a pragmatic, context-sensitive bundles that combine brief team training, unit-level champions and visible leadership, point-of-care audit-and-feedback, light-touch SCC adaptation that preserves core content and structured supervision or peer coaching, with systematic involvement of women and families where feasible. Future SCC work should draw on theory-informed frameworks (eg, EPIS, CFIR), use standardised reporting of implementation strategies and outcomes, and address gaps in consumer engagement, system-level strategies, and long-term sustainment and scale-up.
