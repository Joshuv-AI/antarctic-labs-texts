Prolonged brain cytokine elevation and microgliosis following exposure to World Trade Center dust particulate matter in male spontaneously hypertensive rats
Authors: Kailey M Jerome, Brandon M Gibson, Matthew W Gorr, Neill A Schwieterman, Mitchell D Cohen, Loren E Wold, Colin K Combs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExposure to World Trade Center (WTC) dust following the September 11, 2001 attacks has been linked to numerous negative health outcomes in First Responders, including cognitive impairment disproportionately impacting First Responders with aging. However, the mechanisms underlying neurocognitive decline remain poorly characterized. This investigation examined the effects of WTC dust exposure on myelin integrity and neuroinflammatory signaling in twelve-week old male spontaneously hypertensive rats. To model Ground Zero exposure in First Responders, WTC dust was intratracheally administered for two consecutive days (2.43 [± 0.78] mg/day). It was hypothesized that WTC dust exposure would enhance neuroinflammatory signaling and promote an earlier, progressive decline of myelin abundance. Rats were euthanized at 3 and 12 mo post-exposure. Changes in myelin abundance (Black-Gold II), myelin basic protein (MBP) and 2’,3′-cyclic nucleotide 3′-phosphodiesterase (CNPase), and microglial responses (ionized calcium-binding protein; Iba1) were assessed through immunohistochemical staining, and cytokines were measured in brain homogenates. WTC dust exposure resulted in increased Black-Gold II staining at 3 mo despite no changes in MBP or CNPase. In contrast, microglial activation and IL-1β concentrations were elevated in dust-exposed rats at 12 mo. Collectively, these data suggest WTC dust exposure induces sustained neuroinflammatory signaling and may elicit a compensatory myelin response indicative of early neurodegeneration.Keywords: World Trade Center dust, myelin, microgliosis, cytokine, IL-1β, brain, neuroinflammation

Introduction

The World Trade Center (WTC) collapse created one of the most concentrated urban pollution events in modern history (Mears et al. 2022). As the dust settled over New York City, Ground Zero became a crucible, not just of heroism but of vulnerability for First Responders, victims, and onlookers alike. It has been seen that the WTC dusts were chemically active and biologically persistent, and exposures to these agents have evolved to become medically consequential. Unlike typical air pollution, the sudden collapse of the World Trade Center generated a highly alkaline coarse- (>10 μm in diameter; >90% of total particles present) and fine (< 2.5 μm in diameter) construction grade particulate matter (PM) (Gavett 2003; Maciejczyk et al. 2005; Vaughan et al. 2014; Cohen et al., 2015). As a component of urban air pollution, PM usually contains numerous toxic and carcinogenic substances (McGee et al. 2003) and repeated exposure to these particles has been found to increase the risk of numerous health conditions, including cardiovascular disease (Dabass et al. 2016), metabolic syndrome (Lee et al. 2019), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (Tsai and Hamblin 2017), and lung cancer (Arias-Pérez et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2021).

When inhaled, fine PM (PM2.5) specifically, is easily translocated into the systemic circulation causing damage to distal organs beyond the lungs (Yang et al. 2020) and in the context of neuro-inflammation, is capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier, perpetuating a decline in neurocognitive health (Calderón-Garcidueñas et al. 2004; Oberdörster et al. 2004). Clinical evidence has revealed a similar pattern of disease progression is present among WTC First Responders repeatedly exposed to airborne dusts at/near Ground Zero in the first days after the disaster. In this cohort (as compared to First Responders who were never at Ground Zero), there have been well-documented increased risks of cardiovascular (Jordan et al. 2013; Cohen et al. 2019; Sloan et al. 2021) and respiratory diseases (e.g., COPD and asthma) (Brackbill et al. 2009; de la Hoz et al. 2023), cancers (Li et al. 2022; Webber et al. 2021), cognitive impairments (Clouston et al. 2019, 2022; Kritikos et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2024), and symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Wisnivesky et al. 2011). Collectively, these outcomes are now well recognized in WTC First Responders, and as they have aged, the severity of disease progression has become more apparent.

In the case of neurodegenerative disease progression, there is evidence that WTC dust exposure in First Responders is associated with an accelerated time-course of cognitive decline (Clouston et al. 2022). However, little mechanistic clarity exists to explain this phenomenon. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine the impact of WTC dust exposure on brain structures, and examine potential mechanisms of neurodegenerative disease. It was hypothesized here that short-term exposure to WTC dust collected from Ground Zero would cause a significant reduction in myelin abundance (Black-Gold II), myelin basic protein (MBP) and 2′,3′-cyclic nucleotide 3′-phosphodiesterase (CNPase), while increasing microglial responses (ionized calcium-binding protein; Iba1) and associated inflammatory cytokines.

To test this, spontaneously hypertensive (SHR) rats were intratracheally instilled with Ground Zero WTC dust on two consecutive days. This approach modeled the length and dose of exposure faced by First Responders and others present at Ground Zero over the first days following the collapse. SHR rats were utilized to simulate a preexisting health condition disproportionately impacting individuals, like First Responders, that are more likely to experience a greater volume of traumatic events (Balint et al. 2016). Following treatment, the dust-exposed and sham control groups were aged normally and assessed for changes in myelination, inflammatory cytokines, and microgliosis at 3 and 12 months. It was anticipated these findings would be used to characterize the deleterious pattern of enhanced neuroinflammatory signaling within First Responders exposed to WTC dust and aid in our understanding of potential health outcomes associated with this disastrous exposure event.

Materials and methods

Animals and exposures

Twelve-week-old male spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHR, Charles River, Wilmington, MA) were anesthetized with a cocktail of ketamine (80 mg·ml−1) and xylazine (4 mg·ml−1) dosed at 1.5 ml·g−1 of body weight under a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Ohio State University, which conformed to guidelines in the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Reporting complies with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. The rats were intubated via intratracheal instillation using a laryngoscope, and a “puff” of WTC dust was administered with a syringe. Using a separate syringe and tube, intubated sham control rats received a “puff” of air. A mirror was used to check for respiration moisture to ensure proper insertion of the tubes before puffs were delivered. 26 rats (n = 13 per group) were successfully exposed to either dust or sham control and survived the duration of the study. Rats were euthanized at 3-months based on lab availability within the time window. The remaining rats were aged to 12-months before being euthanized. However, two WTC dust rats (n = 2) died prior to the 12-month mark and were excluded from analysis. Brains were harvested from all mice that reached 3- and 12-months.

The total dose of dust was determined by change in weight from the syringe on a balance and a total of 2.43 ± 0.78 mg (mean ± SD) was delivered once each day for two consecutive days to rats (n = 13); sham controls only received an empty puff each day (n = 13). Each day, the rats were returned to their normal housing after the exposure. All rats were maintained in normal housing following the second exposure until scheduled sacrifice at 3 or 12 mo after the final exposure. The dosages were extrapolated from what would be expected to be entrained via mouthbreathing by a reference First Responder at Ground Zero based on 4–8 hr of exposure (Lorber et al. 2007; Cohen et al. 2015). Individuals caught in the initial fallout were exposed to airborne PM2.5 in the mg·m−3 range with subsequent days exceeding the EPA National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS) for PM2.5 (65 μg·m−3, 24-hr) at the perimeter of the site (Cohen et al. 2020). This dust level was similar to that used in previous exposure studies (Cohen et al., 2015), and was determined using the Multiple Path Particle Deposition Model program (MPPDep Version1.11, CIIT, Research Triangle Park, NC; RIVM, Bilthoven, the Netherlands). For this WTC dust dose, a conversion was made to generate rat equivalents (based on dose/surface area of trachea, bronchial and alveolar regions of respiratory tracts of humans and rats) (Cohen et al. 2015).

World Trade Center dust

World Trade Center dust was generously provided by Dr. Mitchell Cohen and collected at representative sites on/around the Main Pile at Ground Zero in the days following September 11th, 2001. All samples were carefully stored in airtight containers in the dark at room temperature to minimize potential for light, heat, or ambient gas-induced physiochemical alterations. Details about the preparation of WTC dust, as well as physiochemical properties have been previously reported elsewhere (Maciejczyk et al. 2005; Vaughan et al. 2014; Cohen et al., 2015,Lioy and Georgopoulos 2006). In brief, while most ambient dusts are classified as fine/ultrafine with a neutral pH (7–8), except for those that may have ambient adherent acids, the total mass of settled WTC dust was comprised of <1% fine (< 2.5 μm in aerodynamic diameter), and over 90% coarse (>2.5–100 μm) (Lioy et al. 2002). Further, WTC dust was characterized by a high percentage of calcium sulfate and gypsum (found in concrete) and was highly alkaline (pH 9–11). Interestingly, WTC dust, when found indoors, had an even greater alkalinity (pH >12) (Gavett 2003; Maciejczyk et al. 2005; Vaughan et al. 2014; Cohen et al., 2015). These Ground Zero dusts were also characterized by their relatively high concentrations of metals (Pb, Cr, Ni, etc.), as well as variable amounts of polychlorinated biphenyls (PCB), dioxins, volatile organic compounds (VOC) and asbestos (Cohen et al. 2020), with negligible levels of endotoxin (McGee et al. 2003).

Tissue processing

Left brain hemispheres were collected from the rats and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed brains were cryoprotected through two incubations of 30% sucrose then embedded in a 15% gelatin matrix block (5–6 brains/block), and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 d at 4°C. Subsequently, the samples were cryoprotected in a 15% sucrose solution for 24 hr at 4°C followed by two more incubations in 30% sucrose solution for 24 hr each at 4°C. Following these steps, the gelatin block was flash-frozen in dry ice/isopentane and serially cut into 24 divisions (at 40-μm thick) using a sliding microtome. Serially-sectioned tissue was mounted onto gelatin-subbed slides and used for staining.

Antibodies and reagents

Anti-CNPase [11–5B] (ab6319) and anti-MBP [IGX4321R-1] (ab216668) antibodies were purchased from Abcam (Waltham, MA). The anti-Iba1 (019–19741) antibody was purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Richmond, VA). Black-Gold® II Myelin RTD™ Staining Kit with Toluidine Blue O counter stain (TR-100-BG) was obtained from Bionsensis (Thebarton, South Australia). Biotinylated secondary antibodies, VECTASTAIN® Elite® ABC-HRP Kit (PK-6100), and Vector® VIP Substrate Kit (SK-4600) were purchased from Vector Laboratories (Newark, CA). Rat Cytokine Array Q3 (QAR-CYT-3) was obtained from RayBiotech (Peachtree Corners, GA)

Immunohistochemistry

Slide-mounted brain sections were rehydrated for ~ 2 min in dH2O. Serial sections containing the entire hippocampus per rat (~6 slides) were used for staining. Black-Gold® II was used to stain tissue in a Coplin jar covered in aluminum foil at 65°C using a rocking incubator for 20–25 min. Following a rinsing step for 2 min in dH2O, the slides were transferred to sodium thiosulfate fixative solution that was included in the Black-Gold® II Myelin RTD™ Staining Kit and allowed to incubate for 3 min at room temperature. The slides were rinsed again three times for 5 min at room temperature in tap water. After staining, slides were dehydrated, placed on coverslips, and then scanned at 20X magnification using a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer 2.0HT Brightfield Scanning System. Separately, slide-mounted brain tissue was used for immunostaining with antibodies recognizing myelin basic protein (1:500 dilution, rabbit monoclonal), CNPase (1:500 dilution, mouse monoclonal), and Iba-1 (1:1000 dilution, rabbit monoclonal). Approximately 6 slides of brain sections containing serial sections of the entire hippocampus per rat were used for each animal. Antigen retrieval steps were performed by heating the tissue to 121°C for 20 min in the appropriate buffer. Tissues for MBP staining were incubated in an acidic citrate buffer, while tissues used for staining Iba1 and CNPase were incubated in a basic EDTA solution. Following antigen retrieval, slides were incubated in 0.3% H2O2 and rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before being blocked in PBS with 0.5% bovine serum albumin, 0.1% Triton X-100, 5% normal goat serum, and 0.02% sodium azide for at least 2 d at 4°C. The slide-mounted sections were then incubated in primary antibody solution (CNPase 1:500, Iba1 1:1000, MBP 1:500) for 4 days at 4°C.

After primary antibody incubation, slides were transferred to a biotinylated secondary antibody solution (1:2000 dilution) and incubated for 3 days at 4°C. Finally, slides were incubated in an avidin/biotin/horseradish peroxidase solution made from the VECTASTAIN® Elite® ABC-HRP Kit made according to manufacturer recommendations, for at least 2 d at 4°C. A Vector® VIP Substrate Kit was used after completing the final incubation to visualize antibody binding. The slides were dehydrated, coverslipped, and then scanned at 20X magnification using a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer 2.0HT Brightfield Scanning System.

Stain quantification

The open-source digitalized image analysis platform, QuPath (Bankhead et al. 2017), was used to quantify the staining of MBP, CNPase, myelin Black-Gold, and Iba-1 from all brain sections containing a portion of the hippocampus. To identify positive staining in our region of interest, the Random Trees (RTrees) pixel classifier was utilized at a resolution of high (1.84 μm/px) or very high (0.92 μm/px).

Cytokine array

Remaining flash frozen right hemisphere parietal cortex samples were weighed (average weight: 0.01–0.03 g) and added to a locking centrifuge containing 0.001% protease inhibitor in 300 μL RIPA buffer and 0.5 g of Bullet Blender® beads. Tubes were placed in the Bullet Blender® and homogenized for 3 min at the 6th-speed setting. Following homogenization, lysates were spun in a centrifuge for 10 min at 13,000 rpm at 4°C. A Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) was employed to determine initial protein concentrations from supernatants. A Rat Cytokine Array Q3 (RayBiotech) was used to assess cytokine levels from parietal cortex lysates following manufacturer protocols. Twenty-seven cytokines were assayed: CD86 (B-2), β-NGF, CINC-1 (CXCL1), CINC-2, CINC-3, CNTF, Fractalkine, GM-CSF, ICAM1 (CD54), IFNγ, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-13, LIX, L-Selectin, MCP-1, PDGF-AA, Prolactin R, RAGE, TCK-1 (CXCL7), TIMP-1, TNFα, and VEGF-A. Completed arrays were mailed to RayBiotech for scanning using a GenePix 4400 scanner with GenePix Pro software. The results were analyzed using the Quantibody Q Analyzer for the given kit provided by RayBiotech. Samples with no detectable levels were not analyzed.

Statistical analyses

Data are reported as means with 95% confidence intervals. Alpha was set at 0.05 for all statistical inferences, including control of family-wise error rates. Measurements obtained from different animals over time were analyzed using two-way mixed-effects models with exposure group and time set as fixed effects (Prism v.10.6.1, GraphPad, San Diego, CA). Planned contrasts between exposure groups at each timepoint were performed with multiplicity-adjusted p-values and confidence intervals using Šídák’s method.

Results

Immunohistochemical staining

As a general assessment of myelin, Black-Gold II staining was performed on brain sections from sham control and WTC dust exposed rats. At 3 mo, the dust-exposed rats had significantly greater percentage of positive staining compared with sham control rats (59 [51, 66] % vs 46 [40, 54] %, p < 0.01). In contrast, percentage of positive staining in sham controls was increased from 3 to 12 mo (61 [57, 66] %, p < 0.01) demonstrating an age-dependent increase comparable with WTC dust rats by 12 mo (p = 0.87).

Myelin-associated proteins were further evaluated by immunostaining for anti-myelin basic protein (MBP) and the mature oligodendrocytes marker, 2′,3′-cyclic nucleotide 3′-phosphodiesterase (CNPase). No significant differences were detected between WTC dust and sham controls at either 3 or 12 mo for MBP (both p > 0.05), or across time between either group (both p > 0.05). Similarly, CNPase staining did not differ between WTC dust and sham controls at 3 (38 [27, 50] % vs 26 [12, 40] %, p = 0.12) or 12 mo (71 [60, 82] % vs 69 [62, 76] %, p = 0.94); however, both groups displayed an increase in percent positive staining from 3 to 12 mo (both p < 0.01).

Microglial activation was assessed via Iba1 immunostaining. WTC dust and sham controls were not different at 3 mo (0.8 [0.5, 1.1] % vs 1.0 [0.6, 1.4] %, p = 0.86). By 12 mo, Iba1 positive staining was greater in WTC dust rats compared with sham controls (3.4 [1.3, 5.6] % vs 2.0 [1.5, 2.4] %, p = 0.01). Sham controls displayed a similar trend for increased Iba1 staining from 3 to 12 mo, although this did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.08).

Inflammatory cytokines

There was a significant increase in IL-1β concentration from 3 to 12 mo in WTC dust rats (78 [26, 130] pg·ml−1 to 554 [−272, 1380] pg·ml−1, p < 0.01). At 12 mo, WTC dust rats exhibited significantly greater IL-1β, compared with sham controls (vs 147 [48, 247] pg·ml−1, p = 0.02) that were unchanged from their 3-mo concentrations (157 [47, 268] pg·ml−1, p > 0.99). IFNγ did not differ significantly between groups or across time. However, there was an increase in WTC dust rats that approached significance from 3 to 12 mo (0.23 [0.05, 0.41] to 0.93 [−0.25, 2.1] pg·ml−1 p = 0.07), a trend not observed in sham controls (0.42 [−0.07, 0.91] pg·ml−1 to 0.48 [−0.09, 1.05] pg·ml−1, p = 0.97).

Fractalkine concentration increased from 3 to 12 mo in both WTC dust (213 [189, 236] pg·ml−1 to 292 [202, 382] pg·ml−1, p = 0.04) and sham controls (252 [174, 329] pg·ml−1 to 356 [309, 404] pg·ml−1, p < 0.01). However, these groups were not different at either timepoint (both p > 0.05). MCP-1 concentrations were similarly increased from 3 to 12 mo but in the sham controls (35 [29, 40] pg·ml−1 to 66 [24, 102] pg·ml−1). Sham control concentration of MCP-1, however, was not elevated compared to WTC dust at either timepoint (both p > 0.05) remaining unchanged from 3 to 12 mo (34 [24, 44] pg·ml−1 to 50 [20, 80] pg·ml−1, p = 0.43).

RAGE concentration was reduced from 3 to 12 mo in sham controls (p = 0.04). However, concentrations of RAGE did not differ between WTC dust and sham controls at 3- (157 [108, 206] pg·ml−1 vs 226 [133, 320] pg·ml−1, p = 0.20) or 12 mo (133 [13, 252] pg·ml−1 vs 119 [33, 204] pg·ml−1, p = 0.95). Prolactin receptor concentrations were reduced in WTC dust rats compared to sham controls at 3 mo (49041 [13469, 84615] pg·ml−1 vs 398488 [−133895, 930871] pg·ml−1, p = 0.05) but not at 12 mo 41597 [15054, 68139] pg·ml−1, vs 129280 [−149720, 408280] pg·ml−1, p = 0.81).

TCK-1, IL-10, IL-4, CNTF, PDGF-AA, and ICAM-1 concentrations did not differ between exposure at either the 3- or 12-mo timepoints (all comparisons p > 0.05). Several cytokines (CD86 (B-2), β-NGF, CINC-1 (CXCL1), CINC-2, CINC-3, GM-CSF, IL-1α, IL-2, IL-6, IL-13, LIX, L-Selectin, TIMP-1, TNFα, and VEGF-A) fell below the limit of detection across exposure.

Discussion

The current study explored potential mechanisms of neurodegeneration associated with WTC dust exposure and hypothesized that WTC dust exposure would reduce Black-Gold II staining, indicative of decreased myelination, and that this decline would manifest earlier in the WTC dust rats compared to sham controls. Contrary to this hypothesis, Black-Gold II staining was increased following WTC dust exposure at 3 mo (Figure 1). In contrast, from 3 to 12 mo, Black-Gold II staining increased in the sham controls, despite no further increase in the dust-exposed rats. By 12 mo, groups did not differ in Black-Gold II staining.

Exposure to ambient air pollutants is associated with demyelination, decreased cortical thickness, and diminished myelin repair (Woodward et al. 2017; Parolisi et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2023; Cho et al. 2023; Duchesne et al. 2023). However, early stages of neurodegeneration or neuroinflammatory signaling may elicit compensatory increases in myelination that preserve neurocognitive function before an eventual decline. Consistent with this concept, Chen et al. (Chen et al. 2021), displayed a similar increase in myelination following a demyelinating injury. Thus, elevated Black-Gold II staining observed at 3 mo may reflect a compensatory remyelination response induced by WTC dust exposure. Although our analyses were limited to 12 mo, it is plausible that later timepoints would reveal a subsequent decline in myelin abundance.

Notably, Black-Gold II staining provides an anatomical marker for myelin and does not capture upstream regulatory or inflammatory mechanisms. These findings highlight the importance of interpreting myelin abundance within the broader context of demyelination, remyelination and neuroinflammatory signaling within the brain across aging.

To further assess myelin regulation within the brain, we performed immunohistochemical staining for anti-myelin basic protein (MBP) antibodies and mature oligodendrocytes via CNPase. MBP immunoreactivity did not differ between WTC dust exposed rats and sham controls at either 3-or 12-mo timepoints (Figure 2). Although CNPase was elevated from 3 to 12 mo in both groups, no exposure-dependent differences were observed at either timepoint (Figure 3). Together, these findings indicate preserved myelin protein abundance despite age-related increases in oligodendrocyte-associated markers.

MBP has been proposed as a biomarker of myelin damage when measured in cerebral spinal fluid or serum (Martinsen and Kursula 2022). However, the stable MBP signal observed within brain sections suggests that overall myelin integrity is maintained across time. In contrast, CNPase, increased from 3 to 12 mo independent of WTC dust exposure, indicating an activation or increased oligodendrocytes associated with the normal aging process. As CNPase is expressed early during oligodendrocyte differentiation and precedes both MBP and proteolipid (PLP) (Lioy and Georgopoulos 2006), these findings support a compensatory oligodendrocyte response rather than an overt demyelination (Yu et al. 1994). Notably, inhalation of PM2.5, which is compositionally similar to WTC dust, has been associated with profound neuroinflammatory effects (Bhatt et al. 2015, Wang et al. 2018). However, such exposure did not further CNPase expression in the present study.

Immunohistochemical staining for ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule-1, Iba1, (Figure 4) revealed age-associated increases in microglial activation independent of exposure groups. Importantly, at 12 mo, WTC dust-exposed rats exhibited significantly greater Iba1 immunoreactivity compared with sham controls, indicating an exposure-specific pattern of inflammatory activation. Prior studies indicate microglial activation as an early neurodegenerative signal following environmental toxin exposure (Arias-Pérez et al. 2020), and have demonstrated that oligodendrocyte associated markers, such as CNPase, may increase in a compensatory manner (Pl et al. 2023). Thus, the combination of stable MBP expression, increased CNPase, and elevated Iba1 suggests an early compensatory response to WTC dust exposure.

Neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, are characterized by progressive neuronal dysfunction accompanied by microglial and astrocyte activation and accumulation and alteration in the inflammatory milieu (Motta et al. 2007). These pathological features correspond with systemic and region-specific increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNFα, and IL-6, which influence neuronal and vascular function (Walker et al. 2023, McGeer and McGeer 2002). Although, IL-1β has been linked to hypertension (Ho et al. 2020) and vascular damage associated cognitive impairment (Poh et al. 2022) interpretation of cytokine changes in the present study requires careful consideration and regional specificity.

A significant increase in IL-1β concentrations was detected at 12 mo in WTC dust-exposed rats, an effect not detected in sham controls. In contrast, other pro-inflammatory cytokines implicated in neurovascular dysfunction and cognitive impairment, including TNFα and IL-6, were below the limit of detection in parietal cortex samples. Additionally, fractalkine concentrations increased in an age-dependent manner in sham controls, consistent with its established role in microglial modulation during aging and hypertension (Ho et al. 2020, Ruchaya et al. 2012). Although fractalkine exhibited a similar trend across time, this effect did not reach statistical significance in WTC dust exposed rats, potentially due to limited sample size at 12 mo.

The absence of robust exposure-dependent cytokine differences in parietal cortex samples should be interpreted in the context of tissue availability and regional specificity. Analysis of cytokines was restricted to homogenates from the parietal cortex, whereas immunohistochemical analyses revealed exposure-specific increases in microglial activation across brain regions. As neuroinflammatory responses are highly region-dependent (Liraz-Zaltsman et al. 2011), it is plausible these findings emphasize a unique pattern of WTC dust exposure preferentially targeting brain regions involved in memory, spatial navigation, and emotional regulation rather than somatosensory and motor processing. To clarify this further, whole-brain and additional region-specific analyses should be performed in future work.

Taken together with the observed increases in microglial activation, via Iba1 staining, and a conservative interpretation of IL-1β changes, these data suggest WTC dust may exacerbate normal patterns of age-related inflammation in a regionally selective manner. Similar patterns of microglial activation following particulate matter exposure have been reported (Connor et al. 2021; Sahu et al. 2021; Shkirkova et al. 2022). These data are consistent with the broader clinical literature documenting that health outcomes among WTC First Responders directly exposed are exaggerated relative to non-WTC-exposed comparison populations (Clouston et al. 2024). Notably, this exaggerated health burden extends to immune-mediated diseases as WTC-exposed responders show a significantly elevated risk of systemic autoimmune conditions (Webber et al. 2015,Webber et al. 2016). Although speculative, these data suggest the possibility that environmental exposures, like WTC dust, contribute to neuroinflammatory signaling pathways relevant to cognitive dysfunction in First Responders, and aligns with an emerging pattern in which WTC dust exposure may drive immune dysregulation across multiple organ systems rather than the brain in isolation (Cohen et al. 2020). Future investigations targeting region-specific cytokine signaling and models integrating cognitive and occupational stressors and pathways of post-traumatic stress disorders will be critical for refining this interpretation.

Several considerations should be made when interpreting the translational relevance of this model. The acute/subacute 2-day exposure paradigm employed currently was intentionally designed to isolate the initial high-intensity fallout exposure characteristic of the first hours to days at Ground Zero, allow us to test whether this acute insult alone is sufficient to elicit a persistent neuroinflammatory phenotype. Therefore, this design choice necessarily omits any potential for decades of continued occupational exposure (e.g., low-level chronic exposure, occupational stress, psychological trauma, etc.) that many First Responders continued to experience beyond the initial event, and which are themselves independently associated with accelerated health decline observed in this population (Clouston et al. 2017,Clouston et al. 2016). Accordingly, while the 12- month observation window represents a substantial proportion of the rat’s adult lifespan based on standard rat-to-human age-scaling relationships (Sengupta 2013), the current work may serve as the foundational, hypothesis-generating timeframe necessary for isolating acute-exposure risk in this population rather than an idealized model that recreates completely the cumulative, multi-decade exposure history of First Responders. Additionally, the Authors acknowledge that the overwhelming proportion of WTC First Responders were male (Dasaro et al. 2017). Inclusion of female rats in future investigations may reveal a sex-dependent pattern of neurodegeneration vulnerability which remains an underexplored aspect of this disastrous event.

Conclusions

The present study utilized a model of exposure to WTC dust exposure collected from Ground Zero to examine processes relevant to neurodegenerative risk in First Responders. Our findings indicate WTC dust exposures increase myelin-associated Black-Gold II staining at 3-months compared with sham controls, suggesting an earlier alteration in myelin dynamics. In parallel, enhanced microglial activation indicates augmented gliosis that exceeds normal age-related changes compared to sham control. Although cytokine profiling was limited to parietal cortex homogenates, these data support a regionally selective pattern of inflammatory activation that may preferentially target higher order brain functions like memory and emotional regulation. Collectively, these findings significantly add to our understanding of how WTC dust exposure may influence neurodegeneration via neuroinflammatory and myelin-associated processes in First Responders.
