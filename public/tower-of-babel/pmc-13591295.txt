The Relationship Between Dysglycemia and Dyslipidemia in First‐Episode, Untreated Chinese Patients With Major Depressive Disorder at Different Ages of Onset
Authors: Hang Tan, Zhanjin Wang, Jie Ma, Zhan Wang, Xiang Yang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeDysglycemia, a common ailment in MDD patients, lacks research regarding its variance across the age of onset (AOO). Therefore, we investigated the correlation between fasting blood glucose (FBG) abnormalities and blood lipids in first‐onset, untreated patients with MDD and to determine the factors that influence glycemic abnormalities in patients differentiated by age of onset.Patients and MethodsThe study enrolled 1718 Han Chinese outpatients with drug‐naïve, first‐episode MDD from a general hospital. Anxiety, depression, and psychotic symptoms were evaluated using the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD), Clinical Global Impression of Severity Scale (CGI‐S), and Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) positive subscale, respectively. we divided patients into early age of onset (EAO, < 45 years) and middle age of onset (MAO, ≥ 45 years) groups.ResultsMAO patients, compared to EAO patients, displayed higher scores on the HAMD, HAMA, CGI‐S, and PANSS positive scales. Furthermore, they exhibited elevated serum levels of thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), FBG, total cholesterol (TC), and low‐density lipoprotein (LDL‐C). In MAO patients, FBG correlated with TSH, triglycerides (TG), and TC, while only TSH correlated with FBG in EAO patients. Path analysis revealed a significant indirect effect of FBG on lipid levels through depressive symptoms, accounting for 35.46% of the total association in EAO patients and 16.22% in MAO patients.ConclusionAge‐stratified analyses revealed that MDD‐associated metabolic dysregulation in younger patients (< 45 years) was mediated by psychiatric symptoms, warranting depression‐focused interventions, whereas older patients (≥ 45 years) demonstrated direct metabolic dysfunction, necessitating integrated psychiatric‐metabolic management.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD), a leading cause of global disability (Chen et al. 2018), is associated with persistent residual symptoms that impair patients' quality of life and social functioning (Hiranyatheb et al. 2016; Nierenberg et al. 2010; Nil et al. 2016). Recent epidemiological studies highlight a rising prevalence of MDD worldwide, with increasing recognition of its heterogeneous clinical manifestations across age groups (Hu et al. 2015; Schaakxs et al. 2017).

Emerging evidence suggests that early‐onset MDD (typically < 45 years) may represent a distinct subtype characterized by more severe depressive symptoms (Park et al. 2014; Xiao et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2011), neurostructural alterations (e.g., reduced hippocampal volume) (Xiao et al. 2020; Nierenberg et al. 2007; Caetano et al. 2007; Schmaal et al. 2016), and elevated suicide risk. Despite growing interest in the metabolic comorbidities of MDD (Peng et al. 2017; Réus et al. 2017), few studies have explored age‐specific patterns of glucose‐lipid dysregulation in untreated patients—a critical gap given the bidirectional relationship between depression and metabolic dysfunction.

Recent meta‐analyses have further elucidated the bidirectional relationship between MDD and metabolic dysfunction. A 2022 systematic review and meta‐analysis by Smith et al. (2022) demonstrated that individuals with MDD have a 1.5‐fold increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes, with younger‐onset depression showing stronger associations. Similarly, longitudinal cohort studies, such as the UK Biobank study (Wang et al. 2021), have highlighted that metabolic abnormalities (e.g., elevated FBG, dyslipidemia) precede the onset of depressive symptoms in middle‐aged adults, suggesting a potential causal pathway. These findings underscore the importance of investigating metabolic dysregulation in untreated, first‐episode MDD populations to disentangle the temporal sequence of these comorbidities. For instance, depression doubles diabetes risk (Bouwman et al. 2010; Bernstein et al. 2013; De la Roca‐Chiapas et al. 2013), while diabetes patients exhibit twice the prevalence of depressive symptoms compared to the general population (Du et al. 2008; De Hert et al. 2011).

Notably, abnormalities in fasting blood glucose (FBG) and lipid profiles are associated with MDD and may hold predictive value for its onset or progression (Detka et al. 2014; Drevets et al. 2002; Li et al. 2014; Xie and Wang 2014). For instance, longitudinal data from large cohorts suggest that dyslipidemia and insulin resistance can precede and increase the risk of developing depressive symptoms (Watson et al. 2021). However, existing research predominantly focuses on medicated or chronic MDD populations, potentially confounding results with treatment effects. Recent studies have emphasised the importance of investigating first‐episode, drug‐naïve (FEDN) populations to understand the intrinsic pathophysiology of MDD, as clinical features and metabolic correlates can be significantly influenced by the duration of untreated illness (Luo et al. 2023a). Furthermore, preliminary evidence suggests a mediating role of thyroid and metabolic parameters in depression‐related somatic comorbidities (Luo et al. 2023b). Building on this evidence and to address the critical gap regarding age of onset, this study systematically examines the modulation of the FBG‐lipid interplay by age of onset in a large sample of FEDN MDD patients.

This study investigates two key questions in 1718 Han Chinese MDD outpatients: The association between FBG abnormalities and lipid profiles in untreated, first‐episode MDD. Based on previous study, we age‐stratified FBG dysregulation into early‐onset [< 45 years] vs. middle‐onset [≥ 45 years] (Park et al. 2014; Xiao et al. 2020).

Participants and Methods

This cross‐sectional study enrolled 1718 Han Chinese outpatients with first‐episode, drug‐naïve MDD from the Department of Psychiatry at Shanxi Medical University First Hospital (ethics approval No. 2016‐Y27). Consecutive eligible outpatients seeking their first psychiatric consultation at the study site were invited to participate. All participants provided written informed consent. Participation was voluntary, and they retained the right to withdraw at any time without affecting their standard medical care. Inclusion criteria were (1) MDD diagnosis confirmed by two psychiatrists using DSM‐IV Structured Clinical Interview (SCID); (2) Age 18–60 years with Han Chinese ethnicity and (3) No prior psychiatric/pharmacological treatment. Exclusion criteria were (1) Comorbid Axis I psychiatric disorders (assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐IV [SCID] (First et al. 1997) by two independent psychiatrists); (2) Severe physical illness (e.g., cardiovascular disease, cancer); (3) Pregnancy, substance dependence (excluding smoking), or refusal to consent.

Clinical and Metabolic Assessments

Socio‐Demographic and Anthropometric Measures

Trained researchers collected: Demographics and Clinical indices. Demographics including Age, sex, marital status, education level, illness duration; Clinical indices: Body mass index (BMI) (overweight threshold: ≥ 24.0) (Ji 2005), seated blood pressure (measured using an Omron HEM‐7120 monitor after 10 min of rest; mean of two readings taken 2 min apart) (Whelton et al. 2018); Age stratification: Early‐onset MDD (< 45 years) vs. middle‐onset (≥ 45 years) (Park et al. 2014; Xiao et al. 2020). Age stratification into early‐onset (< 45 years) and middle‐onset (≥ 45 years) groups was operationalised based on prior research in Chinese MDD populations, which has demonstrated distinct clinical and metabolic profiles between these age categories (Xiao et al. 2020). This cutoff also approximates the onset of midlife, a period characterized by increasing vulnerability to metabolic syndrome, allowing us to explore the interplay between depression and age‐related metabolic changes.

Psychometric Evaluations

Four validated scales assessed symptom severity: the 17‐Item Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD‐17) (depression severity; cutoff ≥ 24 (Lin et al. 2014), Cronbach's α = 0.88–0.99) (Sun et al. 2017), which was used as the primary continuous measure of core depressive symptom burden for correlation and mediation analyses; the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) (anxiety; 0–4 Likert scale) (Konstantakopoulos et al. 2013), employed to quantify the co‐occurring anxiety symptoms that frequently accompany MDD and may influence metabolic profiles; the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)‐positive subscale (psychotic symptoms; cutoff ≥ 15 (Shen et al. 2019), α = 0.73–0.83) (Aboraya and Nasrallah 2016), administered to screen for and quantify the severity of any co‐existing psychotic features, which define a more severe subtype of depression; and the Clinical Global Impression of Severity Scale (CGI‐S) (global illness severity) (Kay et al. 1987), which provided a clinician‐rated global assessment of overall illness severity and functional impairment.

Laboratory Procedures

After an overnight fast, blood samples were collected between 6 and 8 a.m. This study measured several biomarker parameters, including blood glucose, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL), thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine 3 (FT3), free thyroxine 4 (FT4), thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb), and anti‐thyroglobulin (TgAb). The determination of serum concentrations of TSH, FT3, FT4, TPOAb, and TgAb was performed using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Fasting blood glucose levels were detected using the glucose oxidase method, while lipid profile was assessed using enzymatic colorimetric assay.

The definition of dyslipidemia was based on the 3rd report of the National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP) Expert Panel on Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Cholesterol in Adults (NCEP‐ATP III) (Cleeman 2001) and the 2016 Chinese guidelines for the prevention and treatment of dyslipidemia in adults (Zhu et al. 2017). Dyslipidemia was defined as the presence of any one of the following abnormalities: TG ≥ 2.3 mmol/L, or LDL‐C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L, or TC ≥ 6.2 mmol/L. Patients with fasting blood glucose levels > 6.1 mmol/L were considered to have impaired glucose metabolism, as 3.9–6.1 mmol/L is the normal range in China.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 25.0. Group comparisons were performed using ANOVA for normally distributed variables, the Mann–Whitney U test for non‐parametric data, and the χ 2 test for categorical variables. To account for multiple comparisons, we applied the Bonferroni correction, which was chosen as the primary method due to its stringent control over family‐wise error rates in this exploratory study. Covariate adjustment was performed using ANCOVA to control for sex, marital status, and education level. Correlations between continuous variables (e.g., FBG, psychometric scale scores, continuous lipid levels) were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients as appropriate. To identify factors independently associated with the presence of dysglycemia (a binary outcome: normal vs. abnormal FBG), binary logistic regression was employed, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (two‐tailed).

To examine the associative pathways among fasting blood glucose (FBG), lipid levels, and depressive symptoms, we conducted a cross‐sectional path analysis using the PROCESS macro (Model 4) in SPSS. Model 4 was selected as it allows for the simultaneous estimation of direct and indirect effects within a single analytical framework, which is appropriate for testing our hypothesis that depressive symptoms may explain part of the statistical association between FBG and lipid levels. We specifically tested whether depressive symptoms (operationalised by HAMD scores) served as an indirect pathway linking FBG to lipid levels. To ensure robustness, we employed bootstrapping with 5000 resamples to derive bias‐corrected 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effects, a method that remains valid even with non‐normally distributed data (Hayes 2018). Importantly, while this analytical approach yields estimates analogous to mediation effects (i.e., indirect effects), the cross‐sectional nature of our data does not permit causal inference or the establishment of temporal sequence. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as identifying statistically supported associative patterns that merit further investigation in longitudinal or experimental designs. A significant indirect effect was indicated if the confidence interval did not include zero. The mediation models were adjusted for potential confounders, including sex, marital status, and education level. The specific pathways tested included: (1) the direct effect of FBG on lipid levels, (2) the indirect effect of FBG on lipid levels through depressive symptoms, and (3) the total effect of FBG on lipid levels.

Discussion

This study provides novel evidence on age‐stratified metabolic dysregulation in 1718 drug‐naïve MDD patients. (1) MAO patients (≥ 45 years) exhibited greater symptom severity (HAMD/HAMA/CGI‐S) and metabolic disturbances (TSH, FBG, TG, LDL‐C) compared to EAO (< 45 years); (2) TSH and HDL‐C emerged as transdiagnostic biomarkers correlating with fasting glucose abnormalities in EAO group; TG, LDL‐C and FT4 emerged as transdiagnostic biomarkers correlating with fasting glucose abnormalities in MAO group; (3) Distinct indirect pathways: in EAO group: A complete indirect effect linking FBG to lipid associations via depressive symptoms (indirect effect accounting for 35.46% of the variance); in MAO group: Partial indirect effect (16.22% of the variance), suggesting direct metabolic interactions. These findings underscore the need for age‐tailored management of MDD‐metabolic comorbidity.

A higher ratio of symptom severity and metabolic abnormalities was observed in patients with MAO compared to those with EAO, which contradicts previous reports associating early‐onset MDD with a poorer prognosis (Park et al. 2014; Xiao et al. 2020). This difference may be attributed to multifactorial mechanisms: age‐related insulin resistance amplifying neuroinflammation (Shaye et al. 2012) and lower education levels in patients with MAO (associated with delayed help‐seeking (Chrzastek et al. 2021)); and Aging is associated with progressive insulin resistance, which not only impairs glucose homeostasis but also exacerbates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress‐key pathways implicated in MDD pathogenesis (Shaye et al. 2012; McLaughlin et al. 2005). For instance, insulin resistance in the central nervous system may reduce synaptic plasticity and serotonin availability, thereby amplifying depressive symptoms (Kleinridders et al. 2014). Additionally, prolonged exposure to subclinical metabolic dysfunction (e.g., prediabetes, dyslipidemia) in MAO patients could create a ‘metabolic memory’ effect, where chronic hyperglycemia and lipid toxicity damage vascular and neuronal tissues, further worsening both psychiatric and somatic outcomes (Zheng et al. 2015; Ceriello et al. 2009). This hypothesis aligns with longitudinal studies showing that metabolic syndrome components predict depressive symptom progression in older adults (Penninx and Lange 2013).

MAO patients had significantly lower education levels compared to EAO patients (p < 0.001), which may influence both help‐seeking behaviours and symptom severity. Lower education is linked to delayed diagnosis and treatment adherence due to reduced health literacy (Hayes 2018), potentially allowing metabolic and psychiatric symptoms to escalate unchecked. Furthermore, socioeconomic constraints associated with limited education (e.g., poor dietary choices, limited access to healthcare) may compound metabolic risks (Stringhini et al. 2017). Although we adjusted for education level in our analyses, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., income, occupational stress) cannot be ruled out. Future studies should incorporate comprehensive socioeconomic assessments to better disentangle these effects.

This study demonstrated that FBG abnormalities in MAO patients were significantly associated with elevated triglycerides (TG: OR = 3.062) and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C: OR = 7.122), as well as reduced free thyroxine (FT4: OR = 0.694). These findings suggest that glucose metabolism dysregulation in middle‐aged and older MDD patients may be exacerbated through lipotoxic mechanisms. Elevated TG and LDL‐C levels can impair pancreatic β‐cell function and worsen insulin resistance by promoting free fatty acid release and oxidative stress (Zheng et al. 2015). Furthermore, decreased FT4 levels may attenuate the inhibitory effect of thyroid hormones on hepatic gluconeogenesis, contributing to elevated FBG (Duntas and Brenta 2018). For instance, subclinical hypothyroidism (characterized by elevated TSH and reduced FT4) has been linked to increased insulin resistance and atherosclerosis risk (Radaideh et al. 2004).

Our finding of distinct thyroid‐metabolic correlates across age groups extends recent work by Luo et al. (2023b), who reported that thyroid hormones and metabolic parameters mediate the relationship between depression and obesity. While their study focused on comorbid anxiety, our age‐stratified analysis suggests that the prominence of these mediating pathways may differ depending on the age of MDD onset, warranting further investigation. In EAO patients, FBG abnormalities were strongly correlated with elevated thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH: OR = 1.287) and reduced high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C: OR = 0.173). Elevated TSH may directly promote hyperglycemia by activating thyroid‐stimulating hormone receptor (TSHR) signalling pathways, which enhance hepatic gluconeogenesis and suppress glucose uptake in adipose tissue (Zhou et al. 2018). Concurrently, decreased HDL‐C levels may reflect systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both hallmark features of depression and key drivers of insulin resistance (Feingold and Grunfeld 2023). For example, proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL‐6, TNF‐α) inhibit lipoprotein lipase activity, reducing HDL‐C synthesis and accelerating its clearance (Rosenson et al. 2016). This mechanism is particularly prominent in younger MDD patients due to their heightened neuroendocrine sensitivity to psychological stress, potentially leading to early dysregulation of metabolic and immune systems.

This study revealed that, before adjusting for confounders, the MAO group (≥ 45 years) exhibited significantly higher depression severity (HAMD, HAMA, CGI‐S) and metabolic abnormalities (TSH, FBG, LDL‐C) compared to the EAO group (< 45 years). However, after adjusting for sex, education level, and marital status, differences in symptom severity disappeared, while metabolic abnormalities (e.g., FBG, LDL‐C) remained partially significant. This suggests that metabolic dysregulation in the MAO group may be more independently linked to age‐related pathophysiological changes, such as insulin resistance and vascular endothelial dysfunction, rather than being solely driven by depressive symptoms. For instance, aging‐associated mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic low‐grade inflammation exacerbate insulin resistance, further promoting glucose and lipid dysregulation (López‐Otín et al. 2013). Additionally, the MAO group's lower educational attainment (proportion with university degrees: 9.1% vs. 32.4%) may indirectly worsen metabolic abnormalities by limiting health literacy and access to medical resources (Stringhini et al. 2017). In contrast, metabolic abnormalities in the EAO group (e.g., elevated FBG) were directly associated with depressive symptoms (HAMD) and fully mediated by them (indirect effect: 35.46%). This supports the ‘depression‐driven metabolic dysregulation’ hypothesis, wherein neuroendocrine disturbances in younger patients (e.g., hyperactivity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis) may directly induce insulin resistance and dyslipidemia via glucocorticoid‐mediated hepatic gluconeogenesis and lipolysis (Réus et al. 2017). Such mechanisms are validated in animal models: chronic stress induces hippocampal glucose metabolism disturbances accompanied by elevated serum TG and LDL‐C levels (Detka et al. 2014).

Our study identified elevated SBP in dysglycemic subgroups, particularly among MAO patients (127.26 ± 7.39 vs. 130.28 ± 8.74 mmHg, p < 0.05). This finding signals an escalated cardiovascular risk in MDD patients with comorbid metabolic dysfunction. Given that hypertension and depression synergistically increase the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (Lichtman et al. 2014), clinicians should adopt a proactive monitoring strategy: (1) Routine BP Monitoring: MDD patients with FBG > 6.1 mmol/L or dyslipidemia should undergo quarterly blood pressure checks; (2) Integrated Care Pathways: Collaborate with cardiologists to implement early interventions (e.g., antihypertensive therapy, dietary counselling) in patients with SBP ≥ 140 mmHg; (3) Patient Education: Empower patients with self‐monitoring tools (e.g., home BP monitors) and educate them on lifestyle modifications (e.g., sodium reduction, aerobic exercise). These measures align with recent guidelines advocating for multidisciplinary management of depression with somatic comorbidities (De la Roca‐Chiapas et al. 2013).

Our results showed that there was an interaction between FBG and lipids in both the EAO and MAO groups. This interaction may be similar to the pathophysiology of diabetes: (1) Stress‐induced hypercortisolism impairs insulin signalling, promoting dyslipidemia (Lustman et al. 2000); (2) TG‐driven lipotoxicity disrupts β‐cell function, creating a vicious cycle (McLaughlin et al. 2005; Zheng et al. 2015; Yang 2004); (3) HAMD‐mediated effects in EAO suggest psychiatric symptoms may initiate metabolic dysregulation, whereas MAO patients exhibit entrenched metabolic syndrome (Radaideh et al. 2004; Szapary et al. 2003).

In Addition this, our findings underscore the necessity of integrating metabolic and psychiatric care in MDD management, particularly through age‐stratified approaches. Younger patients (< 45 years) may benefit from screening for lipid profile (including TG and LDL‐C) and thyroid function (FT4). Management should prioritise intensive treatment of depressive symptoms (e.g., cognitive behavioural therapy, SSRIs), as alleviating depression may indirectly ameliorate associated metabolic dysregulation. Middle‐aged and older patients (≥ 45 years) require early and regular screening for TSH and HDL‐C, alongside monitoring of glucose and lipid metabolism. A multidisciplinary care approach integrating psychiatry and endocrinology is recommended to address both psychiatric and metabolic components concurrently. Future studies should longitudinally investigate the thyroid‐metabolic‐depression interplay and explore targeted therapies (e.g., TSHR antagonists or anti‐inflammatory agents).

Limitations and Future Directions

While this study provides novel insights into the age‐stratified metabolic dysregulation in first‐episode MDD patients, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design limits our ability to draw causal inferences regarding the relationship between dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, and MDD symptoms. While we observed significant associations between these variables, the temporal sequence of events cannot be determined. Meantime, due to the cross‐sectional design, the observed indirect effects should be interpreted as statistical associations indicative of potential pathways, rather than evidence of temporal mediation or causality. Future longitudinal studies are needed to explore the causal pathways and temporal dynamics of metabolic dysregulation in MDD patients. For example, prospective studies could track the progression of metabolic abnormalities in relation to depressive symptoms over time, providing insights into whether metabolic dysfunction precedes or follows the onset of MDD.

Additionally, our study population was limited to Han Chinese outpatients, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups or clinical settings. Cultural, genetic, and lifestyle differences across populations could influence the relationship between MDD and metabolic dysregulation. Future research should aim to validate these findings in multi‐ethnic cohorts, including patients from different geographic regions and healthcare systems. Such studies would help determine whether the observed associations are consistent across diverse populations and whether age‐specific management strategies for MDD and metabolic comorbidities should be tailored based on ethnic or cultural factors.

Third, our assessment of glucose metabolism relied solely on fasting blood glucose (FBG). Although practical for large‐scale clinical studies, FBG is an indirect and relatively insensitive measure of insulin sensitivity and does not capture dynamic aspects of glycemic regulation, such as postprandial hyperglycemia or beta‐cell function. The use of more precise measures—such as fasting insulin, the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA‐IR), glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), or oral glucose tolerance tests—in future studies would provide a more robust characterization of dysglycemia and clarify its pathophysiological links with lipid metabolism in MDD.

The clinical management of MDD with metabolic dysfunction is further complicated by factors such as gender and severe anxiety, which can interact with thyroid function (Luo, Jing, et al. 2024). Additionally, metabolic and psychosocial risk factors for severe outcomes like suicide attempts require careful attention, especially in specific patient subgroups (Luo, Wang, et al. 2024). Therefore, future age‐tailored interventions must adopt a multidimensional perspective that integrates metabolic monitoring with assessments of anxiety, thyroid function, and suicide risk, particularly in light of the gender differences reported in recent studies (Luo, Jing, et al. 2024; Luo, Wang, et al. 2024).
