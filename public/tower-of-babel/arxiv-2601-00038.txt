Active learning for data-driven reduced models of parametric differential systems with Bayesian operator inference
Shane A. McQuarrie, Mengwu Guo, Anirban Chaudhuri

Abstract
This work develops an active learning framework to intelligently enrich data-driven reduced-order models (ROMs) of parametric dynamical systems, which can serve as the foundation of virtual assets in a digital twin. Data-driven ROMs are explainable, computationally efficient scientific machine learning models that aim to preserve the underlying physics of complex dynamical simulations. Since the quality of data-driven ROMs is sensitive to the quality of the limited training data, we seek to identify training parameters for which using the associated training data results in the best possible parametric ROM. Our approach uses the operator inference methodology, a regression-based strategy which can be tailored to particular parametric structure for a large class of problems. We establish a probabilistic version of parametric operator inference, casting the learning problem as a Bayesian linear regression. Prediction uncertainties stemming from the resulting probabilistic ROM solutions are used to design a sequential adaptive sampling scheme to select new training parameter vectors that promote ROM stability and accuracy globally in the parameter domain. We conduct numerical experiments for several nonlinear parametric systems of partial differential equations and compare the results to ROMs trained on random parameter samples. The results demonstrate that the proposed adaptive sampling strategy consistently yields more stable and accurate ROMs than random sampling does under the same computational budget.



Keywords:
Parametric model reduction, data-driven reduced models, Bayesian inference, active learning, operator inference, adaptive sampling, optimal experimental design, adaptive surrogates

1  Introduction

Numerical simulation of complex physical phenomena is a core enabling technology for digital twins, which are comprised of physical and virtual assets with a two-way flow of information: data from the physical asset is used to construct and/or calibrate the virtual asset (a numerical model), while numerical predictions from the virtual asset are used for control or decision-making for the physical asset
[
42
]
. To be viable for practical application, the virtual asset must be able to produce predictions rapidly and reliably; however, the underlying physics that are of interest for digital twin applications can typically only be accurately simulated using a large number of degrees of freedom, leading to computationally expensive numerical simulations. The explainability and computational efficiency of decisions made by the digital twin play a key role in safety-critical applications, making explainable artificial intelligence an essential ingredient
[
24
]
. Model reduction techniques are one such explainable scientific machine learning technique that construct low-dimensional systems, called reduced-order models (ROMs), to serve as computationally inexpensive surrogates for a high-dimensional physics simulation
[
4
,
20
]
. This paper introduces a technique for adaptively constructing ROMs to emulate systems with parametric dependence, that is, systems whose behavior varies with some set of parameters, usually representing physical properties. We focus on systems where the parametric dependence manifests in the operators defining the model, not merely in initial conditions or external inputs. Building parametric ROMs requires training data at different parameter combinations, which can incur significant training computational cost. Moreover, state-of-the-art parametric ROMs—especially those learned directly from data—are not typically equipped with automated methods for determining how many and which parameters to select for training data generation within a limited training budget. Our goal is to develop a framework for intelligently selecting training parameters in order to learn a high-quality ROM with as few training data as possible, in particular in the context of data-driven model reduction.

Classical model reduction techniques, including those that are designed for parametric systems, intrusively construct a ROM by compressing a large numerical model, called the full-order model (FOM), through direct projection
[
4
]
. On the other hand, non-intrusive data-driven model reduction techniques, such as dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)
[
6
,
50
]
and operator inference (OpInf)
[
20
,
34
,
43
]
, construct a ROM by learning from available simulation data while imposing appropriate model structure without needing access to the implementation details of the FOM. The non-intrusive nature of these procedures eases the implementation burden and thus widens the applicability of model reduction. Since ROMs of parametric systems have application in large-scale optimization
[
20
,
56
]
, inverse problems
[
20
,
36
]
, and uncertainty quantification
[
10
]
, data-driven parametric model reduction is a particularly attractive prospect in the context of the digital twin framework. Examples of such techniques include the Loewner framework
[
29
]
, interpolation-based methods
[
17
,
59
]
, parametric DMD
[
1
,
14
,
28
,
49
]
, parametric OpInf
[
15
,
31
,
41
,
54
]
, Gaussian process surrogate modeling
[
10
,
25
]
, and deep learning
[
16
]
. However, parametric data-driven models are only as good as the data they are trained on, and we can only obtain limited training data from expensive-to-evaluate complex systems due to training cost constraints. Therefore, what training data to obtain for optimal learning of the parametric ROMs within a given training budget is important to understand, since not all observations are equally useful.

Active learning (or sequential optimal experimental design) is a way to select new training data given some existing knowledge about the current approximation, such as prediction uncertainty. In this work, the goal is to improve the global accuracy of the ROM by adaptively sampling the parameter space and, hence, selecting new training data. Some early work for active learning methods for global accuracy include maximizing mean squared error metrics
[
47
]
, entropy
[
12
,
47
,
37
]
, and maximizing the expected reduction in predictive variance
[
11
]
as acquisition functions for selecting the next training sample. The standard active learning literature uses Gaussian process regression (or Kriging)
[
46
]
as the surrogate model. There is limited work on active learning for adaptively updating parametric ROMs since they lack a measure of uncertainty in the predictions. Traditionally, greedy algorithms based on
a posteriori
error estimators were used to generate snapshots for adaptive reduced basis construction
[
7
,
22
,
21
,
45
]
; however, these methods are limited to the context of intrusive ROMs with certain structure that give rise to the error estimators. Finally, there are recent works on adaptive sampling for parametric ROMs
[
5
,
58
]
and for surrogate models fitted in the reduced input-output dimensions
[
27
]
that utilize additional surrogate models for error estimates to drive the parameter selection. In this work, we introduce an active learning method based on the prediction uncertainty from a probabilistic surrogate model, without the need for additional surrogates to provide ROM error estimates. Specifically, we propose a Bayesian parametric ROM equipped prediction uncertainty, which we use to design an active learning framework for adaptively selecting new training parameters.

The major contributions of this work are (i) a Bayesian parametric OpInf ROM method for affine-parametric systems that provides uncertainty in spatiotemporal predictions with respect to underlying model parameters and (ii) acquisition functions targeting stability and global accuracy for active learning in ROMs. We develop a probabilistic parametric ROM, building on our previous work in affine-parametric OpInf
[
41
]
and Bayesian OpInf
[
26
]
. We derive closed form expressions for the posterior distribution of the affine reduced operators for the proposed Bayesian parametric OpInf. The Bayesian parametric OpInf is a probabilistic ROM that provides a measure of uncertainty in the predictions. The statistics obtained from the posterior ROM are used to formulate the proposed acquisition functions of the probability of ROM instability and the total prediction variance. We present numerical experiments on a heat equation with nonlinear reaction term and a two-dimensional viscous Burgers’ problem for shock propagation. The results show that the proposed active learning methodology significantly results in satisfactory stability and accuracy at a fraction of the training computational cost when compared to random sampling of the parameter space.

2  Bayesian parametric operator inference

This section briefly reviews operator inference for affine-parametric systems as developed in
[
41
,
55
]
in §
2.1
. Then we present the proposed Bayesian parametric operator inference method that provides prediction uncertainties in §
2.2
. The technique gives rise to a regression problem, which is endowed with a probabilistic interpretation to form a Bayesian linear regression.

2.1  Non-intrusive reduced models for affine-parametric system

Let
{\bf\it\xi}\in\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote a vector of
p
variables parameterizing a time-dependent partial differential equation (PDE).
After introducing a spatial discretization, a finite-dimensional numerical model for the PDE may be written generically as

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\mathbf{q}(t;{\bf\it\xi})={\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{q}}(t),{\mathbf{u}}(t),{\bf\it\xi})\,,\quad\mathbf{q}(t_{0};{\bf\it\xi})=\mathbf{q}_{0}({\bf\it\xi})\,,\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]\,,

(2.1)

where
\mathbf{q}(t;{\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the time-dependent spatial discretization of the PDE state and
\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
represents time-dependent system inputs, for example arising from nonstationary boundary conditions or source terms. This is the full-order model (FOM). The state depends implicitly on the parameter vector
{\bf\it\xi}
through the function
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
which governs the dynamics, and through the initial condition
\mathbf{q}_{0}({\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Many systems of interest can be written with the following parametric polynomial structure (we suppress the dependence of
\mathbf{q}(t;{\bf\it\xi})
and
\mathbf{u}(t)
on
t
and
{\bf\it\xi}
going forward):

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}\mathbf{q}}{\textrm{d}t}=\mathbf{c}({\bf\it\xi})+\mathbf{A}({\bf\it\xi})\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{H}({\bf\it\xi})[\mathbf{q}\otimes\mathbf{q}]+\mathbf{B}({\bf\it\xi})\mathbf{u},

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}({\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad\mathbf{A}({\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\quad\mathbf{H}({\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N^{2}},\quad\mathbf{B}({\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m},

(2.2b)

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product
[
53
]
. This work considers the class of problems where each of the “operators” in
eq.

2.2b
depend affinely on the parameter vector
{\bf\it\xi}
, meaning there exist

\mathbf{c}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{c}^{(n_{c})}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\mathbf{A}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{A}^{(n_{a})}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,

\mathbf{H}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{H}^{(n_{h})}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N^{2}}
, and

\mathbf{B}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{B}^{(n_{b})}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}

such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{c}({\bf\it\xi})&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{c}}\theta_{c}^{(\ell)}\!({\bf\it\xi})\,\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}\!,&&&\mathbf{A}({\bf\it\xi})&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{a}}\theta_{a}^{(\ell)}\!({\bf\it\xi})\,\mathbf{A}^{\!(\ell)}\!,\\
\mathbf{H}({\bf\it\xi})&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{h}}\theta_{h}^{(\ell)}\!({\bf\it\xi})\,\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}\!,&&&\mathbf{B}({\bf\it\xi})&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{b}}\theta_{b}^{(\ell)}\!({\bf\it\xi})\,\mathbf{B}^{(\ell)}\!,\end{aligned}

(2.2c)

where each
\theta_{x}^{(\ell)}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
is a scalar-valued function of the parameter vector. In many cases, including the numerical examples presented later, the number of terms
n_{c}
,
n_{a}
,
n_{h}
, and
n_{b}
in each expansion is small.

State-polynomial, affine-parametric structure such as in
eq.

2.2
is advantageous for projection-based model reduction because linear dimensionality reduction preserves this structure. Given a matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
with orthonormal columns, making the low-dimensional approximation
\mathbf{q}\approx\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}
for some
\hat{\mathbf{q}}=\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t;{\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
leads to the low-dimensional evolution equations
\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\hat{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}},\mathbf{u},{\bf\it\xi})
. For
eq.

2.2
, this can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{q}}}{\textrm{d}t}=\hat{\mathbf{c}}({\bf\it\xi})+\hat{\mathbf{A}}({\bf\it\xi})\hat{\mathbf{q}}+\hat{\mathbf{H}}({\bf\it\xi})[\hat{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}]+\hat{\mathbf{B}}({\bf\it\xi})\mathbf{u},

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{c}}({\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{r},\quad\hat{\mathbf{A}}({\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\quad\hat{\mathbf{H}}({\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r^{2}},\quad\hat{\mathbf{B}}({\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}.

(2.3b)

Critically, the affine-parametric structure
eq.

2.2c
is preserved in the reduced system matrices: there exist

\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(n_{c})}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,

\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(n_{a})}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,

\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{(n_{h})}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r^{2}}
, and

\hat{\mathbf{B}}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{B}}^{(n_{b})}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}

with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{c}}({\bf\it\xi})&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{c}}\theta_{c}^{(\ell)}\!({\bf\it\xi})\,\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(\ell)}\!,&&&\hat{\mathbf{A}}({\bf\it\xi})&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{a}}\theta_{a}^{(\ell)}\!({\bf\it\xi})\,\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\!(\ell)}\!,\\
\hat{\mathbf{H}}({\bf\it\xi})&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{h}}\theta_{h}^{(\ell)}\!({\bf\it\xi})\,\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{(\ell)}\!,&&&\hat{\mathbf{B}}({\bf\it\xi})&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{b}}\theta_{b}^{(\ell)}\!({\bf\it\xi})\,\hat{\mathbf{B}}^{(\ell)}\!,\end{aligned}

(2.3c)

where each expansion has the same number of terms
n_{x}
and the same coefficient functions
\theta_{x}^{(\ell)}
as in
eq.

2.2c
. This means that if the polynomial form and the affine-parametric structure of a high-dimensional model are known, we can stipulate the same structure in a low-dimensional space to design an effective ROM. The main advantage is that if
r\ll N
, solving
eq.

2.3
is much less computationally expensive than solving
eq.

2.2
.

Remark 2.1

(Dimensionality reduction)
.

The linearity of the approximation
\mathbf{q}\approx\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}
is the reason that the structure of
eq.

2.2
is preserved in
eq.

2.3
. If a shifted approximation is made, i.e.,
\mathbf{q}\approx\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}+\bar{\mathbf{q}}
for a fixed nonzero reference vector
\bar{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, additional terms arise in the reduced dynamics, but the new structure is straightforward to derive. The situation is further complicated by partially nonlinear approximations, such as quadratic manifolds
[
3
,
18
,
30
,
51
]
, but the model form can usually still be written out explicitly. However, when using a fully nonlinear approximation, for example with autoencoders
[
8
,
9
,
35
]
, it is no longer straightforward to write down the expected parametric structure of the reduced dynamics.

Operator inference (OpInf)
[
20
,
34
,
43
]
is a non-intrusive strategy for learning ROMs with polynomial structure. In the original work
[
43
]
, parametric models of the form
eq.

2.2a
–
eq.

2.2b
are constructed by learning nonparametric models for each of several training parameter instances, then the learned models are interpolated in parameter space to extrapolate to new parameter instances. Later,
[
41
,
55
]
extended the methodology to the case of affine-parametric structure as in
eq.

2.2c
. Affine-parametric OpInf (pOpInf) learns ROMs that can be written generally as

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{q}}}{\textrm{d}t}=\hat{\mathbf{O}}\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}},\mathbf{u};{\bf\it\xi})\,,\quad\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t_{0})=\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\,,\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]\,,

(2.4)

where the operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d(r,m)}
contains the (parameter-independent) vectors and matrices defining the ROM and
\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}},\mathbf{u};{\bf\it\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,m)}
encodes the polynomial and parametric structure of the system. In the case of
eq.

2.3
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{O}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|ccc|ccc|ccc}\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(1)}&\cdots&\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(n_{c})}&\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(1)}&\cdots&\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(n_{a})}&\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{(1)}&\cdots&\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{(n_{h})}&\hat{\mathbf{B}}^{(1)}&\cdots&\hat{\mathbf{B}}^{(n_{b})}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}},\mathbf{u};{\bf\it\xi})

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}\theta_{c}^{(1)}\!({\bf\it\xi})&\cdots&\theta_{c}^{(n_{c})}\!({\bf\it\xi})&\theta_{a}^{(1)}\!({\bf\it\xi})\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\theta_{h}^{(1)}\!({\bf\it\xi})[\hat{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}]^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\theta_{b}^{(n_{b})}\!({\bf\it\xi})\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{array}\right]^{\mathsf{T}},

with
d(r,m)=n_{c}+rn_{a}+r^{2}n_{h}+mn_{b}
. The vector-valued function
\mathbf{d}
is known
a priori
because the structure of the ROM
eq.

2.4
is stipulated to match the structure of a classical projection-based ROM, which, in the case being examined, inherits the structure of the original model. The operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
, on the other hand, is not known
a priori
in cases where the high-dimensional operators (e.g.,
\mathbf{A}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{A}^{(n_{a})})
are unknown or inaccessible. The goal is to learn an appropriate
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
from data, as we now explain.

Consider a limited number of training parameter instances
{\bf\it\xi}_{1},\ldots,{\bf\it\xi}_{n_{p}}
for which solution data are available from the FOM
eq.

2.2
. Let
\{(\mathbf{q}_{ij},\mathbf{u}_{ij})\}_{j=1}^{n_{t}}
denote state-input pairs that represent the solution of
eq.

2.2
at
n_{t}
time instances
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n_{t}}
with parameter vector
{\bf\it\xi}={\bf\it\xi}_{i}
. The training states are compressed to produce reduced snapshots
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{ij}=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{q}_{ij}
and then used to estimate time derivatives
\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{ij}\approx\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\mathbf{q}(t;{\bf\it\xi}_{i})|_{t=t_{j}}
, for example with finite differences. The operator matrix is selected by solving the regression problem

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{O}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{O}}\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{ij},\mathbf{u}_{ij};{\bf\it\xi}_{i})-\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{ij}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(2.6a)

which minimizes the residual of
eq.

2.4
with respect to available observations. Letting
\mathbf{d}_{i,j}=\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{ij},\mathbf{u}_{ij};{\bf\it\xi}_{i})
and defining the data matrices

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}

\displaystyle=[~\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{1,1}~~\cdots~~\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{1,n_{t}}~~\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{2,1}~~\cdots~~\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{n_{p},n_{t}}~]\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n_{p}n_{t}},

(2.6b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=[~\mathbf{d}_{1,1}~~\cdots~~\mathbf{d}_{1,n_{t}}~~\mathbf{d}_{2,1}~~\cdots~~\mathbf{d}_{n_{p},n_{t}}~]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}n_{t}\times d(r,m)},

(2.6c)

we can write
eq.

2.6a
in the standard linear least-squares form

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{O}}}\left\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{O}}^{\mathsf{T}}-\dot{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\,.

(2.6d)

Furthermore, this regression decouples into
r
independent least-squares problems. Letting
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,m)}
and
\mathbf{z}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}n_{t}}
denote the
k
-th rows of
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
and
\dot{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}
, respectively,
eq.

2.6d
can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}}\left\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}-\mathbf{z}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,\quad k=1,\ldots,r,\qquad\qquad\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{O}}&=[~\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{1}~~\cdots~~\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{r}~]^{\mathsf{T}},\\
\dot{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}&=[~\mathbf{z}_{1}~~\cdots~~\mathbf{z}_{r}~]^{\mathsf{T}},\end{aligned}

(2.6e)

where each subproblem can be solved independent of the others.

Data-driven ROMs
eq.

2.4
generated through the pOpInf regression
eq.

2.6
are not guaranteed to be stable. One standard method for promoting model stability is to add a regularization term to the regression, replacing
eq.

2.6e
with, for instance, a Tikhonov-regularized (ridge) regression,

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}}\left\{\left\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}-\mathbf{z}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\bf\it\Gamma}_{\!k}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\,,\qquad k=1,\ldots,r,

(2.7)

where
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\!k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,m)\times d(r,m)}
.
The regularization matrix
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\!k}
is often parameterized with a small number of hyperparameters and designed so that like terms are penalized equally
[
40
,
44
,
48
]
. Alternative strategies for promoting stability typically involve constraining the optimization problem
eq.

2.6
, for example to preserve certain structure
[
2
,
13
,
19
,
23
,
52
,
54
,
57
]
, or by post-processing the operators
[
48
]
or the states at runtime
[
32
]
; see also
[
33
]
for an analysis of stability of nonlinear polynomial models, including data-driven ROMs.

2.2  Bayesian framework for affine-parametric operator inference

We now present the Bayesian parametric OpInf method that can account for underlying model errors to provide prediction uncertainties. The reduced snapshot time derivatives
\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{ij}
are typically estimated from the reduced snapshots
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{ij}
, incurring errors to which the regression problems
eq.

2.6
and
eq.

2.7
are sensitive. Modeling the estimation error as uncorrelated Gaussian noise, we obtain the Bayesian linear regression

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{k}=\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}+{\bf\it\varepsilon}_{k},\qquad p({\bf\it\varepsilon}_{k})=\mathcal{N}({\bf\it\varepsilon}_{k}\mid\mathbf{0},\sigma_{k}^{2}\mathbf{I}),\qquad k=1,\ldots,r,

(2.8)

in which
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}n_{t}}
denotes the zero vector and
\mathbf{I}
is the
n_{p}n_{t}\times n_{p}n_{t}
identity matrix. Each
\sigma_{k}^{2}>0
describes the noise variance in the regression for
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}
, the
k
-th row of the operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
. The vector
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}n_{t}}
corresponds to noise over the
n_{t}
time instances for each of the
n_{p}
training parameter samples. The noise is not correlated in time or across parameter values. The formulation builds on our previous work in
[
26
]
.

We immediately have a Gaussian likelihood function for
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}
given data in
\mathbf{D}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \pi_{\text{like}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k})=p(\mathbf{z}_{k}\mid\mathbf{D},\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})&=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_{k}\mid\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}\mathbf{I})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}-\mathbf{z}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right).\end{aligned}

(2.9)

To facilitate a Bayesian inference, we impose a Gaussian prior on the operator vector, namely

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \pi_{\text{prior}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k})=p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}\mid{\bf\it\gamma}_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})&=\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}\mid\mathbf{0}_{d(r,m)},\sigma_{k}^{2}\operatorname{diag}({\bf\it\gamma}_{k})^{-1})\\
&\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\operatorname{diag}({\bf\it\gamma}_{\!k})\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\big\|\operatorname{diag}({\bf\it\gamma}_{\!k})^{1/2}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}\big\|_{2}^{2}\right)\end{aligned}

(2.10)

where
{\bf\it\gamma}_{1},\ldots,{\bf\it\gamma}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,m)}
and
\sigma_{k}^{2}\operatorname{diag}({\bf\it\gamma}_{\!k})^{-1}
is the prior covariance for
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,r
. The scalar likelihood variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
is included in the prior for convenience in matching to terms in
eq.

2.9
. Equipping the prior with a diagonal covariance implies that the entries of the operator vector
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}
are uncorrelated, and the zero prior mean disincentives the entries of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}
from becoming too large.

The goal now is to establish a probability distribution for
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}
given training data for
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{z}_{k}
.
Applying Bayes’ rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \pi_{\text{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k})\propto\pi_{\text{like}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k})\pi_{\text{prior}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k})&\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\big\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}-\mathbf{z}_{k}\big\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\big\|\operatorname{diag}({\bf\it\gamma}_{\!k})^{1/2}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{k}\big\|_{2}^{2}\right)\\

The performance quality of data-driven models, including operator inference ROMs, depends strongly on the quality of the training data. In the model reduction setting, acquiring new training data amounts to either solving an expensive FOM or performing a physical experiment and collecting observations. The goal of this section is to present a novel active learning method that can use Bayesian pOpInf to iteratively determine new points in the parameter domain such that new training data corresponding to these points are optimal in some sense, allowing us to learn accurate pOpInf ROMs with as little training data as is practical and provide prediction uncertainties.

3.1  Acquisition functions for new training parameters

Given a probabilistic pOpInf ROM constructed through
Algorithm

1
, our goal is to choose a new parameter vector, not yet used for training, at which to solve the FOM and supplement the training set. There are two main considerations to use as selection criteria: (i) ROM stability and (ii) global accuracy of predictions. The regularization selection procedure promotes ROM stability at the training parameter vectors. However, we do not have guarantees for ROM performance in terms of accuracy, uncertainty, or stability at non-training parameter vectors, nor do we know beforehand how adding a particular parameter vector to the training set might improve ROM performance throughout the parameter domain. We choose parameter vectors based on two proposed active learning acquisition functions that target the stability and the variance of the current Bayesian parametric ROM.

The first acquisition function,
\psi:\mathcal{P}\to[0,1]
, is the probability of ROM instability, since an unstable surrogate model cannot be used for prediction or uncertainty estimation. Determining the stability of a probabilistic ROM analytically is challenging, but for a fixed parameter vector
\tilde{{\bf\it\xi}}
, stability can be estimated empirically by drawing
n_{d}>0
ROM samples, integrating each sampled deterministic ROM in time at
{\bf\it\xi}=\tilde{{\bf\it\xi}}
, and counting the number of times
n_{\alpha}
that the integration becomes numerically unstable. The probability of ROM instability,
\alpha=\psi(\tilde{{\bf\it\xi}})\in[0,1]
, can then be estimated as

\alpha=n_{\alpha}/n_{d},

(3.1)

which is a Monte Carlo estimator at
{\bf\it\xi}=\tilde{{\bf\it\xi}}
. This strategy is computationally tractable for moderate
n_{d}
because each ROM sample is, by design, cheap to assemble and solve.

Our second acquisition function,
\varphi:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, targets global accuracy of the ROM by scoring parameter candidates based on the prediction uncertainties obtained from stable realizations of the Bayesian parametric ROM. We start by defining an ideal acquisition function based on the accuracy of the ROM as

\displaystyle\varphi_{\textrm{ideal}}({\bf\it\xi})=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\mathbf{q}(t;{\bf\it\xi})-\mathbb{E}\big[\breve{\mathbf{q}}_{\textrm{rom}}(t;{\bf\it\xi})\big]\right\|_{2}^{2}\,dt,

(3.2)

where
\mathbf{q}
is the solution of the FOM and
\breve{\mathbf{q}}_{\textrm{rom}}=\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{\textrm{rom}}
is the ROM solution mapped to the original state space. Maximizing
\varphi_{\textrm{ideal}}
would find the point in the parameter domain where the ROM is least accurate in expectation. However,
\varphi_{\textrm{ideal}}({\bf\it\xi})
cannot be computed without first computing the full-order solution
\mathbf{q}(t;{\bf\it\xi})
. Instead, we propose an alternative based on the prediction variance of the Bayesian parametric ROM solution,

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}({\bf\it\xi})=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathbb{V}\big[\hat{q}_{k}(t;{\bf\it\xi})\big]\,dt,

(3.3)

where
\hat{q}_{k}
denotes the
k
-th entry of the vector-valued stochastic process
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{\textrm{rom}}
. This acquisition function is a measure of the total variance of the ROM solution at the given parameter vector.

Remark 3.1

(Equality of total variance in reduced and original state spaces)
.

Letting
V_{i,k}
denote the entries of the basis matrix
\mathbf{V}
, the
i
-th entry of the ROM solution in the original state space is given by
\breve{q}_{\textrm{i}}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}V_{i,k}\hat{q}_{k}
, and we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\mathbb{V}[\breve{q}_{i}(t,{\bf\it\xi})]&=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathbb{V}\big[V_{i,k}\,\hat{q}_{k}(t;{\bf\it\xi})\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\sum_{k=1}^{r}V_{i,k}^{2}\mathbb{V}\big[\hat{q}_{k}(t;{\bf\it\xi})\big]=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathbb{V}\big[\hat{q}_{k}(t;{\bf\it\xi})\big],\end{aligned}

(3.4)

since
\sum_{i=1}^{n_{x}}V_{i,k}^{2}=1
. Hence, defining

\displaystyle\breve{\varphi}({\bf\it\xi})=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\mathbb{V}[\breve{q}_{i}(t,{\bf\it\xi})]\,\textrm{d}t,

(3.5)

we have
\hat{\varphi}({\bf\it\xi})=\breve{\varphi}({\bf\it\xi})
. In other words, the total variance in the reduced space equals the total variance in the original state space.

Remark 3.2

(Shifted approximation)
.

If the low-dimensional state approximation is given by
\mathbf{q}\approx\bar{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}
as in
Remark

2.1

\hat{\varphi}({\bf\it\xi})=\breve{\varphi}({\bf\it\xi})
still holds because variance is translational. Specifically, we have
\breve{q}_{i}=\bar{q}_{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{r}V_{i,k}\,\hat{q}_{k}
, and
\mathbb{V}\big[\bar{q}_{i}+V_{i,k}\,\hat{q}_{k}(t;{\bf\it\xi})\big]=V_{i,k}^{2}\mathbb{V}\big[\hat{q}_{k}(t;{\bf\it\xi})\big]

3.4

4  Numerical results

In this section, we apply our active learning approach to learn probabilistic ROMs for two parametric partial differential equations (PDEs) with affine-parametric structure. Both examples are prototypical, yet they highlight the difficulty of constructing parametric ROMs that effectively capture the effects of the parameters on the solution, even with moderately benign parameter spaces, without an effective sampling strategy.

Our goal in this experiment is to construct a ROM that accurately mirrors the FOM over the parameter domain
(\kappa,\rho)\in\mathcal{P}=[10^{-3},10^{-1}]\times[1,5]
. To exactly preserve the boundary conditions, we consider affine approximations of the discretized state,
\mathbf{q}\approx\bar{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}
, where
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
is a reference vector such that
\mathbf{q}(t)-\bar{\mathbf{q}}
corresponds to homogeneous Dirichlet conditions. The corresponding ROM has the form

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\text{d}\hat{\mathbf{q}}}{\text{d}t}=\kappa\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(1)}+\rho\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(2)}+\left(\kappa\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\!(1)}+\rho\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\!(2)}\right)\hat{\mathbf{q}}+\rho\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{(1)}[\hat{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}],\end{aligned}

(4.4)

where

\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(1)},\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\!(1)},\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\!(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r^{2}}
. This is the generic ROM
eq.

2.3
with coefficient functions inherited from
eq.

4.3
, plus additional coefficient functions
\theta_{c}^{(2)}\!({\bf\it\xi})=\theta_{a}^{(2)}\!({\bf\it\xi})=\rho
arising due to the nonzero reference vector
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
. To write this as a pOpInf ROM
\frac{\textrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{q}}}{\textrm{d}t}=\hat{\mathbf{O}}\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}},{\bf\it\xi})
akin to
eq.

2.4
, we define

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{O}}

\displaystyle=[~\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(1)}~~\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{(2)}~~\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\!(1)}~~\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{\!(2)}~~\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{(1)}~]\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d(r)},

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}},{\bf\it\xi})

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\kappa\\
\rho\\
\kappa\hat{\mathbf{q}}\\
\rho\hat{\mathbf{q}}\\
\rho(\hat{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}})\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r)},

with data dimension
d(r)=2+2r+r^{2}
.

Remark 4.1

(Compressed Kronecker product)
.

The quadratic mapping
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{(1)}[\hat{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}]
with
\hat{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r^{2}}

r\times r(r+1)/2
matrix to avoid redundant computations, see, e.g.,
[
38

for details. Hence, in practice, the data dimension for
section

4.1
is reduced to
d(r)=2+2r+r(r+1)/2

To assess our adaptive sampling strategy, we discretize the two-dimensional parameter domain
\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle 1-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}^{2}\middle/\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)=\left.\sum_{i=r+1}^{K}\sigma_{i}^{2}\middle/\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{k}^{2}\right.,

is less than

Next, we discuss how the sampling strategies compare in terms of ROM stability. In each trial and at each iteration, we record the probability of ROM instability

\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{1}{400}\sum_{i=1}^{400}\beta_{i},\qquad\beta_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}~\alpha_{i}>0,\\
0&\text{if}~\alpha_{i}=0.\end{cases}

5  Conclusion

We have introduced an uncertainty-aware active learning method for constructing data-driven parametric reduced-order models, tackling the challenge that ROM accuracy is sensitive to training data while full-order simulations are costly and hence the amount of available training data is limited. Our approach adaptively selects training parameters based on two proposed acquisition functions ensuring model stability and targeting regions of maximum predictive uncertainty. Numerical experiments on two nonlinear partial differential equations for thermal analysis and shock propagation demonstrate that our approach outperforms principled random sampling, achieving comparable ROM accuracy and superior stability with only about half as many full-order solves. Our approach shows significantly more robust results when repeated over different initial training sets, that is, the adaptive sampling scheme consistently leads to stable and accurate ROMs with as few samples as possible. While our applications focused on parameters appearing affinely in the operators, the methodology readily extends to parameters in initial conditions or input terms. Overall, we provide a general active learning framework for data-driven model reduction that leverages prediction uncertainty to produce efficient, robust, and uncertainty-informed reduced order models.

Future work may include exploring alternate acquisition functions for goal-oriented design, moving beyond greedy sequential selection to lookahead and batch selection strategies, and extending the framework to other ROM types beyond affine-structured operator inference. In general, the proposed active learning acquisition functions are directly applicable to any ROMs that provide prediction uncertainty estimates. One bottleneck for POD-based ROMs is that they can only be as good as the dimensionality reduction driven by linear manifolds, a problem that is exacerbated when the system behavior changes significantly with the parameters. Improving dimensionality reduction through nonlinear manifolds remains an important direction to further enhance ROM accuracy and efficiency.
