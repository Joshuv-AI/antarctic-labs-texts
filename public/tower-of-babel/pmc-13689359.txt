Identification of the miR-210-3p–EFNA3–YAP axis and its targeted modulation via biomimetic supramolecular nanoparticles to promote ischemic flap survival
Authors: Jie Xu, Xiuxiu Zhuang, Zichang Wu, Fuyi Yan, Deen Shi, Fan Ye, Ruihua Ding, Qi Gao, Qimin Yao, Yiying Liu, Xiaoyu Dong, Fan Zhang, Binbiao Li, Xiangwei Xu, Hongfei Tong, Yanzhi Yao, Wenbing Jiang, Jian Xiao, Zhenglin Li, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ischemic skin flaps are highly susceptible to distal necrosis because of insufficient perfusion, oxidative stress, and dysregulated cell death. Here, we investigated the role of Hippo/YAP signaling in ischemic flap survival and identified its upstream regulatory mechanism. Hippo pathway inactivation, characterized by reduced MST1/2 and LATS1/2 phosphorylation and enhanced YAP nuclear localization, was preferentially associated with viable flap regions. Mechanistically, ischemia-induced HIF-1α upregulated miR-210-3p, which suppressed ephrin-A3 (EFNA3), weakened the EFNA3–YAP interaction, and promoted YAP nuclear translocation. Re-expression of EFNA3 partially reversed miR-210-3p-induced YAP activation and cytoprotection, confirming its functional involvement. Mechanistically, ischemia-induced HIF-1α increased miR-210-3p expression, which suppressed EFNA3 and facilitated YAP nuclear localization. EFNA3 re-expression partially reversed miR-210-3p-induced YAP activation, supporting its functional involvement. MiR-210-3p treatment was further associated with reduced oxidative injury, lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ferroptosis-related molecular alterations, together with improved perfusion and flap survival. In vivo, miR-210-3p improved local perfusion and flap survival. We further developed a virus-inspired biomimetic nanoparticle for miR-210-3p delivery, which achieved protective effects comparable to AAV-mediated delivery in vitro and in vivo. These findings identify the miR-210-3p–EFNA3–YAP axis as an important regulator of ischemic tissue adaptation and provide a non-viral miRNA delivery strategy for ischemic flap repair.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04833-0.

Introduction

Ischemic skin flaps are widely used in plastic and reconstructive surgery to repair complex tissue defects caused by trauma, tumor resection, or congenital anomalies. However, distal regions of these flaps are particularly vulnerable to insufficient perfusion, leading to ischemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation. These pathological processes culminate in cell death and tissue necrosis, substantially compromising flap survival and increasing postoperative complications. Despite extensive efforts to promote angiogenesis, modulate inflammation, and enhance antioxidant defenses, ischemic flap necrosis remains a major clinical challenge [1, 2]. This persistent therapeutic difficulty likely reflects the coordinated disruption of vascular perfusion, cellular stress adaptation, inflammatory regulation, and regulated cell death, rather than failure of a single pathway. The molecular mechanisms governing tissue survival under ischemic stress are not fully understood, highlighting an urgent need to identify novel regulatory targets and therapeutic strategies.

The Hippo signaling pathway is a highly conserved regulatory cascade that controls tissue homeostasis, cell fate, and adaptive responses to mechanical and metabolic cues [3]. Yes-associated protein (YAP), the central transcriptional co-activator of this pathway, translocates to the nucleus to activate gene programs involved in cell survival, metabolic adaptation, and tissue regeneration [4]. Accumulating evidence implicates Hippo/YAP signaling not only in organ development and tumorigenesis but also in ischemic injury, angiogenesis, and tissue repair [5, 6]. However, the role of Hippo/YAP signaling during ischemic injury is strongly context dependent. Transient YAP activation may support cellular adaptation and tissue repair during acute stress, whereas sustained or dysregulated activation may promote pathological remodeling or tumorigenesis. This raises an important unresolved question in ischemic flap biology: although hypoxia can activate YAP-dependent adaptive programs, severely ischemic flap regions remain unable to mount an effective survival response. Whether spatially insufficient or temporally delayed Hippo/YAP adaptation contributes to distal flap necrosis has not been established. We therefore considered this apparent discrepancy a central “Hippo paradox” in ischemic flap repair.

Hypoxia-induced microRNAs (miRNAs) are critical mediators of cellular adaptation to ischemic stress [7]. Among them, miR-210-3p, a canonical hypoxia-responsive miRNA, is consistently upregulated under hypoxic conditions and exerts pleiotropic effects on mitochondrial metabolism, oxidative stress, angiogenesis, and cell survival [8–10]. Notably, miR-210 directly represses Ephrin-A3 (EFNA3) under hypoxia, thereby modulating angiogenic responses [11]. Thus, rather than representing an entirely unrecognized miRNA–target relationship, the miR-210-3p–EFNA3 branch provides an established biological foundation from which to investigate its function in ischemic flaps. What remains unknown is whether EFNA3 functionally connects hypoxia-responsive miR-210-3p signaling to Hippo/YAP regulation and thereby influences YAP subcellular localization and tissue adaptation under ischemic stress. Moreover, despite the therapeutic potential of miRNAs, clinical application is hindered by poor stability, rapid degradation, and inefficient intracellular delivery [12, 13]. Although viral vectors achieve high transduction efficiency, their immunogenicity and biosafety concerns limit clinical translation, underscoring the need for non-viral delivery system with favorable preliminary compatibility [14, 15].

In this study, we characterize the spatial and temporal dysregulation of Hippo/YAP signaling in ischemic flaps and investigate whether the HIF-1α–miR-210-3p–EFNA3 axis contributes to adaptive YAP activation. Mechanistically, we examine whether miR-210-3p-mediated suppression of EFNA3 facilitates YAP nuclear localization and enhances antioxidant and cytoprotective responses. Rather than assuming that Hippo pathway inactivation is universally beneficial, we evaluate its association with tissue survival in the specific context of acute ischemic injury.

To address the translational challenge of miRNA delivery, we further draw inspiration from viral gene delivery strategies. Natural viruses achieve highly efficient nucleic acid transport through compact genome condensation, hierarchical structural organization, and membrane-interacting peptide motifs [16, 17]. Virus-inspired and biomimetic nanomaterials have recently emerged as promising non-viral gene delivery platforms capable of recapitulating key viral functions while minimizing immunogenic risk [18–20]. Motivated by these principles, we rationally construct a modular biomimetic virus-inspired nanoparticle composed of (i) a cationic TAT–PEG component enabling miRNA condensation and cellular internalization, and (ii) a negatively charged succinylated dextran (GSE) outer layer for surface charge regulation and colloidal stability. This stepwise self-assembly process yields a stable core–shell nanostructure that functionally recapitulates key aspects of viral delivery while avoiding the use of viral vectors and their associated vector-specific limitations.

Collectively, this study links hypoxia-responsive miRNA signaling with Hippo/YAP adaptation through the EFNA3–YAP regulatory branch and evaluates a structurally engineered non-viral platform for therapeutic miR-210-3p delivery. The principal mechanistic advance lies not in identifying miR-210-3p or EFNA3 individually, but in defining their functional connection to YAP regulation in ischemic tissue and translating this relationship into a nanotherapeutic strategy.

Results

Hippo signaling pathway inactivation is associated with ischemic flap survival

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying ischemic flap repair, we established a modified skin flap (FLAP) model on the dorsal region of mice. To characterize the spatial heterogeneity of ischemic injury, the flaps were divided into Area I, the proximal and relatively viable region, and Area II, the distal and severely ischemic region prone to necrosis. Differentially expressed genes and associated signaling pathways were subsequently identified by comparing these two regions (Fig. 1A, B). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed a distinct separation of the global gene expression profiles between area I and area II (Fig. 1C), indicating significant transcriptional differences between the two regions. Specifically, 743 genes were upregulated and 301 genes were downregulated in area I compared to area II. Genes significantly upregulated in area I were primarily associated with skin development, epidermal differentiation, keratinization, and angiogenesis. Conversely, area II showed an enrichment of genes involved in the inflammatory response, chemokine signaling, and neutrophil recruitment (Fig. 1D, Fig. S1A). Gene Ontology (GO) biological process analysis further indicated that the DEGs were predominantly enriched in processes such as epidermal development, skin development, keratinization, and hair follicle development (Fig. 1E, Fig. S1B).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the Hippo signaling pathway was significantly enriched in area I, alongside changes in cornified envelope formation and cytokine-receptor interactions (Fig. 1F). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) results demonstrated that the Hippo signaling pathway, skin development, and cornified envelope formation were significantly positively enriched in area I, whereas pro-inflammatory pathways were relatively enhanced in area II (Fig. 1G).

Detailed KEGG analysis and DEG heatmaps further showed increased expression of protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A), protein phosphatase 1 (PP1), and apoptosis-stimulating protein 2 of p53 (ASPP2) in area I. These proteins are known to modulate the phosphorylation of core kinases MST1/2 and LATS1/2, as well as the downstream effectors YAP/TAZ (Fig. S1C, Fig. 1H). A reduction in the phosphorylation levels of these core kinases is a critical hallmark of Hippo signaling pathway inactivation [21]. A schematic diagram summarizes the molecular mechanism whereby Hippo pathway inactivation facilitates YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation and subsequent activation of downstream transcriptional programs (Fig. 1I).

Histological analysis revealed that area I maintained intact skin architecture with densely arranged dermal collagen. In contrast, area II exhibited obvious tissue edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, and disordered collagen structures (Fig. 1J). Western blot analysis revealed higher protein levels of p-MST1, p-LATS1, and p-YAP in area II than in area I, accompanied by significantly reduced nuclear YAP expression in area II (Fig. 1K, L). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining further confirmed that the signals of p-MST1, p-LATS1, and p-YAP were attenuated in area I, whereas YAP was markedly enriched within the nuclei (Fig. 1M, N). Taken together, these results indicate that reduced Hippo pathway kinase phosphorylation and enhanced YAP nuclear localization are preferentially associated with the relatively viable regions of ischemic flaps. This spatial association suggests that YAP activation may contribute to tissue adaptation and preservation during the acute phase of ischemic injury, although its biological effects are likely to depend on the timing, magnitude, and cellular context of pathway activation.

miR-210-3p inhibits EFNA3 and YAP interaction, promoting YAP nuclear localization in HUVECs

Reducing cellular oxidative stress and mitigating hypoxic injury are critical factors for improving skin flap survival [22]. To investigate the self-repair processes during flap transplantation, we established two cell models of oxidative stress: tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP) stimulation and hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) treatment (Fig. 2A). Increasing concentrations of TBHP or prolonged H/R exposure led to a significant decrease in cell viability, increased LDH release, and marked upregulation of HIF-1α, indicating successful model establishment (Fig. 2B, C). Concurrently, DCFH-DA fluorescence staining demonstrated that oxidative stress and H/R treatment significantly elevated intracellular ROS levels (Fig. S2A, B). Western blot analysis revealed that following TBHP or H/R treatment, p-YAP levels significantly decreased while nuclear YAP expression markedly increased (Fig. 2D, E). Immunofluorescence (IF) further confirmed that YAP underwent prominent nuclear translocation under these conditions (Fig. 2F, G), suggesting that YAP is activated and that HIF-1α may modulate YAP phosphorylation.

To elucidate how HIF-1α regulates YAP phosphorylation, we first screened for key miRNAs involved in the precise regulation of target genes. RNA was extracted from areas I and II of the flaps for miRNA sequencing (miRNA-seq) (Fig. S2C). A total of 39 differentially expressed miRNAs were identified, with 22 upregulated and 17 downregulated in the ischemic area (Fig. 2H, Fig. S2D). Heatmaps of qPCR data validated the expression changes of selected miRNAs, consistent with the sequencing results (Fig. S2E). Given that HIF-1α is known to influence miR-210 expression [23], we performed qPCR which showed a correlation between miR-210-3p and HIF-1α expression, with miR-210-3p levels significantly elevated in area II (Fig. S2F). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays confirmed that HIF-1α directly binds to the miR-210 promoter region, and qPCR further verified that HIF-1α significantly promotes miR-210-3p transcription (Fig. 2I-K), identifying miR-210-3p as a hypoxia-inducible miRNA.

Functional assays showed that overexpression of miR-210-3p in HUVECs significantly enhanced tube formation and migratory capacity under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, whereas miR-210-3p inhibition produced the opposite effects (Fig. S2G–J). These results indicate that the HIF-1α–miR-210-3p axis participates in ischemic flap repair by modulating angiogenesis.

To investigate whether miR-210-3p target genes are functionally associated with YAP regulation, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) followed by Western blotting and mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of YAP-associated proteins (Fig. S3A, B). Integration of the YAP co-IP–MS dataset with TargetScan-predicted miR-210-3p targets and differentially expressed genes identified EFNA3 as a candidate mediator linking miR-210-3p to YAP regulation. Co-IP followed by Western blotting supported an association between EFNA3 and YAP (Fig. 2L). In parallel, dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that miR-210-3p directly targets the EFNA3 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR), as miR-210-3p significantly reduced the activity of the wild-type EFNA3 3′UTR reporter, whereas mutation of the predicted binding site abolished this effect (Fig. 2M, N).

We next performed an EFNA3 rescue experiment to determine whether EFNA3 functionally contributes to miR-210-3p-mediated YAP activation. Under TBHP-induced oxidative stress, miR-210-3p overexpression markedly reduced EFNA3 fluorescence intensity and increased YAP nuclear accumulation in HUVECs. EFNA3 re-expression restored EFNA3 abundance and attenuated YAP nuclear localization. Importantly, co-expression of EFNA3 with miR-210-3p partially reversed the effects of miR-210-3p alone, as evidenced by recovery of EFNA3 expression and reduced nuclear accumulation of YAP (Fig. 2O, P). These findings indicate that EFNA3 functionally contributes to miR-210-3p-mediated regulation of YAP localization under oxidative stress.

To determine whether this regulatory relationship was restricted to endothelial cells, we further performed EFNA3 rescue experiments in fibroblasts. Consistent with the observations in HUVECs, miR-210-3p treatment increased YAP fluorescence intensity and reduced EFNA3 expression under TBHP stimulation, whereas EFNA3 re-expression restored EFNA3 abundance and attenuated the increase in YAP intensity (Fig. S3C, D). Together, these results demonstrate that EFNA3 functionally mediates miR-210-3p-induced YAP activation in both endothelial cells and fibroblasts.

Subsequently, we synthesized siEFNA3 and treated HUVECs with either miR-210-3p mimics or inhibitors (Fig. S3E). WB results for core Hippo kinases showed that both miR-210-3p mimics and siEFNA3 significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of MST1, LATS1, and YAP, whereas miR-210-3p inhibitors had no significant effect (Fig. S3F). These findings demonstrate that miR-210-3p modulates the phosphorylation status of core Hippo kinases in endothelial cells. By suppressing EFNA3 expression, miR-210-3p reduces the interaction between dephosphorylated YAP and EFNA3, thereby promoting YAP nuclear localization and acting as a critical regulatory switch for this pathway.

miR-210-3p inhibits oxidative stress and cell death in vivo to promote ischemic flap survival

To substantiate that miR-210-3p promotes ischemic flap survival by inhibiting EFNA3 expression, we constructed AAV-miR-210-3p, AAV-miR-210-3p inhibitor, and AAV-EFNA3-shRNA viral vectors (Fig. S4A). Following administration, viral transduction efficiency was validated in HUVECs via qPCR (Fig. S4B-D). A FLAP model was established on the depilated dorsal skin of C57BL/6 mice, followed by subcutaneous viral injection using a micro-syringe (Fig. 3A, Fig. S5A). To assess the impact of YAP nuclear localization on ischemic flaps, we utilized CIL56 (CA3), a novel YAP-targeted inhibitor that reduces cell proliferation and induces apoptosis by suppressing YAP transcriptional activity [24]. Flap survival across groups was evaluated using digital photography (Fig. 3B). Compared to the control group, injections of AAV-miR-210-3p and AAV-EFNA3-shRNA significantly increased the flap survival area (Fig. 3F). Real-time infrared thermography at postoperative day 7 (POD7) revealed higher temperatures in the distal necrotic regions of the miR-210-3p and EFNA3-shRNA groups compared to controls (Fig. 3C). To account for environmental factors and anesthesia, we analyzed the relative temperature difference (Delta T) between the flap and the neck (a normal tissue site). At POD7, the Delta T was significantly elevated in the miR-210-3p and EFNA3-shRNA groups compared to the PBS group, whereas it was reduced in the miR-210-3p inhibitor and CIL56 groups (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, Laser Speckle Contrast Imaging (LSCI) demonstrated subcutaneous vascular network reconstruction (Fig. 3D). While no significant differences in blood flow signals were observed at POD0 (Fig. S5B-C), the miR-210-3p and EFNA3-shRNA groups exhibited significantly stronger signals than the PBS, miR-210-3p inhibitor, and CIL56 groups at POD7 (Fig. 3H). Qualitative observation revealed that miR-210-3p and EFNA3-shRNA treatment markedly ameliorated the severe edema and subcutaneous venous congestion seen in the other groups (Fig. 3E), which was further confirmed by reduced tissue water content (Fig. 3I).

The flap was divided into three areas: area I (proximal survival area), area II (survival-necrosis interface), and area III (distal necrotic area) [25]. Histological analysis of area II at POD7 showed that miR-210-3p and EFNA3-shRNA treatment resulted in less neutrophil infiltration and reduced skin thickness compared to the PBS, miR-210-3p inhibitor, and CIL56 groups, the latter of which remained in a heightened inflammatory state (Fig. S5D). IF staining for CD31 and alpha-SMA demonstrated a significant increase in the number of positive vessels in the miR-210-3p and EFNA3-shRNA groups (Fig. 3J, K). Additionally, Masson’s trichrome staining confirmed that these treatments promoted superior dermal collagen remodeling (Fig. 3L, M), collectively suggesting that AAV-miR-210-3p and AAV-EFNA3-shRNA facilitate the repair of distal ischemic flaps.

Oxidative stress and cell death are pivotal pathological drivers of ischemic flap injury [26]. Dihydroethidium (DHE) staining revealed that miR-210-3p overexpression and EFNA3 knockdown significantly curtailed oxidative stress in the tissue (Fig. 3N, O). TUNEL staining further showed a reduction in cell death in these groups (Fig. 3N, P). IF staining for Collagen I and III indicated increased collagen deposition in the miR-210-3p and EFNA3-shRNA groups, characterized by higher fluorescence intensity and an increased ratio of Collagen I (Fig. 3Q-T). Moreover, these groups exhibited enhanced epidermal regeneration, as evidenced by increased CK10 expression (Fig. S6A, B). At POD7, the cell proliferation capacity in these groups was lower than in the control groups, suggesting that the tissue had transitioned into the subsequent phase of the wound healing cascade (Fig. S6C, D). Reinnervation, indicated by β3-tubulin staining, was also significantly enriched in the miR-210-3p and EFNA3-shRNA groups (Fig. S6E, F). Finally, macrophage polarization analysis demonstrated that miR-210-3p overexpression significantly decreased the proportion of CD86⁺ (M1) macrophages while increasing CD206⁺ (M2) macrophages, thereby improving the inflammatory microenvironment (Fig. 3U-X). Heatmap analysis of cytokines further confirmed a downregulation of M1-associated cytokines and an upregulation of M2-associated cytokines in the miR-210-3p group (Fig. S6G).

In summary, our in vivo findings demonstrate that miR-210-3p improves blood perfusion, promotes angiogenesis and collagen remodeling, and shifts macrophage polarization toward a pro-healing phenotype by suppressing oxidative stress and cell death, thereby significantly enhancing ischemic flap survival.

miR-210-3p reduces the phosphorylation level of the core kinase of the hippo signaling pathway

To further elucidate the in vivo regulatory role of miR-210-3p on the Hippo signaling pathway, we utilized Western blot, immunofluorescence (IF), and biochemical analysis to assess the expression and phosphorylation status of key components within the ischemic flaps. Western blot results revealed that, compared to the PBS group, miR-210-3p overexpression significantly decreased the phosphorylation levels of MST1 and LATS1 (p-MST1, p-LATS1), as well as YAP (p-YAP), which was accompanied by a marked increase in nuclear YAP expression. Conversely, miR-210-3p inhibition significantly enhanced the phosphorylation of MST1, LATS1, and YAP, while suppressing YAP nuclear translocation (Fig. 4A, B).

The temporal expression profiles of miR-210-3p and EFNA3 were examined at POD3, POD5, and POD7. Following exogenous miR-210-3p delivery, miR-210-3p expression was highest at POD3 and subsequently declined, whereas its expression in the PBS, miR-210-3p inhibitor, and CIL56 groups increased gradually toward POD7 (Fig. 4C). EFNA3 generally showed an inverse temporal pattern, with lower early expression after miR-210-3p treatment followed by gradual recovery during the repair period (Fig. 4D).

Consistent with these expression dynamics, nuclear YAP accumulation was prominent during the early phase after miR-210-3p delivery and gradually decreased from POD3 to POD7, whereas a later increase in nuclear YAP was observed in the PBS and inhibitory-treatment groups (Fig. 4E–G). These findings suggest that exogenous miR-210-3p advances the activation of the EFNA3–YAP-associated adaptive response to an earlier postoperative stage. In contrast, the later increase in endogenous miR-210-3p observed in the control groups may represent a delayed compensatory response after substantial ischemic injury has already developed. The subsequent decline in miR-210-3p and nuclear YAP following treatment is consistent with a transient and stage-restricted response rather than sustained pathway activation.

To further validate the in vivo inhibitory effect of miR-210-3p on Hippo pathway activation, IF staining of flap tissues at POD7 was performed for p-YAP, p-LATS1, and p-MST1 (Fig. 4H). Consistent with the western blot results, miR-210-3p overexpression and EFNA3 knockdown markedly reduced the fluorescence intensities of p-YAP, p-LATS1, and p-MST1, whereas miR-210-3p inhibition and CIL56 treatment maintained relatively high phosphorylation levels of these Hippo pathway components (Fig. 4I–K). These data further confirm that miR-210-3p suppresses Hippo pathway kinase phosphorylation in vivo and promotes YAP activation through nuclear translocation.

Furthermore, biochemical assays demonstrated that miR-210-3p overexpression significantly reduced lipid peroxidation markers (LPO: lipid peroxidation; MDA: malondialdehyde) and augmented antioxidant capacity (GSH: glutathione) in ischemic flap tissues, while miR-210-3p inhibition produced the opposite effects (Fig. 4L–N). This indicates a close correlation between the activation state of the Hippo pathway and tissue oxidative stress levels.

In summary, these results indicate that miR-210-3p suppresses EFNA3 expression in vivo, thereby reducing the phosphorylation of core Hippo signaling kinases and activating YAP nuclear translocation, ultimately promoting ischemic flap survival via the miR-210-3p–EFNA3–YAP axis.

Construction and characterization of biomimetic virus-inspired nanoparticles for efficient miRNA delivery

Given the pivotal protective role of miR-210-3p in ischemic flap survival, we engineered a virus-inspired nanoparticle (GSE-PVLP) for in vivo miRNA delivery with high loading efficiency and structural stability (Fig. 5A). The system utilized succinylated dextran as a backbone, which was modified with PEGylation and TAT peptide to ensure excellent cell-penetrating capability as well as biocompatibility and cell-penetrating capabilities. Efficient miRNA encapsulation was achieved through electrostatic interactions. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that GSE-PVLP and miRNA self-assembled into uniform, regularly shaped nanoparticles at various N: P ratios (Fig. 5B). The N: P ratio was defined as the molar ratio of protonatable amine groups (N) in the cationic carrier to phosphate groups (P) in the miRNA backbone, a standard parameter used to describe electrostatic complexation of cationic vectors with nucleic acids in non-viral delivery studies [27]. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that the particle size increased with the N: P ratio, with the overall distribution ranging between 80 and 150 nm (Fig. 5C), which is ideal for in vivo delivery. Concurrently, the Zeta potential shifted from negative to nearly neutral or slightly positive, suggesting successful miRNA encapsulation and charge shielding (Fig. 5D). The encapsulation efficiency progressively improved with increasing N: P ratios, exceeding 90% when N/P = 10 and reaching saturation a plateau at higher ratios, indicating saturation (Fig. S7A).

The CCK-8 assay results indicated that GSE-PVLP exhibit negligible no significant effect on cell viability at N: P ratios ≤ 10, demonstrating its favorable preliminary biocompatibility. When the N: P ratio increased to 20, cell viability showed a slight decrease (Fig. 5E). Agarose gel blocking assays further confirmed that GSE-PVLP effectively bound miRNA at N: P ≥ 1 and achieves complete blocking at N: P ≥ 5 (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, at N: P = 10, miRNA loaded onto GSE-PVLP exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to degradation by RNase A and RNase I compared to free miRNA, indicating the delivery system effectively preserves miRNA structural integrity (Fig. 5G). Stability testing demonstrated that N: P = 10 maintained reliable storage stability and complete siRNA encapsulation for at least 48 h (Fig. S7B-D).

Confocal microscopy revealed substantial intracellular co-localization of fluorescence-labeled miRNA (FAM-miRNA) with the rhodamine-labeled carrier, predominantly within the cytoplasm, indicating efficient cellular internalization and intracellular delivery mediated by GSE-PVLP (Fig. 5H, I). Functional validation showed that GSE-PVLP-oe markedly increased miR-210-3p expression and reduced the expression of its target gene, EFNA3, whereas GSE-PVLP-in produced the opposite effects (Fig. 5J). In an in vitro angiogenesis assay, GSE-PVLP-oe significantly enhanced endothelial tube formation and increased the number of branching points, whereas GSE-PVLP-in impaired vascular network formation (Fig. 5K, L).

To evaluate the effects of GSE-PVLP in different flap-relevant cell types, intracellular ROS and macrophage phenotypes were examined. DCFH-DA staining showed that GSE-PVLP-oe significantly reduced TBHP-induced ROS accumulation in both endothelial cells and HaCaT keratinocytes, whereas GSE-PVLP-in further aggravated oxidative stress (Fig. S7E–H). Under LPS stimulation, GSE-PVLP-oe decreased the pro-inflammatory marker CD86 and increased the reparative marker CD206, while GSE-PVLP-in produced the opposite effects (Fig. S7I, J). These findings indicate that GSE-PVLP-mediated miR-210-3p delivery exerts antioxidant effects across multiple flap-relevant cell types and promotes a less inflammatory macrophage phenotype.

In summary, these findings demonstrate that the GSE-PVLP virus-inspired nanoparticle—designed based on the key miRNA in ischemic flaps—can efficiently load and stabilize miRNA. It significantly enhances the functional effects of miRNA while showing favorable preliminary biocompatibility, providing a robust delivery platform for the in vivo application of miR-210-3p.

GSE-PVLP-mediated miR-210-3p delivery significantly enhances ischemic flap survival

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the engineered virus-inspired nanoparticles in vivo, miR-210-3p-loaded GSE-PVLPs were administered in a mouse ischemic flap model. A head-to-head comparison with viral miR-210-3p delivery was also performed. Macroscopic observation showed that GSE-PVLP-mediated miR-210-3p overexpression (GSE-PVLP-oe) reduced flap necrosis, whereas GSE-PVLP-in aggravated tissue injury. Viral miR-210-3p overexpression produced a comparable protective effect, while viral miR-210-3p inhibition showed a trend similar to GSE-PVLP-in (Fig. 6A).

Laser speckle contrast imaging and infrared thermography showed that GSE-PVLP-oe increased local blood perfusion and surface temperature at postoperative day 7. Quantitative analysis further demonstrated an increased flap survival area, reduced temperature difference, enhanced blood-flow signal intensity, and decreased tissue water content. No significant differences in these therapeutic outcomes were observed between GSE-PVLP-oe and viral miR-210-3p overexpression, indicating comparable in vivo efficacy (Fig. 6A, B).

Immunofluorescence analysis showed that GSE-PVLP-oe reduced DHE and TUNEL signals in ischemic tissues. It also decreased CD68⁺/CD86⁺ signals and increased CD68⁺/CD206⁺ signals, indicating attenuation of the pro-inflammatory macrophage response and enhancement of a repair-associated phenotype (Fig. 6C, E). Preliminary biosafety was further evaluated by histological examination and hemolysis assays. No apparent pathological abnormalities were observed in the lung, spleen, liver, kidney, or heart after GSE-PVLP administration, and the hemolysis ratios remained low across the tested concentrations (Fig. S8A, B), indicating favorable histocompatibility and hemocompatibility.

In summary, the GSE-PVLP nanoplatform effectively improved ischemic flap survival by enhancing local perfusion, reducing oxidative tissue injury, modulating inflammation, and promoting adaptive YAP activation. Importantly,GSE-PVLP-oe achieved therapeutic efficacy comparable to viral miR-210-3p delivery while exhibiting favorable preliminary biosafety, supporting its potential as a non-viral miRNA delivery strategy for ischemic tissue repair.

Discussion

The central finding of this study is that the most severely ischemic region of the flap failed to mount the strongest YAP-associated adaptive response. Instead, the distal region retained relatively high Hippo kinase phosphorylation and limited YAP nuclear localization, whereas the relatively viable region showed greater YAP activation. This spatial discordance suggests that hypoxia alone may be insufficient to ensure an effective YAP-dependent response. Our data further connect the established miR-210-3p–EFNA3 branch to YAP regulation and suggest that delayed or insufficient engagement of this pathway may contribute to the failure of severely ischemic tissue to adapt before irreversible injury develops.

This apparent Hippo paradox may reflect a mismatch between the intensity of ischemic stress and the capacity of cells to respond. Although hypoxia can promote YAP nuclear translocation in several cellular contexts [30, 31], severe ischemia can simultaneously disrupt energy metabolism, redox homeostasis, and cytoskeletal integrity [32, 33], thereby limiting the capacity of cells to coordinate an effective adaptive response. Once these processes are extensively impaired, the tissue may no longer be able to coordinate timely Hippo pathway attenuation and YAP nuclear translocation. The viable portion of the flap may therefore retain sufficient cellular integrity to activate an adaptive program that is delayed or lost in the distal region. YAP activation should thus be viewed as a potential component of successful tissue adaptation rather than an inevitable consequence of hypoxia.

The biological effects of Hippo/YAP signaling are strongly dependent on timing, magnitude, cell type, and duration. Transient YAP activation during acute injury can support stress tolerance, metabolic adaptation, and regenerative responses [34–36], whereas sustained or excessive activity may promote pathological proliferation, fibroinflammatory remodeling, and tumorigenesis [37, 38]. The present findings should therefore be interpreted within the acute ischemic period examined here. The association of nuclear YAP with viable tissue and the adverse effects of YAP inhibition support an adaptive role during early flap injury, but do not imply that persistent Hippo pathway inactivation would remain beneficial during later remodeling. The gradual reduction in nuclear YAP after early miR-210-3p delivery is consistent with a temporally restricted response.

MiR-210-3p is a canonical HIF-1α-responsive hypoxamir [39], and EFNA3 is an established direct target under hypoxic conditions [11]. The contribution of this study therefore lies not in identifying a new miRNA–target pair, but in demonstrating that EFNA3 functionally contributes to YAP regulation in ischemic flap tissue. Target-site validation, protein-association analysis, EFNA3 re-expression, and EFNA3 silencing together support EFNA3 as an important mediator linking miR-210-3p to Hippo/YAP activity. EFNA3 re-expression attenuated miR-210-3p-induced YAP nuclear accumulation, supporting its functional involvement in this regulatory relationship. Given the broad target repertoire of miR-210-3p [39], additional targets may also contribute to the overall tissue response.

The biochemical basis of the EFNA3–YAP association remains unresolved. Co-immunoprecipitation indicates that the two proteins are present within an associated molecular complex but does not establish direct binding. Because EFNA3 is membrane associated and YAP is regulated through a broader network of membrane receptors, cytoskeletal tension, kinases, and intracellular scaffolds, EFNA3 may influence YAP through intermediate signaling components [40]. Domain-mapping and identification of these intermediates will be required to define the molecular architecture of this regulatory relationship.

The temporal kinetics provide an additional explanation for the therapeutic effect of miR-210-3p. Exogenous delivery shifted the miR-210-3p response toward the early postoperative phase, whereas the endogenous increase in untreated tissue occurred later. Therapeutic benefit may therefore depend not only on increasing miR-210-3p abundance, but also on activating the response while ischemic cells remain salvageable. A delayed endogenous response may represent compensation for persistent injury but may arise too late to prevent distal tissue loss. The subsequent recovery of EFNA3 expression and decline in nuclear YAP further support a stage-restricted model in which early pathway engagement facilitates adaptation and then subsides as tissue conditions improve. The therapeutic effects of miR-210-3p were accompanied by reduced oxidative injury and iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, together with preservation of mitochondrial membrane potential and calcium homeostasis. These coordinated changes suggest improved cellular stress tolerance and provide a plausible explanation for the enhanced preservation of ischemic tissue.

Viral vectors provide efficient gene transfer and sustained transgene expression, but their immunogenicity, production complexity, and limited suitability for repeated administration may restrict their use in settings that require local and temporally controlled intervention [41]. Virus-inspired nanomaterials offer an alternative strategy by incorporating selected viral delivery functions, including nucleic acid condensation, cellular attachment, and intracellular cargo transport, into tunable non-viral structures [42, 43]. Recent advances in programmable nanoassembly have further demonstrated that particle architecture and surface chemistry can be engineered to improve nucleic acid stability, cellular uptake, and intracellular delivery [44–46].

Guided by these principles, GSE-PVLP was designed to combine cationic miRNA condensation, TAT-mediated cellular internalization, and an anionic outer coating to support cargo protection, cellular uptake, and colloidal stability. The head-to-head comparison with AAV provided a relevant functional benchmark for this platform. Under the conditions examined, both delivery strategies improved flap survival, tissue perfusion, and oxidative stress-related outcomes, with no statistically significant differences in the principal functional endpoints. Although these findings do not establish formal equivalence, they demonstrate that GSE-PVLP can achieve a robust therapeutic response through a non-viral approach. This feature may be particularly relevant to acute ischemic injury, in which local and temporally restricted delivery may be more appropriate than prolonged transgene expression.

The major-organ histology and hemolysis results provide initial support for the biocompatibility of GSE-PVLP under the dosing conditions used in this study. Together with its therapeutic efficacy, these findings support further preclinical development of the platform. Nevertheless, the present safety evaluation was limited to short-term observations, and future studies incorporating quantitative biodistribution, local retention and clearance analyses, repeated administration, and longer-term systemic and immune-response assessment will be valuable for defining its translational profile more comprehensively.

The present study primarily focused on the acute adaptive response to flap ischemia. Although EFNA3 re-expression attenuated miR-210-3p-induced YAP nuclear accumulation, the molecular intermediates connecting membrane-associated EFNA3 to the Hippo kinase cascade remain to be clarified. In addition, given the broad target repertoire of miR-210-3p, other downstream targets may also participate in the overall tissue response, although their relative contributions were not directly examined here. Further studies resolving the cell-specific distribution and activity of GSE-PVLP in vivo will help refine the mechanistic and translational framework established in this study.

In conclusion, this study establishes a functional connection between the established miR-210-3p–EFNA3 branch and Hippo/YAP regulation in ischemic flap injury. The spatial and temporal findings support a model in which severely ischemic tissue fails to activate an effective YAP-associated adaptive response early enough to prevent irreversible damage, whereas therapeutic miR-210-3p delivery advances this response into a more favorable intervention window. EFNA3 contributes to this regulation, while additional miR-210-3p targets may also participate in the broader tissue response. GSE-PVLP reproduced the major therapeutic effects observed with AAV and showed favorable preliminary biocompatibility, supporting its continued preclinical evaluation as a non-viral miRNA delivery strategy.

Cell lines and animals

The HUVECs, RAW264.7, HDFα, HaCaT and HEK 293T cell line were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. The cell lines were cultured in an incubator maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% air. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Procell, PM150210) was supplemented with 10% sterile fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The mice were maintained under standard conditions (21–25 °C, 50–60% humidity, 12-hour light-dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All animal experiments conducted in this study adhered to the Guidelines for the Use of Laboratory Animal Care established by the Chinese Academy of Health Sciences and received approval from the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Oujiang Laboratory (OJLAB23030313).

Randomised flap model

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of chloral hydrate (350 mg/kg). Following hair removal, a 1.5 cm × 4.5 cm skin flap was randomly excised from the back of the mice, and both sacral arteries were severed. The skin flap was subsequently closed using a 5 − 0 suture. As illustrated in the Fig. 1A, the flap was divided into three equal area, ordered from the distal end to the tip: area I, area II, and area III. As noted in the Introduction, necrosis of skin tissue occurs when the aspect ratio of the flap exceeds 1.5:1 to 2:1. Therefore, in our experiments, on postoperative day 7, area I was normal and healthy, whereas area III was necrotic without intervention. Conversely, area II was typically ischemic and was predisposed to necrosis. To enhance flap survival, area II was designated as a watershed area to mitigate ischemia and potential necrosis. Furthermore, differences in the pathomechanisms of tissues in area I and II are frequently compared to identify potential targets for mitigating ischemic injury.

Groups and treatments

C57BL/6 J mice (n = 12) that received no treatment after skin flap surgery were euthanized on day 7. The skin from area I and II was collected for functional comparison and sequencing analysis; based on these results, subsequent animal experiments and group assignments were conducted. C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to the PBS group (PBS, n = 24), the AAV-miR-210-3p inhibitor group (210-3p in, n = 24), the AAV-miR-210-3p group (210-3p oe, n = 24), the AAV-EFNA3-shRNA (EFNA3-shRNA, n = 24), Cil56 group (n = 24), GSE-PVLP-in (n = 24), and GSE-PVLP-oe (n = 24). Two weeks prior to skin flap surgery, mice in the AAV-miR-210-3p inhibitor, AAV-miR-210-3p, and AAV-EFNA3-shRNA groups received subcutaneous injections of 25 µL of viral vectors containing 2 × 10^9 packaged genome particles at four sites using a microinjector. In the PBS and Cil56 groups, PBS was administered using the same method. The Cil56 group received intraperitoneal injections of 100 µL of Cil56 (5 mg/kg) daily post-surgery, while the remaining groups were administered PBS using the same method. The same number of mice was used in the remaining experiments (n = 6), except for LC-MS, which included (n = 3). Each group was sampled on POD3 (n = 6), POD5 (n = 6), and POD7 (n = 12), with the flap II area serving as the primary tissue source for subsequent validation experiments.

Synthesis of GSE-PVLP nanoparticles

GSE (succinylated dextran) was synthesized by succinic anhydride modification of dextran. Briefly, dextran was dissolved in anhydrous DMSO and reacted with succinic anhydride in the presence of DMAP and triethylamine. After completion of the reaction, the product was precipitated with ethanol, followed by dialysis and lyophilization to obtain GSE powder. The PVLP component was constructed via a thiol–maleimide coupling reaction. Cysteine-terminated TAT peptide was reacted with MAL-PEG-NHS in PBS buffer, and the unreacted components were removed by ultrafiltration to obtain the TAT–PEG conjugate. miRNA-loaded GSE-PVLP nanoparticles were prepared using a stepwise self-assembly strategy. Briefly, miRNA was first mixed with the cationic PVLP component at different N/P ratios to form core complexes, followed by the addition of GSE solution for surface coating and charge regulation. The mixture was incubated at room temperature to yield stable virus-inspired nanoparticles.

Characterization of GSE-PVLP nanoparticles

The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS). The morphology of the nanoparticles was observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The encapsulation efficiency of miRNA was determined using the RiboGreen fluorescence assay. Free miRNA was separated by ultrafiltration, and the encapsulation efficiency was calculated based on fluorescence intensity. The physicochemical properties and encapsulation performance of nanoparticles prepared at different N/P ratios were systematically evaluated to determine the optimal assembly conditions.

Agarose gel retardation assay

The binding ability of GSE-PVLP nanoparticles to miRNA was evaluated by agarose gel retardation assay. miRNA-loaded nanoparticles prepared at different N/P ratios were subjected to electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. The migration behavior of miRNA was visualized using a nucleic acid staining dye.

Internalization of GSE-PVLP into macrophages in vitro

Cellular uptake of GSE-PVLP/miRNA complexes by Raw264.7 cells was investigated using confocal laser scanning microscopy. FAM-labeled miRNA and rhodamine-labeled GSE-PVLP were used to visualize the intracellular distribution of nucleic acid cargo and carrier materials, respectively.

Infrared thermography

Thermal images of the ischemic skin flap were captured using a FLIR One Pro external infrared thermography probe connected to a mobile device. Mean temperatures of the surgical area (flap) and the head and neck (normal skin) were measured independently. Delta-T was defined as the difference between the flap temperature and the temperature of normal skin. The temperature differences between the groups were analyzed and compared statistically. A closer proximity of Delta-T to 0 °C indicated better recovery of the skin flap. To mitigate the influence of significant fluctuations in the basal body temperatures of the mice on the results, the basal body temperatures of each group were analyzed. The results indicated no statistically significant differences between the two groups.

Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI)

LSCI analysis illustrated the vascular network of the flap. After anesthesia on postoperative day 7 (POD7), mice were placed in a controlled and secure environment. The blood supply to the skin flap was subsequently assessed using a laser speckle blood flow imager (PeriCam PSI, Perimed, Sweden). LSCI analysis was conducted following previously established protocols. Blood flow was evaluated by calculating perfusion units (PU) using PIMSoft software (Version 1.11, Sweden). Blood flow measurements were taken three times per mouse and averaged for subsequent statistical analysis.

mRNA-seq

Libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. The raw FASTQ format files were initially analyzed using FastQC for quality control, including the removal of low-quality sequences and splice sequences, to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data. Clean reads from each sample were retained for subsequent analysis. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome with HISAT2. FPKM was calculated for each gene, and the number of reads for each gene was obtained using HTSeq-count. The resulting .sam files were converted to .bam files with Samtools, and the .bam files were sorted using Samtools. The reads were assembled using StringTie, and TopHat comparison was performed using HiSeq software. The results were quantified using HiSeq software. Using R, the HiSeq quantification results were summarized into an expression matrix and subjected to PCA to evaluate the biological replicates of the samples. Based on the hypergeometric distribution, GO, KEGG pathway, Reactome, and WikiPathways enrichment analyses were performed on the expression matrices using R (v 4.3.1) to identify significantly enriched terms. Column, chord, and bubble plots of the significantly enriched terms were generated using R (v 4.3.1).

MicroRNA-seq

Convert basic reads to sequence data, also known as raw reads, through base conditioning. Low-quality reads were filtered to remove those containing 5’ primer contaminants and poly (A) sequences. Clean reads were obtained by filtering the raw data for reads without 3’ adapters and insertion tags, as well as for reads less than 15 nt or greater than 41 nt. The length distribution of clean sequences in the reference genome was determined, and the sequences were subsequently aligned. Bowtie searches were performed using Rfam v.10.1 (http://www.sanger.ac.uk/software/Rfam). rRNAs, including scRNAs, cis-regulatory RNAs, snRNAs, tRNAs, and other RNA types, were annotated and filtered. The cDNA sequences of repetitive sequences from the species and the Repbase database were identified using Bowtie software. Mature miRs were identified by comparing them to the miRBase v22 database (http://www.mirbase.org/), and the expression patterns across different samples were analyzed. Differentially expressed miRs were identified and filtered based on the following thresholds: q < 0.05, FC > 2, or FC < 0.5. q-values were calculated using the DEG algorithm.

qPCR

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, total mRNA was extracted from wound tissues using Trizol reagent, and subsequently reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing). qRT-PCR was conducted using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing) on a LightCycler® 96 Instrument (Roche, Switzerland). Each specimen was analyzed in triplicate. Upon completion of the PCR cycles, a melting curve assay was conducted to confirm the production of the expected PCR products. Primer sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech using the mRNA sequences obtained from the NCBI database (Table S2). The expression levels of mRNAs and miRs were normalized to GAPDH mRNA and U6 RNA expression levels, respectively. The 2-ΔΔCt method was employed for qPCR analyses.

Tissue immunofluorescence

Mouse skin flap tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, P1110). Following dehydration, the skin flaps were paraffin-embedded and sectioned into 5 μm slices from area II. The sections were deparaffinized using xylene. The dewaxed tissue was initially rehydrated and subsequently subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer. Sections were cooled to room temperature and blocked using 10% goat serum (Beyotime, C0265) in PBS (Procell, PB180327) containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Aladdin, T109027). The sections were then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature the following day. Cell nuclei were stained using DAPI. Primary antibodies were utilized to target α-SMA (1:500), CD31 (1:200), CD68 (1:6000), CD86 (1:500), CD206 (1:500), Ki67 (1:500), CK10 (1:400), COL I (1:1000), COL III (1:500), β3-tubulin (1:1000), EFNA3 (1:100), YAP (1:400), p-YAP (1:200), p-LATS1 (1:200), p-MST1 (1:200), NOX4 (1:500), and GPX4 (1:100). Secondary antibodies included Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (ab150073), Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647) (ab150075), Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488) (ab150105), and Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647) (ab150107). The TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (FITC) (Yesen, 40306ES20), Modified Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) Stain Kit (Solarbio, G1121), and Modified Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (Solarbio, G1346) were used for TUNEL, H&E, and Masson assays. Frozen skin sections were stained using DHE (Beyotime, S0063) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell immunofluorescence

HUVECs were incubated on sterile slides for 12 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, P1110) for 30 min, subsequently permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (Procell, PB180327) for 5 min, and blocked with 10% goat serum (Beyotime, C0265) in PBS for 30 min. Coverslips were incubated with the primary antibody in the blocking solution at 37 °C overnight, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for 1 h. Cell nuclei were stained using DAPI. Primary antibodies were utilized to target EFNA3 (1:100), YAP (1:400), p-YAP (1:200), p-LATS1 (1:200), and p-MST1 (1:200). Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (ab150073), Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647) (ab150075), Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488) (ab150105), and Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647) (ab150107) were used as secondary antibodies. The mitochondrial membrane potential in HUVECs was assessed using JC-1 (Beyotime Biotechnology, C2006), mitochondrial Ca2 + content was measured using Rhod-2 AM (MedChem Express, HY-D0989), and ROS content in HUVECs was evaluated using DCFH-DA (Beyotime Biotechnology, S0033M).

Luciferase reporter assay

The 3′-UTR of EFNA3 mRNA, containing wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) sequences, was synthesized via PCR from mouse tissue cDNA extracted in our laboratory. These sequences were cloned and inserted into the pmirGLO luciferase control reporter vector to produce EFNA3 3’-UTR reporter constructs (i.e., pmirGLO-EFNA3-WT and pmirGLO-EFNA3-MUT). 293T cells were transfected with miR-210-3p mimics (210-3p) or a negative control (210-3p-NC) at a density of 2 × 10^4 cells per well in 24-well culture plates, followed by co-transfection with 2 µg of pmirGLO-EFNA3-WT (EFNA3-WT) or pmirGLO-EFNA3-MUT (EFNA3-MUT). The promoters of miR-210, containing wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) sequences, were synthesized via PCR from mouse tissue cDNA extracted in our laboratory. These sequences were cloned and inserted into the pmirGLO luciferase control reporter vector to produce miR-210 promoter reporter constructs (i.e., pmirGLO-miR-210-promoter-WT and pmirGLO-miR-210-promoter-MUT). 293T cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-Hif1α or a negative control (pcDNA3.1-NC) at a density of 2 × 10^4 cells per well in 24-well culture plates, followed by co-transfection with 2 µg of pmirGLO-miR-210-promoter-WT (promoter-WT) and pmirGLO-miR-210-promoter-MUT (promoter-MUT). After 5 h, the transfection medium was removed and replaced with complete DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (LONSERA, A511-001). Two days post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime, RG027).

Tube formation assays

HUVECs were plated in 16-mm wells (4 × 10^4 cells per well) that were previously coated with reconstituted basement membrane (Cultrex, Trevigen), in the presence or absence of EGM-2 growth factors. After 8 h, the cells were fixed in 3% paraformaldehyde. To quantify capillary-like structures, internodal points formed in 14 fields per well (magnification 10X) were counted by a blinded reader. HUVECs were stained with a cell-permeable dye (calcein AM; Beyotime, C2012) for 30 min. ImageJ software was utilized to determine tube lengths in duplicate wells.

Scratch assays

A 100 µL pipette tip was used to make a scratch in the middle of overgrown HUVECs in a six-well plate, and the status of the scratch was observed and recorded under an inverted microscope. The HUVEC were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the cells that migrated to the scratch were observed with an inverted microscope. HUVEC were stained with a cell-permeable dye (calcein AM; Beyotime, C2012) for 30 min. Statistical analysis of the area of cell migration was conducted using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests, with paired tests used for matched samples. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. Experiments involving two independent factors were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Šídák’s multiple-comparisons test. Nonparametric tests were used when the data did not meet the assumptions of parametric analysis. P values for omics analyses were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
