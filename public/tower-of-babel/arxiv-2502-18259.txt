Generalization of terms via universal algebra
Tommaso Flaminio and Sara Ugolini

Abstract
. We provide a new foundational approach to the generalization of terms up to equational theories. We interpret generalization problems in a universal-algebraic setting making a key use of projective and exact algebras in the variety associated to the considered equational theory. We prove that the generality poset of a problem and its type (i.e., the cardinality of a complete set of least general solutions) can be studied in this algebraic setting. Moreover, we identify a class of varieties where the study of the generality poset can be fully reduced to the study of the congruence lattice of the 1-generated free algebra. We apply our results to varieties of algebras and to (algebraizable) logics. In particular we obtain several examples of unitary type: abelian groups; commutative monoids and commutative semigroups; all varieties whose 1-generated free algebra is trivial, e.g., lattices, semilattices, varieties without constants whose operations are idempotent; Boolean algebras, Kleene algebras, and Gödel algebras, which are the equivalent algebraic semantics of, respectively, classical, 3-valued Kleene, and Gödel-Dummett logic.



1.
Introduction

A term
s
is a
generalization
of a term
t
if
t
can be obtained from
s
through a variable substitution. The problem of identifying common generalizations for two or more terms has been the focus of substantial research, initiated in a series of papers by Plotkin
[
44
]
, Popplestone
[
45
]
, and Reynolds
[
49
]
, all collected in the same volume published in 1970. The objective of these initial papers was to formalize an abstraction of the process of
inductive reasoning
. As a terminological aside, this abstraction is named “inductive generalization” in
[
44
]
, whilst more recent literature simply speaks of “generalization processes” (see e.g.
[
16
]
). Here we conform to this latter usage.
The main aim in this context is to find the solutions, i.e., the generalizing terms, that are
as close as possible
to the initial terms that define the problem. The cardinality of this set of “best” solutions is encoded in what is called the
generalization type
.

The present paper provides a novel foundational approach to
equational
generalization, i.e., where terms are understood to be equivalent up to an equational theory. Our approach is based on universal algebra, which is a most natural environment to handle equational theories from the side of their classes of models, i.e.,
varieties
.
The extension to generalization up to equational theories has been considered by several authors in theoretical computer science
[
5
,
11
,
46
,
8
]
. Most relevant results so far have been obtained with ad hoc techniques (see, e.g., results on semirings
[
15
]
and idempotent operations
[
16
]
), and there is a growing interest in a general, foundational approach (see the recent survey
[
17
]
).

In the literature,
the collection of methods and techniques developed to compute solutions to a generalization problem often goes under the name of
anti-unification
. This terminology suggests a connection between generalization and the arguably better-known
unification
problems, where one seeks common instantiations to pairs of given terms.
Our approach is indeed inspired by Ghilardi’s algebraic setting for the study of equational unification problems
[
30
]
. This has been widely used in the literature since it allows the use of (universal) algebraic techniques
[
1
,
2
,
26
,
33
,
52
]
and duality theoretic methods employing Priestley-like
[
10
,
13
,
12
,
32
]
and geometrical
[
29
,
40
,
53
]
dualities.
As we shall show also in the present paper, an advantage given by the universal algebraic perspective is that it brings to light the
invariants
of both the problems and the solutions: translating a problem to the algebraic setting allows to identify which problems share the same solutions, and which solutions are essentially equivalent up to the considered equational theory.

In this manuscript we translate equational generalization problems (
e-generalization problems
from now on) and their solutions to homomorphisms between special classes of algebras, using in particular
projective
and
exact
algebras in the variety associated to the considered equational theory. Our first main result demonstrates how the generality type can be studied in the fully algebraic setting (Theorem
3.6
, Corollary
3.7
). A key role in our analysis is played by 1-generated algebras in the considered variety. While being 1-generated is not a categorical notion, we prove that our results are preserved under categorical equivalences that preserve free algebras, called just
equivalences
in
[
41
]
(Theorem
3.8
); moreover, categorical methods can and will be used to study e-generalization problems, an example of this being shown in the last section with the variety of Kleene algebras.

After developing the general theory, we take advantage of some basic universal-algebraic tools, and develop a methodology based on the study of the congruence lattice of the 1-generated free algebra in the considered variety; particularly, we identify a class of varieties where the study of the generality type can be fully reduced to the study of this congruence lattice (Theorem
4.19
). Finally, we identify a sufficient condition for problems in a variety to always have a best solution, i.e.,
unitary type
(Corollary
4.25
).

With our methods we are able to tackle a variety of examples, coming both from algebra and
algebraizable
logics
[
9
,
28
]
. Particularly, we show that problems in the following varieties have unitary type: abelian groups, commutative semigroups and monoids (Example
4.26
); all varieties whose 1-generated free algebra is trivial, e.g., lattices, semilattices, varieties without constants whose operations are idempotent (Example
4.22
); Boolean algebras, Kleene algebras, and Gödel algebras, which are the equivalent algebraic semantics of, respectively, classical, 3-valued Kleene, and Gödel-Dummett logic (Section
5
).

The paper is organized as follows: after a section of preliminaries, in Section
3
we develop the general theory of algebraic e-generalization problems; in Section
4
we study the e-generalization type via the congruence lattice of the 1-generated free algebra in the considered variety; lastly, in Section
5
we apply our results to algebraizable logics.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Universal algebraic preliminaries: projectivity and exactness

For all the unexplained notions of universal algebra we refer to
[
42
]
.
If
\rho
is a type of algebras, a
\rho
-equation
is a pair
(p,q)
of
\rho
-terms that we write suggestively as
p\approx q
. A set
\Sigma
of equations over the same type will be said to be an
equational theory
. In what follows we usually omit the type, and just consider equations assuming a fixed type and, whenever we consider a class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
, we assume that the algebras in
\mathsf{K}
have the same type.

We denote by
{\textbf{T}}_{\rho}(X)
the absolutely free algebra of
\rho
-terms over a set
X
of generators, and by
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{K}}(X)
the free algebra for a class
\mathsf{K}
over
X
. If
X=\{x\}
, we will often write
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{K}}(x)
for
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{K}}(\{x\})
.

Given any set of variables
X
, we call
assignment
of
X
into an algebra
A
over the same type a function
h
mapping each variable
x\in X
to an element of
A
, extending (uniquely) to a homomorphism, which we also call
h
, from
{\textbf{T}}_{\rho}(X)
to
A
.
An algebra
A
satisfies an equation
p\approx q
with an assignment
h
, and we write
{\textbf{A}},h\vDash p\approx q
, if
h(p)=h(q)
in
A
;
A
is a
model
of
p\approx q
, and we write
{\textbf{A}}\vDash p\approx q
, if for all assignments
h
in
A
,
{\textbf{A}},h\vDash p\approx q
. If
\Sigma
is an equational theory then
A
is said to be a
model
of
\Sigma
, and we write
{\textbf{A}}\vDash\Sigma
, if
{\textbf{A}}\vDash\sigma
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
. Finally, a class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
is a
class of models
of
\Sigma
, and we write
\mathsf{K}\models\Sigma
, if all algebras in
\mathsf{K}
are a model of
\Sigma
.

A class of algebras is a
variety
if it is the class of all models of an equational theory. Equivalently, varieties are those classes of algebras closed under the algebraic operations of taking homomorphic images, subalgebras, and direct products. As a consequence, varieties contain all of their free algebras.

Fixed a variety
\mathsf{V}
, by a
substitution

\sigma
we mean an endomorphism of
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(\omega)
, the free algebra in
\mathsf{V}
over a denumerable set of variables. Note that a substitution is fully determined by the values it assigns to each generating variable. Usually we consider substitutions that are different from the identity map only on a subset of variables
X
, mapping them to terms written over variables over a set
Y
; we highlight this fact by considering such a substitution as a homomorphism from
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
to
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(Y)
.

We now introduce projective algebras, which play a key role in our investigation.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
, an algebra
{\textbf{P}}\in\mathsf{K}
is
projective in
\mathsf{K}
if for all
{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{K}
, homomorphism
h:{\textbf{P}}\to{\textbf{B}}
, and surjective homomorphism
g:{\textbf{A}}\to{\textbf{B}}
, there is a homomorphism
f:{\textbf{P}}\to{\textbf{A}}
such that
h=g\circ f
.

While in general it is difficult to determine the algebras that are projective in a class
\mathsf{K}
, whenever
\mathsf{K}
contains all of its free algebras (hence in particular for
\mathsf{K}
being a variety) projectivity admits a more intuitive characterization. Let us call an algebra
A
a
retract
of an algebra
F
if there are homomorphisms
i:{\textbf{A}}\to{\textbf{F}},j:{\textbf{F}}\to{\textbf{A}}
such that
j\circ i=id_{{\textbf{A}}}
(and then necessarily
i
is injective and
j
is surjective); the following theorem was first proved by Whitman for lattices
[
54
]
, but it is well-known to hold in general.

Theorem 2.2
.

If a class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
contains all its free algebras, an algebra
P
is projective in
\mathsf{K}
if and only if it is a retract of a free algebra in
\mathsf{K}
.

Thus, free algebras in a class
\mathsf{K}
are clearly projective in
\mathsf{K}
.
Since the above characterization of projective algebras in a variety will be extensively used along the paper, it is convenient to graphically represent it as in the following diagram:

P
\scriptstyle{j\circ i={\rm id}_{{\textbf{P}}}}
\scriptstyle{i}
\textstyle{{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(Y)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{j}

Given the above representation, and to highlight the role of the two mappings
i
and
j
that determine the retraction, we will henceforth say that the projective algebra
P
is an
(i,j)
-retract of
{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(Y)
.

We now introduce
exactness
, a weaker notion than projectivity relevant to this work.

Definition 2.3
.

Consider a variety
\mathsf{V}
; an algebra
{\textbf{E}}\in\mathsf{V}
is called
exact in
\mathsf{V}
if it is isomorphic to a finitely generated subalgebra of some (finitely generated) free algebra.

Evocatively, if we consider an exact algebra that is (isomorphic to) a subalgebra of a free algebra
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
generated by a term
t
, we write it as
{\textbf{E}}_{{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)}(t)
; whenever there is no danger of confusion, we write simply
{\textbf{E}}(t)
.

Similarly to above, to highlight the role of an embedding
e
that maps an exact algebra
E
into a free algebra
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
, we will henceforth say that
{\bf E}
is an
e
-subalgebra of
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
.

Let us further observe that another way of thinking about exact algebras is to see them as images of substitutions from a finitely generated free algebra to another (and in fact,
exact
formulas are those that represent the theory of a substitution as in
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
).
By the characterization in Theorem
2.2
, every finitely generated projective algebra is exact, while the converse is not true in general.

Projective and exact algebras in varieties, due to their close relation with free algebras, have shown to serve a key role in the algebraic study of problems coming from logic, particularly, in unification problems and the related notion of structural completeness (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
14
,
30
]
). This work shows their importance in the study of e-generalization problems as well.

In order to study the above notions, we often make use of congruences and the associated quotients. Generally, given an algebra
A
, we write
\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
for its set of congruences. Given a set
\Phi\subseteq\mathscr{P}(A\times A)
of pairs of elements of
A
, one can consider the congruence generated by
\Phi
in
A
,
{\rm Con}_{{\textbf{A}}}(\Phi)
; if
\Phi=\{(x,y)\}
, and if there is no danger for confusion, we simply write
\langle x,y\rangle
for
{\rm Con}_{{\textbf{A}}}(\{(x,y)\})
. Given any
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}})
, we write
n_{\theta}
for the
natural epimorphism

n_{\theta}:{\textbf{A}}\to{\textbf{A}}/\theta
mapping each
a\in A
to its congruence class
a/\theta
.
Moreover, given any homomorphism
h:{\textbf{A}}\to{\textbf{B}}
one can consider the congruence given by its
kernel
:

\ker(h)=\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A\times A:h(a)=h(a^{\prime})\}\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{A}}).

We recall that varieties are closed under taking quotients (equivalently, homomorphic images) and every algebra
A
in a variety
\mathsf{V}
is (isomorphic to) a quotient of some free algebra, say
{\textbf{A}}\cong{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)/\theta
for some set of variables
X
and
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{{\bf F}}}_{\mathsf{V}}(X))
. We can then transfer the notions above from the algebras to the corresponding congruences.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a variety. We say that a congruence
\theta
of a free algebra
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
is
projective
(or
exact
) if
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)/\theta
is projective (or exact) in
\mathsf{V}
.

The relation between congruences, homomorphic images, and quotients of algebras is a basic tool from universal algebra; the main facts are expressed in the so-called
Isomorphism Theorems

[
42
, §4.2]
. We recall the First Isomorphism Theorem (often called just Homomorphism Theorem) and Second Isomorphism Theorem here for the reader’s convenience, since they will be widely used in proofs.

Theorem 2.5
.

(1)

(First Isomorphism Theorem) Let
h:{\textbf{A}}\to{\textbf{B}}
be a homomorphism, and consider the natural epimorphism
n_{\theta}
from
A
to
{\textbf{A}}/\theta
; the unique function
f:{\textbf{A}}/\theta\to{\textbf{B}}
such that
f\circ n_{\theta}=h
is an embedding. Moreover,
f
is an isomorphism if and only if
h
is onto
B
.

(2)

(Second Isomorphism Theorem) Let
f:{\textbf{A}}\to{\textbf{B}}
and
g:{\textbf{A}}\to{\textbf{C}}
be homomorphisms such that
\ker(f)\subseteq\ker(g)
and
f
is onto
B
. Then there is a unique homomorphism
h:{\textbf{B}}\to{\textbf{C}}
such that
g=h\circ f
.
Moreover,
h
is an embedding if and only if
\ker(f)=\ker(g)
.

As a consequence of the Second Isomorphism Theorem one can actually see that given any variety
\mathsf{V}
, any assignment
h:{\textbf{T}}_{\rho}(X)\to{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{V}
induces a homomorphism
\bar{h}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)\to{\textbf{A}}
; precisely, if
\bar{x}
is the representative in
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
of a variable
x\in X
,
\bar{h}(\bar{x})=h(x)
. Therefore, the validity of equations in a variety can be equivalently checked by considering assignments with domain over a free algebra
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
for some
X
(instead of the absolutely free algebra
{\textbf{T}}_{\rho}(X)
) to algebras in
\mathsf{V}
. We will use this fact without further notice, and just consider terms as elements of free algebras in the considered variety. Lastly, given a term
t
, we write
{\rm Var}(t)
for the variables appearing in
t
.

2.2.
E-generalization problems

Observe that given any equational theory
\Sigma
, we can consider the associated class of models
\mathsf{V}_{\Sigma}
, which is a variety of algebras. Similarly, as recalled in the previous subsection, any variety
\mathsf{V}
is exactly the class of models of some equational theory
\Sigma_{\mathsf{V}}
:
\Sigma=\Sigma_{\mathsf{V}_{\Sigma}}
and
\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}_{\Sigma_{\mathsf{V}}}
. Moreover, given two terms
t,u
over the same type,
t=u
up to
\Sigma
if and only if
\mathsf{V}\models t\approx u
. Therefore one can use equational theories and varieties interchangeably; we will henceforth focus on varieties with no loss of generality.

The following definition of an e-generalization problem corresponds to the usual one used in the literature, rephrased in the (equivalent) context of varieties and their free algebras; we only observe that while in the literature e-generalization problems are often considered to be a pair of terms, we here consider the more general case of allowing a finite multiset of terms of any (finite) cardinality. The choice of considering
multi
sets of terms is to allow the terms to be repeated up to the considered equational theory.

Definition 2.6
.

A
symbolic e-generalization problem
for a variety
\mathsf{V}
is a finite multiset
{\bf t}
of terms
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\in{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
for some finite set of variables
X
. A
solution
(or
generalizer
) is a term
s\in{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(Y)
, with
Y={\rm Var}(s)
, for which there exist substitutions
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}
such that
\mathsf{V}\models\sigma_{k}(s)\approx t_{k}
for all
k=1,\ldots,m
. In this case we say that
s
is
witnessed
(or
testified
) by
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}
.

As already noticed by Plotkin for syntactic generalization
[
44
]
, any symbolic generalization problem
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
always has a solution:
a fresh variable
z
, testified by the obvious substitutions
\sigma_{k}(z)=t_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,m
.
This is the
most general solution
for
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
, in the sense that every other solution can be obtained from it by further substitution.
In this context the interesting solutions are the
least general
ones, that are as
close
as possible to the initial terms representing the problem. Let us make this precise.

Consider two terms over the same language
s
and
u
; we say that
s
is
less general
than
u
, and write

(1)

s\preceq u\mbox{, iff there exists a substitution }\sigma\mbox{ such that }\sigma(u)=s.

Let us then fix a problem
{\bf t}\subseteq{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
and let
\mathscr{S}({\bf t})
be the set of its solutions. It is easy to see that
\preceq
is a preorder on
\mathscr{S}({\bf t})
. With a slight abuse of notation we denote by

(\mathscr{S}({\bf t}),\preceq)
its associated poset of equally general solutions, that we call the
generality poset
of
{\bf t}
.

For a problem
{\bf t}
, one is usually interested in determining a set of minimal solutions in its generality poset, whether it exists, and its cardinality. This information is encoded in the
e-generalization type
, which intuitively gives the cardinality of a class of best (i.e., minimal) solutions. Let us be more precise.

Given a poset
(P,\leq)
, we call a
minimal (or maximal) complete set
a subset
M\subseteq P
such that:

(1)

for every
p\in P
, there is an element of
a\in M
such that
a\leq p
(or
p\leq a
);

(2)

no two distinct elements of
M
are comparable with respect to the order.

Given a symbolic e-generalization problem
{\bf t}
, its
symbolic e-generalization type
is:

•

unitary: if
(\mathscr{S}({\bf t}),\preceq)
has a minimal complete set of generalizers (or
mcsg
) of cardinality 1;

•

finitary: if
(\mathscr{S}({\bf t}),\preceq)
has an mcsg of finite (greater than
1
) cardinality;

•

infinitary: if
(\mathscr{S}({\bf t}),\preceq)
has an mcsg of infinite (not finite) cardinality;

•

nullary: if
(\mathscr{S}({\bf t}),\preceq)
has no mcsg.

Given a variety
\mathsf{V}
, its symbolic e-generalization type is the worst possible type occurring among all its e-generalization problems, the best-to-worst order being: unitary
>
finitary
>
infinitary
>
nullary.

In order to recover some of the results present in the literature, we also introduce the notion of
e-generalization
m
-type
for
\mathsf{V}
, for
m\in\mathbb{N}-\{0\}
, which is the worst possible type occurring among all the e-generalization problems for
\mathsf{V}
where the cardinality of the multiset of terms is at most
m
. To conform to this notation, we will also call the e-generalization type of
\mathsf{V}
the
e-generalization
\omega
-type of
\mathsf{V}
.

Notice that if
m\leq n
, the e-generalization
m
-type of
\mathsf{V}
is better or equal to the
n
-type of
\mathsf{V}
. Moreover:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a variety. If
\mathsf{V}
has unitary e-generalization
2
-type, then
\mathsf{V}
has unitary e-generalization type.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathsf{V}
has unitary e-generalization
2
-type, and consider a problem
{\bf t}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}
. If
m=1
, it is clear that the e-generalization type of
{\bf t}
is unitary, since we can consider the completely equivalent problem
\{t_{1},t_{1}\}
. Let now
m>2
. We can find a least general solution to
{\bf t}
with the following procedure:

(1)

Partition the terms in
{\bf t}
into the 2-element sets

{\bf t}_{1,2}:=\{t_{1},t_{2}\},\ldots,{\bf t}_{m-1,m}:=\{t_{m-1},t_{m}\}

if
m
is even, or, if
m
is odd, consider the last multiset to be
{\bf t}_{m,m}:=\{t_{m},t_{m}\}
. Let
s_{1},\ldots,s_{\lceil m/2\rceil}
(where
\lceil n\rceil
is the least integer greater than or equal to
n
) be the set of least general solutions to the above sets of problems, which exist since
\mathsf{V}
has unitary e-generalization
2
-type.

(2)

Repeat the above process on the set of solutions, until there is only one solution, call it
s
.

Note that the procedure in point (1) is done
\lceil m/2\rceil
times. While it is clear that
s
is a solution for
{\bf t}
, we argue that
s
is minimal, i.e., it is a least general solution for
{\bf t}
. Indeed, consider another solution
s^{\prime}
. Then
s^{\prime}
is also a solution for all the pairs in the first step of the procedure,
{\bf t}_{1,2},\ldots,{\bf t}_{m-1,m}
(or
{\bf t}_{m,m}
). Thus
s_{1},\ldots,s_{\lceil m/2\rceil}
are all less general than
s^{\prime}
, i.e., there are substitutions
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\lceil m/2\rceil}
such that
\sigma_{k}(s^{\prime})=s_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,\lceil m/2\rceil
. This means that
s^{\prime}
is also a solution for all the pairs considered in the second iteration of the procedure. Applying the same reasoning at each repetition, we finally get that
s^{\prime}
is a solution also to the last pair of terms considered, whose least general generalizer is
s
. Thus,
s\preceq s^{\prime}
and the proof is complete.
∎

Note that the same reasoning cannot be applied if
\mathsf{V}
has, for instance, finitary e-generalization
2
-type to conclude that it has finitary e-generalization type, since the procedure described in the proof above might not terminate.
We leave the question whether the e-generalization type coincides with the 2-type for every variety open.

One last observation before we conclude the preliminary section.

Remark 2.8
.

For the sake of the reader more familiar with unification problems, it is worth stressing that both the solutions to e-generalization problems and their order have a different behavior than those of unification theory. Indeed, the solutions to unification problem are
substitutions
that unify (possibly up to some equational theory) all the pairs of terms considered in the problem. Here solutions are identified as terms, while substitutions only serve as witnesses. Importantly, note that one could have two different solutions
s,s^{\prime}
to some e-generalization problem
{\bf t}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}
, witnessed by the same substitutions
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}
, i.e.,
\sigma_{k}(s)=\sigma_{k}(s^{\prime})=t_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,m
, but
s
and
s^{\prime}
could be incomparable in the generality order. Indeed, there might be no substitution mapping
s
to
s^{\prime}
or vice versa. This observation is crucial to gain a deeper understanding of the algebraic approach we put forward in this work.

3.
An algebraic presentation of e-generalization

In this section we present a novel universal-algebraic approach to e-generalization problems.
The starting intuition is that, given a symbolic problem
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}
, each term defines a 1-generated subalgebra of some free algebra, i.e., a 1-generated exact algebra
{\textbf{E}}(t_{k})
. As we hinted at in Remark
2.8
, it is necessary for the study of the generalization order to fix the specific term
t_{k}
, for each
k=1,\ldots,m
, and not just the exact algebra it generates (which could have different generators).
The key idea that makes our translation works is to see the terms
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}
as a
single element

(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})
of the direct product of the exact algebras
{\textbf{E}}(t_{k})
; it will be convenient to represent this tuple as the image of a fresh variable
z
via some homomorphism
h
on the 1-generated algebra in the associated variety,
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)
. Then, each term
t_{k}
can be recovered as
p_{k}\circ h(z)
, where

Definition 3.2

Theorem 3.6
.

The following hold:

(1)

Given a symbolic problem

is isomorphic to the poset of algebraic solutions of
\mathsf{Alg}({\bf t})
,
(\mathscr{A}(\mathsf{Alg}({\bf t})),\sqsubseteq)
.

(2)

Given an algebraic problem

, and any choice of embeddings

Proof.

As for (1), let
{\bf t}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}\subseteq{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
. We first observe that given two symbolic solutions
s\in{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(Y),s^{\prime}\in{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(Y^{\prime})

Indeed,
s\preceq s^{\prime}
if and only if there exist

\sigma

\mathsf{Alg}(s)\sqsubseteq\mathsf{Alg}(s^{\prime})
.
From here it immediately follows that
\mathsf{Alg}
can be regarded as a (well-defined) map from

to
(\mathscr{A}(\mathsf{Alg}({\bf t})),\sqsubseteq)

We will now demonstrate that
\mathsf{Alg}
is surjective. Consider an algebraic solution
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}\in\mathscr{A}(\mathsf{Alg}({\bf t}))

is projective, there is a free algebra

and homomorphisms

such that

. Consider then

is a solution for the symbolic problem, witnessed by the substitutions
\sigma_{k}=p_{k}\circ f\circ j
for

is the

-th projection. Indeed,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\sigma_{k}(s)&=&\sigma_{k}\circ i\circ g(z)\\
&=&p_{k}\circ f\circ j\circ i\circ g(z)\\
&=&p_{k}\circ f\circ g(z)\\
&=&p_{k}(\mathsf{Alg}({\bf t})(z))\\
&=&p_{k}(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\\
&=&t_{k}\end{array}

and in the third equality we use that
j\circ i=id_{{\textbf{P}}}
. Thus
s=i\circ g(z)\in\mathscr{S}({\bf t})
. Finally, note that
\mathsf{Alg}(s):{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(Z)
is equally general to

, as testified by the homomorphisms

which are such that:
\mathsf{Alg}(s)=i\circ g
(by the definition of

) and

. Thus
\mathsf{Alg}
is surjective and the first claim is proved.

As for (2), consider an algebraic e-generalization problem
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
, where
{\textbf{E}}_{k}
is an
e_{k}
-subalgebra of
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(X_{k})

\mathsf{Alg}\circ\mathsf{Sym}(h):{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}(t_{k})
is the homomorphism defined by mapping

to

for

. Notice that the embeddings

can be seen as isomorphisms onto their image; to avoid confusion, let us denote these isomorphisms as

, mapping

l_{k}^{-1}:{\textbf{E}}(t_{k})\to{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be their inverses, mapping

p_{k}\circ h(z)
, for
k=1,\ldots,m

\displaystyle l:=(l_{1}\circ p_{1},\ldots,l_{m}\circ p_{m})\;

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\;\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}(t_{k}),

\displaystyle l^{-1}:=(l_{1}^{-1}\circ p_{1},\ldots,l_{m}^{-1}\circ p_{m})\;

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\;\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}(t_{k})\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}

define inverse isomorphisms, and are such that
l\circ h=\mathsf{Alg}\circ\mathsf{Sym}(h)
and
l^{-1}\circ\mathsf{Alg}\circ\mathsf{Sym}(h)=h
.

Indeed, note that

l\circ h(z)=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})=\mathsf{Alg}\circ\mathsf{Sym}(h)(z)

and

l^{-1}\circ\mathsf{Alg}\circ\mathsf{Sym}(h)(z)=l^{-1}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})=h(z).

It follows that any
g:{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
that is a solution to

is also a solution to
\mathsf{Alg}\circ\mathsf{Sym}(h)
. Indeed, if

, and hence it is is such that

and hence

Therefore,

Therefore, the poset of all solutions for a symbolic problem, and in particular the existence of a least general solution, can be studied algebraically.
We can then derive that the same holds also for the
e-generalization type
of a variety
\mathsf{V}
, which is the worst occurring type for its e-generalization problems.

Corollary 3.7
.

Given a variety
\mathsf{V}
, its symbolic and algebraic e-generalization
\kappa
-types coincide, for any cardinal
\kappa\leq\omega
.

Proof.

The above Theorem
3.6
demonstrates that given a symbolic e-generalization problem for
\mathsf{V}
, there is an algebraic one with the same type and vice versa, which implies the claim.
∎

The reader familiar with the algebraic study of unification problems developed by Ghilardi in
[
30
]
will appreciate that our results have a similar flavor, but also some significant differences. Importantly, the study of unification problems and their type only involves homomorphisms between finitely presented and projective algebras in a variety; since these are all
categorical
notions, it directly follows that the unification type is preserved under categorical equivalence. With the approach outlined above, we do not get this same result, since we make a key use of 1-generated (free) algebras, and being 1-generated is not generally preserved by categorical equivalences.
Nonetheless, we prove that the e-generalization type is preserved under a stronger notion of categorical equivalence, called
equivalence
in
[
41
]
, which is quite common among algebraic categories. Let us be more precise. For the unexplained notions of category theory we refer the reader to
[
39
]
.

Given any class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
, one can consider the associated algebraic category, denoted by the same symbol, whose objects are the algebras in
\mathsf{K}
and whose morphisms are the homomorphisms.
Two algebraic categories
\mathsf{K}
and
\mathsf{L}
are
equivalent
if they are categorically equivalent via a functor
\Gamma
that preserves free algebras, in the sense that
\Gamma({\bf F}_{\mathsf{K}}(X))={\bf F}_{\mathsf{L}}(X)
for all
X
.
If one considers in particular two varieties
\mathsf{V}
and
\mathsf{W}
, these are equivalent if and only if their algebraic theories are isomorphic (from a category theoretical perspective), or if their clones are isomorphic (from a universal algebraic perspective), see
[
41
]
for a discussion.

All the notions involved in the definition of an algebraic e-generalization problem, its solutions, and the generality order, are preserved by equivalence; therefore we can conclude this section with the following result.

Theorem 3.8
.

The e-generalization
\kappa
-type of a variety is preserved by equivalence, for any cardinal
\kappa\leq\omega
.

4.
A congruence-based approach to the poset of solutions

In this section we exploit the insight given by the algebraic representation of e-generalization problems and develop a study of the generality poset of solutions guided by the congruence lattice of the 1-generated free algebra in the considered variety.

We start by characterizing the kernels of e-generalization problems and their solutions, and using them to study the poset of solutions.
Then, we identify a class of varieties, later named
1ESP varieties
, where the study of the generality poset of solutions completely reduces to the study of projective congruences of the 1-generated free algebra.
Finally, we develop an algebraic analysis of the generalization type, deriving some general results and a sufficient condition for a variety to have unitary type, i.e., finite direct products of 1-generated exact algebras being projective.

In particular, we will demonstrate that the following varieties have unitary e-generalization type: abelian groups, commutative semigroups and monoids, and all varieties whose 1-generated free algebra is trivial (e.g., lattices, semilattices, varieties without constants whose operations are idempotent).

4.1.
E-generalization problems via congruences

Fix a variety
\mathsf{V}
and an algebraic e-generalization problem
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
; its kernel
\ker(h)
is a congruence in the 1-generated free algebra
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)
. The following lemma comes from a first easy observation, namely that the kernel of the problem gives an upper bound to the kernels of the solutions in the congruence lattice of
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be an algebraic e-generalization problem for a variety
\mathsf{V}
. If
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
is a solution, then
\ker(g)\subseteq\ker(h)
.

Proof.

The claim simply follows from the fact that if
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
is a solution, there is a testifying homomorphism
f:{\textbf{P}}\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
such that
f\circ g=h
.
∎

We are now going to characterize which congruences of the 1-generated free algebra can appear as
\ker(h)
for an e-generalization problem given by a homomorphism
h
, but first we need a technical lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be any variety. Let
h:{\textbf{A}}\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{B}}_{k}
be a homomorphism, and consider
h_{k}=p_{k}\circ h
for
k=1,\ldots,m
, where
p_{k}
is the
k
-th projection. Then
\ker(h)=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\ker(h_{k})
.

Proof.

Since
h_{k}=p_{k}\circ h
, it directly follows that
\ker(h)\subseteq\ker(h_{k})
for all
k=1,\ldots,m
, and then
\ker(h)\subseteq\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\ker(h_{k})
. For the converse, let
a,a^{\prime}\in A
and assume that
(a,a^{\prime})\notin\ker(h)
, i.e.,
h(a)\neq h(a^{\prime})
. This implies that
h(a)
and
h(a^{\prime})
differ in some factor, that is to say,
p_{k}\circ h(a)=h_{k}(a)\neq h_{k}(a^{\prime})=p_{k}\circ h(a^{\prime})
for some
k\leq m
. Therefore
(a,a^{\prime})\notin\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\ker(h_{k})
, which shows that
\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\ker(h_{k})\subseteq\ker(h)
and concludes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to characterize which congruences can appear as kernels of algebraic e-generalization problems; let us make this notion precise for future reference.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a variety.
We call a congruence
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{{\bf F}}}_{\mathsf{V}}(z))
an
E-congruence
if
\theta=\ker(h)
for some e-generalization problem
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
.

As a consequence of Lemma
4.2
above, we obtain the following.

Theorem 4.4
.

For every
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{{\bf F}}}_{\mathsf{V}}(z))
,
\theta
is an E-congruence if and only if there are exact congruences
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{{\bf F}}}_{\mathsf{V}}(z))
such that
\theta=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\theta_{m}
.

Proof.

Let
\theta
be an E-congruence and let
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be an algebraic e-generalization problem such that
\theta=\ker(h)
. By Lemma
4.2
,
\ker(h)=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\ker(h_{k})
where
h_{k}=p_{k}\circ h
for all
k=1,\ldots,k
. Since
h_{k}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{E}}_{k}
is onto, by the First Isomorphism Theorem we get that
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\ker(h_{k})
is isomorphic to the exact algebra
{\textbf{E}}_{k}
, hence exact itself, and then
\ker(h_{k})
is exact by definition. Thus, the claim follows by taking
\theta_{k}=\ker(h_{k})
for all
k=1,\ldots,m
.

Conversely, if
\theta=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\theta_{k}
for
\theta_{k}
exact congruences, by definition of exact congruence there are embeddings
e_{k}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta_{k}\to{\textbf{F}}_{\mathsf{V}}(X)
for some
X
(
X
can be taken to be the same for all
k
). Thus, letting
n_{k}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta_{k}
be the natural epimorphism, one can define an e-generalization problem
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta_{k}
as
h(x)=(n_{1}(x),\ldots,n_{m}(x))
, since clearly each
p_{k}\circ h
is onto
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta_{k}
. Moreover:

\theta=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\theta_{k}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\ker(n_{k})=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\ker(p_{k}\circ h)=\ker(h),

using Lemma
4.2
in the last identity. Therefore,
\theta
is an E-congruence.
∎

In other words, a congruence
\theta
of the 1-generated free algebra is the kernel of an algebraic e-generalization problem if and only if it is a finite intersection of exact congruences.

4.2.
Algebraic solutions via congruences

Now we turn to describing those congruences that are kernels of solutions of an algebraic problem.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be an algebraic e-generalization problem for a variety
\mathsf{V}
. We say that
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{{\bf F}}}_{\mathsf{V}}(z))
is a
generalizing congruence for
h
(or
G-congruence for
h
for short) if
\theta=\ker(g)
for some solution
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
. Moreover, we denote the set of G-congruences for
h
by
\mathscr{G}(h)
.

Note that
\mathscr{G}(h)
is the image under the function
\ker
of the set
\mathscr{A}(h)
, given by all generalizers for the e-generalization problem
h
:

\mathscr{G}(h)=\ker[\mathscr{A}(h)].

Next theorem presents, respectively, necessary and sufficient conditions for a congruence to be a G-congruence of an e-generalization problem.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be an algebraic e-generalization problem for a variety
\mathsf{V}
and let
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{{\bf F}}}_{\mathsf{V}}(z))
. The following hold:

(1)

If
\theta\in\mathscr{G}(h)
, then
\theta
is exact and
\theta\subseteq\ker(h)
.

(2)

If
\theta
is projective and
\theta\subseteq\ker(h)
, then
\theta\in\mathscr{G}(h)
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that
\theta
is a G-congruence for
h
. Thus, by definition,
\theta=\ker(g)
for some solution
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
, and then by Lemma
4.1
,
\theta=\ker(g)\subseteq\ker(h)
. Moreover,
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\ker(g)
embeds into
P
by direct application of the First Isomorphism Theorem. In turn,
P
embeds into some free algebra
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(Y)
. Therefore,
\ker(g)
is exact.

(2) Suppose
\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{{\bf F}}}_{\mathsf{V}}(z))
is projective and
\theta\subseteq\ker(h)
. Then,
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta
is projective and by the Second Isomorphism Theorem (Theorem
2.5
)
there is
f:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
such that
f\circ n_{\theta}=h
. Hence
f
testifies that
n_{\theta}
is a solution for
h
, and therefore
\theta=\ker(n_{\theta})
is a G-congruence for
h
.
∎

Of particular interest are then those varieties where the necessary and sufficient conditions in Theorem
4.6
collapse. This happens in particular whenever every 1-generated exact algebra is projective; in this case we get the following characterization of G-congruences.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a variety such that every 1-generated exact algebra is projective. Then given any e-generalization problem
h
,
\mathscr{G}(h)=\{\theta\in\operatorname{Con}({\textbf{{\bf F}}}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)):\theta\subseteq\ker(h),\theta\mbox{ projective in }\mathsf{V}\}
.

Let us call the above property, namely that all 1-generated exact algebras are projective,
1EP property
, and the varieties that satisfy it
1EP varieties
. 1EP varieties seem to abound.
They include all varieties where the 1-generated free algebra is trivial (e.g., lattices and semilattices), and moreover:

Example 4.8
.

Groups, abelian groups, monoids, semigroups, and their commutative subvarieties are easily seen to be 1EP varieties.

Let us first consider the varieties of groups and abelian groups. The 1-generated free algebra is isomorphic to the additive group of the integers,
{\textbf{Z}}=(\mathbb{Z},+,-,0)
in both cases. Its quotients are:
Z
itself; the trivial group
0
; for every
n
, the finite cyclic groups
{\textbf{Z}}_{n}
of each order
n
. While clearly
Z
and
0
are subalgebras of the 1-generated free algebra
Z
, and hence they are exact, no finite nontrivial group can be a subalgebra of a free group. Therefore, the exact quotients of
Z
are itself and
0
, which are both projective since they are free (
0
is the 0-generated free (abelian) group). Thus both groups and abelian groups are 1EP.

Now, the 1-generated free algebra for (commutative) monoids is the additive monoid of the natural numbers
{\textbf{N}}=(\mathbb{N},+,0)
, and the free (commutative) semigroup is the additive semigroup of positive natural numbers
{\textbf{N}}^{+}=(\mathbb{N}-\{0\},+)
.
Therefore, the situation is similar to the above: the 1-generated exact algebras in monoids and commutative monoids are the trivial algebra and the free algebra itself, which are both free hence projective; all other quotients are finite nontrivial algebras that cannot embed in a free monoid. For semigroups and their commutative subvariety, the trivial algebra is not exact, so the only exact (and projective) 1-generated algebra is the free algebra. Therefore, all these varieties are 1EP.

\Box

Moreover, many varieties coming from logic are 1EP; for instance, Heyting algebras (since all exact Heyting algebras are projective,
[
31

), and as we shall see in the last section, Boolean, Gödel, and Kleene algebras. However, one can construct examples of varieties without the 1EP property; the following is due to Tommaso Moraschini (private communication).

In 1ESP varieties we can characterize solutions by means of their kernels.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a 1ESP variety. Consider an e-generalization problem
h

, with

\mathsf{V}

if and only if
\ker(g)\in\mathscr{G}(h)
.

Proof.

If
g
is a solution then
\ker(g)
is in
\mathscr{G}(h)
by definition. Conversely, assume
\ker(g)\in\mathscr{G}(h)
, and let us decompose
g:=e\circ n_{\ker(g)}
as in the above Figure

\ker(g)
is projective and below
\ker(h)
. By the Second Isomorphism Theorem, we get that there exists a homomorphism
f^{\prime}
such that
f^{\prime}\circ n_{\ker(g)}=h
. Since
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\ker(g)
is projective (and exact), and embeds via

into the projective algebra

such that

is the identity map. Hence,

testifies that

f\circ g=f^{\prime}\circ j\circ e\circ n_{\ker(g)}=f^{\prime}\circ n_{\ker(g)}=h.

This concludes the proof.
∎

As a consequence we get the following invariance result for e-generalization problems in 1ESP varieties.

Corollary 4.13
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be 1ESP. Two algebraic e-generalization problems
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
and
h^{\prime}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{r}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
have the same solutions if and only if
\ker(h)=\ker(h^{\prime})
.

Let us highlight some examples of 1ESP varieties.

Example 4.14
.

Every variety
\mathsf{V}
whose 1-generated free algebra is trivial has the 1ESP property. To see this, notice that
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)
, whose domain is

, is the only 1-generated exact algebra in
\mathsf{V}
and it is strongly projective. Indeed, let
A
be any algebra in
\mathsf{V}
and let
i:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{A}}
be an embedding. The trivial homomorphism

is such that
j\circ i=id_{{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)}
, and then
\mathsf{V}
is 1ESP.

This reasoning covers, for instance, the cases of: lattices, semilattices, and more generally all varieties without constants and whose operations are idempotent (in the sense that, given an

,
f(x,\ldots,x)=x
).

.

Varieties where 1-generated exact algebras are

\mathsf{V}

4.3.
A universal-algebraic methodology for the e-generalization type

Let
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be an algebraic e-generalization problem for a variety
\mathsf{V}
. Given the results in the previous subsections, we shall develop a methodology to study the poset of solutions
(\mathscr{A}(h),\sqsubseteq)
via the set
\mathscr{G}(h)
.

To this end, we first observe that one can partition the algebraic solutions by means of their kernels. For every G-congruence
\theta\in\mathscr{G}(h)
, consider the following set:

\Pi_{\theta}(h)=\{g\in\mathscr{A}(h)\mid\ker(g)=\theta\}.

The set
\{\Pi_{\theta}(h):\theta\in\mathscr{G}(h)\}
is a partition of
\mathscr{A}(h)
.
Moreover, the inclusion order among the G-congruences of an algebraic e-generalization problem
h
gives some insight on the generality order among solutions belonging to different classes of the partition, as the following result shows.

Proposition 4.16
.

Let
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be an algebraic e-generalization problem for a variety
\mathsf{V}
. Given two solutions
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
and
g^{\prime}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}^{\prime}
in
(\mathscr{A}(h),\sqsubseteq)
, the following conditions hold:

(1)

if
g^{\prime}\sqsubseteq g
, then
\ker(g)\subseteq\ker(g^{\prime})
;

(2)

if
\ker(g)\subseteq\ker(g^{\prime})
, then
g^{\prime}\sqsubseteq n_{\ker(g)}
.

Proof.

(1) By definition of the order in the poset of algebraic solutions, if
g^{\prime}\sqsubseteq g
there exists
f:{\textbf{P}}\to{\textbf{P}}^{\prime}
such that
f\circ g=g^{\prime}
. Hence,
\ker(g)\subseteq\ker(g^{\prime})
.

(2) Consider the natural epimorphism
n_{\ker(g)}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\ker(g)
; since
\ker(g)\subseteq\ker(g^{\prime})
, the Second Isomorphism Theorem yields directly that there exists a homomorphism
f:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\ker(g)\to{\textbf{P}}^{\prime}
such that
f\circ n_{\ker(g)}=g^{\prime}
. Thus,
g^{\prime}\sqsubseteq n_{\ker(g)}
by definition of the generality order.
∎

Note that in point (2) of Proposition
4.16
,
n_{\ker(g)}
is not necessarily a solution itself. This indeed happens exactly when
{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\ker(g)
is a projective algebra. In general the following holds.

Proposition 4.17
.

Let
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be an algebraic e-generalization problem for a variety
\mathsf{V}
. Given any
\theta\in\mathscr{G}(h)
,
n_{\theta}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta
is a solution if and only if
\theta
is projective. Moreover, if that is the case,
n_{\theta}
is the top element of
\Pi_{\theta}
by the generality order.

Proof.

Consider
\theta\in\mathscr{G}(h)
. Note that projectivity is a necessary condition for
n_{\theta}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta
to be a solution by definition of an algebraic solution.

Assume now
\theta
projective. Since
\theta\in\mathscr{G}(h)
, by definition there is some solution
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
such that
\theta=\ker(g)
, and a testifying homomorphism
f:{\textbf{P}}\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
such that
f\circ g=h
. By the Second Isomorphism Theorem applied to
n_{\theta}
and
g
, the fact that
\theta=\ker(g)
yields a homomorphism
f^{\prime}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta\to{\textbf{P}}
such that
f^{\prime}\circ n_{\theta}=g
. Thus, the composition
f^{\prime\prime}=f\circ f^{\prime}
testifies that
n_{\theta}
is a solution to
h
. Indeed:

f^{\prime\prime}\circ n_{\theta}=f\circ f^{\prime}\circ n_{\theta}=f\circ g=h.

Therefore, if
\theta
is projective and in
\mathscr{G}(h)
,
n_{\theta}
is a solution.
The reasoning above also proves that
n_{\theta}
is the top element of
\Pi_{\theta}
by the generality order; indeed, any
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
in
\Pi_{\theta}(h)
is such that
\theta=\ker(g)
and therefore, as above, there exists
f^{\prime}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta\to{\textbf{P}}
such that
f^{\prime}\circ n_{\theta}=g
, and then
g\sqsubseteq n_{\theta}
.
∎

The above results allow one to have a “rough” idea of the generality poset of a problem.
In the rest of the section we use this intuition towards the study of the e-generalization type.
In the general case, we can derive that the cardinality of a set of complete maximal congruences in
\mathscr{G}(h)
gives a best-case scenario for the e-generalization type of
h
. To be precise, let us define the
type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
to be:

•

unitary: if there is a maximal complete set of congruences (
mcsc
for short) in
(\mathscr{G}(h),\subseteq)
of cardinality 1;

•

finitary: if there is an mcsc in
(\mathscr{G}(h),\subseteq)
of finite (greater than
1
) cardinality;

•

infinitary: if there is an mcsc in
(\mathscr{G}(h),\subseteq)
of infinite (not finite) cardinality;

•

nullary: if there is no mcsc in
(\mathscr{G}(h),\subseteq)
.

Theorem 4.18
.

Let
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be an algebraic e-generalization problem for a variety
\mathsf{V}
. Then:

(1)

If the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
is nullary or infinitary, then the e-generalization type of
h
is at best infinitary.

(2)

If the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
is finitary, the e-generalization type of
h
is at best finitary.

Proof.

Assume first that the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
is nullary or infinitary; then we prove that there cannot be a finite minimal complete set of solutions to
h
. Assume by way of contradiction that there is, and call it
\mathscr{M}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}
, where each
g_{i}:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}_{i}
for some projective algebra
{\textbf{P}}_{i}\in\mathsf{V}
.
Consider now the set of congruences
\{\theta_{i}=\ker(g_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}\subseteq\mathscr{G}(h)
; since the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
is nullary or infinitary, there must be an exact congruence
\theta\in\mathscr{G}(h)
such that
\theta\not\subseteq\theta_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. For otherwise one would have that the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
is finitary.

Now, since
\theta\in\mathscr{G}(h)
, by definition there exists a solution
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
such that
\theta=\ker(g)
.
Then
g_{i}\not\sqsubseteq g
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. Indeed, if
g_{i}\sqsubseteq g
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, by Proposition
4.16
we would get that
\theta\subseteq\theta_{i}
, a contradiction. Thus there is no element in
\mathscr{M}
that is below
g
, which contradicts the fact that
\mathscr{M}
is a complete set of minimal solutions. It follows that the type of
h
is at best infinitary, and (1) holds.

For (2), suppose now that the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
is finitary. Then the same argument as above, setting
n=1
and
\mathscr{M}=\{g_{1}\}
, shows that the e-generalization type of
h
cannot be unitary.
∎

Note that if the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
is unitary, we can draw no conclusion on the type of
h
because we cannot make any assumptions on the generality poset restricted to
\Pi_{\theta}(h)
, where
\{\theta\}
is a complete maximal set in
\mathscr{G}(h)
. Moreover, if the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
is nullary, we cannot directly conclude that the type of
h
is also nullary. Indeed, for instance, it could be the case that in
\mathscr{G}(h)
there is a countable cofinal chain of congruences
\theta_{1}\subseteq\theta_{2}\subseteq\ldots
such that the union of the minimal elements in all the
\Pi_{\theta_{i}}(h)
are all incomparable, and therefore constitute a (infinite) complete minimal set of solutions.

In 1ESP varieties the situation is considerably clearer, and in fact the study of the generality poset is fully reduced to the poset of projective congruences of the 1-generated free algebra.

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a 1ESP variety and consider an algebraic e-generalization problem
h
. Its poset of solutions
\mathscr{A}(h)
is dually isomorphic to the poset of congruences in
\mathscr{G}(h)
.

Proof.

We claim that the map
\ker
establishes a dual isomorphism from
\mathscr{A}(h)
to
\mathscr{G}(h)
.
First, note that the map is well defined since if two solutions
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathscr{A}(h)
are equally general, they have the same kernel. Indeed,
g\sqsubseteq g^{\prime}
implies
g=f\circ g^{\prime}
for some
f
, and therefore
\ker(g^{\prime})\subseteq\ker(g)
; similarly
g^{\prime}\sqsubseteq g
implies
\ker(g)\subseteq\ker(g^{\prime})
. Moreover, by Theorem
4.12
,
g
is a solution if and only if
\ker(g)\in\mathscr{G}(h)
, which implies that
\ker
is a surjective function from
\mathscr{A}(h)
to
\mathscr{G}(h)
. Let us demonstrate that the map is injective. Suppose that
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathscr{A}(h)
are such that
\ker(g)=\ker(g^{\prime})=\theta
, we prove that
g
and
g^{\prime}
are equally general. Let us write
g:=e\circ n_{\theta},g^{\prime}:=e^{\prime}\circ n_{\theta}
for some embeddings
e,e^{\prime}
, which exist by Theorem
2.5
(1). By 1ESP, there exist
j,j^{\prime}
such that
j\circ e=id_{{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)/\theta}=j^{\prime}\circ e^{\prime}
. Hence:

e\circ j^{\prime}\circ g^{\prime}=e\circ j^{\prime}\circ e^{\prime}\circ n_{\theta}=e\circ n_{\theta}=g,

which verifies that
g\sqsubseteq g^{\prime}
, and similarly
g^{\prime}\sqsubseteq g
since:

e^{\prime}\circ j\circ g=e^{\prime}\circ j\circ e\circ n_{\theta}=e^{\prime}\circ n_{\theta}=g^{\prime}.

We have proved that
\ker
is a bijection from
\mathscr{A}(h)
to
\mathscr{G}(h)
; it is a dual poset isomorphism as a consequence of Proposition
4.16
.
∎

Corollary 4.20
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be 1ESP, and consider an e-generalization problem

. Then the e-generalization type of
h
is exactly the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
.

Remark 4.21
.

An interesting consequence of Theorem
4.19
above is that in 1ESP varieties each element of the generality poset has a representative given by one of the natural epimorphisms
n_{\theta}
, which has as codomain a 1-generated projective algebra. This means that, given a symbolic problem
{\bf t}
, and a solution for it given by a term
s
, there is a 1-variable term
s^{\prime}
that is equally general to
s
. Essentially, in the poset of symbolic solutions one can restrict to consider 1-variable terms.

Example 4.22
.

In Example
4.14
we have seen that every variety
\mathsf{V}
whose 1-generated free algebra is trivial has the 1ESP property. This includes lattices, semilattices, and more generally all varieties without constants and whose operations are idempotent.
Since the 1-generated free algebra is trivial, the type of
\mathscr{G}(h)
is clearly unitary for all problems

; it follows from Corollary
4.20
that every such variety has unitary
\kappa
-type for every cardinal
\kappa\leq\omega
.

\Box

Remark 4.23
.

It is proved in
[
16
]
that idempotent anti-unification is infinitary, which seems to contradict the result outlined in the above example. However, the result in
[
16
]
makes key use of two constants, assumed to be in the language, in order to show that the type is (at least) infinitary. If we add constants, the 1-generated free algebra is not trivial, and therefore there is no contradiction. We also note that our result is incomparable with the one in
[

, in the sense that while ours is more general with respect to the fact that we do not restrict the equational theory to idempotency (our result holds for any equational theory that includes idempotency of all symbols), we do need to restrict to not having constants.

Let us conclude this section by identifying a sufficient condition for deriving the unitary type of a problem.

Theorem 4.24
.

Let
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
be an algebraic e-generalization problem. If
\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
is finitely generated and projective then the type of
h
is unitary and
h
is the minimum of
(\mathscr{A}(h),\sqsubseteq)
.

Proof.

Note that if
\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
is finitely generated and projective then
h
is a solution to itself, testified by the identity map.
Moreover, let
g:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to{\textbf{P}}
be any other solution; then there exists
f:{\textbf{P}}\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
such that
f\circ g=h
, and hence
h\sqsubseteq g
, whence
h
is the minimum in
\mathscr{A}(h)
with respect to the generality order.
∎

The previous theorem entails a sufficient condition for varieties to have unitary e-generalization type.

Corollary 4.25
.

If
\mathsf{V}
is a variety such that finite direct products of 1-generated exact algebras are finitely generated and projective, then
\mathsf{V}
has unitary type.

Proof.

If
h:{\bf F}_{\mathsf{V}}(z)\to\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
is any e-generalization problem, under our hypothesis,
\prod_{k=1}^{m}{\textbf{E}}_{k}
is finitely generated and projective and hence its type is unitary by Theorem
4.24
. Thus, the
\kappa

\mathsf{V}
, for all cardinals
\kappa\leq\omega
, is unitary as well.
∎

Example 4.26
.

Abelian groups, commutative monoids, and commutative semigroups have unitary e-generalization type. Indeed, in Example
4.8
we have seen that the only 1-generated exact algebras are the free 1-generated algebra itself,

and, in all cases except commutative semigroups, the trivial algebra (which in such cases is projective). Thus, all nontrivial finite direct products of 1-generated exact algebras are finite powers of

In all three cases, the

5.

) in Theorem

, there exists
d

d=\iota(d)
therefore necessarily
\varphi(d)=z
since it is the only negation fixpoint. Note that
d,b\in\{x\in P:x\leq\iota(x)\}
which is a meet-semilattice by (
3

d\wedge b

d\wedge b\leq\iota(d\wedge b)
. Moreover,

\mathcal{P}
,
d\wedge b\leq d\leq\iota(d\wedge b)

\varphi

\varphi(d\wedge b)\leq\varphi(b)=\neg z

\varphi(d\wedge b)=\neg z

whence
\varphi(\iota(d\wedge b))=1

\varphi(d)=z

6.
Conclusions

The algebraic approach we presented offers a novel perspective and new techniques for the study of e-generalization problems, for equational theories in general and (algebraizable) logics in particular. We have highlighted the invariants up to generality of e-generalization problems and their solutions, using the congruences of the 1-generated free algebras; these congruences always shed some light on the poset of solutions of a problem, and in some cases they fully characterize it.

As hinted at in the last section, our methods can be used fruitfully to study a plethora of different examples. Among other varieties that should be amenable to our methods, a most interesting example is given by Heyting algebras, or, from the logical perspective, intuitionistic logic. Indeed, Heyting algebras are a 1EP variety. Another relevant 1EP variety coming from logic is the variety of MV-algebras, the equivalent algebraic semantics of infinite-valued Łukasiewicz logic. To tackle these examples one would require an in depth study that goes beyond the boundaries of this work; nonetheless we plan to pursue it in future work.

Another relevant direction to follow is the role played by e-generalization in
refutation systems
, i.e., axiomatic systems meant to derive non-valid formulas
[
50
,
51
,
18
]
; in such systems the
reverse substitution rule
derives from a non-valid formula the refutation of all its generalizers. A connection with the present theory should be investigated.

Finally, generalization has found many applications in several domains, such as inductive logic programming
[
43
,
20
,
35
]
, parallel programming
[
7
]
, conceptual blending
[
27
]
, case-based reasoning
[
4
]
; addressing the connections with our theoretical setting could be wortwhile.
