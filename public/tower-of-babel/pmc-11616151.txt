Arachnoid cysts predominantly follow a benign course; they are often discovered incidentally and remain asymptomatic.1, 2 The adult population exhibits an incidence rate of arachnoid cysts ranging from 0.2% to 1.7%, with a marginally higher prevalence in males.3, 4 In the largest retrospective review of 661 adults identified with arachnoid cysts, 94.7% of patients were asymptomatic.4

While the vast majority of intracranial arachnoid cysts are asymptomatic, there have been notable reports of symptomatic arachnoid cysts in adults.1, 5–10 Symptoms are caused by mass effect and have a wide variety of presentations depending on the location and size of the arachnoid cyst. The most common presentation in adults is headache.1, 11 Other symptoms include cranial nerve palsies, parkinsonism, hydrocephalus, impaired cognitive functioning, urinary incontinence, trigeminal neuralgia, psychiatric disorders, and paresthesias.2, 4, 6, 8, 12

Surgical intervention has been shown to be a safe and effective treatment option for both pediatric and adult patients with symptoms related to mass effect from arachnoid cysts. Endoscopic cyst fenestration, in particular, has been shown to have beneficial outcomes in adults with a relatively low risk profile.13 Our case series presents three adults with symptomatic intracranial arachnoid cysts who underwent endoscopic cystoventriculostomy.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 53-year-old female presented with 1 year of intermittent left face numbness and pain with occasional headaches relieved with Tylenol. She denied any history of triggering activities, and she was able to speak and chew normally. She also reported occasional forgetfulness.

On examination, the patient was alert, oriented, and appearing well. There was mild decreased sensation to light touch in the left V2 and V3 divisions. The mastication muscles demonstrated full and symmetric strength. Her face was symmetric. The rest of her cranial nerve examination was unremarkable. Her speech was fluent. Her strength was full throughout all extremities.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed a cystic intra-axial right frontoparietal lesion measuring 5.7 × 5.8 × 4.5 cm, with mass effect resulting in right to left subfalcine herniation, asymmetry of the perimesencephalic cisterns, and compression of the dorsal root entry zone of the left trigeminal nerve (Fig. 1A and B). No hydrocephalus was present.

Case 1. Preoperative MRI (A), preoperative T2-weighted axial MRI (B, note the cisternal portion of the left trigeminal nerve), and postoperative T2-weighted axial MRI 2 months following surgery (C).

Given the concern that the arachnoid cyst could have been causing local mass effect on the midbrain and subsequently causing pressure on the contralateral trigeminal nerve root entry zone, resulting in the patient’s facial numbness and pain in the V2 and V3 regions, a neuroendoscopic cystoventriculostomy was recommended. A right parietal trajectory was planned using stereotactic navigation. Upon entering the ventricle with the introducer sheath, a brisk egress of clear cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) under high pressure was noted. An endoscopic cystoventriculostomy was performed, connecting the arachnoid cyst to the atrium of the right lateral ventricle (Video 1).

The patient noted improvement in her left facial pain, with unchanged left facial numbness, immediately in the postoperative period. At an outpatient appointment 3 weeks following surgery, she noted that her headaches had significantly improved. The left face pain and ongoing decreased sensation in the V2 distribution were improved compared to her preoperative symptoms but were still intermittently present. Postoperative MRI revealed a decreased cyst size, re-expansion of the physiological CSF spaces, decreased brainstem compression, and a decrease in midline shift (Fig. 1C).

Clinical follow-up was performed 7 months after surgical intervention. The patient reported ongoing substantial improvement in her headaches. She did report some ongoing mild face pain, well controlled on carbamazepine and improved compared to her presurgical pain.

Case 2

A 74-year-old female presented with a long history of headaches and a history of a right paramedian arachnoid cyst identified on imaging 10 years prior. Her headaches were worse in the morning and would wax and wane throughout the day. The headaches were not associated with any additional signs of increased intracranial pressure, such as somnolence, visual disturbance, or emesis. Recently, she also noted increasing difficulty with ambulation due to slowly progressing weakness of the distal left lower extremity.

On neurological examination, she was awake and alert with appropriate affect. Her speech was clear. Extraocular movements were intact, but the examination was notable for dysconjugate gaze. The motor examination was notable for mild left upper- and lower-extremity weakness (4+/5 throughout). Sensation was intact to light touch, but she did have some difficulty with proprioception in the left foot. On cerebellar testing, she was noted to have difficulty with finger-nose maneuvers on the left. The Romberg test was positive. She did not have any hyperreflexia.

MRI of the brain was notable for a large right parietal extra-axial cyst with mass effect on the medial right hemisphere extending to the ependymal region of the right lateral ventricle. Compared to what was observed in 2014, the cyst had grown substantially (Fig. 2A and B). The mass effect from the cyst caused 17 mm of leftward midline shift (Fig. 2B).

Case 2. Preoperative T2-weighted axial MRI 9 years prior to surgery (A), preoperative T2-weighted axial MRI a few months prior to surgery (B), and postoperative T2-weighted axial MRI 2 months following surgery (C).

Due to the chronic headaches, progressive weakness, and interval cyst growth, the patient was offered surgery. She underwent a right parietal stereotactic burr hole craniotomy and endoscopic fenestration of the cyst into the right lateral ventricle (Video 1). Postoperatively, she initially had headaches and nausea that quickly resolved. Two weeks following surgery, she noted significant improvements in cognition, headaches, and ambulation. She was able to decrease and ultimately stop taking daily analgesics due to the significant improvement in her headaches.

At the 3-month follow-up, repeat brain MRI revealed an interval decrease in cyst size (8.1 × 6.4 × 6.3 cm to 6.5 × 4.1 × 5.8 cm) and midline shift (17 mm to 6 mm) (Fig. 2C). She continued to report improved cognition, headaches, and gait.

At 20 months following surgery, the patient reported ongoing substantial improvement in her headaches without any further current complaints. She reported that the strength in her left arm and leg as well as her gait had improved and returned to baseline.

Case 3

A 46-year-old male presented with headaches that would worsen with head rotation. He denied experiencing emesis, lethargy, or cognitive problems, but he had taken a leave of absence from his work due to his symptoms. He denied any changes in vision. He reported significant urinary frequency and urgency. A formal urological evaluation was not remarkable for any anatomical or functional issue.

On neurological examination, he was awake and alert. The extraocular movements were intact. His face was symmetric, and his speech was fluent. He had full strength in all four extremities and ambulated with a steady gait.

MRI of the brain revealed a large (> 7 cm) anterior interhemispheric cyst with mass effect on the rostrum of the corpus callosum, frontal horns, and medial frontal lobes (Fig. 3A and B). The cyst fluid was consistent with CSF, suggesting the diagnosis of an arachnoid cyst. There was scalloping of the anterior cranial fossa, consistent with a likely chronic/congenital etiology. No hydrocephalus was present.

Case 3. Preoperative T2-weighted axial MRI (A), preoperative T1-weighted sagittal MRI (B), postoperative T2-weighted axial MRI 2 months following surgery (C), and postoperative T1-weighted sagittal MRI 2 months following surgery (D).

Given the debilitating symptoms in the setting of an arachnoid cyst causing significant mass effect, he underwent a right frontal endoscopic fenestration of the cyst with a cystoventriculostomy into the right frontal horn (Video 1). CSF was noted to be cloudy initially upon entering the cavity but cleared throughout the operation.

Postoperatively, he recovered very well. His positional headaches resolved, and he reported improvements in his gait and urinary symptoms. Postoperative MRI performed 2 months following surgery revealed an interval decrease in the size of the arachnoid cyst (5.6 × 6.6 × 6.9 cm from 7.4 × 8.0 × 7.3 cm) and decreased mass effect on the medial frontal lobes (Fig. 3C and D).

At the 10-month follow-up, he reported ongoing complete resolution of his urinary complaints as well as no further issues with his gait. His headaches had resolved nearly entirely with occasional mild discomfort in certain positions, though much improved compared to the debilitating headache he was experiencing prior to surgery.

Discussion

Observations

These illustrative cases highlight the efficacy of endoscopic cystoventriculostomy for symptom reduction in carefully selected patients with symptomatic arachnoid cysts. Despite the prevalence of asymptomatic cases, a subset of patients can present with debilitating, chronic symptoms. Arachnoid cysts are thought to have a higher risk of becoming symptomatic when located within the CSF pathways, in the eloquent cortex, around the cisterns of the brainstem, in the cerebellopontine angle cistern, or when enlarging.1 While case 2 illustrates the potential relationship between slow, progressive cyst enlargement and symptom development, the other two cases lacked long-term serial imaging for comparison. In addition, these parietal and frontal lobar cysts were not located in the typical locations thought to be prone to developing symptoms. Despite the location and lack of clear evidence of cyst growth in two of the three patients, all three patients had significant symptom relief following surgery.

Surgery has been shown to be an effective treatment for symptomatic arachnoid cysts. The main surgical approaches involve craniotomy and resection of the cyst wall, cyst fenestration, or cyst shunting.13, 14 In a meta-analysis of middle cranial fossa arachnoid cysts, the authors found that the efficacy, as measured by symptomatic improvement, was comparable in all three treatment options. Complications varied by surgical approach: the microsurgical fenestration group experienced higher rates of short-term complications, the cystoperitoneal shunting group experienced higher rates of long-term complications, and the neuroendoscopic fenestration/cystoventriculostomy group had lower rates of cyst size reduction.14 In our case series, we describe three endoscopic approaches for cystoventriculostomy that resulted in significant improvement in symptoms and uncomplicated, short hospital stays (all three patients were discharged on postoperative day 1). Cystoventriculostomy offers a relatively safe approach to symptomatic arachnoid cysts in adults, resulting in equalization of pressure across intracranial compartments, and is relatively less invasive than more aggressive surgical interventions for arachnoid cysts, including craniotomy and resection of the cyst wall. In addition, cystoventriculostomy offers a potential cure for ongoing symptoms without the need for implanted devices, decreasing the risk for infection and device complication, as well as the need for surgical interventions in the future.

As demonstrated in our case series, the presentation of symptomatic arachnoid cysts is highly variable and often involves a multitude of symptoms, with headache being the most common.1, 11 All three of the patients in the case series presented to multiple providers at different healthcare facilities for evaluation of their chronic symptoms without being offered any surgical intervention. When surgical intervention was eventually offered, improvement in symptoms related to arachnoid cysts was rapid following endoscopic cystoventriculostomy. The nearly immediate improvement in symptoms, without full resolution of the cyst, suggests that an equalization in pressure between the arachnoid cyst and the ventricular system is sufficient to provide significant symptom reduction. It is important to consider surgical intervention in symptomatic arachnoid cyst cases, even in those in which headaches alone are the presenting symptom. The three patients in our case series demonstrate that headaches alone can be a sufficient indication for surgical intervention when the headaches are chronic, debilitating, and clearly associated with the presence of a large arachnoid cyst.

In terms of surgical technique, performing an endoscopic cystoventriculostomy involves careful planning of the trajectory with stereotactic navigation. The ultimate objective is to enter the cyst at the point where it comes closest to the surface of the cortex while avoiding eloquent areas and vascular structures. The endpoint of the planned trajectory should be the portion of the ventricle closest to, or even abutting, the arachnoid cyst. As demonstrated in our case series, creating a small hole (using cautery and balloon inflation) between the arachnoid cyst and the ventricle allows for pressure equalization and can effectively decrease bothersome symptoms.

Lessons

Although the vast majority of intracranial arachnoid cysts in adults are asymptomatic, a small subset of patients can develop a wide variety of debilitating symptoms that can benefit from surgical intervention. Cystoventriculostomy offers a safe approach to symptomatic arachnoid cysts in adults, with the goal of equalizing pressure across intracranial compartments without the implantation of hardware. Improvement in symptoms associated with the arachnoid cyst was observed nearly immediately following surgery, with durable long-term outcomes, even with the cyst remaining on postoperative imaging. These cases demonstrate that a safe and effective trajectory can be planned using stereotactic navigation, with the goal of creating a small hole in the cyst wall closest to the ventricle. Ultimately, it is important to recognize symptomatic cases of arachnoid cysts and appreciate the potential for surgical intervention that can result in lasting, significant clinical improvement.
