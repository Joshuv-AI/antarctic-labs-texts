Towards Contextual Sensitive Data Detection
Liang Telkamp, Madelon Hulsebos

Abstract
The emergence of open data portals necessitates more attention to protecting sensitive data before datasets get published and exchanged. While an abundance of methods for suppressing sensitive data exist, the conceptualization of sensitive data and methods to detect it, focus particularly on personal data that, if disclosed, may be harmful or violate privacy. We observe the need for refining and broadening our definitions of sensitive data, and argue that the sensitivity of data depends on its context . Based on this definition, we introduce two mechanisms for contextual sensitive data detection that consider the broader context of a dataset at hand. First, we introduce type contextualization , which first detects the semantic type of particular data values, then considers the overall context of the data values within the dataset or document. Second, we introduce domain contextualization which determines sensitivity of a given dataset in the broader context based on the retrieval of relevant rules from documents that specify data sensitivity (e.g., data topic and geographic origin). Experiments with these mechanisms, assisted by large language models (LLMs), confirm that: 1) type-contextualization significantly reduces the number of false positives for type-based sensitive data detection and reaches a recall of 94% compared to 63% with commercial tools, and 2) domain-contextualization leveraging sensitivity rule retrieval is effective for context-grounded sensitive data detection in non-standard data domains such as humanitarian datasets. Evaluation with humanitarian data experts also reveals that context-grounded LLM explanations provide useful guidance in manual data auditing processes, improving consistency. We open-source mechanisms and annotated datasets for contextual sensitive data detection at https://github.com/trl-lab/sensitive-data-detection .

Background

Conceptualization of Sensitive Data

The notion of “sensitive data” is multifaceted, spanning legal, theoretical, and domain-specific views. Legal frameworks often equate sensitivity with personal identifiable information. The GDPR defines personal data broadly as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person,” specifying sensitive categories like health, ethnicity, or politics requiring extra protection
[
7
]
. Similarly, HIPAA defines identifiers that make health information “Protected Health Information (PHI)” when linked to individuals
[
5
]
. These protocols provide a baseline: data that directly or indirectly identifies a person is sensitive. Yet static, type-level definitions are insufficient for modern governance
[
22
]
, as they emphasize direct identifiers while overlooking non-personal but risky data such as geolocation data, community-level attributes, or institutional records. Sensitivity is often contextual: seemingly harmless data can become sensitive when combined, repurposed, or shared across contexts
[
17
]
.

Theoretical perspectives deepen this nuance. Nissenbaum’s Contextual Integrity frames sensitivity as the appropriateness of information flows within social norms
[
19
]
. Violations occur when flows breach expectations, not from the data type itself. Building on this, researchers advocate context-grounded definitions
[
17
,
25
]
. For instance, shopping data may be ordinary in retail but sensitive in behavioral profiling. Recent taxonomies further refine this view.
14
distinguish: (1) always-sensitive data (e.g., direct identifiers); (2) combination-sensitive (sensitive when linked together); (3) context-sensitive (sensitivity by use); and (4) value-sensitive (sensitivity from specific values). This shows that not all personal is equally sensitive, and not all sensitive data is on individuals.

In short, conceptualizations have shifted from static identifiers to context-dependent models, where frameworks like Contextual Integrity and multi-layer taxonomies emphasize context, use, and value. This motivates our focus: effective data protection must ensure coverage beyond personal-level information toward context-aware, domain-specific approaches.

Methods for Detecting Sensitive Data

Most automated sensitive data detection methods concentrate on detecting sensitive data of individuals. Widely adopted industry tools like Microsoft Presidio
[
18
]
and Google Cloud DLP
[
9
]
are largely rule-based, relying on regular expressions, keyword lists, and simple machine learning heuristics to recognize well-defined patterns like email addresses or phone numbers. Research has advanced towards more semantic understanding.
12
introduced Sherlock, a deep learning model that classifies types of table columns, including numerous PII, by learning from column-level representations.
15
develop this further with CASSED, a BERT-based model that integrates column headers, data types, and sample values to classify columns into sensitive categories. These approaches outperform regular expressions and dictionary-based baselines, especially on noisy or inconsistently formatted data. Recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as powerful tools for sensitive data detection, as particularly shown for PII detection outperforming both rule-based and traditional machine learning methods in accuracy and adaptability
[
24
,
33
]
. However, a key limitation remains: LLMs excel at identifying known PII types but struggle with assessing
contextual
sensitivity, such as domain-specific sensitive types, unless explicitly provided with the relevant operational context.

We identify a significant gap in detecting sensitive data: methods focus on detecting personal sensitive data, overlooking non-personal data that is sensitive due to its context. Our work bridges this gap by developing a framework that addresses both personal and non-personal sensitive data detection through contextualization.

3
Revisiting the Concept of Sensitive Data

The main conceptual, legal, and methodological focus regarding
sensitive data
is on personal- and individual level data, including personal identifiable information (PII) which refers to data that can identify an individual
[
7
]
. Regulatory data protocols, such as GDPR
[
7
]
and HIPAA
[
5
]
, specify standards for data sensitivity, hence provide concrete guidelines for data protection measures. However, we argue for the need of a more contextual view of sensitive data (Def.
1
) for developing new detection methods.

Definition 1
(Contextual Sensitive Data)

Data whose sensitivity depends on external factors and requires protection due to how, by whom, and in what context it can be misused.

Advancing this definition, we posit two contextual aspects that matter in order to assess sensitivity of data:
type contextualization
and
domain contextualization
.

Following the existence of generic standards and protocols, sensitive data detection methods often focus on detecting data
types
reflecting personal sensitive data (e.g., names, addresses, phone numbers, birth dates). But the appearance of a name or address does not immediately make a data value sensitive. For example, an address may reflect the address of a person or organization, each having significantly different consequences, hence different sensitivity levels. We term this
type contextualization
: whether a data value corresponding to a sensitivity type of interest (e.g.
name
) truly is sensitive requires considering the context in which it appears in the entire dataset (e.g., the entire document or table).

Besides a more fine-grained contextual notion of a data value its sensitivity, we also argue for a more coarse-grained contextual perspective. Nissenbaum introduced the theory of Contextual Integrity
[
19
]
, which states that privacy risks arise when data is used outside its intended setting, particularly addressing personal privacy. This contextual perspective extends beyond personal privacy, in domains such as humanitarian aid, national security, and company-sensitive context. We pose that data protection should account for risks beyond identity exposure, for example, harming vulnerable populations or organizations. We refer to this broader perspective on data sensitivity as
domain contextualization
: sensitivity emerges from the dataset’s nature and potential misuse.

4
Toward
Contextual
Sensitive Data Detection

Protecting sensitive data generally follows a three-step procedure: ➀ specifying sensitive data, ➁ detecting sensitive data, ➂ remedying data sensitivity (Figure
1
). In this work, we focus on the second step, which has received little attention in research. Existing methods for detecting sensitive data suffer from two key limitations to address the contextual nature of sensitive data:

1.

Insufficient specificity
: sensitive data detection methods treat all values of types of interest as equally sensitive, while the subject of the value that implies its sensitivity. The lack of specificity leads to low precision hence high false positive rates.

2.

Insufficient coverage
: sensitive data detection methods typically focus on personal-level data, particularly PII, and do not generalize to domain-specific sensitive data. This lack of coverage leads to low recall of methods, hence leaving sensitive data unprotected.

Figure 1
:
Data protection process, starting with the specification of sensitivity, detection sensitive data in inputs accordingly, followed by remedying sensitive data by suppression methods.

The contextualization framework introduced in Section
3
addresses these limitations. Type contextualization would improve specificity as potential sensitive values are only considered sensitive based on the broader context of the value. In turn, domain contextualization ensures higher coverage of sensitive data assessment by grounding the assessment in domain-specific context. In this paper, we introduce two concrete mechanisms to address these contextual aspects of sensitive data: the
detect-then-reflect
mechanism for type contextualization and the
retrieve-then-detect
mechanism for domain contextualization. In what follows, we specifically focus on tabular data but these mechanisms extend to other modalities, such as free-form text documents.

(a)

The
detect-then-reflect
mechanism for detecting sensitive table columns using type contextualization to increase the precision. This mechanism entails: ➀ detecting potentially sensitive columns based on their type, ➁ having an LLM reflect on the actual sensitivity of the column within the context of the entire table.

(b)

The
retrieve-then-detect
mechanism for detecting sensitive data that requires domain contextualization. This mechanism entails: ➀ retrieving relevant contextual information such as rule-based heuristics, ➁ using an LLM to detect sensitive data using its parametric knowledge and the retrieved expert heuristics.

Figure 2
:
Mechanisms for detecting sensitive data in a contextual manner.

4.1
Type contextualization:
detect-then-reflect

Existing methods typically classify sensitive data based on signals indicating a type of interest, e.g. names, addresses, or phone numbers. If a value matches this type, it is flagged as sensitive. We introduce a two-step pipeline for detecting type-informed sensitive data based on a
detect-then-reflect
procedure (Figure
2(a)
). In the initial
detection
step, potentially sensitive columns are identified by first classifying columns corresponding to types of interest that are specified upfront, e.g.
email
,
phone number
, based on column names and data values. This step prioritizes recall in order to avoid missing any columns that may carry data of the type that can potentially be sensitive. In the second step,
reflection
, the model assesses the columns in the context of the full table. We prompt an LLM with the table, including the column’s detected type. The LLM determines whether the column is truly sensitive, taking into account inter-column relationships and data across the entire table. This step is crucial for mitigating false positives from the detection stage by incorporating inter-column relationships and table semantics.

4.2
Domain contextualization:
retrieve-then-detect

While type-based data sensitivity can often be inferred from structural context, the data sensitivity may depend on domain-specific and geopolitical factors that lie beyond the data itself. For such cases, requiring domain contextualization, we introduce the
retrieve-then-detect
mechanism (Figure
2(b)
). This mechanism considers external contextual information, such as rule-based heuristics specified in data governance documents, in the classification process. Relevant documents are retrieved based on, for example, the origin of a dataset and included in the prompt alongside the table and column under analysis. The LLM leverages both its internal parametric knowledge and the retrieved external data sensitivity instructions to assess whether a column contains data that should be considered sensitive in the given operational context. Unlike type-informed detection, where there is a predefined list of entities (e.g.
email
,
phone number
) as relevant for for example PII detection, domain-contextual sensitive data has no fixed ontology. This mechanism establishes domain-specific column sensitivity based on the broader table context and retrieved contextual knowledge.

5
Experimental Setup

Here, we outline the implementation details, the LLMs used, baselines, datasets, and metrics to evaluate our proposed framework for contextually sensitive data detection. Code and datasets can be found at
https://github.com/trl-lab/sensitive-data-detection
.

5.1
Method implementation

Type-contextualization

In the
detection
step, given the well-defined nature of PII types, we parse the problem as a multiclass classification task over a taxonomy of personal identifiable types (e.g.,
email_address
,
generic_identifier
) derived from GDPR
[
7
]
and UN OCHA guidelines
[
6
]
(see the code repository for a complete list). The LLM is prompted with a column name and five values, to return only the most appropriate type or
None
. We consider zero-shot and fine-tuned approaches. The zero-shot method leverages the model’s inherent ability to follow instructions, while the fine-tuned approach enhances performance by training the models on a synthetic dataset of 1,000 labeled columns. Fine-tuning is done using Low-Rank Adaptation
[
10
]
for parameter efficiency. The models are optimized with AdamW for a single epoch using an 80/20 train-validation split.

In the
reflection
phase, the LLM re-evaluates each candidate column corresponding to types of interest within the context of the entire table. We provide the LLM with a markdown-formatted representation of the full table, including headers and five sample rows per column, along with the specific type detected for the target column and, if applicable, the entities of other columns which are detected as PII entities. The LLM is then instructed to assign one of three contextual sensitivity levels:
non_sensitive
, referring to information that cannot identify a person (e.g., aggregate data, organization names);
moderate_sensitive
, referring to data that could potentially identify a person when combined with other attributes (e.g., demographics, partial information); and
high_sensitive
, referring to data that definitively identifies a person (e.g., full name, email, national ID).

Domain-contextualization

For the
retrieval
step, we aim to identify relevant heuristics indicating sensitivity rules from domain-specific data governance documents in order to integrate this external domain knowledge in the LLM’s context. In our experiments we focus on a case study on the Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX)

[
29
]
, hence on humanitarian datasets. The system retrieves relevant policy fragments from Information Sharing Protocols (ISPs) based on the dataset’s geographical context (e.g., country). We use GPT-4o-mini to extract the specific sensitivity rules from these documents (e.g., data types for
low
,
moderate
,
high
,
severe
sensitivity), which we store in a dictionary. If no country-specific ISP is identified, a default fallback template based on cross-country consensus is used.

In the
detection
phase, the LLM is provided with a curated prompt containing table context (headers and five representative rows), relevant ISP extracts containing explicit instructions to classify each column’s sensitivity according to the ISP guidelines while justifying its decision by directly citing the relevant policy passages. Based on the policy definitions, the model assigns one of four sensitivity levels,
non_sensitive
,
moderate_sensitive
,
high_sensitive
, or
severe_sensitive
. This mechanism ensures that sensitivity assessments are grounded in explicit domain policies, thereby enhancing both interpretability and practical usability for domain experts.

5.3
Baselines

We compare our methods with two common baselines for sensitive data detection. We include Google Cloud DLP
[
9
]
, a rule-based system that combines built-in
infoTypes
, predefined detectors for common PII entities, which are associated custom-defined dictionaries and regular expressions. For our experiments, we configure the minimum likelihood threshold to “Likely”. The second baseline is Microsoft Presidio, an open-source tool that leverages regular expressions, pattern-based rules, context windows, and ML-based named entity recognition to detect PII types. To enable consistent evaluation, each Presidio type has been mapped to a corresponding category in our unified taxonomy (this mapping can be found in the code repository). Both systems output column-level predictions and are widely adopted in enterprise settings. However, neither tool supports contextual reflection or table-wide reasoning; any match to a known PII pattern is automatically treated as sensitive.

5.4
Datasets

To evaluate the contextual sensitive data detection mechanisms we use a combination of synthetic and real-world datasets, to maintain ground-truth validity for sensitive data detection in generic context and the domain-specific context of humanitarian datasets.

•

Type contextualization with sensitive PII
: We extracted 66 anonymized tables (2,061 columns) from the GitTables corpus
[
11
]
representing a variety of real-world database schemas. Each column was manually annotated with (i) its PII entity type and (ii) its contextual sensitivity level (non-sensitive, moderate or highly sensitive).

•

Domain contextualization with humanitarian tables
: To evaluate the retrieve-then-detect mechanism, we generated 9 synthetic tables with sensitive data closely resembling typical humanitarian data (e.g., for displacement tracking, public health monitoring) for different countries. Each table contains columns that could be potentially sensitive within country-specific Information Sharing Protocols (ISPs). Humanitarian data quality experts labeled each column with a sensitivity level (low, moderate, high, severe). We also add 14
real
tables from the Humanitarian Data Exchange
2
2

2

https://data.humdata.org/
that do not contain any sensitive data, to evaluate the false positive rate.

5.5
Metrics

We evaluate along three axes: PII detection, contextual reflection for PII, and non-PII sensitivity detection. To account for class imbalance, we report both weighted (support-weighted) and macro averages. Weighted averages reflect overall performance across examples, while macro averages treat each class equally and reduce dominance by frequent classes. Unless otherwise noted, we focus on weighted averages.

Performance is assessed at both the column and table level. At the column level (type contextualization), PII detection is treated as a multiclass task over 27 PII types (plus a “None” category), while sensitivity reflection reduces to a binary decision between sensitive (moderate/high) and non-sensitive (low). At the table level (domain contextualization), a table is classified as sensitive if it contains any moderate/high/severe sensitive information, resulting in a binary task (sensitive vs. not). Precision, recall, and F1 are used as evaluation metrics.

For labeling, sensitivity levels are binarized:
low
is treated as non-sensitive, while
moderate
,
high
, and
severe
are grouped as sensitive. This aligns with operational practice where any moderate or higher sensitivity triggers safeguards, consistent with UN OCHA guidance that such data warrants protective measures
[
20
]
.

7
Contextual Sensitive Data: importance, practice, and consequences

With the emergence of public data sharing portals, and their adoption in critical areas such as the Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX) for humanitarian aid coordination, it is key to protect sensitive datasets before they are automatically retrieved from the portal by malicious users or web crawlers for AI training datasets. Not doing so, induces significant risk to vulnerable populations. We reconsider and broaden the definition of what data is sensitive, and argue that sensitive data should be assessed
contextually
. This shift is critical as prior research for detecting and handling sensitive data typically focuses mainly on personal-level sensitive data. To broaden this practice, we present mechanisms for detecting sensitive data based on the principles of type contextualization and domain contextualization, which form a steppingstone towards this ambition.

On real-world humanitarian datasets, we show that more contextual methods lead to higher precision, hence less false positive alarms, while preserving coverage. These mechanisms can enable automation but also assist humans in data reviewing by providing context-grounded model explanations and concrete citations to relevant sensitivity rules in domain-specific documents. For successful adoption in practice, reasonable sensitivity instructions should be available in the form of 1) fine-grained semantic types of data that are deemed sensitive for type contextualization, and 2) reasonably specified assessment rules in domain-specific documents.

A potential negative consequence of automated data sensitivity detection is that critical datasets might be blocked that are time-sensitive and could be instrumental for solving societal problems, for example, in humanitarian domains. The mechanisms could be tailored around the desired precision/recall trade-off to reduce false alarms. At this moment, the methods are only developed for data sensitivity detection, and not for automated remedies. In practice, a report could be generated based on existing cases to instruct the user on how to remedy the sensitive data or potentially even automatically remedy this.

Conclusion

The rise of open data portals are key in facilitating the democratization of access to important data. This development, while positive, comes with risks of having sensitive data being used for harmful purposes by malicious actors as well as indirect leakage into language models which are fueled by any data on the web. Protecting sensitive data from getting published through such portals, by accident, is therefore key. In this paper, we first revisit the concept of sensitive data which is typically focused on personal-level data like personal-identifiable information (PII). Methods for detecting sensitive data are limited:
they are too specific to cover all sensitive data, and too generic to only flag data that is actually sensitive
. We have introduced a broader, contextual, perspective on sensitive data, and present a framework that formalizes contextual sensitive data from two angles. Experiments show that
type-contextualization
through an LLM-assisted mechanism that first detects the type of the data values (e.g. names) followed by a reflection on their sensitivity, significantly improves precision and recall over commercial tools and out-of-the-box LLM baselines. A case study with humanitarian datasets on the Humanitarian Data Exchange shows that the
domain-contextualization
through automated retrieval of rule-based heuristics from domain-specific documents effectively grounds an LLM’s assessment of data sensitivity. With our conceptualization of contextual sensitive data, we intend to encourage researchers and practitioners to consider a broader view on data sensitivity. We believe that our framework for detecting sensitive data provides a stepping stone in this important direction.
