Integrating neurological expertise into One Health strategies for pediatric neurocysticercosis-associated epilepsy control in Sub-Saharan Africa: a narrative review
Authors: Franck Katembo Sikakulya, Olivier Kambere Kavulikirwa, Simon Binezero Mambo, Hervé Monka Lekuya, Larrey Kasereka Kamabu, Jeannot Baanitse Munihire, Fatuma Djuma Sonia, Claude Kasereka Masumbuko, Amir Abdallah Mbonde, David Kitya, Patrick Kyamanywa, Amanda Ash, Malcolm K. Jones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeurocysticercosis (NCC), caused by the larval stage of Taenia solium, is a leading preventable cause of epilepsy in children, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where inadequate sanitation practices and limited veterinary control strategies persist. A missing link in the One Health response to NCC has been the absence of communication between neurospecialists and the other partners in control strategies. This narrative review explores how integrating neurological expertise into One Health strategies can enhance the prevention and control of NCC-associated epilepsy in children across the region.MethodsWe reviewed literature from PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Google Scholar, Embase, Web of Science, Global Health, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and the African Index Medicus, including peer-reviewed articles and organizational reports published between 2013 and March 2025.Findings and ConclusionOut of 1,509 records screened, 28 studies met the inclusion criteria, focusing on neuroparasitosis-associated epilepsy in children across SSA. These included research on disease burden and care challenges (n = 10), pathogenesis (n = 8), One Health control strategies (n = 4), and implementation barriers and solutions (n = 6). NCC remains a major contributor to pediatric epilepsy and associated disability in SSA. A One Health approach informed by direct input from neuro-specialists and better recording of infectious causes of epilepsy can assist teams to implement key strategies, including community education, improved sanitation, food safety measures, pig vaccination, and mass drug administration. Strengthening intersectoral collaboration and healthcare access is critical to reducing NCC burden and improving neurological outcomes for affected children.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder of significant public health concern, often characterized by an increased risk for recurrent unprovoked seizures [1]. The disorder is particularly important as it affects a substantial number of people globally and is associated with a suite of cognitive, psychological, and social adverse outcomes [2].

According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), an average of 5 million new epilepsy cases are diagnosed annually, with nearly 80% of all new cases living in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3]. Of note, more than 90% of epilepsy cases in Sub-Saharan African countries are reported in children and adolescents aged below 20 years [4]. The highest incidence of epilepsy has been reported among school-aged children, and in countries where parasitic infections are endemic [4]. This is likely due to the additional burden of a higher prevalence of risk factors for symptomatic epilepsy, including perinatal asphyxia, head injury, and parasitic infections of the central nervous system [5].

Infections of the central nervous system are a particularly common cause of seizure occurrence in tropical regions of the world. Such infections and seizures are also over-represented in LMICs, as classified by the World Bank [6]. One of the major infections associated with epilepsy is neurocysticercosis (NCC) [7], a parasitic infection of the central nervous system caused by the larval stage (or metacestode) of Taenia solium (the pork tapeworm) [8]. The metacestode has been classically termed a cysticercus, and the condition is known as cysticercosis. Neurocysticercosis (NCC) is a condition in humans that arises when cysticerci are located in the brain. While NCC-associated epilepsy is highly preventable through personal and population-level hygiene and disease eradication campaigns, it persists in endemic areas. The prevalence of neurocysticercosis (NCC) among children with epilepsy in Sub-Saharan Africa varies widely, with estimates ranging from approximately 4% in community-based studies to over 50% in hospital-based cohorts, depending on the diagnostic criteria and study setting [9, 10]. Comprehensive analyses indicate that adolescents and adults are also substantially affected by NCC. A meta-analysis revealed that the overall prevalence of NCC among people with epilepsy (PWE) across Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is approximately 22% (95% CI: 17–27%), with regional peaks of 45% in Southern Africa, 25% in Eastern Africa, and lower rates in Central and Western regions [11]. This vulnerability is linked to poor hygiene, limited access to safe water and sanitation, and exposure to Taenia solium eggs in contaminated environments [12]. Additionally, close contact with free-roaming pigs and consumption of undercooked pork in rural areas further increase transmission risk [13, 14].

Diagnosis of epilepsy and ascertaining its etiology can be difficult tasks, especially in LMICs, where socioeconomic and cultural constraints can be obstacles to the recognition and acceptance of the disease [15]. If patients can seek medical attention, NCC can be diagnosed with neuroimaging of brain cysts; however, this requires neurosurgeons and neurologists within these endemic areas to be aware of NCC as a differential diagnosis [16]. Enhanced diagnostic capacity, early treatment with antiparasitic and anti-epileptic medications, and other modalities of treatment are essential in mitigating long-term neurological sequelae [17].

Ongoing One Health interventions in Sub-Saharan Africa have shown potential in controlling neurocysticercosis (NCC). In Uganda and Tanzania, field trials using a combination of TSOL18 vaccination and oxfendazole in pigs effectively eliminated porcine cysticercosis, supporting the feasibility of veterinary-led strategies [18, 19]. Educational tools like The Vicious Worm have also improved knowledge and promoted behavior change around sanitation and pig management among schoolchildren and communities [20]. These efforts reflect the value of a multisectoral One Health approach in reducing NCC transmission and epilepsy burden.

This narrative review was conducted to highlight the need for addressing NCC-associated epilepsy in children through a multidisciplinary approach using the One Health perspective in SSA. Consideration is given to how neurosurgical and neurological specialists in SSA can contribute to reducing the burden of epilepsy and improving patient outcomes.

Method

We conducted a narrative literature review using a systematic search guided by the PRISMA framework (Fig. 1) to explore the role of One Health in the control of neurocysticercosis-associated epilepsy among children in Sub-Saharan Africa. A comprehensive search was carried out across multiple databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Google Scholar, Embase, Web of Science, Global Health, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and the African Index Medicus, using specific keywords such as: “Neuroparasitosis”, “Neurocysticercosis”, “Burden”, “Epilepsy”, “Children”, “One health approach”, “Control and Surveillance”, “Challenges and Barriers”, “leveraging”, “prevention”, “Sub-Saharan” and “Africa”. Eligibility criteria

This review included studies conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa that addressed neurocysticercosis (NCC) and/or NCC-associated epilepsy in pediatric populations, within a One Health context. Eligible studies provided empirical or contextual insights into human-animal-environment interactions relevant to epilepsy control. Only articles published in English or French were considered; bilingual experts (FKS, OKK, SBM, LKK, and HML) translated French-language studies to ensure accuracy.

Study selection and quality assessment

All records were imported into Mendeley Reference Manager and deduplicated both automatically and manually. Two authors (FKS and AA) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review of potentially eligible studies. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with authors (AM, OKK, HML, and MKJ). Data extraction was conducted systematically to ensure consistency and relevance to the narrative review objective. Quality assessments were performed (CKM, AM, and MKJ) using relevant tools (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklists and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS)) to evaluate methodological rigor, credibility, and relevance according to study design, with disagreements resolved by consensus.

Results and discussion

The search yielded 1509 records, from which only 28, that focused on neuroparasitosis-associated epilepsy among children in SSA, were considered for this review (Fig. 1). Of these included studies, 10 focused on the burden, impact and challenges in care delivery for children with epilepsy in SSA [4, 5, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 28, 15, 30]; 8 on neuroparasitosis and pathogenesis of epilepsy [33–40]; 4 on One Health and neuroparasitosis control [43, 44, 46], and [48] and 6 on barriers and solutions to implement one health approach in the control of epilepsy in SSA [35, 36, 39, 55, 59] and [61].

Epidemiology of pediatric epilepsy in Sub–Saharan Africa

The annual burden of epilepsy globally is estimated at 50 million people, with the incidence of childhood epilepsy three times higher in LMIC [4]. The global prevalence of childhood epilepsy was reported to be between 0.5 to 1% with an estimated 1% prevalence for SSA children [5, 21]. Recent studies across Sub-Saharan Africa have demonstrated variability in the incidence and prevalence of epilepsy among children. A meta-analysis estimated the pooled incidence of epilepsy in children and adolescents at approximately 250 per 100,000 per year [4]. In rural Kenya, the adjusted prevalence estimates of lifetime and active epilepsy were 41 per 1,000 and 11 per 1,000, respectively, in children aged 6–9 years [4]. In Tanzania, a community-based study reported a prevalence of 10.2 per 1,000 in children [22], while Nigeria showed a lower prevalence at 5.3 per 1,000 [19]. In Rwanda, a nationwide study found a prevalence of 7 per 1,000 [24]. These figures indicate a significant burden due to childhood epilepsy exists within SSA, and the need to institute sustainable public health measures for the control of epilepsy.

Furthermore, since epilepsy commonly occurs early in these children’s lives [25], its impact is more significant, resulting in greater disability and negative socioeconomic effects on their families. In Africa, epilepsy typically begins during childhood, especially within the first few years of life [26]. A multicountry study conducted in Tanzania, Uganda, Malawi, South Africa, and Ethiopia found that over 60% of individuals with active epilepsy had their first seizure before age 13 [27].

Gaining access to appropriate healthcare for epilepsy is challenging in LMICs. In SSA, access to modern anti-seizure medicine is limited for most people, and a lack of awareness and education concerning epileptic seizures and epilepsy, combined with traditional beliefs and subsequent social stigma, leads to underdiagnosis and a large treatment gap of the disease [28].

The diagnosis and treatment of epilepsy in paediatric populations in SSA is further complicated by a lack of adequate healthcare infrastructure, such as shortages of trained health workers, limited diagnostic and treatment equipment, sociocultural beliefs, and the stigma associated with epilepsy [29–31].

Epilepsy poses a significant public health challenge in Sub-Saharan Africa, especially among children, due to limited specialist capacity, inadequate diagnostic infrastructure, and delayed access to care. Addressing these gaps requires multifaceted interventions, including early neurological screening, strengthening training of practitioners in pediatric neurology and neurosurgery, improving access to diagnostic tools such as electroencephalography (EEG) and neuroimaging, and establishing efficient referral systems for timely specialized care. Strengthening these areas is essential to improving outcomes for children with epilepsy in the region [14, 32].

Pathogenesis of Neurocysticercosis and epilepsy in children in SSA

Neuroparasitosis refers to parasitic infections that affect the nervous system. In contrast with most bacterial and viral infections of the brain, parasitic infection usually affects the host brain chronically. In SSA, exposure to parasitic infections has been associated with an increased prevalence of epilepsy and, importantly, co-infection with multiple parasites has also been found to increase the risk of epilepsy [33].

Neurocysticercosis (NCC) is the most extensively documented parasitic infection of the central nervous system and represents a major cause of acquired epilepsy, particularly in endemic regions. Although other parasitic diseases, such as cerebral malaria, onchocerciasis, and toxoplasmosis [34], have been linked to seizure disorders, NCC remains the most clearly defined in terms of its neuropathological mechanisms, diagnostic profile, and public health impact [34, 35]. Studies conducted in SSA have shown that exposure to helminths, such as Toxocara canis, Onchocerca volvulus, and Taenia solium, which follow severe cases of Plasmodium falciparum malaria, is associated with epilepsy [36]. The relationship between Toxoplasma gondii infection and epilepsy was investigated in a single study conducted in SSA [37]. The study suggested a potential association; however, this relationship may be confounded by co-infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).

In some SSA regions, parasitic infections are believed to cause up to 27% of cases of pediatric epilepsy [38], with NCC the most recognized and most frequent cause of seizures and epilepsy. Indeed, a study done in Madagascar found that NCC was the most common pattern of cysticercosis distribution in humans and was responsible for pediatric morbidity, causing more than 50% of epilepsy cases [39].

In patients with NCC, initial infection with the parasite leads to invasion of the cortical brain regions, inducing local inflammation, edema, and eventually calcification of affected tissues. This site selection and parasite development lead to the formation of a focal epileptogenic network capable of producing recurrent unprovoked seizures [40]. In addition to this process, parasite-induced epileptogenesis can also arise from an autoimmune phenomenon triggered by the parasite, but this is seen in instances where cortical involvement is either absent or minimal [40–41]. These insights provide avenues for early, targeted therapeutic and preventive interventions for preventing the deleterious long-term consequences of epilepsy in children in SSA [42]. Additionally, improved understanding of the process of epileptogenesis in parasitic diseases will have many implications for the development of trials to halt or delay the onset of epilepsy in NCC [40].

One health interventions

The One Health approach is an integrated framework that recognizes the interdependence of human, animal, and environmental health. Neuroparasitic infections such as T. solium are often zoonotic and exemplify the intricate interplay between human, animal, and environmental health. Taenia solium has a 2-host life cycle in which humans are the definitive host and pigs are typically the intermediate host. Pigs, serving as intermediate hosts, acquire cysticercosis upon ingesting eggs passed in human feces. The cysticerci lodge in striated muscle of the pig, and the transmission cycle is perpetuated when undercooked pork is consumed by humans (Fig. 2) [43]. Given the transmission dynamics of T. solium, control strategies will require a multisectoral strategy that encompasses improved sanitation, food safety regulations, community education, and dual human/veterinary interventions such as mass drug administration and pig vaccination [44]. This one health approach is essential for the effective and sustained control of NCC and subsequently reducing the burden of epilepsy in endemic regions [45].

In resource-limited settings, inadequate sanitation and hygiene, free-roaming pigs, and environmental degradation that increases pig-human interactions create ideal conditions for the persistence of Taenia solium in the environment. These factors play a critical role in completing the parasite’s transmission cycle and are commonly found within SSA [46, 47].

While no large-scale reductions in neurocysticercosis prevalence have been conclusively demonstrated in SSA using a One Health approach, ongoing initiatives in other regions, such as integrated community education, improved sanitation, pig management, and healthcare capacity-building programs, have shown promising early results in raising awareness and improving case detection [31].

For instance, in Salamá, Honduras, an integrated community-based intervention combining health education, sanitation improvements, and pig management led to a significant decline in epilepsy cases attributed to NCC, from 36.9% in 1997 to 13.9% in 2005 [47, 48]. Similarly, in Peru, a large-scale program integrating human mass drug administration, pig vaccination, and porcine anthelmintic treatment successfully interrupted Taenia solium transmission in 105 of 107 intervention villages [49]. These findings underscore the promise of One Health strategies for controlling NCC and its associated epilepsy burden, and they highlight the potential for adapting such integrated interventions encompassing human, animal, and environmental health measures to the unique contexts of SSA.

Neurocysticercosis-driven epilepsy in children underscores significant health inequities, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where Taenia solium is endemic [50]. In these regions, inadequate sanitation, limited access to healthcare, and insufficient public health education contribute to higher infection rates among children. The disease is a leading cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic areas, imposing substantial social and economic burdens on affected families. Children from marginalized communities are disproportionately affected, as they are more likely to be exposed to environments conducive to the transmission of the parasite. Evidence suggests that parasites are the major preventable risk factor for epilepsy in low- and middle-income countries [40].

Barriers and solutions to implementing the one health approach in SSA and strategies for improved adoption

While the One Health concept has widely gained momentum and the approach is accepted as the best strategy for addressing global health challenges, it is not yet significantly operationalized in many SSA countries due to weak implementation capacity, socio-economic challenges, entrenched cultural practices, inadequate surveillance, and fragmented policies that hinder effective multisectoral collaboration [39].

Implementing the One Health approach for epilepsy control in children across Sub-Saharan Africa faces multifaceted challenges, such as COVID-19, which has worsened the treatment gap for epilepsy in SSA, reinforcing the need for robust, integrated health strategies like One Health [51]. In addition to COVID-19, political instability, cultural diversity, and variable health systems complicate coordinated health interventions [52]. Additionally, poor data quality resulting from inadequate surveillance, medical recording, and reporting systems hampers effective disease monitoring and response [53]. The high cost and limited accessibility of diagnostic tools further exacerbate these issues, making it difficult to accurately diagnose and manage epilepsy in pediatric populations [54]. These factors collectively hinder the applicability of the One Health approach in the region.

A major solution to these barriers is ensuring collaboration between Ministries of Health in different regions of the sub-Saharan countries in association with government and non-governmental organisations, all of which can play a pivotal role in effecting changes [24, 25]. Involving a top-down approach incorporating national governments, international alliances, and local authorities at all levels is effective [55]. Along with physical changes such as improvements in sanitation and animal husbandry, sociological steps need to be incorporated, which raise awareness, such as radio broadcasts, the distribution of educational posters in schools and communities, and behaviour change activities that mitigate zoonotic transmission.

The role of neurological medical specialists in promoting one health strategies for disease prevention

Neurological medical specialists, especially neurosurgeons and neurologists in endemic countries, could play a crucial role in promoting One Health strategies for disease prevention, particularly regarding zoonotic diseases and those affecting the nervous system. With regards to surveillance and data collection, neurologists are often the first point of contact for patients with neurological symptoms, some of which may be caused by zoonotic diseases. These specialists can identify unusual patterns of neurological illness and report them to public health authorities, contributing to early detection and outbreak prevention, and they also play a critical role in managing epilepsy-related conditions, such as posttraumatic hydrocephalus, which may contribute to the seizure burden in SSA [56, 57].

Despite the scarcity of neurological care resources in SSA and the need for policy-driven solutions to enhance specialist involvement in epilepsy management [58], Neurologists and neurosurgeons play an essential role in neurocysticercosis (NCC) control in Sub-Saharan Africa by enabling accurate diagnosis and effective management through interpretation of neuroimaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [26, 59, 60]. Their clinical expertise facilitates the precise identification of cystic lesions and calcifications critical for guiding treatment decisions in resource-limited endemic settings [60]. Beyond diagnostic contributions, neurosurgeons assist in definitive NCC diagnosis by obtaining tissue biopsies during surgical procedures. These samples undergo histopathological and molecular analyses, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing, which have been instrumental in confirming NCC in diagnostically challenging cases [61, 62].

Capacity building through specialist training programs further strengthens NCC control efforts. Enhanced training and infrastructure improvements have been reported to increase access to essential diagnostic tools, thereby improving clinical management and surveillance of NCC-associated epilepsy in endemic regions [63, 64]. Additionally, the CURE Children’s Hospital of Uganda has established the region’s only pediatric neurosurgery training program, significantly enhancing local expertise in managing NCC-associated epilepsy [65]. Further, both specialists can collaborate with veterinarians, epidemiologists, and other public health professionals to investigate outbreaks of zoonotic diseases affecting the nervous system. The medical staff can share their clinical expertise and data to better understand the disease and develop effective prevention and control strategies [62].

Neurologists and neurosurgeons have a pivotal role in addressing the neurological consequences of zoonotic diseases through public education, clinical advocacy, and policy engagement. By disseminating knowledge on transmission risks and preventive measures such as responsible animal ownership, wildlife conservation, and improved sanitation and hygiene, they contribute to reducing the incidence of neurologically relevant infections. Moreover, their involvement is essential in promoting the integration of One Health principles into health systems, recognizing the intrinsic links between human, animal, and environmental health. However, the success of One Health strategies in controlling neuroparasitic infections is contingent upon the inclusion and prioritization of neurosurgical care within health policies and system-strengthening efforts. Without this integration, the diagnosis, management, and long-term care of affected individuals remain critically undermined [63].

Conclusion

In Sub-Saharan Africa, epilepsy linked to parasitic infections is still a public health concern. Leveraging the One Health approach by integrating efforts across sectors can reduce the prevalence of the disease and increase the resilience of the community. Effective treatment for epilepsy will reduce the lifelong stigma associated with the disease for the affected patient. It should be noted that parasites are among the most important risk factors for epilepsy, and gliosis plus calcific cortical lesions are the main determinants of epilepsy among children in Sub-Saharan Africa. Effective control of neurocysticercosis (NCC) necessitates a One Health approach that unites human, animal, and environmental health strategies. Core interventions include robust meat inspection, community and professional education on transmission prevention, equitable healthcare access, and targeted control measures such as anthelmintic treatment and pig vaccination. Embedding neurological specialists, particularly neurologists and neurosurgeons, within this framework is critical, not only for accurate diagnosis and management of NCC-related neurological disorders, but also for shaping clinical training, informing public health policy, and directing strategic focus in endemic regions.
