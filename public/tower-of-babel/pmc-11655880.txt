A chromosome-level genome assembly of tropical purple sea urchin Heliocidaris crassispina
Authors: Yang Liu, Ting Chen, Bo Ma, Jiayue Yin, Chunhua Ren, Xiao Jiang, Yanhong Wang, Wenjie Pan, Jiasheng Huang, Suzhong Yu, Peng Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Heliocidaris crassispina is a tropical sea urchin that holds both ecological and economic value and serves as an ideal experimental animal. This study first employed a combination of PacBio HiFi and Hi-C sequencing data to present a high-quality, chromosome-level assembly of H. crassispina genome. This assembled genome spanned 709.77 Mb, with a contig N50 length of 4.98 Mb and a scaffold N50 length of 33.23 Mb. The assembly was anchored on 21 pseudo-chromosomes, covering 98.32% of the genome. A total of 18,665 protein-coding genes were identified, and BUSCO analysis revealed a completeness score of 97.1%. Additionally, non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) were annotated, including miRNAs, rRNAs, tRNAs and snRNAs, providing a more complete functional understanding of the genome. The high-quality reference genome will enrich the current echinoderm genomic resources and provide a solid foundation for future studies on H. crassispina, including resource surveys, genomic breeding, and marine ranch management.

Background & Summary

Sea urchins, belong to the class Echinoidea1, are widely distributed on the seafloor of various oceans. The habitats, lifestyles, colors, and shapes of sea urchins exhibit a high diversity among different species. Based on their ability to consume benthic algae, sea urchins play a crucial ecological role in maintaining ecological balance between corals and macroalgae in tropical marine ecosystems2,3. However, the populations of some valuable sea urchins have been seriously depleted by overfishing of them. In recent years, the gonads of sea urchins have often considered as a rare delicacy on the table in coastal regions in the world, especially in East Asia, North America, Mediterranean countries and Australia4,5, whether served as fresh on the table or processed food6. Furthermore, sea urchins are rich in a variety of bioactive compounds7. Biological effects are present in organic and aqueous extracts from their gonads, spines, intestines, and body shells8. Thus, sea urchins have a high demand in Asia markets, serving not only as a food source but also as a traditional medicinal product.

H. crassispina (A. Agassiz, 1864) is a common benthic echinoderm in the South China Sea, particularly along the coast of Guangdong Province, China9 (Fig. 1A). In recent years, the species has expanded its distribution range northward to Toga Bay along the Oga Peninsula in Japan mainly due to ocean warming10, leading to a wide latitudinal distribution from 39.93 N to 19.00 N. H. crassispina is the most economically valuable species of sea urchins in southern coastal areas in China due to its big size and high gonad index11, which also lead to the sharp decline of wild resource of H. crassispina along with the rise of marine tourism12. To our knowledge, sea urchins serve as model organisms in developmental biology and immunology15–17. Since 2006, the genomes of several sea urchins have been sequenced18–20. In this study, we utilized a combination of PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) sequencing and chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) sequencing technologies to generate a chromosome-level draft genome of H. crassispina (Fig. 1C). The final genome assembly is 709.77 Mb in size, with scaffold N50 sizes of 33.23 Mb. We annotated the gene structure and predicted 18,665 genes. By comparing the protein sequences to public databases, 97.6% of the genes could be predicted to have a function. The assembly of genomes at the chromosomal level will enhance our understanding for the life processes of echinoderms21. This will be of significant benefit in the future, particularly in genomic breeding projects22, management in marine ranches, and germplasm restoration.

Methods

Sample collection

Mature sea urchin was obtained by diver scuba diving in Daya Bay of Shenzhen City (22.56 N, 114.54 E), China. By observing the microstructures of the gonad stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) under a light microscope, the sex of the sea urchin was determined (Fig. 1B). Genomic library construction and long-read and short-read whole genome sequencing were performed on female gonad tissue. Furthermore, various tissues (muscle, spines, and tube feet) were collected for transcriptome sequencing, which aided in structural annotation. Total RNA was extracted from each tissue sample using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and the integrity and concentration of the RNA samples were evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and a NanoDrop™ 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA), respectively.

Library construction, and sequencing

For short-read whole genome sequencing, the genomic DNA samples that passed the quality inspection were randomly fragmented into fragments of approximately 350 bp. The libraries were constructed following polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification using NEB Next® UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) and double-end sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, resulting in a clean reads 61.95 Gb. For long-read sequencing, the high-quality DNA (main band >30 kb) was randomly fragmented into fragments (15–18 kb) and libraries were constructed using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, USA) and sequenced with circular consensus sequencing (CCS) mode on the PacBio Sequel IIe platform. A total of 24.18 Gb CCS data were obtained with a mean length of 15.92 kb. For Hi-C sequencing, libraries were constructed using standard protocols as previously reported and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform23, resulting in the acquisition of a total of 85.54 Gb of Hi-C clean reads. Furthermore, the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) was employed to prepare RNA sequencing libraries, and the quality of the libraries was evaluated using an Agilent 5400 system (Agilent, USA). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted to quantify the libraries, yielding a concentration of 1.5 nM. Chromosome-level genome assembly and quality assessment

Analysis of the genome characteristics of H. crassispina based on short-reads sequencing data obtained from the Illumina platform24. Based on 17-mer frequency distribution was conducted using the Jellyfish (v2.2.7)25, and the genome size calculated by the formula K-mer number/depth was about 680.55 Mb, the heterozygosity rate was 1.31%, and the repeated content was 43.29%. HiFi reads were obtained using the PacBio platform, data quality control and assembly were performed using CCS (v6.0.0) algorithm and Hifiasm (0.18.7)26,27, respectively, with the default parameters. Following the assembly process, the contig-level genome assembly spanned 709.75 Mb, contained 380 contigs, had a N50 length of 5.03 Mb, and the longest contig was 17.23 Mb (Table 2). Furthermore, data obtained from Hi-C sequencing was employed to anchor contigs to chromosomes. The contigs were anchored to the near-chromosome level using ALLHIC (0.9.8)28, and then Juicebox and manual correction were employed to obtain a chromosome-level genome29. The approximately 697.87 Mb long sequence was anchored on 21 pseudo-chromosomes, with an anchoring rate of 98.32% (Fig. 2A). The assembly results were evaluated using BUSCO (v5.2.2)30, and the analysis revealed that 97.1% of the complete BUSCO genes were detected in the final assembly. Moreover, we performed a genome alignment between the H. crassispina with two species belonging to the family Echinometridae (Heliocidaris erythrogramma and Heliocidaris tuberculata)31,32, and MCscanX with parameters “-e 1e-05 -s 5” was employed to identify syntenic regions33. Repetitive elements and Non-coding RNA annotation

For repetitive sequence annotation, RepeatModeler (v2.0.3) was used to de novo annotate and build a repeat library34, and then merged with Repbase to form the final comprehensive repeat library35. Subsequently, RepeatMasker (v4.1.2-p1) was then used to annotate the repeats of H. crassispina36. Finally, repetitive sequences accounted for 44.76% of the total genome length (Table 3), of which DNA transposons accounted for the largest proportion (25.67% of the genome), followed by long terminal repeats (LTR, 8.63%), long interspersed nuclear elements (LINE, 6.77%) and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINE, 0.38%). Gene prediction and functional assignment

We adopted a strategy combining de novo prediction, homology-based prediction and RNA-Seq assisted prediction to obtain a fully annotated gene set of H. crassispina. For de novo prediction, Augustus (v3.5)39 and SNAP (v2013.11.29)40 were used to identify gene structures. For homology-based prediction, protein sequences for the following species were downloaded from NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and Echinobase (https://www.echinobase.org/echinobase/): purple sea urchin (Strongylocentrotus purpuratus), Japanese sea cucumber (Apostichopus japonicus), and sea cucumber (Holothuria leucospilota) (Fig. 4A). These were then aligned with the genome of H. crassispina using tBLASTn (v2.2.26)41, and the gene structures were predicted using Genewise (v2.4.1)42. For RNA-Seq assisted prediction, RNA-Seq data from different tissues of H. crassispina were assembled using Trinity (v2.8.5)43 and aligned to the genome using Hisat (v2.2.1)44 in order to detect gene structures. Concurrently, the Program to Assemble Spliced Alignments (PASA) was utilised to identify potential coding regions. Subsequently, EvidenceModeler (EVM, v1.1.1)45 was employed to integrate the genes predicted by the three methods, thereby generating a non-redundant reference gene set. After a series of iterative processes, we were able to obtain a gene set comprising 18,665 protein-coding genes (Table 5). The protein sequences were then aligned with Swiss-Prot using Blastp (threshold E value ≤ 1e−5), and InterProScan (v5.39)46 was employed to search public databases (including ProDom, PRINTS, Pfam, SMRT, PANTHER and PROSITE) for functional annotation (Fig. 4B). The annotated gene set was retrieved to obtain GO (Gene ontology) terms and mapped to KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathways. Data Records

All the raw sequencing data of H. crassispina in this study have been uploaded to the NCBI BioProject database under PRJNA1122378. The genomic PacBio sequencing data were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database with accession number SRR2958656747. The genomic Illumina sequencing data were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database with accession number SRR2943006848. The Hi-C sequencing data were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database with accession number SRR29430433-SRR2943043649–52. The transcriptomic sequencing data were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database with accession number SRR29433402- SRR2943341053–61. The final chromosome assembly has been deposited in GenBank with accession number GCA_044090015.162. The chromosome assembly, annotation coding sequences sequence, protein sequence and H. crassispina annotation files were deposited at Figshare with doi number 10.6084/m9.figshare.2632423963.

Technical Validation

Nucleic acid quality validation

The RNA samples utilized in this study were subjected to analysis via Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in order to ascertain their concentration. Furthermore, agarose gel electrophoresis and Agilent 5400 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) were employed in order to evaluate the quality and integrity of the RNA, respectively. The concentration of the DNA samples was determined via a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), while their integrity and quality were assessed through agarose gel electrophoresis.

Evaluation of the genome assembly

To ascertain the completeness of the assembled sequence, the completeness of the H. crassispina genome was evaluated using the Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO, v5.2.2) with the metazoa_odb10 databases. The results demonstrated that the genome assembly encompassed 97.1% of the complete BUSCO genes (Table 6). Subsequently, the clean short reads were mapped to the assembled genome using the BWA (v0.7.8)64. The results demonstrated that the mapping rate of the short reads reached 98.06%, while the coverage rate on the genome was 99.91%. Finally, Merqury (v1.3) was used to assess the accuracy of the genome sequence65. The evaluation yielded a consensus quality value (QV) of 42.51.
