Retrospective study on transfusion-related acute lung injury after intraoperative and postoperative blood transfusion in patients at a single medical center
Authors: Jing Liu, Xin Ye, Huaqin Liang, Shuangfeng Xie, Yanhong Chen, Dawei Chen, Jing Deng, Xiuzhang Xu, Haoqiang Ding, Yangkai Chen, Wenjie Xia, Yili Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Introduction Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) is a life-threatening syndrome that occurs within six hours of a blood transfusion. Materials and methods The objective of this study was to investigate the characteristics, clinical manifestations, outcomes, and risk factors of TRALI in patients who underwent surgery at a single medical centre of China. The retrospective study included 5,846 patients who received intraoperative and postoperative blood transfusions. All donors implicated in TRALI cases were tested for anti-HLA class I and II antibodies using LABScreen Multi assay and for granulocyte antibodies using granulocyte agglutination test. Results Four patients (4/5846, 0.068%) developed TRALI, with specific incidence of 0.26/100,000 blood product; 9/10,000 units of packed red blood cells (PRBC) (1/1,065); 16/10,000 units of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) (1/645); and 5/10,000 units of Apheresis platelets (AP)(1/2,120). In one patient, TRALI was found to be secondary to a human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I antibody (anti-HLA-A*11); in another, it was secondary to an HLA class II antibody (anti-HLA-DRB*04). The one with possibility of reverse TRALI was identified as a result of anti-A*24. One of the four patients was non-immunologically mediated TRALI. Conclusions To reduce the incidence and prevent TRALI, it is recommended that male donors and female donors who have never been pregnant or have tested negative for HLA antibodies since their last pregnancy should be selected for blood donation as much as possible. In addition, it is important to initiate clinical intervention and treatment at an early stage in order to improve the patient's prognosis.Keywords: Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), Blood transfusion; non-immune mediated TRALI, Reverse TRALI

Introduction

Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) is characterised by the occurrence of respiratory insufficiency and hypoxemia within six hours of blood or blood products transfusion. Imaging reveals bilateral fluffy infiltrates, which are consistent with pulmonary edema [1, 2]. TRALI has been observed in association with most blood products, apheresis platelet (AP), fresh frozen plasma(FFP) and whole blood [3, 4]. However, high plasma volume products, including FFP and certain platelet products like platelet-rich plasma (PRP), platelet concentrate (PC)and AP, have been most frequently implicated [5–8]. FFP is the most common cause which was reported in 44% of patients [9]. Since the advent of male-only plasma, AP has emerged as the transfusion product associated with the highest TRALI risk [10, 11]. Although TRALI occurs with relatively low frequency, it continues to rank among the most significant causes of transfusion-related mortality. According to US Food and Drug Administration surveillance data (2016–2020), TRALI was responsible for 21% of all transfusion-associated fatalities [12], which demands serious clinical consideration.

The pathogenesis of TRALI is generally explained by a two-hit mechanism. The first hit comprises risk factors, such as inflammation, in the patient receiving transfusion, and factors within the transfused product, such as anti-leukocyte antibodies or biological response modifiers (e.g., lipids), are assumed to cause the second hit [2, 13]. TRALI syndrome is primarily driven by two distinct mechanisms: immune-mediated and nonimmune pathways. Immune-mediated TRALI is caused by antibodies against human leukocyte antigens (HLA) classes I and II and human neutrophil antigens (HNA), particularly HLA class II and HNA-3a antibodies [14, 15], which were present in the donor’s serum and react with the recipient’s leukocytes [16, 17]. On the other hand, nonimmune-mediated TRALI can be attributed to transfusion of biologically active compounds, which were accumulated in stored blood components; examples of such were bioactive lipids, proinflammatory cytokines, and platelet microparticles with high procoagulant activity [18]. While donor-derived leukoagglutinating antibodies are hypothesized to play a central role in the pathogenesis of TRALI, a few cases of TRALI mediated by recipient-derived antibodies have been reported.soluble HLA antigens in RBC products could result in antigen/antibody complexes that may induce neutrophil activation, leading to reverse TRALI in the patient [19–21].

TRALI represents a serious transfusion complication and is among the leading causes of transfusion-related morbidity and mortality in most developed countries. Considering the inevitable inflammatory response during the perioperative period, patients who receive blood transfusions have relatively high risk of TRALI, The mortality rate is estimated to range from 5% to 25%, with a higher mortality rate of approximately 47% observed among the patients who require intensive care unit (ICU) admission and mechanical ventilation support [22–24]. In China, however, clinician awareness of TRALI remains limited, resulting in underreporting of cases and a lack of epidemiological prevalence data. To facilitate the development of targeted prevention and treatment strategies, this study sought to characterize the frequency and clinical patterns of TRALI among surgical patients at a single medical center of China.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective analysis was conducted on patients who received blood transfusion during and after surgery under regional or general anaesthesia from November 2020 to May 2022 at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, which is a large public hospital in Guangzhou, China. This study obtained the requisite approval and was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the hospital.

The analysis was conducted using data extracted from the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital’s digital medical records system, which comprised standardized forms and database cards containing demographic data, patient clinical characteristics and comorbidities, anaesthetic techniques, and perioperative anaesthetic management details. These details include monitoring, drugs or fluids administered, transfusion of blood products, and minor or major perioperative complications. The study excluded patients under the age of 18 years; those who were not transfused, and included those who had the risk factors for acute lung injury, such as pneumonia, sepsis, and trauma.

Serological analysis

All donors associated with TRALI cases were subjected to serological testing for anti-HLA class I and II antibodies using LABScreen Multi assay, and for granulocyte antibodies, utilising granulocyte agglutination test. The HLA antibody class I and class II specificity testing was conducted in accordance with the LIFECODES LSA Single Antigen protocol. The subsequent analysis of the results was conducted through the Luminex platform. HNA typing was conducted using a polymerase chain reaction method. HLA antigen typing was performed using the HLA High Resolution Genotyping Assay HLA-A, B, DRB1, DQB1 Gene PCR-SBT Typing Kit (Texas BioGene Inc., Texas, USA). All assays were conducted in an International Society of Blood Transfusion granulocyte serology reference laboratory. All the experimental methods and reagents used were as follows:

HLA class I and HLA class II antibody detection

HLA antibody screening employed LIFECODES LMX beads per manufacturer’s instructions ((Tepnel Lifecodes Molecular Diagnostics, Stamford, CT, USA)). Positivity required bead-set reactivity meeting all criteria [25]. Samples positive in LMX were further tested with LIFECODES LSA TM class I and class II. Anti-HLA with a mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) >2500 was considered positive.

HNA antibody detection and GIFT

Neutrophil antibodies (HNA-1a, −1b and − 2) were screened by the LABScreen assay (One Lambda Inc.,Canoga Park, CA, USA) and rapid enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using recombinant HNA-1a, −1b, −1c and − 2 antigens. Flow cytometric analysis of granulocytes was conducted using both direct and reverse GIFT protocols, with identical technical specifications maintained between methods [19, 26, 27].

Analysis of HNA antigens by genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from Ethylene Diamine Tetraacetic Acid anti-coagulated blood using a QIA Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Blood samples were typed for HNA-1, −3, −4 and − 5 antigens using a HNA Ready Gene kit (Inno Train, Inc., Taunus, Germany).

Flow cytometry analysis of CD36 expression on platelets and monocytes

CD36 antigen expression on platelets and monocytes was evaluated by flow cytometry following established protocols [28]. In brief, aliquots of 50 µl platelets suspension were incubated with 10 µl fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labelled anti-human CD36 monoclonal antibody (mab FA6-152; Immunotech, Marseille, France) for 30 min at room temperature. For the analysis of CD36 expression on monocytes, 100 µl of Ethylene Diamine Tetraacetic Acid (EDTA) anticoagulated blood were incubated with 10 µl FITC-labelled mab FA6-152 and phycoerythrin (PE)-labelled anti-CD14 (mab RMO52, Immunotech) at room temperature for 20 min. Washed cells were analyzed on a BD FACSCanto II.

HLA antigen

Genomic DNA was extracted from EDTA anti-coagulated blood using a QIA Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Blood samples were typed for HLA-A, B, C, DR*B1, DQ*B1. HLA typing was performed using the HLA High Resolution Genotyping Assay HLA-A, B, C, DR*B1, DQ*B1 Gene PCR-SBT Typing Kit (Texas BioGene Inc., Texas, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using statistical software package SPSS 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All variables were subjected to descriptive statistical analyses, including calculation of the mean and standard deviation for continuous variables.

The details of the four TRALI cases

All four patients developed dyspnoea and hypoxemia within 6 h after initiating blood transfusion during the perioperative period under inhalational general anesthesia. All patients exhibited the diagnostic criteria for TRALI (i.e., PaO₂/FiO₂ <300 mmHg and imaging findings of patchy hyper dense shadows in both lungs) [19]. In this case series, Cases 2 and 4 developed TRALI during the postoperative period, and subsequently developed fever, with temperatures of 37.6 °C and 37.9 °C, respectively. In contrast, Cases 1 and 3 developed TRALI during the intraoperative period, each demonstrating a ≥ 1 °C increase in core body temperature from baseline (35.6 °C to 36.8 °C and 35.8 °C to 36.9 °C, respectively). After receiving oxygen therapy and ventilator support varied among these patients, the dyspnea and hypoxemia were relieved. Details of these four patients were provided in Table 5.

Case 1 and 2 received both PRBCs and FFP. Following disease onset, both cases developed hypotension and bilateral pulmonary exudative lesions. With mechanical ventilation support, they achieved complete TRALI resolution and were successfully extubated within five days of symptom onset.

Case 3 received both FFP and AP. Five minutes after initiating intraoperative platelet transfusion, the patient acutely developed hypotension and bradycardia accompanied by hypoxemia with an decreased oxygenation index. This progression resulted in severe respiratory failure with bilateral diffuse pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography. Despite intensive care management, Case 3 ultimately succumbed to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), manifesting as acute respiratory failure (PaO₂/FiO₂ 74 mmHg), hepatic impairment (total bilirubin 353.7 µmol/L), acute kidney injury (creatinine 262 µmol/L), circulatory failure requiring vasopressor support (blood pressure 93/47 mmHg), coagulopathy (platelets 18 × 10⁹/L), and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

Case 4 received PRBCs exclusively. Approximately 1 h post-transfusion of 2 units, the patient developed 37.9 °C with oxygen desaturation to 92%. Concurrently, the patient manifested tachycardia and hypotension. Chest Computed Tomography imaging demonstrated bilateral lower lobe atelectasis with consolidation. Following administration of high-flow oxygen via face mask, oxygen saturation rapidly normalized to 95–98%.

Discussion

TRALI was one of the leading causes of transfusion-related morbidity. Importantly, approximately 50% of all blood product transfusions take place in the perioperative environment [29, 30]. Surgery represents a significant risk factor for TRALI, warranting particular attention to perioperative transfusion recipients. In this study, the incidence of TRALI among patients who underwent surgery was found to be 0.068%, which was slightly lower than that 0.1% reported in Tsai et al. [31]. In Queensland, Australia, the incidence of TRALI was 1 per 130,000 blood products issued between 2006 and 2019. Plasma products carried the highest risk (1:13,000), followed by platelet (1:21,000) and red blood cell (RBC) products (1:42,000) [32]. Our study, however, revealed significantly higher risks: 1:1,065 for RBCs, 1:2,120 for platelets, and 1:645 for plasma transfusions. Despite these differing proportions, plasma consistently demonstrated the highest TRALI risk.Reported TRALI mortality ranges from 5 to 25% in the literature [33]. Our observed mortality rate of 25% compares with the 17% reported by SHOT (2003–2017) [34].

In our study, all four patients exhibited the diagnostic criteria for TRALI (i.e., PaO₂/FiO₂ <300 mmHg and imaging findings of patchy hyper dense shadows in both lungs) after receiving blood transfusions during or after surgery [35]. Moreover, no cases of congestive heart failure or valvular disease were recorded, and cardiogenic pulmonary edema was excluded based on the basis of clinical symptoms and laboratory test results (N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide(NT-proBNP) < 1,200 pg/mL, Table 3). Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload(TACO) was excluded on the grounds of a slight intraoperative negative fluid balance, limited diuretic efficacy and the absence of an increase in blood pressure at the time of symptom presentation [36–38].The anaesthetic records indicated exudation without incident eliminated the possibility of negative-pressure pulmonary edema secondary to laryngospasm or upper airway obstruction during exudation.

Cases 1 and 2 received TRALI diagnoses, with both cases linked to multiparous donors exhibiting HLA antibodies - one demonstrating HLA class I specificity (anti-HLA-A*11) and the other HLA class II (anti-HLA-DRB*04). Epidemiological studies estimate that 50–80% of TRALI cases result from transfusion of blood products containing HNA or HLA antibodies, with these antibodies being predominantly identified in blood components from previously pregnant female donors [39]. Our prior investigation of Chinese blood donors revealed significantly elevated HLA and HNA antibody levels in women with pregnancy history [40]. Furthermore, multiple studies demonstrate a positive correlation between the number of pregnancies and the incidence of HLA antibody positivity [11, 41, 42]. As emphasized by Kopko et al. [43], failure to recognize TRALI may result in continued utilization of blood products from high-risk donors. This practice can precipitate additional TRALI episodes in susceptible recipients.

In case 3, despite the absence of immunogenic antibodies, a comprehensive analysis of the test results and clinical presentation, as well as the exclusion of other potential causes of dyspnoea and pulmonary edema, the clinical manifestations and laboratory tests were consistent with the diagnosis of TRALI, in accordance with the 2019 consensus on its redefinition led to the reasonable diagnosis of non-immunologically-induced TRALI [36]. While 80% of TRALI cases involve HLA/HNA antibodies, the remaining 20% were attributed to biological response mediators (e.g., lipids, extracellular vesicles) [36, 44]. Multiple experimental studies have demonstrated that prolonged storage of blood components was associated with non-immune/biological response modifier (BRM)-mediated TRALI. Stored blood products contain injurious soluble factors that significantly increase TRALI risk compared to fresh products [45–48]. The patient received AP that had been stored for 4 days. In this case, the potential contribution of platelet storage duration to TRALI development cannot be excluded, as experimental evidence demonstrates that TRALI severity increases with platelet storage time and prolonged storage may elevate TRALI risk [48].

This case 4 demonstrates reverse TRALI mediated by patient-derived anti-HLA-A*24 antibodies. A multiparous woman with pre-existing HLA antibodies developed respiratory distress, hypoxemia, and bilateral pulmonary infiltrates following transfusion. Symptomatic resolution with high-flow oxygen therapy confirmed the TRALI diagnosis. Pathophysiologically, soluble HLA antigens in RBC products may form immune complexes that activate neutrophils, potentially inducing reverse TRALI. Case 4 exhibited significant pre-transfusion neutrophilia (10.11 × 10⁹/L, 92.7%). These findings suggest that HLA antigen/antibody complexes in blood components could activate recipient neutrophils, particularly when combined with cytokine release from transfused monocytes, ultimately leading to reverse TRALI [19–21, 48]. This supports neutrophilia may have increased susceptibility to lung injury via enhanced response to immune complexes and co-stimulatory signals from transfused monocytes, consistent with findings that “neutrophil priming and HLA antibody interactions are pivotal in TRALI pathogenesis” [49]. These findings corroborate the established paradigm that HLA-mediated polymorphonuclear neutrophil activation constitutes a central pathogenic mechanism in TRALI [50].

The incidence of immune-mediated TRALI has significantly declined in Western countries following implementation of male-predominant plasma strategies [11, 51]. However, China has not yet adopted this approach. We recommend preferentially selecting male donors and female donors without pregnancy history or with confirmed HLA antibody negativity post-pregnancy as primary blood donor populations. Concurrently, increased clinical awareness of TRALI among practitioners was essential. The current study contributes to elucidating TRALI pathogenesis and guiding the development of preventive measures tailored to China’s transfusion medicine landscape. Moving forward, we will continue to pursue both clinical and basic science research on TRALI to make meaningful contributions to its prevention and clinical management.

Conclusions

To minimize TRALI risk, blood donations should preferentially be obtained from male donors or female donors without pregnancy history or with confirmed HLA antibody negativity following pregnancy. For patients at high risk of TRALI, transfusion of blood products with shorter storage duration was clinically recommended. Prompt recognition and management were crucial for improving clinical outcomes.
