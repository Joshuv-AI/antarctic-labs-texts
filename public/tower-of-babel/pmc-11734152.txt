Multimodality Approach to a Neonate With a Pericardial Mass and a Hemorrhagic Pericardial Effusion
Authors: Marc Delaney, Kali Hopkins, Monica Chugh, Raghav Murthy, Nadine Choueiter
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 7-week-old infant with a 1-week history of a SARS-CoV2 respiratory infection presented with tachypnea. Cardiomegaly was noted on chest roentgenogram. Echocardiogram showed a large pericardial effusion, with tamponade physiology and a large pericardial mass. Serosanguinous fluid was noted on pericardiocentesis. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed a well-circumscribed vascular mass suggestive of a pericardial hemangioma. Serial echocardiograms showed reaccumulation of the pericardial effusion. The mass was surgically removed. A feeding vessel from the right coronary artery was ligated.Key Words: cardiac magnetic resonance, congenital cardiac mass, neonatal pericardial mass, tissue characterization

A 7-week-old infant presented with tachypnea to the emergency department at an outside hospital a week after being diagnosed with a SARS-CoV2 respiratory infection. Chest roentgenogram showed cardiomegaly. Echocardiogram showed a large pericardial effusion with right atrial free wall diastolic collapse, no structural cardiac abnormalities, normal biventricular systolic function, and a possible small mass in the pericardial space. Infant was transferred to our institution for pericardiocentesis and further management. On arrival, the patient was tachypneic (respiratory rate: 66 breaths/min) with oxygen saturation 100% on room air, hypertensive (blood pressure 117/72 mm Hg), and in sinus tachycardia (heart rate: 163 beats/min). Ancillary labs showed an elevated C-reactive protein (28.8 mg/L, lab normal: 0.0-5.0 mg/L), normal white blood cell count (10.4 × 103 cells/μL), no anemia (9.7 g/dL), and normal liver and renal function. Pericardiocentesis yielded 106 mL of hemorrhagic fluid. A pericardial drain was placed. Fluid analysis including microbiology and cytology showed increased red blood cells (223 × 103 cells/μL) without evidence of malignant cells or infection. Electrocardiogram-gated multidetector chest computed tomography with contrast was performed while drain was in place and showed the drain in the posterior and inferior pericardial space and a lobulated right anterior pericardial mass with a mildly hypodense area mixed with air closely associated with the heart. The etiology was thought to be either blood clot or mass. The drain was removed after 48 hours of no fluid reaccumulation. Follow-up echocardiogram after drain removal showed reaccumulation of the pericardial effusion and clearly demonstrated a non-mobile echogenic pericardial mass (1.9 × 1.3 cm) adherent to the right ventricular free wall at the base and extending anterior to the right atrioventricular junction (Video 1, Video 2, Video 3, Video 4, Video 5). The mass appeared relatively homogeneous with a slightly echobright rim and without signs of cystic cavities or a color signal on spectral Doppler imaging.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging study was performed under general anesthesia after removal of the pericardial drain. T1-weighted imaging showed a hyperintense border surrounding an isointense core, whereas T2-weighted images disclosed a hypointense border surrounding a hyperintense core, and gadolinium first-pass and late-enhancement imaging showed a central, hyperintense core (Figure 1, Video 5). Findings were consistent with a pericardial hemangioma or vascular abnormality. A large pericardial effusion was seen. The infant remained stable on room air without inotropic support. Given the rapid reaccumulation of fluid on serial echocardiograms after removal of the pericardial drain, the mass was surgically resected. Dissection revealed a feeding vessel arising from the distal right coronary artery that was ligated. Surgical pathology confirmed the diagnosis of hemangioma, with numerous capillary-sized blood vessels and a fibrous capsule (Figure 2). The patient had an uneventful recovery.

Multimodal Imaging of a Pericardial Mass

(A) Transthoracic echocardiogram (Echo) subcostal short-axis view at the midpapillary level showing a well-circumscribed pericardial hyperechoic mass attached to the right ventricular free wall. A large circumferential pericardial effusion is also seen. (B to D) Tissue characterization of the mass by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. The mass is hyperintense (rim more than core) on T1 imaging compared to the myocardium (B) and the core is hyperintense on T2 images (C), whereas the rim is hypointense compared to the myocardium except for its inferior aspect. The mass enhances on first-pass perfusion (FPP) (D) and on late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging 10 minutes after contrast administration (E). (F) Surgical specimen after excision of the mass.

Pathology Specimen of Excised Pericardial Mass

Microscopic hematoxylin and eosin staining at 1× (A) and 100× (B) power along with CD34 staining at 100× (C) power. The mass is well circumscribed and consists of predominantly capillary-sized blood vessels, consistent with hemangioma. Images courtesy of Dr Mary Beasley.

CMR with its tissue characterization capabilities is critical to differentiate neonatal tumors, and should be used in conjunction with echocardiography.1 Primary neonatal cardiac tumors are rare (incidence range: 0.001%-0.03%), and the most common neonatal pericardial tumor is a teratoma, which is often heterogeneous, avascular, and associated with nonbloody pericardial effusions.1 Pericardial hemangioma is a rare cause of a hemorrhagic effusion previously reported in adults and not yet in childhood.2, 3
