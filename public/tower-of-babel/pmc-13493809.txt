Dietary Guinea hen weed (Petiveria alliacea) leaf meal improves growth performance, redox balance and inflammatory indices in rabbits raised under hot-humid tropical conditions
Authors: Olugbenga David Oloruntola, Olawumi Boladale Olusegun, Ayotunde Nathanael Mafimidiwo, Adewale Isaac Olutumise, Samuel David Sudik, Deborah Adebukola Oloruntola, Bobola Philip Akinrinmade, Abiola Toyin Ajayi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heat stress severely compromises rabbit productivity in tropical environments by inducing metabolic dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation. 80 weaner rabbits with an average weight of 627.63 ± 6.56 were randomly assigned to 5 dietary treatments (16 rabbits/treatment; 2 rabbits/replicate): GLM0 (Basal diet with 0 g GLM/kg diet); GLM1 (Basal diet + 5 g GLM/kg diet); GLM2 (Basal diet + 10 g GLM/kg diet); GLM3 (Basal diet + 15 g GLM/kg diet) and GLM4 (Basal diet + 20 g GLM/kg diet). Dietary GLM significantly (P < 0.05) improved final body weight (2084.97 g in GLM0 vs. 2447.75 g in GLM4) and feed conversion ratio (3.29 vs. 2.77). Antioxidant enzyme activities in muscle increased markedly (P < 0.05), including superoxide dismutase (2.10 × 10⁻² to 5.80 × 10⁻² U/mg protein) and catalase (3.51 to 7.30 U/mg protein), while malondialdehyde concentration decreased (0.80 to 0.54 µM). Hepatic Tumour necrosis factor-α was significantly (P < 0.05) reduced (288.81 to 81.39 pg/mg protein), accompanied by an increase in interleukin-10 (52.37 to 139.05 pg/ml). Serum Aspartate Aminotransferase activity declined from 26.29 to 11.72 IU/L, and cholesterol decreased from 2.79 to 1.06 mmol/L. In conclusion, dietary GLM at 15–20 g/kg feed improved growth performance, antioxidant status, cytokine balance, and serum biochemical indices of rabbits raised under sustained hot-humid tropical conditions. However, the absence of a thermoneutral control limits direct inference regarding heat stress-specific alleviation.

Introduction

Rabbit production plays an increasingly important role in ensuring animal protein availability in tropical and subtropical regions due to rabbits’ high feed efficiency, rapid growth rate, and ability to utilise diverse feed resources1. However, the sustainability of rabbit production in hot-humid environments is severely constrained by chronic heat stress, which disrupts metabolic homeostasis, suppresses growth performance, compromises immune competence, and reduces product quality2. As global climate change intensifies thermal stress conditions, there is a growing urgency to develop nutritional strategies that enhance thermotolerance and physiological resilience in rabbits raised under tropical conditions.

Heat stress occurs when ambient temperature and humidity exceed the animal’s thermoneutral zone (18–21 °C), leading to impaired heat dissipation and activation of stress-response pathways. In rabbits, prolonged exposure to elevated temperatures triggers excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and cellular damage2. This redox imbalance is closely linked to the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, characterised by increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6)3. Collectively, these disturbances impair nutrient utilisation, compromise organ function—particularly of the liver and kidneys—and ultimately depress growth performance and carcass yield.

Conventional approaches to mitigating heat stress in livestock, including housing modifications and synthetic antioxidants, are often costly, environmentally unsustainable, or associated with regulatory and consumer concerns. Consequently, there is increasing interest in phytogenic feed additives as natural, safe, and multifunctional alternatives capable of enhancing antioxidant defense, modulating immune responses, and improving metabolic efficiency4. Plant-derived bioactive compounds such as phenolics, flavonoids, saponins, and alkaloids have been shown to scavenge free radicals, regulate inflammatory cascades, and support organ integrity under stress conditions5.

Guinea hen weed (Petiveria alliacea) is a medicinal plant widely distributed in tropical regions and traditionally used for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory properties. Phytochemical analyses of P. alliacea leaves have revealed high concentrations of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, tannins, saponins, and bioactive sulfur-containing constituents, all of which are implicated in redox regulation and immune modulation6. Beyond its general phytochemical richness, Petiveria alliacea is particularly recognised for its unique sulfur-containing metabolites. Studies have identified organosulfur compounds such as dibenzyl trisulfide, dibenzyl disulfide, and related thiosulfinates as major characteristic constituents of the plant, alongside benzylated derivatives and other bioactive secondary metabolites7[,8. These sulfur-based compounds, in addition to polyphenols and flavonoids, are believed to contribute substantially to the plant’s antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties8[,9. In animal production, emerging evidence suggests that P. alliacea supplementation may improve nutrient utilisation, health indices and immune responsiveness. For instance, aqueous root and leaf extracts of P. alliacea have been reported to exert antimicrobial, immunomodulatory and hepatomodulatory effects in growing pullets, while dietary inclusion has also been associated with improved digestibility and reduced parasite burden in West African dwarf goats7[,8[,9. Despite its extensive ethnomedicinal use and emerging biomedical evidence, the application of P. alliacea as a dietary phytogenic supplement in rabbit nutrition, particularly under heat stress conditions, remains poorly explored.

Existing studies on phytogenic interventions in rabbits have largely focused on growth performance or limited biochemical indices, with scant attention to the integrated interactions among oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokine networks, metabolic function, and organ health under thermal stress4[,10. Addressing these gaps is essential for translating phytogenic research into climate-resilient feeding strategies for rabbit production in the tropics.

Therefore, the present study evaluated the effects of graded dietary inclusion of Guinea hen weed leaf meal (GLM) on growth performance, carcass characteristics, oxidative status, inflammatory cytokine profiles, and serum biochemical indices of growing rabbits reared under naturally prevailing hot-humid tropical conditions. We hypothesised that GLM supplementation would improve antioxidant defence and inflammatory balance, thereby supporting physiological adaptation and productive performance of rabbits under sustained tropical thermal load. By integrating performance, biochemical, oxidative, and immunological endpoints, this study provides comprehensive insight into the functional potential of Guinea hen weed leaf meal as a natural nutritional strategy for improving rabbit resilience to thermal stress.

Results

Phytochemical richness and antioxidant potential of GLM

The phytochemical composition and antioxidant activity of Guinea hen weed leaf meal are presented in Table 1. The leaf meal contained appreciable concentrations of bioactive phytochemicals, with phenolic compounds being the most abundant (62.93 ± 0.04 mg GAE/g), followed by saponins (23.06 ± 0.05 mg/g) and tannins (16.15 ± 0.04 mg/g). The flavonoid content was 4.97 ± 0.04 mg QE/g, while alkaloids constituted 1.82 ± 0.06% of the leaf meal. In addition, the antioxidant potential of the leaf meal, as assessed by 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl radical scavenging activity, was 27.54 ± 0.05.

Environmental conditions during the experimental period

Daily ambient temperature recorded throughout the 56-day trial indicated persistent exposure of rabbits to a hot tropical environment (Fig. 1). Maximum ambient temperature remained consistently high, fluctuating approximately between 34 and 37 °C, with minor daily variations but no cooling period across the study duration. Minimum daily temperature also remained elevated, ranging roughly from 22 to 24.5 °C, indicating limited nocturnal thermal relief. A slight upward trend in both maximum and minimum temperatures was observed toward the latter half of the experimental period. The Temperature–Humidity Index (THI) values further confirmed continuous heat stress conditions (Fig. 2). Rabbit THI values mostly ranged between 27 and 29.5, exceeding the established rabbit thermal comfort threshold (THI ≈ 27) for the majority of the experimental period. From approximately day 30 onward, THI values consistently approached or remained close to the heat stress threshold (THI ≈ 30), indicating progressive thermal discomfort and sustained exposure of rabbits to heat stress conditions. Only brief fluctuations below the comfort threshold were observed in the early phase of the trial.

Growth performance and feed utilization

The effects of dietary supplementation with Guinea hen weed leaf meal on the performance of growing rabbits are presented in Table 2. Final body weight was significantly affected by GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control diet (GLM0) attained a final weight of 2084.97 g, which was comparable to those fed 5 and 10 g GLM/kg feed (2118.01 and 2208.42 g, respectively) but significantly (P < 0.05) lower than rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed, which recorded final weights of 2349.88 and 2447.75 g, respectively. Daily weight gain increased significantly with increasing GLM inclusion (P = 0.02). Rabbits in the control group recorded the lowest daily weight gain (26.09 g/rabbit/day), which was similar to the GLM1 group (26.53 g/rabbit/day). Higher daily weight gains were observed in rabbits fed 10 g GLM/kg feed (28.31 g/rabbit/day), while the highest gains were recorded in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (30.81 and 32.38 g/rabbit/day, respectively).

Total feed intake differed significantly among treatments (P = 0.01), increasing progressively with GLM supplementation. Rabbits fed the control diet consumed 4804.43 g, while those fed 5 and 10 g GLM/kg feed consumed 4829.20 and 4919.40 g, respectively. The highest feed intake values were observed in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (4963.81 and 5010.22 g, respectively). A similar trend was observed for daily feed intake, which increased from 85.79 g/rabbit/day in the control group to 89.47 g/rabbit/day in the GLM4 group (P = 0.01). Feed conversion ratio was significantly improved by GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control and GLM1 diets recorded higher FCR values (3.29 and 3.25, respectively), whereas lower and more efficient FCR values were observed in rabbits fed 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed (3.11, 2.88, and 2.77, respectively). Protein efficiency ratio increased significantly with dietary GLM inclusion (P = 0.01). The lowest PER values were observed in the control (2.14) and GLM1 (2.17) groups, intermediate values in the GLM2 group (2.28), and the highest PER values in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (2.46 and 2.56, respectively).

Carcass traits and relative organ weights

The effects of dietary supplementation with Guinea hen weed leaf meal on carcass characteristics and relative organ weights of growing rabbits are presented in Table 3. Slaughter weight was significantly influenced by dietary GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control diet (GLM0) recorded a slaughter weight of 1961.25 g, which was comparable (P > 0.05) to those fed 5 and 10 g GLM/kg feed (1991.95 and 2066.47 g, respectively), but significantly (P < 0.05) lower than rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed, which recorded higher slaughter weights of 2207.01 and 2298.10 g, respectively. Dressing-out percentage increased progressively with increasing GLM inclusion (P = 0.01). Rabbits in the control group recorded the lowest dressing percentage (55.22%), while those fed 5 and 10 g GLM/kg feed had intermediate values (56.11 and 58.07%, respectively). The highest dressing percentages were observed in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (60.53 and 61.11%, respectively). Relative heart weight differed significantly among treatments (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control and GLM1 diets recorded higher heart weights (0.20 and 0.21%, respectively), whereas lower values were observed in rabbits fed 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed (0.18, 0.15, and 0.16%, respectively). Dietary GLM supplementation significantly affected lung weight (P = 0.03). Lung weight decreased from 0.56% in the control group to 0.53 and 0.47% in the GLM1 and GLM2 groups, respectively, with the lowest values observed in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (0.31 and 0.36%, respectively). Relative liver weight was significantly reduced by GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control diet recorded the highest liver weight (2.42%), while those fed 5 g GLM/kg feed showed a slightly lower value (2.21%). Further reductions were observed in rabbits fed 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed, with values of 1.98, 1.97, and 1.93%, respectively. Kidney weight was also significantly affected by dietary treatment (P = 0.01). The highest kidney weight was recorded in the control group (0.54%), followed by rabbits fed 5 g GLM/kg feed (0.49%), whereas significantly lower values were observed in rabbits fed 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed (0.45, 0.42, and 0.44%, respectively).

Muscle antioxidant defense and lipid stability

The effects of dietary supplementation with Guinea hen weed leaf meal on meat oxidative status and biochemical composition of rabbits are presented in Table 3. Superoxide dismutase activity was significantly influenced by dietary GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control diet (GLM0) recorded the lowest SOD activity (2.10 × 10⁻² U/mg protein), while progressive increases were observed in rabbits fed 5 and 10 g GLM/kg feed (2.85 and 4.08 × 10⁻² U/mg protein, respectively). The highest SOD activities were recorded in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (4.72 and 5.80 × 10⁻² U/mg protein, respectively). Catalase activity increased significantly with increasing GLM inclusion (P = 0.01). Catalase activity in the control group was 3.51 U/mg protein, which increased to 4.96 and 5.49 U/mg protein in rabbits fed 5 and 10 g GLM/kg feed, respectively. Higher catalase activities were observed in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (6.05 and 7.30 U/mg protein, respectively). Glutathione peroxidase activity was significantly elevated by GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control diet recorded the lowest GPx activity (13.38 U/mg protein), while those fed 5 and 10 g GLM/kg feed showed higher activities (21.96 and 34.72 U/mg protein, respectively). The highest GPx activities were observed in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (60.04 and 77.20 U/mg protein, respectively). Malondialdehyde concentration decreased significantly (P < 0.05) with increasing dietary GLM supplementation. The highest MDA concentration was recorded in the control group (0.80 µM), followed by rabbits fed 5 and 10 g GLM/kg feed (0.66 and 0.57 µM, respectively). The lowest MDA concentrations were observed in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (0.52 and 0.54 µM, respectively). Cholesterol concentration was significantly affected by GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control diet recorded the lowest cholesterol level (1.38 mmol/L), while cholesterol concentration increased progressively in rabbits fed 5, 10, and 15 g GLM/kg feed (1.45, 1.51, and 1.54 mmol/L, respectively). The highest cholesterol concentration was observed in rabbits fed 20 g GLM/kg feed (1.59 mmol/L).

Hepatic oxidative status and redox homeostasis

The effects of dietary supplementation with Guinea hen weed leaf meal on the hepatic oxidative status of rabbits are presented in Table 4. Hepatic catalase activity was significantly influenced by dietary GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control diet (GLM0) recorded the lowest catalase activity (7.09 U/mg protein), which increased progressively in rabbits fed 5 and 10 g GLM/kg feed (11.58 and 15.57 U/mg protein, respectively). Higher catalase activities were observed in rabbits fed 15 and 20 g GLM/kg feed (19.33 and 21.47 U/mg protein, respectively). Glutathione peroxidase activity in the liver was also significantly affected by dietary treatment (P = 0.01). The lowest GPx activity was recorded in rabbits fed the control diet (39.41 U/mg protein), followed by rabbits fed 5 g GLM/kg feed (44.19 U/mg protein). Marked increases in GPx activity were observed in rabbits fed 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed, with values of 62.53, 73.88, and 81.43 U/mg protein, respectively. Malondialdehyde concentration in the liver decreased significantly with increasing GLM inclusion (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control and GLM1 diets recorded higher MDA concentrations (1.79 and 1.72 nmol/mg protein, respectively), whereas significantly lower MDA concentrations were observed in rabbits fed 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed (1.31, 0.98, and 0.82 nmol/mg protein, respectively).

Modulation of hepatic inflammatory cytokines

The effects of dietary supplementation with Guinea hen weed leaf meal on the hepatic inflammatory cytokine profile of rabbits are presented in Table 4. Tumour necrosis factor-α concentration in the liver was significantly affected by dietary GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control diet (GLM0) recorded the highest TNF-α concentration (288.81 pg/mg protein), which was comparable to the GLM1 group (268.54 pg/mg protein) but significantly higher than values observed in rabbits fed 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed (180.17, 114.78, and 81.39 pg/mg protein, respectively). Interleukin-1β concentration decreased significantly with increasing GLM inclusion (P = 0.01). The highest IL-1β level was recorded in the control group (146.36 pg/ml), followed by rabbits fed 5 g GLM/kg feed (119.87 pg/ml). Lower concentrations were observed in rabbits fed 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed, with values of 96.61, 86.08, and 77.83 pg/ml, respectively. A similar trend was observed for interleukin-6, which was significantly reduced by GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). Rabbits fed the control diet recorded the highest IL-6 concentration (205.71 pg/ml), while progressively lower concentrations were observed in rabbits fed 5, 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed (143.76, 100.76, 88.10, and 78.18 pg/ml, respectively). In contrast, interleukin-10 concentration increased significantly with increasing dietary GLM supplementation (P = 0.01). The lowest IL-10 concentration was observed in the control group (52.37 pg/ml) and GLM1 group (60.92 pg/ml), while higher concentrations were recorded in rabbits fed 10 and 15 g GLM/kg feed (75.11 and 107.04 pg/ml, respectively). The highest IL-10 concentration was observed in rabbits fed 20 g GLM/kg feed (139.05 pg/ml). Interleukin-4 concentration was also significantly influenced by dietary treatment (P = 0.03). Rabbits fed the control diet recorded the highest IL-4 concentration (273.83 pg/ml), with values decreasing progressively in rabbits fed 5, 10, 15, and 20 g GLM/kg feed (246.13, 231.72, 202.15, and 184.96 pg/ml, respectively).

Liver tissue histopathology

Representative photomicrographs (×400) of hepatic sections from rabbits fed Guinea hen weed leaf meal (GLM)–supplemented diets under tropical ambient conditions are presented in Fig. 3. In the control group (basal diet without GLM supplementation), the liver architecture was largely preserved, consisting of well-defined hepatic parenchyma and portal tracts. Hepatocytes appeared polygonal, arranged in cords or sheets, and contained distinct vesicular nuclei. Hepatic sinusoids were clearly delineated with thin endothelial linings and were devoid of cellular debris or inflammatory infiltrates. The portal areas comprised branches of the hepatic portal vein and bile duct structures that retained normal morphology. However, mild hepatic portal congestion was observed, suggestive of a vascular response possibly associated with heat-induced inflammatory challenge.

Discussion

Heat stress is a major constraint to rabbit production in tropical environments, primarily through its disruptive effects on metabolism, oxidative balance, immune regulation, and organ integrity13. The appreciable concentrations of phenolic compounds, saponins, tannins, flavonoids, and alkaloids observed in GLM confirm its strong phytochemical profile and provide a biochemical basis for its biological efficacy. Phenolics, which constituted the most abundant class, are well-established hydrogen-donating antioxidants capable of scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and modulating redox-sensitive signalling pathways12. The notable DPPH radical scavenging activity further supports the intrinsic antioxidant capacity of GLM. Collectively, these findings suggest that GLM functions not merely as a feed ingredient but as a functional phytogenic additive, capable of buffering oxidative insults associated with chronic heat exposure6[,10.

The environmental data obtained during the experimental period confirm that rabbits were continuously exposed to elevated thermal conditions typical of hot-humid tropical environments, thereby providing a relevant production context for evaluating the physiological and productive responses of rabbits to dietary Guinea hen weed leaf meal supplementation under sustained tropical heat load. The persistently high maximum ambient temperatures (≈ 34–37 °C) and elevated minimum temperatures (≈ 22–24.5 °C) indicate that animals experienced sustained daytime heat load with minimal nocturnal cooling. Such conditions are known to compromise thermoregulation in rabbits, a species characterised by limited sweating ability and high sensitivity to environmental heat, leading to physiological and metabolic disturbances13. The Temperature–Humidity Index (THI) values further substantiate the existence of chronic thermal stress during the trial. In rabbits, thermal comfort is generally maintained at THI values below approximately 27, while THI values between 27 and 30 indicate moderate heat stress, and values above 30 are associated with severe heat stress conditions14. During the present study, THI values frequently exceeded the comfort threshold and often approached the heat stress limit, particularly during the latter half of the experiment, indicating sustained exposure of rabbits to thermal stress conditions14. Sustained exposure to THI levels above comfort thresholds has been associated with reduced feed intake, impaired growth performance, oxidative imbalance, immune suppression, and inflammatory activation in rabbits. The progressive increase in THI observed over time suggests cumulative thermal pressure, which likely intensified physiological stress responses as the experiment progressed. Overall, the recorded environmental parameters confirm that rabbits were maintained under persistent heat stress conditions, providing a robust framework for evaluating the protective role of Guinea hen weed leaf meal in supporting physiological resilience and metabolic stability under sustained tropical thermal load under tropical production conditions.

Heat stress is typically associated with suppressed feed intake, impaired nutrient utilisation, and reduced growth efficiency in rabbits15. In contrast, GLM supplementation significantly improved final body weight, daily weight gain, feed conversion ratio, and protein efficiency ratio, particularly at GLM3 and GLM4. The progressive enhancement in performance indices suggests improved metabolic efficiency rather than a simple increase in feed intake alone17.

The improvement in FCR and PER indicates more efficient conversion of dietary nutrients into body mass, which may be attributed to reduced oxidative damage to metabolic tissues, improved gut functionality, and increased enzymatic activity. Phytochemicals such as saponins and flavonoids have been reported to improve intestinal integrity and nutrient absorption, while phenolic compounds may preserve mitochondrial efficiency under thermal stress16. Thus, these findings suggest that GLM may support physiological resilience during sustained thermal load by stabilising metabolic homeostasis. However, apparent nutrient digestibility was not evaluated in this study; therefore, it cannot be confirmed whether the improved feed conversion ratio observed in GLM-supplemented rabbits was due to enhanced dietary digestibility or improved post-absorptive metabolic efficiency. Further studies incorporating digestibility trials are required to clarify this mechanism. In addition, The response pattern observed in this study suggests a clear dose-dependent threshold for Guinea hen weed leaf meal efficacy. While GLM inclusion at 5–10 g/kg produced modest improvements, the most consistent and statistically significant benefits occurred at 15–20 g/kg, indicating that lower inclusion levels may not have supplied sufficient quantities of active phytochemicals to elicit measurable physiological responses under sustained heat stress. Given the relatively high phenolic concentration and appreciable levels of saponins, tannins, and flavonoids detected in GLM, it is plausible that antioxidant and immunomodulatory actions require a minimum intake level to overcome oxidative and inflammatory load induced by thermal challenge. The plateauing of responses between 15 and 20 g/kg suggests a potential effect saturation phenomenon, where further increases in bioactive intake yield diminishing marginal physiological gains. However, because dietary inclusion was not tested beyond 20 g/kg, it remains uncertain whether 20 g/kg represents the maximum effective dosage or whether higher supplementation may induce inhibitory effects, possibly due to anti-nutritional factors such as tannins or excessive saponin exposure. Future studies evaluating GLM inclusion above 20 g/kg, alongside palatability, digestibility and toxicity indices, are required to establish the true optimal and maximum safe inclusion level.

The increases in slaughter weight and dressing percentage with GLM supplementation further reflect improved growth efficiency and carcass yield. Importantly, the reductions in relative liver, kidney, heart, and lung weights at higher GLM inclusion levels suggest alleviation of organ hypertrophy commonly associated with metabolic overload, inflammation, and oxidative stress.

Under heat stress, increased relative organ weights often reflect adaptive or pathological enlargement due to detoxification demands and inflammatory processes17. The observed reductions in liver and kidney weights in GLM-fed rabbits align with the concomitant decreases in serum AST, ALT, and creatinine activities, indicating improved hepatic and renal functional integrity. Alterations in bile weight may reflect modulated lipid metabolism and hepatobiliary function18.

Oxidative stress is a central mechanism through which heat stress compromises meat quality and cellular integrity19. The elevation of SOD, CAT, and GPx activities in muscle and liver tissues indicates enhanced endogenous antioxidant defence capacity in GLM-supplemented rabbits19. This enzymatic response likely contributed to the reduction in MDA concentration, reflecting decreased lipid peroxidation and improved redox stability20. Although the present results do not establish direct molecular mechanisms, the observed pattern supports the hypothesis that GLM phytochemicals may have supported antioxidant enzyme synthesis or preserved enzymatic activity under heat-induced oxidative challenge. Such effects have been reported for plant phenolics and flavonoids, which can scavenge reactive oxygen species and indirectly stabilise cellular antioxidant systems20[,21.

Another notable finding is that muscle cholesterol concentration increased with GLM supplementation (although these values remained within physiological ranges of 0.40–1.60 mmol/kg meat), whereas serum cholesterol decreased. This opposite trend may suggest compartmental redistribution, although this remains speculative and requires further lipoprotein profiling. Serum cholesterol primarily represents circulating lipoprotein-associated cholesterol, influenced by hepatic synthesis, intestinal absorption, and lipoprotein clearance. The reduction in serum cholesterol in GLM-supplemented rabbits may suggest improved lipid metabolism and enhanced hepatic regulation of cholesterol transport, possibly mediated by phytochemicals such as saponins and phenolic compounds known to reduce intestinal cholesterol absorption and promote bile acid excretion. Conversely, muscle cholesterol concentration reflects local lipid deposition and membrane-associated sterol content, which may increase moderately with improved growth rate and enhanced muscle accretion. Therefore, the increase in muscle cholesterol may be related to improved tissue development and metabolic deposition rather than dyslipidemia22. Importantly, the concurrent decline in serum cholesterol and improved antioxidant status indicates that GLM supplementation may have supported healthier systemic lipid regulation while allowing normal tissue-level lipid incorporation required for growth and membrane stability. This interpretation remains hypothetical because lipoprotein fractions were not measured.

The liver is a primary target of heat-induced oxidative injury due to its central role in metabolism and detoxification23. The pronounced increases in hepatic catalase and GPx activities, alongside substantial reductions in hepatic MDA levels, demonstrate that GLM confers strong hepatoprotective effects. These findings suggest that GLM not only supplies exogenous antioxidants but also stimulates endogenous antioxidant enzyme systems, possibly through activation of redox-sensitive transcription factors9. The dose-responsive decline in hepatic lipid peroxidation underscores the capacity of GLM to preserve membrane integrity and mitochondrial function under thermal stress, thereby reducing cellular injury and inflammatory signalling.

Chronic heat stress is known to trigger sterile inflammation characterised by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines such as Tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), Interleukin 1β (IL-1β), and Interleukin-6 (IL-6)24. In the present study, GLM supplementation significantly suppressed these cytokines in the liver while simultaneously enhancing the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. The reduction in hepatic TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 concentrations, together with increased IL-10 levels, suggests a shift in hepatic cytokine balance toward a less pro-inflammatory profile and improved immunological homeostasis in GLM-supplemented rabbits. Since oxidative stress and inflammation are closely linked during heat stress, the improved antioxidant status observed may have contributed to limiting inflammatory signaling. However, the present data do not directly establish causality or identify the precise cellular sources of cytokine production. The cytokine responses should therefore be interpreted as biochemical indicators of altered inflammatory status rather than definitive evidence of pathway-level immune modulation25,26.

The present findings demonstrate that GLM supplementation was associated with improved antioxidant enzyme activities, reduced lipid peroxidation, and a shift in cytokine balance toward lower pro-inflammatory mediators and higher anti-inflammatory IL-10. While these outcomes are consistent with the reported antioxidant and immunomodulatory potential of phytogenic compounds, the present study did not directly measure molecular pathway activation. Therefore, proposed mechanisms involving redox-sensitive transcription factors and inflammatory signaling cascades should be interpreted as plausible explanations rather than confirmed mechanistic evidence. The observed responses may be associated with redox-sensitive pathways such as Nrf2 and may plausibly involve modulation of inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB; however, these were not directly assessed and require molecular confirmation. Future investigations incorporating gene expression profiling and pathway-specific biomarkers are necessary to establish whether GLM directly influences Nrf2-, NF-κB-, or heat shock protein–related responses under hot–humid tropical conditions.

The increases in serum total protein and albumin concentrations with GLM supplementation indicate improved protein synthesis, nutrient utilisation, and hepatic synthetic capacity. Albumin, a major antioxidant and transport protein, also contributes to systemic redox balance, further supporting the observed reductions in oxidative stress markers27. The progressive decreases in AST, ALT, creatinine, and serum cholesterol concentrations reflect improved hepatic and renal function, as well as improved regulation of lipid metabolism28.

Serum AST and ALT activities decreased markedly with increasing GLM inclusion, suggesting reduced leakage of these enzymes from hepatocytes and potentially lower hepatic metabolic strain under tropical heat stress. However, interpretation of AST and ALT should be made cautiously, as these enzymes are not exclusively specific to hepatic injury and can be influenced by nutritional status, protein metabolism, and physiological adaptation. While elevated AST and ALT are widely used indicators of hepatocellular damage, very low enzyme values do not necessarily confirm superior liver function in isolation. In the present study, the decline in AST and ALT is better interpreted in combination with the improvement in serum total protein and albumin, the reduction in serum creatinine, and the preservation of hepatic architecture on histological evaluation. Collectively, these findings support the likelihood that GLM supplementation reduced hepatocellular stress and improved metabolic stability, but confirmatory functional hepatic assays would strengthen this conclusion in future studies.

The serum cytokine profile mirrored hepatic findings, with significant reductions in circulating IL-1β and IL-6 and marked increases in IL-4 and IL-10 concentrations. This systemic anti-inflammatory shift is critical under heat stress, as chronic low-grade inflammation contributes to growth suppression, immune dysfunction, and oxidative damage29. By suppressing pro-inflammatory mediators and enhancing regulatory cytokines, GLM appears to interrupt the vicious cycle linking heat stress, oxidative stress, and inflammation. This coordinated response likely underpins the improvements observed in growth performance, organ integrity, and antioxidant status30.

An important observation in the present study is the divergent pattern of IL-4 regulation between hepatic tissue and systemic circulation. Hepatic IL-4 concentrations decreased progressively with increasing GLM inclusion, whereas serum IL-4 increased. This discrepancy highlights the compartment-specific nature of cytokine regulation, where cytokine concentrations in tissue homogenates reflect the local immune microenvironment, while serum cytokine concentrations represent systemic immune signaling and spillover from multiple organs. IL-4 is primarily associated with Th2-mediated immune responses and can exhibit both pro-inflammatory and regulatory functions depending on context. The reduction in hepatic IL-4 alongside decreased hepatic TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 may indicate suppression of local hepatic immune activation and reduced inflammatory signaling within the liver under thermal challenge. In contrast, the increase in circulating IL-4 may reflect a systemic shift toward Th2-biased immune regulation or compensatory anti-inflammatory signaling, potentially supporting immune homeostasis under stress. Therefore, the opposing hepatic and serum IL-4 trends should not be viewed as contradictory but rather as evidence of differential, compartment-specific immune regulation, which is common during stress and immune adaptation; however, assay-related variation cannot be completely excluded.

Although the present study did not conduct formal correlation or regression analysis, the observed trends strongly suggest functional interrelationships among redox status, inflammatory signaling, hepatic enzyme activity and productive performance. The reduction in malondialdehyde concentration, alongside increased superoxide dismutase and catalase activities, indicates reduced lipid peroxidation and improved antioxidant defence. This improved redox environment likely contributed to the observed suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, while promoting anti-inflammatory cytokine activity (IL-10). Given that oxidative stress and inflammation are tightly coupled biological processes, reduced ROS generation and improved antioxidant buffering may have limited cytokine-driven hepatic injury. This interpretation is consistent with the concurrent reduction in serum AST and ALT activities and the preservation of liver microarchitecture in GLM-supplemented groups, suggesting improved hepatocyte membrane integrity and reduced leakage of intracellular enzymes. Collectively, these responses indicate that GLM-mediated redox stabilisation may have reduced inflammatory burden and protected hepatic metabolic capacity, thereby supporting improved nutrient utilisation efficiency and enhanced growth performance under heat stress.

Heat stress is widely recognized to compromise hepatic integrity through oxidative overload, inflammatory activation, and vascular disturbances, particularly under tropical production conditions22. The liver, being central to metabolic regulation and detoxification, is highly susceptible to thermal stress–induced circulatory and redox imbalance. In the present study, histopathological evaluation demonstrated that dietary supplementation with Guinea hen weed leaf meal (GLM) preserved hepatic microarchitecture and was associated with fewer structural alterations typically associated with heat stress exposure. The mild portal congestion observed in the control group suggests vascular perturbation consistent with systemic heat load. Thermal stress can induce peripheral vasodilation and redistribution of blood flow, potentially leading to hepatic circulatory disturbances and localized inflammatory signaling31. Such vascular responses are often early indicators of hepatic stress and may precede overt cellular degeneration. The similar mild congestion observed in the GLM1 group (5 g/kg) indicates that lower inclusion levels may not be sufficient to fully counteract heat-induced vascular responses. In contrast, rabbits receiving 10–20 g/kg GLM (GLM2–GLM4) exhibited preserved hepatic histoarchitecture, with intact hepatocyte morphology, patent sinusoids, and structurally normal portal triads. The absence of hepatocellular degeneration, necrosis, or inflammatory cell infiltration in these groups suggests that GLM supplementation could mitigated heat stress–associated hepatic injury. Maintenance of hepatocyte integrity under thermal challenge is indicative of improved cellular resilience, likely mediated through improved antioxidant defense and modulation of inflammatory pathways32. Histopathological evaluation showed largely preserved hepatic architecture in rabbits receiving higher GLM inclusion levels, with minimal evidence of hepatocellular degeneration, necrosis, or inflammatory infiltration. This structural preservation supports the biochemical findings of improved hepatic antioxidant status and reduced inflammatory cytokine concentrations. Kupffer cell prominence was observed in the GLM4 group. However, this observation should be interpreted cautiously, as routine H&E staining does not permit definitive differentiation between physiological macrophage activation, inflammatory stimulation, or nonspecific hepatic response. Kupffer cells are resident hepatic macrophages involved in immune homeostasis and clearance of cellular debris, and their increased visibility may reflect altered hepatic immune activity under heat stress conditions33. Further studies involving immunohistochemical macrophage markers or functional macrophage phenotyping would be required to confirm whether the observed Kupffer cell prominence represents adaptive immunomodulation or inflammatory activation. Nevertheless, the absence of overt inflammatory lesions and hepatocellular damage in the GLM-supplemented groups suggests that GLM inclusion did not induce pathological hepatic inflammation. This finding aligns with the immunomodulatory properties attributed to Petiveria alliacea, which contains bioactive phytochemicals such as flavonoids, organosulfur compounds, and phenolic constituents known to exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. The hepatoprotective effect observed in GLM-supplemented groups may therefore be mechanistically linked to improved oxidative status and enhanced antioxidant defence. Heat stress increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, disrupts mitochondrial function, and impairs cellular membranes through lipid peroxidation. Phytogenic compounds with free radical–scavenging capacity can stabilize hepatocyte membranes, preserve sinusoidal integrity, and prevent inflammatory cascades triggered by oxidative damage34. The preservation of normal hepatic microanatomy in GLM2–GLM4 groups supports this protective paradigm and suggests dose-dependent efficacy. Furthermore, the absence of inflammatory infiltrates across higher GLM inclusion levels indicates effective modulation of hepatic inflammatory signaling. Chronic heat exposure often activates pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading to vascular congestion and tissue remodeling35. The structural normalcy observed in GLM-fed rabbits implies that Guinea hen weed leaf meal may suppress excessive inflammatory activation while promoting physiological immune responsiveness, as evidenced by Kupffer cell engagement without tissue injury. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that dietary inclusion of Guinea hen weed leaf meal, particularly at 10–20 g/kg, confers structural protection to the liver under tropical heat stress conditions. The maintenance of hepatic microarchitecture, combined with the observed cytokine profile, underscores the potential of GLM as a phytogenic feed additive for supporting metabolic stability and inflammatory balance in rabbits raised under hot-humid tropical conditions. These histological observations complement the biochemical evidence of improved oxidative and inflammatory status, reinforcing the role of Guinea hen weed as a functional feed additive for enhancing physiological resilience in rabbits raised under thermally challenging environments. Although phenolic concentration was relatively high, the biological effects observed may reflect the combined influence of multiple bioactive compounds in Guinea hen weed leaf meal, including flavonoids, tannins, alkaloids, and saponins, which likely acted synergistically to enhance antioxidant and immunomodulatory responses. A limitation of this study is the absence of a thermoneutral control group, which restricts inference on whether GLM specifically alleviates heat stress or generally promotes growth under different environmental conditions.

Conclusion

Dietary inclusion of Guinea hen weed leaf meal improved growth performance, feed utilisation, antioxidant enzyme activities, inflammatory cytokine profiles, and serum biochemical indices in growing rabbits reared under naturally hot-humid tropical conditions. The most consistent responses were observed at 15–20 g/kg feed, suggesting that GLM has potential as a phytogenic supplement for improving physiological stability and productivity in tropical rabbit production. The threshold effect observed at 15–20 g/kg may reflect the minimum intake level required to deliver sufficient phenolic and organosulfur compounds to exert measurable systemic antioxidant and cytokine-modulating effects. Mechanistic interpretations remain inferential because molecular biomarkers were not assessed, and the lack of a thermoneutral control limits heat stress–specific inference. Thus, the findings should be interpreted as beneficial physiological responses under hot-humid tropical conditions. Future studies incorporating thermoneutral comparisons and pathway-specific biomarkers are needed.

Materials and methods

Study location and ethical approval

The feeding trial was conducted between February and March 2025 under naturally prevailing hot-humid tropical conditions at the Teaching and Research Farm of the School of Agriculture and Agricultural Technology, Rufus Giwa Polytechnic, Owo, Ondo State, Nigeria (7.1962° N, 5.5868° E). The experimental site is located within the tropical savannah ecological zone, characterised by a pronounced dry season and elevated ambient temperatures.

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with internationally accepted guidelines for the care and use of experimental animals and in strict compliance with institutional and national regulations governing animal research. Ethical approval was obtained from the Laboratory Animal Use and Management Committee of the School of Agricultural Technology, Rufus Giwa Polytechnic, Owo, Nigeria (Approval No.: AT-RGP-RABEX-2024-003B). Animal handling, housing, and experimental interventions were conducted to ensure welfare throughout the study.

Plant material collection was undertaken in accordance with relevant institutional and national regulations governing access to and use of plant resources for research purposes. Collection was conducted with institutional authorization from naturally occurring vegetation within designated research land, ensuring compliance with applicable biodiversity and conservation guidelines. All procedures adhered strictly to recognized ethical, environmental, and regulatory standards for both animal experimentation and plant material collection.

Preparation of guinea hen weed leaf meal and chemical analysis

Fresh leaves of Guinea hen weed (Petiveria alliacea) were collected from naturally occurring wild vegetation on fallow land within the Agricultural Technology Teaching and Research Farm, Rufus Giwa Polytechnic, Owo, Nigeria. Collection was conducted with institutional authorization and in compliance with relevant institutional and national guidelines governing the use of plant resources for research purposes. No specific governmental permit was required, as the species is neither protected nor restricted. Guinea hen weed is not listed as threatened or endangered under the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List and is not included in the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES).

Botanical authentication was independently carried out by Dr. A.T. Ajayi (Department of Plant Science and Biotechnology, Adekunle Ajasin University, Akungba-Akoko, Nigeria) and Mr. Tomiwa Johnson (Department of Crop, Soil and Pest Management, Rufus Giwa Polytechnic, Owo, Nigeria). A voucher specimen (No. RUG/PA/2025/011) was prepared and deposited in the Herbarium of the Department of Crop, Soil and Pest Management, Rufus Giwa Polytechnic, Owo, Nigeria for future reference. The collection of plant material complied with relevant institutional and national biodiversity conservation guidelines for the use of plant resources in research. Guinea hen weed is not listed as threatened or endangered under the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List of Threatened Species and is not included in the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES) of Wild Fauna and Flora.

The plant was collected from naturally occurring vegetation within the Agricultural Technology Teaching and Research Farm of Rufus Giwa Polytechnic, Owo, Nigeria, with institutional authorization. No specific governmental permit was required for its collection. Fresh Guinea hen weed (Petiveria alliacea) leaves were harvested, rinsed with clean water to remove dust and debris, and air-drained. The leaves were thereafter shade-dried under well-ventilated indoor conditions at ambient temperature (average 26–30 °C) for 7–10 days until a constant weight was achieved. Drying was conducted away from direct sunlight to reduce degradation of heat-sensitive compounds. The dried leaves were milled using a hammer mill to pass through a 1-mm sieve. The resulting Guinea hen weed leaf meal (GLM) was stored in airtight containers at room temperature until used for laboratory analysis and diet formulation.

For phytochemical and antioxidant analyses, powdered Guinea hen weed leaf meal (GLM) was extracted using 80% methanol (v/v). Briefly, 1 g of GLM powder was mixed with 20 mL of extraction solvent (sample-to-solvent ratio 1:20 w/v) in a sealed flask. The mixture was shaken intermittently and allowed to extract for 24 h at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). Extracts were filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the filtrates were stored at 4 °C until analysis. All assays were conducted in triplicate.

The quantification of saponins, tannins, phenols, alkaloids, and flavonoids in GLM was conducted according to the procedure outlined by Oloruntola and Ayodele36, which was adapted from earlier validated analytical protocols. Total saponin concentration was determined using a vanillin–sulfuric acid colourimetric assay. The extract was treated with ethanol, vanillin reagent, and sulfuric acid, followed by incubation at 60 °C to allow colour development. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 545 nm using an i3 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Hanon Advanced Technology Group Co., Ltd., Jinan, China). Saponin content was calculated and reported as milligrams of tea saponin equivalents per gram of dry sample, following the procedure described by He et al.37. Tannin content was evaluated from ethanolic extracts employing the Folin–Ciocalteu assay. After reagent addition and reaction development, absorbance readings were taken at 680 nm with the same spectrophotometric system. Tannin concentration was expressed as milligrams of tannic acid equivalents per gram of dry matter in accordance with the method of Biswas et al.38. Total phenolic content of GLM extract was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method as described by Otles and Yalcin39. Briefly, 0.5 mL of the extract was mixed with 2.5 mL of 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and allowed to stand for 5 min. Thereafter, 2.0 mL of 7.5% sodium carbonate solution was added and the mixture incubated in darkness at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Total phenolics were calculated from a gallic acid standard calibration curve and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per g dry sample (mg GAE/g DM). Alkaloid content was determined through a gravimetric procedure as described by Adeniyi et al.40. Samples were first extracted in ethanol containing acetic acid, after which the solution was concentrated, and alkaloids precipitated using ammonium hydroxide. The resulting precipitate was filtered, rinsed, dried in an oven, and weighed, with alkaloid levels expressed as a percentage of sample dry weight. Total flavonoid content was determined using the aluminium chloride colourimetric method. Briefly, 0.5 mL of extract was mixed with 1.5 mL methanol, 0.1 mL of 10% aluminium chloride, 0.1 mL of 1 M potassium acetate, and 2.8 mL distilled water. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 510 nm. Flavonoid concentration was determined from a rutin standard curve and expressed as mg rutin equivalents per g dry sample (mg RE/g DM). Absorbance measurements were taken at 510 nm using the i3 UV–Vis spectrophotometer, and flavonoid concentration was expressed as milligrams of rutin equivalents per gram of dry sample following the method of Surana et al.41.

The free radical scavenging capacity of GLM powder was determined using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay according to the procedure described by Oloruntola42. In this assay, a methanolic DPPH solution (6 × 10⁻⁵ M) was mixed with either the sample extract or gallic acid standard solution, after which the reaction mixtures were kept in darkness at room temperature for 20 min to enable stable colour development. The reduction in absorbance, indicative of radical scavenging activity, was then measured at 515 nm against a methanol blank using an i3 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Hanon Advanced Technology Group Co., Ltd., Jinan, China). Antioxidant activity of the samples was subsequently calculated from a calibration curve generated using gallic acid standards ranging from 10 to 100 ppm. Experimental animals and management

Growing rabbits of comparable physiological status, purchased from a reputable commercial farm (Ola Olu Farms, Ikire, Osun State, Nigeria), were used in the study. At the beginning of the experiment, 80 weaner rabbits (average weight 627.63 ± 6.56 g) were individually weighed and randomly allocated to five dietary treatments (16 rabbits per treatment) in a completely randomised design. Rabbits were housed in hutches such that each replicate consisted of two rabbits per hutch, giving 8 replicates (hutches) per treatment. Animals were provided free access to feed and clean drinking water throughout the 56-day feeding trial. No antibiotics or growth promoters were administered during the study.

Experimental diets and treatments

A basal diet was formulated to meet the nutrient requirements of growing rabbits according to NRC43 recommendations (Table 1). All experimental diets were prepared in pellet form (4 mm) and offered fresh daily to the rabbits throughout the 56-day feeding period. In addition to proximate nutrient composition, the basal diet was evaluated for its estimated mineral and amino acid profile, and the calculated values are presented in Table 1. Guinea hen weed leaf meal was incorporated into the basal diet at graded levels to produce five experimental diets: GLM0: 0 g GLM/kg basal diet; GLM1: 5 g GLM/kg basal diet; GLM2: 10 g GLM/kg basal diet; GLM3: 15 g GLM/kg basal diet; and GLM4: 20 g GLM/kg basal diet.

Growth performance measurements

Body weights were recorded at the start and end of the experiment using a digital weighing scale. Daily weight gain was calculated as total weight gain divided by the number of experimental days. Feed intake was determined by subtracting feed refusals from feed offered. Daily feed intake was calculated as total feed intake divided by the number of experimental days.

Feed intake measurements were recorded on a replicate (hutch) basis, by weighing feed offered and refusals per replicate cage, and dividing by the number of rabbits housed in the replicate.

Slaughter procedure and carcass evaluation

At the end of the feeding trial, rabbits were fasted for 12 h prior to slaughter to allow gastrointestinal emptying, while clean drinking water was provided ad libitum throughout the fasting period. After fasting, rabbits were weighed to obtain slaughter weight and were then humanely slaughtered following standard procedures. Carcass weight was recorded after evisceration, and dressing percentage was calculated. Internal organs (liver, kidneys, heart, lungs, spleen, and bile) were excised and weighed, with relative organ weights expressed as percentages of slaughter weight.

Meat oxidative status and biochemical analysis

Hind-leg muscle samples were collected immediately after slaughter, rinsed with ice-cold saline, and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Muscle tissues were homogenized in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and the homogenates were used for biochemical assays.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined according to the method of Gao et al.,45. Catalase (CAT) activity was measured following the method of Holovska et al.46. Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity was determined as early described by Payne and Southern47. Lipid peroxidation was assessed by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method described by Tokur et al.48. Muscle cholesterol concentration was determined spectrophotometrically by using commercial kits (Asan Pharm. Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) as described by de Almeida et al.49.

Hepatic oxidative status and inflammatory cytokines

Liver samples were collected, washed in ice-cold saline, and stored at − 20 °C. Hepatic CAT46, GPx activities47 and MDA concentration48 were assayed. Hepatic concentrations of Tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Interleukin-4 (IL-4), and Interleukin-10 (IL-10) were quantified using rabbit-specific ELISA kits (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Serum biochemical and serum inflammatory cytokines analysis

Blood samples were collected via jugular venipuncture into plain tubes and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min to obtain serum. Serum total protein, albumin, cholesterol, creatinine, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were determined using commercial diagnostic kits (Randox Laboratories Ltd., Crumlin, UK) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Globulin concentration was calculated by difference. Serum concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-4, and IL-10 were measured using rabbit-specific ELISA kits (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) in accordance with the manufacturers’ guidelines.

Histopathological evaluation

Liver tissues were harvested post-slaughter from the same experimental rabbits and immediately preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin for a fixation period of 48 h. Following fixation, the samples underwent standard histological processing, including graded alcohol dehydration, paraffin wax embedding, and microtome sectioning at a thickness of 5 μm. The prepared sections were stained using the Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) technique to facilitate morphological assessment. Histological evaluation was performed under light microscopy to assess hepatic architecture, hepatocellular degeneration, sinusoidal congestion, cytoplasmic vacuolation, necrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Representative photomicrographs were captured using a digital imaging system. The histopathological assessment was based on routine H&E morphology and therefore provides qualitative evidence of hepatic structural integrity50.

In this study, the experimental unit was defined according to the nature of the response variable. For body weight traits, feed intake, daily feed intake, and feed conversion ratio, the replicate hutch (two rabbits per hutch) was considered the experimental unit, giving eight replicates per treatment (n = 8). For slaughter-related traits, including slaughter weight, carcass yield, and relative organ weights, the individual rabbit served as the experimental unit, resulting in sixteen rabbits per treatment (n = 16). For oxidative stress biomarkers, inflammatory cytokine assays, serum biochemical indices, and histopathological evaluation, one rabbit was randomly selected per replicate hutch, yielding eight sampled rabbits per treatment (n = 8).

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) prior to analysis. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to evaluate the effect of dietary Guinea hen weed leaf meal inclusion level. The experimental unit was defined according to the nature of the response variable. For feed intake, daily feed intake, and feed conversion ratio, the replicate hutch (2 rabbits per hutch) was considered the experimental unit (n = 8 replicates per treatment). For individual body weight traits, carcass yield, and relative organ weights, the individual rabbit was considered the experimental unit (n = 16 rabbits per treatment). For serum biochemical indices, oxidative stress biomarkers, cytokine assays, and histopathology evaluation, one rabbit per replicate was sampled, giving n = 8 rabbits per treatment. Where significant differences were detected (P < 0.05), treatment means were separated using Duncan’s multiple range test. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM), and statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05.
