Epidemiological trends of syphilis and the analysis of knowledge and attitudes of future healthcare professionals on its prevention in the autonomous Province of Vojvodina, Serbia
Authors: Zoran Golušin, Nataša Čapo, Nemanja Maletin, Nikola Denda, Milan Matić, Ljuba Vujanović, Tatjana Roš, Olivera Levakov, Siniša Babović, Mila Crnojević, Svetlana Ilić, Smiljana Rajčević, Tihomir Dugandžija
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objectivesSyphilis remains a growing public health concern, with increasing reported incidence globally. Future healthcare professionals play a key role in prevention, early detection, and education. This study aimed to analyze the epidemiological trends of syphilis in the Autonomous Province (AP) Vojvodina and to assess the level of factual knowledge about syphilis and prevention measures, as well as attitudes toward education, stigma, and preventive strategies, among students at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Novi Sad.MethodsEpidemiological data on syphilis in the AP Vojvodina from 1997 to 2024 were analyzed using annual case numbers, crude incidence rates, age-specific and sex-specific incidence rates, and age-standardized incidence rates calculated by direct standardization to the European standard population. Second, a structured survey was administered to 356 students at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Novi Sad from December 1 to 30, 2024 to assess their knowledge and attitudes toward syphilis and its prevention.ResultsFrom 1997 to 2024, 1,034 syphilis cases were reported in AP Vojvodina, mostly among individuals aged 25–34. The age-standardized rate of incidence showed a rising trend, peaking in 2023 at 9.33 per 100,000. Among 356 surveyed students (mean age 22.2; 74.4% female), medical students had significantly higher self-assessed knowledge scores compared to students from other health-related study programmes (36.99% vs. 8.03%; p < 0.001). Prior sexually transmitted infections education was strongly associated with better knowledge scores (OR = 5.98; 95% CI: 3.41–10.49; p < 0.001). Most students (60%) cited stigma as a barrier to care, and while 87% supported the need for syphilis education, only 15% felt confident advising patients on prevention.ConclusionsThe increasing syphilis incidence in AP Vojvodina, particularly among young men, mirrors EU trends and highlights a growing public health concern. Despite favourable attitudes, students demonstrated notable knowledge gaps. Targeted education on sexually transmitted infections, with emphasis on practical prevention strategies and counselling skills, is essential to equip future healthcare professionals for effective prevention and stigma reduction, while taking current epidemiological trends into account.Keywords: Syphilis, Public health, Epidemiology, Knowledge and attitudes, Prevention, Students, Education

Introduction

Syphilis is a bacterial infection transmitted through sexual contact, vertically from mother to fetus during pregnancy or to a newborn during childbirth, posing a significant cause of morbidity and mortality [1]. Despite being a treatable disease with relatively low prevalence in most developed countries, recent years have seen a marked increase in syphilis cases, particularly among men who have sex with men (MSM) [2]. This trend has been observed across many middle- and high-income countries, with MSM representing the largest share of new cases [3, 4]. In Europe, approximately 27% of new syphilis cases are reported with HIV co-infection, largely linked to high-risk sexual behaviour and suboptimal uptake of preventive measures [5, 6]. Moreover, transmission through individuals with both same-sex and opposite-sex partners may facilitate further spread of syphilis across different population groups, including heterosexual individuals [7].

According to reports from the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), the incidence rate of syphilis in the European Union/European Economic Area (EU/EEA) region has nearly doubled since 2010, with MSM, particularly in urban areas, being the most affected group [8]. A similar rise has been reported globally, including in the United States, where behavioural changes have been identified as key drivers of increasing incidence [9]. Reinfection remains common, and previous infection does not provide lasting immunity complicating public health responses. However, STI surveillance outside the EU/EEA remains inconsistent, making direct comparisons and trend assessments challenging [10].

Pre-exposure prophylaxis for HIV (PrEP) has become an increasingly used preventive therapy, following evidence of its effectiveness in reducing HIV risk. PrEP users have been shown to have higher rates of bacterial sexually transmitted infections, including syphilis [11, 12]. Understanding syphilis epidemiology and its behavioural determinants is essential for effective prevention. Previous studies on students’ STI knowledge and attitudes have mostly focused on common infections, including syphilis [13–21]. It is imperative that medical students, as future healthcare professionals, receive adequate education to effectively provide knowledge-based support and counselling. Given the documented rise in syphilis incidence globally, well-prepared healthcare professionals play a key role in early detection, prevention, and patient education.

This study aimed to analyze the epidemiological trends of syphilis infections in the Autonomous Province of Vojvodina (AP Vojvodina), Northern Serbia from 1997 to 2024. By providing insight into the local epidemiological context, the study aims to highlight the public health relevance of syphilis and the need for targeted prevention strategies. Considering the relevance of the topic and globally reported increase in syphilis incidence, this study also evaluated the knowledge and attitudes of future healthcare workers, current students at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Novi Sad (MFNS), regarding syphilis and its prevention.

Materials and methods

Epidemiological data

Data on syphilis incidence in AP Vojvodina for the period 1997 to 2024 were obtained from the official records of the Institute of Public Health of Vojvodina, which collects, monitors, and reports notifiable diseases based on mandatory surveillance protocols. Surveillance data on syphilis were obtained through the mandatory communicable disease reporting system, regulated by the “Rulebook on Reporting Communicable Diseases” issued by the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Serbia [22]. All probable and confirmed cases diagnosed in healthcare facilities are required to be reported to the local public health institute within 24 h, using standardized case notification forms. These reports are aggregated by the Institute of Public Health of Vojvodina and forwarded to the national surveillance database [23]. Throughout the study period (1997–2024), the surveillance system for syphilis in AP Vojvodina was passive, relying on routine case notifications from healthcare providers. From 2018 onwards, the national case definition was aligned with the European Union criteria, which include categories of possible, probable, and confirmed cases (Official Journal of the European Union L 170/43, 6.7.2018). In Serbia, only probable and confirmed cases are reported in surveillance statistics, based on clinical presentation and laboratory confirmation (serological testing and/or direct pathogen detection) [23]. Prior to 2018, case definitions were based on clinical and serological confirmation in accordance with national guidelines valid at that time.

Age-specific incidence was calculated for predefined groups (0–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–34, 35–44, and ≥ 45 years), while sex-specific incidence trends were monitored separately for males and females across the entire period. To enable temporal comparisons and adjust for changes in population structure, the annual age-standardized incidence rates (ASRI) of syphilis were calculated using the direct standardization method. The numerator was the annual number of reported syphilis cases in AP Vojvodina, and the denominator was the estimated mid-year population for each year from 1997 to 2024, obtained from the Statistical Office of the Republic of Serbia. Age-specific incidence rates were then weighted using the 2013 European Standard Population (ESP) as the reference [24]. Direct standardization was used to enable temporal comparisons by adjusting for differences in the age structure over time. Unlike the crude notification rates reported in the ECDC Annual Epidemiological Report, our analysis applies direct standardization to account for changes in population age distribution over the 28-year period. Annual ASRI values were expressed per 100,000 population.

Cross-sectional data on knowledge and attitudes

This study employed a cross-sectional design conducted at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Novi Sad (MFNS), recruiting students across all years and study programmes. The female predominance in the sample reflects the actual student body structure at MFNS, where approximately 80% of students are women, and thus mirrors the population from which the sample was drawn.

Data collection occurred from 1 to 29 February 2025 utilizing a customized questionnaire developed specifically for this research. Exclusion criteria were: students not actively attending classes in the 2024/25 academic year, international students enrolled in English-taught programmes, and participants with incomplete responses (more than 50% unanswered questions). In this study, age was categorized as 19–21, 22–24, and ≥ 25 years to reflect typical educational stages: early undergraduate (years 1–3), late undergraduate (years 4–6), and postgraduate or extended studies. Participants were divided into two groups based on their study programme: medical students and students from other health-related fields (pharmacy, dentistry, nursing, medical rehabilitation, radiological technology, special education and rehabilitation, and postgraduate studies). This grouping aimed to assess potential differences in knowledge and attitudes, given that medical students typically receive more comprehensive education on infectious diseases, including sexually transmitted infections.

Based on the total number of actively enrolled students at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Novi Sad (N = 2,634), the minimum required sample size was estimated at 327 participants to achieve a 95% confidence level with a 5% margin of error. To ensure sufficient response and to account for possible exclusions, 500 questionnaires were distributed. A proportional stratification approach was applied across different study programmes (medicine, pharmacy, dentistry, nursing, medical rehabilitation, radiological technology, special education and rehabilitation) and years of study. Trained researchers visited pre-scheduled lectures and practical classes across these strata, and all present students were invited to participate. Questionnaires were completed anonymously, self-administered, and collected immediately on site, preventing duplication. A pilot study with 20 students was conducted beforehand to assess clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire, which resulted in minor adjustments. Although the calculation was based on the assumption of a simple random sample, the applied stratification ensured broad representativeness across programmes and study years. We did not adjust for intra-class correlation, as students were recruited across multiple courses and groups, minimizing clustering effects. The final analysis included 356 valid, fully completed questionnaires, yielding a response rate of 71%.

The questionnaire was specifically developed for this study, drawing on items from previously published surveys on sexually transmitted infections among medical and non-medical students [13–20] and adapted to the local context. Content validity was ensured through consultation with experts in epidemiology, dermatovenerology, and medical education, who reviewed the draft instrument for relevance, clarity, and alignment with the study objectives. To minimize the risk of overly difficult or ambiguous items, a pilot study was conducted with 20 students representing different study programmes and years, after which minor adjustments were made to improve comprehensibility and to balance item difficulty across the knowledge section. The seven attitude statements were based on key themes identified in earlier research on STI-related stigma, prevention, and education [18, 19], and were adapted to the Serbian setting. This process ensured that the questionnaire, while not a standardized international instrument, had adequate face and content validity for the purpose of assessing knowledge and attitudes in this exploratory cross-sectional study. The questionnaire comprised three sections: the first section included nine questions focusing on demographic details, prior education about syphilis, and participants’ self-assessed knowledge. The second section assessed factual knowledge of syphilis and its prevention using twelve items, with each correct response scored with one point (range 0–12). For the purpose of logistic regression analysis, a binary variable was created by applying a cut-off of more than six correct answers, reflecting above-average knowledge (> 50%). The third section featured seven statements exploring participants’ attitudes toward syphilis, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).

Ethics

This research adhered to ethical standards and the epidemiological part was approved by the Ethical board of the Institute of Public Health of Vojvodina (ethical approval number: 01–795/2, date: 16.05.2025). Ethical approval for the survey was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Novi Sad (ethical approval number: 01–39/12/1, date: 31.01.2025). The data collection was conducted in accordance with national ethical standards and the Declaration of Helsinki, and formal approval was granted retrospectively. Participation in the survey was entirely voluntary, anonymous, and uncompensated. Prior to questionnaire completion, participants received a clear explanation of the study’s purpose and provided informed consent. The informed consent was presented in the introductory part of the questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze demographic characteristics, knowledge scores, and attitude ratings. The ASRI of syphilis was calculated using the direct method of standardization, based on age-specific incidence rates in AP Vojvodina and the European standard population (ESP). Annual ASRI values were expressed per 100,000 population. Time trend analysis and linear regression were applied to assess changes in ASRI over the observed period. Variables with normal distributions were presented as means with standard deviations (± SD), while non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Comparisons between groups were performed using independent t-tests for normally distributed variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables, such as knowledge scores. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test where applicable. To explore the relationship between predictor variables and knowledge levels, univariable logistic regression was performed, defining a high knowledge level as correctly answering more than 6 out of 12 knowledge-based questions. Internal consistency of the questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s α. Reliability was calculated separately for the knowledge Sect. (12 items) and the attitudes Sect. (7 items). The knowledge scale consisted of dichotomous items scored as correct/incorrect, while the attitude scale included seven statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Cronbach’s α values were interpreted according to widely accepted thresholds, with α ≥ 0.70 indicating acceptable internal consistency. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0.2.

Results

Epidemiological characteristics of syphilis in the AP Vojvodina from 1997 to 2024

Between 1997 and 2024, a total of 1,034 cases of syphilis were registered in the AP Vojvodina. The ASRI fluctuated from a low of 0.51 per 100,000 inhabitants in 2011 to a peak of 9.33 per 100,000 in 2023. The data demonstrated a variable but overall increasing trend in age-adjusted syphilis incidence over the past two and a half decades (R2 = 0.43) (Fig. 1).

Age-standardized rate of incidence (ASRI) of syphilis per 100,000 Population in the AP Vojvodina (1997–2024)

Between 1997 and 2024, syphilis cases were reported across all age groups in the AP Vojvodina. Age-specific incidence analysis revealed that the highest rates were recorded in the 25–34 age group (107.8 per 100,000), followed by the 35–44 age group (104.4 per 100,000), while the lowest incidence was observed in the 0–14 years age group (2.9 per 100,000). Except for the youngest age group, the incidence rate of syphilis among males was higher than in females across all other age groups (Fig. 2).

Incidence rate of syphilis by age group and sex in the AP Vojvodina, 1997–2024

Sex-disaggregated analysis of syphilis cases in AP Vojvodina between 1997 and 2024 revealed a marked predominance among males, with 869 (84%) male and 165 (16%) female cases reported. This corresponds to cumulative incidence rates of 91.5 per 100,000 for men and 16.5 per 100,000 for women. The peak annual incidence in men occurred in 2023 (17.5 per 100,000; n = 148), with notable increases also in 2022 (12.4 per 100,000; n = 105) and 2021 (7.8 per 100,000; n = 73). In contrast, incidence rates among women remained low and stable throughout the entire period, with a maximum of only 1.3 per 100,000 in 2023 (Fig. 3).

Comparison of annual syphilis incidence rates (trends) between males and females in AP Vojvodina, 1997–2024

The results presented in Supplementary Table S2 show the annual distribution of syphilis cases and incidence rates across the districts of the AP Vojvodina from 1997 to 2024. The data demonstrate pronounced inter-district and temporal variation, with the highest cumulative number of cases and incidence consistently reported in South Bačka, peaking at 22.9 per 100,000 in 2023 (Supplementary Figure S1). Other districts, such as South Banat, North Bačka, and Central Banat, exhibited lower but sustained levels of incidence. In contrast, regions like West Bačka and North Banat initially reported sporadic cases, with gradual increases emerging in later years.

Supplementary Figure S2 displays the male-to-female ratio of reported syphilis cases across districts of the AP Vojvodina. The highest disparities were observed in Srem (9.33) and South Bačka (7.67), indicating a markedly higher proportion of male cases. In contrast, North Bačka showed the lowest sex ratio (1.46).

Supplementary Table S3 summarizes the distribution of 1,034 syphilis cases (869 men, 165 women) by diagnosis and sex. Syphilis recens (363 cases) and unspecified syphilis (243 cases) were the most common forms. Neurosyphilis and late forms (e.g., Syphilis tarda, tarda latens) were proportionally more frequent in women, while early forms like Syphilis secundaria (69 men vs. 15 women) and genitalis primaria (78 men vs. 24 women) were more common in men. Congenital forms were rare (4 cases total).

Knowledge and attitudes toward syphilis and its prevention among students

Among the total of 356 respondents who participated in the questionnaire-based survey, women comprised the majority (74.44%), with no significant difference in gender distribution between medical students and students from other study programmes. The average age was significantly higher among medical students (22.69 ± 2.69 years) compared to students from other programmes (21.47 ± 1.86 years; p < 0.001). Respondents who had received prior education on sexually transmitted infections were more likely to know that syphilis is transmitted through sexual contact (97.7% vs. 87.4%; p < 0.001) and that it can cause severe consequences if left undiagnosed and untreated (90.2% vs. 63.4%; p < 0.001). Students enrolled in study programmes that included lessons on syphilis were significantly more likely to report prior education on the topic (72.99% vs. 27.01%; p < 0.001). Figure 4 presents the attitudes toward syphilis and its prevention among all participating students. The vast majority (87%) strongly agree that it is important for medical students to be well-informed about syphilis, while 78% believe that education on syphilis and its prevention should be further strengthened within medical curricula. Most participants (60%) identified stigma as a key barrier to seeking medical assistance, and 67% stated that the importance of sexual education in syphilis prevention is underestimated in society. Furthermore, 57% emphasized the need for additional learning about syphilis, its transmission, and protection. Interestingly, 70% of respondents disagreed with the statement that they cannot contract syphilis because it is exclusively linked to the MSM population. Despite this, only 15% of respondents felt confident in their ability to advise patients on prevention. The internal consistency of the attitude scale was low (Cronbach’s α = 0.38), reflecting the multidimensional nature of the items, which were intended to cover different domains of attitudes toward syphilis education and stigma.

Discussion

Based on the presented findings, syphilis continues to emerge as a growing public health concern in the Autonomous Province of Vojvodina, northern Serbia. The data show a clear upward trend in incidence over the past decades, with the highest burden observed among sexually active young adult population. Geographically, the South Bačka district stood out as the most affected area. Beyond the epidemiological trends, the study also underscored the role of targeted education in shaping students’ knowledge about syphilis. Participants who had previously been exposed to structured learning on sexually transmitted infections generally demonstrated greater understanding and awareness. However, despite acknowledging the relevance of syphilis education, many students still lacked the confidence to effectively communicate preventive measures to patients, pointing to a gap between theoretical knowledge and practical readiness.

Syphilis, once a nearly eradicated infection in many developed healthcare systems, is now experiencing a concerning resurgence. Data from the ECDC indicate a continuous increase in confirmed cases of syphilis in the EU/EEA region over the past decade. In 2023, 41,051 cases were reported, representing a record rate of 9.9 per 100,000 inhabitants—almost double the number recorded in 2014 [8]. The results of our study in the AP Vojvodina confirm a similar pattern. From 1997 to 2024, a total of 1,034 cases of syphilis were registered, with a particularly notable rise in the past five years. The highest number of syphilis cases in AP Vojvodina was recorded in 2023, with a total of 160 reported infections, corresponding to an ASRI of 9.33 per 100,000 inhabitants. This increase, both at the European and local levels, is predominantly associated with the MSM population. In 2023, as many as 72% of syphilis cases with a known mode of transmission in the EU/EEA region were reported among MSM [8]. A significant epidemiological insight from our research is that men accounted for 84% of reported cases, aligning with the EU average. Additionally, the most affected age groups were 25–34 and 35–44 years, confirming that the highest risk exists among the sexually active young adult population. This group often simultaneously uses PrEP therapy for HIV prevention, which, according to European reports, may be linked to an increased prevalence of other bacterial sexually transmitted infections, including syphilis [12, 21]. It should be noted that the epidemiological part of this study is based on notification rates, which reflect reported cases in the surveillance system and may not fully capture the true incidence of syphilis in the population.

Compared to ECDC reports from 2018 to 2019, which showed rates of 7.0 and 7.4 per 100,000 inhabitants, respectively, AP Vojvodina had a significantly lower incidence at the time [25, 26]. However, the most recent data indicate a rapid increase in the incidence of syphilis both in Europe and in AP Vojvodina, which may be the result of a genuine rise in the number of infections, as well as improvements in surveillance systems and diagnostics.

In our research, we demonstrated that the highest prevalence of syphilis was recorded in the South Bačka district. This predominance is most likely explained by demographic and healthcare-related factors. The district includes Novi Sad, the largest city in AP Vojvodina, which has the highest population density in the province and serves as its main university and healthcare hub. The wider network of healthcare facilities, including specialized dermatovenerology clinics and voluntary counselling and testing centers, facilitates easier access to diagnostic services and increases the likelihood of case detection. Therefore, higher notification rates in this district may partly reflect a detection bias related to more intensive testing and better surveillance coverage, rather than a substantially higher underlying transmission rate. Similar post-2021 increases in syphilis cases have also been recorded in other parts of Serbia, particularly in major urban centers [27], and the same pattern has been observed across Europe, as highlighted in recent ECDC reports [8, 25, 26].

The COVID-19 pandemic temporarily influenced syphilis notification trends across Europe, largely due to reduced access to STI care services, decreased testing opportunities, and a decline in STI surveillance capacity, as reported by the ECDC [28, 29]. A similar pattern of relative stability in the number of cases was observed in AP Vojvodina during the pandemic years. However, in some countries where STI services remained uninterrupted or only partially disrupted, and syphilis testing continued, an increase in reported cases was observed during the pandemic period (e.g., Türkiye, Croatia) [30, 31]. This suggests that the apparent slowing of syphilis notifications in certain settings may reflect reduced detection rather than a true decline in transmission. In AP Vojvodina, growth resumed as early as 2021, coinciding with the relaxation of COVID-19 control measures, mass immunization of the population, and a consequent increase in social interactions, with a further acceleration noted in 2023 [32–34]. When comparing these data with previous studies, including those by Peterman et al. and Kojima et al. [35–38], it has been suggested that syphilis trends can reflect broader changes in sexual behaviour and gaps in preventive measures, particularly within the MSM population. Some authors also point to the increasing use of dating applications, which facilitate rapid and often anonymous sexual encounters, as a factor potentially contributing to higher transmission rates and complicating epidemiological monitoring and partner notification [38, 39].

This study provides an analysis of future healthcare professionals’ knowledge and attitudes regarding syphilis and its prevention. The findings revealed that students who had previously received education related to syphilis and its prevention exhibited significantly higher levels of knowledge. Additionally, these students were more inclined to offer accurate and practical advice on preventive methods, both to their patients and to family members and friends, compared to those who had not attended such educational sessions. When analyzing student attitudes across different study years and programs, it was observed that the majority of respondents demonstrated a positive outlook toward syphilis, its destigmatization, and the importance of preventive practices.

The knowledge and attitudes of future healthcare professionals about syphilis and prevention methods are crucial for improving public health. Raising awareness of syphilis and implementing preventive measures requires maximizing the use of available resources. Highly motivated medical students, supported by a network of mentors, educators, and peers, play a pivotal role in this process. Overcoming specific barriers, such as a lack of information about symptoms, diagnostic methods, and treatment options, as well as fear of stigmatization, can significantly enhance syphilis prevention and control efforts. Recent studies emphasize the importance of educational programmes for medical students, which not only increase their knowledge but also foster positive attitudes toward the importance of preventing STIs [40]. High-quality educational programmes, incorporating interactive methods, practical workshops, and case simulations, have proven effective in improving students’ knowledge and skills. Research on medical education in the field of STIs has highlighted the need for additional information, modern educational materials, and communication skills to enable future healthcare workers to confidently convey information to patients and reduce the stigma associated with syphilis [35, 41]. These findings underscore the need for continuous education and training for medical students, aligning with the conclusions of our study. Enhancing the knowledge and attitudes of future healthcare professionals is essential for creating a foundation for more effective syphilis prevention in the future, alongside active advocacy and education of the broader population on the importance of early recognition and treatment of this infection.

Acceptance of testing for STIs, including syphilis, marks significant progress in case detection, explaining the rise in incidence over time [42]. Low testing rates among young adults and limited case detection are partly attributed to stigma and shame [43]. Ignoring the impact of stigma on healthy sexual behaviour risks undermining open discussions about condom use and seeking support after an STI diagnosis. Fighting STI-related stigma requires raising awareness and educating society. Medical students, as future healthcare professionals, must advocate for open communication to reduce stigma and promote healthy sexual behaviours. This is essential for creating a supportive environment where individuals feel safe seeking help and support [44].

STIs are re-emerging, necessitating adequate sexual education in schools [45]. Our findings highlight the urgent need for integrating sexual education into Serbia’s school system to enhance awareness and reduce STIs incidence. Among surveyed students, 87% recognized the importance of being informed about syphilis, and 78% advocated for increased education on its prevention. However, only 15% felt confident advising patients, revealing gaps in practical skills and confidence. Additionally, 57% called for further learning, and 67% believed sexual education’s role in prevention is undervalued. These results underline the necessity of enhancing medical curricula with practical training and communication skill development, ensuring future healthcare professionals are equipped to counsel patients effectively. Beyond academia, implementing comprehensive sexual education in society is essential to reduce stigma and improve awareness about preventing syphilis and other STIs [45].

One of the key concerns regarding syphilis is its ability to progress through multiple stages, from primary and secondary infections to latent and tertiary forms [46], which can cause severe complications if left untreated. Congenital syphilis, in particular, remains a serious public health issue, as maternal syphilis can result in stillbirths, neonatal deaths, or lifelong health complications in infants. This may also explain the presence of reported syphilis cases in the 0–14 age group, which are most likely attributable to congenital infections rather than sexually acquired transmission. Despite the availability of penicillin-based treatments, many cases go undiagnosed due to lack of routine screening, especially in low-resource settings where prenatal care services are limited [47].

While certain differences between student groups may be expected, our findings should be viewed as exploratory. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies in Serbia to combine regional surveillance data with an assessment of future healthcare professionals’ knowledge and attitudes toward syphilis prevention. In light of the rising incidence of syphilis, these results underscore the importance of strengthening STI-related education during undergraduate training so that students are better prepared to provide preventive counselling and reduce stigma in their future clinical practice. Expanding such research to other centers in Serbia could provide a more complete national overview and inform strategies for developing comprehensive educational programs on STIs within the broader public health framework.

Strengths and limitations

The epidemiological component of this study is based on notification rates derived from a passive surveillance system, which has inherent limitations, including potential underreporting, a higher proportion of notifications from urban clinics, variability in diagnostic practices over time, incomplete or inaccurate case records, and occasional duplicate reporting. Changes in case definitions over time, particularly alignment with EU criteria in 2018, may have influenced data comparability. The aggregated surveillance data from AP Vojvodina lacked individual-level details such as risk behaviours, HIV status, or MSM identification, limiting the ability to explore specific transmission dynamics. As the data were restricted to one province, the findings may not fully reflect national trends.

Regarding the student component, the sample was drawn from a single medical faculty, limiting generalizability to other institutions. Although the sample size met the requirements for the primary objectives, it may have constrained subgroup analyses. Including students from different years of study inevitably introduces variation in knowledge levels, as senior students are expected to have greater exposure to infectious disease content. Sociocultural and institutional factors could also influence perceptions of STIs, and despite pilot-testing, some questions may have been misinterpreted. The cross-sectional design precludes assessing whether awareness leads to behavioural changes or sustained involvement in prevention. The low Cronbach’s α of the attitude scale indicates that the items capture multiple attitudinal domains rather than a single latent construct, which should be taken into account when interpreting composite attitude scores. This study did not assess practical skills related to syphilis prevention, as the questionnaire design was focused on knowledge and attitudes. Evaluating practical competencies would require a different methodology (e.g., OSCE or simulated counselling), which was beyond the scope of this research.

Despite these limitations, combining surveillance data with a cross-sectional survey provides complementary perspectives: long-term notification data illustrate epidemiological trends in syphilis, while the student survey offers a point-in-time snapshot of knowledge and attitudes among future healthcare professionals. Together, these findings highlight gaps in education and the need for targeted training to improve preparedness for syphilis prevention and control, while also contributing regional epidemiological evidence relevant to public health planning.

Conclusion

The rising incidence of syphilis in AP Vojvodina, consistent with trends across the EU/EEA, highlights the continued public health relevance of this infection. Our findings reveal knowledge gaps among healthcare students, despite generally positive attitudes toward prevention. Students who had previously received education on sexually transmitted infections demonstrated significantly higher levels of knowledge and a greater willingness to support preventive measures, highlighting the importance of formal education in this area. These findings clearly emphasize the need for the implementation of structured and targeted educational programs within medical training, as well as stronger involvement of the public health sector in designing and carrying out interventions aimed at prevention, destigmatization, and early diagnosis of syphilis. By strengthening the knowledge and practical skills of future healthcare professionals, a foundation is established for a more effective response to both current and emerging challenges in the control of STIs.
