Olfactory dysfunction among gas station employees exposed to fuel vapors using digital odor identification testing
Authors: José Maria Viana dos Santos, Márcio Nakanishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGas station employees are chronically exposed to fuel vapors containing benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene (BTEX), and other volatile organic compounds (VOCs), raising occupational concerns about olfactory function. In this cross-sectional study, 116 Brazilian gas station employees (86 men and 30 women; median age, 37.5 years; range, 19–58) completed a questionnaire and underwent objective olfactory assessment with the MultiScent-20 digital odor identification test. Overall, 58.6% had olfactory dysfunction, including 46.6% with hyposmia and 12.1% with anosmia, whereas only 23.3% reported that their work affected their sense of smell. Longer employment duration was associated with lower olfactory scores (Spearman’s ρ = −0.41; p < 0.001) and independently increased the odds of dysfunction (odds ratio [OR] = 1.15 per additional year; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.06–1.26; p = 0.003). These findings support incorporating olfactory assessment into occupational health surveillance for workers chronically exposed to fuel vapors.

Introduction

Occupational exposure to volatile chemicals present in fuel vapors, including benzene and other volatile organic compounds (VOCs), poses potential risks to olfactory function.1 Gas station employees are chronically exposed to gasoline vapors containing BTEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene), a group of toxic aromatic hydrocarbons.2 Benzene is a volatile, sweet-smelling compound3 classified as carcinogenic to humans, with strong evidence linking exposure to acute myeloid leukemia.4 Although benzene can typically be detected by smell at concentrations of approximately 1–5 parts per million (ppm),3 olfactory perception cannot be relied upon as a safety indicator. Odor perception varies substantially between individuals, may be influenced by olfactory adaptation and previous exposure, and does not provide a sufficiently reliable warning of hazardous airborne concentrations of benzene or other fuel-vapor constituents.5 As a result, workers may experience harmful exposure without awareness, underscoring the importance of systematic health monitoring.6

Olfactory function plays a critical role in both quality of life and occupational safety among gas station workers, who may use smell as one of several warning cues to detect fuel leaks and spills, thereby reducing fire and explosion risks. Olfactory dysfunction is also associated with impaired well-being and a reduced ability to detect environmental hazards such as smoke, gas leaks, or spoiled food.7 Case reports illustrate the severe consequences of olfactory loss in fuel-related settings, including incidents involving the ignition near gasoline spills by individuals with anosmia.8 Despite these risks, the impact of chronic fuel-vapor exposure on olfactory health remains underexplored in occupational health research. Occupational chemical exposure is estimated to account for 0.5%–5% of olfactory dysfunction cases,8 and a substantial proportion of so-called idiopathic smell loss may be attributable to workplace exposures.9 Epidemiological studies addressing olfactory outcomes following chronic solvent or fuel exposure remain scarce, and recent reviews confirm the limited evidence specifically linking fuel-vapor-related occupational exposures to olfactory impairment among fuel-industry workers.8

Emerging evidence indicates that several industrial chemicals can damage the olfactory system. Chronic exposure to heavy metals and solvents, including cadmium, manganese, formaldehyde, styrene, and acrylates, has been associated with impaired olfaction in humans and experimental models.8 Inhaled toxins may damage the olfactory epithelium and receptor neurons through oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic activation via cytochrome P450 enzymes.8 However, direct uptake through the olfactory nerve is more clearly established for ultrafine particles and nanomaterials, whereas for VOCs and solvent mixtures, mucosal irritation, epithelial inflammation, oxidative stress, and injury to olfactory receptor neurons are more plausible pathways10 The irritant properties of petrochemical vapors may also induce chronic rhinitis, leading to reversible or persistent olfactory loss.8,11

Gasoline vapor is a complex mixture, and the specific contribution of benzene to olfactory toxicity remains incompletely defined. Nevertheless, studies of analogous exposures support an association between hydrocarbon solvents and olfactory dysfunction. For example, Wartono and Herqutanto1 reported olfactory impairment in 84% of furniture and manufacturing workers chronically exposed to toluene. An Indonesian cross-sectional study found that 77.5% of gas station employees exhibited olfactory dysfunction compared with none of the unexposed controls, with longer job tenure significantly increasing the risk.12 Occupational exposure to aromatic hydrocarbons in service stations has also been described in workers exposed to mixtures of BTEX.13 In addition, ambient air pollution has been associated with reduced chemosensory sensitivity and anosmia, suggesting that traffic-related pollution may act as a concomitant exposure in urban gas station settings.14,15 These findings support the notion that occupational olfactory impairment is often subclinical and progressive, with many workers unaware of deficits until formal testing is performed.8,12,16 Despite international evidence linking BTEX exposure to olfactory dysfunction, data from Latin American populations remain limited, reinforcing the need for region-specific investigations.

In Brazil, gas station employees represent a substantial workforce subject to continuous exposure to fuel vapors and benzene-containing mixtures. National legislation prohibits self-service fueling, placing full responsibility for refueling on attendants and increasing cumulative inhalation of fuel vapors.17,18 Regulatory guidelines such as NR-09 mandate biological monitoring of benzene exposure using urinary trans,trans-muconic acid (ttMA).19 Brazilian studies demonstrate that even at ambient benzene concentrations below 1 ppm, workers exhibit elevated ttMA levels and biomarkers of benzene-related biological effects.6,19,20,21 Employees frequently report nasal irritation and epistaxis, consistent with mucosal toxicity.19 Nevertheless, preventive measures remain insufficient, with limited adoption of vapor recovery systems and low adherence to personal protective equipment (PPE), often due to discomfort or perceived low risk.22 Recent Brazilian studies further report clinical and laboratory alterations consistent with the need for enhanced occupational surveillance.23

Standardized olfactory assessment tools, such as the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) and Sniffin’ Sticks, are widely used in clinical and research settings.8 However, both require trained personnel, are time-consuming, and are costly for large-scale occupational screening.24 To address these limitations, the automated digital device MultiScent-20 (NOAR, São Paulo, Brazil) was developed. This portable system delivers 20 odor stimuli via a tablet interface, records responses electronically, and allows self-administration. Validation studies demonstrate high test-retest reliability and strong concordance with Sniffin’ Sticks (r ≈ 0.88), supporting its suitability for field-based research.24 Normative data from Brazilian populations establish reference values and cutoffs for normosmia and olfactory dysfunction categories, including hyposmia and anosmia.24

Given the limited information available in the literature on fuel-vapor-related occupational exposure and olfactory function, and the limited evidence regarding the use of digital odor identification testing in this occupational context, we aimed to investigate olfactory performance among gas station employees. We used the MultiScent-20 to objectively measure olfactory function in a group of gas station workers. Our objectives were to determine the prevalence of olfactory dysfunction classified as normosmia, hyposmia, and anosmia in workers chronically exposed to fuel vapors, examine the relationship between duration of exposure (years of tenure) and olfactory performance, and compare the results with international data from other smell tests (e.g., Sniffin’ Sticks and UPSIT) in similar contexts of chronic fuel-vapor or solvent exposure. We also discuss the implications for occupational health policy, including the need for routine smell testing and interventions to prevent olfactory impairment in these workers.

Results

Olfactory test results

MultiScent-20 identification scores ranged from 6 to 19, with a mean score of 13.9 ± 2.6. Overall, 58.6% of participants (68/116) met criteria for olfactory dysfunction. Most cases were classified as hyposmia (46.6%), while 12.1% presented severe impairment compatible with anosmia. The remaining 41.4% scored within the normosmic range. Certain odors were more frequently misidentified by participants with olfactory impairment, particularly apple, eucalyptol, and orange, whereas odors such as garlic, clove, and cinnamon were identified correctly by most participants. Gasoline was correctly identified by 69.8% of the total sample, indicating that the pattern of errors was not specific to fuel-related odors.

Subjective olfactory complaints

Only 27 participants (23.3%) reported that their work as gas station employees was affecting their sense of smell. Among these, 20/27 (74.1%) presented objective olfactory impairment on testing. Conversely, 48/68 (70.6%) of participants with objectively confirmed olfactory dysfunction did not report that their work was affecting their sense of smell. Several participants reported surprise at their test results and attributed perceived changes to aging or recurrent nasal conditions.

Association between duration of exposure and olfactory dysfunction

Olfactory identification scores decreased with increasing duration of occupational exposure. In the bivariate analysis, mean job tenure was 10.2 ± 7.1 years among participants with olfactory impairment and 5.9 ± 4.8 years among normosmic participants (p = 0.002). Spearman correlation analysis showed a moderate negative correlation between years of exposure and olfactory score (ρ = −0.41; p < 0.001). Figure 1 presents the distributions of MultiScent-20 scores in the total sample and among workers with ≥5 years of service, allowing direct visual comparison; the subgroup distribution was not used as evidence of the correlation analysis.

In multivariable logistic regression including age, smoking history, and years of exposure, duration of exposure remained independently associated with olfactory dysfunction (odds ratio [OR] = 1.15 per additional year; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.06–1.26; p = 0.003). Age was not significantly associated with impairment, and smoking status showed a marginal association (OR = 1.9; p = 0.08).

Other occupational factors

Use of PPE was infrequent: only 3 participants (2.6%) reported using PPE, whereas 113/116 (97.4%) reported no use. Among the 113 participants who answered the question on whether PPE use was necessary, 71 (62.8%) answered affirmatively. Awareness of benzene-related health risks was reported by 63% of participants, who cited discomfort, inconvenience, interference with customer service, or lack of employer enforcement as reasons for not using PPE.

Discussion

In this study, we found that chronic occupational exposure to fuel vapors, including benzene and other fuel-vapor constituents, was associated with olfactory dysfunction among gas station employees. Almost 60% of workers had objectively measurable olfactory impairment, and many did not report that their work was affecting their sense of smell. Rather than demonstrating toxicity from benzene alone, these findings should be interpreted in the broader context of repeated workplace exposure to gasoline-vapor mixtures, BTEX compounds, and traffic-related pollutants. The use of the MultiScent-20 digital device enabled efficient field testing and produced results consistent with, and complementary to, previous research using traditional smell tests in similar occupational settings.2,24,25

Our findings align with international evidence on solvent-related olfactory impairment. The prevalence of dysfunction observed in our cohort (58.6%) is comparable to the 77.5% reported by Sagala et al.26 among Indonesian gas station workers assessed with the Sniffin’ Sticks test, who also demonstrated an increased risk after approximately four years of exposure. Other cross-sectional studies similarly report impaired olfaction among gas station workers compared with non-exposed groups.11 In our data, longer occupational tenure was associated with lower olfactory scores, and regression analysis suggested that each additional year of exposure increased the odds of olfactory impairment by approximately 15%. Although this temporal gradient supports an exposure-related hypothesis, the possibility of a healthy worker effect cannot be excluded in a cross-sectional design.

The magnitude of impairment observed is consistent with findings from studies of other industrial chemicals. Chronic exposure to toluene has been associated with hyposmia in up to 84% of factory workers,1 while formaldehyde exposure has been linked to self-reported olfactory impairment in approximately one-third of anatomy laboratory staff.8 Notably, the prevalence observed in our study (≈59%) was high within this occupational sample. Occupational chemical exposure has been estimated to account for 0.5%–5% of olfactory dysfunction cases,1,9 supporting the relevance of considering mixed VOCs rather than benzene alone in occupational settings. Gobba9 previously hypothesized that occupational olfactory disorders are frequently subclinical and underreported, a proposition supported by our findings: only 20 of 68 workers with objective dysfunction (29.4%) reported that their work was affecting their sense of smell, consistent with earlier observations that olfactory impairment may go unnoticed unless severe.8 This underrecognition limits the effectiveness of routine occupational health surveillance based solely on symptom reporting.

From a mechanistic perspective, fuel-vapor-related olfactory toxicity is biologically plausible. Benzene and its metabolites can induce oxidative stress and DNA damage,8 and biomonitoring studies among Brazilian gas station workers have documented genotoxic and related biological effects consistent with such exposure.6,21 The olfactory epithelium is highly metabolically active and rich in cytochrome P450 enzymes, facilitating xenobiotic bioactivation.8 Chronic inhalation may therefore damage olfactory receptor neurons or supporting cells. However, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of direct local DNA damage in the olfactory mucosa. For solvent mixtures and VOCs, mucosal irritation, epithelial inflammation, oxidative stress, and injury to olfactory receptor neurons are more plausible mechanisms, whereas direct uptake through the olfactory nerve is more clearly related to ultrafine particles and nanomaterials.10

In addition, benzene and other petrochemical vapors are mucosal irritants, and prolonged exposure can cause subclinical inflammation of the nasal mucosa, impairing odor detection. Reports of frequent epistaxis among highly exposed workers, also described in Brazilian biomonitoring studies,19 support this mechanism. The presence of self-reported nasal or sinus conditions in a subset of participants may represent an independent source of olfactory impairment or may coexist with repeated irritant exposure, although these relationships could not be tested longitudinally in the present study. The item-level pattern was not specific to gasoline, as errors were also frequent for several non-fuel-related odors, suggesting that selective adaptation to fuel odor alone is unlikely to explain the observed dysfunction.

The safety implications of olfactory impairment are substantial. Reduced smell sensitivity may hinder the early detection of spills or leaks, diminishing one potential warning cue in hazardous workplace environments. Symptom-based studies among workers exposed to BTEX commonly report headache and throat irritation, underscoring the value of combining symptom surveillance with objective testing.27,28 In some countries, such as Germany, intact olfactory function is required for specific occupations involving gas detection.9 No such requirement exists for gas station workers in Brazil, despite the potential safety consequences of anosmia or hyposmia, which may render workers effectively less able to perceive odor-related hazards.

A major strength of this study was the use of the MultiScent-20 digital odor identification test, which enabled standardized, automated, and portable assessment in field conditions. Conventional tests such as UPSIT or Sniffin’ Sticks, although well validated, are less practical for large-scale workplace screening due to time, cost, and staffing requirements. The MultiScent-20 allowed self-administration, automatic scoring, and efficient testing workflows, features particularly advantageous in occupational health contexts.24,25 The focus on identification ability reflects functional relevance for workplace safety, although threshold testing may detect additional subclinical deficits and should be explored in future research.

The consistency of our findings with toxicological evidence and prior epidemiological studies supports the relevance of olfactory assessment in this occupational group. This study quantitatively characterizes olfactory dysfunction in this occupational setting and indicates that regulatory approaches focused primarily on the hematological and carcinogenic outcomes2 should be expanded to include sensory effects. Recent reviews of BTEX exposure similarly emphasize the need to broaden occupational surveillance beyond traditional endpoints.29

In terms of occupational health implications, this study highlights an unmet need for olfactory monitoring among gas station workers and other solvent-exposed occupations (e.g., shoemakers, printers, refinery workers).2 Currently, regulatory frameworks in many countries do not require sensory assessment for chemical-exposed workers. Medical surveillance for benzene exposure remains largely focused on hematological monitoring, including periodic blood counts and biomarkers of exposure, such as urinary benzene metabolites.19 Recent Brazilian studies reporting biological and hematological alterations among gas station workers further reinforce the need to strengthen surveillance frameworks.6,23

In this context, incorporating a simple olfactory test into occupational health programs may represent a valuable addition. Olfactory screening is a low-cost, low-risk approach, particularly when using reusable digital devices that can detect early functional impairment, even in the absence of severe clinical outcomes. Monitoring trends in olfactory decline over time may prompt timely investigation of workplace controls, such as ventilation and work practices, before more serious toxicity develops. Thus, olfactory testing may function as a complementary early warning indicator of possible sensory or neurotoxic effects, consistent with the principles of preventive medical surveillance.

Raising awareness among workers and employers is equally important. Many participants were unaware that olfactory impairment could be work-related. Educational initiatives should emphasize that anosmia or hyposmia is not merely an inconvenience, but a potential safety hazard and marker of hazardous exposure. Workers should be encouraged to report sensory changes, and employers should investigate exposure conditions when such reports emerge.

From a preventive standpoint, reducing exposure to benzene and other volatile compounds remains the most effective strategy. Engineering controls, such as stage II vapor recovery systems at fuel pumps, can substantially reduce emissions during refueling and should be considered where not yet implemented. Additional measures include job rotation or limiting prolonged continuous exposure at the pump. Substitution remains challenging, as benzene is an inherent component of gasoline, but lower-benzene fuel formulations may further mitigate the risk. The use of PPE, particularly respirators with organic vapor cartridges, remains controversial. Although effective in reducing inhalation exposure, sustained use may be impractical without enforcement and cultural change due to discomfort and work-related constraints. Nevertheless, respirators should be available for high-exposure tasks, such as tank cleaning or spill response, alongside simple protective practices such as minimizing proximity to nozzles and taking regular fresh-air breaks.

Finally, regulatory agencies should reassess assumptions that exposures below permissible limits are fully protective. Risk-assessment studies have documented benzene concentrations exceeding occupational limits in real-world fuel station settings, with associated elevated cancer risk.27,28 For benzene, the odor does not provide a reliable warning,5 and olfactory dysfunction may represent a relevant early sensory endpoint in workers chronically exposed to fuel vapors. Although cancer risk appropriately drives stringent exposure limits, our findings suggest that chronic exposure at subacute levels may still result in measurable sensory effects.

Based on these findings, we recommend: (1) periodic olfactory screening for workers chronically exposed to solvents, with follow-up for early hyposmia; (2) preventive workplace measures, including engineering controls, smoking bans, and job rotation; (3) worker education on the sensory effects of fuel-vapor exposure and strengthened PPE training; (4) longitudinal and interventional studies addressing long-term olfactory outcomes; and (5) regular neurological assessment to identify broader neuropsychological effects of solvent exposure.

This study identified a high prevalence of olfactory dysfunction among gas station employees, associated with prolonged occupational exposure to fuel vapors containing benzene and other VOCs. Using a digital odor identification test (MultiScent-20), we showed that most affected workers were unaware of their impairment, indicating that olfactory impairment may remain unrecognized in this occupational setting. Beyond systemic toxicity related to benzene and other gasoline-vapor constituents, chronic occupational exposure to these mixtures may compromise sensory function and worker safety, underscoring the need to strengthen attention to olfactory health in occupational settings. Routine smell testing and improved exposure control may help preserve an often-overlooked but essential component of workers’ health and safety.

Protecting gas station workers from fuel-vapor exposure is therefore not limited to preventing long-term systemic outcomes, but also to supporting workers’ ability to detect everyday hazards in the workplace. Chronic exposure to fuel vapors has long been linked to hematologic toxicity, reinforcing the relevance of early sensory monitoring as part of preventive occupational health strategies. Our findings support the inclusion of olfactory assessment as a complementary element of comprehensive occupational health and safety programs for gas station workers.

Limitations of the study

This study had some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inference, and longitudinal follow-up would strengthen exposure-outcome assessment. Second, although benzene was treated as an important exposure, gas station employees inhale complex mixtures of hydrocarbons, including toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene, other VOCs, and traffic-related pollutants. Thus, the olfactory effects observed are likely attributable to combined exposure to fuel-vapor components, including BTEX compounds, and other environmental pollutants. Air monitoring studies confirm measurable BTEX exposure in gas stations and indicate a non-trivial cancer risk from benzene in these workplaces.27,28 Ambient air pollution and traffic-related pollutants may also have contributed to the observed olfactory deficits, particularly because air pollution has been associated with reduced chemosensory sensitivity and anosmia.14,15

Individual exposure levels were not measured, and the use of personal monitoring or biomarkers such as ttMA would allow more precise exposure-response analyses. Future studies should integrate environmental or biological monitoring, as demonstrated in Brazilian biomonitoring research.6,21,23 The timing of testing, conducted during working hours, may also have influenced performance, because workers assessed later in the day could have experienced different cumulative exposure than those assessed earlier. In addition, testing in semi-open workplace areas may have allowed residual background odors from the gas station environment to interfere with odor identification, although the procedures were standardized and conducted in ventilated areas to minimize this bias.

Third, factors such as smoking, recent respiratory symptoms, and nasal conditions may confound olfactory testing. These conditions were captured in the questionnaire and considered during interpretation; however, their potential influence could not be fully disentangled. Self-reported nasal or sinus conditions may represent an independent source of olfactory impairment or coexist with repeated irritant exposure. Geographic variability was not analyzed and should be explored in future studies. Smoking may also have exacerbated olfactory loss independently of fuel-vapor exposure; therefore, residual confounding by smoking cannot be excluded.

Another limitation concerns generalizability. The sample was region-specific and relatively small; nevertheless, similar findings reported in Asian populations suggest broader relevance.11,12 Furthermore, only current workers were evaluated, raising the possibility of survivorship bias if more affected individuals had already left employment. In addition, dysfunction cut-offs were based on general population norms,24 as no occupational standards exist. Although these norms allow standardized classification, occupationally specific reference values may be needed for future surveillance and fitness-for-work assessments.

Experimental model and study participant details

This cross-sectional study included gas station employees (fuel pump attendants) in a metropolitan region of Brazil. Participants were recruited from 55 distinct gas stations across the metropolitan area. The Federal District Union of Fuel and Petroleum Derivatives Service Station Employees (SINPOSPETRO-DF) collaborated with us in recruiting participants from several service stations. Gas stations were selected in collaboration with the local workers’ union (SINPOSPETRO-DF) to ensure representation across urban and peripheral areas. Eligible individuals were active gas station employees aged 18 years or older with at least 6 months of work experience in the position. A total of 138 gas station workers completed the questionnaire, and 116 participants with complete questionnaire and MultiScent-20 data comprised the final analytic sample. Health conditions potentially affecting olfactory performance were recorded in the structured questionnaire and considered during interpretation of the findings. The study size was determined by feasibility and access to workers available during the data collection period through the union and participating stations, and no a priori sample size calculation was performed.

The final sample included 116 gas station employees, of whom 86 were men and 30 were women based on self-reported biological sex. The median age was 37.5 years (range, 19–58), with most participants aged 30–49 years. Approximately 66% had completed high school or had incomplete higher education. Race/color composition was predominantly mixed race (60.3%), followed by Black (17.2%), White (15.5%), Asian (3.4%), and Indigenous (3.4%) workers. More than 60% of participants reported ≥5 years of occupational tenure. All participants worked full time (8 h/day). Sex, race/color, age, and educational level were self-reported by participants. Gender identity was not separately collected and therefore could not be analyzed as an independent variable. Socioeconomic status beyond educational level was not directly measured, and this limitation is addressed in the interpretation of generalizability.

Method details

Data collection

The study was conducted on-site at participating gas stations or at union facilities. After obtaining informed consent, a structured questionnaire to collect demographic data (age, sex, and educational level), work history (years as a gas station employee, use of protective equipment), and health information (history of smoking, nasal or respiratory symptoms, and subjective changes in smell over time) was administered. To reduce information bias, all interviews followed the same standardized script and were applied by trained researchers.

Data collection was conducted from May to November 2024, following ethics approval. The questionnaire also captured self-reported chronic rhinitis or frequent nasal allergies because nasal inflammatory conditions may influence olfactory performance and were specifically considered during interpretation of the findings.

Olfactory test procedure

Olfactory function was objectively evaluated using MultiScent-20, which contained 20 microencapsulated odor cartridges, an internal air pump with a dry air filter, and a controlled outlet that releases one odor at a time. The device’s standard 20-odor identification test was used, which employs a four-alternative forced-choice (4-AFC) format for each odor. The odor stimuli include common aromas (e.g., coffee, cinnamon, gasoline, banana) chosen for their universal familiarity, as established by the device’s developers.24 For each test, the device displays four scent options on the touchscreen, and each participant is instructed to select the alternative that best matches the scent provided. A scent is emitted for approximately 5 s after the participant activates it by tapping “Start” on the screen. A constant airflow, driven by a fan, carries the scent to the outlet, ensuring a consistent concentration of the scent. Participants were instructed on how to use the device and then performed the test at their own pace, generally completing the 20 items in 10–15 min. They could take short breaks between items if necessary (the minimum interval between stimuli was 6 s, but reading the options often imposed a longer interval). The device’s software automatically recorded each response (correct/incorrect) and calculated the total identification score out of 20 points.

The manufacturer’s guidelines for administration and scoring were followed. All assessments were conducted during regular working hours (8 a.m.–5 p.m.). This testing window reflected the feasibility of assessing active workers during their shifts; however, because time of day was not analyzed as an independent exposure variable, possible variation between morning and afternoon testing was considered in the limitations. Each cartridge in the device contained approximately 35 μL of odor solution in an oil-based medium, enough for almost 100 presentations with stable intensity.24 The device was calibrated and loaded with new odor cartridges prior to the study. The test was conducted in a semi-open, well-ventilated area of the workplace to reduce strong background odors while maintaining field conditions. Because testing occurred in workplace environments, residual background odors from fuel vapors could not be fully eliminated; therefore, this potential source of measurement bias was explicitly considered in the revised discussion and limitations.

All measurements were performed by two trained researchers using pre-calibrated devices before each testing session. To minimize cross-exposure between participants, an interval of at least 5 min was ensured between successive tests, allowing residual odors to dissipate. All procedures were non-invasive and conducted in ventilated open areas to ensure participants’ safety. The test interface was available in Portuguese (the participants’ native language), and a researcher was present to clarify the instructions, but did not interfere with the test once it began. To reduce measurement bias, the same test protocol, device configuration, and testing conditions were applied to all participants.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was olfactory identification score (0–20 correct responses). Based on normative data for the MultiScent-20, olfactory function was categorized into three groups: normosmia (≥15 points), hyposmia (11–14 points), and anosmia (≤10 points).24 For the multivariable logistic regression, olfactory dysfunction was defined as hyposmia or anosmia versus normosmia, because this binary grouping allowed a more stable model in the available sample.

As a secondary outcome, participants were asked whether they perceived that their work as gas station employees was affecting their sense of smell (yes/no).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Results are presented as means ± standard deviations, medians and ranges, or proportions (%), as appropriate. The exact value of n represents the number of gas station employees included in each analysis, and the corresponding denominators were reported when available. The prevalence of olfactory dysfunction was calculated as the percentage of participants with identification scores in the affected range.

The association between duration of exposure (years working as a gas station employee) and olfactory score was assessed in two ways. First, a correlation analysis (Spearman’s rho) was performed, treating years of employment as a continuous variable and olfactory score as an ordinal or non-normally distributed continuous outcome. Second, job duration was evaluated descriptively using occupational tenure categories when applicable. Figure 1 is presented as a combined descriptive visualization of olfactory scores in the total sample and among workers with ≥5 years of service; the subgroup distribution was not used as evidence of the Spearman correlation.

The model evaluated years of exposure while accounting for age and smoking history, since older workers and smokers may have poorer smell independently. Sex was additionally assessed as a potential covariate and was described in the participant characteristics; because no statistically significant difference in olfactory scores was observed between male and female participants, this finding was reported together with the regression results. Gender identity was not separately collected and therefore could not be included as an independent covariate. Model fit and residual analysis were verified, with no multicollinearity observed. A significance level of α = 0.05 was used for hypothesis testing.

Comparisons between male and female participants were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test because olfactory scores were not normally distributed. Exploratory comparisons across educational levels and race/ethnic groups were assessed using analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis tests for olfactory scores, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical outcomes when applicable.

Missing data were assessed for all variables before analysis; when occasional missingness occurred, analyses were conducted using available data with the corresponding denominators reported, and regression models were fitted using complete cases for the included covariates. No formal interaction terms were modeled; subgroup comparisons were limited to the prespecified stratification by job tenure (<5 vs. ≥ 5 years) when applicable. No sensitivity analyses were prespecified or conducted. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v25) and R.
