Short-term safety and feasibility of laparoscopic–endoscopic cooperative surgery for gastric subepithelial tumors: a retrospective observational study of the first single-center experience in Mongolia
Authors: Khaliunaa Battulga, Amarbat Baatarnum, Ganbaatar Rentsenbalbar, Jargalsaikhan Duntei, Erdene-Ochir Yansan, Souya Nunobe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeLaparoscopic–endoscopic cooperative surgery (LECS) is a minimally invasive, organ-preserving approach for gastric subepithelial tumors (SETs). Although well established in high-volume East Asian centers, its use in Mongolia has not been reported. We report on our first experience and assess short-term safety and feasibility.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients who underwent LECS for gastric SETs at the National Cancer Center of Mongolia (November 2022–May 2025). Clinicopathologic characteristics, operative outcomes, and postoperative events were analyzed; complications were graded by the Clavien-Dindo classification.ResultsThirty-five patients underwent LECS during the study period. The mean age was 55 ± 12 years, and 60% were female. The mean tumor size was 27 ± 11 mm, and most tumors originated from the fourth endoscopic ultrasonography layer (muscularis propria). Histopathological analysis revealed that gastrointestinal stromal tumors accounted for 65.7% of all resected lesions. The mean operative time was 118 ± 57 minutes, and the mean blood loss was 44 ± 20 mL. All procedures were completed laparoscopically with R0 resection and no conversion to open surgery. No postoperative complications of Clavien-Dindo grade ≥II occurred. The mean postoperative hospital stay was 5.9 ± 0.9 days, and no reoperation, readmission, or recurrence was observed during a mean follow-up of 17.8 ± 7.2 months.ConclusionLECS was safely introduced in Mongolia, achieving excellent perioperative outcomes and complete resection. These findings support LECS as a feasible, function-preserving option for appropriately selected gastric SETs.

INTRODUCTION

Gastric subepithelial tumors (SETs), including gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), are a heterogeneous group of lesions that originate from the muscularis propria or submucosal layers of the stomach. These tumors are generally slow growing but carry a risk of malignant transformation, and surgical resection remains the standard curative treatment to achieve histological diagnosis and complete tumor clearance [1,2].

With the widespread use of diagnostic endoscopy and imaging, detection of gastric SETs has increased, including in Asia [3,4]. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is a minimally invasive option for early gastric neoplasms and selected small subepithelial lesions confined to the submucosa, allowing en bloc resection and rapid recovery [5,6]. However, its feasibility is limited by tumor size, depth, and involvement of the muscularis propria. For muscle-layer lesions or tumors in anatomically challenging locations (e.g., cardia or pylorus), ESD carries higher risks of perforation and incomplete resection, and surgical management is often required [7,8].

To address these limitations, laparoscopic–endoscopic cooperative surgery (LECS) was developed in Japan by Hiki et al. [9] in 2008. LECS combines endoscopic margin definition with laparoscopic full-thickness resection and secure closure, enabling complete tumor removal while minimizing unnecessary resection of normal gastric wall and preserving gastric anatomy. Multiple Japanese studies have since demonstrated favorable short-term safety and oncologic adequacy for gastric SETs, with high R0 resection rates and low complication rates [10,11].

LECS was approved for reimbursement under the Japanese National Health Insurance system in 2014, further supporting its role as a standardized, organ-preserving minimally invasive technique [12].

Although LECS is widely accepted in Japan, Korea, and other advanced Asian centers, evidence from Central Asia remains limited. Mongolia has one of the highest gastric cancer burdens worldwide (incidence 32.5 and mortality 24.6 per 100,000). Since 2022, the implementation of a national gastric cancer screening program in Mongolia has led to increased detection of both malignant and premalignant gastric lesions. At the same time, detection of gastric SETs—particularly GISTs—has increased with broader use of diagnostic endoscopy and greater clinical awareness. In Mongolia, these lesions have traditionally been managed with open or laparoscopic wedge resection, which may remove excess normal gastric wall and risk postoperative deformity.

Recognizing these challenges, the National Cancer Center of Mongolia—the nation’s only tertiary referral cancer center—introduced the LECS technique in November 2022 as part of efforts to establish minimally invasive and function-preserving surgical options. Therefore, here, we report the first clinical experience with LECS in Mongolia and present results on its short-term surgical outcomes, safety, and feasibility within a newly developed minimally invasive surgical environment.

METHODS

Patients

We conducted a retrospective, single-center observational study at the National Cancer Center of Mongolia, Ulaanbaatar. Consecutive adult patients who underwent LECS for gastric lesions between November 2022 and May 2025 were identified from institutional records. Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) with gastric SETs considered suitable for organ-preserving local resection after multidisciplinary evaluation. Inclusion criteria included lesions suspected to arise from the muscularis propria or deep submucosal layer on endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS), absence of distant metastasis on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), and technical feasibility for LECS based on tumor size, anatomical location, and intraluminal or mixed growth pattern. LECS was preferentially selected for lesions located in anatomically challenging areas, including the cardia, fornix, posterior wall, or pyloric region, where conventional laparoscopic wedge resection could result in excessive gastric wall sacrifice or postoperative deformity. LECS was also favored for lesions clinically suspected to represent GISTs or other tumors requiring precise full-thickness resection while preserving gastric anatomy. In contrast, predominantly extraluminal lesions suitable for conventional laparoscopic wedge resection were generally not considered candidates for LECS. Lesions requiring wider gastric resection or formal lymphadenectomy were considered for formal gastrectomy.

Exclusion criteria were an upfront indication for open surgery or formal gastrectomy, palliative procedures, planned laparoscopic local resection without luminal endoscopy, concomitant major abdominal operations, or missing key perioperative outcome data.

All patients initially scheduled for LECS were included in the planned-LECS cohort. Intraoperative conversion to conventional laparoscopic wedge resection was performed when the lesion demonstrated a predominantly extraluminal growth pattern with a clearly identifiable external contour, making cooperative endoscopic assistance unnecessary. These converted cases were excluded from the per-protocol analysis of completed LECS procedures (Fig. 1).

Preoperative assessment

All patients underwent routine preoperative evaluations, including laboratory tests, 12-lead electrocardiography, chest radiography, and performance status assessment. No preoperative endoscopic biopsy was performed. Lesions were assessed by diagnostic upper endoscopy and EUS to determine layer of origin, size, and proximity to the esophagogastric junction or pylorus. Contrast-enhanced CT was performed in all patients to assess extraluminal disease and exclude metastasis. Suitability for LECS was confirmed through multidisciplinary discussion.

Selection of surgical procedure

The surgical strategy was defined in a multidisciplinary meeting of laparoscopic surgeons and interventional endoscopists. For clinically suspected gastric GIST, organ-preserving local resection was selected according to Japanese practice guidelines with the approach tailored to tumor size, site, and growth pattern [13]. For other subepithelial lesions, LECS was chosen when endoscopic resection alone was unlikely to achieve negative margins or when wedge resection was expected to cause meaningful gastric deformity.

As no preoperative biopsy was performed, decisions relied on esophagogastroduodenoscopy, EUS, and contrast-enhanced CT; lesions anticipated to require extensive resection were considered for formal gastrectomy rather than local resection.

Surgical procedures

LECS was performed under general anesthesia using a classic two-team approach (laparoscopy plus luminal endoscopy). Standard patient/team positioning and a four-port setup were used (12-mm umbilical camera port, 12-mm right lower quadrant working port, and two 5-mm ports in the right upper quadrant and left side). After pneumoperitoneum, the abdomen was inspected and the stomach was gently mobilized to expose the target area.

The lesion was identified endoscopically and circumferentially marked to define the resection margin. Following submucosal injection (sodium hyaluronate with 0.5% epinephrine), a mucosal and submucosal incision was created using a needle knife/insulated-tip knife, and a controlled full-thickness entry was made under laparoscopic visualization. Resection was completed along the predetermined line with endoscopic traction and orientation. The specimen was retrieved in a bag (Fig. 2).

A modified closed LECS approach was selectively applied in cases where intentional communication between the gastric lumen and abdominal cavity was considered undesirable. In this approach, ESD and laparoscopic seromuscular closure were performed without intentional gastric wall opening into the peritoneal cavity.

Defect closure was selected based on defect size and location. Closure was performed either with laparoscopic hand-sewn seromuscular sutures or with a linear stapler; stapled closures were reinforced with interrupted seromuscular sutures at staple-line ends and overlap points (Fig. 3). Integrity was confirmed by intraluminal endoscopic inspection and an air leak test.

Postoperative follow-up

Patients with pathologically confirmed GIST were followed according to Japanese/Asian surveillance recommendations, with risk-adapted contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT after R0 resection [12]. For non-GIST gastric subepithelial lesions (and selected mucosal lesions), follow-up primarily consisted of surveillance endoscopy (±EUS) at defined intervals based on lesion size, with cross-sectional imaging performed when clinically indicated; higher-risk cases were co-managed with a medical oncologist in a multidisciplinary setting [14].

Outcomes and definitions

The primary endpoint was feasibility, defined as completion of LECS as planned; conversions to open surgery or noncooperative laparoscopic wedge resection were considered incomplete. Secondary endpoints included safety (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥II complications considered clinically relevant), operative outcomes (operative time, estimated blood loss, specimen retrieval route, and closure method), recovery (postoperative length of stay), and oncologic adequacy (R0/R1 margin status and recurrence during follow-up).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were descriptive. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (range) and categorical variables as number (%). No inferential testing was performed (no planned comparisons), and p-values are not reported. Missing data were handled by complete-case reporting. Time-to-event analyses were not performed due to the absence of recurrence or major events. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.x (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and/or IBM SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Thirty-nine patients were initially scheduled for LECS during the study period. Two patients elected to undergo open surgery based on patient preference before treatment and were excluded from the planned-LECS cohort. Consequently, 37 patients were planned for LECS, of whom 35 successfully underwent LECS for gastric SETs between November 2022 and May 2025 at National Cancer Center of Mongolia. The remaining two patients were intraoperatively converted to conventional laparoscopic wedge resection because the tumors demonstrated predominantly extraluminal growth with clearly identifiable external contours. The mean patient age was 55 ± 12 years (range, 30–76 years), and 21 patients (60%) were female. The mean body mass index was 25.7 ± 3.8 kg/m2, with 10 patients (28.6%) classified as normal, 14 (40.0%) overweight, and 11 (31.4%) obese. Twelve patients (34%) had at least one comorbidity. Tumors were located most frequently in the upper third of the stomach (18, 51.4%), followed by the lower third (12, 34.3%) and the middle third (5, 14.3%). Circumferentially, posterior-wall lesions predominated (18, 51.4%), with anterior-wall (9, 25.7%), lesser curvature (5, 14.3%), and greater curvature (3, 8.6%) being less common. EUS revealed that most lesions (32, 91.4%) originated from the fourth EUS layer (muscularis propria); three (8.6%) arose from the third layer. The mean tumor diameter was 27 ± 11 mm (range, 7–55 mm) (Table 1).

Operative details

The 35 patients in whom LECS was completed were analyzed. Classical LECS was performed in 34 patients (97.1%), whereas a modified closed LECS technique was applied in one patient (2.9%). The mean operative time was 118 ± 57 minutes (range, 31–280 minutes), and the mean estimated blood loss was 44 ± 20 mL (range, 10–100 mL). Specimens were retrieved transorally in 27 patients (77.1%) and transabdominally in eight (22.9%). Gastric wall closure was achieved with a linear stapler in 34 patients (97.1%) and by hand-sewn suturing in one patient (2.9%). Complete resection with negative margins was achieved in all cases (R0, 100%) (Table 2).

Completion of laparoscopic–endoscopic cooperative surgery

All operations were completed laparoscopically without conversion to open surgery. The completion rate of LECS was 94.6% (35/37). No cases required conversion to open surgery.

Pathological findings

Final histopathological examination confirmed that GISTs were the most frequent diagnosis, identified in 23 of 35 patients (65.7%). The remaining cases included ectopic pancreas (four cases, 11.4%), neuroendocrine tumor (four cases, 11.4%), schwannoma (two cases, 5.7%), leiomyoma (one case, 2.9%), and Vanek’s inflammatory fibroid polyp (one case, 2.9%). Among the GISTs, the majority were of low-risk histological grades, and all achieved R0 resection with negative margins. There were no instances of tumor rupture, peritoneal spillage, or margin positivity (Table 3).

Postoperative outcomes

All patients recovered uneventfully. There were no postoperative complications of Clavien-Dindo grade ≥II. Specifically, there were no cases of postoperative bleeding, anastomotic or staple-line leak, delayed gastric emptying, surgical-site infection, or intra-abdominal abscess formation. Minor postoperative discomfort, such as mild epigastric pain or nausea, resolved within the first 24 hours under conservative management. The mean postoperative hospital stay was 5.9 ± 0.9 days (range, 4–8 days). No patient required reoperation or readmission within 30 days of discharge. Oral intake was typically resumed on postoperative day 2, and all patients were able to ambulate independently by the end of the second postoperative day (Table 4).

Follow-up outcomes

During a mean follow-up of 17.8 ± 7.2 months (range, 4–34 months), no local recurrence, metachronous gastric lesions, or distant metastasis was detected on surveillance endoscopy or imaging.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that LECS enables safe, reproducible, and organ-preserving resection of gastric SETs, consistent with outcomes from high-volume East Asian centers. It represents the first structured assessment of LECS in Mongolia, showing the procedure can be effectively adapted within an emerging healthcare system. Establishing a reproducible LECS program in such settings carries important implications for surgical training, resource use, and equitable access to organ-preserving cancer care in developing regions.

Our institutional experience shows that LECS effectively addresses the limitations of conventional wedge resection for gastric SETs in a referral setting. Developed in Japan, LECS combines endoscopic marking with laparoscopic serosal control to achieve accurate, organ-preserving full-thickness excision, even in challenging sites such as the posterior wall or esophagogastric junction [9,15,16]. Previously, management at our center relied on open or laparoscopic wedge resection, both of which often remove excess gastric wall, risk deformity or stenosis in endophytic or proximal lesions, and cause more pain and slower recovery—drawbacks also noted in Japanese series [12].

In our 35-patient completed-LECS series, LECS enabled precise full-thickness excision with preservation of gastric anatomy. Standardized steps—linear-stapled closure with endoscopic confirmation and transoral specimen retrieval—aligned with established protocols and likely contributed to the absence of Clavien-Dindo grade ≥II complications, leaks, or bleeding [17]. Oncologic outcomes were reassuring, with R0 resection in all cases, no tumor rupture or spillage, and no recurrence during a mean follow-up of 17.8 ± 7.2 months, consistent with contemporary Asian LECS series for presumed GISTs [18,19]. Collectively, these findings support LECS as a reliable, organ-preserving alternative to wedge resection.

With respect to tumor characteristics, our cohort closely mirrored established Japanese LECS series. Mean tumor diameter was 27 ± 11 mm (range, 7–55 mm), comparable to Hanayama et al. (25 ± 9 mm, n = 40) and Hashimoto et al. (30.7 ± 12.5 mm; range, 5–80 mm; n = 201) [20]. Upper-third lesions accounted for 51.4%, similar to Fukushima (55%) and the multicenter cohort (61.2%), although lower-third lesions were more frequent in our series (34.3% vs. 7.5% and 10.9%) [18,20]. Posterior wall tumors predominated (51.4%), exceeding the multicenter report (25.4%), indicating enrichment for technically demanding posterior lesions [20]. As in prior LECS reports, most tumors originated from the muscularis propria on EUS (91.4%), and GIST was the most common diagnosis (65.7%), comparable to Hashimoto et al. (71.6%) [18]. Overall, these similarities in size and distribution support the generalizability of our findings and suggest that LECS can be applied safely even for anatomically challenging gastric sites in an emerging program. Notably, posterior wall lesions accounted for more than half of cases, indicating that LECS was successfully applied to technically demanding tumor locations.

Our perioperative outcomes were generally consistent with the range reported in established East Asian LECS series. Operative time, blood loss, postoperative recovery, and complication rates were comparable to previously published studies from high-volume centers [18,20]. No Clavien-Dindo grade ≥II complications occurred, and all completed LECS cases achieved negative margins without recurrence during follow-up, although the current follow-up duration remains limited. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because our cohort reflects the early implementation phase of the program, with careful case selection, predominance of classical LECS procedures, and a relatively high proportion of small and low-risk tumors. These factors may have contributed to the favorable short-term outcomes observed in the present study.

Beyond the quantitative outcomes, our experience highlights a stepwise pathway for introducing LECS. After the first case in November 2022 was performed with visiting experts from Hong Kong and Japan, with active participation of our local surgical and endoscopic team, we formed a dedicated multidisciplinary team, adopted standardized Japanese protocols, and expanded indications gradually with careful case selection and internal review. During the early implementation phase, subsequent cases were performed independently with ongoing case-by-case consultation and technical advice from the visiting experts. This structured approach facilitated a safe transition from proctored procedures to an independent and sustainable LECS program, which may be informative for other emerging centers introducing complex organ-preserving minimally invasive gastric surgery.

Moreover, prior Japanese experience suggests that LECS may extend beyond SETs to selected early gastric cancers that are technically difficult to treat with ESD [21]. This concept is supported by reports on LECS for challenging mucosal lesions and by the potential expansion of function-preserving local resection within a sentinel node-guided framework for carefully selected early gastric cancers [21]. At our institution, LECS for gastric cancer (LECS-G), using a non-exposure technique such as non-exposed endoscopic wall-inversion surgery has recently been applied in a small number of ESD-difficult early gastric cancers with high perceived perforation risk; although outside the scope of the current analysis, these cases suggest that LECS-G may complement ESD as our program matures. In a high-incidence setting such as Mongolia, gradual adoption of LECS-G within a function-preserving strategy may broaden organ-preserving treatment options while maintaining oncologic safety [21].

This study has important limitations. It is a retrospective, single-center analysis with a small sample size (n = 35), which may limit statistical power and generalizability. Because the cohort reflects the early phase of our LECS program, outcomes may also be influenced by learning-curve effects and careful case selection. In addition, the mean follow-up of 17.8 months, although with R0 resection and predominantly low-risk histology, is insufficient to assess long-term oncologic durability, particularly for higher-risk lesions and continued long-term surveillance will therefore be necessary. Finally, the absence of a control group precludes direct comparison with alternative approaches (e.g., laparoscopic or open wedge resection) in terms of functional outcomes, costs, and quality of life.

Our findings represent an initial step in a broader effort to develop organ-preserving gastric surgery in Mongolia. Future work will focus on maintaining a prospective institutional LECS database and moving toward a national registry to capture perioperative, functional, and long-term oncologic outcomes across centers. We plan to expand LECS through structured training and proctoring, with stepwise extension of indications to carefully selected ESD-difficult early gastric cancers and integration of sentinel node-guided strategies as local expertise evolves. Parallel assessment of quality of life, nutritional outcomes, and cost-effectiveness will be important to inform wider adoption and national treatment planning. Our findings further demonstrate that advanced organ-preserving techniques such as LECS can be safely and effectively implemented in emerging healthcare systems, even in the setting of limited prior experience in minimally invasive gastric surgery. Through these efforts, the present single-center experience may serve as a practical reference for other institutions seeking to establish LECS programs in emerging healthcare systems.

This early single-center experience shows that LECS can be introduced safely for gastric SETs within an emerging minimally invasive program. Among 37 patients initially scheduled for LECS, 35 successfully underwent LECS procedures, whereas two patients were intraoperatively converted to conventional laparoscopic wedge resection because of predominantly extraluminal tumor growth. No cases required conversion to open surgery. Completed LECS cases achieved 100% R0 resection without Clavien-Dindo grade ≥II complications, followed by rapid recovery and no recurrence during follow-up. As the first structured LECS series from Mongolia, these findings support LECS as an organ-preserving option even for anatomically challenging lesions. Longer follow-up and prospective multicenter studies, including stepwise evaluation of LECS-G for ESD-difficult early gastric cancers, are needed to define long-term oncologic and functional outcomes and to clarify the role of LECS within national care pathways.
