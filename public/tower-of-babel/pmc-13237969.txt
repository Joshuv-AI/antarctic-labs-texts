Spatial and temporal inequalities in malaria incidence and mortality among children aged 0–4 years in Somalia: a subnational analysis, 2010–2019
Authors: Yahye Sheikh Abdulle Hassan, Mohamed Abdirahim Omar, Abdirasak Sharif Ali, Uthman Okikiola Adebayo, Olalekan John Okesanya, Jamilu Sani, Mohamed Mustaf Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria is a leading cause of under-5 mortality in sub-Saharan Africa. In Somalia, Plasmodium falciparum predominates and transmission shows spatial and seasonal variation. This study analyzed subnational inequalities in malaria incidence and mortality among children aged 0–4 years to inform targeted control strategies.MethodsThis retrospective subnational analysis used modeled annual estimates of Plasmodium falciparum incidence, Plasmodium vivax incidence, and malaria mortality among children aged 0–4 years across Somalia’s 18 regions (2010–2019), sourced from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation via the World Health Organization Health Equity Assessment Toolkit. Five inequality measures (Difference, Ratio, Between-Group Variance, Population Attributable Risk, Population Attributable Fraction) were calculated within the Toolkit’s online software, following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.ResultsPlasmodium falciparum incidence rose sharply between 2010 and 2015, peaking at 51.6% in Awdal, and remained elevated in central and southern regions through 2019 (Bay 32.0%; Juba Dhexe 31.1%). Plasmodium vivax stayed low and spatially homogeneous (Difference ≤ 0.3). The between-group variance for Plasmodium falciparum peaked at 64.3 in 2015 and declined to 20.2 by 2019, while the ratio fell from 5.6 to 1.9. Mortality inequalities widened, with the difference rising from 33.9 (2010) to 118.5 (2014), the between-group variance from 100.6 to 1152.7, and the ratio remaining at 4.24 in 2019.ConclusionsDespite narrowing incidence gaps, children in central and southern Somalia were over four times more likely to die than those in least affected regions, reflecting structural healthcare inequalities.Keywords: Malaria incidence, Malaria mortality, Under-five malaria, Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, Childhood malaria, Somalia

Introduction

Malaria remains a devastating public health challenge across sub-Saharan Africa, where it accounts for a disproportionate share of global morbidity and mortality [1]. Despite concerted international efforts to eradicate the disease, the burden persists in East Africa due to a complex interplay of biological, environmental, and socioeconomic drivers [2]. Children under the age of five are the most vulnerable demographic, bearing the brunt of severe complications such as cerebral malaria and severe anemia due to their lack of acquired immunity [3, 4]. In Somalia, the disease is endemic and remains a primary cause of illness and death, particularly in the riverine regions of the south and center where transmission is most intense [5]. Although children aged 0–4 face the highest mortality risk, malaria is a generalized threat across Somalia. Risks to pregnant women closely mirror under-five spatial patterns in riverine zones, while older, partially immune populations serve as critical transmission reservoirs for the disease.

The epidemiology of malaria in Somalia is characterized by distinct spatial and temporal heterogeneity. Transmission intensity varies widely, from the epidemic-prone arid zones in the north to the stable transmission zones in the southern floodplains [5]. Plasmodium falciparum is the dominant species, accounting for over 95% of infections [6, 7]. These spatial patterns are heavily influenced by environmental factors such as altitude and vegetation cover [8], as well as climatic variations, with transmission peaks closely following the Gu (April to June) and Deyr (October to November) rainy seasons [9, 10]. Fine-scale spatial analysis has shown that malaria risk often clusters in “hotspots” within these regions, necessitating targeted rather than blanket interventions [11].

Between 2010 and 2019, Somalia’s malaria control landscape was shaped by significant challenges, including political instability, internal displacement, and climate shocks. While national strategies were deployed to scale up interventions such as the distribution of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS); coverage remained uneven [12, 13]. Populations in internally displaced persons (IDP) settlements and rural areas faced compounded risks due to poor housing and limited access to healthcare [12]. Furthermore, the emergence of drug resistance and gaps in vector control have threatened to reverse gains made in previous years [2, 14].

Understanding the subnational variations in malaria burden is essential for transitioning from control to elimination. National-level aggregates often mask the localized inequalities that drive persistent transmission [15]. Advanced geostatistical mapping has proven critical in other African contexts, such as Ghana and Uganda, for identifying high-risk areas and optimizing resource allocation [15, 16]. Consequently, this study aims to analyze the spatial and temporal inequalities in malaria incidence and mortality among children aged 0–4 years in Somalia from 2010 to 2019, providing evidence to guide more precise and effective public health interventions.

Methods

Study background and data sources

This study utilized annual geospatial estimates to conduct a retrospective, longitudinal analysis of malaria incidence and mortality among children aged 0–4 years across the 18 regions of Somalia from 2010 to 2019. The primary data source for this analysis was the malaria dataset produced by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) in collaboration with the Malaria Atlas Project. These estimates were derived from a sophisticated spatiotemporal geostatistical modeling framework that integrates geographically referenced data from national household surveys, routine clinical surveillance, and geospatial covariates.

The primary data inputs for these models were derived from nationally representative surveys, including the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) and the Malaria Indicator Survey (MIS). As noted in similar subnational analyses, these surveys employ distinct sampling strategies: the DHS utilizes a stratified, multi-stage cluster sampling design that may result in substantial “seasonal mixing” over extended survey periods, potentially limiting its ability to capture peak transmission intensity. Conversely, the MIS is specifically designed to assess malaria burden and intervention coverage, often oversampling rural and high-transmission areas during peak seasons to better capture risk heterogeneity. These diverse inputs were integrated into the IHME geostatistical framework, which accounts for environmental drivers such as rainfall, temperature, and vegetation indices to generate predictive subnational estimates at a high spatial resolution. All estimates are reported with 95% uncertainty intervals to account for survey design variations and measurement error.

The 2010–2019 dataset was accessed via the World Health Organization (WHO) Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT) online platform (2025 update). The HEAT dataset provides only the modeled epidemiological indicators (incidence and mortality) and does not include environmental or climatic variables such as rainfall, temperature, or vegetation indices. Accordingly, the present analysis was restricted to the epidemiological indicators available within the HEAT platform. This toolkit facilitates the immediate exploration and analysis of health inequality data, providing a standardized environment for calculating complex metrics. The study design and reporting were strictly informed by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines to ensure methodological transparency and rigor.

Setting description

Somalia is administratively organized into 18 regions: Awdal, Bakool, Banadir, Bari, Bay, Galgaduud, Gedo, Hiraan, Juba Dhexe, Juba Hoose, Mudug, Nugaal, Sanaag, Shabelle Dhexe, Shabelle Hoose, Sool, Togdheer, and Woqooyi Galbeed. These regions exhibit diverse ecological and climatic conditions, ranging from the arid and semi-arid landscapes of the north to the fertile, riverine southern and central plains. These variations influence the seasonality and intensity of malaria transmission, with different regions experiencing distinct epidemiological profiles.

Inequality variables

The outcome variables in this study were malaria incidence and mortality, specifically targeted toward children aged 0–4 years, the group most vulnerable to severe disease.

Plasmodium falciparum incidence: Reported as the percentage (%) of the population aged 0–4 years experiencing new infections annually.

Plasmodium vivax incidence: Reported as the percentage (%) of the population aged 0–4 years. This variable allowed for a comparison of spatial homogeneity between the two primary malaria species.

Malaria mortality: Reported as the number of deaths per 100,000 population per year.

These variables reflect the state-level malaria burden and provide the basis for measuring the magnitude of subnational disparities.

Statistical tool and inequality metrics

The WHO HEAT online software was used for all analyses. Five key measures were employed to quantify inequality from both absolute and relative perspectives. In this context, “disadvantaged” refers to regions with the highest burden, and “advantaged” refers to those with the lowest burden. The analysis was conducted at the administrative regional level, as required by the WHO HEAT framework; risk stratification based on epidemiological or environmental variables was beyond the scope of this dataset.

Difference (D)

Difference is an absolute measure that quantifies the arithmetic gap between the most disadvantaged and most advantaged regions.

A D-value of zero indicates equality; higher values indicate greater absolute disparity.

Ratio (R)

Ratio is a relative measure that compares the rate in the most disadvantaged region with that in the most advantaged region.

An R-value greater than 1.0 indicates that the disadvantaged region bears a higher relative burden.

Between-group variance (BGV)

BGV is an absolute measure of inequality that quantifies the degree to which regional rates deviate from the national average, weighted by regional population shares ( pi).

where yi is the rate in region i and μ is the national mean. A higher BGV signifies greater geographical dispersion and inequality.

Population attributable risk (PAR)

PAR is an absolute measure estimating the potential reduction in the national rate if all regions achieved the same rate as the most advantaged (best-performing) region.

Negative values reflect the direction of inequality, where the national burden exceeds that of the reference region.

Population attributable fraction (PAF)

PAF is a relative measure expressing the proportion of the total national malaria burden attributable to regional inequalities.

This identifies the potential percentage reduction in the national burden achievable through equitable intervention.

Temporal trends in Plasmodium falciparum incidence

The longitudinal data in Table 1 reveals a marked rise in P. falciparum incidence across nearly all 18 regions during the first half of the decade. In 2010, regional incidence among children aged 0–4 years was relatively low, ranging from 2.5% in Banadir to 13.8% in Bari. However, between 2010 and 2015, nearly every region saw a substantial rise in burden. This trend was most pronounced in Awdal, which saw incidence rise sharply from 7.0% in 2010 to a peak of 51.6% in 2015. While Table 1 shows that incidence rates generally declined or stabilized after 2015, they remained substantially higher in 2019 than they were in 2010. By the end of the decade, the localized burden remained highly heterogeneous: central and southern regions such as Bay (32.0%) and Juba Dhexe (31.1%) maintained high burdens, whereas the capital region of Banadir (17.0%) and Galgaduud (16.7%) reported significantly lower rates.

Inequalities in Plasmodium falciparum incidence

Table 2 quantifies the magnitude of these regional disparities using several inequality metrics. The BGV, which measures how much regional rates deviate from the average, peaked at 64.3 in 2015, coinciding with the period of highest national incidence. This indicates that as malaria cases surged, the gap between the most-affected and least affected regions widened significantly. However, by 2019, the R had dropped to 1.9, the lowest in the study period. This suggests a “convergence” effect: while the disease remained prevalent, the relative difference between the highest burden and lowest burden regions narrowed toward the end of the decade.

Incidence of Plasmodium vivax

In sharp contrast to the volatile trends of P. falciparum, the incidence of Plasmodium vivax among children aged 0–4 years remained consistently low and stable. As illustrated in the heatmap in Fig. 2, P. vivax incidence rates were characterized by pronounced spatial homogeneity, with the majority of regions reporting values between 0.1 and 0.4% throughout the decade. While a minor peak was observed in Awdal (0.7% in 2014), rates generally stayed well below the 1% threshold. These findings suggest that the environmental or socioeconomic factors driving P. vivax transmission are more uniform across the country, making it less susceptible to the epidemic spikes seen with P. falciparum.

Inequalities in Plasmodium vivax incidence

Inequality measures for P. vivax, presented in Table 3, reflect much lower and more stable levels of disparity compared to P. falciparum. The D never exceeded 0.3, and the R remained largely stable, fluctuating between 1.4 and 2.2 across the study period. The low BGV and narrow range of incidence confirmed that P. vivax was more evenly distributed across the Somali regions.

Regions with low, stable mortality

Northern regions such as Awdal and Woqooyi Galbeed maintained the lowest and most stable mortality rates, with mean levels remaining near the 40 per 100,000 thresholds.

Mortality was most severe in the southern and central clusters, specifically Juba Hoose, Juba Dhexe, and Bay, which experienced the highest and most volatile burdens throughout the decade.

Inequalities in malaria mortality

The most important findings emerge from the mortality inequality data in Table 4. While incidence inequalities showed signs of narrowing by 2019, mortality inequalities remained severe. The D in mortality rates increased nearly 3.5-fold between 2010 and 2014 (from 33.9 to 118.5), and the BGV increased from 100.6 to 1152.7. Even as national incidence stabilized, the R remained high at 4.24 in 2019, meaning children aged 0–4 years in the hardest hit regions were still more than four times as likely to die from malaria as those in the least affected regions. This persistent mortality gap, despite narrowing incidence gaps, suggests that disparities in healthcare access, treatment-seeking behavior, or late-stage clinical management are more deep-seated and ingrained in the subnational infrastructure than the factors driving transmission itself.

Discussion

The subnational analysis of malaria in Somalia from 2010 to 2019 reveals a highly volatile epidemiological landscape characterized by a dramatic surge in Plasmodium falciparum prevalence and a persistent, disproportionate mortality burden among children under five. The dramatic rise in P. falciparum incidence between 2010 and 2015, particularly the peak of 51.6% in Awdal, mirrors the resurgence patterns observed in the Amhara region of Ethiopia, where environmental shifts and control challenges have compromised previous gains [17]. This volatility stands in stark contrast to the extreme spatial homogeneity of Plasmodium vivax, which remained stable and below 1% across all Somali regions. Such stability in P. vivax is also documented in low-transmission settings like the highlands of Cameroon and parts of the Brazilian Amazon, suggesting that this species may be less sensitive to the epidemic triggers that drive P. falciparum spikes [18, 19].

The high degree of geographical heterogeneity identified in Somalia, with heavy burdens concentrated in the southern riverine clusters of Bay and Juba Dhexe, aligns with findings from Ghana and Indonesia, where fine-scale spatial mapping has consistently identified localized “hotspots” of transmission [20, 21]. In Somalia, these spatial patterns are heavily influenced by climatic variability, similar to the trends observed in coastal Sindh, Pakistan, where transmission peaks are closely tied to seasonal rainfall [22]. Furthermore, the concentration of risk in specific districts suggests an urban-to-rural continuum of risk, a phenomenon characterized in Malawi where rurality remains a primary determinant of high parasite prevalence [23]. Comparisons with other sub-Saharan contexts, such as Rwanda and Liberia, further emphasize that national averages often mask these critical subnational disparities in incidence and death rates [24, 25].

The most critical finding of this study is the severe and widening inequality in malaria mortality. While incidence gaps showed signs of narrowing by 2019, the mortality R remained high at 4.24, indicating that children in the most disadvantaged regions are over four times more likely to die from the disease. This decoupling of incidence and mortality suggests that the drivers of death in Somalia are rooted in structural inequities rather than transmission intensity alone. Similar disparities have been noted in Nigeria, where paediatric malaria outcomes are heavily dictated by the “shadow of poverty” and limited healthcare access [26]. The persistent mortality gap in Somalia, despite stabilizing incidence, points toward deep-seated deficiencies in late-stage clinical management and treatment-seeking behavior, as seen in other sub-Saharan contexts [27].

The overlapping burden of malaria with other childhood conditions further complicates the public health response. In Somalia, the co-morbidity of malaria and malnutrition is a significant driver of severity, a relationship also observed in the high-burden regions of Tanzania and Uganda [28, 29]. These findings suggest that malaria control cannot be viewed in isolation; rather, it must be integrated with broader maternal and child health interventions. Evidence from the Democratic Republic of Congo and Malawi demonstrates that scaling up ITNs can significantly reduce all-cause child mortality, but only when coverage is equitable across subnational locations [30, 31].

Somalia’s transition from control to elimination will require a shift toward subnational tailoring of interventions [25]. The use of policy-relevant prevalence thresholds to identify low-transmission areas, as applied in Somalia, provides a roadmap for resource optimization [32]. However, the high mortality in southern clusters necessitates urgent improvements in health service readiness. As seen in Burkina Faso, the association between health service availability and reduced malaria mortality is significant, highlighting the need for localized infrastructure investment [33]. Ultimately, the findings from Somalia can contribute to the global understanding of subnational health inequalities, reinforcing the need for geostatistical surveillance to guide equitable public health action [34, 35].

Limitations of the study

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, reliance on modeled geospatial estimates may underrepresent the impact of insecurity on data accuracy in conflict-affected zones. Second, the underlying survey inputs are subject to recall bias and seasonal mixing, which can mask peak transmission intensities. Third, the absence of supply-side indicators, such as facility readiness and drug availability, and climatic covariates limits the ability to fully elucidate the structural and ecological drivers behind the persistent mortality inequalities. Finally, the retrospective, ecological design of the study precludes causal inference between specific interventions and the observed subnational trends.

Future recommendations

To address the persistent subnational disparities identified in this study, the Federal Ministry of Health should transition from centralized, blanket interventions toward a data-driven model of precision public health. Strategic priority must be directed toward the southern riverine clusters, specifically Juba Hoose, Juba Dhexe, and Bay, where excess mortality persists despite the relative stabilization of infection rates. Given the structural barriers to facility-based care in conflict-affected and IDP settings, the expansion of Integrated Community Case Management (iCCM) is essential to provide prompt, decentralized diagnosis and treatment at the household level. Furthermore, as the decoupling of incidence and mortality suggests deficiencies in clinical management rather than transmission alone, interventions must focus on health service readiness at the secondary care level, ensuring that district hospitals in high-burden zones are consistently equipped with injectable artesunate and blood transfusion services. Because malaria severity is significantly compounded by co-morbidities, control efforts should be horizontally integrated with nutritional support programs to bolster the physiological resilience of children under five. To bridge the 4.24-fold mortality gap, the government should prioritize a subnational, phased introduction of the malaria vaccine in these high-risk clusters as a critical equity-based tool. Finally, Somalia must institutionalize a geostatistical surveillance framework that integrates climate-based early warning systems, accounting for the Gu and Deyr rainy seasons, alongside verbal autopsy protocols to identify and rectify the specific socio-ecological drivers of treatment delay in the country’s most disadvantaged regions.

Conclusion

This longitudinal analysis of subnational data reveals that while Somalia made progress in narrowing regional inequalities in malaria incidence between 2010 and 2019, a severe mortality inequality persists in the southern and central riverine regions. The study identifies a critical decoupling of epidemiological trends: although the relative disparity in Plasmodium falciparum incidence contracted to a ratio of 1.9 by 2019, mortality inequalities remained nearly fourfold higher in disadvantaged regions compared to the most advantaged. These findings indicate that the burden of malaria mortality in Somalia is driven less by transmission intensity and more by structural inequities in healthcare access, clinical management, and treatment-seeking behavior. Furthermore, the volatility of P. falciparum surges, most notably the 2015 peak, contrasts with the spatial homogeneity of P. vivax, highlighting the need for species-specific and climate-resilient interventions. Ultimately, this study underscores that national-level aggregates mask localized health crises; therefore, achieving malaria elimination in Somalia necessitates a transition toward precision public health strategies that prioritize subnational resource allocation, targeted vaccine introduction, and the strengthening of clinical infrastructure in high-mortality clusters.
