Influence of biological pretreatment of coconut husk lignocellulosic biomass for biogas production on archaeal community and volatile fatty acids
Authors: Senyene Umana, Mfoniso Iwatt, Maria Bassey, Otobong D Akan, Samuel Eduok, Uchechi Ekwenye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLignocellulosic agricultural residues such as coconut husk are abundant but recalcitrant substrates that limit efficient biogas production during anaerobic digestion. We evaluated the effect of biological pretreatment of coconut husk on archaea, volatile fatty acid (VFA) profiles, and biogas production during anaerobic digestion. Coconut husk was pretreated for 50 days using a consortium of cellulolytic bacteria isolated from poultry feces and subsequently digested alongside untreated husk (control) in batch reactors. Cellulose-degrading bacteria were presumptively characterized based on morphological, cultural, and biochemical characteristics and included Bacillus megaterium, Bacillus licheniformis, Lactobacillus plantarum, Bacillus cereus, and Bacillus subtilis based on morphological, cultural, and biochemical characteristics. Ten archaeal genera were detected across treatments, including Methanobrevibacter, Methanomassiliicoccus, Methanobacterium, Methanoculleus, Methanosarcina, Methanomicrobium, Desulfurococcus, Thermosphaera, Methanogenium, and Methanoregula. The control digestate exhibited higher archaeal diversity with a low methane production. The pretreated reactor (Ch + Cdb) produced a cumulative biogas yield of 88,355 mL gVS⁻1, compared with 67,105 mL gVS⁻1 in the control, representing a significant 1.32-fold increase (p ≤ 0.05). Methane production commenced on day 3 in Ch + Cdb relative to day 15 in the control indicating a shortened lag phase and enhanced breakdown of substrate. Mean methane concentration increased from 44.52 ± 25.4% in the control (Ch) to 56.38 ± 16.54% in the Ch + Cdb treatment representing a 1.27-fold increase in methane content relative to the control. Carbon dioxide concentration also increased from 11.41 ± 6.24% in the control to 15.01 ± 5.54% in the treated sample, corresponding to a 1.32-fold increase. Total VFA concentration was higher in Ch + Cdb at 596.43 mg L⁻1 than in the control (562.24 mg L⁻1), with acetic acid as the dominant component. Increased concentrations of acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid in the treated system did not inhibit methanogenesis, suggesting improved intermediate conversion and process stability. Biological pretreatment enhanced hydrolysis, accelerated methanogenesis, and improved biogas yield with a stable reactor performance, and highlight the whole-cell biocatalyst potential of cellulose-degrading bacteria for optimum anaerobic digestion of lignocellulosic biomass.Keywords: Biological pretreatment, Cellulose-degrading bacteria, Biogas, Volatile fatty acids, Anaerobic digestion, Lignocellulosic biomass, Archaea

Introduction

Global environmental challenges and the increased scarcity of resources have driven interest in valorizing agricultural residues, including coconut husk, corn straw, wheat straw, and rice straw, for energy and other value-added products [16]. In Nigeria, large quantities of coconut husk are often left to decompose or are burned in fields with associated environmental and health hazards, and a waste of potential valuable resource [11]. Coconut husk is a rich lignocellulosic biomass, a promising feedstock for renewable energy, biogas production and a sustainable alternative to fossil fuels. Biogas is produced via anaerobic digestion, a microbial process in which complex organic materials are sequentially converted into simpler compounds such as volatile fatty acids (VFAs), hydrogen (H₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂), and methane (CH₄) under oxygen-free conditions. Anaerobic digestion occurs in four stages: hydrolysis, acidogenesis, acetogenesis, and methanogenesis. During hydrolysis, complex polymers such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and proteins are broken down into monomers (e.g., glucose, amino acids, and long-chain fatty acids), which are then fermented into VFAs, hydrogen, and acetic acid. Acetogenic bacteria further convert VFAs into hydrogen, CO₂, and acetate, which are ultimately transformed into methane by methanogenic archaea [14, 31]. Biogas technology is considered a promising strategy to mitigate greenhouse gas emissions while providing renewable energy [9, 39].

Coconut husk, like other lignocellulosic residues, is composed mainly of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. The complex cross-linked structure of these components makes it recalcitrant and limits hydrolysis during anaerobic digestion [16, 21, 33]. Pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass is therefore essential to improve substrate break down, enhance porosity, and facilitate enzymatic hydrolysis [25, 36]. Various pretreatment methods including chemical, mechanical, thermal, nanotechnological, and biological approaches have been employed to improve digestion and biogas yield. While chemical and thermal methods are often energy-intensive and environmentally challenging, biological pretreatment using microorganisms or enzymes is eco-friendly and cost-effective [6, 2, 20].

Microbial consortia including fungi (white-rot, brown-rot, and soft-rot fungi), bacteria (Clostridium, Pectobacterium, Klebsiella, Serratia, Enterobacter, Citrobacter, Providencia, and Pseudomonas), and enzymes (lignin peroxidases, versatile peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases) enhance the breakdown of lignocellulosic materials and improve biogas production [3, 29]. Effective hydrolysis and fermentation of lignocellulose during pretreatment are critical for the formation of VFAs such as acetic, propionic, butyric, valeric, isovaleric, and isobutyric acids, which serve as key intermediates in methane production [28].

Although VFAs play an important role in anaerobic digestion, their excessive accumulation can inhibit methanogenesis and reduce biogas yield, depending on substrate composition and operational conditions [1, 4, 41]. While numerous studies have investigated pretreatment strategies for improving lignocellulosic biomass digestion, most have focused primarily on methane yield and substrate degradation, with limited attention to the combined effects of biological pretreatment on VFA dynamics and methanogenic archaeal communities. In addition, studies specifically using coconut husk as a substrate remain scarce, particularly under tropical waste management conditions relevant to Nigeria. Therefore, this study was conducted to address these knowledge gaps by simultaneously assessing the impact of pretreatment of coconut husk with a cellulose-degrading bacterial consortium on biogas production, VFA profiles, and archaeal community structure during anaerobic digestion. This work integrates microbial community analysis with process performance indicators and provides new insights into the mechanisms through which biological pretreatment enhances methane generation from coconut husk biomass.

Materials and methods

Sources of materials

Fresh cow dung and poultry feces were collected from the Animal Science Department, University of Uyo, Akwa Ibom State, in sterile polyethylene bags and transported to the laboratory for processing and analysis. Coconut husk was obtained from the fruit shells of Cocos nucifera L. collected from a coconut farm in Ikot Ekpene, Akwa Ibom State, Nigeria. The husk was air-dried and milled to a particle size of 0.5–2 mm to increase the surface area. Plantain peel was collected from a local food vendor in Uyo, Akwa Ibom State, Nigeria, sun-dried, and incinerated. Eighty grams of the plantain peel ash was dissolved in 8 L of distilled water. The resulting solution (pH 11.40) was used to buffer all reactors on day 30 to maintain a stable pH during anaerobic digestion.

Isolation and characterization of cellulose-degrading bacteria

Cellulose-degrading bacteria were isolated from poultry feces using an enrichment technique. One gram of poultry feces was inoculated into minimal salt medium (0.2% KH₂PO₄, 0.2% K₂HPO₄, 0.2% NH₄NO₃, 0.05% NaCl, 0.05% MgSO₄·7H₂O, 0.001% FeSO₄·7H₂O, 0.001% MnSO₄·H₂O, pH 7.0) supplemented with 1% cellulose powder and incubated for 7 days at 37 °C on a shaker at 100 rpm. Cultures were serially diluted and plated on carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) agar (peptone 10 g/L, CMC 10 g/L, K₂HPO₄ 2 g/L, MgSO₄·7H₂O 0.3 g/L, (NH₄)₂SO₄ 2.5 g/L, gelatin 2 g/L, agar 15 g/L; pH 6.8–7.2) and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. Discrete colonies were selected and purified by repeated subculturing. The isolates were presumptively characterized based on morphological and biochemical properties and maintained at 4 °C for further use.

Mass cultivation and quantification

Pure bacterial isolates were inoculated into CMC broth and incubated for 10 days at room temperature. A 0.5 McFarland standard (equivalent to ~ 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was prepared to standardize bacterial suspension turbidity. The density of bacterial cultures was adjusted and compared visually against the McFarland turbidity standard to ensure consistent inoculum concentration.

Experimental design

Milled coconut husk was subjected to solid-state fermentation under low-moisture conditions. Two treatments were prepared in duplicate.

Pretreated substrates were mixed in plastic containers, allowed to stand for 50 days at greenhouse temperature (28 ± 2 °C). This pretreatment period was selected to provide sufficient time for microbial colonization, partial degradation of lignocellulosic components, and structural loosening of the recalcitrant coconut husk fibers, thereby improving substrate biodegradability prior to anaerobic digestion. The experimental design was limited to these two treatments in order to allow direct evaluation of the effect of biological pretreatment under identical inoculum conditions. Cow dung was used as a standardized methanogenic inoculum to simulate practical anaerobic digestion systems and to ensure consistent methane-producing microbial activity across treatments; therefore, additional controls such as substrate-only (without inoculum) or bacteria-only reactors were not included, as the study primarily focused on comparative performance between untreated and pretreated substrates under realistic digestion conditions. The pretreated material was subsequently fed into pilot-scale reactors. The pretreated material was subsequently fed into pilot-scale reactors. Prior to inoculation, the cow dung (inoculum) was incubated at 37 ± 0.2 °C for 25 days until no biogas production was observed.

Four cylindrical plastic digesters of 25 L capacity were fabricated, each with top and bottom openings for gas and sample collection, respectively. Duplicate digesters were loaded with:

The substrates were loaded to 70% of total volume of reactor, manually agitated daily, and augmented with 2 kg cow dung on day 30 via intubation. pH was adjusted on day 30 using plantain peel ash solution with pH 11.40.

Each treatment was run in duplicate independent digesters, which served as the biological replicates (n = 2 per treatment). All biogas production, gas composition, VFA profiling, and archaeal community analyses were based on these biological replicates.

16S metagenomic analyses of samples

Community DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted using Zymo-Spin™ IV-HRC Spin Filters. Briefly, 0.25 g of each sample was transferred into ZR BashingBead™ Lysis Tubes, to which 750 µL of Lysis Solution was added and vortexed for 5–10 min. Tubes were bead-beaten using a 2 mL tube holder and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 min. The supernatant was transferred to a Zymo-Spin™ IV Spin Filter in a collection tube and centrifuged at 7,000 × g for 1 min. To the filtrate, 1,200 µL of Soil DNA Binding Buffer was added, and 800 µL of this mixture was transferred to a Zymo-Spin™ IIC Column and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 min. The flow-through was discarded, and the column was sequentially washed with 200 µL DNA Pre-wash Buffer and 500 µL DNA Wash Buffer, centrifuging each at 10,000 × g for 1 min. DNA was eluted with 100 µL of DNA Elution Buffer, filtered through a Zymo-Spin™ IV-HRC Spin Filter at 8,000 × g for 1 min, and collected as genomic DNA extracts.

Polymerase chain reaction and next-generation sequencing

Extracted DNA samples were amplified using a 96-well thermal cycler (2ETM, UK) with universal primers 341 F and 785R targeting the V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene, which was selected due to its strong balance between phylogenetic resolution and sequence length compatibility with Illumina MiSeq paired-end (2 × 300 bp) sequencing platforms, as well as its wide applicability in microbial community profiling studies. Amplicons were gel-purified, end-repaired, and ligated with Illumina adapters using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit. Following quantification, each sample was indexed with NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (Dual Index Primers Set 1) and purified using AMPure XP beads. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform using a MiSeq v3 (600-cycle) kit, generating 20 Mb of paired-end reads (2 × 300 bp) per sample.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw paired-end 16S rRNA gene sequences generated on the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 300 bp) were subjected to quality control and downstream analysis using a standardized amplicon sequencing workflow. Initial quality assessment of raw reads was performed using FastQC, and low-quality bases, ambiguous nucleotides, and adapter sequences were removed using Trimmomatic to obtain high-quality clean reads. Only reads passing quality filtering (Phred score ≥ Q30) were retained for downstream analysis. High-quality reads were processed using the DADA2 pipeline (R package) to infer amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). This included quality filtering, error model learning, dereplication, paired-end merging, chimera removal, and construction of an ASV abundance table. Only high-confidence ASVs were retained for subsequent analyses. Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using a naïve Bayesian classifier against the SILVA reference database (version 138). Mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences were removed prior to analysis to ensure accurate microbial community profiling. Archaeal sequences were specifically extracted from the ASV table based on SILVA domain-level classification (Archaea) and used for downstream archaeal community structure and relative abundance analyses. Microbial community composition, relative abundance, and diversity indices were computed using the phyloseq and vegan packages in R. Where necessary, representative sequences were additionally validated using NCBI BLAST (version 2.24) and visualized using CLC Genomics Workbench (version 7.5.1) for confirmatory taxonomic comparison. All raw sequencing data have been deposited under the NCBI BioProject accessions PRJNA1439350 and PRJNA1439513, with associated GenBank accession numbers KJLG00000000 and KJLH00000000, respectively.

Measurement of biogas volume and composition

Biogas production in the digesters was monitored at three-day intervals from day 0 to 42 and from day 82 to 150, and at two-day intervals between day 42 and 82. The variation in monitoring frequency was based on the production dynamics observed during the digestion process. Biogas volume was determined using a graduated inverted cylinder connected to each reactor, with water serving as a displacement barrier.

The composition of the biogas was measured using an IRCD4 portable biogas analyzer (Shi’an Tech Instrument and Co., Ltd, Beijing, China), which measures methane (CH₄) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) concentrations over a range of 0–100%.

Determination of volatile fatty acid

Concentrations of volatile fatty acids, including acetic, propionic, butyric and isobutyric, valeric and isovaleric and caproic and isocaproic acids, were measured using an HP 6890 Gas Chromatograph with a flame ionization detector (GC-FID). For analysis, 20 mL of each sample was transferred into a 40 mL glass vial and acidified to pH 2 with phosphoric acid while stirring. The acidified sample was transferred to a polycarbonate centrifuge tube, and 10 mL of diethyl ether was added. The mixture was centrifuged to separate the supernatant from the solid fraction. The supernatant was collected using a disposable syringe, filtered through an in-line filter, and transferred into a 1 mL glass vial. A 1 µL aliquot of this extract was injected into the GC-FID. Calibration was performed using 1 µL of a VFA standard mixture, and concentrations were calculated using the instrument’s data processing software.

Statistical analysis

Replicate data obtained from independent duplicate digesters (biological replicates) and repeated analytical measurements where applicable were subjected to statistical analysis. Prior to analysis of variance (ANOVA), data were assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance to confirm suitability for parametric testing. For statistical comparisons, endpoint measurements such as cumulative biogas yield, methane concentration, carbon dioxide concentration, and volatile fatty acid concentrations were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Where significant differences were observed, Duncan’s multiple range test was applied for pairwise comparison of means at a significance level of p < 0.05. Exact p-values are reported where appropriate in the Results section and tables. Time-dependent variables, including daily biogas production trends, were summarized using descriptive statistics based on duplicate digesters and presented graphically to illustrate process dynamics over time, rather than being subjected to repeated-measures ANOVA.

To ensure consistency in data interpretation, biogas production results are reported as cumulative values, while gas composition data (methane, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen sulphide) are expressed as percentage (%) of total biogas on a daily measurement basis. Summary comparisons between treatments are based on mean values calculated across the entire digestion period and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Peak values are reported only for descriptive illustration of temporal trends and are not used for statistical comparison. Fold-change expressions are derived exclusively from mean values to maintain consistency across all reported parameters.

Results

Cellulose degrading bacteria from poultry feces

The characterization and identification of cellulose-degrading bacteria from poultry feces (Table 1) indicates the presence of five isolates. All isolates were Gram-positive rods exhibiting distinct colony morphologies that ranged from milky to creamy yellow pigmentation, with circular to irregular margins and glistening, smooth, flat, or rough surfaces. Biochemical profiling revealed that all isolates were positive for starch hydrolysis and spore formation, except Lactobacillus plantarum, which was negative for citrate utilization, catalase production, motility, and sporulation. Based on morphological, cultural, and biochemical characteristics, the isolates were tentatively identified as: Bacillus megaterium (PD1), Bacillus licheniformis (PD2), Lactobacillus plantarum (PD3), Bacillus cereus (PD4) and Bacillus subtilis (PD5).

The total volatile fatty acid (VFA) concentrations in the digestates (Table 3) indicated a concentration of 596.43 mg/L in Ch + Cdb compared with 562.24 mg/L in the control, representing a 1.06-fold increase. Discussion

Cellulose degrading bacteria isolated from poultry feces

The cellulolytic bacterial isolates obtained from poultry feces were characterized and identified as Bacillus megaterium, Bacillus licheniformis, Lactobacillus plantarum, Bacillus cereus, and Bacillus subtilis (Table 1). The isolation of these organisms from poultry feces highlights the rich lignocellulose-degrading potential of this substrate. Poultry waste is known to harbour diverse microbial communities because of its high organic matter content and continuous exposure to plant-derived feed residues, which likely select for cellulose-degrading microorganisms [24].

The predominance of Bacillus species such as B. megaterium, B. licheniformis, B. cereus, and B. subtilis is consistent with previous reports identifying members of this genus as efficient producers of extracellular cellulases [26, 27, 38]. Members of this genus are widely recognized for their resilience, endospore-forming capacity, and ability to secrete a wide array of hydrolytic enzymes, including cellulases, hemicellulases, and amylases, which facilitate lignocellulosic biomass degradation. Their spore-forming ability enhances environmental persistence, making them suitable for industrial and biotechnological applications. Reports of cellulase production by B. megaterium [27], B. licheniformis [19], B. cereus [26, 38], and B. subtilis [26, 32] further support their relevance in biomass conversion processes.

The positive starch hydrolysis observed in all isolates suggests broad-spectrum carbohydrate-degrading ability and indicates that these organisms produce multiple extracellular enzymes in addition to cellulases. This enzymatic versatility is critical for complex substrate degradation, particularly in anaerobic digestion systems where synergistic microbial activity enhances biogas production. Lactobacillus plantarum differed phenotypically, being unable to utilize citrate, non-motile, non-sporulating, and catalase-negative. This result is consistent with its classification as a non-spore-forming facultative anaerobe commonly associated with fermentation processes [23]. Although not traditionally regarded as a primary cellulase producer, L. plantarum may contribute indirectly to cellulose degradation through organic acid production and synergistic interactions with other cellulolytic bacteria. Moreover, the organism is a versatile biotransformation agent capable of producing extracellular cellulolytic and hemicellulolytic enzymes [34, 42].

Effect of biological pretreatment on archaeal genera in digestates

Methanogenesis represents the terminal step of anaerobic digestion and is mediated by specialized archaea known as methanogens. The archaeal community abundance and diversity were higher in the control reactor (Ch), which harboured all ten genera, compared to Ch + Cdb, where only four genera were detected (Fig. 1). Although the control exhibited higher archaeal diversity, methane production was lower than in the Ch + Cdb treatment, suggesting that community composition rather than diversity was the primary driver of methane yield.

Hydrogenotrophic methanogens play a critical role in anaerobic digestion by scavenging hydrogen, maintaining low hydrogen partial pressure, and stabilizing fermentation processes [37]. The dominant hydrogenotrophic genera detected were Methanobacterium, Methanoculleus, and Methanobrevibacter. Notably, Methanobacterium showed a pronounced enrichment in Ch + Cdb, increasing from 3.22% in the control to 97.4% following pretreatment. This strong selection suggests that biological pretreatment created environmental conditions that specifically favoured hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis.

The enrichment of Methanobacterium can be attributed to enhanced hydrolysis and acidogenesis driven by the cellulolytic bacterial consortium, which increased the availability of soluble intermediates and hydrogen (H₂) and carbon dioxide (CO₂)—the primary substrates for this genus. In addition, improved breakdown of lignocellulosic structures likely reduced mass transfer limitations, thereby accelerating syntrophic interactions between fermentative bacteria and hydrogenotrophic archaea. Under these conditions, Methanobacterium outcompeted other methanogens due to its strong affinity for hydrogen and its tolerance to fluctuating reactor conditions, leading to competitive exclusion and reduced community diversity but enhanced methane production efficiency.

In contrast, Methanoculleus was highly abundant in the control (55.39%) but undetected in Ch + Cdb. This genus is a widespread hydrogenotrophic methanogen in biogas reactors [43, 44], but its absence in the treated reactor suggests that the altered environmental conditions favoured a more specialized and competitive hydrogen-utilizing population dominated by Methanobacterium. Methanobrevibacter, commonly associated with ruminant digestive systems and hydrogenotrophic methane production [22], was detected in both treatments but at low abundance.

Methanosarcina, a metabolically versatile methanogen capable of acetoclastic, hydrogenotrophic, and methylotrophic methanogenesis [7, 40], was more abundant in the control than in Ch + Cdb. The reduced presence of this genus in the treated reactor suggests that acetoclastic methanogenesis contributed less to methane formation following pretreatment. Methanomassiliicoccus, a methylotrophic methanogen that utilizes methanol and methylamines [8, 30], occurred at low abundance (< 1%) in both treatments, though slightly enriched (0.80%) in Ch + Cdb.

Other minor genera, including Methanomicrobium, Methanogenium, Methanoregula, and Thermosphaera, were detected primarily in the control reactor at low relative abundance. Their presence reflects broader metabolic diversity but contributed minimally to overall methane production. The results indicate that cellulolytic pretreatment reshaped the archaeal community from a diverse assemblage in the control to a highly specialized, Methanobacterium-dominated consortium. This community shift likely enhanced hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis through improved substrate availability and strengthened syntrophic bacterial–archaeal interactions, thereby contributing to the higher methane yield observed in Ch + Cdb.

Effect of biological pretreatment on biogas volume

Biogas is a mixture of biologically produced gases, primarily methane, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen sulphide, generated under anaerobic conditions. Biogas production depends on the organic content of the substrate, pretreatment methods, and the efficiency of microbial digestion. The control reactor (Ch) produced biogas between Days 15 and 21, after which no further biogas was detected until enrichment with fresh inoculum and buffering on Day 30. In contrast, the reactor fed with coconut husk pretreated with cellulose-degrading bacteria (Ch + Cdb) generated biogas as early as Day 3 (Fig. 2). The early production observed in Ch + Cdb suggests that cellulolytic pretreatment accelerated substrate hydrolysis and provided readily available sugars and fermentation intermediates for methanogens.

The total biogas volume was higher in Ch + Cdb, reaching 88,355 mL gVS⁻1, compared with 67,105 mL gVS⁻1 in the control (Fig. 3). The pretreated digestate generated a 1.32-fold higher biogas volume than the control, and this difference was statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05), indicating that the improvement was unlikely to be due to random variation. The enhanced biogas yield is attributed to the combined action of the cellulolytic bacteria and the native microbial consortium, which improved hydrolysis and acidogenesis and increased substrate availability for methanogenesis. This result is consistent with previous studies suggesting that inoculation with cellulolytic microorganisms shortens the lag phase and increases biogas production from lignocellulosic substrates [16, 38]. Overall, biological pretreatment of coconut husk with cellulose-degrading microorganisms reduced the lag period for biogas generation and increased methane production.

Effect of biological pretreatment on biogas composition

The effect of biological pretreatment on methane production was evaluated using batch anaerobic digestion of untreated coconut husk (Ch) and cellulolytic bacteria-pretreated coconut husk (Ch + Cdb) over 150 days. Methane content is a key indicator of anaerobic digestion efficiency and substrate conversion. Methane production commenced after a 3-day start-up period in Ch + Cdb, whereas a prolonged lag phase of 15 days was observed in the control (Fig. 4). The absence of methane during the initial stage suggests the dominance of hydrolytic and acidogenic processes prior to methanogenesis [36]. The earlier onset and higher peak methane concentration (80% on Day 131) in Ch + Cdb, compared with 74% on Day 137 in the control, indicate enhanced methanogenic efficiency and improved substrate utilization.

The mean methane content was higher in Ch + Cdb (56.38 ± 16.54%) than in the control (44.52 ± 25.40%), representing a 1.26-fold increase. The higher methane yield is attributed to structural modifications of the lignocellulosic matrix during pretreatment, including partial hemicellulose removal, lignin redistribution, and increased cellulose accessibility. These changes reduced biomass recalcitrance, enhanced microbial hydrolysis, and promoted methane formation.

Carbon dioxide concentrations ranged from 0–20% in the control and 0–30.5% in Ch + Cdb. The treated reactor reached its maximum CO₂ concentration on Day 18, whereas the control peaked on Day 92, indicating intensified early-stage hydrolytic and acidogenic activity in the treated system. Mean CO₂ concentrations were also higher in Ch + Cdb (15.01 ± 5.54%) than in the control (11.41 ± 6.24%). The early CO₂ peak followed by increased methane levels suggests efficient syntrophic metabolism and effective conversion of intermediate products into methane. Although CO₂ levels in this study were lower than those reported for maize silage [12], such variations are expected because of differences in substrate composition and digestion conditions. However, increases in methane and CO₂ following pretreatment have also been reported [13, 18].

Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S), a toxic by-product generated from the reduction of sulphur-containing compounds under anaerobic conditions, remained low in both reactors throughout the digestion period. Concentrations ranged from 0–0.110% (0–1103 ppm) in the control and 0–0.039% (0–393 ppm) in Ch + Cdb, with peak values observed on Day 36. Although the treated reactor exhibited lower maximum H₂S values, differences in daily concentrations were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Mean H₂S levels were also comparable between treatments (0.01 ± 0.02% in the control and 0.01 ± 0.01% in Ch + Cdb). These findings indicate that cellulolytic pretreatment did not significantly alter sulphur metabolism during anaerobic digestion. Instead, its primary effect was observed in carbon conversion pathways, as reflected by enhanced methane production. The consistently low H₂S concentrations in both reactors contributed to improved biogas quality and minimized the risk of inhibition, as excessive H₂S is known to adversely affect methanogenic activity.

Volatile fatty acids

Volatile fatty acids (VFAs) are short-chain carboxylic acids produced during the hydrolysis and acidogenesis stages of anaerobic digestion and serve as key intermediates in methane formation. Total VFA concentrations were higher in the pretreated reactor (Ch + Cdb: 596.43 mg L⁻1) than in the control (562.24 mg L⁻1). Despite this increase, methane production was enhanced in Ch + Cdb, indicating that VFA accumulation did not inhibit methanogenesis. VFA concentrations in both digesters were below the inhibitory threshold of 6000 mg L⁻1 reported for anaerobic digestion [15] and within the maximum limit of 10.0 g L⁻1 associated with methanogen inhibition [5].

Acetic acid was the dominant VFA in both treatments and accounted for 26.9–38.3% of total VFAs. Acetate is the principal precursor for methane production and plays a central role in both acetoclastic and hydrogenotrophic methanogenic pathways. The higher concentration observed in Ch + Cdb suggests enhanced hydrolysis and fermentative conversion of lignocellulosic biomass following biological pretreatment. High acetate concentrations have been reported in stable anaerobic systems. For instance, acetate was the dominant VFA with mean concentrations of 1963.2, 1447.1, and 2365 mg L⁻1 in control, metal salt-spiked, and engineered nanoparticle-spiked digestates, respectively [10]. The acetate concentrations observed in the present study remained well below levels associated with process instability. Propionic acid was the second most abundant VFA, with concentrations of 97.79 mg L⁻1 in Ch and 106.58 mg L⁻1 in Ch + Cdb. The propionate-to-acetate ratio remained below the critical 1:4 threshold associated with digester imbalance and further indicated stable syntrophic oxidation and effective conversion of intermediates to methane.

Butyric, valeric, caproic, and isocaproic acids were also detected at moderate concentrations and were generally higher in the treated reactor, suggesting enhanced fermentative metabolism. These VFAs are typically converted through syntrophic pathways into acetate, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide prior to methanogenesis. High butyric acid concentrations may indicate elevated hydrogen partial pressure when the process is diverted from optimal biogas production [17]. However, the butyric acid concentrations observed in this study (33.14–51.19 mg L⁻1) were not inhibitory to microbial activity and were consistent with enhanced biogas production. Branched-chain VFAs such as isobutyric and isovaleric acids, which are commonly associated with amino acid degradation and microbial stress responses [10, 35], were slightly higher in the control reactor. Elevated isovaleric acid in the control may reflect increased cellular stress or less efficient downstream conversion of intermediates, whereas the lower concentration in Ch + Cdb suggests a more balanced metabolic state and improved substrate utilization. The VFA profile indicates that biological pretreatment enhanced hydrolytic and fermentative activity without causing intermediate accumulation or process instability. Efficient conversion of VFAs, particularly acetate, likely contributed to the improved methane yield and overall digester performance observed in the treated reactor.

Conclusion

Biological pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass altered archaeal community dynamics in the digestates and directly influenced biogas production. Hydrogenotrophic methanogens, particularly Methanobacterium, Methanobrevibacter, and Methanoculleus, dominated the community, with increased Methanobacterium abundance correlating with higher biogas volume, greater methane yield, and stable methanogenesis. Pretreatment of coconut husk with a consortium of cellulose-degrading bacteria accelerated hydrolysis, shortened the lag phase, and enhanced both biogas and methane production, with treated reactors producing 1.32-fold more biogas and generating methane earlier than the control. Elevated acetic acid concentrations reflected a metabolic shift toward methane production, consistent with the prevalence of hydrogenotrophic methanogens, while higher isovaleric acid levels in the control suggested microbial stress. Total volatile fatty acid concentrations were higher in pretreated digestates, indicating intensified intermediate-stage metabolism without inhibiting methanogenesis. Cellulose-degrading bacterial pretreatment enhanced substrate biodegradation, optimized microbial dynamics, and improved both the quantity and quality of biogas, indicating its potential for efficient lignocellulosic biomass valorization.
