Automated external defibrillator cabinets without scissors: a hidden barrier to effective resuscitation
Authors: Nino Fijačko, Guglielmo Imbriaco, Filip Bračko, Kristin Alm-Kruse, Sergio Cazorla-Calderón
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the recent article by Omatsu et al., published in Resuscitation Plus, examining the use of scissors in conjunction with automated external defibrillators (AEDs) to facilitate rapid clothing removal during resuscitation.1 While the study was conducted using a training AED under controlled conditions, its implications are highly relevant to real-world out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), where AEDs are most commonly retrieved from public AED cabinets. In such settings, the availability and quality of scissors may vary considerably, yet this important aspect was not addressed. The authors demonstrated that standard stainless steel trauma scissors can efficiently remove clothing. However, this finding may not be directly transferable to real-world scenarios. In our local setting in Maribor, Slovenia, scissors were present in only six out of 102 AED cabinets (5.9%).2 Furthermore, the scissors available were typically standard steel scissors (Fig. 1), lacking the sharpness and durability of dedicated trauma scissors used in the study. This discrepancy raises concerns regarding their effectiveness, particularly when cutting through thicker clothing layers.

Examples of scissors used in Slovenian AED kits.

Correspondence with international colleagues suggests that this issue is not unique. In Italy, existing regulations governing AED implementation do not specify mandatory accessories. In Spain, only 3 out of 17 Autonomous Communities explicitly require scissors as part of the minimum kit. In Norway, although scissors are recommended as part of AED kits by the Norwegian Resuscitation Council,3 there is no legal requirement or centralized oversight to ensure their presence, especially given that many AEDs are privately owned. This results in variability in AED kit contents across these countries.

Beyond scissors, other basic accessories may further support effective and safe AED use. These include razors for removing chest hair to improve electrode adhesion, gloves for infection prevention, and face shield masks or pocket masks to enable safer ventilation during cardiopulmonary resuscitation.3, 4 The absence of standardized requirements for such items further contributes to variability in preparedness across public AED systems.

These observations highlight a broader lack of standardization in AED cabinet contents across different regions. We believe that future research and guidelines could not only consider the effectiveness of accessories such as scissors but also address the real-world availability, minimum quality standards, and cost of additional items, including razors for chest hair removal, gloves for infection prevention, and face shield or pocket masks for safer ventilation. Cost variability may further influence implementation; for example, high-quality trauma scissors may be relatively expensive, which could limit their inclusion in some settings. Improving this aspect of AED preparedness may represent a simple yet meaningful step toward optimizing early defibrillation and resuscitation outcomes.
