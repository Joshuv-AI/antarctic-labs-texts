Quantification of energy consumption of bridge crane in service under mechanical-electrical coupling field
Authors: Qisong Qi, Lei Zhang, Yule Zhang, Cong Li, SongLei Wang, Hui He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To improve the evaluation accuracy of bridge cranes within a mechanical-electrical coupling field, this study proposes a transient energy consumption modeling framework that integrates mechanism dynamics with induction-motor electrical behaviors. Dynamic models of the hoisting, trolley-traveling, and gantry-traveling mechanisms are established to describe the coupled motion features of the transmission system, thereby establishing the relationship between mechanical transmission and electrical control. Considering the time-varying characteristics of the electrical system, an electromechanical model is developed to quantify instantaneous power conversion and cumulative energy expenditure. The proposed method is validated through simulations and experiments on a 1.5 t-6 m double-girder bridge crane. To capture the boundary of the model’s physical validity, the experiments are strictly bounded within 20%–30% of the rated capacity (300 kg and 450 kg payloads), representing the intended operational boundary of this research. Under these representative conditions, the simulated total energy consumptions over one hoisting cycle are 16161.38 J and 18449.20 J, whereas the corresponding experimental measurements are 16439.36 J and 18787.40 J, respectively. The relative deviations (1.69% and 1.80%) lie well within the ± 2.96% expanded measurement uncertainty. The results demonstrate that the proposed framework effectively captures the dominant electromechanical energy-transfer behavior within the defined boundary, providing a theoretical basis for the green performance assessment of hoisting machinery.

Introduction

Industrial cranes are the core equipment for precise heavy-load lifting and spatial displacement, playing an indispensable role in enhancing operational productivity across manufacturing, port logistics, and energy infrastructure. In China, according to official statistics released by the State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR)1, the registered lifting machinery fleet reached approximately 3.048 million units by the end of 2024. Given their extensive 15–20-year service life and energy-intensive nature characterized by frequent start-stop operations and multi-mechanism coordination, exploring crane energy-saving pathways holds profound significance under the national dual-carbon strategy. Achieving substantial efficiency improvements requires a comprehensive deconstruction of energy conversion and dissipation mechanisms. Consequently, investigating the impact of operational parameters—such as lifting capacity, speed, and working frequency—is crucial for developing targeted optimization strategies and advancing green equipment manufacturing.

Extensive research has been globally undertaken to enhance crane lifecycle energy efficiency, focusing primarily on structural lightweight design to reduce material consumption2, operational energy recovery technologies3, and intelligent control strategies such as path planning and lifting optimization4,5. For structural optimization, Professor Jiang Jingang’s team and Omar Mendoza-Trejo et al. established energy-saving parameter systems through kinematic analysis of mechanical structures6,7. Professor Li Congbo’s team optimized machine tool spindle parameters using algorithms targeting energy minimization8, and Professor Zhao Shengtan’s team designed a frictionless clutch to eliminate energy loss during operation9. For energy-saving control and scheduling, Professor Zhen Lu’s team and Professor Zhao Ning’s team developed multi-crane task allocation systems integrating path planning and operational trade-offs10,11. Lü Yan et al. proposed dynamic workshop scheduling protocols for energy-efficient process routes under disturbance conditions12, and Victor Zhidchenko et al. implemented trajectory optimization for crane booms13. In energy recovery systems, Tomasz Haniszewski and Kanzumba Kusakana developed non-electrical energy recovery systems to reduce external power dependency14–16, while Stefano Pietrosanti et al. introduced energy efficiency management algorithms via operational data analytics. Furthermore, lifecycle energy evaluation remains pivotal in green crane research17. Kosucki et al. established a hybrid energy model for overhead cranes to identify hoisting mechanisms as primary consumers mitigated by supercapacitors18. Sun et al. balanced energy minimization and swing suppression in double-pendulum cranes via quadratic programming19, whereas Takalani et al. demonstrated that combining particle swarm trajectory optimization with energy storage achieves port crane decarbonization20. Additionally, Kłodawski et al. simulated container loading strategies to reduce bridge crane energy use21, and Liu et al. developed a multi-objective model to concurrently improve efficiency and energy economy22. Although previous studies have provided useful insights into crane energy-saving design, scheduling, and energy recovery, important gaps still remain in the transient electromechanical analysis of crane operational energy demand. During lifting operations, bridge cranes exhibit complex time-dependent motion-energy interactions caused by coordinated multi-mechanism motion, including transient changes in speed, acceleration, and torque transmission. These factors directly affect the energy-conversion behavior of the drive motors and the cumulative overall energy expenditure. Existing studies have identified several important energy-consumption drivers. For example, Xiaowei Dong et al. formulated mathematical principles for energy-optimal motion in forestry cranes23, while Stefano Pietrosanti et al. quantified gantry-crane motor power consumption as a function of load and velocity24. However, a unified framework that explicitly links multi-mechanism transient motion, induction-motor electrical response, and cycle-wide energy accumulation over a complete operating cycle is still lacking.

To address this gap, this study makes three main contributions: First, in terms of coupling mechanism, an electromechanical coupling framework is established by integrating multi-mechanism dynamic models with a three-phase induction motor machine model, enabling explicit cross-domain mapping from mechanism motion to electromagnetic responses. Second, in terms of identification, a physics-based quantification method is developed based on transient energy transfer and time-resolved electric accumulation, determining energy expenditure in the continuous time domain from instantaneous active power rather than empirical estimations. Third, in terms of validation, a closed-loop evaluation route combining theoretical derivation, Simulink/Simscape co-simulation, and prototype experiments is constructed. Consequently, this work provides a physically interpretable basis for transient energy evaluation and subsequent optimization of bridge cranes.

Calculation model of energy consumption of bridge crane in service under mechanical-electrical coupling field

Analysis of electric energy consumption mechanism of bridge crane motor

Bridge cranes universally employ electric motors to drive the operation of integral components and the entire system. The total energy required during operation and transmission is supplied by the power grid and consumed through electromechanical conversion within the motor. The grid-input power comprises two components: active power, defined as the rate of electrical energy conversion into other forms (e.g., mechanical work) per unit time; and reactive power, representing the reversible energy exchange rate between the power supply and magnetic fields, with a time-averaged value of zero over a full cycle. The service energy consumption of the crane’s electromechanical system is derived from the temporal integration of instantaneous active power.

Under standard operating conditions, motor losses primarily include copper losses in stator and rotor windings, stator core losses (hysteresis and eddy current losses), mechanical losses induced by frictional resistance torque, and stray losses (e.g., windage and high-frequency harmonic effects). These losses are governed by the motor’s inherent physical parameters and exhibit strong state-dependence, resulting in a complex nonlinear correlation between actual power consumption and operational variables. Currently, accurate quantification of dynamic losses remains a critical technical challenge in motor energy consumption evaluation. Given the strong correlation between power loss and actual load, exploring their mapping relationship offers a viable solution. Calculation model of transient motion of bridge crane transmission system

The integral framework of a bridge crane comprises structural and mechanical components, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The structural section bears the crane’s operational loads, while the mechanical section provides transmission security for realizing kinematic motions. The hoisting mechanism governs vertical lifting/lowering of loads; the trolley traveling mechanism drives horizontal transportation of the entire machine and payload; and the crab traveling mechanism controls horizontal movement of the trolley and cargo assembly. Both trolley and crab traveling mechanisms employ identical driving and transmission modes, with their motion directions mutually perpendicular. To investigate the dynamic characteristics and transient energy responses during mechanical transmission across crane mechanisms, differential motion modeling must be performed separately for the hoisting mechanism and traveling mechanisms.

Bridge crane and mechanism composition.

Kinematic model of hoisting mechanism of bridge crane

Given the complex dynamics and kinematics of the hoisting mechanism during service, accurately investigating the transient response mechanism of motor energy consumption during cargo lifting necessitates appropriate and rational model simplification. First, friction between the wire rope and the drum/pulley during winding is neglected. Second, the influence of hook oscillation and load swinging on the hoisting mechanism’s energy consumption is disregarded. Third, the impact of load shock on energy consumption is excluded. The applicability of the above assumptions can be further clarified as follows. The above simplifications are mainly applicable to smooth lifting conditions without severe impact, slack-rope events, or large-amplitude payload oscillation. Under the operating scenario considered in this study, the neglected rope-drum friction and small payload swing are treated as second-order effects. As demonstrated by the subsequent experimental validation, their collective impact is bounded within the overall system discrepancy of less than 3%. In contrast, under abrupt start-stop motion, strong load swing, or impact-dominated handling, these neglected effects may become non-negligible and should be incorporated into the model. The hoisting mechanism exhibits periodic motion characteristics during service operations. The process of lifting cargo from the ground to a safe height involves the motor transitioning from forward startup to plug braking. Conversely, when cargo descends from a safe height to the ground, reverse motor rotation achieves descent, while reverse motor braking brings the cargo to a stationary state. Crucially, during cargo ascent, the motor operates in a forward state, whereas during descent, it operates in reverse. Given that both phases demonstrate nearly identical motor load characteristics and motion accumulation parameters, only the cargo lifting phase is analyzed (Fig. 2).

The cargo lifting process comprises four distinct stages: (1) Motor idling phase: The wire rope gradually tightens, with its stiffness increasing from zero. (2) Load acceleration phase: The motor load progressively increases, tensioning the wire rope and initiating cargo acceleration. (3) Steady-state phase: Cargo reaches maximum lifting velocity, with motor speed and torque stabilizing. (4) Deceleration braking phase: Cargo decelerates to rest at the highest lifting position. It is necessary to make a special clarification as follows: (1) During the lifting process of the goods from the ground, due to the relatively short action time of the wire rope tensioning process, and the initial values of various kinematic parameters are generated after the motor runs idle. Kinematic model of traveling mechanism of bridge crane

Crane service motion necessitates coordination between the hoisting mechanism and traveling mechanisms to achieve horizontal cargo transportation. Consequently, a translational dynamics model must be established under the following fundamental assumptions: (1) The trolley frame and bridge structure are treated as equivalent rigid bodies with concentrated masses, neglecting component dissipation effects; (2) Energy losses due to crane skew motion and wheel slippage are disregarded; (3) The lateral force arising from cargo deviation perpendicular to the vertical axis is assumed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}_{s}$$\end{document} and acts parallel to the track direction; (4) Rotational inertia variations on the horizontal plane during trolley movement along tracks exhibit slow and continuous evolution.The validity of the above simplifications is mainly limited to representative traveling conditions. These assumptions are reasonable for regular traveling operations with stable wheel-rail contact, moderate acceleration/deceleration, and no obvious collision or impact excitation. Under such conditions, the omitted higher-order effects mainly influence detailed transient fluctuations rather than the dominant power-flow trend, and their contribution to the total service energy is empirically bounded by the macroscopic experimental error margin (approximately 1%~3%). However, if severe vibration, impact loading, or unstable contact occurs, the present simplified model may underestimate the transient energy response.

Theoretical calculation model of electrical system

Based on the preceding analysis, the transient energy consumption of cranes can be quantified. However, the transient voltage-current relationship characterizing drive motor energy consumption is governed by complex electrical system dynamics, typically expressed through nonlinear differential equations due to inherent nonlinear components. Consequently, the following simplifications are introduced within acceptable error margins: (1) Three-phase windings exhibit perfect symmetry, neglecting space harmonic losses from air-gap magnetic field distortions; (2) Magnetic saturation effects and core losses are disregarded; (3) Temperature and frequency dependencies of motor resistance are excluded. The electrical assumptions adopted in this study are also subject to a practical operating envelope. The above electrical simplifications are mainly valid for short-cycle crane operation in the normal working region of the induction motor, where no severe magnetic saturation, excessive thermal accumulation, or prolonged overload occurs. Under these conditions, the neglected factors mainly affect the detailed loss distribution and the peak transient current/torque response, while their impact on the integrated cycle energy is moderate (quantitatively bounded to less than 3% based on prototype validation). Nevertheless, under repeated heavy-duty operation, strong over-flux conditions, or significant temperature rise, the omission of saturation, iron loss, and thermal effects may reduce the model accuracy and should be further considered.

Calculation procedure for service energy consumption of bridge cranes

Based on the theoretical models established in Sect. 1.1 to 1.3, the calculation procedure for the service energy consumption of a bridge crane under a mechano-electrical coupled field is outlined as follows:

Step 1: Identify a complete hoisting cycle of the bridge crane.

Step 2: Determine the time-varying displacements of the gantry traveling mechanism, trolley traveling mechanism, and hoisting mechanism during this cycle.

Step 3: Calculate the motion torque of the drive motor for each mechanism based on their time-varying displacements (from Step 2), considering the motion and load characteristics of the crane.

Step 4: Compute the power consumption of the drive motors for the gantry traveling, trolley traveling, and hoisting mechanisms, respectively.

Step 5: Calculate the transient service energy consumption of the bridge crane.

Dynamic parameters, including damping coefficients and stiffness factors, were initially calculated via physical formulas and subsequently fine-tuned based on the experimental motion trajectories. For independent measurements, high-precision sensors were deployed as illustrated in Fig. 13, where laser distance sensors were utilized for real-time bridge and trolley positioning, and current/voltage sensors were integrated to capture the electrical characteristics of the drive system. All parameters are reported using standard SI units.

The metal structure of the crane consists of an elevated base, a gantry traversing mechanism, a trolley traversing mechanism, and a hoisting mechanism. A three-dimensional model created using SOLIDWORKS software is shown in Fig. 7, and the multibody dynamics simulation model for the entire crane is presented in Fig. 8. The hoisting mechanism is modeled according to Eqs. (7)-(12). In addition to the angular velocity of the drum, support forces and braking forces are incorporated to simulate both grounded and in-air braking conditions of the load. The stiffness and damping coefficients of the wire rope are set in the Planar module, while the damping coefficients of the reducer and coupling are configured in the Revolute Joint module. All required parameters are provided in Table 1. The interactions between the traversing mechanisms are characterized by friction models. As indicated in Eqs. (15)-(18), the driving force of the traversing mechanisms must overcome frictional resistance, acceleration forces, and oscillatory horizontal forces. The frictional resistance is related to wheel pressure. Since the trolley’s movement affects the wheel pressure distribution of the gantry, the gantry traversing mechanism incorporates real-time trolley position data to determine instantaneous wheel pressure. In contrast, the trolley’s wheel pressure remains largely constant under a fixed load. Wheel pressure and resistance coefficients are defined using the Spatial Contact Force module. Acceleration-related and oscillation-induced horizontal forces are displacement-dependent and are modeled through the Prismatic Joint module. All necessary parameters are listed in Table 2.

Experimental equipment and scheme

To validate the theoretical model (consistent with Fig. 7), experimental investigations were conducted on a 1.5 t double-girder bridge crane (Fig. 13). Laser distance sensors captured the positional data (x, y) of the bridge and trolley, while a high-resolution (0.01 mm) draw-wire displacement sensor monitored the hoisting height (z). Dynamic payload tracking was achieved via a tension sensor (0.1% F.S. accuracy) integrated into the hook block. Concurrently, Class 0.5 precision voltage and current meters were interfaced between the electrical system and the power grid to record transient electrical parameters. To eliminate ambient noise and guarantee high-fidelity data fusion, all acquired analog signals underwent sequentially structured amplification, hardware conditioning, and high-speed analog-to-digital conversion (ADC). Within the Industrial Personal Computer (IPC), these diverse electromechanical signals were precisely time-aligned via a unified global clock trigger, restricting the comprehensive synchronization offset to less than 20ms. Following digital filtering and feature extraction, the fully synchronized dataset was streamed to the host computer through the Modbus communication protocol for real-time data management. The detailed installation and configurations are shown in Fig. 13.

Fig. 13Experimental layout of the 1.5 t double-girder bridge crane and its integrated multi-sensor monitoring system.

Experimental layout of the 1.5 t double-girder bridge crane and its integrated multi-sensor monitoring system.

The validation experiments are restricted to 20%–30% of the rated capacity (300 kg and 450 kg payloads), establishing the operational boundary for this study. Within this range, the crane operates under unsaturated conditions, allowing the framework to verify the core electromechanical coupling relationships without higher-order interferences. At higher loads (above 50%), secondary effects like magnetic saturation, motor thermal drift, bridge resonance, and payload swings become dominant. Because these dynamics extend beyond the current linear inductance and constant stiffness assumptions, this 20%–30% envelope serves as a reliable baseline for standard operating conditions.

Conclusion

This study proposes an integrated electromechanical modeling framework to accurately quantify bridge crane energy demands under coupled dynamic conditions. Theoretical simulations and experimental validation confirm the feasibility of the methodology, yielding the following principal conclusions:Multi-mechanism coupled motions dominate the crane lifting process. Establishing differential motion models via mechanical dynamics analysis effectively characterizes energy conversion under dynamic operational profiles.Cumulative energy expenditure is a continuous temporal accumulation process governed by nonlinear electromechanical interactions. The proposed framework successfully captures energy feedback behaviors during motion mode transitions, though unmodeled effects such as magnetic saturation and structural vibrations remain to be addressed in future research.Co-simulation results show good agreement with prototype experiments, with relative deviations falling well within the ± 2.96% expanded uncertainty margin. Nevertheless, the reported quantitative accuracy remains restricted to the experimental boundaries of a 1.5 t crane operating within a 20%–30% load range (300 kg and 450 kg payloads) under the tested trajectory. This relative error may fluctuate under broader operating scenarios where unmodeled factors, such as wheel slip or motor thermal drift, become more prominent. Consequently, while the proposed framework serves as a robust baseline for transient energy estimation, energy transfer mechanisms under more intricate and varied motion patterns warrant subsequent exploration.
