Combined prediagnostic lifestyle factors and survival of breast, colorectal and lung cancer in the Norwegian Women and Cancer (NOWAC) study: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Sairah Lai Fa Chen, Kristin Benjaminsen Borch, Torkjel Manning Sandanger, Jill Tinmouth, Tonje Braaten, Therese Haugdahl Nøst
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith improvements in cancer treatment and early detection, the number of people living with cancer is increasing.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between combined prediagnostic lifestyle factors, assessed by a Healthy Lifestyle Index (HLI) score, and mortality among women diagnosed with breast cancer (BC), colorectal cancer (CRC) and lung cancer (LC).DesignProspective cohort.SettingWomen residing in Norway, general population.ParticipantsOur analysis included 5032, 2468 and 1594 women from the Norwegian Women and Cancer study diagnosed with BC, CRC and LC, respectively, who responded to a questionnaire between 1996 and 2014.Exposure measuresHLI score measured prior to cancer diagnosis. The HLI combines physical activity level, body mass index, smoking behaviour, alcohol consumption and dietary habits.Outcome measuresWe estimated HRs and 95% CIs using Cox proportional hazard models for all-cause and site-specific cancer mortality.ResultsAfter median follow-up times of 9.8, 7.1 and 5.9 years for BC, CRC and LC cases, respectively, there were 912, 902 and 1094 all-cause deaths; and 509 BC deaths, 679 CRC deaths and 961 LC deaths. For women diagnosed with BC, a 1-point HLI score increment was associated with a 6% lower all-cause mortality rate (HR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.92 to 0.97). The data were compatible with no association for the estimated 3% lower BC mortality rate (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94 to 1.00) among women diagnosed with BC, and for the estimated 3% lower all-cause (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95 to 1.00) and 2% lower CRC mortality rates (HR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.95 to 1.01) among women diagnosed with CRC. For women diagnosed with LC, prediagnostic HLI score was not associated with all-cause (HR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.98 to 1.02) or LC mortality rates (HR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.98 to 1.03).ConclusionsWe observed that a higher HLI score measured before cancer diagnosis was associated with lower all-cause and, possibly, lower BC mortality among Norwegian women diagnosed with BC; and a possible lower all-cause and CRC mortality among those diagnosed with CRC. Smoking was likely responsible for the observed associations.

Background

Cancer is a leading cause of global premature death.1 However, survival has improved over the past several decades in high-income countries with developments in treatment and early detection.2 Cancer encompasses a heterogeneous group of diseases, with widely varying survival rates across cancer sites. In order of decreasing survival, breast cancer (BC), colorectal cancer (CRC) and lung cancer (LC) are the most commonly diagnosed cancers among women in Norway3 and globally.4 For each cancer, stage at diagnosis and treatment are the most important prognostic factors for cancer survival.5 Yet, there is variation in the survival of patients presenting with the same cancer at the same stage and receiving the same treatment, suggesting the involvement of other prognostic factors.5

Associations have been repeatedly established between adherence to healthier lifestyles and lower cancer incidence. Physical activity, body fatness, smoking habits, alcohol consumption and dietary habits are established cancer risk factors and are part of well-recognised recommendations for cancer prevention by the WHO and the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF).6 The evidence is less clear for the role of healthier lifestyles in prolonging survival. As summarised by the WCRF Continuous Update Project 2018, there is limited evidence for the survival benefits of postdiagnostic physical activity, fibre and soy intake and normal body fatness.7 Further, the impact of the timing and duration of prognostic factors, prediagnostic and/or postdiagnostic, for each cancer type is unclear.7

There is emerging evidence that greater adherence to healthy lifestyle behaviours prior to cancer diagnosis could be associated with higher survival, estimated as lower mortality rates among persons diagnosed with cancer.7 8 To our knowledge, four studies have examined this question while considering lifestyle as a composite whole.912 They largely indicate that an overall prediagnostic healthy lifestyle, as defined by concordance with cancer prevention recommendations, is associated with better prognoses among persons diagnosed with BC in terms of all-cause mortality10 and CRC in terms of all-cause mortality11 12 and CRC mortality.11 12 This association has not been examined for LC.

A larger number of studies have investigated the association of single prediagnostic lifestyle factors with mortality rates among persons diagnosed with cancer. According to meta-analyses including persons diagnosed with BC, higher Body Mass Index (BMI)13 and current compared with never smoking14 have been consistently associated with higher all-cause and BC mortality. Investigations of prediagnostic physical activity levels have provided indications of weakly protective or null associations1518; while associations in both directions (and null) have been observed for higher alcohol intake1925 among women diagnosed with BC. Evidence from literature on survival after CRC has indicated prediagnostic smoking12 26 27 as associated with higher mortality. Although associations for physical activity,1112 26 2832 greater adiposity,33 alcohol intake11 12 19 30 and diet3438 before CRC diagnosis have been inconsistent. Meeting or exceeding physical activity guidelines compared with low physical activity levels before LC diagnosis has been associated with lower mortality.39

Given the increasing number of people being diagnosed with cancer globally, it is of interest to investigate whether prognosis can be modified before diagnosis by adherence to lifestyle recommendations for cancer prevention as an additional impact. In the present study, we examined associations between combined prediagnostic lifestyle factors and mortality rate among women diagnosed with BC, CRC and LC in the Norwegian Women and Cancer (NOWAC) study. This is the first study to investigate this among persons diagnosed with LC in any cohort, as well as persons diagnosed with BC or CRC in a large Norwegian prospective cohort.

Methods

Study population

The NOWAC study has been presented in detail elsewhere.40 Briefly, NOWAC includes approximately 172 000 Norwegian women enrolled in three waves from 1991 to 2007. Participants were randomly sampled from the National Population Register and were mailed questionnaires containing questions related to sociodemographic characteristics, reproductive and hormonal factors, self-reported health, physical activity, height, weight, smoking habits, dietary habits and familial BC history. Participants were mailed up to three follow-up questionnaires approximately every 6 years. To obtain more detailed information on dietary habits, some questionnaire mailings included Food Frequency Questionnaires (FFQ), dependent on wave and follow-up questionnaires.

Cancer cases were identified by linkage to the Cancer Registry of Norway, which employs the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision to categorise cancer diagnoses. The present study considered women who had both completed at least one FFQ (n=101 316) and who had been diagnosed with BC (C50), CRC (C18–C20), or LC (C34) at any point through to the end of the study period in December 2020 (n=9287). Women with a date of emigration or date of death prior or within the same month as cancer diagnosis were excluded (nemigration=1; ndeath=153). Women reported as deceased between the completion of the questionnaire and the end of follow-up, but missing on the date of death were excluded (n=38). The present study therefore included three analytical samples consisting of 5032 BC, 2468 CRC and 1594 LC cases (see online supplemental file 1 for sample selection flow chart) who responded to the questionnaire between 1996 and 2014.

Exposure assessment

A Healthy Lifestyle Index (HLI) based on cancer recommendations and scientific evidence for cancer risk factors was used as a measure of overall lifestyle. This HLI has been previously constructed and applied within the NOWAC cohort with respect to cancer incidence.41 Briefly, the HLI consisted of five modifiable lifestyle factors—physical activity level, body fatness assessed by BMI (kg/m2), smoking behaviour, alcohol consumption (g/day) and a dietary score, which were assessed from the self-reported response. Participants were invited to rate their physical activity level (considering the entirety of activity at work, outside work, at home, exercise and other forms of physical activity) on a 1 (not active) to 10 (very active) scale. Smoking behaviour was measured with smoking status, time since cessation for former smokers and number of daily cigarettes smoked for current smokers. Alcohol intake and the dietary score were assessed from the FFQ. Six food groups were included in assessing diet: whole grains, fruit, vegetables and dairy, which scored favourably; and red and processed meat, which scored unfavourably. Each lifestyle factor was assigned a score ranging from 0 to 4, which was summed to a total HLI score that ranged from 0 to 20, where higher scores indicated a healthier lifestyle (see online supplemental file 2 for details on HLI construction).

Covariates

Relevant covariate information collected from the questionnaire included height (cm), education (≤9, 10–12, 13–16, ≥17 years schooling), enrolment wave (1996–1998, 2002–2005, 2011–2014), menopausal status (premenopausal, postmenopausal), age at menarche (years), hormone replacement therapy (HRT) status (never, former, current), oral contraceptive (OC) status (never, ever), history of BC in a first degree relative (yes, no), parity (number), cumulative breastfeeding (months) and prevalent type II diabetes mellitus, heart failure, stroke and heart infarction.

The Cancer Registry of Norway provided information for the remaining covariates. BC cases were classified according to the stage using the tumour, node and metastasis (TNM) Classification of Malignant Tumours (I, II, III, IV, unknown),42 while stages for CRC and LC cases were classified according to the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) programme summary staging (local, regional, distant, unknown).43 For BC, molecular subtype and hormone receptor status information included oestrogen receptor (ER) status (positive if ≥1 %), progesterone receptor (PR) status (positive if ≥10%) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) (positive if >10% tumour cells stained by immunochemistry). Classification of cancer subtypes was also obtained for CRC (colon, rectal) and LC (small-cell LC (SCLC), non-small-cell LC (NSCLC)) cases.

Follow-up and mortality ascertainment

Follow-up began at the calendar month of cancer diagnosis and ended on 30 December 2020. The NOWAC cohort has passive linkage to the National Population Registry of Norway and the Cause of Death Registry, supplying annual follow-up on participants for endpoint information, including date of emigration, date of death and cause of death.

Statistical analysis

BC, CRC and LC cases were analysed separately. Median follow-up times were calculated with the reverse Kaplan-Meier method.44 Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate associations between the prediagnostic HLI score and mortality rates (all-cause and site-specific cancer) with HRs and 95% CIs. The HLI score was modelled as a continuous (per 1-point increment) variable. The proportional hazards assumption was tested by assessing the correlation between Schoenfeld residuals and survival time.45 To test for possible departures from linearity, we additionally modelled the HLI score with restricted cubic splines. The number of knots was chosen by minimising the Akaike Information Criterion, which was placed at percentiles, as recommended by Harrell.46

All models were adjusted for age at diagnosis, height, education and questionnaire period (1996–1998, 2002–2005, 2011–2014) and stratified on cancer stage. BC models were additionally adjusted for menopausal status, age at menarche, HRT status, OC use, history of BC in a first-degree family member, parity and cumulative breastfeeding.

We suspected the associations may be modified by age at diagnosis. As such, we performed subgroup analysis in two age groups, with the median age at diagnosis used as the cut-off (median age at diagnosis for BC cases=63, CRC cases=68, LC cases=68). Given that BC, CRC and LC survival are known to be influenced by tumour stage, subtype, location, additionally molecular subtype, hormone receptor status and menopausal status for BC,47 it is possible that lifestyle in the prediagnostic period may have differential effects across these clinicopathological characteristics. To investigate associations within separate levels of mortality risk as defined by clinicopathological characteristics, subgroup analysis was performed by stage and subsite (colon/rectal cancer; SCLC/NSCLC). For BC cases, subgroup analysis was also performed by molecular subtype and hormone receptor status (ER+, ER–, ER+/PR–, ER–/PR–/HER2– (triple negative), ER–/PR–/HER2+ (HER2-enriched)) and menopausal status (premenopausal/postmenopausal). As routine molecular subtyping was not performed prior to 2005, subgroup analysis for molecular subtypes and hormone receptor status was conducted among participants diagnosed with BC in or after 2005 (n=4097).

Additional analysis was performed to test the contribution of individual lifestyle factors to the HLI score associations by removing each lifestyle factor, in turn, from the HLI. The time between questionnaire administration and cancer diagnosis (prediagnostic interval) differed between participants. To test if the associations differed by the duration of the prediagnostic interval, we modelled an interaction term for the prediagnostic interval and compared the interaction model to the model without the interaction term with a likelihood ratio test. To visualise interactions between prediagnostic interval and prediagnostic HLI score flexibly, we modelled the interaction term with restricted cubic splines using three knots placed at the percentiles.

To minimise potential reverse causation, we performed sensitivity analyses that excluded women diagnosed with cancer less than 2 years after the questionnaire and women who were deceased less than 5 years after the questionnaire. To avoid short follow-up time, we tested the exclusion of new cancer cases that occurred within 2 years before the end of the study period. We tested the impact of self-reported prevalent comorbidities (type II diabetes mellitus, heart failure, stroke and heart infarction) at the time of the questionnaire, on the associations by including them in the multivariable analysis. For all sensitivity analyses, we considered a meaningful change due to a sensitivity ≥5% difference between estimates.

The non-informative censoring assumption is foundational to the validity of the hazard function performed in Cox proportional hazards regression models.48 To account for competing risks in the site-specific cancer mortality models, associations were also estimated with the proportional subdistribution hazards (SH) regression model developed by Fine and Gray.49 Here, the cumulative incidence function estimated the marginal probabilities for each competing event (ie, BC mortality events were precluded by competing events, including death by other causes), which was then modelled with SH regression to estimate SH ratios (SHRs) and 95% CIs.

Outcome data were complete; however, the listwise proportion of missing data due to the exposure and covariates was 28%. Missing data among covariates were handled by multiple imputation by chained equations (MICEs) under the assumption that data were missing at random.50 A MICE model was executed for each cancer site (BC, CRC and LC) and outcome (all-cause and site-specific cancer mortality). For each MICE model, all explanatory variables, complete or incomplete, binary outcome variables and Nelson Aalen cumulative hazard estimator were included. MICE analysis employed fully conditional specification, whereby each incomplete variable was modelled iteratively by a series of multivariable regression models.51 A total of 100 datasets were generated with 10 iterations each, as recommended by White et al.50 Parameter estimates in the Cox models from each imputed dataset were averaged through Rubin’s Rule52 to account for uncertainty in the MICE models to impute missing values. Model parameters were also estimated in complete-case analyses. Descriptive statistics for each imputed variable were compared between observed and imputed values. The convergence of MICE models was assessed by visual inspection of plots of the mean HLI score against the iteration number for each MI dataset (not shown). Due to computational limitations, interaction analysis with restricted cubic splines was performed only on complete-case observations.

All data treatment and statistical analysis were conducted in RStudio V.2023.03.0 with R V.4.2.3.53 The analysis code will be made available on request. Statistical hypotheses were tested two-sided, allowing a Type I error rate of 5%. We used the STROBE cohort checklist when writing our report.54

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the conduct of this study.

At the time of the questionnaire, women later diagnosed with BC tended to be younger (55.5 years) than women later diagnosed with CRC (59.0 years) or LC (58.1 years). Women diagnosed with BC more frequently had ≥13 years of education (42%) compared with women diagnosed with CRC (29%) and LC (22%).

Over the period of follow-up from cancer diagnosis to December 2020, there were 912 all-cause deaths of which 509 were BC deaths among BC cases; 902 all-cause deaths of which 679 were CRC deaths among CRC cases; and 1094 all-cause deaths of which 961 were LC deaths among LC cases. The median follow-up time was 9.8, 7.1 and 5.9 years for women diagnosed with BC, CRC and LC, respectively. The 5-year survival was the poorest for women diagnosed with LC (29%), followed by CRC (67%) and BC (90%) (online supplemental file 3).

The main results are presented in figure 1a. For every 1-point increment on the HLI, there was a 6% lower all-cause mortality rate (HR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.92 to 0.97) and a 3% lower BC mortality rate (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94 to 1.00), where the data were compatible with no association for the latter, among women diagnosed with BC. Among women diagnosed with CRC, a 1-point HLI increment was associated with lower rates, compatible with no association, for all-cause (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95 to 1.00) and CRC mortality (HR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.95 to 1.01). For women diagnosed with LC, the associations for all-cause mortality (HR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.98 to 1.02) and LC mortality (HR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.98 to 1.03) were null. No departures from linearity in the associations were observed, as demonstrated by the modelling of the prediagnostic score with restricted cubic splines (online supplemental file 4). In analysis performed in subgroups by stage, all-cause mortality rates among women diagnosed with BC for a 1-point HLI increment were 7% lower among stage I (HR: 0.93 95% CI: 0.89 to 0.98), 5% lower among stage II (HR: 0.95 95% CI: 0.91 to 0.98) and 7% lower among stage IV (HR: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.86 to 1.01) (figure 1b), although the data for stage IV were compatible with no association. There was no association observed for stage III BC in terms of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.93 to 1.08). We observed lower rates for a 1-point HLI increment for BC mortality across all stages that were compatible with no association. Women diagnosed with localised stage CRC and LC had 7% (HR: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.86 to 1.01) and 6% (HR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.87 to 1.01) lower all-cause mortality rates, respectively, and 9% (HR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.76 to 1.09) and 4% (HR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.87 to 1.05) lower CRC and LC mortality rates, respectively, where the data were also compatible with no association. Among women diagnosed with regional or distant stage CRC or LC, the data were compatible with no association for estimates of all-cause, CRC and LC mortality.

Removal of single factors from the HLI, in turn, did not alter the estimates more than ±5% compared with the main estimates in all cases, with one exception (table 3); the association between the prediagnostic HLI score and all-cause mortality rate among BC cases was attenuated to null when smoking was removed. This was also observed in complete-case analysis (online supplemental file 6).

Among women who were postmenopausal at the time of BC diagnosis, prediagnostic HLI score was associated with lower rates of all-cause mortality (HR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.92 to 0.97) and BC mortality (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.93 to 1.00) (online supplemental file 7). Among women presenting ER+ and ER+/PR− BC receptor status, prediagnostic HLI score was associated with lower all-cause (ER+HR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.88 to 0.95; ER+/PR− HR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.85 to 0.97) and BC mortality rates (ER+HR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.90 to 0.99; ER+/PR− HR: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.95 to 1.01). The evidence was not strong for a negative association among women diagnosed with ER− BC receptor status for all-cause mortality (HR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.90 to 1.03) or BC mortality (HR: 0.98, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.07) or with HER2-enriched BC receptor status for all-cause mortality (HR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.86 to 1.03) or BC mortality (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.87 to 1.07). There were no associations for triple-negative BC receptor status (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 0.77 to 1.49) in terms of all-cause mortality and an inadequate number of events to estimate BC mortality. All associations observed between prediagnostic HLI score and mortality rates for colon and rectal subsites or NSCLC and SCLC subtypes were compatible with no association. However, there were indications of a lower all-cause mortality rate among women diagnosed with colon cancer (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95 to 1.00) and a lower rectal cancer mortality rate (HR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.91 to 1.01) with a higher prediagnostic HLI score. Comparison of associations in subgroups of below and above median age at diagnosis revealed a less than 5% difference among women diagnosed with BC, CRC and LC cases.

Sensitivity analyses excluding women with less than 2 years of follow-up time after cancer diagnosis, women diagnosed with cancer less than 2 years after responding to the questionnaire or women who exited the study less than 5 years after the questionnaire resulted in estimates within ±5% of the main estimates (online supplemental file 8). Comparing models with and without interaction terms for prediagnostic intervals suggested better goodness of fit with the interaction (p<0.05 for all model comparisons). Modelling of prediagnostic interval with restricted cubic splines in an interaction term with the HLI score to visualise the influence of the prediagnostic interval revealed that all-cause and BC mortality rate differences were greater with longer prediagnostic intervals among women diagnosed with BC (online supplemental file 9). For prediagnostic intervals of less than approximately 5 years, the mortality rate among women diagnosed with LC was higher per 1-point increment on the HLI score. There were indications of a similar trend among BC cases for prediagnostic intervals of less than 2 years. However, the data were also compatible with no association. Estimates from complete-case analyses were within ±5% of the main estimates derived from multiple imputed datasets (online supplemental file 5).

Discussion

This is the first study to investigate the association between combined prediagnostic lifestyle factors, assessed by an HLI score, and mortality among Norwegian women diagnosed with BC, CRC or LC. The results indicated that a healthier prediagnostic lifestyle was associated with lower all-cause mortality and, possibly, BC mortality among women diagnosed with BC. Notably, smoking was responsible for driving these associations. Among persons diagnosed with CRC, a negative trend between overall healthier prediagnostic lifestyle and all-cause and CRC mortality was suggested, although the data were compatible with no association. Among women diagnosed with LC, we did not observe that prediagnostic lifestyle was associated with all-cause or LC mortality.

A 1-point increment in HLI score was associated with 6% lower all-cause mortality rates among women diagnosed with BC, suggesting that a healthier overall lifestyle before BC diagnosis was protective against death from all causes. A negative association for BC mortality was indicated, where a 1-point increment in HLI score was associated with a 3% lower BC mortality rate, although the data were compatible with no association. Our results are consistent with all-cause mortality observations in another Norwegian cohort, where three to five unfavourable prediagnostic lifestyle factors compared with none were associated with higher all-cause mortality among women diagnosed with BC.10 Unlike our results, they did not observe a negative trend for BC mortality with fewer unfavourable lifestyle factors. Further comparison with that study, in terms of the magnitude of association, was challenging as the authors used a less granular score that did not include diet. To our knowledge, no other studies have investigated the association between overall prediagnostic lifestyle and survival after a BC diagnosis.

In terms of the impact of single lifestyle factors among women diagnosed with BC, our results implicated smoking as driving the negative associations. This suggested a negligible impact of physical activity levels, BMI, alcohol consumption and diet. As summarised in two meta-analyses on BC survivors, currently compared with never smoking14 and higher prediagnostic BMI13 have been consistently associated with higher all-cause and BC mortality. In contrast to the literature on single prediagnostic lifestyle factors, any association to reduced mortality risk by adherence to healthy BMI before BC diagnosis was not detected after smoking was removed from the HLI in our study. The association for physical activity before BC diagnosis has been shown to be weak or null.1518 Still, the timing of exposure to physical activity may be important, whereby more recent physical activity is associated with higher survival. Indeed, in a pooled cohort from USA, Canada and Australia,55 higher recreational physical activity 3 months before BC diagnosis was associated with lower all-cause mortality, while recall of higher lifetime recreational physical activity was not. In the NOWAC cohort, Borch et al16 reported that higher postdiagnostic physical activity levels were associated with lower all-cause and BC mortality, while not observing an association for prediagnostic physical activity levels. Associations reported for alcohol consumption before BC diagnosis have been contradictory, with one study observing a negative association with BC mortality,20 others observing protective associations with mortality,21 22 while some observed no association.2325 We were unable to compare this result to the results from another Norwegian cohort10 as they did not investigate the effect of single factors, nor the removal of single factors from their lifestyle score.

Overall, associations were weaker among women diagnosed with CRC compared with BC. The data were compatible with no association for the observed 3% lower all-cause and 2% lower CRC mortality rates for every 1-point increment in HLI score. Our results were largely consistent with the one publication investigating the effect of the same lifestyle factors combined12 and two investigating the effect of adherence to WCRF/American Institute for Cancer Research (AICR) recommendations9 11 on mortality among persons diagnosed with CRC. All three studies observed that a healthier prediagnostic lifestyle was associated with lower all-cause mortality. Further, an association with CRC mortality was additionally observed in one of these studies.11

Regarding the importance of single factors to the HLI score, our additional analyses did not reveal lifestyle factors driving or antagonising the associations among women diagnosed with CRC. However, it is possible that smoking was driving the weak, protective HLI score associations given marginally weakened estimates when smoking was removed. Our results are consistent with the current pool of evidence on the effect of prediagnostic single factors on mortality among persons diagnosed with CRC. Investigations of single prediagnostic lifestyle factors have pointed towards smoking as being associated with poorer prognoses for CRC survivors.12 26 27 56 Studies of other single prediagnostic lifestyle factors have demonstrated inconsistent results for physical activity,1112 26 2832 56 greater adiposity,33 alcohol intake11 12 19 30 56 and diet.343656

LC is the leading cause of death worldwide57 and had the highest mortality among women diagnosed with BC, CRC and LC in our sample of Norwegian women. In our study, a prediagnostic healthy lifestyle was not associated with mortality among women diagnosed with LC. We did not observe that specific single lifestyle factors were influential for the estimates in this study. Unlike the strong, causal association of smoking on increased LC risk, the association did not appear to extend to mortality among women diagnosed with LC. To our knowledge, no studies have investigated the effect of prediagnostic lifestyle factors jointly on mortality among patients with LC. Still, there are some studies that have investigated single factors. A pooled cohort study on prediagnostic physical activity observed that meeting or exceeding the recommendations for metabolic equivalent hours assessed by leisure-time physical activity with an exposure window between 1 and 10 years during adulthood was associated with lower all-cause and LC mortality.39 A meta-analysis, including mostly European and North American samples, reported that adults who had obesity before LC diagnosis had higher survival compared with normal weight.58 Smoking before LC diagnosis compared with non-smoking was associated with two times higher LC mortality and high compared with low vegetable intake was associated with lower LC mortality.59

In our study, consideration of clinicopathological characteristics was essential as they are known to impact survival. Our results suggest that a healthier prediagnostic lifestyle is more likely to be associated with lower mortality if women are diagnosed with a cancer disease with fewer poor prognostic factors. The 5-year survival was three times greater for BC compared with LC in our study, which, in part, reflects the later stage at LC detection compared with BC. This can be attributed to the existence of an organised mammography screening programme since 1996,60 compared with no organised CRC screening (introduced in 2022)61 and no LC screening (in trial) in Norway. For stage at diagnosis, we observed that the association between prediagnostic HLI score and all-cause mortality was generally less negative for stages representing more advanced disease among women diagnosed with BC, CRC or LC but was most apparent for women diagnosed with BC. Women diagnosed with stage IV BC were an exception as the direction of association was negative, although there was high uncertainty. The same trends were not apparent for site-specific cancer mortality.

Women diagnosed with hormone receptor-negative BC, who are premenopausal at diagnosis, or both, typically have a worse prognosis due to less effective treatment options and higher aggressiveness compared with women diagnosed with hormone receptor-positive BC, are postmenopausal, or both.62 Our results suggest that healthier lifestyle before BC diagnosis was associated with lower all-cause mortality for ER-positive, not negative; and postmenopausal, not premenopausal women. Although reduced statistical power and multiple comparisons should be considered when performing subgroup analyses, it is likely that any effect by overall prediagnostic lifestyle on survival is more challenging to detect for cancer diseases with properties of faster progression, late stage at detection or being challenging to treat. No detected association among women diagnosed with LC and weaker or no associations observed within cancer stages representing more advanced disease and among women diagnosed with BC in premenopause or with hormone receptor-positive BC were likely exemplifications of this.

We did not observe that self-report of current or previous diabetes, heart failure, stroke or heart infarction at baseline impacted the associations. However, we did not have information on comorbidities that occurred after baseline. A review study reported that persons diagnosed with cancer who have comorbidities have higher mortality rates compared with persons without comorbidities.8 In line with the suggested modifying effect of strong prognostic factors in our study, they found that, if survival of a particular cancer type is poor, the additional effect of comorbidity on mortality is relatively small. Since survival of BC is relatively high compared with CRC and LC63 and the deviation between all-cause and cause-specific mortality risk was greatest for BC, we can infer that any additional effect of comorbidity on mortality was highest for BC cases. As cancer and other main causes of death among adults, such as cardiovascular disease, share common lifestyle factors,64 we suspect that the presence of comorbidity played mediating and/or confounding roles in the associations between prediagnostic lifestyle and survival of BC especially, but potentially in CRC and LC to lesser extents. Our results demonstrate that the driving effect of smoking is consistent with the known strong link between smoking, risk of cardiovascular disease and other chronic diseases, and overall mortality. This may have explained our stronger observed negative associations among women diagnosed with BC compared with CRC and LC.

The timing and duration of exposure is important when evaluating the relations between lifestyle and mortality. The long, undefined latency period and long sufficient duration of exposure between first exposure and BC, CRC or LC incidence, and subsequent mortality renders a critical window of exposure in adulthood challenging to define.65 We assessed lifestyle in the prediagnostic adult period by proxy of one measurement, which is typical and undertaken in epidemiological studies. As such, the duration of lifestyle exposure was unknown and ignored possible lifestyle changes before and after measurement which would have likely been relevant for survival. Further, the associations were modified by different durations of the prediagnostic interval suggesting that the timing of the exposure and diagnosis in the life course was important, particularly in BC, and that there was possible reverse causation by subclinical cancer or other comorbidities that may have manifested around the same time for women diagnosed with LC and possibly BC.

Investigations of the prediagnostic interval with similar study designs in the literature were limited to two studies.11 12 In contrast to our results, both studies observed that associations were stronger among persons with shorter, compared with longer, durations between questionnaire and diagnosis. Romaguera et al11 suggested that greater lifestyle changes leading up to diagnosis may have occurred with longer prediagnostic durations, implying that lifestyle closer to diagnosis represents a more critical exposure window. We believe it is more likely that, given the long latency period of cancers, exposure occurring earlier may be most relevant for the survival of BC, as our results suggest. Regarding reverse causation, the associations remained within 5% of the main association after the exclusion of women who were diagnosed with cancer less than 2 years after responding to the questionnaire, suggesting that any effect of reverse causation was minimal in the sample. However, our analysis of the interaction between the prediagnostic interval and prediagnostic HLI score demonstrates the importance of investigating time scales that are not strictly relevant in the time-to-event analysis.

In terms of relevant age of exposure, we observed that, among persons diagnosed with CRC before age 68, a higher HLI score was associated with lower all-cause mortality, but no association was observed for persons diagnosed at age 68 and older. It is possible that since diagnosis at a younger age tends to be more severe, that lifestyle would play a role in resilience during treatment and remission during which the cancer survivor is susceptible to the onset of other morbid conditions. There was no difference in associations in subgroups of age at diagnosis for site-specific cancer mortality among any BC, CRC or LC cases and all-cause mortality for BC and LC cases.

When analyses are based on multifactor exposure measures, an investigation of the contribution of its constituent factors is warranted. While this is typically undertaken by estimating associations for single factors, our approach observed estimates of association for reduced HLI scores by way of the removal of single factors. The HLI serves to measure the combined exposure of lifestyle factors, whereby its strength lies in its simple approach to detect possible synergistic effects between many factors when used in explanatory settings. Estimating associations using single factors mutually adjusts for other lifestyle factors rather than appreciating possible interactions. Consequently, the approach we used to detect the contribution of single factors honours the fundamental difference between employing the HLI and lifestyle factors separately. This approach has been used by Naudin et al66 67 and in our previous HLI publications.41 68 Although examining single factors as separate estimates is important, our approach aligns more appropriately with the rationale for investigation associations using combined exposure measures, such as the HLI score.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of the present study include its prospective design, virtually complete identification of cancer cases and follow-up until death due to linkage to the national registries, and the large number of cancer cases. The 5-year BC, CRC and LC survival rates in our sample were comparable to national figures,63 which supports the national representativeness of NOWAC. Since exposure was measured before participants were diagnosed with cancer, any recall bias would likely be non-differential, compared with common retrospective designs.15 19 27 29 35 69 Further, NOWAC is considered externally valid as it was observed that there was little difference in the distribution of exposures between responders and non-responders.70 Nevertheless, generalising the results to other populations should be done with caution considering the culturally specific nature of lifestyle behaviours and region-specific nature of cancer care and treatment.

There were some limitations to the study. The advantages and disadvantages of indices created from combined factors as well as equally-weighted compared with weighted indices have been discussed previously.41 71 72 A clear interpretation of the risk estimate is made challenging by the combination of lifestyle factors. Thus, as in Pelser et al,12 we would like to emphasise the importance of the observed trend rather than the meaning of the point estimate. Further, it is possible that, for BC cases, our associations were attenuated by the shortness of follow-up time as some BC cases had as little as 6 years between responding to the questionnaire and the end of the study. Longer follow-up time for LC and CRC cases likely would not have allowed stronger associations to be detected as the 5-year survivals were low. Our estimates were also likely attenuated by the unavoidable variation in prediagnostic interval with the prospective study design. In addition, small case numbers among some subgroups limited the ability to detect potential associations. Misclassification of the exposure may have occurred, particularly since the HLI score depended on self-reported information. While this misclassification was likely non-differential, the estimates of association may have been biased towards or away from the null. We also cannot rule out residual confounding from differential measurement error among adjusted confounders. In addition, unmeasured confounding was likely present, although to an unknown degree, from factors such as comorbid conditions occurring after baseline, family history of CRC and BC screening attendance. Due to missingness in the Cancer Registry of Norway and the complexity of evolving treatment regimens over the course of the follow-up period, treatment was not accounted for in the models. However, the stage may have performed a proxy role for treatment during this period given that treatment has, in the vast majority of cases, been administered according to stage-dependent standard practice in Norway. Due to the design of the study, we cannot rule out the possibility of collider bias introduced by the selection of the study sample based on BC, CRC and LC diagnoses. Full adjustment for causal factors of BC, CRC and LC would have blocked the bias. However, as mentioned with respect to unadjusted confounding, it is conceivable that there was measurement error in these covariates as well as the presence of unmeasured factors, known and unknown.

Conclusion

We observed that a higher HLI score measured before cancer diagnosis was associated with lower all-cause and, possibly, lower BC mortality among Norwegian women diagnosed with BC. Among women diagnosed with CRC, there were indications that a higher prediagnostic HLI score was associated with lower all-cause and CRC mortality. We did not detect associations between the prediagnostic HLI score and mortality after LC diagnosis. Further investigation highlighted smoking behaviour as the lifestyle factor within the HLI that drove the all-cause mortality associations, emphasising the importance of smoking avoidance. Our results suggest that the associations could be dependent on stage at diagnosis, indicating the importance of early cancer detection, or that a healthy lifestyle reduces mortality of other causes. Given that BC and CRC are the most commonly diagnosed cancers among women in Norway, our findings add to the pool of evidence suggesting the wide-reaching, positive impact of adhering to healthy lifestyles during women’s disease-free, adult years—especially the protective role of smoking avoidance.
