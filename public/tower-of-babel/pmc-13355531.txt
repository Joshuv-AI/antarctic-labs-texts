Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and cardiovascular outcomes in dialysis patients with type 2 diabetes: a real-world propensity score-matched study
Authors: Jia-Jin Chen, Ming-Hsien Tsai, Wen-Yu Ho, Chieh-Li Yen, Yu-Wei Fang, Meng-Ting Chen, Ching-Chung Hsiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsPatients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) undergoing dialysis have extremely high cardiovascular and mortality risks, yet evidence for effective therapies is limited. The benefits of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) in this population remain unclear.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, propensity score-matched cohort study using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network (2013–2022). Among 1688 matched pairs of new GLP-1 RA and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP-4i) users, the primary outcome was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), with secondary outcomes of all-cause mortality, heart failure, sepsis, hospitalization, and emergency visits.ResultsGLP-1 RA users experienced significantly lower risk of MACE compared with DPP-4i users [hazard ratio (HR) 0.88, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.78–0.99]. GLP-1 RAs were also associated with reduced all-cause mortality (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.99), myocardial infarction (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.70–0.99), heart failure (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.78–0.98), and sepsis (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.71–0.92). Healthcare utilization outcomes, such as hospitalization and emergency visits, were also reduced. Findings were consistent across sensitivity and subgroup analyses.ConclusionsIn this large real-world dialysis cohort, GLP-1 RAs were associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes, survival, infection risk, and healthcare utilization, supporting their potential role in T2DM patients receiving dialysis.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) represent major global health burdens, with diabetes being the leading cause [1]. Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) who progress to dialysis face extraordinarily high risks of cardiovascular (CV) morbidity, infection, and premature mortality [2, 3]. Conventional glucose-lowering agents, such as insulin or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP-4i), are widely used in this population owing to their safety in advanced kidney disease, yet they have demonstrated little impact on long-term CV or survival outcomes [4]. Thus, there remains an urgent need for therapies that not only achieve glycemic control but also improve cardiorenal and survival outcomes in this vulnerable group.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have emerged as a class of antidiabetic agents with proven CV benefits in the general T2DM population and in patients with earlier stages of CKD. Large CV outcome trials, including LEADER, REWIND, and SUSTAIN-6, have shown that GLP-1 RAs reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and all-cause mortality [5–7]. However, patients with advanced CKD or on dialysis have largely been excluded from these pivotal trials, leaving considerable uncertainty regarding their effectiveness and safety in this setting.

Recently, several real-world and observational studies have begun to fill this knowledge gap. A nationwide Taiwanese cohort demonstrated that GLP-1 RAs’ use in patients with stage 5 CKD or ESKD was associated with lower all-cause and infection-related mortality compared with DPP-4i [8]. In the United States Renal Data System, GLP-1 RAs therapy among dialysis patients was linked to reduced mortality, increased likelihood of kidney transplant waitlisting, and clinically meaningful weight loss [9]. Another multinational TriNetX analysis reported that GLP-1 RAs’ use at dialysis initiation significantly reduced mortality and MACE compared with long-acting insulin [10]. A US Veterans cohort study demonstrated that GLP-1 RAs decrease the rate of acute healthcare utilization and all-cause mortality in patients with moderate-to-severe CKD compared with DPP-4i [11].

Collectively, these data suggest that GLP-1 RAs may improve clinical outcomes in dialysis patients with T2DM; however, conclusive evidence was still lacking. Furthermore, the correlation between GLP-1 RAs and the optimization of medical resource use in this cohort had not been established. To further address this gap, we conducted a large real-world, propensity score-matched study comparing GLP-1 RAs with DPP-4i therapy in dialysis patients with T2DM, focusing on CV outcomes, infection-related complications, and healthcare utilization.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data sources

TriNetX is an advanced healthcare research platform that connects hospitals, pharmaceutical companies, and investigators to accelerate medical research and improve patient care. At its core, the platform hosts a continuously updated database of de-identified patient information derived from medical records and insurance claims. Researchers can use TriNetX to define patient cohorts, assess study feasibility, and conduct robust statistical analyses for reliable comparisons. Built-in visualization and analytics tools support outcome evaluation, trend forecasting, and treatment effectiveness assessment. For clinical trials, the platform streamlines participant recruitment and study design based on real-world data. By linking institutions through specialized networks, TriNetX enables collaborative studies on longitudinal health outcomes and rare diseases. With strict compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and the General Data Protection Regulation standards, patient confidentiality is ensured. Overall, TriNetX integrates authentic real-world data with advanced analytic capabilities, fostering evidence-based decision-making and driving medical innovation.

Ethics statement

This study adhered to the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki [12], and all applicable regulations governing noninterventional and observational research. The TriNetX platform provides only de-identified data, with aggregated statistics and summaries available to researchers, ensuring the protection of patient confidentiality. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Review Board of Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital (IRB number: 20240812R), with the requirement for informed consent waived. Reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology recommendations for cohort studies.

Study design and study population

This retrospective cohort study used a new-user, intention-to-treat, active-comparator design to evaluate outcomes among adults with T2DM and ESKD on dialysis within the TriNetX US Collaborative Network (N = 124 377 671). Figure 1 shows the flowchart of patient selection. “T2DM” was defined using ICD-10-CM codes (E11), and “dependence on renal dialysis” was defined using specific ICD-10-CM codes (Z99.2) and CPT codes (90945 and 1012740) as detailed in Supplementary Method S1. Between 1 January 2013 and 31 December 2022, patients aged ≥18 years with at least three healthcare visits were screened. Among these, those meeting both the diabetes and dialysis criteria were identified, yielding 169390 candidates. Two exposure cohorts were defined: GLP-1 RAs users and DPP-4i users as the active comparator group. The index date was the date of their first prescription during the study period. To establish a new-user active comparator cohort, we first excluded individuals who had prior exposure to the alternative drug class within the preceding 6 months or on the same day. Patients with a history of organ transplantation and malignancy were also excluded, as these conditions could confound treatment effects and affect clinical outcomes. After applying exclusions, there were 1942 new GLP-1 RA users and 4645 new DPP-4i users. To minimize confounding, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching to balance baseline characteristics between groups, yielding 1688 GLP-1 RA users and 1688 DPP-4i users for analysis. Outcomes were assessed in an intention-to-treat framework from 1 day after the index date through up to 5 years, with follow-up censored at 27 July 2025. This design enables robust comparison of the two therapeutic strategies in a high-risk, real-world population. By restricting to new users and applying propensity score matching, internal validity is strengthened, and bias is reduced. The use of a comprehensive network with standardized data capture ensures reliable outcome assessment, while a clearly defined index date and fixed follow-up period provide consistent temporal alignment across cohorts.

Covariates

Baseline demographics included age at index (years), sex, and race categorized as White, African American, Asian, and Other race. Lifestyle history included assessment of nicotine dependence. Comorbid conditions encompassed hypertensive diseases, dyslipidemia, ischemic heart diseases, heart failure, atrial fibrillation and flutter, cerebrovascular diseases, diseases of arteries, arterioles and capillaries, fatty liver, liver cirrhosis, disorders of glycoprotein metabolism, disorders of the thyroid gland, gout, anxiety, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Medication use featured insulin, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, statins, fibrates, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) agents, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, antiarrhythmics, aspirin, clopidogrel, dipyridamole, and peripheral vasodilators. Moreover, laboratory measurements included creatinine, sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphate, hemoglobin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, body mass index (BMI), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). Together, these comprehensive baseline characteristics—spanning demographics, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, medication exposures, and laboratory values—were assessed in the 2 years preceding the index date to ensure thorough characterization of study participants before treatment initiation. For covariates with multiple measurements during the 2-year pre-index period, the most recent value before the index date was selected for analysis. This approach was adopted to ensure that baseline characteristics accurately reflect each patient’s physiological state and disease severity immediately before the index date.

Study outcomes

The study assessed a comprehensive set of clinical outcomes occurring within a time window of 1 day to 5 years after the index event, with follow-up data collected until 27 July 2025. The primary outcome was MACE, defined as a composite of acute myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, nontraumatic intracranial hemorrhage, subsequent ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), cardiac arrest, and death. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, acute myocardial infarction as an individual event, heart failure, atrial fibrillation or flutter, peripheral artery disease, sepsis, and pneumonia. To further capture healthcare utilization, the study also evaluated all-cause hospitalization and emergency department visits. Collectively, these outcomes provide a broad view of CV events, infectious complications, and healthcare use during the prespecified post-index period.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and continuous variables were compared using standardized mean differences (SMDs). Baseline confounding was addressed through propensity score matching, which created two comparable groups and ensured balanced covariates for valid treatment effect comparisons. Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics were evaluated, with SMD values below 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance [13]. Propensity scores were generated using logistic regression and matched with a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm, applying a caliper of 0.1 on the pooled SMD. Matching procedures were implemented using Python and R (version 3.4.4), with additional details provided in Supplementary Method S2.

The risk of clinical outcomes between groups was assessed based on the hazard ratios (HRs), which were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards regression model. The assessment of the assumption of proportional hazards was done using the generalized Schoenfeld approach integrated into the TriNetX platform. Event-free probabilities were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves. We conducted prespecified subgroup analyses of MACE by demographics (sex, age, race, and BMI), HbA1c, calcium, phosphate, hemoglobin, albumin, and comorbidities, including ischemic heart disease, congestive heart failure, and cerebrovascular diseases. For each subgroup (e.g., age <65 vs ≥65 years), we reconstructed a subgroup-restricted cohort, re-estimated the propensity score using the same covariates as the primary analysis, and repeated 1:1 matching. Cox models within each subgroup-specific matched cohort yielded HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Because matching was repeated within subgroups, sample sizes and event counts differ from the overall cohort. Between-subgroup heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran’s Q (χ2) test [14]. All statistical analyses were conducted using the TriNetX platform. A two-sided P-value of <.05 was considered significant.

Sensitivity analysis

To strengthen our results, we incorporated negative outcome controls into the analysis. For negative controls, we selected melanoma in situ and conjunctivitis as they are not linked to GLP-1 RAs or DPP-4i, helping confirm our methods did not yield false positives and supporting our study’s validity. We performed three sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of our results. First, we used multiple propensity score matching models with stepwise adjustment for confounders to better isolate treatment effects. Second, we assessed risks over various time periods to address potential violations of the proportional hazards assumption and ensure the stability over time. Finally, we extended the exposure lag window to 3 months to examine the impact of treatment duration on outcomes. The relevant diagnostic codes are listed in the eMethod 1 section of the supplementary material.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of patients

The study compared two treatment groups: GLP-1 RA users (1942 before matching and 1688 after matching) and DPP-4i users (4645 before matching and 1688 after matching). Before matching, notable differences were observed between the groups: patients receiving GLP-1 RAs were younger (57.2 vs 61.7 years), had higher BMI (35.2 vs 30.3 kg/m2²), higher HbA1c levels (7.8% vs 7.2%), and higher hemoglobin levels (10.9 vs 10.2 g/dl). The GLP-1 RAs group had higher proportions of patients with dyslipidemia (71.5% vs 62.6%), anxiety (16.5% vs 9.8%), and insulin use (82.9% vs 69.5%). After matching (1688 patients per group), demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced, with SMDs <0.1 for most variables. Residual differences were observed in calcium (9.0 vs 8.8 mg/dl), phosphate (4.6 vs 4.8 mg/dl), hemoglobin (10.9 vs 10.5 g/dl), and albumin (3.7 vs 3.6 mg/dl). Both groups had a high prevalence of comorbidities, including hypertension (∼90%), dyslipidemia (∼70%), and ischemic heart disease (∼32%), reflecting a cohort with substantial CV risk burden in addition to diabetes (Table 1).

Major adverse cardiovascular outcomes of GLP-1 RA use in dialysis patients

The Kaplan–Meier plot (Fig. 2) illustrates event-free survival over 5 years, showing consistently higher probabilities in the GLP-1 RAs group compared with the DPP-4i group. Although CIs overlapped, the GLP-1 RAs curve generally remained superior, with increasing separation over time. The log-rank test confirmed a statistically significant difference between groups (P = .036). In the matched cohort, GLP-1 RA users experienced fewer MACE events than DPP-4i users (526 [31.2%] vs 645 [38.2%]; Table 2). The HR for GLP-1 RAs versus DPP-4i was 0.88 (95% CI, 0.78–0.99), indicating a significant reduction in the risk of the primary outcome with GLP-1 RAs therapy.

Secondary outcomes of GLP-1 RA use in dialysis patients

Kaplan–Meier plots for secondary outcomes (Fig. S3) demonstrated significant separations favoring GLP-1 RAs for all-cause mortality (log-rank P = .031), acute myocardial infarction (P = .049), heart failure (P = .016), sepsis (P = .002), hospitalization (P = .001), and emergency visits (P = .001). Although not statistically significant, trends also favored GLP-1 RAs for atrial fibrillation/flutter (P = .222), peripheral artery disease (P = .103), and pneumonia (P = .117). Confidence bands consistently showed superior event-free probabilities with GLP-1 RAs, with widening separation over time for several endpoints.

In the matched cohort (Table 2), GLP-1 RA was associated with reduced risks of mortality (16.8% vs 22.5%; HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.99), acute myocardial infarction (12.6% vs 16.4%; HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.70–0.99), heart failure (34.4% vs 40.4%; HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.78–0.98), and sepsis (22.6% vs 29.6%; HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.71–0.92). Hospitalizations (52.8% vs 59.8%; HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.79–0.95) and emergency visits (41.5% vs 49.3%; HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.76–0.93) were also significantly lower. Nonsignificant but favorable trends were observed for atrial fibrillation/flutter, pneumonia, and peripheral artery disease.

Subgroup analysis

Across matched subgroups, GLP-1 RAs generally reduced the MACE versus DPP-4i, with most HRs favoring GLP-1 RAs (Fig. 3). Significant benefits were seen in males, age ≥65 years, BMI ≥30 kg/m2, calcium ≥9 mg/dl, hemoglobin ≥10 g/dl, albumin ≥3.5 g/dl, ischemic heart disease, and those without congestive heart failure. Nonsignificant trends favored GLP-1 RAs in females, younger patients, various races, HbA1c strata, phosphate strata, and cerebrovascular disease.

Subgroup analysis for MACE. The forest plot shows HRs with 95% CIs for within-subgroup comparisons, and values > 1 indicate a higher risk with the GLP-1 RAs group.

Across matched subgroups, GLP-1 RAs consistently outperformed DPP-4i. Mortality (Fig. S4), sepsis (Fig. S5), and hospitalization (Fig. S6), were generally lower with GLP-1 RAs, with benefits seen in both sexes, older adults, White patients (and Asians for sepsis); BMI ≥30; higher calcium; hemoglobin ≥10 g/dl; albumin ≥3.5 g/dl, and those with ischemic heart disease. Trends were neutral in several smaller strata—particularly younger patients, low albumin, and cerebrovascular disease—while no subgroup clearly favored DPP-4i.

Sensitivity analysis

Negative outcome control analyses found no significant differences in the incidence of melanoma in situ or conjunctivitis between the comparison groups, as summarized in Table S1. Multiple sensitivity checks, including models adjusted for numerous potential confounders (Table S2), alternative specifications of the time scale (Table S3), and an extended evaluation window from 1 day to 3 months (Table S4), all yielded consistent results that GLP-1 RAs confer more benefit in MACE in DM dialysis patients than DPP-4i.

DISCUSSION

In this large real-world cohort of patients with T2DM receiving maintenance dialysis, we found that the use of GLP-1 RAs was associated with substantial clinical benefits compared with DPP-4i. First, GLP-1 RA therapy was associated with lower risks of MACE, all-cause mortality, acute myocardial infarction, and heart failure. Second, in addition to CV benefits, GLP-1 RAs’ use was associated with lower risks of sepsis, hospitalization, and emergency visits, indicating broader protective effects in this high-risk population. These findings provide valuable evidence that GLP-1 RAs may improve both survival and healthcare outcomes in dialysis patients, a group in whom treatment options remain limited.

Our results are consistent with growing evidence from international cohorts. A Taiwanese nationwide study demonstrated that GLP-1 RAs’ use in patients with stage 5 CKD or ESKD was associated with significant reductions in both all-cause mortality and infection-related mortality compared with DPP-4i [8]. However, MACE-related mortality did not differ between the two treatments in the study, which may be attributed to the relatively short follow-up period, limited sample size, and the similarity to the lack of demonstrated clinical benefit of statin therapy in the dialysis population. In the United States Renal Data System, GLP-1 RA therapy among over 150 000 dialysis patients was associated with a 23% reduction in mortality and a 66% higher likelihood of being listed for kidney transplantation, with additional evidence of weight loss [9]. Similarly, a TriNetX-based analysis of >82 000 patients initiating dialysis showed that GLP-1 RAs reduced mortality, MACE, and major adverse kidney events compared with long-acting insulin [10]. More broadly, a recent Cochrane systematic review including 42 randomized controlled trials concluded that GLP-1 RAs probably reduce all-cause death and CV events in patients with diabetes and CKD, although robust evidence in dialysis is lacking [15]. Collectively, these studies, together with our findings, suggest a consistent and reproducible benefit of GLP-1 RAs across different stages of CKD, extending into the dialysis setting.

The biological plausibility of these findings is supported by multiple mechanisms. Several randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the superiority of GLP-1 RAs in glycemic control [16–19], weight reduction [16–18, 20], and β-cell function preservation [16,17, 19] compared to DPP-4i. Moreover, GLP-1 RAs exert favorable CV effects, including improved endothelial function, reduced oxidative stress, and anti-inflammatory activity [21–23]. They have also been shown to improve left ventricular function in patients with T2DM and reduce hospitalization for heart failure in patients with heart failure with mildly reduced or preserved ejection fraction [24–26]. The observed reduction in sepsis risk is noteworthy, as GLP-1 signaling has been implicated in modulation of innate immunity and attenuation of inflammatory responses [27, 28]. A national cohort study in Taiwan revealed GLP-1RAs decrease the risk of hospitalization due to lower respiratory tract infections (adjusted HR: 0.56; 95% CI, 0.49–0.65) and diabetic foot infections (adjusted HR: 0.77; CI, 0.65–0.91) compared with DPP-4i [29]. This may be particularly important for dialysis patients, who face heightened infection risk due to the need for repeated vascular punctures and the additional immune dysfunction induced by uremia. However, the potential immunomodulatory effects of GLP-1 RAs in reducing sepsis risk remain speculative in our study, as the TriNetX database lacks specific inflammatory markers or immune parameters. While basic research suggests that these agents may modulate inflammation, this should be viewed as a hypothesis rather than a confirmed mechanism in our cohort. The observed reduction in sepsis-related outcomes may be multifactorial, and further studies with detailed laboratory data are needed to clarify the biological pathways in dialysis patients.

Compared with other antidiabetic agents, GLP-1 RAs appear uniquely positioned for patients with advanced CKD and dialysis. Insulin, while indispensable for glycemic control, is associated with hypoglycemia, weight gain, and no proven survival advantage. DPP-4i are generally considered safe in CKD but are neutral in terms of CV and kidney outcomes [30, 31]. SGLT2i, now a cornerstone in CKD and T2DM management, is not recommended in dialysis patients due to loss of efficacy at very low estimated glomerular filtration rate [32]. By contrast, GLP-1 RAs remain feasible across the full CKD spectrum, including dialysis, and have demonstrated CV and mortality benefits. These results resonate with recent guidelines from the American Diabetes Association and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes, which endorse GLP-1 RAs as preferred agents when SGLT2i or metformin cannot be used [32, 33].

The clinical implications of our findings are considerable. CV disease and infection remain the leading causes of death in dialysis patients [3, 34], and our results suggest that GLP-1 RAs may mitigate both. Notably, our MACE definition included all-cause mortality, as specific causes of death are often indistinguishable in the TriNetX database. While this introduces competing risks, it is possible that GLP-1 receptor agonists provide broad systemic benefits beyond cardioprotection. A national Taiwanese cohort demonstrated that GLP-1 RAs’ use in patients with stage 5 CKD or ESKD was associated with lower all-cause and infection-related mortality [8]. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the specific CV and non-CV benefits in this population.

Reductions in hospitalization and emergency visits imply not only improved patient outcomes but also potential cost savings in this resource-intensive population. The association with increased transplant waitlisting seen in other studies further underscores the relevance of GLP-1 RAs for long-term care planning in dialysis patients [9]. These findings suggest that GLP-1 RAs may represent more than a glucose-lowering therapy, offering broad benefits across survival, CV protection, infection risk, and healthcare utilization in dialysis populations. Nonetheless, as an observational study, potential indication bias cannot be excluded. Patients prescribed GLP-1 RAs may represent a healthier subgroup with better functional status or more intensive clinical follow-up. This “healthy user” effect could independently contribute to improved clinical outcomes, potentially driving the observed reductions in hospitalizations and emergency department visits. Therefore, randomized controlled trials are essential to mitigate these inherent selection biases and definitively confirm the clinical benefits of GLP-1 RAs in the dialysis population.

Safety is also an important consideration. Concerns about biliary disease and thyroid cancer have been raised, but these were not confirmed in dialysis cohorts [35–37]. However, the potential for increased risk of diabetic retinopathy, particularly with semaglutide, has been reported in clinical trials such as SUSTAIN-6 [6] and in retrospective observational studies [38], and thus warrants careful monitoring in clinical practice. Gastrointestinal side effects remain common but are generally manageable with gradual dose escalation. Overall, the accumulating data support an acceptable safety profile, particularly in light of the substantial survival benefits.

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational analysis, residual confounding and indication bias cannot be fully excluded despite the use of propensity score matching and sensitivity analyses. Second, a notable limitation of this study is the inability to definitively establish a temporal sequence between the diagnosis of T2DM and the initiation of dialysis, given the retrospective nature of the TriNetX database. Consequently, we could not verify whether diabetic kidney disease was the primary etiology of ESKD for all participants. The inclusion of patients with diabetes who may have reached kidney failure due to unrelated causes introduces potential heterogeneity that could influence clinical outcomes. While this reflects the inherent constraints of large-scale electronic health record (EHR) data, readers should interpret the findings with caution regarding the specific pathophysiology of ESKD. Third, medication exposure was inferred from prescription records rather than confirmed adherence, and discontinuation or intolerance could not be captured, potentially leading to misclassification. Laboratory parameters such as HbA1c, lipid levels, and inflammatory markers were not consistently available, limiting mechanistic insights and adjustment for key confounders. Outcomes were defined by diagnosis codes, which may introduce misclassification despite prior validation. Fourth, despite matching, residual imbalances (SMD > 0.1) remained in serum calcium, phosphate, hemoglobin, and albumin. While these factors could potentially confound CV outcomes, our sensitivity analyses using multivariate Cox regression yielded consistent results after further adjustment. This suggests that the observed benefits of GLP-1 RAs are robust, and the impact of these baseline laboratory disparities on the primary findings is likely minimal. Lastly, a key limitation is the lack of granularity in the TriNetX database regarding specific dialysis-related confounders. We could not adjust for dialysis vintage, vascular access types, or C-reactive protein (CRP) levels due to inconsistent EHR coding and missing data. Additionally, precise details on Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents (ESA) dosages and phosphate binder formulations were unavailable. Although these factors significantly impact mortality in ESKD patients, the inherent constraints of federated EHR systems precluded their inclusion. Future studies using specialized renal registries are necessary to confirm our observations.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that GLP-1 RAs’ use in dialysis patients with T2DM is associated with reductions in mortality, CV events, sepsis, and healthcare utilization compared with DPP-4i. Together with consistent findings from international cohorts and meta-analyses, these results suggest that GLP-1 RAs may represent an important therapeutic option in a population historically excluded from pivotal clinical trials. Considering the persistent burden of CV disease and infection in dialysis, GLP-1 RAs have the potential to transform the management of diabetes and advanced kidney disease.
