High-affinity agonists reveal recognition motifs for the MRGPRD GPCR
Authors: Chunyu Wang, Yongfeng Liu, Marion Lanier, Adam Yeager, Isha Singh, Ryan H Gumpper, Brian E Krumm, Chelsea DeLeon, Shicheng Zhang, Marcus Boehm, Richard Pittner, Alain Baron, Lisa Dvorak, Corinne Bacon, Brian K Shoichet, Esther Martinborough, Jonathan F Fay, Can Cao, Bryan L Roth
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe human MRGPRD protein is a member of the Mas-related G protein-coupled receptors (MRGPRs) that is involved in the sensing of pain, itch, and other inflammatory stimuli. As with other MRGPRs, MRGPRD is a relatively understudied receptor with few known agonists. The most potent small-molecule agonist of MRGPRD reported so far is β-alanine, with an affinity in the micromole range, which largely restricts its functional study. Here, we report two MRGPRD agonists, EP-2825 and EP-3945, that are approximately 100-fold more potent than β-alanine and determine the structures of MRGPRD-Gq in complex with EP-2825 and EP-3945, respectively. The structures reveal distinct agonist binding modes of MRGPRD and large conformational plasticity of the orthosteric pocket. Collectively, the discovery of high-affinity MRGPRD agonists and their distinct binding modes will facilitate the functional study and the structure-based design of ligands targeting this understudied receptor.

INTRODUCTION

The Mas-related G protein-coupled receptor (MRGPR) family in humans represents a family of 8 receptors that are predominantly expressed on sensory neurons and/or innate immune cells. MRGPRs have been implicated in the sensing of noxious stimuli and in mediating neuroimmune responses to such stimuli, including inflammation, pain, and pruritus.1,2 Of these, the MRGPRD receptor is an essential mediator of peripheral pain and itch sensations1–4 and, given its localization to the gut,5,6 has also been implicated as a potential mediator of gastrointestinal perfusion and motility. As with other MRGPR receptors, MRGPRD has considerable basal or constitutive activity.7 MRGPRD has been reported to be activated by many agonists, including β-alanine,8 angiotensin(1–7),9 and allantonin,10 which leads to intracellular calcium,8 inhibition of cyclic AMP formation,8 and arachidonic acid release.9 Collectively, these results imply that MRGPRD can couple to both Gq- and Gi-family G proteins.8 In ex vivo studies, activation of MRGPRD enhances the excitability of primate nociceptive neurons.11 The activation of MRGPRD is also reported to inhibit mast cell hyperresponsiveness and skin inflammation to maintain skin homeostasis.12 Thus, MRGPRD medications have potential utility for a number of disorders, including pain, itch, and inflammatory disorders. However, there are currently no high-affinity and selective chemical probes available for MRGPRD. To our knowledge, the most potent small-molecule agonist of MRGPRD reported so far is β-alanine, which is a weak agonist with a half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) of around 15 μM.8 Therefore, the development of MRGPRD agonists with higher affinity and selectivity is urgently needed.

We and others recently reported single-particle cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of MRGPRX2 coupled to Gi,13,14 MRGPRX2 coupled to Gq,13 MRGPRX4 coupled to Gq,13 MRGPRX1 coupled to Gq,15–17 and MRGPRD coupled to Gi,18 respectively. Using structure-based large-scare docking and high-throughput drug screening, we also discovered the small molecule probe (R)-ZINC-3573 for MRGPRX219 and MS47134 for MRGPRX413 and were able to verify their binding mode via cryo-EM.13 Even though it has been 19 years since the discovery of β-alanine as a low-micromole agonist of MRGPRD,8 there is still no high-affinity MRGPRD agonist reported so far. Here, we report two high-affinity agonists of MRGPRD and determined the high-resolution structures of both in agonist-bound MRGPRD-Gq complexes. These structures reveal distinct agonist binding modes and provide insights into the structure and agonist recognition mechanism of MRGPRD, which should accelerate the search for chemical tools and drugs for this important and understudied MRGPR-family receptor.

RESULTS

Agonist discovery and structure determination of the MRGPRD-Gq complex

The current MRGPRD agonists are of low affinity. To discover high-affinity MRGPRD agonists for both functional study and structure determination, we performed high-throughput drug screening at MRGPRD. In general, a ~250,000-compound library was screened at 10 μM using the PathHunter arrestin assay at Beacon Discovery (San Diego, CA, USA). A total of 250 compounds were initially identified that provided at least 20% of the β-alanine response. Among them, EP-2825 displayed the highest agonist activity at MRGPRD. Subsequent chemistry optimization studies identified EP-3945 as another active compound (Supplementary Data 1). Structurally, both EP-2825 and EP-3945 have different chemical structures with a molecular size significantly larger than β-alanine and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) (Figure 1A). In our bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) assay, EP-2825 and EP-3945 displayed an EC50 value of 92.1 nM and 211.6 nM at Gq, respectively, which is approximately 188-fold more potent than that of β-alanine (Figure 1B; Table S1). To assess specificity, we profiled EP-2825 and EP-3945 at 318 G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) using the PRESTO-Tango resource.20 These assays showed that both EP-2825 and EP-3945 are inactive against over 300 GPCRs, with EP-2825 displaying low off-target activity at SSTR4 and GPR97 (Figure S1; Table S2).

We then used the two synthetic agonists EP-2825 and EP-3945 for our structural studies. Previous functional assays have suggested that MRGPRD couples to both Gi and Gq.21 In our study, EP-2825 and EP-3945 also display high potencies at Gi1 (Figure S1F; Table S1). Given that MRGPRD enhances neuronal firing11,22 and that Gq signaling generally induces neuronal excitation,23 we sought to first assemble the MRGPRD-Gq complex for structural studies using our previously described approach. Briefly, MRGPRD-Gq complexes were purified from sf9 cells co-expressed with an N-terminal-Bril-fused MRGPRD and an engineered miniGq heterotrimer.24 Our functional assays showed that the N-terminal-Bril MRGPRD has a pharmacological profile similar to that of the wild type (WT) (Figures S1G and S1H; Table S1). The purified MRGPRD-Gq complexes were then stabilized with the indicated agonists and a G protein heterotrimer stabilizing antibody scFv16.25 We then determined the Gq-coupled MRGPRD structures in complex with EP-2825 and EP-3945 at a nominal resolution of 2.92 and 2.90 Å, respectively (Figures 1C, 1D, and S2). The high-resolution structures of MRGPRD allow us to model both the ligand and most portions of the receptor (Figures 1C, 1D, and S3). Structurally, EP-2825 and EP-3945 are analogs of each other. As the EP-2825-bound MRGPRD-Gq complex displays clearer density of the ligand (Figures 1C and 1D), we use this structure for detailed analysis unless otherwise noted.

The overall structure of the agonist-bound MRGPRD-Gq complex

The overall structure of the EP-2825-bound MRGPRD-Gq complex is similar to the previously determined apo and β-alanine-bound MRGPRD-Gi complexes.18 The receptor region root-mean-square deviation for the EP-2825-bound MRGPRD-Gq complex versus the apo MRGPRD-Gi complex and the EP-2825-bound MRGPRD-Gq complex versus the β-alanine-bound MRGPRD-Gi complex structures is 0.50 Å and 0.55 Å, respectively. The extracellular end of transmembrane (TM) helices of EP-2825-bound MRGPRD resemble a conformation similar to that of the apo MRGPRD-Gi structure (Figures S4A and S4B). When aligned, TM1-TM7 of EP-2825-bound MRGPRD overlayed well with TM1-TM7 of the apo MRGPRD structure (Figures S4A–S4C). However, relative outward movements of TM5 and TM6 are observed in the EP-2825-bound MRGPRD compared to their conformation in the β-alanine-bound state (Figure 2A). As β-alanine and EP-2825 are different chemotypes, these helical movements could represent different receptor conformational states that could be selectively stabilized by distinct agonists.

As with other MRGPR-family receptors, the canonical disulfide bond between TM3 and extracellular loop 3 (ECL3) does not exist in MRGPRD. Instead, it contains an inter-helix disulfide bond between TM4 and TM5 (Figure 2A). The short ECL2 of MRGPRD remains on the top of TM4 and TM5, resulting in a large solvent-exposed pocket for ligand binding (Figure 2A). In the extracellular side, both EP-2825 and EP-3945 bind to the receptor at the orthosteric sub-pocket 1 formed by TM3–TM6. The relatively large sub-pocket 2, which is formed by TM1–TM3 and TM6 and TM7, is empty and not directly involved in ligand binding (Figure 2A). This agonist binding mode is consistent with the previously identified agonist recognition mode of MRGPRX1 and MRGPRX2,13–15,26 in both of which the agonist also primarily interacts with the small sub-pocket 1 (Figure S5A).

Like other MRGPR receptors, many of the canonical motifs associated with receptor activation, including the sodium binding motif, DRY motif, PIF motif, and CWxP motif, are not conserved in MRGPRD.13 Notably, MRGPRD lacks the canonical toggle switch residue W6.48 (superscript denoted as Ballesteros-Weinstein number27), which is replaced with a small residue, S2346.48. Both EP-2825 and EP-3945 bind to MRGPRD at a position that is approximately 10 Å away from S2346.48 without forming any direct interaction (Figure 2B). Thus, the MRGPRD agonists do not seem to rely on S2346.48 to initiate the receptor activation. Last, a TM1–TM7 disulfide bond, which is predicted to exist in all primate-exclusive MRGPRX family receptors, is absent in MRGPRD.

Distinct agonist binding modes of EP-2825 and EP-3945

In the EP-2825-bound MRGPRD structure, EP-2825 adopts an L shape to interact with the MRGPRD receptor. The relatively large size of EP-2825 allows it to form more interactions with the receptor, which results in a much larger ligand pocket than that of β-alanine (Figures 2C–2E). At the top of the ligand, the tetrahydroquinazolinone ring of EP-2825 occupies the orthosteric site. The carbonyl group of the tetrahydroquinazolinone ring forms charge interactions with K1023.29 and R1033.30, whereas its two nitrogen atoms forms hydrogen bonds with D1795.37 (Figure 3A). The ligand-receptor charge interactions with R1033.30 and D1795.37 are also observed in the β-alanine-bound MRGPRD structure.18 Given that most known MRGPRD agonists consist of both positively charged nitrogen atoms and negatively charged carbonyl or carboxyl moiety (Figure 1A), the charge interactions between ligands and residues R1033.30 and D1795.37 are likely crucial for their agonist activity. To test this hypothesis, we performed mutagenesis studies using the TRUPATH G protein dissociation assay28 and found that alanine substitutions of these two charged residues abolish the agonist activity of EP-2825 (Figure 3D; Table S3). By contrast, the agonist activity of EP-2825 is largely maintained in the K1023.29A mutant, suggesting that K1023.29 is not essential for receptor activation (Figure 3D; Table S3). Therefore, the activation of MRGPRD should be mainly driven by the charge interactions between the agonist and residues R1033.30 and D1795.37.

In the middle part of the ligand, the piperidine group of EP-2825 binds deeper to the receptor and mainly forms interactions with residues Y1063.33, N1574.57, T1604.60, and W2416.55 (Figure 3B). Both W2416.55 and Y1063.33 adopt a vertical conformation to wrap around the agonist in EP-2825-bound MRGPRD. Alanine substitutions of W2416.55 and Y1063.33 abolish the receptor activation stimulated by EP-2825 (Figure 3E; Table S3), suggesting that MRGPRD requires the large side chain of these two residues to form a suitable vertical pocket for the agonist binding. In the bottom part of ligand, the chlorophenyl group of EP-2825 inserts into an extended side cavity between TM5 and TM6 and mainly forms hydrophobic interactions with residues Q1825.40, A1835.41, I1865.44, S2386.52, F2426.56, and Y2456.59 (Figure 3C). The mutagenesis study shows that removal of the hydrophobic moieties from F2426.56 and Y2456.59 reduced the agonist-stimulated activation of MRGPRD (Figure 3F; Table S3), whereas the alanine substitutions of the hydrophilic residues N1574.57 and Q1825.40 slightly enhanced the receptor activation (Figures 3E and 3F; Table S3). Apparently, the hydrogen bonds and charge interactions with residues R1033.30 and D1795.37 are mainly responsible for ligand-stimulated receptor activation. Therefore, these additional interactions between the hydrophobic chlorophenyl group of EP-2825 and the extended pocket of MRGPRD may mainly contribute to the high-affinity binding properties of EP-2825.

As closely related analogs of each other, EP-3945 displays a ligand binding pose and mutational profile similar to that of EP-2825 (Figures S6A–S6E; Table S4). Most of the ligand-receptor interactions, including the key charge interactions between the ligand and residues R1033.30 and D1795.37, found between EP-2825 and MRGPRD could be observed in the EP-3945-bound MRGPRD (Figures S6A–S6C). In addition, EP-3945 recaptures the agonist-stabilized large conformational changes of TM6 and adopts a similar L-shape to interact with MRGPRD (Figures 2D and S6D). Due to its larger size, the methoxyphenyl group of EP-3945 inserts deeper into the extended side pocket than the chlorophenyl group of EP-2825, which results in additional outward movements of residues A1835.41 and F2426.56 (Figure S6D). To further validate the binding pose of EP-2825 and EP-3945, we conducted triplicate molecular dynamics simulations (200 ns) for MRGPRD. During the simulation courses, both ligands remained stable within the binding pocket (Figures S7A and S7B), although alternative conformational states of W2416.55 and Y2456.59 may stably exist during the ligand binding process for EP-2825 and EP-3945 (Figures S7C and S7D). In both EP-2825- and EP-3945-bound MRGPRD structures, the extended side pocket is sealed by hydrophobic residues A1835.41, M1875.45, and F2426.56. Any large chemical substitute of the methoxy group or the chloro group of the agonists will clash with these three residues and result in reduced binding of the ligand.

Conformational plasticity of the extracellular pocket

GPCRs could undergo considerable conformational change upon binding to different ligands.29 A previous structural study on MRGPRD revealed conformational rearrangement of the pocket residues upon binding to β-alanine.18 In this work, both EP-2825 and EP-3595 stabilized a ligand binding pocket that was different from the previously determined MRGPRD structures. Compared with its β-alanine-bound state, we observed an outward movement of TM5 and TM6 in the EP-2825-bound MRGPRD structure (Figures 2A and 4A). In addition, the extracellular end of TM6 undergoes a counterclockwise rotation from residue F2426.56 to W2466.60 with large conformational changes of the side-chain rotamer states (Figures 4A and 4B). Specifically, the F2426.56 rotates from the central pocket to the cell membrane and creates an extended pocket to accommodate the chlorophenyl group of EP-2825 (Figures 2C and 4A). By contrast, F2426.56 faces inward to the central pocket (Figure 4A) and makes this cavity occluded in the β-alanine-bound state (Figure 2E). In addition, W2416.55 adopts a vertical configuration in the EP-2825-bound MRGPRD structure, whereas it gradually rotates downward to adopt distinct rotamer states in both the β-alanine-bound and apo MRGPRD structures (Figures 4A and 4C). Last, residues Y2456.59 and F1765.34 undergo additional conformational changes to further open the orthosteric pocket for EP-2825 binding (Figures 4B and 4D).

Although F2426.56 adopts similar conformations in the apo and EP-2825- and EP-3945-bound MRGPRD structures (Figures 4A and 4C), the downward rotation of the side chain of W2416.55 blocks the entry of the extended pocket in the apo MRGPRD structure (Figure 4C). Thus, MRGPRD also does not have the extended pocket in the apo state. Apparently, the orthosteric sub-pocket 1 in the apo and β-alanine-bound MRGPRD structures is too small to accommodate the large molecular size of EP-2825 and EP-3945 (Figures 2A–2C and 4C). Thus, the observed conformational changes of TM5 and TM6 in our structures are likely required to reshape a larger pocket for the binding of EP-2825 and EP-3945. Large conformational changes of the extracellular part of TM6 are also observed in the transition from the apo state to the β-alanine-bound state,18 suggesting that MRGPRD has a flexible pocket to recognize ligands with distinct scaffolds.

The G protein binding mode of MRGPRD

MRGPRD has been shown to couple to both Gi and Gq proteins.8 Structural comparison of the MRGPRD-Gq complex with the previously determined MRGPRD-Gi complex revealed similar intracellular conformations of MRGPRD with almost identical TM6 outward movements (Figures S4A and S4C), suggesting that no obvious structural determinants are responsible for the selective engagement of different G protein subtypes. Therefore, the selective coupling of Gi or Gq proteins to MRGPRD may mainly depend on the availability of G protein subtypes in a specific cell.

As observed in many GPCR-G protein complexes, MRGPRD mainly interacts with the α5 and αN helices of Gq. Specifically, the α5 helix of Gq inserted into the canonical intracellular cavity of MRGPRD and formed extensive interactions with surrounding residues in TM2 and TM3 and TM5–TM7 (Figure S4C). Due to the presence of the bulky residue Y243H5.23 (superscript denoted as CGN number for G protein30) at the C terminus, the α5 helix of Gq does not bind to the central cavity of MRGPRD as deeply as the α5 helix of Gi (Figure S4D). Consequently, the αN helix of Gq also shifted approximately 6 Å outward to interact with the intracellular loop 2 (ICL2) of the receptor (Figure S4E). Despite the distinct binding mode of G proteins, most of the G protein interface residues in the TM core of MRGPRD adopt similar conformations to interact with both Gq and Gi (Figure S4D). The only major difference is that the side chain of F612.39 is pushed upward by the α5 helix in the Gq-coupled MRGPRD structure (Figure S4D). Outside of the core, residues R2828.49 and N592.37 form strong charge interactions with D350 of Gi1 (Figure S4F). In the Gq-coupled MRGPRD, these interactions are not observed.

In both Gq- and Gi-coupled receptors, a short ICL3 of MRGPRD is resolved (Figure S4G). In addition to the interaction with the intracellular core and ICL2, Gq and Gi proteins also form interactions with the ICL3 of MRGPRD. Notably, most of the known MRGPR-family receptors, including MRGPRD, MRGPRX1, and MRGPRX2, have been reported to couple to more than one G proteins.26 These MRGPRs all have a short ICL3 consisting of 8–10 residues. As the long ICL3 of a GPCR may act as a selectivity filter to block the weakly coupled G proteins,31 the promiscuous coupling of different G protein subtypes in MRGPR-family receptors may partly result from their short ICL3.

Comparison of MRGPRD with MRGPRX-family receptors

Unlike the primate-exclusive MRGPRX-family receptors, MRGPRD also exists in non-primate animals, including mice.32 From an evolutionary perspective, MRGPRD is relatively conserved across different species. Both mouse MRGPRD and human MRGPRD could be activated by β-alanine with a similar potency.8,32 In addition, the MRGPRD protein also has lower mutation rates in humans compared to MRGPRX receptors. According to the data in the gnomAD database,33 the allele frequencies of the missense mutations in human MRGRD are 50–100 times lower than that of MRGPRX1, MRGPRX2, and MRGPRX4. Therefore, the evolutionarily conserved MRGPRD may respond to common itch stimuli shared by many species. By contrast, MRGPRX-family receptors are likely recently evolved receptors.

Despite the functional differences, MRGPRD and MRGPRX-family receptors transduce the extracellular stimuli through a shared downstream signaling pathway. Both MRGPRD and MRGPRX-family receptors have been reported to couple to Gq protein for signaling.26 In MRGPRD, the Gq protein engages the intracellular activity of receptor in a way similar to other solved MRGPRX structures (Figures S5B and S5C), which is consistent with their conserved intracellular residues.15 In the extracellular side, however, the MRGPR agonists bind to their corresponding receptors at distinct positions (Figures 5A and 5B). The agonists of MRGPRX1, MRGPRX2, and MRGPRX4 bind shallowly to the extracellular surface of the receptor with few overlaps, suggesting that MRGPR-family receptors do not have a conserved agonist binding mode (Figures 5A and 5B). Due to the TM6 remodeling, EP-2825 and EP-3945 bind to MRGPRD much deeper than other known MRGPR agonists and directly interact with residue Y3.36, which forms polar interactions with the carbonyl group of residue G/S6.48. (Figure S8). As MRGPRs lack the canonical toggle switch residue W6.48, the polar interactions between residue Y3.36 and the carbonyl group of residue G/S6.48 are thought to be critical for the agonist-stimulated activation of MRGPRs.14,18 For instance, alanine substitutions of Y1063.36 and S2346.48 are reported to result in reduced G protein signaling in MRGPRD.18 However, no direct interactions between the agonist and residue Y3.36 have been observed in previously determined MRGPR receptors. In our structure, both EP-2825 and EP-3945 form hydrophobic interactions with residue Y3.36 of MRGPRD (Figure S8), suggesting that they could directly interact with residue Y3.36 to modulate receptor activation. While other MRGPRs agonists are usually of low affinity, the deep binding mode of EP-2825 and EP-3945 could also partly explain their high-affinity binding nature.

Besides the binding position, the MRGPR agonists also recognize the receptor through different mechanisms (Figures 5C–5G). In MRGPRX1 and MRGPRX2, the receptor activation is mainly mediated by the interaction between the positively charged agonist and the negatively charged residues E4.60 and D5.40 on the receptor (Figures 5D and 5E). By contrast, MRGPRX4 recognizes negatively charged agonists through its positively charged residues (Figure 5F). In MRGPRD, both positively and negatively charged residues are involved in agonist recognition (Figures 5C and 5G) as its agonists, such as β-alanine, GABA, and EP-2825, are composed of oppositely charged chemical groups. Although the structures of the understudied MRGPRs, such as MRGPRE, MRGPRF, MRGPRG, and MRGPRX3, have not been experimentally determined, the AlphaFold34 models of these receptors suggest that their key orthosteric residues involved in agonist recognition are different (Figures 5H–5K). The diverse agonist recognition modes as well as the conformational flexibility of the extracellular pocket observed in MRGPR-family receptors may allow them to recognize itch stimuli with different chemotypes and charge properties.

DISCUSSION

MRGPRs play important roles in inflammatory itch and pain sensations. As these are understudied receptors, both the agonist discovery and structure determination of MRGPRs have been challenging. In particular, few high-affinity small-molecule agonists have been reported for MRGPRs. In this study, we report two high-affinity agonists, i.e., EP-2825 and EP-3945, for MRGPRD, which potently activate the receptor (Figures 1A and 1B). We have previously discovered the small-molecule agonists (R)-ZINC-3573 for MRGPRX2 and MS47134 for MRGPRX4, respectively. The discovery of the MRGPRD agonists EP-2825 and EP-3945 further shows how structure-based drug discovery and high-throughput drug screening can help to find MRGPR ligands for both structural and functional studies.

Due to the shallow binding of current known MRGPR agonists, it is questionable whether the discovery of high-affinity ligand at many MRGPRs is possible. In our structures, both EP-2825 and EP-2945 bind to MRGPRD at a position that is much deeper than the previously identified agonist binding sites of MRGPRD, MRGPRX1, MRGPRX2, and MRGPRX4 (Figure S8). Notably, the binding of EP-2825 and EP-2945 unexpectedly causes large conformational changes of the orthosteric pocket residues (Figure 4) and creates an extended pocket, which likely accounts for their high-affinity binding. Large conformational changes of TM6 residues are also observed in the previously determined apo and β-alanine-bound MRGPRD structures.18 This raises the possibility that other MRGPRs might also undergo similar conformational changes to reshape their pocket to bind structurally distinct ligands with higher affinity. Moreover, the large orthosteric pocket shaped by the binding of EP-2825 or EP-3945 could also facilitate the structure-based discovery of MRGPRD agonists that could not be obtained using previous MRGPRD models. Of all solved MRGPRs structures, MRGPRD is the only receptor that displayed considerable flexibility in its orthosteric pocket. As many endogenous or exogenous substances may activate MRGPRs to induce various neuroimmune responses, the flexibility of the extracellular surface of MRGPRD might give it the ability to recognize different noxious stimuli.

In summary, our MRGPRD structures reveal a distinct orthosteric pocket for agonist binding, which could be used as a template to perform structure-based large-scale docking to discover MRGPRD agonists with high affinity. In addition, the two MRGPRD agonists reported in this study, EP-2825 and EP-3945, could be used as high-affinity probes to study the function of MRGPRD. Collectively, our structures along with the agonists could accelerate the functional study and drug discovery of MRGPRD receptors.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we discovered two high-affinity agonists, EP-2825 and EP-3945, for MRGPRD and screened the off-target activity of these two compounds at other non-olfactory GPCRs using GPCRome (Figures S1A–S1B). However, the GPCRome assay is only sensitive to the agonist activity of tested ligands; we do not know whether EP-2825 and EP-3945 could bind to the screened GPCRs with the off-target antagonist activity. Additionally, due to the lack of commercially available radioligands for MRGPRD, a binding assay was not performed for the mutagenesis studies. Instead, we used a BRET assay to explore the potential effects of these mutations on the agonist-stimulated activation of MRGPRD. Compared to the apo and β-alanine-bound states of MRGPRD-Gi1 complexes, we observed large conformational changes of the extracellular ligand binding pocket in our structures. However, we did not obtain the MRGPRD structure in the “real” apo state, which is both ligand-free and G protein free. Therefore, the exact structural mechanism underlying the orthosteric pocket rearrangement for the binding of the different ligands remains unknown. Lastly, our analyses of the orthosteric pocket of MRGPRF, MRGPRG, MRGPRH, and MRGPRX3 are based on the AlphaFold predicted models (Figures 5H–5K). Structural determinations of these MRGPRs are required to provide experimental models for accurate interpretation of the orthosteric pockets for ligand recognition.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Three eukaryotic cell lines, Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9, Expression systems) cells, HEK293T cells (ATCC) and HTLA cells, were used in this study. Sf9 cells were purchased from Expression systems and used for protein expression of MRGPRD-miniGq complex for structural study. Sf9 cells were grown in ESF-921 medium at 27°C, 120 rpm without further validation. HEK293T cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CRL-11268) and used for BRET assays in this study. HTLA cells (a HEK293 cell line stably expressing a tTA-dependent luciferase reporter and a β-arrestin2-TEV fusion gene) were a gift from R. Axel and used for Tango assay in this study. Both HEK293T and HTLA cells were grown in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2 using DMEM medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 I.U./mL penicillin and 100 mg/mL streptomycin. HEK293T cells were authenticated by the supplier (ATCC) using morphology, growth characteristics and STR profiling. HTLA cells were authenticated by morphology, growth characteristics and the successful tango assay which demonstrates that both tTA-dependent luciferase reporter and a β-arrestin2-TEV fusion gene are presented in the cells.

METHOD DETAILS

Generation of MRGPRD and Gq heterotrimer constructs

For the expression of MRGPRXD-Gq protein complex, the full-length DNA of human MRGPRD (UniProtKΒ-Q8TDS7) was subcloned into a modified version of pFastBac1 (Invitrogen) baculovirus expression vector with a hemagglutinin (HA) signal peptide followed by a FLAG tag, a 10×His-tag, and a TEV protease site at the N terminus. To facilitate protein expression, a thermostabilized apocytochrome b562RIL (BRIL) and HRV3C protease sites were further introduced at the N terminus of MRGPRD. For the Gq protein, we used the same mini-GaqiN heterotrimer (referred to Gq hereinafter) construct that was firstly reported in the expression of HTR2A-Gq-NBOH complex.24 For the functional assays, the human MRGPRD gene was cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector containing an N-terminal FLAG tag with a C-terminal Rluc8 gene. All the mutations were generated using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Cloning Kit (New England Biolabs) and verified by DNA sequencing.

Expression of the MRGPRD-Gq complex

The widely used Bac-to-Bac system was used to generate the baculovirus for protein expression. Briefly, the expression constructs of MRGPRD and Gq were transformed into DH10Bac competent cells to generate the recombinant bacmid. Then Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) insect cells were plated into a 12-well plate at a concentration of 4.5×105 cells/well and transfected with 3 μg of purified bacmid to obtain recombinant baculovirus. After 96 h of incubation, 1 mL of the supernatant was collected as the P0 viral stock to generate P1 virus stock by infection 40 mL of 2×106 sf9 cells/ml. After 96 h of incubation, the P1 viruses were harvested and tittered by flow cytometric analysis of sf9 cells stained with gp64-PE antibody (Expression systems). For the expression of the MRGPRD-Gq complex, MRGPRX1 and Gq heterotrimer were co-expressed by infection sf9 cells at a density of 2 million cells per mL with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) ratio of 3:1.5. After 48 h of infection, the cells were harvested by centrifugation. The cell pellets were then stored at −80°C for future use.

scFv16 expression and purification

The expression and purification of scFv16 was performed as previously described.13 In brief, the scFv16 gene was cloned into a modified pFastBac1 vector containing a gp67 signal peptide sequence at the N terminus. The virus generation and protein expression of scFv16 was performed with the same protocol as that of MRGPRD. After 96 h of protein expression, the supernatant containing secreted scFv16 was collected and adjusted to pH 7.8 by the addition of Tris base powder. Chelating agents were quenched by the addition of 1 mM nickel chloride and 5 mM calcium chloride and stirred for 1–2 h at room temperature. After another centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and incubated with 1 mL of His60 Ni Superflow Resin (Takara) overnight at 4°C. The resin was collected the next day and washed with 20 column volumes of buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl and 10 mM imidazole. The protein was then eluted with a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl and 250 mM imidazole. Finally, the scFv16 protein was concentrated and subjected to size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 10/300 GL (GE healthcare). The peak fractions were collected and concentrated to 2–5 mg/mL for future use.

The purification of MRGPRD-Gq complex

The pellet of cells expressed the apo MRGPRD-Gq complex was thawed on ice and resuspended in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2 supplemented with protease inhibitor at room temperature. After 30 min, the cell suspension was homogenized by a Dounce homogenizer. The membranes were collected by centrifugation at 25,000 rpm (Ti45 rotor) for 30 min and solubilized with a buffer containing 20 HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5% (w/v) glycerol, 0.6% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG), 0.06% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS) for 5 h at 4 °C. The solubilized complex proteins were isolated by centrifugation at 32,000 rpm for 30 min using a Ti70 rotor and incubated overnight at 4 °C with Talon IMAC resin (Clontech) and 20 mM imidazole. On the next day, the resin was collected and washed with 25 columns volume buffer containing 20 HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5% (w/v) glycerol, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, 0.001% (w/v) CHS and 20 mM imidazole. The protein was then eluted with 4 columns volume buffer consisting of 20 HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5% (w/v) glycerol, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, 0.001% (w/v) CHS and 250 mM imidazole. The eluted MRGPRD-Gq complex proteins were concentrated and subjected to size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (GE healthcare) that was pre-equilibrated with a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.00075% (w/v) LMNG, 0.00025 (w/v) glyco-diosgenin (GDN), 0.00075% (w/v) CHS and 100 μM TCEP. The peak fractions were collected and incubated with 15 μl PreScission protease (GeneScript) and 2 μL PNGase (NEB) and 200 μg scFv16 at cold room overnight. On the next day, the sample was concentrated and subjected to size exclusion chromatography for the second time to remove PreScission protease, PNGase and free scFv16. The monomer peak fractions were concentrated to 3–5 mg/mL and incubated with 100 μM EP-2825 and 100 μM EP-3945, respectively, for 2 h prior to the cryoEM grid making.

CryoEM data collection and 3D reconstitution

3.2 μL MRGPRD-Gq samples were then added to the glow-discharged R1.2/1.3 Au300 holey carbon grids (Ted Pella) individually and flash frozen in a liquid ethane/propane (40/60) mixture using Virobot mark IV (FEI) at 4°C and 100% humidity with a blot time range from 3 to 4.5 s. CryoEM micrographs were then collected using a 200 keV Talos Arctica microscope with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector at a physical pixel size of 0.88 Å . Micrograph recorded movies were automatically collected using SerialEM as previously described.35,41 Using cryoSPARC36,42 particles from each manually curated dataset were selected using Blob particle picker. A subset of these particles was used to train Topaz43 and the particles were repicked from the micrographs. The combined particles were subjected to 2D and/or 3D classification to resolve a final stack of particles that produced a map with resolutions (by FSC using the 0.143 Å cut-off criterion)44 reported in Table S5. Additional sharpening was carried out using deepEMhancer.45 For more details see Figure 2.

Model building and refinement

Maps generated from deepEMhancer45 were used for model building, refinement and subsequent structural interpretation. The MRGPRD model predicted by AlphaFold34 along with the miniGq heterotrimer and scFv16 models taken from the MRGPRX2-Gq complex (PDB 7S8N) were docked into the MRGPRD maps using ChimeraX.39 The initial models were manually refined in Coot46 and then subjected to several rounds of real-space refinement in Phenix47 and manually refinement in Coot to obtain the final models. The model statistics were validated using Molprobity.48 Structural figures were prepared by either ChimeraX or PyMOL (https://pymol.org/2/). The detailed refinement statistics are provided in Table S5.

BRET assays

To measure the MRGPRD mediated Gq recruitment, we performed the bioluminescence resonance energy transfer assay (BRET1) as previously described.49 Briefly, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with either wild-type (WT) or mutated MRGPRD-RLuc8 and mVenus-miniGq at a 1:1 ratio. After 16–24 h, the transfected cells were seeded into poly-lysine coated 384-well white plates at a density of 15,000–20,000 cells/well and incubate overnight in DMEM medium supplemented with 1% (v/v) dialyzed FBS. The next day, the medium was discarded, and the cells were incubated with 20 μL of drug buffer (1 × HBSS, 20 mM HEPES, 0.1% (w/v) BSA, pH 7.4) containing 5 μM coelenterazine h (Promega) for 5 min. Hereafter, the cells were further incubated with 10 μL of 3 × designated drug for another 5 min. Finally, the plates were counted for both luminescence at 475 nm and fluorescent eYFP emission at 535 nm at a 0.6 s per well interval by using PHERAstar FSX (BMG Labtech). The ratio of eYFP/RLuc was calculated, and the net BRET value was normalized to the percentage of WT and fitted with nonlinear regression model either “log(agonist) vs. response” or “log(agonist) vs. response-variable” in the GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. For BRET2 GI 1 protein dissociation assay, MRGPRD, Gαi1-Rluc8, Gβ3 and Gγ9-GRP2 plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells with a ratio of 1:1:1:1. The assay protocol was same to BRET1 G protein recruitment assay except for dye coelenterazine 400a was used as the substrate for Rluc8. The plates were counted for both luminescence at 395 nm and fluorescent GFP2emission at 510 nm. The ratio of GFP2/RLuc was calculated, and the net BRET value was normalized to the percentage of WT. All the curves were fitted with nonlinear regression model either “log(agonist) vs. response” or “log(agonist) vs. response-variable” in the GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (Tables S1–S4). The relative expression levels of MRGPRD mutant proteins are shown in Figure S9.

GPCRome and β-arrestin recruitment assay

PRESTO-Tango GPCRome assays were performed as previously published20 to interrogate the receptor selectivity of the compound EP-2825 and EP-3945. In brief, HTLA cells were seeded into poly-lysine coated 384-well white plates in DMEM medium supplemented with 1% (v/v) dialyzed FBS at a density of 15,000–20,000 cells/well. After 6 h, the cells were transfected with 20 ng per well of Tango receptor constructs overnight. On the following day, the cells were treated with 10 μM of the compound EP-2825 or EP-3945 and incubated overnight. At last, the medium was decanted and 20 μL per well of diluted BrightGlo (Promega) was added to determine the luminescence activity. All the plates were counted in a MicroBeta TriLux counter (PerkinElmer). The results were plotted as fold of average basal against individual receptors in GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

In addition, full dose-response Tango β-arrestin recruitment assays were used to verify the receptors with more than 3-fold of basal activity increases. The HTLA cells were transiently transfected with the targeted receptor Tango DNAs overnight and plated into poly-lysine coated 384-well white plates as described above. After 6 h, the cells were further treated with 10 μL of different concentrations of 5× compound EP-2825 or EP-3945 overnight. The next day, the medium was removed, and the cells were incubated with 20 μL per well of diluted BrightGlo (Promega) for 20 min. The plates were read using a MicroBeta TriLux counter (PerkinElmer). Results (raw value) were plotted in GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Surface expression levels of WT MRGPRD and mutants were determined by using Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. To achieve this, HEK293T cells (ATCC CRL-11268) were passed to 6-well plates and transfected with 200 ng of the given receptor. After 24 h, transfected cells were plated in poly-lysine coated 96-well white plates in DMEM medium supplemented with 1% (v/v) dialyzed FBS at density of 50,000 cells per well. The next day, the media was aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with 100 μL of 1× phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Then the cells were incubated with 100 μL of 5% (w/v) BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Afterward, the cells were probed with 100 μL of 1:10,000 diluted anti-Flag horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, A8592) for another 1 h. After six times of wash with 1× PBS, 50 μL per well of chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 37069) was added, and the luminescence signal was counted using PHERAstar FSX (BMG Labtech). The data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9.0 software and normalized to the signal of WT MRGPRD.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All the functional data was analyzed in Graphpad Prism 9.0 using nonlinear regression “log(agonist) vs. response” or “log(agonist) vs. response-variable”. Data in figures and tables are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) with the number of biological replicates indicated in the figure and table legends where “n” represents the number of independent biological replicates performed.
