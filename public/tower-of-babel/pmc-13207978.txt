Molecular Mechanisms and Biomedical Applications of Ferritin Nanocages: A Comprehensive Review of Self-Assembly, Engineering, and Multifunctional Delivery Platforms
Authors: Fukang Gao, Yu Wu, Xinglin Chen, Jinping Chen, Yuelin Liu, Aixia Huang, Yutong Fu, Dongfeng Chen, Feng Lin, Yang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFerritin is a heteropolymer or homopolymer composed of 24 polypeptides forming a cage-shaped sphere. Its main function is to maintain soluble and non-toxic states of iron ions in the body. In mammals, ferritin is primarily located in the cytoplasm and the mitochondria. Cytoplasmic ferritin consists of heavy (H) and light (L) subunits, whereas mitochondrial ferritin is a homopolymer composed exclusively of a single subunit type. A notable feature of ferritin is its ability to self-assemble into nanocages in vitro, which is reversible and controllable. This unique property has enabled its widespread application in various fields including drug delivery, disease diagnosis, and vaccine development. In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of ferritin, focusing on the following aspects: 1) structural characteristics, biological functions, dynamics, and molecular mechanisms underlying ferritin self-assembly, 2) recent advances and applications of ferritin-based self-assembled nanocages in biomedicine and bioengineering, and 3) progress in the molecular modification of ferritin using genetic engineering techniques. Additionally, we critically examined the current limitations of ferritin in research and practical applications, and proposed potential strategies for further improvement, with the aim of providing insights into the future development and utilization of ferritin in medical and scientific contexts. This work integrates the kinetics and molecular mechanisms of ferritin self-assembly with advanced genetic engineering strategies, offering a systematic framework for the rational design of ferritin-based multifunctional platforms.

Introduction

Ferritin is ubiquitous in plants, mammals and bacteria. As an important iron storage protein, it exists as either a heteropolymer or homopolymer composed of 24 subunits, forming a nearly spherical protein shell. Its main function is to maintain iron ions in a soluble, non-toxic state within biological systems, thereby playing a pivotal role in cellular and organismal iron homeostasis. Ferritin subunits can be divided into three types based on molecular weight and functional characteristics: heavy subunit (H subunit, FtH), light subunit (L subunit, FtL), and middle subunit (M subunit, FtM).1 The designations “heavy” and “light” originated from their initial isolation sources - “H” from heart tissue and “L” from liver tissue - with modern terminology retaining these historical labels.2 Functionally, FtH possesses ferroxidase activity, catalyzing the oxidation of ferrous iron (Fe2+) to ferric iron (Fe3+), whereas FtL contains a mineral core nucleation site involved in iron core formation and electron transfer. Notably, FtM combines the features of both FtH and FtL, harboring both a ferroxidase center and an iron nucleation site. Biochemically, FtH and FtL are conserved in higher vertebrates, whereas FtM is exclusively found in lower vertebrate species.3 Mitochondrial ferritin (FtMt), first discovered in humans, is a homopolymeric protein that functions specifically within the mitochondria, is efficiently imported, and is localized to the mitochondrial matrix.4 The FtMt gene is intronless, encoding a precursor peptide with a mitochondrial targeting signal, with 80% and 55% sequence homology with cytoplasmic FtH and FtL, respectively.5

The most prominent characteristic of ferritin is its capacity for self-assembling. Under extreme pH conditions (strongly acidic or basic), the cage-like quaternary structure dissociates into individual subunits, which can spontaneously reassemble into functional nanocages under neutral conditions. In addition, ferritin also exhibits salt-ion-sensitive dissociation-reassembly behavior in certain species, adding to the biophysical versatility.6 Leveraging this self-assembly property, small molecules and therapeutic agents can be efficiently encapsulated within ferritin’s central cavity,7 positioning ferritin as a promising nanocarrier for drug delivery. However, the assembly patterns of ferritin subunits in the cytoplasm and mitochondria, structural characterization, physiological and biochemical properties of ferritin nanocages, and assembly kinetics and mechanisms have not been systematically summarized. In this review, we summarize 1) the structure and biological functions of ferritin; 2) the kinetics and molecular mechanisms governing self-assembly; 3) recent progress and applications of ferritin-derived self-assembled nanocages in biomedicine and bioengineering. In addition, we discuss the current limitations and propose potential improvements using genetic engineering techniques to enhance the utility of ferritin in future research and translational applications. Many reviews have discussed ferritin structure or its biomedical applications separately. However, fewer have tried to bring together the dynamic process of self-assembly, from kinetic pathways to the molecular features that govern it, with the engineering strategies that make use of these dynamics for therapy and diagnosis. This review attempts to fill that gap by offering a more mechanism-focused perspective, with the aim of providing a useful reference for researchers working on next-generation ferritin nanocages with controllable assembly, disassembly, and versatile cargo-delivery capabilities.

Classification and Subunit Composition of Ferritin

The ferritin family comprises three subfamilies: 1) classical ferritin, a 24-meric protein that can be either homomeric or heteromeric; 2) bacterial ferritins, subdivided into non-heme 24-meric homomeric ferritin and heme-containing 24-meric homomeric ferritin (bacterioferritin, Bfr); and 3) 12-meric DNA-binding proteins (Dps) from starved cells. Classifiable ferritin is widely present in bacteria, plants, and animals, whereas Bfr and Dps are restricted to bacteria and archaea, respectively. Generally, ferritin is a heteropolymer composed of FtH and FtL subunits, or a homopolymer formed by a single type of FtH or FtL subunit, as well as the rare FtMt formed by single mitochondrial ferritin subunits.8 Bai et al investigated ferritin genes in the genomes of 248 organisms including viruses, bacteria, archaea, and eukarya. The results showed that mammals and eudicots harbor a wealth of ferritin genes, which were also found in some viruses, probably originating from horizontal transfer from the host (Figure 1A).9 Recently, Sultana et al explored the evolutionary patterns and subclass distribution of ferritins by analyzing a dataset comprising 237 sequences across 15 phyla. Phylogenetic analyses revealed distinct clusters that corresponded to different ferritin subfamilies (Bfr, Dps, and ferritin), thereby elucidating the evolutionary relationships between phyla and among subfamilies, such as Dps, Bfr, and classical ferritins. (Figure 1B).10 Amrita et al constructed a phylogenetic tree using the maximum-likelihood method and analyzed the evolutionary relationships among different ferritin subfamilies. Bfr forms a long, separate branch distinguished by unique inter-subunit heme binding at the 2-fold IntA site, a feature absent from the other three groups, supporting the view that it emerged through convergent evolution. Dps proteins diverged the earliest, followed by 24-subunit Bfrs and classical ferritins. Classical ferritins further evolved into heteropolymerization and more negatively charged interfaces, which enhanced their stability and tissue specificity. Animal Ferritin

As an iron storage protein, ferritin is ubiquitously distributed across the animal kingdom and plays a pivotal role in maintaining iron homeostasis and engaging in diverse physiological processes. Animal ferritins are taxonomically classified into mammalian, avian, fish, insect, and shellfish ferritins. Similar to ferritins in other organisms, both avian and fish ferritins are typically composed of 24 subunits forming a hollow spherical structure.12,13 These subunits are divided into two main types: H and L chains. However, the proportions of these two chains and their amino acid sequences varied among species.

Mammals possess three functional ferritin-encoding genes: FtH (cytosolic H-chain), FtL (cytosolic L-chain), and the intronless FtMt (mitochondrial ferritin precursor).14 The amino acid sequences of FtH and FtL share 55% homology and exhibit similar assembly properties; both can independently form 24-subunit homopolymers and are predisposed to co-assemble into 24-subunit heteropolymers. The composition of these heteropolymers varies depending on the tissue type and developmental stage, resulting in a range of subunit combinations (eg, H24L0, H22L2, H0L24) that are thought to form through homodimeric subunit interactions.15 In contrast, the mature subunits of FtMt assemble into 24-subunit homopolymers with ferroxidase activity.16 Functionally, cytoplasmic ferritin primarily mediates iron ion storage and the systemic iron balance, whereas FtMt acts as an antioxidant defense mechanism to mitigate iron-induced cytotoxicity.17

Compared to mammals, insect ferritins exhibit distinct traits. 1) Although ferritins in certain insect species, such as Bombyx mori (silkworm), Drosophila melanogaster (fruit fly), and Apis mellifera (honey bee), exist as heteropolymers, most insect ferritins are composed of a single type of homologous subunit, forming homopolymers. 2) unlike mammalian, insect subunits contain N-terminal signal peptides, undergo glycosylation, and localize to the endoplasmic reticulum and hemolymph; 3) besides its roles in iron ion metabolism, insect ferritin may participate in regulating the immune response.18 For instance, silkworm ferritin is a prototypical insect ferritin that includes cytoplasmic subunits (FtH and FtL) and FtMt. Compared with other insects, silkworm ferritin has the closest homologous sequence to that of the typical insect model Drosophila. Their H subunits are far apart from those of other vertebrates in terms of phylogenetic evolution, forming an independent branch that reflects the differences in ferritin between insects and vertebrates.18 Our previous study showed that neither FtH nor FtL of Bombyx mori can self-assemble into 24-subunit homopolymers; instead, they can only form 24-subunit heteropolymers.19

In addition, a third type of ferritin subunit, called the middle (M) subunit (FtM), has been identified in lower vertebrates, such as fish and amphibians, including Rana catespiana, Salmo salar, and Lampetra fluviatilis.20,21 FtM has a specific structure that can form a cavity, guiding it to form a homopolymer, as has been reported in Trematomus bernacchii.20–22

Phytoferritin

Phytoferritins are critical for several physiological processes such as mitigating heavy metal stress and counteracting oxidative stress, etc.23 Unlike animal ferritins, phytoferritin consists exclusively of H-type subunits that are further divided into two distinct isoforms with molecular weights of 26.5 (H-1 subunit) and 28 kDa (H-2 subunit), sharing approximately 80% amino acid sequence homology.24

Phytoferritins are predominantly localized in plastids, such as amyloplasts in seeds and chloroplasts in leaves. These subunits can self-assemble into 24-subunit homopolymers or combine to form 24-subunit heteropolymers.25,26 The ratio of H-1 to H-2 subunits varies significantly among plant ferritins; for example, it is 1:1 in soya ferritin, 1:2 in pea ferritin, 2:1 in black bean ferritin, and 1:0 (only H-1) in both adzuki bean and chickpea ferritin.27 Structurally, phytoferritins feature eight vertices of 4-fold symmetry and six vertices of 3-fold symmetry, with hydrophilic pores at the 3-fold axes lined by glutamate, aspartate, and serine residues, which facilitate iron entry and release while preventing nonspecific interactions.25

Additionally, phytoferritins possess a unique N-terminal extension peptide (EP) containing approximately 30 amino acids, which is not found in their animal counterparts.23 This evolutionarily conserved EP, located on the exterior of the protein, contributes to iron-binding, oxidation, core nucleation, surface hydrophobicity, and structural stability. The N-terminal targeting signal, called the transit peptide (TP) in phytoferritin, is critical for directing proteins to plastids, primarily chloroplasts, where iron storage and homeostasis are maintained. Unlike animal ferritins, which are cytosolic, phytoferritin is synthesized as a precursor with TP, which facilitates its translocation across the double-membrane envelope of plastids. This process involves recognition by the translocon complexes at the outer (TOC) and inner (TIC) chloroplast membranes, which mediate ATP/GTP-dependent import.10,28,29

Microbial Ferritin

Microbial ferritins, including bacterial, archaeal, and fungal variants, are central to microbial iron homeostasis with structural and functional conservation and divergence across taxa.

Bfr and classical ferritin proteins are both nearly spherical hollow structures assembled from 24 subunits (~12 nm outer diameter and ~8 nm inner diameter). The only difference between classical ferritins and Bfr is the ability of Bfr to bind to the heme molecule at the interface between its two individual subunits.30 Taking Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 as instance, the genome encompasses genes encoding FtnA (PA4235) and bfrB (PA3531).8 The product of the PA4235 gene is ferritin, which cannot bind heme because the closest methionine to heme (Met48) is too distant to coordinate the heme iron. Therefore, PA4235 has been annotated as FtnA. Recombinant expression of the FtnA and BfrB genes in E. coli, followed by structural characterization of the recombinant proteins, demonstrated that the products of each gene produced 24-mer proteins. P. aeruginosa also forms a single heterooligomeric 24-mer Bfr, composed of FtnA and BfrB subunits. Notably, the relative abundance of the FtnA and BfrB subunits in Bfr was closely associated with oxygen availability during cell culturing.8,31 Two distinct Bfr subunits (Bfr1 and Bfr2) of Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense were identified in M. gryphiswaldense. Bfr1 possesses ferroxidase activity, which is involved in iron oxidation, whereas Bfr2 contributes to heme binding. Bfr1 alone cannot efficiently form stable homo-oligomeric complexes Only when Bfr1 and Bfr2 assemble into heterooligomeric complexes do they form the typical 24-meric structures that are essential for protecting cells from oxidative stress in vivo.32

Dps is a class of ferritin-like proteins found in bacteria and archaea that shares functional similarities with ferritin. Unlike ferritin and Bfr, which are assembled from 24 subunits, the quaternary structure of Dps consists of 12 subunits that form a nearly spherical shell enclosing a hollow interior cavity.10 Each Dps subunit adopts a four-helix bundle-fold and contains a single iron-binding site, suggesting that it plays a key role in iron coordination and storage. The dodecameric oligomeric structure was stabilized primarily through hydrophobic interactions.33 The absence of a fifth helix (helix E) in Dps proteins, a structural element found in canonical ferritins that mediates four-fold symmetry interactions, probably explains why Dps assemble into 12-subunit oligomers instead of 24-mers.34 Nair et al showed that Dps protects cells from oxidative stress, UV and gamma irradiation, iron and copper toxicity, thermal stress, and acid and base shock.35

The P. aeruginosa PAO1 genome also harbors two genes encoding distinct miniferritins, Pa Dps (PA0962) and Pa DpsL (PA4880). PA0962 encodes for a protein with a characteristic p-fold. At neutral pH in the presence of divalent metal ions, the protein adopts a distinctive 12-mer quaternary structure (Pa Dps) that harbors two diiron ferroxidase sites at the interface of each subunit dimer. Pa Dps exhibited H2O2-dependent ferroxidase activity in vitro and contributed to the survival of P. aeruginosa cells exposed to H2O2-mediated oxidative stress. PA4880 encodes a protein with a quaternary dodecameric assembly, in which each subunit exhibits a four-helix bundle-fold characteristic of Dps.36 However, unlike Dps, ferroxidase centers are buried within each subunit, akin to the ferroxidase centers in ferritin or bacterioferritin. Structure and Biological Functions of Ferritin

The biological functions of ferritin are closely linked to its unique structural design. Ferritin assembles into a hollow, cage‑like architecture that provides a confined space for iron storage and creates precisely arranged pores and interfaces to regulate iron entry, oxidation, and release. This structural framework enables ferritin to maintain iron homeostasis, protect against oxidative damage, and participate in immune regulation and host defense.

Structure of Ferritin

The highly conserved quaternary structure of ferritin exhibits remarkable evolutionary preservation across animal, bacterial, and plant species. Ferritin is composed of 24 identical or similar subunits, which self-assemble into a hollow, roughly spherical protein cage with an internal diameter of 8 nm, outer diameter of 12 nm, and thickness of 2 nm (Figure 3A).38 Each subunit contains a conserved structural motif, a four-α-helical bundle (helices A-D) with parallel (A/B) and antiparallel (C/D) pairings capped by a fifth C-terminal α-helix (helix E). Flexible nonhelical segments connect these structural elements at the N-/C-termini and the interhelical junctions (AB, CD, and DE turns). Notably, the extended BC loop (residues 78–94) bridges helices B and C, whereas helix E deviates by 60°from the main-bundle axis. During self-assembly, the subunits are structurally connected by two-, three-, and four-fold symmetry (4-3-2 symmetry) axes and pack tightly together, forming a narrow pore traversing the shell along the three- and four-fold axes.41,42 At the two-fold symmetry axes (C2 interfaces), subunit interactions primarily involve anti-parallel helical associations. The C3 interface is located near the three-fold symmetry axis, where subunits form trimeric contacts in this region, typically involving interactions between B helices and adjacent subunits. The C4 interface is situated near the four-fold symmetry axis, where the subunits establish tetrameric contacts, primarily involving the A helix. The C3-C4 interface pertains to the contact regions of the subunits near the three- and four-fold symmetry axes. The spherical structure of ferritin is composed of six 4-fold axes (C4 interfaces), eight 3-fold axes (C3 interfaces), twelve 2-fold axes (C2 interfaces), and twenty-four hybrid C3-C4 interfaces (Figure 3C). The structural characteristics of these interfaces may include specific hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and hydrophobic interactions, which ensure the correct assembly and functional regulation of the subunits. This sophisticated interface engineering enables the dynamic regulation of iron flux while maintaining nanocage stability, illustrating the evolutionary optimization of the dual storage-protection function.43

Biological Functions of Ferritin in Antioxidation and Immune Defense

The primary function of ferritin is to oxidize Fe2⁺ to Fe3⁺ and store the trivalent iron as ferrihydrite within its central cavity. It provides a safe, bioavailable iron reserve for cellular needs, and more importantly, it sequesters Fe2⁺ from participating in the Fenton reaction, thereby suppressing the generation of hydroxyl radicals and protecting cells from oxidative damage.44 Consequently, cellular iron stored in ferritin directly influences susceptibility to ferroptosis — an iron-dependent, non-apoptotic form of cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species accumulation.45 Reduction of ferritin (especially the heavy chain) expands the labile iron pool and sensitizes cells to ferroptosis, whereas inhibition of NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy confers resistance to ferroptosis.46 FtMt contains a mitochondrial targeting sequence that directs it specifically into the mitochondrial matrix. Despite sharing ~80% sequence identity with cytosolic human H-chain ferritin, FtMt has much lower ferroxidase activity and does not contribute to systemic or cytoplasmic iron storage; instead, it acts primarily as a mitochondrial antioxidant to protect cells from iron‑dependent free radical damage.47

Ferritin also actively participates in innate immune defense against microbial pathogens. In mammals, serum ferritin levels rise during inflammation and infection, serving as a routine clinical biomarker for conditions such as sepsis, metabolic syndrome, coronary heart disease, and notably COVID-19 severity.48,49 During bacterial infection, ferritin limits iron availability to pathogens by sequestering iron, thereby suppressing bacterial growth. In insects, such as Bombyx mori, ferritin heavy chain homolog (BmFer1) can reduce bacterial loads and inhibit the growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus by sequestering excess iron ions, thereby limiting iron availability for pathogenic proliferation.50 Similarly, in Tribolium castaneum, stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) significantly upregulates the expression of ferritin H subunit,48 and fungal challenge can induce a marked increase in iron content in beetle hemolymph.51 In addition to antibacterial immunity, ferritin is extensively involved in antiviral host responses. Hyperferritinemia has been documented in a wide range of viral diseases, such as hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), dengue virus, and influenza A virus.48,52,53 Elevated serum ferritin often correlates with disease severity, viral load, and inflammatory response, reflecting the host’s iron redistribution and immune activation.54

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ferritin nanocages are not merely passive iron containers but actively participate in antioxidant defense, ferroptosis regulation, and innate immunity against bacteria and viruses, highlighting their intrinsic anti-disease potential.

Self-Assembly Properties and Kinetics of Ferritin

Self-Assembly and Dissociation of Ferritin

Ferritin nanocages exhibit remarkable stability toward chemical denaturants and extreme pH conditions. For example, horse spleen ferritin (HSF) can maintain its structural integrity unless exposed to temperatures exceeding 80 °C for 10 min or extreme pH values below 1.0 or 13.0.55 In addition, ferritin shows pH-sensitive assembly characteristics: it deploymerizes into dimers in acidic or alkaline environments and reassembles into its native cage-like structure under neutral conditions.56 Kim et al reported that ferritin dissociates into subunits, and secondary subunit interactions are disrupted when the solution pH fall below 3.4 or 10.57 Below pH 3.4, ferritin was unstable and underwent a stepwise disassembly. At pH values below 0.80, the ferritin subunits undergo aggregation, leading to denaturation. Upon restoration of pH to neutral from values below 2 or above 12, ferritin subunits reassembled into nanocages, although the structural dimensions were smaller than those of wild-type ferritin. This size discrepancy is likely correlated with the rate of pH recovery and time required for complete structural reorganization. Additionally, both the protein shell thickness and internal iron core were smaller than those of wild-type ferritin, presumably because of the formation of unfolded subunits during dissociation and incomplete recovery from acid- or base-induced iron release.57 Ji et al showed that po-pig pancreatic ferritin (apoPPF) could be completely disassociated into 24 free subunits at pH 13.0, and these subunits could be quickly reassembled into the original apoPPF once the pH of the reactive medium was returned to pH7.0.58 The pH-induced ferritin disassembly and reassembly processes were not completely reversible, although they were pseudo-reversible over a limited pH range, of 10.0 and 2.66.

Lucignano et al showed that the denaturing agent sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) can significantly influence the disassembly and reassembly of homopolymeric ferritin nanocages (hHFt) derived from human heavy-chain proteins. The key factor is the ratio of protein to SDS, as a submicellar SDS concentration of around 0.1% can only efficiently disassemble hHFt within a narrow protein concentration range (1.0–3.0 mg/mL). Rapid reassembly of hHFt was achieved by promptly diluting the SDS concentration to < 0.01%. Furthermore, a small metallodrug, [Ru(bpy)3]2+ complex (Ru1), and a 13-residue antimicrobial peptide were encapsulated in the hHFt shell using this SDS-based procedure.59

Recently, ferritins from the hyperthermophilic bacteria Thermotoga maritima ferritin (TmFtn) and Archaeoglobus fulgidus ferritin (AfFtn) have been shown to exhibit reversible assembly abilities under salt conditions.6 Ceci et al demonstrated that TmFtn can reversibly dissociate into dimers at low ionic strengths and reassemble into native ferritin at high ionic strength.60 Zhang et al reported that TmFtn could form a 24-meric hollow protein nanocage in the presence of Mg2+ or Ca2+.61 Chakraborti et al showed that TmFtn could assemble into nanocages in a salt-induced manner in the presence of both monovalent (NaCl) and divalent (MgCl2, CaCl2) metal ions whereas the cage spontaneously disassembled into dimers in the presence of EDTA.62 Pulsipher et al observed that AfFtn could disassemble into stable dimers in 200 mM NaCl and assemble into a hollow 24-mer assembly in 800 mM NaCl.63 This assembly process can also be initiated by highly charged gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) in solution. They further found that glutamate 65 in AfFtn plays a crucial role in its salt-mediated assembly, and a point mutation (E65R) leads to the complete abolition of AfFtn, which indicates that salt solutions or charged AuNPs can shield unfavorable electrostatic interactions at the AfFtn dimer–dimer interfaces. Kumar et al emphasized the importance of inter-dimeric interface interactions among side chains with possible involvement of divalent metal ions, with E65 also being crucial for TmFtn salt-mediated assembly.6

Assembly Preferences of Ferritins: Heteropolymers

Generally, FtH and FtL tend to assemble into heteropolymers rather than homopolymers when the concentration ratio of the two subunits is adjusted in vitro conditions.64 A study on the kinetics of the assembly of human ferritins was performed using fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) technology by labeling FtH with a fluorescent donor probe and labeling both FtH and FtL with an acceptor probe.65 The results showed that the assembly process was completed within approximately one hour, and that the initial rate of self-assembly of FtH/FtL heteropolymers was faster than that of the FtH/FtH or FtL/FtL homopolymers, which could explain the preferential formation of FtH/FtL heteropolymers over the FtH/FtH or FtL/FtL homopolymers. Recently, Bou-Abdallah et al revealed unique architectural features in heteropolymer ferritin self-assembly, a significant preference for H–H-H or L-L homodimers, and that while dimers are essential building blocks, the overall assembly mechanism proceeds randomly through diverse pathways.66

The heterodimeric FtH/FtL is the first intermediate during self-assembly, and interactions of the subunit dimer can be engineered in a copper-dependent manners.67 Recombinant FtH mutant studies also support the formation of dimers, followed by aggregation around a 3- or 4-fold axis. Subsequently, the heterodimer is assembled into different heteropolymers depending on the ratio of the two subunits.68 The HSF assembly studies further revealed the formation of dimers, tetramers, and other oligomers.69 Thus, ferritin intermediate polymers could be generated from FtH24FtL0 to FtH0 FtL24. Srivastava et al engineered a novel plasmid design capable of generating ferritin heteropolymers with specific H-to-L subunit ratios, allowing the synthesis of a full spectrum of heteropolymer ferritins, from H-rich ferritin to L-rich ferritins, and any combinations between.70 Moreover, the remarkable hyperthermostability of the heteropolymer ferritin over homopolymers has been attributed to an extensive cooperative network of inter- and intra-subunit interactions (ie, hydrophobic, electrostatic, hydrogen bonds, and salt bridges) and secondary structural composition.71

The Pathways and Kinetics of Self-Assembly of Ferritin

The self-assembly of ferritin is influenced by various factors including subunit concentration, pH, ionic strength, heme, and protein mutations, etc.43,72 The pathways and kinetics of self-assembly have been comprehensively studied using horse spleen-ferritin (HSF). Gerl et al investigated the folding and assembly of HSF from the monomeric unfolded state and found that 24-mers formed exclusively from dimer and trimer intermediates, not tetramers.73 Stefanini et al analyzed the sedimentation velocity in assembled HSF solutions and revealed rapid equilibria among dimers, tetramers, and octamers (or dimers, trimers, and hexamers).74 Sudarev et al obtained an octameric ferritin-based protein complex with an SUMO tag (FerSUMO). They found that the resulting 8xFer - SUMO octamers, assembled around the ferritin 4-fold channel, do not progress to 24-meric globules due to topological reasons of rhombic dodecahedron, therefore such oligomers are being fixed in a so-called “deadlock” state on the ferritin self-assembly pathway. Mohanty et al used laser light scattering (LLS) investigated ferritin self-assembly kinetics and regulatory parameters using laser light scattering. At pH≤1.5, the partially formed dimers served as the primary starting materials for the self-assembly reaction. Upon increasing the pH, the assembly exhibited biphasic behavior, with a rapid process (coupled with subunit folding) followed by a slower reassembly step, which was completed within 10 min. The self-assembly was accelerated with a high subunit concentration and ionic strength, and slowed down within the pH range of 5.5 to 7.5. Additionally, the results indicated the possible presence of intermediates comprising both odd numbers (eg, M1 and M3) and an even number of subunits (Figure 5B).78

In 2024, Yousefi et al used optical nanotweezers with double nanohole structures to study the effects of ascorbic acid and pH on the conformational dynamics of individual ferritin molecules. Ferritin dynamics increased with higher ascorbic acid concentrations, approaching saturation. At a pH of 2.0, ferritin exhibited large structural fluctuations and was disassembled stepwise. This study revealed the disassembly pathway and kinetics of a single ferritin molecule in solution, and identified four key fragments (22 - mer, 12 - mer, tetramer, and dimer subunits) during disassembly. It also provided single-molecule evidence for the cooperative disassembly of ferritin (Figure 5C).43

Recently, Gette et al engineered hybrids of H. pylori ferritin subunits (HF) and those fused with a homolog of a Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier protein (HSF) to study the ability of H. pylori ferritin to form hybrids. Through co-expression in E. coli cells and pH-dependent disassembly/reassembly, this study is the first to observe the stochastic nature of self-assembly in bacterial ferritin-based hybrids, revealing a continuous range of stoichiometric distributions. Heterodimers were identified in the hybrids, and certain hexamers exhibited unexpected disfavored stoichiometric preferences during assembly. This preference constrained the structural diversity patterns of the 24-mers and shifted the actual stoichiometric distribution from the random theoretical predictions. These findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying changes in the structural diversity patterns of ferritin hybrid globules (Figure 5D).79

Molecular Mechanism of Ferritin Self-Assembly

The Impact of Gene Expression and Protein Transportation on Ferritin Self-Assembly

Taking Drosophila as example, ferritin FtH and FtL are present in equal numbers and are cross-linked to each other via disulfide bonds to form a highly ordered and symmetric complex composed of 12 FtH and 12 FtH.80 Both subunits contain signaling peptides that guide them to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Moreover, the FtH and FtL genes in Drosophila are equipped with co-enhancers to ensure approximately equal expression of FtH and FtL, the co-regulation mechanism of which is conserved across insects.81 Furthermore, FtH and FtL can be detected at the initial stage of the biosynthesis process and are present in different vesicles for transport and post-translational modifications,82 suggesting that subunit-specific transport and post-translational modifications may precede ferritin complex formation. Once iron is loaded into the lumen of ferritin, transport of the complex from the cytoplasm to the endoplasmic reticulum is regulated by zinc- and iron-regulated transporter 13 (Zip13).83

The Impact of Key Amino Acid Residues on the Self-Assembly of Ferritin

Key amino acid residues may influence the molecular basis of the specific assembly of heterodimers or homodimers. For instance, FtH and FtL of silkworm ferritin can only be assembled into the 12 FtH/12 FtL heterodimer in vitro, rather than the homodimers 24 FtH or 24 FtL.84 Two tryptophan residues at the I/II interface may contribute to the specificity of the heterodimer assembly in insect ferritin. Computer simulations revealed that salt bridges formed between FtH Arg68 and the cation-π interactions from FtL generated among the Tyr53 and Trp84 residues with Asp81 of FtL could only occur in the heterodimeric 12FtH/12FtL structure,85 which might facilitate the preferential formation of heterodimers from a mixture of FtH and FtL. Furthermore, crystal structure analyses showed that interactions, including salt bridges and cationic interactions, are crucial for rapid assembly of the 12FtH/12FtL heterodimer, which then serves as a nucleation site for subsequent oligomerization. FtL Trp84 is located in a “heterogen” binding site (where horse spleen ferritin binds hemes) near the pseudo 2-fold axis related to FtH and FtL, whereas its counterpart in mammalian ferritin is arginine, which plays a vital role in heme binding.85

Modification of the Ferritin

Understanding the kinetics and mechanisms of ferritin nanoscale self-assembly/dissociation is key to developing protein-based drug delivery systems.86 Mutants that affect ferritin folding and self-assembly can alter its conformational state, lattice structure, and oligomeric configuration. Interactions at the dimer-monomer and dimer-dimer interfaces may play crucial roles in ferritin heteropolymer assemblies. Bradley et al demonstrated the importance of hydrogen bond formation at various dimer interfaces for ferritin stability and structural integrity, with the N- and C-termini of each subunit being pivotal in influencing conformational changes in the threefold and fourfold channels.87 Single and multipoint mutations at key interfacial amino acid sites can rearrange subunits to generate new self-assembled forms, modulate self-assembly regulation, and alter physicochemical properties. Types of Genetic Modification for Ferritin

Modification of the Ferritin Interfaces

Modifications of the C3-C4 and C2 interfaces directly affected the self-assembly of ferritin. Zhang et al introduced a new interface strategy called protein engineering-key subunit interface redesign (KSIR), which can alter the natural subunit–subunit interface by selectively deleting a small number of “silent” amino acid residues to trigger the generation of a non-native protein cage. Using the KSIR strategy, the introduction of a small (hexapeptide) deletion into helix D of each subunit located at the C3–C4 interface resulted in the conversion of the native 24-mer ferritin cage into a 48-mer non-native analog with a 17 nm diameter.104,105 Santambrogio et al engineered human ferritin heavy chain (FTH1) variants near the 4-fold (L169R), 3-fold (D131I/E134F), and 2-fold (I85C) axes, demonstrating that residue alterations near these symmetry axes could produce subunit monomers, dimers, and higher-order oligomers, respectively.106 These intermediates can be reassembled into 24-mers via concentration or co-renaturation with wild-type FtH.

Huard et al mutated the amino acid residues located at the C2 interface of ferritin (K86Q+C90E+C102A+C130A+Y39E+N74E+P88A) using a reverse metal-templated interface redesign method, which could render the self-assembly of the cage-like ferritin protein controlled by divalent copper binding at neutral pH.64 Wang et al obtained two new ferritin variants by deleting two and three amino acid residues at the C3–C4 interface, and the two variants were disassembled at pH 3.0, 4.0, and reassembled at pH 7.0.107 Gu et al incorporated a His motif into the C4 interface of FtH, enabling metal-ion- and pH-responsive self-assembly with higher cargo-loading efficiency under milder conditions than acid-denaturation methods.95 To optimize drug release, Ahn et al truncated a non-essential subunit region, creating recombinant ferritin with a notched hydrophobic channel that could preserve cellular uptake, while enabling pH-responsive cargo release.91 Hence, modifications of the C3-C4 and C2 interfaces could not only control the reversible self-assembly ability of ferritin but also manipulate the drug release of ferritin nanocarriers.

Modifications of Ferritin Helices

The B-C loop region also plays an important role in ferritin assembly. Yohizawa et al deleted eight N-terminal residues in horse FtL, yielding a mutant that could only form dimers at pH 2.0.88 Liu et al altered the self-assembled form of ferritin by modifying the D-helix of ferritin, changing the 24-aggregate cage-shaped ferritin to a 16-aggregate ellipsoidal structure or a 48-aggregate spherical shape with a larger particle size.108 In addition, removing residues L82 and I85 in the B-C loop of human FtH could lead to disassembly of the protein cage by disrupting dimer intermediate.41

E-helix modifications may also have different effects on ferritin assembly across species. Fan et al showed that removal of the E-helix from E. coli ferritin resulted in an unstable protein that could only assemble into dimers.89 Levi et al found that the deletion of 22 C-terminal amino acid residues, including the E-helix in human FtH, did not impair its iron oxidation activity.109 Chen et al found that deleting the last 23 amino acids involved in the formation of the DE-turn and E-helix of ferritin had little effect on shell structure, thermal stability, and iron oxidative deposition, yielding a variant that could dissociate at pH 4.0 (vs. pH 2.0, for wild-type) and reassemble at neutral pH.92 Wang et al obtained a variant without new pores by engineering the AB loop, which could dissociate at pH 3.0–4.0 and reassemble at pH 7.0.110 Yang et al showed that deleting the EP domains of red bean (adzuki) ferritin could lead to dissociation at pH 4.0 and reversible reassembling of an intact cage protein when the pH was increased to 6.7.93

Point Mutation

Point mutations can also affect the physicochemical functions and assembly of ferritin. Bernacchioni et al found that the mutation His54→Ala in bullfrog FtM could increase the reaction rates between ferritin and ferrous ion.98 Kumar et al showed that mutating residue E65 to a positively charged amino acid in TmFtn leads to TmFtn assembly via salt bridges in the absence of metal ions, forming a more stable complex.6 Li et al demonstrated that mutation of negatively charged Asp120 to uncharged Gly could reduce the interaction energy and improve the thermal stability of oyster ferritin.94 Sana et al revealed that replacing Lys150 and/or Arg151 residues with Ala in AfFtn resulted in a symmetry change from tetrahedral to octahedral, thus enhancing the hydrophobic interactions of subunits at the 4-fold interface and improving the stability of protein cages at different salt concentrations.97 Pulsipher et al designed three AfFtn mutants (E65R, D138K, and A127R) by introducing a single positive charge at sites along the dimer-dimer interface.63 The E65R mutant showed enhanced cage formation, as well as thermal stability and self-assembly in octahedral symmetry rather than tetrahedral symmetry, while A127R showed a slower assembly speed of nanocages compared to the wild-type, but D138K assembled faster than the wild-type.

Modification of Ferritin Amino Acid Sequence

Modifications of the ferritin amino acid sequence, such as insertion, replacement, or linking exogenous sequences, are also effective alternative strategies to modulate the assembly and function of ferritin. Jin et al found that fusing the human ferritin H chain with a blood circulation-prolonging (BCP) peptide could significantly improve the pharmacokinetics of doxorubicin (DOX), enhancing its tumor cell uptake and anticancer efficacy.100 Huang et al modified the N-terminus of FtH with different amounts of lysine to obtain a poly L-lysine-modified FtH nanocage and found that siRNA carried by genetically modified ferritin exhibited stronger interference and anti-tumor effects than that carried by unmodified ferritin.101 Li et al linked the tumor-homing peptide tLyP-1 with the human FtH N-terminal, obtaining a dual-targeting nanoparticle delivery system that could specifically deliver paclitaxel (PTX) into tumor cells with better efficacy than natural ferritin.102 Wang et al generated amphiphilic ferritin by replacing the fifth helix of human FtH with a hydrophobic-hydrophilic-RGD motif, and found that drug-loaded ferritin nanoparticles could reduce side effects and significantly enhance synergistic efficacy against brain tumors, metastatic liver cancers, and drug-resistant breast tumor.103 Yang et al added a Pro-Ala-Ser linker to FtH N-termini at the human FtH N-terminus, enhancing encapsulation efficiency, stability, and drug half-life.96

Additionally, modifying the outer surface of ferritin with active compounds can enhance its functionalization and retain its inner surface to retain bioactive molecules. Meng et al covalently spliced hesperidin onto the outer surface of ferritin to fabricate hesperetin modified ferritin.111 They found that the modified ferritin successfully retained its cage-like structure and exhibited reversible pH-dependent self-assembly properties. Moreover, modification with hesperetin improved the digestive stability of ferritin and enhanced the stability of the encapsulated quercetin against thermal treatment. Zang et al incorporated gold nanoclusters into the iron oxide enzyme center of human mitochondrial ferritin subunits, which not only encapsulated drugs but also did not affect iron mineralization and endowed ferritin with fluorescence function.99 Yang et al prepared a red bean seed ferritin (RBF)-epigallocatechin (EGC)-chitosan nanoparticle (REC), in which the binding of chitosan protected the ferritin cage and encapsulated nutrients.112 Liu et al glycosylated phytoferritin with chitosan to fabricate ferritin-chitosan Maillard reaction products (FCMPs), which were more resistant to pepsin and trypsin digestion than unmodified ferritin. Furthermore, FCMPs encapsulating epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) molecules (FCMPs-EGCG) showed slow EGCG release in simulated gastrointestinal environments.113 Huang et al designed PEGylated FtH conjugated to DOX via acid-sensitive linkers to achieve high drug loading and pH-triggered release in acidic vesicles.114

Ferritin Nanocarriers for Medical and Bioengineering Applications

As mentioned above, ferritin can dissociate into individual subunits under extreme acidic/alkaline conditions (pH ≤ 2.0, or pH ≥ 12.0), while the natural secondary and tertiary structures of a single subunit are well maintained.115 When the pH returned to neutral, the subunits reassembled into a 24-aggregate spherical structure (Figure 6A). Additionally, ferritins from certain species exhibit salt-sensitive assembly characteristics. For example, they exist as dimers under low-salt conditions, and reassembly as cage-like structures in high-salt environments.6 These properties offer an efficient strategy for encapsulating molecules and drugs that cannot permeate the protein shell pores, enabling their entrapment within the internal cavity.116–118 As a nanoscale protein-based drug carrier in pharmaceutics, ferritin has distinct advantages:49,119–1221) small particle size and excellent dispersibility make it easy to target for modification; 2) loading of small-molecule drugs into the ferritin cavity can be readily controlled via pH- or salt ion-induced depolymerization-reassembly processes; 3) high stability and biocompatibility minimize the induction of non-autoantibodies and T-cell immune responses; and 4) abundant surface functional groups enable straightforward genetic and chemical modifications. Application of Ferritin in Medicine and Bioengineering

Ferritin Nanocarriers in Drug Delivery

By harnessing its self-assembly and targeting capabilities, ferritin shows promising potential for drug delivery applications.198–200 Du et al developed an ROS/pH dual-responsive intelligent controlled-release drug delivery system using an apoferritin cage to deliver doxorubicin (DOX), a drug targeting receptor SCARA 5 for breast cancer treatment, which could specifically release DOX in response to the acidic microenvironment of tumor cells.155 Macone et al utilized humanized Archaeoglobus ferritin to encapsulate, transport, and release cytochrome c, inducing apoptosis in an acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell line.124 Falvo et al used genetically modified FTH1 to embed the anti-cancer drug mitoxantrone, which can effectively kill different types of cancer cells such as colon, breast, sarcoma, and pancreatic cancer cells, etc.126 Compared to free drug therapy, ferritin-embedded mitoxantrone treatment resulted in increased accumulation of mitoxantrone in tumor cells in vivo. Shuvaev et al employed thermophilic ferritin to deliver superoxide dismutase (SOD) into endothelial cells, where SOD efficiently mediates superoxide internalization via pro-inflammatory signaling pathways activated by cytokines or LPS.127 Chen et al constructed pH-responsive, cartilage-targeting ferritin nanocages to carry hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) for osteoarthritis therapy, demonstrating that ferritin as a carrier could not only control HCQ release under acidic conditions to target cartilage but also extend the drug retention time to 14 days.133 Ren et al engineered a bifunctional ferritin nanocage (RC-Fn) displaying RGD (targeting BMSCs) and WYRGRL (binding collagen II). Li et al coated horse-spleen apoferritin (HSF) with sodium alginate to transport the peptide Ala-His-Leu-Leu. The dual shell markedly shielded the cargo from UV and thermal stress.149,205 Collectively, these studies illustrate that the programmable assembly, receptor-mediated targeting, and stimulus-responsive release of ferritin can be exploited to enhance drug accumulation, retention, and therapeutic efficacy in diverse pathologies.

Overexpression of transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) in numerous human solid tumors has spurred interest in TfR-1-targeted nanomedicine for cancer therapy. Studies have identified specific binding of ferritin to TfR1 in humans.206 The inherent tumor-targeting properties of ferritin-based nanomedicines have garnered widespread attention in the field of targeted cancer therapy.27 By exploiting this interaction, Rensi et al developed an HFn nanocage loaded with DOX, showing that HFn-DOX nanoparticles could specifically bind to TfR1 and deliver the drug to tumor cells, resulting in decreased cell proliferation and increased tumor cell apoptosis.207 Jiang et al developed a recombinant human H-ferritin-based drug-loading platform for DOX encapsulation that demonstrated excellent biosafety and anti-tumor activity.125 Liu et al engineered endogenous FTH1 nanocages as paclitaxel carriers, enabling blood-brain barrier crossing and enhancing glioma cell accumulation via specific TfR1 binding, a receptor overexpressed on both the blood-brain barrier and glioma cells.128 Liu et al developed a ferritin assembly toolbox system (FATS), that enables human ferritin heavy chains (FH) and light chains (FL) to self-assemble into ferritin hybrid nanocages (FHn) in E. coli. It was shown that FHn carrying DOX exhibited strong tumor penetration and mainly accumulated in tumor tissue due to a balance between TfR-1 binding and prolonged blood circulation from FL subunits, thus revealing superior anti-tumor efficacy in high-permeability tumors.208

Recombinant HFn nanocages also enable efficient loading of indocyanine green (ICG) for tumor-targeted photodynamic therapy (PDT) via TfR1-mediated internalization. Notably, oxidative stress during PDT upregulates endogenous ferritin expression, which can be exploited as a “Trojan horse” mechanism to further enhance HFn-ICG uptake and improve therapeutic efficacy.209 Sitia et al loaded ICG into H-ferritin (HFn) nanocages to overcome the lack of tumor specificity of free ICG in fluorescence image-guided surgery. HFn-ICG exhibited specific cancer cell uptake and more efficient tumor delivery than free ICG in a triple-negative breast cancer model, supporting its application in intraoperative cancer detection (Figure 7B).202 Recently, a DHA-loaded manganese-mineralized ferritin nanoplatform (DHA@MFn) was constructed for targeted ovarian cancer therapy, featuring tumor microenvironment-responsive release, ferroptosis induction via Fenton-like reactions and GSH depletion, synergistic efficacy with radiotherapy, and reduced off-target accumulation.210 Beyond small-molecule drugs, ferritin nanocages have also been exploited for the delivery of gene-editing tools. Pan et al used tetralysine-modified H-chain apoferritin (TL-HFn) to individually encapsulate CRISPR/Cas9 (one per nanocage). TL-HFn enabled TfR1-mediated delivery, endosomal escape, and nuclear gene editing (eg, EGFP knockout in HeLa cells) with higher efficiency (up to 28.96%) than free CRISPR/Cas9 both in vitro and in vivo (Figure 7C).131

These studies underscore the importance of nanoparticle heterogeneity in regulating biological fate and provides a robust platform for designing protein-based nanocarriers with precise control over functional components for targeted cancer therapy.211–213

Ferritin Nanocarriers in Medical Imaging

The biomineralization capacity and readily modifiable surface properties of ferritin internal cavities make it an ideal molecular carrier for biological imaging, with widespread applications in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and photoacoustic imaging (PAI). For instance, Nasrollahi et al encapsulated graphene quantum dots (GQDs) and iron into the core of an Archaea flavescens ferritin nanocage to develop a multifunctional nanoplatform for multimodal imaging and cancer treatment, which exhibited high relaxivity in MRI and pH-sensitive fluorescence.153 Song et al embedded iron-oxide magnetic nanoparticles into the inner cavity of human ferritin nanocages and conjugated I200 radionuclides to the surface of nanocages.166 Following tumor-specific internalization via the ferritin-TfR1 pathway, nuclear medicine/MR multimode imaging of cancer cells can be easily achieved with a single-dose administration, highlighting the efficiency of ferritin-mediated targeted delivery. Sanchez et al embedded water-soluble gadolinium oxide into the ferritin cavity, which could avoid the toxicity caused by direct metal transfer, and had higher relaxivities compared to the clinically approved paramagnetic Gd-chelates, underscoring that ferritin embedded with water-soluble gadolinium oxide has the potential to be used as an MRI contrast agent.152

In addition to its application in MRI, ferritin has promising prospects in PAI. Wang et al successfully synthesized ultrasmall copper sulfide (CuS) nanoparticles with strong near-infrared absorbance and high photothermal conversion efficiency within ferritin nanocages.151 Radiolabeling with64 Cu revealed that CuS-loaded ferritin nanoparticles preferentially accumulated in large amounts at tumor sites compared with free copper, positioning them as superior positron emission tomography (PET) imaging agents. Huang et al loaded the photosensitizer sinoporphyrin sodium (DVDMS) into RGD-modified ferritin nanocages for imaging-guided photodynamic and photothermal therapy. Compared with naked DVDMS, the ferritin nanocarrier significantly enhanced tumor treatment efficacy.150 Gu et al conjugated dicarboxylic cypate to apoferritin to construct a triple-near-infrared nanoprobe enabling photoacoustic and fluorescence imaging as well as photothermal ablation of tumors. This nanoprobe provided real-time structural and quantitative metabolic information and guided efficient tumor photothermal therapy (Figure 7D).161 Men et al constructed a size-controlled enzyme nanocomposite (ENC) by self-assembling streptavidin-labelled horseradish peroxidase (SA-HRP) into autobiotinylated ferritin nanoparticles. In cardiac troponin immunoassays, these ENCs improved the detection sensitivity by up to 10,000-fold compared with conventional ELISA, demonstrating the utility of ferritin in ultrasensitive biosensing.154 He et al developed apoferritin-based tunable nano-indicators by co-loading pH-sensitive and pH-insensitive dye pairs into ferritin cavities (Figure 7E).156 This design minimizes cytotoxicity relative to free dyes and reduces environmental interference with signal readout, thus enhancing diagnostic reliability. Sun et al designed near-infrared fluorescent ferritin hybrid nanocomposites by embedding multiple gold (Au) clusters into nanocages, yielding a strong tissue-penetrating ability, high fluorescence efficiency, and good kidney targeting specificity.137 These examples collectively demonstrate the versatility of ferritin as a nanoplatform for advanced medical imaging that integrates diagnostic precision and therapeutic functionality.

Ferritin Nanocarriers in Food Applications

Small bioactive molecules in food, such as β-carotenoids,214,215 curcumin,216,217 rutin,218,219 anthocyanins,220,221 astaxanthin,222,223 and lycopene,224,225 exhibit biological activities such as antioxidant, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory properties.226,227

Li et al developed a novel class of soluble and edible calpain complexes by encapsulating approximately 140 calcium ions within a plant ferritin nanocage, thus providing a unique strategy for replenishing intracellular calcium ion.170 Chen et al reported that encapsulating β-carotene in ferritin nanocages could significantly improve the water solubility and thermal stability.171 Chakraborti et al used salt-mediated self-assembly to encapsulate lysozyme in TmFtn (ferritin from Thermus maltophilus), and demonstrated that the encapsulated enzyme retained high activity at 80 °C, whereas free lysozyme activity significantly decreased under the same condition.62 Sun et al embedded baicalein into chitosan-modified alfalfa ferritin at low urea concentrations, which significantly improved baicalein bioavailability.173 Chen et al embedded curcumin and rutin separately into chitooligosaccharide/ferritin (COS/Fn) particles in a compartmentalized manner, improving their thermal stability and enabling precise control over the curcumin-rutin ratio.172 Zhu et al encapsulated calcium ions in the ferritin casein phosphopeptide shell-core complex, which not only improved the encapsulation efficiency of calcium ions through casein phosphopeptide, but also increased the bioavailability of calcium ions.175

Meng et al embedded epigallocatechin gallate and hydrophobic quercetin into the apoferritin cavities of red bean seeds to prepare four nanocomplexes using reversible assembly and urea-induced methods.228 It was found that the nanocomplexes with the highest encapsulation rate could be prepared using the pH 2.0/6.7 transition scheme, and the stability of quercetin in all four nanocomplexes was significantly improved. Chen et al encapsulated epigallocatechin gallate in phytferritin, whose outer surface was attached to salvianolic acid B through a weak bond, which could significantly improve the thermal stability of epigallocatechin gallate.229 Zhang et al embedded proanthocyanidin dimers into horse spleen ferritin, which improved the bioavailability and stability of proanthocyanidins (Figure 7F). Recently, Herrera-Balandrano et al extended the same platform to blueberry anthocyanins, demonstrating not only improved chemical stability, but also controlled gastrointestinal release.176 Collectively, these studies established ferritin nanocages as versatile and reproducible vehicles for stabilizing and delivering diverse arrays of small bioactive molecules.

Additionally, Zhang et al connected trans-astaxanthin (AX) to the outer surface of shrimp ferritin (M. japonicus ferritin, MjF), which markedly improved AX’s water solubility, thermal stability, and photostability of AX and protected AX from oxidative damage induced by Fe2+.230 Xiao et al used highly stable marine-derived ferritin nanocages, named GF1, to encapsulate lycopene (LYC), a carotenoid with potent singlet oxygen quenching activity that is abundant in ripe red fruit.231 They found that the water solubility, thermal stability, and antioxidant capacity of LYC encapsulated in GF1-LYC nanoparticles were notably improved, suggesting that the shell-like marine ferritin nanoplatform could enhance the stable delivery of LYC and promote its use in food nutrition and other industries.

Taken together, utilizing the reversible dissociation and assembly properties of ferritin to encapsulate water-soluble or lipophilic active nutrients is not only easy to manipulate and environmentally friendly but also significantly superior to their free states in terms of water solubility, photothermal stability, and cell uptake activity of encapsulated small nutrient molecules.232–234

Ferritin Nanocarriers in Vaccine Development

Displaying antigens on the ferritin surface offers distinct advantages, including uniform presentation of 24 epitopes, nanocage monodispersity, and stability (to temperature and pH), which can enhance vaccine-induced immune response and improve efficacy with low heterogeneity. As highly organized supramolecular particles, ferritin nanocages elicit stronger immunomodulatory effects than soluble peptides do. Owing to their small size (10–12 nm), antigens on ferritin nanocages are readily absorbed by dendritic cells (DCs), which migrate to the lymph node to drive the initiation of adaptive immune responses.

For instance, Li et al displayed the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein receptor-binding domain (RBD) on self-assembled ferritin nanoparticles to prepare a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, which could induce specific humoral immunity and T cell immune responses after immunization of rhesus monkeys three times and produce neutralizing antibodies that can cross-neutralize four different SARS-CoV-2 strains from different sources (Figure 7G).178 Joyce et al utilized a structure-based design strategy to generate four S-domain ferritin nanoparticles, named S-trimer-ferritin nanoparticles (SpFN), RBD-ferritin nanoparticles (RFN), S1-ferritin nanoparticles, and RBD-NTD-ferritin nanoparticles, and found that immunization with these four types of nanoparticles not only increased the SARS-CoV-2 neutralization titer, but also expanded the neutralization breadth against the heterologous SARS-CoV-1 virus.235 Recently, Wang et al developed a self-assembling ferritin-based nanoparticle vaccine targeting the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 omicron variant, which promoted early DC recruitment/activation and robust germinal center and antigen-specific cellular immune responses compared to RBD monomers.193

In addition to targeting SARS-CoV viruses, Kanekiyo et al utilized genetic engineering techniques to insert influenza virus hemagglutinin into the adjacent subunit interface of Helicobacter pylori non-heme ferritin, generating fused ferritin that could self-assemble and display eight trimeric virus spikes on its surface.177 Immunized with this influenza nanoparticle vaccine, the titer of antibodies produced against hemagglutinin was 10 times higher than that of licensed inactivated viral vaccines. In addition, it can induce the host to express neutralizing antibodies targeting two highly conserved vulnerable hemagglutinin structures. Yan et al engineered Helicobacter pylori ferritin nanoparticles to display a truncated hepatitis C virus (HCV) E2 envelope protein, which exhibited superior immunogenicity and higher neutralizing antibody titers in mice than unfused soluble E2.179

Kamp et al developed a vaccine by fusing Borrelia outer surface protein A (OspA) and bacterial ferritin to form OspA-ferritin self-assembling nanoparticles, which were composed of six types of nanoparticles targeting the Borrelia serotypes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7.180 After immunization, the nanoparticles induced durable, high-titer antibody responses against major serotypes in mice and non-human primates (NHPs), with titers surpassing those of previously licensed vaccines. Moreover, inoculation with adjuvant OspA-ferritin nanoparticles enhanced the protective antibody immunity against two other types of bacteria. Kanekiyo et al prepared self-assembled ferritin nanoparticles displaying different domains of Epstein Barr virus (EBV) glycoprotein gp350, which could effectively induce gp350 neutralizing antibodies in mouse and NHP models with 10–100 fold higher potency than that induced by soluble gp350.181 Qu et al compared human H‑ferritin (HFn) and hepatitis B virus core (HBc) nanoparticles displaying the same Epstein‑Barr nuclear antigen 1 (EBNA1) epitope. With alum adjuvant, HBc induced ~5‑fold higher antibody titers than HFn, whereas HFn elicited stronger cell‑mediated immunity (Figure 7H).204 Swanson et al fused respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) pre-fusion protein with ferritin nanoparticles (Pre-F-NP) and modified them with glycans to mask non-neutralizing or poorly neutralizing epitopes to prepare a multimeric Pre-F-NP that induced persistent Pre-F-specific antibodies in NHPs.182 Pre-F-NP stimulation induced more potent antibody responses than the representative pre-F-trimer, DS-Cav1.

Li et al developed an inactive, self-assembled recombinant rotavirus nanovaccine, rVP6-ferritin, which can be expressed in E. coli and self-assembled into a homogeneous spherical structure.183 Nanovaccine rVP6-ferritin effectively induced humoral and mucosal immunogenicity in mice after oral administration. In addition, these nanoparticles were transgenically expressed in mouse milk, and newborn mice consuming rVP6-ferritin-expressing milk showed strong immunogenicity, reducing rotavirus-induced diarrhea severity, duration, and growth impact compared to wild-type newborn mice. Wang et al prepared a self-assembling fusion ferritin vaccine by linking hemagglutinin (HA) of canine distemper virus (CDV) to the N-terminus of ferritin, which could induce high-titer, long-lasting antibodies that recognize target cell surface antigens and activate NK cell-mediated killing of infected cells, following subcutaneous or intramuscular injection.236 Qiao et al constructed a biepitope ferritin nanovaccine (3MCD‑f) displaying two conserved influenza epitopes (M2e and CDhelix) in tandem. Without adjuvant, it induced robust humoral and cellular immunity, providing complete protection against H3N2 and partial protection against H1N1 in mice (Figure 7I).188 Veggi et al used ferritin from H. pylori to display Neisseria meningitidis trimeric adhesin NadA.192 Two fragments of NadA were individually fused with ferritin, and each fusion polypeptide was self-assembled into nanoparticles. The results showed that the two nanoparticles elicited comparable NadA antibody levels that were 10–100 fold higher than those elicited by the corresponding NadA trimer subunits, and potently induced complement-mediated serum bactericidal activity. Recently, Fu et al developed a universal ferritin-adaptor delivery platform based on structural insights into the natural ferritinophagy complex comprising FTH1 and NCOA4. A self-assembling rabies virus (RABV) vaccine candidate was constructed by noncovalently conjugating engineered ferritinophagy (Fagy-tagged RABV GDIII to ferritin nanoparticles) with superior homogeneity, stability, and immunogenicity. This candidate induced potent, rapid, and durable immune responses, and protected female mice from authentic RABV challenge after a single dose.194

Cystic echinococcosis (CE) is a severe parasitic zoonosis, while the conventional Quil-A-adjuvanted Eg95 vaccine is limited by high cost, side effects, and inadequate immunity. A ferritin-based Eg95 nanoparticle vaccine combined with CpG ODN induces potent, long-lasting humoral and Th1-dominant cellular immune responses with good safety and cost-effectiveness, showing great potential as an alternative for CE control.195

Ferritin self‑assembly was also used to construct a recombinant nano‑allergen rFtn‑Blo t 2 for dust mite allergy, which exhibited favorable IgE immunoreactivity, regulated epithelial cytokine secretion, and alleviated airway inflammation in mice by shifting toward a protective Th1‑dominant immune response, offering a new nanoplatform for allergy vaccine development.196 Furthermore, a ferritin‑based fenobody strategy was established against SARS‑CoV‑2 by fusing the neutralizing nanobody H11‑D4 to ferritin; this fenobody assembled efficiently in the silkworm baculovirus system and displayed significantly enhanced neutralizing activity, demonstrating that ferritin self‑assembly can effectively improve the antiviral potency of nanobodies.197

Taken together, its remarkable antigen presentation capacity, coupled with its intrinsic stability conferred by its robust nanoscale cage architecture, suggest that ferritin is a highly versatile and efficient platform for vaccine development. Recent advances in nanomedicine have further expanded the potential of nanomaterials in cancer immunotherapy.237–239

Overview of Nanocarrier Platforms for Drug Delivery and Vaccine Applications

Liposomes, as the first FDA-approved nanomedicines in the 1990s, represent the most clinically mature platform, benefiting from well-established good manufacturing practice (GMP) production protocols and versatile encapsulation of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic cargoes. However, liposomes face unresolved challenges including premature drug leakage, manufacturing irreproducibility, and limited precision targeting.246

Polymeric nanoparticles are particles ranging from 10 to 1000 nm in diameter, which can be loaded with active compounds either entrapped within or adsorbed onto the surface of the polymeric core. As drug carriers, polymeric nanoparticles offer several advantages, including the potential for controlled release, the ability to protect drugs and other biologically active molecules from environmental degradation, and improved bioavailability and therapeutic index. However, they often suffer from batch‑to‑batch variability, abnormal hepatic accumulation, and potential long‑term toxicity concerns associated with non‑degradable polymers.241,242

VLPs are effective vaccine candidates due to their structural resemblance to native viruses and repetitive antigen display, which induce both humoral and cellular immunity. Moreover, VLPs can traffic from the injection site to regional lymph nodes to drive potent vaccine-specific immune responses.247 However, VLPs pose safety concerns regarding residual host cell DNA and protein impurities. Furthermore, enveloped VLPs (eVLPs) suffer from environmental instability and low secretory expression. When produced in the baculovirus expression vector system (BEVS), eVLPs also face the additional challenge of co-purification with baculovirus particles, which hinders downstream processing and raises safety concerns.243,244.

Encapsulins, bacterial protein nanocompartments emerging as ferritin analogs, offer a tenfold higher iron-loading capacity than ferritin.245 Encapsulin shell protomer proteins self-assemble to form icosahedral structures of various diameters (24–42 nm). These nanocompartments possess several advantageous properties: small size, genetic tractability, stability, biocompatibility, and the ability to encapsulate a broad range of cargo molecules. In addition, their surfaces can be functionalized for selective interaction with specific proteins. These features enable the creation of targeted drug/label delivery systems and provide outstanding opportunities for practical biotechnology.245,248 However, encapsulins face several key bottlenecks: narrow shell pores restrict the passage of large molecules; cargo loading is limited by steric hindrance; stable expression in mammalian cells remains challenging; and their native secretion mechanism and physiological role are still unclear. Moreover, all current applications are at the preclinical stage, with insufficient in vivo data regarding stability, biodistribution, and immunogenicity.249

Challenges and Considerations for Clinical Translation

The promising applications of ferritin nanocages in drug delivery, imaging, and vaccines must be balanced against the practical hurdles of clinical translation. Two critical aspects require prioritization: immunogenicity and biosafety, as well as scalability and manufacturability.

Immunogenicity and Biosafety

Despite the generally favorable biocompatibility of ferritin, its immunogenicity remains an important consideration for clinical translation. Ferritin from non-human sources may elicit anti-ferritin antibodies upon repeated administration, potentially leading to accelerated clearance or hypersensitivity reactions. Human ferritin is considered less immunogenic, but even self-proteins can break immune tolerance when assembled into highly ordered, multivalent nanoparticles.

Macone et al systematically discussed the physiological constraints of ferritin-based drug delivery and emphasized the need to evaluate in vivo immune responses, noting that many studies overlook the immunogenicity of exogenous ferritin, while its natural accumulation in the liver, spleen, and kidneys may lead to off-target effects.250 Sitia et al evaluated H-ferritin nanodrugs and found that residual mild immunoreactivity persisted even after removing all pyrogenic contaminants, underscoring the importance of comprehensive immunogenicity testing.251 Surface modifications such as PEGylation or grafting with endogenous peptides can shield immunogenic epitopes and reduce recognition by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), but such alterations may compromise the nanocage’s targeting efficiency or cellular uptake.252–254

Beyond immunogenicity, in vivo metabolic pathways, half-life, and clearance mechanisms are essential for assessing the safety and therapeutic potential of ferritin nanoparticles. After systemic administration, unmodified ferritin is rapidly cleared from the bloodstream (within minutes) and accumulates mainly in the liver, spleen, and kidneys, driven by RES recognition and high CD71 (TfR1) expression in these organs.243 Although initial blood clearance is fast, animal studies indicate that the ferritin protein shell can persist in tissues for several tens of hours. Ferritin nanoparticles are primarily eliminated from circulation via the RES, especially through uptake by liver and spleen macrophages. A full understanding of these clearance pathways is vital for optimizing nanoparticle design and tissue distribution, since particle size, surface charge, and surface chemistry strongly affect clearance kinetics and biodistribution.250

To address rapid clearance, diverse surface engineering strategies have been developed to extend circulation half-life. Lee et al used intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) as a stealth layer, achieving a 10‑fold half-life increase over wild-type ferritin.255 Yin et al demonstrated that PASylation (fusion with proline–serine–alanine repeats) prolonged ferritin half-life by approximately 4.9‑fold and greatly improved plasma stability.256

Nevertheless, these half-life–extending modifications may introduce new safety risks that require careful evaluation. Prolonged circulation raises the chance of off-target accumulation in the liver and spleen, potentially causing iron deposition and unknown long-term effects.250 Furthermore, in vivo degradation of ferritin nanocages releases iron, which could theoretically contribute to iron overload or oxidative stress if unregulated, especially with repeated dosing.257 PEGylation extends systemic circulation and reduces immunogenicity but may interfere with CD71 recognition and tumor targeting.258

To date, comprehensive pharmacokinetic and toxicological studies on ferritin nanocages—especially those with surface modifications—remain limited, and long-term safety data are scarce. Macone et al highlighted that many studies fail to fully evaluate the in vivo pharmacokinetic properties of the ferritin shell, neglecting key factors including biodistribution, clearance, cellular trafficking, and immune responses.250 Therefore, future research should include systematic assessments of biodistribution, clearance kinetics, iron metabolism, and chronic toxicity to establish a reliable safety profile for the clinical translation of ferritin-based platforms.

Scalability and Manufacturability

The suitability of ferritin as a nanocarrier has created substantial demand for the human protein in pure form. However, extracting ferritin from animal sources raises ethical and safety concerns, and also poses risks of cross‑species receptor recognition that may hinder clinical translation. Therefore, recombinant production has become the primary route for obtaining ferritin.

Current recombinant human ferritin prices are estimated at approximately €3.5 million per gram,259 largely due to limited production capacity and the inability to meet demand, making it generally unaffordable for widespread use as a nanocarrier. While laboratory-scale production of recombinant ferritin in E. coli is straightforward, large-scale GMP production faces several practical hurdles. These include: (1) relatively low expression yields compared to simpler biologics; (2) high purification costs, often requiring multiple chromatography steps to achieve >95% purity; (3) safety concerns due to endotoxin contamination from Gram‑negative bacterial hosts. Although yields of up to 129 mg/L have been reported for human H‑ferritin in E. coli, these achievements do not fully address the cost and scalability barriers that limit widespread clinical application.260

Mammalian cell systems have also proven effective for producing complex ferritin-based vaccines. For instance, a spike ferritin nanoparticle (SpFN) vaccine against SARS-CoV-2 was produced using transient transfection of Expi293F cells, yielding approximately 10 mg of purified protein per liter of culture supernatant. This GMP-grade material exhibited favorable stability, remaining intact after two years of storage at −20 °C, one month at room temperature, and multiple freeze‑thaw cycles.261

Given the persistent cost and scalability limitations of bacterial and mammalian systems, alternative production platforms are being actively explored. Plant-based expression systems offer a promising alternative with substantial cost advantages. Recent advances in upstream production have achieved yields exceeding 3 g per kilogram of plant material, while downstream processing can be simplified using scalable size-based separation methods such as ultrafiltration/diafiltration.262 In a specific study using transient expression in Nicotiana benthamiana, Knödler et al achieved ferritin yields of ~40 mg/kg fresh biomass and >90% purity through pH/heat treatment combined with ultrafiltration, at an estimated cost of only 3% of the current sales price.259

Taken together, these advances demonstrate that scalable production of ferritin‑based products is achievable across multiple platforms, each with distinct advantages: bacterial systems offer relatively high yields and low upstream costs but require rigorous endotoxin control; mammalian systems enable complex post‑translational modifications essential for certain vaccines; and plant systems provide a cost‑effective alternative with significant economic advantages.

Summary and Perspective

Ferritin is present in almost all living organisms and exhibits high stability, excellent biocompatibility, and unique self-assembly capabilities. The in vitro synthesis and modification of ferritin are straightforward, which is conducive to the loading of multiple active substances and targeted therapy. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the kinetic processes and molecular regulatory mechanisms governing the self-assembly and disassembly of ferritin nanoparticles is critical for developing mature ferritin-based drug delivery systems. From the perspective of kinetic processes and molecular regulatory mechanisms, several fundamental questions warrant further investigation.

First, although different species of ferritin have low amino acid sequence homology, they can self-assemble into 24 polymers, suggesting that ferritin assembly is structurally conserved, rather than sequence-dependent. Therefore, analyzing the differences in ferritin protein sequences is essential for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying protein self-assembly.

Secondly, FtH and FtL of human cytoplasmic ferritin can self-assemble into 24 homologous and heterologous aggregates of 12FtH: 12FtL, whereas in silkworms, FtH and FtL can only self-assemble into heterologous 24 aggregates of 12FtH: 12FtL rather than homologous 24 aggregates. Investigating these differences can enrich our understanding of the protein homology/heterologous self-assembly processes.

Thirdly, the physicochemical properties of ferritin self-assembly kinetics in the cytoplasm and mitochondria differ. Further investigation into these disparities will aid in designing ferritin nanocarriers with tailored functional properties.

Fourth, leveraging genetic engineering to enhance the encapsulation efficiency, loading capacity, and controlled-release capabilities of ferritin-based nanomaterials could increase the number of drug reaction sites, while preventing nanoparticle aggregation and degradation.

Fifth, exploring the molecular mechanism of ferritin assembly may contribute to targeted modification via genetic engineering to alter the conditions required for self-assembly, such as depolymerization pH and salt ion concentration, and to generate novel oligomeric forms, octamers, 12-mers, 48-mers, etc, thereby tailoring the physicochemical properties of ferritin.

Lastly, surface modification of ferritin to improve water solubility and cellular uptake efficiency may reduce gastrointestinal degradation, thereby enhancing the bioavailability of encapsulated molecules.

Beyond these mechanistic considerations, several technical challenges also need to be addressed in the development of ferritin-based platforms. As a non-viral vector, ferritin has shown great potential for applications in disease diagnosis and therapy, vaccine development, and drug delivery. However, key challenges remains, including 1) size limitations for target proteins or peptides loaded by different ferritin subunits, 2) stability of genetically modified ferritin nanoparticles, 3) understanding the metabolic pathways and potential side effects of exogenous ferritin nanocages and their derivatives in vivo, 4) elucidating the kinetic mechanism of drug release loaded with ferritin, and 5) optimizing the encapsulation efficiency and drug loading capacity of ferritin nanocages for diverse bioactive substances.

From the perspective of clinical translation and regulation, several translational challenges remain to be addressed. Immunogenicity remains a key concern, particularly for ferritin derived from non‑human sources or for applications requiring repeated administration. Comprehensive preclinical assessment, including anti‑ferritin antibody titers, complement activation, and cytokine profiling, is essential for establishing safety profiles. Additionally, large‑scale manufacturing under GMP conditions poses challenges related to expression yields, purification costs, batch‑to‑batch consistency, and long‑term stability. Standardized characterization protocols and clear regulatory guidelines will be critical to facilitate the clinical translation of ferritin‑based platforms.

With continued research addressing these scientific and translational challenges, ferritin nanoparticles hold great promise for broad applications in disease diagnosis and therapy, vaccine development, and drug delivery.
