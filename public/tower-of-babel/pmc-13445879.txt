Factors associated with malnutrition among under five children in Bangladesh: a multivariate analysis
Authors: Md Tahidur Rahman, Md Lotifur Rohman, Md Jahangir Alam, Dulal Chandra Roy, Papia Sultana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChild malnutrition remains a major global public health concern, with a disproportionately higher burden in developing countries such as Bangladesh. It is a leading contributor to childhood morbidity and mortality and has long-term consequences for physical growth, cognitive development, educational attainment, and adult productivity. Multiple socioeconomic and demographic factors are responsible for this condition. Therefore, this study aims to identify the factors associated with child malnutrition in Bangladesh using multivariate analytical approaches.MethodsIn this study, malnutrition-related data from the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) 2022 were used. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) and multivariate linear regression models were performed to identify significant factors of child malnutrition in Bangladesh (p\:<0.05).ResultsMultivariate linear regression analysis showed that the Z-scores for height-for-age (HAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-height (WAH) among children aged 12–23 months were significantly lower compared to those aged < 12 months, with reductions of 32.63, 24.01, and 16.40 units, respectively (p < 0.001). Maternal underweight was negatively associated with all three anthropometric outcomes, whereas higher maternal education and better household wealth status were positively associated with improved nutritional outcomes.ConclusionThe findings of this study suggest that both governmental and non-governmental organizations should prioritize improving maternal education and nutritional status, enhancing household income-generating activities among disadvantaged populations, and promoting adequate birth spacing.Keywords: Child undernutrition, MANOVA, Multivariate linear regression

Introduction

Malnutrition is one of the most serious public health concerns affecting at least half of children under five years of age in developing countries [1–3]. Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses, or imbalances in energy and/or nutrient intake, or impaired nutrient utilization, and includes undernutrition, overweight, and obesity [4]. In this paper, we considered under-nutrition and malnutrition equivalently. The indicators of childhood undernutrition involve stunting, underweight, and wasting. Stunting, underweight, and wasting occur in children under five years who are below 2 standard deviations from the median height-for-age, weight-for-age, and weight-for-height, respectively, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards [5]. Malnutrition in early childhood is associated with functional damage in adult life, as malnourished children are physically and intellectually less productive when they become adults [6, 7]. Children who are malnourished tend to have an increased risk of morbidity and mortality, and often suffer from delayed mental development, poor school performance, and reduced intellectual achievement [8]. According to the UNICEF–WHO–World Bank Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates, in 2024, the global prevalence of stunting (height-for-age), underweight (weight-for-age), and wasting (weight-for-height) among children under five years was 23.2%, 12.2%, and 6.6%, respectively [9–11]. According to recent reports for 2024, approximately 150.2 million children under five years of age are stunted, 42.8 million are wasted, and 35.5 million are underweight worldwide [12]. In 2024, an estimated 4.9 million children died before reaching their fifth birthday worldwide [13]. Moreover, in 2024, 51% of children under five affected by stunting lived in Asia, and 43% lived in Africa [9]. For wasting, WHO reported that in 2020, 70% of affected children lived in Asia and 27% in Africa [14].

According to the report for 2025 published by the International Food Policy and Research Institute (IFPRI), Bangladesh is ranked as the 85th hunger-prone country out of a total of 123 countries based on the Global Hunger Index [15]. Malnutrition remains a major public health concern in Bangladesh, with more than half of the population affected, including approximately 450,000 children with severe acute malnutrition and nearly two million with mild acute malnutrition [16]. Children’s nutritional status has gradually improved over the past decades: the prevalence of stunting among under-five children declined from 51% in 2004 to 43% in 2007, 41% in 2011, 36% in 2014, 31% in 2018, and 24% in 2022; underweight decreased from 43% in 2004 to 41% in 2007, 36% in 2011, 33% in 2014, and 22% in both 2018 and 2022; similarly, wasting showed some fluctuation but an overall decline from 15% in 2004 to 17% in 2007, 16% in 2011, 14% in 2014, 9% in 2018, and 11% in 2022 [17–22]. However, Bangladesh still has a higher than acceptable rate of malnutrition, with 10% of the population being underweight and 30% of the population being stunted [23–25].

The government of Bangladesh has strongly committed to achieving the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). End hunger, achieve food security, improve nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture; all these nutrition-related factors are included in the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [26]. The country won’t achieve its SDG goal of reducing child mortality if undernutrition is not properly controlled [24]. To plan and implement effective community-level interventions, the health system must identify malnutrition early.

There were lots of studies about under-five child malnutrition around the world. In the past, many researchers have used several statistical methods, such as binary logistic regression, ordinal logistic regression, and multinomial logistic regression, to identify risk factors for child malnutrition [25, 27–32]. They have used a cut-off value of the outcome variable as a binary variable based on the z-score. When dividing continuous variables into categories it will loss of power and a loss of precision of the estimated mean, odds, hazards, etc. [33–36]. Since our response variables are continuous, to improve the precision of the estimator, we can use the multivariate technique. Multivariate multiple linear regression is used when more than one continuous dependent variable and a large set of independent variables, often dependent variables are correlated [37]. Major advantages of the Multivariate technique are (i) parameter estimates for all effects within a single modeling framework, (ii) describe the relationship between the multiple effects, and (iii) parameter estimates with better statistical properties [38].

The majority of earlier studies on undernutrition were carried out using univariate analysis, while a few earlier studies used multiple regression with response variables as categorical [39, 40]. However, in multivariate regression analysis, the response variables are typically continuous in nature [41]. None of the researchers has used the z-score directly as an outcome variable to identify the significant risk factors for child malnutrition by applying a multivariate linear regression. To bridge this gap, this study has been made to uncover the associated factors for malnutrition among under-five Bangladeshi children by using the BDHS, 2022 data with an application of the multivariate linear regression model. This information should help to reassess the current policies of child development and plan for significant new interventions to improve the health of children.

Materials and methods

Dataset and study design

This study utilized malnutrition data from the 2022 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), which is publicly available [42]. It was the 8th nationwide DHS, covering the entire population. The list of enumeration areas (EA) of the 2011 census population was provided by the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS). The samples of households of BDHS, 2022 were collected using two-stage stratified sampling. In the 1 st stage, 675 EAs were selected with probability proportional to EA size, with 237 EAs in urban areas and 438 in rural areas and one average 45 households were chosen using systematic sampling. Approximately 30,330 ever-married women (age: 15–49 years) were selected for an interview and 30,018 (%) women were successfully interviewed [43]. We have used a kid’s recode file from BDHS, 2022, comprised of 8784 (have child less than 5 years) respondents. A total of 4087 respondents were selected after eliminating 4697 missing height and weight values for the final analysis, as shown in fig. 1

Outcome and explanatory variables

Outcome Variables

The outcome or response variables are malnutrition index in children indicated by z-scores for height- for- age, z-scores for weight-for - height, and z-scores for weight-for - age. Thus, there are three dependent variables in this study.

Explanatory variables: From various literatures review and considering the available dataset information, this study includes the following explanatory variables: age in month of child, sex of child, preceding birth interval, mother body mass index, frequency of antenatal visits during pregnancy, duration of breastfeeding, mother age of 1 st birth, mother’s employment status, father education level, mother education level, father occupation, division, type of place of residence and wealth index [25, 27, 30–32, 39, 44–46].

Statistical methods of data analysis

Descriptive analysis has been performed to determine the characteristics of the study participants, which has been presented with the frequency and percentage. Continuous variables have been reported as mean and standard deviation. T-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) are widely used statistical methods to compare group means. The Student’s t-test is used to compare continuous variables based on their data distribution, while the one-way ANOVA homogeneity of variance test is employed to assess the homogeneity of variables when dealing with more than two means. MANOVA is the simple extension of ANOVA by considering multiple responses to investigate the mean differences instead of assessing a single response variable. The MANOVA provides additional information regarding the statistical model when the multiple response variables are correlated. It provides greater statistical power in case of correlated traits, evaluates patterns among the multiple correlated traits, and maintains the joint error rate, which is not possible by using ANOVA [47]. In multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), four different multivariate tests (Wilks’ Lambda, Pillai’s Trace, Hotelling Trace, and Roy’s Largest Root) were considered to identify the significant effect of the independent variables on all of the dependent variables, as a group [46, 48]. The multivariate multiple linear regression model is fed the explanatory variables if they are statistically significantly associated with malnutrition. To identify the risk factors of malnutrition, a multivariate linear regression model has been used. Based on the p-value (p < 0.05), the significant risk factors have been selected. Statistical software StataSE version 15 (Stata Corp, USA) has been used to carry out statistical analyses. Missing values had been avoided in advanced analysis.

Multivariate linear regression analysis

Sometimes the model or the relationship among variables may be more complicated. In such cases, more than one response variable can be affected by one predictor or by the same set of predictors. In a multivariate multiple linear regression model, we consider the relationship between more than one dependent variable and more than one independent variable [41].

Statistical Assumptions and Model Diagnostics

To access the key assumptions of MANOVA, the density plot was used for multivariate normality, and Pillai’s trace statistic was used for homogeneity across groups [49, 50]. In addition, model adequacy was evaluated by Eta-squared (η²) [51].

Results

A total of 4087 children aged 0–59 months had been included in the study, of whom 2096 (51.28%) children were male and 1991 (48.72%) were female (Table 1). Among the respondents, 13.10% of mothers were underweight, and 60.07% gave birth for the first time before the age of 20. Approximately 7.47% of mothers had no antenatal visits during pregnancy, and 6.19% had no formal education. Additionally, 68.02% of the respondents were from rural areas, and 21.53% were classified under the poorest wealth index.

Independent samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed to assess variations in mean height-for-age z-score (HAZ), weight-for-height z-score (WHZ), and weight-for-age z-score (WAZ) according to selected explanatory variables. The results are presented in Table 2. Overall, the analyses demonstrated statistically significant differences in mean anthropometric indices across comparison groups (p < 0.001), providing strong evidence against the null hypothesis. The results further demonstrate that child’s age in months, preceding birth interval, maternal body mass index (BMI), frequency of antenatal care (ANC) visits during pregnancy, maternal age at first birth, maternal employment status, parental educational level, father’s occupation, administrative division, place of residence (urban/rural), and household wealth index were significantly associated with the nutritional outcome variables (HAZ, WHZ, and WAZ) at conventional levels of statistical significance. However, the sex of the child was not consistently insignificant across all outcomes and showed a significant association with weight-for-age Z-score (WAZ) (p = 0.019). Therefore, it was retained for subsequent analyses. In contrast, duration of breastfeeding was not significantly associated with any of the anthropometric outcomes (all p > 0.05) and was therefore excluded from further analysis.

Regular ANOVA tests can assess only one dependent variable at a time in our model. Even when we fit a general linear model with multiple independent variables, the model only considers one dependent variable. The problem is that these models can’t identify patterns in multiple dependent variables. This restriction can be very problematic in certain cases where a typical ANOVA won’t be able to produce statistically significant results. In this study, we have extended ANOVA in multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) because all of predictors are categorical. We used multivariate ANOVA when our dependent variables are correlated.

As shown in Table 3, the dependent variables were correlated with each other. The correlation coefficients were 0.7079 between height-for-age and weight-for-height Z-scores, 0.0847 between height-for-age and weight-for-age Z-scores, and 0.7528 between weight-for-height and weight-for-age Z-scores. Since the dependent variables exhibited intercorrelations and Z-scores are assumed to follow an approximately normal distribution (Fig. 2), the assumptions for multivariate analysis were considered reasonable. In addition, the homogeneity of covariance matrices was assessed using Pillai’s trace (More robust than Box’s M test) to support the interpretation of the MANOVA results. Therefore, multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was deemed appropriate for identifying significant effects.

The multivariate multiple linear regression analysis revealed that the Z scores for height-for-age, weight-for-age, and weight-for-height of children aged 12 to 23 months were significantly lower, with reductions of 32.63, 24.1, and 16.40 units, respectively, compared to those of children aged below 12 months. On the other hand, similarly, the z-score of height-for-age, weight-for- age, and weight-for-height of the children aged 24 to 35 months, 36 to 47 months, and 48 to 59 months were significantly lower (P\:<0.001). According to the findings, the z-scores of height-for-age, weight-for-age, and weight-for-height among female children were 3.90, 8.18, and 3.98 units lower, respectively, than those among male children. Maternal body mass index (BMI) was significantly associated with height-for-age z-score (HAZ), weight-for-age z-score (WAZ), and weight-for-height z-score (WHZ) among children. Compared with children of mothers with normal BMI, children of overweight mothers had increases of 15.02, 19.64, and 16.98 units in height-for-age (HAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-height (WHZ) z-scores, respectively. Corresponding increases among children of obese mothers were 28.00, 52.82, and 55.12 units. In contrast, children of underweight mothers had decreases of 13.76, 29.57, and 33.01 units in HAZ, WAZ, and WHZ, respectively. Compared with the first preceding birth interval, children with a preceding birth interval of less than 24 months had significantly lower height-for-age (HAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-height (WHZ) z-scores, with decreases of 12.93, 29.57, and 32.30 units, respectively. Similarly, children with preceding birth intervals of 24–47 months showed reductions of 12.51, 16.73, and 13.07 units in HAZ, WAZ, and WHZ, respectively, while those with birth intervals of 48 months or more had decreases of 2.71, 7.35, and 10.12 units compared with the first preceding birth interval. Other factors significantly associated with higher height-for-age (HAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-height (WHZ) z-scores among children included higher maternal educational attainment and household wealth status. Children from poorer, middle, richer, and richest wealth quintiles had significantly higher z-scores compared with those from the poorest wealth quintile.

Discussion

The goal of this research work was to identify associated risk factors with malnutrition using multivariate multiple linear regression analysis. We found that children aged 12–59 months, those born after a preceding birth interval of less than 24 months, those with underweight mothers, those whose mothers had no formal education, and those from the poorest wealth quintile had significantly lower height-for-age (HAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-height (WHZ) z-scores compared with their respective counterparts.

A multivariate multiple linear regression study revealed that children’s chances of having more stunting (Z-score of low height for age), underweight (Z-score of low weight for age), and wasted (Z-score of low height for weight) increased as they were growing up. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Bangladesh [30–32] and in neighboring countries, including India [52], China [53], Nepal [54–56], and Ethiopia [39, 40]. Researchers have discovered that an escalating pattern of stunting by age does not deviate from an escalating pattern of communicable childhood infections by age [57]. As suggested by Hong, Banta, and Betancourt [58], this might result from giving a baby solid food along with breastfeeding after the age of six months, which makes eating unsafe food more likely and decreases the vital safety that breast milk provides. Furthermore, kids start crawling nearby around this age and are more likely to be carried outside, which exposes them to extra toxins. Therefore, caring in early childhood is more likely to be protective, and undernutrition becomes more prospective as the child becomes more dependent on caloric intake from foods that should be improved. To establish this, more in-depth studies are required.

Male children are observed to have insignificantly more stunting, wasting, and underweight than females. Numerous studies have indicated gender as a risk factor [59, 60]. Mother’s age at first birth is the most important factor of child malnutrition. Mothers with an age of less than twenty years have a lower Z-score, which increases the percentage of stunted, wasted, and underweight children than other age groups.

Maternal education was inversely associated with child malnutrition, with children of educated mothers exhibiting a lower risk of malnutrition than those whose mothers had no formal education. Children of educated mothers exhibited the highest positive mean values of height-for-age (HAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-height (WHZ) z-scores, and the differences were statistically significant. Education always has a positive influence on diseases and health [61, 62]. Mothers with higher educational attainment are generally more knowledgeable about hygiene, child care, health services, and nutrition, and parental education also contributes to higher household income, which facilitates provision of a more nutritious diet for children. In addition, it was highlighted by Islam and his coworkers [63] that educated moms are enable to maintain their family by limited financial support and offered healthcare services efficiently, reduce the size of their family, retain healthier behaviors, and provide their children better healthcare [64, 65]. Furthermore, this study found that children from households in the richer and richest wealth index were more likely to receive adequate nutrition. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Bangladesh and other countries [41–45, 56, 57]. This may be explained by the fact that wealthier households are better able to allocate financial resources for nutritious food, whereas poorer households often face greater constraints in providing adequate dietary intake for their children. By reducing various health risks, parents can improve their children’s health status by providing adequate resources in a cost-effective manner.

Strength and Limitation

This study utilized nationally representative data with a large sample size and comprehensive information on child malnutrition, enhancing the generalizability and robustness of the findings. However, the study had some limitations. Data on several important factors, such as children’s dietary intake and mobility patterns, were unavailable. Additionally, substantial missing data were observed for variables including child size at delivery, number of antenatal visits, and duration of breastfeeding.

Conclusion

Malnutrition is one of the most important health hazard and welfare problems in Bangladesh. This study identified several significant risk factors for child malnutrition using multivariate multiple linear regression analysis. Child age in months, preceding birth interval, maternal BMI, maternal education level, and household wealth index were found to have direct associations with height-for-age, weight-for-age, and weight-for-height z-scores. The results of this study strongly highlight the necessity of raising the educational attainment of mothers, increasing household income, and keeping a normal mother BMI during pregnancy in order to achieve better nutritional status among Bangladeshi children under the age of five. Therefore, government officials or policymakers should take the appropriate actions to regulate these issues by changing some of the existing intervention strategies in order to improve the nutritional condition of children.
