Integrative single-cell RNA-seq and transcriptomic analysis uncovers cellular heterogeneity and immunotherapy resistance mechanisms in colorectal cancer
Authors: Yirou Wang, Yingying Zhang, Ziyong Mao, Wenle Du, Xiyuan Cao, Minxuan Jiang, Qingchen Zhang, Shenghua Zhan, Zutao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the molecular and cellular features associated with immunotherapy resistance in colorectal cancer (CRC) and identify potential biomarkers.MethodsThe single-cell RNA sequencing data from 14 pre-treatmant CRC samples (11 complete respondence and 3 stable disease) were collected from GSE236581. Clustering, pseudotime trajectory analysis, and intercellular communication analysis were performed to explore the tumor microenvironment and malignant cell characteristics. Bulk transcriptome datasets TCGA-COAD and GSE39582 were collected to assist in screening and validating key genes involved in tumorigenesis and prognosis. Key findings were validated in independent cohorts and via immunohistochemistry and T cell cytotoxicity experiments.ResultsUnsupervised clustering identified 21 cell clusters and 8,810 malignant epithelial cells. Differential gene expression analysis revealed 7,648 genes significantly differing between groups, with 568 upregulated in stable disease samples, enriched in ECM organization, ERK1/2 signaling, and metabolic reprogramming. Intercellular communication analysis revealed stronger malignant cell-CD8 + T-cell interactions in stable disease samples. ASPHD1, associated with poor survival, was upregulated in CRC tissues and may serve as a candidate biomarker associated with immunotherapy response.ConclusionOur findings highlighted the molecular and cellular features associated with immunotherapy resistance in CRC, involving malignant cell adaptations and TME remodeling. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with an estimated 1.1 million new cases and 0.57 million deaths annually [1]. A significant proportion of CRC patients, especially those with microsatellite stable (MSS) tumors, exhibit primary or acquired resistance against immunotherapy although immunotherapy has developed and immune checkpoint inhibitors have been discovered overtime [2]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) orchestrates complex interactions among malignant, immune, and stromal cells, as well as extracellular matrix (ECM) components, and plays a decisive role in tumor progression and therapeutic response [3]. These interactions modulate tumor growth, immune evasion, and resistance to therapies through dynamic molecular and cellular processes. However, the precise mechanisms underlying differential immunotherapy outcomes, especially the immune evasion and therapy resistance in CRC remain poorly understood.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a powerful approach for dissecting cellular heterogeneity and molecular programs within the tumor microenvironment (TME), enabling cell-type–specific analysis of gene expression, differentiation, and intercellular communication [4]. Recent scRNA-seq studies have revealed substantial immune and stromal diversity in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, including highly immunosuppressive CD4 regulatory T-cell subsets [5], but systematic comparisons of malignant cell states and their interactions with the TME between immunotherapy-responsive (complete response, CR) and non‑responsive (stable disease, SD) CRC patients are still lacking. Prognostic biomarkers that distinguish drug-resistant CRC phenotypes are needed, and the extent of cellular-level heterogeneity remains underexplored. While bulk RNA-seq analyses from TCGA have identified CRC prognostic gene signatures, these approaches overlook cellular heterogeneity and fail to capture key molecular drivers of therapy resistance [6]. Notably, transcription factors and metabolic reprogramming pathways are implicated in CRC progression, yet their cell-type–specific regulatory roles within the TME remain poorly understood [7]. Integrating scRNA-seq with bulk RNA-seq analyses may uncover novel molecular insights and improve clinical stratification and treatment of CRC.

In this study, we integrated scRNA-seq and bulk RNA transcriptomics data to uncover the underlying molecular and cellular underpinnings of immunotherapy resistance in CRC. Specifically, by integrating cell clustering, differential gene expression, pathway enrichment, pseudotime trajectory analyses, immune microenvironment profiling, and characterizing intercellular communication networks, we aimed to uncover the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving therapy resistance. Furthermore, we sought to identify promising biomarkers by cross-referencing single-cell findings with external independent bulk RNA-seq datasets, such as TCGA-COAD, and in vitro experiments. This work provides an exploratory basis for investigating features associated with immunotherapy resistance in CRC and may inform future hypothesis-driven biomarker and therapeutic studies.

Materials and methods

Data sources

We obtained the publicly available scRNA-seq data of colorectal cancer (CRC) from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Specifically, we accessed the scRNA-seq from a recent study involving 14 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy (GEO number: GSE236581, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE236581), in which samples were grouped according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) with three samples being defined as stable disease (SD), reflecting a stable clinical state, and the rest 11 samples being categorized as complete response (CR), indicating successful therapy outcomes. We acknowledge that stable disease does not necessarily represent non-response, but it serves as a distinct comparative group to complete responders in this cohort. All these samples were collected prior to treatment. To further reveal the transcriptional cellular heterogeneity, we also accessed bulk transcriptome data (RNA-seq) of CRC from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database(https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-COAD). A total of 524 samples with detailed RNA transcriptomics sequencing profiles, comprising 483 colon adenocarcinoma samples, together with complete survival data were included. In addition, RNA-seq data from the work by Marisa et al. that included 443 colon cancer (CC) samples alongside 19 non-cancerous colorectal mucosa samples were also retrieved from GEO (GSE39582, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi? acc=GSE39582). The basic population characteristics of these studies were shown in Table S1 and also available in the original articles [8].

Notably, the scRNA-seq cohort exhibited an inherent imbalance between response groups (11 CR vs. 3 SD). To mitigate potential statistical biases arising from this imbalance, we conducted non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) for group comparisons, and validated all key findings in above independent bulk RNA-seq cohorts with balanced clinical outcomes.

Single-cell data processing and annotation

Details on samples processing, scRNA-seq data library preparation, quality controls, and sequencing were described in original article [8]. The downloaded data were subjected to quality control and further processing using the R package Seurat v4.1.1 [9]. In analyses, we excluded cells if they have mitochondrial content above 10%, hemoglobin levels over 5%, or gene counts outside the 200–5000 range [10–12]. Doublets were eliminated using DoubletFinder, and batch effects were adjusted with the “RunHarmony” function from the harmony R package [13]. Prior to differential, enrichment and statistical analyses, data normalization (i.e., log-transformation), clustering, and dimensionality reduction analyses were performed to all scRNA-seq data using Seurat. After data normalization, we identified 2000 highly variable genes via “FindVariableFeatures” function and selected top 20 components via principal component analysis (PCA) (“RunPCA”function) in Seurat for downstream analyses. Cell clustering (resolution: 0.6) and nonlinear dimensionality reduction were performed using FindClusters and RunUMAP functions in Seurat, respectively. Cell populations were annotated using the CellMarker2.0 database and known marker genes [14].

Functional enrichment analysis

We identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between examined groups within each cluster using Seurat’s “FindMarkers” function, prioritizing upregulated genes for downstream analyses. Significant DEGs with false discovery rate (FDR, Benjamini-Hochberg method) less than 0.05 were included in the downstream analyses. Herein, we only focused on DEGs upregulated in CRC groups. We utilized the clusterProfiler (v4.8.2) [15] to conduct the functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways analyses (multiple testing–adjusted P value < 0.05 were considered significantly).

Malignant epithelial cell identification

To identify malignant from non-malignant epithelial cells, we utilize Python package Infercnvpy, a powerful computational tool designed for the inference of somatic copy number variations (CNVs) in scRNA-seq data to facilitate the identification of putatively malignant cells. T cells were used as reference cells in this study because they represent a genetically stable, non-malignant cell population present across all samples and are commonly employed as a baseline in inferCNV analyses. The algorithm operates on the principle of comparative transcriptomic analysis, whereby the gene expression profiles of target cells are juxtaposed against a defined reference population of non-malignant cells. Epithelial cells exhibiting low CNV scores and clustering with reference cells were classified as non-malignant, whereas those with elevated scores (over the scores of 95% T cells) were designated as malignant Epithelial cells. Malignant cells frequently harbor substantial CNVs, characterized by the gain or loss of extended chromosomal segments. These genomic aberrations are reflected at the transcriptional level as concordant up- or down-regulation of genes located within the affected regions. Through the visualization and analysis of these differential gene expression patterns across the genome, cells exhibiting extensive and contiguous CNV profiles can be identified as likely representing the malignant cellular compartment within the sample. This inferential approach utilizes the established association between genomic instability which can be inferred from CNVs, and oncogenesis.

Transcription factor analysis

To calculate the Regulon activity scores (RAS) for malignant epithelial cells, we conducted SCENIC analysis via pySCENIC Python package (v0.12.1) [16]. Specifically, co-expression modules linking transcription factors (TFs) and target genes were inferred via GRNBoost2. Enriched motifs (defining regulons as TFs with direct targets) were identified using RcisTarget. Regulon activity was quantified per cell using AUCell.

Pseudotime analysis

We constructed pseudotime trajectories for malignant epithelial cells based on their gene expression profiles using Monocle (v2.28.0) [17]. Following dimensionality reduction and cell ordering, cells were mapped onto branching trajectories, with cells on the same branch interpreted as sharing similar states. To explore mechanisms of cell fate determination, we further conducted branched expression analysis modeling (BEAM) which pinpointed branch-specific genes expression.

Cell interaction analysis

We explored cell-cell interactions using CellChat (v1.6.1) and normalized prior to interaction inference [18]. A combined Seurat object that integrates epithelial and other TME cell types was analyzed. With the outputs from CellChat, we built a reference database of secreted signaling pathways and used “computeCommunProb” and “computeCommunProbPathway” to deduce receptor-ligand interactions and communication likelihoods between cell populations.

AUCell scoring

Gene set activity in individual cells was assessed with AUCell. T cell cytotoxicity and quiescence function-related gene sets examined in this study were listed in Table S2.

Differential analysis and functional enrichment

Differential expression analyses were performed using limma (v3.56.2 [19]) to compare transcriptomic profiles between tumor and adjacent normal tissues, as well as between ASPHD1-high and ASPHD1-low CRC samples in the TCGA-COAD cohort. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1 and a multiple testing–adjusted P value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 (v3.5.1) to visualize differentially expressed genes. Functional enrichment and gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) of the significantly differentially expressed genes were conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.16.0 [16], multiple testing–adjusted P value < 0.05).

Cox analysis

We evaluated the prognostic impact of key differentially expressed genes in tumor samples using univariate Cox regression. Genes significantly associated with survival (p < 0.05) were selected as DEGs for downstream analyses. Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) correction to the P values were calculated for univariate Cox regression to control the false discovery rate. For DEGs of interest, we further classified the samples into high and low genes expression groups based on optimal cutoff that generated from “surv_cutpoint” and the survival outcomes were illustrated via Kaplan-Meier curves across groups using survminer (v0.4.9) and survival (v3.5-7).

Immune infiltration analysis

The xCell algorithm (v1.1.0) was employed to estimate the absolute infiltration abundance of each sample. This was achieved by performing enrichment scoring on gene sets characterizing 64 types of immune and stromal cells within the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) framework. Subsequently, utilizing the characteristic matrices of 28 immune cell types provided by the TISIDB database (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB), a secondary deconvolution was performed on colorectal cancer samples from the TCGA-COAD cohort to calculate relative infiltration proportions.

Protein interaction networks

A Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network of the hub genes was constructed utilizing the GeneMANIA online platform (https://genemania.org). ASPHD1 was entered as the query gene, with the organism restricted to Homo sapiens. Interaction data falling under five categories (co-expression, physical interactions, genetic interactions, pathways, and protein domain similarity) were retained for the analysis.

Drug sensitivity analysis

The ASPHD1 gene and associated significantly differentially expressed genes were subjected to the drug sensitivity prediction module of the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) comprehensive analysis platform (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/#/drug). The “GDSC drug sensitivity and expression correlation” parameter was selected to identify therapeutic agents whose half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values exhibited significant positive or negative correlations with the expression levels of the input gene set.

Human specimens, immunohistochemical staining and T cell cytotoxicity

We included patients aged 18 years or older who underwent surgical resection for Colorectal Cancer at The Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Suzhou Dushu Lake Hospital) between January 1, 2025, and December 31, 2025. Tumor and adjacent normal human tissues were fixed in 4% formaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and cut into 5 μm sections with a microtome. Sections were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated in graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citric acid solution (Fuzhou Maixin Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd., China). Endogenous peroxidase was quenched with a blocker (Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) for 10 min at room temperature. After three PBS washes (3 min each), sections were blocked with 10% goat serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-ASPHD1 antibody (1:200; PA5-60942, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Following three additional PBS rinses, a secondary antibody was applied for 30 min at 25 °C, followed by DAB development for 10 min and hematoxylin counterstaining for 2 min. Slides were examined under a Zeiss microscope (Germany).

Cells

We purchased human Colorectal Cancer cell lines HCT116 and HCT8 from the Cell Bank of the FuHeng BioLogy (Shanghai, China). The HCT116 and HCT8 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM-H) (Gibco) in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cells transfected with siRNA-ASPHD1 were cultured for further experiments.

Cell transfection

HCT116 and HCT8 cells were seeded overnight to grow to 50% confluence and were then transfected with siRNA-ASPHD1. siRNAs that target the human ASPHD1 gene (siRNA-ASPHD1) and nontargeting negative control siRNA (siRNA-NC) were purchased from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The siRNA sequences are listed in Table S3.

Lipid-based transient transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 6000 Transfection Reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the culture medium was replaced 6 h later. After 48 to 72 h of transfection, quantitative reverse transcription qRT-PCR was used to assess transfection efficiency.

qRT-PCR

For qRT-PCR analysis, cells were harvested and lysed in TRIzol reagent. After adding 200 µL of chloroform, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4℃. An equal volume of pre-chilled isopropanol was added to the aqueous phase, followed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4℃. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in RNase-free water. RNA concentration was measured with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was performed using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (AU341; TRANS, China), and gene expression levels were quantified with SYBR Green PCR Mix (RR420A; Takara, Japan). Primer sequences are listed in Table S4. All experiments were performed in triplicate and analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCT method using β-ACTIN as the reference gene.

T cell cytotoxicity assay

PBMCs(PBC-1000, Charles river, China) were activated by incubation with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 dynabeads (Thermofisher, US) for 24 h, yielding preparations of aPBMCs. The plates were incubated for 48 h to obtain aPBMCs, the anti-CD3/anti-CD28 Dynabeads were removed magnetically, and the cells were resuspended in DMEM containing IL-2 (10 ng/ml) for another 5 days. This process can cool aPBMC to prevent non-specific killing. HCT116 and HCT8 cells were transfected with negative control short interfering RNA (si-NC) or si-ASPDH1 for 48 h and plated at 10,000 cells per well in 96-well plates. The next day, 40,000 aPBMCs and anti-PD1 monoclonal antibody (4 µg/ml) were added as appropriate to each well. Killed HCT116 and HCT8 cells were measured after 48–72 h, with the results verified by microscopy and crystal violet staining.

Statistical analysis

Differences in continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (more than two groups), while differences in distributions of categorical variables were examined via the chi-square test. All analyses were performed in R (v4.0.5), with significance set as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Results

Cell clustering and annotation

We obtained a total of 63,751 cells from 14 pre-treatment CRC samples (11 CR and 3 SD tissues) that included in GSE236581 (Fig. 1A and Fig S1A) after quality control and filtering. Through UMAP-based unsupervised dimensionality reduction and clustering analyses, we identified 21 cell clusters (Fig S1A). By mapping with marker genes, these cells were annotated as eight major types (Fig. 1B, C, Fig S1B), including T cells, epithelial cells, plasma cells, B cells, myeloid cells, stromal cells, endothelial cells, and mast cells, which is reflected by the patterns of expressions of marker genes across these cell types. (e.g., T-cell cluster has high expressions of CD30, CD3E, Fig. 1C). We further calculated the CNV scores using inferCNVpy to identify malignant cells between CR and SD group (Fig S1C, D). We have analyzed the changes in the proportion of various types of cells in the two groups of samples. Among them, the number of T cells is the highest, at 28,406; while the number of malignant cells is 8,810 (Fig S1E). Analysis of the proportion changes of various cell types in two groups of samples revealed that the proportions of malignant cells, T cells, and endothelial cells were increased in the CR group. (Fig. 1D, E and Fig S1F, G).

Differential gene expression and pathway enrichment in malignant cells

We further conducted exploratory comprehensive analyses to identify DEGs of malignant cells between SD and CR groups and the potential biological pathways involved. Overall, we identified 7,648 differentially expressed genes (DEGs, p < 0.05), with 568 genes upregulated and 7,080 downregulated in SD samples (Fig. 2A). GO analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed that, compared to the CR group, malignant cells in the SD group exhibited activation of pathways related to the extracellular matrix (ECM), including Positive Regulation of Extracellular Matrix Organization and Extracellular Matrix Organization, as well as the ERK1 and ERK2 Cascade and Cytoplasmic Translation. In the SD group, activation of ECM organization and its positive regulation may indicate a potency forming tumor-supportive microenvironment. Furthermore, the observed activation of the ERK1 and ERK2 cascades signaling pathways and cytoplasmic translation associated DEGs in SD group may indicate the prolonged tumor cell survival and therapy resistance given the known roles of these pathways in cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival [20]. Together, these GO findings connect the DEG profile to specific cellular processes, suggesting that SD cells may adapt their microenvironment and intracellular machinery to resist therapy.

We also conducted in parallel KEGG analyses (Fig. 2C) and found that metabolic pathways, including Oxidative Phosphorylation, Galactose Metabolism, Fructose and Mannose Metabolism, and the Pentose Phosphate Pathway, were significantly activated in malignant cells of the SD group. Furthermore, pathways such as EGFR Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor Resistance and Notch Signaling were also enriched in SD group. Activation of these metabolic pathways likely reflects the increased energy demands and metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells, enabling enhanced energy production to support rapid proliferation [20].The EGFR signaling pathway is known for its critical role in various cancers [21], the observed activation of EGFR Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor Resistance pathway may thus indicate an adaptive response of tumor cells to immunotherapy, facilitating immune evasion in SD group. Similarly, it has been long documented that the Notch signaling pathway regulates cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis [22], whose activation found in our study may highlight the potency of prolonged tumor cell survival and therapy resistance in SD group. These results suggest that SD-associated malignant cells may display distinct metabolic and signaling states; however, these enrichment findings remain inferential and should not be overinterpreted as direct evidence of pathway activation or therapeutic resistance mechanisms. As a complementary component to the above GO and KEGG analyses, we also conducted HALLMARK analysis (Fig. 2D) and found that pathways of TNF-α Signaling via NF-κB, Notch Signaling, and KRAS Signaling were significantly upregulated in malignant cells in SD group. Existing literature has shown that the activation of TNF-α- NF-κB signaling pathway in inflammation and immune regulation [23] indicates a chronic inflammatory microenvironment that promotes tumor survival and therapy resistance. The ECM remodeling observed in above GO analysis suggests the consistency with the discovery in the existing literatre. In addition, aligning with findings from above KEGG analyses (i.e., activations of EGFR and Notch signaling pathways) the activation of Notch [22, 24] and KRAS [25] signaling pathways collectively reinforces the roles of these pathways in maintaining tumor cell viability and promoting proliferation and resistance of tumor cells in SD group. Together with the GO and KEGG results, these findings support the presence of distinct transcriptional programs in SD-associated malignant cells. However, given the cohort size, they should be viewed as exploratory.

To further pinpoint the regulatory mechanisms behind these pathway alterations, we conducted transcription factor analysis. As shown in Fig. 2E and F, HOXD13, SOX15, ZNF18, MAFF, and NR2F6 were identified as candidate transcription factors with high inferred regulon activity in SD malignant cells. Because regulon analysis is predictive in nature, these putative regulatory interactions require independent experimental validation. Current evidence regarding the roles of these TFs remains elusive [26].Our findings here may suggest their roles in regulating the expression of genes involved in ECM remodeling, metabolic shifts, and signaling pathways and contributing to the malignant cell differentiation, proliferation, and immune evasion in CRC. For example, HOXD13 has been found to promote the malignant progression of colon cancer by upregulating PTPRN2 [27]. Elevated expression of ZNF18 has been linked with poorer prognosis of kidney cancer and noted in cancer formation and progression [28]. In addition, NR2F6 has been emerged as a promising intracellular immune checkpoint involved in suppressing anti-tumor immune responses [29].

Pseudotime trajectory of malignant cells

To further characterize differences in inferred cellular states of malignant epithelial cells (ECs) across CR and SD groups, we conducted pseudotime trajectory analysis. Using Monocle, we identified three distinct cell states of malignant epithelial cells in all included CRC samples (Fig. 3A and B): Cells in state1 are regarded as potential initiating points in the pseudotime trajectory, cells in state2 progress toward the left branch of the trajectory following a bifurcation point, and cells in state3 occupy a distinct transcriptional state on the right branch. Notably, malignant cells in SD samples predominantly in state2 subsequent to the cell differentiation bifurcation, while those from CR samples were primarily distributed across state1, state3, and the early phase of state2 (Fig. 3C). This pattern was also illustrated in the ridge plot (Fig. 3D), where malignant cells in the SD group showed a distinct inferred state distribution compared with those in the CR group. We further utilized the BEAM to identify genes that are potentially involved in modulating the cell differentiation process. Specifically, a total of 50 branch-dependent genes were identified, which were clustered into four modules based on their expression dynamics along the pseudotime trajectory (Fig. 3E). Of note, we found that the expressions of genes in Cluster1 and Cluster2 display a triphasic pattern, characterized by an initial decline, a subsequent rise, and a final reduction along the pseudotime trajectory. By contrast, Cluster3 genes demonstrate a consistent upward trend in expression, and Cluster4 genes demonstrate a progressive decrease. Enrichment analysis further indicates that Cluster4 genes are predominantly enriched for processes governing leukocyte chemotaxis and migration (Fig. 3F). These observations collectively suggest that the transcriptional differences between malignant cells from treatment-responsive and treatment-resistant samples is accompanied by a marked reduction in their regulation of leukocyte chemotaxis and migration. Such alterations may underpin a mechanism whereby malignant cells circumvent immune surveillance and therapeutic efficacy by modulating immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment.

Immune microenvironment

Given the supportive role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in the onset and progression of cancer, we then explored the potential subpopulations of the previously annotated eight major cell types in CRC samples. For T cells, we identified six subpopulations in the total samples, including naïve T cells (T naïve), natural killer (NK) cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, regulatory T cells (Treg), and proliferating cells (see Fig. 4A and B), among which CD8 + T cells were the most abundant subpopulation (Fig S2D). While these T cell subpopulations were observed in both CR and SD samples, there exist heterogeneous cellular proportions across these two groups (Fig S2A-C). In addition, the proportions of CD8 T cells, CD4 T cells, and proliferating cells increased in the CR group (Fig. 4C and D), indicating tumor samples from the CR group may demonstrate enhanced immune activation and a more robust anti-tumor immune response following immunotherapy. We further calculated the cytotoxicity and quiescence scores of identified cell subpopulations using AUCell and found that all T cell subpopulations in the CR group exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity scores as compared to those in SD group (Fig. 4E). Meanwhile, quiescence scores for CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, naïve T cells, and Treg cells were substantially reduced in the CR group (Fig. 4F). These observations imply that T cell subpopulations in the CR group are likely in a more activated state with greater cytotoxic potential, enabling them to target and eliminate tumor cells more effectively. The concurrent reduction in quiescence scores suggests that fewer T cells in the CR group remain in a dormant or suppressed state, which likely enhances their immune effector functions. Collectively, these results bolster the hypothesis that tumor samples from the CR group manifest stronger immune activation and anti-tumor immune responses post-immunotherapy.

Similarly, we conducted cell subpopulations analyses for identified myeloid cells and identified four subpopulations, dominated by macrophages, then followed by myeloid dendritic cells (mDC), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), and monocytes (Fig. 4G and H, Fig S2H), with each cell subpopulations presenting different proportions across CR and SD groups (Fig S2E-G). Notably, the proportion of mDC cells increased in the CR group (Fig. 4I and J), a pattern consistent with enhanced antigen presentation capacity.

We further evaluated the potential role of macrophages by conducting Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA). Compared to the CR group, macrophages in the SD group demonstrated significant upregulation of several pathways (Fig. 4K), including regulation of glycolysis by fructose-2,6-bisphosphate metabolism, activated NTRK2 signaling through PI3K, MET activation of PI3K-Akt signaling, PI3K cascades associated with FGFR2, FGFR3, and FGFR4 signaling pathways. By contrast, pathways related to activation of the AP-1 transcription factor family, RUNX3 regulation of immune response and cell migration, and TNFR2 non-canonical NF-κB signaling pathway were remarkably downregulated in macrophages in SD group (Fig. 4K). The observed pathway alterations suggest that macrophages in the SD group undergo metabolic reprogramming and have reduced capacity for immune surveillance and response, which together contributes to a microenvironment that suppresses overall anti-tumor immunity, promoting tumor immune evasion and malignant progression.

Intercellular communication

To elucidate the communication among cellular components within TME, we constructed cell-cell interaction networks depicting potential receptor-ligand pairs for the SD and CR groups, respectively (Fig. 5A and B). We observed considerable variation in the interactions among different cellular components in samples from the CR group compared to the SD group, particularly concerning the communication between malignant cells and CD8 + T cells. As compared to SD group, CD8 + T cells in the CR group were subject to less regulation by malignant cells (Fig. 5C and D). Specifically, within the SD group, malignant cells were predicted to interact with CD8 + T cells via inferred ligand-receptor pairs distinct ligand-receptor pairs, including MDK-NCL, MDK-(ITGA4 + ITGB1), LGALS9-CD45, and LGALS9-CD44 (Fig. 5E–G, Table S5). MDK is recognized for its role in promoting tumor cell invasion and metastasis [30], while LGALS9 regulates immune homeostasis and tumor cell survival through its interaction with its receptor Tim-3 [31]. Accordingly, these inferred interactions indicate that potential communication axes may contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment in SD samples, but functional validation is required before drawing mechanistic conclusions. Additionally, MDK, by engaging with integrins like ITGA4β1, may enhance the survival and migratory capabilities of tumor cells, aiding their evasion of immune surveillance [30]. Furthermore, analysis based on TCGA-COAD data revealed a lower proportion of CD8 + T cells within tumor tissues relative to normal tissues (Fig. 5H). This finding further supports our hypothesis that tumor tissues employ specific intercellular communication mechanisms, such as the aforementioned ligand-receptor interactions, to modulate and inhibit the differentiation and function of CD8 + T cells, thus cultivating an immunosuppressive milieu conducive to tumor growth and dissemination. This also indicates that restoring or enhancing the immune function of CD8 + T cells, or disrupting the immunosuppressive mechanisms within the tumor tissue, could represent crucial strategies for improving immunotherapy prognosis and overcoming treatment resistance in CRC.

Association of ASPHD1 with prognosis and treatment resistance

Through analysis of the TCGA-COAD dataset, we identified 2556 DEGs, of which 1131 were upregulated and 1425 were downregulated (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, we examined the associations of expressions of DEGs with patient prognosis and identified 161 genes significantly associated with prognosis (HR ≠ 1, P < 0.05) (Fig. 6B). Interestingly, ASPHD1 was the only gene that was simultaneously identified in the set of upregulated DEGs from the TCGA-COAD dataset (1131 genes), the DEGs upregulated in malignant cells (SD vs. CR group) from single-cell data (568 genes), and the genes associated with poor prognosis (HR > 1, P < 0.05) (126 genes) (Fig. 6C). Notably, ASPHD1 remained statistically significant after stringent FDR correction (FDR = 0.0394), providing supportive context for its association with CRC progression. To assess whether ASPHD1 expression was an independent prognostic factor, multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed using clinical covariates including age, sex, and pathological stage (Fig S3). We further evaluated the expression levels of ASPHD1 (Fig. 6D and E) and its relationship with prognosis (Fig. 6G and H) in TCGA-COAD and GSE39582 datasets. The results showed that ASPHD1 was significantly enriched in tumor cells (Fig. 6F). Stratified analyses by age, sex, microsatellite stability status, TNM classification, and pathological stage revealed that ASPHD1 expression was significantly higher in T1 compared with T2 and T3 tumors, N2 compared with N0, and stage IIIA and IIIC compared with stage IIB, while no significant differences were observed across other subgroups (Fig S4A–G).

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the pro-tumorigenic and immunotherapy-resistant roles of ASPHD1, CRC samples from the TCGA-COAD cohort were stratified into ASPHD1-high and ASPHD1-low groups based on median expression levels. Differential expression analysis identified 581 significantly DEGs, including 374 upregulated and 207 downregulated genes (Fig S5A). GSEA revealed that DEGs associated with high ASPHD1 expression were significantly enriched in pathways related to cellular responses to copper ions and ECM structural components, which were markedly activated in ASPHD1-high tumors (Fig. 6I). Consistently, functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that ASPHD1-associated upregulated DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways involving the collagen-containing extracellular matrix (Fig S5B). Protein–protein interaction network analysis using GeneMANIA further revealed that ASPHD1 exhibits structural or functional associations with approximately 20 genes, including ASPHD2 and ASPH (Fig S5C). Analysis of the tumor immune microenvironment showed that Th2 cells, mast cells, and neutrophils were significantly enriched in the ASPHD1-low expression group (Fig S5D-E), suggesting that ASPHD1 may be involved in shaping immune infiltration patterns. Finally, drug sensitivity prediction analysis of ASPHD1 and its associated DEGs indicated a significant negative correlation between ASPHD1 expression and the IC50 of 17-AAG (Fig S5F), suggesting that 17-AAG may preferentially target tumors with high ASPHD1 expression; however, this observation requires further experimental validation.

Experimental validation of ASPHD1 in immunotherapy resistance

Figure 7A presents IHC staining images demonstrating markedly higher ASPHD1 protein expression in CRC tumor tissues compared with adjacent non-tumor tissues. Quantitative analysis of IHC scores in Fig. 7B further reveals that ASPHD1 expression progressively increases with worsening immunotherapy response, with the lowest expression observed in patients achieving partial response (PR) and the highest expression in patients with progressive disease, consistent with transcriptomic findings. To functionally validate the role of ASPHD1, Fig. 7C shows the efficient knockdown of ASPHD1 mRNA using two independent siRNAs in the HCT116 and HCT8 colorectal cancer cell lines, as confirmed by qPCR. Crystal violet staining showed that the cytotoxic capacity of T cells was significantly enhanced upon addition of anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody after ASPHD1 knockdown (Fig. 7D).

Discussions

Substantial evidence has indicated that TME constitutes a dynamic and intricate ecosystem that profoundly shapes the pathogenesis, metastatic potential, and therapeutic responsiveness of CRC [32, 33]. Comprising a heterogeneous ensemble of immune cells, stromal elements, and extracellular matrix (ECM), the TME orchestrates a dynamic interplay of cellular and molecular components that critically shapes CRC pathogenesis and therapeutic outcomes [34]. Our comprehensive integrative scRNA-seq and RNA transcriptomics analysis of CRC samples provides a high-resolution dissection of TME heterogeneity, delineating distinct immune and stromal profiles that differentiate complete response from stable disease following immunotherapy. Given the small and imbalanced scRNA-seq cohort, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. By integrating unsupervised clustering, identified DEGs, pseudotime trajectory, intercellular communication, and immune microenvironment analyses, we delineated candidate transcriptional and cellular features associated with immune activation and suppression, and identified ASPHD1 as a potential biomarker linked to immunotherapy response in CRC.

The TME’s cellular composition profoundly influences CRC outcomes. Our data highlight an immune-active TME in CR specimens, characterized by enriched CD8 + T cells with elevated cytotoxicity and increased mDCs, which likely enhance antigen presentation and anti-tumor immunity [35, 36]. In contrast, SD specimens exhibit an immunosuppressive TME, with reduced T-cell activation and macrophages skewed toward a tumor-supportive phenotype via PI3K-Akt activation and suppressed AP-1/TNFR2 signaling [37, 38]. These findings corroborate single-cell studies linking immune infiltration to therapeutic efficacy [39, 40] and underscore the TME’s role in modulating immunotherapy response. Our findings regarding the intercellular communication further suggest candidate immunosuppressive mechanisms in SD tumors, potentially mediated by inferred ligand-receptor pairs such as MDK-NCL and LGALS9-CD44, which may impair CD8 + T-cell function [41]. These predicted interactions are computationally inferred and require experimental validation. The lower CD8 + T-cell infiltration observed in TCGA-COAD tumors is broadly consistent with an immune-suppressive tumor milieu, but does not directly validate these specific inferred ligand-receptor interactions [42]. Similarly, pseudotime analysis of cross-sectional pre-treatment samples suggested differences in inferred malignant-cell states between CR and SD groups, rather than temporal progression toward resistance. The reduced leukocyte chemotaxis signature observed in specific inferred states is consistent with prior reports of immune suppression in resistant tumors [43], but should be interpreted cautiously. A key observation of this study is the identification of ASPHD1 as a candidate prognostic biomarker in CRC. Our data shows that ASPHD1 was consistently upregulated in malignant cells from immunotherapy-resistant, SD tumors and in bulk RNA-seq cohorts (TCGA-COAD, GSE39582), and its overexpression in CRC tissues was validated by immunohistochemistry. Previous studies have revealed that high ASPHD1 expression was associated with pathways critical for tumor microenvironment remodeling, including extracellular matrix organization, Notch, EGFR, and TNF-α/NF-κB signaling [44, 45]. Transcription factor analysis identified putative regulators including HOXD13, SOX15, ZNF18, MAFF, and NR2F6, that may be associated with ASPHD1 expression in SD malignant cells, although these upstream interactions remain exploratory and require experimental validation [46–48]. Collectively, these results support an association of ASPHD1 with an immunosuppressive TME characterized by stromal activation and immune evasion, supporting its potential as a prognostic marker of poor clinical outcomes, distinct from other established markers such as STAT1, UCP1, and FOXP3 [49].

Nevertheless, the biological significance of ASPHD1 appears to be context dependent. In melanoma, ASPHD1 expression has been linked to cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and more favorable outcomes [50], whereas in CRC, it is associated with immune exclusion and a pro-tumorigenic phenotype. These differences likely reflect variations in tumor microenvironment composition, such as the enrichment of immunosuppressive fibroblasts and myeloid populations in CRC that may interact with ASPHD1-related pathways to reinforce extracellular matrix remodeling and physical immune barriers [51]. Given the limited sample size of the present study, whether ASPHD1 plays a direct mechanistic role or serves primarily as a prognostic indicator requires to be verified in in-depth functional experiments and external clinical cohorts.

Our study bridges tumor cell-autonomous and non-cell-autonomous mechanisms in CRC, extending beyond canonical mutations (e.g., APC, KRAS) [52] to highlight TME-driven resistance in immunotherapy in this heterogenous cancer. The immune-active TME in CR specimens aligns with molecular subtypes characterized by high immune infiltration [53], while the immunosuppressive SD profile mirrors subtypes with poor immunotherapy response [54, 55]. The identification of ASPHD1 as a TME-associated biomarker introduces a novel dimension absent from earlier signatures [56], while the inferred immunosuppressive ligand-receptor interactions in SD tumors are consistent with, rather than checkpoint-driven resistance mechanisms [42]. The metabolic reprogramming of SD macrophages, driven by PI3K-Akt activation, further aligns with TME metabolic alterations linked to immune escape [57].

Overall, our findings may have implications for CRC management. The immune-active TME in CR specimens, marked by elevated CD8 + T-cell and mDC proportions, suggests that strategies aimed at enhancing effector function may warrant further study, such as PD-1 or Tim-3 inhibitors [39]. The immunosuppressive TME in SD tumors, driven by inferred MDK and LGALS9 interactions, raises the possibility that integrin-targeted or Tim-3-directed therapies could be explored to restore immune surveillance [30]. The metabolic reprogramming of SD macrophages highlights PI3K-Akt inhibitors as candidate adjunctive approaches [57]. ASPHD1’s prognostic significance suggests its potential as a biomarker for risk stratification, particularly in microsatellite-stable CRC, enabling identification of patients prone to resistance Its association with TME remodeling suggests that ASPHD1 and its putative regulators (e.g., HOXD13, NR2F6) merit further mechanistic investigation, rather than immediate therapeutic interpretation. Accordingly, therapeutic hypotheses involving ASPHD1-targeted strategies remain speculative at this stage. Preclinically, combining ASPHD1 inhibitors (e.g., αKG competitors) with immune checkpoint blockers or CAF-targeted therapies (e.g., FAK inhibitors) could counteract ECM-driven resistance [58]. The activated pathways in SD tumors, including Notch and EGFR, may represent candidate pathways for combination strategies in future studies [59], offering avenues for combination therapies. Because our pseudotime analysis was based on cross-sectional pre-treatment samples, it does not support conclusions about dynamic TME evolution over time. Although we identified candidate transcription factors potentially regulating ASPHD1 based on transcriptional activity analyses, their direct functional roles in ASPHD1 regulation and immunotherapy resistance were not experimentally validated and warrant further mechanistic investigation. Specifically, more studies are still needed to unravel whether and how ASPHD1 hydroxylates ECM components (e.g., collagens) or receptors (e.g., integrins), the relationship between TME metabolites (e.g., αKG, lactate) and activity of ASPHD1, and the potential function of ASPHD1 in cytokine/chemokine secretion that is required for reshaping immune cell recruitment in cancer.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the scRNA‑seq dataset is small in size and lacks progressive disease samples, limiting response comparisons to CR and SD groups. Additionally, the limited number of SD cells in trajectory analyses and the absence of comprehensive microsatellite status stratification may constrain the generalizability and interpretability of response-based comparisons. Future validation in larger, multi-center cohorts with harmonized pipelines and inclusion of PD samples will be essential. Second, our pseudotime analysis was based on cross‑sectional, pre‑treatment data and therefore captures differences in inferred cellular states rather than true temporal progression toward resistance. Functional experiments are required to establish causal relationships of these observed patterns. Third, the study focuses primarily on malignant and immune cell populations, potentially underrepresenting contributions from other TME components such as cancer‑associated fibroblasts. Fourth, transcription factor predictions (e.g., HOXD13) and other computational inferences (regulon, ligand–receptor) remain exploratory and await experimental confirmation. Fifth, although ASPHD1 expression was supported by transcriptomic and IHC data, its mechanistic role in immunotherapy response has not been established. Genetic perturbation and functional assays are needed to determine whether ASPHD1 acts as a causal driver or a correlative biomarker. Finally, survival and tumor‑versus‑normal comparisons provide supportive clinical relevance but should be viewed as exploratory and not as direct validation of treatment response. Larger, response‑stratified cohorts and functional studies will be needed to more robustly characterize the mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the tumor microenvironment in CRC spans a spectrum from immune-active to immunosuppressive states, which are closely associated with ASPHD1 expression and patient prognosis. Our findings identify ASPHD1 as a candidate gene linked to immunotherapy response and a potential biomarker for TME stratification, which may inform precision strategies aimed at overcoming immunotherapy resistance in CRC. Given the exploratory nature of this work, these observations and the functional significance of ASPHD1 warrant further validation in larger cohorts and through in-depth experimental studies.
