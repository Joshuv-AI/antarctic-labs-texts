The 9th Edition of the UICC TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours: Updates and Rationale for Change
Authors: James D. Brierley, Liesbet J. Van Eycken, Meredith E. Giuliani, Brian A. Rous, Shao Hui Huang, Hisao Asamura, Mary K. Gospodarowicz, Anne W. Lee, Brian O'Sullivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe standard for assessing and recording the extent of the tumour, a necessity for most malignancies, is the Tumour Node Metastases (TNM) classification. The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM committee has defined this since the early 1950s. Due to the expanding medical knowledge of tumour behaviour and advances in imaging, pathology and therapeutic options, TNM has been updated in collaboration with the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC), the International Association for the study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) and the Federation Internationale de Gynecologie et d'Obstetrique (FIGO) on a regular basis. The 9th edition of the UICC TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours has recently been published. This review summarized the changes between the 8th and 9th edition. The evidence basis used for revision is described and referenced, with a focus on the changes in head and neck and lung cancers.

Introduction

Knowing the extent of a cancer is one of the prerequisites for treatment of any patient. The standard for assessing and recording the extent of the tumour for most malignancies is the TNM classification. This has been defined by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM committee since the early 1950s. More recently, the UICC TNM prognostic factor project, along with the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC), has been collaborating with other international bodies involved in staging, such as the International Association for the study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) and the Federation Internationale de Gynecologie et d'Obstetrique (FIGO), to update Stage classifications.

The need for these updates reflects the expanding medical knowledge of tumour behaviour and response to treatment, as well as advances in imaging, pathology and therapeutic options. The UICC TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours has recently been updated with the 9th edition [1]. Wherever possible the updates are based on published evidence‐based recommendations and analysis of databases, reflecting contemporary management. In areas where high level evidence is not available updates are based on international consensus. These updates are implemented in collaboration with the AJCC so that the UICC and AJCC TNM and stage remain congruent. The World Health Organization (WHO) topography morphology and behaviour codes (e.g., malignant, benign) and definitions are used throughout and updated to comply with the nomenclature of the WHO International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD‐O‐4) [2]. The terminology used in WHO definitions of HPV‐associated and HPV‐independent oropharyngeal cancer was introduced in this edition.

UICC continues to have as its objective to present a classification of the anatomical extent of disease for global use in high‐, middle‐ and low‐income countries. In addition, important prognostic factors required for treatment guideline adherence as well as additional recognized prognostic factors are published in tabular form. As many prognostic factors are not universally available in all low‐ and middle‐income countries, UICC maintains a separation from the anatomical extent of disease, facilitating the staging of cancers in all jurisdictions. There are rare exceptions, such as differentiated thyroid cancer, where age has been included within stage definitions.

The following is a description of the changes between the 8th (TNM8) and the 9th edition (TNM9) of the TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours that have been necessitated by advances in medical knowledge [1]. The rationale for these changes will be described in greater depth than in the TNM9 publication, in particular, using nasopharyngeal and HPV‐associated oropharyngeal cancers as examples of the clinical need for change in classification due to advances in investigation and treatment, and lung cancer as an example of the use of extensive databases to instigate change. The changes are summarized and references to the relevant open access papers containing the details are given, and where extensive are included in this paper's Supporting Information. Complete new classifications for two uncommon malignancies, parathyroid carcinoma and adrenal medulla and extra‐adrenal paraganglia tumours, are also available in the Supporting Information. Head and Neck Cancers

Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma

Substantial advances in radiology and treatment have resulted in improved prognosis for patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). To better reflect outcomes under current treatment paradigms, two multicenter working groups were established to derive and independently validate the proposed TNM9 classification [3, 4]. The derivation group involved investigators from eight academic centres in mainland China and Hong Kong, together with experts from UICC and AJCC. The cohort comprised 4914 patients (4701 M0; 213 M1) imaged by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and treated during 2014–2015 [3]. The recommended changes were externally validated in a six‐centre cohort comprising 8834 M0 and 939 M1 patients treated during 2016–2017 [4].

HPV‐Associated Oropharyngeal Carcinoma

A subset of patients with Stage I or II as defined by the TNM8 HPV‐associated oropharyngeal carcinoma classification exhibit poorer‐than‐expected outcomes. To improve prognostic stratification, the International Collaboration of Oropharyngeal Cancer Network for N‐Classification (ICON‐N) dataset was established, comprising 2053 patients treated at four institutions [5]. Radiologists reviewed pre‐treatment computerized tomography (CT) or MRI for lymph node features, including the number of abnormal lymph node and unequivocal imaging‐detected extranodal extension (iENE). Analysis revealed that presence of iENE was independently associated with worse overall survival, whereas the number of involved lymph node was not. Reclassifying each N category 1 stratum higher for iENE‐positive disease improved disease‐free and overall survival prediction, outperforming TNM8 in risk stratification. The results were validated in an independent dataset from the Centre Hospitalier de l'Université de Montréal. Importantly, in contrast to NPC where only advanced‐iENE (involvement of adjacent muscles, skin and/or neurovascular bundles on imaging) was incorporated, ICON‐N demonstrated that any unequivocal iENE, when declared with high radiologic certainty, was prognostic. Accordingly, any extent of unequivocal iENE was adopted as a clinical N category modifier. iENE is defined as unequivocal radiologic evidence of tumour extension through the lymph node capsule into perinodal fat or adjacent tissues (e.g., skin, muscle or neurovascular structures, Figure 1A), or the presence of a coalescent nodal mass comprising two or more adjacent lymph nodes with loss of intervening tissue planes and nodal capsules (Figure 1B), resulting in a single indivisible structure (Figure 1C). The use of ‘unequivocal’ evidence in the definition is underpinned by the principle to not overcall iENE by including spurious or less relevant findings that could reduce its prognostic value and are likely unimportant and remediable using contemporary radiotherapy ± chemotherapy, the usual standard treatment for these diseases.

It should be noted that in HPV‐associated OPSCC, any unequivocal iENE should be considered a cN modifier, whereas in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, only advanced iENE or clinical ENE should modify the N category.

The limitations of TNM8 pathologic TNM (pTNM) are evident in its poor alignment with post‐surgical risk stratification and its markedly skewed stage distribution, with more than 80% of surgically treated patients classified as pN1 or Stage I. Large‐scale studies have demonstrated that pathologic extranodal extension (pENE) is a critical prognostic factor that was not incorporated into TNM8. An analysis of the National Cancer Database (NCDB), comprising 14,447 patients treated across 984 facilities in the United States between 2010 and 2019 (derivation cohort, n = 7768; validation cohort, n = 6679), confirmed the independent prognostic significance of pENE and supported reclassifying pENE‐positive disease by one N category higher relative to TNM8 [6]. Within this framework, patients with 1–4 involved lymph nodes without pENE demonstrated the most favourable outcomes and were designated as pN1. Further stratification within this group revealed that patients with a single lymph node without pENE had exceptionally favourable outcomes, substantially superior to those with 2–4 lymph nodes without pENE. Notably, many patients in this lowest‐risk subgroup were treated without postoperative radiation therapy. It was proposed that a single lymph node without pENE be classified separately as N1a, while involvement of 2–4 lymph nodes without pENE be designated as N1b.

These revisions reflect the impact of contemporary therapies. Its important to be aware that the improvement in prognosis that led to this revision is due to the adoption of contemporary therapies, and that a change in stage for a given anatomical extent of disease does not mean that therapy intensity can be safely reduced, nor does it mean that subsets with less favourable outcome should have treatment intensity increased. Any changes require well structured clinical trials and more accurate risk strata definitions facilitated by a refined TNM classification can improve the design of such trials by enhancing eligibility and stratification.

Salivary Glands

Salivary gland carcinoma (SGC) encompasses a wide spectrum of histologic subtypes where surgical treatment is the foundation for curable presentations and distinct biological behaviour is evident compared with other mucosal head and neck cancers. The histopathological types included for TNM9 classification are numerous and generally follow the WHO 5th edition nomenclature which are also parsed into 2 tiers of aggression: low and high based on grade and type specific intrinsic biologic behaviour. However these do not influence the TNM classification discussed herein. In SGC of a major gland, contralateral nodal involvement is exceedingly rare (< 0.5%); therefore the TNM8 N2c category is of little added value. Analysis of the NCDB comprising 8409 patients with SGC of major glands demonstrated indistinguishable overall survival among TNM8 N2a, N2b and N2c categories, prompting refinement of the pN classification [7]. Lymph node count and pathologic extranodal extension (pENE) emerged as the dominant prognostic factors. Patients with 1–3 lymph nodes without pENE showed comparable outcomes and were consolidated as N1, whereas those with > 3 lymph nodes without pENE had outcomes similar to pENE‐positive disease and were grouped as N2. This revised nodal system was validated in an independent, multicenter international cohort [7]. Given that most SGCs are managed surgically, a parallel clinical TNM classification was adopted despite limited direct validation.

In addition, SGC may arise from either major or minor salivary glands. Prior to the 9th edition, minor SGC was staged according to the schema for squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the same anatomic site, although several SCC‐specific modifiers are not biologically relevant to SGC. Application of the major SGC–derived staging system to a single‐institution cohort of minor SGC demonstrated comparable prognostic performance, supporting a unified staging approach for major and minor SGC.

The final changes adopted were:

Unified disease entity: Major and minor SGC were unified under the single disease site ‘Salivary Gland’ and staged using the same TNM staging protocol. Cervical Lymph Nodes

In TNM8 staging for non‐virus‐associated head and neck cancers, clinical extranodal extension (cENE), but not iENE, was included as a cN modifier, with any cENE‐positive case classified as cN3 disease. Compelling evidence demonstrates that iENE provides additional prognostic information beyond cENE. Therefore in TNM9, unequivocal iENE is now accepted as a criterion for defining cN3 disease, aligning clinical staging more closely with observed outcomes [8, 9].

Upper GI Tract

There are no changes in this edition. However it is noted that the AJCC publishes prognostic groups for adenocarcinoma and SCC after neoadjuvant therapy (categories with the prefix ‘y’). A modified Ryan classification of pathological assessment after neoadjuvant therapy may be used [10].

Lower GI Tract

Colon and Rectum

A new definition has been incorporated for tumour deposits (TDs) as discrete tumour nodules in pericolic or perirectal fat, distinct from lymph nodes, extramural vascular invasion or perineural invasion [11].

It has been recognized that patients with a high T category and a low N category (Stage II) can sometimes have worse outcomes than those with low burden nodal Stage III disease, indicating that the current TNM system is non‐hierarchical [12]. The staging for Colon and Rectum carcinomas is currently undergoing revision taking these under consideration and based on analysis of NCDB data with external validation from global databases and will be published later in 2026.

It was noted that for patients treated with neoadjuvant therapy, the prefix ‘y’ is used to describe the yT, yN and y Stage. There is no separate stage classification. A modified Ryan classification of pathological assessment after neoadjuvant therapy may be used [13].

Anal Canal and Perianal Skin

Stage was revised following analysis of the National Cancer Data Base (NCDB) database, that confirmed primary tumour size is a more important prognostic factor than nodal involvement. A T4 tumour in TNM8 has a 3 year survival of 59.2% compared to 67.5% for an N1 tumour [14].

Lung, Pleural, and Thymic Tumours

Lung

The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) developed a large database to inform changes to the 8th edition which continues to facilitate decisions to make changes to TNM9. The data for the new database were obtained from Asia and Australia (56.0%), Europe (24.7%), North America (15.7%), South/Central America (3.4%), Africa and the Middle East (0.1%). After exclusions, 87,043 cases remained for analysis. External validation was performed.

There had been extensive changes in T category between the 7th and 8th editions, but analysis of this database confirmed the validity of the previous revision; however, there were revisions to: cN2 and cM1 category and stage [15, 16, 17, 18].

Breast Tumours

The classification remains unchanged. The yp classification is clarified.

Post‐treatment y‐pathological (yp) classification should be based on the y‐clinical stage information supplemented and/or modified by operative findings and pathological evaluation of the resection specimen. ypT and ypN categories correspond to the pT and pN categories, respectively. The ypT category must be based on the largest continuous focus of residual invasive cancer (if present and not including treatment‐related fibrosis). Multiple foci of residual cancer should be classified accordingly with the ‘m’ suffix. The inclusion of additional information in the pathology report to estimate the extent of residual disease by the ‘residual cancer burden method’ is recommended [25].

Although the Genitourinary malignancy classifications were not revised, carcinoma of the prostate gland is probably the malignancy most affected by stage migration. Stage migration describes a change in the proportion of T, N or M categories among patients within a defined population, following the introduction of a new means of assessing the extent of disease. Advances in imaging techniques have resulted in increased stage migration [29].

TNM8 solely used digital examination to determine the cT Category; however, in high‐income countries, MRI is frequently used to determine the T category, resulting in increased frequency of detecting more locally advanced disease. Similarly, the use of PSMA‐PET (Prostate‐Specific Membrane Antigen Positron Emission Tomography) has resulted in increased frequency of detecting nodal involvement (Figure 2A,B), which is classified as Stage IV, as well as distant metastatic disease. Many patients with Stage IV disease diagnosed by PSMA‐PET may be expected to have a better prognosis because of smaller volume disease than patients diagnosed before the introduction of modern imaging or in regions where such investigations are not available [30]. It was therefore recommended that if the imaging technique used is known, the suffix abbreviations MRI: mr and PET: PET should be used; for instance, if an MRI reveals a cT2b tumour involving more than half of one lobe, the T category would be cT2b (mr), and if node negative Stage II (mr), whereas if a PSMA‐PET revealed an involved lymph node the category would be N1 (PET), Stage IV (PET). If not known or not recorded, it is assumed that the cT category has been determined by palpation.

Essential TNM

In TNM8, an Essential TNM Classification was introduced in collaboration with the International Agency for Research in Cancer and the National Cancer Institute to facilitate the collection of anatomical extent of disease by cancer registries in low resourced areas, when complete TNM data is not available. Essential TNM schemas were originally developed for breast, cervix, colon and prostate cancer, and have been expanded to now include: liver tumours, lymphoma, oesophageal, ovarian and pancreatic, carcinomas and are available for download at www.uicc.org.

Paediatric Tumours

UICC TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours has not incorporated any classifications of paediatric tumours, since the 4th edition in 1987. This was due to the lack of an international standard staging system for many paediatric tumours. However, there was a perceived need for stage data to be collected by population‐based cancer registries. Therefore a consensus meetings was held in 2014 and 2019 resulted in the publication of recommendations on the staging of paediatric malignancies for the purposes of population surveillance [35, 36, 37]. The classifications are not intended to replace the classifications used by clinicians when treating individual patients or in treatment guidelines, but are intended to facilitate the collection of stage by population‐based cancer registries. These remain unchanged, with the exceptions of the introduction of Acute Lymphoblastic Leukaemia.

Conclusion

The staging of the anatomical extent of a carcinoma by TNM continues to evolve alongside increasing medical knowledge and progress in investigation and therapy. Increasingly, analyses of multi‐institutional and multinational databases are used to formulate and validate revisions in conjunction with the AJCC. Tumour sites that have not been revised in the UICC TNM Classification 9th edition will be reviewed alongside the AJCC and it is intended to publish further updates in this journal and further tumour sites will continue to be updated.
