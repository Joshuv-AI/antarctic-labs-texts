Examination of the healthy aging center data
Authors: Yasin Altun, Aysima Bulca Acar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackroundThis study aimed to characterize the health, functional, cognitive, nutritional, and psychological profiles of very old adults (≥ 80 years) enrolled in a community-based Healthy Aging Center (HAC) program.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted among community-dwelling individuals aged ≥ 80 years who completed home-based interviews between October 2023 and March 2024. Participants were assessed using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), Katz Index of Activities of Daily Living (ADL), Lawton–Brody Instrumental ADL (IADL), Mini-Cog, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Geriatric Depression Scale–Short Form (GDS-SF), Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2), Mini Nutritional Assessment–Short Form (MNA-SF), Timed Up and Go (TUG), and Sit-to-Stand (STS) tests. Descriptive analyses, bivariate group comparisons (χ² and t-tests), and correlation analyses were performed. Missing data were reported for each scale, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to minimize bias.ResultsA total of 337 participants (mean age 85.3 ± 4.5 years; 51.9% women) were included. Based on the CFS, 27.9% (n = 94) were classified as vulnerable, 20.8% (n = 70) as mildly frail, and 18.1% (n = 61) as moderately frail. According to the Katz ADL, 61.7% were partially dependent and 37.4% fully independent; similar patterns were observed with Lawton IADL. Cognitive impairment was more prevalent in women (57.3% vs. 32.6%, p < 0.001), who also had higher rates of depression (p < 0.001), frailty (p = 0.011), and functional dependence. Men demonstrated better mobility performance on the TUG and STS (p = 0.004). Depressive symptoms correlated with both poorer health status (r = 0.421, p < 0.001) and malnutrition risk (r = − 0.364, p < 0.001). Malnutrition was associated with greater frailty (p = 0.022) and depression (p < 0.001). Dementia was more common in the oldest participants (p = 0.005), particularly those with neurological conditions (p = 0.035).ConclusionFrailty, malnutrition, cognitive impairment, and depressive symptoms were frequent among community-dwelling very old adults, particularly women. These cross-sectional findings emphasize the need for multidisciplinary and gender-sensitive strategies within HACs to support functional independence and mental well-being. Longitudinal and multivariate studies are warranted to confirm these associations.Keywords: Healthy aging, Community-dwelling elderly, Cognitive impairment, Frailty, Malnutrition, Depression

Introduction

Population aging represents one of the most profound demographic transitions of the twenty-first century. Advances in science and technology have markedly increased life expectancy worldwide, resulting in a rapidly growing population of very old adults. By 2050, the number of people aged 80 years and older is projected to reach 426 million globally [1]. This shift is accompanied by a rise in age-related conditions such as dementia, malnutrition, frailty, reduced mobility, and functional dependency, which collectively challenge health and social care systems.

Dementia represents one of the most pressing health challenges of aging, with an estimated 75 million individuals expected to be affected by 2030 [2]. Frailty, defined as a clinical state of decreased physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to stressors, is also highly prevalent among older adults. Evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between frailty and falls, with each exacerbating the other [3]. Frailty has also been linked with depressive symptoms and reduced social support, further impacting well-being and independence in later life [4].

Understanding how these conditions cluster and interact in the “oldest old” (≥ 80 years) is essential for developing preventive and integrative care models. However, evidence from community-based or primary care settings—especially in low- and middle-income countries—remains limited. In Turkey, the Ministry of Health established Healthy Aging Centers (HACs) to provide community-level screening, health monitoring, and multidisciplinary support for older adults. Despite this initiative, empirical data from these centers are scarce, particularly concerning the interaction between frailty, mood, cognition, and nutrition in very old adults.

The present study aimed to characterize the multidimensional health profiles of adults aged 80 years and older attending a HAC in Niğde, Turkey. Specifically, we examined frailty, cognitive performance, depressive symptoms, nutritional status, and functional capacity, and explored their associations with demographic and health-related variables. By focusing on a community-dwelling, advanced-age population, this study provides baseline evidence for improving integrated care within HACs. We hypothesized that frailty, malnutrition, depression, and cognitive impairment would be interrelated and more prevalent among women than men.

Methods

Study design and participants

This community-based, cross-sectional observational, descriptive-analytical study was conducted between October 2023 and March 2024 in Turkey. Eligible participants were community-dwelling individuals aged ≥ 80 years who were identified through family physicians’ records and agreed to participate. A total of 525 individuals were invited, of whom 337 agreed to participate (response rate 64.2%). Participants were contacted by telephone, and verbal informed consent was obtained prior to home-based interviews.

Patient exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: age ≥ 80, community-dwelling, ability to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria: inability to complete assessments due to severe hearing impairment, illiteracy for specific instruments, or communication difficulties (Fig. 1).

Participant flow diagram

The study protocol was approved by the Niğde University Research Ethics Review Committee (Approval No: 2024/50) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures adhered to ethical guidelines, and participant anonymity was strictly maintained.

Data collection

Face-to-face interviews were conducted by trained medical personnel who received standardized training on assessment administration and scoring procedures. Demographic information, health history, and medication use were collected through interviews and verified against medical records. All data were entered into SPSS 25, checked for inconsistencies, and coded according to pre-defined criteria. Outliers and missing values were reviewed and handled using listwise deletion for each specific outcome.

Outcome measurements

A multidimensional geriatric assessment was performed using validated Turkish versions of standardized instruments:

Clinical frailty scale (CFS): Assesses functional capacity, physical performance, mental status, and social support. A score of ≥ 5 indicates frailty [5]. Turkish validation was conducted by Özsürekci et al. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.80 [6].

Katz index of independence in activities of daily living (Katz ADL): Evaluates basic daily living skills (0 = total dependence, 6 = full independence) [7], with Turkish validation by Arik et al. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.84 [8].

Lawton instrumental activities of daily living (Lawton IADL): Assesses instrumental activities (0 = complete dependence, 8 = complete independence) [9]. Turkish validation was performed by Işık et al. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.84 [10].

Mini-Cog: Combines three-word recall and clock drawing. Scores 0–2 indicate cognitive impairment [11]. The sensitivity of the Mini-Cog was 0.76 and the specificity was 0.73 [12].

Mini-Mental state examination (MMSE): Cognitive screening tool (≥ 25 = normal, 19–24 = mild, 10–19 = moderate, ≤ 10 = severe impairment). Validated Turkish versions are available for literate and illiterate populations. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.86 [13–15].

Geriatric depression Scale – Short form (GDS-SF): A 15-item tool; 0–4 = no depression, 5–8 = mild, 9–11 = moderate, 12–15 = severe [16]. Turkish validation by Durmaz et al. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.92 [17].

Patient health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2): Two-item depression screener [18] The Cronbach’s alpha for PHQ-2 was 0.76 [19].

Mini nutritional Assessment–Short form (MNA-SF): Evaluates nutritional status (0–7 = malnourished, 8–11 = at risk, 12–14 = normal) [20]. The Turkish version was validated by Sarıkaya et al. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.66 [21].

Timed up and go (TUG): Assess mobility and fall risk. Participants stood from a chair, walked 3 m, returned, and sat; time in seconds recorded [22].

Sit-to-Stand (STS): Evaluates lower extremity strength and mobility; number of stands in 30 s recorded [23].

Instruments were chosen based on prior evidence linking frailty, cognitive function, depression, nutritional status, and mobility with functional dependence and quality of life in very old adults. The scale were selected by Ministy of Health in a standartized format, which is why similar scales were also included.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations, medians (IQR 25–75), and ranges, while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test, linear-by-linear association, or Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test for categorical variables. We applied Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons when testing the KATZ-ADL, Lawton IADL, GDS-SF groups and MMSE Groups. Adjusted α = 0.05/4 = 0.0125. Correlations between continuous variables were analyzed using Spearman’s rho. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to identify optimal age cutoffs for Katz ADL and MMSE scores, with the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals reported. The Youden index was used to determine cutoff points. Given the exploratory nature of this study and sample size constraints, analyses were limited to descriptive, bivariate, and correlation tests, without multivariate modeling. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Age was inversely correlated with cognitive performance, as indicated by both Mini-Cog (r = − 0.165, p = 0.004) and MMSE (r = − 0.208, p < 0.001) scores. A strong positive correlation was observed between Mini-Cog and MMSE (r = 0.597, p < 0.001).

Higher GDS-SF scores were moderately associated with lower MNA-SF (r = − 0.364, p < 0.001), Mini-Cog (r = − 0.234, p < 0.001), and MMSE (r = − 0.217, p < 0.001) scores.

In terms of depression, women scored significantly higher on PHQ-2 (p = 0.009) and GDS-SF (p < 0.001) scales. Performance in physical function tests also differed by sex: success in the sit-to-stand and timed up-and-go tests was lower in women (59.4%) than men (74.1%, p = 0.004). Cognitive assessments showed that cognitive impairment prevalence was significantly higher in women according to Mini-Cog (57.3% vs. 32.6%, p < 0.001) and dementia prevalence higher according to MMSE (mild dementia: 28.8% women vs. 14.5% men; p = 0.004). Furthermore, malnutrition was associated with increased frailty and depressive symptoms, with participants classified as malnourished showing significantly higher GDS-SF (p < 0.001) and CFS (p = 0.022) scores compared to those with normal nutritional status.

Age was significantly higher in participants with dementia as assessed by MMSE compared to those without dementia (median [IQR]: 85 [82–90] vs. 83 [81–87] years; p = 0.005). Dementia was also associated with higher frailty (CFS, p < 0.001), greater dependency on Katz ADL (p = 0.001), and higher failure rates in balance and mobility tests (p = 0.021) (Table 5).

In the multivariate logistic regression model, higher functional independence (Lawton IADL), beter nutritional status (MNA-SF), and higher Mini-Cog scores were independently associated with beter cognitive performance as measured by MMSE (Table 6). Other variables, including age, gender, frailty, depressive symptoms, and comorbidity count, were not significant predictors. The model demonstrated a good fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test p = 0.616). The Lawton IADL score showed a statistically significant association with MMSE status. Specifically, each one-point increase in IADL score was associated with approximately a 24% reduction in the odds of cognitive impairment (OR = 0.756). Nutritional status, as measured by MNA-SF, emerged as a significant independent predictor of cognitive performance. Each unit increase in the MNA-SF score was associated with a 25% increase in the likelihood of maintaining normal cognitive function (OR = 1.250). The Mini-Cog score demonstrated a strong inverse relationship with cognitive impairment. Each one-point increase in Mini-Cog score nearly halved the odds of poor MMSE performance (OR = 0.480), confirming Mini-Cog as a robust and independent predictor of cognitive status.

ROC analysis revealed that age > 85.5 years was a significant cutoff for predicting dementia. ROC analysis (N = 298) yielded AUC = 0.596 (95% CI 0.528–0.665; sensitivity 47,8%, specificity 71%, p = 0,005) (Fig. 2). Sex-stratified AUCs were: males (N = 145), AUC = 0.671 (95% CI 0.571–0.772; p = 0,001) (Fig. 3); and females (N = 153), AUC = 0.542 (95% CI 0.449–0.636; p = 0,366) (Fig. 4). In the male population, a particular emphasis has been placed on the identification of individuals over the age of 84.5 as a distinguishing factor for the presence of dementia. The findings of this study demonstrate that, in the case of women, age does not have a significant distinguishing effect on the presence of dementia.

A moderate positive correlation was observed between Katz ADL and Lawton IADL scores (r = 0.465; p < 0.001), indicating consistency between basic and instrumental activities of daily living measures. These results emphasize the interconnected nature of frailty, cognitive impairment, functional dependency, depression, and malnutrition among the oldest-old, with women exhibiting higher vulnerability across multiple domains.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, frailty, cognitive impairment, depression, and malnutrition were highly prevalent among adults ≥ 80 attending a HAC in Turkey, with women showing greater vulnerability across multiple domains.

This study involving 337 elderly participants (mean age 85.27 ± 4.51 years), 44.9% were classified as frail, 62.6% exhibited dependency in daily activities, 28.4% had depression, 4.2% were malnourished, and 31.9% were diagnosed with dementia. These findings underscore the complexity of health issues in the oldest-old population and highlight the need for comprehensive assessments in community-based care settings.

The use of the CFS in our study aligns with international trends, as the tool is increasingly applied outside its original Canadian and UK contexts to capture functional and social vulnerabilities [24]. The prevalence of frailty in our cohort (44.9%) is consistent with the higher end of global estimates among adults aged ≥ 65 years, which range from 4.0% to 59.1% depending on the population and assessment tool used [25].

Frailty is influenced by multiple determinants, including comorbidities, polypharmacy, self-rated health, and education [26]. Our results further suggest that malnutrition and dementia contribute substantially to frailty risk. Evidence suggests that inadequate nutrition contributes directly to functional decline and vulnerability to adverse outcomes in older adults [27], while neurodegenerative conditions such as vascular dementia and Alzheimer’s disease can exacerbate frailty, mobility limitations, and functional dependency [28]. These associations support the concept of a reciprocal, detrimental cycle linking frailty, malnutrition, and cognitive impairment.

Loneliness and social isolation in older adults have been recognized as key psychosocial factors that contribute to frailty progression. These conditions trigger complex brain–body interactions, increasing vulnerability to physical and cognitive decline through mechanisms such as stress, inflammation, and behavioral changes. Consequently, they should be carefully considered in geriatric assessments and intervention strategies to mitigate adverse health outcomes [29, 30].

Frailty was more common in women in our cohort. Previous studies from Turkey reported a similar trend, though statistical significance was inconsistent [31]. Elderly women are not only physically but also socially more vulnerable, and this vulnerability increases their risk of financial exploitation. There is a strong association between frailty and financial abuse, which appears to be particularly pronounced among older women [32].

Other investigations suggest that frailty may also increase in older men, possibly due to testosterone deficiency, underutilization of healthcare services, and greater vulnerability to social isolation [33]. These findings indicate that both biological and sociocultural factors shape frailty risk, and sex-related differences remain inconclusive.

Another key finding was the association between malnutrition and depression. Prior studies consistently demonstrate that malnutrition elevates the risk of depressive symptoms in older adults [34–36]. For example, a Bangladeshi study found that elderly individuals with malnutrition had a significantly higher risk of depression, with an overall prevalence of depressive symptoms reaching 83.75% [34]. Similar evidence has shown that malnourished elders are nearly three times more likely to develop depression compared with their well-nourished peers [36]. Among the elderly, depression may significantly predispose individuals to malnutrition [37]. Although depression is often more common in women, social isolation, comorbidities, reduced quality of life, and fear of death may contribute to gender differences. Supporting this, both PHQ-2 and GDS-SF scores were higher among women in our sample.

Women in our cohort also showed significantly higher rates of cognitive impairment, dementia, functional dependency, and frailty compared with men. A Chinese study likewise found associations between poor nutritional status, depressive symptoms, and cognitive decline, with mild cognitive impairment prevalence reported at 14.35% [35]. Although our data did not show a statistically significant difference in malnutrition between participants with and without cognitive impairment, the extended family structure in Turkish society may help protect nutritional status, as 60.7% of participants had normal nutrition.

Dementia prevalence increases markedly with advancing age, representing a major public health concern worldwide. A large U.S. cohort study (n = 106,150) demonstrated a steep rise in dementia incidence, from 7.6 per 1,000 person-years at age 66 to 159.6 per 1,000 person-years by age 100, highlighting the exponential effect of aging on cognitive decline [38]. Global estimates suggest higher dementia prevalence in women; however, in some populations, men below 70 years show higher diagnosis rates [39]. In our study, 21.8% had mild dementia and 10.1% had severe dementia, with dementia significantly more frequent in women and in those aged > 85 years. A study from Thailand reported a dementia prevalence of 18% and identified risk factors such as advanced age, low education, diabetes, and low social participation [40]. Additionally, earlier onset of type 2 diabetes has been linked to greater dementia risk [41]. These findings indicate that age, sex, cardiovascular factors, and educational status are key determinants of dementia in late life. Early identification of high-risk individuals and targeted interventions addressing modifiable risk factors may therefore be crucial in reducing dementia burden in aging populations.

The association between dementia and frailty observed in our study aligns with prior evidence. Dementia-related symptoms—including memory loss, impaired executive function, and mood disturbances—contribute to reduced independence and increased frailty, creating a cycle of vulnerability. In our cohort, individuals with dementia had significantly higher rates of frailty, dependency in activities of daily living, and impaired mobility.

Limitations

While frailty and dependency were assessed in all participants, some standardized instruments (Mini-Cog, MMSE, GDS-SF) could not be administered to all due to illiteracy, hearing impairments, speech difficulties, or mobility limitations, resulting in missing data. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences, and the single-center, community-based sample limits generalizability to broader populations. Additionally, the sample sizes for certain subgroup analyses were small, which may have affected statistical power and increased the risk of Type I error.

Conclusion

Our findings revealed a high prevalence of frailty, malnutrition risk, cognitive impairment, and depression among the oldest-old in Turkey, with notably higher risks in women. Cognitive decline and malnutrition may interact bidirectionally, highlighting the importance of addressing nutritional deficiencies in preventing cognitive deterioration. Preventive approaches—including healthy diets, physical activity, and early screening—should be prioritized to mitigate frailty and dementia risk. A multidisciplinary, integrated healthcare strategy, such as that implemented in Healthy Aging Centers, is essential for safeguarding elderly health at local, national, and global levels.
