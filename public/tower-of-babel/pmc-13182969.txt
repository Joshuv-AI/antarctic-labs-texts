Antenatal depressive symptoms impair offspring neurodevelopment by inducing maternal gut microbiota dysbiosis during pregnancy
Authors: Fangyue Zhou, Lulu Wang, Yi Zhao, Chuqiao Hu, Yicong Meng, Jianxia Fan, William D Fraser, Jean-Patrice Baillargeon, Fengxiu Ouyang, Stephen James Lye, Cindy-Lee Dennis, Jian Shen, Yanting Wu, Hefeng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe effects of maternal antenatal depression (AND) across different stages of pregnancy on offspring neurodevelopment remain poorly understood, and the underlying microbiota-related mechanisms are largely unknown. In a multicenter prospective cohort of 2053 pregnant women, we found that elevated depressive symptoms at any trimester were significantly associated with delayed infant neurodevelopment. Using a nested case‒control design with 16S rRNA sequencing of 504 maternal fecal samples, we identified a consistent reduction of butyrate-producing bacteria and disruption of amino acid metabolism in women with AND symptoms – features that correlated with poorer infant cognitive outcomes. To establish causality, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from women with AND symptoms was administered to germ-free dams, resulting in impaired intestinal barrier integrity, heightened neuroinflammatory signaling, and altered polyunsaturated fatty acid and amino acid metabolism in fetal brains at E18.5, leading to postnatal cognitive deficits in the offspring. Remarkably, maternal butyrate supplementation partially rescued these molecular and neurodevelopmental abnormalities. Together, these findings reveal a mechanistic link between maternal mood, gut microbial ecology, and fetal brain development, and identify the maternal gut microbiota and its metabolites as potential therapeutic targets to prevent the intergenerational effects of antenatal depression.Keywords: Antenatal depression, gut microbiota, neurodevelopment, fetal brain, butyrate supplementation

Introduction

Despite increasing recognition of its clinical significance, research specifically focused on antenatal depression (AND) remains limited compared to the extensive literature on postpartum depression. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that the global prevalence of AND – whether defined by symptoms or clinical diagnosis – approaches approximately 20% worldwide1 and 19.7% in Mainland China,2 highlighting its substantial impact on public health. AND is associated with a range of adverse maternal and infant outcomes, including increased risks of miscarriage, preterm birth, and low birth weight.3-5 Many studies have explored the relationship between maternal depressive symptoms during pregnancy and infant neurodevelopment; however, the results are inconsistent. For example, in a meta-analysis of eight studies, only five found a significant association between antenatal depression and poorer infant cognitive outcomes.6 These discrepancies may stem from differences in the timing, duration, and chronicity of fetal exposure to maternal depressive symptoms. Importantly, most existing research to date has concentrated on depression during the third trimester, with limited attention paid to the possible effects of earlier or persistent depressive symptoms throughout pregnancy.

The mechanisms underlying the impact of AND symptoms on offspring neurodevelopment also remain limited. Previous studies found that adverse emotional states, such as depression, can alter the composition of the gut microbiota.7 Recently, several studies have explored the association between the gut microbiota and antenatal depression. Some research has found that anxiety and depressive symptoms during pregnancy are associated with decreased gut microbiota diversity.8 Several specific bacteria, such as Intestinibacter and Escherichia-Shigella, are protective factors against AND.9,10 However, existing research primarily consists of cross-sectional studies or studies with small sample sizes and does not examine microbiota dynamic changes across the entire pregnancy to guide clinical intervention. Furthermore, maternal mood disorder during pregnancy can also influence offspring gut microbiota composition,11 and offspring microbiota may be linked to their neurodevelopmental abnormalities.12 Maternal microbiota can have a significant impact on offspring growth and development. Research has shown that short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced by maternal gut bacteria during pregnancy can affect the differentiation and development of embryonic neural, intestinal, and pancreatic cells.13 Nevertheless, it remains unknown whether antenatal depressive symptoms could affect offspring neurodevelopment through alterations in maternal or infant gut microbiota.

To address this significant clinical limitation, a multicenter prospective cohort study was designed to examine the effect of maternal depressive symptoms across different trimesters on infant neurodevelopment. This study also included a nested case‒control component to investigate the association between AND symptoms and maternal gut microbiota, which was combined with the use of a fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) model in germ-free mice to clarify how the gut microbiota may mediate the effects of AND symptoms on offspring neurodevelopment.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study involves two population-based investigations. The first is a prospective cohort study including a subsample from the Sino-Canadian Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (SCHeLTI) intervention study (trial registration number: ChiCTR1800017773).14 The SCHeLTI study, currently underway in Shanghai, China, is a multicenter, cluster-randomized, parallel-group superiority trial of a community-family-mother-child intervention designed to prevent childhood overweight and obesity. Participants were recruited during the preconception period or the first trimester. Women who were recruited preconceptionally were intended to become pregnant within 12 months, and those recruited in the first trimester had to have a singleton pregnancy and be less than 14 weeks of gestation. This prospective study included women who gave birth between July 2019 and July 2022. The exclusion criteria included: (1) not completing at least one depression assessment during pregnancy using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS); (2) incomplete pregnancy and neonatal outcome data; (3) a history of major mental health disorders such as schizophrenia; and (4) the use of antipsychotic medications. All participants provided informed consent.

Participant data were collected using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform for data management and storage. The demographic details of the eligible population included age, partner's age, height and weight before pregnancy, marital status, ethnicity, residency status, education level, occupation, income, reproductive history (parity, number of previous abortions), and lifestyle factors (history of smoking and drinking). Height and pre-pregnancy weight were documented to calculate the pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI). A trained data clerk administered the EPDS during the first (6–14 weeks), second (24–28 weeks), and third (34–36 weeks) trimesters. In this study, participants with an EPDS score ≥10 were considered to be at increased risk of depression. The sensitivity and specificity of a cut-off value of 10 or higher have been reported as 0.85 and 0.84, respectively, for detecting depression in pregnant and postpartum women.15 Infant neurocognitive assessments were collected by trained personnel at scheduled research visits and included assessments of cognitive development (Ages and Stages Questionnaire-316 at 3, 6, and 12 months of age), as well as early language acquisition in Mandarin (Putonghua) (MacArthur Short Form Vocabulary Checklist: Level 117 at 12 months of age.

The second study is a nested case‒control study based on a prospective cohort. The additional inclusion criteria were: (1) completion of the EPDS at all three trimesters and (2) no acute infectious diseases such as gastroenteritis. Participants with at least one EPDS score ≥10 during any trimester were assigned to the antenatal depression (AND) at-risk group, while those with all three scores < 10 formed the control group. A total of 135 women were enrolled in the AND at-risk group and matched to 135 controls using a 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) approach. PSM was conducted using the MatchIt package in R 4.1.2, matching on age, pre-pregnancy BMI, education level, parity, delivery mode, gestational age, Group B Streptococcus (GBS) infection, corticosteroid use, antibiotics, and probiotics.

Human fecal sample collection and DNA extraction

Maternal fecal samples were collected during the first (6–14 weeks), second (24–28 weeks), and third (34–36 weeks) trimesters. At the same time, mothers were invited to voluntarily provide infant fecal samples at three time points (0–4 d, 6 weeks, and 3 months after birth). All samples were stored at −80 °C for long-term preservation (Figure 1a).

Following the manufacturer’s protocols, the DNeasy 96 PowerSoil Pro QIAcube HT Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands) was used to extract DNA from the collected fecal samples. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 5'-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNG GCWGCAG-3' and 5'-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGG ACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3'. The libraries were generated and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were obtained by denoising each paired-end FASTQ file using the R package DADA2. Sequence normalization was performed per sample to minimize potential bias resulting from variations in sequencing depths across samples. ASVs were then taxonomically classified by aligning them with the SILVA database.

Microbiome analysis

For microbiome biostatistical analyses, the R language (version 4.1.2) was used. Alpha diversity indices, including evenness, observed ASVs, the Shannon index, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (Faith’s pd), were calculated using QIIME2. Beta diversity analysis was conducted with four distance metrics: Unweighted, weighted UniFrac, Bray‒Curtis, and Jaccard, and visualized through principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) with the R packages “ape” and “ggpubr”. The significance of differences in microbiota structure among groups was evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) via the R package “vegan”. R² values and P-values were displayed using the R package “pheatmap”. To identify differential gut microbiota, we first performed co-abundance network analysis on all microorganisms with the FastSpar algorithm through QIIME2. Subsequently, co-abundance groups (CAGs), which have similar functions, were identified using the ‘vegan’ and ‘WGCNA’ packages.18 Generalized linear model (GLM) analysis was subsequently performed using the “QuantPsyc” and “lmtest” packages to identify differentially expressed CAGs between the PND and control groups. The functional modules predicted by PICRUSt2.3 were used to estimate metagenomic functions. LDA effect size (LefSe) analysis between groups was conducted via the “microbiomeMarker” package to explore the impact of prenatal depressive symptoms on microbial functional pathways. For vertical transmission analysis of maternal and infant gut microbiota, we paired maternal samples from different trimesters with infant samples from various time points. Shared ASVs analysis was conducted, followed by an assessment of the impact of ASVs showing significant between-group differences in shared rates on infant neurodevelopment. Additionally, the relationship between changes in microbial feature abundance and infant neurodevelopment scores was examined using MaAslin2 (Figure 2).

Fecal microbiota transplantation in germ-free mice and sample collection

To validate how the maternal gut microbiota is linked to AND impacts offspring neurodevelopment, we performed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in germ-free mice using a rational donor selection and multi-donor pooling strategy. To specifically highlight the impact of typical AND-associated gut microbiota dysbiosis on offspring neurodevelopment, we selected three mothers in the AND at-risk group who exhibited consistent depressive symptoms (EPDS ≥10) across all trimesters. The gut microbiota at three trimesters mirrored the core dysbiotic signature of the AND at-risk group, notably the depletion of butyrate-producing bacteria-containing CAGs. To capture the dynamic gestational microbiome, fecal samples from the first, second, and third trimesters of these three donors (9 samples total) were pooled to generate the AND-FMT inoculum. A Control-FMT inoculum was similarly prepared by pooling 9 samples from three consistently healthy donors (EPDS <10 each trimester). Thirty 6- to 8-week-old female germ-free C57BL/6J mice (GFM) were randomly assigned to three groups based on body weight: (1) Group 1 (Control_M, Control group): received FMT from control mothers; (2) Group 2 (AND_M, AND group): received FMT from AND at-risk mothers; (3) Group 3 (AND + butyr_M, Rescue group): received FMT from AND mothers plus sodium butyrate (NaB) in the drinking water during pregnancy. FMT was administered via oral gavage three times weekly for two weeks, after which the mice were allowed to mate. Half of the dams underwent cesarean sections at embryonic day 18.5 to collect samples; the others delivered naturally, and their offspring completed the Morris water maze and open field test at 12 weeks of age. Fecal samples from dams were collected at three points – pre-FMT, pre-mating, pre-terminal (E18.5) – for microbiota-sequencing. Maternal serum, intestine, placenta, and fetal brain samples were collected for analysis (Figure 3a).

Morris water maze test

To investigate the impact of FMT on offspring learning and memory abilities, the Morris water maze (MWM) test was conducted on offspring mice at 12 weeks of age. This test involves a circular pool, 90 cm in diameter and 60 cm deep, filled with water, and randomly divided into four quadrants. A 9 cm diameter hidden platform was placed in a random quadrant and submerged 1 cm below the water surface. From days 0–4, the mice learned the location of the platform. On the fifth day, the platform was removed, and the mice were placed in the pool from the opposite quadrant to the target area, then allowed to swim for 60 s. The time spent in the target quadrant and the latency to reach the previous platform location were recorded.

Open field test

To investigate the impact of FMT on offspring anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, the open field test was conducted on offspring mice at 12 weeks of age. The apparatus consisted of a square arena (42.5 × 42.5 × 40 cm). The area was virtually divided into a central zone and a peripheral zone, with the peripheral zone defined as the area within 8 cm of the walls. After a period of environmental habituation, each mouse was placed in the arena and allowed to explore freely for 5 min in the absence of the experimenter. An automated video tracking system was used to record moving distance, moving distance of the center area, speed, number of entries into the center area, and time spent in the center zone.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry, LC–MS/MS

Fetal brain samples were analyzed using LC–MS/MS to examine metabolomics. The tissues were placed in tubes with glass beads, mixed with methanol-water (4:1), cooled, homogenized, ultrasonicated, and frozen overnight. The samples were then centrifuged, filtered, and analyzed using a Waters UPLC HSS T3 C18 column at 45 °C with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min, employing a mixture of acetonitrile and water with 0.1% formic acid. The MS parameters included a spray voltage of 3800 V and a capillary temperature of 320 °C. Data processing was performed using Progenesis QI, applying OPLS-DA to identify metabolites with a variable importance in the projection (VIP > 1, P < 0.05). KEGG pathway enrichment identified significant pathways (Q < 0.2).

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic data processing

Fetal mouse brain samples underwent RNA-seq for transcriptomics. The RNA was extracted, measured for concentration and purity with a NanoDrop, and checked for integrity using an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared with VAHTS V5 and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Low-quality reads were removed using fastp, and the reads were then aligned to the reference genome with Hisat2. Gene expression was quantified as FPKM, normalized, and analyzed with DESeq2. DEGs (P < 0.05, fold change >2) were identified, and KEGG pathway enrichment was performed (Q <0.25), visualized in R 4.1.2.

Immunohistochemistry, IHC

The maternal mouse colon was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, cryoprotected, and embedded in OCT. The colon was then snap-frozen and sectioned. The sections were washed, permeabilized, and blocked with goat serum, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (anti-ZO-1: ab221546, Abcam, UK; anti-ZO-2: 18900-1-AP, Proteintech, USA; anti-Claudin1: ab307692, Abcam, UK) at 4 °C overnight and then with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. After washing, a mounting medium containing DAPI was applied to seal the specimen. The sections were observed under an upright fluorescence microscope. SlideViewer 2.5 scanning software was used to select target tissue areas for imaging. After imaging, three fields of view per slide were randomly selected. The integrated optical density (IOD) of the positive staining and the corresponding positive pixel area (Area) within the selected fields were measured using ImageJ software. The mean optical density (MOD) value was then calculated with the formula: MOD = IOD/Area.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, ELISA

To assess the levels of the inflammatory response in the maternal serum and fetal brain, we employed ELISA to quantify TNF-α and IL-1β levels. Experimental protocols involving various inflammatory factors were followed according to the ELISA kit instructions (TNF-alpha Mouse Uncoated ELISA Kit, 88-7324-88; Mouse IL-1β Uncoated ELISA, 88-7013A-88, ThermoFisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The kit had been coated on the reaction well and processed with antibodies. The optical density was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader. A standard curve was generated, and the sample concentrations were calculated using the standard curve equation.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 software and R language (version 4.1.2) were used to perform the statistical and data analysis. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Student's t-tests were employed for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann‒Whitney U test was used for non-normal distributions. The chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was applied to categorical variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For the prospective cohort study, we initially divided the patients into groups based on the EPDS scores. The AND at-risk group had an EPDS ≥10 in three trimesters during pregnancy, while the AND-free group had an EPDS <10 at all three time points. Neurodevelopmental outcomes (assessed by the ASQ-3 and MacArthur Level I) in the offspring were compared between the two groups using Spearman's correlation analysis. Additionally, we treated the EPDS score as a continuous variable and analyzed the association between EPDS scores across different trimesters and infant neurodevelopmental outcomes at various time points. Since the infants had repeated assessments of their neurodevelopmental outcomes, repeated measure analyses were conducted using generalized estimating equations (GEE) models to obtain crude and adjusted β and 95% CI.

Results

Antenatal depressive symptoms influence infant neurodevelopment

In the cohort study involving 2053 women, 326 (15.9%) had at least one EPDS ≥10 during pregnancy (AND at-risk group), and 1634 (79.6%) did not (control group) (Supplementary Tables 1–2). Infant neurodevelopment was assessed up to 1 y of age. Infants in the AND at-risk group scored lower on problem-solving tasks at both 3 and 6 months (P = 0.039, 0.040) (Table 1). EPDS scores as a continuous variable showed negative correlations with infant problem-solving and social skills at 3 months (Supplementary Table 3). Repeated-measures GEE models also indicated that higher EPDS scores in all trimesters were associated with poorer problem-solving (Table 2). In the nested case‒control study involving 270 participants (Figure 1a), infants of mothers with AND symptoms exhibited lower problem-solving abilities at 3 months and poorer social skills at 6 months (Supplementary Table 5, Figures 1b and c). These results suggest lasting effects of maternal AD symptoms on infant neurodevelopment, despite a smaller sample size.

AND symptoms and gut microbiota dysbiosis during pregnancy

After quality control, 504 maternal fecal samples from different trimesters were available for analysis. Based on whether the maternal EPDS score was ≥10 at the time of fecal sample collection, all samples were divided into three groups, as shown in Figure 1d: the Red group, comprising samples collected from the at-risk group when EPDS score was ≥10 (depressive phase, AND-D group); the Blue group, comprising samples collected from the at-risk group when EPDS score was < 10 (non-depressive phase, AND-N group); and the Green group, consisting of samples from the healthy control group, which had EPDS scores < 10 throughout pregnancy. The EPDS scores in both the AND-D and the AND-N groups were significantly higher than those in the control group (P < 0.0001) (Figure 1e–f, Supplementary Figure 1a–h). These findings suggest that changes in the gut microbiota of mothers with depressive symptoms may occur not only during depressive episodes but also persist during non-depressive phases.

Initially, we assessed how host factors influence the maternal gut microbiota during pregnancy using PERMANOVA. The results showed that the trimester was the most significant factor, with AND symptoms second (Figure 2a). Notably, microbiota differences were significant between the first trimester and both the second (P < 0.05) and third trimesters (P < 0.001), but not between the second and third trimesters (Supplementary Figure 2a–i). PERMANOVA, adjusting for the gestational period, revealed significant differences between AND-D and controls (FDR P = 0.019) and AND-N and controls (FDR P = 0.010) (Figure 2b). Stratified by trimester, AND-D versus controls differed significantly in the first and third trimesters, while AND-N versus controls showed marginal significance in the second and third trimesters (Supplementary Table 6). These findings suggest AND symptoms affect the gut microbiota throughout pregnancy, although alpha-diversity showed no significant group differences. (Supplementary Figure 2j–q).

To identify the gut microbiota linked to AND symptoms, all microbial samples were divided into 50 co-abundance groups (CAGs) representing functional ecological networks using the FastSpar algorithm (Supplementary Table 7). After adjusting for key confounders – pre-pregnancy BMI, education, gestational age, maternal age, gestational diabetes, antibiotics, probiotics, growth hormone deficiency, and GBS infection – generalized linear modeling analysis identified 15 significantly altered CAGs (Figure 2d). Compared to controls, the abundances of CAG3, CAG5, CAG6, CAG7, and CAG29 (mainly Eubacterium, Ruminococcaceae, Faecalibacterium, and Oscillospiraceae, producers of butyric acid) were consistently reduced in the AND-D and AND-N groups, while CAG36 (mainly Streptococcus) increased (Figure 2c, Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). Seven CAGs had significant negative correlations with EPDS scores, with their genera largely matching those depleted in the AND groups (Supplementary Table 10). Functional prediction revealed disrupted pathways, including C5-branched dibasic acid metabolism, peptidoglycan synthesis, polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism (valine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, cysteine, and methionine) during both the depressive and non-depressive phases (Figure 2e, f).

Maternal gut microbiota dysbiosis in women with AND symptoms and infant neurodevelopmental abnormalities

We collected 290 infant fecal samples for 16S rRNA analysis. PERMANOVA showed that sampling timepoints, delivery mode, and feeding type significantly affected microbial composition, with sampling timepoints being the most influential (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure 3a). This finding indicates notable temporal changes in the infant gut microbiota (Supplementary Figure 3b, c). Maternal AND symptoms had no significant effect (Supplementary Figure 3a, d, Supplementary Table 11). Regarding maternal-neonatal microbial transmission, only ASV_13642 (Clostridium sensu stricto 1) demonstrated significantly higher mother-infant sharing in the AND at-risk group (P = 0.034) (Supplementary Figure 3e), showing a nominal association with infant neurodevelopmental scores (Supplementary Figure 3f-g); however, it was rarely found in only five samples.

After adjusting for maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, gestational age, education, GHD, GDM, antibiotics, and probiotics, AND symptom-associated CAGs correlated with neurodevelopmental outcomes (Figure 2g). Specifically, CAGs with significantly reduced abundance in the third trimester demonstrated numerous positive correlations with infant neurocognitive scores. Further analysis, controlling for confounders, found that downregulated microbial pathways in the AND-D and AND-N groups during the second and third trimesters were positively correlated with infant problem-solving at 3 months and personal-social skills at 6 months (Figure 2h).

FMT from AND at-risk group induces fetal brain developmental abnormalities in offspring

To assess the effect of maternal AND symptom-associated gut microbiota on offspring neurocognitive development, we established an FMT model using germ-free mice before conception (Figure 3a). The gut microbiota composition of dams in the AND_M group before mating (post-FMT) and at E18.5 more closely resembled that of the donors from the AND at-risk group, whereas the gut microbiota of dams in the Control_M group before mating (post-FMT) and at E18.5 was more similar to that of the donors from the Control group (Figure 3b). This indicates successful establishment of the FMT animal model. Additionally, the level of placental butyrate was higher in the Control_M group compared to the AND_M group (P < 0.1) (Figure 3c).

The results from the Morris water maze test showed that, compared to the Control_M group, the AND_M offspring had a longer escape latency on Day 3 (P < 0.05), took more time to cross the platform during testing (P < 0.05), and crossed fewer times (P < 0.05). This indicates impaired cognition in the AND_M group (Figure 3d–f). AND_M offspring showed no significant differences in the open field test parameters (including moving distance of the center area, speed, number of entries into the center area, and time spent in the center zone) compared to the control group (Supplementary Figure 4). Maternal AND-related gut microbiota are linked to abnormal neurodevelopment, suggesting that the maternal gut microbiota may influence fetal brain development.

Metabolomic analysis showed distinct metabolite profiles in fetal brains between the Control_M and AND_M groups (Supplementary Figure 5a, b). The AND_M group exhibited decreased PUFA pathway activity, including in linoleic, α-linolenic, and arachidonic acids, with lower levels of metabolites such as anandamide and PC (14:0/20:4). The results also showed downregulated pathways in amino acid and taurine metabolism, with reduced metabolites, including L-alanine (Figure 3g–k). An increase in inflammation-related Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis was noted (Supplementary Figure 5h). Transcriptomic sequencing revealed lower expression of lipid metabolism pathways and synapse pathways (including glutamatergic and serotonergic) in AND_M, with key genes such as Pla2g4b and Pla2g4d being reduced (Figure 4a, c, e, f). Conversely, inflammation pathways, including TNF, IL-17, Toll-like receptor, NOD-like receptor, chemokine, and cytokine interactions, were upregulated, with genes such as ccl21b and cd14 increasing (Figure 4a, c, g, h). ELISA confirmed significantly higher levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in the AND_M group (Figure 4j, k).

FMT from AND at-risk group induced maternal impaired gut barrier function and heightened inflammatory responses

16S rRNA analysis showed maternal gut microbiota dysbiosis in the AND at-risk group. IHC revealed significantly lower expression of tight junction proteins (claudin-1, ZO-1, and ZO-2) in the AND_M group (Figure 5a-d), indicating impaired gut barrier function. Metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses of the fetal brain showed increased inflammation-related pathways. The ELISA results indicated higher levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in the maternal serum of the AND_M group (Figure 5e-f), suggesting inflammatory activation in mothers and offspring.

Butyrate supplementation during pregnancy ameliorates abnormal neurodevelopment

Analysis of the maternal gut microbiota showed a significant reduction in bacteria such as Eubacterium, Ruminococcaceae, Faecalibacterium, and Oscillospiraceae in the AND at-risk group during pregnancy. These bacteria are essential for producing SCFAs, especially butyrate. Additionally, during the development of the FMT model, placental butyrate levels in the AND_M group were lower than those in the Control_M group (Figure 3c). Therefore, we introduced the AND + butyr_M group (Figure 3a). The placental butyrate level in this group was significantly increased (Figure 3c). Morris water maze results indicated that offspring mice in the AND + butyr_M group, compared to the AND_M group, showed a shorter escape latency on day 3 of the learning curve and a reduced time to first platform crossing (Figure 3d-f).

Fetal brain metabolomic analysis showed differences in metabolite distribution between the AND + butyr_M and AND_M groups (Supplementary Figure 5c). PUFAs and amino acid pathways downregulated in the AND_M group were upregulated in the AND + butyr_M group (Figure 3l). Transcriptomic analysis revealed an increased arachidonic acid pathway and a decreased asthma pathway in the AND + butyr_M group (Figure 4d). Pla2g4b expression was significantly increased (Figure 4e), while the expressions of the inflammation-related genes ccl21b, cd14, and the asthma-related gene ms4a2 were decreased (Figure 4g–i). Maternal intestinal barrier function was partially restored in the AND + butyr_M group (Figure 5a–d), and inflammatory cytokine expression in the maternal serum and fetal brain was reduced (Figure 4j–k, Figure 5e–f). These findings suggest that butyrate supplementation during pregnancy may mitigate fetal brain developmental issues linked to AD-induced gut dysbiosis.

Discussion

In this multi-center prospective cohort study, we show that elevated EPDS scores during any trimester are robustly associated with impaired infant neurodevelopment. Using a nested case‒control design combined with 16S rRNA sequencing, we further reveal that antenatal depression (AND) symptom is associated with profound alterations in maternal gut microbiota composition throughout pregnancy. Specifically, women at risk for AND exhibited marked reductions in butyrate-producing bacteria and predicted disruptions in amino acid metabolism pathways, which were, in turn, correlated with poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes in their infants. Mechanistic insights from a germ-free FMT model, in which dams received microbiota from AND at-risk mothers, demonstrated that AND-associated gut dysbiosis compromises intestinal barrier integrity, perturbs polyunsaturated fatty acid and amino acid metabolism, and triggers aberrant inflammatory signaling in the fetal brain. These maternal microbiota-driven perturbations ultimately lead to abnormal postnatal neurocognitive development. Importantly, supplementation with butyrate during pregnancy partially rescued these neurodevelopmental deficits, highlighting a potential therapeutic avenue for mitigating AND-associated offspring risk (Figure 6).

Schematic diagram. Maternal AND symptoms can induce a reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria in the maternal gut microbiota, compromised intestinal barrier function, increased activation of inflammatory pathways, and disturbed polyunsaturated fatty acid and amino acid metabolism pathways in the fetal brain, ultimately harming the neurocognitive development of the offspring. Butyrate supplementation during pregnancy can partially reverse these molecular and behavioral problems.

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective cohort study to examine the trimester-specific relationship between EPDS scores and infant neurodevelopment. Our findings indicate that elevated maternal depressive symptoms at any trimester are associated with poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes in the first year of life, with problem-solving abilities being particularly affected. These results align with previous work by Roy et al., who reported reduced cognitive attainment at 12 months in children of mothers with AND symptoms.19 The prenatal period, together with the first postnatal year, represents a critical window for neural development. While the third trimester is traditionally recognized as a pivotal phase for brain maturation,20 our study demonstrates that depressive symptoms as early as the first trimester can already compromise infant cognitive outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring and supporting maternal emotional well-being from the beginning of pregnancy to optimize early neurodevelopmental trajectories in offspring.

Our study provides novel evidence that the maternal gut microbiota undergoes distinct and biologically meaningful restructuring during pregnancy, with clear differences appearing between the first trimester and later stages. While earlier studies have documented dynamic changes in the microbiota throughout gestation,21 our findings expand this knowledge by connecting trimester-specific shifts to AND symptoms and subsequent neurodevelopmental effects in offspring. Notably, the CAGs that decreased in both the AND-D and AND-N groups were mainly composed of Eubacterium, Ruminococcaceae, Faecalibacterium, and Oscillospiraceae—key butyrate-producing taxa with well-established anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective roles.22-25 Their reduction implies a microbiota-driven decrease in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) availability, especially butyrate, which is vital for maintaining gut barrier function and modulating neuroimmune activity.23,26-28 Meanwhile, the increase of Streptococcus, a genus previously linked to major depressive disorder and increased inflammation, indicates a shift towards a pro-inflammatory microbial environment.29 Collectively, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized pathway through which antenatal depression may impair the maternal gut microbiota’s metabolic and immune regulatory roles, potentially affecting fetal brain development via decreased SCFA production and heightened systemic inflammation.

Previous literature has found a significant correlation between maternal and offspring gut microbial compositions,30 and it is widely recognized that the offspring's own gut microbiota plays a vital role in their neurodevelopment.31,32 However, in our study, we observed no significant effect of maternal AND symptoms on the composition of the offspring’s postnatal gut microbiota. Vertical transmission analysis of the gut microbiota between mothers and offspring indicated generally low rates of shared ASVs. Notably, specific CAGs representing butyrate-producing bacteria, which were significantly reduced in both the AND-D and AND-N groups, showed positive correlations with infant neurodevelopmental scores. These findings collectively suggest that AND symptoms likely impede fetal brain development in utero by altering the maternal gut microbiota during pregnancy, rather than exerting indirect effects through the postnatal dysbiotic gut microbiota in the offspring. This aligns with previous studies demonstrating that maternal gut microbiota-derived metabolites can directly impact fetal thalamocortical axonogenesis.12

The FMT experiment showed that compared with the control offspring, the AND_M group offspring exhibited significant differences in water maze test indicators, rather than in the open field test. These findings indicate that the gut microbiota-driven effects (particularly the depletion of butyrate-producing bacteria) may preferentially impact neurocognitive development rather than emotional abnormalities. Mechanistically, multi-omics analysis of the E18.5 fetal brain revealed significant upregulation of several inflammatory pathways in the AND_M group. Previous studies have shown that prenatal exposure to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) results in the enrichment of TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways in the placenta and uterus, subsequently impacting offspring development.33 Fetal brain neuroinflammation can increase the risk of postnatal neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder.34,35 Regarding amino acid metabolism, we observed downregulation of the cysteine and methionine pathways, as well as taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, in AND_M fetal brains. Methionine, an essential amino acid and precursor to cysteine,36 plays a vital role, with cysteine itself being a precursor to the antioxidant glutathione.37 Taurine, which is abundant in the brain, is critical for combating neuroinflammation, exerting antioxidant effects, and providing neuroprotection.38,39 In lipid metabolism, the expression of PUFAs metabolic pathways – including alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), linoleic acid (LA), and arachidonic acid (ARA) – was decreased in the AND_M group. ALA is a key precursor for other omega-3 PUFAs, such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA).40 LA and ARA are omega-6 PUFAs, accounting for approximately one-quarter of all brain fatty acids.41 During late gestation, the placenta transports substantial amounts of ARA to the fetus for storage, thereby supporting postnatal neurodevelopment.42 PUFAs and the balance of omega-3 and omega-6 PUFAs are essential for anti-inflammatory effects, antioxidation, and neuroprotection.43,44 Anandamide, which was significantly reduced in the AND_M group, also plays an important role in reducing inflammation.45 Additionally, the expression of key genes related to PUFAs metabolism – Pla2g4b and Pla2g4d – was significantly decreased. Both genes belong to the phospholipase A2 family, which produces PUFAs by hydrolyzing glycerophospholipids.46 However, research on Pla2g4b and Pla2g4d remains limited, and their precise mechanisms warrant further study. Moreover, we found that butyrate supplementation during pregnancy helps mitigate these abnormalities. Butyrate supports gut health by upregulating tight junction proteins, thereby maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and function.47 It can also cross the blood‒brain barrier, participating in the regulation of microglial maturation and neurogenesis, while helping to suppress the LPS-induced production of IL-1β and TNF-α.48 Therefore, for pregnant women experiencing AND symptoms, butyrate supplementation during pregnancy may promote offspring neurodevelopment.

However, in our study, maternal butyrate supplementation only partially rescued the neurodevelopmental deficits. Functional predictions from 16S rRNA sequencing also revealed other disrupted pathways, including peptidoglycan synthesis and amino acid metabolism (valine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, cysteine, and methionine), during both the depressive and non-depressive phases. Peptidoglycan acts as a crucial gut–brain signaling molecule modulating innate immunity and is linked to autism spectrum disorder.49 Branched-chain amino acids (such as valine, leucine, and isoleucine) are indispensable for proper cerebral cortex development,50 and microbiota-driven amino acid deficiencies have been implicated in the pathogenesis of early-onset depression.51 Additionally, we observed a marked enrichment of Streptococcus in both the AND-D and AND-N groups. Streptococcus is known to activate monocytes and stimulate the production of cytokines, potentially orchestrating systemic chronic inflammation.29 Collectively, alongside butyrate, this synergistic interplay of microbial metabolic and immune-related pathways provides a broader mechanistic framework mediating offspring neurodevelopment, warranting further investigation.

The present study has several limitations. First, a self-report measure (i.e., the EPDS) was used to assess depressive symptoms, instead of a clinical diagnostic interview. Random measurement errors in self-report questionnaires can influence the strength of the observed associations. Second, 16S rRNA sequencing only allows for functional prediction and cannot perform detailed functional analysis, such as metagenomics. Additionally, the absence of maternal blood samples prevented the measurement of SCFA levels and inflammatory cytokine expression. Third, for the FMT experiment, we used whole fecal material, which means we cannot identify individual key bacteria. However, the effect of any single bacterium is likely limited. Analyzing the microbiota's function might be more appropriate.

In summary, this study shows that maternal AND symptoms at any stage of pregnancy adversely affect infant neurodevelopment. These effects appear to be mediated through alterations in the maternal gut microbiota, marked by a reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria, compromised intestinal barrier function, and increased activation of inflammatory pathways in the fetal brain, as demonstrated in a germ-free mouse model. This imbalance also disturbed polyunsaturated fatty acid and amino acid metabolism – both of which are vital for neuroimmune function – ultimately harming the neurocognitive development of the offspring. Importantly, butyrate supplementation during pregnancy partially reversed these molecular and behavioral problems, highlighting the maternal gut microbiota and its metabolites as potential therapeutic targets for addressing these issues. Collectively, these findings uncover a mechanistic connection between maternal mood, microbial communities, and fetal brain development, opening new avenues for early intervention to prevent the intergenerational effects of AND. These findings provide a foundation for microbiota-based strategies and early mental health interventions that could promote both maternal well-being and healthy neurodevelopment in the next generation.
