Interpolating between Tikhonov regularization and spectral cutoff
Martin Sæbye Carøe, Mirza Karamehmedović, Pierre Maréchal

Abstract
Regularizing a linear ill-posed operator equation can be achieved by manipulating the spectrum of the operator’s pseudo-inverse. Tikhonov regularization and spectral cutoff are well-known techniques within this category. This paper introduces an interpolating formula that defines a one-parameter family of regularizations, where Tikhonov and spectral cutoff methods are represented as limiting cases. By adjusting the interpolating parameter taking into account the specific operator equation under consideration, it is possible to mitigate the limitations associated with both Tikhonov and spectral cutoff regularizations. The proposed approach is demonstrated through numerical simulations in the fields of signal and image processing.

Introduction

We consider a general linear ill-posed operator
equation

Tf=g,

(1)

in which
T\colon F\to G
is a bounded linear
operator from
F
to
G
, where
F
and
G
are infinite
dimensional separable Hilbert spaces. In a number of real world
applications,

is such that

\inf\left\{\mskip 2.0mu\|\mskip 1.0muTf\mskip 1.0mu\|\vphantom{\big|}\mskip 2.0mu\left|\vphantom{\bigl|}\vphantom{\|\mskip 1.0muTf\mskip 1.0mu\|\vphantom{\big|}\,f\in(\mathop{\mathrm{ker}}T)^{\perp},\;\|\mskip 1.0muf\mskip 1.0mu\|=1}\right.\mskip 2.0mu\,f\in(\mathop{\mathrm{ker}}T)^{\perp},\;\|\mskip 1.0muf\mskip 1.0mu\|=1\mskip 2.0mu\right\}=0.

(2)

When
T
is injective, an assumption which
will be in force throughout, Condition (
2
)
boils down to

\inf\left\{\mskip 2.0mu\|\mskip 1.0muTf\mskip 1.0mu\|\vphantom{\big|}\mskip 2.0mu\left|\vphantom{\bigl|}\vphantom{\|\mskip 1.0muTf\mskip 1.0mu\|\vphantom{\big|}\|\mskip 1.0muf\mskip 1.0mu\|=1}\right.\mskip 2.0mu\|\mskip 1.0muf\mskip 1.0mu\|=1\mskip 2.0mu\right\}=0.

(3)

The latter condition results in ill-posedness, meaning that:

(i)

the range
\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}T
of

is not closed in

;

(ii)

the densely defined pseudo-inverse
T^{\dagger}\colon\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}T+\mathop{\mathrm{ker}}T^{*}\to F

is unbounded, so that the minimum-norm least squares solution to the linear
equation
Tf=g
does not depend continuously on the data

.

The purpose of regularization theory is to provide approximate solutions
to (
1
) that depend continuously on the
data, so as to avoid instability in the inversion process. See
[
1
]
and the references therein for a thorough treatment of regularization.
The main objective of this paper is to bridge the gap between two
of the most frequently used regularization methods, namely the Tikhonov
regularization technique and the so-called spectral cutoff method. We shall propose
a family of regularization techniques depending on some
interpolating
parameter
that encompasses both Tikhonov regularization and the spectral cutoff
as special (extreme) cases. The proposed filters can be naturally included in learning schemes
[
1
]
for further, data-driven refinement.

A family of regularization schemes

2.1
Spectral regularization

Prior to defining our interpolating family of regularization schemes,

The following result is well-known; however, we here propose a novel proof:

Theorem 2
.

Let
T\colon F\to G
be an injective bounded operator with
singular value expansion
T=W[\sigma]V^{*}
as in Theorem
1
.
Assume that
\sigma
is not bounded away from zero, so that the
inverse problem
Tf=g
is ill-posed.
Let
q\colon\mathds{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathds{R}_{+}\to\mathds{C}
be such that

(A1)

for every
(\alpha,\sigma)\in\mathds{R}_{+}^{*}\times\mathds{R}_{+}
,

|\mskip 1.0muq(\alpha,\sigma)\mskip 1.0mu|\leq M
for some positive constant
M
,

(A2)

for every
\sigma\in\mathds{R}_{+}
,
|\mskip 1.0muq(\alpha,\sigma)\mskip 1.0mu|\leq c_{\alpha}\sigma
,
in which
c_{\alpha}
is some positive constant.

Then
R_{\alpha}:=V[\sigma^{-1}q(\alpha,\sigma)]W^{*}
is well-defined and bounded,
and
\|\mskip 1.0muR_{\alpha}\mskip 1.0mu\|\leq c_{\alpha}
.
Moreover, if

(A3)

for every
\sigma\in\mathds{R}_{+}
,

\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}q(\alpha,\sigma)=1
,

then
(R_{\alpha})
is a regularization of
T^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathscr{D}(T^{\dagger})
and let
\bar{g}
be its projection onto the
closure of the range of
T
. Observe that, since
g

belongs to the domain of
T^{\dagger}
, its projection
\bar{g}

must actually belong to the range of
T
. Hence
\bar{g}=Tf

for some
f\in F
, from which we deduce that

[\sigma^{-1}]W^{*}\bar{g}=[\sigma^{-1}]W^{*}W[\sigma]V^{*}f=V^{*}f\in L^{2}(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{B},\mu).

By Assumption (A1),
[q(\alpha,\sigma)][\sigma^{-1}]W^{*}g=[q(\alpha,\sigma)]V^{*}f

also belongs to
L^{2}(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{B},\mu)
, which in turn implies that

V[q(\alpha,\sigma)][\sigma^{-1}]W^{*}g=:R_{\alpha}g

is well-defined.
Under Assumption (A2), we have, for every
g\in G
,

\displaystyle\left\|\mskip 2.0muR_{\alpha}g\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\mskip 2.0muV[\sigma^{-1}q(\alpha,\sigma)]W^{*}g\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\mskip 2.0mu[\sigma^{-1}q(\alpha,\sigma)]W^{*}g\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\mskip 2.0mu\sigma^{-1}q(\alpha,\sigma)\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|\mskip 2.0muW^{*}g\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\mskip 2.0mu\sigma^{-1}q(\alpha,\sigma)\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|\mskip 2.0mug\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}^{2}\left\|\mskip 2.0mug\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2},

in which the second equality is due to the unitarity of
V

and the third equality is due to the fact that
W
is an isometry
(so that
W^{*}W=I
, where

denotes the identity).
The first assertion in the theorem follows.

Next, let
f^{\dagger}:=T^{\dagger}g
and
f_{\alpha}:=R_{\alpha}g
.
From the definition of
R_{\alpha}
and the formula for
T^{\dagger}
,
we readily see that

f^{\dagger}-f_{\alpha}=V\big[\sigma^{-1}(1-q(\alpha,\sigma))\big]W^{*}g=V\big[1-q(\alpha,\sigma)\big][\sigma^{-1}]W^{*}\bar{g}=V\big[1-q(\alpha,\sigma)\big]V^{*}f.

Therefore, using the unitarity of
V
,

\left\|\mskip 2.0muf^{\dagger}-f_{\alpha}\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}=\left\|\mskip 2.0mu\big[1-q(\alpha,\sigma)\big]V^{*}f\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{B},\mu)}^{2}=\int\left|\mskip 2.0mu1-q(\alpha,\sigma)\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right|^{2}\big(V^{*}f(\sigma)\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\sigma).

Finally, under Assumption (A3), the function

\sigma\mapsto\left|\mskip 2.0mu1-q(\alpha,\sigma)\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right|^{2}
converges pointwise
to zero, and Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem then implies
that
f_{\alpha}\to f^{\dagger}
as
\alpha\downarrow 0
.
∎

Notice that in the above theorem,
q
is
allowed to take complex values. Notice also that
q(\alpha,\cdot)

need only be defined on the range of
\sigma
.

The latter theorem generalizes Theorem 4.9 in
[
4
]
,
in which compactness of

was assumed. This special case now appears
as a corollary. If we let

be a compact operator, then
the singular value expansion takes the form

is a Hilbert basis of

.

, in which
the Borel space is

, the space of square summable real sequences, endowed with

Let

for every
\alpha\in\mathds{R}_{+}^{*}
and every

|\mskip 1.0muq(\alpha,\sigma_{n})\mskip 1.0mu|\leq M

\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}q(\alpha,\sigma_{n})=1

(R_{\alpha})

.

An important example of an ill-posed inverse problem
is the standard deconvolution problem.
Given a function
\gamma
in
L^{1}(\mathds{R}^{n})
,
we consider the convolution operator

T_{\gamma}:L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
defined by

T_{\gamma}f=\gamma*f.

The deconvolution problem consists in solving
T_{\gamma}f=g
for
f

when
g
is empirically known. This problem is ubiquitous in many areas
of applied sciences, including signal and image processing,
physics, statistics etc.
If
\gamma
has an almost everywhere positive-valued Fourier
transform
\hat{\gamma}
then the singular value expansion is explicit:

T_{\gamma}=W[\hat{\gamma}]V^{*}\quad\hbox{with}\quad W=V=U^{-1}

where
U\colon L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
is the
Fourier-Plancherel operator. Recall that the Fourier-Plancherel
operator can be defined as the closure to
L^{2}(\mathds{R})
of the
Fourier transform, denoted likewise, on
L^{1}(\mathds{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
.
Our definition of the Fourier transform of an integrable function
f

on
\mathds{R}^{n}
is:

(Uf)(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi):=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Under the standard assumption that
\mathop{\mathrm{meas}}\{\mskip 1.0mu\xi\in\mathds{R}^{n}\mskip 1.0mu|\mskip 1.0mu\hat{\gamma}(\xi)=0\mskip 1.0mu\}=0

the operator
T_{\gamma}
is injective.
Remember that by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma,
\widehat{\gamma}
is continuous and
vanishes at infinity, which implies (via
[
5
, Theorem 4]
)
that the deconvolution problem is ill-posed.

The last example reveals potential drawbacks of both Tikhonov regularization
and the spectral cutoff. Recall that the variational formulation
of the Tikhonov regularization consists in the minimization of
the functional

\mathscr{F}(f)=\left\|\mskip 2.0mug-T_{\gamma}f\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}+\left\|\mskip 2.0muf\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}=\left\|\mskip 2.0mu\hat{g}-\hat{\gamma}\hat{f}\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}+\left\|\mskip 2.0mu\hat{f}\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2},

where we used Parseval’s identity. We see that the penalty term
acts with equal strength everywhere in the Fourier domain. This
can’t be optimal since the
low frequencies
are constrained
by the data,
\hat{\gamma}
being close to 1 near the origin (Charibdis).
On the other hand, the spectral cutoff multiplies the Fourier
transform of
f
by a
mask
, which is equivalent to convolving
f

by a sinc-like function, thereby creating the Gibbs phenomenon (Sylla).

Our intuition is that we may somehow find a reasonable compromise
placing us in between these two choices for
q
. In the next subsection,
we propose an interpolation formula for the function
q
, and show that it fits
the regularization framework of Theorem
2
.
Later on, we shall determine optimal values for the interpolating parameter.
Finally, we shall illustrate the performance of our new
family of regularization schemes by means of
numerical simulation.

2.2
Interpolating between Tikhonov and spectral cutoff

Two important special cases of the function
q
that appears
in Theorem
2
are

q_{0}(\alpha,\sigma)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\alpha+\sigma^{2}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad q_{\infty}(\alpha,\sigma)=\begin{cases}1&\hbox{if }\sigma>\sqrt{\alpha},\\
1/2&\hbox{if }\sigma=\sqrt{\alpha},\\
0&\hbox{if }\sigma<\sqrt{\alpha}.\end{cases}

The function
q_{0}
corresponds to Tikhonov regularization.
It satisfies (A1), (A2) and (A3) with
M=1
and

c_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}
.
The function
q_{\infty}
corresponds to spectral cutoff.
It satisfies (A1), (A2) and (A3) with
M=1
and

c_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}
.

We now define the family
(q_{\tau})_{\tau\in\mathds{R}_{+}}
by

q_{\tau}(\alpha,\sigma)=\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sigma}\right)^{2+\tau}}.

(4)

It is readily seen that
\tau=0
yields the Tikhonov case
while in the limit
\tau\to\infty
one retrieves the spectral
cutoff case.

As can be seen in Figure
1
, the filter functions
q_{\tau}
operate somewhere in-between the Tikhonov regularization and TSVD by making the cut-off around
\sqrt{\alpha}
more steep than for the Tikhonov regularization, but less steep than the TSVD.

A plot of
q_{\tau}
with
\alpha=0.005
(left) and
\alpha=0.05
(right) and four different values of
\tau
.

The above function
q_{\tau}
corresponds to the minimization of the functional

f\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mskip 2.0mug-Tf\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mskip 2.0muH_{\alpha}f\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right\|^{2},

(5)

in which
H_{\alpha}
is the closed operator on
F
given by

H_{\alpha}=V\left[\sqrt{\alpha}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sigma}\right)^{\tau/2}\right]V^{*}.

The closedness of
H_{\alpha}
guarantees the existence and uniqueness of solution of the minimization problem (
5
).
[
10
, Theorem 1 on p. 3]

As a matter fact, using the expansion
T=W[\sigma]V^{*}
,
we then have (see
[
10
, Section 25, p. 214]
):

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T^{*}T+H_{\alpha}^{*}H_{\alpha})^{-1}T^{*}g

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(V[\sigma^{2}]V^{*}+V\left[\alpha\left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sigma}\right)^{\tau}\right]V^{*}\right)^{-1}V[\sigma]W^{*}g

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V\left[\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\alpha\left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sigma}\right)^{\tau}}\right]W^{*}g

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V\left[\frac{1}{\sigma}q_{\tau}(\alpha,\sigma)\right]W^{*}g,

with
q_{\tau}
as in (
4
).
We stress here that the operator
H_{\alpha}
depends
both on
\alpha
and the function
\sigma
. Its closedness
is established in Appendix
A
.
In a number of cases, including the deconvolution
problem considered in the previous subsection,
\sigma
is explicitly
known, so that the dependence of
H_{\alpha}
on
\sigma
is not an obstacle.
We also emphasize that, strictly speaking, the above regularization
does not pertain to the so-called
generalized Tikhonov
class.
As a matter of fact, the dependence of
H
on
\alpha

is not that one would have if
\alpha
was merely a weight
in front of some
\alpha
-independent quadratic penalty
in the objective functional displayed in (

).

3
Numerical examples

We test the family of regularization schemes on a 1D
deconvolution problem. Recall that the operator

T_{\gamma}\colon L^{2}(\mathds{R})\to L^{2}(\mathds{R})
of
convolution by
\gamma\in L^{1}(\mathds{R})
is given
by
T_{\gamma}=\gamma*f
. It can be diagonalized
by the Fourier-Plancherel operator.

Let
\mathbf{f},\bm{\gamma}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
be samples of functions
f,\gamma
in
L^{2}(\mathds{R})

and
L^{1}(\mathds{R})
, respectively. We assume that
the support of
f
and
\gamma
is contained in
the interval
[-1,1]
, and that the samples are taken at a uniform grid in this box. We can compute the discrete convolution to obtain
\mathbf{g}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
, given by

\begin{split}g_{j}&=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n}f_{j-j^{\prime}}\gamma_{j^{\prime}}h,\end{split}

(6)

where
\mathbf{f}
and
\bm{\gamma}
are extended by zero to
all indices
j\notin\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and where
h

is the spacing between points on the grid.

Note that the discrete convolutions
(
6
) correspond to the
Riemann sum approximations to the continuous
convolution.

Consider a noisy measurement
\mathbf{g}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
.
We compute reconstructions
\mathbf{f}_{\alpha,\tau}\in\mathds{R}^{n}

by computing

\mathbf{f}_{\alpha,\tau}=U^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{U\bm{\gamma}}q_{\tau}(\alpha,U\bm{\gamma})\right)U\mathbf{g}.

(7)

Here
U:\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}^{n}
is a linear operator
that approximates the continuous Fourier transform.
The discrete Fourier transform operator can be used
to approximate the Fourier transform, as shown
in
[
2
]
.

For the 2D example, we will consider the Shepp-Logan
phantom, a piece-wise constant grey-scale image with
values between
0
and
1
. We convolve
f
with a
kernel that is equal to the characteristic function
times a scalar multiple. In particular,

\gamma=c\cdot\chi_{A}
, where
c>0
is chosen such
that
\int_{A}\gamma(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
. In the numerical example,

A=[-s_{\rm blur},s_{\rm blur}]
, for some number

s_{\rm blur}>0
.

g^{i}_{j}=g^{i}(x_{j})
.
Random Gaussian i.i.d. noise,
\varepsilon_{j}^{\sigma}

with standard deviation
\sigma>0
is added to obtain
a noisy measurement
\mathbf{g}^{i,\sigma}=[g^{i}_{j}+\varepsilon_{j}^{\sigma}]
.
In the implementation the ”exact” measurement
g_{j}
is in
fact computed using a discrete convolution on a much
finer grid than the grid
(x_{j})
. As explained,
reconstructions are computed using
Equation (
7
),
where
U
is an approximation to the Fourier transform
using the discrete Fourier transform.

The error in the reconstruction, relative to the true signal
f^{i}
, is computed by

\|\mathbf{f}_{\alpha,\tau}^{i}-\mathbf{f}^{i}\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{f}^{i}\|_{2},

where
\mathbf{f}^{i}=[f^{i}(x_{j})]
.

The values of
\alpha
are chosen in two different ways:

•

The
Morozov principle

[
10
]
:

\alpha
is chosen as

\alpha=\sup\{a^{\prime}>0\,:\,\|\bm{\gamma}*\mathbf{f}_{\alpha,\tau}-\mathbf{g}^{\sigma}\|_{2}\leq 1.1\cdot\mathbb{E}(\|\varepsilon\|_{2})\}.

where the expected value of the norm of the error
is
\mathbb{E}(\|\varepsilon\|_{2})=\sigma\sqrt{N}
.

•

Optimal choice:
\alpha
is found by running through
a large collection of values and choosing the value of
\alpha>0

that gives the lowest reconstruction error

\|\mathbf{f}_{\alpha,\tau}^{i}-\mathbf{f}^{i}\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{f}^{i}\|_{2}
.

3.2
2D example

We consider the multi–frequency inverse source problem in two dimensions.
Let
D_{0},D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be concentric disks with radii
R_{0},R
satisfying
R_{0}<R
, and let
D_{0}
include the support
of an unknown acoustic or electromagnetic source function
s\in L^{2}(D_{0})
. For each wavenumber (’frequency’)
k>0
,
the field
u_{k}
radiated by
s
satisfies the Helmholtz equation

(\Delta+k^{2})u_{k}(x)=s(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(8)

together with the Sommerfeld radiation condition

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\sqrt{|x|}\big(\partial_{|x|}-ik\big)u_{k}(x)=0,\quad\text{uniformly for }x/|x|\in S^{1}.

(9)

The measured data are given by the restriction of
u_{k}
to the boundary

\partial D
. The corresponding forward operator
F_{k}:s\mapsto U_{k}:=u_{k}|_{\partial D}
, compact from
L^{2}(D_{0})
to
L^{2}(\partial D)
, is given by the radiation integral

U_{k}(x)=(F_{k}s)(x)=\int_{y\in D_{0}}\Phi_{k}(x-y)s(y)dy,\quad x\in\partial D,

(10)

where
\Phi_{k}(x)=\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(k|x-y|)
is the outgoing fundamental solution of the
Helmholtz equation in the plane, and
H_{0}^{(1)}
is the Hankel function of order zero and of first kind. A singular value expansion of
F_{k}
was given and characterized in
[
8
]
; see also
[
3
]
. For a set of frequencies
Q=\{k_{j}\}_{j\in I}
and corresponding boundary
measurements
\{U_{k_{j}}\}
, the multi–frequency inverse source problem is to
reconstruct
s\in L^{2}(D_{0})
such that

U_{k_{j}}=F_{k_{j}}s,\qquad\forall k_{j}\in Q.

(11)

The inverse source problem is ill-posed since
\mathop{\mathrm{ker}}F_{k}=(\Delta+k^{2})H^{2}(D_{0})
.
[
9
]
The degree of ill–posedness of the inverse problem depends on the
choice of the frequency set
Q

[
7
]
, with broader frequency coverage leading to a larger subspace of source functions that can be
reconstructed in a stable manner.

For the numerical experiment, we discretize the domain
D
using a triangular mesh, generate a synthetic ground truth source
s
, and compute boundary data using the forward operator (
10
). For this experiment we select the frequencies
k_{j}

according to

k_{j}R_{0}=j\pi,\qquad j=2,\dots,30.

(12)

We assemble the individual-frequency discretized forward operators into a single joint system matrix and compute its singular value
decomposition. Finally, we reconstruct the source using the spectral filtering method defined in (
7
). Our code is available at
https://github.com/msaca-okse/tikhonov_ISP
.

Figure
5
shows the ground truth (top left) and reconstructions corresponding to different values of
\tau
.

Figure
6
shows the singular value spectrum of the combined forward operator (all used frequencies) together with the projections of the ground truth onto the corresponding right singular vectors. We clearly see that neither the Tikhonov regularization (
\tau=0
) nor the truncated SVD regularization (
\tau
large) give the best solution of the inverse source problem, in that the first includes speckle-like artifacts and the latter exhibits the Gibbs phenomenon near the piecewise constant component of the source. In fact, the choice
\tau=3
seems to be better than both of the extreme options.

to be the disc centered at the origin with radius
0.99
.

Appendix A
Appendix: Closedness of the operator
H_{\alpha}

We may consider the operator
H=H_{1}
, since the positive
constant
(\sqrt{\alpha})^{1+\tau/2}
doesn’t play any role:

H=V\left[\sigma^{-\tau/2}\right]V^{*}.

Recall that
V\colon L^{2}(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{B},\mu)\to F
is unitary.
If
\phi\in\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}H
, then

H^{-1}\phi=V\left[\sigma^{\tau/2}\right]V^{*}\phi
.
It is readily seen that the domain of
H
is given by

\mathscr{D}(H)=\left\{\mskip 2.0muf\in F\mskip 2.0mu\left|\vphantom{\bigl|}\vphantom{f\in F\sigma^{-\tau/2}V^{*}f\in L^{2}(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{B},\mu)}\right.\mskip 2.0mu\sigma^{-\tau/2}V^{*}f\in L^{2}(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{B},\mu)\mskip 2.0mu\right\}.

Now assume that
f_{n}\in\mathscr{D}(H)
, that
f_{n}\to f
,
and
Hf_{n}\to\varphi
in
F
. We have:

\displaystyle\varphi=\lim Hf_{n}\;\hbox{in}\;F

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle V^{*}\varphi=\lim V^{*}Hf_{n}\;\hbox{in}\;L^{2}(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{B},\mu)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\big[\sigma(x)^{\tau/2}\big]V^{*}\varphi=\lim\big[\sigma(x)^{\tau/2}\big]V^{*}Hf_{n}\;\hbox{in}\;L^{2}(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{B},\mu)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle V\big[\sigma(x)^{\tau/2}\big]V^{*}\varphi=\lim V\big[\sigma(x)^{\tau/2}\big]V^{*}Hf_{n}\;\hbox{in}\;F,

in which the first and third equivalence stem
from the unitarity of
V
,
while the second equivalence follows from the boundedness
of the function
\sigma(x)^{\tau/2}
.
Therefore, since
Hf_{n}\in\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}H
and

V\big[\sigma(x)^{\tau/2}\big]V^{*}=H^{-1}
, we obtain that

f=H^{-1}\varphi
, which implies in turn that
f\in\mathscr{D}(H)

and
Hf=\varphi
.
As a matter of fact,

\displaystyle\int\sigma(x)^{-\tau}\left|\mskip 2.0mu(V^{*}f)(x)\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\sigma(x)^{-\tau}\left|\mskip 2.0muV^{*}H^{-1}\varphi(x)\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\sigma(x)^{-\tau}\sigma(x)^{\tau}\left|\mskip 2.0muV^{*}\varphi(x)\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left|\mskip 2.0muV^{*}\varphi(x)\vphantom{\bigl|}\mskip 2.0mu\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

the last integral being finite since

V^{*}\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{B},\mu)
.
This proves the closedness of
H
.
