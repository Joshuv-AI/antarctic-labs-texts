Strengthening pandemic preparedness in Peru: a qualitative study of high-level leaders involved in the COVID-19 response
Authors: Arantxa Noelia Sanchez Boluarte, Tripura Talagadadeevi, Daniel Granda Calderon, Patricia Garcia, James T Pfeiffer, Judith N Wasserheit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic exposed critical gaps in Peru’s preparedness and response, including the absence of sustainable, intersectoral strategies to address systemic vulnerabilities and improve resilience. Through interviews with national leaders, we sought to identify key issues and actionable strategies for sustainable mechanisms that could improve preparedness and response for future pandemics.MethodsWe conducted in-depth, semistructured key-informant interviews using snowball sampling to recruit 14 high-level leaders who were engaged in the COVID-19 pandemic response. Participants informed decision-making in Peru within health and other sectors, and within government, private sector, academia, multilateral agencies and non-governmental organisations. Data were analysed thematically.ResultsParticipants highlighted key challenges and opportunities in Peru’s pandemic response. Two overarching themes emerged: (1) contextualising Peru’s response, which explores the structural, cultural and systemic factors shaping the pandemic response, and (2) coordinating intersectoral mechanisms, which highlights the dynamics of collaboration, leadership and resource sharing across sectors. Participants emphasised the need to improve healthcare and emergency response systems, foster transparent and culturally sensitive communication, and institutionalise multisectoral collaboration. They also highlighted the importance of involving communities in planning and ensuring financial readiness for future emergencies.ConclusionsFindings emphasise the urgent need for a cohesive, sustainable approach to pandemic preparedness in Peru, emphasising robust multisectoral collaboration, equitable resource distribution and transparency. These recommendations provide a pathway to strengthen Peru’s resilience and offer insights applicable to other low- and middle-income countries facing similar challenges.

However, there is limited understanding of the critical role and challenges of intersectoral collaboration beyond the human health sector and implementation mechanisms that could strengthen pandemic preparedness and response.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic catapulted the world into confronting far-reaching health and societal impacts and inconvenient truths about pandemic prevention, preparedness and response (PPPR). As of December 2024, over 7 million deaths were reported due to COVID-19 with excess mortality potentially almost triple this level.1 2 Although unprecedented resources were dedicated to pandemic response, launching major policy initiatives,3 4 the pandemic generated significant health burdens, while the response extensively affected multiple critical sectors beyond health such as business, education, agriculture and tourism, aggravating existing inequalities.57

Latin America was among the regions most severely affected by the COVID-19 pandemic,8 with Peru experiencing the highest mortality rate (670/100 000 people) in the world.1 9 Despite being relatively well positioned prior to the pandemic and adopting early, strict lockdown measures, against a backdrop of longstanding limited investment in health10 and systemic weaknesses in Peru’s healthcare infrastructure, such as a lack of intensive care unit beds and trained personnel, limited molecular testing and surge capacity hindered effective response efforts.11 These constraints reflected deeper structural issues, including a fragmented health system and high out-of-pocket costs for care.12 These challenges were further intensified by severe shortages of medical oxygen, temporary closures of primary healthcare services due to inadequate personal protective equipment and high levels of comorbidity, all of which constrained timely access to care.13 Economic inequality exacerbated these vulnerabilities, as insufficient and delayed financial aid forced many to disregard lockdown restrictions, contributing to the spread of the virus.14 Barriers to social distancing were also significant, driven by overcrowded multigenerational housing, high housing costs, limited access to basic services and reliance on crowded public transport and markets for daily survival.13 Funding challenges in Peru, compounded by political instability, high levels of job informality (around 70%)15 and bureaucratic inefficiencies, further limited the implementation of public health measures such as lockdowns and wage subsidies.1618

Despite persistent PPPR gaps, investment and policy attention are rapidly declining. Yet there is a short, unique window of opportunity as countries emerge from the COVID-19 pandemic to rigorously examine and learn from national multisectoral efforts to strengthen PPPR strategies and systems for future pandemics. We interviewed Peruvian stakeholders to explore experiences and perspectives on Peruvian and international responses to the COVID-19 pandemic. We sought to leverage the lessons from the pandemic to identify key considerations for developing sustainable, intersectoral strategies and mechanisms that could improve PPPR within Peru and globally.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a qualitative study using semistructured key informant interviews (KIIs). 14 interviews were conducted from January to June 2023. An interview guide was developed and used during the KIIs (online supplemental appendix 1). Interviews of approximately 1 hour were conducted via Zoom with both video-recording and audio-recording with permission. Non-verbal cues were observable through the video component and noted when relevant.

A team experienced in qualitative research from the University of Washington and Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia conducted the interviews. Professors with extensive expertise in qualitative methods from both universities trained and guided the team. All participants provided verbal and written informed consent. This study followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist.19

Patient and public involvement

This study did not involve patients or members of the public. We plan to share our results prior to publication with the study participants.

Selection and recruitment of participants and profiles

Based on our literature review, we employed purposeful sampling20 21 to recruit politically important leaders from key sectors that played central roles in Peru’s COVID-19 decision-making and policy response. Our sample frame therefore comprised policymakers and leaders across health and non-health sectors including agriculture, education, communication, academia, non-profits and private sectors. The research team then used a snowball sampling strategy20 within each sector to identify and contact key leaders.

These individuals were chosen not only for their sector-specific knowledge but also for their high-level decision-making roles and extensive experience in managing health crises. Accordingly, the sample size (n=14) reflects a targeted, information-rich group rather than an attempt to capture perspectives across all health system levels in order to achieve data saturation.22

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim in Spanish by four Peruvian bilingual, native Spanish-speaking study team members. Translations into English were conducted by the same team of physician-researchers. Each transcript and corresponding translation were independently completed and reviewed by two different team members, with discrepancies resolved through consensus to ensure semantic accuracy and reliability.

A priori deductive themes from our literature review included the country’s environment prior to the pandemic, successes and challenges of PPPR, and multisectoral collaboration efforts. The KII guide was developed based on the literature review and expert consultation and included questions that sought to elicit responses around the key themes while also ensuring inductive exploration of new unexpected themes.23 It was pilot-tested with one informant, resulting in minor refinements prior to data collection.

Initially, a deductive coding approach was employed, informed by our preidentified themes and aligned with the domains covered in the KII guide. A coding scheme based on these categories was developed and systematically applied to the data. Subsequently, the analysis transitioned into an inductive coding approach to capture the richness and diversity of experiences that emerged from our participants.20 23 This phase involved an open exploration of novel patterns and themes emerging directly from the interviewees and allowing the codebook to be iteratively refined. We developed 30 codes describing facilitators and barriers for PPPR and multisectoral collaboration (online supplemental appendix 2).

Two coders conducted the data analysis using ATLAS.ti 23 software.24 Intercoder agreement was assessed and improved through team discussions and jointly cross-checking coding of a subset of transcripts, demonstrating a high level of consistency in the coding process.23 We engaged in iterative comparison and refinement of codes and themes to ensure the accuracy and depth of our analysis.

Thematic analysis was used to explore and understand the themes, patterns and insights present in the data.25 This method allowed for the identification of key findings and the generation of meaningful insights related to multisectoral collaboration and important contextual factors during the COVID-19 pandemic in Peru.

Results

14 semistructured interviews were conducted with participants from diverse roles and areas of expertise (table 1). Seven of the 14 participants came from sectors beyond human health, including communication, industry, civil society, education and agriculture. Five were female. All held high-level positions in government organisations, multilateral agencies, non-governmental organisations (NGOs) and private institutions during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Theme 1: contextualising Peru’s response

Gaps and shortcomings of the healthcare system

Most participants underscored the urgent need for capacity strengthening and effective resource management within Peru’s healthcare system. The pandemic revealed significant gaps in the country’s preparedness:

How did the pandemic find us? A single laboratory from the National Institute of Health, with the capacity to process 500 PCR samples per day for the entire country. In warehouses, I only found 10,000 PCR tests. (…) Even if we had had 40 million tests in the warehouse, we wouldn’t have had the labs to process them. Today Peru has 120 laboratories to do PCR, but everything was late. - Health/Government participant

While the health sector adapted and expanded rapidly, interview data pointed to the critical role of efficient resource management, and response financing and sustainability in navigating a pandemic. However, participants noted that bureaucratic hurdles often delayed resource allocation and utilisation, impacting the overall effectiveness of the response and emergency management. In particular, they highlighted the need to strengthen Peru’s emergency and disaster response agencies (eg, the National Institute of Civil Defence) to improve coordination, rapid mobilisation and local response capacity in future health crises.

Beyond immediate resource constraints, eight participants emphasised the need for systemic structural reform within Peru’s health system. The importance of a unified public health system was mentioned to strengthen the nation’s capacity to manage future health crises which would require significant organisational changes and increased investment in public health infrastructure:

I believe that Peru needs a single public health system. (…) The Peruvian health system needs to be strengthened. Our public health arm remains weak. Does it need money? Yes. But it also needs organizational change. If we're not going to unify the health system, let’s unify the public health area. - Health/Government participant

The importance of strong leadership and governance

Effective leadership emerged as a critical determinant of the PPPR. However, many participants pointed out significant challenges in decision-making processes and adaptability to rapidly evolving circumstances. Many described frequent leadership turnover and political instability which further hindered the continuity of the response. All participants emphasised the importance of strong, decisive leadership within governing entities. There was a need for a health sector-led response, under the authority of the ministry of health, integrating the role of academia in providing leadership and aligning stakeholders.

When facing a pandemic, leaders of governing entities are the most important. The governing body is the Ministry of Health, which has knowledge of the educational policy and the situation of the health policy and system perfectly. The role of the sector leader is crucial. We had a series of ministers who, unfortunately, were not up to respond to the need. (…) The leader was totally weakened and discredited, which delayed the processes of the solution we all wanted. - Education/Government participant

What is required first is leadership. That leadership can only come from the health sector, ideally from the Ministry of Health. If not feasible, academia could assume leadership and align public and private sectors. - Communication/Government participant

The procurement and distribution of COVID-19 vaccines in Peru was seen as a complex interplay between the public and private sectors that highlighted the importance of government leadership. One participant mentioned the government assumed primary responsibility for vaccine negotiation and acquisition, with the private sector playing a vital role in logistics.

The entire negotiation, once vaccines were selected, passed into the hands of the Ministry of Economy, and the Ministry of Foreign Relations, with the technical support of the Ministry of Health. - Industry/Private sector participant

Pivotal role of communication

Participants largely agreed that while initially trust in government measures existed, frequent turnover of governing leaders, inconsistent messaging, unclear directives and historically weak relationships between the government and the population eroded public confidence. Overcoming these challenges emerged as a priority for enhancing the effectiveness of pandemic communication strategies. The media, including print media, TV, radio and social media, was seen as vital to disseminate prevention information. Some also highlighted that challenges in intersectoral communication and misinformation hindered the development of a unified message. Participants emphasised the role of the media in educating the population with clear, consistent messaging and robust mechanisms to counter misinformation.

The media mostly aligned in the same point of view, conducting pro-vaccination communication campaigns that were very good. That was very important so that once the vaccines arrived, the vaccination process would advance quickly. - Communications/Private sector participant

While one participant believed the media communicated consistently well, most believed that despite initial efforts, intersectoral communication challenges and response politicisation complicated the dissemination of accurate information and the coordination of response efforts. The spread of misinformation weakened the overall response:

We should have had a standard message in relation not only to the pandemic evolution but also to our resources availability. As different actors began to propose alternatives, we needed a unified communication. The lack of it allowed fake news to spread rapidly, which was not adequately neutralized. - Health/Multilateral agency participant

In addition to improving media strategies, a participant from a health multilateral agency emphasised the need to create spaces for community engagement. They viewed citizen participation not only as a means to increase trust, but also as a mechanism for co-developing solutions and promoting transparent decision-making, highlighting that such efforts require political will more than significant financial investment.

Benefits and limitations of academic involvement

While several participants viewed academic institutions as key to informing policy and response, they noted systemic barriers like poor communication with government, regulatory challenges and missed opportunities limited their impact. The communications/private sector participant noted that Peruvian academia operates within a ‘distinct bubble’, disconnected from policy and societal needs. A health/government participant stated that universities played no role during the pandemic. Overall, the lack of paths to bridge academic research with actionable public policies seems to confine research outcomes to academic circles and limit their broader societal impact:

There is no motivation or interest for research to correlate with public policies. - Education/Government participant

Many exposed critical gaps in leveraging academic expertise for public health crises, highlighting the need for better dissemination of research findings. There is also a prevailing perception among Peruvians that research conducted abroad is superior, leading to a reliance on international rather than local research in public health decision-making. To enhance national preparedness for future crises, some participants advocated for a paradigm shift in academia, urging universities to prioritise practical education over traditional academic pursuits.

However, others disagreed and believed that despite these challenges, there were relevant research efforts during the pandemic:

Academia had a role, and they showed it. I think the best example was the ventilators and the oxygen plants. They tried, but the government didn’t take advantage of that. Collaborative efforts between universities and sectors outside academia have proven effective. - Health/Academia participant

Some interviewees raised the potential of academic institutions to contribute meaningfully to public policy and societal needs. Health sector participants recommended integrating data-driven research into policymaking, emphasising the necessity for evidence-based decision-making frameworks in national health strategies. Strengthening the linkages among academia, government and the private sector was felt to be crucial for maximising the impact of research on public health.

Theme 2: coordinating intersectoral mechanisms

Participants highlighted the need for coordinated work across sectors and proposed several strategies and considerations to strengthen collaboration mechanisms and streamline governance processes, although there was a lack of consensus on how to achieve intersectoral collaboration.

Partnering and trust-building across sectors

Participants identified successful partnerships, such as the collaboration between the ministries of health, housing and education to establish COVID-19 screening sites and others as described below:

I have had the opportunity to work with the Ministry of Education and the Ministry of Housing which supported us in [COVID-19] screening areas. We have also worked with the Ministry of the Environment to manage temporary isolation centers. We have worked with the Ministry of the Interior on the security of work environments; with the Ministry of Defense, on border control, and supervision of people moving from one place to another; with the Ministry of Development and Social Inclusion, on supporting unprotected children; and with the Ministry of Tourism, on hotel management for isolation of suspected COVID-19 cases. - Health/Government participant

Additionally, the private sector seemed to play a crucial role in logistical operations, such as the transportation of vaccines. This collaboration involved both national airlines and private companies:

We called all national airlines and asked for help. The idea was to set up a “solidarity plane” to transport all vaccines. We coordinated with mining companies that had small planes to reach places without commercial flights. We spoke with the 8 regional governors for them to join private companies with vehicles to transport vaccines to various places. - Industry/Private sector participant

However, participants also noted that these collaborations were insufficient and that there were significant challenges related to intersectoral communication and a lack of trust among some stakeholders. These issues were seen as barriers to effective collaboration, highlighting the impact of distrust on collaborative efforts:

When parties trust each other, the possibility of working together is much greater. We can move forward together as I know there are no hidden interests. I believe that trust is something that, unfortunately, has been greatly weakened. In a framework of distrust, it is very difficult to work collaboratively. Here there was no trust. - Education/Government participant

A few participants noted that private sector involvement could enhance Peru’s autonomy and innovation, in partnership with academia. They believed the private sector could invest in underdeveloped medical supply and device industries in Latin America, including Peru. A participant suggested government incentives for private sector contributions could stimulate economic growth while building resilience to future health crises.

Strengthening, streamlining and building new mechanisms to improve preparedness and response

Several participants discussed existing frameworks, agreements and platforms that could be strengthened to leverage multisectoral collaboration for PPPR. Integrating PPPR into Peru’s economic projections (eg, the Multiannual Macroeconomic Framework26) was highlighted as one key opportunity. Similarly, the National Agreement,27 a multisectoral dialogue space for societal policy development, could potentially serve to promote proactive intersectoral policies by incorporating pandemic scenarios into long-term economic and policy planning:

In Peru, there is the Multi-annual Macroeconomic Framework projected for three years. In reality, the possible scenario of a pandemic should be part of any prospect that we make, even if it is for three years. (…) It would be important for the National Agreement to be leveraged by Peruvian authorities. The action of the State and the private sector in case of a new pandemic should be to define these joint policies. - Communications/Private sector participant

Participants emphasised the importance of collaboration between the health and agriculture sectors, especially among environment and wildlife subsectors. Participants noted the need for greater prioritisation, clear leadership and budget allocation for the wildlife sector to establish surveillance and monitoring networks, pathogen identification and diagnostic laboratory resources.

In Asia, they are quite prepared to identify and control it [a pathogen emergency]. Here, we do not have that vision, and I think we should consider it. Senasa may have human capabilities, maybe even infrastructure, but if it does not have the budget to transport samples, do necropsies, or monitor poultry farms, they will not be able to fulfill their work. - Agriculture/Government participant

Furthermore, this participant stressed the importance of addressing deforestation, land use and urban planning to mitigate the emergence of zoonotic diseases and urged the ministries of housing, environment and transportation to work collaboratively on this issue.

All participants emphasised the need to embed pandemic scenarios into intersectoral planning to ensure resilience. Effective public–private partnerships were seen as crucial to harnessing diverse resources and expertise, for example, maintaining educational continuity during lockdowns using multiple media platforms like the Internet and television.

A public-private partnership against future pandemics seems important and necessary. It should be capable of convening academia, private companies, NGOs, and social actors. - Communications/Private sector participant

Participants discussed the need for enhanced public policies and multisectoral collaboration as essential to effective PPPR. They noted the importance of government support for research. A participant suggested following other countries where the government funds research to generate evidence on critical topics, invests in capacity building and technology, and strengthens research capabilities for enhanced PPPR’s sustainability and autonomy.

I understand that in other countries the State even funds research centers, right? With precisely the aim of having more evidence on certain topics of interest. So, maybe we should think of something like that for Peru. - Communications/Private sector participant

Likewise, the need to streamline bureaucratic processes was highlighted as critical for improving PPPR’s agility and effectiveness. Some considered that simplifying administrative procedures and establishing regular meetings would enable quicker resource allocation, better navigation of governance structures and sustaining partnerships.

A few participants flagged the need for institutional reforms and depoliticising emergency response entities, involving all sectors, public, private, civil society and academia and ensuring continuity and effectiveness regardless of political changes. Creating an independent government agency was suggested to manage health emergencies:

We need a government autonomous entity, akin to the Central Reserve Bank, to manage emergencies and ensure continuity irrespective of political changes. - Education/Government participant

These perspectives collectively emphasised the complexity and importance of multisectoral collaboration in responding to pandemics. They advocate for integrated planning, community engagement and adaptive national, regional and local governance structures, including public–private partnerships and institutional reforms, to bolster Peru’s resilience against future health crises.

Financing Peru’s intersectoral mechanism

Interview data shows financing a PPPR intersectoral mechanism in Peru requires a multifaceted approach. A participant noted an existing emergency fund managed by the government which had been crucial for meeting emergency needs. This fund, which was activated during the pandemic, highlights the importance of financial preparedness:

The country has an emergency fund administered by the government. So, it’s not for lack of resources, it hasn’t been a lack of financial resources that has led us to have the biggest crisis. What it has been is a lack of [other} resources, skills, people, knowledgeable people, and delayed processes, right. It wasn't a lack of money; it was a lack of emergency management capacity. We have an emergency fund that was activated at that time, and we have enough stock now to be able to meet emergency needs. - Education/Government participant

Expanding the roles of existing government staff was proposed to reduce costs. A private sector participant suggested that using existing personnel from ministries and assigning them specific tasks within the intersectoral mechanism could help minimise additional financial burdens. However, this approach contrasts with all health sector participants who see them as already overburdened workers and emphasises the need for improved understanding across public and private sectors.

Budget allocation for intersectoral plans was deemed critical, with the ministry of economy and finance playing a key role in securing and distributing budgets effectively. To supplement government budgets, integrating international funds and cooperation, including loans from international financial institutions, was recommended:

You can think about financing through international funds, cooperation, and international banks. There is the IMF (International Monetary Fund), there is the KfW (State Development Bank of the Federal Republic of Germany). There are a lot of international banks. - Agriculture/Government participant

By combining government funding, international cooperation and strategic planning, it was felt that Peru could develop a sustainable intersectoral mechanism to improve PPPR.

Key recommendations to strengthening PPPR in Peru

Participants recommended initial priority actions, reflecting key insights on strengthening PPPR (table 3). These ideas emphasised the need to improve healthcare and emergency response systems, foster transparent and culturally sensitive communication, and institutionalise multisectoral collaboration. Most also highlighted the importance of involving communities in planning and ensuring financial readiness for future emergencies.

Discussion

This exploratory study provides a preliminary identification of salient concerns around Peru’s PPPR. These findings highlight the need to enhance intersectoral collaboration and suggest important new questions to more fully comprehend Peru’s response and lessons learnt. Leaders interviewed in this study agreed that the COVID-19 pandemic exposed significant shortcomings in Peru’s health system, including human and financial resource constraints, gaps and inequities in clinical, laboratory and public health infrastructure, and fragmented governance that undermined effective PPPR.2830 Conversely, countries like Argentina and Costa Rica that prioritised evidence-based interventions, engaged health experts in decision-making and maintained leadership credibility and trust in health authorities, and implemented decentralised governance reported lower incidence and mortality rates.31 32 While addressing these gaps requires sustained investment, recent projections suggest that government health spending would decrease from 2021 to 2026 in Peru and Brazil, in contrast to increases in countries such as Ecuador, Colombia, Paraguay and Cuba.33 Despite these challenges, Peru’s response demonstrated significant strengths, including its ability to expand healthcare capacity rapidly, implement effective COVID-19 vaccination campaigns34 35 and engage in public–private collaborations.

As our respondents emphasised, the pandemic disrupted multiple sectors beyond healthcare, including business and education, amplifying systemic inequalities and the need for multisectoral approaches.53638 These inequalities were further compounded by response measures such as lockdowns and mobility restrictions. Businesses, particularly small enterprises and informal workers that dominate Peru’s labour market, faced severe economic losses, while supply chain disruptions and limited financial relief further deepened economic instability.18 39 40 Strengthening public–private partnerships is likely to be important in mitigating economic shocks and promoting equitable recovery during future crises.41 For instance, several countries used public–private partnerships for vaccine distribution and contact tracing, demonstrating that well-coordinated partnerships can enhance public health responses.4244 Applying such approaches in Peru would likely require addressing governance fragmentation and fostering trust through mechanisms like intersectoral councils and public accountability systems. Politicisation of the response, unclear articulation of available resources and absence of shared communication mechanisms contributed to confusion and created space for misinformation to spread.28 While consistent public messaging can support compliance, overemphasis on uniform narratives without mechanisms for transparency, uncertainty and feedback may limit institutional learning and adaptive response during rapidly evolving crises.45 46

Similarly, as our interviews and other literature suggest, the education sector suffered from prolonged school closures, widening educational disparities, especially in rural areas where limited access to digital platforms marginalised vulnerable populations.47 Education systems must be integrated into a multisectoral framework to enhance health literacy and resilience in public health emergencies.

Another key gap identified by interviewees in Peru’s PPPR was the limited involvement of academic institutions in informing public health strategies. Globally, academia has played critical roles during health crises, including epidemiological modelling; vaccine, drug and test development; and rapid policy evaluations. Examples from the UK and the USA highlight how strong collaborations between academia and policymakers support evidence-based interventions.4850 However, in Peru and many other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), key informants suggest that the disconnect between academic research and public health practice weakened evidence-based decision-making and facilitated misinformation dissemination, exacerbating the crisis.51 This finding aligns with global literature emphasising the need for academic engagement with communities during health emergencies. Strengthening partnerships among local universities and public and private sectors, fostering collaborative research with policymakers and ensuring institutional commitments to translational research are essential for building pandemic-resilient systems.52 53

The One Health framework also provides a blueprint for multi-sector collaboration to address zoonotic disease risks.54 Global analyses, including the Lancet One Health Commission, highlight the need to weigh investments in specialised zoonotic surveillance against their opportunity costs in a health system with significant foundational gaps.55 56 Strengthening primary care, laboratory infrastructure and emergency response capacity is key for surveillance systems to function effectively.55 Importantly, economic evaluations show that prevention-oriented investments, including integrated zoonotic surveillance, are far more cost-effective than the economic and social costs of responding to large outbreaks.57 Therefore, balancing surveillance expansion with core system strengthening is critical to ensure PPPR investments are cost-effective and equitable.

This study also suggests the need for a more robust multisectoral coordinated approach to PPPR in Peru to address systemic barriers and enhance resilience. Interviews suggest that while some multisectoral mechanisms existed,58 59 they often lacked coordination, expertise, experience, supporting systems, authority and sustainability to respond effectively. Countries like New Zealand and Singapore demonstrated how integrating health, education and economic policies can minimise disruptions and support equitable recovery.6062 Peru must adopt similar strategies to bridge institutional silos, foster trust among stakeholders and improve resource allocation. Regional organisations, including Pan American Health Organization, along with development banks and research centres have critical roles in strengthening PPPR in Latin America.63 Successful PPPR requires not only strong health-sector leadership, but also integrated, multisectoral efforts to ensure equity, trust and efficiency64 65 across public, private and civil sectors,53 66 addressing social determinants of health which disproportionately affected vulnerable populations.61 62 Formalising cross-sectoral roles and fostering mutual accountability could help address these gaps for improving PPPR.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, as an exploratory study, while the findings do not fully capture the complexity of the issues involved in PPPR, identified themes could help orient further research. Second, our research question focused on high-level multisectoral leadership. Further research is needed to capture the experiences and challenges faced by regional and local actors. While the study identified the need for an intersectoral mechanism, it did not delve deeply into the implementation challenges and strategies required for its success. Third, the study did not sufficiently address the perspectives and roles of the animal health and agriculture sectors, or broader One Health collaborations, which are critical for preventing zoonotic disease transmission and strengthening PPPR. Fourth, interviews were conducted in 2023 and analysed in 2024. Since January 2025, many changes in US support for PPPR and other global health programmes may affect Peru’s future PPPR approach. Finally, as the study reflects the Peruvian context, findings should be tailored to the healthcare systems, governance structures and cultural factors of other countries.

Conclusion

Our findings point to recommendations for strengthening PPPR in Peru in four key arenas, with relevance for countries facing similar challenges. First, targeted investment in healthcare infrastructure, particularly in under-resourced areas, is critical to ensuring sufficient surge capacity and equitable access during crises. Second, stronger partnerships with academic institutions can help bridge the gap between research and policy, enabling evidence-based decision-making that addresses local needs. Third, improving communication protocols to provide timely, accurate, transparent information and counter misinformation is essential to rebuilding public trust and ensuring adherence to health measures. Finally, implementing intersectoral mechanisms requires sustainable financing and policy support from national and international stakeholders. This includes integrating PPPR into broader economic and policy planning, ensuring both formal and informal sectors are represented. By leveraging lessons during the COVID-19 pandemic, Peru has the opportunity to strengthen resilience and serve as a model for LMICs navigating similar challenges. This is the time to reinforce systems, rather than scale back critical measures. Sustainable collaboration, equity-focused strategies and systemic reforms are not only essential for future pandemics, but also for long-term population health resilience globally.
