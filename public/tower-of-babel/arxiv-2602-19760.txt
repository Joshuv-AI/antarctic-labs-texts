Extreme L p L_{p} discrepancy, numerical integration and the curse of dimensionality
Erich Novak, Friedrich Pillichshammer

Abstract
The classical notion of extreme L p L_{p} discrepancy is a quantitative measure for the irregularity of distribution of finite point sets in the d d -dimensinal unit cube. In this paper we find a dual integration problem whose worst-case error is exactly the extreme L p L_{p} discrepancy of the underlying integration nodes. Studying this integration problem we show that the extreme L p L_{p} discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality for all p ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) p\in(1,\infty) . It is known that the problem is tractable for p = ∞ p=\infty ; the case p = 1 p=1 stays open.



Keywords:
Discrepancy, numerical integration, curse of dimensionality, tractability, quasi-Monte Carlo

Introduction

For a set
\mathcal{P}
consisting of
N
points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
in the
d
-dimensional unit-cube
[0,1)^{d}
the local discrepancy with respect to a measurable test set
C
is defined as

\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(C)=\frac{|\{k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\in C\}|}{N}-{\rm volume}(C).

Using a class of test sets that are suitably parametrized, the local discrepancy can be considered as a function of this parameter. This is usually emphasized by the nomenclature “local discrepancy function”. Taking a norm of the local discrepancy function leads to the notion of a discrepancy, which is a quantitative measure for the irregularity of distribution of the point set
\mathcal{P}
with respect to the uniform measure and the class of test sets. Many notions of discrepancy are based on a suitable
L_{p}
-norm and often a discrepancy is related to the worst-case error of a quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) algorithm for numerical integration of a function from suitable function spaces.
The most prominent example is the usual star
L_{p}
discrepancy, which uses as test sets the class
of anchored hypercubes in
[0,1]^{d}
of the form
[\boldsymbol{0},{\boldsymbol{b}})=[0,b_{1})\times\ldots\times[0,b_{d})
with
{\boldsymbol{b}}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
.
In this case the relation between star
L_{p}
discrepancy and the
corresponding integration problem such that the worst-case error equals the star
L_{p}
discrepancy
is well understood (see
[
12
, Chapter 9]
or the recent papers
[
9
,
11
]
).
We call this behavior a “discrepancy-integration duality”.

The inverse of a discrepancy for dimension
d
and error threshold
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
is the minimal number of points in
[0,1)^{d}
required such that the minimal discrepancy is less or equal an
\varepsilon
share of the initial discrepancy. For applications of QMC to high-dimensional problems, it is of great importance whether the inverse grows exponentially with the dimension (the curse of dimensionality) or not (tractability). Again, for the classical star
L_{p}
discrepancy the behavior is for
p\in(1,\infty]
well understood. We have tractability for
p=\infty
(see
[
4
]
) and the curse of dimensionality for
p\in(1,\infty)
(see
[
11
]
). The case
p=1
is still open.

In this paper we study the extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy which uses as class of test sets all hypercubes
[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\subseteq[0,1)^{d}
. At least for
p=\infty
, this discrepancy concept is a classic one that is discussed in the literature (see e.g.
[
1
,
7
]
), but the more general
L_{p}
case has also been investigated (see e.g.
[
2
,
6
,
8
]
or
[
12
, Sec. 9.5.5]
). However, in comparison to the star discrepancy, relatively little is known for extreme discrepancy. We know neither of a corresponding integration problem, such that a discrepancy-integration duality holds true, nor do we know about the behavior of the inverse extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy with respect to the dimension for
p\not\in\{2,\infty\}
.

In this article, we investigate these questions and identify an integration problem where the worst-case error and the extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy coincide (discrepancy-integration duality). Furthermore, we prove that the extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy for
p\in(1,\infty)
suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give a rigorous definition of extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy and its extension to generalized extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy. A related dual integration problem is introduced in Section
3
. The main result of this section, which is Theorem
2
, states that the worst-case error for integration with arbitrary linear rules (which comprise the concept of QMC algorithms) equals the generalized extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy. Furthermore, we study in detail the initial error of our integration problem and figure out a so-called worst-case function, i.e., a function whose integral equals the initial error. In Section
4
we prove that the extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy for
p\in(1,\infty)
as well as the dual integration problem suffers from the curse of dimensionality. The proof of this result is given in Section
5
.

2
Extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy

The
extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy
uses as test sets arbitrary hypercubes contained in the unit cube. Let

\mathbb{D}_{d}:=\{({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\in[0,1]^{d}\times[0,1]^{d}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{a}}\leq{\boldsymbol{b}}\},

where for
{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d})
and
{\boldsymbol{b}}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{d})
in
[0,1]^{d}
we write
{\boldsymbol{a}}\leq{\boldsymbol{b}}
if and only if
a_{j}\leq b_{j}
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. For
({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\in\mathbb{D}_{d}
let
[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})=[a_{1},b_{1})\times[a_{2},b_{2})\times\ldots\times[a_{d},b_{d})
. Throughout, let
{\bf 1}_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})}
denote the indicator function of
[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})
. Then

\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}))=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})-\prod_{j=1}^{d}(b_{j}-a_{j})

and
the extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy of
\mathcal{P}
is then defined as

L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P}):=\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}))|^{p}\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\right)^{1/p},

for
p\in[1,\infty)
and

L_{\infty,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P}):=\sup_{({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\in\mathbb{D}_{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}))|,

for
p=\infty
.

An extension of the classical definition additionally uses a set of real weights
\mathcal{A}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\}
corresponding
to the points
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{t}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{t}}_{N}\}
. Then the generalized discrepancy function with weights
\mathcal{A}
is

\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}))=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\,\mathbf{1}_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})-\prod_{j=1}^{d}(b_{j}-a_{j}),

and the generalized extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy with weights
\mathcal{A}
is

L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})=\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}))|^{p}\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\right)^{1/p},

for
p\in[1,\infty)
with the usual modification for
p=\infty
.
If
c_{1}=\dots=c_{N}=1/N
, we recover the classical extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy
L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P})
of the point set
\mathcal{P}
.

It is easily seen that the so-called initial extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy, i.e., the extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy of the emptyset, or, in other words, the
L_{p}
-norm of the volume function
({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\in\mathbb{D}_{d}\mapsto{\rm volume}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}))
, equals

L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}((p+1)(p+2))^{-d/p}&\mbox{if $p\in[1,\infty)$,}\\
1&\mbox{if $p=\infty$.}\end{array}\right.

This is clear for
p=\infty
. For
p\in[1,\infty)
we have

\displaystyle(L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}})^{p}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(b_{j}-a_{j})^{p}\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{a}^{1}(b-a)^{p}\,{\rm d}b\,{\rm d}a\right)^{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{((p+1)(p+2))^{d}},

and the result follows by taking the
p
-th root.

3
A related dual integration problem

Throughout we assume that
p,q\in[1,\infty]
are Hölder conjugates, i.e.,
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. For
p,q\in(1,\infty)
this implies that
\frac{q}{p}=q-1
.

Representation-space definition via box indicators.

For
d
-fold hypercubes
[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times\ldots\times[a_{d},b_{d}]
we use the indicator function

{\bf 1}_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\bf 1}_{[a_{j},b_{j})}(x_{j})\quad\mbox{for ${\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})$.}

Definition 1

(Representation-space definition of
F_{d,q}
)
.

Define a linear operator
T_{d}
on
L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})
via

(T_{d}c)({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\,{\bf 1}_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\quad\text{for }c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d}).

(1)

Define

F_{d,q}:=\{f\ :\ c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d}),\ f=T_{d}c\}

and equip this space with the norm

\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}:=\inf\left\{\|c\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}:\;c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d}),\ f=T_{d}c\right\}.

(2)

Remark 1
.

For every
c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})
the function
f=T_{d}c
is well defined a.e., belongs to
L_{q}([0,1]^{d})
, is absolutely continuous in each variable, and satisfies the boundary condition
f({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
whenever some
x_{j}\in\{0,1\}
.

Remark 2
.

We confess that we do not know whether the norm
\|\cdot\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}
is a tensor-product norm. In any case, for functions
f({\boldsymbol{x}})=f_{1}(x_{1})\cdots f_{d}(x_{d})
with univariate functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}
we have
\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\leq\|f_{1}\|_{F_{1,q},{\rm box}}\cdots\|f_{d}\|_{F_{1,q},{\rm box}}
.

Remark 3
.

Likewise, one might equip
F_{d,q}
with a mixed Sobolev norm

\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm mix}}:=\|Df\|_{L_{q}([0,1]^{d})},

with the mixed derivative
Df:=\partial_{1}\cdots\partial_{d}f
(which exists in the weak sense and belongs to
L_{q}([0,1]^{d})
).
The two norms are equal for
q=2
, i.e.
\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}=\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm mix}}
, and it can be shown that the two norms are always equivalent, although with implied constants that depend on the dimension
d
. In more detail,

2^{-d}\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm mix}}\leq\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\leq 2^{d}\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm mix}}.

(3)

See Appendix
A
for details.

In the following we will work with the space
F_{d,q}
equipped with the box-norm
\|\cdot\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}
.

Integration problem and worst-case error.

As above, let
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
be an
N
-element point set in
[0,1)^{d}
and
let
\mathcal{A}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\}
be corresponding weights. For
f\in F_{d,q}
let

I(f):=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(f):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}).

(4)

If we restrict to weights
c_{1}=\ldots=c_{N}=1/N
, we denote these by
\mathcal{A}^{{\rm QMC}}
,
then
Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{{\rm QMC}}}(f):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})
, which is a so-called quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) algorithm.
Define the error functional

L(f):=I(f)-Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(f).

With
\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}}-\lambda
, where
\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}}
denotes the Dirac measure concentrated in
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
and
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d}
, we have

L(f)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}\left(\lambda-\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}}\right)({\boldsymbol{x}})=-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}}).

(5)

The worst-case error in
F_{d,q}
(with respect to the box-norm
\|\cdot\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}
) is

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,q}):=\sup_{\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\leq 1}|L(f)|.

Theorem 2

(Discrepancy-integration duality)
.

Let
p,q\in[1,\infty]
be Hölder conjugates. For every point set

\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
in
[0,1)^{d}
and arbitrary corresponding real weights
\mathcal{A}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\}
we have

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,q})\;=\;L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

Proof.

Let
f\in F_{d,q}
and choose
c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})
representing
f
as in (
1
).
Using (
5
) and Fubini, we compute

\displaystyle L(f)

\displaystyle=-\int f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})=-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\,{\bf 1}_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\right)\,{\rm d}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}{\bf 1}_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\,\mu([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\,\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}).

Now, by Hölder’s inequality,

|L(f)|\leq\|c\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}\,\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}=\|c\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}\,L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

Taking the infimum over all representations of
f
yields

|L(f)|\leq\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\,L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

Hence

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,q})\leq L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

It remains to prove equality. First we assume
p,q\in(1,\infty)
and discuss the needed modifications
for the endpoints later. If
\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\equiv 0
then
L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})=0
and also
L(f)\equiv 0
,
so equality holds. Assume now
\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\not\equiv 0
. Define

c^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}):=\frac{|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])|^{p-2}\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}^{p-1}}\quad\text{so that}\quad\|c^{\ast}\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}=1.

(6)

Let
f^{\ast}=T_{d}c^{\ast}
with coefficient
c^{\ast}
. Then
\|f^{\ast}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\leq 1
by definition of the norm (
2
), and

\displaystyle L(f^{\ast})=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\,\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}^{p-1}}\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})|^{p}\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}.

Therefore
|L(f^{\ast})|=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}=L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
and hence

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,q})\geq L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

Combining with the upper bound yields the identity.

For the case
p=1
we take
c^{*}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})={\rm sign}(\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}))
and obtain
again
|L(f^{\ast})|=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}=L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
.
Assume now that
p=\infty
and
q=1
. Let

M:=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, define

E_{\varepsilon}:=\{({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}):|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})|\geq M-\varepsilon\}.

Assume
m(E_{\varepsilon})>0
.
Define

c_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})=\frac{{\rm sign}(\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}))\,{\mathbf{1}}_{E_{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})}{m(E_{\varepsilon})}.

Then
\|c_{\varepsilon}\|_{L_{1}}=1
and

\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\,\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\geq M-\varepsilon.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
gives

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,1})=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\|_{L_{\infty}}=L_{\infty,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

∎

Minimal discrepancy and minimal worst-case error.

For
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
the
N
-th minimal extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy in dimension
d
is defined as

{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm ext}}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})

and the
N
-th minimal worst-case error is defined as

e_{q}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}|e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,q})|,

where
Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}
is a linear algorithm of the form (
4
) and where in both cases the
minimum is extended over all
N
-element point sets
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
from
[0,1)^{d}
and real weights
\mathcal{A}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\}
.

Later we will restrict ourselves to non-negative weights
c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\geq 0
. We denote sets of such weights by
\mathcal{A}^{+}
and define

{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm ext},+}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+})

and

e_{q}^{+}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}|e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};F_{d,q})|,

where in both cases the minimum is extended over all
N
-element point sets
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
from
[0,1)^{d}
and non-negative weights
\mathcal{A}^{+}
.

If we restrict to QMC weights
\mathcal{A}^{{\rm QMC}}
, then

{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm ext},{\rm QMC}}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P}}L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P})

and

e_{q}^{{\rm QMC}}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P}}|e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{{\rm QMC}}};F_{d,q})|

where in both cases the minimum is extended over all
N
-element point sets
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
from
[0,1)^{d}
.

It is obvious, that

e_{q}(N,d)\leq e_{q}^{+}(N,d)\leq e_{q}^{{\rm QMC}}(N,d)

and

{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm ext}}(N,d)\leq{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm ext},+}(N,d)\leq{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm ext},{\rm QMC}}(N,d).

From Theorem
2
we obtain the following result:

Corollary 1
.

For Hölder conjugates
p,q
, for
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
we have

e_{q}^{\bullet}(N,d)={\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm ext},\bullet}(N,d)\qquad\mbox{for }\bullet\in\{{\rm blank},+,{\rm QMC}\}.

Initial error and worst-case function.

The initial error is the worst-case error for the zero integration rule
Q\equiv 0
which uses no point evaluation. It is denoted by
e(0;F_{d,q})
. Hence

e(0;F_{d,q})=\sup_{\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\leq 1}|I(f)|.

Furthermore, we call a non-zero function
h\in F_{d,q}
a worst-case function, if

|I(h)|=e(0;F_{d,q}).

Proposition 3
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and let
q
be the corresponding Hölder conjugate. We have

e(0;F_{d,q})=L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}

and the function

h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\left(\frac{p+2}{p}\frac{1}{((p+1)(p+2))^{1/p}}\right)^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1-x_{j}^{p+1}-(1-x_{j})^{p+1}\right)

is a worst-case function. We have
h_{d}\geq 0
,
\|h_{d}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}=1
and

I(h_{d})=L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}=\frac{1}{((p+1)(p+2))^{d/p}}.

Proof.

For the zero integration rule we have
\mu=-\lambda
and we obtain from the first part of the proof of Theorem
2
that

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=I(f)=L(f)=\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\lambda([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}),

where
c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})
is any representer of
f
. Hence

|I(f)|\leq\|c\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}\,\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})},

where obviously
\Delta_{\emptyset}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})=-\lambda([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])
. Taking the infimum over all representers
c
and noting that
\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}=L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}
we obtain
|I(f)|\leq\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\,L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}
and hence

e(0;F_{d,q})\leq L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}.

Put

\displaystyle c_{0}^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}):=

\displaystyle\frac{|\Delta_{\emptyset}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})|^{p-1}}{\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}^{p-1}}=\frac{(\lambda([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]))^{p-1}}{\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}^{p-1}}

(7)

if
p>1
and
c_{0}^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}):=1
if
p=1
. Then
c_{0}^{\ast}\geq 0
and
\|c_{0}^{\ast}\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}=1
. Consider
h_{d}:=T_{d}c_{0}^{\ast}
. Then
\|h_{d}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\leq\|c_{0}^{\ast}\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}=1
and

\displaystyle I(h_{d})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c_{0}^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})\,\lambda([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}^{p-1}}\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}(\lambda([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]))^{p}\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}=L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|h_{d}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\,L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}.

Therefore
e(0;F_{d,q})\geq L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}
. Combining with the upper bound we obtain

e(0;F_{d,q})=L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\frac{I(h_{d})}{\|h_{d}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}}=e(0;F_{d,q}),

i.e.,
h_{d}
is a worst-case function.
Note that from
\|h_{d}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\,L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}\geq I(h_{d})\geq L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}
it even follows that
\|h_{d}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}=1
.

It remains to compute the explicit form of
h_{d}
. We have

h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=(T_{d}c_{0}^{\ast})({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c_{0}^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}){\bf 1}_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}).

With
c_{0}^{\ast}\geq 0
also
h_{d}\geq 0
. From (
7
),
\Delta_{\emptyset}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})=-\lambda([{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}])
and
\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}=((p+1)(p+2))^{-d/p}
we obtain

\displaystyle c_{0}^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}):=((p+1)(p+2))^{\frac{d(p-1)}{p}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(b_{j}-a_{j})^{p-1}.

Hence

\displaystyle h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=

\displaystyle((p+1)(p+2))^{\frac{d(p-1)}{p}}\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(b_{j}-a_{j})^{p-1}{\bf 1}_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((p+1)(p+2))^{\frac{d(p-1)}{p}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{D}_{1}}(b-a)^{p-1}{\bf 1}_{[a,b]}(x_{j})\,{\rm d}(a,b).

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}_{1}}(b-a)^{p-1}{\bf 1}_{[a,b]}(x_{j})\,{\rm d}(a,b)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x_{j}}\int_{x_{j}}^{1}(b-a)^{p-1}\,{\rm d}b\,{\rm d}a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-x_{j}^{p+1}-(1-x_{j})^{p+1}}{p(p+1)}.

Thus

h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\left(\frac{p+2}{p}\frac{1}{((p+1)(p+2))^{1/p}}\right)^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1-x_{j}^{p+1}-(1-x_{j})^{p+1}\right)

as desired.
∎

Inverse discrepancy and information complexity.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
the inverse of the
N
-th minimal extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy is defined as

N_{p}^{{\rm disc},\bullet}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ {\rm disc}_{p}^{\bullet}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\ L_{p,0}^{{\rm ext}}\}

where
\bullet\in\{{\rm blank},+,{\rm QMC}\}
. The information complexity of the integration problem is defined as the minimal number of function evaluations necessary in order to reduce the initial error by a factor of
\varepsilon
, i.e., for
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

N^{{\rm int},\bullet}_{q}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ e_{q}^{\bullet}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\ e_{q}(0,d)\},

where
\bullet\in\{{\rm blank},+,{\rm QMC}\}
.

Obviously,

N_{p}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq N_{p}^{{\rm disc},+}(\varepsilon,d)\leq N_{p}^{{\rm disc},{\rm QMC}}(\varepsilon,d)

and likewise for
N^{{\rm int},\bullet}_{q}(\varepsilon,d)
. From Corollary
1
we obtain:

Corollary 2
.

For Hölder conjugates
p,q
, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

N_{p}^{{\rm disc},\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)=N^{{\rm int},\bullet}_{q}(\varepsilon,d)\qquad\mbox{for $\bullet\in\{{\rm blank},+,{\rm QMC}\}$.}

The curse of dimensionality

It is known from
[
4
]
that the extreme
L_{\infty}
discrepancy is polynomially tractable with the upper bound
N_{\infty}^{{\rm disc},{\rm QMC}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq C\,d\varepsilon^{-2}
. The proof is as for the star-discrepancy; see also Hinrichs
[
5
]
. Gnewuch
[
2
,
3
]
proved explicit upper bounds. In particular,
[
3
, Theorem 2.2]
states that

N_{\infty}^{{\rm disc},{\rm QMC}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq\left\lceil 2\varepsilon^{-2}\left(2d\log\left(\frac{10{\rm e}}{\varepsilon}\right)+\log 2\right)\right\rceil

for
d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
and for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

On the other hand, the extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality, as shown in
[
12
, p. 93-94]
. This means that the inverse of the
N
-th minimal extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy grows at least exponential in
d
. In more detail,

N_{2}^{{\rm disc},+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq(1-\varepsilon^{2})\left(\frac{9}{4}\right)^{d}.

The behavior for general
p\not\in\{2,\infty\}
was unknown so far and is the topic of this section. As in the case of
p=2
in
[
12
]
, our method only allows a result for linear rules with non-negative weights. In any case, this includes the case of QMC rules. The case
p=1
remains as an open problem.

Theorem 4
.

For every
p\in(1,\infty)
there exists a number
C_{p}>1
such that

N_{p}^{{\rm disc},+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)\qquad\mbox{for $d\in\mathbb{N}$ and $\varepsilon\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2})$.}

In other words, the extreme
L_{p}
discrepancy for non-negative weights (and hence also for QMC weights) suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

According to Corollary
2
, an equivalent formulation in terms of integration is the following.

Theorem 5
.

For every
q\in(1,\infty)
we have

N_{q}^{{\rm int},+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)\qquad\mbox{for $d\in\mathbb{N}$ and $\varepsilon\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2})$,}

where
C_{p}>1
is the number from Theorem
4
with
p
the Hölder conjugate of
q
.
In other words, integration in
F_{d,q}
with non-negative linear rules suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

The proof of Theorem
5
, and therefore also of Theorem
4
, is presented in the following section.

Spline interpolation for the worst-case function.

We consider the univariate worst-case function

h_{1}(x)=\frac{p+2}{p}\frac{1}{((p+1)(p+2))^{1/p}}\left(1-x^{p+1}-(1-x)^{p+1}\right).

(8)

For
x\in[0,1]
set

E_{x}:=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{D}_{1}:a\leq x\leq b\}.

Then for every
c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{1})
we have

(T_{1}c)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{D}_{1}}c(a,b){\bf 1}_{[a,b]}(x)\,{\rm d}(a,b)=\int_{E_{x}}c(a,b)\,{\rm d}(a,b).

The area of
E_{x}
, denoted by
m(E_{x})
, is

m(E_{x})=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{x}^{1}\,{\rm d}b\,{\rm d}a=x(1-x).

In particular,
m(E_{0})=m(E_{1})=0
and thus also
(T_{1}c)(0)=(T_{1}c)(1)=0
.

For
y\in[0,1]
put

c_{y}(a,b):=\frac{h_{1}(y)}{m(E_{y})}{\bf 1}_{E_{y}}(a,b)\qquad\mbox{for $(a,b)\in\mathbb{D}_{1}$,}

and

s_{y}(x):=(T_{1}c_{y})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{D}_{1}}c_{y}(a,b){\bf 1}_{[a,b]}(x)\,{\rm d}(a,b)=\int_{E_{x}}c_{y}(a,b)\,{\rm d}(a,b).

(9)

Proposition 6
.

For the function
s_{y}
defined by (
9
) we have
s_{y}(x)\geq 0
and
s_{y}(0)=s_{y}(1)=0
and

s_{y}(y)=h_{1}(y).

Furthermore,

\|s_{y}\|_{F_{1,q},{\rm box}}=\frac{h_{1}(y)}{(y(1-y))^{1/p}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{1}s_{y}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\frac{h_{1}(y)}{2}.

Proof.

The properties
s_{y}(x)\geq 0
and
s_{y}(0)=s_{y}(1)=0
and
s_{y}(y)=h_{1}(y)
are obviously true.

We compute the
\|\cdot\|_{F_{1,q},{\rm box}}
-norm of
s_{y}
. For any function
c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{1})
such that
\int_{E_{y}}c(a,b)\,{\rm d}(a,b)=h_{1}(y)
we have by Hölder

h_{1}(y)=\left|\int_{E_{y}}c(a,b)\,{\rm d}(a,b)\right|\leq\|c\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{1})}m(E_{y})^{1/p}

and hence

\|c\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{1})}\geq\frac{h_{1}(y)}{m(E_{y})^{1/p}}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\|c_{y}\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{1})}^{q}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}_{1}}\left(\frac{h_{1}(y)}{m(E_{y})}\right)^{q}{\bf 1}_{E_{y}}(a,b)\,{\rm d}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{E_{y}}\left(\frac{h_{1}(y)}{m(E_{y})}\right)^{q}\,{\rm d}(a,b)=\left(\frac{h_{1}(y)}{m(E_{y})}\right)^{q}m(E_{y}).

Hence

\|c_{y}\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{1})}=\frac{h_{1}(y)}{m(E_{y})^{1/p}}.

This implies that

\|s_{y}\|_{F_{1,q},{\rm box}}=\frac{h_{1}(y)}{m(E_{y})^{1/p}}=\frac{h_{1}(y)}{(y(1-y))^{1/p}}.

We compute the integral of
s_{y}
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}s_{y}(x)\,{\rm d}x=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{D}_{1}}c_{y}(a,b){\bf 1}_{[a,b]}(x)\,{\rm d}(a,b)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}_{1}}c_{y}(a,b)\int_{0}^{1}{\bf 1}_{[a,b]}(x)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}_{1}}c_{y}(a,b)(b-a)\,{\rm d}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{h_{1}(y)}{m(E_{y})}\int_{E_{y}}(b-a)\,{\rm d}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{h_{1}(y)}{y(1-y)}\int_{0}^{y}\int_{y}^{1}(b-a)\,{\rm d}b\,{\rm d}a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{h_{1}(y)}{2}.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Consider a linear algorithm
Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}
of the form (
4
) based in nodes
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
in
[0,1]^{d}
and with non-negative weights
\mathcal{A}^{+}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\}
. For
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
let
x_{k,j}
be the
j
-th coordinate of the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
. For
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
we define functions

P_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=s_{x_{k,1}}(x_{1})s_{x_{k,2}}(x_{2})\cdots s_{x_{k,d}}(x_{d}),\quad\mbox{for ${\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}$.}

Consider the two functions
h_{d}
(the worst-case function from Proposition
3
) and
f^{*}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}
.
Since
Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}
uses only non-negative weights we have

\displaystyle Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}(f^{*})=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\geq\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}P_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}s_{x_{k,1}}(x_{k,1})s_{x_{k,2}}(x_{k,2})\cdots s_{x_{k,d}}(x_{k,d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}(h_{d}).

Now for real
y
we use the notation
(y)_{+}:=\max(y,0)
. Then we have the error estimate

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};F_{d,q})\geq\frac{\left(I(h_{d})-I(f^{*})\right)_{+}}{2\max(1,\|f^{*}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}})},

(10)

which is trivially true if
I(h_{d})\leq I(f^{*})
and which is easily shown if
I(h_{d})>I(f^{*})
, because then

\displaystyle\left(I(h_{d})-I(f^{*})\right)_{+}\leq

\displaystyle I(h_{d})-Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}(h_{d})+Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}(f^{*})-I(f^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|h_{d}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\,e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};F_{d,q})+\|f^{*}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\,e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};F_{d,q})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\max(1,\|f^{*}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}})\,e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};F_{d,q}),

where we used that
\|h_{d}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}=1
. This implies (
10
).

From the triangle inequality and from the definition of
A_{p}
and
B_{p}
in Proposition
7
we obtain

\|f^{*}\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}\leq NB_{p}^{d}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad I(f^{*})\leq NA_{p}^{d}\,I(h_{d}).

Inserting into (
10
) and taking into account that
I(h_{d})=e(0;F_{d,q})
yields

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};F_{d,q})\geq\frac{e(0;F_{d,q})\left(1-NA_{p}^{d}\right)_{+}}{2\max(1,NB_{p}^{d})}.

This yields

e_{q}^{+}(N,d)\geq\frac{e(0;F_{d,q})\left(1-NA_{p}^{d}\right)_{+}}{2\max(1,NB_{p}^{d})}.

Now let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and assume that
e_{q}^{+}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,e_{q}(0,d)
. This implies that

2\varepsilon\max\left(1,NB_{p}^{d}\right)\geq\left(1-NA_{p}^{d}\right)_{+}.

Proposition
7
yields

C_{p}:=\min\left(\frac{1}{A_{p}},\frac{1}{B_{p}}\right)>1.

If
N\leq C_{p}^{d}
, then we obtain

\displaystyle 1-NA_{p}^{d}=

\displaystyle\left(1-NA_{p}^{d}\right)_{+}\leq 2\varepsilon\max\left(1,NB_{p}^{d}\right)=2\varepsilon.

Hence

N\geq\frac{1}{A_{p}^{d}}(1-2\varepsilon)\geq C_{p}^{d}(1-2\varepsilon).

If
N\geq C_{p}^{d}
, then we trivially have
N\geq C_{p}^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)
.

This yields

N_{q}^{{\rm int},+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}(1-2\varepsilon),

and we are done.
∎

F_{d,2}
can be written as

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,2})^{2}=\|h_{d}\|^{2}-2\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})+\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}c_{j}c_{k}K_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}),

see, e.g.,
[
12
, Eq. (9.31)]
. Hence the error is given by the kernel and this formula also determines the kernel
if all worst-case errors are known. Now it is known that

K_{d}^{\rm mix}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\prod_{k=1}^{d}(\min(x_{k},y_{k})-x_{k}y_{k})

is the kernel of
F_{d,2}
with the mix norm and yields the correct error for the extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy,
see
[
12
, Eq. (9.41)]
.
Hence the dual integration problem is already known for
q=2
.
Together with Theorem 2 we obtain that
K_{d}^{{\rm mix}}
is also the kernel of
F_{d,2}
with the box norm
and the norms coincide. Both norms or kernels
describe the same integration
problem.
We add that a more direct calculation of the kernel
K_{d}^{{\rm box}}
is possible,
based on the fact that the infimum in the definition of

\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}

can be easily described in a Hilbert space via orthogonality.
∎

Appendix B
Appendix

Proof of Proposition
7
.

First we deal with
A_{p}
. We have

\frac{\int_{0}^{1}s_{y}(x)\,{\rm d}x}{\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x}=\frac{h_{1}(y)/2}{((p+1)(p+2))^{-1/p}}

and hence

\max_{y\in[0,1]}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}s_{y}(x)\,{\rm d}x}{\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x}=\frac{((p+1)(p+2))^{1/p}}{2}\max_{y\in[0,1]}h_{1}(y).

From (
8
) it is easily seen that

\max_{y\in[0,1]}h_{1}(y)=h_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2})=\frac{p+2}{p}\frac{1}{((p+1)(p+2))^{1/p}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{p}}\right)

and hence

\max_{y\in[0,1]}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}s_{y}(x)\,{\rm d}x}{\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x}=\frac{p+2}{2p}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{p}}\right)<1.

Now we deal with
B_{p}
.
We have
\|h_{1}\|_{F_{1,q},{\rm box}}=1
and

\|s_{y}\|_{F_{1,q},{\rm box}}=\frac{h_{1}(y)}{(y(1-y))^{1/p}}=T_{p}S(y)=:F_{p}(y),

where
S(y):=\frac{1-y^{p+1}-(1-y)^{p+1}}{(y(1-y))^{1/p}}
and
T_{p}:=\frac{p+2}{p}\frac{1}{((p+1)(p+2))^{1/p}}
. Thus we have to show
F_{p}(y)<1
for all
y\in[0,1]
. By symmetry, any extremum of
F_{p}(y)
must occur at
y=\tfrac{1}{2}
or come in symmetric pairs
y
and
1-y
. Observe that
S(y)\rightarrow 0
for
y\rightarrow 0
or, likewise,
y\rightarrow 1
.

Since the behavior of the function
F_{p}(y)
differs fundamentally for small values of
p
from that for larger
p
(see Figure
2
), we must distinguish between three cases.

Figure 2:
The function
F_{p}(y)
,
y\in[0,1]
for
p\in\{2,8\}
(first row – the maximum is attaind in
y=\tfrac{1}{2}
) and
p\in\{20,50\}
(second row – the maximum is
not
attained in
y=\frac{1}{2}
, rather in
y=\tfrac{1}{2}
a local minimum is attained).

p\leq 8
:

We consider
L(y):=\log S(y)=\log N(y)-\tfrac{1}{p}\left(\log y+\log(1-y)\right)
, where

N(y)=1-y^{p+1}-(1-y)^{p+1}.

Then

N^{\prime}(y)=-(p+1)(y^{p}-(1-y)^{p})\quad\mbox{and}\quad N^{\prime\prime}(y)=-p(p+1)(y^{p-1}+(1-y)^{p-1}).

By symmetry,
y=\tfrac{1}{2}
is a critical point, i.e.,
N^{\prime}(\tfrac{1}{2})=0
and also

L^{\prime}(y)=\frac{N^{\prime}(y)}{N(y)}-\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{1}{y}-\frac{1}{1-y}\right)

such that
L^{\prime}(\tfrac{1}{2})=0
. Thus
y=\tfrac{1}{2}
is also a critical point of
L(y)
. The second derivative is

L^{\prime\prime}(y)=\frac{N^{\prime\prime}(y)N(y)-N^{\prime}(y)^{2}}{N(y)^{2}}+\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{1}{y^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1-y)^{2}}\right)

and hence

L^{\prime\prime}(\tfrac{1}{2})=\frac{N^{\prime\prime}(\tfrac{1}{2})}{N(\tfrac{1}{2})}+\frac{8}{p}.

Since
N(\tfrac{1}{2})=1-\frac{1}{2^{p}}
and
N^{\prime\prime}(\tfrac{1}{2})=-\frac{p(p+1)}{2^{p-2}}
we obtain

L^{\prime\prime}(\tfrac{1}{2})=-\frac{p(p+1)2^{2-p}}{1-2^{-p}}+\frac{8}{p}.

For
p\in(1,8]
this is strictly negative. So there is a unique maximizer in
y=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
S(y)\leq S(\tfrac{1}{2})=4^{1/p}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{p}}\right)
. Thus, for
p\in(1,8]
we have

F_{p}(y)\leq\frac{p+2}{p}\frac{1}{((p+1)(p+2))^{1/p}}4^{1/p}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{p}}\right)<1.

p\geq 11
:

Let
y\in(0,1/2]
and set
a:=(p+1)y
. Then
a\in(0,(p+1)/2]
. Since
y^{p+1}\geq 0
we have
1-y^{p+1}-(1-y)^{p+1}\leq 1-(1-y)^{p+1}
. Since
y\in(0,1/2]\subseteq(0,1)
we have
\log(1-y)\geq-y/(1-y)\geq-2y
it follows that

(1-y)^{p+1}\geq{\rm e}^{-2(p+1)y}={\rm e}^{-2a}.

Thus,

1-(1-y)^{p+1}\leq 1-{\rm e}^{-2a}.

On the other hand, for
y\leq 1/2
we have
1-y\geq 1/2
and hence
y(1-y)\geq y/2=a/(2(p+1))
. Therefore

((p+1)(p+2)y(1-y))^{1/p}\geq\left((p+1)(p+2)\frac{a}{2(p+1)}\right)^{1/p}=\left(\frac{(p+2)a}{2}\right)^{1/p}.

Together we obtain

F_{p}(y)\leq\frac{p+2}{p}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2a}\right)\left(\frac{2}{(p+2)a}\right)^{1/p}=:G_{p}(a).

(13)

Thus it suffices to prove that
G_{p}(a)<1
for all
a\in(0,(p+1)/2]
.

Consider

\log G_{p}(a)=\log\frac{p+2}{p}+\log\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2a}\right)+\frac{\log 2-\log(p+2)-\log a}{p}.

(14)

Now we show that

\log\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2a}\right)-\frac{\log a}{p}<0.

(15)

For
a\geq 1
the inequality (
15
) trivially holds true.

For
2^{-p/(p-1)}\leq a\leq 1
we have
\tfrac{1}{p}\log a\geq\tfrac{1}{p}\tfrac{p}{p-1}\log\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{1}{p-1}\log\tfrac{1}{2}
and hence

-\frac{\log a}{p}\leq\frac{\log 2}{p-1}\leq\frac{\log 2}{7},

since
p\geq 8
. Furthermore, from
a\leq 1
we obtain

\log\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2a}\right)\leq\log\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2}\right).

Together this gives

\displaystyle\log\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2a}\right)-\frac{\log a}{p}\leq\log\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2}\right)+\frac{\log 2}{7}=-0.0463\ldots<0,

and hence (
15
) is shown also in this case.

For
a<2^{-p/(p-1)}
we obtain
a^{(p-1)/p}<\tfrac{1}{2}
and hence
2a<a^{1/p}
and further
-a^{1/p}<-2a
. This yields
\log\left(1-a^{1/p}\right)\leq-a^{1/p}<-2a
and hence
1-a^{1/p}<{\rm e}^{-2a}
. Hence
1-{\rm e}^{-2a}<a^{1/p}
and thus

\log\left(1-{\rm e}^{-2a}\right)<\frac{\log a}{p},

which proves (
15
) also in the final case.

Combining (
14
) and (
15
) we obtain

\log G_{p}(a)\leq\log\frac{p+2}{p}+\frac{\log 2-\log(p+2)}{p}<0

for all
p\geq 11
. Hence, for
p\geq 11
and any
a\in(0,(p+1)/2]
we have
G_{p}(a)<1
.

8<p<11
:

We continue studying
G_{p}(a)
from (
13
). It is easily seen that
G_{p}^{\prime}(a)=0
if and only if
a=a_{\ast}
where
a_{\ast}
satisfies
1-{\rm e}^{2a_{\ast}}+2a_{\ast}p=0
. This
a_{\ast}
is the unique stationary point. We obtain

1-{\rm e}^{-2a_{\ast}}=\frac{2a_{\ast}p}{1+2a_{\ast}p}.

Let

\widetilde{G}_{p}(a):=\frac{p+2}{p}\frac{2ap}{1+2ap}\left(\frac{2}{(p+2)a}\right)^{1/p}.

Then we have

G_{p}(a)\leq G_{p}(a_{\ast})=\widetilde{G}_{p}(a_{\ast}).

Now we maximize
\widetilde{G}_{p}(a)
. It is easily seen that
\widetilde{G}_{p}(a)=0
if and only if
a=\frac{p-1}{2p}
. Again, this is the unique stationary point and we have

G_{p}(a)\leq\widetilde{G}_{p}(a_{\ast})\leq\widetilde{G}_{p}\left(\tfrac{p-1}{2p}\right)=\left(\frac{(p-1)(p+2)}{p}\right)^{1-1/p}\frac{4^{1/p}}{p}<1

for
8<p<11
.
