Host-associated mitochondrial structure and broad geographic connectivity in Drepanocephalus associated with migratory cormorants
Authors: Daniel Muñoz, Stephen E. Harris, Erika T. Ebbs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Digenetic trematodes are important parasites of aquaculture systems, where transmission may be amplified by interactions among aquatic birds, fishes, and snail intermediate hosts. Species boundaries and transmission dynamics within the widespread echinostomatid genus Drepanocephalus remain poorly understood despite reports of pathogenicity in North American aquaculture facilities. Here, we characterize Drepanocephalus recovered from Double-crested Cormorants (Nannopterum auritus) in New York State and evaluate their evolutionary relationships using multilocus phylogenetic analyses and next-generation sequencing. Fifteen cormorants were examined, of which seven (47%) were infected with Drepanocephalus. Long-read and short-read sequencing recovered the complete rRNA operon and a near-complete mitochondrial genome, providing the first mitochondrial genomic resources for the genus. Phylogenetic analyses of mitochondrial (cox1, nad1) and nuclear (28S, ITS1–5.8S–ITS2) markers recovered shallow but persistent mitochondrial structure within the D. spathans/auritus complex. Mitochondrial loci and GMYC analyses consistently recovered two major intraspecific lineages, whereas nuclear markers recovered substantially weaker divergence and limited phylogenetic resolution, suggesting incomplete lineage sorting, ongoing gene flow, or both. Haplotype analyses further indicated that mitochondrial structure was more strongly associated with host use than with geographic locality, with lineages largely partitioned between Double-crested and Neotropic cormorant (N. brasilianus) transmission systems. Together, these results support broad parasite connectivity across the Americas while revealing previously undescribed host-associated mitochondrial structure. Our findings provide foundational genomic resources for future studies and highlight the role of host ecology in shaping parasite diversification and transmission across continental scales.

Introduction

Despite contributing substantially to global animal protein production, aquaculture facilities are frequently impacted by parasitic disease outbreaks that result in significant economic losses (FAO, 2020). Among these parasites, digenetic trematodes are of particular concern because of their complex life cycles involving aquatic intermediate hosts and piscivorous birds (Madsen et al., 2022). Within aquaculture systems, transmission typically involves a snail first intermediate host, a fish or other aquatic vertebrate second intermediate host, and an avian definitive host, all of which may occur at high densities (Roberts et al., 1996; Griffin et al., 2012; Madsen et al., 2022). Consequently, aquaculture facilities can serve as ecological interfaces that facilitate and amplify parasite transmission (Paladini et al., 2017; Madsen et al., 2022). Understanding the diversity, transmission dynamics, and geographic distribution of trematode parasites is therefore critical for predicting outbreaks and managing economically important host–parasite systems (Griffin et al., 2012, 2014).

Several digenean trematodes associated with aquaculture belong to the family Echinostomatidae Paladini et al. (2017). Echinostomatids are cosmopolitan parasites capable of infecting a broad range of definitive hosts, including birds and mammals (Detwiler et al., 2010). One echinostomatid genus commonly reported from North American aquaculture systems is Drepanocephalus (Griffin et al. (2012). Adult Drepanocephalus infect piscivorous birds, particularly the Neotropic Cormorant (Nannopterum brasilianum) and Double-crested Cormorant (Nannopterum auritus) (Hernández-Cruz et al., 2017).

These host species differ markedly in their geographic distributions and movement ecology (Kennedy and Spencer, 2014; Harrell, 2016). Double-crested cormorants are highly migratory, breeding throughout much of the United States and southern Canada and moving along continental flyways between breeding and wintering grounds (King et al., 2010). In contrast, Neotropic cormorants are largely resident throughout Central and South America, with the northern extent of their range overlapping that of Double-crested Cormorants in Mexico and the southeastern United States (Harrell, 2016). Previous work has demonstrated that parasite assemblages differ between migratory and resident cormorant populations (Sheehan et al., 2017), suggesting that host movement ecology may influence parasite transmission dynamics, population connectivity, and phylogeographic structure.

Reports of Drepanocephalus from first intermediate hosts remain relatively limited, but available records demonstrate that several members of the family Planorbidae participate in transmission, including Biomphalaria straminea, B. havanensis, and Planorbella trivolvis (Pinto et al., 2016; Alberson et al., 2017; McPhail et al., 2025). Among these, B. havanensis has been repeatedly implicated in transmission within aquaculture systems in the southeastern United States (Griffin et al., 2012; Alberson et al., 2017), whereas P. trivolvis has been shown to host Drepanocephalus cf. spathans under both natural and experimental conditions (Gordy and Hanington, 2019; Alberson et al., 2022).

These snail hosts differ markedly in their geographic distributions. Biomphalaria straminea is widespread throughout tropical and subtropical regions of South America, whereas B. havanensis occurs throughout the Caribbean, Mexico, and the southeastern United States (Yong et al., 2001; Thompson, 2011; Alberson et al., 2017). In contrast, Planorbella trivolvis is broadly distributed across temperate North America, extending from Canada to Mexico (Cózatl-Manzano and Naranjo-García, 2007; Gordy and Hanington, 2019; Martin et al., 2020). The broad distributions of these planorbid hosts suggest that suitable intermediate hosts for Drepanocephalus transmission occur across much of the parasite's known range and may contribute to patterns of parasite connectivity and phylogeographic structure.

Even more scant are reports of Drepanocephalus from second intermediate hosts outside of the aquaculture setting. Metacercariae of Drepanocephalus have been reported from several freshwater fish species, including economically important hosts such as the Channel Catfish (Ictalurus punctatus) (Griffin et al., 2012). In aquaculture systems, these parasites encyst within the cranial tissues and branchial chambers of juvenile catfish, resulting in tissue damage, impaired respiration, and, in severe cases, mortality (Griffin et al., 2012, 2014). Consequently, Drepanocephalus has emerged as an economically important parasite of North American aquaculture.

Molecular studies have also suggested broad geographic connectivity among Drepanocephalus populations. Alberson et al. (2017) demonstrated that parasites transmitted within aquaculture ponds in the southeastern United States were genetically similar to populations reported from Mexico and South America. These findings suggest either extensive parasite dispersal across the Americas or unresolved species boundaries within the genus.

Accurate species identification is essential for understanding the transmission ecology, host specificity, and geographic distribution of trematode pathogens within aquaculture systems. However, species boundaries within Drepanocephalus remain unresolved, particularly among North and South American populations, which have historically been identified as D. spathans. Kudlai et al. (2015) examined specimens from multiple localities in the United States and reported morphological differences relative to D. spathans from Mexico and South America. Based on these observations, they described Drepanocephalus auritus from Double-crested Cormorants (Nannopterum auritus), proposing that North American populations represented a distinct species.

Increased geographic and molecular sampling subsequently challenged this interpretation. Pinto et al. (2016) and Hernández-Cruz et al. (2017) recovered shallow genetic divergence among North and South American populations, and Hernández-Cruz et al. (2017) treated D. auritus as a junior synonym of D. spathans. Alberson et al. (2022) further demonstrated substantial morphological plasticity among experimentally and naturally infected specimens, although their study did not explicitly compare the mitochondrial lineages identified here. Thus, D. auritus has generally been treated as synonymous with D. spathans following Hernández-Cruz et al. (2017). However, expanded geographic and genetic sampling provides an opportunity to reevaluate lineage structure within this broadly distributed taxon.

Mitochondrial genomes have become valuable resources for resolving phylogenetic relationships, assessing population structure, and examining evolutionary divergence among closely related parasite taxa (Littlewood, 2006; Brabec et al., 2015). Compared with many nuclear markers, mitochondrial loci typically exhibit elevated substitution rates, limited recombination, and high copy number, making them particularly useful for detecting recent evolutionary divergence and cryptic diversity (Avise, 2000; Brabec et al., 2015). Despite their utility, genomic resources for Drepanocephalus remain limited, and no complete mitochondrial genome has been reported for the genus. Mitogenomic data have also proven informative for resolving phylogenetic relationships in other echinostomatids (Li and Durbin, 2009; Gacad et al., 2023), including the closely related genus Chaunocephalus (Zhou et al., 2025), suggesting that similar approaches may help clarify species boundaries and phylogeographic patterns within Drepanocephalus. Consequently, generating mitochondrial genomic resources represents an important step toward understanding the evolutionary history, geographic structure, and taxonomic status of this economically important parasite group.

The present study integrates mitochondrial genomics, multilocus phylogenetics, and haplotype network analyses to investigate species boundaries and phylogeographic structure within Drepanocephalus. Specifically, we evaluate whether populations recovered from Double-crested Cormorants (Nannopterum auritus) in the northeastern United States represent a distinct lineage or form part of a geographically widespread transmission system extending across the Americas. By expanding geographic sampling and generating the first near-complete mitochondrial genomes for Drepanocephalus, this study provides new insights into species boundaries, host-associated population structure, and parasite connectivity across continental transmission systems.

Materials & methods

Host collection and parasite recovery

The Double-crested Cormorant (Nannopterum auritus) is considered a nuisance species in portions of New York State, where population management is periodically conducted by the New York State Department of Environmental Conservation (NYSDEC). In 2022, a cohort of N. auritus culled from a breeding colony on the Niagara River near Buffalo, New York, USA, was donated for parasitological examination. Hosts were stored frozen (−20 °C) until 24 h prior to necropsy, when they were thawed at 4 °C. A total of 15 hosts were necropsied for this study.

Necropsies were conducted following standard parasitological procedures (Pritchard and Kruse, 1982). The gastrointestinal tract, liver, heart, and associated viscera were examined for helminth parasites. Recovered helminths were rinsed in deionized water and preserved in either 70% or 95% ethanol for subsequent morphological and molecular analyses. In addition, respiratory and cloacal swabs, stomach contents, and fecal samples were collected and preserved for future investigations.

All host and parasite specimens were accessioned into the Museum of Southwestern Biology, Division of Parasites (MSB:Para:54101–54129), and are publicly accessible through Arctos (https://arctos.database.museum). Helminths were examined using stereoscopic and compound light microscopy and identified to the lowest possible taxonomic level using published taxonomic keys. Large echinostomatid trematodes recovered from the intestine were tentatively identified as Drepanocephalus sp. based on gross morphology and preserved in 95% ethanol at −20 °C for downstream molecular analyses. Representative specimens were selected from multiple hosts for molecular analyses to capture variation in infection intensity and parasite morphology.

DNA extraction

Fifteen adult Drepanocephalus-like specimens recovered from Nannopterum auritus were selected for molecular analyses. To maximize the likelihood of recovering mitochondrial DNA, specimens were preferentially selected from infrapopulations with the highest infection intensities. Genomic DNA was extracted from whole individual trematodes using the QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol for animal tissues. DNA was eluted in 60 μL of AE buffer and stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

DNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) prior to downstream molecular analyses.

Oxford nanopore sequencing

Sequencing libraries were prepared from genomic DNA of eight Drepanocephalus-like specimens using the Rapid Barcoding Kit v14 (SQK-RBK114.24; Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) following the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were pooled and sequenced on a FLO-MIN114 flow cell using MinKNOW v21.05.12 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies).

Raw signal data were basecalled and demultiplexed using Guppy v5.0.12 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Reads were quality filtered with NanoFilt v2.8.0, retaining reads with a minimum Phred quality score of Q10. To reduce contamination, filtered reads were mapped against reference genomes representing common contaminants (avian host, human, and bacterial genomes) using Minimap2 v2.22 (Li, 2018), and unmapped reads were retained for downstream analyses.

Filtered trematode reads were imported into Geneious Prime v2026.1.1 (Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand) and mapped to the mitochondrial genome of Chaunocephalus ferox (GenBank accession PV685907) and the nuclear rRNA operon of Drepanocephalus auritus (GenBank accession KP053260). Reference-guided consensus sequences were generated from mapped reads and incorporated into locus-specific alignments for subsequent phylogenetic analyses.

Illumina sequencing

Three Drepanocephalus specimens were submitted to Novogene (Sacramento, California, USA) for whole-genome Illumina sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from individual adult specimens as described above. DNA quality and concentration were assessed prior to library preparation. Sequencing libraries were generated and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform using paired-end 150-bp chemistry, generating approximately 12 Gb of raw sequence data per sample.

Raw reads were quality filtered by Novogene to remove adapter contamination and low-quality sequences prior to downstream analyses. Mitochondrial genome assemblies were generated using GetOrganelle v1.7.6 (Jin et al., 2020). Resulting contigs were assessed for completeness and annotated using MITOS2 (Bernt et al., 2013), implemented through the Galaxy platform (The Galaxy Community, 2024), specifying the trematode mitochondrial genetic code and metazoan reference database. Gene annotations were manually inspected and refined in Geneious Prime v2026.1.1 (Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand) through comparison with published echinostomatid mitochondrial genomes (e.g., Li and Durbin, 2009; Gacad et al., 2023).

To evaluate assembly quality, cleaned reads were mapped back to assembled mitochondrial contigs using BWA-MEM v0.7.17 (Li and Durbin, 2009), and alignment statistics were calculated using SAMtools v1.22 (Danecek et al., 2021). Genome completeness was assessed based on recovery of the expected trematode mitochondrial gene complement, read-mapping coverage, and assembly continuity. Assemblies were considered near-complete when all expected protein-coding genes, tRNAs, and mitochondrial rRNA genes were recovered.

Partial nuclear rRNA operons were reconstructed by reference-guided assembly using the published Drepanocephalus auritus rRNA operon (GenBank accession KP053260). Filtered reads were mapped using BWA-MEM v0.7.17 (Li and Durbin, 2009), and alignment files were processed with SAMtools v1.22 (Danecek et al., 2021) for sorting, indexing, and coverage estimation. Consensus sequences were generated using BCFtools v1.22 (Danecek et al., 2021) and incorporated into locus-specific alignments for subsequent phylogenetic analyses. Sequence depth and coverage statistics were calculated from mapped reads using SAMtools.

Phylogenetic analyses

All alignments were generated in Geneious Prime using CLUSTALW (Larkin et al., 2007). Alignments were manually inspected and trimmed to preserve reading frame integrity and remove ambiguous terminal regions. For each gene, all published Drepanocephalus sequences were included. Additional echinostomatid and outgroup taxa were selected based on the phylogenetic framework of Hernández-Cruz et al. (2017).

Maximum likelihood (ML) analyses were performed using RAxML v8.2.12 (Stamatakis, 2014) within Geneious Prime with 1000 bootstrap replicates under a GTR + GAMMA substitution model. Bayesian inference (BI) analyses were conducted in BEAST v2.5 (Bouckaert et al., 2019). Models of nucleotide substitution were selected using jModelTest v2.1.10 (Posada, 2008) under the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and implemented in Bayesian analyses. Broad phylogenetic datasets including outgroup taxa were analyzed under a Yule speciation prior, whereas analyses restricted to Drepanocephalus haplotypes were performed under a Constant Population Coalescent prior. All analyses employed a strict molecular clock and were run for 10,000,000 generations, sampling every 1000 generations. Convergence and parameter mixing were assessed in Tracer v1.6 (Rambaut et al., 2014), and all reported analyses achieved ESS values > 200. Maximum clade credibility trees were generated following a 10% burn-in and visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 (Rambaut, 2018).

Individual gene tree datasets consisted of cox1 (n = 98, 537 bp), nad1 (n = 66, 429 bp), 28S (n = 48, 1025 bp), and ITS1+5.8S + ITS2 (n = 96, 1010 bp). Species boundaries within Drepanocephalus were assessed using a Generalized Mixed Yule Coalescent (GMYC) approach (Fujisawa and Barraclough, 2013). An ultrametric cox1 tree comprising Drepanocephalus sequences (n = 83, 537 bp) was generated in BEAST v2.5 under a Constant Population Coalescent prior and an HKY + GAMMA substitution model with four gamma categories and a chain length of 5,000,000 generations. Posterior distributions were evaluated in Tracer v1.6, and all parameters achieved ESS values > 200. A maximum clade credibility ultrametric tree was generated following a 10% burn-in. The resulting ultrametric tree was analyzed using the R package splits (Fujisawa and Barraclough, 2013) to perform a single-threshold GMYC species delimitation.

Genetic distance and haplotype analyses

The Drepanocephalus-only cox1 alignment used for GMYC analyses was additionally used to calculate population genetic summary statistics in DnaSP v6 (Rozas et al., 2017), including the number of segregating sites (S), nucleotide diversity (π), haplotype diversity (Hd), Watterson's theta (θW), and Tajima's D. Sequences were subsequently collapsed into unique haplotypes, resulting in a dataset of 48 haplotypes comprising 123 variable sites. Relationships among haplotypes were visualized using minimum spanning networks (MSNs; Bandelt et al., 1999) constructed in PopArt v1.7 (Leigh and Bryant, 2015). Separate networks were generated to examine patterns associated with host taxon, geographic locality, and GMYC cluster assignment.

To further evaluate relationships among unique haplotypes, Bayesian phylogenetic analyses were conducted in BEAST v2.5 (Bouckaert et al., 2019) under a strict molecular clock and Constant Population Coalescent prior. Analyses were run for 10,000,000 generations, sampling every 1000 generations. Maximum clade credibility trees were generated following a 10% burn-in and visualized in FigTree v1.4.4 (Rambaut, 2018). Pairwise uncorrected p-distances were calculated in Geneious Prime to quantify genetic divergence within and among mitochondrial lineages and Drepanocephalus species.

Results

Host infection patterns

A total of 15 Double-crested Cormorants (Nannopterum auritus) were necropsied and examined for helminth parasites (Supplementary File 1). Overall, 93% of hosts were infected with at least one helminth species. Drepanocephalus (Digenea: Echinostomatidae) was the most frequently recovered taxon, occurring at a prevalence of 47% and a mean intensity of 10.5 worms per infected host. Specimens from three infected hosts (HP1010, HP1183, and HP1189) were selected for genomic sequencing and subsequent phylogenetic analyses. All published reports of Drepanocephalus spp. were assembled in Supplementary File 2.

Next-generation sequencing

MinION ONT sequencing summary

Genomic DNA from 8 individual Drepanocephalus specimens was prepared for ONT long-read sequencing. Overall, coverage of phylogenetically informative markers was low. No complete or near-complete mitochondrial genomes were recovered, and all samples lacked sufficient cox1 coverage for reliable contig assembly. However, most of the rRNA operon was recovered from all specimens and assembled into useable consensus sequences (Supplementary File 3). Consequently, ONT data were used primarily to recover rRNA markers, whereas mitochondrial analyses were based on Illumina sequencing. Across samples, the rRNA operon had a mean sequencing depth of 4.18×, with an average of 132.67 reads mapping to the 4127-bp reference sequence.

Mitochondrial genome assemblies

MITOS annotation recovered the expected trematode mitochondrial gene complement in all three assemblies (13,818 bp; Supplementary Files 4 and 5). Although gene content was complete, none of the assemblies fully circularized, suggesting incomplete recovery of the mitochondrial control region. Each assembly contained 12 protein-coding genes, 22 tRNAs, and 2 ribosomal RNA genes, consistent with published echinostomatid mitochondrial genomes (Supplementary File 6). As reported for other trematodes (Zhou et al., 2025), the mitochondrial genomes lacked the ATP synthase subunit 8 (atp8) gene and exhibited an overlap between the 3′ end of nad4L and the 5′ end of nad4. The GC content was identical among all three assemblies (37.4%).

Comparisons among mitochondrial genomes recovered from different hosts identified relatively low levels of sequence variation. Commonly used phylogenetic markers (cox1and nad1) contained comparatively few polymorphic sites, whereas genes such as cytb and nad5exhibited greater sequence variation among samples (Supplementary File 5). These results suggest that additional mitochondrial loci may provide greater resolution for future population genetic and phylogeographic studies of Drepanocephalus.

Across all loci, phylogenetic analyses recovered short internal branch lengths and shallow genetic divergence throughout the genus. Consistent with Hernández-Cruz et al. (2017), neither D. spathans nor D. auritus was recovered as a monophyletic taxon. Consequently, all samples falling within the D. spathans/auritus complex are hereafter referred to as Drepanocephalus cf. spathans.

Nuclear ribosomal markers (ITS1+5.8S + ITS2 and 28S; Fig. 3, Supplementary File 7) strongly supported the monophyly of Drepanocephalus (ITS: bs = 100, pp = 1; 28S: bs = 100, pp = 1). The ITS phylogeny (Fig. 3) strongly supported the monophyly of D. mexicanus (bs = 100, pp = 1), whereas the 28S dataset provided only weak support for this relationship (bs = 62, pp = 0.57; Supplementary File 7). Eight sequences generated from Nannopterum auritus collected in New York State were included in these analyses. All were confirmed as Drepanocephalus; however, one specimen (HP1010_bc6) grouped as sister to D. mexicanus without support (ITS: bs = 67, pp = 0.68), whereas all remaining New York specimens grouped within the D. cf. spathans complex. Despite these placements, nuclear divergence among taxa was extremely low, averaging <0.01% for partial 28S and 0.06% for the ITS region between D. cf. spathans and D. mexicanus. Likewise, HP1010_bc6 differed from other New York specimens by only 0.04% and from D. mexicanus by 0.08%. No consistent host-associated or phylogeographic structure was recovered using nuclear markers. The 28S phylogeny (Supplementary File 7) was largely congruent with the ITS tree but, consistent with its more conserved nature, provided lower resolution of species relationships.

The topology of deeper relationships was largely congruent among nuclear and mitochondrial datasets; however, relationships within the D. cf. spathans complex differed substantially (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Both cox1 and nad1 phylogenies strongly supported two mitochondrial lineages, hereafter referred to as D. cf. spathans I and D. cf. spathans II. Placement of D. mexicanus differed between analytical methods. Maximum likelihood analyses recovered D. mexicanus within the broader D. cf. spathans complex as sister to either D. cf. spathans I (cox1, Fig. 1) or D. cf. spathans II (nad1, Fig. 2), although support for these relationships was weak or absent (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). In contrast, Bayesian analyses consistently recovered D. mexicanus as sister to the D. cf. spathans complex with strong support (pp = 1; Supplementary File 8). Bayesian analyses also provided stronger support for the distinction between D. cf. spathans I and D. cf. spathans II than did ML analyses.

To further evaluate mitochondrial lineage structure within Drepanocephalus, a Generalized Mixed Yule Coalescent (GMYC) analysis was performed using cox1 sequences. The single-threshold GMYC model recovered 11 putative clusters, eight of which occurred within the D. cf. spathans complex (Supplementary File 3). GMYC clusters were largely congruent with mitochondrial phylogenies, with clusters 1 and 2 corresponding to D. cf. spathans I and clusters 3 and 4 corresponding to D. cf. spathans II (Table 2). Genetic diversity and connectivity within D. cf. spathans

Mitochondrial markers were used to quantify genetic divergence within and among Drepanocephalus lineages (Table 3). Within D. cf. spathans I and II, uncorrected p-distances were <1% for both cox1 and nad1, although D. cf. spathans II exhibited slightly greater intraspecific variation. Between D. cf. spathans I and II, divergence ranged from 3 to 4% for both mitochondrial markers. In contrast, comparisons between either D. cf. spathans lineage and D. mexicanus recovered substantially greater divergence (12–13.9%), with both D. cf. spathans lineages approximately equidistant from D. mexicanus. Discussion

Shallow divergence and species boundaries within Drepanocephalus

This study expands mitochondrial sampling of the widespread cormorant trematode Drepanocephalus cf. spathans and provides evidence for the persistence of multiple shallow mitochondrial lineages across the Americas. Collectively, our multilocus analyses support unresolved species boundaries within the D. spathans/auritus complex and reveal clear mito-nuclear discordance.

Kudlai et al. (2015) described Drepanocephalus auritus based on morphological differences relative to D. spathans, including a narrower, more elongate body, an extended uterine field, and more widely separated testes. However, the description was accompanied by limited molecular and phylogenetic analyses. Recent work suggests that these diagnostic characters may not be evolutionarily stable. Alberson et al. (2022) demonstrated substantial morphological plasticity within the D. spathans/auritus complex, showing that many of the characters used to distinguish D. auritus from D. spathans varied with host age, infection intensity, developmental stage, and whether infections were experimental or naturally acquired.

Our multilocus dataset supports broad genetic connectivity across the D. spathans/auritus complex while simultaneously revealing previously undescribed host-associated mitochondrial structure (Fig. 4). Although mitochondrial markers consistently recovered two well-supported lineages (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), nuclear ribosomal loci exhibited substantially weaker differentiation and limited phylogenetic resolution (Fig. 3). Collectively, these findings support previous molecular studies (Hernández-Cruz et al., 2017) in demonstrating shallow divergence within the D. cf. spathans complex. However, the mito-nuclear discordance observed here does not distinguish among ongoing gene flow, incomplete lineage sorting following recent divergence, or incipient speciation. Consequently, the available data are insufficient to determine whether D. cf. spathans I and II represent population-level mitochondrial structure or independently evolving lineages.

Migratory cormorants and parasite connectivity

These data support a growing body of literature demonstrating that highly vagile hosts can maintain broad parasite connectivity despite shallow lineage structure (Locke et al., 2021; Ebbs et al., 2025; Perrin et al., 2025). Parasite distributions are often shaped by the movements of their most vagile host (Blasco-Costa et al., 2012; Ebbs et al., 2016; Poulin and de Angeli Dutra, 2021). Accordingly, migratory birds provide an effective mechanism for maintaining parasite dispersal and population connectivity across broad geographic scales (Ebbs et al., 2016, 2025; Leung and Koprivnikar, 2016; Perrin et al., 2025). Drepanocephalus is a core trematode of Double-crested Cormorants (Sheehan et al., 2017), and its widespread occurrence throughout the host's range is consistent with high levels of continental parasite connectivity.

The Double-crested Cormorant (N. auritus) is highly migratory throughout North America, whereas the Neotropic Cormorant (N. brasilianum) is largely resident throughout Central and South America, although regional movements do occur. These contrasting movement patterns may help explain the shallow but persistent mitochondrial structure recovered within the D. cf. spathans complex. Minimum spanning networks and mitochondrial phylogenies suggest that lineage structure corresponded more closely with definitive host association than with geographic locality, although broader geographic and host sampling will be required to test this hypothesis. Drepanocephalus cf. spathans I has only been recovered from Double-crested Cormorants, whereas D. cf. spathans II has only been reported from Neotropic Cormorants (Supplementary File 3, Fig. 4). Likewise, D. mexicanus has only been reported from the Neotropic Cormorant.

Significant negative Tajima's D values (Table 4) recovered for both mitochondrial clades suggest recent demographic expansion and ongoing parasite connectivity across the Americas. Although these processes reduce opportunities for deep lineage divergence, differences in host ecology and movement patterns may promote partial ecological separation between Nearctic and Neotropical transmission systems. Combined with differences in intermediate-host availability, these ecological factors may facilitate the persistence of shallow mitochondrial lineages despite incomplete reproductive isolation.

Interestingly, parasite surveys of cormorants outside the Americas (Molnár et al., 2015; Cech et al., 2017; Chibwana and Katandukila, 2021) have not reported Drepanocephalus. Instead, surveys of Phalacrocorax carbo from Eurasia and Africa consistently recover related echinostomatids, primarily species of Petasiger, which also utilize Biomphalaria and other planorbid snails (Laidemitt et al., 2019; Outa et al., 2020; Hüsken et al., 2026). Kennedy and Spencer (2014) recognized New World (Nannopterum) and Old World (Phalacrocorax) cormorants as distinct genera based on molecular phylogenetic evidence. Together, these observations suggest that the evolutionary history of Drepanocephalus may be closely tied to New World cormorant–planorbid transmission systems rather than to cormorants globally. In contrast, Hysteromorpha spp. (Diplostomidae), which also cycle between planorbid snails and cormorants, exhibit mitochondrial evidence of continentally restricted species, including a distinct Old World representative (Achatz et al., 2025).

Intermediate host associations and ecological structuring of mitochondrial lineages

Colonial breeding by cormorants can facilitate parasite transmission and mixing when suitable intermediate hosts are present. Although records of Drepanocephalus from first intermediate hosts remain limited, available studies demonstrate that transmission occurs through multiple planorbid snail species, including Biomphalaria havanensis, B. straminea, and Planorbella trivolvis (Griffin et al., 2012; Pinto et al., 2016; Gordy and Hanington, 2019; Alberson et al., 2017; Hernández-Cruz et al., 2017). These snail species differ substantially in their geographic distributions and ecological associations, potentially creating regional differences in parasite transmission opportunities.

Among molecularly characterized records currently available, D. cf. spathans I has been recovered from B. havanensis and P. trivolvis, whereas D. cf. spathans II has been recovered from B. havanensis and B. straminea (Fig. 4). Although these records remain sparse, the currently documented host associations differ between mitochondrial lineages and are consistent with the hypothesis that differences in intermediate-host availability may contribute to the maintenance of shallow mitochondrial structure across the geographic range of D. cf. spathans.

Interestingly, D. mexicanus has also been reported from B. straminea, indicating that portions of the geographic ranges of D. mexicanus and D. cf. spathans II overlap in both intermediate and definitive host use. Despite this ecological overlap, D. mexicanus remained distinct from the D. cf. spathans complex in all mitochondrial analyses. However, the limited divergence recovered from nuclear markers suggests that species boundaries within Drepanocephalus remain incompletely resolved.

Implications for parasite transmission and aquaculture

Biomphalaria havanensis and Planorbella trivolvis both occur in catfish aquaculture facilities in the southeastern United States (George, 2008; Griffin et al., 2012), while Double-crested Cormorants increasingly exploit these systems as foraging habitats (King et al., 2010; Scherr et al., 2010; Burr et al., 2020). Together, these conditions create ecological interfaces that facilitate Drepanocephalus transmission between definitive, intermediate, and second intermediate hosts (Sheehan et al., 2017). Griffin et al. (2012) demonstrated that Drepanocephalus infections can cause substantial pathology and mortality in juvenile channel catfish, highlighting the economic importance of understanding parasite transmission within aquaculture systems.

Our phylogenetic analyses suggest that outbreaks reported from catfish aquaculture facilities in the southeastern United States were most likely associated with D. cf. spathans I, the lineage currently associated with the highly migratory Double-crested Cormorant and the broadly distributed planorbid snails P. trivolvis and B. havanensis. Although this hypothesis requires confirmation through additional sequencing of historical and contemporary aquaculture isolates, it illustrates how improved parasite systematics can inform disease surveillance and transmission studies.

To our knowledge, Drepanocephalus spp. have not yet been reported from snail intermediate hosts in New York State. Moreover, apart from surveys conducted in Alberta, Canada (Gordy and Hanington, 2019; McPhail et al., 2025), records of naturally infected intermediate hosts remain scarce throughout North America. Expanding surveys of planorbid snails, together with molecular characterization of larval Drepanocephalus, will improve our understanding of transmission ecology and help identify regions where aquaculture systems may be vulnerable to parasite emergence.

Conclusion

This study used multilocus phylogenetic and genomic data to evaluate the placement of Drepanocephalus collected from New York State and to investigate phylogeographic patterns within the geographically widespread D. cf. spathans complex. Our results indicate that the D. cf. spathans complex occurs across the Americas and comprises shallow but structured mitochondrial lineages that are likely associated with host use. The shallow mitochondrial divergence and limited nuclear differentiation observed here are consistent with recent divergence accompanied by incomplete lineage sorting, ongoing gene flow among transmission systems, or a combination of both processes. The observed mitochondrial structure is further consistent with partially distinct Nearctic and Neotropical transmission systems shaped by host movement ecology and intermediate-host distributions. Future studies should expand sampling throughout South America, particularly within the southern range of D. cf. spathans II and generate complete mitochondrial genomes from additional lineages to better resolve mitochondrial divergence, lineage demographics, gene flow, and species boundaries. Together, these findings demonstrate how host movement ecology and transmission networks can maintain shallow but persistent parasite population structure across continental scales despite limited nuclear differentiation.
