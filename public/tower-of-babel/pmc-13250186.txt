Impact of emotional competence on clinical reasoning: a scoping review
Authors: Louise Joly, Alexandra Nunes de Sousa, Marjorie Bardiau, Valérie Dory, Marie Bayot, Anne-Laure Lenoir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesGiven the role of emotions in reasoning, we hypothesised that emotional competence (EC), defined as the ability to identify, understand, express, regulate and use emotions, would increase the quality of physician’s clinical reasoning (CR). The objectives of this study were to map the existing literature on the impact of EC on physician’s CR and to identify any literature gaps.DesignThis study is a scoping review.Data sourcesWe included articles from Medline (via Ovid), Embase and Psychinfo and articles found manually, with no limitations in terms of publication date or location.Eligibility criteriaInclusion criteria were all physicians and medical students and articles focusing on one or more dimensions of EC (based on the model of Mikolajczak et al) and of CR (based on the framework of Young et al). We excluded non-research articles and articles concerning other health professionals, physicians’ psychiatric disorders or the impact of emotions on well-being, instrument-handling skills or students’ academic learning.Data extraction and synthesisData were extracted by two independent reviewers. Results were summarised using descriptive analyses and a narrative synthesis.ResultsAfter removing duplicates, we identified 12 046 articles. Following a review of their titles and abstracts, we assessed 268 articles for eligibility and included 98 in the scoping review. Most studies examined the effects of emotional regulation strategies, empathy and global EC scores on clinical performance. Positive effects of EC on uncertainty management and intuitive reasoning have been well documented, as have its effects on diagnostic and therapeutic performance and the development of CR in the context of life-threatening situations. A smaller number of studies suggest a favourable impact on clinical performance in the surgical context and on the quality of prescriptions. The impact of EC on diagnostic and therapeutic performance outside emergency and intraoperative contexts and on patient outcomes remains uncertain. The effects of EC on CR were most evident under conditions of high acute stress, likely due to the increased cognitive load associated with such conditions.ConclusionsEC has been found to have a positive impact on CR mainly when dealing with uncertainty and life-threatening emergencies. Stress regulation and the resulting reduction in cognitive load appear to be a key modulator of EC’s impact on CR. Further research should focus on the impact of EC on diagnostic and therapeutic performance outside emergency conditions and on patient outcomes.

Introduction

Rationale

Clinical reasoning (CR) is a critical skill for physicians.1 Previous research drawing on cognitive psychology has set out to describe how CR works.1 2 It is commonly conceptualised through dual-process theory, which differentiates between a fast, intuitive process (type 1) and a slower, analytical process (type 2).1 2

Cognitive and emotional biases can affect CR and impact patient care.137 Although enhancing the quality of CR is crucial, few interventions have proved effective.13 4 811 Debiasing strategies have shown limited effectiveness.91214 Forcing physicians to engage in analytical reasoning can be useful; however, it may lack feasibility, as intuitive reasoning, commonly used by experts, is more cognitively efficient and capable of incorporating information that solely analytical approaches may overlook.9 10 12 14 15 The most credible strategy to date involves increasing physicians’ knowledge base, but this may not always be feasible with the ever-increasing amount of knowledge to learn.9 15

One potential novel approach to improving CR is to leverage the key role that emotions play in reasoning and decision-making.21618 Emotions influence how working memory functions and whether or not individuals recall events and knowledge; they modulate cognitive load and can improve or disrupt reasoning.23 8 11 16 1926 Emotions could therefore have either a positive or detrimental impact on CR. We hypothesised that the way in which physicians manage their emotions may be pivotal in determining whether these emotions ultimately help or hinder their CR.

Could improving physicians’ emotional competence (EC), that is, their ability to effectively identify, understand, use, regulate and express their own emotions (intra-personal EC) or the emotions of others (inter-personal EC),1624 2729 enhance the quality of their CR? To help answer this question, we set out to map and describe the existing literature on the impact of EC on CR in a scoping review.

Objectives

The primary objective of this scoping review was to summarise current knowledge on the impact of EC on CR among physicians and medical students, both in real and virtual contexts. We also aimed to describe (1) the type and scope of available literature, (2) the different methods used to examine the relationship between EC and CR and (3) the trend of publication numbers over time.

Methods

The following section is presented according to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis30 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR) checklist.31

The protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) platform (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/GM7YD) and published.15

In accordance with the protocol, we included search terms referring both to EC as an overarching construct and to each of the five components described by Mikolajczak et al. The related framework distinguishes five emotional skills (understanding, expression, regulation, identification and use of emotions), both intrapersonal and interpersonal, each comprising three levels: (1) the individual’s knowledge of emotional processes, (2) their ability to apply this knowledge in a given situation and (3) their natural propensity to behave in accordance with this knowledge.2729 Although the terms ‘emotional intelligence’ and ‘emotional competence’ are often used interchangeably in the literature, EC more specifically refers to the latter two levels, whereas emotional intelligence refers to all three levels.2729

Gut feeling and empathy were considered, as they include several core EC. Empathy encompasses the identification, understanding and use of interpersonal emotions.35 36 Gut feeling is defined as the ability to identify, use and understand one’s own emotions (and to a lesser extent, the emotions of the patient), often triggered by subtle or non-specific clinical cues, that generate a sense of alarm or reassurance about a patient’s prognosis.37 38

Information sources

Three bibliographic databases were identified for the literature search: MEDLINE (via Ovid), Psychinfo and Scopus (via Elsevier). However, Scopus generated numerous irrelevant publications. Scopus was therefore replaced by Embase, which is more specific to the medical field and entails record types more specific to our eligibility criteria.39 Additional references were hand-searched in the bibliography of selected articles. The grey literature was explored through Open Grey. All databases were explored from their creation date to the 31 December 2024. The literature was first explored on 3 March 2023 and a re-run phase was performed on 7 January 2025.

Search strategy

The full electronic search strategy for Medline can be found in the online supplemental file 2.

Selection of sources of evidence

Articles were imported into the Covidence software (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), and duplicates were removed. Title, abstract and full-text screening of the entire database were performed independently by LJ and AS based on eligibility criteria. In cases of conflict, consensus was sought between reviewers. A third reviewer (VD) resolved the remaining conflicts. If the exact content of the article could not be fully understood, it was included for full-text review. One article could not be retrieved.40

Agreement between reviewers was tested using the first 25 articles during the title and abstract selection phase. A Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.92 was found. To test the robustness of this result and eliminate the risk of a result obtained by chance, we confirmed this level of agreement on the next 25 articles (0.90).

Data charting and data items

Data were extracted using an Excel form, which was pre-tested by the two reviewers during a pilot phase carried out on the first 10 articles. Based on JBI guidelines for scoping reviews, each reviewer analysed five articles, which were checked by the other reviewer.30 Discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached and the extraction form was refined (see online supplemental file 3). For the remaining articles, each reviewer analysed half of the articles and cross-checked the other half. No critical appraisal of sources of evidence was conducted.

Synthesis of the results

To facilitate data analysis, we grouped individual EC and CR outcomes of retrieved studies into categories. To describe the scope of the available literature, we computed frequencies of study designs, population, context, EC outcomes examined, CR outcomes examined and the measurement instruments used for EC and CR. We also cross-tabulated the EC and CR outcomes to help identify gaps in the literature. To examine trends over time of interest in this topic, we displayed the number of publications by year in a diagram. We then synthesised results narratively to describe the main findings from the identified publications.

Clinical performance

Because the definition of clinical performance varied across contexts, we distinguished between performance in the operating room and outside of it (see table 2).

Outside the operating room

As presented in table 2, based on the different outcomes found in the articles, we defined clinical performance outside of the operating room as the quality of history taking and clinical examination, quality of medical knowledge and diagnostic and/or therapeutic quality, as well as prognostic performance.

The effect of EC as measured by global scores remains unclear. On the one hand, higher scores of EC were positively associated with the Medical Knowledge component of the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) examination.4143 In particular, two specific components of global EC scores—emotionality and self-control—were positively associated with the acquisition of knowledge and critical thinking, respectively.42 On the other hand, other studies showed no added value of EC (global scores or empathy) over medical knowledge.4448 Global scores of EC were also examined in relation to diagnostic and therapeutic performance. In life-threatening situations, residents showing a high level of EC had higher diagnostic and therapeutic performance than those with a lower level of EC.49 Outside of an emergency context, the evidence of a relationship between global EC scores and diagnostic or therapeutic performance is inconsistent.4347 5052

Empathy, based on evidence predominantly drawn from studies involving student samples, did not appear to be a predictive factor for the quality of clinical examination or diagnostic and therapeutic performance.414446 50 53 54 An association was observed only when evaluation criteria incorporated interpersonal interactions with patients or the medical team alongside the technical aspects of the examination.4553 5558 However, it was associated with better self-perceived quality of history taking and a better understanding of patients’ symptoms.555962

Many studies found that physicians’ gut feelings had a high predictive value for reaching the correct diagnosis (detection of severe illnesses, suicidal risk or child abuse)376369 and determining prognosis, with gut feelings sometimes outperforming clinical scores in predicting patient mortality/morbidity.70 71

Emotional regulation was linked to faster and higher-quality diagnoses, more appropriate therapeutic actions, an improved sense of self-efficacy and an increased perception of clinical performance as rated by peers in emergency situations.497278 In non-emergency settings, emotion regulation has been associated with a greater sense of self-efficacy in diagnostic and therapeutic tasks55 79 80; however, its actual impact on performance remains uncertain.2550 74 8185 Evidence showed, nevertheless, that when medical students regulated their emotions by distancing themselves from their emotions (emotional avoidance), their anxiety increased and their performance decreased as measured on an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), a standardised assessment used to evaluate clinical examination and communication skills in healthcare training.86

Inside the operating room

Based on the different outcomes found in the literature, clinical performance in the operating room was defined as surgical planning, stages of surgery, diagnostic, therapeutic and prognostic reasoning during surgery as well as intraoperative decision-making.

We found no direct evidence that global scores of EC directly and positively influence non-technical skills training in surgeons.87 88 However, we identified clear evidence of their effectiveness in enhancing decision-making.61 The role of EC in surgical training has been discussed and deemed sufficiently relevant to warrant greater integration into training programmes.61 87 88

Programmes to train physicians in emotional regulation showed positive effects on perceived stress, confidence and the ability to manage pressure.8993 Although some studies reported improvements in surgical decision-making and non-technical skills,89 90 92 93 others found no significant impact on performance.91 The multifactorial nature of the evaluation scales, which included communication and leadership dimensions, complicates efforts to isolate the specific influence of emotion regulation on intraoperative CR.

Functional and dysfunctional emotion regulation strategies during surgery were reported in the literature. Functional strategies that promote adaptive behaviour and effective decision-making16 include anticipation, relaxation techniques, positive self-talk, cognitive reframing, seeking help and team communication.259498 Dysfunctional strategies that reduce emotional discomfort in the short term while impairing the quality of decision-making16 include silence and expressions of stress through shouting.259498

Development of clinical reasoning in learners

A positive effect of empathy on the quality of history taking and clinical examination learning has been suggested, but from a highly heterogeneous body of literature, preventing any clear conclusion.53 62

Global EC scores have been explored as predictors of CR development in residents. One study found that high EC at the start of residency did not predict better clinical performance by year’s end, whereas another showed that high EC at the end of the year correlated with stronger performance.48 99 Thus, a low initial EC does not preclude progress, provided residents are exposed to meaningful clinical and life experiences.49 99 These experiences influence both EC and clinical performance, with clinical exposure shown to be a stronger predictor than EC scores.84

Poor stress regulation abilities have been inversely correlated with the ability to learn new skills, establish a differential diagnosis and determine treatment in students facing emergency situations—especially when their clinical experience is limited.49 75 78 83 As a result, the ability to regulate perceived stress was lower in medical students and newly qualified physicians compared with more experienced practitioners.78 83 However, the evidence linking emotional regulation and the development of CR is scant outside of emergency situations.62 100 Studies based on reaching a diagnosis in clinical vignettes showed that students with stronger emotional regulation reported more positive emotions, but this did not necessarily improve diagnostic performance learning.62 100 101 Interestingly, mild stress during diagnostic tasks was linked to better performance.101 Emotional regulation also contributed to managing the negative emotions caused by diagnostic errors, with rationalisation being the primary strategy supporting improvements in CR.102 103

One study sought to support students’ learning of diagnostic accuracy by implementing an analytical reasoning checklist that included an item on identifying one’s emotions.82 The checklist proved effective, but the study design did not allow the specific effect of this component of the checklist to be isolated from the rest of the checklist.82

Qualitative findings suggest that supervising students’ CR helped tutors to identify and use their emotions, particularly in recognising the role of gut feeling and becoming more aware of their own emotional responses in their own CR.104 105 The supervision process enhanced supervisors’ self-monitoring of their psychological needs and supported the development of their metacognitive skills,106 although emotional aspects were still rarely discussed during supervision.63 105 One study found that when students had better emotional regulation, they were more receptive to feedback about their CR, which could potentially improve their CR in the future.104

Intuitive reasoning

Intuitive reasoning has been mostly studied through the lens of gut feeling. Many studies have described gut feelings and their predictive value in a variety of clinical situations (see above).2537 38 58 6369 71 107 It can directly inform a diagnostic, therapeutic or prognostic orientation or trigger analytical reasoning aimed at rationalising it.25 65 69 110 111 The use of gut feeling increases with time pressure, uncertainty level and empathy.64 69 109 110 112

Uncertainty management

Tolerance of uncertainty mainly depends on emotional regulation, which enables physicians to work with provisional, intuition-based, gut-feeling-guided hypotheses and maintain cognitive flexibility in dynamic or ambiguous clinical situations.2537 38 58 64 6769 71 85 109 When uncertainty becomes unmanageable, it can trigger anxiety related to fear of litigation, loss of control in the event of an error or difficulty coping with unpredictable clinical outcomes.59 85 113 114 116 Physicians thus attempt to down-regulate this emotion by using strategies such as discussing uncertainty with colleagues (mostly novice physicians) or patients (mostly experienced physicians) or searching for practical solutions (time delay, therapeutic testing, safety netting and reducing uncertainty through testing).113 115 Some of these strategies can contribute to overtreatment and the overprescription of diagnostic testing.116118

Prescriptions

Physicians’ ability to regulate negative emotions, such as anxiety or irritation in response to patient demands, may contribute to reducing inappropriate prescriptions including of antibiotics, antidepressants, opioids and unnecessary diagnostic tests.25117121 No significant correlation was found between physician empathy and the volume or quality of prescriptions.117 122 123

Patients’ outcomes

When patients were treated by physicians with higher global scores of EC or empathy, they were more satisfied with care and displayed fewer symptoms of anxiety and depression.61 124

A gut feeling is a valuable predictor of patient outcomes and can also guide certain therapeutic decisions, such as whether or not to perform a caesarean section during labour.70 71 112

Emotional regulation and empathy were associated with increased physicians’ sense of self-efficacy in patient management, especially in emergency situations.4955 7275 79 80 104 116 125 Self-efficacy was itself associated with significantly fewer adverse effects in patients.126

They were also associated with a better understanding of patients’ symptoms and needs.5360 80 106 127129 However, this did not translate into clinical improvement for the patient, such as pain reduction or improved function of an operated limb.130

Limited evidence was found concerning the direct impact of emotional regulation on patient outcomes. Obstetricians exhibiting greater emotional regulation (eg, lower anxiety and more reflective coping) were associated on one hand with fewer operative deliveries, a trend towards more spontaneous births and on the other hand with increased risks of postpartum haemorrhage and chorioamnionitis.131 132 Poor stress regulation was also associated with an increased risk of intraoperative adverse events.94 Emotional regulation based on mindfulness did not show any conclusive effect on medical errors.81 103 133 134 The role of emotion avoidance has been examined in connection with patient outcomes, and its role remains ambiguous. It may help manage life-threatening emergencies by limiting the influence of caregivers’ emotions, though this remains unproven. However, in ethically complex therapeutic decisions, it may hinder judgement and potentially harm patients.77 135

Section 2: Factors moderating the interaction between EC and CR

Various factors may modulate the impact of EC on CR: clinical and life experience, work context, burnout and sense of self-efficacy. Clinical and life experience were mentioned as factors promoting both the development of EC and CR in residents.48 49 75 83 99 A correlation between the acquisition of high levels of CE and CR performance was identified after 1 year of practice.48 99

Two studies examined the role of burnout as a moderating factor between EC and clinical performance. Although a lower level of EC was linked to a lower level of burnout risk,52 133 burnout was not associated with a lower level of clinical performance.52 133

Physicians with a stronger sense of self-efficacy in managing clinical cases tend to have higher EC.497375 77 126 Self-efficacy is also associated with a lower risk of adverse patient outcomes.126

The work context appears to be a major influencing factor in the relationship between EC and CR. We identified three distinct contexts, each marked by specific stressors that directly affect cognitive load and modulate the relationship between EC and CR.78 83

Emergency and intraoperative settings are associated with acute stress stemming from the patient’s condition, leading to high cognitive demands.49 75 78 94 95 97 98 The surgical environment adds interpersonal stress due to its hierarchical structure and potential for conflict.135 In contrast, non-urgent, non-surgical contexts are characterised by chronic stress, mostly arising from interactions with patients and teams, as well as from the uncertainty surrounding diagnosis, treatment and prognosis.5480 85 113 114 118121 127 In medical emergencies, the literature is consistent and robust, showing a positive impact of EC on diagnostic and therapeutic performance.497275 131 132 Similarly, in intraoperative contexts, EC appears to enhance CR performance, although many CR outcome measures include leadership components, limiting our ability to isolate the specific effect of EC.878998

Outside of emergency and surgical contexts, the literature is abundant but methodologically heterogeneous, precluding firm conclusions about the role of EC in CR.2537 38 54 5860 63

Discussion

Summary of evidence

The impact of EC on CR has attracted particular interest over the past decade, mostly in Western countries. The available literature is primarily composed of non-interventional studies, relying on semi-structured interviews, self-reported questionnaires or external evaluations. Most of these studies have examined the effects of emotional regulation, empathy and global EC scores on clinical performance. The evidence suggests that EC positively affects multiple dimensions of CR. The most consistent evidence supports the role of high global EC scores and emotion regulation abilities in life-threatening emergencies, where they clearly enhance both diagnostic and therapeutic CR, as well as CR development. A substantial and consistent body of literature also supports the role of gut feeling and emotional regulation in managing uncertainty, prognostic reasoning and intuitive decision-making. A positive impact of emotional regulation and global scores of EC on intraoperative CR were also reported. Emotional regulation may also positively influence prescribing practices, although evidence remains limited.

We identified three major gaps in the literature. The first one concerns the effects of EC on diagnostic and therapeutic CR and CR learning in non-urgent, non-surgical settings, environments in which most physicians practice. The evidence in those contexts remains fragmented and uncertain. The second one concerns the effects of EC on patient outcomes. There is still little evidence showing that EC could enhance healthcare quality for patients. We nonetheless found some encouraging findings, such as a reduction in patients’ anxiety and depressive symptoms. The third gap in the literature concerns the lack of data from non-Western countries. The cultural context strongly shapes CE by influencing, namely, how emotions are expressed, perceived, interpreted and regulated, with potential direct implications for CR.16

Our findings also point to additional directions for future research. In particular, analysis of the factors influencing the interaction between EC and CR revealed three major areas of interest.

The first is about the correlation observed between CR development and a high level of CE. Correlation is not causation, and further studies are needed to examine whether there is a causal link between EC and CR development.

The second focuses on the identified association between a high level of EC, increased self-efficacy and reduced patients’ adverse events. These findings suggest that high EC may enhance self-efficacy, which in turn could reduce clinical risks. However, again, a causal relationship has yet to be established.

The third highlighted research pathway arises from the recognition that work context is a major moderating factor since contexts differ in terms of the nature of stress involved. This could explain the observed difference between well-documented effects of EC on diagnostic and therapeutic CR in life-threatening, urgent contexts and their poorly described effects on the same dimensions of CR in non-surgical and non-emergency contexts. This gives rise to three hypotheses.

The first hypothesis supports the fact that the link between EC and CR is moderated by the level of acute stress. The higher the level of acute stress, the greater the effect of EC on CR, likely by helping individuals to manage their cognitive load. This is congruent with the literature on cognitive load.161921 136 137

The second hypothesis is of a methodological nature: it may be easier to identify the impact of EC on CR in highly demanding contexts involving critical medical conditions. In emergency settings, clinical priorities are more explicitly defined, particularly the preservation of vital functions.137 138 Therapeutic interventions are typically guided by well-established decision algorithms, which facilitate the assessment of the appropriateness of clinical decisions.137 138 Furthermore, diagnostic reasoning is primarily focused on identifying the immediate causes of an acute event, thereby narrowing the range of possible hypotheses and enabling a more rapid clinical approach.137 138 This contrasts with non-urgent clinical settings, where decisions unfold over a longer period of time and notions of accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment are more ambiguous.139 As a result, EC may influence CR in non-surgical and non-urgent practice to the same extent as in emergency settings, but current assessment tools may lack the sensitivity or precision required to capture this effect.

The third hypothesis is based on the assumption that the impact of intra- and interpersonal EC on CR may actually differ. Intrapersonal EC (EC in relation to one’s own emotions) may play a more critical role in ensuring diagnostic and therapeutic accuracy under acute stress related to diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making. In contrast, interpersonal EC (EC related to the emotions of others) may be more relevant in managing interactions with patients, families and the healthcare team. While they may have less direct influence on diagnostic and therapeutic quality outside of acute stress situations, they likely contribute more to coping with the chronic stress associated with ongoing interpersonal dynamics, such as those encountered in non-urgent outpatient care.

Further research should focus on the effects of EC on diagnostic and therapeutic CR as well as its development in non-urgent care settings. The challenge is to define what is considered sound CR in these contexts and to develop the right instruments to assess diagnostic and therapeutic quality.

Limitations

The identified literature displays significant heterogeneity, particularly regarding the evaluation criteria used in studies. Some used concepts such as ‘professionalism’, ‘self-efficacy’ or ‘psychological characteristics’ that appeared ill-defined in some articles, rendering data interpretation and synthesis challenging. Many studies also relied on self-administered questionnaires, which introduce bias in the assessment of one’s own abilities, whether they relate to EC or CR.140

Heterogeneity in study samples constitutes an important limitation of the current evidence base, as studies variably include students, residents and/or practicing physicians, either examined separately or aggregated, thereby limiting fine-grained comparisons across those populations; however, the overall patterns of association between EC and CR remained broadly consistent across these groups, and we explicitly indicated when findings relied predominantly on student samples to avoid overgeneralisation.

Some relevant publications may not have been identified, particularly those related to patient outcomes, as our search

strategy may have overlooked studies focused on specific clinical conditions or outcomes. Including such a wide range of pathology-specific endpoints would have significantly broadened the scope of the review beyond its intended focus.

Conclusions

This scoping review highlights that the literature on the impact of EC on CR is heterogeneous and largely based on non-interventional methods mainly exploring the effect of emotional regulation, empathy and global EC scores on clinical performance. ECs appear to enhance CR particularly in life-threatening emergency management, where they likely help in managing stress and cognitive load, in uncertainty management and in intuitive reasoning. Evidence for these outcomes is relatively consistent. However, their impact in non-urgent care remains unclear, possibly due to methodological limitations. Further research is needed to clarify the role of EC on CR in those contexts by using outcomes able to address their specific complexity.
