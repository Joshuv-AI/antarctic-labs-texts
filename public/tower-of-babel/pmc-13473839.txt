Myelodysplasia From Valproic Acid Resolved by Cessation
Authors: Chidiebube Ugwu, Angimar Uriepero Palma, Rekha Bhat, Alankrita Taneja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDS) are clonal blood disorders that cause ineffective hematopoiesis, low blood counts, and a risk of progression to acute myeloid leukemia. Chemotherapy and radiation usually drive these changes, but a small set of medications, including valproic acid (VPA), can also produce reversible myelodysplastic features. We report a case of VPA‐induced myelodysplasia in a middle‐aged woman with complex neurologic and psychiatric comorbidities. The patient, with a history of paraplegia secondary to traumatic brain injury and epilepsy, was referred for evaluation of pancytopenia and significant weight loss. She had been treated with VPA for seizure prophylaxis for over a year, with dose escalation to 1000 mg three times daily occurring approximately 3 months prior to presentation. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia. Peripheral smear showed pseudo‐Pelger–Huët cells and thrombocytopenia. Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated mild hypocellularity, erythroid hyperplasia, and dysplasia in the megakaryocytic lineage, but cytogenetic and molecular studies were negative for MDS‐associated mutations. We promptly stopped VPA and switched her to lacosamide. Supportive care included a PEG tube for nutrition and one packed red cell transfusion for procedure‐related anemia. Her blood counts improved within a week of stopping VPA and normalized completely over the following weeks, with no further cytopenia. This case shows why clinicians should recognize drug‐induced myelodysplasia as a reversible mimic of primary MDS. VPA’s known hematologic toxicity may stem from its histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor activity, which disrupts normal hematopoiesis. Clinicians should consider VPA‐induced myelodysplasia in patients on long‐term therapy who present with unexplained cytopenias. Stopping the drug early can produce full hematologic recovery and prevent unnecessary interventions.

1. Introduction

Myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS) describes a heterogeneous group of clonal blood disorders that produce ineffective hematopoiesis, cytopenias, and a heightened risk of progression to acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [1]. The World Health Organization, in the 5th edition of its hematolymphoid tumor classification, reclassified these disorders as myelodysplastic neoplasms while retaining the abbreviation MDS [2]. Continuous exposure to certain medications can trigger myelodysplasia, and in rare cases stopping the offending agent resolves it. Several mechanisms drive drug‐related MDS: direct toxicity to CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells, genotoxic damage (for example, DNA alkylation or topoisomerase II inhibition), immune‐mediated suppression of hematopoiesis, and disruption of the bone marrow microenvironment [1, 3, 4].

Chemotherapeutic agents, including alkylating agents and topoisomerase II inhibitors, top the list, with immunosuppressants such as azathioprine and antivirals or antifungals such as zidovudine and flucytosine also contributing [5, 6]. Antiepileptics such as valproic acid (VPA) rarely produce this picture [7]. Key risk factors include prolonged exposure and high cumulative doses of the offending drug, older age, comorbidities such as renal or hepatic dysfunction, and genetic predispositions (for example, TP53 mutations in Li–Fraumeni syndrome) [5, 8]. Diagnosis requires a detailed drug history, bone marrow biopsy with cytogenetics, and exclusion of de novo MDS. Primary MDS calls for supportive care, sometimes chemotherapy, and allogeneic stem cell transplant; in contrast, managing valproate‐induced myelodysplasia centers on stopping the drug immediately and providing supportive care, and most patients regain normal blood counts within months [4].

VPA, a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor used as a first‐line antiepileptic and mood‐stabilizer, causes dose‐dependent thrombocytopenia in 5%–32% of patients through bone marrow suppression or immune‐mediated platelet destruction [9–11]. Myelosuppression occurs commonly, but VPA‐induced MDS remains rare, and only a limited number of cases have appeared in the literature since 1990 [12–15]. In most of these cases, cytopenias resolve after VPA stops [12–15]. Here, we report the case of a middle‐aged woman with a complex medical history, including epilepsy and bipolar disorder, who developed myelodysplasia while taking VPA.

2. Case Description

A 44‐year‐old woman presented for evaluation of pancytopenia and weight loss; her history included traumatic brain injury (TBI), post‐traumatic epilepsy, and bipolar disorder. Her primary care physician started the evaluation after noticing persistently low blood counts for over 3 months.

She sustained a TBI 6‐7 years earlier in a motor vehicle accident, and her team subsequently started her on VPA 250 mg three times daily to control generalized seizures. By the time she reached our hematology clinic, she was taking VPA 1000 mg every 8 h. This dose escalation appears to have happened about 3 months before presentation, likely during a nursing home stay, though the reason for the change remains unclear. Her other medications included mirtazapine 30 mg at bedtime, quetiapine 100 mg, and sertraline 150 mg in the morning and 300 mg in the evening. We admitted her inpatient at this visit because of failure to thrive and pancytopenia.

The bone marrow biopsy showed mild hypocellularity with trilineage hematopoiesis, relative erythroid hyperplasia, and morphologic dysplasia in the megakaryocytic lineage (see Figure 2). Flow cytometry identified no abnormal cell populations. Our top differentials at that point included primary MDS and secondary myelodysplasia from continuous drug exposure. We stopped VPA because we suspected VPA‐induced myelodysplasia (VIM) and transitioned her to lacosamide (Vimpat) 100 mg twice daily, later increasing to 200 mg twice daily. Serial blood counts showed progressive improvement in her cytopenia within a week of stopping VPA (Figure 3). Given her history of failure to thrive and poor oral intake, the team placed a PEG tube 4 days after we stopped VPA, and her hemoglobin dropped to 6.9 because of blood loss during the procedure. She therefore received one unit of packed red blood cells, the only blood product she received during this admission.

Her pancytopenia resolved completely after we stopped VPA, which supported the diagnosis of VIM. She remained seizure‐free on lacosamide and had no recurrence of hematologic abnormalities (Figure 4). We obtained signed informed consent.

3. Discussion

VPA, a widely used antiepileptic and mood‐stabilizer, can cause a diverse range of adverse effects that may limit its clinical utility. These include teratogenicity, hepatotoxicity, and hematologic abnormalities, with dose‐dependent thrombocytopenia as the most common hematologic complication [9–11]. Severe hematologic toxicities such as myelosuppression and myelodysplasia occur less often but appear in the literature [15].

Most reported cases of VPA‐associated myelodysplasia involve pediatric patients [12, 13, 16–19]. Three cases of cytopenia with myelodysplastic changes in elderly patients show that this complication is not exclusive to children. In most reported cases, including ours, stopping VPA led to hematologic recovery, although the outcome is not universal. Allan described a case of MDS with cytogenetic abnormalities after prolonged VPA therapy [15], and Coyle et al. reported three patients who developed acute leukemia while taking VPA [20]. Although it remains unclear whether genetic alterations represent a late‐stage consequence of VIM, clinicians should stay vigilant for this serious, though uncommon, complication. Early recognition matters, because stopping VPA typically reverses cytopenia and prevents transfusion‐related morbidity, costs, and infection risk. We considered several alternative explanations for her cytopenias. Her severe malnutrition, failure to thrive, and dysphagia raised concern for nutritional deficiency, but her serum vitamin B12 and folate were normal, and her ferritin and transferrin saturation did not suggest iron deficiency. We did not measure serum copper, and because copper deficiency can produce a myelodysplasia‐like marrow picture, we cannot fully exclude it; this is a limitation of our evaluation. She had no clinical or laboratory evidence of active infection at presentation. She was also taking mirtazapine, quetiapine, and sertraline, each of which has rarely been linked to cytopenias; however, her hematologic decline tracked specifically with VPA, worsening after the dose escalation and resolving after she stopped the drug, a temporal pattern her longstanding psychotropic regimen did not share. We did not obtain serum VPA concentrations, which is an additional limitation; even so, VPA‐associated hematologic toxicity appears in the literature even within the therapeutic range and seems to depend more on cumulative dose and duration of exposure than on peak serum concentration, which fits this patient’s course.

The mechanisms behind VPA‐induced hematologic toxicity are multifactorial and not fully understood [21] (Figure 5). VPA exerts its primary therapeutic effects in the central nervous system by enhancing GABAergic activity and blocking voltage‐gated ion channels [21], but these pathways are unlikely to explain bone marrow suppression. VPA also acts as a HDAC inhibitor and upregulates proapoptotic genes, which has positioned it as a candidate antineoplastic agent [22]. Early‐phase clinical trials evaluating VPA, either alone or with other therapies, for MDS and AML have shown promising results [23–28]. Paradoxically, cytopenia emerged as a frequent adverse effect in these studies.

Current evidence suggests that drug concentration shapes the balance between VPA’s therapeutic efficacy and its hematologic toxicity [23]. We argue, however, that exposure duration matters as much as concentration. Dysplastic myeloid cells may indeed be vulnerable to VPA‐induced apoptosis, but prolonged treatment can also impair normal myeloid and erythroid progenitors, potentially precipitating myelodysplasia in nononcologic patients, such as those receiving long‐term VPA for neurologic or psychiatric conditions. Supporting this view, in vitro studies show that VPA significantly reduces the viability of hematopoietic stem cells [29] (Figure 5).

In summary, VIM, though rare, likely arises from VPA’s HDAC inhibitor activity. Clinicians should suspect drug‐related MDS in patients on VPA who present with cytopenia because stopping the drug often reverses the bone marrow changes. Advanced cases, however, can progress to AML or MDS with cytogenetic aberrations, which raises unresolved questions about whether these represent the same pathology advancing or idiosyncratic susceptibility. Understanding VPA’s dual role, both therapeutic agent and potential hematotoxin, will help clinicians optimize patient outcomes.
