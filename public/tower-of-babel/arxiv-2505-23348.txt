Rigidity and functional properties of BD dev ​ ( Ω ) \mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\Omega)
Marco Caroccia, Nicolas Van Goethem

Abstract
. We provide a structural analysis of the space of functions of bounded deviatoric deformation, BD dev \mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}} , which arises in models of plasticity and fluid mechanics. The main result is the identification of the annihilator and a rigidity theorem for BD dev \mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}} maps with constant polar vector in the wave cone characterizing the structure of singularities for such maps. This result, together with an explicit kernel projection operator, enables an iterative blow-up procedure for relaxation and homogenization problems, allowing for integrands with explicit dependence on u u as well as ℰ d ​ u {\mathcal{E}}_{d}u . Our approach overcomes several difficulties compared to the BD \mathrm{BD} case, in particular due to the lack of invariance of ℰ d {\mathcal{E}}_{d} under orthogonalization of the polar directions. Applications to integral representation and material science are discussed.



1.
Introduction

Functional spaces have long occupied a foundational role in modern analysis, furnishing a natural and robust framework for the formulation and resolution of both abstract and applied problems across Mathematics and theoretical Physics. Their power stems from the capacity to encode, within a unified formalism, properties of functions—such as regularity, integrability, and decay—often in a manner that reflects the intrinsic geometry and symmetries of the underlying structures or physical systems under consideration

The importance of choosing an appropriate functional setting becomes evident when dealing with problems characterized by singularities, multi-scale phenomena, or by the presence of differential constraints, as in continuum mechanics, materials science, or image processing. In these contexts, rigidity properties play a central role: they capture the extent to which the imposition of certain differential constraints limits the possible structure of admissible functions, especially at singular points or along lower-dimensional sets where concentrations may occur.

Rigidity for functions satisfying some differential properties in the sense of measures is encompassed in the general study of the fine properties of these functions, and serves as a powerful tool to tackle several problems in Mathematical physics. In the field of Calculus of Variations, rigidity serves in particular to determine blow-ups at some singular points (Cantor points for instance), that are a crucial tool for homogenization and relaxation purposes (see
[
13
]
). In particular, in phase transition problems, image segmentation, material design, or variational frameworks in continuum mechanics involving multi-scale phenomena, field concentration often occurs on lower-dimensional sets, such as cracks and dislocations.. Typical examples of functions with differential constraints are the
\mathrm{BV}
functions (see
[
3
]
), whose gradient are defined in the sense of measures, or functions of bounded deformations, denoted as
\mathrm{BD}
(see
[
2
,
21
,
32
]
), whose symmetric gradient is constrained to be a finite Radon measure. This latter space is typically of use in linearized elasticity models of fracture mechanics or in infinitesimal elasto-plasticity. However, in the field of Plasticity or Fluid mechanics (where the shear deformation is the kinematical variable used to define the viscous stresses), shear deformations are favored and the corresponding functional space is the space of bounded deviatoric deformations named as
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
. Note that such a prominent phenomena as crystal plasticity is also mainly governed by shear efforts due to the formation and motion of dislocations in shear planes, as plasticity is not affected by traction or compression efforts in the material.

Despite their significance, the fine properties and structural results for the space
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
remain much less explored compared to the classical settings of
\mathrm{BV}
or
\mathrm{BD}
. The aim of the present work is to address this gap by providing a thorough analysis of
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
. The main novelty of this manuscript is the characterization of
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
maps with constant polar vector lying in the
wave cone
, which is useful in the theory of relaxation and homogenization via iterated blow-ups, as in
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
16
]
. A notable advantage of this approach to homogenization, based on iterated blow-ups through rigidity and kernel projection, is that it does not restrict the energy to depend solely on
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
, but also accommodates integrands with explicit dependence on
u
itself, up to an
L^{\infty}
bound.

1.1.
The space of bounded deviatoric deformations

For a general
(n\times n)
-matrix-valued tensor
T
, two classical orthogonal decompositions apply:

T=\underset{\mathrm{sym}T}{\underbrace{\frac{T+T^{t}}{2}}}+\underset{\mathrm{skew}T}{\underbrace{\frac{T-T^{t}}{2}}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ T=\underset{\mathrm{dev}T}{\underbrace{T-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(T)}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}}}+\frac{\mathrm{tr}T}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}.

The first decomposition is into the symmetric and skew (or anti-symmetric) part. The second decomposition is into the shear part and the volumetric part, with
\mathrm{tr}T
denoting the trace of the matrix
T
and
{\mathrm{Id}}
the
n\times n
identity matrix. Combining both decompositions, we get the so-called Cartan decomposition of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{gl}(n)=\left(\mathfrak{sl}(n)\cap\mathrm{Sym}(n)\right)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(n)\oplus\mathbb{R}{\mathrm{Id}}
(see e.g.,
[
8
]
), namely

T=\mathrm{sym}\ T+\mathrm{skew}\ T=\mathrm{dev\ sym}\ T+\mathrm{skew}\ T+\frac{\mathrm{tr}T}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}.

Take now
T=Du
for
u
a vector-valued function of bounded variation; one obtains

Du={\mathcal{E}}u+\mathrm{skew}\ Du=\mathrm{dev}\ {\mathcal{E}}u+\mathrm{anti}\ {\rm curl}u+\frac{{\rm div}u}{n}{\mathrm{Id}},

where
{\mathcal{E}}u:=\mathrm{sym}\ Du=\left(\frac{Du+Du^{t}}{2}\right)
,
\mathrm{anti}\ {\rm curl}u:=\mathrm{skew}\ Du
, thus showing a decomposition of the deformation gradient
Du
into an (infinitesimal) rotation term,
\mathrm{anti}\ {\rm curl}u
, a volumetric term,
\frac{{\rm div}u}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}
, and, eventually, a pure shear term,
\mathrm{dev}\ {\mathcal{E}}u
.

It turns out that several issues arising from continuum mechanics, such as fluid mechanics or linearized elasticity (but also in general relativity), involve the deviatoric operator

{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u:=\mathrm{dev}\ {\mathcal{E}}u={\mathcal{E}}u-\frac{{\rm div}(u)}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}.

{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
is defined, in principle, on functions
u\in C^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
; but, as for the larger spaces
\mathrm{BV}
and
\mathrm{BD}
, it becomes relevant to introduce the space of functions of
bounded deviatoric deformations
:

\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\Omega):=\left\{\left.u\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \right|\ {\mathcal{E}}_{d}u\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{sym}_{0}}^{n\times n})\right\}\subset\mathrm{BD}(\Omega).

where
\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{sym}_{0}}^{n\times n})
is the space of finite
trace-free, symmetric

\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}
-valued Radon measures.

1.2.
Energy minimization problems in Materials science

As far as variational problems are concerned, in elasticity or elasto-plasticity, but also soft matter and material engineering, it is classical to consider the stored elastic energy

\int_{\Omega}W({\mathcal{E}}u)dx

and to consider the additive decomposition
W({\mathcal{E}}u)=W_{\rm{shear}}(\rm{dev}{\mathcal{E}}u)+W_{\rm{bulk}}({\rm div}u)
with some growth properties on each kinematical variable. Simply putting
W_{\rm{bulk}}({\rm div}u)=0
is not a reasonable choice, at least for any natural material, since it implies the unphysical assumption of a material with vanishing bulk (compression) modulus. However, it might be of interest and studied as a limit case for the fabrication of some metamaterials, within the challenging field of material synthesis. These modern, artificial solids or liquids, like colloidal crystals, polymer foams, or unscreened metals, exhibit plenty of surprising properties and therefore open the way to many modern industrial applications
[
14
,
20
,
26
]
. Many of these properties are related to materials with negative Poisson ratios
[
25
,
30
]
, like auxetic materials, which, in contrast to classical materials, exhibit a reverse deformation mechanism
[
30
]
. The huge diversity of mechanical properties of modern and natural materials can also be viewed in plots of the bulk modulus
B
versus shear modulus
G
. For instance, materials with a small Poisson’s ratio are more easily compressed than sheared (small
B/G
), whereas those with a high Poisson’s ratio resist compression in favour of shear (large
B/G
). As a limit case, when
B/G\ll 1
(hence with Poisson’s ratio at its lower bound,
\nu\to-1
), materials are extremely compressible, examples being re-entrant foams and molecular structures
[
25
]
.

As a matter of fact, one may be interested in studying homogenization problems like
W_{\varepsilon}({\mathcal{E}}u)=g(\varepsilon)W_{0}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)+{b(\varepsilon)W_{\rm{vol}}({\rm div}u)}
with
b/g(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. At the limit, no bound on
{\rm div}u
exists, and for linear type
W_{0}
as
C^{-1}|\xi|\leq W_{0}(\xi)\leq C|\xi|
the relevant space is thus
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
. In mathematical physics, the problem of minimizing

\int_{\Omega}\left(W_{\rm{shear}}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)-f\cdot u\right)dx

is relevant in general relativity (specifically for the study of black holes), for the so-called “momentum constraint” issue (see
[
15
]
and the references therein).

In terms of functional spaces, one has
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}\supset\mathrm{BD}\supset\mathrm{BV}
, and, as we said, the mechanics of the phenomena under analysis do not always allow one to consider the full deformation gradient
Du
as a kinematical variable on its own in any mathematical model, i.e., one often has no control on all components of the full gradient. Now, suppose for instance we have a functional
\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{S}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{Bor}(\Omega)\to[0,+\infty]
(here
\mathrm{Bor}(\Omega)
is the family of Borel sets of
\Omega
) defined on the functional space
\mathrm{S}(\Omega)=\mathrm{BV}(\Omega),\mathrm{BD}(\Omega)
, or
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\Omega)
, lower semicontinuous on
\mathrm{S}
, and satisfying the bound

(1.1)

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{F}(u)\leq C|\mu|(\Omega),

for some
C>0
, and where
\mu=Du,{\mathcal{E}}u
, or
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
, respectively, with
|\mu|
the total variation measure of
\mu
. Then, according to the physical problem under study, the analysis of
\mathcal{F}
and in particular the question of the existence of an integral representation for
\mathcal{F}
arises naturally in each
\mathrm{S}
. This issue is prominent if, for instance,
{\mathcal{F}}
arises from a relaxation process, since then it will be lower semicontinuous, even if its integral expression is not known, or even if it would exist. Also, in homogenization processes, one might wish to pass to the limit with respect to a small scale parameter present in the deviatoric part of the energy
W_{\rm{bulk}}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)
and eventually determine the integral form of the limit. For
\mathrm{BV}
, the pioneering work can be found in
[
10
]
, while in
\mathrm{BD}
it was recently achieved by the authors in
[
12
]
. It thus became natural to raise the question of an integral representation for
\mathcal{F}
as defined in
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
.

The strategy developed in
[
12
]
goes through an iterative blow-up procedure that is based on rigidity properties of
\mathrm{BD}
maps with constant polar vectors and a specific projection operator onto
\mathrm{Ker}({\mathcal{E}})
appearing in the Korn-Poincaré inequality. Actually, as shown in
[
13
]
, where the authors refined a double blow-up technique for this purpose, these two ingredients are enough to tackle homogenization and integral representation problems.

We here provide a rigidity property for maps with constant polar in
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
and a specific projection operator
\mathcal{R}:L^{1}\rightarrow\mathrm{Ker}({\mathcal{E}}_{d})
. With these two results at hand, we will establish the two main ingredients needed to solve integral representation and homogenization problems in
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}

1.3.
Rigidity result

Given a map
u\in\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}(K)
, suppose that

(1.2)

{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u=M\mu,\ \ \mu\in\mathcal{M}(K;\mathbb{R}^{+})

for
\mu
a real-valued non-negative Radon measure and
M\in\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{sym}_{0}}^{n\times n}
a constant matrix. Then what can be said about
u
? Actually, for the purposes of the present analysis it is not necessary for
M
to be a generic matrix, but just a matrix in the
wave cone

\Lambda_{{\mathcal{A}}}
: a specific subspace of
\mathbb{M}_{\mathrm{sym}_{0}}^{n\times n}
depending on the differential operator
{\mathcal{A}}
annihilating
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}
(such that
{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{E}}_{d}=0
). Indeed, with classical tools (as done in
[
17
]
), it is easy to show that if
M\notin\Lambda_{{\mathcal{A}}}
, then
u
must be actually
C^{\infty}
and thus the relevant case from the technical point of view is the case
M\in\Lambda_{{\mathcal{A}}}
. Not only this: given
u\in\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\Omega)
, we can consider the Radon-Nikodým decomposition

{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u}{\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}{\mathcal{L}}^{n}+{\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{s}u.

Blowing up
u
at some absolutely continuous point
x
, we have convergence to an affine map given by
y\mapsto e_{d}(u)(x)y+u(x)
(here
e_{d}(u):=e(u)-\frac{1}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}
,
e(u)=\frac{\nabla u+\nabla u^{t}}{2}
, where
\nabla u(x)
is the
approximate differential of
u
at
x
that exists
{\mathcal{L}}^{n}
-a.e.). But for integral representation and homogenization, a characterization of the blow-ups on
\spt({\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{s}u)
is also required. A celebrated result (valid for any elliptic differential operator) due to De Philippis and Rindler
[
16
]
implies that

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{s}u}{\,\mathrm{d}|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{s}u|}\in\Lambda_{{\mathcal{A}}}\ \ \text{for $|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{s}u|$-a.e. $x\in\Omega$ }.

If we then consider a blow-up at
x\in\spt({\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{s}u)
on a specific convex set
K
:

(1.3)

u_{K,\varepsilon,x}(y)=\frac{u(x+\varepsilon y)-\mathcal{R}_{K}[u](y)}{\frac{|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u|(K_{\varepsilon}(x))}{|K|\varepsilon^{n-1}}},

we see that
u_{K,\varepsilon,x}\rightarrow v
where

{\mathcal{E}}_{d}v=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{s}u}{\,\mathrm{d}|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{s}u|}(x)|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}v|.

Thus, in order to characterize the blow-ups, we need to study the structure of solutions to (
1.2
) in the case when
M\in\Lambda_{{\mathcal{A}}}
.

Note that the annihilator of a differential operator is not unique, since if
{\mathcal{A}}
annihilates
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}
then
\partial_{i}{\mathcal{A}}
still annihilates
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}
. But it is true that
\Lambda_{{\mathcal{A}}}\subset\Lambda_{\partial_{i}{\mathcal{A}}}
. In particular, to constrain as much as possible the polar of the singular part, one wants to find the annihilator
{\mathcal{A}}
of
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}
of lowest order. We are able to compute such an annihilator, and with this differential operator at hand we can focus our rigidity result only on
M\in\Lambda_{{\mathcal{A}}}
.

Remark 1.1
.

A major challenge in the present setting, compared to the classical
\mathrm{BD}

{\mathcal{E}}_{d}
does not behave well under changes of variables that orthogonalize the vectors

and

\mathrm{BD}

We recall the notation:

.

Let

. There exists an annihilator

for

If

The operator

.

The constraint

1.4.
Kernel projection

While the rigidity result presented above constitutes the main analytical cornerstone of our approach, the development of a comprehensive blow-up strategy in the space
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
requires a further structural ingredient. In particular, although the following result has a somewhat less pronounced impact compared to the rigidity theorem, it remains an essential tool in iterative blow-up procedure as clarified in Subsection
1.5

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
K
be a center symmetric convex body. Upon defining the integral operators
\mathrm{s}_{K},\mathrm{A}_{K},\gamma_{K},\mathrm{b}_{K}
and

(see Section

), (

as

(1.9)

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{K}[u](y):=&\left(\mathrm{A}_{K}[u]+\gamma_{K}[u]{\rm Id}\,\right)(y-\mathrm{bar}(K))\\
&+(\mathrm{s}_{K}[u]\cdot(y-\mathrm{bar}(K)))(y-\mathrm{bar}(K))-\mathrm{s}_{K}[u]\frac{|y-\mathrm{bar}(K)|^{2}}{2}+\mathrm{b}_{K}[u]\end{split}

is a linear, bounded operator satisfying
\mathcal{R}_{K}(L)=L
for all

Note that the Poincaré inequality is actually well-known for a whole series of operators (see

, which linearizes one direction, resulting in one blow-up of the form (for some
\psi\in\mathrm{BV}_{loc}
)

w(z)=\kappa a(b\cdot z)+\psi(z\cdot a)b;

2.4)

By employing that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{K}(w)=0
and the knowledge of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{K}
, the constant
\kappa
is computed, together with some useful properties of
\psi
;

2.5)

By a general principle (blow-ups of blow-ups are blow-ups, see also
[
13
, Proposition 3.6]
), we conclude that
w
must be a blow-up of
u
at
x
, namely that along a specific sequence
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
,
u_{K,\varepsilon_{i},x}\rightarrow w
.

At
|{\mathcal{E}}u|
-a.e. point we then have either convergence to an affine or a jump function, or convergence to a partially linear map. This information is now all that is required to identify the limit in (
1.11
).

Therefore, rigidity theorem
1.2
and kernel projection
1.4
provide the main steps for running the same strategy also on local, lower-semicontinuous energies on
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
.

The main advantage of this approach is that it allows the energy
{\mathcal{F}}
to depend also on
u
and not only on
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
.

1.6.
Fine properties

Once the annihilator is computed, as a corollary we can derive a structure theorem for
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
in the spirit of the one holding for
\mathrm{BV}
. In particular, we can prove that
|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u|\ll{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}
, and obtain the specific structure of the jump part (see also
[
11
]
):

{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u=e_{d}(u){\mathcal{L}}^{n}+\left([u]\odot\nu_{u}-\frac{([u]\cdot\nu_{u})}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}\right){\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}\,\raisebox{-0.5468pt}{\reflectbox{\rotatebox[origin={br}]{-90.0}{$\lnot$}}}\,_{J_{u}}+\left(a(x)\odot b(x)-\frac{(a(x)\cdot b(x))}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}\right)|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{c}u|

where
|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{c}u|
is the Cantor part. Let us spend a few words about a major difference that we actually encounter when looking at the decomposition of
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
compared to the decomposition of
{\mathcal{E}}u
or
\mathcal{D}u
. Calling
S_{u}
the set of points where
u
is not
approximately continuous
(i.e.,
x
is not a Lebesgue point for
u
), it is known that
\mathrm{BD}
and
\mathrm{BV}
-maps charge this set almost all on
J_{u}
, namely
|{\mathcal{E}}u|(S_{u}\setminus J_{u})=0
for
\mathrm{BD}
, and
{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}(S_{u}\setminus J_{u})=0
for
\mathrm{BV}
. It is actually an open problem whether the same property holds for
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
maps:
|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u|(S_{u}\setminus J_{u})=0
? The slicing technique developed in
[
4
]
, as a generalization of
[
2
]
, seems not to work for
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
due to a specific missing property of the operator: it is not true in general that—not even in some special directions—the one-dimensional slices of a general
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
function are
\mathrm{BV}
functions.

As a corollary of Theorem
1.4
, by applying an argument similar to the one developed in
[
2
,
27
]
, we can rewrite the integral operators defining
\mathrm{s}_{K},\mathrm{A}_{K},\mathrm{\gamma}_{K}
and
\mathrm{b}_{K}
(in (
6.3
)-(
6.6
)) as nonlocal interaction integrals against
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
. This allows us to control the infinitesimal behavior of these quantities, providing quasi-continuity
|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u|
-a.e. for
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
functions, a weaker notion than approximate continuity.

We therefore report this very natural conjecture about the fine properties of
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
functions.

Conjecture 1.5
.

For all
u\in\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\Omega)
it holds
|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u|(S_{u}\setminus J_{u})=0
.

The conjecture might be true only for

, since in

the operator fails to be
\mathbb{C}
-elliptic, although it is not clear how important this property is for the
|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u|
-a.e. approximate continuity.

1.7.
Strategy of the proof for the rigidity

The most important result, where the highest non-triviality lies, is the proof of the rigidity part in Theorem
1.2
. The proof is quite computational, so we spend a few words to explain the underlying strategy, which is fully developed in Section
5
.

We first consider a general function
u\in C^{\infty}(K;\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying

(1.14)

{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u=\left(a\odot b-\frac{(a\cdot b)}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}\right)g

for some
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
. We exploit this structure of
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
and apply Schwarz’s Theorem to the differential relation

\nabla\left(\frac{\nabla u-\nabla u^{t}}{2}\right)_{ij}=\partial_{i}(({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)e_{j})-\partial_{j}(({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)e_{i})+\partial_{i}\left(\frac{{\rm div}(u)}{n}\right)e_{j}-\partial_{j}\left(\frac{{\rm div}(u)}{n}\right)e_{i}

(which is a variant of the differential relation exploited for the rigidity of
\mathrm{BD}
maps with constant polar vector). This leads to some general considerations and to a set of PDEs (listed in Lemma
5.1
) that
u
must solve. In particular, we give these equations in terms of
u
,
g
, and
f:=\frac{{\rm div}(u)-(a\cdot b)g}{n}
, which quantifies how much
u
fails to satisfy
\mathrm{BD}
rigidity, since

{\mathcal{E}}u=(a\odot b)g+f{\mathrm{Id}}

(
f=0
implies that
u
has the structure of (
1.12
)). To perform this computation, we use specific coordinates. Having established this set of PDEs in Lemma
5.1
, for
u\in C^{\infty}
solving (
1.14
), we proceed to treat separately the cases
a\not\parallel b
and
a\parallel b
.

The first case, treated in Subsection
5.1
, which is the most technical one, is handled by showing that the set of PDEs in Lemma
5.1
—after suitable manipulation—actually leads to wave equations for
\partial_{a}g
and
\partial_{b}g
. The D’Alembert formula then implies the one-dimensionality of
\partial_{a}g
and
\partial_{b}g
up to a polynomial remainder. This characterizes
g
as being the sum of two waves plus a polynomial part, and this gives the precise structure (
1.5
) for
u\in C^{\infty}
. This is done in Subsection
5.1.1
. Now, for a generic
u\in\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\Omega)
solving (
1.4
), we consider a mollification
u_{\varepsilon}:=u\star\varrho_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}
. Since mollification preserves the structure of (
1.4
),
u_{\varepsilon}
will solve (
1.14
) (with
g_{\varepsilon}=\mu\star\varrho_{\varepsilon}
). Thus the mollified
u_{\varepsilon}
must have the claimed structure — being
C^{\infty}
— and the main point now consists in showing that such a structure is stable when taking the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The most difficult part here is handling the polynomial part
Q_{\varepsilon}
. Indeed, the coefficient of
Q_{\varepsilon}
will involve the quantity
\tau_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{12}\left(\frac{{\rm div}(u_{\varepsilon})-g_{\varepsilon}}{n}\right)
, and such a quantity might in principle have no limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, since
{\rm div}(u_{\varepsilon})
might not converge for
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
maps (
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
does not provide any control on
{\rm div}(u)
). However, the constant polar structure of
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
suggests that a
u
solving (
1.5
) is somewhat more than just
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
. Thus, in Subsection
5.1.2
, with an explicit computation of (part of) the polynomial remainder
Q
, we gain control on
\tau_{\varepsilon}
and are able to pass to the limit in Subsection
5.1.3
.

The second case, when
a\parallel b
, is treated in
5.2
, where we again argue first for
u\in C^{\infty}
, and we see that the equations given by Lemma
5.1
are easily integrable, yielding a
g
which has a one-dimensional part plus a purely polynomial part plus a mixed term, which is a polynomial in
x_{j}
for
j\geq 2
with coefficients depending on
x_{1}
. Again, once
g
is identified, the shape of
u
follows. For a generic function, we again need to pass to the limit, and we need to handle the floating constants that might diverge. We obtain the required control by testing (
1.5
) against specific test functions. In this part, we find it convenient to treat separately the case
n=3
and
n\geq 4
due to a slight difference between the two cases.

In the end, being able to pass to the limit somehow amounts to showing that
u
is actually more regular than
\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}
(at least
\mathrm{BD}
). This fact should not be a surprise, since
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
has constant polar vector, and a posteriori, as Theorem
1.2
clarifies, such a

is actually in
\mathrm{BV}
.

is made by Killing vector fields

\mathrm{Ker}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}):=\left\{L(y)=(A+\gamma\mathrm{Id})y+s\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}-(s\cdot y)y+b\ \left|A\in\mathbb{M}^{d\times d}_{skew},\ s,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \gamma\in\mathbb{R}\right.\right\}.

Since the proof of the Kernel structure and the ingredients required, are quite enlightening in order to deepen the approach used to prove rigidity we here present the proof of this result.

Set, for
u\in C^{\infty}(A;\mathbb{R}^{n})
the quantity
Wu:=\frac{\nabla u-\nabla u^{t}}{2}
and observe that

(3.1)

\nabla(Wu)_{ij}=\partial_{i}(({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)e_{j})-\partial_{j}(({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)e_{i})+\partial_{i}\left(\frac{{\rm div}(u)}{n}\right)e_{j}-\partial_{j}\left(\frac{{\rm div}(u)}{n}\right)e_{i}.

Proposition 3.1

(Kernel structure)
.

Let
n\geq 3
,
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
a connected set. Suppose that
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u=0
. Then there exists
s,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
A\in\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{skew}
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

u(y)=(A+\gamma{\mathrm{Id}})y+s\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}-(s\cdot y)y+b.

Proof.

Because of (
3.1
) and
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u=0
we have for all
i\neq j

\nabla(Wu)_{ij}=\partial_{i}\left(\frac{{\rm div}(u)}{n}\right)e_{j}-\partial_{j}\left(\frac{{\rm div}(u)}{n}\right)e_{i}.

In particular, by taking the curl we get

(3.2)

\partial_{ii}{\rm div}(u)+\partial_{jj}{\rm div}(u)=0\ \ \ \text{for all $j\neq i$}

and

(3.3)

\partial_{ki}{\rm div}(u)=0\ \ \ \text{for all $k\neq i$}.

Since we have
n\geq 3
we have at least another index
m\neq i\neq j
for which

\partial_{mm}{\rm div}(u)+\partial_{ii}{\rm div}(u)=0,\ \ \ \partial_{mm}{\rm div}(u)+\partial_{jj}{\rm div}(u)=0,\ \ \ \partial_{ii}{\rm div}(u)+\partial_{jj}{\rm div}(u)=0.

But then

\partial_{ii}{\rm div}(u)=-\partial_{mm}{\rm div}(u)=\partial_{jj}{\rm div}(u)

from which it follows
\partial_{ii}{\rm div}(u)=\partial_{jj}{\rm div}(u)=\partial_{mm}{\rm div}(u)=0
. By combining this with (
3.3
) we have

\nabla(\partial_{i}{\rm div}(u))=0

this being true for every fixed
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then, since
\Omega
is connected, for some
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\partial_{i}\frac{{\rm div}(u)}{n}=s_{i},\ \ \ \nabla({\rm div}(u))=ns

which again implies that
{\rm div}(u)=n(s\cdot y+\gamma)
for
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. This implies

0={\mathcal{E}}u-\frac{{\rm div}(u)}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}\ \Rightarrow\ {\mathcal{E}}u=(s\cdot y+\gamma){\mathrm{Id}}.

Observe that, setting
p(y):=(s\cdot y)y-s\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}+\gamma y
we have

{\mathcal{E}}p(y)=(s\cdot y+\gamma){\mathrm{Id}}

and thus
{\mathcal{E}}(u-p)=0
. By now invoking the characterization of the kernel of
{\mathcal{E}}
we conclude that, for some
A\in\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{skew}
,

u(y)-p(y)=Ay+b\ \Rightarrow\ u(y)=Ay+p(y)+b

as claimed.
∎

3.2.
Annihilator

Given a function
F\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{M}_{sym_{0}}^{n\times n})
we seek for an operator
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathcal{A}F=0
whenever
F={\mathcal{E}}_{d}u
for some potential
u
. In particular the existence of such object for
\mathbb{C}
-elliptic operators is always guaranteed by a result of Van Shaftingen
[
33
, Remark 4.1, Lemma 4.4]
(see also
[
6
, Proposition 17]
for an extension of the Van Shaftingen’s construction). The result in the papers are abstract and not constructive.

The annihilator, together with the powerful result in
[
16
]
, will allows us to determine the structure of the singular measure
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{E}}^{s}_{d}u}{\,\mathrm{d}|{\mathcal{E}}^{s}_{d}u|}
. Indeed, setting

(3.4)

\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}:=\bigcup_{|\xi|=1}\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbb{A}[\xi])

then (cf. with
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
)

(3.5)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u}{\,\mathrm{d}|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}u|}(x)\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}\ \ \ \text{for $|{\mathcal{E}}_{d}^{s}u|$-a.e. $x\in\Omega$}

Since the annihilitor is not unique (think about
\mathrm{curl}
and
\nabla(\mathrm{curl})
both annihiling
\nabla u
) we need to seek for the operator with the lowest possible order so to have the Kernel of its symbol (and thus its wave cone) the smallest possible in order to find the sharpest constraint on the polar vector of the singular part.

Proposition 3.2

(Annihilator)
.

Define for
F\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{M}_{sym_{0}}^{n\times n})
the fourth-order operator
\mathcal{A}:C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{M}_{sym_{0}}^{n\times n})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{M}_{sym_{0}}^{n\times n})

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}(F))_{jk}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{iij\ell}F_{k\ell}+\partial_{iik\ell}F_{\ell j}-\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{ii\ell\ell}F_{jk}

(3.6)

\displaystyle-\frac{n-2}{n-1}\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{i\ell jk}F_{i\ell}-\frac{\delta_{jk}}{n-1}\sum_{i,\ell,m=1}^{n}\partial_{ii\ell m}F_{\ell m}.

Then, if
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
it holds

\mathcal{A}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)=0.

Proof.

It is verifiable via a direct computation or by arguing on the symbol as explained in Remark
3.3
. We report, for the sake of completeness, the main step of the computations where we make repeatedly use of Schwarz’s Theorem.

\displaystyle\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{iij\ell}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)_{k\ell}+\partial_{iik\ell}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)_{\ell j}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\ell=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{iij\ell}\left(\partial_{\ell}u_{k}+\partial_{k}u_{\ell}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{iijk}\mathrm{div}(u)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\ell=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{iik\ell}\left(\partial_{\ell}u_{j}+\partial_{j}u_{\ell}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{iikj}\mathrm{div}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\ell=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{ii\ell\ell}(\partial_{j}u_{k}+\partial_{k}u_{j})+\frac{n-2}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{iijk}{\rm div}(u)\,,

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{ii\ell\ell}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)_{jk}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{ii\ell\ell}\left(\partial_{j}u_{k}+\partial_{k}u_{j}\right)+\frac{\delta_{jk}}{n}\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{ii}\partial_{\ell\ell}{\rm div}(u)\,,

\displaystyle-\frac{n-2}{n-1}\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{i\ell jk}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)_{i\ell}=

\displaystyle-\frac{n-2}{2(n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\ell=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{i\ell jk}(\partial_{i}u_{\ell}+\partial_{\ell}u_{i})+\frac{n-2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{iijk}{\rm div}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n-2}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{iijk}{\rm div}(u)\,,

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta_{jk}}{n-1}\sum_{i,\ell,m=1}^{n}\partial_{ii\ell m}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)_{\ell m}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta_{jk}}{2(n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\ell,m=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{ii\ell m}(\partial_{\ell}u_{m}+\partial_{m}u_{\ell})+\frac{\delta_{jk}}{n(n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\ell=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{ii\ell\ell}{\rm div}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta_{jk}}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\ell=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\partial_{ii\ell\ell}{\rm div}(u)\,.

Now by simply adding up the above relations we obtain
\mathcal{A}({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u)=0
.
∎

Remark 3.3

So somehow the fourth order is the minimum required in order to find a linear function

Proposition 3.6

(Wave cone of
\mathcal{A}
)
.

If

\mathbb{A}[\xi]M=0\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ M=v\odot\xi-\frac{(v\cdot\xi)}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}\ \text{for some $v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

In particular

\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}=\left\{\left.v\odot\xi-\frac{(v\cdot\xi)}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}\ \right|\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \xi\neq 0\right\}.

Proof.

If
M=v\odot\xi-\frac{(v\cdot\xi)}{n}{\mathrm{Id}}
for some

then it is immediate that
\mathbb{A}[\xi]M=0

This gives us

M_{11}=(e_{1}^{t}Me_{1})=:\varrho,\ \ M_{ij}=0\ \ \text{for $i\neq j$, $i,j>1$},\ \ M_{ii}=-\frac{\varrho}{(n-1)},\ i>1.

Thus, setting

for

\vdots&\vdots&-\frac{\varrho}{n-1}&\ldots&0\\

4.1.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

If

If

\mu_{1}=(a\cdot\xi)=|a|

We treat the two cases separately.

Case 1).

\frac{(a\cdot e_{2})}{2}&0\end{array}\right).

To find the eigenvalues we need to solve

whose solutions are precisely

which are the claimed values.

Case 2).
Without loss of generality we can suppose that
\xi=|\xi|e_{1}
,
a=|a|e_{1}
. Then, the only eigenvector is

itself with eigenvalue

\mu_{1}=|a||\xi|=(a\cdot\xi).

5.1.3.
Approximation argument

The following is a standard approximation argument.

Lemma 5.8

(Approximation Lemma)
.

Let
u\in\mathrm{BD}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let
\varrho_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{\varepsilon}(0))
be a mollifying kernel. Then it holds
u_{\varepsilon}:=u\star\varrho_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}
and

(1)

{\mathcal{E}}_{d}(u_{\varepsilon})(x)=({\mathcal{E}}_{d}u\star\varrho_{\varepsilon})(x)
;

(2)

u_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow u
in
L_{{\rm loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
s_{\varepsilon}(x)=\psi_{\varepsilon}(x\cdot\ell)
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for some sequence of measurable functions
\psi_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{BV}_{{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R})
. Suppose that
s_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow s
in
L^{1}_{{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then
s(x)=\psi(x\cdot\ell)
for some measurable function
\psi\in L^{1}_{{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Suppose without loss of generality that
\ell=e_{1}
. Since
\psi_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{BV}_{{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R})
then
s_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{BV}_{{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
Ds_{\varepsilon}=e_{1}D\psi_{\varepsilon}(\,\mathrm{d}x_{1})\otimes{\mathcal{L}}^{n-1}(\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}\ldots\,\mathrm{d}x_{n})
. Here we recall the notation

\mu(\,\mathrm{d}x_{1})\otimes\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}\ldots\,\mathrm{d}x_{n})(\varphi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x_{2})\ldots\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x_{n}).

For
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we also have for
k\neq 1
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}s\partial_{k}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}s_{\varepsilon}\partial_{k}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=-\lim_{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(e_{1}\cdot e_{k})\varphi\,\mathrm{d}D\psi_{\varepsilon}(\,\mathrm{d}x_{1})\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}\ldots\,\mathrm{d}x_{d}=0.

Thence, distributionally
\partial_{k}s=0
for all
k\neq 1
. This implies that
s(x)=\psi(x\cdot e_{1})
for a measurable function
\psi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Finally, to handle the polynomial part we need to employ the following Lemma

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
Q_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a polynoms in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
of degree
k
. Suppose that
Q_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow Q
in
L_{loc}^{1}
for some
Q\in L^{1}_{loc}
. Then
Q
is a polynom of degree at most
k
and the coefficients of
Q_{\varepsilon}
converges to the coefficient of
Q
.

Proof.

Fix an open bounded set

and note that the space

S:=\{Q:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ |\ \text{$Q$ is a polynom of degree at most $k$}\}

is a closed finite dimensional vector space of finite dimension. Let
\underline{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}^{n}
be a multindex and, for
Q\in S
,
c^{Q}_{\underline{i}}
be denoting the coefficient of
Q
in front of a monom
x^{\underline{i}}:=x_{1}^{i_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{i_{n}}
,
|\underline{i}|=i_{1}+\ldots+i_{n}\leq k
. Then the application

T:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N},\ \ T(Q):=(c^{Q}_{\underline{i}})_{\underline{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}^{n}}

is a linear application between finite dimensional vector spaces and hence is continuous. Thus, since
Q_{\varepsilon}
is converging in
L_{loc}^{1}
, we have
Q_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow Q
in
L^{1}(A)
for some

. Thence

|T(Q_{\varepsilon})-T(Q)|\leq C_{T}\|Q_{\varepsilon}-Q\|_{L^{1}}\rightarrow 0.

By the very definition of

we have that the coefficient of
Q_{\varepsilon}
converges to those of
Q
.
∎

We are now in the position for proving Theorem
5.3
