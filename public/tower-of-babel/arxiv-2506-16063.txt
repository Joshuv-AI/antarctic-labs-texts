(Re)-Defining Planets – the Fundamental Plane of Planets 2026 (Re)-Defining Planets – the Fundamental Plane of Planets – 4
Madhu Kashyap Jagadeesh, Arkil D. P, Margarita Safonova, Bernard Foing

Abstract
6000+ exoplanets are now detected using various techniques, with estimates of billions of planets existing in our Galaxy alone. They are called super-Earths, hot Earths, mini-Neptunes, hot Neptunes, sub-Neptunes, Saturns, Jupiters, hot Jupiters, Jovians, gas giants, ice giants, rocky, terran, subterran, superterran, and so on. This prompted the emergence of many recent works on taxonomy, or classification, of exoplanets. However, there is still no basic, fundamental definition of ‘What is a planet?’, as opposing to stars/asteroids/moons. IAU has forwarded a definition (of planets and other Solar System bodies) in 2006 which, however, raised more questions than it solved. The first ambitious task here is to establish if there are limits on the size/mass of planets. The lower mass limit may be assumed as of Mimas (0.03 EU) – approximately minimum mass required to attain a nearly spherical hydrostatic equilibrium shape, following IAU 2nd law of planets. The upper mass limit may be easier – there is a natural lower limit to what constitutes a star: ∼ \sim 0.08 SU. But then there are brown dwarfs: IAU has defined brown dwarfs as objects that exceed the deuterium burning limit ( ∼ \sim 13 JU), and giant exoplanets generally have masses of ∼ \sim 0.3 to ∼ \sim 60 JU. Additionally, the IAU 3rd law of planets states that a planet has to clear the neighbourhood around its orbit, which is still very difficult to determine even for closest exoplanets. The resolution requires assembling the basic physical parameters that define planets quantitatively. Mass and radius are the two fundamental properties, and we propose to use a third correlated parameter: the moment of inertia. Based on this, we create the fundamental planet plane where the two parameters are correlated with the third, similar to a fundamental galactic plane. The fundamental planetary plane with turn-off point diagrams is constructed for the first time in this work. We define a planet as "A celestial spherical object, bound to a star or unbound, that lies on the fundamental planetary plane, within a mass range between 10 23 10^{23} Kg (0.02 EU) to 2.5 × 10 28 2.5\times 10^{28} Kg (13 JU)".

Introduction

What is a planet? The term ‘planet’ originates from the ancient Greek word
planētēs
, meaning ‘wanderer’. In modern age, Merriam-Webster encyclopedia
1
1

1

https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/encyclopedia
defines a planet as "any of the large bodies that revolve around the Sun in the Solar System." In 2006, the International Astronomical Union (IAU)
2
2

2

https://www.iau.org/Iau/Publications/List-of-Resolutions
passed a new resolution on the definition of a planet, which led to Pluto’s reclassification as a dwarf planet. According to the IAU definition, we have three laws of planets:

Ist law of planets:
is in orbit around the Sun.

IInd law of planets:
has sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape.

IIIrd law of planets:
has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit.

The IAU definition did not include exoplanets and free-floating planets. Also, the definition does not properly explain what it means to clear the neighborhood. Even the hydrostatic equilibrium clause is problematic – for example, Mercury is not actually in hydrostatic equilibrium
(
Balogh et al. 2008
)
, though nobody is suggesting to removing it from the family of planets.

These problems with the planetary laws stimulated a lot of work, and numerous new definitions and criteria have been proposed. In 2022, the IAU Working Group (WG) on Exoplanetary System
(
Lecavelier des Etangs & Lissauer 2022
)
suggested the following working definition of exoplanets: "Objects with true masses below the limiting mass for thermonuclear fusion of deuterium (currently calculated to be 13 Jupiter masses for objects of solar metallicity) that orbit stars, brown dwarfs or stellar remnants and that have a mass ratio with the central object below the L4/L5 instability", adding that it is irrelevant how they were formed. However, the free-floating bodies (’rogue planets’) were still excluded from planethood.

More recently,
Margot, Gladman, & Yang 2024
proposed the following definition:

"A planet is that

(a) orbits one or more stars, brown dwarfs, or stellar remnants,

(b) is more massive than
10^{23}
kg,

(c) is less massive than 13 Jupiter masses (
2.5\times 10^{28}
kg).

In addition, rogue planets ought to satisfy criteria (b) and (c) to be called a planet."

"Clearing the neighbourhood", also called dynamical dominance, means that a planet must be the gravitationally dominant object in its orbit. The idea was first introduced by Stern and Levison in a year 2000 to indicate that a planet must clear its orbit of the planetesimals during the final stages of formation
Soter 2006
.

If a large object meets the other two planet criteria but hasn’t cleared its neighbourhood, it is called a dwarf planet as is the case with Pluto, which shares its orbit with many Kuiper Belt objects and crosses paths with Neptune.

Others have proposed ways to measure how well a body clears its orbit, using what they called planetary discriminants (
Soter 2006
;
Margot 2015
). Some prefer the term dynamical dominance because it is clearer and less confusing (
Margot 2015
). However, when it comes to planets outside our Solar System (exoplanets), it is still very hard to tell if they have cleared their orbits because we do not yet have the technology to detect small object near them (
Margot 2015
). Even in our Solar System, and only recently, using such powerful telescopes as HST and Keck Observatory, we started detecting objects we never seen before: such as, e.g., the hierarchical triple systems in not so far out Kuiper Belt (e.g.
Nelsen et al. 2025
). And even then it required accumulating data for 17 years. How can we expect to know the orbital neighbourhood of planets that are hundreds of parsecs away?

In addition, in our own Solar System, the planetary orbits are not completely clear. At least 100,000 trojan asteroids near Jupiter’s L4 and L5 points are still a mystery with respect to their understanding of evolution and dynamics (
Bottke et al. 2023
); it is obvious that Jupiter’s neighborhood is not cleared. Earth immediate neighbourhood is very complex, it coorbits with many thousands of near-Earth-objects (NEO) with a discovery rate of over 3000 NEOs per year (
Grav et al. 2023
). According to recent studies, Earth temporarily captures minimoons, which represent a subset of objects from the delayed bound population (
Jedicke et al. 2025
). It is possible that there are about 1,000 0.1-m diameter TCOs (temporarily-captured orbiters) in orbit around Earth at any given time
(
Granvik et al. 2012
)
. Similarly, 20 co-orbital asteroids of Venus are known till date (
Carruba et al. 2025
).

In addition, what exactly does the term dominance imply? For example, Earth is much more massive that the Moon and anything else in its orbit. But if we place Earth, say, beyond Neptune, it will become gravitationally dominated by the icy giant, and thus will stop being a planet?

In this paper, we propose to define planets by introducing the fundamental planet plane. The resolution requires assembling the basic physical parameters that define planets quantitatively. Mass and radius are the two fundamental physical properties, and we propose to use additional correlated parameter: the moment of inertia. Based on this, we create the fundamental planet plane where the two parameters are correlated with the third, similar to a fundamental galactic plane. The fundamental planetary plane with turn-off point diagrams is constructed for the first time in this work. We redefine a planet as "A celestial spherical object, bound to a star or unbound, that lies on the fundamental planetary plane, within a mass range between
10^{23}
Kg (or 0.02 EU) to
2.5\times 10^{28}
Kg (or 13 JU)".

Mathematical formulation of moment of inertia of planets

The moment of inertia of a planet provides insight into its mass distribution, which is critical for understanding its structure and internal dynamics. The derivation depends on the planet’s shape and mass distribution.

The moment of inertia
I
of a rigid body is given by

I=\int_{V}r^{2}\,\mathrm{d}m\,,

(1)

where
r
is the perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation to the mass element
\mathrm{d}m
,

\mathrm{d}m=\rho\,\mathrm{d}V
, where
\rho
is the density and
\mathrm{d}V
is the volume element.

For a planet modeled as a uniform solid sphere, the axis of rotation is typically the polar axis. Using the spherical coordinates, the volume element is

\mathrm{d}V=r^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi
. The distance from the rotation axis is
r\sin\theta
, therefore,

I=\int_{V}\rho(r\sin\theta)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V\,.

(2)

On solving, we get the moment of inertia for unfirom mass distribution,

I=\frac{2}{5}MR^{2}\,.

(3)

For planets with non-uniform mass distributions, the moment of inertia factor,
k
, was introduced:

I=kMR^{2}\,,

(4)

where
k
depends on the planet’s internal density structure (e.g., core, mantle). For example,
k\approx 0.33
for Earth due to its dense core (
Stevenson 1982
).
k
is a normalized moment of inertia value, calculated based on the gravitational moments measured using space crafts. For example, Jupiter’s normalized moment of inertia is calculated using Juno spacecraft (
Helled et al. 2011
). Since we cannot do these measurements for exoplanets, we have chosen a uniform solid-sphere approach.
In addition, we use the same technique for asteroids and moons, which may have irregular, often rubble-pile shapes, making it difficult to calculate their moment of inertia accurately. Therefore, a spherical model provides a simplified, albeit imperfect, representation that allows for easier calculations.

3.2
Dwarf planets and Planet X (or Planet Nine)

According to IAU definition, a "dwarf planet is a celestial body that (a) is in orbit around the Sun, (b) has sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape, (c) has not cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit, and
(d) is not a satellite". The definition of a dwarf planet (like Pluto) is different from the brown dwarf (like Jupiter) definition. But dwarf galaxies are still galaxies, and similarly, dwarf stars are still stars (see discussion in Appendix A). Therefore, dwarf planets can still be called planets. Ceres and Pluto are classified as dwarf planets because of the concept of internal differentiation.
Metzger et al. 2022
developed the argument that aligning the planetary taxonomy with geological complexity represents the most scientifically effective classification system. And they argued that moons are planets.

Figure 3:
Pluto turn-off in the moons plane: The magenta and blue colored triangles represent the moons and asteroids in the Solar System, respectively, while the blue stars denote IAU planetary candidates.

Pluto’s debate of being or not being a planet can be fixed using Fig
3
. According to it, we confirm that Pluto has most similar properties with moons. We see a clear position of Pluto on the moon plane and not on the fundamental planet
plane. This should not be seen as a demotion of Pluto, that has been shown a fascinating world from New Horizons 2015 flyby mission. Also outer Solar System moons and some dwarf planets are promising objects to study for geology and even astrobiology.

Neumann et al. 2020
results suggest that Ceres’ material may have undergone aqueous alteration on small precursor bodies, with some remaining dry if the dwarf planet formed within Pluto’s orbit. Differentiation in the water percolation regime progresses gradually over several hundred million years, ultimately resulting in a partially differentiated structure. This includes a liquid layer that may still persist between the porous, undifferentiated crust and the silicate core, consistent with observations from the Dawn mission. Both Ceres and Pluto show internal differentiation, with a distinct core separate from the mantle.
Interestingly, there has been a new discovery recently of a dwarf planet called 2017 OF201 with a wide orbit (
Cheng, Li, & Yang 2025
).

A central pillar supporting the Planet Nine hypothesis lies in the unexpected clustering of orbital elements among distant trans-Neptunian objects (TNOs). Observations reveal that a subset of these objects – particularly those with perihelia beyond 250 AU and semi-major axes exceeding 250 AU – exhibit statistically significant alignment in both argument of perihelion and longitude of ascending node. This orbital confinement cannot be readily explained by interactions with the known planets, leading researchers to postulate the presence of an external gravitational perturber (
Batygin et al. 2019
).

High Mass Threshold

The upper mass limit threshold of the fundamental planetary plane may be more straightforward: There is a natural lower limit to what constitutes a star:
\sim
0.08 SU.
But then there are brown dwarfs: IAU has defined brown dwarfs as objects that exceed the deuterium burning limit (
\sim
13 JU for solar metallicity) irrespective of formation mechanism.
The distinction between brown dwarfs and actual stars is subtle: both low-mass stars and brown dwarfs burn primordial deuterium at first, but a star will eventually settle down to stable H-burning; in the case of a 0.08 SU star this will take
\sim

Massey & Meyer 2001

Below a mass of 0.015 SU (
\sim
13 JU) not even burning of deuterium can occur, and these objects are perhaps best called planets. But it has been suggested that giant planets formed by core accretion in a proplyd can exceed the deuterium burning, and indeed, most exoplanets and brown dwarfs discovered to date include bodies ranging from
\sim
1 to
\sim

Discussion on Planetary Formation and Death of Planets

The main process of planetary formation is the process by which planets emerge from dust and gas surrounding a star. It begins when a cloud of gas and dust contracts under gravity, leading to the formation of a spinning protostar at its center. As the gas compresses further and heats up, the remaining surrounding material from the star’s formation starts to collapse into protoplanets. Dust grains stick together, forming mountain-sized planetesimals, which then collide and merge to create larger protoplanets. Over time, these protoplanets continue to grow, eventually developing into fully formed planets (
Fortier et al. 2013
). Other factors influencing planetary formation include disk turbulence, radial drift, and planet migration (
Chambers 2007
). Out of all exoplanets, free-floating planets are fascinating because they roam the galaxy without a host star, and may play a role in spreading life across interstellar distances (e.g.
Abbot&Switzer 2011
;
Durand-Manterola 2010
). Their numbers could surpass those of bound planets, as they are a natural outcome of various planetary formation processes, including ejection from planetary systems and formation in interstellar space (
Safonova et al. 2016
).

What stands out most about our Solar System are two key features: the absence of super-Earths (or hot Jupiters) with short orbital periods (ranging from days to months), and the absence of planets inside the orbital radius of Mercury. Overall, these broad characteristics suggest that our Solar System is not exceptionally rare, which gives reason for optimism in the search for life beyond Earth (
Martin & Livio 2015
).
The simulation study of
Kaib & Raymond 2025
, incorporating thousands of stellar flybys over 5 Gyr, reveals that close-passing stars can significantly amplify the long-term orbital instabilities of the Solar System, increasing the chances of events such as Pluto becoming unstable (5% probability), and boosting Mercury’s instability risk by 50–80%. These findings suggest that field star encounters may pose the most likely trigger for multi-planet instabilities over the next 4–4.5 billion years (
Kaib & Raymond 2025
). This suggests that our Solar System planetary orbits can be disturbed, resulting in planets being kicked out of the system, colliding with each other, or with the host star.

Planets can die during the death of the host star. But
Parsons 2020
detected a planet transiting in a tight orbit around the remains of a dead star. This suggests that planetary death is not a fully understood phenomenon. Planet’s physical death has to be in the lines of planetary disintegration, evaporation, or collision with a star, or a black hole. Future research in this direction will be very interesting.

Faber & Jackson 1976
) the two parameters are correlated with the third.

The HR diagram for stars shows a turn-off point of various stellar life cycles starting after star formation. Similarly, we see a turn-off for planets in Fig.

for rocky planets, which we define as the rocky planetary plane for low-mass planets.

In Fig.

, we see that IAU planetary candidates are away from the fundamental plane of all known planetary objects. Hence, the IAU candidates are not planets, they are most probably asteroids.

•

According to Fig.

•

We notice an interesting break on a log-log plane from brown dwarfs to low-mass stars in Fig.

•

We observe a clear turn-off from the gas-giant planetary plane to low-mass stars (Fig.

•

The proposed fundamental planetary plane depends on basic parameters such as mass and radius. We want to bring it to the notice that the currently available data on exoplanets seem to have an artifact of fitting (Fig.

). However, this could be due to the modeling. In the future, fundamental planetary plane work could be evolved further based on obtaining both mass and radius measurements.

•

Finally, we define a planet as "A celestial spherical object, bound to a star or unbound, that lies on the fundamental planetary plane, within a mass range between
10^{23}
Kg (0.02 EU) to
2.5\times 10^{28}
Kg (13 JU)".

We can see that celestial objects, such as asteroids, moons, and planets, lie on a specific fundamental plane of their own. In this paper, we are only concerned with redefining planets. Future research can be done for the fundamental asteroid and the fundamental moons plane.

Appendix A
Additional notes about stars

What is a star?
Everything that shines is not actually a star. Example: neutron stars, quark stars, and dark stars do not shine.

According to Merriam-Webster encyclopedia, a star is defined as "a self-luminous gaseous spheroidal celestial body of great mass which produces energy by means of nuclear fusion reactions."

However, we have neutron stars, white dwarfs, quark stars, and dark stars – objects which do not fuse hydrogen to produce energy, but they are nevertheless referred to as stars.

Neutron stars are the collapsed cores of massive stars that underwent a supernova explosion, which compressed the core to the density of atomic nuclei. They are supported against further gravitational collapse by neutron degeneracy pressure and repulsive nuclear forces.

White dwarfs are the remnant cores of stars not massive enough to undergo supernova explosion and become a neutron star or a black hole. They are mainly composed of carbon and oxygen, and are supported against further gravitational collapse by electron degeneracy pressure.

There is no nuclear fusion occurring within the material inside either white dwarfs or neutron stars. As a result, no heat is produced, and they cool down gradually over a period of time (
Glendenning 2000
).

Theoretically, it is believed that in the early universe, there existed dark stars – stars made of normal matter in combination with high amounts of dark matter. The dark matter particles in these stars undergo annihilation reactions to generate heat and prevent the normal matter from forming dense cores to trigger nuclear reactions like normal stars (
Spolyar, Freese & Gondolo 2008
). As clearly seen from the above descriptions, all dead stars are still defined as stars.
