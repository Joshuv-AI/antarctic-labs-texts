On a general identity and a resulting class of umbral operators
Kei Beauduin

Abstract
We prove a new universal identity for umbral operators. This motivates the definition of a subclass obeying a simplified identity, which we then fully characterize. The results are illustrated with common examples of the theory of umbral calculus.

3
A special class of umbral operators

In this section, we characterize the umbral operators satisfying the identity

\phi\text{x}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi}\text{x}^{k}U^{k}V^{n}\phi,

The first step is to characterize the functions satisfying a generalized form of Cauchy’s classical functional equation.

Lemma 2
.

Let
g
be an invertible formal power series of the form

g(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{k}}{k!}x^{k},\quad c_{1}\neq 0.

There exist formal power series
u,v
such that for all
x
and
y
,
g
satisfies the functional equation

g(x+y)=g(x)+u(x)g(v(x)y)

(15)

if and only if
g
has of one of the forms listed in
equation

16
below. In addition, we give the corresponding possible expressions for
u
and
v
:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}g(x)&u(x)&v(x)\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr\rule{0.0pt}{9.90276pt}ax&A(x)&A(x)^{-1}\\
\hline\cr\rule{0.0pt}{10.0pt}a(e^{bx}-1)&e^{bx}&1\\
\hline\cr a\log(1+bx)&1&(1+bx)^{-1}\\
\hline\cr a((1+bx)^{c}-1)&(1+bx)^{c}&(1+bx)^{-1}\end{array}

(16)

provided
a,b,c\neq 0
, and where
A
is any power series satisfying
A(0)\neq 0
.

Note that the set of solutions is closed under inversion, that is, if
g
is a solution of
equation

15

Proof
.

After differentiating

g^{\prime}(x)=u(x)v(x)c_{1},\qquad g^{\prime\prime}(x)=u(x)v(x)^{2}c_{2}.

If

g^{\prime\prime}(x)=0
and with the additional fact that

, we infer that
g(x)=c_{1}x
. For this function, the condition

is necessary to satisfy

15

c_{2}\neq 0
, we can solve for
u
and
v

u(x)=\left\lparen\dfrac{g^{\prime}(x)}{c_{1}}\right\rparen^{2}\dfrac{c_{2}}{g^{\prime\prime}(x)},\qquad v(x)=\dfrac{g^{\prime\prime}(x)}{c_{2}}\dfrac{c_{1}}{f^{\prime}(x)}.

Equation

15
can be rearranged, and by replacing
y
by
yg^{\prime}(x)/c_{1}
, we get

\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(x)}{c_{2}}\left\lparen g\left\lparen x+\frac{g^{\prime}(x)}{c_{1}}y\right\rparen-g(x)\right\rparen=\left\lparen\frac{g^{\prime}(x)}{c_{1}}\right\rparen^{2}f\left\lparen\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(x)}{c_{2}}y\right\rparen.

We now differentiate at

\frac{c_{3}}{c_{2}}g(y)+\left\lparen 1+\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}y\right\rparen g^{\prime}(y)-c_{1}=2\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}^{2}}g(y)+\frac{c_{3}}{c_{2}}yg^{\prime}(y).

Therefore, we only need to solve the linear differential equation in

(1+\alpha y)g^{\prime}(y)=\beta g(y)+c_{1},\quad g(0)=0,

where

\alpha:=\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}-\frac{c_{3}}{c_{2}},\qquad\beta:=2\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}^{2}}-\frac{c_{3}}{c_{2}}.

There are four cases, but the (linear) case

•

If

These cases are equivalent to that of
