Seronegative Granulomatous Myositis With Bulbar Weakness and Reversible Cardiomyopathy: A Case Report
Authors: Balachandran R. Vaidyanathan, Muhamed Abubacker, John Ponugupati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 64‐year‐old woman presented with seronegative idiopathic granulomatous myositis, featuring severe bulbar weakness, reversible cardiomyopathy (ejection fraction 20%–25%), hepatic injury, and acute kidney injury. Muscle biopsy confirmed granulomatous inflammation. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin resulted in multi‐organ recovery, highlighting the importance of early immunosuppressive therapy.

Introduction

Granulomatous myositis is an uncommon inflammatory muscle disorder characterized histologically by non‐caseating granulomatous inflammation within skeletal muscle [1]. It most commonly occurs in association with sarcoidosis, accounting for approximately half of reported cases, but may also arise in association with autoimmune diseases, inclusion body myositis, infections, or as an isolated idiopathic entity [1, 2, 3]. Granulomatous inflammation has been identified in approximately 0.4% of muscle biopsies in large series evaluating inflammatory myopathies, highlighting the rarity of this condition.

Clinical manifestations are heterogeneous and may include proximal muscle weakness, myalgias, fatigue, or asymptomatic elevation of muscle enzymes [3]. Bulbar involvement such as dysphagia and dysphonia is uncommon but may lead to aspiration and respiratory compromise [3, 4]. Cardiac involvement in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies has been reported in approximately 6%–9% of patients in clinical registries, although subclinical myocardial abnormalities may be detected in up to 50% of patients using advanced imaging such as cardiac magnetic resonance imaging [5].

Hepatic abnormalities are frequently observed in inflammatory myopathies and may reflect skeletal muscle injury rather than primary hepatic disease because aminotransferases are released from injured muscle fibers [6]. Renal involvement occurs in approximately 10%–23% of inflammatory myopathy cases and is most commonly related to myoglobinuria‐induced acute tubular necrosis [7].

We report a case of seronegative idiopathic granulomatous myositis presenting with severe bulbar weakness, reversible myocardial dysfunction, hepatic injury, and acute kidney injury with a favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy.

Case History/Examination

A 64‐year‐old woman with Hashimoto thyroiditis and obstructive sleep apnea presented with 1 month of progressive dysphagia, hoarseness, and generalized weakness. Her symptoms began with difficulty swallowing solids and progressed to inability to swallow pills or liquids without gagging. She also reported increasing fatigue, joint pain, and progressive difficulty raising her arms or rising from a seated position. She also reported occasional palpitations and mild shortness of breath but denied chest pain. Home medications included aspirin, bupropion, cetirizine, colestipol, cyclobenzaprine, famotidine, fexofenadine, meloxicam, nebivolol, pantoprazole, vitamin C, vitamin D3, topical tacrolimus, and topical nystatin. Notably, she had no history of statin exposure; colestipol was her only lipid‐lowering therapy. Approximately 1 week before admission, she had been hospitalized for left upper extremity cellulitis and was treated with ceftriaxone, doxycycline, linezolid, and cefepime.

Physical Examination Findings Are Described Below

Neurologic examination demonstrated proximal muscle weakness graded 4/5 in the upper extremities and 3/5 in the lower extremities. Hoarseness with severe hypophonia and decreased salivary clearance were present. Cranial nerve examination demonstrated intact extraocular movements, facial sensation, hearing, symmetric palate elevation, preserved shoulder shrug, and a midline tongue without facial weakness. Deep tendon reflexes were 1+ and symmetric, with intact sensation and coordination. Weakness was predominantly proximal without distal or finger flexor predominance. No cutaneous findings of dermatomyositis were observed, including absence of heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, or mechanic's hands. There was no history or examination finding suggestive of Raynaud phenomenon.

Cardiovascular examination revealed a regular rhythm without murmur, gallop, or rub. There was no jugular venous distension and no peripheral edema. Telemetry demonstrated sinus rhythm. Electrocardiography showed low voltage and a possible prior anterior infarct pattern without acute ischemic changes. Pulmonary examination revealed slightly diminished breath sounds at the bases without significant respiratory distress at presentation.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included inflammatory myopathies (polymyositis, dermatomyositis, inclusion body myositis, and immune‐mediated necrotizing myopathy), sarcoidosis‐associated myopathy, infectious myositis, drug‐induced myopathy, and neuromuscular junction disorders such as myasthenia gravis. Although linezolid, administered during the preceding hospitalization for cellulitis, has been associated with mitochondrial myotoxicity, the granulomatous histology on biopsy was inconsistent with drug‐induced myopathy. Immune‐mediated necrotizing myopathy was considered because of the rapid onset of weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase. However, the absence of statin exposure, negative anti‐HMGCR and anti‐SRP antibodies, and muscle biopsy demonstrating granulomatous inflammation without necrotizing features argued strongly against this diagnosis. The absence of characteristic dermatologic findings, negative serologies, lack of rimmed vacuoles on biopsy, and favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy helped narrow the diagnosis toward idiopathic granulomatous myositis.

Investigations

Laboratory testing revealed markedly elevated muscle enzymes with creatine kinase peaking at 2213 U/L and aldolase 89 U/L. Inflammatory markers were elevated with C‐reactive protein 31.3 mg/L and erythrocyte sedimentation rate 49 mm/h. Following initiation of intravenous methylprednisolone and intravenous immunoglobulin, serial creatine kinase measurements demonstrated progressive improvement (2213 → 2068 → 1298 → 174 U/L), paralleling clinical recovery. Detailed serial laboratory trends are provided in Tables S1–S3.

Cardiac biomarkers were markedly abnormal. Serial troponin T measurements increased from 2741 to a peak of 5193 ng/L despite minimal non‐obstructive coronary artery disease on coronary angiography. N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) was elevated at 5398 pg/mL. Initial echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 65%. Repeat echocardiography on hospital day 8 revealed severe systolic dysfunction with an ejection fraction of 20%–25%. Serial echocardiographic findings are detailed in Table S5.

Severe hepatic injury developed during hospitalization with alanine aminotransferase peaking at 4441 U/L and aspartate aminotransferase exceeding 7000 U/L. Lactate dehydrogenase exceeded 2500 U/L. Serial laboratory values demonstrated gradual improvement following immunosuppressive therapy.

Acute kidney injury also occurred with creatinine rising to 2.68 mg/dL, requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. Renal function subsequently normalized.

Comprehensive serologic testing for inflammatory myopathies was negative, including myositis‐specific antibodies (anti‐Jo‐1, anti‐Mi‐2, anti‐MDA5, anti‐NXP2, anti‐TIF1‐γ, anti‐SAE), antisynthetase antibodies, anti‐HMGCR antibodies, anti‐signal recognition particle antibodies, and myasthenia gravis antibodies. Myositis‐specific antibodies were analyzed using a commercial line immunoblot assay (Quest Diagnostics Nichols Institute), while anti‐HMGCR antibody testing was performed separately by Quest Diagnostics Nichols Institute. All results were negative. Angiotensin‐converting enzyme level was normal. A comprehensive summary of serologic evaluation is provided in Table S4.

Computed tomography demonstrated multifocal pneumonia, cardiomegaly, and moderate pericardial effusion without mediastinal lymphadenopathy. The pneumonia was treated empirically with ceftriaxone and doxycycline, followed by cefepime to complete a 14‐day course. Blood cultures, respiratory pathogen PCR, COVID‐19 testing, Legionella urinary antigen, and Streptococcus pneumoniae urinary antigen were all negative. Abdominal imaging revealed hepatic steatosis and hepatomegaly. Electromyography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging were not available at our facility.

Left quadriceps muscle biopsy performed on hospital day 7 demonstrated focal granulomatous inflammation with scattered eosinophils and rare multinucleated giant cells (Figure 1). Immunohistochemistry revealed patchy endomysial T‐cell infiltration with sarcolemmal HLA‐DR activity. Rimmed vacuoles and sarcoplasmic inclusions were absent. Acid‐fast bacilli and fungal stains were negative. Detailed histopathologic and immunohistochemical findings are summarized in Table S9.

The patient subsequently developed worsening bulbar weakness and respiratory compromise requiring intensive care monitoring and noninvasive ventilatory support with BiPAP.

Treatment

Treatment was initiated with intravenous methylprednisolone 500 mg daily for 3 days followed by maintenance corticosteroids. Intravenous immunoglobulin (2 g/kg) was administered over 3 days. The patient demonstrated progressive biochemical improvement following treatment, with creatine kinase declining from 2213 U/L to 174 U/L, accompanied by improvement in dysphagia, dysphonia, and muscle strength.

Hepatic enzymes normalized over 3 weeks and renal function recovered. The patient was discharged on a prednisone taper, mycophenolate mofetil 2 g daily, and monthly intravenous immunoglobulin. Additional details regarding treatment timeline and response are provided in Table S6.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Follow‐up echocardiography demonstrated improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction from 20%–25% to 45%–50%. At the three‐month follow‐up, the patient had complete recovery of dysphonia and partial recovery of proximal muscle strength with ongoing rehabilitation. Discussion

This case highlights several notable features of idiopathic granulomatous myositis. First, the patient presented with prominent bulbar symptoms progressing to respiratory compromise. Dysphagia has been reported in granulomatous myositis but is more commonly associated with inclusion body myositis overlap [2, 3]. In this case, the absence of rimmed vacuoles on biopsy and the favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy argue against inclusion body myositis.

In this case, several features argued strongly against inclusion body myositis. IBM typically presents with insidious onset over at least 12 months, asymmetric weakness predominantly affecting deep finger flexors and quadriceps, creatine kinase levels ≤ 15 times the upper limit of normal, and refractoriness to immunosuppressive therapy. In contrast, this patient demonstrated rapid symptom progression over approximately 4 weeks, predominantly proximal weakness without finger flexor involvement, markedly elevated creatine kinase, absence of rimmed vacuoles on biopsy, and an excellent response to corticosteroids and IVIG. In large granulomatous myositis cohorts, patients with concurrent IBM exhibited treatment refractoriness and more severe asymmetric proximo‐distal weakness, unlike the favorable outcomes observed in isolated idiopathic granulomatous myositis.

Immune‐mediated necrotizing myopathy was also considered because of the acute onset of weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase. However, the absence of statin exposure, negative anti‐HMGCR and anti‐SRP antibodies, and lack of necrotizing features on muscle biopsy made this diagnosis unlikely.

Second, the patient developed reversible myocardial dysfunction characterized by markedly elevated troponin T levels (peak 5193 ng/L) and severe reduction in ejection fraction without obstructive coronary artery disease. Cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies is increasingly recognized, and cardiac MRI studies have identified myocardial abnormalities in approximately 47% of newly diagnosed patients [5]. Recovery of cardiac function following immunosuppressive therapy supports an inflammatory mechanism. Notably, troponin T is less specific for cardiac injury than troponin I in the setting of inflammatory myopathies, as regenerating skeletal muscle may re‐express troponin T. However, the combination of markedly elevated troponin T, NT‐proBNP of 5398 pg/mL, severe echocardiographic decline, and non‐obstructive coronary disease on angiography collectively support true myocardial involvement.

Third, the patient experienced severe hepatic injury and acute kidney injury requiring dialysis. Although aminotransferase elevation in inflammatory myopathies may reflect skeletal muscle injury, the magnitude of transaminase elevation suggests concurrent hepatic involvement [6]. Normalization of hepatic and renal function following treatment supports a systemic inflammatory process. Multi‐organ involvement in this case is further contextualized with existing literature in Table S8.

Diagnosis was challenging due to negative serologic testing for myositis‐specific antibodies and normal ACE levels. Muscle biopsy therefore played a critical role in establishing the diagnosis [1].

Corticosteroids remain the primary treatment for granulomatous myositis, with approximately 75% of patients demonstrating improvement, although relapse rates may approach 60% [4]. IVIG may provide benefit in severe disease [8]. A comparison with previously reported granulomatous myositis cohorts is provided in Table S7.

This report has several limitations. As a single case, the findings may not be generalizable. Electromyography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging were not available at our facility; these modalities would have provided additional characterization of myopathic changes and myocardial involvement, respectively. Additionally, troponin I was not measured; given its higher specificity for cardiac injury compared with troponin T in the setting of active myositis, this represents a limitation in confirming the degree of myocardial involvement. Cardiac MRI studies have identified myocardial abnormalities in approximately 47% of newly diagnosed inflammatory myopathy patients [5]. The follow‐up period of 3 months is relatively short, particularly given reported relapse rates of up to 60% in granulomatous myositis [4]. Longer‐term follow‐up will be important to assess for disease relapse and durability of cardiac recovery.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates a rare presentation of seronegative idiopathic granulomatous myositis with severe bulbar involvement, reversible myocardial dysfunction, hepatic injury, and acute kidney injury. Early recognition and prompt immunosuppressive therapy may lead to substantial recovery across multiple organ systems.
