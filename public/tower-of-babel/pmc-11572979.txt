A conserved juxtamembrane motif in plant NFR5 receptors is essential for root nodule symbiosis
Authors: Simon Boje Hansen, Thi Bich Luu, Kira Gysel, Damiano Lironi, Christina Krönauer, Henriette Rübsam, Ingeborg Bitsch Jensen, Magdalini Tsitsikli, Thea Gramkov Birkefeldt, Alen Trgovcevic, Jens Stougaard, Simona Radutoiu, Kasper Røjkjær Andersen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnderstanding the molecular mechanisms that enable nitrogen-fixing symbiosis is crucial for ongoing efforts to engineer this trait into nonlegume crops. The plant LysM receptor kinase NFR5 is required for perception of bacterial Nod factor and facilitates signal transduction leading to root nodule symbiosis in legumes. Here, we describe the structure and function of the Lotus NFR5 intracellular part and provide unique insights into the sparsely explored area of pseudokinase biology by identifying a conserved juxtamembrane αA and αA′ motif that mediates protein–protein interaction and is important for the signaling function of NFR5.

Nod Factor Receptor 5 (NFR5) is required for the perception of rhizobial Nod factors and the subsequent initiation of root nodule symbiosis between legumes and nitrogen-fixing bacteria (1–4). Lotus japonicus (hereafter Lotus) NFR5 contains an extracellular domain of three lysin motifs (LysM), which selectively binds the Nod factor produced by the symbiont of Lotus, Mesorhizobium loti (3, 5–7). NFR5 is embedded in the plant plasma membrane via a single transmembrane helix and intracellularly comprises an N-terminal juxtamembrane region followed by a protein kinase domain and a C-terminal tail (2). The NFR5 kinase domain contains degenerated kinase motifs (e.g., degenerated glycine-rich loop, activation loop, and DFG motif) and is consequently a catalytically inactive pseudokinase (2, 8, 9). In contrast, the receptor partner NFR1 contains a conventional protein kinase domain, and NFR1 phosphorylation activity is required for symbiotic signaling (1, 8). Despite its lack of catalytic activity, the pseudokinase domain of NFR5 is required for establishing root nodule symbiosis, but the molecular mechanism of NFR5 signaling is unknown (10). Kinase phosphorylation signaling is well described in biology, while the signaling mechanisms for catalytically inactive pseudokinases are poorly understood, despite being ubiquitous throughout eukaryotes (11). Generally, receptor kinases form homo- and/or heterocomplexes that are important for their signaling function (12, 13). In Arabidopsis thaliana, these include the leucine-rich repeat (LRR) receptor kinases BRI1, SUB, FLS2, EFR, BIR2, and BAK1 as well as LysM receptor kinases CERK1, LYK4, and LYK5 (14–26). Likewise, an NFR1–NFR5 heterocomplex is required for nodulation in Lotus, while the detailed mechanisms for complex assembly and complex composition are still unclear (4). For receptor pseudokinases, complex formation and signaling have in some cases been linked to conformational toggling of the αC helix, catalytic spine, and regulatory spine that resemble small G-protein signaling, while other pseudokinases mediate protein–protein interactions through flexible regulatory tail regions (27–32). NFR5-type receptors are prevalent throughout plants, and several studies have demonstrated the functional importance of this class of receptors in establishing symbioses between plants and microbes (2, 33–36). Investigating NFR5 complex formation and signaling can inform on how plant receptor kinases work to initiate cellular programs for symbiotic interactions. In this study, we provide insights into NFR5-type receptor signaling mechanisms. We present the crystal structure of the Lotus NFR5 intracellular domain that comprises the core kinase and two juxtamembrane α-helices. We find that NFR5 homo-oligomerizes and that an exposed hydrophobic motif in the juxtamembrane α-helices promotes the assembly of NFR5–NFR5 and NFR1–NFR5 complexes. Our analysis reveals that this motif is conserved throughout NFR5-type receptors and that it is essential for symbiosis signaling in both Lotus NFR5 and barley RLK10.

Results

A Nanobody Facilitates Structure Determination of the NFR5 Intracellular Domain.

Previously, NFR5 has been reported to contain a catalytically inactive pseudokinase, as opposed to NFR1, which contains a catalytically active protein kinase (8). This was validated in a radiolabeling phosphorylation assay, using pure and stable Escherichia coli expressed protein, confirming that NFR5 is catalytically inactive (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Despite its lack of catalytic activity, previous reports found that NFR5 signaling requires the kinase domain (10, 37). We tested the ability of an NFR5 receptor lacking the kinase domain to drive M. loti nodulation when expressed from the native Nfr5 promoter and terminator in Agrobacterium rhizogenes–induced roots in a Lotus nfr5 mutant plants. Our results confirm that the NFR5 kinase core domain is essential for nodule formation in Lotus (Fig. 1B). To understand the signaling mechanisms of NFR5, we set out to determine the structure of the kinase domain. Production of well-diffracting crystals failed despite exhaustive attempts using various constructs of NFR5. To facilitate crystallization, we sought alternative strategies and immunized a llama with a recombinant NFR5 kinase protein to obtain a nanobody crystallization chaperone. Using phage-display selection and biolayer interferometry, the nanobody Nb200 was characterized as a high-affinity NFR5 binder with an equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) in the nanomolar range (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–D). A stable complex between Nb200 and a NFR5 variant (residues 276–563), containing the juxtamembrane and pseudokinase but lacking the last 32 residues in the predicted flexible C-terminal tail, was isolated by size-exclusion chromatography, and crystallized (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). A diffraction dataset was collected to 3.0 Å resolution (anisotropically extended to 2.29 Å), and the nanobody-bound structure of the NFR5 intracellular domain was determined (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2F andTable S1). All secondary structural elements of the pseudokinase are resolved in the electron density map, including the αB helix and the gatekeeper tyrosine residue hallmarks of IRAK/Pelle-type kinases (Fig. 1 D and E) (38, 39). Nb200 contacts the αG helix of NFR5, so we assayed whether Nb200 could interfere with NFR5 signaling by blocking this structural element (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 F and G). Overexpression of Nb200 in wild-type Lotus did not affect nodulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S2H), and assembling an artificial NFR1–NFR5 complex by fusion of Nb200 to the C terminus of NFR1 led to spontaneous nodule formation independent of M. loti inoculation, demonstrating that Nb200 does not inhibit signaling (Fig. 1F) (4). In a recent study, we showed an essential function for the C-terminal tail of Lotus SYMRK in root nodule symbiosis signaling (40), and we therefore tested whether the C-terminal tail of NFR5 plays a similar role. However, an NFR5 construct lacking the C-terminal tail complements Lotus nfr5 mutants with nodule numbers comparable to wild-type (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). We therefore conclude that our nanobody-bound structure represents the signaling-competent intracellular domain of NFR5.

The degenerated glycine-rich loop is among the features that classify NFR5 as a pseudokinase. Despite this, an ATP molecule can be docked into the ATP binding pocket with no steric clashes and can thereby assemble the catalytic spine (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Interestingly, the regulatory spine of NFR5 is intact and fully formed along with the hallmark K339-E349 salt bridge and the αC helix “in” conformation, mimicking an activated conformational state in conventional protein kinases (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Signaling by conformational toggling of the αC, C- and R-spines was investigated by mutating the catalytic site, K339-E349 salt bridge, ATP binding pocket, and R-spine. All NFR5 variants were fully capable of driving nodulation in Lotus nfr5 comparable to wild-type NFR5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This suggests that conventional protein kinase motifs and conformational toggling of the αC helix, C-spine, and R-spine are unlikely to regulate NFR5 signaling.

Two Juxtamembrane α-Helices Define a Conserved Hydrophobic Motif in NFR5-Type Receptors.

Two NFR5 molecules constitute the asymmetric unit of the crystal, and the pseudokinase core spanning residues 300–563 was modeled in both cases (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). However, one molecule (chain A) is better resolved in the electron density map and revealed an ordered structure at the N terminus of the kinase core. This corresponds to the juxtamembrane region of NFR5 that connects the kinase domain to the transmembrane helix (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). The juxtamembrane region appears to be a separate structural segment distinct from the kinase core with only a few intramolecular contacts to the αC helix. The juxtamembrane region forms two clearly resolved α-helices that we name αA and αA′ (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). The αA and αA′ helices are connected by a small serine-glycine linker that bends the two helices by approximately 65° relative to each other. Notably, αA and αA′ each contain a cluster of exposed hydrophobic residues (L290 and L291) and (V294, Y297, and V298), respectively (Fig. 2C). Surface mapping of the electrostatic potential revealed that these residues form a spatially continuous hydrophobic surface (Fig. 2D). Alignment of NFR5-type receptors from different plant species shows a striking conservation in sequence and hydrophobicity in this area, suggesting a functional role for this region, which we name the “juxtamembrane motif” (Fig. 2 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). This is further supported by previous extensive phylogenetic analyses showing conservation of the juxtamembrane motif within the whole NFR5 containing LYRI-A group, underscoring its potential functional importance for this family of receptors (42, 43).

The Juxtamembrane Motif Promotes NFR5-NFR5 and NFR1-NFR5 Interactions.

We hypothesized that the exposed juxtamembrane motif could function as a hydrophobic protein–protein interaction surface. Purified NFR5 (residues 276–595) migrates to the expected molecular mass in a denaturing SDS-PAGE experiment but shows a characteristic oligomeric migration pattern when assayed in parallel with native PAGE (Fig. 3 A and B). The NFR5 pattern resembles the pattern formed by the well-known oligomer forms of bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Fig. 3B). Using dynamic light scattering (DLS), the size dispersity of in-solution NFR5 particles was measured. A polydispersity index (PDI) >0.2 and distinct particle populations larger than monomers were consistently observed, showing that populations of different particle sizes are present in the sample (Fig. 3C). This shows that the NFR5 intracellular domain forms oligomers in vitro. To investigate whether the NFR5–NFR5 interaction is mediated by the juxtamembrane motif, we generated two variants with substitutions in either αA (L290E and L291E) or αA′ (V294E, Y297E, and V298E), referred to as 2E and 3E, respectively (Fig. 3A). We reasoned that the introduction of charged glutamates would abolish possible hydrophobic interactions formed by the motif. Conversely, NFR5 2E and 3E appear to be monomeric with a minor dimeric population and do not show the characteristic oligomer pattern observed for NFR5 WT in native PAGE (Fig. 3B). A second DLS experiment was performed to compare the in-solution self-interaction of NFR5 WT with 2E and 3E by measuring their self-interaction parameter (kSI) over a protein concentration series (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–C). A negative kSI and concentration-dependent self-interaction were observed for all samples. Notably, the kSI for NFR5 WT showed the greatest negative value, indicating a larger degree of self-interaction compared to 2E and 3E. Next, we purified N-terminally Avi-tagged NFR5 and Avi-tagged NFR1 to immobilize the intracellular domains on streptavidin biosensors (Fig. 3 D–H and SI Appendix, Figs. S2A and S6D). The immobilized NFR5 or NFR1 was used in a biolayer interferometry experiment to measure interaction with NFR5 WT, 2E or 3E variants (Fig. 3 D–K). A clear concentration-dependent interaction with NFR5 WT was observed for both immobilized NFR5 and NFR1, while no interaction was detected for 2E or 3E. In summary, our in vitro data demonstrate that the NFR5 intracellular domain engages in NFR5–NFR5 and NFR1–NFR5 interactions and that the juxtamembrane motif promotes these interactions.

The Juxtamembrane Motif in NFR5-Type Receptors Is Required for Symbiosis Signaling.

To understand whether the juxtamembrane motif is functionally important for symbiosis signaling, we performed nodulation complementation experiments in Lotus nfr5 mutant plants inoculated with M. loti. Nodulation capability of full-length NFR5 WT, 2E, and 3E was assayed on A. rhizogenes–induced hairy roots, using the native Nfr5 promoter and terminator (Fig. 4 A and B). As expected, no nodules were observed on roots transformed with a vector containing only a triple YFP transformation marker (negative control), while roots transformed with NFR5 WT complement the nfr5 phenotype and form nodules (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Interestingly, both NFR5 2E and 3E failed to form any nodules in a total of 68 plants (Fig. 4B). Receptor constructs were expressed and localized as expected to the plasma membrane in transformed Lotus root protoplasts (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Together with the high yield of soluble protein from E. coli expressions (Fig. 3B), the 2E and 3E substitutions appear not to compromise protein stability. As an additional control experiment, we generated NFR5 L290F, L291F, and NFR5 Y297I versions to test whether the specific residues or simply maintaining the hydrophobicity of the motif is important for signaling. Both these variants complemented to wild-type levels, suggesting that the hydrophobicity of the motif is the essential feature for signaling (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). S292 and S295 were previously identified as NFR1 phosphorylation sites on NFR5 and are positioned adjacent to the juxtamembrane motif in the linker between αA and αA′ [Fig. 2C, (8)]. Madsen and colleagues did not observe any effect on nodulation in the single phosphorylation ablation mutants S292A and S295A (8). Double ablation (S292A/S295A) and double phosphorylation mimic mutations (S292D/S295D) of these residues did not significantly affect nodulation compared to wild-type NFR5, indicating that the signaling function of the juxtamembrane motif is likely not regulated by S292 and S295 phosphorylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The conservation of the juxtamembrane motif suggests that other NFR5-type receptors may require it for signaling as well [Fig. 2 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 (42, 43)]. We previously discovered that the barley NFR5-type receptor, RLK10, does not restore nodulation in Lotus nfr5, but can induce nodule organogenesis when forced into a complex with NFR1 using nanobodies (4). This suggests that the RLK10 intracellular domain has the capacity to mediate symbiosis signaling in Lotus. We speculated that the inability of full-length RLK10 to drive nodulation in Lotus nfr5 could be due to its inability to perceive the specific M. loti Nod factor required to initiate symbiosis signaling. We therefore created a chimeric receptor composed of the NFR5 extracellular and transmembrane segments coupled to the intracellular domain of RLK10 (Fig. 4A). This chimeric receptor can drive nodulation in Lotus nfr5 to a degree comparable to wild-type NFR5, confirming that the intracellular domain of RLK10 is competent in symbiosis signaling (Fig. 4B). To test the signaling role of the juxtamembrane motif in RLK10, we created a 2E and 3E variant of the NFR5-RLK10 chimera, substituting L322E/L323E and V326E/F329E/I330E, respectively. In a total of 82 plants, we observed no nodules for the RLK10 2E variant and only a single nodulating plant for the RLK10 3E variant, underlining the important signaling function for the juxtamembrane motif in barley RLK10 (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

Discussion

In this study, we present the crystal structure of the Lotus NFR5 intracellular domain, a result facilitated by using a nanobody as a crystallization chaperone. Through NFR5 mutant complementation, Nb200-mediated NFR1–NFR5 complex formation, and Nb200 overexpression, we show that our experimental structure represents a signaling-competent NFR5. The structure reveals two α-helices in the NFR5 juxtamembrane region that we named αA and αA′ based on the established nomenclature from the IRAK/Pelle-type kinases (38, 39). Although the αA and αA′ were ordered in one NFR5 molecule (chain A), they were not resolved in the other (chain B), suggesting the juxtamembrane region is dynamic (31). Intriguingly, the two α-helices form an exposed hydrophobic surface that is accessible for protein–protein interaction. It is noteworthy that the human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor constitutively homodimerizes through a juxtamembrane α-helix that contains a hydrophobic motif with striking resemblance to the NFR5 juxtamembrane motif (44). We similarly observe NFR5–NFR5 interaction and a clear contribution from the juxtamembrane motif in mediating homo- and NFR1 heterocomplex assemblies. We therefore find it likely that the phenotypic effect in our 2E and 3E variants is due to the disruption of an NFR5–NFR5 complex required for NFR1 interaction and symbiotic signaling (Fig. 4C). However, our findings do not exclude a broader scaffolding function for NFR5 and the juxtamembrane motif in mediating protein–protein interactions to other components of the symbiotic signaling pathway. It was previously shown that symbiosis relies on slow on/off rate binding kinetics to enable NFR5-mediated kinetic proofreading of Nod factors (7). In this study, we observe slow on/off kinetics for both NFR5–NFR5 and NFR1–NFR5 intracellular interactions, suggesting that receptor complex stability is a distinct biophysical feature important for symbiotic signaling.

Our in planta analyses show that the juxtamembrane motif is important but not sufficient for signaling, as the pseudokinase core is also required for nodulation (Fig. 1B). We find that signaling from the pseudokinase core is not dependent on kinase motifs normally required for phosphorylation activity, nucleotide binding, or R-spine formation. Similarly, A. thaliana EFR does not require phosphorylation activity for immune signaling, but rather forms a ligand-induced complex and allosterically activates BAK1, which in turn drives phosphorylation (30, 45). Based on this we suspect one or several surface-exposed sites within the NFR5 pseudokinase core are important for protein–protein interactions that mediate symbiosis signaling. Inspection of the crystal packing revealed that part of the juxtamembrane motif contacts the C-lobe αH and αI of a symmetry-related NFR5 molecule (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). The αA L290 and L291 primarily mediate this contact, with αA′ V294 and Y297 contributing to a lesser degree and V298 making no contact. This crystal packing between two NFR5 molecules generates a PISA complexation significance score = 0.89, suggesting this assembly could form in solution (for reference NFR5-Nb200 has a CSS = 1). Even so, the C-lobe surface does not share the same hydrophobic characteristics or high conservation as the juxtamembrane motif, suggesting this is solely a crystal contact (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Mutagenesis of the relevant hydrophobic residues L537 and L562, contacted by L290 and L291, would likely destabilize the NFR5 pseudokinase core. We have not been able to resolve the potential function of this C-lobe surface, and future investigations can hopefully apply alternative approaches to investigate whether it is important for NFR5 signaling.

We previously showed that a nanobody-induced complex between barley RLK10 and Lotus NFR1 or the NFR1-type barley receptor RLK4 is sufficient to induce nodule organogenesis in Lotus (4). Here, we further build upon our dissection of the RLK10 receptor as a promising target for nitrogen-fixation engineering in cereals. Ligand perception and signaling modulation have previously been achieved through chimeric protein engineering of homologous legume NFR1- and NFR5-type LysM receptor kinases (6, 7). Our investigations using chimeric receptors show that equipping the nonlegume receptor RLK10 with the extracellular domain of NFR5 is sufficient to drive nodulation in Lotus (Fig. 4B). This underlines a general capability to engineer plant LysM receptor kinases to perceive specific rhizobial Nod factors and induce symbiosis signaling. Furthermore, these findings validate our earlier observation that the RLK10 intracellular domain is fully capable of symbiosis signaling in Lotus (4). Importantly, the finding that the juxtamembrane motif is conserved and functional in nonlegume receptors supports a more ancient function for this interaction motif in the common symbiotic signaling pathway. This study focuses on NFR5 in root nodule symbiosis, but we speculate that the juxtamembrane motif in NFR5-type receptors could be important for arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis as well.

Materials and Methods

Protein Expression and Purification.

NFR5 and NFR1 expression constructs were cloned into the pET32 Ek/LIC vector backbone containing an N-terminal thioredoxin and 6x Histidine tag as described in ref. 46. A tobacco etch virus protease (TEV) cleavage site was introduced to allow fusion-tag removal. Nb200 was cloned into the pET-22b(+) vector containing a C-terminal 6x Histidine tag (SI Appendix, Table S2). Rosetta II E. coli were used for NFR5 and NFR1 expression, and LOBSTR (47) E. coli were used for Nb200 expression. E. coli was heat-shock transformed and cultured in lysogeny broth (LB) supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin (AMP) and 50 µg/mL chloramphenicol (CAM) at 37 °C. At OD600 = 0.6, protein expression was induced by addition of isopropylthio-β-galactoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.4 mM, and cultures were incubated overnight at 18 °C, 180 RPM. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 5,000×g, 4 °C for 15 min. Cell pellets were suspended in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 10% v/v glycerol, and 1 mM benzamidine (lysis buffer). E. coli suspension was lysed by sonication using a Labosonic M sonicator (Sartorius). Lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 16,000×g, 4 °C for 30 min. The supernatant was collected and applied to a Protino Ni-NTA column (Macherey-Nagel) using an ÄKTA Start system (Cytiva). After sample application, the column was washed in lysis buffer until UV280 baseline, and protein was eluted in 5 column volumes of 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 5% glycerol (buffer B). The protein sample was dialyzed overnight at 4 °C against 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol (dialysis buffer) using a 14 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) dialysis membrane (Spectra/Por). A 3.5 kDa MWCO dialysis membrane was used for Nb200. Histidine-tagged TEV protease was added into the dialysis bag containing NFR5 or NFR1 constructs in a 1:50 molar ratio. A second Ni-AC purification was performed to remove TEV protease and cleaved fusion-tag. Protein purifications were finalized by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) on an ÄKTA Pure FPLC system using a HiLoad Superdex 75 16/600 pg column (Cytiva). Proteins were eluted in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 200 mM NaCl, and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol (SEC buffer). All purification steps were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and relevant fractions were pooled to a final purity of >95% estimated by Coomassie staining (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Nanobody Selection by Phage Display.

One Lama glama was immunized with 400 μg NFR5 (residues 276–595), and a blood sample was retrieved after 8 wk. Peripheral blood lymphocytes were isolated by Ficoll gradient centrifugation, and total RNA was purified using an Rnase Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was amplified using random hexamer primers and a SuperScript III First-Strand Kit (Invitrogen). The VHH/nanobody regions were amplified by PCR and cloned into phagemid vectors as E-tag pIII fusions. E. coli ER2738 were transformed with nanobody-inserted phagemid, and M13 phage-display libraries were generated using VCSM13 helper phages. 96-well Nunc Maxisorp Immuno-Microplates (ThermoScientific) were coated with 1 μg NFR5 overnight at 4 °C and blocked by addition of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 2% w/v BSA for 1 h at room temperature. 3 × 1012 phages were blocked in PBS, 2% w/v BSA for 1 h before applying to NFR5-coated wells for an additional 1 h. Wells were washed 15 times in PBS supplemented with 0.1% Tween 20 (PBS-T), followed by 15 washes in PBS. Phages were eluted by addition of 100 μL 0.2 M glycine pH 2.2 and incubated on a shaking table at room temperature for 10 min. Elutions were pH adjusted by addition of 15 μL 1 M Tris-HCl pH 9.1 and used for infecting 0.8 mL ER2738 OD600 = 0.6. The phage library was amplified in ER2738 and used in a second selection round against 0.1 μg NFR5. After the second selection round, infected ER2738 were plated on LB agar (LA), supplemented with 2% w/v glucose, 100 μg/mL AMP, and 10 μg/mL tetracyclin (TET), and grown overnight at 37 °C. Single colonies were picked and inoculated into a 96-well plate filled with LB supplemented with 100 μg/mL AMP and 10 μg/mL TET. The E. coli 96-well plate was covered with an airpore sheet (Qiagen) and incubated for 6 h, 37 °C, 95 RPM, at high humidity before addition of IPTG to 0.8 mM final concentration. E-tagged nanobodies were expressed overnight at 30 °C, 95 RPM. Nanobody binding was screened in an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using 96-well Maxisorp plates coated and subsequently blocked with 0.1 μg NFR5 and PBS, 2% w/v BSA as described above. The E. coli 96-well plate was centrifugated at 1,000×g for 15 min, and 50 μL supernatant containing expressed E-tagged nanobody was transferred to the coated ELISA plate. After 1 h at room temperature, the ELISA wells were washed six times in PBS-T. A secondary anti-E-tag antibody coupled to horseradish peroxidase (Bethyl) was diluted 10,000-fold in PBS, 2% w/v BSA. Diluted anti-E-antibody (100 μL) was added to each ELISA well for 1 h at room temperature. Wells were washed 3 times in PBS-T, and the ELISA plate was developed by addition of 100 μL 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (ThermoFisher). Reactions were quenched with 100 μL 1 M HCl, and ELISA plates were read at 450 nm using a Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (ThermoScientific). Positive clones were propagated in 5 mL LB supplemented with 100 μg/mL AMP, and phagemid DNA was purified and sequenced. Nanobody candidates were selected based on phylogenetic alignment analysis in CLC workbench 20.0.4 (Qiagen) using neighbor joining based on Jukes–Cantor distances.

Crystallization and Structure Determination.

Purified NFR5 (residues 276–563) and Nb200 were incubated on ice in a 1:1.5 molar ratio for 30 min before the complex was isolated by SEC as described above, using a Superdex 75 increase 10/300 column (Cytiva). Crystals were obtained by sitting drop vapor diffusion in 24-well plates (Molecular Dimensions) in a condition containing 0.1 M sodium cacodylate pH 6.5, 1.2 M sodium acetate. Droplets were manually pipetted 1 + 1 µL sample/reservoir at room temperature, and plates were subsequently stored at 19 °C. Crystals were manually retrieved using Litholoop-CryoCaps (SPINE format, Molecular Dimensions) attached on a Magnetic Cryo Wand (Molecular Dimensions) and cryoprotected by step soaking in reservoir solution supplemented 20% w/v PEG-200 final concentration. Crystals were flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen. X-ray diffraction experiments were performed at BioMAX (MAX IV, Lund Sweden) under cryogenic conditions (48). A native dataset was collected at λ = 0.92 Å using an Eiger 16 M detector (Dectris). Diffraction data were processed in xds and xscale (49). The NFR5-Nb200 dataset was analyzed in xtriage from the PHENIX program suite (50) and was due to high anisotropy elliptically truncated using the STARANISO Server [Global Phasing Ltd. (50)] to define an ellipsoid diffraction surface (CC(1/2) ≥ 0.3, I/sigI ≥ 2.0, Rpim ≤ 0.6). The crystallographic phase problem was solved by molecular replacement in Phaser (51), using the A. thaliana BAK1 protein kinase structure (PDB-ID:3UIM) as a search model (52). Data refinement was performed using phenix.refine, and the atomic model was built in Coot (53). The model was validated using MolProbity (54) and the wwPDB Validation Server (validate-rcsb-1.wwpdb.org). Data collection statistics for both ellipsoidal and spherical datasets and refinement statistics are reported in SI Appendix, Table S1. Structural analyses and figures were done using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, version 2.4 Schrödinger, LLC. ATP from Protein Kinase A [PDB-ID: 1ATP (41)] was docked into the NFR5 ATP binding pocket by superposition of PKA onto NFR5. Electrostatic surface potentials were calculated and visualized in PyMOL using the PDB2PQR and APBS Electrostatics applications with default settings; ±5 kT/e, k = Boltzmann constant, T = temperature in Kelvin, e = elementary charge (55). Conservation scores were exported from the alignment in SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and surface visualized using the b-factor column. Coordinates and structure factors for the NFR5-Nb200 structure were deposited in the PDB under ID: 8S79 (56).

Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis Assays.

Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was performed using homemade gels and run on a Mini-PROTEAN tetra cell system (Bio-Rad). Discontinuous gels were prepared using a 12% acrylamide, Tris-HCl pH 8.8 separation gel solution, 5% acrylamide, Tris-HCl pH 6.8 stacking gel solution and supplemented or without 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) for denaturing or native PAGE, respectively. A 25 mM Tris-HCl, 192 mM glycine pH 8.3 running buffer with or without 1% w/v SDS was used. SDS-PAGE was performed at room temperature, and native PAGE was performed at 4 °C. Gels were stained with Coomassie brilliant blue.

Phosphorylation Assay.

To measure kinase activity, 3 µg of recombinant NFR1 or NFR5 intracellular domain in 10 µL SEC buffer was supplemented with 5 mM MgCl2 and 100 nCi [γ-32P] ATP (PerkinElmer) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Phosphorylation reactions were stopped by addition of SDS loading dye and 95 °C incubation. Phosphorylation reactions were assayed by SDS-PAGE, and the gel was placed in an Autoradiography Hypercassette (Amersham/Cytiva) overnight. The radiograph was developed using a Typhoon FLA 9500 (Amersham/Cytiva), and the gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue.

DLS Assay.

A concentration series (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 mg/mL) of NFR5 constructs in SEC buffer were loaded into Prometheus NT.48 Series nanoDSF Grade Standard Capillaries (NanoTemper Technologies). Particle diffusion was measured in a DLS experiment using a NanoTemper Prometheus (NanoTemper Technologies). Samples were assayed in technical triplicates, with 10 measurements performed for each capillary in High Sensitivity mode. Polydispersity indices, diffusion coefficients, and particle radii were calculated in Panta Analysis (NanoTemper Technologies) and plotted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software). Diffusion coefficients/concentration plots were used to derive self-interaction parameters, kSI (also known as the diffusion interaction parameter kD), by linear regression in GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software).

Biolayer Interferometry.

Streptavidin (SA) biosensors (kinetic quality, Molecular devices, FortéBio) were equilibrated in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.01% Tween 20 (binding buffer) before use. Avi-tagged NFR5 or Avi-tagged NFR1 in binding buffer was immobilized on SA biosensors until 0.1 nm using an Octet RED96 (Molecular devices, FortéBio) at 25 °C. SA biosensors with immobilized Avi-tagged NFR5 or Avi-tagged NFR1 were washed for 120 s in binding buffer before interaction was assayed with 600 s association and 300 s dissociation phases over a 1:1 v/v dilution series (0.07 to 3.6 mg/mL, 1.56 to 100 µM) of NFR5 WT, NFR5 2E (L290E and L291E), and NFR5 3E (V294E, Y297E, and V298E). Unspecific binding was measured to a biotin-loaded SA sensor and was subtracted from the interaction datasets. Assays using Nb200 and the Nb200 control (A51E, Y60E, and V100E) were performed similarly except using an immobilization degree of 1 nm Avi-tagged NFR5 and binding was assayed with 150 s association and 250 s dissociation phases over a 1:1 v/v dilution series (0.08 to 5 µM). Data were processed and analyzed in FortéBio Data Analysis 7 (Molecular devices, FortéBio). GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software) was used to generate plots and calculate the NFR5/Nb200 dissociation constant (Kd) using an association/dissociation nonlinear regression model.

Sequence Alignment and Conservation Analysis.

Sequences spanning the juxtamembrane to αB regions in NFR5-type receptors were aligned in CLC Workbench 22 (Qiagen) using high gap cost settings. The alignment spanning the Lotus NFR5 αA, αA′, β0, and αB was used in structural conservation analysis and generation of sequence logo. Analyzed sequences were retrieved from publicly available databases: Lotus NFR5 UniProt: Q70KR1, Medicago NFP UniProt: Q0GXS4, Pea SYM10 UniProt: Q70KR3, Bean NFR5 UniProt: V7CHW9, Soybean NFR5a UniProt: A5YJV9, Chickpea NFP UniProt: A0A1S3EF43, Lupin NFR5 UniProt: A0A1J7GXG0, Peanut NFR5 NCBI: XP_025698788.1, Fragaria NFR5 NCBI: XP_004300586, Apple NFP UniProt: A0A498KGG5, Poplar NFR5 UniProt: A0A2K2AG15, Parasponia NFP2 GenBank: PON37437.1, Datisca NFR5 gene: Datgl376S09111, Rice MYR1 gene: Os03g13080, Barley RLK10 NCBI: XP_044979240.1, and Maize NFP NCBI: XP_020399958.1.

Hairy Root Transformation and Nodulation Assays.

The full-length L. japonicus NFR5, receptor variants and indicated chimeras with Hordeum vulgare (barley) RLK10 were fused with a C-terminal 3xHA tag and expressed under the control of the native Nfr5 promoter and terminator. L. japonicus NFR1 and the NFR1-Nb200 fusion were expressed from the native Nfr1 promoter and terminator (SI Appendix, Table S3). The CAMV35S promoter (p35S) was used to drive expression of Nb200 and its control variant (SI Appendix, Table S2). Multilevel Golden Gate cloning was used to assemble constructs with a transformation marker containing a pLjUbi:tYFPnls-tNOS module in the pIV10 cloning vector. Constructs were conjugated into A. rhizogenes AR1193 strain for hairy root transformation as described (57). An A. rhizogenes strain containing only the transformation marker (pLjUbi:tYFPnls-tNOS) was used as negative control. Lotus seeds were scarified by sandpaper followed by a surface sterilization in bleach (3% chlorine) for 5 min, washed five times with water, then soaked in water, and kept at 4 °C overnight. Seeds were placed on wet filter papers for 4 to 5 d at 21 °C, under 16 h photoperiod for germination. Germinated seedlings were transferred to square plates with ½ Gamborg’s B5 nutrient (Duchefa Biochemie) solidified by 0.8% Gelrite (Duchefa Biochemie). 1-wk-old seedlings were transformed by using A. rhizogenes AR1193 strains containing constructs of interest by punching the hypocotyl with a syringe needle and dropping bacteria solution on top of the wounding area. Transformed seedlings were kept in the dark overnight and left to grow at 21 °C, under a 16-h photoperiod. After 3 wk, untransformed roots were cut off, and transformed plants were transferred to Magenta boxes (Sigma-Aldrich) filled with lightweight expanded clay aggregate (LECA, 2 to 4 mm; Saint-Gobain Weber A/S), supplemented with 80 mL of ¼ B&D nutrient solution per box. Each Magenta box was inoculated with 1 mL of M. loti R7A carrying DsRED marker at OD600 = 0.04. Plants were harvested 6 wk after inoculation, and nodules were counted and imaged using a Leica FluoStereo M165FC microscope equipped with a Leica DFC310 FX camera.

Protein Subcellular Localization Studies.

L. japonicus Nfr5, receptor variants, and respective chimeras with Hordeum vulgare RLK10 were fused at the C-terminus with sfGFP and expression was driven by the Lotus polyubiquitin promoter (pLjUbi). Lotus protoplasts were isolated from roots of 4-d-old Gifu wild-type seedlings as previously described (58). The viable protoplasts were resuspended in a freshly prepared 40% PEG4000 solution, and 40 µg of the plasmid of interest was added. The mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The reaction was stopped by adding 10 mL of the W5 buffer (145 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl, and 5 mM glucose). With a gentle stir, the mixture was transferred to the centrifuge and spun down, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of W5 buffer. Protoplasts were imaged after 16 h of incubation in the dark at room temperature with the LSM 980 confocal microscope.
