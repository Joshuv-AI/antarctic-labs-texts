Deterministic and randomized Kaczmarz methods for A ​ X ​ B = C AXB=C with applications to color image restoration
Wenli Wang, Duo Liu, Gangrong Qu, Michiel E. Hochstenbach

Abstract
We study Kaczmarz type methods to solve consistent linear matrix equations. We first present a block Kaczmarz (BK) method that employs a deterministic cyclic row selection strategy. Assuming that the associated coefficient matrix has full column or row rank, we derive matrix formulas for a cycle of this BK method. Moreover, we propose a greedy randomized block Kaczmarz (GRBK) method and further extend it to a relaxed variant (RGRBK) and a deterministic counterpart (MWRBK). We establish the convergence properties of the proposed methods. Numerical tests verify the theoretical findings, and we apply the proposed methods to color image restoration problems.

The classical Kaczmarz method is a particular row-action method that has been extensively utilized to solve linear system (

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\alpha\ \tfrac{b_{i_{k}}-A_{i_{k},:}\,x^{k}}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\,A_{i_{k},:}^{T},\quad i_{k}=(k\ \text{mod}\ m)+1.

Related work.

Numerous variants have been proposed for the Kaczmarz method.
Strohmer and Vershynin
ref18
, instead of following the traditional sequential manner, select indices at random with probabilities proportional to the row norms. This results in a randomized Kaczmarz (RK) method with an expected rate of convergence that is called exponential or linear, depending on the community.
Subsequently, Bai and Wu
ref19
propose a row selection strategy based on weighted residuals.
Specifically, they prioritize the rows with larger residuals, which may lead to faster convergence compared to the method of
ref18
.
Their approach is referred to as the greedy randomized Kaczmarz (GRK) method.
Furthermore, the GRK method can be generalized by inserting a parameter
\theta\in[0,1]
into the row selection strategy (see Section
4.2
for more details), which results in a relaxed greedy randomized Kaczmarz (RGRK) method proposed in
ref20
.
Du and Gao
ref25
consider the limit case
\theta=1
, which is a deterministic method which directly selects the row with the largest weighted residual. They provide a theoretical estimate of its convergence rate and refer to this approach, originally introduced by McCormick
Mc
, as the maximal weighted residual Kaczmarz (MWRK) method.
We will extend these GRK, RGRK, and MWRK methods to problem (
1.1
).

We now focus our attention on extensions of Kaczmarz type methods for solving (

Some connections with other methods.

The stochastic gradient descent (SGD) method (see, e.g.,

\displaystyle\min_{X}F_{i}(X):=\min_{X}\tfrac{1}{2}\,\|A_{i,:}\|^{-2}\ \,\|A_{i,:}\,XB-C_{i,:}\|^{2}.

It may be checked that the gradient with respect to
X
is

\displaystyle\nabla F_{i}(X)=\|A_{i,:}\|^{-2}\ A_{i,:}^{T}\,(A_{i,:}\,XB-C_{i,:})\,B^{T}.

As Algorithm
1
, the computational cost per iteration of Algorithm
2
is quadratic:
\mathcal{O}(q(p+n))
.

Algorithm
2
can also be considered as a row version of the GI method of Ding, Liu, and Ding
ref8
.
Unlike the method in
ref8
, Algorithm
2
first decomposes matrix equation (
1.1
) into
m
subsystems of the form
A_{i,:}XB=C_{i,:}
and updates
X^{k}
at each iteration using only a single row of
A
and the corresponding row of
C
.
The method of
ref8
involves matrix-matrix products, and therefore one step is relatively expensive.
One can check that an update of the GI method requires
\min(\mathcal{O}(mq(p+n)),\,\mathcal{O}(pn(m+q)))
work.
(This minimum arises because one can choose to first compute either
AX
or
XB
.)
This method results in cubic computational complexity with respect to the sizes
m,n,p,q
. By contrast, Algorithm
2
has quadratic complexity and is therefore much cheaper per iteration.
The hope is that the
m
steps of Algorithm
2
make more progress than one step of the GI method.
This is indeed confirmed by the experimental results presented in Section
6
.

We now study the convergence of Algorithm
2
.
In
ref17
, Jiang and Wang study the convergence of a row-action method for solving (
1.3
).
We extend their
(
ref17
, Thm. A.3)
to (
1.1
), by proving that Algorithm
2
converges to
X_{\ast}^{0}
, given by

\displaystyle X_{\ast}^{0}:=X_{\ast}+X^{0}-A^{+}A\,X^{0}\,BB^{+},

(3.2)

where
X_{\ast}
is as in (
2.2
).
The following lemma plays a role in the subsequent convergence analysis.

Lemma 3.1
.

The iteration matrix
X^{k}
obtained by Algorithm
2
from an arbitrary initial guess
X^{0}
satisfies

\displaystyle X^{k}-A^{+}A\,X^{k}BB^{+}=X^{0}-A^{+}A\,X^{0}BB^{+},\quad k=0,\,1,\,2,\,\ldots\,.

Proof.

From Algorithm
2
, we get

\displaystyle X^{k}=X^{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\tfrac{\alpha}{\|A_{i_{j},:}\|^{2}}\,A_{i_{j},:}^{T}\,(C_{i_{j},:}-A_{i_{j},:}\,X^{j}B)\,B^{T}.

(3.3)

Subsequently, it is obtained that

\displaystyle A^{+}A\,X^{k}BB^{+}

\displaystyle=A^{+}A\,\Big[X^{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\tfrac{\alpha}{\|A_{i_{j},:}\|^{2}}\,A_{i_{j},:}^{T}\,(C_{i_{j},:}-A_{i_{j},:}\,X^{j}B)\,B^{T}\Big]BB^{+}

\displaystyle=A^{+}A\,X^{0}BB^{+}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\tfrac{\alpha}{\|A_{i_{j},:}\|^{2}}\,A_{i_{j},:}^{T}\,(C_{i_{j},:}-A_{i_{j},:}\,X^{j}B)\,B^{T}.

(3.4)

Let
e_{i}
be the
i
th column of the identity matrix.
The last step is valid because

A^{+}A\,A_{i_{s},:}^{T}=(A^{+}A)^{T}\,A^{T}\,e_{i_{s}}=A^{T}\,e_{i_{s}}=A_{i_{s},:}^{T}

and

B^{T}B\,B^{+}=B^{T}\,(BB^{+})^{T}=B^{T}\,(B^{T})^{+}\,B^{T}=B^{T}
.
By (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), the conclusion is proved.

Theorem 3.2
.

(Extension of
(
ref17
, Thm. A.3)
)
Let matrix equation (
1.1
) with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

be consistent.
If
0<\alpha<\tfrac{2}{\|B\|^{2}}
,
then the sequence
\{X^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
, obtained by Algorithm
2
from an arbitrary initial matrix
X^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
, converges to the solution
X_{\ast}^{0}
.

Proof.

By Algorithm
2
, one has

\displaystyle\|X^{k+1}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|X^{k+1}-X^{k}+X^{k}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|X^{k+1}-X^{k}\|_{F}^{2}+2\,\langle X^{k+1}-X^{k}\,,\,X^{k}-X_{\ast}\rangle+\|X^{k}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{4}}\,\|A_{i_{k},:}^{T}\,(C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B)\,B^{T}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{MMM}+2\,\tfrac{\alpha}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\,\big\langle A_{i_{k},:}^{T}\,(C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B)\,B^{T}\,,\,X^{k}-X_{\ast}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\phantom{MMM}+\|X^{k}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}.

(3.5)

Note that (making use of the Frobenius norm of a rank-one matrix)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{4}}\,\|A_{i_{k},:}^{T}\,(C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B)\,B^{T}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\,\|(C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B)\,B^{T}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\,\|B\|^{2}}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\,\|C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B\|^{2}.

Using
AX_{\ast}B=C
, one has

\displaystyle\big\langle A_{i_{k},:}^{T}\,(C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B)\,B^{T}\,,\,X^{k}-X_{\ast}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\,[(C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B)^{T}A_{i_{k},:}\,(X^{k}-X_{\ast})B]

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\,[(C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B)^{T}(A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B-C_{i_{k},:})]=-\|C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B\|^{2}.

Substituting the results of the previous two math displays into (
3.5
) yields

\displaystyle\|X^{k+1}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}-\|X^{k}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\,\|B\|^{2}}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\,\|C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B\|^{2}-\tfrac{2\alpha}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\,\|C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{2\alpha-\alpha^{2}\,\|B\|^{2}}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\ \|C_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}B\|^{2}.

(3.6)

If
0<\alpha<\tfrac{2}{\|B\|^{2}}
, then
\left\{\|X^{k}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}\right\}
is a monotonically decreasing sequence and converges to a nonnegative limit
\beta
.
After
s+1
cycles, that is,
k=(s+1)\,m
steps, we have

\displaystyle\|X^{s+1}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}\,\leq\,\|X^{s}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tfrac{2\alpha-\alpha^{2}\,\|B\|^{2}}{\|A_{i,:}\|^{2}}\,\|C_{i,:}-A_{i,:}\,X^{s,\,i}\,B\|^{2},

(3.7)

where
X^{s,\,i}
denotes the
i
th iterate in the
s
th sweep.
Because of the constraint on
\alpha
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\,\leq\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tfrac{2\alpha-\alpha^{2}\,\|B\|^{2}}{\|A_{i,:}\|^{2}}\,\|C_{i,:}-A_{i,:}\,X^{s,\,i}\,B\|^{2}\,\leq\,\|X^{s}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}-\|X^{s+1}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}.

Obviously,
\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow\infty}\left(\|X^{s}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}-\|X^{s+1}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}\right)=\beta-\beta=0
.
Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tfrac{2\alpha-\alpha^{2}\,\|B\|^{2}}{\|A_{i,:}\|^{2}}\ \|C_{i,:}-A_{i,:}\,X^{s,\,i}\,B\|^{2}=0.

This means that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow\infty}(C_{i,:}-A_{i,:}\,X^{s,\,i}B)=\mathbf{0},\quad i=1,\,\ldots,\,m.

(3.8)

The error sequence
\{\|X^{s}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}\}
is bounded, and therefore
\{X^{s}\}
is bounded.
Suppose that the subsequence
\{X^{s_{p}},\ p=1,\,2,\,\dots\}
converges to a limit
\widehat{X}
.
From Algorithm
2
and (
3.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{p\rightarrow\infty}\big(X^{s_{p},\,i}-X^{s_{p},\,i-1}\big)=\mathbf{0},\quad i=2,\,\ldots,\,m.

This implies that
\widehat{X}
is the limit of every subsequence
\{X^{s_{p},\,i}\}
for
i=1,\,\ldots,\,m
.
From (
3.8
), we get

\displaystyle C_{i,:}=\lim\limits_{p\rightarrow\infty}A_{i,:}\,X^{s_{p}m,\,i}B=A_{i,:}\,\widehat{X}B,\quad i=1,\,\ldots,\,m,

which implies that
\widehat{X}
is a solution of (
1.1
).

According to (
3.6
), it is obvious that

\|X^{k+1}-\widehat{X}\|_{F}\leq\|X^{k}-\widehat{X}\|_{F}
.
Therefore,

\{\|X^{k}-\widehat{X}\|_{F}\}
is a monotonically decreasing and bounded sequence.
Hence, we conclude that
\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|X^{k}-\widehat{X}\|_{F}
exists. Because
\lim\limits_{p\rightarrow\infty}\|X^{k_{p}m}-\widehat{X}\|_{F}=0
, it follows that
\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|X^{k}-\widehat{X}\|_{F}=0
, i.e.,
\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}X^{k}=\widehat{X}
.

Define
\mathcal{N}:=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}\ |\ AXB=\mathbf{0}\}
.
Let
Z=\widehat{X}-A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+}
.
Because
Z\in\mathcal{N}
and

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{X}-Z\,,\,Z\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+}\,,\,\widehat{X}-A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+}\,,\,\widehat{X}\rangle-\langle A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+}\,,\,A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\widehat{X}^{T}A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+})-\text{tr}((BB^{+})^{T}\widehat{X}^{T}(A^{+}A)^{T}A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\widehat{X}^{T}A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+})-\text{tr}(\widehat{X}^{T}A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+})=0,

we have
\|\widehat{X}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\widehat{X}-Z\|_{F}^{2}+\|Z\|_{F}^{2}\geq\|\widehat{X}-Z\|_{F}^{2}
.
Therefore,
\widehat{X}-Z
is the minimum norm solution, i.e.,
\widehat{X}-Z=X_{\ast}
.
According to Lemma
3.1
, we get

\displaystyle\widehat{X}-A^{+}A\widehat{X}BB^{+}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}X^{k}-A^{+}AX^{k}BB^{+}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}X^{0}-A^{+}AX^{0}BB^{+}=X^{0}-A^{+}AX^{0}BB^{+}.

It follows from this that
\widehat{X}=X_{\ast}+X^{0}-A^{+}AX^{0}BB^{+}=X_{\ast}^{0}
.

Define

g(\alpha)=2\alpha-\alpha^{2}\,\|B\|^{2}
.
Inequality (
3.6
) reveals that
g(\alpha)
is maximized over
0<\alpha<2\,\|B\|^{-2}
when
\alpha=\|B\|^{-2}
, which implies the upper bound of the convergence factor is minimized.
In experiments in Sections
5
and
6
, we therefore adopt this parameter
\alpha=\|B\|^{-2}
.
Note that the optimal
\alpha
may be slightly differ from the value minimizing the upper bound (see also Figure
5.4
).

We have investigated the BK method in a general setting (with no restrictions on
A
or
B
) and now turn our attention to the effects of the cases when
B
is of full column or row rank.

3.2
The BK method with
B
of full column rank

If
B
has full column rank (which implies
q\geq n
), we can express
B
as
B=QR
, where

has orthonormal columns and

is an upper triangular matrix. This step requires an investment of
\mathcal{O}(qn^{2})
work; note that
B
may be of small size in our applications. Then (
1.1
) becomes

\displaystyle AXQ=\widehat{C},

(3.9)

where
\widehat{C}=CR^{-1}
.
Some notes on this initial transformation are in order.
It is usually only a sensible idea when
B
is not of large size, and not nearly rank-deficient, especially in the presence of noise in
C
(which we do not consider in this paper).
In the same line, we can also consider
AX=CB^{+}\ (=C\,(B^{T}\!B)^{-1}B^{T})
, in which case
Q=I
in (
3.9
).
However, for the theoretical results in this section, an orthogonal

is sufficient; a transformation with
B^{+}
is not needed.

The update formula for the BK method corresponding to problem (

) is given by

\displaystyle X^{k+1}=X^{k}+\tfrac{\alpha}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\,A_{i_{k},:}^{T}\,(\widehat{C}_{i_{k},:}-A_{i_{k},:}\,X^{k}\,Q)\,Q^{T}.

(3.10)

A pseudocode for a complete cycle of the iterative scheme (

3
.
As stated before, we slightly reformulate the algorithm in terms of sweeps (using

as notation) to derive a formula in Proposition
3.3
.
As with Algorithm
2
, the rank-one update in line 5 requires
\mathcal{O}(q(p+n))
operations per iteration.

We recall the following general result regarding a linear iterative process.

Lemma 3.4

(See, e.g.,

\displaystyle\text{vec}(X^{s+1})

\displaystyle=\text{vec}(X^{s})+\text{vec}(A^{T}\,L_{\alpha}^{-1}\,(\widehat{C}-A\,X^{s}\,Q)\,Q^{T})

\displaystyle=\text{vec}(X^{s})-\text{vec}(A^{T}\,L_{\alpha}^{-1}\,A\,X^{s}\,Q\,Q^{T})+\text{vec}(A^{T}\,L_{\alpha}^{-1}\,\widehat{C}\,Q^{T})

\displaystyle=[I_{pq}-(Q\,Q^{T}\otimes A^{T}\,L_{\alpha}^{-1}\,A)]\,\text{vec}(X^{s})+\text{vec}(A^{T}\,L_{\alpha}^{-1}\,\widehat{C}\,Q^{T}),

\text{range}(B)
.

B

If

\displaystyle AX=\widetilde{C},

4.1
The GRBK method

Let us for the moment shift focus to linear system (

Bai and Wu

and are balanced using a fixed factor of

Extending the greedy strategy in

Remark 4.1

From line 6 of Algorithm

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{k}=\big\{i~\big|~\tfrac{\|R_{i,:}^{k}\|^{2}}{\|A_{i,:}\|^{2}}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}\tfrac{\|R_{i,:}^{k}\|^{2}}{\|A_{i,:}\|^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{\|R^{k}\|_{F}^{2}}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}\big\},

To do so, we extend

We first introduce some relevant quantities that will play a central role in several results to follow.

It is obvious that

In what is to follow, we make the very mild assumption that not all ratios

Therefore, we can write
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in[m]}\frac{\|R_{i,:}^{k}\|^{2}}{\|A_{i,:}\|^{2}}=\varepsilon\cdot\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}\frac{\|R_{i,:}^{k}\|^{2}}{\|A_{i,:}\|^{2}}

Then, let

Using this

(4.4)

From the lower bound on
\varphi_{k,\,\theta}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\delta_{k,\,\theta}<1-(2\alpha-\alpha^{2}\,\|B\|^{2})\cdot\|A\|_{F}^{-2}\cdot\sigma_{\min}^{2}(A)\,\sigma_{\min}^{2}(B)=\delta,

where

We note that

In the next theorem,

and

\mathbb{P}({\rm row=}\ i_{k})=\|\widetilde{R}_{i_{k},:}^{k}\|^{2}\,/\,\|\widetilde{R}^{k}\|_{F}^{2}

9:

Update
X^{k+1}=X^{k}+\tfrac{\alpha}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\,A_{i_{k},:}^{T}\,(R^{k}_{i_{k},:}\,B^{T})

10:

Compute
R^{k+1}=R^{k}-\tfrac{\alpha}{\|A_{i_{k},:}\|^{2}}\,(AA_{i_{k},:}^{T})\,((R^{k}_{i_{k},:}\,B^{T})\,B)

11:

end for

Note that Algorithm
6
retains the same per-iteration computational complexity as Algorithm
5
. The reason for the non-emptiness of the index set

is analogous to Remark
4.1
.
In a manner analogous to Theorem
4.2
, we derive Theorem

; its proof is omitted.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let matrix equation (
1.1
) with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

be consistent.
If
0<\alpha<\tfrac{2}{\|B\|^{2}}
,
then the sequence

, obtained by Algorithm

converges in expectation to a solution of (

). If
\text{range}(X^{0})\subseteq\text{range}(A^{T})
and
\text{range}((X^{0})^{T})\subseteq\text{range}(B)
, then Algorithm

converges in expectation to the unique minimum norm solution

.
Moreover, the solution error satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}\big[\|X^{k+1}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}\big]\leq\delta_{k,\,\theta}\cdot\|X^{k}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\delta\cdot\|X^{k}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2},

As a result, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|X^{k+1}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}\big]\leq\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=0}^{k}\delta_{\ell,\theta}\cdot\|X^{0}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\delta^{k+1}\cdot\|X^{0}-X_{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}.

We will compare the upper bounds of the convergence factors for the algorithms discussed in this section and the RBK algorithm proposed in

5
Numerical examples

This section provides some numerical examples to validate the main theoretical findings.
All experiments are tested using MATLAB R2021b on a laptop with
Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-12500H CPU @ 2.50GHz.
We examine the methods from the aspects of the number of iterations (denoted by ‘IT’), elapsed CPU time in seconds (denoted by ‘CPU’), and relative solution error (denoted by ‘RSE’).
In each experiment, the randomized methods (GRBK, RGRBK, and MWRBK) are executed for 20 independent trials. For randomized methods, the values of the IT and CPU are the averages of 20 trials.
We also show the standard deviation (denoted by ‘SD’) and the range (denoted by ‘Range’) of the CPUs derived from 20 independent experiments. Based on the discussions in Sections
3
and
4
, we take the parameter
\alpha=\|B\|^{-2}
. In addition, as discussed in Section
4.3
, the upper bound on the convergence factor of the RGRBK method is smaller than that of the GRBK method for
\theta\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
; therefore, we focus on this case.

The initial guess
X^{0}
is the zero matrix for all experiments.
This is a common choice in Kaczmarz type methods, as it tends to give good approximation results.
Also, this satisfies the requirements of the various theorems (namely
\text{range}(X_{0})\subseteq\text{range}(A^{T})
and
\text{range}(X_{0}^{T})\subseteq\text{range}(B)
), so that all methods converge to the minimum norm solution
X_{*}=A^{+}CB^{+}
.
The coefficient matrices
A
and
B
selected as test matrices include sparse matrices from the University of Florida sparse matrix collection
ref33

;

;
ref25
;
ref12
;

The matrix

\sf randn(p,q)
.
To create a consistent system, the matrix

is generated by

.
We present the size, rank, and density of the coefficient matrices, where the density is defined as

\displaystyle\text{density}:=\text{number of nonzeros of an}\ m\times n\ \text{matrix}\,/\,(m\,n).

We now briefly discuss possible stopping criteria.
A practical rule for all methods may be based on the norm of the rank-one update (cf. Table
4.4
):

\|X^{k+1}-X^{k}\|=\alpha\ \|R_{i_{k},:}^{k}\,B^{T}\|\,/\,\|A_{i_{k},:}\|.

Another natural stopping criterion is to use
\|R^{k}\|
, especially in Algorithms

–

However, rather than comparing these rules in details, we focus on the behavior of the methods considering the solution error:

An application to color image restoration

Many imaging systems, such as magnetic resonance imaging, computerized tomography, etc., can be modeled by the system of linear equations (
1.3
)
ref16
;
ref17
.
Similarly, matrix equation (
1.1
) plays a significant role in certain color image restoration problems
ref5
.

We first give a description of the forward model for color image restoration; see also
ref5
.
The desired original color image
\mathcal{X}
(the observed color image
\mathcal{C}
) can be shown as a three-dimensional array of size
m\times n\times 3
, where the red (R), green (G) and blue (B) channels are correspondingly three matrices
X_{{\text{r}}}:=\mathcal{X}(:,:,1)
,
X_{{\text{g}}}:=\mathcal{X}(:,:,2)
,
X_{{\text{b}}}:=\mathcal{X}(:,:,3)

(and similarly,
C_{{\text{r}}}:=\mathcal{C}(:,:,1)
,
C_{{\text{g}}}:=\mathcal{C}(:,:,2)
,
C_{{\text{b}}}:=\mathcal{C}(:,:,3)
).
With
X=(\text{vec}(X_{{\text{r}}}),\text{vec}(X_{{\text{g}}}),\text{vec}(X_{{\text{b}}}))
and
C=(\text{vec}(C_{{\text{r}}}),\text{vec}(C_{{\text{g}}}),\text{vec}(C_{{\text{b}}}))
, the forward model is expressed as

C=AXA_{\rm c}^{T}+E
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
is the within-channel blurring,
A_{\rm c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
represents the cross-channel blurring and
E\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times 3}
is the additive noise. Ideally, if there is no noise in the generation of the blurred image, the forward model becomes (
1.2
).
In this paper, we only consider this noise-free case.

We take
face
of size
92\times 92\times 3
,
bird
of size
96\times 96\times 3
and
mandril
of size
125\times 120\times 3

as test images, as shown in the top row of Fig.
6.1
, to investigate the performance of the following iterative methods: GI
ref8
, RBK
ref12
, and our proposed BK, GRBK, RGRBK, and MWRBK methods.
To obtain blurred images, the Matlab function
fspecial
is used to generate a rotationally symmetric Gaussian lowpass filter of size
5
with standard deviation
6

to simulate the blurred images caused by the Gaussian filter.
The cross-channel blurring matrix
A_{\rm c}
captures the relations among different color channels (i.e., RGB) that may arise due to the blurring. In typical systems, the dominant contribution to a channel originates from itself, implying
(A_{\rm c})_{i,i}>(A_{\rm c})_{i,j}
for
i\neq j
. To ensure the preservation of total intensity, each row of
A_{\rm c}
is constrained to sum to 1, i.e.,
\sum_{j}(A_{\rm c})_{i,j}=1
.
In our examples, we choose the (rather typical) cross-channel blurring matrix as

\displaystyle\small A_{\rm c}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}0.90&0.05&0.05\\
0.00&0.90&0.10\\
0.05&0.10&0.85\end{array}\right].

The bottom row of Fig.
6.1
shows the blurred images, with their corresponding peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) values 19.31, 17.52, and 18.86, respectively.

m=p
(i.e.,
A

q=n

p\gg q
(

We remark that in this specific experiment the matrix
B=A_{c}^{T}
is a small
3\times 3

4
.
We test this approach in Experiment
6.2
, but also stress that we have developed methods to solve (
1.1
) for
A
and
B
of arbitrary size.

7
Conclusions

We have explored deterministic and randomized Kaczmarz methods to solve the consistent matrix equation (
1.1
), which arises commonly in many applications.
Our methods can be used for
A
and
B
of any size (i.e., overdetermined and underdetermined cases), with the exception of Algorithms
3
and
4
, which require
B
to have full column or row rank.
The gradient type GI method in
ref8
uses a full-rank update in each iteration.
The methods in
ref11
use a rank-one addition with a single element of
C
and one column of
B
in each step.
All of our proposed methods also perform a rank-one update at each iteration using one row of
C
and the entire matrix
B
.
As summarized in Table
4.4
, the methods in
ref11
and this paper require quadratic costs in terms of the matrix dimensions
m,n,p,q
, while the GI method in
ref8
incurs a cubic complexity; see also Section
6
.

We have first studied a (deterministic, cyclic) BK method in Section
3.1
(Algorithm
2
).
This is a straightforward extension of the standard Kaczmarz method to matrix equation (
1.1
) and also a deterministic (cyclic) version of the RBK method of
ref12
.
We have shown that this method converges to the solution
X^{0}_{\ast}

(=A^{+}CB^{+}+X^{0}-A^{+}AX^{0}BB^{+})
if
0<\alpha<2\,\|B\|^{-2}
.
We have also considered variants of this BK method under the assumption that
B
is of full column rank (Section
3.2
, Algorithm
3
) or full row rank (Section
3.3
, Algorithm
4
).
These additional constraints enable us to derive a matrix expression for a sweep (cycle) of the BK method.
Note that Algorithm
2
imposes no requirements on
B
, but the last two methods require
B
to be of full row or column rank (with nonsingularity as a special case) and of modest size.
In addition, as shown in Proposition
3.7
, the last two methods are equivalent when the coefficient matrix
B
is nonsingular.
In Experiment
6.2
, we have confirmed that these three BK methods exhibit a similar convergence behavior when
B
is
3\times 3
.

Next, we have proposed and studied the GRBK method (Algorithm
5
) and its relaxed and deterministic variants: RGRBK (Algorithm
6
) and MWRBK (Algorithm
7
) in Section
4
, which select rows based on the residual norm.
Based on the experiments in Section
6
, the extra cost of computing this residual norm is worthwhile; in addition, the residual can also serve as a suitable stopping criterion.
We have proven that they converge to the unique minimum norm solution
A^{+}CB^{+}
if
0<\alpha<2\,\|B\|^{-2}
.
According to the convergence analysis in Sections
3
and
4
, the upper bounds of the convergence factors for the proposed methods are minimized when
\alpha=\|B\|^{-2}
.
As shown in Section
4.3
, the upper bound of the convergence factor for the GRBK, RGRBK, and MWRBK methods is not larger than that of RBK. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that they may require fewer iterations to achieve convergence.
The experiments in Sections
5
and
6
show that this is indeed the case.
The RGRBK method reduces to the GRBK method when
\theta=\tfrac{1}{2}
and to the MWRBK method when
\theta=1
, as illustrated in Fig.
4.3
and Table
4.2
.
Its convergence factor upper bound is decreasing with
\theta
. Therefore, the upper bound for the convergence factor is minimal at
\theta=1
, where RGRBK coincides with MWRBK.

Finally, based on the theoretical analysis and experimental results, the overall performance of the proposed methods can be summarized as follows.
The BK method converges asymptotically (see Theorem
3.2
); its convergence rate may be sensitive to the ordering of the rows in
A
. It may be (much) faster than its randomized counterpart (RBK), but it may also be slower. With a randomized initial ordering of the rows, the BK method typically behaves similarly to the randomized method RBK, without the drawback of the variance of RBK.
We note although its convergence is often slower than other methods (see Section
6
), BK is valuable for understanding the influence of row selection through comparisons with the randomized (RBK) and greedy (GRBK, RGRBK, MWRBK) counterparts.
The greedy extensions (GRBK, RGRBK, and MWRBK) consistently achieve faster convergence than RBK, also observed in the numerical tests in Sections
5
and
6
.
Although the greedy approaches are slower than the BK and RBK methods per iteration, they use fewer iterations with a lower runtime.
The randomized algorithms (RBK, GRBK, and RGRBK) have modest standard deviations in the experiments.
Overall, both theory and experiments suggest that the (deterministic) MWRBK method (Algorithm
7
) seems to be the most attractive among the considered methods in terms of the number of iterations and computational time.
Finally, we remark that in this paper we have considered matrix equations without noise. Kaczmarz type methods may also be particularly suitable for noisy right-hand sides
C
; this is left for follow-up work.
