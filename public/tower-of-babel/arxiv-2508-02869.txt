Attenuation of the ultra-high-energy neutrino flux by dark matter scatterings
Ivan Esteban, Alejandro Ibarra

Abstract
A flux of ultra-high-energy (UHE) neutrinos is generally expected to be produced by astrophysical sources at cosmological distances and to reach Earth. In this paper, we investigate the impact of neutrino scattering with dark matter (DM) particles in both the intergalactic medium and the Milky Way on the total flux, energy spectrum, and arrival directions of UHE neutrinos. We emphasize the complementarity of neutrino detectors at different latitudes to probe the anisotropy in the flux at Earth due to the attenuation of the neutrino flux in the Milky Way dark matter halo. We also discuss that, with mild astrophysical assumptions, limits on the DM- ν \nu scattering cross section can be placed even if the neutrino sources are unknown. Finally, we explore all this phenomenology with the recent UHE neutrino event KM3230213A, and place the corresponding limits in the DM- ν \nu scattering cross-section.



1
Introduction

The origin of the ultra-high-energy cosmic rays (UHECRs) remains a mystery to this day. The most accepted explanation is that UHECRs are accelerated in extreme astrophysical environments via electromagnetic processes like Fermi acceleration (for reviews, see
e.g.

[
1
,
2
]
). If this is the case, one expects that the UHECRs could interact with the radiation and the matter surrounding the sources, together with background radiation en-route to Earth, producing a flux of ultra-high energy (UHE) neutrinos that could be detected at Earth.

In the Standard Model of Particle Physics neutrinos interact very weakly with matter and radiation, and the UHE neutrino flux is expected to arrive to Earth isotropically and practically without attenuation. On the other hand, it is now firmly established that the Universe is permeated by an additional component of matter, called Dark Matter (DM). While the interactions of neutrinos with ordinary matter are well described by electroweak theory (at least up to the energies that have been probed), the interactions of neutrinos with dark matter are completely unknown. Upper limits on the dark matter-neutrino cross-section have been derived for
E_{\nu}\sim 100
eV
from the Lyman-alpha forest
[
3
]
or from the number of Milky Way dwarf spheroidal galaxies
[
4
]
; for
E_{\nu}\sim 10
MeV from supernova neutrinos
[
5
,
6
]
; for
E_{\nu}\sim 1
TeV from the Seyfert Galaxy NGC 1068
[
7
]
and for
E_{\nu}\sim 100

[
8
,
9
]
. However, the scattering cross-section for
E_{\nu}\gtrsim 100

In this paper, we investigate the attenuation of the ultrahigh-energy neutrino flux due to scatterings with dark matter in both the intergalactic medium and the Milky Way halo. Our analysis is conservative since we do not account for potential attenuation effects near the sources of UHE neutrinos. We argue that dark matter–neutrino interactions can lead to significant and distinctive modifications to the observed flux, energy spectrum, and arrival directions of astrophysical neutrinos. As a case study, we focus on the recently reported event KM3-230213A
[
10
]
, and discuss its consistency with the null results from IceCube and the Pierre Auger Observatory
[

3
Attenuation of the ultra-high energy neutrino flux

Ultra-high energy neutrinos could be produced at astrophysical sources, when protons become accelerated to extremely high energies and collide with photons surrounding the source, thereby producing charged pions that eventually decay into neutrinos. Assuming that the sources are optically thin to protons, and in view to the measured flux of ultra-high energy cosmic rays, it is possible to derive a conservative upper bound on the expected ultra-high energy neutrino flux at Earth

\displaystyle E_{\nu}^{2}\,\Phi_{\nu}\lesssim 4.5\times 10^{-8}\,\text{GeV}\text{cm}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}\text{sr}^{-1}\;;

(13)

this is the renown Waxman-Bahcall bound
[
22

[
23

\nu

4
Complementarity of UHE neutrino detectors

In Section
2
, we discussed the anisotropy in the neutrino flux at the surface of the Earth due to the attenuation by DM-
\nu
scatterings in the Milky Way halo. The UHE neutrinos must then traverse the Earth before reaching the detector, which induces an additional source of anisotropy due to the attenuation of the neutrino flux by interactions with the nuclei in the interior of the Earth. The combination of different experiments at different locations. will then offer the possibility of obtaining additional information about the source of the UHE neutrino events, and possibly on the attenuation of the flux due to DM scatterings.

With current and several future detectors, the most promising candidates for ultra-high-energy neutrino events are those entering the atmosphere near the horizon. This geometry maximizes the amount of matter traversed by neutrinos, increasing the probability for them to produce a detectable particle while avoiding Earth attenuation at angles well below the horizon. Therefore, experiments “sweeping” the sky as the horizon moves against the fixed stars are well-suited for the detection of UHE neutrino flux generated by distant sources.

We show in Fig.
9
the day averaged effective areas (
i.e
integrating over the right ascension) of IceCube, the Pierre Auger Observatory and KM3NeT as a function of the declination. KM3NeT (located at a latitude of
36.27^{\circ}
N) has therefore sensitivity to a wide range of declinations, being only blind to UHE neutrinos arriving with
-90^{\circ}\leq\delta\lesssim 36^{\circ}
, since this region of the sky remains below the horizon over the whole year. IceCube (located at a latitude of
90^{\circ}
S) is practically blind to neutrinos arriving from positive declinations, and presents a high peak of sensitivity at
\delta\simeq 0
, since this direction lies at the horizon of the South Pole the whole year. Finally, the Pierre Auger Observatory (located at a latitude of
35.0^{\circ}
S) is only sensitive to neutrinos from a very narrow range of incoming directions. Although the effect is partly alleviated by the rotation of the Earth, the day-averaged effective area is still quite angle-dependent. Notice that the effective area of KM3NeT is a factor of
\sim 10
smaller than the one from IceCube, even though they are both km
3
experiments, the reason being that KM3NeT is still under construction.

Clearly, for an unattenuated flux (purely isotropic) IceCube has a very good sensitivity. On the other hand, if the flux is attenuated by DM scatterings in the Milky Way halo, the flux at negative declinations decreases, and IceCube loses sensitivity. Other instruments, such as KM3NeT or Auger, which are sensitive to UHE neutrinos coming from positive declinations, may observe signals while escaping detection at IceCube.

This non-trivial interplay of the effective areas of both experiments could in principle open the intriguing possibility that KM3Net could observe UHE neutrino events, while escaping detection at IceCube, and thereby potentially explaining the observation of KM3
-
230213A. Quantitatively, however, the difference in effective areas without attenuation is so large that the impact of DM attenuation is mild: using a saturated Poisson likelihood test

5
Conclusions

We have presented a comprehensive analysis of the attenuation of a diffuse neutrino flux due to scattering with DM particles in the intergalactic medium and in the Milky Way halo. We have argued that when the scattering cross-section over DM mass is sizable, the attenuation in the Milky Way generates a neutrino flux with a characteristic anisotropy.

We have then studied the implications of DM-
\nu
interactions for the flux, energy spectrum and arrival direction of ultra-high energy neutrinos, assuming that they are of diffuse origin. Concretely, we have shown that the Waxman-Bahcall limit becomes more stringent in the presence of DM-
\nu
interactions. Further, we have investigated the implications of the detection of the event
KM3
-
230213A for the DM-
\nu
interactions, assuming that the event is of diffuse origin. If this is the case, future instruments should detect hundreds of ultra-high-energy neutrinos, which will allow to perform a detailed spectral and morphological analysis of the signal, which encode invaluable information about the magnitude and energy dependence of the scattering cross-section. Finally, we have highlighted the complementarity of different neutrino detectors at different latitudes in the Earth, in order to reveal the existence of particle DM through the anisotropy in the arrival directions of ultra-high-energy neutrinos due to their passage through the DM halo of our Galaxy and the Earth.

Appendix A
Evaluation of the effective areas

In this Appendix, we detail how we compute the effective areas for the relevant experiments discussed in the main text. While for Auger the effective area as a function of neutrino energy and incoming direction is publicly available
[
12
]
, to our understanding for IceCube and KM3NeT only sky-averaged effective areas exist. Directional effects are particularly relevant for UHE neutrino attenuation by DM, so a proper implementation of angle-dependent effects is mandatory.

We compute the angular dependence of the IceCube and KM3NeT effective areas following Ref.
[
37
]
. The effective area for a neutrino with energy
E_{\nu}
and zenith angle
\theta
that interacts inside the detector is given by

A_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{in}}(E_{\nu},\theta)=\sigma(E_{\nu})N_{\mathrm{nuc}}\varepsilon(E_{\nu},\theta)e^{-\sigma(E_{\nu})\int n(s,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}s}\,,

(A1)

with
\sigma
the neutrino-nucleon interaction cross section,
N_{\mathrm{nuc}}
the number of nucleons in the detector,
\varepsilon
the detection efficiency, and
n(s,\theta)
the number density of nucleons along the traversed Earth path. The last term factor into account neutrino attenuation by Earth. In all our calculations, we assume the Preliminary Earth Reference Model (PREM)
[
38
]
.

In turn, for a neutrino that interacts outside the detector and produces a throughgoing lepton (
\mu
or
\tau
) that traverses the detector,

A_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{through}}(E_{\nu},\theta)=A(\theta)P_{\ell}(E_{\nu})\varepsilon(E_{\nu},\theta)e^{-\sigma(E_{\nu})\int n(s,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}s}\,,

(A2)

with
A(\theta)
the projected area of the detector perpendicular to the neutrino direction, and
P_{\ell}(E_{\nu},\theta)
the probability for a neutrino with energy
E_{\nu}
to produce a throughgoing lepton that reaches the detector. The total effective area is given by

A_{\mathrm{eff}}(E_{\nu},\theta)=A_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{in}}(E_{\nu},\theta)+A_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{through}}(E_{\nu},\theta)\,.

(A3)

To compute
A(\theta)
, we approximate the IceCube and KM3NeT detectors as cylinders with volumes of
1\,\mathrm{km^{3}}
and
0.15\,\mathrm{km^{3}}
, and heights of
1\,\mathrm{km}
and
0.7\,\mathrm{km^{3}}
; respectively
[
10
]
.

For muons,
P_{\mu}(E_{\nu},\theta)
is given by
[
37
]

P_{\mu}(E_{\nu},\theta)=\int_{E_{\mathrm{th}}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}E_{\mu}\,\frac{N_{A}}{\alpha(1+E_{\mu}/\epsilon)}\int_{y^{\prime}_{\mathrm{min}}}^{y^{\prime}_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}}\,,

(A4)

where
E_{\mu}
is the energy of the throughgoing muon,
N_{A}
is Avogadro’s number (equal to the number of nucleons per gram of matter),
\alpha=2\times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{GeV/(g/cm^{2})}
is the muon ionization energy loss rate,
\epsilon=440\,\mathrm{GeV}
is the muon energy where radiative energy losses equal ionization energy losses
[
39
]
,
E_{\mathrm{th}}
is the smallest detectable muon energy (its effect is negligible as long as
E_{\mathrm{th}}\ll E_{\nu},\,\epsilon
; which we assume to hold),
y^{\prime}\equiv 1-E_{\mu}^{\prime}/E_{\nu}
with
E_{\mu}^{\prime}
the muon energy before energy losses, and
\sigma
is the neutrino-nucleon interaction cross section.
y^{\prime}_{\mathrm{max}}=1-E_{\mu}/E_{\nu}
, and

y^{\prime}_{\mathrm{min}}=\max\left[0,\,1-\frac{(E_{\mu}+\epsilon)e^{X_{\mathrm{max}}(\theta)/\xi}-\epsilon}{E_{\nu}}\right]\,,

(A5)

where
\xi\equiv\epsilon/\alpha
and
X_{\mathrm{max}}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(s,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}s
is the integrated grammage along the incoming direction, with
\rho
mass density. This expression can be derived by following Ref.
[
37
]
but noting that the maximum integrated grammage along the incoming direction is necessarily finite.

For
\tau
leptons,
\tau
decay has to be taken into account, leading to

P_{\tau}(E_{\nu},\theta)=\int_{E_{\mathrm{th}}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}E_{\tau}\,\frac{N_{A}}{\alpha(1+E_{\tau}/\epsilon)}e^{-\frac{m_{\tau}}{\rho\lambda_{\tau}}\frac{\xi}{\epsilon}\ln\left[\frac{E_{\tau}^{\prime}(E_{\tau}+\epsilon)}{E_{\tau}(E_{\tau}^{\prime}+\epsilon)}\right]}\int_{y^{\prime}_{\mathrm{min}}}^{y^{\prime}_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}}\,,

(A6)

where for
\tau
leptons
\epsilon=3.4\times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{GeV}

[
40
]
,
m_{\tau}
is the
\tau
mass, and
\lambda_{\tau}\simeq 8.7\times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{cm}
is the
\tau
decay length. This expression can be derived by following Ref.
[
37
]
but noting that the
\tau
lepton flux gets attenuated after travelling a distance
s
by a factor
e^{-\frac{m_{\tau}}{\lambda_{\tau}}\int_{0}^{s}\frac{1}{E(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s}
, and changing variables from
s
to integrated grammage
X
.

We have checked that following this procedure gives a very good approximation to the angular dependence of lower-energy publicly available IceCube effective areas
[
41
]
.

The procedure described above allows to compute the effective area up to the detection efficiency
\varepsilon(E_{\nu},\theta)
. To estimate it, we assume that, at the high energies relevant for UHE neutrino detection, the angular dependence of
\varepsilon
is negligible compared to that of Earth absorption and throughgoing leptons. We thus obtain
\varepsilon(E_{\nu})
by requiring the sky-averaged effective areas to match those in Refs.
[
11
,
10
]
.
