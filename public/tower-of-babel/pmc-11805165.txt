Prevalence, Severity, and Predictors of Apical Root Resorption Following Orthodontic Treatment: A Cone-Beam Computed Tomography Study
Authors: Kunal Bhardwaj, Nikita Patel, Arvind Mengi, Ranu S. Ingole, Gaurav Ahuja, Arti Butoliya, Miral Mehta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:To investigate the prevalence, severity, and predictors of apical root resorption following orthodontic treatment using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted on the CBCT scans of 141 orthodontic patients (76 males and 65 females). CBCT images were meticulously assessed for the presence and severity of apical root resorption using standardized grading criteria.Results:The prevalence of apical root resorption was 45.4%. Among those with root resorption, 35.5% exhibited grade 1 resorption, while 10.8% showed grade 2 resorption. Sex was not significantly associated with the severity of root resorption (P = 0.312); however, age had a significant effect (P < 0.05). Younger age groups were more prone to severe root resorption. Additionally, the treatment duration was significantly correlated with the severity of root resorption (P = 0.021), with longer treatment durations associated with increased resorption severity.Conclusion:This study provides valuable insights into the prevalence, severity, and predictors of apical root resorption following orthodontic treatment using CBCT imaging. These findings highlight the importance of considering patient demographics and treatment duration in treatment planning to mitigate the risk of root resorption and enhance treatment outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Apical root resorption, a common problem in orthodontic treatment, is characterized by the shortening of tooth roots due to the remodeling of surrounding tissues and bone.[1] Although excessive or severe root resorption is considered a typical physiological response to orthodontic forces, it can threaten the long-term stability and health of the dentition.[2] Therefore, orthodontic practitioners must have a comprehensive understanding of the prevalence, severity, and predictors of root resorption to maximize treatment outcomes and minimize adverse effects on oral health.

A considerable percentage of orthodontic patients experience root resorption; studies have shown that prevalence rates can range from 20% to 100%, depending on a various factors, including patient characteristics, treatment duration, and force magnitude.[3] Although most cases are mild and clinically insignificant, severe resorption can lead to complications such as impaired tooth movement, pulp necrosis, and eventual tooth loss.[4] Additionally, root resorption may lead to shortened or blunted tooth roots, which may disrupt the overall balance and harmony of the smile, thus impacting aesthetics.[5] Therefore, identifying patients at risk of significant root resorption and implementing preventative measures is essential for effective orthodontic treatment planning.

Apical root resorption has been associated with a number of factors, including anatomical factors (such as root morphology and proximity to cortical bone) and patient-related factors (such as age, sex, and genetic predisposition).[6] Treatment-related factors include force magnitude, treatment duration, and appliance type. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and traditional periapical radiographs are the most widely used radiographic imaging modalities for the diagnosis and monitoring of apical root resorption.[7]

Using CBCT, this study aimed to investigate the frequency, severity, and predictors of apical root resorption following orthodontic treatment.

METHODOLOGY

This study employed a retrospective design. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (Ref# GGSCDS&RC/2021/IEC/022). As the study involved a retrospective review of anonymized CBCT images, informed consent was not required. The study included 141 participants, comprising 65 females and 76 males, who had previously undergone orthodontic treatment. Participants were selected based on the availability of CBCT images taken both before and after orthodontic treatment. The inclusion criteria for subject selection required individuals to have completed orthodontic treatment and to have undergone baseline (pretreatment) and post-treatment CBCT scans. This criterion ensured that changes in apical root morphology following orthodontic intervention were accurately recorded in the study. Digital archives were used to retrieve the CBCT images. CBCT images were created using cone-shaped X-ray beams, which provided precise views of the surrounding soft tissues, alveolar bone, teeth, and roots. The obtained CBCT images were analyzed using software specifically designed for dental imaging analysis. Commonly used software platforms for CBCT image analysis include invivo, OnDemand3D, and dolphin imaging. These software programs offer a range of tools and features that facilitate detection and measurement of apical root resorption, allowing for precise viewing, measurement, and alteration of CBCT images.

The presence and degree of apical root resorption in the participants’ teeth were evaluated using software tools by two qualified dental practitioners or researchers skilled in CBCT image interpretation. Precise standards for classifying the extent of root resorption, such as those outlined by Levander and Malmgren, were applied during the evaluation.[8] Additionally, the duration and type of orthodontic treatment received by each patient influenced the interval between baseline and post-treatment CBCT scans. This heterogeneity allowed for a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomena, facilitating the evaluation of apical root resorption across various treatment regimens and time periods.

DISCUSSION

The findings showed that 63.8% of orthodontic patients exhibited some degree of apical root resorption, indicating that this condition is common among these patients. This result aligns with other studies showing that have shown that root resorption frequently occurs during orthodontic treatment.[9] The study also found variations in the degree of resorption, with the majority of cases classified as mild (28.4%) and moderate-to-severe (7.8%). These results highlight the importance of monitoring root morphology during orthodontic treatment and implementing preventative measures to minimize the risk of significant root damage. An intriguing aspect of this study was the investigation of sex variations in the occurrence and severity of apical root resorption. The study did not show any significant differences in mean resorption measures or resorption grades between males and females, which was unexpected. This finding aligns with another study that reported a higher frequency of root resorption in men.[10] Further research is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms and the role of sex in susceptibility to root resorption. Although age groups did not significantly correlate with resorption grades, there was a tendency for older individuals to have higher mean resorption values. This finding raises the possibility that prolonged exposure to orthodontic forces, especially in adult patients with fully developed teeth, may eventually lead to cumulative root injury.[11] Similarly, while not achieving statistical significance, a significant correlation was observed between treatment duration and mean resorption values. These findings underscore the importance of carefully planning and monitoring therapy to minimize the risk of severe root resorption, particularly during prolonged orthodontic treatments.[12] The study compared the outcomes of patients treated with clear aligners, removable appliances, and fixed braces. Remarkably, there were no discernible differences in mean resorption measures across the treatment groups. As a result, careful monitoring of root resorption throughout therapy is required. CBCT imaging should be routinely incorporated into orthodontic practitioners’ diagnostic procedures to evaluate root morphology and identify patients who may be at risk for excessive resorption. Furthermore, customized treatment regimens tailored to each patient’s unique characteristics—such as age, sex, and duration of therapy—may help reduce the likelihood of root damage and improve treatment outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Although age, sex, duration of therapy, and type of treatment did not emerge as significant predictors of root resorption, their possible impact still needs to be explored. Future studies should focus on elucidating the fundamental processes of root resorption, investigating innovative prophylactic methods, and assessing the long-term effects of orthodontic treatments on root health.
