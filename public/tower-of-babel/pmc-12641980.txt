The association between adverse childhood experiences and co-occurrence of health risk behaviors in adolescents: the chained mediating role of self-efficacy and self-control
Authors: Tao Xu, Yongsheng Wang, Zhengying Han, Lvhao Liu, Jiajia Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors (ACEs) among adolescents has become a significant public health issue that urgently needs to be addressed. This study aims to investigate the prevalence of Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors among adolescents and to explore the mediating role of self-efficacy (SE) and self-control (SC) in the relationship between adverse childhood experiences and Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors (CHRBs). MethodsA total of 7901 adolescents in China were surveyed using the following instruments: the Adolescent Health Risk Behaviors Survey Questionnaire, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), the Mobile Phone Addiction Index (MPAI), the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACEQ), the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), and the Self-Control Scale (SCS). Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 software.Results(1) Adverse childhood experiences significantly and positively predicted Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors among adolescents (p < 0.05). (2) Adverse childhood experiences indirectly influenced Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors through the partial mediating effects of self-efficacy and self-control (p < 0.05). (3) Adverse childhood experiences also indirectly influenced Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors through the chained mediating effects of self-efficacy and self-control (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study further elucidates the relationship between adverse childhood experiences and Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors among adolescents, as well as the chained mediating role of self-efficacy and self-control. These findings confirm the mechanism by which adverse childhood experiences influence Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors. The results suggest that creating a favorable living environment for children and adolescents should become a societal consensus. Additionally, when intervening with adolescents who exhibit problematic behaviors, it is important to focus on enhancing their self-efficacy and self-control to mitigate the long-term impact of adverse childhood experiences.Keywords: Adolescents, Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), Co-occurrence of health risk behaviors, Chained mediating

Background

Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors refer to the phenomenon where adolescents engage in multiple types of Health Risk Behaviors simultaneously. This may include various combinations of substance use, unhealthy dietary habits, lack of physical activity, and risky sexual behaviors [1].

In addition to several conventional health risk behaviors, adolescents’ mobile phone dependence and depressive symptoms have shown a marked global increase in the current digital age, with a strong correlation between the two [2], making them a major focus in the field of public health. This study also centers on these two health risk behaviors. The Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors is highly prevalent. A global perspective study showed that over 30% of adolescents engage in Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors [3]. In China, a study reported that 27.45% of adolescents exhibit Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors [4]. When Health Risk Behaviors are clustered, their harmful effects are significantly magnified [5]. For instance, a study on nicotine intake and all-cause mortality indicated that compared with individuals who only smoke (a single health risk behavior), smokers with four coexisting Health Risk Behaviors have more than double the mortality risk [6]. Moreover, Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors are closely related to the development of various chronic diseases and mental disorders [5, 7]. These findings highlight the destructiveness of Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors, which exacerbates the policy burden, financial burden, and healthcare burden on the nation [8], making it one of the major issues in the field of public health [9]. Numerous studies have concluded that comprehensive interventions targeting Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors are more effective than interventions focusing on a single health-compromising behavior [6, 7, 10]. Research on the influencing factors of clustering is fundamental for identifying the causes of clustering and developing comprehensive interventions. However, a review and summary of previous studies revealed that there are limited findings on the influencing factors of Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors. Most existing studies have focused on the characteristics of clustering (cluster analysis) [5, 11–15], with few in-depth investigations into the influencing factors. Therefore, this study selected adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), self-efficacy, and self-control as three potential influencing factors and employed structural equation modeling (SEM) for analysis, attempting to uncover the mechanism by which ACEs influence Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors. This exploration is of great significance for a comprehensive understanding of the influencing factors of Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors. It also promotes the discussion of the clustering mechanism, enhances the specificity of prevention and control strategies, improves intervention efficiency, and holds important value for the further improvement of global public health services [9].

Adverse childhood experiences and co-occurrence of health risk behaviors

A comprehensive review of previous studies indicates that adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have a significant impact on the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors. ACEs are highly prevalent, particularly in their broader definition. It is estimated that more than half of the global population has been exposed to at least one type of ACE. These experiences have cascading effects on children’s health and profoundly influence individual development [16]. Similarly, ACEs are widespread in China and have long-term impacts on people’s physical and mental development as well as their future life trajectories [17]. Adverse childhood experiences can affect brain development, leading to impaired cognitive abilities and poor social functioning [18]. Exposure to ACEs may lead to negative self-perceptions and maladaptive coping strategies in adolescents, heightening their risk for serious mental health conditions (e.g., depression). Adolescents experiencing such conditions are, in turn, more likely to display a range of health risk behaviors [19]. Based on these findings, this study proposes Hypothesis 1 (H1): Adverse childhood experiences positively predict the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors.

The mediating role of self-efficacy

A review of prior research indicates that self-efficacy is associated with the occurrence of various Health Risk Behaviors among adolescents. Studies have shown that self-efficacy is a positive response to cognitive change. For example, individuals with high self-efficacy are typically more aware of the dangers of smoking, which in turn reduces the likelihood of smoking behavior [20]. Additionally, low self-efficacy is a significant characteristic of adolescents with depression, and the impact of self-efficacy on depressive symptoms has been widely recognized [21]. High levels of self-efficacy enable individuals to more effectively utilize resources to overcome difficulties and achieve goals. In contrast, adolescents with low self-efficacy are more likely to avoid problems when faced with challenges, leading to an accumulation of issues [22]. This can trigger negative emotions such as loneliness, depressive symptoms, and suicidal ideation, and may lead to the use of relatively easy methods to alleviate these negative emotions, such as smoking, drinking, internet addiction, and self-harming behaviors. Furthermore, other studies have demonstrated a significant negative correlation between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and self-efficacy. The development of self-efficacy is largely based on the primary caregivers. A higher number of ACEs disrupts the learning environment for adolescents. Adolescents with fewer ACEs often believe that their needs can be met through effort, while those who have experienced abuse, neglect, or other adverse events during childhood tend to lack a sense of security and hold a pessimistic attitude towards the outcomes of their efforts, resulting in lower self-efficacy [23]. Based on these findings, this study proposes Hypothesis 2 (H2): Self-efficacy mediates the relationship between adverse childhood experiences and the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors.

The mediating role of self-control

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have a significant impact on the development of self-control. According to social learning theory, adolescents benefit from observing and imitating positive role models in the development of self-control. However, adolescents with a higher number of ACEs may be exposed to family members who engage in behaviors such as alcohol abuse, substance misuse, and domestic violence. The intentional or unintentional observation and imitation of these behaviors are detrimental to the development of self-control in adolescents, Furthermore, from a neuroscientific perspective, ACEs can disrupt the normal development of the brain, particularly affecting the endocrine system and the development of the prefrontal cortex, which is crucial for self-control [16, 24]. This disruption can lead to a reduction in self-control abilities. Previous studies have shown that higher self-control in adolescents is significantly associated with positive outcomes such as subjective well-being [25], prosocial behavior [26], emotional regulation [27], and academic performance [28]. Conversely, lower self-control is significantly associated with higher prevalence of Health Risk Behaviors, including suicidal ideation [29], anxiety and depression [30], and internet addiction [31]. These findings highlight the critical role of self-control in mitigating the risk of engaging in behaviors that are detrimental to one’s health. Based on these findings, this study proposes Hypothesis 3 (H3): Self-control mediates the relationship between adverse childhood experiences and the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors.

The chained mediating role of self-efficacy and self-control

Adolescents with a higher prevalence of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are more likely to encounter setbacks and failures, which can lead to negative self-assessments and doubts about their capabilities, thereby weakening their self-efficacy [32]. Adolescents with high self-efficacy tend to believe in their ability to achieve goals and are more likely to plan effectively, resist temptations, and regulate their behavior to achieve self-control. In contrast, those with low self-efficacy are more prone to self-doubt and are likely to give up when faced with setbacks, resulting in poorer self-control [33]. Adolescents with weak self-control often struggle to resist immediate temptations and are more likely to engage in impulsive behaviors. They may simultaneously fall into multiple Health Risk Behaviors, such as smoking, drinking, and smartphone addiction. Moreover, once they engage in one type of unhealthy behavior, they are susceptible to peer influence and may sequentially engage in other harmful behaviors, leading to the co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors. Previous studies have also demonstrated a strong correlation between self-control and the clustering of multiple types of Health Risk Behaviors [34]. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes Hypothesis 4: Self-efficacy and self-control play a sequential mediating role between adverse childhood experiences and the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors.

Current research

From the literature review above, it is evident that current research on the Co-occurrence of health risk behaviors primarily focuses on the prevalence of Co-occurrence [35–37] and the models of co-occurrence [38, 39]. Some studies have also explored the influencing factors of Co-occurrence, such as sociocultural factors [40]and socioeconomic status [41]. However, the impact of Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) on the Co-occurrence of health risk behaviors and the underlying mechanisms have not yet been investigated. In this study, we employed structural equation modeling (SEM) to examine the relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and the Co-occurrence of health risk behaviors and introduced variables such as self-efficacy and self-control to explore the mechanisms through which adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) influence the Co-occurrence of health risk behaviors. This study provides a novel theoretical model for understanding the psychological mechanisms underlying the Co-occurrence of health risk behaviors in adolescents. The findings hold substantial significance for adolescent health education and psychological intervention, as well as for promoting the overall physical and mental well-being of adolescents. Theoretically, they elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which adverse childhood experiences influence the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors, addressing the gap in research on chain mediation mechanisms and enriching the theoretical framework in the field of ACEs and adolescent health. Practically, they offer more concrete and actionable guidance for interventions at multiple levels—including family, school, and clinical settings—thereby enhancing their effectiveness. At the policy level, the results provide an empirical foundation and valuable reference for public health authorities in formulating prevention and control strategies.

Materials and methods

Participants

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated using the simple random sampling formula [n = z2×p×(1-p)/d2]. Based on previous studies on adolescent Health Risk Behaviors, the probability of clustering of these behaviors ranges from 48% to 69% [42]. When p = 48%, p༈1-p༉reaches its maximum value. The z-value was set at 1.96 corresponding to a 95% confidence interval (CI), and the allowable error (tolerance) was set at 0.05. Therefore, the sample size per group was estimated to be 384. Considering additional factors such as gender, educational stage, urban vs. rural background, and whether the child is an only child, the final actual sample size was determined to be 7,901 participants.

Sampling method

A multi-stage stratified cluster sampling method was employed to select the study participants. In the first stage, two counties were randomly selected from each of Hunan, Hubei, Guizhou, and Chongqing, resulting in a total of eight counties. In the second stage, two rural junior high schools, two rural senior high schools, two urban junior high schools, and two urban senior high schools were selected from each county, yielding a total of 64 schools. In the third stage, one class was randomly selected from each grade in every school. Students with intellectual disabilities, significant cognitive impairments, severe psychological instability, or who declined to provide informed consent were excluded. All eligible students were included, resulting in 8,132 participants. After excluding 231 invalid questionnaires due to less than 90% completion, logical inconsistencies, patterned responses, or abnormal response times, 7,901 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective recovery rate of 97.2%. This high recovery rate suggests that the data are highly reliable. This survey was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jishou University (Approval No.: JXDX-2023-0011), and all data collected will be used solely for scientific research purposes. During the research process, no information that could identify the identities of the research subjects was collected, and the data was managed using encoded anonymous identifiers. Prior to the survey, the research team contacted the relevant school management departments, introduced the purpose, process, and ethical approval of the survey, and obtained the schools’ consent and support. Given that the study participants were minors, informed consent from parents or legal guardians was obtained in accordance with ethical guidelines for research involving minors. Before the survey, participants were informed about the purpose, process, ethical approval, and confidentiality measures of the study. Finally, this study follows the principle of voluntary participation, and participants can freely withdraw from the survey at any time.

Methods

The study utilized the “Adolescent Health Risk Behaviors and Influencing Factors Survey Questionnaire” for data collection. This questionnaire was designed by referencing the “Comprehensive Report on Health-Related/Harmful Behaviors Among Chinese Adolescents 2005” [43] and was refined through a pilot survey to align with the objectives of this study. The pilot survey included 261 participants. Results indicated that most unintentional injuries were attributable to accidents, while the response rate for items on sexual behaviors leading to sexually transmitted diseases and unintended pregnancies was low. Consequently, these items were removed, and the wording of several questions was refined to improve the accuracy of the survey. Ultimately, nine categories of health risk behaviors—picky eating, smoking, drinking, violent behavior, insomnia, self-harm, depressive symptoms, smartphone dependence, and insufficient physical exercise—were retained to align with the objectives of this study. The questionnaire consists of two main sections.

The first section collects basic demographic information. This part was developed in-house and includes items such as gender, grade, ethnicity, whether the respondent is an only child, living arrangements (boarding or not), whether the respondent is a left-behind child, academic performance, parental education levels, parental occupations, and family structure.

The second section assesses Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs), Self-Efficacy, Self-Control Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors:

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs). This study utilized the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACEQ), translated and revised by Xiao Qin et al. [44]. The questionnaire assesses 10 types of adverse experiences during childhood, including abuse, neglect, and family dysfunction. It comprises 10 items (e.g., “Did you experience physical abuse such as being beaten or kicked during your childhood?“). Responses are scored on a dichotomous scale (0 = no, 1 = yes), with scores summed to generate an ACE score for each participant, ranging from 0 to 10. Higher scores indicate a greater number of adverse childhood experiences. In this study, the questionnaire demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.73 and a 14-day test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.89 (p < 0.05).

Picky eating behavior was assessed using the question, “Do you dislike eating certain types of food, such as vegetables, fruits, meats, or soy products?” Respondents who answered “yes” were classified as having picky eating behavior.

Smoking behavior was assessed with the question, “How many days have you smoked in the past month?” Those who answered “1 day or more” were considered to have smoking behavior.

Alcohol consumption behavior was assessed with the question, “How many days have you drunk alcohol in the past month?” Those who answered “1 day or more” were considered to have alcohol consumption behavior.

Violent behavior was assessed with the question, “How many times have you physically fought with others in the past month?” Those who answered “1 time or more” were considered to have violent behavior.

Insomnia behavior was assessed with the question, “Have you had insomnia due to excessive worry about something in the past month?” Those who answered “Yes” were considered to have insomnia behavior.

Self-injurious behavior was assessed with the question, “How many times have you engaged in self-injurious behaviors (such as hitting, scratching, cutting, pinching, burning yourself, head - banging, hair - pulling, etc.) in the past month?” Those who answered “1 time or more” were considered to have self-injurious behavior.

Lack of physical exercise was assessed with the question, “How many days per week do you engage in physical exercise for at least 1 hour in the past month?” Those who answered “2 days or less” were considered to have a lack of physical exercise.

Quality control

Prior to the survey, enumerators underwent unified training. Questionnaires were distributed with the cooperation of class teachers. Enumerators supervised the survey process on-site, answered any questions, and checked the completion status of the questionnaires upon collection to ensure the quality of the responses. The survey was conducted on a class-by-class basis using anonymous questionnaires. All questionnaires were distributed and collected on the spot. The average time taken by participants to complete the questionnaires was approximately 20 min.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0. Common method bias was assessed using factor analysis. The correlations between variables were examined using Pearson correlation analysis. The chained mediating effects were tested using the SPSS Process v 3.5 macro (Model 6) with a Bootstrap method involving 5000 resamples. The significance level for all tests was set at α = 0.05.

Mediating effect analysis

After controlling for covariates including gender, education level, urban-rural background, and only-child status, we examined the mediating roles of self-efficacy and self-control in the relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) (independent variable) and the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors (dependent variable).

Table 3 systematically presents the regression coefficients and significance levels among all variables, while Fig. 2 intuitively illustrates the path association patterns between variables. The results showed that: ACEs negatively predicted self-efficacy and self-control (self-efficacy: β = −0.159, t = −14.321, 95% CI: −0.181 ~ −0.137; self-control: β = −0.246, t = −23.555, 95% CI: −0.267 ~ −0.226), indicating that higher ACEs exposure was associated with lower self-efficacy and self-control ability; Self-efficacy positively predicted self-control (β = 0.275, t = 26.301, 95% CI: 0.255 ~ 0.296), suggesting that improved self-efficacy could enhance individuals’ self-control; ACEs positively predicted the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors (β = 0.347, t = 36.791, 95% CI: 0.328 ~ 0.365), whereas both self-efficacy and self-control negatively predicted the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors (self-efficacy: β = −0.074, t = −7.812, 95% CI: −0.093 ~ −0.056; self-control: β = −0.369, t = −37.701, 95% CI: −0.389 ~ −0.350). Specifically, lower ACEs exposure, higher self-efficacy, and stronger self-control were linked to a reduced risk of co-occurring health risk behaviors. Table 4 further reports the specific proportions of mediating effects: the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of all three mediating paths did not include 0, confirming the significance of the mediating effects. In terms of effect contribution, the direct effect of ACEs on the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors accounted for 74.5% of the total effect, serving as the primary pathway. The three mediating paths collectively accounted for 25.5% of the total effect, with the “ACEs → self-control → co-occurrence of health risk behaviors” path contributing the most (19.6%), followed by the “ACEs → self-efficacy → co-occurrence of health risk behaviors” path (2.5%) and the “ACEs → self-efficacy → self-control → co-occurrence of health risk behaviors” path (3.4%).

Discussion

The study results indicate that adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are significantly associated with self-efficacy, self-control, and the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors. Mediation effect analysis revealed that the impact of ACEs on the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors is mediated through both direct and indirect pathways. Specifically, ACEs exhibited the most substantial direct effect on the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors, accounting for the largest proportion (74.5%). Even after introducing self-efficacy and self-control as mediating variables, the positive predictive effect of ACEs on the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors remained statistically significant. All three mediation pathways demonstrated partial mediation effects. Of these, the pathway mediated by self-efficacy explained the smallest proportion (2.5%), whereas the pathway mediated by self-control accounted for the largest proportion (19.6%). These findings suggest that ACEs and self-control are critical intervention targets for addressing the clustering of health risk behaviors among adolescents. When designing intervention strategies, priority should be given to mitigating the long-term consequences of ACEs and enhancing adolescents’ self-control capabilities.

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have been shown to significantly and positively predict the co-occurrence of health-risk behaviors, thereby supporting Hypothesis H1 and aligning with previous findings [19]. ACEs encompass a variety of adverse exposures, such as domestic violence, abuse, neglect, witnessing family violence, family member suicide or death, and the presence of severe mental health problems or substance abuse within the family [16]. According to social learning theory and ecological systems theory, individuals have a strong ability to imitate during childhood and adolescence. Exposure to violence and substance abuse during this stage can significantly influence the development of health-risk behaviors, including violent behavior, smoking, and alcohol consumption [45]. Latent profile analysis has further revealed that exposure to violence is a key determinant of adolescents’ involvement in school bullying, with the type and context of violence exposure playing a significant role [53]. It is also noteworthy that under China’s specific sociocultural and socioeconomic conditions, factors such as incomplete family structures, unequal access to educational resources due to regional development disparities, and the influence of traditional cultural norms (e.g., son preference) may contribute to regionally distinct patterns of adverse childhood experiences among adolescents. These contextual factors may further increase the likelihood of several specific health risk behaviors co-occurrence, including smoking, alcohol use, violent behavior, school bullying, and depressive symptoms [42].The findings of this study underscore the critical role of early life experiences in shaping long-term developmental outcomes, highlighting the need to establish supportive environments for children’s growth across multiple levels—family, school, and society. Implementing protective interventions and providing positive psychosocial guidance are essential to preventing the onset of adverse childhood experiences.

The mediation effect analysis revealed that self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors, thereby supporting Hypothesis H2. Self-efficacy is a critical factor influencing adolescents’ learning motivation [54], directly affecting how they cope with and resolve stress and challenges in their academic and daily lives. Adolescents who experience a happy childhood tend to develop higher self-efficacy, and as they grow up, they are more likely to believe that they can overcome difficulties and achieve success through their own efforts when facing stress and challenges. In contrast, adolescents who experience a higher number of adverse events during childhood, such as abuse, domestic violence, or parental emotional detachment, often accumulate a sense of learned helplessness and lack sufficient security, leading to low self-efficacy. As they mature, they are more likely to doubt their abilities when facing setbacks, stress, and challenges, and may resort to negative emotions and extreme behaviors to cope with stress and adversity. Moreover, adolescents with higher self-efficacy typically possess better emotional regulation skills. These skills play a self-regulatory and protective role when adolescents encounter setbacks or emotional distress, helping to reduce the occurrence of various Health Risk Behaviors [55]. Although the mediation effect of self-efficacy was statistically significant in this study, the actual effect size was relatively small, indicating that self-efficacy only played a minor mediating role between adverse childhood experiences and Health Risk Behaviors. Therefore, it is suggested that self-efficacy be considered in combination with other mediating variables for a more comprehensive analysis. When designing interventions, it is also essential to take into account other mediating variables to ensure the effectiveness of the interventions.

The mediation effect analysis demonstrated that self-control partially mediated the relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors, thereby validating Hypothesis H3. This finding is consistent with the previous research results [46]. According to Kremen, self-control is formed and developed early in life, with key family functions during child-rearing, such as parent-child relationships, parenting styles, parental acceptance and control, and support, being critical determinants of self-control development [56]. Relevant studies have shown that self-control is an important predictor of multiple health risk behaviors, including smoking, drinking, fighting, and aggressive behaviors [57]. Adolescents with higher self-control exhibit more rational control over their behaviors and emotions, enabling them to better resist external temptations and reduce both internal and external levels of aggression. The current study not only corroborates previous research findings but also elucidates a pathway through which Health Risk Behaviors co-occurrence in adolescents. Self-control serves as a key target for intervening in the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors. Strengthening self-control can effectively mitigate the long-term impacts of adverse childhood experiences on adolescents. When designing interventions, it is recommended to incorporate training programs aimed at improving self-control to disrupt the negative trajectory initiated by adverse childhood experiences.

The fourth objective of this study was to investigate the chain mediation effect of self-efficacy and self-control between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors. Our study results confirmed Hypothesis H4, namely that self-efficacy and self-control exert a chain mediating effect between ACEs and the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors. Specifically, self-efficacy influences self-control, which in turn affects the co-occurrence of health-risk behaviors, thereby forming the chain mediating pathway. This chain mediation effect demonstrates that self-efficacy positively predicts the development of self-control, corroborating previous research findings [58]. To explore the underlying mechanisms, Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory posits that self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to complete specific tasks and serves as a core driver of behavioral choices, effort, and persistence [59]. Adverse childhood experiences can undermine self-efficacy, thereby reducing an individual’s sense of control over life. Additionally, according to Baumeister’s ego depletion theory [60], self-control relies on limited psychological resources. Individuals with low self-efficacy, who are often in a state of “self-doubt” are more likely to deplete these limited resources, further weakening their self-control abilities and leading to the occurrence of multiple Health Risk Behaviors. This suggests that enhancing self-efficacy can reduce unnecessary mental resource depletion, thereby preserving an individual’s self-control resources and sustaining their self-control ability, which in turn further prevents the occurrence of multiple health risk behaviors. Furthermore, integrating previous studies, the present research finds a complex bidirectional relationship between self-efficacy and self-control. Self-efficacy is not only an antecedent factor of self-control but also an outcome of self-control, forming a dynamic system that collectively influences the behavioral development of adolescents. Individuals with high self-efficacy are more willing to face challenges, thereby exercising their self-control abilities. Conversely, good self-control helps achieve goals, which in turn enhances self-efficacy. Therefore, in specific practice, self-efficacy and self-control require collaborative intervention so as to achieve the sustainability of behavioral change.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, due to the nature of the research content, we adopted a self-reported questionnaire, which is well-suited to capturing participants’ subjective experiences. However, inherent biases in self-report measures (e.g., recall bias) may have introduced some degree of error. Second, the study employed a cross-sectional design. While this allows examination of associations among variables, it does not permit strong causal inferences. Future research should therefore consider large-scale longitudinal or cohort studies. Third, the sample was drawn from four provinces/municipalities—Hunan, Hubei, Guizhou, and Chongqing—which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Subsequent studies should expand to other regions to test the broader applicability of the conclusions.

Conclusions

Teenagers are the future of the world, and their health is of vital importance to humanity’s tomorrow. This study investigated the impact mechanisms of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) on the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors in adolescents, as well as the chain mediation roles of self-efficacy and self-control in this process. Our findings revealed that: (1) ACEs significantly and positively predict the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors; (2) self-efficacy partially mediates the relationship between ACEs and the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors; (3) self-control partially mediates the relationship between ACEs and the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors; and (4) self-efficacy and self-control act as chain mediators between ACEs and the Co-occurrence of Health Risk Behaviors. These findings enhance our understanding of the underlying mechanisms linking ACEs to the co-occurrence of health risk behaviors and provide valuable insights for interventions targeting adolescents’ Health Risk Behaviors in schools, hospitals, and other relevant departments. When formulating intervention plans, it is recommended to incorporate relevant training programs such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), parent-child interaction therapy (PCIT), mindfulness intervention, trauma-informed education, and related exercise prescriptions to mitigate the long-term impact of adverse childhood experiences. In addition, school-based courses integrating sports and health education, emotional training, delayed gratification exercises, and attribution reconstruction training can be carried out to enhance teenagers’ self-control ability and self-efficacy, and prevent the emergence of the Co-occurrence of health risk behaviors among teenagers.
