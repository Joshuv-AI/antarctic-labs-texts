Nonlinear Association Between Metabolic Syndrome Severity and Advanced Fibrosis in Metabolic Dysfunction–Associated Steatotic Liver Disease
Authors: Mohamed I. Elsaid, Yesung Kweon, Na Li, Soledad Fernández, Electra D. Paskett, Khalid Mumtaz, Vinod K. Rustgi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsMetabolic dysfunction drives hepatic progression in metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), yet conventional binary metabolic syndrome (MetS) definitions may obscure dose-response risk of fibrosis. We evaluated the association between MetS severity and advanced fibrosis probability in a representative US MASLD cohort.MethodsAdults aged 20–74 years with ultrasound-defined MASLD in the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey were analyzed. Exposure was the validated MetS severity Z-score (quartiles, percentile categories, and continuous percentile with restricted cubic splines). Outcome was the Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease Fibrosis Score (NFS)–defined advanced fibrosis probability (intermediate-to-high: NFS ≥ −1.455; high: NFS > 0.676). Complex survey logistic regression estimated adjusted odds ratios (aORs).ResultsAmong 3036 participants, 29.6% had intermediate fibrosis probability and 5.2% had high advanced fibrosis probability. High fibrosis probability was most prevalent in non-Hispanic Black adults (8.0%) and least in Mexican Americans (2.6%; P value <.001). Approximately 2.5% of the low MetS severity group had a high probability of advanced fibrosis compared with 11.4% in the very high MetS severity group (P value <.001). Each one-quartile decrease in MetS severity was associated with 21% (aOR = 0.79; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.68–0.91) and 32% (aOR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.48–0.96) lower odds of intermediate-to-high and high probability of advanced fibrosis, respectively. In dose-response analyses, advanced fibrosis risk rose modestly through midrange severities and accelerated beyond the 70th severity percentile, reaching aOR of 3.59 (95% CI: 2.74–4.70) at the 90th vs the 50th percentile.ConclusionMetS severity demonstrates a graded and nonlinear association with advanced fibrosis probability in MASLD. A continuous metabolic-burden measure may enhance upstream triage and risk stratification alongside existing noninvasive pathways.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most common cause of chronic liver disease globally, with an estimated prevalence of 30% in adults.1 Over the years, the global prevalence of NAFLD has risen significantly, increasing from 26% in studies conducted before 2005 to 38% in those conducted after 2016.1 This rise is closely linked to the recent surge in obesity-induced metabolic syndrome (MetS),2 particularly in the United States, where over 40% of adults are affected by obesity.3 Consequently, the current prevalence of NAFLD among the US adults is estimated to be between 19% and 40%.4, 5, 6 Recognizing the critical role of cardiometabolic risk factors, a recent consensus redefined NAFLD as metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), underscoring the need for more nuanced risk assessment in this evolving field.7 While both NAFLD and MASLD are characterized by hepatic steatosis without significant alcohol consumption or other secondary causes of chronic liver disease, MASLD is distinguished by steatosis accumulation in individuals exhibiting key metabolic dysfunction factors, including obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and hypertension.8

MASLD increases the risk of more advanced liver diseases, such as metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), MASH fibrosis, MASH cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. In fact, an estimated 20% of patients with MASLD progress to MASH, of which 10%–25% progress to MASH fibrosis and a further 2%–13% progress to hepatocellular carcinoma.4 In the United States, the annual prevalence of MASH has increased from 1.5% in 2010 to 2.8% in 2020.9 While patients with MASLD typically do not require medical therapy, MASH, especially MASH fibrosis, is the target for innovative interventions.10,11 Patients with MASLD-related fibrosis have a higher risk of developing end-stage liver disease compared to those with simple steatosis.12,13 A higher fibrosis stage is associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality, liver-related mortality, and liver transplant in patients with MASLD.14,15 As a result, MASH-related fibrosis is the most rapidly rising cause of liver transplantation and is projected to be the leading cause in the coming years.16,17

Because hepatic steatosis in MASLD is closely linked to obesity-driven insulin resistance, MASLD is often described as the hepatic manifestation of MetS.4,18 MetS refers to a group of metabolic syndrome features (MSFs; ie, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, and central obesity) that increase the risk of insulin resistance and cardiovascular disease.2,19 In practice, MetS is diagnosed using the harmonized Adult Treatment Panel (ATP) III criteria when ≥3 of the 5 MSFs are present.2 While insulin resistance has been suggested as the primary risk factor for fibrosis in MASLD, the combined effects of MetS severity and hepatic progression in MASLD remain poorly understood.2,20 Specifically, the harmonization approach to defining MetS has the following 3 key limitations for studying hepatic progression in MASLD: (1) it treats all combinations of any 3 MSFs as equivalent, although the prevalence and pathophysiology of individual components differ across patients21; (2) it disregards severity, capturing only whether thresholds are crossed; and (3) it cannot quantify worsening of metabolic burden over time.

These limitations of the traditional MetS definition necessitate a more precise tool for assessing metabolic burden and its impact on hepatic progression. To address these limitations, the MetS severity score was developed and validated as a sex- and race/ethnicity-specific continuous Z-score that integrates all 5 MetS components through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).22 Conceptually, this continuous severity metric should better represent the dose-response relationship between metabolic dysfunction and the risk of hepatic fibrosis than the dichotomous MetS. Understanding the link between MetS severity and fibrosis will enhance understanding of the risk factors associated with hepatic progression in MASLD, aid risk stratification, and support the development of personalized treatment plans for patients with MASLD. To further elucidate this link, our study aimed to examine the effect of MetS severity on the probability of advanced fibrosis in patients with MASLD.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

The National Center for Health Statistics of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention conducted the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES III) between 1988 and 1994. The survey aimed to evaluate the health status of the US population, focusing on minority groups, including non-Hispanic Black individuals, Mexican Americans, and individuals aged ≥60 years. A stratified multistage clustered probability design was employed to construct a representative sample of noninstitutionalized members of the US population aged 2 months or older. The survey incorporated cross-sectional examinations, interview questionnaires, and laboratory sample collection. Of all the interviewed participants, 78% participated in the physical examination phase of the survey.23 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention ethics review board approved the NHANES III protocol. The NHANES III database is publicly available and meets the criteria for limited use datasets.

Study Sample

For all NHANES III participants aged 20–74 years, hepatic steatosis was assessed by 3 trained ultrasound readers using gallbladder ultrasound videos recorded during physical examinations. Following the initial assessments, all ultrasound readings were re-evaluated and validated by a certified radiologist specializing in hepatic imaging. Hepatic images were classified into the following 4 steatosis-based groups: normal, mild, moderate, and severe.24 MASLD was identified by the presence of mild, moderate, or severe hepatic steatosis in the absence of excessive drinking (ie, more than 3 alcoholic beverages per day for males and more than 2 alcoholic beverages per day for females), binge drinking (ie, frequent consumption of 5 or more alcoholic drinks per day), alcohol consumption restrictions due to illness, positive hepatitis B virus surface antigen test, positive hepatitis C virus RNA test, or iron overload (ie, transferrin saturation ≥50%). Because MetS severity was the primary exposure in our study, we did not include cardiometabolic assessment in our MASLD definition to avoid potential confounding. Accordingly, our current MASLD definition falls under the cryptogenic steatotic liver disease or possible MASLD categories of the new MASLD nomenclature.7

Participants were excluded if they had missing values for exposure, outcome, alcohol intake, ultrasound images, or any covariates included in the adjusted analyses. Participants who self-identified as “Other” race/ethnicity were also excluded because the exposure assessment applied only to non-Hispanic Whites, non-Hispanic Blacks, and Hispanics. Of the 16,573 individuals aged ≥20 years who attended the examination phase of the survey, 14,707 qualified for gallbladder ultrasound, of whom 13,856 had readable images.25 A total of 5484 participants had mild, moderate, or severe hepatic steatosis on ultrasound, of which 3036 MASLD participants met the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Exposure

The MetS severity score was the primary exposure variable in this study. The MetS severity score is a clinically validated sex- and race/ethnicity-specific Z-score that captures the relative MetS severity of all 5 metabolic abnormalities.22 The score was quantified using CFA with nationally representative data from the 1999 to 2010 NHANES surveys.22 The MetS severity score is significantly correlated with pathophysiological biomarkers of MetS, including the homeostasis model for insulin resistance, C-reactive protein, uric acid, and adiponectin.22,26 Multiple studies have also shown that the MetS severity score is a significant predictor of long-term risks of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease,26, 27, 28, 29 and hepatic outcomes, including MASLD and mortality risk in participants with MASLD.30,31

All 5 metabolic features were used in the CFA to construct a summary score, a continuous representation of the conventional MetS characterization. Participants in the 1999–2010 NHANES were classified into the following 6 groups by sex and self-identified race/ethnicity: non-Hispanic Whites, non-Hispanic Blacks, and Hispanics. Different loading coefficients were quantified to determine a single latent MetS factor across all the 6 participant subgroups. Individual-level data for high-density lipoprotein, systolic blood pressure, waist circumference, triglyceride, and fasting blood glucose were used to calculate sex- and race/ethnicity-specific MetS severity Z-scores according to the score’s standardized equations.22 The MetS severity Z-score was transformed into 4 percentile-based categories (low [0–50th], moderate [>50th–75th], high [>75th–90th], and very high [>90th]). We also examined the effects of increased MetS severity quartiles.

The ATP III guidelines were used to define the traditional MetS classification. As such, MetS is clinically determined by the presence of 3 of the following 5 metabolic factors: (1) hyperglycemia (ie, fasting blood glucose over 100 mg/dL, or pharmacological treatment); (2) dyslipidemia (ie, fasting high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level less than 40 mg/dL, men, or 50 mg/dL, women, or pharmacological treatment); (3) hypertriglyceridemia (ie, triglyceride level over 150 mg/dL, or pharmacological treatment); (4) central obesity (ie, waist circumference over 40 inches for men, or 35 inches for women); or (5) hypertension (ie, systolic blood pressure over 130 mmHg, or pharmacological treatment).19

Outcome

The primary outcome of this study was the presence of advanced fibrosis in adult patients with MASLD. The Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease Fibrosis Score (NFS) was used to estimate the probability of advanced fibrosis in NAFLD.15 The NFS uses the body mass index, aspartate aminotransferase to alanine aminotransferase ratio, platelet count, albumin levels, and diabetes status.32 The NFS score has an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.85 for predicting advanced fibrosis. An NFS score of 0.676 or above has a specificity of 97% and 67% sensitivity for detecting bridging fibrosis in NAFLD.4 A cutoff point of less than −1.455 has a negative predictive value of 88% for excluding participants with advanced fibrosis. With this in mind, an individual NFS value ≥−1.455 was used to define an intermediate-to-high probability of advanced fibrosis. At the same time, a score cutoff of >0.676 was used to indicate a high probability of advanced fibrosis.

Statistical Analyses

Complex survey methods were used to yield nationally representative estimates using sampling weights, strata, and clusters. Taylor series linearization was used to estimate variance and account for the effects of the survey design. Missing values related to the variance estimation were assumed not to be missing completely at random. The baseline characteristics of participants with MASLD, stratified by MetS severity quartiles, were examined using t-tests for continuous variables, analysis of variance, and Rao-Scott chi-square tests for categorical variables. To assess the association between MetS and the probability of advanced fibrosis in MASLD, age-, sex-, and race/ethnicity-adjusted mean estimates of clinical characteristics associated with MetS were quantified. We also evaluated the probability distribution of advanced fibrosis among participants with MASLD by self-identified race and ethnicity. Moreover, we quantified the probability distribution of advanced fibrosis across MetS severity groups in all adults with MASLD.

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to test the association between an increase in MetS severity and the odds of (1) intermediate-to-high advanced fibrosis probability and (2) high advanced fibrosis probability in MASLD. The dose-response relationships between MetS severity and the odds of advanced fibrosis probabilities in MASLD were evaluated using MetS severity score percentiles modeled as a continuous variable with a 3-knot restricted cubic spline in the adjusted logistic regression models. The 3 knots were placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th weighted MetS severity percentile for US adults. Wald chi-square tests were used to assess the overall and nonlinear associations between MetS severity score percentiles and the odds of advanced fibrosis in MASLD. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. All analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Participants Baseline Characteristics

The baseline characteristics of adults with MASLD, categorized by MetS severity quartiles, are presented in Table 1. The study sample consisted of 3036 adults from NHANES III who met the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Male sex was significantly associated with greater MetS severity (P value <.001). The mean age of participants with MASLD in the fourth quartile (highest severity) was 14.4 years higher than that in the first quartile (P value <.001). Increased MetS severity was associated with a higher prevalence of White non-Hispanics and Mexican Americans and a lower prevalence of Black non-Hispanic individuals. The proportion of current smokers and those who reported an average alcohol intake of >1 drink/day significantly decreased with increasing MetS severity.

Obesity-related factors were more prevalent among participants with MASLD in the higher MetS severity quartiles than among those in the lower MetS severity quartiles. Discussion

In a MASLD cohort, a continuous, sex- and race/ethnicity-calibrated MetS severity score showed a graded and ultimately nonlinear association with advanced fibrosis probability. Odds increased modestly across midrange severities and accelerated beyond the 70th percentile, reaching an OR of 3.59 (95% CI: 2.74–4.70) at the 90th percentile vs the 50th. Each one-quartile decrease in MetS severity corresponded to a 21% lower odds of intermediate-to-high fibrosis probability (aOR 0.79; 95% CI: 0.68–0.91) and a 32% lower odds of high probability (aOR 0.68; 95% CI: 0.48–0.96), with concordant gradients across severity categories. By demonstrating a dose-response link between MetS severity and advanced fibrosis probability in MASLD, these findings extend prior work, including ours, showing that a CFA-derived, continuous MetS severity construct captures pathophysiologic burden and predicts adverse cardiometabolic events and hepatic outcomes more effectively than the dichotomous ATP-III MetS criteria.30,31,33

The threshold-like rise in fibrosis probability that we observe at higher MetS severity percentiles is mechanistically coherent with MASLD pathogenesis. Excess metabolic load drives lipotoxicity, the accumulation of bioactive lipids (eg, ceramides, diacylglycerols) that trigger endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial stress, n-terminal kinase activation, and insulin resistance signals in hepatocytes.34 In turn, these processes promote hepatocyte injury, cell death, and senescence-associated inflammatory signaling.34, 35, 36 Injured hepatocytes and activated Kupffer cells release profibrogenic mediators (notably transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, platelet‑derived growth factor (PDGF), and inflammatory cytokines) that activate hepatic stellate cells into matrix-producing myofibroblasts, establishing feed-forward loops of fibrogenesis that could accelerate once compensatory mechanisms are exceeded.37,38 In parallel, disruption of the gut-liver axis (dysbiosis, increased permeability, and altered bile-acid signaling) delivers microbial products, such as lipopolysaccharide, to the portal circulation, engaging toll-like receptor pathways in hepatic cells and potentiating stellate-cell activation and matrix deposition, another potential threshold effect when barrier function deteriorates.39,40 The convergence of lipotoxic, inflammatory, and hepatic stellate cell–activating pathways offers a biological rationale for the observed inflection point beyond the 70th percentile of MetS severity, as well as the progressive deterioration of metabolic characteristics across different strata of fibrosis probability.

The distribution we observe, 29.6% with intermediate and 5.2% with high advanced fibrosis probability, fits within contemporary population estimates. In NHANES 2011–2018, among adults with NAFLD defined by the US Fatty Liver Index, 18.1% were high-probability by NFS ≥ 0.676. In contrast, only 3.7% were high-risk by fibrosis (FIB)-4 ≥ 2.67, underscoring that NFS typically flags a larger “at-risk” fraction than FIB-4.41 Transient elastography analyses yield lower absolute prevalence when expressed at the population level. In NHANES 2017–2018, the age-adjusted prevalence of NAFLD with F0–F1 and F2–F4 fibrosis was 18.9% and 4.4% of US adults,42 while other US transient elastography–based studies reported fibrosis (liver stiffness ≥7.9 kPa) prevalence between 10.4% and 14.5% pre-COVID-19.43,44 Methodological reviews corroborate that the choice of noninvasive test and thresholds (NFS vs FIB-4) materially shifts prevalence estimates, with NFS tending to overclassify risk in older/metabolic populations, whereas FIB-4 offers greater sensitivity at lower cutoffs.45 Taken together, our NFS-based proportions from an earlier NHANES era fall at the lower end of the range reported in more recent US cohorts using comparable instruments.46

In MASLD, we found the prevalence of advanced fibrosis to be highest amongst Black non-Hispanics (8.0%). This finding is consistent with results from a study using NHANES 2005–2016 data.47 In our sample, the age- and sex-adjusted prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in MASLD was 11.5%, 22.1%, and 18.5% among White non-Hispanics, Black non-Hispanics, and Mexican Americans, respectively. This translated into 10% and 3.7% higher prevalence of T2DM in Blacks with MASLD compared to Whites and Mexican Americans. Insulin resistance is a key risk factor for the progression of liver fibrosis in MASLD.48,49 In a liver biopsy-based study, an increase in insulin resistance in severely obese patients was associated with 9.3 (95% CI: 3.4–26.0) higher adjusted odds of advanced fibrosis.50 In a cohort study with a median follow-up of 7.5 years, the risk of advanced fibrosis among individuals with T2DM was 1.88 times the risk among those without T2DM (hazard ratio = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.40–2.52).48 Those findings are in line with our results of an increase in the prevalence of advanced fibrosis with higher MetS severity, especially amongst Black, non-Hispanics, due to the higher burdens of diabetes and insulin resistance.

In our cohort, participants classified as Mexican Americans had the lowest prevalence of high advanced fibrosis probability. Although this appears discordant with the broader literature, it is important to distinguish NAFLD/MASLD prevalence from advanced fibrosis risk. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that Hispanics carry the highest overall NAFLD burden in the US but also noted that racial/ethnic differences in fibrosis-related outcomes are inconsistent across studies.51 Indeed, US population-based analyses have reported mixed findings for advanced fibrosis. Namely, 1 NHANES study found that Mexican American ethnicity was protective against NFS-defined advanced fibrosis,52 whereas another reported the highest advanced fibrosis prevalence among non-Hispanic Black adults.53 NHANES III oversampled Mexican Americans, but it was not designed to represent the full spectrum of US Hispanic/Latino subgroups. Accordingly, our finding may therefore reflect, at least in part, the specific composition of the NHANES III Mexican Americans sample rather than the broader US Hispanic population. This distinction matters because Hispanic adults are not a homogeneous group. Mexican-origin adults have been shown to have substantially higher NAFLD prevalence than Puerto Rican or Dominican adults, suggesting meaningful subgroup heterogeneity in liver disease risk.54 Finally, this result should be interpreted in light of growing evidence that noninvasive fibrosis tools do not perform uniformly across racial/ethnic groups, raising the possibility of differential calibration in population-based comparisons.55,56

A growing body of evidence indicates that the continuous MetS severity score provides incremental prognostic value over the traditional binary ATP-III MetS definition for both cardiometabolic and hepatic outcomes. In large US cohorts, the sex- and race-specific MetS severity Z-score was more strongly associated with future disease risk than a yes/no MetS diagnosis, even after accounting for individual risk factors.27, 28, 29 For example, Gurka et. al. found the participants in the highest MetS severity quartile had a significantly higher hazard of incident diabetes (17-fold vs the lowest quartile), whereas the presence of ATP-III MetS conferred only a 4-fold increased risk.28 In addition, the MetS severity score remained a significant predictor of incident diabetes when ATP-III-defined MetS components were included in the models. Incorporating the MetS severity score into risk models has also improved predictive discrimination. One analysis showed that adding the score to standard diabetes risk equations modestly but significantly boosted model fit and discrimination and yielded net reclassification gains, particularly by more accurately excluding individuals who would remain event-free.27 This suggests the severity construct captures subtle metabolic risk that binary criteria can miss.

The MetS severity score has demonstrated value in MASLD/NAFLD settings as well.33 Cross-sectional analyses of NHANES data show that a 1 standard deviation increase in MetS severity is associated with a 1.6-fold increase in the odds of advanced fibrosis. Furthermore, in a US NAFLD cohort, a higher MetS severity was an independent predictor of mortality: every one-quartile increase in the score corresponded to a 36% increase in all-cause mortality risk, with even larger relative hazards for cardiovascular and diabetes-related death.30 By quantifying metabolic derangement on a continuum, the MetS severity score thus enables more nuanced risk stratification and tracking than the dichotomous MetS definition, offering clinicians a practical tool to identify high-risk patients and to monitor risk trajectory for timely interventions. This incremental prognostic utility over binary MetS underscores the potential of the continuous MetS severity construct in improving risk assessment and clinical decision-making for cardiometabolic and hepatic outcomes in MASLD.

Our findings are not without limitations. The study used cross-sectional data, which did not allow for risk quantification. However, the prevalence of high probability of advanced fibrosis is well below the 10% threshold for the OR to estimate relative risk. MASLD was assessed by ultrasonography, which may lead to misclassification. The probability of advanced fibrosis was assessed using noninvasive scores. However, a cutoff >0.676 was chosen because it yields 97% specificity for advanced fibrosis.4 This is essential since patients must undergo a liver biopsy to confirm a fibrosis diagnosis. Increased specificity will ensure the score correctly identifies 97% of those without advanced fibrosis. Ascertainments of exposure and baseline characteristics were conducted cross-sectionally. Alcohol intake was assessed by self-report, which may have led to underestimation. Although the MetS severity score was derived from nationally representative NHANES 1999–2010 data, secular shifts in metabolic risk patterns may limit perfect calibration in contemporary populations and support the need for validation in newer cohorts. An additional limitation is that NHANES III was conducted from 1988 to 1994 and thus reflects a historical US metabolic risk profile. Because obesity, diabetes, and MASLD-related fibrosis burden have risen substantially since then, our absolute prevalence estimates and severity-threshold calibration may not fully generalize to contemporary MASLD populations.47,57,58 Nevertheless, the identified association between cumulative metabolic burden and the probability of advanced fibrosis remains biologically plausible and clinically informative and warrants validation in modern cohorts.

Conclusion

In a MASLD cohort, higher MetS severity was associated with a graded, nonlinear increase in the NFS-defined probability of advanced fibrosis. These findings support incorporating a continuous, sex- and race/ethnicity-calibrated MetS severity score into upstream noninvasive pathways to enrich fibrosis risk assessment and guide individualized management. Prospective studies using contemporary fibrosis tools and histology should evaluate calibration across racial/ethnic groups and test whether reductions in MetS severity translate into lower fibrosis risk and improved outcomes.
