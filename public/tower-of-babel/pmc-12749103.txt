Neurosurgical perspective on Pott’s puffy tumor management of both adult and pediatric patients in Tasmania, Australia: illustrative cases
Authors: Jim Weston, Imogen Ibbett, Nova Thani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDPott’s puffy tumor (PPT) is a subperiosteal abscess formation of the frontal bone with associated osteomyelitis. Few studies have described the range of neurosurgical techniques used or compared patient outcomes across different management strategies. These illustrative cases aim to compare the clinical course, surgical approaches, and outcomes in 4 pediatric patients and 1 adult patient with PPT.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report 5 illustrative cases of PPT in Tasmania. There were 3 males and 2 females with a mean age of 11 years. All patients presented with frontal headache, fever, and forehead edema after a trial of oral antibiotics in the community. One case resolved with medical therapy alone, while the remaining 4 required neurosurgical intervention. Among the operative cases, 1 patient underwent multiple procedures including a bifrontal craniectomy. Two patients underwent a frontal surgical incision and drainage, and 1 patient underwent needle aspiration. Fortunately, there were no long-term sequelae for any of the patients.LESSONSThe failure of medical management underscores the need for prompt neurosurgical referral with various surgical techniques available. Serious intracranial infection can result in significant surgical morbidity and mortality. Pott’s puffy tumor (PPT) is a rare but serious condition characterized by osteomyelitis of the frontal bone with associated subperiosteal abscess formation.1–5 It most commonly arises as a complication of inadequately treated or untreated frontal sinusitis.1–6 Alternative etiologies, including craniofacial trauma, intranasal drug use, postoperative complications, mastoiditis, and odontogenic infections, have also been implicated.7,8

Although PPT itself is uncommon, its complications are well documented and potentially life-threatening, including orbital cellulitis, intraorbital abscesses, epidural and subdural abscesses, and dural venous sinus thrombosis.3 Intracranial involvement is reported in 60%–85% of cases, with children at significantly higher risk, and up to three times more likely than adults to develop intracranial complications.1–5 Early recognition and specialist referral are essential for timely diagnosis, investigation, and management.9–11

Treatment typically involves a combination of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics and surgical intervention. Surgical options may include endoscopic sinus surgery, external drainage of abscesses, and intracranial procedures requiring neurosurgical input.9–15 Despite frequent neurosurgical involvement, there are limited studies addressing neurosurgical perspectives on the approaches and management of patients with PPT.10–15

This series of illustrative cases describes 4 pediatric and 1 adult patient presentations of PPT, demonstrating a spectrum of disease severity and treatment strategies. As there was 1 conservatively managed case and 4 requiring neurosurgical intervention, a wide range of neurosurgical techniques with various surgical approaches and outcomes were explored.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

An 8-year-old male with a history of otitis media treated with oral amoxicillin presented to the emergency department with headache, forehead swelling, right periorbital edema, and fever. MRI of the brain confirmed acute sinusitis with a right frontal epidural collection, consistent with PPT (Fig. 1). Inflammatory markers were elevated (C-reactive protein [CRP] 59 mg/L, white blood cell [WBC] count 23.1 × 109/L, neutrophils 19.4 × 109/L). He was managed nonoperatively and discharged from the hospital 2 weeks after admission on a regimen of intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g/day, planned for 6 weeks) and oral metronidazole (7.5 mg three times daily for 2 weeks). Three months postadmission, MRI of the brain demonstrated complete radiological resolution (Fig. 1), the antibiotics were ceased, and he was discharged from neurosurgical follow-up.

Admission (left) and follow-up (right) axial T1-weighted contrast MR images demonstrating resolution of the right frontal abscess.

Over the subsequent 12 months, while traveling across Australia, the patient experienced multiple ear and dental infections. The patient developed left-sided otalgia and was found to have a left second molar dental abscess that was surgically drained. The patient developed multiple left-sided ear infections requiring various topical ointments with and without antibiotics. The pattern of infections revealed a striking correlation with environmental exposures. When the patient consistently swam in saltwater for 6 months, he experienced no infections. However, during another 6-month period where he frequently swam in freshwater (e.g., hot springs in northern Australia), he experienced repeated episodes of otitis externa, including those confirmed to be due to Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection.

Despite the initial resolution of his intracranial and sinus disease, the recurrent ear and dental infections raise concerns regarding long-term mucosal vulnerability and highlight the need for multidisciplinary follow-up. Given a positive viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test, absence of bacterial growth, and the potential morbidity of surgery, the subsequent clinical course suggests that earlier or more frequent surgical follow-up, considering intervention or surgical management, may be warranted in select nonoperative cases.

Case 2

A 12-year-old male presented to the emergency department with left forehead swelling, periorbital edema, fever, and clinical features of sinusitis. He had been prescribed oral antibiotics in the community 1 week prior. Initial laboratory tests revealed elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 123 mg/L, WBC count 11.5 × 109/L). MRI of the brain demonstrated left-sided sinusitis with a rim-enhancing extracranial collection in the left temporalis region, without intraorbital or intracranial extension (Fig. 2).

Admission (left) and follow-up (right) coronal T1-weighted contrast MR images demonstrating resolution of the left temporalis abscess and left sinusitis.

He underwent surgical drainage via needle aspiration under general anesthesia. Four milliliters of purulent fluid was aspirated. Microscopy revealed leukocytes on Gram stain, and Streptococcus intermedius was isolated on culture. A respiratory PCR test was negative.

He was treated with 2 weeks of intravenous benzylpenicillin, followed by 4 weeks of oral amoxicillin. His total inpatient admission was 10 days. Two months postoperatively, MRI demonstrated complete radiological resolution, with no residual dural enhancement or fluid collection (Fig. 2). He remained clinically well and was discharged from neurosurgery.

Case 3

An 8-year-old girl initially presented to her general practitioner with left frontal forehead swelling and was empirically treated with cephazolin for presumed periorbital cellulitis. Despite 3 weeks of antibiotics, her symptoms progressed and she presented to the emergency department with worsening pain and swelling. Laboratory investigations revealed elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 49 mg/L, WBC count 17.1 × 109/L). MRI of the brain demonstrated acute sinusitis with a left subgaleal abscess, without intracranial extension (Fig. 3).

Admission sagittal (A) and axial (B) T1-weighted contrast MR images. Follow-up sagittal (C) and axial (D) T1-weighted contrast MR images.

Surgical drainage of the subgaleal abscess was performed under general anesthesia via a left frontal incision posterior to the hairline. Purulent material was evacuated, and a suction drain was inserted, which was subsequently removed on postoperative day 4 when outputs were negligible. Microbiological culture identified Streptococcus intermedius, and a respiratory PCR test was positive for parainfluenza virus, which was managed conservatively.

She was treated with 2 weeks of intravenous ceftriaxone and oral metronidazole, followed by 2 weeks of high-dose oral amoxicillin-clavulanate. Her total inpatient stay was 9 days, and she had two follow-up MRI studies. This was due to an initial noncontrast MRI study due to needle phobia. The 6-month postoperative MRI study confirmed complete resolution of the infection (Fig. 3), and she was discharged from neurosurgery.

Case 4

A 9-year-old girl presented to the emergency department with a 1-week history of headache, fever, and right periorbital swelling. She had been started on oral antibiotics by her general practitioner prior to presentation. Initial blood investigations revealed elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 38 mg/L, WBC count 12.6 × 109/L). MRI of the brain on admission demonstrated frontal sinusitis with a subperiosteal abscess and associated meningeal thickening (Fig. 4).

Admission (left) and follow-up (right) axial T2-weighted MR images.

She underwent surgical drainage of the subgaleal abscess under general anesthesia via a right frontal incision behind the hairline. A suction drain was placed intraoperatively and removed on postoperative day 3 when the output was negligible. Microscopy of the purulent material showed abundant leukocytes and gram-positive cocci; however, cultures remained negative.

She was discharged after 9 days in the hospital on a 2-week course of intravenous ceftriaxone, followed by 4 weeks of oral amoxicillin-clavulanate. Two months postoperatively, her MRI demonstrated complete resolution (Fig. 4). She remained clinically well and was discharged from neurosurgery.

Case 5

A 17-year-old male presented to the emergency department with bilateral periorbital edema and a 1-week history of headache. He had an emergency department visit 3 months prior for presumed viral tonsillitis. His history included intranasal drug use and frequent saline nasal spray use for presumed sinusitis.

On admission, he was afebrile but had markedly elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 289 mg/L, WBC count 13.5 × 109/L, neutrophils 9.6). Initial contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated pansinusitis with a subperiosteal abscess and a peripherally enhancing left frontal collection. He was started on intravenous flucloxacillin, ceftriaxone, and metronidazole. MRI of the brain confirmed a left frontal epidural collection, without evidence of venous sinus thrombosis or cerebritis (Fig. 5). Blood cultures grew Parvimonas micra. He initially responded to medical therapy and underwent functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) with bilateral frontosphenoethmoidectomy and septoplasty, and he was discharged soon thereafter following clinical and radiological stability.

Sagittal admission (A), post–bifrontal craniotomy (B), pre-craniectomy (C), and post-craniectomy (D) T1-weighted contrast MR images. Sagittal T1-weighted contrast MR image showing complete resolution of infection (E). Sagittal noncontrast CT scan of the brain showing cranioplasty (F).

He re-presented to the hospital 1 month after hospital discharge with fever, worsening headache, and nausea. MRI demonstrated progression of the left frontal epidural collection, new subdural abscess, and early cerebritis (Fig. 5). He underwent urgent bifrontal craniotomy with evacuation of purulent collections (frontal sinus, subdural and extradural) and cranialization of the frontal sinus. Intraoperative cultures grew Streptococcus milleri and Cutibacterium acnes. His postoperative course was complicated by a generalized tonic-clonic seizure on day 11 postoperatively, and MRI confirmed worsening cerebritis. He was started on levetiracetam and discharged 3 weeks postoperatively with intravenous flucloxacillin and oral metronidazole. The antibiotics were continued for 6 weeks postoperatively.

He re-presented 2 months after hospital discharge with worsening frontal headache and swelling. MRI demonstrated recurrent invasive frontal sinus disease with extradural extension (Fig. 5). He underwent bifrontal craniectomy and a modified Lothrop procedure with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgeons, including reexploration and washout of the frontal sinus, and clearance of a small extradural collection. ENT findings included obstructive polyposis, mucopurulence, and bony osteitis. He recovered uneventfully and was discharged later that month.

Three months postoperatively, outpatient MRI showed no residual extra-axial collection and improving dural enhancement (Fig. 5). Six weeks postoperatively, the intravenous antibiotics were switched to oral Augmentin Duo Forte. Five months postoperatively, the patient underwent a revision FESS procedure due to persistent sinus pathology. Oral antibiotics were ceased 6 months postoperatively following clinical resolution.

Eight months postoperatively, the patient re-presented to the hospital with fever and headache. Inflammatory markers were mildly elevated (CRP 39 mg/L, WBC count 12.8 × 109/L, neutrophils 9.6), and a respiratory PCR test was positive for parainfluenza virus. Blood cultures were negative. MRI excluded recurrent osteomyelitis or intracranial extension (Fig. 5). Swabs from the left frontal sinus grew P. aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. Initial intravenous Augmentin was de-escalated to oral ciprofloxacin and doxycycline. After a 6-week antibiotic-free period and a MRI study demonstrating no recurrence (Fig. 5), he successfully underwent cranioplasty with a custom 3D-printed PEEK implant (Fig. 5) and was formally discharged from neurosurgery.

Discussion

These 5 cases demonstrate the diverse clinical spectrum and management challenges of PPT.1–5 Conservative management may be successful in select patients but requires careful monitoring given the risk of intracranial spread.4,5 Given the limited studies addressing neurosurgical management of patients with PPT,10,12 a wide range of approaches were used,7,10,13–15 from minimally invasive drainage to extensive craniotomies or craniectomies, depending on the severity of bone involvement and intracranial extension.

Environmental factors, comorbid infections, and risk behaviors such as intranasal drug use may influence outcomes and recurrence risk.6,16 Multidisciplinary management with otolaryngology, infectious disease, and neurosurgery specialists is critical for optimizing outcomes.15

Imaging modalities such as MRI and CT play vital roles in diagnosis and surgical planning, with MRI with contrast providing superior soft tissue detail and early detection of intracranial involvement.12,14–16

Observations

Case 1 demonstrated a conservative, nonoperative management approach over a prolonged period of approximately 12 months. The patient experienced recurrent infections, and a concurrent dental infection may have contributed to ongoing symptoms, complicating the clinical picture. Notably, the patient remained infection free while swimming in saltwater, suggesting a possible environmental influence. Although initial cultures were negative, a Pseudomonas infection emerged on re-presentation. This raises the consideration that earlier surgical intervention may have prevented multiple hospital visits given that the PPT was thought to be the primary source.

Case 2 involved a more lateral collection within the temporalis muscle. Minimally invasive needle aspiration under general anesthesia, without drain placement, resulted in complete recovery. This patient did not re-present after discharge, suggesting that early targeted intervention in select cases may be appropriate.

Cases 3 and 4 shared similar clinical features and underwent small incisions and drainage behind the hairline with subgaleal drains on suction. Drains were removed once output was negligible, on days 4 and 3, respectively. Both cases demonstrated good recovery without recurrence at the 3-month follow-up. Interestingly, while case 4 showed meningeal thickening on MRI, both cases had similar outcomes, indicating that mild meningeal involvement may not necessarily predict poorer prognosis.

Case 5 was the most complex and only adult case, with repeated hospital admissions due to recurrent infections and headaches. A history of intranasal cocaine use was identified, potentially contributing to his persistent sinonasal and intracranial infections. Initial surgical management included a craniotomy and evacuation of epidural and subdural collections. However, the patient re-presented with an epidural collection, necessitating a craniectomy and involvement of ENT surgeons for bilateral frontosphenoethmoidectomy and septoplasty. The bone flap was discarded, and a prolonged intravenous antibiotic regimen commenced. Despite aggressive management, questions remain regarding whether earlier removal of infected bone during the first operation might have prevented recurrence. Intraoperative findings included purulent material under pressure from the frontal sinuses and subdural pus surrounding friable pia-arachnoid tissue. Although the bone appeared largely viable, a portion overlying the dura mater (posterior table) may have contributed to reinfection. A modified Lothrop procedure was later performed due to polyp-related obstruction and mucopurulence. Despite extensive efforts, the etiology of recurrent infections remained unclear, prompting referral to immunology for further evaluation.10,11 Continued intranasal drug use could not be excluded as a contributing factor to recurrent disease.

Four of 5 patients underwent respiratory PCR testing, with only the conservatively managed patient (case 1) returning a positive result—potentially influencing the initial nonsurgical decision-making. All patients had received empirical antibiotics in the community without improvement. Four of the 5 cases grew identifiable organisms on culture and were subsequently treated with intravenous antibiotics. No patients developed dural venous sinus thrombosis, a potentially fatal complication of PPT.

Lessons

This series highlights the diverse clinical presentations and neurosurgical management strategies of PPT. Despite limited literature guiding neurosurgical management, it is evident that surgical intervention is often warranted, even in cases initially managed conservatively. The extent of intracranial involvement should guide the aggressiveness of surgical intervention.14,15 Craniotomy is appropriate when viable bone is present and collections are confined to the epidural or subdural spaces.14,15 In contrast, when osteomyelitis is suspected, craniectomy may be necessary.14,15

Early recognition and intervention are paramount. Delays in diagnosis can lead to more invasive procedures and increased risk of morbidity and mortality.13,14 This underscores the need for general practitioners to consider early MRI and neurosurgical referral for patients with PPT unresponsive to antibiotics within 5–7 days,15–17 regardless of age. Given the increasing demand on outpatient clinics, patients with suspected PPT should be referred via the emergency department for expedited imaging and multidisciplinary specialist review.15,17 Prompt diagnosis and treatment are essential to improving outcomes and reducing the burden of recurrent infections and potentially more invasive surgeries.
