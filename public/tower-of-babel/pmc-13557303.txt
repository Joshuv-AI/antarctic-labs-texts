Negative Emotions Induce Urticaria by Regulating Metabolites: A Two-Step Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Keyi Zhang, Yibo Hu, Li Lei, Chuhan Fu, Shu Zhou, Yaqing Wen, Jiangfeng Huang, Jinhua Huang, Jing Chen, Qinghai Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSeveral studies have indicated a potential link between emotions and urticaria. However, the causal relationship between different emotions and urticaria and its subtypes, as well as whether circulating metabolites act as mediators, remains unclear.ObjectivesTo investigate the causal relationships between negative emotions, circulating metabolites, and urticaria and its subtypes, and to assess the mediating effects of circulating metabolites.MethodsInstrumental variables for eight negative emotions, 1400 circulating metabolic factors, and urticaria were sourced from publicly available GWAS summary data. We used Mendelian randomization (MR) to explore the causal links between emotions, circulating metabolites, and urticaria, utilizing inverse variance weighting (IVW) as the main statistical approach.ResultThe MR analysis results showed that nervous feelings (P=0.046; 95% CI:1.02–7.65) and worrier/anxious feelings (P=0.016; 95% CI:1.17–4.60) may be risk factors of urticaria. In addition, we found that 17 metabolites and three metabolite ratios increased the risk of urticaria, while 18 metabolites and two metabolite ratios were protective factors.ConclusionThe mediating MR analysis revealed that worrier/anxious feelings increase urticaria risk by decreasing mannose, mannonate, and glycosyl-N-(2-hydroxynervonoyl)-sphingosine (d18:1/24:1(2OH)) levels and by increasing the phosphate-to-mannose ratio and glucose-to-mannose ratio. However, the relationship between nervous feelings and urticaria is not mediated by metabolites. These findings provide new perspectives and potential strategies for the comprehensive prevention and treatment of urticaria through emotional and metabolic interventions.Keywords: Negative emotions, Urticaria, Metabolites, Mendelian randomization analysis

Introduction

Urticaria is a prevalent inflammatory skin condition characterized by itchy wheals and angioedema. Its pathogenesis primarily involves the activation and degranulation of mast cells, which release histamine and other inflammatory mediators [1]. The onset of urticaria is associated with various factors, such as infections, autoimmunity, food, and drug allergies as well as with neuropsychiatric factors [2]. Previous research has consistently demonstrated a link between psychological factors and urticaria. For example, the severity of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) has been associated with chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) severity [3], and stress management has shown early treatment benefits patients [4]. In addition, a cross-sectional study showed that patients with chronic urticaria had significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression than did healthy individuals [5]. These findings demonstrate that there may be a correlation between emotions and urticaria. However, the precise mechanism by which negative emotions trigger urticaria remains largely unknown. Variations in emotional states may influence metabolite levels in the blood through the neuroendocrine system, thereby affecting the function of the immune system [6]. Under conditions of stress and anxiety, activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis leads to impairment of the skin barrier, which facilitates the penetration of allergens and irritants [7]. Some researchers propose that psychological stress may exacerbate urticaria by directly facilitating mast cell (MC) activation and degranulation through increased release of cortisol, catecholamines, neuropeptides, and nerve growth factor (NGF) [8, 9].

Observational studies of causal relationships are often limited by confounding factors and reverse causation. To overcome these limitations and provide more robust evidence, we conducted a Mendelian randomization (MR) study. This approach, which uses genetic variants as instrumental variables, allowed us to investigate the causal effects of emotions and circulating metabolites on urticaria and to explore the potential mediating role of metabolites. MR is a method that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to effectively reduce the impact of confounding factors and address issues of reverse causation [10]. This approach provides more robust evidence for causal inference.

Objectives

This study aimed to systematically investigate the causal relationships between different negative emotions and urticaria, including its subtypes. It also sought to identify circulating metabolites associated with urticaria and to determine whether these metabolites mediate the effects of specific negative emotions on the risk of developing urticaria.

Methods

Study Design

This study was organized into three main sections (Figure 1) and mainly utilized a two-step MR approach. First, we explored the causal relationships between eight types of emotions and urticaria. Second, we examined the causal effects of 1,400 circulating metabolites on urticaria. Third, we investigated the mediating role of circulating metabolites in the relationship between emotions and urticaria.

Overview of Mendelian randomization analysis.

Data Source

Genetic instrumental variables for negative emotions, including guilty feelings, worrier/anxious feelings, nervous feelings, worry too long after embarrassment, hurt/sensitive feelings, miserableness, fed-up feelings, and loneliness/isolation, were obtained from publicly available GWAS datasets. Genetic instrumental variables for 1,091 metabolites and 309 metabolite ratios were sourced from a GWAS study with 8,299 individuals from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA) cohort [11]. Summary GWAS data for urticaria included 1,057 cases and 483,949 controls, with a focus on European ancestry data for this study [12]. The urticaria validation cohort and subtype GWAS data were sourced from FinnGen R11. Detailed information on the GWAS data utilized is presented in Table 1.

Instrumental Variables Selection

Firstly, genetic instrumental variables strongly linked to negative emotions were selected using a significance threshold of P<5×10−8. A clumping procedure was then performed, with an r2<0.001, threshold used to identify independent instrumental variables. SNPs with the lowest p-values within a 10,000 kb window were retained. Given the limited availability of genetic instrumental variables for circulating metabolites, applying the same selection criteria used for emotion-related instrumental variables would preclude the investigation of potential causal effects between some circulating metabolites and urticaria. To obtain a larger number of genetic variables for analysis, SNPs were selected using a threshold of P<1×10−5. The selected metabolite-related instrumental variables were required to meet the conditions of r2<0.1 and linkage disequilibrium (LD) >500 kb. Secondly, we excluded SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤0.01. Thirdly, to ensure that the SNP’s effect on exposure corresponded to its effect on the outcome via the same allele and to avoid strand orientation biases or allele coding inaccuracies affecting the results, we removed palindromic SNPs. Following this, the F-statistic was calculated using the formula F=beta2/se2. SNPs with an F-statistic less than 10 were considered weak instrumental variables and were removed from the analysis. Additionally, potential confounding SNPs that might be directly associated with urticaria or its etiology were excluded using Phenoscanner.

Primary Two-Sample MR Analysis

To investigate the potential causal effects of negative emotions and circulating metabolites on urticaria, two-sample MR analyses were conducted separately. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was employed as the primary analytical approach, as it assumes the validity of each instrument without being influenced by pleiotropy effects. However, the IVW method is sensitive to heterogeneity and outliers, making it susceptible to horizontal pleiotropy. To verify the robustness of the results, supplementary methods, including MR-Egger regression, weighted mode, simple mode, weighted Mendelian methods, and a range of sensitivity analyses, were also utilized. Statistical significance was considered when the IVW p-value was <0.05 and the direction of the effect was consistent with MR-Egger results.

Reverse MR between Urticaria and Negative Emotions

To exclude the impact of reverse causation on the results, a reverse MR analysis was conducted between urticaria and negative emotions associated with urticaria. The same five MR methods—IVW, MR-Egger regression, weighted mode, simple mode, and weighted Mendelian methods—along with a series of sensitivity analyses, were used as supplementary approaches.

Two-Sample MR analysis between Blood Metabolites and Urticaria

To investigate the causal influence of circulating metabolites on urticaria, a two-sample MR analysis was conducted with 1,400 circulating metabolites as the exposures and urticaria as the outcome.

Metabolic Pathway Analysis

For the blood metabolites identified (PIVW <0.05), metabolic pathway analysis was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) to uncover potential metabolic pathways that could be associated with the biological processes of urticaria.

Two-Sample MR Analysis between Emotions and Urticaria-Related Blood Metabolites

In the aforementioned MR analyses, a series of negative emotions and circulating metabolites related to urticaria were identified. Subsequently, a two-sample MR analysis was conducted to investigate whether circulating metabolites mediated the relationship between emotions and urticaria.

Validation MR Analysis and Subtypes MR Analysis

To further confirm the potential role of metabolites in the pathogenesis of urticaria and to explore which subtypes of urticaria these metabolites might affect, we obtained GWAS data related to urticaria and its subtypes from the FinnGen R11 database. We used the metabolites identified associated with both emotions and urticaria in previous steps as exposures and the validation dataset and subtype data for urticaria as outcomes in the MR analysis.

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of the study results, a comprehensive sensitivity analysis was performed. Initially, the heterogeneity among different genetic instruments (SNPs) in the IVW and MR-Egger methods was evaluated using the Cochran’s Q test. Additionally, potential horizontal pleiotropy issues were assessed through the MR-Egger intercept test and the MR-PRESSO global test. A leave-one-out analysis was conducted to examine the influence of individual SNPs on the MR results.

Results

Instrumental Variable Selection

Firstly, we identified 51, 59, 25, 37, 45, 54, 15, 38 SNPs associated with nervous feelings, worrier/anxious feelings, guilty feelings, worry too long after embarrassment, miserableness, fed-up feelings, sensitive/hurt feelings, and loneliness/isolation, respectively, as well as 41428 SNPs related to blood metabolites. During the process of excluding confounding SNPs, no SNP directly associated with urticaria was found. All SNPs had F-statistics greater than 10.

Two-Sample MR Analysis between Emotions and Urticaria

As shown in Figure 2 and Table S1, IVW method indicated that genetic predispositions to two types of emotions were associated with an increased risk of urticaria. Nervous feelings (P=0.046, 95% CI:1.02–7.65) and worrier/anxious feelings (P=0.016, 95% CI:1.17–4.60) significantly increased the risk of urticaria.

Forest plot for the causal association between emotions and urticaria.

Reverse Mendelian Randomization Analysis between Emotions and Urticaria

To evaluate the robustness of our findings against the potential influence of reverse causation, we performed an MR analysis examining the relationship between urticaria and the associated emotional states. The results indicated no evidence of reverse causation between urticaria and these emotional states (Table S2).

Two-Sample MR Analysis between Blood Metabolites and Urticaria

To explore the relationship between blood metabolites and urticaria, we conducted a two-sample MR analysis between 1400 blood metabolite and urticaria. As shown in Figure 3 and Table S3, our results revealed 48 blood metabolites associated with urticaria. Of these, 20 were protective factors (17 known metabolites and three metabolite ratios), while 20 were risk factors (18 known metabolites and two metabolite ratios). Among these, mannose levels (P=0.01; 95% CI: 0.859–0.981), mannonate levels (P=0.039; 95% CI: 0.872–0.996), glycosyl-N-(2-hydroxynervonoyl)-sphingosine (d18:1/24:1(2OH)) levels (P=0.002; 95% CI: 0.865–0.968) also demonstrated significant protective effects. The phosphate to mannose ratio (P=0.009; 95% CI:1.025–1.193),and glucuronide of piperine metabolite C17H21NO3 (4) levels (P=0.013; 95% CI:1.020–1.184), glucose to mannose ratio (P=0.013; 95% CI:1.015–1.137) also exhibited significant risk effects.

The circular heatmap of the Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis illustrates the relationships between significant metabolites and urticaria.

Metabolic Pathway Analysis

The metabolic pathway analysis revealed that pathways including starch and sucrose metabolism, galactose metabolism, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, fructose and mannose metabolism, neomycin, kanamycin, and gentamicin biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, and glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism may play significant roles in the development of urticaria. Detailed results of the metabolic pathway analysis are presented in Table S4.

MR Analysis of Emotions and Urticaria-Associated Metabolites

In the Mendelian randomization analysis of emotions and urticaria-related metabolites, we examined the effects of worrier/anxious feelings and nervous feelings on these metabolites. For worrier/anxious feelings, we found that they were associated with increased levels of the glucose-to-mannose ratio (P=0.013; 95% CI:1.176–3.893) and the phosphate-to-mannose ratio (P=0.010; 95% CI:1.194–3.758). Additionally, worrier/anxious feelings were linked to decreased mannose levels (P=0.015; 95% CI:0.251–0.862), mannonate levels (P=0.006; 95% CI: 0.261–0.793), glycosyl-N-(2-hydroxynervonoyl)-sphingosine (d18:1/24:1(2OH)) levels (P=0.0238; 95% CI: 0.294–0.917), and glucuronide of piperine metabolite C17H21NO3 (4) (P=0.025; 95% CI: 0.233–0.908) (Table S5). However, the analysis of nervous feelings did not reveal any significant impact on urticaria-related metabolites.

Validation analysis and subtype analysis

In the validation MR analysis using urticaria-related GWAS data from FinnGen R11, the causal relationships between worrier/anxious feelings and urticaria remained consistent with the initial analysis after meta-analysis (Figure S1). Additionally, mannose and mannonate levels continued to act as protective factors against urticaria, whereas the glucose-to-mannose ratio was confirmed as a risk factor, aligning with the findings of our primary analysis. The subtype MR analysis did not identify any significant association between worrier/anxious feelings, nervous feelings, and urticaria subtypes. However, the associations between positive metabolites and urticaria subtypes were confirmed in the analysis. These findings are detailed in Table S6 and Figure S2.

Sensitivity Analysis

The robustness of the Mendelian randomization estimates was confirmed through a series of sensitivity analyses. We found no evidence of bias from heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q test, P>0.05), horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO global test, P>0.05), or influential outliers (leave-one-out analysis). The symmetric funnel plots further suggested an absence of directional pleiotropy. The comprehensive results for these analyses are presented in the Supplementary Material (Tables S8–S9, Figures S3–S6).

Mediation MR Analysis

In this study, both emotions and metabolites were found to have causal effects on urticaria. Metabolites may mediate the relationship between emotions and the development of urticaria. The results indicated that worrier/anxious feelings could increase the risk of urticaria through the following mechanisms: by decreasing mannose levels (mediating effect= 0.0658; mediating proportion=8.07%), mannonate levels (mediating effect=0.0552; mediating proportion=6.74%), glycosyl-N-(2-hydroxynervonoyl)-sphingosine (d18:1/24:1(2OH)) levels (mediating effect=0.0584; mediating proportion=6.93%), and increasing the phosphate-to-mannose ratio (mediating effect=0.0759; mediating proportion=9.26%), glucose-to-mannose ratio (mediating effect=0.0548; mediating proportion=6.58%). Glucuronide of piperine metabolite C17H21NO3 (4) levels exhibited a masking effect in the pathway from worrier/anxious feelings to urticaria (Figure 4 and Table 2).

Discussion

Our MR findings raise the possibility that worrier/anxious feelings might influence the risk of urticaria, potentially mediated by alterations in circulating levels of mannose, mannonate, glycosyl-N-(2-hydroxynervonoyl)-sphingosine (d18:1/24:1(2OH)), and the glucose-to-mannose and phosphate-to-mannose ratios. These findings offer potential, albeit speculative, mechanistic clues for understanding the emotion-urticaria association.

To explore the biological plausibility of these metabolite-mediated pathways, we considered the immunopathogenesis of urticaria. Mast cells are pivotal effector cells in the pathogenesis of urticaria. In response to cytokines and chemokines, mast cells undergo degranulation, leading to the release of histamine and a range of other inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), serotonin, proteases, and proteoglycans. The release of these mediators significantly contributes to the inflammatory process associated with urticaria [13, 14]. Th17 cells can promote the proliferation and differentiation of mast cells through the secretion of IL-6 [15]. It has been reported that in urticaria, the levels of cytokines associated with helper T cells (Th1/Th2 and Th17) are significantly elevated and closely correlated with disease activity [15–17]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) prevent excessive inflammation and promote tissue repair and homeostasis by targeting various immune cell subsets and tissues [18]. Additionally, macrophages play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of urticaria [19]. Research indicates that macrophages can promote mast cell proliferation and maturation by secreting IL-6 and IL-1β [20].

Our results suggest that lower levels of mannose, mannonate, and Glycosyl-N-(2-hydroxynervonoyl)-sphingosine (d18:1/24:1(2OH)) and increased glucose-to-mannose and phosphate-to-mannose ratios may elevate the risk of urticaria. This finding resonates with the existing evidence. For instance, high-glucose environments may be associated with metabolic disturbances and enhanced inflammatory responses, leading to autoimmune diseases [21]. Studies in mouse models have also shown that high glucose intake can exacerbate autoimmune responses by promoting the differentiation of T helper 17 (Th17) cells [22]. In contrast to the pro-inflammatory properties of glucose, mannose exhibits distinct immunomodulatory functions. Mannose is an isomer of glucose and is commonly found in foods such as cranberries, peaches, aloe vera, and apples [23] and is a primary monosaccharide involved in glycosylation modifications. Inside the cell, mannose is first phosphorylated by hexokinase (HK) to form mannose-6-phosphate (M-6-P). Subsequently, M-6-P is metabolized through two main pathways: i) it is partially converted by phosphomannose isomerase (PMI) to mannose-1-phosphate (M-1-P), which participates in N-glycosylation; ii) the majority is converted to fructose-6-phosphate (F-6-P) through the action of mannose-6-phosphate isomerase (MPI), entering the glycolytic pathway [24]. This fundamental metabolic process is closely linked to the remarkable immunomodulatory capacity of mannose. Recently, a number of studies have demonstrated that mannose plays an important role in regulating autoimmunity and reducing the inflammatory response [25–28], and it has been shown to be an inhibitor of many autoimmune diseases, including type I diabetes, asthma, colitis, lupus, and osteoarthritis [29–31]. Therefore, we speculate that reduced mannose levels may affect the pathogenesis of urticaria through a similar mechanism; this speculation is supported by the known immunomodulatory functions of mannose. For instance, studies have reported that mannose is able to reduce the inflammatory response by inhibiting the activation of inflammatory macrophages and IL-1β production, which in turn reduces the inflammatory response [26], suggesting a potential link to urticaria pathogenesis. The anti-inflammatory effects of mannose can be attributed to its ability to induce regulatory T cells (Tregs). Zhang et al. have elucidated that mannose stimulates Treg cell generation predominantly through the activation of TGF-β. This induction has been observed in both in vivo and in vitro studies [29], providing a plausible mechanistic explanation for the potential association between mannose levels and the risk of developing urticaria. Several studies have shown that mannose degrades the PD-L1 molecule by affecting the glycosylation of the PD-L1 molecule [32, 33] and that the degradation of PD-L1 promotes the proliferation of Treg cells [34, 35], which in turn reduces mast cell degranulation. Other researchers have observed that mannose can also exert anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting IL-6 production from mesenchymal stem cells, thereby inducing Treg cells [36]. It is noteworthy that mannose can modulate the immune system by fostering the proliferation of anti-inflammatory microorganisms such as those from the Bacteroides genus [30]. The role of the genus Bacteroides in immune regulation has been well established [37, 38]. Therefore, we hypothesize that mannose may also elevate the abundance of Bacteroides or other anti-inflammatory gut microbiota in individuals with urticaria, thereby exerting an antiinflammatory effect. Regarding the mechanisms by which the phosphate-to-mannose and glucose-to-mannose ratios contribute to the pathogenesis of urticaria, it remains unclear whether these effects are mediated by increased phosphate and glucose levels or by decreased mannose levels. Further research is needed to elucidate the impact of these substances on urticaria. Additionally, we found that glycosyl-N-(2-hydroxynervonoyl)-sphingosine (d18:1/24:1(2OH)) and other metabolites may be associated with urticaria. Based on the aforementioned prior research, we hypothesize that these metabolites may exert a protective effect during the pathogenesis of urticaria through similar immunomodulatory pathways. However, due to the lack of in-depth studies, we cannot definitively determine the exact mechanism by which mannose exerts its effects, and further research is warranted. Negative emotions are considered to contribute to the onset and exacerbation of urticaria by influencing metabolism and immunity. The role of the brain-gut-skin axis in mediating the relationship between emotions and skin conditions has gained attention in recent studies [39, 40]. Our study suggests that worrier/anxious feelings may contribute to the onset of urticaria by reducing blood levels of mannose and mannonate while increasing the glucose-to-mannose and phosphate-to-mannose ratios. Studies have reported that patients with depression, anxiety, and stress-related disorders have higher glucose levels compared to healthy individuals [41]. When under stress or other negative emotions, the body secretes stress hormones such as cortisol, which stimulate the liver to produce more glucose [42]. Additionally, prolonged negative emotions and stress may lead to insulin resistance, which could partly explain the mechanisms by which emotions alter glucose metabolism [43]. However, the mechanisms by which worrier/anxious feelings influence mannose levels, mannonate levels, and phosphate to mannose ratio remain poorly studied. Notably, mannose has been reported in several studies to have effects in resisting diseases such as depression and anxiety [44]. This finding may partially indicate a potential association between worrier/anxious feelings and mannose levels. Worrier/anxious feelings may influence the absorption and metabolism of mannose through various mechanisms, thereby increasing the risk of urticaria. Firstly, worrier/anxious feelings are often associated with dysfunction of the digestive system [45]; such dysfunction may reduce the intake and absorption of mannose, thereby disrupting the immune balance. Studies have reported that the enzymes MEase and CEase are capable of directly converting D-glucose into D-mannose [23]. Therefore, it can be inferred that prolonged worrier/anxious feelings may lead to reduced glucose conversion by inhibiting the activity of these two enzymes. Moreover, mannose is a crucial monosaccharide involved in the glycosylation process [24]. Worrier/anxious feelings may disrupt the glycosylation process and impair immune function. Further research is needed to clarify how these emotions affect mannose levels and the development of urticaria. We conducted the first MR study investigating the relationship between negative emotions, blood metabolites, and urticaria. However, our study has several limitations. First, as it was conducted exclusively in European populations, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups remains uncertain. Second, the validity of MR relies on key instrumental variable assumptions, including the absence of horizontal pleiotropy. Although we employed methods like MR-Egger to assess and correct for pleiotropy, the possibility of residual confounding through unmodeled pathways cannot be entirely ruled out, which is a particular challenge when studying complex traits like emotions. Third, although we excluded SNPs directly associated with urticaria, other unknown confounding factors might exist. Forth, despite screening instruments using F-statistics (all F>10), weak instrument bias remains a potential concern. Particularly in emotional phenotypes with fewer instruments, the explanatory power of genetic instruments may be relatively limited, potentially leading to underestimation of our causal estimates. Fifth, the proposed "emotion-metabolite-urticaria" pathway is an inferential conclusion based on MR findings, and the underlying biological mechanisms require confirmation through future experimental research.

Conclusion

In summary, our MR findings raise the possibility that worrier/anxious feelings might influence the risk of urticaria, potentially mediated by alterations in circulating levels of mannose, mannonate, glycosyl-N-(2-hydroxynervonoyl) -sphingosine (d18:1/24:1(2OH)), and the glucose-to-mannose and phosphate-to-mannose ratios. These results suggest that, in managing patients with urticaria, it is important to focus not only on traditional physiological indicators and dietary factors but also on psychological factors and related metabolic pathways as potential intervention targets. This finding provides a new perspective and possible strategies for the comprehensive prevention and treatment of urticaria and lays the groundwork for future research on the role of these metabolic pathways.
