Prussian blue nanoparticles-based dual-mode immunochromatographic assays for norfloxacin detection in animal-derived foods
Authors: Gaoshuang Hu, Xiaodie Wang, Qingxiu Yu, Pingping Yu, Jun Wang, Jinhui Zhang, Mei Qiu, Shan Gao, Jianxiong Hao, Wei Sheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Prussian Blue Nanoparticles (PBNPs)-based dual-mode immunochromatographic assays (ICAs) were developed for the detection of norfloxacin (NOR) in animal-derived foods. The assays used the inherent bright blue color and peroxidase-like catalytic activity of PBNPs. The visual limit of detection (vLOD) of the developed ICAs were both determined to be 1 ng mL−1. The calculated limit of detection (cLOD) of the ICA based on the inherent bright blue color of PBNPs was 0.5 ng mL−1, while that of the ICA using the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of PBNPs was 0.34 ng mL−1. Analysis of spiked samples demonstrated that NOR could be effectively detected in pork, chicken, beef, sea bass and shrimp, with recoveries ranging from 80.43 % to 106.33 %. The results showed strong correlation with those obtained by HPLC, with R2 values of 0.9653 and 0.9715, respectively. Therefore, this study established a sensitive, accurate, rapid and cost-effective method for detecting NOR in animal-derived foods.

Introduction

Animal-derived foods are an important source of nutrition in the human diet, and their safety has a direct impact on public health and social stability (Wei et al., 2024). In recent years, the extensive use of veterinary drugs in livestock and poultry farming has improved animal health and production efficiency; however, the issue of drug residues has become an increasingly serious concern (Eissa et al., 2025). Quinolones (QNs) are extensively used for the prevention and treatment of bacterial infections in animals owing to their broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, high bioavailability, and low cross-resistance. Among them, norfloxacin (NOR), a third-generation fluoroquinolone antibiotic, is particularly common in the livestock industry (Patyra et al., 2020). However, due to their long half-life and high chemical stability, the misuse or overuse of these drugs can result in persistent residue of the parent drug or active metabolites in food products and the environment (Wang et al., 2021). Studies have shown that residues of these drugs can inhibit the activity of DNA gyrase and DNA topoisomerase in the human body through the food chain, interfere with cellular DNA replication, disrupt the ecological balance of microbial communities, and promote the evolution and spread of drug-resistant bacteria. The effects pose a dual threat to public health and environmental safety (Jin et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025; Zahari et al., 2023). Therefore, the development of efficient, sensitive, and rapid on-site detection technologies is urgently needed.

Currently, the primary methods for detecting NOR residues include high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Gaballah Mohamed et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2024), liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) (Zhi et al., 2020), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Hu et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2022), and electrochemical analysis (Liao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2024). While these methods are well-established and highly reliable due to their accuracy and sensitivity, they are limited by high equipment costs, complex operations, and long detection cycles, which restrict their suitability for rapid detection. Immunochromatographic assays (ICAs) are rapid detection platforms based on the specific binding between antigens and antibodies. They have gained significant attention due to their simple operation, rapid detection, and ability to provide visual results (Hao et al., 2021). Dual-mode ICAs that integrate two characteristic signal transduction pathways into a single assay generate simultaneous dual signals, enhancing analytical performance and improving result reliability (Mohammad et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). Wang et al (Wang et al., 2024). developed a photothermal and surface-enhanced Raman scattering dual-mode immunochromatographic sensor, based on Au nanospheres and waxberry-like core satellite nanoparticles, achieving highly sensitive detection of aflatoxin B1. Jiang et al (Jiang et al., 2024). constructed a colorimetric/photothermal dual-mode detection platform using platinum‑nickel nanoparticles as oxidase-like nanozymes, enabling sensitive detection of organophosphorus pesticides. Liu et al (Liu, Jiang, et al., 2024a). developed a colorimetric/fluorescent dual-mode immunochromatographic method for detecting phenamacril residues in agricultural products using signal synergy between poly(dopamine)-coated gold nanoparticles and quantum dots. However, these labeling materials, including waxberry-like core-satellite nanoparticles, platinum‑nickel nanoparticles, gold nanoparticles or quantum dots, face drawbacks such as low stability, high cost, and complex preparation processes. Therefore, these limitations have constrained the widespread application of such methods in food safety detection. In recent years, nanozymes have emerged as promising alternatives, significantly enhancing detection sensitivity by mimicking the catalytic activity of natural enzymes, efficiently catalyzing the oxidation of colorimetric substrates, and amplifying colorimetric signals (Liu, Zhang, et al., 2021a). Prussian Blue Nanoparticles (PBNPs) have attracted considerable attention in biomedical research due to their inherent colorimetric properties, high specific surface area, excellent biocompatibility, facile surface modification and functionalization, good solubility and stability, as well as low cost and simple synthesis process (Dong et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2020; Liao et al., 2025). While a few studies have employed the inherent bright blue color of PBNPs for detecting ofloxacin (OFL) in fish (Tian et al., 2020) or used peroxidase-like catalytic activity for signal amplification in ochratoxin A (OTA) detection (Li et al., 2024), studies on dual-mode ICAs based on PBNPs remain limited.

In this study, bright blue and high-affinity PBNPs were synthesized via a hydrothermal method, and the monoclonal antibody against NOR was conjugated to their surface through electrostatic adsorption to prepare signal probes. Using both the inherent bright blue color and the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of PBNPs, dual-mode ICAs were developed for the rapid, sensitive, and cost-effective detection of NOR in animal-derived foods.

Materials and methods

Reagents and apparatus

Anti-NOR monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were obtained from Shandong Lvdu Bioscience Technology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). NOR and other QNs analogs were purchased from Ron Reagents Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Potassium ferricyanide (K3[Fe (CN)6]·3H2O), polyvinylpyrrolidone K-30 (PVP K-30), and ferric chloride were also obtained from Ron Reagents Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Nitrocellulose (NC) membranes were purchased from Sartorius GmbH (Germany). Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) base plates, sample pads, and absorbent pads were obtained from Shanghai Jinbiao Bioscience Technology Co., Ltd. Citric acid was purchased from Tianjin Yongda Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was obtained from Yuanye Bioscience Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rabbit anti-mouse IgG was purchased from China Proteintech Co., Ltd.

The morphological characteristics of the nanoparticles were analyzed using a JEM-2010 FEF transmission electron microscope (TEM, 200 kV; produced by Japan Electron Optics Laboratory Co., Ltd.). The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a Great 20 spectrometer (Tianjin Zhongke Ruijie Technology Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China). The prepared nanomaterials were characterized using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) nanoparticle size analyzer (Horiba Co., Japan) and an X-ray powder diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku Co., Japan). Ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra were obtained using a Shimadzu UV-2300 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Japan).

Preparation of PBNPs and PBNPs-NOR-mAbs

PBNPs were prepared using the hydrothermal method as illustrated in Fig. 1A (Ren et al., 2022). Briefly, 80 mL of 10 mM dilute hydrochloric acid solution was prepared, followed by the sequential addition of PVP K-30 (6 g) and K3[Fe (CN)6]·3H2O (264 mg) under vigorous magnetic stirring for 1 h to ensure complete dissolution of all components. The homogeneous transparent mixture was transferred to polytetrafluoroethylene-lined high-pressure reactor and maintained at 80 °C for 2 h under constant temperature conditions. After the reaction, the mixture was naturally cooled to room temperature. The resulting product was washed three times by alternating centrifugation with anhydrous ethanol and deionized water to remove impurities. Finally, the purified blue solid was dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C overnight to obtain PBNPs powder, which was then sealed and stored in the drying oven for future use.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of PBNPs-based dual-mode ICAs. (A) A preparation process of PBNPs and PBNPs-NOR-mAbs; (B) Schematic diagram of detection principle for ICAs mediated by PBNPs.Fig. 1

Schematic diagram of PBNPs-based dual-mode ICAs. (A) A preparation process of PBNPs and PBNPs-NOR-mAbs; (B) Schematic diagram of detection principle for ICAs mediated by PBNPs.

PBNPs-NOR-mAbs were prepared following previously reported methods (El Aamri et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 1A, an appropriate amount of PBNPs was dispersed in 1 mL of distilled water to form a suspension. Then, anti-NOR mAbs (5 mg mL−1, 8 μL) were added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow sufficient assembly of the two components. Thereafter, 10 % BSA was added, and the reaction was continued for another hour to block unbound sites on the PBNPs surface. The solution was then stored at 4 °C for 3 h, followed by centrifugation at 9000 rpm for 30 min to remove unbound anti-NOR mAbs. This process was repeated twice to ensure complete separation. The precipitate was resuspended in borate buffer solution (0.2 mL) and the mixture was stored at 4 °C for later use.

Preparation of PBNPs-based dual-mode immunochromatographic assays

As shown in Fig. 1B, the diagram illustrates the detection principle of the proposed method. The test strip comprises four main components: a sample pad, a PVC base plate, an NC membrane, and an absorbent pad. The sample pad, composed of a glass fiber membrane with dimensions of 15 mm × 3 mm, required no pretreatment. The coating antigen (NOR-BSA) and rabbit anti-mouse IgG were dispensed onto the NC membrane (25 mm × 3 mm) to form the test line (T line) and control line (C line), respectively, with an approximate spacing of 4 mm between them. The treated membrane was then dried at 37 °C overnight. Subsequently, the sample pad, dried NC membrane, and absorbent pad were sequentially assembled onto the PVC base plate, ensuring an overlap of 1–2 mm between each component. Finally, the assembled test strips were cut into individual strips with a width of approximately 3–4 mm and stored under dry, sealed conditions for later use. The detection was performed using a competitive immunochromatographic method, in which the free antigen in the sample competes with the coated antigen on the T line of the strip for antibody binding.

Immunochromatographic assays mediated by the inherent bright blue of PBNPs

NOR-BSA was prepared according to previous studies (Hu et al., 2017). The optimal concentrations of NOR-BSA and rabbit anti-mouse secondary antibody were then sequentially immobilized at designated positions on the nitrocellulose membrane (4 nm apart) to form the T line and C line, respectively. NOR standards were serially diluted with PBS buffer (0.01 M, pH = 7.4) to obtain concentrations of 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 20, 30, and 50 ng mL−1. A 100 μL aliquot of each diluted solution was mixed with an appropriate amount of optimized PBNPs-NOR-mAbs. PBS buffer alone was used as a blank control. The prepared solution was dropped onto the assembled test strip. Under the driving force of capillary action, the diluted food sample solution and PBNPs-NOR-mAbs migrated toward the absorbent pad. During this process, the NOR-BSA on the T line bound competitively to the PBNPs-NOR-mAbs in the sample solution until the binding sites on the probe surface became saturated, preventing further binding to NOR.

Based on the relationship between the color intensity of the test strip and the target concentration, the visual detection limit (vLOD) was defined as the target concentration at which the T line color intensity was significantly weaker than that of the negative control (Hu, Gao, et al., 2020a). The cut-off value was defined as the target concentration at which the T line color completely disappeared to the naked eye (Su et al., 2021). These parameters constituted the criteria for semi-quantitative analysis (Ren et al., 2022).

For quantitative analysis, the test strips were placed under the built-in incandescent lamp of a UV analyzer (Shanghai Qinke Analytical Instruments Co., Ltd., dimensions 320 × 220 × 310 mm, and equipped with a 4 W visible incandescent lamp). The images of the test strips were then captured using the rear camera of a vivo X80 smartphone (50 million pixels, sRGB, auto white balance, and exposure). Subsequently, the ImageJ software was used to measure the grayscale values (GSV) of the test images. The T/C ratio was then calculated to obtain the relative GSV, and a standard curve was established. Based on this curve, the calculated limit of detection (cLOD) was defined as three times the standard deviation of the signal from the negative control, thus enabling quantitative analysis (Hu et al., 2016; Jiang et al., 2018).

Immunochromatographic assays mediated by peroxidase-like catalytic activity of PBNPs

After the above reaction was completed, the optimal TMB: H2O2 working solution was immediately added to the T and C lines. After a specific incubation period, the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of PBNPs catalyzed the oxidation of TMB to TMBox at a specific H2O2 concentration, increasing the color of T and C lines, and amplifying the signal. Thus, the required amounts of PBNPs-NOR-mAbs, secondary antibody, and NOR-coated antigen could be reduced, increasing the sensitivity of the assay. Using the same evaluation methods, the vLOD, cLOD, and quantitative detection range under catalytic signal measurement were determined.

Specificity analysis

Given the structural similarity of QNs, it is essential to evaluate the specificity of the test strip to aviod false-positive results from related compounds. Seven QNs were selected for specificity testing: enrofloxacin (ENR), ofloxacin (OFX), marbofloxacin (MAB), enoxacin (ENX), pefloxacin (PEF), gatifloxacin (GAT), and ciprofloxacin (CIP). Standard solutions of these QNs were prepared at 1, 10, 100, and 1000 μg L−1, applied to the test strip, and compared with a PBS control. Each experiment was performed in triplicate to minimize experimental error.

Sample analysis

Fresh pork, chicken, beef, sea bass, and shrimp samples were purchased from a local supermarket. Different concentrations of NOR (0, 10, 50, and 100 μg L−1) were added to NOR-free samples and stored frozen until use. A 1.0 g portion of each sample was placed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of acetic acid (0.1 M, 0.2 mL) and methanol (0.8 mL). The mixture was then shaken vigorously for 5 min to ensure complete dispersion of NOR in the solution. After centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 15 min), the supernatant was collected and diluted 5, 8, or 10-fold using PBS (pH 7.4). The appropriately diluted samples were used for PBNPs-based dual-mode ICAs.

The spiking concentration of NOR was set at 0, 10, 50, and 100 μg L−1. To further validate the accuracy of the two ICAs, the same NOR-spiked samples (pork, chicken, beef, sea bass, and shrimp) were also analyzed using the HPLC method (Hu et al., 2024). Detailed procedures for the HPLC analysis are provided in the supplementary material.

Results and discussion

Characterization of PBNPs and PBNPs-NOR-mAbs

PBNPs are coordination polymers with a three-dimensional framework, typically represented by the molecular formula Fe4[Fe (CN)6]3·nH2O. Their structure consists of alternating divalent (Fe2+) and trivalent (Fe3+) irons connected by cyanide (C ≡ N−) bridges (Wang & Cheng, 2020). Each cyanide (CN−) atom coordinates with Fe3+, while the nitrogen (N) atom coordinates with Fe2+, forming an alternating C-Fe3+-N-Fe2+ coordination network that constructs a three-dimensional framework composed of stacked cubic structural units (Joan & Antònia, 2021). This framework of endows PBNPs with unique adsorption properties and peroxidase-like catalytic activity, making them highly promising for biosensing and related applications (Liu et al., 2022). The morphology of PBNPs was characterized using TEM. As shown in Fig. 2A, the PBNPs showed a well-defined cubic morphology with excellent dispersion and a highly uniform particle size distribution. The average particle size ranged from 130 to 150 nm, indicating that the PBNPs synthesized following the described protocol possessed superior structural uniformity and met the experimental requirements.Fig. 2Characterization of PBNPs and PBNPs-NOR-mAbs. XRD analysis was performed to examine the phase purity of the PBNPs, and the obtained diffraction pattern was compared with the standard Prussian blue reference card (PDF#73–0689). As shown in Fig. 2C, the XRD diffraction peak positions (2θ values) and intensities of the sample closely matched those of the standard reference, indicating that the PBNPs displayed high crystallinity and were free from significant impurity interference, confirming the successful synthesis of high-purity PBNPs (Hu et al., 2025).

The UV–vis spectra of PBNPs and PBNPs-NOR-mAbs were recorded using a full-wavelength multi-functional ELISA instrument. As shown in Fig. 2D, a broad absorption band was observed between 500 and 800 nm, with PBNPs displaying a characteristic peak at 740 nm in the near-infrared region. After antibody labeling, the absorption peak exhibited an evident red shift from 740 nm to 760 nm, indicating successful binding of the antibody to the PBNPs surface. This spectral shift is likely due to changes in the local electronic environment induced by biomolecule binding.

The PBNPs and PBNPs-NOR-mAbs aqueous solutions were characterized using DLS with a nanoparticle analyzer (Fig. 2E). The hydrated particle size of PBNPs was 248.96 ± 1.20 nm, while that of PBNPs-NOR-mAbs increased to 323.90 ± 11.59 nm, confirming successful antibody conjugation to the PBNPs. The zeta potential of PBNPs and PBNPs-NOR-mAbs was measured using a nanoparticle analyzer (Fig. 2F). The PBNPs exhibited a zeta potential of −16.1 mV, while PBNPs-NOR-mAbs showed −10.6 mV. This decrease in negative charge is attributed to the assembly of positively charged NOR antibodies with negatively charged PBNPs via electrostatic interactions, confirming the successful conjugation of the antibody to the PBNPs (Zhao et al., 2018).

Performance evaluation of immunochromatographic assays based on PBNPs signal amplification

Immunochromatographic assays mediated by the inherent bright blue of PBNPs

The sensitivity of the test strip is influenced by factors such as the NC membrane type, the concentration of rabbit anti-mouse IgG, the concentration of coated antigen, and the amount of PBNPs-NOR-mAbs probes added to the sample solution. As shown in Table S1, HF 135 was identified as the most suitable NC membrane for both assays, as it allows an optimal antigen-antibody interaction time. The optimal conditions were 3 μL PBNPs-NOR-mAbs, 0.50 mg mL−1 rabbit anti-mouse IgG on the C line, and 0.50 mg mL−1 NOR-coated antigen on the T line. In that condition, the assay can not only achieve reliable detection, but also present good sensitivity.

The experimental results under the optimized conditions are shown in Fig. 3a. After 15 min of reaction, the color intensity of the T line decreased with increasing NOR concentration. At 1 ng mL−1, the color intensity of the T line was significantly lower than that of the blank control, and at 10 ng mL−1, the T line color completely disappeared. ImageJ software (Fig. 3b) was further used to analyze the GSV of the T and the C lines (T/C ratio), which showed a negative correlation with NOR concentration (Fig. 3c). Within the range of 0.5–10 ng mL−1, the linear regression equation for NOR was y = 0.4306 x + 1.0121 (R2 = 0.9868). As shown in Table S2, the correlation coefficient R-value (0.9934) and the R2-value (0.9868) indicate a good correlation. The overall significance test yielded an F-statistic of 149.9066 with a corresponding p-value (significance) of 0.0066, which is less than 0.05. The significance test for the regression coefficients showed a T-statistic of 50.9625 with a p-value of 3.8481 × 10−4, well below 0.05. Immunochromatographic assays mediated by the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of PBNPs

This study developed an ICA using PBNPs for both labeling and catalytic signal amplification to detect NOR. After the first colorimetric signal, arising from the inherent bright blue of the PBNPs label, was observed, a second signal was generated through the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of PBNPs, which catalyzed the TMB:H2O2. Therefore, after optimizing each working parameter of the test strip, including PBNPs-NOR-mAbs probes addition, secondary antibody concentration, and NOR-coated antigen concentration, the TMB: H2O2 ratio, its addition volume, and reaction time were also optimized to maximize the TMB-mediated signal intensity. As shown in Table S1, the ICA achieved optimal NOR detection with the addition of 2 μL of PBNPs-NOR-mAbs, a rabbit anti-mouse IgG concentration of 0.40 mg mL−1, and a NOR-coated antigen concentration of 0.30 mg mL−1. The catalytic signal color originates from PBNPs, which convert TMB into blue TMBox in the presence of H2O2. The TMB: H2O2 ratio strongly influenced both the color intensity and the measurable range for NOR. The optimized conditions were determined to be a 1:1 TMB: H2O2 ratio, an addition volume of 0.50 μL, and a catalytic reaction time of 30 s.

Under the optimized working conditions and signal amplification strategy, the sensitivity of the TMB signal-amplified ICA for NOR was evaluated. By incorporating TMB as a catalytic substrate into the conventional colorimetric test strip, the blue intensity of the T line gradually decreased with increasing NOR concentration in the standard solution. The sensitivity of the TMB signal-amplified ICAs is shown in Fig. 3d, with the vLOD of 1 ng mL−1. When the NOR concentration reached 30 ng mL−1, the T line completely disappeared, indicating complete signal quenching. The vLOD of the TMB signal-amplified ICAs for NOR was 1 ng mL−1, with a cut-off value of 30 ng mL−1. As illustrated in Fig. 3e, ImageJ software was used to process the images, and the T/C values were calculated from the grayscale measurements to construct a standard curve. As shown in Fig. 3f, the T/C value was negatively correlated with NOR concentration. Within the range of 0.5–30 ng mL−1, the linear regression equation for NOR was y = 0.2583 x + 0.6817 (R2 = 0.9930), and the cLOD for NOR was 0.34 ng mL−1. As shown in Table S2, the correlation coefficient R-value (0.9965) and the R2-value (0.9930) indicate a strong correlation. The overall significance test yielded an F-statistic of 569.9459 with a p-value of 1.8257 × 10−5, well below 0.05. The significance test for the regression coefficients showed a T-statistic of 142.0036 with a p-value of 1.4751 × 10−8, well below 0.05, confirming that the regression model is statistically significant.

In summary, owing to the TMB-mediated colorimetric signal amplification, the TMB-amplified test strips demonstrated higher sensitivity and a suitable detection range, meeting testing requirements across of different regions and broadening the method's application scope.

Specificity analysis

The above optimization results demonstrated that the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of PBNPs in the ICAs produced colorimetric and catalytic signals with higher sensitivity. This method was therefore applied to a specificity analysis. To evaluate the specificity of the ICAs, seven QNs, including CIP, OFX, ENR, PEF, ENX, MAB, and GAT, were tested to assess their potential interference with the assay results. The negative control group consisted of PBS buffer, and the target substance NOR was set at 10 ng mL−1. As shown in Fig. 4, when the concentrations of ENR and CIP reached 100 ng mL−1, the T line displayed significant coloration. At 1000 ng mL−1, OFX, MAB, ENX, PEF, and GAT showed no interference with T line color development, with color intensity comparable to the negative control. These results indicate that the ICAs can not only be used for the detection of NOR, but also for the detection of ENR and CIP.Fig. 4Specificity analysis of ICAs mediated by the peroxidase-like activity of PBNPs. Sample analysis

Although ICAs can be used to analyze samples rapidly and easily, various components in food samples, such as proteins and lipids, can interfere with antibody-analyte interactions and affect detection performance (Zhang et al., 2021). To address this, the matrix effect was evaluated based on previous studies (Hu et al., 2025). Visual observation showed that a10-fold dilution of samples with PBS increased the color intensity of both the T and C lines, indicating an effective reduction in matrix effects (Table S3). As shown in Fig. S1 and Fig. S2, a 10-fold dilution of different samples with PBS produced calibration curves from both PBNPs-based ICAs (with and without TMB) that perfectly overlapped with the standard method curve, indicating effective reduction of matrix effects.

The HPLC was used to confirm the accuracy of the ICAs. Pork, chicken, beef, sea bass and shrimp samples spiked with NOR at 0, 10, 50 and 100 μg L−1 were analyzed using the HPLC and two proposed strips. As shown in Table 1, positive results (+) were observed at 10 μg L−1 NOR. NOR recoveries for the ICAs ranged from 80.43 % to 106.33 %, with coefficients of variation (CV) between 0.35 % and 12.5 %. Conclusions

This study developed an innovative PBNPs-based dual-mode immunochromatographic assay for detecting NOR in animal-derived foods, showing advantages of high sensitivity, rapid reaction, low cost, and an acceptable linear range. Specificity analysis indicated that the developed method could detect not only NOR but also ENR and CIP. Analysis of spiked samples at 0, 10, 50, and 100 μg L−1 showed that NOR could be accurately detected in pork, chicken, beef, sea bass, and shrimp, with recoveries ranging from 80.43 % to 106.33 % and CV between 0.35 % and 12.5 %. Furthermore, a strong correlation was observed between the data obtained using the developed methods and HPLC, highlighting the reliability of assays. The developed methods extend the application of PBNPs-based dual-mode ICAs in food safety detection. In the future, the application of PBNPs could be further expanded in multi-mode immunoassay systems to increase detection stability and range. Various novel detection platforms, including microfluidic systems and immunochip systems, could be developed based on PBNPs, providing new ideas for achieving rapid detection of single and multiple small-molecule contaminants.
