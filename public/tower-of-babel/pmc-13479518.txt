Mapping stakeholders for developing cancer research in Small Island Developing States: a descriptive study from the Eastern Caribbean
Authors: Stephen B Johnson, Tricia Black, Boris Atabe, CaSIDEC study group, Aviane Auguste
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHigh cancer mortality in Small Island Developing States (SIDS) is compounded by the insufficient evidence from local epidemiological studies. Stakeholder engagement is particularly important in SIDS to ultimately drive these studies and cancer control. We aimed to map cancer control stakeholders as part of the development of the CaSIDEC study.MethodsThe Cancer in Small Island Developing States of the Eastern Caribbean (CaSIDEC) study investigates the role of overseas travel on cancer survivorship in the six countries of the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS). Stakeholders were identified during the study planning phases through professional networks, snowballing and public directories. Persons interested in research/cancer care or those capable of influencing it were included in the analysis. We assessed power and interest by attributing scores based on publicly available content and previous interactions. Based on scores, stakeholders were categorised into engagement typologies: promoters, latent, advocates or bystanders. Descriptive statistics were computed, and country-specific variations were examined.ResultsOf 159 identified stakeholders, 96 were analysed. The stakeholder subgroups included supporters (36%), policymakers (32%), implementers (18%) and civil society leaders (14%). The overall mean scores for interest and power were low (4.5/10, SD=2.6 and 4.7/10, SD=2.5, respectively). 29% of stakeholders were classified as latent, 28% as bystanders, 23% as advocates and 20% as promoters. Of these promoters, the majority were implementers and policymakers (36% each). Antigua, Saint Vincent and Saint Kitts had more representation from policymakers but less from implementers compared with other islands.ConclusionWe carried out the first analysis in the Caribbean, mapping stakeholders for an epidemiological study. Like other developing countries, interest in developing a large-scale epidemiological study was low among stakeholders. Cancer support groups and implementers were strong advocates for this research. Our findings will help guide efforts to increase the political will needed to generate new knowledge and policy.Trial registration number NCT07695116 Keywords: Cancer, Implementation Science, Epidemiology, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the most pressing global health challenges, accounting for nearly 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths in 2022 alone.1 Projections suggest that by 2050, the global burden will rise to 33 million new cases and 18 million deaths annually, driven by population ageing, lifestyle transitions and environmental exposure.1 While cancer affects all regions, its impact is disproportionately severe in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which account for over 65% of global cancer deaths.1 In LMICs, fragile health systems, limited access to early detection and inadequate treatment infrastructure contribute to late-stage diagnoses and thus poorer survival outcomes.2

Building on this global disparity, Small Island Developing States (SIDS), including those in the Caribbean, face even more severe challenges in cancer care than larger and more populous LMICs. Cancer mortality rates are noticeably higher in SIDS than in larger LMICs, such as those in Africa.3 Compared with Africa, age-standardised mortality rates for cancer are higher in small islands from the Caribbean and Polynesia (87.4 vs 99.2 and 118.9 per 100 000 inhabitants) in 2022.1

Contributing factors include low health literacy, limited screening initiatives, systemic barriers to timely care,4 lack of radiotherapy, and insufficient number of oncologists and pathologists.5 6 These countries are experiencing rapid population ageing, and consequently, the demand for oncology services is expected to increase.7 Unlike larger LMICs, SIDS cannot benefit from economies of scale. Their small, dispersed populations make it financially and logistically difficult to maintain specialised cancer infrastructure—leading to fragmented services and reliance on overseas referrals that delay diagnosis and treatment.8 Additionally, frequent natural disasters in SIDS disrupt health systems, care delivery and raise the risks of mortality among cancer patients.9

Despite this elevated burden, cancer control efforts in SIDS remain underdeveloped. A major barrier is the lack of locally generated data and research to inform policy and practice. Countries from the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS), in particular, have historically had some of the lowest health research outputs globally.10 This paucity of scientific publication stems from limited research capacity, insufficient funding, inadequate physical infrastructure and, more importantly, a lack of sustained commitment from key stakeholders.11 Without stakeholder support, efforts to build robust data resources for cancer research and evidence-based interventions are unlikely to succeed.12 In the OECS, the few research projects conducted have been historically led by organisations from the Global North with little emphasis on the needs of local stakeholders. Addressing this gap requires a deliberate effort to identify and engage stakeholders who hold influence and insight into the planning and development of local epidemiological studies.13 14

Recognising this need, stakeholder engagement is increasingly viewed as a cornerstone of effective health research, particularly in LMICs. It plays a vital role in shaping research priorities, improving relevance and enhancing the sustainability of interventions.12 Studies across LMICs have demonstrated the value of understanding stakeholder positioning in the complex landscape of health policy and research to ensure sustainable project outcomes. In Latin America and the Caribbean, stakeholder insights were instrumental in identifying policy issues and research priorities related to access to medicines.15 In Zambia, stakeholder involvement helped identify barriers to public participation and inform strategies for improvement; though key end users such as community members and patients were notably under-represented.12 This pattern reflects a broader challenge in LMICs, where research processes often prioritise institutional actors while neglecting those most affected by health outcomes. Similar findings have emerged from the South Pacific, where intentional stakeholder engagement, particularly through coalition-building and co-design frameworks, has proven effective in strengthening cancer control efforts.16 These studies collectively highlight that assessing stakeholder positioning during the early project phases is essential for inclusive policy development and ultimately more context-appropriate interventions and sustainable impact.17–19

Given these insights, low political interest has been consistently described as a significant barrier to developing quality epidemiological research in larger LMICs.13 14 However, previous academic work reported a distinct governance structure in SIDS due to their small population size. Moreover, the presence of fewer government organisations, hierarchical levels and positions facilitates coordination in SIDS via informal networks as well as inclusiveness and citizen participation in programme design and delivery.20 This political configuration in SIDS may encourage greater interest and direct involvement of policymakers and influential stakeholders in the planning and codevelopment of health policy. Previous studies from the Caribbean have shown successful mobilisation of key stakeholders, leading to the implementation of programmes for the prevention of diabetes and mosquito-borne diseases.17 20 21 Notwithstanding, the involvement of prominent community groups in Saint Lucia was a strong determinant for the success of our academic work on cancer survivorship on the island.22–24 Little is known about the dynamics between stakeholders and the development of large-scale epidemiological studies on cancer in SIDS, which could be different to what has been observed in larger LMICs. To our knowledge, no study has assessed stakeholder positioning for research studies on cancer in the Caribbean.

We aimed to identify and map stakeholders involved in an epidemiological study in the OECS, based on their interest and power. This analysis will inform the implementation of the Cancer in Small Island Developing States of the Eastern Caribbean (CaSIDEC) study and ultimately contribute to an increased likelihood of translating future study findings into evidence-based policy to reduce the cancer burden in the OECS.

Method

Study setting and positionality

This current analysis was based on stakeholder engagement conducted during the planning and development phases of the CaSIDEC study,25 26 which started on 17 February 2023 and is currently ongoing. The CaSIDEC study is a multicountry project examining the impact of overseas travel on cancer survivorship in the OECS. It covers six protocol member states of the OECS (Antigua and Barbuda, Dominica, Grenada, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Lucia, and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines). The protocol for the CaSIDEC study has been described in detail elsewhere.25 The principal investigator and the core team were based in Saint Lucia, where the pilot study was carried out from 2019 to 2020. This core team consists entirely of individuals of Afro-Caribbean descent. The principal investigator holds an academic appointment at a Canadian university and is a member of the leadership of a local research institute in Saint Lucia.

Study design

For this stakeholder analysis, we adapted the approach originally developed by Rietbergen-McCracken and Narayan.27 Using this approach, we identified stakeholders, assessed their interest and power, and provided recommendations for effective stakeholder engagement.

Defining the phenomenon of interest

To better identify these stakeholders, we define the phenomenon of interest as the planning and development of an epidemiological study. This is described as the systematic process of generating, synthesising and contextualising evidence to support health systems, policies and practices. This phenomenon extends beyond the design and interpretation of findings to include the logistics and activities involved in operationalising a protocol for an epidemiological study. Therefore, stakeholders in our research were individuals or groups with an interest in and the ability to directly or significantly influence the planning and development of epidemiological studies on cancer across the OECS. These stakeholders participate in a series of interconnected activities, including setting priorities, funding, ethics approval, data collection, analysis, dissemination and ultimately, translating findings into policy or practice.

Stakeholder identification

During the planning and development phases of the CaSIDEC study, the core team identified stakeholders to be engaged using several approaches. For this current analysis, we considered stakeholders who were engaged from 17 February 2023 until 1 May 2025. The start date corresponds to the initiation of structured outreach across the six participating countries. Initial participants were selected from existing professional networks, social media, online directories and during regional health conferences. This was supplemented by a snowballing approach, where initial contacts were asked to recommend additional individuals who were actively involved in planning and development of epidemiological or health research, the delivery of oncology services, or health policy in the OECS. This included officials from Ministries of Health, national bodies coordinating cancer programmes and policies, such as universal health coverage, civil society organisations, including cancer support groups, non-governmental organisations (NGO), patients and their caregivers. Stakeholders from Saint Lucia were over-represented due to existing relationships with the core team and the fact that they are based in Saint Lucia.

Stakeholders were eligible for inclusion in our analysis if they had been engaged by the core research team either in person or remotely via emails and telephone calls. Outside of formal meetings, they were considered influential figures within the OECS if they met at least one of these criteria: (1) direct involvement in cancer care; (2) influence on health systems or policy inclusive of regional organisations; (3) civil society and community-based actors and (4) corperate organisations involved in the social protection of cancer survivors. Stakeholders were excluded if they had no relevance to cancer or overseas medical travel, if they were not directly involved in regional care or policy or if we had insufficient information available to reliably assess their interest and power scores.

Assessment of interest and power

Each stakeholder was assigned an interest and power score ranging from 0 to 10. The total scores comprised several components. Components were scored and weighted according to their importance relative to the demonstrated commitment and potential to influence the success of our study. In this study, ‘interest’ is an established component of the stakeholder analysis framework. It does not reflect personal concern, professional values or intrinsic motivation regarding cancer research.

Interest scores were created to measure the stakeholders’ level of engagement, their alignment with project goals and past involvement in relevant initiatives. Specifically, we assessed five components: (1) the stakeholder’s attendance rate at project-related meetings, which accounted for 30% of the final interest score. This score also considered the representatives of stakeholders at the meetings; (2) participation in the project, including completing action items from meetings and performing tasks that advance the project (25%); (3) their previous involvement in similar epidemiological or public health research (20%); (4) any clear expressions of support, documented in correspondence or official statements (15%) and (5) the alignment of the stakeholder’s role with key project functions (10%).

Power scores evaluated a stakeholder’s ability to influence project outcomes based on their structural authority, access to resources and relational influence. This score is derived from five components: (1) the stakeholder’s job title and formal decision-making authority (40%); (2) control over key resources such as funding, personnel or data infrastructure (20%); (3) influence in relevant policy or practice (15%); (4) the strength and reach of their network connections, including links to regional or international actors (15%) and (5) the stakeholder’s visibility in publicly available content and media (10%).

To enhance consistency in scoring and interpretation, the scoring process was led by the principal investigator, and they were assisted by a second member of the research team in case of uncertainty. Scores for uncertain cases were discussed, and consensus was reached through iterative review. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (V.2025.05.1+513).

Stakeholder mapping and data analysis

We mapped stakeholders using a power–interest matrix and provided recommendations for targeted engagement to implement the CaSIDEC study. Stakeholders were divided into four quadrants based on their assigned scores for power and interest: no/almost no power or interest (score 0 to <3), low (score ≥3 to <5), medium (score ≥5 to <8), and high (score ≥8). They were categorised into four engagement typologies: promoters, latent, advocates and bystanders, depending on their levels of power and interest in the project. This framework adapts the power–interest matrix,28 a widely used tool in implementation science and project planning. Promoters are stakeholders with medium to high levels of interest and power. Latent stakeholders possess medium to high power but low interest. Advocates have medium to high interest but low power. Bystanders show little to no power or interest regarding the development and implementation of health research. We also conducted a country-specific mapping to identify gaps in representation, which helped us determine which OECS countries had adequate representation of stakeholder categories. The Precaution Adoption Process Model (PAPM) was used as a conceptual framework to guide interpretation of stakeholder engagement and to inform the development of tailored engagement strategies.

To aid in interpreting our results and strategic engagement, we classified stakeholders into four subgroups based on their organisational affiliation and role in cancer control: policymakers, implementers, civil society leaders and supporters. Policymakers held formal authority and were responsible for high-level decisions on national health priorities, budget allocations, cancer policy development, and regional or international collaborations. Implementers were clinicians and other frontline professionals directly responsible for coordinating, managing or delivering cancer care services across the care pathway, including screening, diagnosis, treatment, follow-up and palliative support. Civil society leaders were members of the executive board or leadership of cancer support groups/local NGOs. Supporters included scientists, academics, cancer survivors and informal caregivers.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and the public were not involved in this current analysis.

Results

We identified 159 individuals who were considered potential stakeholders for the CaSIDEC study. Of those stakeholders, a total of 96 were engaged and met the eligibility criteria for this analysis. This included persons across the six OECS countries involved in the CaSIDEC study, as well as other development partners outside of the OECS protocol members. Individuals were excluded primarily due to insufficient information to assess interest and power, limited engagement with the core research team or unclear relevance to cancer research.

Table 1 shows the characteristics of stakeholders engaged included in the current analysis. The stakeholder subgroups were diverse, with supporters (36%) and policymakers (32%) constituting the largest subgroups, followed by implementers (18%) and civil society leaders (14%). The largest proportion of stakeholders was from Saint Lucia (38.5%), followed by Saint Kitts and Nevis and Dominica (12.5% each), then Antigua and Barbuda (10.5%). Saint Vincent and the Grenadines and Grenada had considerably fewer stakeholders engaged (9.4% and 6.2%, respectively). The remaining stakeholders included were development partners based outside of the OECS member states, not represented in the CaSIDEC study. Women comprised nearly two-thirds of stakeholders (64%). They were particularly well represented in roles such as supporters and civil society leaders. However, implementers were mostly men (76.5%). Across the region, noticeable differences existed in the roles played by stakeholders in each country (figure 1). Saint Lucia had the largest representation across stakeholder subgroups. All subgroups of stakeholders were represented for Saint Lucia, and their proportions were evenly distributed relative to other countries. In contrast, Antigua, Saint Vincent and Saint Kitts had primarily policymakers (56%, 50% and 42%, respectively). Relative to the other countries, Antigua and Saint Vincent had a higher number of policymakers, but we did not engage any implementers in those countries during the period covered in this analysis. These divergent country distributions likely reflect differences in the timing and depth of stakeholder engagement across countries during the study period.

We also examined the workplace of persons by subgroups. Most implementers worked at healthcare establishments (88.2%). The workplace for supporters was the most diverse. More than half of them were salaried by an academic institution (51.4%), and a third were members of cancer support groups, including cancer survivors and informal caregivers.

We also assessed interest and power quantitatively across stakeholder subgroups. The average interest score across all stakeholders was 4.5 (SD=2.6), indicating a low level of commitment and motivation to be involved in the development and implementation of health research. Similarly, the overall average power score was low (4.7, SD=2.5). Importantly, policymakers demonstrated the lowest average interest (2.5, SD=2.3), in contrast with civil society leaders (5.7, SD=1.6), supporters (5.6, SD=2.4) and implementers (5.2, SD=1.7), who showed moderate interest. Regarding power, policymakers had moderate power on average. Power was highly variable among civil society leaders, with some scoring power levels comparable to those of policymakers.

Stakeholder mapping using the power–interest matrix (figure 2) provided further insight into engagement typology and dynamics. Nearly 20% of stakeholders were identified as promoters. Promoters had the capacity and commitment to advance the research agenda. Of these promoters, the majority were implementers and civil society leaders (36% each). Over a third of supporters (37%) and civil society leaders (38%) were classified as bystanders, reflecting their lower formal power and/or a lower commitment to the study. Meanwhile, latent stakeholders comprised 29% of the total sample. Policymakers comprised a little over half of this group (54%), representing significant potential to influence study implementation, particularly with increased engagement.

Discussion

This analysis was conducted in part to inform the CaSIDEC study, a multicountry initiative examining the impact of overseas travel on cancer survivorship in the OECS.25 This first-of-its-kind mapping study in the OECS also helped us learn more about the unique stakeholder configuration for health research in these SIDS. Our work will enhance the development of local data resources, as well as their potential impact on cancer control policy and practice.

The results revealed that of the 96 stakeholders, 29% were classified as latent, 28% as bystanders, 23% as advocates and 20% as promoters. The study also revealed that stakeholders across the OECS generally exhibited low levels of interest in health research, with civil society leaders and supporters being the most interested but lacking power. In contrast, policymakers held the highest levels of power but had the lowest average interest. This result opposes the hypothesised higher interest owing to more informal government structures and closer proximity to the community relative to larger LMICs.20 Given the local context, it is important to avoid interpreting these low interest scores as a lack of concern by policymakers since their commitment to research may be shaped by competing priorities, structural constraints or indirect modes of participation unknown to the core team.

Low interest among policymakers is not unique to the OECS. Similarly, low interest has been observed in other LMICs, where policymakers engage minimally in health research despite their considerable influence. A study investigating stakeholder engagement in the health policy process in Malawi found that political actors in LMICs often deprioritise research because of competing national priorities, limited institutional capacity and a lack of incentives for evidence-based policymaking, which in turn weakens the integration of research evidence into national health planning.29 Similarly, a study about priorities for cancer research in LMICs emphasised that in LMICs such as India, the lack of public and political prioritisation for biomedical research, stemming from misconceptions, limited awareness and competing socioeconomic needs, hindered investment and engagement in research processes, even for pressing health issues like cancer.13 Another study further highlighted that cancer research in LMICs often struggles for political attention due to systemic underfunding, fragmented health systems and the perception that other immediate priorities outweigh research investment.13 14 Direct comparisons of our engagement typologies with findings from previous literature are challenging. Indeed, few stakeholder analyses conducted in LMICs were based on implementing health interventions and not on epidemiological research. Additionally, these studies did not employ our quantitative method combining stakeholder roles and the power–interest matrix. Nonetheless, we can widely situate the positioning of our stakeholders by engagement typology relative to other LMICs. In Uganda, qualitative interviews showed that high-level policymakers displayed power–interest profiles equivalent to promoters. A female member of parliament and a senior medical officer at the Ministry of Health both displayed high influence and elevated agreement (moderate and high, respectively) for a proposal on an integrated maternal/newborn care project in Eastern Uganda.

As in other LMIC contexts, strategic alliances with scientists and policymakers are needed in the OECS to elevate the perceived relevance of research—framing it as directly responsive to national and community priorities and building sustained political commitment.

Our stakeholder analysis revealed a notable gendered pattern. Although women comprised the majority of stakeholders overall, they were significantly under-represented among implementers, who were primarily healthcare professionals and clinicians. Gender imbalance within the implementer group may limit the integration of perspectives from all genders and perpetuate inequities in service provision. Stakeholder engagement processes must be intentionally inclusive by promoting women’s participation in clinical leadership and embedding gender equity goals into health system governance structures. These results do not necessarily reflect the gender distribution in the healthcare workforce in the OECS. However, gender is an important parameter to consider.

Stakeholder configuration varied across the study sites. Saint Lucia showed the most balanced engagement among all four stakeholder subgroups. This likely results from earlier efforts during the pilot study in Saint Lucia, where engagements took a grassroots, bottom-up approach involving primarily community-based cancer support groups and health professionals. These community groups played a crucial role in building credibility, interest and trust in the research process among study participants for the pilot. Although the pilot study in Saint Lucia may have influenced the number and distribution of stakeholder subgroups, this result underscores the value of grassroots engagement strategies in getting stakeholder buy-in, especially in Small Island States where community support is vital.20 A systematic review emphasised the importance of community engagement in LMICs, noting that bottom-up approaches are not only ethically valid but also practically effective. The review found that involving grassroots actors enhances credibility, participation and the relevance of health initiative projects.30

In contrast, in countries like Antigua and Saint Vincent, a top–down approach was used to engage stakeholders, leading to delays in engaging healthcare professionals in those countries. In Antigua, Grenada and Saint Kitts, implementers were not engaged by our core team during the planning period. This reveals a limitation of a top–down approach in reaching frontline healthcare professionals, who are crucial for implementing health research. Our findings align with a study that posited that implementers in Asia and Africa are often excluded from research planning despite their close connection to service delivery and understanding of local challenges.18 These insights emphasise the need for inclusive engagement strategies that involve clinicians in the early phases of research. Similarly, a realist implementation study in New South Wales, Australia, showed that system-wide healthcare reforms were successful largely because of the active participation of clinicians. Their involvement helped adapt research outcomes to local needs and drove change efforts, demonstrating that clinician engagement is vital for effective and sustainable implementation.19

These observed differences in stakeholder representation may also reflect the staged nature of stakeholder engagement in multicountry research. In the first months of the study period, we adopted a uniform approach for stakeholders across countries. Our strategy consisted primarily of introductory meetings and formal written correspondence to Ministries of Health, local cancer support groups and key stakeholders. Thereafter, stakeholder engagement occurred differentially. We leveraged regional events, research conferences and informal interactions to advance stakeholder engagement. Given the disparate distribution of stakeholder roles and typologies across countries, our study underscores the need for sustained and iterative engagement over time to achieve a substantial list of stakeholders that is representative of diverse profiles.

This current study has laid the groundwork to help us develop a dynamic stakeholder engagement plan to optimise the success of a new epidemiological study in understudied SIDS of the Eastern Caribbean. The categorisation of the stakeholders based on interest and power will allow us to develop our plan by fitting our findings into the PAPM.31 The PAPM will guide interpretation and enable us to broadly differentiate the stage at which stakeholders are situated currently. We then propose stage-appropriate engagement strategies for each engagement typology.31 This model identifies seven stages of decision making. It explains how an individual might move from being unaware of the problem to making decisions and taking action. Recommendations will be proposed, and tailored engagement interventions will be applied to specific stages presented in the model.31 Figure 4 outlines the broad strategy that will be developed based on the PAPM model. For instance, promoters, who are likely to be at stages 5 (‘decided to act’) and 6 (‘acting’) according to the model, are stakeholders who are part of the project from the start, helping to shape plans and make things happen. They should be provided with clear, specific instructions on how they can get involved, with well-defined roles and responsibilities, to help them understand their role. They should receive regular updates, progress reports and success stories to demonstrate the impact of their efforts and should also have a channel for providing feedback and recommendations to the CaSIDEC research team. Promoters who are already acting (Stage 7) are most likely members of the CaSIDEC Project Steering Committee26 and their active participation should be maintained. They can be involved in the supervision of research trainees working on the CaSIDEC study. Likewise, advocates would have decided to act and should be provided with resources and platforms to amplify their voices and influence. They should be offered similar opportunities to the promoters.

Latent stakeholders, who are mostly policymakers, are likely to be in the third and fourth stages (‘undecided about acting’ and ‘decided not to act’) of the PAPM model. They should be engaged in interactive workshops/forums where they can ask questions, share opinions and delve deep into the details of the CaSIDEC study. They should strategically be consulted and gradually moved along the engagement and PAPM continuum through clear framing of policy benefits and alignment with national agendas. Latent stakeholders who have decided not to act (Stage 4) should be engaged in one-on-one interviews to understand the reasons for their decision; then, re-engage them based on these findings. Bystanders may be unaware of the cancer burden and mortality in the OECS and are therefore not heavily involved with the CaSIDEC study because they do not see the potential impact it could have on cancer care in the OECS. To enhance the interest among these bystanders, the CaSIDEC study team should present a lay summary of the study to these stakeholders to illustrate how our work can affect them.

It is also interesting to note that while most civil society leaders had lower power scores overall, a noteworthy proportion was classified as promoters and showed power scores comparable to policymakers. This is a novel finding in the OECS context, as we were able to quantify the influence of civil society leadership for the first time. This suggests that civil society actors in the OECS may play a more important role in research than is typically observed in other LMICs.32 33 This contrasts with trends seen in other LMICs, where civil society actors usually have a high interest but relatively limited formal power in implementation research processes.32 33 However, in the OECS region, the role of civil society leaders may reflect strong community trust established through previous grassroots efforts and the responsiveness of civil society to local health needs. This indicates that civil society actors in the area may be uniquely positioned to play a key role in mobilising public support, linking policy and community, and coleading the implementation of research such as the CaSIDEC study.

Those stakeholders considered to have lower power (civil society leaders and supporters) may enhance their influence in research studies through formal integration into Ministry-level advisory committees, research methods training and leveraging platforms like the Caribbean Cancer Portal for collective advocacy.34

While this study provides important insights into stakeholder engagement dynamics in the OECS, several limitations should be acknowledged. Stakeholder identification relied heavily on professional networks and snowballing strategies, which may have excluded less visible or informal actors, especially from under-represented groups and islands. The core team was based in Saint Lucia and would greatly influence the approach used to engage with stakeholders and the persons with whom we would potentially come into contact. Patterns for excluded stakeholders suggest that exclusions were more common among individuals with limited engagement, incomplete information or indirect roles. This may have disproportionately affected stakeholders from countries where engagement with the core team was less intense. The researcher’s positionality would also affect the level of interest displayed by stakeholders for our research proposal. Indeed, OECS stakeholders outside of Saint Lucia may have been more inclined to buy into the study if the core members were from their own country. Also, the stakeholder engagement was built on existing relationships in Saint Lucia. As a result, the volume of stakeholders and interest levels would be skewed in favour of Saint Lucia. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. Particularly, the volume by country/role that was observed is not a true reflection of the absolute volume of stakeholders or their desire for engaging in research. Although the power and interest scores were based on a structured scoring system, they still involved subjective judgments, which could introduce inaccurate measures of the domains being evaluated. Moreover, while efforts were made to ensure consistency through iterative discussions, formal inter-rater reliability was not assessed, which may affect the reproducibility of the scoring process. It is important to note that our position as Saint Lucian nationals in the core team determines our interpretation of interest and power of stakeholders across the OECS. Our understanding of how project buy-in is perceived by stakeholders may therefore be limited. For instance, meeting attendance and execution of key action items were important parameters, and the quality of our assessment was further limited by our knowledge of the working arrangements of stakeholders across the OECS. In the OECS, for example, it is possible that Ministry leadership may rely closely on representatives to act on their behalf at meetings without the core research team knowing. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that the interest may have been underestimated for certain stakeholders. In terms of power, the individual components were assessed mostly through publicly available content. Power was determined heavily based on job titles and assumed resources; thus, our scores may not be representative of their true power and ability to influence the implementation of the CaSIDEC study. Notwithstanding, the assessment period was over 2 years, and the length of engagement was not equal for all stakeholders. Therefore, the quality of the assessment would not be uniform, and our criteria may favour persons with a longer history of engagement with the core team. The lack of an in-depth qualitative investigation restricted our ability to thoroughly explore the underlying reasons behind stakeholder positions or their willingness to engage in health research. This also limited the possibility of positioning stakeholders precisely across the different stages in the PAPM.

It is also interesting to note that while most civil society leaders had lower power scores overall, a noteworthy proportion was classified as promoters and showed power scores comparable to policymakers. This is a novel finding in the OECS context as we were able to quantify the influence of civil society leadership for the first time. This suggests that civil society actors in the OECS may play a more important role in research than is typically observed in other LMICs.32 33 This contrasts with trends seen in other LMICs where civil society actors usually have a high interest but relatively limited formal power in implementation research processes.32 33 However, in the OECS region, the role of civil society leaders may reflect strong community trust established through previous grassroots efforts and the responsiveness of civil society to local health needs. This indicates that civil society actors in the area may be uniquely positioned to play a key role in mobilising public support, linking policy and community, and coleading the implementation of research such as the CaSIDEC study.

Conclusion

This study presents the first stakeholder analysis of health research planning and development in the Caribbean. It offers novel insights into the roles and positioning of stakeholders in terms of interests and power across six OECS countries and their development partners. Our findings by country underscore the importance of time and repeated engagement in building diverse stakeholder profiles needed to successfully develop epidemiological research in SIDS. Our work revealed low interest overall among stakeholders in conducting the CaSIDEC study, which examines cancer survivorship. However, 20% of the stakeholders engaged so far were well positioned (promoters) to successfully drive the research implementation and subsequent dissemination. We observed that civil society organisations in the OECS had considerable potential to influence the research relative to policymakers who were largely categorised as latent (54%). Nearly a third of the stakeholders engaged were classified as latent, and this represents a great potential to advance the study, provided additional and consistent engagement. While more in-depth research is warranted to elucidate these results, this first analysis lays the groundwork for more inclusive and strategic collaborations that can strengthen cancer research and policy in understudied SIDS and LMICs globally.
