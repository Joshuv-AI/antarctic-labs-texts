Revolutionizing automated pear picking using Mamba architecture
Authors: Peirui Zhao, Weiwei Cai, Wenhua Zhou, Na Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the emergence of the new generation vision architecture Vmamba and the further demand for agricultural yield and efficiency, we propose an efficient and high-accuracy target detection network for automated pear picking tasks based on Vmamba, aiming to address the issue of low efficiency in current Transformer architectures. The proposed network, named SRSMamba, employs a Reward and Punishment Mechanism (RPM) to focus on important information while minimizing redundancy interference. It utilizes 3D Selective Scan (SS3D) to extend scanning dimensions and integrates global information across channel dimensions, thereby enhancing the model's robustness in complex agricultural environments and effectively adapting to the extraction of complex features in pear orchards and farmlands. Additionally, a Stacked Feature Pyramid Network (SFPN) is introduced to enhance semantic information during the feature fusion stage, particularly improving the detection capability for small targets. Experimental results show that SRSMamba has a low parameter count of 21.1 M, GFLOPs of 50.4, mAP of 72.0%, mAP50 reaching 94.8%, mAP75 at 68.1%, and FPS at 26.9. Compared with other state-of-the-art (SOTA) object detection methods, it achieves a good trade-off between model efficiency and detection accuracy.Keywords: Vmamba, Agricultural, Pear picking, Transformer, FPN, Small targets, Object detection

Introduction

Pears are among the top five most economically and nutritionally valuable fruits worldwide, popular for both fresh consumption and processing [1, 2]. Their cultivation is widespread, offering substantial economic benefits and boosting farmers' incomes due to the fruit’s adaptability and productivity. However, pear harvesting remains largely manual, labor-intensive, and inefficient, which increases costs and faces challenges from a declining rural workforce [3]. In tackling these issues, robotic harvesting has emerged as a promising solution, with the development of vision systems being critical [4]. These systems are essential for accurately locating and identifying fruits, stems, and calyxes within complex orchard environments. Despite advancements in fruit recognition technologies, challenges such as fruit occlusion, variable lighting, and diverse pear appearances limit the effectiveness of conventional detection methods. Deep learning-based visual systems have shown promise in overcoming these challenges, enhancing precision and adaptability. This progress could lead to broader adoption of robotic pear pickers, reducing labor needs, cutting costs, and improving harvesting efficiency [5]. Earlier robotic fruit picking relied on basic sensor technologies that performed well only under specific conditions, struggling with environmental variations and different pear types [6, 7]. The integration of deep learning with robotic systems has proven more effective and adaptable in various agricultural settings [8, 9]. Unlike traditional methods that required manual feature selection, deep learning algorithms automatically learn optimal features from large datasets, providing superior performance and flexibility.

Many methods [10–16] leverage deep learning to automate fruit processing, making it a superior choice for efficient fruit harvesting systems, e. g Hongwei Li et al. [17] utilized a lightweight improved YOLOv5s model featuring a novel convolutional architecture for enhanced detection of pitaya fruits under varying light conditions, achieving significant improvements in precision and processing speed critical for real-time applications in agritech; Fan Meng et al.[18] introduced a spatio-temporal convolutional neural network leveraging the shifted window Transformer, which enhances the detection of pineapples by addressing the challenges posed by variable environmental conditions and fruit overlaps, thus optimizing the accuracy and efficiency of unmanned pineapple picking;Mingyou Chen et al. [19] developed advanced visual servo control methods, integrating a set of vision algorithms for autonomous and continuous fruit harvesting in orchards, which is crucial for improving the operational efficiency and adaptability of robotic harvesters.

Indeed, deep learning has been recognized as a superior method for enhancing the efficiency of automated pear harvesting systems [20–25]. Xiao et al. [20] reviewed deep learning applications in fruit harvesting, but their insights on pear detection are limited. Yang et al. [21] focused on spectral analysis for fruit quality, while Zhao et al. [22] introduced a high-precision object detection network for pear picking, although its narrow focus may restrict broader use.BORJA [23] developed real-time algorithms for pear counting, essential for immediate feedback, but these may overlook important metrics like fruit size and health. Xie et al. [24] created ECLPOD, a lightweight model designed for efficiency in smart agriculture, though this can impact accuracy. Sun et al. [25] proposed YOLO-P, a fast model optimized for orchard conditions, balancing speed and precision despite challenges in lighting.These studies collectively highlight the evolving landscape of pear detection technologies, emphasizing the need for advancements to increase efficiency and reduce labor intensity in traditional harvesting. In summary, pear detection frameworks have evolved more slowly than general object detection, and this work investigates the efficacy of the Mamba architecture for high-precision pear detection.

In automated pear picking, accurate identification and positioning are crucial for the robotic arm's effectiveness, while speed impacts production efficiency. Thus, there is a pressing need for a high-precision, high-efficiency object detection method. Transformer methods [26–28] generally offer wider receptive fields than CNNs [29–32], achieving higher accuracy but facing efficiency challenges due to self-attention's quadratic complexity. The Mamba network [33] shows clear advantages over traditional networks like YOLO [32], Faster R-CNN [30], and DETR [34], particularly in challenging scenarios such as detecting small or occluded objects. Vmamba, a variant of the Mamba network tailored for agricultural applications, excels in fruit detection within dynamic environments, thanks to its lightweight design that enables rapid processing of multiple images.

Vmamba effectively enhances fruit recognition in complex backgrounds by integrating deep separable convolutions and attention mechanisms, surpassing YOLO's limitations in detecting small or partially occluded fruits. While Faster R-CNN has high accuracy, its computational demands limit its use in resource-constrained settings, a limitation that Vmamba overcomes. DETR's lengthy training times and high requirements make it less suitable for fast deployment in evolving agricultural environments. In contrast, Vmamba's streamlined training process minimizes the need for complex pre- and post-processing, facilitating rapid updates as fruit types and maturity levels change.

Despite YOLOv7s having a slight edge in processing speed, its lower accuracy in detecting small or occluded objects diminishes its suitability for fruit detection tasks. Vmamba strikes a balance between speed and accuracy, making it an optimal choice for real-world agricultural applications. The Mamba network is pivotal in automated pear picking systems, offering unique architecture and optimizations that enhance detection capabilities in complex agricultural scenes.

One key challenge in automated pear picking is accurately identifying fruits amid occlusions. The Mamba network improves feature extraction and enhances learning algorithms, increasing detection rates under challenging conditions. Additionally, the training process has been optimized with customized data augmentation techniques and targeted fine-tuning to adapt to the diverse characteristics of agricultural imagery.

In summary, the advantages of the Mamba network not only enhance the operational efficiency and accuracy of automated pear picking systems but also provide valuable insights for other agricultural automation tasks, advancing technology and applications within the field.

Dataset acquisition

In the field of automated pear harvesting, there is a scarcity of existing datasets, and no publicly available datasets exist for automatic pear object detection exist, making it challenging for models to achieve accurate identification and detection. Therefore, before conducting the research outlined in this paper, it is necessary to collect and create a dataset. Firstly, we used a Canon professional camera to capture im-ages, then removed low-quality images caused by factors such as camera shake, resulting in a total of 7,366 images of pear trees in farmlands and orchards (see Fig. 2 for a dataset example). The dataset is diverse, highlighting the richness of pear varieties in automated pear harvesting scenarios. The dataset will be available for public download at https://github.com/zwhculture/SRSMamba_dataset/tree/main.

Vmamba

Architecture overview of Vmamba

VMamba integrates state space models with a selective scan mechanism to process visual data. The architecture commences by partitioning input images into patches, maintaining their 2D structure to form feature maps. These maps are then processed through several Visual State Space (VSS) blocks, which constitute the model's fundamental building blocks. VMamba also employs a hierarchical representation, similar to CNN models and some Vision Transformers (ViTs) [35], by down-sampling feature maps at various stages.

Core innovations and differences from transformer

The primary innovation in VMamba lies in its combination of global receptive fields and dynamic weights while achieving linear computational complexity, which is in contrast to the quadratic complexity of traditional Transformers. VMamba introduces the Cross-Scan Module (CSM) to address the direction-sensitive issue in visual data processing. Unlike Transformers, which use attention mechanisms, CSM adopts a four-way scanning strategy, ensuring each element in a feature map integrates information from all other locations in different directions. This approach provides a global receptive field without increasing linear computational complexity, a significant departure from the traditional Transformer models.

The Cross-Scan Module (CSM) in the VMamba architecture is an innovative component designed to address the direction-sensitive issue in visual data processing. It adopts a four-way scanning strategy, scanning from the four corners of the image feature map to the opposite location. This method ensures that each element in the feature map integrates information from all other locations in different directions, enabling a global receptive field without increasing linear computational complexity. The CSM allows for a comprehensive understanding of spatial relations in images, making it a key differentiator from traditional models that use attention mechanisms.

SRSMamba

Faster R-CNN, an advancement in object detection for computer vision, represents a significant leap in the field. Developed in 2015, it extends the capabilities of its predecessors, R-CNN and Fast R-CNN, by introducing a novel Region Proposal Network (RPN). This network autonomously suggests potential object regions from the feature maps generated by a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), dramatically enhancing efficiency and accuracy. The integration of the RPN with a Fast R-CNN detector enables the sharing of convolutional features between the proposal and detection networks, streamlining the process. Additionally, the Faster R-CNN employs RoI (Region of Interest) Pooling to extract a fixed-size feature vector from each proposed region, which is then used for classification and bounding box regression. This model has set a new standard in object detection, particularly in terms of speed and accuracy, making it a pivotal development in computer vision research. This article selects Faster R-CNN as the detector and Vmamba as the backbone to establish a baseline for the automatic detection of pears, while the loss function continues to use Faster R-CNN's Smooth L1 Loss. While S6 possesses its unique characteristics, it processes input data causally, meaning it can only capture information within the scanned portion of the data. This naturally aligns S6 with NLP tasks involving temporal data but presents significant challenges when adapting to non-causal data types such as images, graphs, and sequence. A direct approach Vmamba takes to address this issue is by scanning the data along/in two different directions (i.e., forward and backward), allowing them to compensate for each other's receptive fields without increasing computational complexity. Vmamba introduces the cross-scanning module for the first time, opting to unfold image patches into sequences along rows and columns (scan unfolding), and then scanning along four different directions: from the top-left corner to the bottom-right, from the bottom-right to the top-left, from the top-right to the bottom-left, and from the bottom-left to the top-right. In this way, any pixel integrates information from all other pixels across different directions. Each sequence is then reshaped back into a single image, and all sequences are combined into a new sequence (Fig. 4).

Reward and punishment mechanism(RPM)

Feature points in images of farmlands and pear orchards often contain relevant semantic information and redundant information which, when excessive, may interfere with other feature points [36]. Previous deep learning methods tend to overlook the contextual relationships between pixels when processing images, leading to potential false positives and missed detections of targets of interest (objects with high similarity to background features). Introducing methods to the model that reduce redundant and irrelevant information can mitigate this phenomenon, however. The key to reducing redundant information lies in extracting important feature information such as texture, shape, and semantics, and finding the accurate contextual relationships between pixels while ignoring the useless/superfluous parts of the feature map. Therefore, removing unnecessary features while retaining important ones is crucial for effectively enhancing the model's resistance to interference.

To reduce the impact of redundant information on object detection performance and to fully utilize the contextual relationships between pixels, we propose a reward and punishment mechanism, which consists of the Swish, Tanh functions, and the proposed reward-penalty operator.

The mathematical principle of removing redundant information can be explained from the perspective of information compression and feature selection. RPM effectively screens and optimizes features by combining different activation functions and threshold operations, thereby achieving the purpose of reducing redundant information in the data. The specific reasons for removing redundant information are as follows: (1) The Swish activation function can smoothly process feature points, and due to its nonlinear characteristics, it can retain important features while compressing those eigenvalues with smaller amplitudes. Such nonlinear mapping helps to improve the model's sensitivity to important features. The Tanh activation function has an output range of [−1, 1]; compressing the data into a fixed interval helps to neutralize and balance the eigenvalues and reduce the instability caused by excessive eigenvalues. At the same time, the symmetry of Tanh also helps to standardize the data. (2) By performing the Hadamard product (element-wise multiplication) on the feature maps processed by Swish and Tanh, this operation combines the advantages of the two processing methods, which can further strengthen useful feature signals and weaken those unimportant or redundant features. This fusion method enhances the expressiveness of the features, and through element-by-element multiplication, only the features that are strong in both feature maps can be reflected in the final fused feature map, effectively compressing the data and reducing redundancy. (3) The setting of threshold T is the key to determining which features are retained and which are discarded; by setting an appropriate threshold, the model sets all feature values below T to zero. This operation is similar to a nonlinear filter, which can eliminate weak features that are considered noise or non-critical, and only retain representative or significant features. This threshold-based selection not only reduces the computational burden of the model, but also improves the processing speed and prediction accuracy because the model no longer needs to process a large amount of invalid data.

In summary, RPM effectively identifies and strengthens useful features by combining multiple processing mechanisms, while weakening or removing redundant and irrelevant features. This method is mathematically implemented through the selection of activation functions, feature fusion strategies, and an intelligent threshold setting, which effectively improves the performance and efficiency of the model. Additionally, theoretically, the RPM can serve as a universal module that can be integrated into other networks. It is broadly applicable for reducing redundant information in agricultural settings, such as noise data in orchards. Its specific applications will be further explored in future work.

Stacked Feature Pyramid Network (SFPN)

In the field of automated fruit harvesting, the processing of scale information plays a crucial role in the accuracy and efficiency of target detection. Due to the varying sizes, shapes, and maturity levels of fruits in their natural environment, changes in their scale in images pose a significant challenge in automated harvesting systems. Effectively utilizing and processing scale information is vital for enhancing the precision and adaptability of fruit detection. The size of pears, for instance, changes noticeably as they grow naturally, meaning their size at different stages of maturity can vary greatly. Therefore, automated harvesting systems must be able to accurately identify and handle these variations in scale to ensure the effective detection of fruits of all sizes. This not only improves the system's applicability but also ensures the efficiency and accuracy of the harvesting process. The effective processing of scale information is also crucial for distinguishing fruits from other objects in their surrounding environment. In complex natural settings, pears often blend in with leaves, branches, and other environmental elements. Through multi-scale analysis, automated harvesting systems can better differentiate between fruits and the background, enhancing the accuracy of recognition. Additionally, the effective use of scale information aids in improving the operational precision of harvesting robots. In actual harvesting processes, robots need to adjust their grabbing actions based on the size and location of the fruit. Accurate identification and handling of scale information enable the embedded deep learning in robots to more precisely locate fruits, thereby increasing the success rate and efficiency of harvesting.

Building upon the background of utilizing scale information in automated fruit harvesting, we aim to augment the Feature Pyramid Network's (FPN) [38] capability to capture multi-scale information via convolutions. Drawing inspiration from the Inception module and Res2Net [39], we introduce the Scale Fusion Convolution (SFC) for FPN. This novel approach comprises a standard 3 × 3 convolution paired with a 1 × 1 convolution. The inclusion of these two convolutional layers is strategic; the 3 × 3 convolution effectively processes spatial information, capturing features relevant to the scale and shape of various fruits, while the 1 × 1 convolution acts as a mechanism for integrating these features, ensuring that finer details are not lost in the feature fusion process. This dual convolution approach in SFPN facilitates the flow of information through multiple local receptive fields during feature fusion, enhancing the network's ability to detect fruits of varying sizes and maturity levels with greater precision. The 3 × 3 convolution layer captures the broader context and the more pronounced scale variations, which is crucial for differentiating fruits from complex backgrounds like foliage and branches. On the other hand, the 1 × 1 convolution layer focuses on finer details and textures, critical for recognizing smaller fruits or those partially obscured by leaves and other environmental elements. Moreover, the stacked 3 × 3 and 1 × 1 convolution is designed to operate seamlessly within the FPN architecture, ensuring that the multi-scale feature extraction does not compromise the network's efficiency. The integration of stacked 3 × 3 and 1 × 1 convolution into FPN results in a more robust and adaptable model, capable of handling the diverse challenges presented in automated fruit harvesting environments, such as varying lighting conditions, fruit orientations, and occlusions. In summary, the introduction of the stacked 3 × 3 and 1 × 1 convolution into the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) marks a significant advancement in the field of automated fruit harvesting. By leveraging the strengths of both the Inception module and Res2Net, SFC enriches the FPN's capability in multi-scale information processing, paving the way for more accurate and efficient fruit detection and, consequently, more effective automated harvesting solutions.

We analyze why SFPN is effective from a deeper perspective: The 1 × 1 convolution acts as a channel-wise transformation that adjusts the depth of feature maps without affecting their spatial resolution. This transformation is critical when merging features from different layers of the pyramid, as it ensures that the network can refine these features without losing essential details necessary for detecting fruits at various stages of growth and under different environmental conditions. By integrating 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 convolutions, the FPN efficiently combines low-level details captured in higher resolution layers with high-level semantic information from lower resolution layers. This fusion is particularly useful for fruit detection, as it ensures that the model can recognize fruits regardless of their scale in the image. The 1 × 1 convolutions facilitate this by compressing and then expanding the feature dimensions, effectively enabling the network to maintain crucial details while integrating broader contextual data from the 3 × 3 convolutions. The stacked arrangement of 1 × 1 followed by 3 × 3 convolutions allows the network to first focus on local features without the influence of their spatial arrangement (through the 1 × 1 convolutions) and then interpret these features within their larger spatial context (via the 3 × 3 convolutions). This sequential processing mimics the cognitive process of focusing on details first, then understanding the context, which is vital for distinguishing between fruits and other objects like leaves or branches that may have similar color and texture. More importantly, the dual convolution setting in FPN makes the network robust to common changes in lighting, size, and partial occlusion in agricultural environments. The 3 × 3 layer provides an elastic framework for understanding larger scene contexts, while the 1 × 1 layer maintains the integrity of local features. This robustness is crucial for accurate detection and classification of fruits, which directly affects the operational efficiency of harvesting robots.

The strategic layering of 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 convolutions within the FPN addresses several practical challenges in automated fruit harvesting. For example, the enhanced network can more effectively handle the varying lighting conditions, orientations, and partial occlusions of fruits in natural environments. The improved detection precision directly impacts the harvesting robots' ability to perform accurate location and size-based picking operations, significantly increasing the efficiency and success rate of automated harvesting systems. Moreover, the increased network capacity from this dual convolution approach potentially offers greater generalization over different types of fruits and more complex scenarios, suggesting a broader applicability of the model beyond pears to other fruits and possibly other agricultural tasks. In summary, the incorporation of both 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 convolutions within the FPN architecture significantly boosts its functionality by providing a robust framework for detailed and dynamic scale processing. This enhancement is pivotal for the demanding requirements of automated fruit harvesting, where accuracy, adaptability, and efficiency are crucial. By elaborating on these aspects, the paper will better align with the reviewers' expectations for a thorough analysis, underpinning the technical contributions with practical outcomes in the targeted application domain.

The SRSMamba model underwent training within the Pytorch environment, leveraging the model weights obtained for predicting pear images to assess the model's performance and precision. Based on Faster RCNN + Vmamba, we add SS3D, RPM, and SFPN using the control variable technique, and by merging the improvements of these three modules with SRSMamba, eight sets of ablation experiments were performed. We analyzed the reasons for performance improvement: (1) The SS3D technology, by adding dimensions to scanning, not only captures more comprehensive global information but also understands the information between channels. This means that the system considers not only spatial information (such as the shape and size of objects) but also semantic information (such as the category or attributes of objects). This consideration of integrated information allows the model to more accurately recognize and understand objects in the scene. Although this method may increase the computational cost slightly, the increase is minimal due to the efficiency of the design, and the benefits to performance improvement far outweigh the additional computational cost. (2) The RPM technology removes redundant information, specifically targeting object detection problems in dense scenes, such as pear detection, where multiple similar or repetitive objects may cause the algorithm to process redundant information, thus reducing efficiency and accuracy. RPM improves detection accuracy and efficiency by identifying and removing these redundant patterns, enabling the model to focus more on unique and important features. (3) The SFPN technology enhances the ability to fuse features at different scales, especially in detecting small objects within a scene. By effectively integrating features from different scales, SFPN provides a richer and more robust feature representation, thereby enhancing the model's ability to detect small objects. In tasks such as detecting small objects like pears, this scale fusion technology is particularly important because it helps the model capture and utilize key information at different scales, thereby improving detection accuracy. The comparative results of 8 sets of tests can completely demonstrate the function of the three techniques described in this study by enhancing the model's accuracy in autonomous pear picking. As a result, we feel that SRSMamba is more suited than Faster RCNN + Vmamba for object detection in autonomous pear picking settings.

Model training process

In this section, we present the training process of the model, showcasing images of accuracy, recall, loss function, and mAP curves. The accuracy curve provides a detailed view of the changes in classification accuracy on the validation set throughout the training process, marking each stage of model optimization to reveal potential issues of overfitting or underfitting. By precisely tracking the progress of accuracy, this study ensures that the direction of model iterations aligns with the experimental objectives. The recall curve offers a quantitative basis for assessing the model's ability to detect positive samples at different thresholds, which is crucial for handling datasets with class imbalance. This metric directly relates to the model's sensitivity and effectively reflects its performance in recognizing minority classes. The loss function curve examines the model's learning process from another angle, displaying the stability and convergence of model training through changes in loss values. Analyzing the loss curve aids in adjusting training parameters, optimizing learning rates, and regularization strategies, and is also crucial for determining whether the model has reached an optimal state. The mAP curve evaluates the model's performance across various object detection tasks by calculating average precision at multiple IoU thresholds, providing a comprehensive assessment of the model's overall efficacy. This curve is a key indicator of the detection model's comprehensive performance and is significant for guiding the practical deployment and application of the model. It is important to note that this section presents a comparison of the training processes between Vmamba and SRSMamba. Consequently, it utilizes results from the training and validation sets, which differ from the test set results shown in other sections (such as the mAP in Table 9), due to the absence of overlap between the training, validation, and test datasets.

Based on the results, we draw the following conclusions: (1) Both training and validation accuracies of SRSmamba and VMamba showed a steady increase with more epochs, indicating that both models are learning effectively and generalizing well. SRSmamba consistently outperformed VMamba throughout the training process, suggesting its superior model structure is better suited for pear detection. (2) Similarly, the recall rate also demonstrated a stable increase over time, meaning the models are increasingly better at identifying positive samples. SRSmamba's recall rates during both training and validation phases were higher than those of VMamba, indicating it is more effective at minimizing false negatives. (3) The loss curves for both models declined rapidly initially and then gradually leveled off, a typical sign of model convergence. The training and validation loss curves of SRSmamba and VMamba were very close, indicating that both models are equally effective at controlling overfitting. (4) As an important metric to assess detection model performance, the mAP curves show that SRSmamba performed better than VMamba, particularly on the validation data. The faster and more stable improvement in SRSmamba’s mAP is attributed to its more suitable model structure.

Practical application test

On the one hand, Internet of Things (IoT) systems are susceptible to interference caused by poor signal quality in orchards, affecting the transmission of images and the final detection performance of the model. On the other hand, a good pear detection model requires testing not only under ideal lighting conditions but also evaluation in real, complex orchard environments. This is because actual application environments may involve more common environmental interferences, such as shadows, changing lighting, and obstacles. To verify the model's generalization ability, we organized and captured 300 real orchard images from the pear orchards in Longjiazu Village, Reshi Town, Taoyuan County, Changde City, Hunan Province. To establish a complete system that facilitates practical detection operations, we set up an IoT system with cameras as the image input interface. The process is shown in Fig. 5 below.

Diagram illustrating the IoT-based SRSMamba pear detection system for automated picking

In Fig. 5, edge computing and server computing are processed within the "Network Processing" and "Host Compute" blocks, respectively. Specifically, the Network Processing block represents the computational tasks performed on the edge device, such as the initial stages of image preprocessing and feature extraction. These tasks are designed to be lightweight and efficient, leveraging the computational resources available on the edge device to reduce the amount of data transmitted to the server. The Host Compute block denotes the computational tasks executed on the server, which typically involves more complex and computationally intensive operations, such as the final stages of object detection and classification. The server utilizes its powerful processing capabilities to analyze the preprocessed data received from the edge device and generate the final detection results. The division of tasks between edge computing and server computing aims to optimize the overall system performance by balancing the trade-off between latency, bandwidth usage, and computational resources. This distributed computing architecture enables efficient and real-time object detection in the context of pear fruit harvesting.

Figure 6 illustrates the performance comparison between Baseline and SRSMamba in three distinct pear detection scenarios. We categorized 300 authentic images taken in Longjiazu Village, Reshi Town, Taoyuan County, Changde City, Hunan Province, into three groups for evaluation. The criteria for classification are primarily based on proximity and density; group 1 contains the easiest categories to detect, while group 3 encompasses the most challenging ones. Given a total of 300 images, we have employed manual classification to sort them. The data indicate that Baseline and SRSMamba achieve identification accuracies of 99% and 92% in the first category, 91% and 73% in the second category, and 78% and 51% in the third category, respectively. It is noteworthy that SRSMamba shows significant improvement over Vmamba in Category 3. This highlights that SRSMamba effectively addresses challenges such as long distances, fruit occlusions, and densely stacked arrangements, which complicate detection.

The performance outcomes of SRSMamba and Baseline across three image categories are as follows: Class 1 encompasses nearby objects with distinct characteristics, Class 2 covers objects at a medium distance where features are less pronounced, and Class 3 includes objects at a distance with features that are challenging to discern

To address the challenges posed by poor signal quality and environmental interferences in pear orchards, the IoT system features high-sensitivity cameras optimized for variable lighting conditions. These cameras adjust dynamically to minimize the effects of shadows and sunlight fluctuations, crucial in outdoor agricultural settings. The system employs a mesh network topology to enhance data transmission reliability. This approach enables each device to connect with multiple nodes, ensuring data rerouting in cases of node failure or signal interference, thereby maintaining continuous network performance across the orchard. Edge computing capabilities are integrated to process data locally at the camera sites, reducing the volume of data sent to central servers and accelerating the overall processing time. This local processing includes preliminary image analyses essential for efficient data management and prompt decision-making. Security measures are rigorously implemented, utilizing encrypted communication protocols and secure storage to safeguard against data breaches and ensure the integrity of transmitted information. Furthermore, the system's scalable design allows for the integration of new technologies and additional sensors, providing flexibility and future growth potential. This scalability ensures that the IoT infrastructure can adapt to evolving technological advancements and changing agricultural practices. This system’s enhancements not only bolster the efficacy of pear detection and health monitoring but also fortify the infrastructure against common issues in agricultural IoT applications.

Discussion

We have created an object detection dataset for automated pear picking that contains a total of 7366 images, covering various varieties of mature pears captured by cameras in scenarios such as farmlands and orchards during pear picking. Our previous research work proposed HDMNet [22] based on YOLOv8, a high-precision object detection network designed for automated pear picking, built on the YOLOv8 architecture. It enhances detection through the High-level Semantic focused attention mechanism (HSA) which removes irrelevant background information, and a Deformation-aware Feature Pyramid Network (DP-FPN) that improves the detection of distant and small pears. In contrast, this study employs a novel architecture, Vmamba, and builds upon it to propose SRSMamba. Through comparative analysis of multiple experiments, the effectiveness of SRSMamba in automated pear harvesting has been confirmed. It specifically addresses three key issues: difficulty in feature extraction under complex backgrounds, interference from redundant background information, and the prevalence of false negatives and false positives in dense scenes. In the actual process of automated pear harvesting, there are conditions that may cause varying degrees of damage to the pears, subsequently affecting the model's ability to detect and locate them accurately. Currently, our work has not yet considered the detection of damaged pears. This would require collecting a more extensive dataset of damaged pears and designing models based on their specific characteristics. We plan to address this in our future work.

Image or video databases of common items are already widely used. However, there is a gap in the field of agricultural automation, leading us to create our own dataset comprised of automated pear picking photos collected by cameras and annotated using LabelImg. We intend to provide some assistance to other researchers in the field of agricultural automation with this dataset, and share it for the training and testing of the SRSMamba model. Additionally, we introduce, for the first time, the new generation visual architecture Vmamba for use in agricultural production, hoping that this attempt will inspire other agricultural researchers.

Conclusions

In recent years, with the increase in agricultural output and the loss of agricultural working population, there has been a shortage of agricultural labor, and consequently the development of deep learning technology has provided new insights into agricultural automation technology. The self-attention mechanism-driven Transformer, with its superior global modeling capability, has further improved the performance of deep learning, but its efficiency is limited by the quadratic computational complexity. Therefore. we introduce a new generation visual architecture, Vmamba, characterized by linear complexity and a visual scanning mechanism, significantly enhancing performance while reducing complexity in pursuit of efficient and practical agricultural automation. Further, we propose SRSMamba for auxiliary target detection in automated pear picking, aiming for high efficiency and precise localization to minimize the loss of fruits during automated picking and maximize agricultural automation. Firstly, we propose SS3D, which improves upon the original scanning mechanism of Vmamba by extending the scanning range from two dimensions to three, efficiently processing semantic information between channels and enhancing the model's capability in complex orchard environments. Next, to eliminate redundant background information in complex agricultural fields and pear orchards, we propose RPM, which enhances useful information and weakens redundant information, retaining important feature information in the network. Lastly, we introduce SFPN, which enhances the scale information in the feature fusion segment, effectively integrating features of different scales, thereby strengthening the ability to locate dense pear clusters. More importantly, SRSMamba represents the first implementation of the new visual architecture Vmamba in the field of agricultural target detection, opening new avenues for agricultural automation tasks. In our experiments, we divided 7366 self-assembled image data into training, validation, and test sets in a 7:1:2 ratio. SRSMamba achieved an mAP50 of 94.8% and an FPS of 26.9, thus surpassing other SOTA methods and proving the effectiveness of the model we developed.

We have not yet attempted to adapt the model dynamically in changing environments to enhance the accuracy and speed of fruit detection. Moreover, the original processing speed of SRSMamba is slightly inferior compared to YOLOv7s. Additionally, this research has not yet considered the impact of disease or damage in fruits on detection. Therefore, we plan to conduct robotic harvesting experiments in orchards to evaluate the performance of the SRSMamba model in dynamic environments and make corresponding optimizations based on the experimental results, including expanding the dataset to include relevant objects and damage assessments.

To address the issue of processing speed, we aim to implement a dynamic inference strategy that adjusts the model's complexity and resource allocation based on the scene's complexity and the number of targets that need to be detected, thereby increasing processing speed. Additionally, we will experiment with combining data from other sensors to achieve multimodal learning for more precise detection.
