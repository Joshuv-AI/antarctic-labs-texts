The Non-commutative Spaces of Higher-Order Networks of Bach’s Solo Violin Compositions: Dimension, Curvature, and Distance through the Spectral Triplet
Sara Najem, Dima Mrad

Abstract
Our work is concerned with simplicial complexes that describe higher-order interactions in real complex systems. This description allows to go beyond the pairwise node-to-node representation that simple networks provide and to capture a hierarchy of interactions of different orders. The prime contribution of this work is the introduction of geometric measures for these simplicial complexes. We do so by noting the non-commutativity of the algebra associated with their matrix representations and consequently we bring to bear the spectral triplet formalism of Connes on these structures and then notions of associated dimensions, curvature, and distance can be computed to serve as characterizing features in addition to known topological metrics.

3. Geometry

To motivate the relation between geometry and spectra in our context, we go back to Kac’s question on whether we can "hear the shape of the drum". For a few aspects of the geometry the answer is the affirmative. For example, Weyl’s law
[
19
,
12
]
reveals the dimension of the manifold through the dependence of

N(\lambda\leq\lambda_{i})\propto\lambda^{n/2}
, where

3.1. Dimension

In the context of higher-order networks, particularly simplicial complexes, the spectral dimension is not adequately described by a single scalar quantity given the complex geometry of these structures. Instead, for a simplicial complex of dimension

, a vector of spectral dimensions
[
8
]
characterizes the geometry :

d_{s}=[d_{s}^{[0]},d_{s}^{[1]},d_{s}^{[2]},\cdots,d_{s}^{[k-1]}]

where

corresponds to the spectral dimension associated with the Laplacian

of a regular pairwise graph, and every other component is associated with the

governing diffusion processes across

For the graph Laplacian, the first component of the spectral dimension vector, the scaling of the cumulative density of the non zero eigenvalues
[
55
]
is given by :

\rho(\lambda)=C_{[0]}\lambda^{d^{[0]}_{S}/2},\quad\text{for }n=0,\\

where

is a constant and

is the spectral dimension of a graph or the
1-

in the Appendix). Specifically, for

\rho(\lambda)=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{[n-1]}}{N_{[n]}}C_{[n-1]}\lambda^{d^{[n-1]}_{S}/2}+\frac{\mathcal{N}_{[n]}}{N_{[n]}}C_{[n]}\lambda^{d^{[n]}_{S}/2},

where

At the upper boundary, when

3.2.2
Spectral Action: the Heat Kernel and Expansion and Scalar Curvature

Now we turn to the last piece of the puzzle and remind ourselves that for a general integral operator the kernel is given by:

(A_{K}f)(x)=\int K(x,y)\,f(y)\,dy

whose trace is given by:

\operatorname{Tr}(A_{K})=\int K(x,x)\,dx=\sum\sigma_{i},

Chamseddine and Connes’s spectral action principle

\mathrm{Tr}\left(F\left(\frac{D}{\Lambda}\right)\right)=\sum_{i}F\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\Lambda}\right),

where

is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of

In the commutative case over ordinary manifolds this spectral action principle was shown to recover the Einstein–Hilbert action for gravity, Yang–Mills actions for gauge fields, and scalar field terms (like the Higgs field). In certain non-commutative geometries, applying the spectral action reproduces the Standard Model of particle physics coupled to gravity.
Thus a suitable function

Therefore, they choose to solve the heat equation on a Riemannian manifold

\Delta=\nabla^{*}\nabla+\mathcal{R}

In this decomposition,

A combinatorial analog

L_{[k]}=L^{\prime}_{[k]}+F_{[k]}

5. Case Study: Musical Data

As a case study, we demonstrate the applicability of our approach by considering simplicial complexes constructed from musical data, particularly the sonatas and partitas for solo violin, composed by J. S. Bach. We group the movements of the sonatas and partitas into three categories based on their musical genre and character, the groups are slow movements, fugues, and dance movements. We construct the simplicial complexes corresponding to each of these movements. Notes are associated with nodes, or 0-simplices, chords of length two or two simultaneously played notes with a 1-simplex, a chord of length three or equivalently three simultaneously played notes with a triangle or a 2-simplex, all the way up to tetrahedron or a 3-simplex. These building blocks are now connected if they occur consecutively, that is if a

[D,E,F]

with tetrahedra as the highest order simplex. We examine the temporal evolution of each simplicial complex by constructing it cumulatively, where at every musical measure

6. Conclusion

What does this all mean? The recovered dimension, curvature, and distance allowed us to see in the mind’s eye the composition space in which notes are positioned according to harmonic rules, which impose selection constraints on the composer. One can then naturally ask how the choice of successive notes/chords is then made? The answer we provide is an image of a composer initially starting at 0, the position of the dominant note or the tonic, and performing a random walk in a curved space of a given dimension, with scalar curvature favoring a direction over others, and proximity defining the next note/chord in that direction of movement. So, if a composer closes his/her eyes and start composing, they see something like Figure
8
, or so we hope!
We also stress that our proposed framework, though adopted on a music data set, is not restricted to this particular application. It generalizes to higher-order networks, and thus we propose metric computations which are application agnostic and can be applied to any simplicial complex. They bring in geometric identifiers and measures to supplement the topological ones setting the ground for a Gauss-Bonnet-like relations. Our proposed definition of distance provides a geometric embedding, which is a highly active field in network science. It started with embedding large networks in hyperbolic latent spaces offering a description of their observed topological metrics
[
31
]
, particularly their scale-free nature and clustering. This hyperbolic embedding formalism does not apply to our setting as our music networks are higher-order ones and neither are they large, nor do they have scale-free degree distributions. Conversely to our definition, which applies to arbitrary network sizes and topologies, be it simple networks or higher-order. As it stands hyperbolic embedding is now applied in neural networks
[
25
,
36
]
, optimal routing
[
10
,
30
]
, which were we also see our definition of distance adopted since its grounded in non-commutative geometry, the home of matrices and operators. Moreover, notions of curvature have recently been used as geometric measures in applications related to resilience, risk, and robustness, with evidence from the literature on how it can be indicative of transitions near criticality and serve as a predictive metric for collapse and fragility in financial and economic networks, cancer, brain and epidemic and a variety of biological networks
[
23
,
51
,
50
,
5
,
49
,
48
,
27
]
. It was also shown to be relevant to community detection through the Ricci-flow, which is based on the observation that curvature dictates the proximity between nodes; If the edge connecting them is negative, then they belong to different communities as opposed to when the sign of the edge is positive
[
53
,
42
]
.

In this work, we have showcased the use of dimension, curvature, and distance on a sample data set and provided a glimpse into the relevance of the geometrical measures that we proposed to the network science community, which we believe will be of use in a variety of contexts and a wide spectrum of applications.

The three operators
\mathbf{L_{k}^{\text{down}}}

\text{im}(\mathbf{L^{{\text{down}}}_{k}})\subseteq\ker(\mathbf{L^{\text{{up}}}_{k}}),

\ker(\mathbf{L_{k}})=\ker(\mathbf{L^{\text{up}}_{k}})\cap\ker(\mathbf{L^{\text{down}}_{k}}).

The eigenvectors of
\mathbf{L}^{k}

-

An eigenvector is a simultaneous eigenvector of
\mathbf{L}^{\text{down}}_{k}

\mathbf{L}^{\text{up}}_{k}

\lambda^{k}
of
\mathbf{L}^{\text{down}}_{k}

\mathbf{L}^{\text{up}}_{k}

-

An eigenvector is a simultaneous eigenvector of
\mathbf{L}^{\text{down}}_{k}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\text{up}}_{k}

\lambda^{k}
of
