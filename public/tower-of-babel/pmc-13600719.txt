Impact of minimal taper canal preparation on the adaptation of glass fiber posts
Authors: Rosine Hajj Chehadé, Antonio Afram, Pascale Habre, Roula El Hachem, Carla Zogheib, Samar Abou Jaoudé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To evaluate the adaptation of a small diameter glass fiber post in minimally prepared root canals and to compare two taper configurations (4% and 7%) within the framework of minimally invasive endodontics (MIE).Materials and Methods:Twenty standardized resin canal models with identical internal anatomy were assigned to two groups (n = 10): Group 1 (4% taper; R-Motion, FKG Dentaire) and Group 2 (7% taper; WaveOne Gold Primary, Dentsply Sirona). Following a formal power analysis to establish sample size validity, root canals were prepared according to manufacturers’ parameters and obturated using standard warm vertical compaction with an epoxy-resin based sealer (ADSEAL, Meta Biomed). Postspace patterns were fabricated using an autopolymerizing acrylic resin. A prefabricated glass fiber post (0.95 mm; Itena Clinical) served as the reference control. All experimental patterns and the reference post were scanned using a high-precision TRIOS intraoral scanner (3Shape). Digital superimposition was conducted using best-fit alignment algorithms in 3D analysis software, and root mean square (RMS) values were calculated to verify alignment accuracy. Cross-sectional post diameters were measured at five standardized levels (−5 mm–−9 mm from the working length). Data normality was verified via the Shapiro–Wilk test, and differences between groups and the reference post were evaluated using Student’s t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test (α = 0.05).Results:Low mean RMS values confirmed excellent digital alignment accuracy across both groups (7% group: 0.0615 ± 0.029 mm and 4% group: 0.1496 ± 0.088 mm). At all five evaluated cross-sectional levels (−5, −6, −7, −8, and −9 mm), the canal-derived diameters were significantly smaller than the 0.95 mm reference post (P < 0.01). While the 7% taper configuration yielded significantly larger internal dimensions than the 4% configuration at all levels, neither taper configuration allowed passive adaptation of the fiber post. Consequently, none of the specimens could accommodate the reference post without secondary mechanical enlargement.Conclusions:Minimally invasive canal preparations utilizing 4% and 7% tapers effectively preserve radicular dentin structural integrity but do not provide sufficient physical space to accommodate standard small diameter (0.95 mm) glass fiber posts. To successfully bridge this gap without sacrificing the biological benefits of conservative endodontics, a balanced approach combining apical preservation with highly controlled coronal modification is required. Alternatively, the dental industry must develop reduced-taper, smaller-diameter fiber posts that match contemporary minimal-preparation file dimensions.

INTRODUCTION

Minimally invasive dentistry has fundamentally reshaped contemporary endodontic philosophy by emphasizing maximal preservation of sound structural tooth architecture while maintaining effective chemomechanical debridement and three-dimensional sealing of the root canal system.[12] Conservative canal preparation explicitly aims to minimize radicular dentin removal during instrumentation, a strategy that directly correlates with an improvement in the long-term fracture resistance of endodontically treated teeth.[345] Extensive mechanical dentin removal during root shaping has been heavily implicated in increasing structural susceptibility to catastrophic vertical root fractures.[678] Recent biomechanical literature suggests that canal taper and final preparation dimensions are the primary determinants of structural stress distribution profiles within radicular dentin.[910] Consequently, instrumentation systems featuring highly reduced tapers, such as 4% rotary instruments, have gained widespread popularity as core components of minimally invasive endodontic (MIE) strategies.[1112]

Following complete root canal treatment, structural restoration frequently requires the placement of an intraradicular post to properly retain the definitive coronal core and restoration.[1314] Glass fiber posts have become the material of choice because their elastic modulus closely approximates that of natural human dentin, which fosters a homogeneous stress distribution under functional occlusal loading and mitigates the risk of non-reparable root fractures.[1516] However, the physical placement of prefabricated fiber posts typically dictates additional mechanical canal enlargement, an intervention that may directly contradict the central biological principles of radicular dentin preservation.[1718]

Clinicians are thus faced with a definitive “structural-prosthetic paradox:” Balancing the preservation of vital radicular dentin with the clinical requirement of achieving optimal macromolecular post adaptation.[1920] Inadequate anatomical adaptation between the post and the root canal walls inevitably results in an excessively thick layer of resin cement and heightened polymerization shrinkage stresses.[2122] Furthermore, this mismatch promotes void formation, compromises resin-dentin hybridization, and diminishes long-term restorative performance.[23] Conversely, aggressive mechanical postspace preparation via dedicated drill systems risks permanently weakening the root structure and increasing susceptibility to vertical root fracture.[24]

Despite extensive exploration of MIE principles, limited clinical evidence exists regarding whether contemporary minimally tapered canal systems provide sufficient internal corporate dimensions to accommodate even the smallest standard prefabricated glass fiber posts without further structural modification.[25] Therefore, the purpose of this comparative in vitro study was to evaluate the precise spatial adaptation of a standard 0.95 mm glass fiber post within root canals prepared using conservative 4% and 7% taper instrumentation systems. The null hypothesis tested was that there would be no statistically significant difference between the canal-derived postspace diameters and the nominal diameter of the reference glass fiber post.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This in vitro comparative study utilized twenty standardized transparent resin canal blocks featuring identical internal anatomy to entirely eliminate confounding morphological and anatomical variables. To establish the statistical validity and power of the chosen sample size, a formal sample size calculation was executed based on pilot study metrics. The effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.2) was estimated based on preliminary pilot data and interpreted according to established benchmarks for effect size magnitude (Cohen, 1988), indicating a large effect suitable for detecting clinically relevant dimensional differences between groups, a type I error probability (α) of 0.05, and a statistical power (1−β) of 0.80, the power analysis indicated that a minimum sample size of n = 10 specimens per experimental group was required to detect a significant dimensional mismatch. The twenty specimens were randomly assigned into two distinct experimental configurations (n = 10/group): Group 1 (4% taper), prepared using the R-Motion rotary system (FKG Dentaire) ISO 25, 4% taper; and Group 2 (7% taper), prepared using the WaveOne Gold Primary reciprocating system (Dentsply Sirona) ISO 25, variable taper with an approximate 7% taper in the apical portion.

The working length (WL) was rigidly standardized at 16.0 mm for all experimental blocks, verified visually under ×4 surgical magnification. Mechanical instrumentation was executed in strict accordance with the manufacturers’ established parameters. For Group 1, R-Motion files were operated at a speed of 600 rpm and a torque of 1.5 N·cm using a gentle in-and-out motion until reaching the full WL. For Group 2, the WaveOne Gold Primary file was utilized in a dedicated reciprocating motion powered by an endodontic motor with factory-preset parameters, employing a light brushing action in three-amplitude waves until the full WL was achieved. The chemical irrigation protocol was completely standardized across all twenty blocks to ensure procedural consistency. Each canal was irrigated with 5 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution delivered via a 30G side-vented needle placed 2 mm short of the WL, followed by a final flush of 2 mL of distilled water to clear any chemical residues.

All the root canals were thoroughly dried with matched paper points and obturated using the warm vertical compaction technique with gutta-percha cones and an epoxy resin-based sealer (ADSEAL, Meta Biomed, Cheongju, Korea). A downpack was carried out using a heated plugger to within 4 mm of the WL, and the remaining canal space was backfilled using a warm gutta-percha injection system. Following complete obturation, excess gutta-percha and sealer material were carefully removed to the coronal orifice level using a hand instrument heated to high temperature, entirely avoiding secondary mechanical enlargement by using peeso reamers to strictly preserve the instrumented canal taper configuration. The specimens were allowed to set completely at 37°C in 100% relative humidity for 48 h.

To measure the precise internal spatial volume of the prepared postspaces, indirect canal impressions were fabricated. The internal spaces were lightly lubricated with a water-soluble separating agent to prevent material adhesion. An autopolymerizing acrylic pattern resin (Pattern Resin LS, GC America, Alsip, IL, USA) was mixed according to the manufacturer’s ratio, introduced into the post space using a micro-applicator, and reinforced with a micro-pin to prevent deformation during withdrawal. After complete chemical polymerization, the resin post patterns were carefully removed and stored in distilled water at room temperature to eliminate potential dimensional distortion or shrinkage. Ten unique resin postreplicas were generated for each experimental group. A standard prefabricated parallel-tapered glass fiber post with a nominal clinical diameter of 0.95 mm (Exacto Size 0, Itena Clinical, Paris, France) served as the reference control group [Figure 1].

All generated resin patterns and the reference glass fiber post were scanned using a high-resolution TRIOS intraoral scanner (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) to produce high-fidelity digital datasets [Figure 2]. The individual three-dimensional surface datasets were exported as standard tessellation language (STL) files and imported into professional 3D metrology and analysis software (Geomagic Control X, 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA). Digital superimposition between the experimental resin replicas and the reference glass fiber postdataset was accomplished utilizing automated best-fit alignment algorithms. The root mean square (RMS) values were systematically calculated for each registration to objectively verify alignment and registration accuracy.

Following successful 3D alignment, cross-sectional diameter measurements were accurately recorded at five standardized levels: −5 mm, −6 mm, −7 mm, −8 mm, and −9 mm measured from the established apical WL [Figure 3].

(a) Superimposition of a 4% taper post with the glass fiber post. (b) Diameter of the glass fiber post at 0 mm

To ensure high intra-examiner reliability, all linear diameter measurements were performed twice by the same blinded examiner at a 1-week interval. Moreover, the mathematical average was recorded as the final value. Because the measurements were continuous variables, intraclass correlation coefficient was used instead of Cohen’s Kappa, which is appropriate for categorical data.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since the dataset exhibited a normal Gaussian distribution, parametric statistical analysis was completed using the Student’s t-test for independent samples to determine significant differences between Group 1 and Group 2, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was utilized to compare the dimensions against the fixed 0.95 mm reference post. The level of statistical significance was set a priori at α = 0.05.

RESULTS

The automated 3D digital superimposition procedure demonstrated an exceptionally high degree of alignment and registration accuracy across all specimens. This precision was objectively confirmed by consistently low mean RMS values in both experimental categories. Group 2 (7% WaveOne Gold Primary) demonstrated a mean RMS value of 0.0615 ± 0.029 mm, while Group 1 (4% R-Motion) demonstrated a mean RMS value of 0.1496 ± 0.088 mm, indicating that the digital alignment was highly accurate and reliable for linear measurements.

The quantitative cross-sectional analysis revealed a distinct and statistically significant dimensional mismatch between the prepared root canals and the reference post at every single level evaluated. At all five physical levels (−5, −6, −7, −8, and −9 mm from the WL), the mean canal-derived post diameters in both groups were significantly smaller than the 0.95 mm nominal diameter of the prefabricated reference glass fiber post [P < 0.01; Table 1]. Although Group 2 (7% taper) exhibited statistically larger internal diameters than Group 1 (4% taper) across all measured intervals, neither instrumentation configuration achieved dimensional compatibility or physical clearance with the prefabricated post. Consequently, 100% of the experimental specimens completely failed to demonstrate passive adaptation of the 0.95 mm reference post, confirming that placement would be physically impossible without additional mechanical enlargement. Based on these findings, the null hypothesis was rejected.

DISCUSSION

The present in vitro comparative investigation evaluated whether contemporary MIE canal preparations provide an adequate internal spatial envelope to accept standard small-diameter prefabricated glass fiber posts without secondary mechanical intervention.[2526] The quantitative outcomes clearly demonstrate that both 4% and 7% taper preparations generate internal dimensions that are significantly smaller than the standard 0.95 mm glass fiber post at all evaluated levels, leading to the definitive rejection of the null hypothesis [Table 2]. These findings strongly emphasize a critical, clinically relevant “structural–prosthetic paradox” that challenges contemporary restorative workflows.[2527]

On one hand, instrumentation systems with reduced tapers (such as 4% and 7%) are highly favored in modern biological endodontics because they optimize radicular dentin preservation, an outcome directly correlated with a reduction in stress concentrations and an increase in long-term resistance to catastrophic vertical root fractures.[34525] On the other hand, these conservative geometries severely restrict the internal spatial dimensions required for the passive seating of postendodontic restorative components.[252829]

These components were originally designed for traditional, more extensively flared root canal configurations.[122529] The observed dimensional mismatch from −5 mm to −9 mm indicates that, if a clinician attempts to use a standard 0.95 mm glass fiber post following conservative preparation, secondary coronal and middle-third mechanical enlargement becomes unavoidable.[30]

No Peeso reamers or post drills were used in this study. This was an intentional aspect of the experimental design because the objective was to evaluate the canal geometry produced solely by the endodontic instrumentation systems without introducing additional enlargement.

If a clinician strictly avoids this secondary mechanical enlargement to preserve dentin structure, the prefabricated post will fail to reach the necessary clinical depth, compromising retention.[31] Alternatively, the clinician would be forced to select an undersized, nonstandard post, which inevitably leads to an excessively wide “cement gap” or interface.[3132] Literature has repeatedly demonstrated that an elevated resin cement thickness is associated with severe polymerization shrinkage stresses, a high incidence of internal micro-void formation, and a subsequent degradation of the adhesive bond stability over time.[171833] This phenomenon is magnified in the deep apical regions of the postspace, where adequate light activation is restricted and the high cavity configuration factor (C-factor) induces high stress, compromising bond performance.[161934]

The choice of obturation materials further complicates this critical interface. While conservative shaping and modern bioceramic or epoxy sealers optimize the biological seal, they do not alleviate technical difficulties during the subsequent restorative phase.[835] Previous micro-computed tomography studies have shown that reduced tapers can challenge the proper adaptation of single-cone obturation techniques.[101136] The results of the present study extend these concerns directly into the postendodontic phase, demonstrating that the “minimalism” achieved during root canal shaping creates a definitive cascading effect of technical difficulties during core reconstruction.[131437]

Interestingly, although the 7% taper reciprocating group (WaveOne Gold Primary) produced larger cross-sectional diameters than the 4% rotary group (R-Motion), it still consistently failed to allow a passive fit for the reference post. This finding indicates that the current commercial industry standard for a “smal-diameterl” post (0.95 mm) remains completely incompatible with modern conservative preparations. Rather than reverting to aggressive, drill-based postspace steps that destroy radicular dentin and negate the biological advantages of MIE, the dental manufacturing industry must prioritize the commercial development of dedicated matched-taper fiber posts. A biologically and mechanically coherent solution would involve fiber posts specifically manufactured with 4% or 6% tapers and highly reduced apical diameters (e.g., 0.6 mm–0.8 mm).[2137] Such an innovative system would allow for postspace preparation to be completed entirely during initial endodontic instrumentation, eliminating the need for secondary, dentin-sacrificing post drills.

This study utilized standardized transparent resin blocks to ensure a highly controlled comparison of canal tapers without the confounding variables of natural human tooth morphology. However, it must be acknowledged that resin models do not fully replicate the complex micro-tubular structure, bonding characteristics, or anisotropic modulus of elasticity of natural human dentin. Furthermore, this study focused purely on dimensional and spatial adaptation rather than functional stress outcomes, bond strength evaluation, or dynamic fatigue resistance.

Future research should therefore employ extracted human teeth and dynamic cyclic loading protocols to determine whether the increased cement thickness associated with conservative preparations has a clinically significant impact on the long-term clinical survival of the postcore-crown complex.

CONCLUSION

Within the inherent limitations of this in vitro comparative study, the following conclusions can be drawn:

Root canal preparations performed with both 4% rotary and 7% reciprocating instrumentation systems result in internal spatial dimensions that are significantly smaller than a standard 0.95 mm prefabricated glass fiber post.

Neither taper configuration provides sufficient clearance to allow optimal, passive postadaptation without requiring secondary coronal and middle-third mechanical enlargement.

Strict adherence to modern MIE shaping protocols is currently structurally incompatible with available standard fiber post geometries.

A balanced clinical approach combining conservative apical shaping with highly controlled, conservative coronal modification appears necessary to reconcile root structural integrity with adequate restorative adaptation.

There is a critical requirement for the development of new glass fiber post systems featuring reduced tapers and smaller apical diameters designed to match contemporary conservative endodontic file sizes.
