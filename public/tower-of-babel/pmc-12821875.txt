Sleep patterns, genetic factors and the risk of cirrhosis: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Fei Lin, Haoyu Zhang, Hongwei Xu, Luying Cheng, Wen Guo, Mengying Wang, Chengxiao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies have shown that unhealthy sleep behaviors are associated with chronic liver disease. However, the association of sleep patterns and genetic susceptibility with the incidence of cirrhosis remains inadequately elucidated.MethodsThis study included 364,308 participants initially free of liver cirrhosis from the UK Biobank. Sleep patterns were derived based on five self-reported sleep behaviors, including sleep duration, chronotypes, insomnia, snoring, and daytime sleepiness. Additionally, a polygenic risk score for cirrhosis was constructed for each participant. Cox regression models were utilized to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of cirrhosis associated with sleep patterns and polygenic risk score.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 12.6 years, we recorded 1,814 cirrhosis events. Compared with healthy sleep patterns, the HRs (95% CI) for moderate and poor sleep patterns were 1.27 (95% CI: 1.14–1.42) and 1.73 (95% CI: 1.45–2.08), respectively. A joint effect of sleep and genetic factors on cirrhosis risk was observed, with HR reaching 3.50 (95% CI: 2.42–5.06) with poor sleep patterns and high genetic risk compared with those with healthy sleep patterns and low genetic risk. In addition, the high genetic risk for participants, poor sleep patterns of standardized decade liver cirrhosis were 0.49%, as opposed to 0.30% for those with healthy sleep patterns. The same trend was witnessed in individuals at low genetic risk.ConclusionsOur results show that the healthy sleep patterns are associated with a lower risk of incident liver cirrhosis, especially in individuals with high genetic susceptibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00872-0.Keywords: Sleep patterns, Sleep behaviors, Liver cirrhosis, Genetic susceptibility, Cohort

Introduction

Cirrhosis of the liver is widely prevalent and associated with high mortality worldwide [1]. While traditional environmental factors such as excessive alcohol consumption, chronic viral hepatitis, and obesity contribute to the development of cirrhosis, increasing evidence has linked various sleep behaviors with the risk of cirrhosis and severe liver disease [2]. In epidemiological studies, a variety of unhealthy sleep behaviors, such as insomnia, late bedtime, daytime sleepiness, have been associated with higher risk of cirrhosis [2–6]. Most of the previous studies, however, investigated the associations between individual sleep behaviors and cirrhosis risk. Of note, sleep traits are highly correlated, and the change of one behavior may trigger a change of others [7, 8]. A healthy sleep pattern that includes five sleep behaviors: chronotype, sleep duration, insomnia, snoring, and daytime sleepiness was recently developed and validated, and was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney disease [9–11]. In the current study, we prospectively investigated the relationship between the healthy sleep pattern and the risk of cirrhosis.

Accumulating evidence has shown that genetic factors also play an important role in the development of cirrhosis [12]. Although each variant can only account for a small to moderate proportion of the heritable risk of cirrhosis, a polygenic risk score (PRS) has been shown to be effective at measuring the cumulative effect of multiple risk-associated variants. In recent years, several studies have shown that genetic susceptibility might interact with lifestyle factors on severe chronic diseases [11, 13] and genetically predetermined rise on severe chronic diseases risk could be offset at least to some extent by healthy lifestyle. However, whether the healthy sleep pattern, may modify the effect of genetic predisposition on cirrhosis remain largely unknown.

Therefore, based on the UK Biobank study, we prospectively assessed the associations between a combination of major sleep behaviors, characterized by a healthy sleep score, and the risk of cirrhosis. aiming to investigate the hypothesis that adherence to a healthy sleep pattern may offset genetic risk for cirrhosis.

Methods

Study design and population

As a large prospective observational cohort study, the UK Biobank has recruited 0.5 million adults across 22 centers located throughout the UK. The design of the study has been described in detail elsewhere, and the full protocol of the UK Biobank is openly accessible to approved applicants 14. In the current study, we excluded individuals with any single International Classification of Diseases (ICD)−10 code and (ICD)−9 code for liver disease at baseline (n = 1,415) (Table S1). Furthermore, those with missing information on sleep data were excluded (n = 91,453), as were those with missing genetic data, non-British participants, and individuals with a mismatch between their self-reported sex and genetic sex (n = 45,235). After these exclusions, a total of 364,308 participants remained for the main analysis (Figure S1).

Assessment of sleep pattern

Sleep patterns are defined by five sleep behaviors: sleep duration, chronotype preference, insomnia, snoring, and daytime sleepiness. Those information on self-reported sleep behaviors were collected using a touch-screen questionnaire at baseline [11]. Those who responded with “Do not know” or “prefer not to answer” were excluded from the analysis. The low-risk sleep behaviors were delineated as follows: sleeping 7–8 h per day; having an early chronotype (“morning” or “more morning than evening”); reported never or rarely experiencing sleeplessness; not self-reported snoring; and experiencing no excessive daytime sleepiness (“never/rarely” or “sometimes”). Each sleep behavior was assigned a code of 0 or 1 to indicate whether it was high risk or low risk, respectively. The scores for the five behaviors were summed to obtain a healthy sleep score ranging from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating a healthier sleep pattern. The participants were classified into three groups: “healthy sleep pattern” (healthy sleep score 4–5), “intermediate sleep pattern” (healthy sleep score 2–3), and “poor sleep pattern” (healthy sleep score 0–1) [11].

Genetic data and polygenic risk score calculation

The UK Biobank study has previously provided comprehensive details regarding genotyping, imputation, and quality control procedures [14]. We utilized 12 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with liver cirrhosis from a prior genome-wide association study to construct a polygenic risk score (PRS) (Table S2) [12]. These SNPs were identified through a multi-trait genome-wide association meta-analysis that involved 5 discovery studies and 3 verification studies, and used these 12 variants to generate a PRS and tested it. The results of test showed that individuals with high polygenic scores had a significantly increased risk of liver cirrhosis and related comorbidities compared with the low polygenic scores. According to the polygenic risk score, participants were categorized into three risk groups: low (lowest quintile), intermediate (quintiles 2 to 4), and high (highest quintile).

Assessment of outcomes

In the UK Biobank, follow-up data on health-related events and mortality were obtained through electronic connections to in-hospital admissions, the death register, and the cancer register. Participants were followed up from the day they attended the assessment center until the date of diagnosis, date of death, or the last date of follow-up (30th September 2021 for England, 28th February 2018 for Wales, and 31 st July 2021 for Scotland), whichever occurred first. In the current study, we used the ICD-10 and ICD-9 codes to define liver cirrhosis by referring to previous literatures based on the UK Biobank [12] (Table S1).

Ascertainment of covariates

Based on prior literature, we incorporated a variety of covariates into the study, including age (years), sex, education level (college or university degree, or others, categorical), Townsend deprivation index, alcohol status (current, previous, or never, categorical), smoking status (current, previous, or never, categorical). Body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2) was calculated through dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. Physical activity (MET-minutes per week) was computed based on the time spent in walking, moderate, or vigorous physical activity, weighted by the energy expended for these types of activity [12] [15]. Consumption of oily fish and processed meat (never, less than once per week, once per week, 2–4 times per week, 5–6 times per week, once or more daily), and red meat consumption. Besides, the first 10 principal components of ancestry were additionally adjusted when genetic factors were involved.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of participants were presented according to the groups of sleep patterns. Continuous variables were reported as means (SD), while categorical variables were reported as numbers (percentages). For continuous covariates with missing data, mean values were imputed, and for categorical variables, missing data were treated as a distinct category. To compare characteristics between different groups, appropriate statistical tests including χ2 tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed, depending on the nature of the data.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were utilized to estimate associations between genetic factors, sleep patterns, and liver cirrhosis incidence adjusting for covariates. Absolute risk was calculated as the percentage of new liver cirrhosis cases within a specific group, and absolute risk reduction was determined by comparing the incidence of liver cirrhosis between different groups and extrapolating the difference in 10-year event rates. The 95% confidence intervals for absolute risk reduction were obtained through drawing 1,000 bootstrap samples from the estimation dataset [16]. For joint effect analyses, participants were categorized into 9 groups based on their genetic risk and sleep patterns to investigate the association between their combined factors and the risk of liver cirrhosis [17].

The robustness of our findings was assessed through multiple sensitivity analyses: (1) Due to sleep apnea syndrome, sleep medications and depressive mood, sleep may be affected, additionally adjusting for sleep apnea, depressive symptoms, and sleep medications (Table S3) [15, 18, 19]; (2) performing independent analyses based on complete data; (3) excluding incident cases of liver cirrhosis occurring during the first 2 years of follow-up; (4) The association between snoring and cirrhosis may be influenced by other variables, therefore, creating a new sleep score that excluded snoring and combining new sleep patterns to re-assess the association between sleep patterns and cirrhosis [20]. Additionally, we conducted stratified analyses by age, sex, smoking status, alcohol status, BMI, and physical activity level. All P values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.0.

Result

The fundamental characteristics of the 364,308 participants were delineated based on their sleep behaviors and liver cirrhosis status (Table 1 and Table S4). Among the 364,308 participants (age: 56.78.02 years; men: 45.2%), 4.3%, 58.2%, and 37.5% exhibited poor, intermediate, or healthy sleep patterns, respectively. During a median follow-up of 12.6 years (interquartile range: 11.7–13.2), there were 1,814 incidents of liver cirrhosis. Participants with lower sleep scores tended to exhibit an increased susceptibility to liver cirrhosis (P < 0.001), and the mean sleep score was 2.86 in patients with cirrhosis and 3.16 in participants without cirrhosis (Table 1, Figure S2-A). A higher distribution of the value of polygenic risk score was observed in participants with liver cirrhosis compared to those without liver cirrhosis, which can be interpreted as higher polygenic risk score value associated with increased risk for liver cirrhosis (Figure S2-B, Figure S3). Furthermore, the PRS was not found to be correlated with any of the sleep behaviors (Table S5).

As sleep quality declined, the risk of cirrhosis increased significantly. Compared to individuals with healthy sleep patterns, those with intermediate sleep patterns was HR of 1.27 (95% CI: 1.14–1.42), while those with poor sleep patterns was HR of 1.73 (95% CI: 1.45–2.08). Moreover, after adjusting for PRS, poor sleep quality remained significantly associated with an increased risk of cirrhosis, with a HR of 1.72 (95% CI: 1.43–2.06; Table 2; Fig. 1-A). This finding underlines the independent role of sleep quality in the development of cirrhosis. Similar results were observed when sleep scores, instead of sleep patterns, were used to assess the association between sleep quality and cirrhosis risk (Figure S4-A). On the other hand, a higher genetic risk was associated with an elevated risk of cirrhosis. Compared to the low genetic risk group (bottom quintile of PRS), individuals in the moderate (2nd to 4th quintile) and high genetic risk (top quintile) groups had significantly higher risks of developing cirrhosis, with HRs of 1.36 (95% CI: 1.18–1.56) and 2.35 (95% CI: 2.03–2.74), respectively (Table 2). These results remained consistent even after adjusting for sleep factors, indicating that genetic susceptibility to cirrhosis was statistically independent of sleep score (Fig. 1-B). Semblable findings were observed when genetic risk quintiles were used instead of classifications (Figure S4-B).

Among participants with a high genetic risk, the standardized 10-year incident liver cirrhosis rates were 0.49% for those with a poor sleep pattern versus 0.30% for those with a healthy sleep pattern. The same downward trend was observed in participants with lower genetic risk. Additionally, in the population in which only baseline liver disease and with missing information on sleep data were excluded (N = 409,543), the risk of cirrhosis also decreased with enhanced sleep quality. The consistent pattern of associations persisted across a series of sensitivity analyses and subgroup analysis. Additional adjustments for sleep apnea, depressive symptoms, and sleep medications (Table S7), or restricting participants with a follow-up time of >2 years (Table S8). One thing to note, adjustments for sleep-related factors did mitigate the observed risk somewhat but did not substantially alter the associations between sleep patterns and liver cirrhosis. Moreover, the significant associations between sleep patterns and liver cirrhosis remained consistent when analyses were conducted based on complete data (Table S9). Besides, we developed a new healthy sleep score incorporating four sleep factors by excluding snoring from the original score (Table S10) [20], considering that the association between snoring and liver cirrhosis might be influenced by covariates (Table S11). These findings remained consistent throughout the analyses. Finally, in the subgroup analysis, our findings suggest that the association between sleep patterns and cirrhosis risk remained consistent across stratifications (Table S12).

Discussion

The current study, involving prospective analyses of 364,308 participants in UK populations, revealed that adherence to a healthy sleep pattern as indicated by a healthy sleep score encompassing early chronotype, 7–8 h of sleep per day, absence of insomnia and snoring, and no excessive daytime sleepiness was linked to reduced risks of incident liver cirrhosis. Participants with poor sleep patterns had a significantly increased risk of developing cirrhosis compared to those with healthy sleep patterns. Besides, high genetic risk for liver cirrhosis was associated with an increased incidence independent of sleep patterns. Participants with both high genetic risk and poor sleep exhibited the highest incidence rate for liver cirrhosis compared to those with low genetic risk and healthy sleep habits. Across different genetic risk groups, adherence to a healthy sleep pattern consistently demonstrated reduced absolute incident rates for liver cirrhosis.

Previous epidemiological studies have established a robust association between individual sleep behaviors and the susceptibility to chronic liver disease. Abnormal sleep duration has been reported to be associated with an increased risk of liver cancer, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and fibrosis, and late sleep type is associated with an increased risk of fibrosis [21–23]. Daytime sleepiness was distinctly correlated with liver enzymes, insulin resistance and hepatic fibrosis [3]. Moreover, snoring and daytime sleepiness have been linked to an elevated risk of metabolic associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) [24]. These findings were consistent with our results, which demonstrated that infrequent insomnia and appropriate sleep duration were associated with lower fibrosis risk. Furthermore, we identified an association between snoring and cirrhosis events; however, this association lost significance after further adjustment for various confounding factors. This is consistent with previous observational studies [10, 11]. These data, taken together, suggested that snoring might not be an independent risk factor for liver cirrhosis, and the observed association with liver cirrhosis events might be partly due to its correlations with other metabolic risk factors.

It is important to evaluate the combination of these sleep behaviors because these sleep-related factors are often interconnected. In the current study, the definition of healthy sleep score provides a comprehensive measure of sleep factors, instead of considering the individual sleep behaviors in isolation. We found that adherence to a healthy sleep pattern, which combined early chronotype, 7–8 h of sleep per day, absence of insomnia and snoring, and no excessive daytime sleepiness, was associated with a lower risk of liver cirrhosis. Our findings suggest that various sleep behaviors may affect liver cirrhosis in an additive manner. Therefore, to analyze the overall sleep patterns would make it easier to interpret the findings and facilitate the application in clinical and public health practice. In contrast to previous studies [17, 25], our study is not only limited to a characteristic population, we comprehensively evaluated the association between sleep patterns and the risk of liver cirrhosis in the general population. In addition, we specifically considered the genetic risk of cirrhosis, and found that a high genetic risk could be partly offset by a healthy sleep pattern. Therefore, treating sleep disorders may play an important role in the primary prevention of liver cirrhosis across the entire population, regardless of individual genetic risk status.

Excessive or insufficient sleep duration, as well as daytime napping, may be an indicator of circadian disruption. The circadian clock plays an important role in cell apoptosis and the DNA damage response, and has a strong impact on metabolism, particularly hepatic metabolic function [26–28]. The multiple converging pathways of abnormal sleep patterns include altered melatonin metabolism, neuromuscular complications, and abnormal thermoregulation, and disrupted sleep can further exacerbate liver disease [2]. In addition, intermittent hypoxia is the main manifestation of obstructive sleep apnea, causing insulin resistance, dysfunction of key steps of hepatic lipid metabolism, and hepatic steatosis, all of which are associated with the onset and progression of cirrhosis [29]. Finaly, poor quality of sleep stimulates interleukin (IL) − 6, IL − 1 beta and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) - the production of alpha, leading to liver inflammation [30]. These damages and changes in the liver also aggravate liver fibrosis and cirrhosis.

Intriguingly, in our stratified analysis, it was discovered that the negative correlation between sleep patterns and cirrhosis was more conspicuous among participants who were currently consuming alcohol and physically inactive (Table S12). This implies that this group of participants might obtain greater benefits from healthy sleep; that is, healthy sleep patterns can counterbalance the potential adverse impacts of insufficient exercise and alcohol intake, which might be one of the more precise and effective approaches to help reduce the risk of cirrhosis in this population [25]. Besides, when further adjusted some covariates (sleep apnea, sleep-related medications, and depressive mood) and excluding missing variables, the association between sleep patterns and cirrhosis was attenuated and became non-significant in the low genetic risk. However, the protective association remained statistically significant among individuals at high genetic risk, suggesting that the benefits of improved sleep quality on reducing cirrhosis risk are particularly robust in this more vulnerable population [31]. Although not statistically significant, the overall trend remains unchanged, which may be attributed to the relatively small number of liver cirrhosis cases, thereby limiting the statistical power of detecting potential true correlations.

Our study has several key strengths. Firstly, a large sample size of approximately 360,000 provided robust statistical power to explore the link sleep patterns and risk of cirrhosis. Additionally, we meticulously collected detailed information on covariates to minimize confounding effects, conducting sensitivity analyses. Another significant aspect was examining how sleep patterns interact with genetic risk in relation to cirrhosis events; individuals with high genetic risk may benefit from maintaining healthy sleep habits. In summary, our findings highlight the importance of healthy sleep patterns in identifying at-risk groups and promoting effective health management.

Of course, there are potential limitations to our study. Firstly, imprecise measurements or unknown factors may lead to residual confounding. Secondly, our research is observational and lacks independent cohort and genetic data, thus making it impossible to conduct external validation and more causal evaluations using methods such as Mendelian randomization. Thirdly, self-reported sleep data raises concerns about misclassification, which tends to attenuate observed associations towards null values. Additionally, for simplicity, we transformed five sleep factors into binary variables when creating a sleep score, potentially resulting in loss of detailed information and discriminative ability. Finally, our study was based on UK biobanks with predominantly European participants, which may limit generalizability but does not affect internal validity.

In brief, we identified that poor sleep patterns increase the risk of liver cirrhosis and that maintaining healthy sleep is associated with mitigate the negative effects of genetic risk on this condition. These findings have significant public health implications, suggesting that efforts to reduce cirrhosis risk should prioritize improving overall sleep behaviors rather than focusing on individual aspects of sleep.
