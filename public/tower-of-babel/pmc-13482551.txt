Comparative nutritional analysis of newly developed interspecific citrus hybrids: multivariate insights for crop improvement
Authors: Dhrumeshkumar Chavda, Narendra Singh, Nimisha Sharma, Akshay, Radha Mohan Sharma, Anil Kumar Dubey, Hatkari Vittal, Nilesh Joshi, Nirmal Kumar, Raushan Kumar, Damini Singh, Shruti Sethi, M. C. Meena, Gyan P. Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhancing the quality and nutrient composition of citrus fruits is essential for ensuring food and nutritional security. In the present study, twenty-four newly developed interspecific citrus scion hybrids (16 Orangelos and 8 Tangelos) from ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange and ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Dancy’ tangerine crosses, along with their parental genotypes, were evaluated for fruit physico-chemical attributes, bioactive composition, and mineral nutrient profiles. Significant variation was observed due to heterozygosity-driven transgressive segregation in the interspecific hybrid progenies. Fruit physico-chemical parameters ranged as follows: juice recovery (6.20-49.52%), juice pH (3.30–4.73), juice TA (0.65–3.42%), juice TSS (7.90-13.33), ascorbic acid (26.00–52.00 mg 100 ml− 1 juice), total phenols (35.33-199.83 mg GAE 100 ml− 1 juice), total flavonoids (17.67–85.33 mg QE 100 ml− 1 juice), and antioxidant activity (1.21–5.20 µmol TE ml− 1 juice). Juice mineral nutrient levels were in the following descending order: potassium (94.87-1467.13 mg L− 1) > calcium (290.20-561.07 mg L− 1) > phosphorus (43.41-171.29 mg L− 1) > magnesium (29.76–82.02 mg L− 1) > iron (1.33–7.08 mg L− 1) > zinc (1.34–2.32 mg L− 1) > manganese (0.56–1.29 mg L− 1) > copper (0.54–0.86 mg L− 1), suggesting considerable genotypic variation following interspecific hybridization. Higher estimates of genetic variability and heritability for juice mineral nutrients and bioactive compounds indicate strong potential for trait-based selection and efficient genetic improvement of citrus fruits. Multivariate analysis and the MGIDI-based approach identified four interspecific scion hybrids, namely ‘Orangelo 2–3’, ‘Tangelo 8–19’, ‘Orangelo 13 − 6’, and ‘Orangelo 7 − 4’, as the most promising.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54939-6.

Introduction

Fruits are among the most important constituents of a healthy human diet and are commonly referred as ‘protective foods’ owing to their nutraceutical richness and health-promoting properties1,2. Among the different fruit crops grown worldwide, citrus ranks third in terms of global production and trade, after mangoes and bananas. Comprehensively, citrus fruits represent a range of cultivars and/or varieties, species, variants, intraspecific and interspecific hybrids resulting from conventional or natural hybridisation3–6. The fruits are traditionally valued for their distinctive flavour, aroma, and refreshing taste, and are an important dietary source of vitamin C, phenols, and flavonoids. In recent years, research attention has expanded over a range of bioactive compounds, which collectively contribute to antioxidant properties and multiple health benefits7. Furthermore, citrus fruits serve as a valuable source of macro-and micro-mineral nutrients such as potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), phosphorus (P), iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), and copper (Cu), which are important for essential functions in the human body but largely remain unnoticed8,9. Malnutrition poses a significant challenge to humankind, and deficiencies in trace mineral nutrients (particularly Zn and Fe) remain prevalent in major parts of the globe10. In recent decades, the genetic biofortification of food crops is considered one of the most effective approaches to combat ‘hidden hunger’, in addition to other strategies, such as diversification of the daily diet and food supplementation11. While most of the food biofortification efforts have primarily focused on staple cereals and vegetable crops12–14, perennial fruit trees have received relatively limited attention, despite their regular consumption, phytonutrient richness, and higher bioavailability. Recent achievements in fruit crops, including bananas15 and pomegranates16, indicates the feasibility of enhancing the mineral nutrients in edible tissues of perennial fruit crops through genetic improvement, highlighting the need to expand similar strategic efforts to citrus fruits.

In various citrus groups, interspecific hybridization has proven to be one of the most effective breeding approaches, resulting in cultivars and/or varieties with improved fruit quality attributes such as juice content, flavour, and aroma4. However, the Indian ‘citriculture’ industry continues to rely majorly on traditional cultivars, including ‘Mosambi’, ‘Valencia’, ‘Sathgudi’, ‘Nagpur’ Santra, ‘Khasi’ and ‘Kinnow’ mandarins. Furthermore, citrus production in the country is predominantly oriented toward fresh fruit consumption (over 80%) rather than juice processing at the global level, largely due to the limited availability of varieties specifically suited for juice processing. Despite global advances in citrus breeding, no interspecific citrus scion hybrids have yet been commercially released in India.

Keeping in view the above-mentioned facts, a citrus interspecific hybridisation programme was initiated at ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute, New Delhi (India). The parental combinations, ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange and ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Dancy’ tangerine, were selected to develop new interspecific scion hybrids with enhanced health-promoting properties, in addition to the traditional desirable traits viz., thin peel, high juice content, seedlessness, and better aroma and flavour. The maternal parent, Pummelo (Citrus maxima Merr.), one of the ancestral species in the genus ‘Citrus’, is increasingly recognised for its rich and diverse phytochemical profiles and health-promoting properties17. Being a typical monoembryonic species, ‘Pummelo’ was utilised as the maternal parent to enhance breeding efficiency and avoid complications arising due to apomixis and/or nucellar embryony. Sweet orange (Citrus sinensis (L.) Osbeck.), are globally dominant citrus species widely appreciated for its higher juice content, low seed content, thin peel, sweetness, and pleasant flavour, and is mainly cultivated for orange juice processing18. In contrast, tangerines (Citrus reticulata Blanco) are primarily grown for fresh consumption, and their health-promoting properties have been well-documented19. Strategic hybridisation between these three genetically distinct citrus species offers potential to combine desirable horticultural traits and improve the nutritional quality of citrus fruits. The newly developed interspecific hybrids and their unique characteristics require an in-depth study of their phytochemicals and minerals to achieve the desired breeding goals. Furthermore, the assessment of the fruit quality composition of developed interspecific hybrid genotypes is also a prerequisite for targeted crossing between hybrids or backcrossing with parental genotypes based on complementary traits.

Despite the recognised nutritional importance of citrus fruits, systematic information on the phytochemical and mineral nutrient composition of these newly developed interspecific citrus hybrids, particularly in comparison with their parental genotypes, remains limited. Moreover, the extent to which interspecific hybridisation can enhance nutritional value and antioxidant potential in citrus fruits has not been adequately quantified through multivariate approaches. The purpose of this study is to comparatively evaluate the physico-chemical attributes, bioactive composition, and juice mineral nutrient profiles of the newly developed interspecific citrus scion hybrids and their parental genotypes, and to identify nutritionally superior hybrids with potential for trait-specific breeding. We hypothesized that interspecific hybridization involving the underutilized citrus species, Citrus maxima Merr., would result in significant variation and the potential enhancement of health-promoting phytochemicals and minerals in the interspecific hybrid progenies, and that multivariate analysis would effectively discriminate hybrids based on their nutritional profiles. The findings of this study provide novel insights into the inheritance and expression of nutritional traits in citrus fruits, supporting the development of nutrient-rich, citrus genotypes and assisting trait-specific breeding strategies for future citrus improvement programmes.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The interspecific hybridization between the ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange and ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Dancy’ tangerine was carried out using the conventional method of emasculation, followed by manual crossing (pollination) and bagging during the year 2012-13. The mature fruits from tagged maternal parent ‘Pummelo’ (♀) flowers/branches were collected to extract seeds and raise the hybrid progeny. Since the ‘Pummelo’ (♀) is a typical monoembryonic species6, all the seedling progeny raised were interspecific hybrids. These hybrids were established at 5 m × 5 m spacing at the citrus hybrid block, experimental orchard of the Division of Fruits and Horticultural Technology, ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI), New Delhi-110012, India (28º64’ N and 77º15’ E; 228 m elevation above the msl). The experimental site is characterised by a typical sub-tropical climate, with a dry, hot summer (May-June) followed by the cold winter (December-January), and rainfall mainly concentrated in the months of July and August. The soil pH at the experimental site was 7.22 (1:2 soil to water ratio), with a sandy loam texture and medium soil organic matter content. Soil nutrient status of the experimental orchard was estimated at three different depths (0–15 cm, 15–30 cm, and 30–45 cm) and are in the following range: pH (7.10–7.38), EC (0.13–0.20 dS/m), organic carbon (0.27–0.85%), N (166–234 kg ha− 1), P (80.6–112 kg ha− 1) and K (213–356 kg ha− 1). Irrigation water characteristics were as follows: pH (7.6), EC (1.6 dS/m), SAR (2.9), CO3− + HCO3− (6.8 meq. L− 1) and regarded as normal. The interspecific citrus scion hybrid progenies and their parental genotypes were subjected to uniform management practices, as per recommended guidelines for citrus in the region.

In the present investigation, sixteen newly developed Orangelo hybrids (Citrus maxima Merr. × Citrus sinensis (L.) Osbeck); eight Tangelo hybrids (Citrus maxima Merr. × Citrus reticulata Blanco) and their parental genotypes ‘Pummelo’ (♀), ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂), and ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) were evaluated during the harvesting seasons, i.e., 2023–2024 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Due to the perennial growth habit and high heterozygosity of citrus and their unique genetic constitution arising from recombination events following interspecific hybridization, each hybrid tree was considered an individual experimental unit. For fruit physico-chemical and mineral nutrient analyses, three biological replications were conducted per genotype. Each replication consisted of an independent composite sample prepared from 15 fruits randomly harvested at optimum maturity from all directions of the canopy to minimize within-tree variability and ensure a representative sample. The harvested fruits were thoroughly washed with clean water to remove surface debris, blotted dry with tissue paper, and subsequently analysed for various traits in the division laboratory. The mean values of the three biological replications were used for all subsequent statistical analyses.

Reagents and chemicals

All chemicals and reagents used in the present study were of analytical reagent (AR) grade. Nitric acid (HNO₃), hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), metaphosphoric acid (HPO3), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), phenolphthalein indicator, methanol (CH3OH), aluminium chloride (AlCl₃), sodium nitrite (NaNO₂), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), ammonium molybdate, and ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3) were procured from Central Drug House (P) Ltd., New Delhi, India. 2,6-Dichlorophenol-indophenol dye solution (DCPIP), Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (FCR), 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), gallic acid (GA), quercetin (QE), Fehling’s reagent, di-sodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and standard solutions for micro-mineral nutrients, namely iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), and copper (Cu), were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (Waltham, MA, USA). Double-distilled water used for all biochemical and mineral nutrient analyses was obtained through a Borosil Quartz Double Distillation water purification system.

Juice physico-chemical parameters

After harvesting at optimum maturity, the collected fruits were cleaned with distilled water and wiped with tissue paper. Individual fruit weight was measured using a digital weighing balance (CTG 3101 Precision Balance, Citizen Scale Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India). The fruits were cut into two symmetrical halves by slicing horizontally through the equatorial plane. A hand juicer/extractor was utilized to press the fruit halves for juice extraction. The purified juice volume (mL) was measured with a measuring cylinder (without seeds) and simultaneously weighed to calculate the juice percentage (% w/w). The fruit juice pH was recorded using a digital pH meter (pH-meter 700; Eutech Instruments, Vernon Hills, NY, USA). Prior to juice pH measurement, the pH meter was calibrated using standard buffer solutions of pH 4.0 and 7.0 at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). Freshly extracted fruit juice was homogenized and filtered through a double layer of muslin cloth to remove all suspended solids. Approximately 20 mL of the clarified fruit juice was transferred into a clean beaker, and the pH electrode was immersed directly in the juice sample. The readings were recorded once the values were stabilized. The juice total soluble solids (TSS; degree Brix) were determined with a digital refractometer (MA871; Milwaukee Instruments, Inc., Rocky Mount, NC, USA) at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). The digital refractometer was standardized with distilled water and a known concentration of sugar solution before taking observations. Juice titratable acidity (TA; percent), expressed as citric acid equivalents, was determined by acid-base titration of 5 mL citrus juice using 0.1 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution with phenolphthalein as an indicator, as per AOAC method number 947.0520. The TSS/TA, also referred to as the maturity index (MI), was calculated by dividing the TSS value by the corresponding TA value for each independent sample.

Ascorbic acid, total phenols, total flavonoids, and DPPH assay

Ascorbic acid (AA) content in citrus juice samples was estimated following the dye titration method as described in the AOAC method number 967.2121, with slight modifications. A 10 mL sample of freshly extracted juice was mixed with 10 mL of 3% (w/v) metaphosphoric acid to prevent oxidation of ascorbic acid. The solution was filtered, and a 10 mL aliquot of the clear extract was titrated against a standard DCPIP dye solution. Titration was continued until a light pink color endpoint was observed, which remained stable for at least 10–15 s. Sample extracts for the estimation of total phenols, total flavonoids, and total antioxidant activity (TAA) were prepared using 10 mL of 80% (v/v) methanol22. The mixtures were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 ◦C (Hermle Z36HK, USA), after which the supernatant was collected. The methanolic extract was transferred to a clean tube, the final volume was adjusted to 10 mL by adding 80% methanol (v/v), and the mixture was stored at − 20 ◦C until further analysis.

Total phenolics content (TPC) of each citrus juice extract was determined using the FCR colorimetric assay23. To 100 µL of supernatant, 2.9 mL double-distilled water was added, along with 0.5 mL FCR (0.2 N). After 10–15 min, 2 mL of NaOH solution (20% w/v) was added. The solutions were mixed and incubated at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 30 min. The absorbance was measured with a spectrophotometer (GENESYS UV-Visible Thermo Scientific, Japan) at 760 nm against a reagent blank. TPC was calculated from the linear calibration curve of gallic acid and expressed as mg GAE 100 mL− 1 juice. The gallic acid standard curve was constructed using concentrations ranging from 0 to 200 mg/L, showing a strong linear relationship (R² = 0.994), with absorbance increasing proportionally with concentration.

Total flavonoid content (TFC) in citrus juice was determined using the aluminium chloride (AlCl₃) colorimetric method24. In brief, 1000 µL of supernatant was mixed with 1.4 mL of distilled water, followed by the addition of 0.3 mL of NaNO₂ solution (5% w/v). After five minutes of incubation at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), 0.3 mL AlCl₃ solution (10% w/v) was added. The solution was allowed to stand for an additional five minutes, after which 2 mL of NaOH solution (1 M) was added. The final volume was adjusted to 5 mL with distilled water, and the mixture was mixed thoroughly. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a spectrophotometer (GENESYS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Japan) against a reagent blank. TFC was calculated from the linear calibration curve of quercetin and expressed as mg QE 100 mL− 1 juice. The quercetin calibration curve was prepared over a concentration range of 0 to 100 mg L⁻¹ and exhibited excellent linearity (R² = 0.987) with a positive correlation between absorbance and flavonoid concentration.

The total antioxidant activity (TAA) of citrus juice extracts was determined in terms of free radical scavenging, using the DPPH assay25. A 2.9 mL aliquot of freshly prepared DPPH solution (0.06 mM in 70% methanol) was added to 0.1 mL of supernatant, and the mixture was kept for 30 min under dark conditions at room temperature. Absorbance of the sample extracts was measured at 517 nm against a methanol (70%) blank, using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (GENESYS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mineral nutrients

The extracted fruit juice obtained from the mature fruits of the studied interspecific citrus hybrids and parental genotypes was subjected to wet-acid digestion26,27, with slight modifications. In brief, 25 mL of juice sample (for each biological replicate) was transferred to a 250 mL Pyrex beaker and mixed with 25 mL concentrated HNO3 (65%). The mixture was heated on a hot plate at 85–100 degrees Celsius for 2–3 h. The heating was continued until the solution became transparent, maintaining a volume of 15–20 mL. The solution was cooled, then 10 mL of 30% H₂O₂ (v/v) was added, and heating was continued until a clear solution was obtained and the volume was reduced to about 5–10 mL. The mixture was filtered through Whatman No. 42 filter paper and washed with double-distilled water. Finally, the filtrate was made up to 50 mL and utilized for mineral nutrient analysis.

For leaf mineral nutrient analysis, 3–4-month-old healthy leaves at the 4–6th position from the growing tip of non-fruiting shoots were randomly plucked from all directions8. Samples were prepared in triplicate with each biological replication consisting of 30 leaves, as recommended for citrus. Collected leaf samples were thoroughly washed with double-distilled water and dried in shade for 3–4 days, followed by oven drying at 65–70 ◦C till constant weight was achieved. The grounded leaf tissues were further subjected to wet-acid digestion with concentrated HNO3 (65%) and filtered through Whatman No. 42 filter paper. The final volume was made up to 100 mL with double-distilled water, and the solutions were stored in clean polypropylene bottles at room temperature until further analysis.

Diluted extracts of leaf and juice samples were used for mineral nutrient analysis following standard established procedures28. Phosphorus (P; %) content was determined using the Vanadomolybdophosphoric acid colorimetric method. Potassium (K) and sodium (Na) were quantified using a flame photometer (Systronics India Ltd., Ahmedabad, India). Calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg) levels were estimated by complexometric titration using disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as a chelating agent, following the protocol described by Singh et al.28. The micro-mineral nutrients, including iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), and copper (Cu), were determined using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Instrument accuracy and analytical precision were ensured through routine calibration with multi-element standard solutions, reagent blanks, and replicated sample analysis. Calibration curves for each element showed high linearity (R² > 0.995). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and necessary precautions were taken to avoid contamination during wet-acid digestion and analytic procedures. Calibration curves were prepared using standard solutions obtained by appropriate dilution of stock solutions in 5% (v/v) hydrochloric acid. Subsequently, the digested and filtered extracts of juice and leaf samples were aspirated into the atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS) to determine micro-mineral nutrient concentrations.

Statistical analysis

The mean data derived from various fruit physico-chemical traits and leaf/juice mineral nutrients from the biological replicates in triplicate were utilized for one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), considering genotype as a fixed effect using SAS software (Version 9.3; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Prior to ANOVA, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. Differences among the genotypic means were assessed using Fisher’s protected least significant difference (LSD) test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were determined to examine relationships among the studied parameters. To explore patterns of variation and genotypic relationships, principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) were performed based on the mean values of all studied variables, using RStudio (version 2022.07.1–554, RStudio PBC). Prior to multivariate analysis, all variables were standardized using z-score transformation to eliminate the influence of differing measurement units and to ensure equal contribution of each variable to the multivariate analyses. For HCA, Ward.D2 method was applied using squared Euclidean distance as the dissimilarity metric, and a dendrogram was constructed to visualise the clustering pattern. The multi-trait genotype–ideotype distance index (MGIDI) was computed in RStudio (‘metan’ package, version 2022.07.1–554, RStudio PBC) to identify superior citrus hybrids based on multiple biochemical and mineral nutrient traits. For MGIDI analysis, all selected variables were standardised by rescaling to a common 0–100 scale to remove differences in measurement units. The key traits were included for MGIDI analysis, i.e., juice content, TSS, ascorbic acid, total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, antioxidant activity, and fruit juice macro-and micro-mineral nutrients, and were therefore coded under a “higher-is-better” desirability criterion. The ideotype was defined as a hypothetical genotype exhibiting maximum rescaled values for the selected traits. MGIDI values were calculated as the Euclidean distance between each genotype and the ideotype in factor space, with lower values indicating greater similarity to the ideotype. The relative contribution of each factor to the MGIDI score was used for strengths and weaknesses analysis, facilitating genotype characterisation and selection based on desirable trait combinations.

Results and discussion

Juice physico-chemical properties

The evaluation of the citrus juice physico-chemical properties, including juice recovery, juice TSS, juice TA, juice pH, and TSS/TA, is important to determine the suitability for fresh consumption as well as juice processing potential. Considerable variation was observed among the studied citrus genotypes, reflecting the underlying genetic diversity and its influence on fruit physico-chemical quality traits (Table 1). These variations provide valuable insights for identifying superior hybrids with desirable juice properties. Juice yield is one of the most important fruit quality criteria for identifying elite citrus interspecific scion hybrids suitable for fresh consumption and juice processing. Significant variations (p ≤ 0.05) were observed for both juice recovery (% w/w) and juice volume (mL) in the present study, reflecting the influence of genetic background and parental combinations (Table 1).

The juice content (mL) in the ‘Orangelo’ hybrids varied from 32.50 to 210.00 mL fruit− 1, while 100–250.00 mL fruit− 1 in ‘Tangelo’ hybrids, compared to the 148.33 mL fruit− 1 in ‘Pummelo’ (♀), 60.00 mL fruit− 1 in ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) and 35.00 mL fruit− 1 in ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂). Juice recovery ranged between 6.20 and 49.52% in ‘Orangelo’ hybrids and 25.12–48.90% in ‘Tangelo’ hybrids. Juice recovery (%) in the parental genotypes ‘Pummelo’ (♀), ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂), and ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) was 24.43%, 42.28%, and 31.32%, respectively. The maternal parent ‘Pummelo’ exhibited the highest juice volume fruit− 1; however, it had the lowest juice recovery, indicating negative traits that need improvement through a systematic breeding programme. Previous studies also reported a similar range of juice recovery in ‘Pummelo’ fruits. This may be attributed to the thick peel, higher seed content, and the presence of a large hollow core in pummelo fruits, all of which collectively contribute to reduced juice recovery29. In contrast, ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) exhibited higher juice percentage but lower juice volume, while ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) showed moderate juice recovery with the lowest juice content. The results confirm the suitability of ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange for juice processing and ‘Tangerines’ for table purposes. Among the Orangelo hybrids evaluated, eleven hybrids (68.75%) showed improvement over the ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂), and six hybrids (37.50%) surpassed the better parent ‘Pummelo’ (♀) for juice content. Among the Orangelos, hybrids ‘2–6’, ‘7 − 3’, ‘11–16’, and ‘13 − 4’ had the juice content ≥ 175 mL fruit− 1 (Table 1). However, the highest juice recovery was recorded in ‘Orangelo 13 − 4’, followed by ‘Orangelo 11–16’, demonstrating superiority over the better parent, ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) for juice recovery. In the Tangelo group, hybrid ‘6–6’ had the highest juice recovery as compared with the better parent ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂). The results indicated that, for improving juice content and/or recovery, ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange emerged as the superior cross combination, with multiple genotypes demonstrating transgressive superiority for both traits. In contrast, ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Dancy’ tangerine crosses, although valuable for their high juice content, but lags in juice recovery. Furthermore, four Orangelos (‘7 − 3’, ‘9–14’, ’11–16’, and ‘13 − 4’) and four Tangelos (‘6–6’, ‘8 − 4’, ‘8–11’, and ‘8–18’) had juice content ≥ 33%, the prescribed minimum international standard for the grapefruit (a natural hybrid between sweet oranges and pummelos) and sweet orange cultivars6,30.

The analysis of juice biochemical attributes among the newly developed interspecific citrus scion hybrids revealed significant genetic variability for juice pH, juice TSS, juice TA, and TSS/TA ratio (Table 1), all of which are critical parameters governing fruit flavour, consumer acceptance, and processing quality. The juice pH values of studied interspecific scion hybrids and their parental genotypes varied from 3.30 in ‘Orangelo 7–8’ to 4.73 in ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange, while juice TA ranged from 0.65% (‘Mosambi’ sweet orange) to 3.42% (‘Orangelo 2–3’), indicating considerable diversity for the estimated biochemical properties. Among the Orangelos, the highest juice pH was observed in hybrids ‘7 − 4’, ‘7–7’ and ‘3–9’, approaching the levels of the parental genotypes, ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) and ‘Pummelo’ (♀). In the Tangelos group, all the evaluated hybrids were statistically at par for juice pH with the pollen parent, ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂). The fruit juice TA in the parental genotypes was found in decreasing order: ‘Pummelo’ (1.20%) > ‘Dancy’ tangerine (0.89%) > ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (0.65%) (Table 1). Within the Orangelos, it was observed that all hybrids (100%) had higher acidity than the pollen parent ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) and thirteen Orangelo genotypes (81.25%) exhibited acidity higher than that of the maternal parent ‘Pummelo’ (♀). Similarly, the juice TA of the Tangelo hybrids was higher compared to ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂), and seven out of eight Tangelo genotypes (87.5%) exceeded Pummelo’s (♀) juice TA levels. Highest juice TA was observed in the evaluated interspecific scion hybrids following descending order: ‘Orangelo 2–3’ > ‘Tangelo 8–19’ > ‘Tangelo 8–11’ > ‘Orangelo 11–16’ > ‘Orangelo 13 − 6’. The range of acidity levels in parental genotypes was consistent with previous findings for different citrus fruits31,32; however, the interspecific scion hybrids were new, and their juice attributes were studied for the first time. The results on juice TA levels indicated that, despite the low-acid nature of the parental genotypes, interspecific hybridization between ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange and ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Dancy’ tangerine resulted in increased TA levels in the interspecific hybrid progenies. This suggests that recombination events between complementary genomic regions have generated novel allelic interactions influencing juice TA levels (i.e., transgressive segregation). The maternal parent ‘Pummelo’ (♀) served as a potential donor for increasing juice TA in the hybrid progenies.

Among the studied interspecific scion hybrids and the parental genotypes, the fruit juice TSS (degree Brix) levels in the Orangelos ranged between 9.23 oB in hybrid ‘3–10’ to 13.33 oB in hybrid ‘2–3’, whereas among the tangelos, it varied from 7.90 oB in hybrid ‘6–6’ to 11.10 oB in hybrid ‘2–2’. In comparison, parental genotypes exhibited juice TSS levels in the following order: ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) > ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) > ‘Pummelo’ (♀) (Table 1). Most of the Orangelo hybrids displayed lower juice TSS values than both parents, except for hybrids ‘2–3’, ‘7 − 4’, and ‘7–8’, which exceeded the pollen parent ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange. All Tangelo hybrids, on the other hand, were found to have juice TSS levels intermediate between those of parental genotypes ‘Pummelo’ and ‘Dancy’ tangerine, except for hybrids ‘6–6’ and ‘8–11’. These results suggested the potential of evaluated interspecific citrus scion hybrids for wide segregation and selection of genotypes for higher juice TSS levels. The TSS/TA ratio, a key quality index for citrus fruits and commonly referred to assess the optimum maturity of fruits, varied widely among the studied interspecific citrus scion hybrids. Among the Orangelo hybrids, the TSS/TA ratio ranged between 3.70 in hybrid ‘11–16’ and 11.23 in hybrid ‘7 − 2’, while in ‘Tangelos’, it varied from 2.90 in hybrid ‘8–11’ to 8.94 in hybrid ‘2–2’. In contrast, the parental genotypes had significantly higher juice TSS/TA ratio values in the following order: ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) > ‘Pummelo’ (♀) > ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂). The range of fruit juice TSS and TSS/TA ratio observed in the present study is consistent with previous findings in various citrus species33–36. A notable observation was that most interspecific scion hybrids had juice TSS/TA ratios intermediate to or lower than the parental means, owing to the higher juice TA levels inherited from the maternal parent ‘Pummelo’ (♀). Collectively, these findings suggest that the Orangelo hybrids, derived from ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) and ‘Pummelo’ (♀), consistently showed greater transgressive segregants, superior juice TSS values, and more favourable juice TSS/TA ratios, making this cross highly desirable for breeding fresh fruit citrus cultivars with improved taste. In contrast, Tangelo hybrids help develop hybrids with elevated juice TA levels. Furthermore, the inclusion of three genetically distinct parental genotypes facilitated a wide segregration for the studied fruit physico-chemical traits in interspecific hybrid progenies, providing valuable opportunities for the development of scion cultivars with enhanced flavour attributes.

Juice bioactive composition

The assessment of the bioactive composition of citrus fruits is crucial for understanding the nutritional and functional value of newly developed interspecific citrus scion hybrids, as these organic compounds are key determinants in promoting human health and serve as essential selection criteria in citrus improvement programmes globally37–39. The results revealed significant variation for fruit juice total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), total antioxidant activity (TAA), and ascorbic acid (AA) levels among the newly developed interspecific citrus scion hybrids compared with their parental genotypes (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the substantial diversity in bioactive profiles underscores the value of these hybrids as promising genetic resources in future citrus improvement programmes for developing nutrient-rich citrus cultivars.

Ascorbic acid (AA; vitamin C) is a well-recognised vital organic compound in citrus juice, playing a pivotal role in determining its nutritional quality and antioxidant potential40. The ascorbic acid content in the studied citrus genotypes ranged from 26.00 to 52.00 mg 100 mL− 1 juice (Fig. 2a). The range of ascorbic acid levels reported in our study is comparable to that reported in previous studies on different citrus species41,42. The parental genotype ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) had the highest juice ascorbic acid levels, followed by ‘Pummelo’ (♀) and ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂). Among the Orangelos, hybrid ‘3–9’ had the highest juice AA levels, and the least was associated with hybrid ‘7–7’, showing two-fold variations. Although none of the hybrids has surpassed the pollen parent ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange, four Orangelo hybrids (‘3–5’, ‘3–9’, ‘3–10’ and ‘9–14’) have surpassed the maternal parent ‘Pummelo’. Within the Tangelo group, all studied hybrids had ascorbic acid content lower than that of both parental genotypes, except for hybrid ‘6–6’ that surpassed the pollen parent ‘Dancy’ tangerine. Secondly, phenolic compounds in citrus fruit juice significantly contribute to the antioxidative properties, enhance nutritional value, and play a key role in promoting human health by reducing the risk of chronic diseases43. Total phenolic content (TPC) of the studied interspecific citrus scion hybrids varied from 35.33 mg GAE 100 ml− 1 juice in ‘Orangelo 4–13’ to 199.83 mg GAE 100 ml− 1 juice in ‘Orangelo 2–3’ (Fig. 2b). In the parental genotypes, TPC was determined in the following order: ‘Pummelo’ (♀) > ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) > ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂). Interestingly, most of the studied interspecific scion hybrids had lower juice TPC values than the parental genotypes. Among the Orangelos, only hybrid ‘2–3’ was superior over both the parents for juice TPC, whereas in the Tangelos, hybrids ‘2–2’ and ‘8–11’ excelled over the parental genotypes for juice TPC.

Flavonoids are the predominant class of polyphenols in citrus fruits, contributing to their antioxidant values and flavour. Genetic variation in flavonoid composition among genotypes offers opportunities for breeding nutrient-rich citrus cultivars44. In our study, the highest juice TFC values were corresponded with the maternal parent ‘Pummelo’ (♀), followed by ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) and ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂). Overall, eight Orangelo hybrids (50%) and six Tangelo hybrids (75%) exhibited superiority for juice TFC levels as compared to the pollen parents ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange and ‘Dancy’ tangerine, respectively (Fig. 2c). The hybrids, ‘Orangelo 2–3’, ‘Orangelo 3–5’, ‘Orangelo 3–6’, ‘Tangelo 2–2’, and ‘Tangelo 8–11’ exhibited higher TFC levels than the maternal parent ‘Pummelo’. The present findings demonstrate the effectiveness of conventional interspecific hybridization in developing citrus genotypes with higher juice flavonoid profiles.

The antioxidant (AOX) activity of juice from the studied citrus genotypes was assessed using the DPPH radical scavenging assay. The AOX activity of the fruit juice for the parental genotypes was in the following order: ‘Pummelo’ (♀) > ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) > ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂). However, in the studied interspecific scion hybrids, the fruit juice AOX activity of Orangelo hybrids ranged from 1.33 to 4.68 µmol Trolox mL− 1 juice and for Tangelos hybrids, it varied between 1.48 and 5.20 µmol Trolox mL− 1 juice (Fig. 2d). These findings indicated that the evaluated interspecific citrus scion hybrids exhibited both higher and lower fruit juice AOX properties than their respective parental genotypes. Furthermore, a total of sixteen interspecific hybrids (66%) excelled over the maternal parent ‘Pummelo’ in terms of juice AOX properties.

The observed variability in juice bioactive composition among the studied citrus genotypes highlights distinct antioxidant profiles in the interspecific scion hybrid progenies and the potential for selecting superior hybrid genotypes. These differences in antioxidant activities are likely influenced by the unique genetic backgrounds of the hybrids and the synergistic interactions among various phenolic compounds45,46. Phytochemical characterisation is also helpful for identifying varietal or botanical origins using chemical markers47. The results further suggested that hybridization represents an effective breeding strategy to enhance the accumulation of phenols, flavonoids, and ascorbic acid, often surpassing the composition of parental genotypes, thereby improving antioxidant properties and associated health benefits. The incorporation of underutilized ‘Pummelo’ as a maternal parent is particularly promising for broadening the genetic base and improving nutritional attributes in citrus breeding programmes.

Juice mineral nutrients

In the present study, mineral nutrient concentrations were determined in the fresh juice of the twenty-four interspecific scion hybrids and their parental genotypes. The results showed significant variation in the estimated macro- and micro-mineral nutrients of the fruit juice, and were in the following descending order in terms of relative contents: potassium (K) > calcium (Ca) > phosphorus (P) > magnesium (Mg) > iron (Fe) > zinc (Zn) > manganese (Mn) > copper (Cu) (Figs. 3 and 4). Previous studies on different citrus species have also reported significant variations in mineral nutrients of citrus fruits8,9,48–50.

Among the macro-mineral nutrients, juice P content of interspecific citrus scion hybrids ranged from 43.41 to 171.28 mg L⁻¹, compared with 137.59 mg L⁻¹ in ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂), 112.49 mg L⁻¹ in ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂), and 78.79 mg L⁻¹ in ‘Pummelo’ (♀), parental genotypes (Fig. 3a). Phosphorus is an essential mineral for the human body, playing key roles in cellular signalling, mineral metabolism, and energy transfer51. Earlier, Czech et al.48 also reported that citrus fruits, particularly sweet oranges, are a good source of phosphorus. Overall, six Orangelos and all Tangelo hybrids exhibited higher juice P content than the maternal parent ‘Pummelo’. Secondly, citrus fruits are recognised as an important dietary source of potassium39, a vital macronutrient involved in maintaining osmotic balance, regulating pH, and ensuring proper electrolyte function in the human body52. Among the evaluated citrus genotypes, juice K content in orangelo hybrids ranged from 428.06 mg L⁻¹ in hybrid ‘3–6’ to 1467.13 mg L⁻¹ in hybrid ‘13 − 6’. In the Tangelo group, juice K content varied from 512.66 mg L⁻¹ in hybrid ‘8–15’ to 996.33 mg L⁻¹ in hybrid ‘8–11’ (Fig. 3b). Within the parental genotypes, juice K concentrations were in the following order: ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) > ‘Pummelo’ (♀) > ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂). The predominance of potassium in the fruit juice of the studied citrus genotypes might be attributed to its natural association with organic acids53. Moreover, six Orangelos (37.50%) and two Tangelos (25%) excelled for juice K levels over the maternal parent ‘Pummelo’.

Calcium is an essential macro-mineral nutrient for the development of bones and teeth in the human body, and its deficiency can lead to several disorders, including osteoporosis, rickets, hypertension, and skeletal deformities54. Plant-based sources of calcium are particularly beneficial due to effective absorption and enhanced bioavailability. In the studied interspecific scion hybrids, juice Ca concentrations showed significant variation, ranging between 290.20 mg L− 1 and 561.06 mg L− 1 (Fig. 3c). The results also suggested that citrus fruits are a superior source of calcium than apples, mangoes, and bananas55. In the parental genotypes, juice Ca levels were in the order of: ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) > ‘Pummelo’ (♀) > ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂). Out of sixteen Orangelos, eight hybrids (50%) surpassed the pollen parent ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange for juice Ca levels. Furthermore, all Tangelo hybrids exhibited superiority for juice Ca levels over the pollen parent ‘Dancy’ tangerine, except hybrid ‘2–2’. The calcium concentrations observed in our study align with previous reports on citrus fruits9. However, Savić et al.56 found lower juice Ca levels in the commercial citrus juices, which might be due to clarification processes that lead to the removal of suspended tissue matrices, which are rich in Ca, since it is an essential constituent of the cell wall and middle lamella. Another macro-mineral nutrient, magnesium, is a key determinant in energy metabolism and protein synthesis57.

Among the parental genotypes, juice Mg concentrations were in the following descending order: ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) > ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) > ‘Pummelo’ (♀). In the studied interspecific citrus scion hybrids, juice Mg levels ranged between 29.75 mg L− 1 in ‘Orangelo 9–14’ and 77.23 mg L− 1 in ‘Orangelo 3–6’ (Fig. 3d). None of the Orangelo hybrids exceeded juice Mg levels over the pollen parent ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange. In contrast, all the Tangelo hybrids exhibited superiority for juice Mg over the pollen parent ‘Dancy’ tangerine except for hybrid ‘8–11’. The juice Mg content in our study corresponded with earlier reports on juice Mg levels in different citrus fruits9,48,50.

Micro-mineral nutrients such as iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), and copper (Cu) are required in trace amounts; however, they play essential roles in maintaining normal physiological functions in the human body. As per the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), preventable micronutrient deficiency is an emerging public health priority in India58. Significant variation was observed in the concentrations of micro-mineral nutrients in the juice of the studied interspecific citrus scion hybrids and their parental genotypes (Fig. 4). Iron is critical for haemoglobin synthesis, and its deficiency can lead to several disorders, including anaemia59. The juice Fe contents in the parental genotypes were as follows: ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) > ‘Pummelo’ (♀) > ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂). Within the Orangelos, twelve out of sixteen hybrids (75%) exhibited superiority over the pollen parent ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange for juice Fe levels, except for hybrids ‘4–13’, ‘7 − 3’, ‘7 − 4’, and ‘11–16’. In contrast, none of the Tangelo hybrids was found superior over the pollen parent ‘Dancy’ tangerine for juice Fe levels, except for hybrids ‘8–15’ and ‘8–19’ (Fig. 4a). Most promising genotypes in the interspecific citrus scion hybrids for juice Fe levels were Orangelo ‘2–6’ and Tangelo ‘8–15’. The juice Fe levels observed in our study are consistent with previous research on citrus fruits9,48. Zinc is an essential micronutrient required for normal physiological functions in the human body. It plays a key role in supporting immune function and also serves as an important component of insulin60. Among the parental genotypes, ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) corresponded with the highest juice Zn levels, followed by ‘Pummelo’ (♀) and ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂). Among the Orangelos, all the hybrids surpassed the juice Zn levels of the better parent ‘Pummelo’ (♀), except for hybrids ‘7 − 3’ and ‘7–8’. In contrast, within the Tangelo group, only three hybrids (37.50%) exhibited superiority for juice Zn levels over the better parent ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂), namely hybrids ‘2–2’, ‘2–3’, and ‘6–6’ (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, despite higher zinc levels in leaves, lower concentrations in fruit tissues may be due to limited phloem mobility and differential nutrient accumulation in different plant organs61. Another micronutrient, manganese, is a vital trace element involved in carbohydrate and fat metabolism, calcium ion absorption, and blood sugar regulation62. In our study, juice Mn levels in Orangelo hybrids ranged from 0.56 mg L⁻¹ to 1.29 mg L⁻¹, while in Tangelo hybrids, the juice Mn levels varied from 0.57 mg L⁻¹ to 1.19 mg L⁻¹. Among the parental genotypes, juice Mn levels were in the following descending order: ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) > ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂) > ‘Pummelo’ (♀). Overall, fourteen Orangelo hybrids and five Tangelo hybrids have surpassed their respective pollen parents for juice Mn levels (Fig. 4c). Copper is an essential micronutrient that plays a key role in regulating metabolic activities in the human body63 by participating in antioxidant defense systems and supporting enzyme functions. Among the parental genotypes, the highest juice Cu content was determined in ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂), followed by ‘Pummelo’ (♀), and ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (♂). In the interspecific citrus scion hybrids, juice Cu concentrations ranged from 0.54 to 0.85 mg L⁻¹ (Fig. 4d). Overall, thirteen out of sixteen Orangelo hybrids (81.25%) surpassed juice Cu levels over the better parent ‘Pummelo’ (♀). In contrast, only two out of eight Tangelo hybrids (25%) surpassed the better parent ‘Dancy’ tangerine (♂) for juice Cu levels.

The results in the present study indicated that several newly developed interspecific citrus scion hybrids exhibited heterosis relative to their respective parental genotypes64. This superiority can be compared to mid-parent in terms of Mid Parent Heterosis (MPH), or to the better parent in terms of heterobeltiosis (BPH). In our study, ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange (Orangelo) hybrids exhibited heterosis for both juice macronutrients (P and K) and juice micronutrients (Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn), whereas ‘Pummelo’ × ‘Dancy’ tangerine (Tangelo) hybrids showed superiority for juice Ca and Mg levels. Hybrid progenies exhibiting higher mid-parent heterosis (MPH) or better-parent heterosis (BPH) for specific mineral nutrients provide promising breeding material for use in citrus improvement programmes aimed at genetic biofortification. The non-intermediate mineral composition observed in several hybrids suggests the occurrence of transgressive segregation and complementary gene action; wherein favourable alleles inherited from both parents interact to enhance the expression of these traits65. Such genetic effects can result in hybrid performance exceeding that of either parent, reflecting the potential for trait improvement through recombination. Similar trends have been reported in citrus, where hybrids have demonstrated superiority over their parental genotypes for important attributes such as peel essential oil content66, fruit volatile compounds67, metabolite profiles68, and various growth-related traits69. Although citrus juice generally contains minerals in relatively low concentrations, their occurrence within an organo-mineral matrix may improve their bioavailability and physiological utility in the human body. This enhances the nutritional relevance of citrus fruits, which may serve as valuable sources of dietary micronutrients. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of exploiting hybrid variability for developing nutritionally superior citrus genotypes with improved functional and health-promoting properties.

Correlation between leaf and juice mineral nutrients

In the present study, leaf mineral nutrient levels were estimated in the studied interspecific citrus scion hybrids and their parental genotypes. The results revealed a significant difference in the leaf mineral nutrient status, indicating pronounced genotypic differences in nutrient uptake and assimilation under uniform field conditions (Supplementary Table 2).

The leaf macro-mineral nutrients in the studied citrus genotypes were in descending order as follows: Ca (0.82–2.94%) > K (0.40–2.88%) > P (0.07–0.94%) > Mg (0.13–0.25%). Moreover, the concentrations of the estimated leaf micro-mineral nutrients were as follows: Fe (45.60–154.67 mg kg⁻¹), Mn (11.09–45.07 mg kg⁻¹), Zn (9.90–28.70 mg kg⁻¹), and Cu (2.40–14.13 mg kg⁻¹). The observed variations in leaf mineral nutrient status are consistent with earlier reports on optimal citrus leaf nutrient levels9, confirming the nutritional adequacy of the experimental trees.

Although the primary focus of the present study was to evaluate juice nutrient composition, the assessment of leaf nutrient status provided valuable insights into the nutritional balance and nutrient uptake efficiency of the evaluated citrus genotypes. Moreover, the observed variations in leaf macro- and micronutrient concentrations among the newly developed interspecific citrus scion hybrids indicated considerable genetic diversity influencing nutrient assimilation and partitioning, which in turn affects fruit quality and nutrient accumulation in the edible tissues. Several hybrids exhibited higher or lower leaf nutrient concentrations than their parental genotypes grown under the same field conditions, suggesting transgressive segregation and the potential for developing nutrient-efficient genotypes through conventional breeding approaches.

The correlation analysis between leaf and juice mineral nutrient concentrations in newly developed interspecific citrus hybrids and their parental genotypes revealed distinct relationships, offering valuable insights into mineral partitioning and potential strategies for agronomic biofortification in citrus fruits (Fig. 5). Interestingly, juice Cu exhibited significant negative associations with juice K (r = -0.41, p < 0.05) and juice Ca (r = -0.44, p < 0.05). Leaf Ca and leaf Fe was inversely correlated with juice Fe (r = -0.43, p < 0.05) and juice Zn (r = -0.42, p < 0.05), respectively, which underscores the complex association between these divalent cations. Moreover, leaf Zn had a significant negative relationship with juice Mn (r = -0.45, p < 0.05), indicating antagonism between Zn²⁺ and Mn²⁺, which often compete for similar uptake pathways and transporter activities. Conversely, it was positively associated with leaf Fe (r = 0.65, p < 0.001). Finally, leaf Cu was positively correlated with leaf Fe (r = 0.56, p < 0.01) and leaf Zn (r = 0.51, p < 0.01). The correlation results suggested the complex physiological and biochemical interactions governing nutrient uptake, transport, and ion competition in citrus trees. These correlations can be attributed to the similar chemical properties of these macro- and micro-mineral nutrients and their interactive effects on absorption, transport, and physiological functions within plant tissues50,70. The positive association between leaf nutrient levels and juice mineral concentrations further indicates that agronomic biofortification may be an effective strategy to enhance mineral content in edible fruit tissues70. Understanding these correlations is important, as it suggests that improving one nutrient may have a positive influence on others within a crop improvement programme. The results also indicated that most correlation coefficients were weak or non-significant, highlighting the complexity of mineral dynamics in citrus fruits50. These results suggest that enhancing the nutrient content of citrus juice through agronomic practices may be challenging, as mineral distribution is complex, largely genotype-dependent, and only weakly influenced by leaf mineral status.

Genetic variability and heritability studies

The assessment of genetic variability-associated traits provides insights into the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors to specific trait expression in the evaluated interspecific citrus scion hybrids, which is crucial for selecting superior genotypes in future breeding programmes. In our study, phenotypic and genotypic coefficients of variation (PCV and GCV), broad-sense heritability (H2), and genetic advance as a percentage of the mean (GAM%) were estimated for the estimated fruit physico-chemical attributes and juice mineral nutrient concentrations (Table 2). These genetic parameters are useful for understanding trait inheritance and for guiding efficient selection strategies in breeding programmes. Traits with high heritability values coupled with greater genetic advance are particularly valuable for genetic improvement, as they indicate the effectiveness of direct selection70. We have estimated the above-mentioned genetic parameters separately for the Orangelo (‘Pummelo’ × ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange) and Tangelo (‘Pummelo’ × ‘Dancy’ tangerine) hybrids.

The heritability estimates obtained for Orangelo hybrids indicated that, for most traits, PCV values were slightly higher than GCV values, suggesting a limited influence of environmental factors and that the observed variability is predominantly genetic in origin. The trend for mineral nutrients was consistent with previous reports in fruit crops, where stable nutrient profiles were largely genotype-dependent64,70. High broad-sense heritability (H² > 0.90) was observed for all the studied macro- (K, P, Ca, Mg) and micro-mineral nutrients (Fe, Zn, Mn, Cu), as well as for the bioactive compounds, including TPC (H² = 0.92), TFC (H² = 0.98), and AOX (H² = 0.97). These results suggested additive gene action and indicate that selection based on phenotypic performance would be effective for genetic improvement of these studied traits. Furthermore, the GAM values were exceptionally high for several traits, including TPC (94.25%), TFC (91.11%), and juice content (115.64%), indicating substantial genetic variability and a strong potential for achieving significant crop improvement through selection. These findings align with the findings of Singh et al.71, who emphasized that traits with high heritability and high GAM values are largely governed by additive genetic effects, making them reliable targets for direct selection in citrus breeding programs. Conversely, traits such as juice pH (H² = 0.65; GAM = 17.34%) and zinc content (H² = 0.98; GAM = 20.62%) exhibited lower genetic advance values, along with moderate to high heritability, suggesting the involvement of non-additive gene action or environmental interactions, which would require recurrent selection or heterosis breeding for meaningful genetic gains70. Juice TSS (H² = 0.85; GAM = 21.43%), juice TA (H² = 0.95; GAM = 74.61%), and TSS/TA ratio (H² = 0.94; GAM = 66.37%) also showed promising improvement potential (Table 3), supporting their importance as fruit quality selection indices for citrus fruits. Notably, juice yield-related traits, including juice volume (H² = 0.97; GAM = 115.64%) and juice recovery (H² = 0.88; GAM = 86.29%), had higher heritability and GAM values, indicating ample scope to select genotypes with higher juice recovery. The combined observation of high heritability and high GAM for key mineral nutrients (K, P, Fe) and bioactive composition (TPC, TFC, AOX) underscores the potential of evaluated Orangelo hybrids to serve as elite breeding material for nutritional enhancement.

Similarly, for the Tangelo hybrids, the heritability studies for the estimated parameters also suggested that the observed variability is primarily under the genetic control and is genotype-dependent. Greater broad-sense heritability estimates (H² > 0.90) were observed for the following traits: TPC (H² = 0.95), TFC (H² = 0.99), AOX (H² = 1.00), juice TA (H² = 0.98), TSS/TA ratio (H² = 0.95), and all the estimated macro- (K, P, Ca, Mg) and micro-mineral (Fe, Zn, Mn, Cu) nutrients, underscoring strong genetic control and high reliability of phenotypic selection for the studied traits. Moreover, higher GAM values (> 75%) for TPC, TFC, AOX, juice TA, TSS/TA ratio, and juice Fe revealed that these traits are predominantly governed by additive gene action and had exceptional selection potential in breeding programmes. In contrast, juice recovery (H² = 0.73; GAM = 36.14%) exhibited moderate heritability and lower GAM, indicating that environmental factors and non-additive gene effects may influence this trait, thereby necessitating recurrent selection or hybridization strategies for crop improvement70.

A comparative analysis of broad-sense heritability and genetic variability parameters in the evaluated Orangelo and Tangelo hybrids revealed considerable similarities, with both groups exhibiting high broad-sense heritability (H² > 0.90) for all the mineral nutrients and greater GAM values (> 75%) for bioactive compounds (TPC, TFC, AOX), underscoring strong genetic control and the predominance of additive gene action. Such traits offer high selection efficiency, making them ideal targets for developing nutrient-dense citrus cultivars following conventional hybridization. Finally, these results demonstrate that a broad genetic base has been successfully introduced into the developed interspecific citrus scion hybrid population, enabling the identification of superior genotypes for mineral biofortification, enhanced antioxidant profiles, and improved fruit quality. Future breeding efforts can integrate these findings with genomic selection and precision nutrient management strategies to accelerate the development of nutrient-rich citrus cultivars. These results align with previous findings in fruit crops4,7, highlighting interspecific hybridization as a promising breeding strategy for enhancing the nutritional value of fruit crops.

Correlation among the juice physico-chemical traits and mineral nutrients

Correlation analysis suggested critical insights into the relationships among different traits in the studied interspecific citrus scion hybrids and their parental genotypes. The results further help to understand how changes in one particular trait influence the variations in other related traits. Moreover, correlation analysis serves as a valuable tool in biofortification and trait-specific breeding programmes, as it assists breeders in identifying key interrelated parameters. In the present study, a significant positive correlation was observed between juice content (mL) and juice recovery (%) (r = 0.54, p < 0.01), indicating that genotypes producing higher juice levels tend to maintain a higher proportionate juice yield in the studied interspecific citrus scion hybrids (Fig. 6). In contrast, a significant negative association was observed between juice content and juice TSS (r = -0.44, p < 0.05), suggesting a potential dilution effect, as previously reported in citrus breeding programmes. Ascorbic acid levels were positively interrelated with juice pH (r = 0.53, p < 0.01) and TSS/TA ratio (r = 0.46, p < 0.05). Conversely, the TSS/TA ratio showed a strong negative association with juice TA (r = -0.77, p < 0.001), consistent with the inverse relationship between acidity and sweetness perception in citrus, as well as the practical utility of the TSS/TA ratio as a maturity index. TPC exhibited a strong positive association with TFC (r = 0.56, p < 0.01), reflecting shared biosynthetic pathways in the phenylpropanoid metabolism. However, in contrast to previous studies7, AOX showed only a weak correlation with TPC and TFC in the present study.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a multivariate approach that reduces dimensionality and is a useful statistical tool for comprehensively evaluating similarities and differences among the studied genotypes72. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the twenty-four newly developed citrus scion hybrids and three parental genotypes, based on juice mineral composition and biochemical quality traits, identified eight principal components (PCs) with eigenvalues ≥ 1.073, collectively accounting for 81.80% of the total variance, highlighting the pronounced multidimensionality of fruit mineral nutrient and biochemical traits (Fig. 7).

The base loading value for significance was kept at 0.5. The PC1 was positively and significantly correlated with the juice volume, juice TA, and AOX; and negative contributions were attributed to juice pH, TSS/TA ratio, and ascorbic acid. The diverse and distant positions of new interspecific citrus hybrid progenies relative to the respective parental genotypes are attributed to transgressive segregation and/or complementary interactions among diverse genomic regions. Multivariate analysis has been widely used in various studies to classify genotypes and reduce redundant data for improved visualization73. Previously, PCA analysis has been effectively used in multiple studies to evaluate maturity indices74, characterize physico-biochemical fruit quality traits75, and aroma/flavour profile76 in different Citrus species.

MGIDI analysis

The multi-trait genotype ideotype distance index (MGIDI) is an effective statistical approach for selecting superior genotypes by integrating multiple desirable traits. Unlike conventional single-trait selection methods, it enables the simultaneous optimization of various breeding objectives, thereby facilitating more comprehensive and efficient genotype selection77. In the present study, the MGIDI approach allowed a simultaneous evaluation of twenty-four interspecific citrus scion hybrids and their parental genotypes based on fruit physico-chemical traits and juice mineral nutrient profiles (Fig. 8a and b). The four newly developed interspecific scion hybrids with the lowest MGIDI score, namely, ‘Orangelo 2–3’ (2.75), ‘Tangelo 8–19’ (3.45), ‘Orangelo 13 − 6’ (3.52), and ‘Orangelo 7 − 4’ (3.64), were identified as most desirable (Fig. 8; Supplementary Table 3). The relatively lower MGIDI values of these genotypes indicate closer proximity to the defined ideotype and thus represent desirable trait combinations. The positive selection differential observed for eleven studied traits (juice TA, Juice K, Juice Ca, Juice Fe, TPC, TFC, Juice Mn, AOX, Juice P, Juice Mg, juice TSS) highlights the effectiveness of MGIDI in achieving simultaneous genetic gains for mineral biofortification and fruit quality improvement, consistent with its reported utility in crop improvement programs77,78. However, negative selection differential was observed for the following studied parameters in descending order: juice volume > juice recovery > ascorbic acid > TSS/TA ratio > Juice Cu > Juice Zn > Juice pH. The strength–weakness plot indicated that the seven principal factors (FA1-FA7) retained in the MGIDI analysis collectively accounted for most of the trait variability, enabling a clear visualisation of the strengths and weaknesses of each evaluated genotype (Fig. 8; Supplementary Table 4). FA1 was primarily associated with juice pH, juice TA, TSS/TA ratio, and ascorbic acid, reflecting their strong influence on overall selection. Lower distance values in FA1 were observed for almost all the studied interspecific scion hybrids, except for ‘Orangelo 2–3’, ‘Orangelo 3–6’, ‘Orangelo 13 − 6’, and ‘Tangelo 8–19’, highlighting the superiority of the evaluated hybrids for the respective parameters. FA2 was dominated by juice macro- (K, Ca) and micro-mineral nutrients (Fe, Cu), with minimum distance values corresponded with ‘Orangelo 7–7’, followed by ‘Tangelo 6–6’, ‘Orangelo 13 − 4’, ‘Orangelo 3–10’, and ‘Orangelo 4–13’. Higher FA2 scores (> 10.0) were observed for six Orangelo hybrids (‘2–6’, ‘7 − 2’, ‘7 − 4’, ‘7–8’, ’11–16’, and ’13 − 6’) and three Tangelo hybrids (‘8 − 4’, ‘8–11’, and ‘8–15’). FA3 corresponded with the organic compounds (TPC and TFC) contributing to the anti-oxidative properties along with juice Mn. Lower FA3 scores (< 5.0) were associated with the following Orangelo hybrids: ‘2–6’, ‘3–5’, ‘3–6’, ’13 − 4’; along with two Tangelo hybrids, namely ‘2–2’ and 8 − 4’. In contrast, the highest FA3 scores were related to the hybrid ‘Orangelo 7–8’, followed by ‘Orangelo 7–7’ and ‘Tangelo ‘6 − 4’. FA4 was strongly linked with juice yield-related parameters (juice volume and juice recovery). Comprehensively, six Orangelo hybrids (‘2–6’, ‘3–5’, ‘3–10’, ‘7 − 3’, ‘11–16’, ‘13 − 4’) and two Tangelo hybrids (‘8 − 4’ and ‘8–18’), surpassed FA4 scores of more than 10. Conversely, lower distance values (< 5.0) for juice yield-related traits were observed for the following Orangelo hybrids: ‘2–3’, ‘7 − 2’, ‘7–7’, ‘7–8’, and ‘13 − 6’; alongwith ‘’Tangelo 2–2’, ‘Tangelo 6 − 4’, and ‘Tangelo 8–15’. FA5 highlighted juice Zn and antioxidant activity, with higher values were observed for all the evaluated interspecific citrus scion hybrids, except for ‘Orangelo 2–6’, ‘Tangelo 8–18’, and ‘Tangelo 8–19’. FA6 corresponded with juice P and juice Mg, whereas FA7 highlighted juice TSS. In FA6, only ‘Tangelo 6 − 4’ showed lower distance values for the respective traits. In FA7, ‘Orangelo 2–3’ and ‘Orangelo 7–8’ exhibited lower scores for juice TSS.

The ability to highlight weaknesses alongside positive traits in otherwise elite genotypes supports ideotype-based selection and resource-efficient breeding77,78. Mineral enrichment in citrus juice is of growing interest for consumer health and nutrition security. The positive selection differentials for most nutrients, particularly K, P, Mg, Ca, Fe, and Mn, indicate significant genetic potential for the development of mineral nutrient-biofortified citrus hybrids. Overall, the MGIDI-based approach successfully identified four high-performing interspecific scion hybrids with superior mineral nutrient and bioactive profiles. These selections represent promising candidates for further evaluation in multi-environment trials and for use as parental lines in citrus biofortification programs. By combining genomic potential with agronomic strategies, this work demonstrates a pathway to enhance the nutritional value of citrus juice for both fresh consumption and juice processing.

Cluster II corresponded with the highest number of interspecific scion hybrids with four Orangelos (‘7 − 3’, ‘2–6’, ’11–16’, ’13 − 4’) and six Tangelos (‘8–19’, ‘8–18’, ‘6 − 4’, ‘6–6’, ‘8–11’, and ‘8–15’). It was associated with lower juice recovery and juice content. Also, these hybrids are characterized with higher juice TSS but lowest TSS/TA ratio, which might be due to higher juice TA. Moreover, this particular cluster was associated with higher juice bioactive composition (TFC and ascorbic acid), and macro-mineral nutrients (K, Ca, and Mg). Cluster III, comprising six interspecific hybrids, namely ‘Tangelo 2–2’, ‘Orangelo 3–6’, ‘Orangelo 7 − 2’, ‘Orangelo 7–8’, ‘Orangelo 4–13’, ‘Tangelo 6 − 4’, and ‘Dancy’ tangerine. In contrast to Cluster II, these hybrids corresponded with higher juice recovery and juice content, moderate mineral nutrient levels (K, Ca, Mg, Mn, and Zn) but lowest ascorbic acid and juice Fe levels. Cluster IV, with five Orangelo hybrids (‘3–9’, ‘7 − 4’, ‘7–7’, ‘3–10’, and ‘9–14’), was associated with higher juice pH, juice TSS, TSS/TA ratio, TPC, juice K, juice Ca and juice Fe. Finally, Cluster 5, comprising of ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange and ‘Pummelo’, were associated with higher juice yield related parameters, juice P, juice Mg, juice Mn, juice Fe, with moderate values for juice pH, juice TSS, TPC, and ascorbic acid.

This study highlights the utility of multivariate clustering analysis in identifying promising interspecific citrus scion hybrids for biofortification programmes. The observed clustering patterns are consistent with earlier reports indicating that interspecific hybridisation substantially enhances phenotypic diversity for mineral nutrient and phytochemical traits in citrus germplasm. The distribution of high-mineral genotypes (Clusters II and III) and high juice-yielding genotypes (Cluster III) suggests that strategic hybridisation and selection can simultaneously improve nutritional quality, consumer acceptability, and commercial performance. Integrating such complementary trait groups provides a strong framework for developing superior citrus cultivars with balanced quality attributes. Notably, the clustering pattern did not correspond strictly to the expected genetic background, as Tangelos and Orangelos were dispersed across multiple clusters. This distribution reflects the high heterozygosity of genetically distinct parental genotypes and the prevalence of transgressive segregation, resulting in hybrids that outperform their parents in both positive and negative directions for key studied traits. These findings underscore the complex inheritance patterns in perennial citrus species and highlight the potential of conventional interspecific hybridisation to generate novel phenotypes with enhanced nutritional characteristics beyond parental limits. However, the study was conducted at a single location under uniform management conditions, which may restrict the broader applicability of the results across diverse environments. Future research should therefore focus on multi-location and multi-season validation of promising hybrids, alongside evaluation of rootstock influences and soil–plant interactions. Incorporating assessments of mineral bioaccessibility and processing stability will further strengthen the practical relevance of the present findings. Moreover, the integration of mineral profiling with advanced genomic and metabolomic approaches, supported by robust statistical tools, can enhance the efficiency of multi-trait selection. Such comprehensive strategies will accelerate the development of nutrient-dense citrus cultivars, contributing to improved fruit quality and addressing mineral deficiencies in human diets.

Conclusion

A significant variation in the studied fruit physico-chemical quality and mineral nutrient profiles was observed among the newly developed interspecific citrus scion hybrids, attributed to the unique genetic constitution, recombination events, and the high heterozygosity of the parental genotypes, which leads to transgressive segregation. Multivariate analyses revealed substantial variability and interrelationships among the studied, while heritability estimates indicated underlying genetic basis and breeding strategies for a trait-specific citrus improvement programme. MGIDI-based multi-trait selection identified four hybrids, namely, ‘Orangelo 2–3’, ‘Tangelo 8–19’, ‘Orangelo 13 − 6’, and ‘Orangelo 7 − 4’, as the most promising. These genotypes combined superior mineral profiles in juice with favourable physico-chemical attributes, outperforming their respective parental genotypes. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of interspecific hybridization between the genetically distinct citrus species, including the underutilized ‘Pummelo’, ‘Mosambi’ sweet orange, and ‘Dancy’ tangerine for developing nutritionally enriched citrus types. The identified hybrids provide valuable genetic resources for future citrus breeding programmes and offer practical potential for genetic biofortification to improve dietary mineral levels in the perennial fruit crops.
