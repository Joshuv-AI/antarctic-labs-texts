Criminal legal and behavioral health pathways in law enforcement-petitioned extreme risk protection orders
Authors: Kelsey M. Conrick, Erika Marts, Rachel Ross, Esprene Liddell-Quintyn, Julia P. Schleimer, Ayah Mustafah, Nicole Asa, Ali Rowhani-Rahbar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtreme Risk Protection Orders (ERPOs) are civil legal tools that temporarily restrict firearm access for individuals deemed to pose a risk of harm to themselves or others. Although ERPOs are strictly civil orders, their implementation often overlaps with other legal and behavioral health interventions. This qualitative study examined patterns among individuals subject to law enforcement-filed ERPOs in Washington State who intersect with arrest and involuntary transportation for behavioral health evaluation, and how these intervention pathways relate to ERPO outcomes.MethodsOf the 859 ERPOs granted at the temporary level in Washington State between 2016 and 2022, 57.6% (n = 495) involved one of the three intervention pathways: arrest (29.6%, n = 254), involuntary transport for behavioral health evaluation (26.1%, n = 224), or both (2.0%, n = 17) during the precipitating event that led to ERPO filing. We conducted qualitative analysis using the pen portrait method for n = 130 of these 495 cases to examine how risk was framed in court narratives and how those narratives corresponded with intervention pathways and ERPO outcomes.ResultsNarratives that emphasized self-harm frequently described involuntary transport, while those highlighting threats to others often involved arrest. Other factors, including firearm presence, mental health history, and how intent was described, emerged as recurring narrative elements and corresponded with specific intervention pathways. Judges ordered behavioral health evaluations in 36.3% of granted one-year ERPOs, but no clear patterns emerged between intervention pathways and this outcome.ConclusionsFindings suggest that law enforcement narratives were associated with how ERPO cases unfolded and which systems became involved. These findings highlight how variation in the way risk is framed in ERPO petitions, particularly around perceived intent, danger to self versus others, and contextual factors, can influence how systems respond, even under similar behavioral circumstances.

Introduction

Extreme Risk Protection Orders (ERPOs) are civil legal tools available in 21 states, Washington, D.C., and the U.S. Virgin Islands (as of October 2025) designed to temporarily restrict firearm access when an individual poses a substantial risk of harm to themselves or others [1]. In most states ERPOs are most commonly petitioned for by law enforcement officers [2], who frequently respond to precipitating events, i.e., crisis situations that prompt the ERPO [3]. These precipitating events are shaped by complex and interconnected factors and often include multiple behaviors that indicate substantial risk to self or others [3]. Certain behaviors, such as reckless firearm use or threats of intimate partner violence (IPV), may also meet statutory criteria for criminal legal and behavioral health interventions. Thus, in addition to filing for an ERPO, law enforcement may arrest respondents for legal violations or involuntarily detain and transport them to a hospital for a behavioral health evaluation [4]. As a result, while ERPOs are a civil intervention, respondents may also interact with criminal legal and behavioral health systems.

When law enforcement officers petition for an ERPO, the court files often include police incident reports, where officers detail their response to precipitating events [5]. These reports contain narratives describing law enforcement officers’ perceptions and framing of respondents’ risk of harm to self or others, as well as documentation of their decisions to arrest or involuntarily transport respondents for a behavioral health evaluation [5]. These narratives, among other evidence, are reviewed by judges, who determine whether to grant the ERPO. In Washington State (WA), judges consider whether to grant a 14-day temporary (also known as the ex parte order) or one-year (also known as full or final order) ERPO and whether to order a behavioral health evaluation as a condition of the ERPO. In their roles, law enforcement officers and judges assess complex circumstances and behaviors during crisis events, applying statutory criteria to determine appropriate interventions. While these assessments are guided by legal frameworks, they are also shaped by the context of the incident and state actors’ (i.e., law enforcement and judges) interpretations of the available information. This may introduce variability into decision-making processes about whether and for whom coincident interventions (namely arrest or involuntary detention) are deployed and ERPOs—potentially with behavioral health evaluation orders—are granted.

Despite the rich narrative data contained in ERPO court files, little research has examined how the framing of court narratives relates to state intervention pathways with legal and behavioral health system interactions and ERPO outcomes (i.e., one-year order granted or denied/dismissed). Analyzing narratives in ERPO court documents can identify patterns in how risk is framed, interpreted, and acted upon. For example, these patterns may provide insight into whether certain groups of respondents are more frequently criminalized or routed into involuntary treatment. Such insights could inform training for law enforcement, prosecutors, and judges; improve procedural fairness; and refine crisis intervention strategies to better support individuals at risk of harm. This study addresses these gaps through the following research questions:

Methods

Epistemology and methodology

This study is grounded in critical social constructionism, which posits that meaning is constructed through language, institutional contexts, and social relationships [6]. We focus on how state actors (e.g., law enforcement and judges) assign meanings to risk, danger, and intervention in ERPO processes. This includes examining how terms like “erratic behavior” or “substance abuse,” for example, which may evoke stereotypes or biases, may relate to decisions to arrest or involuntarily transport respondents for behavioral health evaluation (i.e., intervention pathway). In ERPO petitions, narrative details of the precipitating event circumstances are—by design—strategically used to justify or persuade judicial decisions within the framework of ERPO statutory requirements. These descriptions offer a valuable opportunity to identify patterns in decision-making processes and outcomes. We used narrative inquiry to analyze how narratives in ERPO petitions construct cognitive, emotional, and moral meanings, and how such meaning corresponds with decisions to arrest or involuntary detain respondents, grant ERPOs, and order behavioral health evaluations [7].

Setting

This study used ERPO court documents from WA, where law enforcement officers file about 90% of ERPO petitions, although household, family members, and intimate partners may also petition for an ERPO. The ERPO petition outlines the respondent’s behaviors that, in the petitioner’s view, indicate that firearm access would pose a substantial risk of harm to the respondent or others. A judge reviews these materials and may issue a temporary, 14-day order. After 14 days, a hearing for a one-year order is held; the petitioner and respondent present their cases, and a judge determines whether to issue the one-year order and/or require the respondent to complete a behavioral health evaluation as a condition of the ERPO.

Similar to other state involuntary treatment laws, WA’s Involuntary Treatment Act (ITA) law establishes the legal framework for the evaluation, detention, and commitment of individuals aged 13 years and older experiencing a mental health or substance use crisis severe enough to require involuntary behavioral health treatment [4]. Referrals for ITA investigations come from family members, first responders (including law enforcement), or medical providers and are based on concerns about an individual’s safety, history, and symptoms. One characteristic of the ITA that is unique to WA is that only a designated crisis responder may detain individuals, and they may do so for up to 120 h for evaluation and treatment, after which a court hearing is held and a judge determines whether additional treatment is warranted. Under the ITA, firearm rights are lost for 6 months upon detention for serious harm risk by a designated crisis responder and permanently lost if a court orders commitment of more than 14 days (RCW 9.41.047), which is more stringent than federal law.

Data collection and construction

Abstracted ERPO data

We obtained court files for all ERPOs in WA for which a temporary order was granted between December 2016 (when the law was implemented) and December 31, 2022. These documents were reviewed by trained coders and systematically abstracted into a database, as described in detail elsewhere [8]. Variables abstracted for this study included the petitioner type, respondent’s behavior in the precipitating event that led to ERPO petition (e.g., suicide threat, mass shooting threat), intervention pathway, circumstances of the precipitating event (e.g., substance use), county rurality, one-year ERPO outcomes (granted or denied/dismissed), and, if granted, whether the judge ordered a behavioral health evaluation. Only ERPOs filed by law enforcement were included in this analysis. Because we expected the specific behaviors that a respondent exhibited during the precipitating event to affect whether they were arrested and/or detained for involuntary transport and their ERPO outcomes, we examined variability among respondents exhibiting the same type of precipitating event behaviors (i.e., conditioning on respondent behaviors). Precipitating event behaviors were: public mass shooting threat or attempt, other threat or attempt of interpersonal violence (excluding public mass shootings), suicide threat or attempt, and reckless use/display/brandishing of a firearm.

Qualitative data construction

The ERPO court documents included the petition, temporary and one-year orders, and any supporting addenda, including court minutes where available. Documents consist of both structured fields (e.g., checkboxes) and open-ended questions and are often accompanied by supplementary materials such as police incident reports and affidavits submitted by law enforcement, family members, witnesses, and health professionals. The length of these records varied widely, ranging up to 900 pages in this study. While the documents typically contain detailed narrative accounts of events before, during, and sometimes after the ERPO filing, their unstructured and non-chronological organization can obscure a comprehensive understanding of the context and timeline of events. We therefore used the pen portrait method, which synthesizes substantial quantities and complexities of longitudinal qualitative data into a manageable format and facilitates a more comprehensive and chronological understanding of all events surrounding the ERPO filing [9].

We constructed a pen portrait for a sample of ERPO cases (details of selection, described below, were informed by the approach of Sheard & Marsh and our prior research [5, 9]). Each pen portrait included three sections—orientation, chronologically reconstructed timeline, and resolution (Fig. 1)—and was designed to capture both the chronological sequence of events as well as how these events were framed and described within the court documents. The orientation described key actors (e.g., respondent, petitioner, law enforcement, judge, or targets of threats) and the respondent’s time, space, circumstances, and characteristics (e.g., race, age, county rurality, military service, or mental health diagnoses). Orientation also included macro- and micro-level geography (e.g., county or specific location), virtual spaces (e.g., social media posts), and high-level temporal markers (e.g., hours, days, or years) to reconstruct the timeline of events leading to the ERPO. The chronologically reconstructed timeline focused on recreating the longitudinal series of events that led to the precipitating event, which could include up to two decades of the respondent’s history and included direct quotes to illustrate how the events were described and framed by law enforcement (and other document authors), such as language used to convey urgency, risk, or ERPO justification. The resolution summarized any criminal legal or behavioral health system actions, the ERPO outcome (e.g., granted, terminated, or reissued), and firearm removal details.

Given the study’s epistemological foundation, trustworthiness was not assessed through quantitative measures such as interrater reliability [10]. Instead, it depended on forming an interpretive community within the research team, where meanings were collaboratively examined, debated, and revised [10]. For each case, a coder (KMC, ELQ, RR, EM, AM) reviewed the entire court record and systematically constructed the pen portrait following a specific protocol developed by the first author, who has prior experience using pen portraits to analyze ERPO court documents [5]. An initial five pen portraits were double- or triple-coded, and the research team met to review their content and structure, refining the protocol as necessary. Subsequent pen portraits were constructed by a single coder and then reviewed by a second coder. Any questions or disagreements were resolved through team discussion until consensus was achieved. Furthermore, information about arrests of ERPO respondents occurring between August 1993 and June 2023 were obtained from Washington State Patrol (WSP) and added into pen portraits to confirm if respondents were arrested for behaviors surrounding the precipitating event, based on manual review. In the few cases in which there was a discrepancy between the ERPO court record and WSP data, we examined the behaviors described to have occurred during the precipitating event, the geographic location of the event, and the timing of arrest or charge. Among these, decisions on whether an arrest was for behaviors that occurred during the precipitating event were made by consensus of the coding team.

Sampling and analysis

Between December 8, 2016 and December 31, 2022 in WA, there were 952 ERPOs granted at the temporary level for 915 unique respondents, and 859 (90.2%) were filed by law enforcement. Of ERPOs filed by law enforcement, 495 (57.6%) met our case criteria by following one of three intervention pathways: (1) involuntarily transported during the precipitating event (and not arrested); (2) arrested during or for behaviors that occurred during the precipitating event (and not involuntarily transported); or (3) both involuntarily transported and arrested during (or for behaviors that occurred during) the precipitating event. We calculated descriptive statistics to summarize respondents’ behaviors during the precipitating event, intervention pathway, ERPO outcome, and behavioral health evaluation orders. Sampling of ERPOs for pen portraits and subsequent analysis was conducted in two phases.

Phase one

Because ERPO abstraction for later cases was not complete at the time qualitative analyses began, the first phase of analysis used cases sampled from the n = 503 ERPOs filed December 8, 2016-September 30, 2020. We identified a stratified random sample of ERPOs (n = 60) that met our case criteria, stratified by intervention pathway (involuntary transport, arrested, or both) and rurality of the county in which the ERPO was filed to ensure geographic representativeness.

For the first phase of analysis, we conducted initial identification of thematic content, listing all factors we believed may reflect patterns in intervention pathways of respondents and ERPO outcomes based on expertise gained reading and constructing the pen portraits [6]. Next, we conducted close, imposture reading and labeling, ascribing each story fragment to one or more factors and adding additional factors as they arose inductively. Imposture reading instructs the coders to look beyond literal meaning to consider how story plots, characters, and morals are embedded in cultural, political, and legal structures [10]. We then categorized the data to identify patterns and unity, trying to understand how character (e.g., law enforcement petitioner, witnesses) voices and the thematic content they produce created coherent patterns within the narratives [6]. Finally, we generated hypotheses about what combination of contextual factors would coincide with intervention pathways and ERPO outcomes (10).

Phase two

Based on preliminary findings from phase one, we conducted theoretical sampling (n = 70) in phase two [11], oversampling ERPOs based on the following factors (pertaining to the precipitating event and recorded in the abstracted data): substance use, alcohol use, intimate partner or domestic violence, respondent veteran status, and “erratic behavior” or delusions or hallucinations.

Pen portraits from both phases (n = 130) were divided based on the precipitating event behavior and grouped into four mutually exclusive categories (public mass shooting, threats/attempts to harm others, suicide threats/attempts, and reckless use/display/brandishing of a firearm). For respondents who exhibited multiple precipitating event behaviors, the analysis team selected a category for analysis by group consensus based on the behavior most heavily emphasized in the ERPO court documentation as probable cause for the ERPO. Within each of the four precipitating event behavior categories, we read the pen portraits to identify patterns within and factors differentiating each intervention pathway, discussing until consensus was reached on patterns and unpatterns (i.e., deviations from patterns identified) [12].

Results

Summary of study population

Among the n = 859 ERPOs granted at the temporary level during the study period with a law enforcement petitioner, 57.6% (n = 495) involved one of three intervention pathways for behaviors that occurred during the precipitating event: 26.1% (n = 224) included involuntary transport only, 29.6% (n = 254) included arrest only, and 2.0% (n = 17) involved both arrest and involuntary transport (Table 1). Cases in which respondents experienced both involuntary transport and arrest were rare; when this dual pathway occurred, respondents were typically detained and transported to a hospital for ITA evaluation during the precipitating event, with arrests often occurring in the following days or weeks for behaviors that occurred during the precipitating event.

Behavioral health evaluations

In descriptive analyses of all 859 ERPOs with a law enforcement petitioner, there were few differences between respondent pathway (involuntary transport, arrest, or both) in ERPO outcomes or behavioral health evaluation orders (Fig. 2). Overall, 81.8% of one-year orders were granted, and 36.3% of granted one-year orders included a behavioral health evaluation order.

Qualitative findings

In qualitative analysis of the pen portraits, two cross-cutting patterns in ERPO outcomes emerged across precipitating event behavior categories. First, for ERPO respondents who were arrested for offenses that would, upon conviction, result in the loss of firearm rights, ERPOs were often described in court documents as a temporary safeguard while criminal proceedings were underway. In some of these cases, the full one-year order was denied because of the ongoing criminal process.

Second, respondents whose behaviors posed a threat only to themselves often described involuntary transport for an ITA evaluation. Conversely, respondents who threatened or used violence against others—or whose actions endangered the immediate safety of others regardless of intent—were often arrested. Notably, however, more nuanced patterns emerged in how these threats were described and interpreted by law enforcement (Table 2). For example, in addition to the specific behaviors the respondent was described to have exhibited, in some cases the narratives in ERPO petitions reflected assessments about the respondent’s intent, mental state, or potential for harm, which were reflected in the intervention pathway the respondent experienced. These patterns underscore the variability in how precipitating event behaviors are framed and categorized during the ERPO process.

Public mass shooting threat

Among all ERPO respondents in WA with a law enforcement petitioner, 5.2% (n = 45) of temporary orders were granted based on a public mass shooting threat. Of these, 8.9% (n = 4) of respondents were involuntarily transported, 33.3% (n = 15) were arrested, and 2.2% (n = 1) were both arrested and involuntarily transported.

Arrest. Most cases in the pen portrait sample involving public mass shooting threats in which the respondent was arrested followed a consistent pattern: respondents made explicit or implied threats targeting public places such as places of worship, grocery stores, or hospitals. In instances where suicidal ideation was noted, it was typically framed as secondary to the mass shooting threat, with respondents indicating plans to end their own lives after carrying out the mass shooting.

Involuntary transport. Only two respondents in the pen portrait sample whose ERPOs were filed due to public mass shooting threats were involuntarily transported. In one case, the threat was vague; the respondent, a minor with an apparent obsession with public mass shootings, responded affirmatively when asked if they would commit one, but had no concrete plans or actions. In the second case, law enforcement involuntarily transported the respondent due to suicidal threats, but the ERPO itself was filed due to a public mass shooting threat made during the ITA evaluation process; the respondent was ultimately transported to an inpatient facility.

Both arrest and involuntary transport. The single respondent from the pen portrait sample who was both involuntarily transported and arrested did not make a specific public mass shooting threat. Instead, they were found standing outside a public location with an “assault-style” rifle, which created an implicit but nonspecific suggestion of a public mass shooting threat.

Threat or use of violence against others

This was the most common precipitating event behavior category. A total of 426 (49.6%) temporary orders were granted due to (non-public mass shooting-related) threats or use of violence against others. Among these, 17.1% (n = 73) involved involuntary transport, 42.5% (n = 181) arrest, and 1.4% (n = 6) both involuntary transport and arrest.

Arrest. Respondents in the pen portrait sample who were arrested for making (non-public mass shooting-related) threats or attempts of violence towards others primarily fell into two subgroups. The first included individuals who made explicit threats or used a firearm against another person, leading directly to their arrest. The second subgroup comprised respondents who did not have a firearm present during the precipitating event but were arrested due to one of the following circumstance subcategories: (1) the precipitating event involved IPV, (2) the respondent had repeated prior interactions with the mental or behavioral health system, or (3) the respondent made direct threats to law enforcement. For respondents in subcategory one, IPV involvement was often described to include physical violence against an intimate partner without a firearm, which fell under mandatory arrest laws. For those in the second subcategory, law enforcement had either previously attempted to refer respondents for ITA, but respondents had not met the criteria, or respondents were described to have refused services or medications for mental health diagnoses. A few respondents in this subcategory had multiple prior voluntary interactions with behavioral health services but continued exhibiting the same concerning behaviors. In the third subcategory, respondents were arrested for making direct threats against law enforcement officers despite the absence of a firearm at the scene.

Involuntary transport. Most respondents in the pen portrait sample who were involuntarily transported for ITA evaluation but were not arrested for threats or attempts of violence towards others shared one key characteristic: no firearm was present when law enforcement initially responded to the precipitating event, though firearms were sometimes discovered later during a search. Two respondents in this group did not follow this pattern; both involved older male veterans whose ERPO petitions focused heavily on their mental health challenges, despite their possession of firearms during the precipitating event.

Both arrest and involuntary transport. Among respondents in the pen portrait who experienced both involuntary transport and arrest for threats or attempts of violence towards others, all had firearms at the scene of the precipitating event, but these cases reflected complex sequences of events. For example, one respondent was involuntarily transported for chemical dependency detoxification and was arrested after assaulting hospital personnel. Another was arrested for threatening law enforcement and firefighters and had ITA transport for substance use concerns. The third case involved a respondent who was considered for ITA referral earlier on the same day as the precipitating event, but who did not meet the criteria for being held at the time. Later in the day, this respondent was arrested for IPV and involuntarily transported based on a recommendation from a crisis hotline.

Suicide threat or attempt

Suicide threat or attempt was the second most common precipitating event behavior category among ERPO respondents with total of 331 (38.5%) temporary orders granted for this reason. Of these, 39.0% (n = 129) involved involuntary transport, 16.3% (n = 54) arrest, and 2.1% (n = 7) both involuntary transport and arrest.

Arrest. Of the four respondents in our pen portrait sample who had a suicide threat or attempt and were arrested, three had documented histories of IPV. In two cases, the suicide attempt also endangered an intimate partner, triggering mandatory arrest laws. In the third case, the respondent had sent a suicide threat via text to a former spouse. The petition framed this act as a coercive tactic in the context of IPV, and the respondent was arrested for violating a no-contact order and an order to surrender and prohibit weapons. One respondent did not follow this pattern: they were arrested for driving under the influence while unlawfully carrying a firearm during a suicide attempt. In this case, the individual was described as having endangered others through impaired driving and was referred to voluntary mental health services rather than being involuntarily transported.

Involuntary transport. Most respondents in the pen portrait sample who were involuntarily transported due to suicide threat or attempt were experiencing acute crises that triggered suicidal ideation, including recent divorces or breakups, interpersonal conflicts, medical issues, or workplace difficulties. Some respondents had a history of chronic suicidal ideation, with the precipitating event marking a sudden escalation involving plan and intent. Substance use was frequently cited as contributing to these crises, and involuntary transport was typically intended to address the imminent risk to the respondent’s safety. One respondent did not follow this pattern: this individual was referred for suicidal ideation, but law enforcement later uncovered stalking behavior that did not result in an arrest.

Both arrest and involuntary transport. Respondents in the pen portrait sample who were both arrested and involuntarily due to suicide threats or attempts transported fell into two subgroups. In the first subgroup, the precipitating event was a suicide attempt that also endangered another individual’s safety, such as intentionally crashing a vehicle with another person inside. In the second, law enforcement initially responded to a suicide threat or attempt and involuntarily transported the respondent. During the subsequent investigation and report preparation, officers discovered that the respondent had verbally threatened someone earlier the same day in a separate incident, leading to their arrest for that behavior. One respondent did not follow this pattern: after threatening “suicide by cop” during the precipitating event, the respondent was arrested for stealing a firearm. However, due to their young age (< 13 years old) and lack of prior convictions, they were transported for an involuntary behavioral health evaluation rather than juvenile detention.

Reckless Use/Brandishing/Display of a firearm

Among all ERPO respondents in WA with a law enforcement petitioner, n = 301 (35.0%) temporary orders were granted due to reckless use, display, or brandishing of a firearm. Of these, 24.6% (n = 74) involved involuntary transport, 44.9% (n = 135) arrest, and 2.3% (n = 7) both involuntary transport and arrest. Almost all respondents in our pen portrait sample in this precipitating event behavior category discharged their firearms, typically while reportedly experiencing hallucinations or delusions. Most narratives described respondents acting on perceived threats, such as imagined break-ins to their homes or properties or paranoia about being watched by government entities. Whether the respondent was involuntarily transported or arrested largely depended on who was at risk when the firearm was discharged.

Arrest. Respondents in the pen portrait sample were arrested for reckless use/brandishing/displaying when their firearm use created a clear and immediate danger to others. For example, several respondents discharged firearms in apartment complexes. Those whose petitions highlighted specific risks to others—such as bullet holes near areas occupied by neighbors, like a child’s bedroom—were arrested.

Involuntary transport. Conversely, respondents in the pen portrait sample who discharged firearms in similar settings but whose narratives did not indicate that others were in direct danger were often arrested and less often involuntarily transported. Respondents who were involuntarily transported were typically isolated in their homes when discharging their firearms, with no one else at immediate risk. These cases often involved respondents experiencing hallucinations or delusions about break-ins or surveillance, and the petitions focused on behavioral health intervention. However, several cases in which respondents displayed firearms around others but neither discharged the weapon nor pointed it directly at anyone did not follow these patterns. Despite the potential risk to others noted in the petitions, these respondents were involuntarily transported rather than arrested.

Both arrest and involuntary transport. All respondents who were initially identified through abstracted data as having recklessly used, displayed, or brandished a firearm and who experienced both arrest and involuntary transport (n = 6) were re-assigned during qualitative analysis to mutually exclusive precipitating event behavior categories based on the primary behavior emphasized in the ERPO petition. As a result, there were no pen portraits in this category.

Discussion

This study identified patterns in narratives described in ERPO court documents and examined whether patterns related to intervention pathways during the precipitating event—involuntary transport for ITA evaluation, arrest, or both—and ERPO outcomes. Broadly, we found that intervention pathways reflected how respondents’ precipitating event behaviors were framed in petitions. Within precipitating event behavior categories (e.g., suicide threat, public mass shooting threat), involuntary transport for ITA evaluations were common in narratives emphasizing risks to the respondent’s own safety, and arrests were common in cases where harm to others was emphasized. However, more nuanced patterns emerged that provide insights into how ERPO respondents interact with criminal legal and behavioral health systems and highlight opportunities for improved training and procedures to promote equity and better support individuals at risk of harm.

A key strength of this study is the use of the pen portrait method, which enabled a contextualized, cross-case analysis of how risk was described and addressed in ERPO filings. By synthesizing extensive narrative data into structured, chronological case summaries, the pen portraits allowed us to analyze not just what behaviors occurred, but how these behaviors were framed—what language was used, what details were emphasized or omitted, and how intent was inferred. For example, we observed differences in intervention pathways among respondents who discharged a firearm due to hallucinations (same behavior) depending on whether the narrative emphasized public endangerment or an individual crisis. This approach allowed us to examine how decisions unfolded over time and how narrative framing may have shaped those decisions—insight that would not have been possible using only abstracted variables. It also made it possible to capture important contextual details, such as repeated system contacts or patterns of escalating concern that appeared relevant to how state actors assessed risk and selected intervention pathways.

The way ERPO petitions are written provides insight into how narrative framing—particularly descriptions of risk and need—relate to intervention pathways (i.e., arrest, involuntary transport, both) the state pursues for respondents. For example, several respondents in our sample discharged firearms in apartment complexes due to hallucinations or delusions. When petitions emphasized the risk such discharges posed to nearby residents (harm to others), respondents were commonly arrested. In contrast, when narratives focused on the respondent’s underlying behavioral health needs, involuntary transport for ITA evaluation was common. This finding may reflect differences in how law enforcement officers assess blameworthiness, perceived dangerousness, and practical constraints—core components of focal concerns theory, which posits that decision-makers weigh these three factors when responding to perceived risk. Originally applied to judicial sentencing, this framework has since been used to examine discretionary decisions by law enforcement and other actors and may help explain why similar behaviors result in divergent intervention pathways based on how risk is framed in ERPO petitions [13–15]. This finding also aligns with prior research on the role of narrative framing in judicial decision-making. For instance, studies of Domestic Violence Protection Orders have shown that the way petitioners frame respondents’ behavior can affect whether an order is granted [16–19]. In the ERPO context, narrative framing may also reflect institutional priorities. Law enforcement petitions often emphasize public safety concerns, which may unintentionally overshadow behavioral health considerations and shape the intervention pathways respondents follow. Additionally, how narratives are constructed and interpreted may be influenced by implicit biases, including stereotypes related to mental illness or perceived dangerousness. Such biases would suggest a need for training and resources that equip petitioners to construct narratives that balance presentation of true risks while avoiding unintended biases, ensuring equitable and consistent outcomes [20].

In our pen portrait sample, respondents whose petitions focused on suicide threats or attempts and who also had a documented history of IPV perpetration were typically arrested but not involuntarily transported for ITA evaluation. In WA, mandatory arrest laws remove officer discretion and require them to arrest a suspected aggressor if they have probable cause to believe a domestic violence offense was committed within four hours prior [21]. As a result, the suicide threat may be subsumed under the legal framework of IPV, shifting the response from behavioral health intervention to criminal enforcement. Additionally, some petitions in our sample described suicide threats as a form of coercive control—for example, threatening to die by suicide if a partner attempted to leave the relationship or blaming the partner for anticipated death [22]. Prior research suggests IPV perpetrators are also at high risk of dying by suicide, particularly by firearm, and these risks may be higher when civil legal issues are present [23, 24]. This duality may present a challenge for traditional law enforcement and judicial tools; a suicide threat in the context of IPV may induce carceral responses yet require supportive intervention for risk of self-harm. These findings suggest a need for protocols that can address both the public safety and behavioral health risks inherent in such cases, including trauma-informed trainings for law enforcement and the development of coordinated intervention pathways that do not rely exclusively on arrest.

In several pen portrait cases of (non-public mass shooting-related) threats or uses of violence, respondents with documented histories of repeated interaction with behavioral health systems were arrested rather than involuntarily detained for behavioral health evaluation. These respondents were often described in ERPO petitions as having declined medication, refused services, or failed to meet ITA criteria in prior encounters. Despite a clear pattern of behavioral health concerns, law enforcement narratives sometimes emphasized the respondent’s continued non-compliance or escalating behavior, framing them as resistant. In these instances, the arrest pathway may reflect accumulated frustration or the absence of an accessible behavioral health intervention, rather than a clear difference in risk profile. These cases illustrate how prior unsuccessful attempts at engagement with the mental health system can shift law enforcement responses from health-oriented to punitive, even when the underlying behavior remains consistent [25]. They also underscore a broader systems gap in that when voluntary services are declined and involuntary thresholds are not met, arrest may be perceived as the only actionable pathway [26, 27].

Among all law enforcement-petitioned ERPO cases, no clear patterns emerged in ERPO outcomes or judicially ordered behavioral health evaluations based on the intervention pathway respondents experienced (arrest, involuntary transport, or both). The proportion of one-year orders granted and the proportion of cases in which judges ordered a behavioral health evaluation were relatively consistent across intervention pathways, conditional on precipitating event behavior. However, in completing the pen portraits, we frequently encountered parenthetical notes or narrative justifications that acknowledged the potential appropriateness of a behavioral health evaluation without a formal order. These narrative explanations suggest that judges may consider multiple factors—such as prior evaluations, the respondent’s current treatment status, or concerns about feasibility or enforcement—when deciding whether to require further evaluation. Further research is needed to better understand judicial decision-making in ERPO cases, including how judges weigh behavioral health considerations in relation to public safety, statutory criteria, and the information presented in law enforcement narratives.

This study has several limitations. Our analysis was intentionally grounded in ERPO court documents, which provided rich narrative accounts of the precipitating event but also constrained the scope of available information. Although we included court minutes when available, these were typically brief, lacked substantive detail, and were not consistently available. We were unable to observe or analyze narratives presented during court proceedings due to the cost of recordings, including verbal testimony, judicial reasoning, or respondent behavior not documented in the written record. In addition, we could not systematically assess the use of voluntary behavioral health interventions, as these were inconsistently described across petitions. Because court documents are typically authored by law enforcement officers or other state actors, the analysis necessarily reflects the perspectives and interpretations of those institutional actors. Future research incorporating court observations, respondent interviews, or data from behavioral health and legal system sources could help fill these gaps and offer a more comprehensive understanding of how ERPO decisions are made and experienced.

Conclusion

This study examined how law enforcement narratives in ERPO court documents correspond with state intervention pathways—arrest, involuntary transport, or both—and how those pathways align with ERPO outcomes. We found that narrative framing related to whether respondents were routed into the criminal legal or behavioral health system, with threats of harm to others often resulting in arrest and threats of self-harm often leading to involuntary transport. However, variation within these categories and the lack of consistent patterns in judicial outcomes, such as behavioral health evaluations, highlight the complexity of ERPO implementation. Future work should include interviews with state actors, including law enforcement, prosecutors, and judges, to better understand how they interpret risk and make decisions within the ERPO process.
