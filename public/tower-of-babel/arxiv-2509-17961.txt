Bringing Pedagogy into Focus : Evaluating Virtual Teaching Assistants’ Question-Answering in Asynchronous Learning Environments Thanks: Co-senior authors
Li Siyan, Zhen Xu, Vethavikashini Chithrra Raghuram, Xuanming Zhang

Abstract
Asynchronous learning environments (ALEs) are widely adopted for formal and informal learning, but timely and personalized support is often limited. In this context, Virtual Teaching Assistants (VTAs) can potentially reduce the workload of instructors, but rigorous and pedagogically sound evaluation is essential. Existing assessments often rely on surface-level metrics and lack sufficient grounding in educational theories, making it difficult to meaningfully compare the pedagogical effectiveness of different VTA systems. To bridge this gap, we propose an evaluation framework rooted in learning sciences and tailored to asynchronous forum discussions, a common VTA deployment context in ALE. We construct classifiers using expert annotations of VTA responses on a diverse set of forum posts. We evaluate the effectiveness of our classifiers, identifying approaches that improve accuracy as well as challenges that hinder generalization. Our work establishes a foundation for theory-driven evaluation of VTA systems, paving the way for more pedagogically effective AI in education.



1
Introduction

Asynchronous learning environments (ALEs) have become a critical complement and extension to traditional synchronous learning (e.g., live lectures and real-time tutoring). By removing time and location constraints, ALEs enhance flexibility and inclusivity, supporting more equitable access to educational resources and opportunities
Tate and Warschauer 2022
;
Farley and Burbules 2022
. However, providing timely and effective instructional support remains one of the biggest challenges for ALE adoption. Their self-paced, decentralized nature makes it difficult for instructors to track progress and manage interactions, often leading to delayed feedback that can reduce student motivation, engagement, and learning effectiveness
Lin and Gao 2020
;
Watts 2016
.

Virtual Teaching Assistants (VTAs) offer a promising approach to address this challenge by providing scalable, timely, and personalized support, helping reduce instructor workload in ALEs
Audras et al. 2022
. They are often powered by Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) approaches to respond quickly to student questions
Liu et al. 2024
. While earlier systems focused on routine and syllabus-based questions
Goel and Polepeddi 2018
;
Sajja et al. 2023
;
Raamadhurai et al. 2019
, recent VTAs powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) can tackle more complex academic queries
Taneja et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Laney and Dewan 2024
;
Qiao et al. 2025
. Although these advancements highlight the growing capabilities of VTAs, the pedagogical effectiveness of their generated content still lacks a systematic and scalable evaluation framework, which is a critical issue. Early approaches often rely on manual qualitative analyses
Lee et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
or general-purpose language metrics
Al-Hossami et al. 2024
;
Vasselli et al. 2023
to assess similarity to human responses. Nevertheless, these metrics are not designed to assess teaching quality and often lack generalizability across different educational contexts.

To bridge this gap, we propose a
pedagogical teaching assistant evaluation framework grounded in learning science
, focusing on VTAs in asynchronous online forums, one of the most common deployment contexts in ALEs. The framework has two components: a five-level pedagogical skill model with progressively increasing “instructional difficulty”:
(1) Clarify Misunderstandings and Confusions
,
(2) Deepen Disciplinary Understanding
,
(3) Foster Higher-Order Thinking
,
(4) Enhance Metacognitive Awareness
, and
(5) Foster Collaborative Knowledge Construction
developed by linking core cognitive mechanisms to pedagogical goals, and an evaluation rubric that operationalizes this model by specifying observable instructional behaviors at each level.

When establishing the usability and reliability of our rubric, we utilize a set of simulated VTA responses on student discussion forum posts collected through an IRB-approved procedure. Note that Level 5 of our framework is currently unattainable for most VTAs, as their responses are typically limited to a single forum post and any retrieved content, excluding broader context from other posts in the discussion. However, asynchronous online forums are inherently community-driven spaces, and achieving collaborative knowledge construction requires access to broader, forum-level context.
To simulate VTAs with broader forum-level post access for Level 5 evaluations, we use RAG to create responses informed by similar peer posts. Expert annotators apply our rubrics to these simulated responses. We then leverage these annotations to build automatic classifiers using LLMs.

Maurya et al. 2024
reports negative correlations between LLM and expert annotations in tutor pedagogical assessment, indicating that LLMs may be unreliable evaluators. Their study uses a single, unoptimized prompt without fine-tuning, which the authors acknowledge as a limitation.
We extend this line of inquiry to the VTA pedagogical evaluation context by systematically exploring whether prompt optimization and fine-tuning can improve alignment between LLM-based evaluators and expert pedagogical judgments.

Our expert annotation results indicate that
including peer forum posts reduces pedagogical capability in Levels 1-4
within our specific simulated VTA environment.
Furthermore, while our automated classifiers perform reliably on lower-level, in-distribution examples, they
struggle at higher pedagogical levels
and
exhibit limited generalization
, even for Level 1.

Overall, our contributions are as follows:

1.

We introduce the first pedagogical evaluation framework designed to be generalizable within the context of asynchronous online forums, grounded in learning science theory.

2.

We demonstrate the generalizability of our evaluation framework by applying it to both open-source and synthesized VTA responses.

3.

We show that current LLMs struggle with our nuanced classification task, highlighting the need for caution when applying LLMs to pedagogical evaluation.

Related Work

2.1
Teaching System Evaluation Frameworks

Pedagogical skills are complex and context-dependent, making automated evaluation difficult. Early work in evaluating VTA relied on manual qualitative analysis, usage logs, measures of learning outcomes, or generic language metrics
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Lee et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Kweon et al. 2025
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2025
;
Al-Hossami et al. 2024
;
Vasselli et al. 2023
;
Dan et al. 2023
;
Dahal et al. 2025
, which lack specificity for educational contexts. Recent studies have proposed more targeted frameworks for pedagogy automated evaluation. For example,
Demszky et al. 2021
introduced “uptake” to capture how instructors build on student input, while
Tack and Piech 2022
outlined three pedagogical dimensions: teaching alignment, student understanding, and helpfulness.
Maurya et al. 2024
expanded this with a taxonomy of eight learning science–grounded dimensions, and
Jurenka et al. 2024
proposed seven diverse educational benchmarks from high-level learning science principles, including an LLM-based automatic evaluation method. Their evaluation framework includes five teaching dimensions and eight metrics. However, these efforts focus on synchronous tutoring settings, whereas asynchronous forums emphasize timely feedback, critical thinking, and peer dialogue. Despite the growing use of VTAs in such forums, evaluation still relies predominantly on student surveys, qualitative content analysis of generated responses, and usage log–based behavioral metrics
Goel and Polepeddi 2018
;
Hicke et al. 2023
;
Baker et al. 2024
;
Sinha et al. 2024
;
Qiao et al. 2025
, with no pedagogical evaluation framework specifically tailored to this context.

2.2
LLMs as Evaluators for Education

Using LLMs as evaluators has become increasingly popular
Chang et al. 2024
;
Chan et al. 2023
, particularly within educational contexts. Recent studies have employed LLMs to evaluate the quality of student writing
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Kim and Kim 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
and to judge the human-likeness of AI tutor responses
Chevalier et al. 2024
. However, their reliability in assessing complex constructs, such as pedagogical capabilities, remains unclear
Maurya et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2023a
.
A key challenge is that general-purpose evaluation methods could fail to align with the complex, ambiguous, and domain-expert inferences required by many educational assessments
Han et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2025
;
Wei et al. 2025
. Research has shown that LLM evaluation performance can vary across task context and design
Liu et al. 2025
. These disparities underscore the need for further investigation into LLM-based educational evaluation, informed by domain expertise, to avoid overestimating what general-purpose LLM evaluators can accurately and reliably achieve.

3
Our Evaluation Framework

Evaluating pedagogical skills is challenging because teaching support is a dynamic process that varies by context, subject, and learner preferences. Therefore, effective evaluation requires selecting the appropriate level of observation granularity and ensuring alignment with the specific learning context. In our framework, we follow three steps to develop the pedagogical skill model and the evaluation rubric:(1) identifying cognitive mechanisms in asynchronous online forums, (2) distilling context-specific pedagogical goals, and (3) translating these goals into observable pedagogical behaviors.

3.1
Mapping Cognitive Mechanisms

Asynchronous online forums are typically structured by instructors through predefined discussion prompts, allowing students to engage at their own pace by asking questions, sharing ideas, and interacting with peers. Compared to synchronous instruction or other forms of asynchronous learning, such as lectures or tutoring, the key feature of online discussion forums is their decentralized, learner-driven, and socially interactive nature. Rather than following a predefined or instructor-led sequence, these forums rely on students to initiate, respond to, and sustain the discourse. The content is also more open-ended and exploratory, with fewer structural constraints.

From a cognitive science perspective, learning in asynchronous online forums is grounded in constructivist and Vygotsky’s sociocultural learning theories
Fosnot 2013
;
Lantolf 2000
, which view learning as an active, socially mediated process of meaning-making. Several cognitive mechanisms support effective learning in this context: clarifying confusion and constructing understanding through questioning, exposing misconceptions, and receiving explanatory feedback
Hew et al. 2010
; fostering higher-order thinking through advanced cognitive processes such as analysis, reasoning, and argumentation
Meyer 2003
;
Wang et al. 2016
; promoting reflection and self-regulation through self-explanation and elaboration
Choi et al. 2005
; and deepening disciplinary understanding through exposure to diverse perspectives and information. Interaction with conflicting ideas can also trigger cognitive conflict, prompting learners to identify gaps in their thinking, facilitate conceptual change, and integrate new knowledge into existing cognitive schema
Limón 2001
.

3.2
Defining Pedagogical Goals

Building on the learning characteristics and cognitive mechanisms summarized in the previous section, we define the instructional support goals for asynchronous online forums and organize them hierarchically by cognitive engagement and instructional complexity, in accordance with well-established theories (ICAP)
Chi and Wylie 2014
of cognitive engagement in the learning sciences.

1.

Clarify Misunderstandings and Confusions:
Identify underlying misunderstandings and address learner confusion by providing clear, instructional explanations to enhance conceptual clarity
Stahl 2013
;
Dykman et al. 2008
;
Nandi et al. 2012
;
Hew et al. 2010
;
Pena-Shaff and Nicholls 2004
.

2.

Deepen Disciplinary Understanding:
Deepening students’ understanding of disciplinary concepts through appropriate and effective instructional support
Engle and Conant 2002
;
Yang et al. 2022
;
Onyema et al. 2019
.

3.

Foster Higher-Order Thinking:
Stimulates students’ engagement in complex cognitive processes and the application of higher-order thinking skills
McLoughlin and Mynard 2009
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Szabo and Schwartz 2011
;
Brierton et al. 2016
;
Yang et al. 2005
;
Onyema et al. 2019
.

4.

Enhance Metacognitive Awareness:
Supports students’ reflection on their understanding, recognition of knowledge gaps, and evaluation of their thinking and learning strategies
Murphy 2009
;
Michalsky et al. 2007
;
Akyol and Garrison 2011
.

5.

Foster Collaborative Knowledge Construction:
Encourages meaningful peer interaction, supports the exchange of diverse perspectives, and fosters a collaborative learning environment and sense of community
Chi and Wylie 2014
;
Zou et al. 2021
;
Akcaoglu and Lee 2016
;
Joksimović et al. 2015
.

3.3
Developing Rubric of Observable Behaviors

Based on the pedagogical goals outlined above, we develop an evaluation rubric to capture distinct pedagogical capabilities within each level. The rubric construction process includes three iterative stages to ensure both theoretical alignment and empirical validity.

In the first stage, we drew upon prior research on effective instructional practice in online forums to draft initial level descriptions. These descriptions operationalized pedagogical behaviors at varying degrees of effectiveness for each level. In the second stage, we refined the rubric by conducting interviews with two experienced instructors, each with over three years of experience facilitating discussions. This empirical review enabled us to revise and align the rubric more closely with authentic instructional practices. In the final stage, we conduct a pilot validation by recruiting two graduate students in education to independently apply the draft rubric to a sample of 20 forum responses. They then discuss any discrepancies and collaborate on refining the rubric descriptions to ensure that all levels are clearly distinguishable and appropriately represented. The final rubric, along with illustrating examples, is in Appendix
B
.
Each pedagogical level has possible ratings of 0, 1, 2, or “NA”.

4
Pedagogical Evaluation of Simulated VTA Responses

We apply our evaluation framework to two sets of simulated VTA responses, one set with similar peer posts and the other without. This is to examine if incorporating peer responses as forum-level context affects the expressed pedagogical capabilities of our LLM-powered VTA.

4.1
Source of Student Discussion Forum Posts

To simulate VTA responses, we use a dataset of real online forum posts in thousands of courses offered at a large public university in the United States, available through our existing research partnership. IRB approval and data use agreements have been in place for the project. Complying with the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA), all personally identifiable information in the raw data was removed or redacted prior to researcher access, and the dataset cannot be made publicly available. Nonetheless, we release our code for data generation and classification at
https://github.com/Columbia-NLP-Lab/VTA_qa_evaluation
.

We construct a classifier using
GPT-4o-mini
to categorize discussion forum posts into five categories, one of which is academic questions (see more details about this simple classifier in Appendix
J
).
While our pedagogical framework is designed to be generalizable across various forms of student engagement, we selected academic questions as our starting point in this work, as this allows us to assess the foundational pedagogical capabilities of VTAs. Note that we aim to identify academic questions with high precision. Thus, the overall question classification accuracy is not our primary concern. The original set of data consists of 600k forum posts, from which we select the first 60k for classification. Additionally, we filtered for posts that are the beginning of their post threads.

For this study, we focus on a diverse sample set of 300 academic questions from 116 students. These questions offer high coverage of academic disciplines, spanning 85 courses and 40 departments, from criminal law to transportation systems.

4.2
Example VTA Response Generation

We select
Llama-3-70B-Instruct

Grattafiori et al. 2024
as our primary LLM to generate simulated VTA responses because we want instances of pedagogical attempts with varying degrees of success according to our framework. Due to the lack of access to course materials, we prompt the model to respond to student questions using its inherent knowledge base instead of taking retrieval-augmented generation approaches.

Two sets of responses are generated: with and without forum-level context.

Context-Free:
For each academic question, the default VTA prompt includes: (1) Course information, when available. We scraped the course catalog from the university to obtain course names and descriptions. (2) Description of the discussion topic. (3) Content of the student’s forum post. Mock example data instances are in Table
1
.

We rewrite the first four levels of our pedagogical framework into goals and incorporate them into the default prompt to define desirable behaviors. We exclude the fifth level here, since there is no peer information available.

Forum-Level Context:
To provide this context, we retrieve the top ten most similar student posts (excluding the post to be responded to) using OpenAI’s
text-embedding-3-small
embedding. When there is a discussion topic associated with the forum post, we select contextual forum posts under the same discussion topic; otherwise, from the entire course. All relevant posts are labeled with consecutive numbers, so that the VTA can refer to the posts as "Similar Post #1", etc.

We include the goal-style rewrite of Level 5 in this prompt version. See the prompts in Appendix
C
and example generations in Appendix
I
. We focus on simulated VTA responses in this work to ensure comparison fairness between the different generation conditions. It is important to note, however, that our framework is already applicable to current VTA systems. We include an example in Figure
1
with a mock student discussion forum post, the corresponding VTA response, and the assessments using our framework.

4.3
Annotation Procedure

Given the pedagogical knowledge and background required for accurate ratings, we recruit two volunteer annotators who are pursuing graduate degrees in education, with specializations in learning sciences and pedagogy. Before the annotation, one of the authors conducts a training session with the two annotators to clarify the coding rubric and the definitions of each rating level. During the session, the annotators independently label a set of 20 sample VTA responses and discuss any disagreements or ambiguities in their interpretations. The rubric descriptions are revised to enhance clarity for subsequent annotations.

Then, two annotators independently rate the full set of VTA responses. At the 80-response milestone, the author conducts an inter-rater agreement check, during which the annotators achieve an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.81, indicating a fine agreement and minimal misunderstanding. Upon completion of the full annotation, the final ICC was 0.78, indicating good reliability
Koo and Li 2016
.

Next, we compare the annotations from two annotators. We first identify items with a rating discrepancy greater than 1 (e.g., one annotator rates an item as 0 while the other rates it as 2, suggesting potentially substantive disagreement), which occurs in 1.8% of our sample. For these cases, one of the authors discusses with both annotators, and the final score for each item is determined by majority agreement. Items with a rating discrepancy of exactly 1, likely reflecting random rating variance, occur in 11.2% of our sample. These cases are divided equally among the author and the two annotators for a second round of review to determine the final score. The distribution of the annotated scores across each level can be found in Table

and Table

in the Appendix.

5
Automated Pedagogical Evaluations of VTAs

We explore automatic approaches for pedagogical assessment using LLMs to address practical challenges in using expert annotators and to investigate the claim from
Maurya et al. 2024
about the limitations of LLM evaluators in education.

The labeling process in Section
4.3
yielded 600 unique annotated post-response pairs. We randomly sample 150 context-free and 150 contextual pairs as our training set, consisting of student forum post information, the corresponding VTA responses generated by
Llama-3
, and ratings on all five levels. Note that the annotators do not label the context-free instances for Level 5; as a result, all levels but Level 5 have 300 training pairs. The rest of the data constitutes a held-out test set. We refer to the training set as
Llama3-Train
and the test set as
Llama3-Test
for the rest of the paper.

5.1
Prompt-based Classifiers

We experiment with
GPT-4o-mini
and
GPT-4o
for zero-shot and prompt-optimized classification. We implement the classifiers as language model programs using the DSPy framework
Khattab et al. 2023
for compatibility with prompt optimization (Appendix
K
). The classifier program receives (1) the course information, (2) the discussion topic, (3) the forum post, (4) the VTA response, and (5) the rubric for
a specific pedagogical level
, and then
outputs the scoring for that specific level
.

For prompt optimization, we leverage DSPy’s SIMBA prompt optimizer
*
*

*

https://github.com/stanfordnlp/dspy/blob/main/dspy/teleprompt/simba.py
. SIMBA stands for Stochastic Introspective Mini-Batch Ascent. This prompt optimizer accepts arbitrary DSPy programs and proceeds in a sequence of mini-batches, seeking to craft incremental improvements as general rules or few-shot examples to the prompts. Improvements are measured using custom metrics, and in our case, we employ classification accuracy.

Prompt optimization yields significant performance gains even with as few as 150 examples. This efficiency allowed us to experiment with two strategies on the
Llama3-Train
dataset when optimizing prompts: (1) using all available annotations per post-response pair (four to five instances), and (2) using only annotations for specific pedagogical levels (one instance per pair).

5.2
Open-Weight Classifiers

To alleviate potential privacy concerns when using API-based frontier LLMs to classify forum posts, we explore open-weight models hosted locally. Unfortunately, our human-annotated dataset is not sufficient to fine-tune these models. Therefore, we perform data synthesis using
Llama3-Train
.

We use
GPT-4.1-nano
to generate pairs of student forum posts and VTA responses, each aligned with a specific pedagogical level score. To ensure class distribution balance, we aim to have 300 examples for every score within each pedagogical level. Rubrics are used to define desired behavioral characteristics of VTA responses for each level-score combination. We include both the requirements and sampled in-context examples for generation (detailed description in Appendix
E
).

The above process yields approximately 1200 total examples (synthetic and non-synthetic) per pedagogical level. We split these examples into training and validation sets using an 85:15 ratio.

We fine-tune
Qwen3-4B
,
Qwen3-8B

Yang et al. 2025
, and
ModernBERT-base

Warner et al. 2024
on synthetic data (detailed in Appendix
F
), training
one classifier per pedagogical level
due to the limited generalization of smaller models. Inputs include (1) the student forum post, (2) the VTA response, and (3) the level-specific rubric. Qwen models directly output their ratings, while ModernBERT performs multi-class classification.

Results

6.1
Human Annotation

To examine whether providing contextual information influences the pedagogical quality of generated responses from
Llama-3-70B-Instruct
, we compare expert annotation scores between the two conditions. Figure
2
records the score differences.

Disciplinary understanding (Level 2) experiences the steepest decline with context
, with 59.7% of responses decreasing by 1 or 2 points.
Similar patterns are observed in the levels of higher-order thinking (Level 3) and metacognitive awareness (Level 4)
, which show declines of 37% and 41%, respectively. We observe that context-free responses focus more on deep engagement with the original post, extending its ideas effectively. In contrast, adding forum-level context often shifts
Llama-3-70B-Instruct
’s focus to linking similar posts, resulting in broader but more superficial instructional guidance.

The level of clarification of misunderstandings (Level 1) appears to be the least affected by the inclusion of context.
Qualitative analysis suggests that context has little impact on how well a response addresses confusion. Instead, the effectiveness of clarification seems to be more dependent on the clarity of the original question.

7
Discussion

By analyzing VTA responses using our pedagogical framework and automating assessment with LLMs, we highlight key insights into the development and evaluation of VTAs in ALEs.

We inspect the consequences of incorporating peer forum posts to aid collaborative knowledge formation, under a simple VTA formulation with no course-specific materials involved. Our expert annotations suggest that including forum-level contexts can be detrimental to VTA performance in other pedagogical levels. This can be a result of the “lost in the middle” phenomenon
Liu et al. 2023
, where language models fixate on information presented at the beginning or the end of a long input. Since Level 5 appears at the end of our prompt, models may over-prioritize it. This underscores the value of our pedagogical framework, which makes such degradations in performance detectable. This result pertains only to
Llama-3-70B-Instruct
, although it is plausible that other LLMs would exhibit similar patterns.

Prior work
Maurya et al. 2024
argues that LLMs may be unreliable annotators for pedagogical assessment. We explore this claim in the context of assessing VTAs in ALEs, and our findings largely support this concern. While prompt optimization and supervised fine-tuning on synthetic data successfully enhance accuracy, the models we examine still struggle with higher-level pedagogical evaluations. This could reflect a broader lack of pedagogical awareness during the training of state-of-the-art models
Macina et al. 2023
;
Jurenka et al. 2024
, which impacts both prompt-based classification and data synthesis.

Another limitation is that all our training data (and in-context examples for data generation) come from a single LLM, which might restrict generalization. Still, the fine-tuned classifiers match and outperform frontier LLM performance on
Llama3-MOOC
, suggesting strong potential. We expect that introducing more diverse VTA response styles to our training data will improve classifier robustness. We leave expanding expert annotations on a wider range of VTA outputs as future work.

One interesting finding is that Markdown formatting from VTA responses affects classifier performance. We hypothesize that removing Markdown formatting brings the VTA responses closer to the output distribution of
Llama-3-70B-Instruct
for the open-weight classifiers, while mitigating self-selection biases in the prompt-based classifiers
Panickssery et al. 2024
. This is notable because it emphasizes a critical difference between human and model evaluators; humans are capable of disentangling stylistic features and pedagogical content, whereas current LLMs experience challenges.

8
Conclusion

We introduce the first evaluation framework for the pedagogical capabilities of teaching assistants in asynchronous online forums. Upon expert labeling of forum VTA responses using our rubric, we utilize these annotations to build and test automatic classifiers using language models. Our results indicate that while certain approaches can benefit classifier performance, language models continue to face challenges with higher-level, nuanced classifications and generalization across response types. Our novel framework paves the way for rigorous comparisons between different VTA systems, and we caution researchers when using LLMs for complex pedagogical evaluations.

Limitations

Our pedagogical evaluation framework is grounded in educational theory, but its practical utility for instructors remains unclear. Future work will include usability studies with university instructors to better validate our framework’s relevance and effectiveness in the real world.

Although the number of annotations is sufficient for prompt optimization in this work, a larger annotated dataset would always be desirable, as it can strengthen model training and evaluation. As an initial exploration into pedagogical assessment of VTAs, our work provides a solid foundation for future researchers in this domain. We encourage researchers to reuse our annotation framework to construct more training data for classifiers. The number of annotators was also limited; however, we achieved high inter-rater reliability. Additionally, recruiting expert annotators remains challenging in this domain, and we also do not provide financial compensation to our annotators due to budgetary concerns. Future work can look into expanding our annotator pool to enable annotating more examples.

This work focuses on simple VTA formulations that rely on models’ internal knowledge to answer academic questions, as we did not have access to vetted course materials. In contrast, existing VTAs often use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to obtain instructor-approved reference content. Studying such systems would offer valuable comparisons, but these implementations are typically not publicly available.

Lastly, our framework evaluates the pedagogical quality of VTA responses, not their factual accuracy or potential for hallucination. However, accurate and reliable content is critical in educational contexts. To be suitable for deployment, our pedagogical framework must be paired with methods that assess and promote response accuracy.

Ethical Considerations

Asynchronous online forum posts made by students contain sensitive personal information such as their names and their grades (e.g. "I was wondering why I just got a B on this assignment"). Our suite of classifiers is designed to have access to the original student forum posts to enhance classification accuracy. While we redacted private information when developing our classifiers, it is important to recognize and minimize the potential risks of data leakage if our classifiers are adopted for real-world use. The most capable models we tested are closed-source and are only available through API access. Companies can train on the user data, and there can be attackers hacking into the data-hosting servers and gaining access to sensitive student data. In order to protect student privacy, future work should prioritize using open-source models securely hosted on local infrastructure.

Annotator Demographic Information

Both annotators are Asian females with an average age of 23. They are both masters students in the field of education enrolled in Teachers College.

Appendix E
Synthetic Data Details

Our detailed process for generating synthetic data is as follows:

1.

Extract the description for each level-rating combination from the standardized rubric.
For example, the description for Level 3’s “Not Present (0)” would be “No effort is made to promote higher-order thinking.”

2.

Repeatedly sample in-context examples for data synthesis.
To avoid data contamination, we sample exclusively from annotations in
Llama3-Train
. For every call to
GPT-4.1-nano
, we use five examples to produce three new post-response pairs.

Furthermore, we prioritize sampling from the same level-rating combination if possible to promote synthetic data quality: if the number of post-response pairs in a specific combination exceeds our generation needs, we restrict sampling to that subset; otherwise, we sample from all post-response pairs.

3.

Synthesize new post-response pairs from in-context examples and VTA response requirements.
We include the description from the first step as the requirement for the VTA response in the newly synthesized post-response pair. For every level-rating combination, we synthesize up to 300 instances; if this level-rating combination already has more than 300 instances, no additional examples are created.

Appendix F
Model Training Details

All models are trained for 10 epochs on an A100 GPU (80 GB GPU memory) with an initial learning rate of
2e\text{-}5
, and the best checkpoints are selected using validation loss. We set the maximum token limit for ModernBERT to be 8192.

See the prompts used to perform supervised fine-tuning for Qwen models in Appendix
G
.
