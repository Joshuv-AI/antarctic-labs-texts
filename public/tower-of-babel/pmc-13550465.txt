Multi-population GWAS meta-analysis identifies bladder cancer susceptibility loci and highlights genetic regulation of smoking-related risk

ABSTRACT
Bladder cancer is the ninth most common cancer worldwide, caused by genetic and environmental risk factors. Here, we report the findings of a multi-population meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies, including 32,470 individuals with and 1,753,462 without bladder cancer. We identify 70 independent risk loci, of which 43 are novel. Using a 70-marker polygenic risk score (HR = 1.63 per standard deviation), we increase the area under the curve from 0.71 (baseline risk model) to 0.75. Integrative analyses reveal the enrichment of the associated variants within accessible chromatin regions, and of the prioritized genes within pathways for xenobiotic metabolism and smoking behavior. Specifically, we show that the 15q25.1 variant rs71581744-ACCCC/A co-localizes with tissue-specific CHRNA3 expression, modulates mRNA stability, and associates with risk of muscle-invasive bladder cancer among current smokers. Together, these findings substantially expand the known genetic architecture of bladder cancer risk and highlight the germline regulation of smoking behavior as a mechanism driving bladder cancer susceptibility.

Introduction

Urinary bladder cancer (BC) is the ninth most common malignancy globally and the second most common genitourinary cancer1. Known risk factors for BC include cigarette smoking2, family history, male sex, European ancestry, and exposure to certain occupational and environmental carcinogens3–7. To date, prior genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified 27 independent BC susceptibility loci8–18. Collectively, 24 of these loci are estimated to account for approximately 14.8% of BC familial risk16. In addition, the vast majority of BC GWAS have been performed among individuals with genetic similarity to European reference populations (EUR)8–16, except for two studies performed in East Asian (EAS) populations19,20.

Here, we report findings from a large-scale multi-population GWAS meta-analysis that integrates prior BC GWAS16 with novel data from multiple large multi-center studies. Our analysis, which includes 32,470 individuals with BC and 1,753,462 without BC from populations genetically similar to EUR, EAS, and African (AFR) reference populations, is the largest study of BC susceptibility to date. The increased sample size and participant diversity enabled us to more than double the number of BC susceptibility variants, bringing the total to 70 independent loci. These results, along with subsequent genetic, bioinformatic, and functional analyses, provide an expanded view of the inherited risk of BC, emphasizing an important role of the genetic regulation of smoking behavior in BC risk.

Results

GWAS meta-analysis identifies 70 independent BC susceptibility loci

We performed a GWAS meta-analysis, integrating summary statistics from previously published BC susceptibility GWAS16 with new datasets from the Million Veteran Program (MVP)21, UK Biobank (UKB)22, FinnGen23, eMERGE Network24, Biobank Japan25, and the University of Tokyo19 (Supplementary Data 1). The resulting dataset comprised 32,470 individuals with BC and 1,753,462 without BC—more than doubling the effective sample size of the previous largest GWAS (neff = 126,379 versus 51,756) (Supplementary Data 1). In addition to the increased power provided by the new datasets, they also increased population diversity by including AFR (n = 1055, 3.2%) and EAS (n = 1259, 3.9%) individuals with BC.

Multi-population and population-specific (EUR and EAS) meta-analyses were performed using both a fixed-effects, inverse-variance weighted model (METAL)26 and MR-MEGA27, a metaregression-based approach that accounts for ancestry and study heterogeneity. METAL yielded 60 genome-wide significant loci (GWS; p < 5 × 10−8), including the 27 previously reported in EUR16,17(Supplementary Data 2). Of the 33 newly detected loci, 29 were significant in both European and multi-population analyses, while four (chr1p36.22-MIR34A, chr1q32.1-INAVA, 3p14.3-ERC2, and chr5q22.2-MCC) achieved significance only in multi-population analyses (Table 1 and Supplementary Data 3). Through conditional analyses, we identified nine additional independent signals: five in established BC susceptibility regions (chr5p15.33-TERT/CLPTM1L, [n = 2], chr6p22.3-CASC15 [n = 3]) and four in novel regions (chr1p31.3-GADD45 [n = 1], chr20p12.3-BMP2 [n = 1], and chr5q35.2-MSX2 [n = 2]) (Supplementary Data 4). In total, we report 70 independent GWS BC loci, of which 43 have not been previously reported in the literature (Table 1, Supplementary Data 3, Fig. 1, Supplementary Figs. No GWS variants were identified in EAS analysis, but the multi-population MR-MEGA27 analysis identified one additional locus (rs12958940, chr18q22.1-CDH7), with a larger effect size in EAS (OR = 1.40, p = 1.15 × 10−7, heterogeneity due to ancestry, phet = 1.46 × 10−7, Supplementary Data 3). Two previously reported loci from a Chinese20 and a Japanese study19 were not replicated in our EAS dataset (Supplementary Data 2).

Due to data restrictions, we were unable to perform population-specific GWAS in AFR, but we provide publicly available results for the 70 GWAS signals for comparison (Supplementary Data 3). Many GWAS signals are shared across ancestries, with comparable effect sizes and directions, but do not reach GWS in the smaller non-European datasets.

Finally, we conducted fine-mapping for each locus using the SUm of the SIngle Effects (SuSiE) model28, followed by the construction of credible sets (CS) with a 95% probability of including putative causal variants. We identified 1,162 unique CS variants (median 11.75, range 1-70 variants per CS), including five common missense/nonsense variants (Supplementary Data 5). Most loci included a single CS, whereas seven harbored two to four independent CS, suggesting the presence of several candidate variants per region (Supplementary Data 5).

Heritability and polygenicity of BC

To estimate the degree of susceptibility captured in our analysis, we calculated the genetic variance explained by the set of 70 independent susceptibility leads, corresponding to heritability on the frailty scale under a polygenic log-additive model. In this framework, the heritability attributable to the current set of 70 BC susceptibility leads accounted for 23.1% of the additive variance. To relate these findings to familial aggregation, we also estimated the proportion of familial BC risk explained by the 70 leads, assuming a familial relative risk of approximately 1.8. Under this model, the expanded set of 70 signals explains approximately 16.9% of familial risk, compared with 14.8% based on the earlier 24-marker set16.

Next, we constructed a polygenic risk score using the 70 BC signals (PRS-70) and tested its performance in an independent EUR set comprising 756 individuals with incident BC and 59,550 individuals without BC from the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial (PLCO)31 (Supplementary Data 7). The PRS was computed as a weighted sum of risk alleles using SNP effect sizes and standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Using a Cox model with terms for age, sex, smoking, and 10 genotyping array-specific principal components, estimates were combined using a random-effects meta-analysis. Stratified analyses and association with clinical and molecular subgroups

To better understand the biological and clinical relevance of BC susceptibility variants, we performed a series of exploratory stratified analyses in a GWAS subset with available individual-level data (Supplementary Data 9). First, we sought evidence of effect modification by sex, as the incidence of BC is greater in men than in women6. By conducting sex-stratified analysis, we identified a stronger effect in women (n = 7330) than in men (n = 16,771) for several novel signals, including rs12958940 (chr18q22.1-CDH7, p-int = 3.46 × 10−3) and rs2145699 (20q13.31-TFAP2C, p-int = 4.83 × 10−2, Supplementary Data 9) and the known signal rs2896518 (chr4p16.3-TACC3/FGFR3)16.

We next stratified on smoking history, since it is the most common modifiable BC risk factor. Using data from 5970 never and 15,605 ever-smokers, we detected two novel signals with evidence of multiplicative interaction with smoking (Supplementary Data 9). For rs6556156 (chr5q35.2-MSX2), we estimated a stronger effect in ever-smokers (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.10-1.22) than in never-smokers (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.89-1.07), p-int = 1.25 × 10−3), while rs112204664 (chr6p22.3-CASC15) showed a stronger effect in never-smokers (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.14-1.44) than in ever-smokers (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 0.98-1.25, p-int = 2.55 × 10−2).

Clinically, BC is divided into two subgroups, non-muscle invasive (NMIBC, stage < pT2 [high (HG) and low grade (LG)]) and muscle invasive (MIBC, stage = pT2 + [all HG]) disease, each with distinct prognosis and treatment recommendations. Muscle invasion is the single most significant predictor of outcome and, as such, we sought to evaluate if variants were differentially associated with risk of these clinical subgroups. We performed analyses using data from ~ 7000 participants with annotated tumor stage/grade data. This subset mirrored the generally observed population prevalence, with ~ 80% of tumors being NMIBC and 20% MIBC16. Multiple variants had stronger effects either with LG and/or NMIBC, including chr5q21.1-SEMA6A (p-hetLG = 5.28 × 10−3), chr5p15.33-CLPTM1L (p-hetNMIBC = 3.31 × 10−2, p-hetLG = 1.74 × 10−2), and chr1p36.22-MIR34A (p-hetNMIBC = 2.90 × 10−2) (Supplementary Data 9). Variants with stronger effects in MIBC included 10q22.1-COL13A1 (p-hetT3+ = 1.26 × 10−2), 13q32.3-DOCK9 (p-hetMIBC = 4.63 × 10−2), while chr19q13.33-FUT2 had a stronger effect in HG tumors (p-hetHG = 7.23 × 10−3).

In addition to the clinical subgroups, molecular subtypes have been described for NMIBC32,33 and MIBC34–37, each with distinct clinical and molecular features. We evaluated heterogeneity for associations based on consensus subtypes of MIBC (TCGA-BLCA, n = 404)37. Among the 70 variants, we observed heterogeneity for rs2145699-A (chr20q13.31-TFAP2C, p = 0.017) and rs67080241-T (chr9q34.13-RAPGEF1, p = 0.0098), with the risk alleles of both being less frequent among the Luminal Papillary tumors (Supplementary Data 10). In addition, we evaluated distributions of risk alleles between tumors with and without somatic loss of chr9p21.3 (CDKN2A/B and MTAP), the most frequent focal deletion in BC and a marker of genome instability36,38. The strongest novel association was seen with the rs2052659-A allele (chr8q13.3-MSC), which was enriched among tumors with chr9p21.3 loss (p = 1.8 × 10−3, Supplementary Data 10). The results of these exploratory analyses should help generate hypotheses for further investigations of the underlying biological mechanisms.

Pleiotropy and cross-trait associations

Germline variants are often associated with multiple traits, providing insight into shared disease mechanisms. Systematic evaluation in the UKB revealed that 36 of 70 BC susceptibility loci were significantly associated with 82 phenotypes other than BC (at p < 1 × 10−5, based on Benjamini–Hochberg FDR p-value = 0.05 corresponding to a nominal p-value = 4.40 × 10−4, Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Data 11). Fifteen loci were pleiotropic for three or more traits, including rs10936599 (chr3q26.2-MYNN, n = 6), rs414965 (chr5p15.33-TERT/CLPTM1L, n = 8), rs2242652 (chr5p15.33-TERT/CLPTM1L, n = 7), rs7173514 (chr15q25.1-CHRNA5, n = 5), and rs602662 (chr19q13.33-FUT2, n = 7). Notably, rs62237617-T (chr22q11.2-TTC28, 0.047% frequency, OR = 1.85, p = 4.75 × 10−9), was also associated with breast cancer (OR = 1.6, p = 2 × 10−22). The rs62237617-T association is likely due to the allele being in complete LD (r2 = 1.0) with the pathogenic frameshift variant rs1555913934-del (1100delC, Thr367fs) within CHEK2, a known multiorgan cancer gene39.

In addition, the lead variants at several risk loci had inverse effect directions compared to BC. For example, the rs2242652-G allele16,40 (chr5p15.33-TERT) was associated with increased risk of BC (OR, 95%CI = 1.20, 1.17-1.23), but with decreased risk of brain cancer (OR, 95%CI = 0.69, 0.59-0.81). Similarly, the rs1414253-A allele (chr9p21.3-MTAP/CDKN2A) was associated with increased risk of BC (OR, 95%CI = 1.07, 1.05-1.08) but decreased risk of melanoma (OR, 95%CI = 0.83, 0.78-0.87) (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Data 11). These results highlight tissue-specific pleiotropy of the BC risk alleles.

Association with gene and protein expression

Because variants located in cis-regulatory regions may alter gene expression, we assessed the 70 BC signals as expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) in MIBC (TCGA-BLCA, n = 404)36 and NMIBC (UROMOL, n = 359)33. Considering all genes within the corresponding topologically associating domains (TADs), we detected at least one significant eQTL for 42 loci in TCGA and 36 loci in UROMOL (Supplementary Data 12). In total, 14 eQTLs were shared between UROMOL (NMIBC) and TCGA (MIBC), Supplementary Data 12. Among novel signals, the shared eQTLs included rs9818576 (3p14.3-WNT5A), rs12648651 (4q21.21-BMP3), rs10077835 (chr5q23.1-SEMA6A), rs11738059 (chr5q32-ABLIM3), rs67080241 (chr9q34.13-RAPGEF1), rs1891374 (chr10q25.2-MXI1), rs7937265 (chr11p15.5-TOLLIP), rs602662 (chr19q13.33-RASIP1/RCN3), and rs62185668 (20p12.2-SNAP25), Supplementary Data 12.

We also evaluated the 70 BC loci for evidence of influencing circulating protein levels via quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) using data from the UKB–Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP)41, which quantified 2940 plasma proteins in 34,557 individuals of European ancestry. Five BC signals were identified as significant cis-pQTLs for 15 proteins (Supplementary Data 13). Of the significant pQTL genes, several were concordant with eQTL signals (GSTM1/GSTM4, PSCA, LY6D, TACC3, and LSP1) either in bladder tumors or whole blood (Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project, Supplementary Data 13).

Gene set and pathway analyses

By implementing a Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA) analysis42 to combine multiple proximal variants and Functional-element MAGMA (F-MAGMA) to integrate cell type-specific regulatory elements, we identified 94 candidate genes related to the BC risk alleles (Supplementary Data 14). In addition, a transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) in normal tissue (GTEx) and MIBC (TCGA) identified 103 and 40 genes, respectively, with 23 genes overlapping in the two analyses (Supplementary Data 15). Finally, a proteome-wide association study (PWAS) identified 10 additional BC-associated proteins from the INTERVAL study43 and 12 proteins from the ARIC study44 (Supplementary Data 16). Overall, we nominated 191 genes as potentially significant for BC risk (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Supplementary Data 17).

In order to provide a context for the candidate gene list, the 191 genes were included in a gene ontology/pathway analysis. We observed enrichment across 29 gene ontology (GO) terms and 21 pathways (FDR < 0.05, Supplementary Data 17). Among the GO terms and pathways, three main themes emerged: (1) regulation of metabolic pathways, such as drug and xenobiotic metabolism, represented by the GSTM, UGT, and NAT gene families; (2) cancer and cell growth, represented by BMP2, TERT, and MYC; and (3) the cellular response to chemical stimuli, represented by LYNX1, PSCA, and the CHRNA gene family (Supplementary Data 17), all of which are related to known aspects of BC susceptibility, including response to cigarette smoking.

Identification of a BC risk locus within the smoking-related chr15q25.1 region

rs71581744-ACCCC/A at chr15q25.1 is associated with CHRNA3 expression and stability

The ~170 kb associated region at chr15q25.1 includes CHRNA3, CHRNA5, and CHRNB4, which encode three of the multiple subunits that form the pentameric neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR)50, regulating response to nicotine. Expression of these subunits is variable and tissue-specific: CHRNA3 is enriched in colon and cerebellum, CHRNB4 is predominantly testis-specific, while CHRNA5 is expressed nearly ubiquitously but at low levels (Supplementary Figs. S6, S7).

Since rs7173514 is located ~ 8 kb upstream of CHRNA5, we asked whether this variant could exert its functional effect by regulating expression at this locus. Despite many strong eQTLs detected for CHRNA3 and CHRNA5 in GTEx, the lead eQTLs were not associated with BC risk (Supplementary Data 21). However, as nAChRs are multi-subunit complexes, increased expression of only one of these subunits might not be sufficient to affect response to nicotine or cancer risk. Using GTEx data, we observed significant colocalization (PP.H4 ≥ 80%) between risk alleles of both rs7173514 and rs71581744 and eQTLs for CHRNA3, but only in the cerebellum (p = 8.60 × 10−7, β = −0.15, Supplementary Figs. S8, S9 and Supplementary Data 21). This association was also observed in MetaBrain datasets51, with risk alleles of rs71581744 and its linked variants being associated with decreased CHRNA3 expression in the cerebellum but not in other brain areas (Supplementary Data 21 and Supplementary Fig. S10). This, together with single-nuclei RNA-seq data from the human brain, led us to hypothesize that the CHRNA3 expression pattern observed in the cerebellum might represent inhibitory neuronal cells that were not abundant in our bulk-tissue expression analysis (Supplementary Fig. S11). One possible explanation for this regulatory effect may be related to the location of rs71581744, which is in the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of the main protein-coding isoform of CHRNA3, suggesting a role in post-transcriptional regulation, through RNA stability/degradation or protein translation (Fig. 5)52,53. In silico allele-specific analysis using miRDB54 did not identify binding sites for microRNAs. However, analysis with RBPmap55 of binding sites for RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) predicted that the non-risk rs71581744-ACCCC, but not the risk rs71581744-A allele, introduces a C-rich element with potential binding sites for multiple poly(C)-binding proteins (PCBPs) (Supplementary Fig. S12). To assess the functional impact of rs71581744 on mRNA stability, we performed an in vitro analysis, in which rs71581744 and two neighboring SNPs (rs578776 and rs660652) were individually cloned into the 3’UTR of the psiCHECK-2 luciferase reporter (Fig. 5b). The reporter was then transfected into cells with variable CHRNA3 expression (high-expressing neuronal cell line SH-SY5Y [DepMap, TPM = 105.2], and five low-expressing cell lines, DepMap, TPM = 0.11-1.17). Interestingly, rs71581744-A risk allele increased mRNA stability and protein abundance of the reporter only in cell lines with low CHRNA3 expression but not in a highly expressing neuronal cell line SH-SY5Y (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. S13). Notably, CHRNA3 expression was significantly decreased in the presence of the rs71581744-A risk allele or its proxy alleles (rs12910984-A or rs578776-G) only in the cerebellum, both in the GTEx and MetaBrain datasets, whereas it was significantly increased in other tissues and brain regions with low CHRNA3 expression (Supplementary Data 21).

Taken together, these findings suggest that the rs71581744-A risk allele may contribute to the stabilization and/or increased translation of CHRNA3 transcripts broadly, except in certain neuronal cell types. The decreased CHRNA3 expression associated with the rs71581744-A risk allele, observed uniquely in the cerebellum (which may serve as a proxy for other adjacent unexamined midbrain regions), might be related to cell-type-specific expression patterns of RBPs that preferentially bind and stabilize transcripts with specific rs71581744 alleles. For example, these RBPs may be expressed at lower levels in specific neurons than in other cell or tissue types, or their expression may be insufficient to stabilize the larger number of CHRNA3 transcripts produced in cells with very high CHRNA3 expression. Overall, our experimental data, together with GTEx and MetaBrain expression profiles, support a model in which the rs71581744-A risk allele modulates CHRNA3 expression by affecting mRNA stability in a cell-type-specific manner. Reduced abundance of the α3-nAChR in specific brain regions could lead to an elevated risk of heavy and persistent smoking and smoking-related outcomes, such as BC.

Discussion

We conducted the largest GWAS meta-analysis to date and identified 70 independent BC susceptibility loci across three populations, including 43 signals that were not previously reported in the literature. The 70-marker PRS improved estimates of BC heritability (23.1%) and model discrimination. Through comprehensive analyses of risk variants, we identified novel gene-environment interactions and emphasized the central role of smoking in BC pathogenesis. Further, our genetic, bioinformatic, and functional analyses implicate downregulation of CHRNA3 expression as a key mechanism in neuronal control of smoking behavior and BC risk.

In terms of risk prediction, we previously demonstrated the potential for using a 24-marker PRS and smoking status for targeted risk stratification16. The updated PRS based on all current BC leads (PRS-70) was validated in an independent dataset and substantially improved risk discrimination, increasing the AUC from 0.71 (with age, sex, and smoking status alone) to 0.75 (including PRS). With growing adoption of liquid biopsy and development of urine-based screening, the ability to stratify individuals within high-risk groups (e.g., smokers, with occupational exposures, or hematuria) who would benefit from early/regular screening is critical. The incorporation of these new loci and broader risk factors into predictive models can provide a more granular view of risk and identify individuals for early targeted non-invasive screening.

Our current study more than doubled the effective sample size of the prior BC GWAS, allowing for the detection of less common risk variants. Of note, we uncovered an association for rs62237617 (0.47% frequency, OR = 1.85), which is completely linked with the pathogenic CHEK2 frameshift variant (rs1555913934-del) overrepresented in familial BC59 and in breast cancer60. Mechanistically, the frameshift variant in CHEK2, which is a regulator of cell cycle progression, abolishes the protein’s kinase activity and results in DNA repair defects61.

Another novel signal with potential clinical interest is the rs2145699-A allele at chr20q13.31. The rs2145699-A risk allele showed a significantly stronger association with BC risk in women than in men. Our eQTL findings from NMIBC showed an association between rs2145699-A and increased expression of TFAP2C, which encodes a transcription factor that promotes squamous differentiation, more aggressive disease, and cisplatin-resistant BC62. This is in line with our findings that patients with the rs2145699-A allele are more likely to develop Basal/Squamous than Luminal Papillary tumors, with significantly different TFAP2C expression between these groups (Supplementary Fig. S14). Taken together, this could, in part, explain why women are more likely to be diagnosed with squamous metaplasia63 and present with more advanced disease than men.

Multiple analyses, including pathway and stratified analyses, add to the evidence and potential mechanisms by which smoking and germline variants influence BC risk. Pathway analysis implicated impaired detoxification (xenobiotic metabolism) and smoking behavior (neuronal circuitry influencing intensity/cessation) as key players in BC susceptibility as a result of increasing bladder exposure to tobacco-associated carcinogens.

The shared susceptibility to smoking and BC risk is further emphasized through our analysis of the smoking-related region at chr15q25.1. Multiple prior GWAS reported association signals at chr15q25.1 with both smoking behavior and lung cancer47,64. This region has also been studied in relation to BC risk in candidate gene analyses in the absence of GWS65. The increased sample size in the current study enabled us to formally link this region with BC risk, exclusively in smokers. Using detailed data from GWAS on smoking behavior45, we mapped the BC signal to two independent LD blocks of variants – one more strongly associated with smoking intensity, explained by the known functional variant rs16969968 (D398N) in CHRNA5, and the other more strongly associated with smoking cessation, accounted for by the newly described rs71581744-ACCCC/A in CHRNA3.

In EUR populations, several variants linked with rs71581744 (r2 ~ 0.80, including rs578776 and rs518425) have been reported as lead markers for smoking intensity49 and persistent smoking despite cessation efforts, such as nicotine replacement therapy66. Notably, in EAS populations, where the rs16969968-A allele is uncommon ( < 3%), rs71581744-A (but not rs578776 or rs518425) was strongly linked with previously reported GWAS lead variants for smoking intensity (rs938682, r2 = 0.98, and rs13329271, r2 = 0.85, Supplementary Data 20)44,46. In AFR individuals, no chr15q25.1 variants reached GWS for smoking intensity or cessation45, but rs71581744 showed a stronger association with smoking intensity than rs16969968-A, which is uncommon in this population ( < 3%, Supplementary Data 20), and can also be excluded from the CHRNA5 transcript due to smoking-induced alternative splicing67. Thus, our novel BC-associated signal, rs71581744, offers critical insights into prior smoking-related genetic associations across multiple populations.

Functionally, our results suggest that the rs71581744-A risk allele protects the CHRNA3 transcript from degradation in tissues with low endogenous CHRNA3 expression; however, analysis in the cerebellum, which has high endogenous CHRNA3 expression, demonstrated the inverse: the risk allele is associated with decreased expression. These results support the critical role of decreased CHRNA3 expression in specific neuronal cell types, which we attribute to the functional effect of the rs71581744-A allele on mRNA stability, as demonstrated in luciferase reporter assays across multiple cell lines.

Our results are in line with prior animal data showing that reduced Chrna3 expression/function diminishes nicotine aversion and enhances nicotine self-administration68,69. However, expression patterns in the cerebellum are likely representative of several midbrain regions. One of these regions, the pons, includes the pontine micturition center (PMC, Barrington's nucleus), which is important for urination control70–72 (Fig. 6). Chrna3 knockout mice exhibit severe bladder dysfunction and typically die within 3 weeks postnatally73. Similarly, rare human CHRNA3 mutations and CHRNA3 expression deficiency are associated with persistent urinary tract abnormalities74,75. Therefore, in addition to smoking-related behaviors, chr15q25.1 variants may also directly impact bladder physiology. Collectively, these mechanisms potentially contribute to BC risk through several independent but related physiologic pathways, including neural reward/aversion circuits and genitourinary function.

From a clinical perspective, we leveraged detailed smoking information from a subset of EUR individuals and identified significant subtype heterogeneity in BC risk in current smokers based on rs71581744-A alone and in combination with rs16969968-A (haplotype combination: ORNMIBC = 1.21, ORMIBC = 1.67, Supplementary Data 25), corroborating previous evidence58 of a greater risk of MIBC in current smokers (ORNMIBC = 4.00, ORMIBC = 6.20) (Supplementary Data 22). Although more work is warranted, our results showed that the risk of MIBC was significantly higher among current smokers, providing additional support for smoking cessation as a key tool for the prevention of this subtype, which has decreased overall survival.

Although our study is the largest and most diverse to date, it still underrepresents non-European populations. While inclusion of EAS (n = 1259) and AFR (n = 1055) participants with BC enabled the discovery of five loci that were not significant in EUR populations alone, we were still not sufficiently powered for robust EAS and AFR-specific analyses. Likewise, the lack of individual-level data for many participants (e.g., smoking history, known exposures, tumor characteristics) limited more detailed stratified analyses. The use of European-derived LD reference panels may differ from population-specific LD patterns in non-European populations, potentially affecting results from multi-ancestry analyses. Additional heterogeneity may arise from differences in imputation reference panels used by individual studies contributing to the GWAS meta-analysis. As research continues to diversify, further multi-population analyses, integrated with comprehensive demographic and clinical annotation, will be key to identifying both shared and unique BC risk factors.

Taken together, our data underscores the complex relationship between environmental exposures and germline genetics influencing both smoking behavior and cancer susceptibility.

Methods

Ethics

All aspects of study design and analyses comply with the relevant regulations governing the use of human data. Data use approval was obtained for all datasets in accordance with their study-specific regulations governing controlled data access, including: UK Biobank (application #47618), NCI (dbGAP: phs003342.v1.p1), MVP (dbGAP: phs002453), eMERGE network (dbGAP: phs000888.v1.p1), FinnGen (FINBB), Biobank Japan (NBDC Human Database: JGAS000114), UROMOL (EBI/CRG: EGAS00001004693 and EGAS00001004862), TCGA (dbGAP: phs000178.v11.p8).

Study populations

The individuals were assigned to specific population subgroups (referred to as EUR, AFR or EAS) based on their inferred genetic similarity to the reference populations of the 1000 Genomes (1KG) project. Unless specified, linkage disequilibrium values (LD, r2) are provided based on individuals of the 1KG European populations (1KG-Eur).

PLCO

The Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian (PLCO) Cancer Screening Trial is a population-based randomized study designed to assess cancer screening programs31. Between November 1993 and July 2001, the trial enrolled 155,000 participants aged 55–74 years, with data collection through December 31, 2009. Individuals with BC were defined based on ICD-O-2/3 codes C670-C679 and restricted to those diagnosed with the first primary cancer post-enrollment, with no prior history of other malignancies except non-melanoma skin cancer. The comparison group included individuals of European ancestry without BC with both genotype and phenotype data. At baseline, participants completed a questionnaire capturing their detailed smoking history, with smoking intensity measured as the number of cigarettes smoked per day, categorized into seven ordinal levels in 10-cigarette increments. All eligible individuals were genotyped across a range of Illumina genotyping platforms, and additional genotypes were imputed based on the TOPMed reference panel 5b through the Michigan Imputation Server.

Subset of GWAS EUR samples with individual-level data

Individual-level genotype and phenotype data were available for 9725 cases with histologically confirmed primary BC and 14,316 without BC from 21 studies, as described in Supplementary Data 1 of our previous GWAS16. Genotyping was conducted on seven genotyping arrays, with each sample processed on a single array only16. Phenotypic data included age, sex, and smoking history (including smoking status, intensity, and duration (years), as available), as well as BC stage and grade.

Meta-analysis and fine mapping

GWAS meta-analysis was performed by aggregating GWAS summary statistics from several studies using trans-ancestry meta-analysis in METAL26 and trans-ancestry meta-regression in MR-MEGA27. Prior to meta-analysis, study-level summary statistics were harmonized to ensure consistent genomic build, variant identifier, and effect allele alignment. Both methods incorporate tests for heterogeneous allelic effects across contributing studies and apply genomic control to correct for population stratification. Genomic control was applied at both the study and meta-analysis levels, and heterogeneity metrics were examined to assess between-study variability. The genomic inflation scores were within the acceptable range (Supplementary Data 1), confirming a negligible effect of population stratification.

Variants present in only a single study were excluded from the analysis. Risk loci were defined based on the presence of at least two genetic variants with associations below a genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10−8. Index variants for each region were selected based on the lowest association p-value. For each GWAS locus, conditional analysis was performed to detect any independent variants within a +/− 1 Mb window upstream/downstream of the index variant, using both GWAS-EUR and GWAS Multi-population summary statistics. Given that the ancestry of most participants was genetically similar to that of Europeans, we extracted LD from a randomly selected subset of 20,000 unrelated “white British” participants in the UK Biobank. Conditional analysis was performed using GCTA-COJO76 software (v1.94.1) with standard parameter settings. Signals were considered independent if they remained genome-wide significant after conditioning on the index variant, with an LD threshold of r2 < 0.8.

Fine-mapping was performed with the Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE)28, using GWAS-EUR summary statistics and LD profiles for individuals of European ancestry in the UK Biobank (as described above). This analysis included Bayesian stepwise regression to estimate the optimal number of causal signals (up to a maximum of ten) and to identify minimal sets of variants containing the “true” statistically causal variant with a posterior probability of at least 95%, generating one or more 95% credible sets per GWAS region. For three loci in which the lead GWAS signal was not captured within the 95% credible set, a more inclusive 99.9% credible set was generated.

Regulatory element functional overlap analysis

Functional enrichment in specific genomic annotations was analyzed using Functional element Overlap analysis of the Results of GWAS Experiments (FORGE2, version 2)29.

FORGE2 analysis was based on an input set of 4196 genome-wide significant (p < 5 × 10−8) variants with rs IDs from the total of 4346 genome-wide significant variants in the EUR set. Functional annotation was based on ATAC-seq data from 410 TCGA tumors (23 tumor types, including 10 primary bladder tumors; a median Fraction of Reads in Peaks (FriP) score was above 0.2 for all tumor types, indicating high quality of ATAC-seq data used for analysis30. FORGE2 analyses (https://github.com/charlesbreeze/FORGE2) were conducted using default settings, excluding LD filtering. A set of background markers was generated to match the input set based on minor allele frequency (MAF), distance to the transcription start site (TSS), and GC content using standard FORGE2 settings. Overlaps were computed for both the input and background sets, and p-values were calculated using a hypergeometric test comparing the observed overlap between GWAS variants and regulatory regions against the expected overlap from the matched background set. From these p-values, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR-adjusted q-values were computed to account for multiple testing across the 23 TCGA tumor types tested.

Gene and gene-set analyses

The aggregated associations of genome-wide significant GWAS loci were assessed using Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA)42 with standard settings and Functional MAGMA (F-MAGMA) analyses using https://analysistools.cancer.gov/gwas-target/ with default settings.

A transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) was performed using FUSION77 with publicly available precomputed predicted models from normal tissues (GTEx analysis V8) and transcriptome data from bladder tumors (TCGA-BLCA). European LD reference data from the 1000 Genomes Project (1KG-Eur) were used for TWAS analysis for each tissue. Since tissues often share similar cis-regulatory patterns, the power to detect candidate susceptibility genes through TWAS can be enhanced by performing a cross-tissue meta-analysis by leveraging similarities in cis-regulatory patterns across multiple tissues78. We performed a cross-tissue meta-analysis using a Cauchy combination test78,79 with 28,513 genes that had an expression imputation model in at least one of the 48 tissues in GTEx analysis V8.

A proteome-wide association study (PWAS) was performed with FUSION77 using publicly available precomputed protein-level models from INTERVAL43 (3153 proteins, n = 3301) and ARIC44 (1318 proteins, n = 7213). In both studies, plasma protein levels were measured with the SomaLogic platform, but differed in terms of sample characteristics, preprocessing, and normalization. The models were built on individuals of European ancestry using the 1KG-Eur LD reference. Unless otherwise indicated, the significance thresholds for multiple testing were determined by an FDR < 0.05.

Overall, stratified and subtype analyses in GWAS samples

In the subset of European studies with individual-level data, unconditional logistic regression was performed to calculate associations between the 70 leads and BC risk, assuming an additive genetic model, overall and stratified by smoking status (ever/never) and sex. Analyses were conducted separately for each genotyping array, adjusting for age and array-specific principal components. P-values for interaction were calculated by fitting models with an interaction term between each locus and the stratification variable. The results across arrays were combined using random-effects meta-analysis.

For analyses by tumor characteristics, polytomous logistic regression with controls as the common reference group was used to calculate associations between each locus and BC risk by stage (Ta, T1/CIS, T2, T3 + ), invasiveness (NMIBC, MIBC), and grade (low, high). P-values for heterogeneity by invasiveness and grade were calculated using logistic regression analyses of cases only. For tumor stage, p-values for heterogeneity were calculated via polytomous logistic regression on the pooled individual-level dataset rather than by array.

eQTL and pQTL analyses

Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analysis for BC-GWAS signals was conducted using data for 404 MIBC samples in TCGA-BLCA36 and 359 NMIBC samples in the UROMOL study33. The analysis was restricted to genes located within topologically associating domains (TADs) that included the associated SNPs. The genomic coordinates of TADs were obtained from high-throughput chromatin interaction (Hi-C) data for bladder tissue and mapped to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome (http://3dgenome.fsm.northwestern.edu).

In TCGA, 97.63% of germline genotypes from blood DNA (404 samples) were directly scored from whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data with BCFtools mpileup software (v1.20), with a minimum of 10 read counts/sample. Genotypes for samples with missing WGS data or < 10 read counts/sample were obtained from Affymetrix SNP6.0 arrays (15 out of 70 markers) or through imputation using the TOPMed Imputation Server (48 markers out of 70 markers). The concordance between the WGS and array genotypes ranged from 0.96 to 1.00 (Cohen’s kappa 0.98–1.00), whereas the concordance between the WGS and imputed genotypes ranged from 0.83 to 1.00 (Cohen’s kappa 0.72–1.00).

The TCGA-BLCA data included in the TCGA Pan-Cancer dataset were downloaded from public resources: RNA-sequencing gene expression profiles for the TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas dataset were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons80; clinical data were obtained from the TCGA Pan-Cancer Clinical Data Resource (TCGA-CDR)81. Information on tumor molecular subtypes and chr9q21.3 copy number status was obtained from corresponding publications37,82. In UROMOL33, germline genotypes from blood DNA were generated with Illumina’s OncoArray-500K BeadChip and scored with GenomeStudio software. Imputation was performed using the 1KG reference panel (Phase 3). Gene expression profiles were generated based on total RNA sequencing with Salmon quantification software (v1.4)83.

eQTL analyses were based on multivariable linear regression models, with each genetic variant coded as 0, 1, or 2 based on the number of risk alleles. All variants included in the analyses met high imputation quality criteria (R² > 0.8). Gene expression levels, measured in transcripts per million (TPM), were transformed into Z-scores using the rank-based inverse normal transformation approach in R. In TCGA, adjustments were made for age, sex, race, tumor stage and molecular subtypes. In UROMOL, adjustment was performed to account for sex and age. The eQTLs were considered significant based on p-values < 0.05 from both standard linear regression and permutation-based testing (with 5000 permutations), thereby controlling the type I error rate. This analysis was conducted using the lm and lmp functions in R. Because the analysis was performed separately for each predefined locus, with a single GWAS signal assessed per locus, permutation-based testing was used to derive region-specific empirical null distributions that account for dependence among tests within each locus, rather than applying a global multiple-testing correction across regions.

Protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) analysis was based on publicly available summary statistics reported by the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) for the discovery cohort of 2940 proteins (2922 unique) assayed in 34,557 individuals of European populations41. The significance threshold was set at 1.7 × 10−11, accounting for the GWAS threshold of p = 5 × 10-8 and the 2940 proteins tested. Summary statistics for each of the BC leads, and all the protein levels were extracted, reporting significant cis-pQTLs within +/− 5 Mb from the gene body. Because GSTM1 deletion was not genotyped, we used its LD proxy (rs36209093, r2 = 0.56, n = 345, 1KG-Eur) and reported proteins significantly associated with this SNP.

Pathway enrichment analysis

The genes identified as significant by GWAS, MAGMA, F-MAGMA, TWAS, and PWAS were combined (n = 191 genes), capturing candidate genes both within and outside GWAS loci and aggregating weaker effects. Of these, ENSEMBL IDs were available for 177 genes. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID)84, with 174 genes successfully mapped to the DAVID default reference genome annotation. Enrichment was assessed across the KEGG and REACTOME pathways, as well as Gene Ontology (GO) terms spanning Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component categories. The enrichment significance was indicated by an FDR p-value < 0.05.

PRS calculation in an independent prospective cohort

A subset of PLCO samples not used in the GWAS meta-analysis was used for independent PRS analysis. To retain high-effect genetic markers with the largest weights (ORs above the 75th percentile among the 70 BC leads), a stepwise filtering strategy was used. Individuals missing genotypes for these critical markers were excluded. Among the 70 markers evaluated, 40 (57.14%) had all the genotypes available in the dataset. The degree of missing data across the markers ranged from 0.0017% to 41.61%, with a median of 0.098%, a mean of 3.63%, and interquartile values of 0.022% (1st quartile) and 0.50% (3rd quartile). The final dataset included 756 BC cases and 59,550 individuals without BC of European ancestry. To assess the association between the PRS and BC risk, hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models separately by genotyping array, adjusting for sex, smoking status (when relevant), and 10 array-specific principal components with age as the time-scale metric defined as the interval between age at enrollment and age at diagnosis. These analyses were performed using the R package ‘survival’ (v3.6-4) and results by array were combined using random effects meta-analysis using the R package ‘metafor’ (v4.8-0). In addition, area under the curve (AUC) values were computed to evaluate model performance, using the R package ‘pROC’ (v1.18.5). DeLong’s test was used to compare the predictive performance of two models: one without the PRS and one with the PRS.

Heritability and familial relative risk of BC

We calculated the genetic variance explained by the 70 BC susceptibility leads, which corresponds to heritability on the frailty scale under a polygenic log-additive model85. For this, the genetic variance attributable to the included variants is determined by their number, effect sizes, and standard errors, and effect allele frequencies86. These were extracted from the EUR-specific BC meta-analyses to reduce bias introduced by population heterogeneity. We also calculated the familial risk of BC explained by the 70 susceptibility leads as in refs. 86,87, similarly using the EUR-specific BC-GWAS results. For this, we assumed a familial relative risk of BC (risk of developing BC given a family history of BC) of approximately 1.8.

Analyses in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project

Gene expression in all tissue samples was analyzed based on the bulk RNA-seq transcript-per-million (TPM) values and read counts downloaded from the GTEx analysis V10 Portal (https://gtexportal.org/home/downloads/). Genes with expression levels >0.1 TPM in at least 20% of the samples and coverage of a minimum of 6 RNA-seq reads in at least 20% of the samples were retained for normalization and downstream analyses. These expression values were normalized between samples using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method implemented in the R package ‘edgeR’ (v4.7.3), followed by an inverse normal transformation applied across samples for each gene. Covariates from GTEx analysis V10, including genotyping principal components and probabilistic estimation of expression residuals (PEER) factors, as well as age and smoking status, were included in the eQTL analysis. Genotype and phenotype data were used for GTEx analysis V9 and downloaded from dbGaP (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gap/) under accession numbers phg001796.v1 and phs000424.v9, respectively.

MetaBrain eQTL summary statistics

Publicly available summary statistics of MetaBrain eQTL were obtained from the MetaBrain portal (https://www.metabrain.nl), a large-scale eQTL meta-analysis of the human brain51. The dataset includes five brain regions from EUR individuals: cortex (n = 2683), basal ganglia (n = 208), hippocampus (n = 168), cerebellum (n = 492), and spinal cord (n = 108).

Association analyses within the chr15q25.1 region

Genotypes of variants located within the 250 kb region of chromosome 15 (GRCh19: chr15: 78,700,000-78,950,000) were obtained from a subset of GWAS EUR individuals (BC 9725 cases and 14,316 controls) and those without BC (n = 35,918) in the PLCO cohort31. Missing variants, including single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (INDELs), were imputed based on a multi-ancestry reference panel of 3202 individuals (high-coverage 30 × 1000 Genomes Project (GRCh38) dataset, International Genome Sample Resource (https://www.internationalgenome.org/data-portal/data-collection/30x-grch38). The genotypes were phased with SHAPEIT4 (v4.2.2) and imputed using IMPUTE5 (v1.2.0), both with default parameters. In both datasets, the rs71581744-ACCCC/A genotypes were recovered with high accuracy (imputation INFO score = 0.99). All other SNPs had INFO scores above 0.80.

Haplotypes of specified markers were constructed and analyzed using PLINK (v1.07)88. Omnibus tests for haplotype associations were conducted using haplotype replacement regression. The reference haplotype was defined as the combination of non-risk variants (such as rs16969968-G and rs71581744-ACCCC). Haplotypes with a combined frequency less than 1.0% were excluded.

Haplotype-based logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations with BC risk in GWAS EUR individuals and individuals without BC from the PLCO cohort with data on smoking intensity (cigarettes/day, groups 1–7, with 10-cigarette increments). The analyses were either stratified by smoking status (current, former, ever, and never smokers) or adjusted for relevant covariates as indicated. All analyses assumed an additive genetic model.

Colocalization analysis

Colocalization analysis was conducted to assess the probability that two traits share a common causal variant within a 250 kb window at the chr15q25.1 locus (GRCh38: chr15:78,407,658–78,657,658). GWAS-GWAS colocalization was conducted between summary statistics for BC-GWAS-EUR and two smoking-related phenotypes (smoking intensity and smoking cessation) from Saunders et al. (2022)45. GWAS-eQTL colocalization was carried out between summary statistics for BC-GWAS-EUR and precomputed cis-eQTL annotations from the GTEx V10, which included all variant–gene associations tested in each tissue, irrespective of statistical significance. These data were obtained from the GTEx portal (https://console.cloud.google.com/storage/browser/gtex-resources). All colocalization analyses were performed using the coloc.abf function from the R package ‘coloc’ (v5.2.3), which implements approximate Bayes factor computation based on p-values and minor allele frequencies89. Visualization of GWAS-GWAS and GWAS-eQTL colocalization was conducted using the R package ‘LocusCompareR’ (v1.0.0)90.

Analyses in the human protein atlas (HPA) project

Single-nuclei transcriptomic profiles from human brain samples were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA), version 24.0 (https://www.proteinatlas.org/about/download). The dataset comprised ~ 2.5 million nuclei91 from ~100 anatomical regions dissected from the brains of three donors91. Pre-calculated expression values, reported as normalized transcripts per million (nTPM), were downloaded and plotted. For visualization, only single-nucleus brain cells with expression > 1 nTPM were retained.

In silico prediction of post-transcriptional regulatory elements within the 3’UTR of CHRNA3

A 100-bp sequence within the 3’-UTR of CHRNA3 centered on rs71581744 was analyzed in the presence of each allele (A and ACCCC). MicroRNA binding sites were predicted using miRDB (https://mirdb.org/)54, and RNA-binding protein (RBP) binding sites were predicted using RBPmap (https://rbpmap.technion.ac.il/)55, both with default parameters for Homo sapiens.

In vitro functional assessment

The BC cell lines UMUC3 (CRL-1749) and 5637 (HTB-9), the lung cancer cell line A549 (CCL-185), the neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y (CRL-2266), and the glioma cell line U-118MG (HTB-15) were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA). The embryonic kidney cell line 293FT was purchased from Thermo Fisher. All the cells were cultured in their recommended media supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin, unless otherwise specified. The cell lines were routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza). If used for more than 6 months after purchase, cells were authenticated with the AmpFLSTR™ Identifiler Plus PCR Amplification Kit (Thermo Fisher).

Non-overlapping DNA fragments of the CHRNA3 3’UTR (150 bp, sense-strand) centered on rs71581744-ACCCC/A, rs578776-A/G, or rs660652-G/A were purchased as custom allele-specific oligos flanked with XhoI (5′) and NotI (3′) restriction sites and cloned into a pUCIDT (Amp) Golden Gate plasmid (IDT). The allele-specific inserts for all SNPs were subcloned downstream of the Renilla luciferase gene in the dual-luciferase reporter vector psiCHECK-2 (Promega). The plasmids were confirmed by Sanger sequencing, expanded, and purified with the EndoFree Plasmid Maxi Kit (Qiagen). To optimize transfection efficiency, cells were transiently transfected with the pmaxGFP control plasmid (Lonza) for 24 h using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher), achieving ~ 50% transfection efficiency across all cell lines except the SH-SY5Y cells ( ~ 15%). Transfection with Metafectene PRO (Biontex) improved the transfection efficiency of SH-SY5Y cells to ~ 35%.

For dual-luciferase reporter assays, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 2-4 × 104 cells per well in 90 μL of culture medium. After 24 h, the cells were transiently transfected with 100 ng of plasmid DNA using either Lipofectamine 3000 or Metafectene PRO.

At 24 h post-transfection, the cells were rinsed with DPBS and lysed in-well with 20 μL of Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega). A 15 μL aliquot of lysate was transferred to a white, flat-bottom 96-well plate for analysis, and Renilla and Firefly luciferase signals were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1960, Promega) on a GloMax Explorer plate reader (Promega). The raw luminescence values were background-corrected by subtracting the average signal from untransfected wells. Renilla luciferase activity was normalized to Firefly luciferase activity. Allelic differences were assessed using two-sided unpaired t-tests, with the data normalized to the risk allele (reference). The empty psiCHECK-2 vector and untreated samples were included as controls. All transfections were performed in 7-14 technical replicates and independently repeated at least 3 times on different days.

To determine whether differences in Renilla luciferase activity were due to changes in mRNA stability or translation efficiency, we performed RT‒qPCR targeting both Renilla and Firefly luciferase mRNAs. The cell lysates were harvested from culture plates with 350 µl/well of RLT lysis buffer (Qiagen) and stored at − 80 °C. RNA was extracted with the Qiagen RNeasy Mini RNA kit using QIAcube with standard on-column DNAse treatment (Qiagen), followed by TURBO DNA-free treatment (Invitrogen). The RNA concentrations were quantified with a NanoDrop 8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). cDNA was generated in 20 µL reactions using total RNA input (1 μg for 293FT and A549 cells and 500 ng for SH-SY5Y cells) with the iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad). Each 5 µL reaction included 2 µL of cDNA, corresponding to 10 ng of total RNA input. The expression of Firefly (vector normalization control) and Renilla (target) luciferase transcripts by the psiCHECK-2 constructs was quantified using a multiplexed custom TaqMan gene expression assay (Thermo Fisher). For Firefly luciferase, the primers used were as follows: Firefly_F: TATTCTGAGCGTGGTGCCATT and Firefly_R: TACATCAGCACCACCCGAAAG with the ACGGCTTCGGCATGT-FAM probe. For Renilla luciferase, the primers used were Renilla_F: ACGTCGTGCCTCACATCGA and Renilla_R: ATGAGCCATTCCCGCTCTT, with the ATGCATCATCCCTGATC-VIC probe. TaqMan reactions were run in technical quadruplicate in 384-well plates on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The experimentally determined PCR efficiencies of the assays were 97–99% in 293FT cells. The fold change in the Renilla transcript (target) was calculated by normalizing by Firefly expression levels to account for the transfection efficiency.
