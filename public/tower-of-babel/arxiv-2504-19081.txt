Lemon limbs of the cubic connectedness locus
Carsten Lunde Petersen and Saeed Zakeri

Abstract
. We describe a limb structure in the connectedness locus of complex cubic polynomials, where the limbs are indexed by the periodic points of the doubling map t ↦ 2 ​ t ​ ( mod ⁡ ℤ ) t\mapsto 2t\ (\operatorname{mod}{\mathbb{Z}}) . The main renormalization locus in each limb is parametrized by the product of a pair of (punctured) Mandelbrot sets. This parametrization is the inverse of the straightening map and can be thought of as a tuning operation that manufactures a unique cubic of a given combinatorics from a pair of quadratic hybrid classes. Our results build a new understanding of the structure of the cubic connectedness locus and its hyperbolic components.



1.
A bird’s-eye view

In this paper we define and investigate a limb structure in the connectedness locus of complex cubic polynomials that is inspired by the well-known case of the Mandelbrot set
\mathcal{M}
described by Douady and Hubbard (see
[
DH1
]
and
[
M3
]
). In their description
\mathcal{M}
is essentially partitioned into a main hyperbolic component—the “main cardioid”—and a countable collection of disjoint limbs attached to it. These limbs are uniquely indexed by the non-trivial rotation cycles of the doubling map
\mathbf{m}_{2}:t\mapsto 2t\ (\operatorname{mod}{\mathbb{Z}})
or, equivalently, by their non-zero rotation numbers. A parameter
c\in\mathcal{M}
belongs to the limb associated with a given rotation cycle precisely when the dynamic rays of the quadratic polynomial
Q_{c}:z\mapsto z^{2}+c
with angles in that rotation cycle co-land at a fixed point. This description allowed for a divide-and-conquer strategy for understanding the structure of
\mathcal{M}
. For example, it was the starting point of Yoccoz’s celebrated work on the problem of local connectivity of the Mandelbrot set.

For cubic maps the situation is far more complicated. Consider the family
\mathcal{P}(3)\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
of complex cubic polynomials in the normal form
P_{a,b}:z\mapsto z^{3}+3az^{2}+b
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
, and its connectedness locus
\mathcal{C}(3)
, the cubic analogue of the Mandelbrot set. Branner and Hubbard
[
BH
]
showed that
\mathcal{C}(3)
is compact and cellular, hence connected. But soon after Lavaurs
[
La
]
proved that
\mathcal{C}(3)
is not locally connected and therefore its topology must be quite intricate. Up until now, there seems to have been no attempt at understanding
\mathcal{C}(3)
through a suitable notion of limbs or open wakes that should contain limbs except for their root points (compare the recent paper
[
BOTW
]
which constructs a locally connected combinatorial model of
\mathcal{C}(3)
analogous to Thurston’s model of
\mathcal{M}
).

To illustrate the varied nature of limbs in the cubic case, consider the “unicritical family”
\{P_{0,b}:z\mapsto z^{3}+b\}_{b\in\mathbb{C}}
where the two critical points coincide, and the “lemon family”
\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)=\{P_{a,0}:z\mapsto z^{3}+3az^{2}\}_{a\in\mathbb{C}}
where one critical point is fixed at
0
and the other is free. These families can be identified with complex lines in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
that meet transversally at the center of the main hyperbolic component
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{C}(3)
corresponding to the map
z\mapsto z^{3}
. Both of these lines intersect
\mathcal{C}(3)
along a closed topological disk (the closure of their intersection with
\mathcal{H}
) decorated with a countable collection of disjoint limbs. But the limbs in these one-dimensional slices of
\mathcal{C}(3)
have completely different dynamical and topological natures. In the unicritical case, the limbs are naturally indexed by non-zero rational rotation numbers
p/q
plus an element of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, where the family has a period-
q
bifurcation at the root parameter, a situation that bears strong resemblance to the case of the Mandelbrot set (Fig.
1
left). For the lemon family, the limbs are naturally indexed by the periodic
points
of
\mathbf{m}_{2}
plus an element of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and the family has a saddle-node bifurcation at the root parameter (Fig.
1
right). Combinatorially speaking, the unicritical limbs are associated with certain cycles of the tripling map
\mathbf{m}_{3}:t\mapsto 3t\ (\operatorname{mod}{\mathbb{Z}})
whose combinatorics have degree
1
, while the limbs in
\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
are associated with pairs of adjacent cycles of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
that can have any combinatorics of degree
1
or
2
(see §
3.1
for details).

We use the combinatorial characterization of limbs in
\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
to define the “lemon limb”
\mathcal{L}(t)\subset\mathcal{C}(3)
for every periodic point
t\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
of
\mathbf{m}_{2}
. The combinatorial pattern of co-landing rays of
P=P_{a,b}
in
\mathcal{L}(t)
implies the existence of two quadratic-like restrictions, a “central”
P:U\to U^{\prime}
about the first critical point
0
and a “peripheral”
P^{\circ q}:V\to V^{\prime}
about the second critical point
-2a
, where
q\geq 1
is the period of
t
under
\mathbf{m}_{2}
. Under favorable conditions these restrictions have connected filled Julia sets and therefore they are hybrid conjugate to unique quadratic polynomials
Q_{c}
and
Q_{\nu}
, respectively. This defines a straightening map
\bm{\chi}:P\mapsto(c,\nu)
from the main renormalization locus
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)\subset\mathcal{L}(t)
to the product of two copies of
\mathcal{M}
. We prove that this map is a homeomorphism between
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
and the product
\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}\times\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
. Here
\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}=\mathcal{M}\smallsetminus\{c(t_{1}),\ldots,c(t_{q})\}
, where
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
is the
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit of
t
and
c(t_{i})
is the parabolic landing point of the external ray of
\mathcal{M}
at angle
t_{i}
(in particular,
\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0}=\mathcal{M}\smallsetminus\{1/4\}
). Thus, the inverse
\bm{\chi}^{-1}
gives a parametrization of
\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
by the product of two finitely punctured Mandelbrot sets. The assignment
\bm{\chi}^{-1}:(c,\nu)\mapsto P
can be thought of as a “tuning” operation that manufactures a unique cubic
P
with a peripheral quadratic hybrid class
Q_{\nu}
having the combinatorics of
t
, tuned by a central quadratic hybrid class
Q_{c}
.

Our results extend and tie together various pieces of prior knowledge. For example, the one-dimensional limbs
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t):=\mathcal{L}(t)\cap\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
have been studied in the works of Milnor
[
M4
]
and Roesch
[
R2
]
, and our introduction of the lemon limbs
\mathcal{L}(t)
can be thought of as a direct generalization from dimension
1
to
2
. Inou and Kiwi
[
IK
]
have studied properties of the straightening map in the general framework of mapping schemata of postcritically finite polynomials and, among other things, shown its injectivity. For cubic polynomials, they proved the existence of homeomorphic copies of
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}
in
\mathcal{C}(3)
associated with the hyperbolic components of primitive disjoint type. Our work on lemon limbs provides a complementary result for
non-primitive
disjoint type renormalizations, where one has to deal with the issue of non-compactness of the domain of the straightening map. The paper
[
IK
]
also showed the existence of injected copies of
\mathcal{K}:=\{(c,z):c\in\mathcal{M}\ \text{and}\ z\in K_{c}\}
in
\mathcal{C}(3)
associated with the hyperbolic components of primitive capture type (see also
[
BuHe
]
). Such copies of
\mathcal{K}
can be observed in our full lemon limb
\mathcal{L}(t)
as the extensions of the capture components in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)
. Another related result is the intertwining surgery studied by Epstein and Yampolsky
[
EY
]
, and by Haïssinsky
[
Ha
]
, which can be interpreted as a special case of our construction of
P=\bm{\chi}^{-1}(c,\nu)
when the combinatorics of
t
under
\mathbf{m}_{2}
is of rotation type
p/q
and the central hybrid class
Q_{c}
is chosen from the
p/q
-limb of
\mathcal{M}
. Finally, lemon limb structures show up in a recent paper of Bonifant, Estabrooks and Sharland
[
BES
]
which establishes a correspondence between escape loci in
\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
and in the curve
\operatorname{Per}_{2}(0)\subset\mathcal{P}(3)
consisting of cubics with a critical point of period
2
.

The ideas presented in this paper open up several new directions and avenues for further exploration. We refer the reader to a list of problems at the end of our expanded introduction in the next section.

2.
Outline of results

We now give a more precise account of the main results. We represent cubic polynomials in the normal form

P_{a,b}:z\mapsto z^{3}+3az^{2}+b\qquad(a,b\in\mathbb{C})

with marked critical points
\omega_{1}=0,\omega_{2}=-2a
. The space
\mathcal{P}(3)
of all such critically marked cubics is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with coordinates
(a,b)
. The space of all affine conjugacy classes of these cubics (the so-called “moduli space”) is also isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with coordinates
(a^{2},b^{2})
. The
cubic connectedness locus

\mathcal{C}(3)
is the set of all cubics in
\mathcal{P}(3)
with connected Julia set, which is known to be compact and connected (in fact, cellular). Consider the complex line
\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)\subset\mathcal{P}(3)
consisting of all cubics
P_{a,b}
for which
\omega_{1}
is fixed, with the normal form

P_{a}=P_{a,0}:z\mapsto z^{3}+3az^{2}\qquad(a\in\mathbb{C}).

The connectedness locus
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\mathcal{C}(3)\cap\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
of this family is shown in Figs.
1
and
7
. The prominent central region
\mathcal{H}_{0}
in these figures, resembling the shape of a lemon, consists of all
a\in\mathbb{C}
for which
\omega_{2}
belongs to the immediate basin of attraction of
\omega_{1}
(equivalently, the Julia set of
P_{a}
is a Jordan curve). For this reason, we informally refer to
\{P_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{C}}
as the
lemon family
of cubics. The limbs of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
attached to
\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}
have been the subject of several investigations (see
[
F
]
,
[
M4
]
, and
[
R2
]
). Although structurally different, they are reminiscent of the primary limbs of the Mandelbrot set attached to the boundary of the main cardioid.

The “lemon limbs” of the connectedness locus
\mathcal{C}(3)
that we study in this paper are the natural extensions of the one-dimensional limbs of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. Their description depends on a basic combinatorial result on the existence and uniqueness of pairs of periodic orbits of the tripling map
\mathbf{m}_{3}:t\mapsto 3t
(mod
\mathbb{Z}
) that simulate the combinatorics of the periodic points of the doubling map
\mathbf{m}_{2}
. By the
combinatorics
of a periodic orbit
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
under a covering map
f:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, labeled so that
0\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{q}<1
, we mean the
q
-cycle
\sigma
in the permutation group
S_{q}
determined by the condition
f(t_{i})=t_{\sigma(i)}
for all
i
.

In §
3.2
we use the general machinery developed in
[
PZ1
]
to prove the following

Theorem A

(Existence and uniqueness of simulating orbits)
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
be a period
q
point of
\mathbf{m}_{2}
with the orbit
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}=t,\ldots,t_{q}\}
and combinatorics
\sigma\in S_{q}
. Then
t
has a unique pair of “simulating orbits,” i.e., period
q
orbits
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\}
and
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}\}
under
\mathbf{m}_{3}
, having the same combinatorics
\sigma
, that interlace in the sense

0<x_{1}<y_{1}<\cdots<x_{k}<\frac{1}{2}<y_{k}<\cdots<x_{q}<y_{q}<1.

The theorem may be interpreted as saying that the
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
can be “blown up” to a pair of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
-orbits of the same combinatorics so that the marked point
t_{k}
blows up to a pair
x_{k},y_{k}
surrounding the fixed point
1/2
(compare Figures
3
and
4
and the associated examples).

In fact, there are precisely
q+1
period
q
orbits
\mathcal{O}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{q}
of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
that have the combinatorics
\sigma
; they are uniquely characterized by the property
\#(\mathcal{O}_{i}\cap[0,1/2[)=i
. We prove Theorem
A
by showing that the neighboring pairs
\mathcal{O}_{i-1},\mathcal{O}_{i}
interlace, so
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\}=\mathcal{O}_{k}
and
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}\}=\mathcal{O}_{k-1}
will be the simulating orbits of
t
(Theorem
3.4
).

We note that the idea of constructing orbits of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
from those of
\mathbf{m}_{2}
(and more generally
\mathbf{m}_{d+1}
from
\mathbf{m}_{d}
) for a combinatorics of rotation type has appeared in
[
BMMOP
]
.

With
t=t_{k}
and
x_{k},y_{k}
as above, the limb
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)\subset\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
attached to
\partial\mathcal{H}_{0}
can be described as the set of cubics
P_{a}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
for which the dynamic rays
R_{a}(x_{k}),R_{a}(y_{k})
co-land (see §
4.2
and §
4.3
). Inspired by this characterization, we define the
lemon limb

\mathcal{L}(t)
by

\mathcal{L}(t)=\big\{P\in\mathcal{C}(3):\text{the dynamic rays}\ R_{P}(x_{k}),R_{P}(y_{k})\ \text{co-land}\,\big\}.

Unlike the slices
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)
or the primary limbs of the Mandelbrot set, the lemon limbs
\mathcal{L}(t)
are not compact (see Example
5.2
). In fact, for
P\in\overline{\mathcal{L}(t)}\smallsetminus\mathcal{L}(t)
the rays
R_{P}(x_{k}),R_{P}(y_{k})
land at distinct points, one of which being parabolic. For an extended discussion of this phenomenon we refer to §
8
.

When
P\in\mathcal{L}(t)
, the dynamic rays
R_{P}(x_{i}),R_{P}(y_{i})
co-land at a point
z_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq q
and the
co-landing orbit

\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
has period dividing
q
, with each
z_{i}
either repelling or parabolic with multiplier
(P^{\circ q})^{\prime}(z_{i})=1
. Define the dynamical wake
W_{i}
as the connected component of
\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus(R_{P}(x_{i})\cup R_{P}(y_{i})\cup\{z_{i}\})
not containing the first critical point
\omega_{1}
. The fact that the interval
(x_{k},y_{k})\subset\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
contains the fixed point
1/2
of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
implies that the wake
W_{k}
contains the second critical point
\omega_{2}
. Under
P
each wake
W_{i}
maps conformally to
W_{\sigma(i)}
if
i\neq k
and to the entire plane if
i=k
. These wakes can be used to define two renormalizations of
P
of periods
1
and
q
as follows (see §
5.2
). If
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
is “peripherally repelling,” i.e., repelling or parabolic with no immediate basin in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}W_{i}
, there is a quadratic-like restriction
P:U\to U^{\prime}
around
\omega_{1}
, where
U^{\prime}
is a slightly thickened copy of
\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}\overline{W_{i}}
cut off by some equipotential. We call
P

centrally renormalizable
if the filled Julia set
K_{\operatorname{cen}}
of
P:U\to U^{\prime}
is connected. Similarly, if
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
is “centrally repelling,” i.e., repelling or parabolic with no immediate basin outside
\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}W_{i}
, there is a quadratic-like restriction
P^{\circ q}:V\to V^{\prime}
around
\omega_{2}
, where
V^{\prime}
is a slightly thickened copy of
W_{k}
cut off by some equipotential. We call
P

peripherally renormalizable
if the filled Julia set
K_{\operatorname{per}}
of
P^{\circ q}:V\to V^{\prime}
is connected. The
main renormalization locus

\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
is the set of all cubics in
\mathcal{L}(t)
that are both centrally and peripherally renormalizable.

According to the straightening theorem of Douady and Hubbard
[
DH2
]
, for each
P\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
there is a unique pair
(c,\nu)\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}
such that the central renormalization
P:U\to U^{\prime}
is hybrid equivalent to
Q_{c}:z\mapsto z^{2}+c
and the peripheral renormalization
P^{\circ q}:V\to V^{\prime}
is hybrid equivalent to
Q_{\nu}:z\mapsto z^{2}+\nu
. This gives two straightening maps

\chi_{\operatorname{cen}},\chi_{\operatorname{per}}:\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)\to\mathcal{M}

defined by
\chi_{\operatorname{cen}}(P):=c,\chi_{\operatorname{per}}(P):=\nu
. If
\varphi
is a hybrid equivalence between
P:U\to U^{\prime}
and
Q_{c}
, then for every
1\leq i\leq q
the dynamic ray
R_{c}(t_{i})
of
Q_{c}
lands at the repelling point
w_{i}:=\varphi(z_{i})
of
Q_{c}
, and if
\psi
is a hybrid equivalence between
P^{\circ q}:V\to V^{\prime}
and
Q_{\nu}
, then
\psi(z_{k})
is the
\beta
-fixed point of
Q_{\nu}
(Lemma
5.5
). Since
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
is repelling, we conclude that the periodic orbit
\{w_{i}\}
of
Q_{c}
and the
\beta
-fixed point of
Q_{\nu}
must be repelling. It follows from the general theory of the Mandelbrot set that
\nu\neq 1/4
and
c\neq c(t_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq q
, where
c(t_{i})
is the parabolic landing point of the external ray
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{M}}(t_{i})
of the Mandelbrot set. Let

\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}:=\mathcal{M}\smallsetminus\{c(t_{1}),\ldots,c(t_{q})\}.

In particular,
\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0}=\mathcal{M}\smallsetminus\{1/4\}
.

Theorem B

(Parametrization of the main renormalization locus)
.

The straightening map

\bm{\chi}:\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)\to\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}\times\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0}

defined by
\bm{\chi}(P)=(\chi_{\operatorname{cen}}(P),\chi_{\operatorname{per}}(P))
is a homeomorphism.

The inverse map
\bm{\chi}^{-1}
gives a homeomorphic embedding of the product
\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}\times\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
into the lemon limb
\mathcal{L}(t)
. The construction of
P=\bm{\chi}^{-1}(c,\nu)
amounts to the aforementioned tuning operation: it is the unique cubic with the peripheral hybrid class of
Q_{\nu}
with the combinatorics of
t
, tuned by the central hybrid class of
Q_{c}
.

Our proof of Theorem
B
is structured as follows:

\bullet

In §
7
we prove injectivity of
\bm{\chi}
by a pull-back argument. The key ingredient is to show that for each
P\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
the residual Julia set

K_{\infty}:=K_{P}\smallsetminus\bigcup_{n\geq 0}P^{-n}(K_{\operatorname{cen}}\cup K_{\operatorname{per}})

has Lebesgue measure zero (Lemma
7.2
). The required bounded distortion argument is handled using hyperbolic geometry estimates for the inclusion map
i:X\hookrightarrow Y
between Riemann surfaces (Lemmas
7.4
and
7.5
).

\bullet

In §
8
we show that the inverse
\bm{\chi}^{-1}
is continuous, that is, if
P_{n},P_{\infty}\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
and
\bm{\chi}(P_{n})\to\bm{\chi}(P_{\infty})
, then
P_{n}\to P_{\infty}
. Since
\bm{\chi}
is a continuous injection, it suffices to prove that if
P_{n}
accumulates on some
P\in\mathcal{C}(3)
, then
P\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
. The non-trivial step here is to ensure that the co-landing orbit of
P_{n}
does not degenerate into a parabolic orbit of
P
and that the rays
R_{P}(x_{k}),R_{P}(y_{k})
continue to co-land at the limit of the co-landing point of
R_{P_{n}}(x_{k}),R_{P_{n}}(y_{k})
(Lemmas
8.4
and
8.5
). The latter amounts to ruling out the creation of heteroclinic arcs in the Hausdorff limit of the pair
R_{P_{n}}(x_{k}),R_{P_{n}}(y_{k})
as
n\to\infty
.

In §
9
we prove surjectivity of
\bm{\chi}
. Given
(c,\nu)\in\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}\times\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
, we first construct a holomorphic map
f:\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\overline{\Lambda}\to\mathbb{C}
with a central renormalization of period
1
hybrid equivalent to
Q_{c}
and a peripheral renormalization of period
q
, with the combinatorics of
t
, hybrid equivalent to
Q_{\nu}
(see §
9.2
). Here the “hole”
\Lambda
is a Jordan domain which, synthetically speaking, plays the role of the preimage of
K_{\operatorname{cen}}
other than
K_{\operatorname{cen}}
. We then use an auxiliary dynamics near
\partial\Lambda
and a careful quasiconformal interpolation to extend
f
across a sector containing
\Lambda
(see §
9.3
and §

). This gives a quasiregular polynomial-like map of degree
3
whose straightening, after suitable normalization, will be the desired
P\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
with
\bm{\chi}(P)=(c,\nu)
.

Every periodic point
t\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
of
\mathbf{m}_{2}
determines an
\mathbf{m}_{3}
-invariant lamination defined by joining the corresponding pairs
x_{i},y_{i}
given by Theorem
A
. This lamination can be thought of as the combinatorial signature of the lemon limb
\mathcal{L}(t)
. The combinatorics arising here is intrinsically quadratic in the sense that the permutation in
S_{2q}
induced by the union
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\}\cup\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}\}
has degree
\leq 2
(see §
3.1
for the notion of degree). Other pairs of periodic orbits of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
can still define co-landing patterns but the combinatorics of their union necessarily has degree
3
(compare the Appendix). It would be desirable to understand which pairs are compatible and to investigate the resulting laminations.

Some open problems.
We conclude this introduction with a list of open problems that suggest possible directions for further exploration and a path toward a divide-and-conquer approach to
\mathcal{C}(3)
.

(1)

Study the topology of lemon limbs. In particular, is
\overline{\mathcal{L}(t)}
connected? Is
\mathcal{L}(t)
connected? Is it contractible? A relevant notion here is that of the “lemon wake”
\mathcal{W}(t)
consisting of all cubics
P\in\mathcal{P}(3)
for which the rays
R_{P}(x_{k}),R_{P}(y_{k})
are
smooth
and co-land at a
repelling
periodic point. By definition
\mathcal{W}(t)
is open. It can be shown that the following relations hold:

\mathcal{W}(t)\cap\mathcal{C}(3)\subsetneq\mathcal{L}(t)\subsetneq\overline{\mathcal{L}(t)}=\overline{\mathcal{W}(t)}\cap\mathcal{C}(3).

Moreover, for
P\in\partial\mathcal{W}(t)\cap\mathcal{C}(3)
one of the following occurs:

(i)

R_{P}(x_{k}),R_{P}(y_{k})
co-land at a parabolic point. In this case
P\in\mathcal{L}(t)
;

(ii)

R_{P}(x_{k}),R_{P}(y_{k})
land at distinct points, one of which being parabolic. In this case
P\notin\mathcal{L}(t)
.

For
P
in the complementary boundary part
\partial\mathcal{W}(t)\smallsetminus\mathcal{C}(3)
there are more possibilities. Both
R_{P}(x_{k}),R_{P}(y_{k})
can be smooth and co-landing at a parabolic point, or they can land at distinct points one of which being parabolic. Also one or both of these rays can be broken.

(2)

Give a dynamical characterization for the cubics in
\overline{\mathcal{L}(t)}\smallsetminus\mathcal{L}(t)
. This set is contained in
\partial\mathcal{W}(t)\cap\mathcal{C}(3)
and therefore every cubic in it must satisfy the condition (ii) above, but there are actually more restrictions. See Remark
8.6
for a discussion of these restrictions and a proposed characterization for this difference set.

(3)

Investigate the extension of the straightening map
\bm{\chi}
to cubics in
\mathcal{L}(t)
with parabolic co-landing orbits. This appears to be a straightforward application of the parabolic-like mappings of
[
Lo
]
. Along the same lines, it seems plausible that the inverse
\bm{\chi}^{-1}
extends continuously to all parameters
(c,\nu)
where at least one of the conditions
c\in\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}
or
\nu\in\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
holds.

(4)

Each one-dimensional limb
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)=\mathcal{L}(t)\cap\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
has a unique root point which is also the root of a copy
\mathcal{M}_{0}(t)
of the Mandelbrot set (see Figs.
7
and
8
). This “main Mandelbrot copy” can be interpreted as the closure of the fiber
\bm{\chi}^{-1}(\{0\}\times\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0})
of the straightening map. It follows from our Theorem
B
that there is a continuous motion
c\mapsto\bm{\chi}^{-1}(\{c\}\times\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0})
as
c
varies over
\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}
, that is, the derooted main Mandelbrot copy persists when the central hybrid class moves from
c=0
to anywhere in the Mandelbrot set with finitely many exceptional parabolic parameters. As noted above, we expect
\bm{\chi}^{-1}(c,\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0})
to have a well-defined root, giving rise to a full copy of the Mandelbrot set. When is this copy quasiconformally homeomorphic to
\mathcal{M}
(in a suitable sense)? With parabolic-like mappings in mind, we speculate that when
c
is an exceptional parameter, a corresponding Mandelbrot copy still exists but it is quasiconformally homeomorphic to the “parabolic Mandelbrot set”
\mathcal{M}_{1}
studied in
[
PR
]
.

(5)

The central renormalization locus in
\mathcal{L}(t)
can be naturally decomposed into one-dimensional slices
\{\mathcal{L}_{c}(t)\}_{c\in\mathcal{M}}
as follows. For
c\in\mathcal{M}
, consider the central renormalization curve
\mathcal{X}_{c}
consisting of all cubics in
\mathcal{P}(3)
which have a quadratic-like restriction around
\omega_{1}=0
hybrid equivalent to
Q_{c}
. This is a one-dimensional complex analytic set in
\mathcal{P}(3)
whose escape region
\mathcal{X}_{c}\smallsetminus\mathcal{C}(3)
is isomorphic to a punctured disk (when

, we recover the subset
\mathcal{X}_{0}=\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)\smallsetminus\overline{\mathcal{H}_{0}}
of the complex line shown in Figs.

and

). We define
\mathcal{L}_{c}(t)
as the intersection
\mathcal{X}_{c}\cap\mathcal{L}(t)
. For
c\in\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}
the slice
\mathcal{L}_{c}(t)
can be viewed as an analytic extension of the embedded copy
\bm{\chi}^{-1}(c,\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0})
of the derooted Mandelbrot set.

By the work of Petersen and Tan
[
PT
]
there is a holomorphic motion
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)\to\mathcal{L}_{c}(t)
as

varies over the main cardioid of the Mandelbrot set

. More generally, one would expect
\mathcal{L}_{c}(t)
to move holomorphically as long as

varies in an interior component of
\mathcal{M}
. What is the structure of
\mathcal{L}_{c}(t)
when

is on the boundary of

? Is it always a quotient of

(6)

Give a description of the cubics in the limb
\mathcal{L}(t)
that are centrally renormalizable. We expect that in some sense these form a continuous motion of the parameters in the one-dimensional limb
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)=\mathcal{L}(t)\cap\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
and often a holomorphic motion of them. However, as there will be collisions due to the change in the topology of the central filled Julia set, the holomorphic motions of quotients of
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)
only exist when the central renormalization vary over an interior component of
\mathcal{M}
(compare (

) above). Similarly, give a description of the cubics in
\mathcal{L}(t)
that are peripherally renormalizable. There is an asymmetry between the two types of renormalizations, but the latter problem can be viewed as a companion of the former. As a further step, one could attempt to give a “complete description” of the whole limb
\mathcal{L}(t)
by analyzing what happens when the forward orbit of
\omega_{1}
or
\omega_{2}
exits the domain of the corresponding renormalization.

A key tool in studying these problems is the machinery of

(compare Roesch’s work in
[

]
where she uses puzzles to study the slice
\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
, although her construction is different from what we propose below). Each lemon limb

gives a separation of the two critical points by a pair of co-landing rays and thus the basis for constructing puzzle pieces, where the critical points are already separated by the depth-zero puzzle. More precisely, let

and

be the simulating angles of

. There are unique angles

with

such that

and
\mathbf{m}_{3}(y_{k}\textquoteright)=y_{\sigma(k)}

). For each

, take the unique angles

with

such that
\mathbf{m}_{3}(x^{\prime}_{j})=x^{\prime}_{\sigma(j)}
and
\mathbf{m}_{3}(y^{\prime}_{j})=y^{\prime}_{\sigma(j)}
. Take the union of the

co-landing pairs

and

for

together with their landing points and some Green’s equipotential curve. The depth-zero puzzle pieces are defined as the closure of the bounded connected components of the complement of this union. The depth-

puzzle pieces are obtained by taking pull backs under

. Denoting by

the depth-zero puzzle piece containing the critical point

is centrally (resp. peripherally) renormalizable precisely when

under

(resp.

under

(resp.

3.1.
Combinatorics of periodic orbits

Let
f:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
be an orientation-preserving covering map of the circle with a period
q
orbit
\mathcal{O}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
. Unless otherwise stated, we label periodic orbits in positive cyclic order, that is, choosing unique representatives in
[0,1)
, we assume
0\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{q}<1
. By the
combinatorics
of
\mathcal{O}
under
f
we mean the cyclic permutation
\sigma
in the symmetric group
S_{q}
defined by

f(t_{i})=t_{\sigma(i)}\qquad\text{for all}\ 1\leq i\leq q.

We often describe this situation by saying that the orbit
\mathcal{O}

realizes

\sigma\in S_{q}
.

Definition 3.1
.

\sigma\in S_{q}
is said to have a
descent
at
i
if
\sigma(i)>\sigma(i+1)
. Here
1\leq i\leq q
and we always adopt the convention
q+1=1
). The number of descents of
\sigma
is called its
degree
:
2
2

2

In the combinatorics literature this invariant is called the
cyclic descent number
of
\sigma
.

\deg(\sigma)=\#\{1\leq i\leq q:\sigma\ \text{has a descent at}\ i\}.

For example,
\sigma=(1243)\in S_{4}
, which can be written in the classical notation as
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3&4\\
2&4&1&3\end{pmatrix}
, has descents at
i=2,4
so
\deg(\sigma)=2
.

It is easy to see that
\deg(\sigma)=1
if and only if
\sigma
is a
rotation cycle
, i.e., a cyclic permutation of the form
\sigma:i\mapsto i+p\ (\operatorname{mod}q)
for some integer
1\leq p<q
relatively prime to
q
. The reduced fraction
p/q
is called the
rotation number
of
\sigma
.

The question of possible combinatorics of periodic orbits of the standard covering maps
\mathbf{m}_{k}:t\mapsto kt\ \ (\operatorname{mod}{\mathbb{Z}})
and the frequency with which they occur is answered in
[
PZ1
]
. The combinatorial invariant
\deg(\sigma)
can be characterized topologically as the minimum degree of an orientation-preserving covering map of the circle having a periodic orbit which realizes
\sigma
. Thus, if
\sigma\in S_{q}
is realized under
\mathbf{m}_{k}
, then necessarily
k\geq\deg(\sigma)
. The following theorem shows sufficiency of this condition and provides a precise count:

Theorem 3.2

(
[
PZ1
]
)
.

Let
\sigma\in S_{q}
be a cyclic permutation with
\deg(\sigma)=d
. Then, for every integer
k\geq\max\{d,2\}
the number of period
q
orbits of
\mathbf{m}_{k}
having combinatorics
\sigma
is

\begin{cases}\ \ \displaystyle{\binom{q+k-d}{q}}&\qquad\text{if}\ \sigma(q)>\sigma(1),\\
\ \ \displaystyle{\binom{q+k-d-1}{q}}&\qquad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The periodic orbits of
\mathbf{m}_{k}
that realize a given
\sigma\in S_{q}
are uniquely determined by their “fixed point distribution,” i.e., the knowledge of how the
k-1
fixed points of
\mathbf{m}_{k}
are distributed among the
q
intervals in between the orbit points, or equivalently, how the orbit points are deployed in the
k-1
intervals between the fixed points.

The following special case of the above theorem plays a central role in this paper. Suppose a cyclic permutation
\sigma\in S_{q}
can be realized under the doubling map
\mathbf{m}_{2}
. Then either
\deg(\sigma)=1
so
\sigma
is a rotation cycle and
\sigma(q)<\sigma(1)
, or
\deg(\sigma)=2
and
\sigma(q)>\sigma(1)
. Whichever the case, Theorem
3.2
shows that
\sigma
has a unique realization under
\mathbf{m}_{2}
and
q+1
realizations under
\mathbf{m}_{3}
.

Remark 3.3
.

The preceding definitions easily generalize to finite unions of periodic orbits in which case the associated combinatorics are products of cyclic permutations with disjoint supports. The definition of degree remains the same and
k\geq\deg(\sigma)
is a necessary (but no longer sufficient) condition for
\mathbf{m}_{k}
having a finite invariant set that realizes
\sigma
.

3.4
.

Consider the intervals
T_{i}:=[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
for

. Since
\mathbf{m}_{2}(T_{i})=T_{\sigma(i)}\cup\cdots\cup T_{\sigma(i+1)-1}
, we have

(3.4)

|T_{\sigma(i)}|+\cdots+|T_{\sigma(i+1)-1}|=2|T_{i}|\qquad\text{for}\ 1\leq i\leq q.

There are two possibilities for how
\mathbf{m}_{2}
acts on the interval
T_{q}
containing the fixed point
0
: Either
\deg(\sigma)=1
and
\sigma(q)<\sigma(1)
in which case
\mathbf{m}_{2}
maps
T_{q}
over the whole circle, or
\deg(\sigma)=2
and
\sigma(q)>\sigma(1)
in which case
\mathbf{m}_{2}
maps
T_{q}
homeomorphically onto
T_{\sigma(q)}\cup\cdots\cup T_{\sigma(1)-1}
.

Now fix
1\leq k\leq q
and take the points
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{q}
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q}
on the circle such that

0<\alpha_{1}<\beta_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{q}<\beta_{q}<1
. The intervals
A_{i}:=[\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}]
and
B_{i}:=[\beta_{i},\alpha_{i+1}]
for
1\leq i\leq q
form a partition of the circle modulo their endpoints. We may choose these points so that
|A_{\sigma^{i}(k)}|=3^{i-1}/(3^{q}-1)
and
|B_{i}|=|T_{i}|/2
for all
1\leq i\leq q
. This is possible because

\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{3^{i-1}}{3^{q}-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{|T_{i}|}{2}=\frac{1}{2}.

Define a covering map
f:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
which sends the
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
affinely to their images as follows:

f(A_{i})=\begin{cases}A_{\sigma(i)}&\quad\text{if}\ i\neq k\\
A_{\sigma(i)}\ \text{plus one full turn}&\quad\text{if}\ i=k\end{cases}

and

f(B_{i})=\begin{cases}\ [\beta_{\sigma(i)},\alpha_{\sigma(i+1)}]&\quad\text{if}\ i\neq q\ \text{or if}\ i=q\ \text{and}\ \sigma(q)>\sigma(1)\\
\ [\beta_{\sigma(i)},\alpha_{\sigma(i+1)}]\ \text{plus one full turn}&\quad\text{if}\ i=q\ \text{and}\ \sigma(q)<\sigma(1).\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
f
is a degree
3
piecewise affine covering map with
f^{\prime}|_{A_{i}}=3
for all
i
. Moreover,
f(B_{i})
contains the union
B_{\sigma(i)}\cup\cdots\cup B_{\sigma(i+1)-1}
, so by (
3.4
),

f^{\prime}|_{B_{i}}\geq\frac{|B_{\sigma(i)}|+\cdots+|B_{\sigma(i+1)-1}|}{|B_{i}|}=\frac{|T_{\sigma(i)}|+\cdots+|T_{\sigma(i+1)-1}|}{|T_{i}|}=2.

Thus,
f
is uniformly expanding with slope
\geq 2
. It is well known that this implies the existence of a unique orientation-preserving homeomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
which fixes
0
and satisfies
\varphi\circ f=\mathbf{m}_{3}\circ\varphi
(see e.g.
[

). Set
\hat{x}_{i}:=\varphi(\alpha_{i}),\hat{y}_{i}:=\varphi(\beta_{i})
. Evidently
\mathcal{O}:=\{\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{q}\}
and
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}:=\{\hat{y}_{1},\ldots,\hat{y}_{q}\}
are periodic orbits of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
with combinatorics
\sigma
. As
[\hat{x}_{k},\hat{y}_{k}]
maps over itself under
\mathbf{m}_{3}
, it must contain a fixed point of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
which can only be

since the other fixed point

. This proves the analog of (
3.2
) for the

. It follows in particular that

\mathcal{O}\cap[0,1/2]=\{\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{k}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{O}^{\prime}\cap[0,1/2]=\{\hat{y}_{1},\ldots,\hat{y}_{k-1}\}.

We conclude from the characterization (

) that
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_{k},\mathcal{O}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}_{k-1}
, and therefore

and
\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}
for all

.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{q}
be the realizations of
\sigma\in S_{q}
under
\mathbf{m}_{3}
as above. Consider the marked interval
[x_{k},y_{k}]
of the simulating pair
\mathcal{O}_{k},\mathcal{O}_{k-1}
which contains the fixed point

. Then
\mathcal{O}_{j}\cap\,]x_{k},y_{k}[\,\neq\emptyset
whenever

.

Proof.

In the natural cyclic order of orbits, let
u_{i}
denote the
i
-th point of
\mathcal{O}_{i}
if

and the
(i+1)
-st point of
\mathcal{O}_{i}
if

. Then Theorem
3.4
and the characterization (

) show that

x_{k}=u_{k}<u_{k+1}<\cdots<u_{q}<\frac{1}{2}<u_{0}<\cdots<u_{k-2}<u_{k-1}=y_{k}.\qed

Remark 3.8
.

It can be shown that for each
k
there is a degree
1
monotone map
\Pi:\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
which fixes
0
, collapses every
I_{i}=[x_{i},y_{i}]
to
t_{i}
, and satisfies the semiconjugacy relation

\Pi\circ\mathbf{m}_{3}=\mathbf{m}_{2}\circ\Pi\qquad\text{outside}\ I_{k}.

The iterated
\mathbf{m}_{3}
-preimages of
I_{k}
form the plateaus of
\Pi
and their union has full measure in
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.

The
180^{\circ}
rotation
\theta\mapsto\theta+1/2\ (\operatorname{mod}{\mathbb{Z}})
commutes with
\mathbf{m}_{3}
and exchanges the fixed points
0,1/2
. Considering the
q+1
realizations
\mathcal{O}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{q}
constructed above, it follows that the rotated sets
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}_{k}:=\mathcal{O}_{k}+1/2
are also
q
-periodic orbits of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
, although they generally have a different combinatorics. In fact, if
\rho
denotes the rotation
i\mapsto i+1\ (\operatorname{mod}q)
, the combinatorics of
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}_{k}
is
\rho^{-k}\sigma\rho^{k}
, where
\sigma
is the common combinatorics of
\mathcal{O}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{q}
. In particular, if
\deg(\sigma)=1
, then
\sigma
commutes with
\rho
and the
\mathcal{O}^{\ast}_{k}
have the same combinatorics as the
\mathcal{O}_{k}
. It follows from the characterization (
3.1
) that

(3.5)

\mathcal{O}^{\ast}_{k}=\mathcal{O}_{q-k}\qquad\text{for}\ 0\leq k\leq q\ \text{if}\ \deg(\sigma)=1.

If
\deg(\sigma)=2
, then (
3.5
) holds only for
k=0
and
k=q
. This follows from the fact that the rotational symmetry group of such
\sigma
is trivial (see
[
PZ1
]
), so
\rho^{-k}\sigma\rho^{k}=\rho
if and only if
k\in\{0,q\}
.

4.
The lemon family of cubic polynomials

We begin by briefly recalling basic facts about the dynamics of cubic polynomials which we assume the reader is generally familiar with (see
[
M1
]
and
[
BH
]
for more details).

4.1.
Green’s function and external rays

We will be working with cubic polynomials in the normal form

P=P_{a,b}:z\mapsto z^{3}+3az^{2}+b\qquad(a,b\in\mathbb{C})

with marked critical points at
\omega_{1}=0,\omega_{2}=-2a
. More precisely, we have

critical point

\omega_{1}=0

\omega_{2}=-2a

critical value

b

4a^{3}+b

co-critical point

-3a

a

(Here by the
co-critical point
associated with
\omega_{i}
is meant the unique point
z\neq\omega_{i}
with
P(z)=P(\omega_{i})
.) We will think of
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
as the “first” and “second” critical points of
P
, respectively. The space
\mathcal{P}(3)
of all such critically marked cubics is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with coordinates
(a,b)
. Note that two distinct cubics
P_{a,b}
and
P_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
are conjugate by an affine map preserving the critical points markings if and only if
a^{\prime}=-a,b^{\prime}=-b
. Thus, the space of all affine conjugacy classes (the so-called “moduli space”) of cubics is also isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with coordinates
(a^{2},b^{2})
.

Let us fix
P\in\mathcal{P}(3)
. We denote by
K=K_{P}
and
J=J_{P}
the filled Julia set and Julia set of
P
, respectively. Let
G=G_{P}:\mathbb{C}\to[0,+\infty[
denote the Green’s function of
K
defined by

G(z):=\lim_{n\to\infty}3^{-n}\log^{+}|P^{\circ n}(z)|.

It is easy to see that
G
is subharmonic in
\mathbb{C}
, harmonic outside
K=G^{-1}(0)
, and satisfies

G(P(z))=3G(z)\qquad\text{for all}\ z\in\mathbb{C}.

There is a canonical Böttcher coordinate
\phi=\phi_{P}
which is tangent to the identity near
\infty
and satisfies

(4.1)

\phi(P(z))=(\phi(z))^{3}.

It extends to a biholomorphism between
\{z:G(z)>G^{\ast}\}
and
\{z:|z|>\mathrm{e}^{G^{\ast}}\}
, where
G^{\ast}=\max_{i=1,2}G(\omega_{i})
, and is related to
G
by

\log|\phi(z)|=G(z)\qquad\text{if}\ G(z)>G^{\ast}.

In particular, if
K
is connected, then
G^{\ast}=0
and
\phi:\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus K\to\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
is a conformal isomorphism. In this case for each
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
the smooth curve
R(\theta)=R_{P}(\theta):=\phi^{-1}(\{\mathrm{e}^{s+2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta}:s>0\})
is called the
external ray
(or the
dynamic ray
) of
P
at angle
\theta
.

The external rays of
P
can also be defined when
K
is disconnected. In this case the equipotential curves
G=\text{const.}>0
form a singular foliation of the basin of infinity
\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus K
and the field lines of
\nabla G
form an orthogonal singular foliation. We denote by
R(\theta)
the maximally extended smooth field line of
\nabla G
given by
s\mapsto\phi^{-1}(\mathrm{e}^{s+2\pi\mathrm{i}\theta})
for large
s
. This naturally parametrizes
R(\theta)
by the potential, so for each
\theta
there is an
s(\theta)\geq 0
such that
G(R(\theta,s))=s
for all
s>s(\theta)
. The set
N=N_{P}\subset\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
of angles
\theta
for which
s(\theta)>0
is countable, dense and backward-invariant under
\mathbf{m}_{3}
. For
\theta\notin N
the field line
R(\theta)
extends all the way to the Julia set, just like the ordinary external rays in the connected case. For
\theta\in N
the field line
R(\theta)
crashes into a singularity of
\nabla G
at the potential
s(\theta)>0
and there is more than one way to extend it to a curve consisting of field lines and singularities on which
G
defines a homeomorphism onto
]0,+\infty[
. But there are always two special extensions:
R^{+}(\theta)=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow\theta}R(\alpha)
which turns immediate right and
R^{-}(\theta)=\lim_{\alpha\uparrow\theta}R(\alpha)
which turns immediate left at each singularity they meet (here the angle
\alpha
tends to
\theta
avoiding the exceptional set
N
). These are called the
broken external rays
at angle
\theta
. We refer to
[
PZ2
]
for a comprehensive treatment of the external rays of disconnected Julia sets.

As a concrete example of the disconnected case, consider the situation where
\omega_{1}\in K
but
\omega_{2}\notin K
. Then there is a unique angle
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
such that the rays
R(\theta_{0}\pm 1/3)
crash into
\omega_{2}
(compare Fig.
6
left). In this case, the exceptional set
N
will consist of all angles
\theta
for which
\mathbf{m}_{3}^{\circ n}(\theta)=\theta_{0}\pm 1/3
for some
n\geq 0
.

For
z_{0}\in J=\partial K
we denote by
A_{z_{0}}
the set of angles
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
for which
R(\theta)
(if
\theta\notin N)
or one of
R^{\pm}(\theta)
(if
\theta\in N
) lands at
z_{0}
. Every periodic
\theta
belongs to
A_{z_{0}}
for some repelling or parabolic point
z_{0}
whose period under
P
divides that of
\theta
under
\mathbf{m}_{3}
. Conversely, if
z_{0}
is a repelling or parabolic point of period
k
, then
A_{z_{0}}
is non-empty and
\mathbf{m}_{3}^{\circ k}:A_{z_{0}}\to A_{z_{0}}
has a well-defined rotation number
\rho
. Moreover, the following dichotomy holds: Either
\rho
is rational in which case
A_{z_{0}}
is a union of finitely many cycles of the same length, or
\rho
is irrational in which case
A_{z_{0}}
is a minimal Cantor set. The first alternative always happens when
K
is connected or more generally when the connected component of
K
containing
z_{0}
is not a single point (see
[
DH1
]
or
[
M1
]
for the connected case and
[
LP
]
or
[
PZ2
]
for the disconnected case).

4.2.
The lemon family: dynamical plane

Consider the complex line
\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)\subset\mathcal{P}(3)
consisting of all cubics
P_{a,b}
for which the critical point
\omega_{1}=0
is fixed, that is, those in the normal form

P_{a}:=P_{a,0}:z\mapsto z^{3}+3az^{2}\qquad(a\in\mathbb{C})

(for simplicity we use the subscript
a
instead of
P_{a}
for the objects associated with the cubics in
\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
). The connectedness locus of this family is shown in Fig.
7
. The prominent central region
\mathcal{H}_{0}
in this figure, resembling the shape of a lemon, consists of all
a\in\mathbb{C}
for which the critical point
\omega_{2}=-2a
belongs to the immediate basin of attraction of
0
(equivalently, the Julia set
J_{a}
is a Jordan curve). We refer to
\{P_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{C}}
as the
lemon family
of cubics.

The study of the slice
\operatorname{Per}_{1}(0)
appears in the works of several authors including Faught
[
F
]
, Milnor
[
M4
]
, and Roesch
[
R1
,
R2
]
. Our brief description in the dynamical plane is closest to
[
M4
]
but differs in some details in order to incorporate the notion of simulating orbits from §
3.2
into the picture.

Fix some
a\neq 0
. The immediate basin of attraction of
0
is a Jordan domain
B_{a}
. There is a canonical Böttcher coordinate
\beta_{a}
which is defined and holomorphic near
0
and satisfies

(4.2)

\beta_{a}(P_{a}(z))=(\beta_{a}(z))^{2}.

The symmetry
P_{-a}(z)=-P_{a}(-z)
implies

(4.3)

\beta_{-a}(z)=\beta_{a}(-z).

We distinguish three cases depending on the position of
\omega_{2}=-2a
:

\bullet

Case 1
.
\omega_{2}\in B_{a}
. Then
P_{a}:B_{a}\to B_{a}
is a degree 3 branched covering and the filled Julia set
K_{a}=\overline{B_{a}}
is a closed topological disk. There is a largest Jordan domain in
B_{a}
containing
0
which maps conformally by
\beta_{a}
to a round disk
|z|<r<1
and contains
\omega_{2}
on its boundary. However,
\beta_{a}
extends analytically to a neighborhood of
\omega_{2}
using the conjugacy relation (
4.2
) (making
\omega_{2}
a critical point of this extension).

\bullet

Case 2
.
\omega_{2}\in K_{a}\smallsetminus B_{a}
. Then
\beta_{a}:B_{a}\to\mathbb{D}
is a conformal isomorphism. This allows a parametrization
z_{a}(t):=\beta_{a}^{-1}(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}t})
of the Jordan curve
\partial B_{a}
by the
internal angle
t\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
which satisfies

(4.4)

P_{a}(z_{a}(t))=z_{a}(\mathbf{m}_{2}(t)).

In this case
K_{a}
is the union of the closed topological disk
\overline{B_{a}}
together with a countable collection of disjoint continua called the
(dynamical) limbs
of
K_{a}
. Each limb meets
\partial B_{a}
at a single point called its
root
. We denote by
L_{a}(t)
the limb with the root
z_{a}(t)
. The countable set of roots is backward-invariant under the degree
2
covering map
P_{a}:\partial B_{a}\to\partial B_{a}
. More precisely, there is a unique internal angle
\tau_{a}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
with the following properties:

(i)

z_{a}(t)
is a root if and only if
\mathbf{m}_{2}^{\circ n}(t)=\tau_{a}
for some
n\geq 0
.

(ii)

L_{a}(\tau_{a})
contains
\omega_{2}
. Under
P_{a}
the limb
L_{a}(t)
maps homeomorphically to
L_{a}(\mathbf{m}_{2}(t))
if
t\neq\tau_{a}
, and to the entire filled Julia set
K_{a}
if
t=\tau_{a}
.

Compare Fig.
5
. Because of (ii), we call
L_{a}(\tau_{a})
the
critical limb
and
\tau_{a}
the
critical internal angle
. Note that
\omega_{2}
can be the root of
L_{a}(\tau_{a})
(see Corollary
4.1
below).

By
[
R1
]
, for each
\epsilon>0
there are at most finitely many limbs
L_{a}(t)
with Euclidean diameter
>\epsilon
. This property can be used to show that every non-root point on
\partial B_{a}
is the landing point of a unique external ray while every root point on
\partial B_{a}
has at least two external rays landing on it (see
[
Z
, §5.2]
for details of this argument in a very similar case). Below we will show that each root has precisely two external rays landing on it.

The external rays landing at a root
z_{a}(t)
divide the plane into finitely many open sectors. By definition, the
(dynamical) wake

W_{a}(t)
is the complement of the closure of the sector containing
B_{a}
. Notice that
W_{a}(t)
contains
L_{a}(t)\smallsetminus\{z_{a}(t)\}
and distinct roots have disjoint wakes. The wake
W_{a}(t)
is bounded by two rays
R_{a}(x),R_{a}(y)
landing at
z_{a}(t)
which we always label counterclockwise, so
R_{a}(\theta)\subset W_{a}(t)
if and only if
\theta\in\,]x,y[
. We think of the length of the interval
[x,y]
as the
angular size
of
W_{a}(t)
. The critical wake
W_{a}(\tau_{a})
can be described as the unique wake whose angular size is
\geq 1/3
. The analogue of the property (ii) above holds for wakes: Under
P_{a}
the wake
W_{a}(t)
maps conformally to
W_{a}(\mathbf{m}_{2}(t))
if
t\neq\tau_{a}
, and to the entire plane if
t=\tau_{a}
.

Corollary 4.1
.

If the critical internal angle
\tau_{a}
is not periodic under
\mathbf{m}_{2}
, then
z_{a}(\tau_{a})=\omega_{2}
and every root is the landing point of precisely two external rays.

Proof.

The forward
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit of
\mathbf{m}_{2}(\tau_{a})
never hits
\tau_{a}
, hence
z_{a}(\mathbf{m}_{2}(\tau_{a}))
is not a root and has a unique ray landing on it. Since there are at least two rays landing at
z_{a}(\tau_{a})
, it follows that
z_{a}(\tau_{a})
is a critical point. The fact that the local degree of
P_{a}
at
z_{a}(\tau_{a})
is
2
now shows that there are precisely two rays landing there, and therefore two rays landing at all other roots since they are all non-critical.
∎

Every periodic point

of period

has combinatorial rotation number zero since the internal ray of angle

landing at

is invariant under

. It follows in particular that only the external rays at angles

or

can land at the fixed point
z_{a}(0)
. After conjugating
P_{a}
by

if necessary (that is, replacing
P_{a}
with

), we may assume that
P_{a}
is
fixed-ray normalized
in the following sense: The external ray
R_{a}(0)
lands at
z_{a}(0)
, and if both
R_{a}(0),R_{a}(1/2)
land at
z_{a}(0)
, then the wake
W_{a}(0)
corresponds to the interval
]0,1/2[
(as opposed to
]1/2,1[
).

Let us now discuss the case where the critical internal angle
\tau_{a}
is periodic of some period
q
under
\mathbf{m}_{2}
. If
q=1
then
\tau_{a}=0
and the critical wake

is bounded by the rays

landing at the fixed point
z_{a}(0)
. Notice that

are the simulating angles of

in the sense of §
3.2
. If

then
\tau_{a}\neq 0
and by our normalization

lands at
z_{a}(0)
but
R_{a}(1/2)
does not. Label the
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit of
\tau_{a}
in positive cyclic order as

, so
t_{k}=\tau_{a}
for some
1\leq k\leq q
. The points
z_{i}:=z_{a}(t_{i})\in\partial B_{a}
form a repelling or parabolic orbit of period

. Let
A_{i}
denote the finite set of angles of external rays that land on
z_{i}
. As the

have combinatorial rotation number zero under

, the iterate
\mathbf{m}_{3}^{\circ q}
acts as the identity map on each

. It follows that all orbits in

have length

with the same combinatorics under

as that of
\tau_{a}
under

. For each

with

. Since the critical wake

maps over itself, it must contain the fixed ray

by the choice of

now shows that

6.
A closer look at the co-landing orbit

Suppose
P\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\operatorname{cen}}(t)
and
\chi_{\operatorname{cen}}(P)=c
. When
c
is in the main cardioid of the Mandelbrot set, the co-landing orbit
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
of
P
has period
q
and each
z_{i}
is the landing point of precisely two external rays at angles
x_{i},y_{i}
. When
c
is outside the main cardioid, this picture may be violated in two mutually exclusive ways: The orbit
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
may have a third cycle of rays landing on it, or it may coalesce into an orbit of lower period. These cases will be studied in §
6.2
and §
6.3
, respectively. As both situations can be directly translated into the dynamical plane of
Q_{c}
, we begin by recalling a few relevant facts about periodic orbits of quadratic polynomials and the external rays landing on them. These descriptions have been known for the most part since the seminal work of Douady and Hubbard
[
DH1
]
, but our brief presentation is closer to Milnor’s in
[
M3
]
to which we refer for further details.

6.1.
Quadratic portraits and the Mandelbrot set

Let
c\in\mathcal{M}
and
Z
be a repelling or parabolic orbit of
Q_{c}
of period
p
. For each
w\in Z
consider the finite set
A_{w}\subset\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
of angles
t
such that the ray
R_{c}(t)
lands at
w
. In the language of
[
M3
]
the collection
\{A_{w}:w\in Z\}
is the
portrait
associated with
Z
. Each restriction
\mathbf{m}_{2}:A_{w}\to A_{Q_{c}(w)}
is an order-preserving bijection, so the first return map
\mathbf{m}_{2}^{\circ p}:A_{w}\to A_{w}
has a well-defined (combinatorial) rotation number
\rho
independent of
w
. It follows that
\bigcup_{w\in Z}A_{w}
is a disjoint union of
N\geq 1
cycles of common period
q=rp
for some
r\geq 1
. Thus, the cardinality of each
A_{w}
is
Nr
and the rotation number
\rho
is a reduced fraction of the form
s/r
with
0\leq s<r
. The portrait of
Z
is necessarily of one of the following types:

1.
Trivial
:
r=N=1\Rightarrow p=q,\rho=0
. In this case the orbit period equals the ray period and each
w\in Z
is the landing point of a unique external ray.

2.
Primitive
:
r=1,N=2\Rightarrow p=q,\rho=0
. Again the orbit period equals the ray period and each
w\in Z
is the landing point of precisely two external rays belonging to different cycles.

3.
Satellite
:
r\geq 2,N=1\Rightarrow p<q,\rho\neq 0
. In this case the orbit period is a proper divisor of the ray period and each
w\in Z
is the landing point of
r
external rays all of which belong to the same cycle.

Every non-zero periodic angle
t\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
under
\mathbf{m}_{2}
has a unique
\mathcal{M}
-
partner
, i.e., an angle
\hat{t}\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
of the same period such that the parameter rays
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{M}}(t),\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{M}}(\hat{t})
co-land. Their common landing point
c(t)=c(\hat{t})
is then the root of a unique hyperbolic component of
\mathcal{M}
. The connected component of
\mathcal{M}\smallsetminus\{c(t)\}
not containing
c=0
union the root point
c(t)
defines the Mandelbrot limb
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}(t,\hat{t})
which is characterized by the following condition: For
c\in\mathcal{M}
the dynamic rays
R_{c}(t),R_{c}(\hat{t})
co-land at a repelling point of
Q_{c}
if and only if
c\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}(t,\hat{t})\smallsetminus\{c(t)\}
and at a parabolic point of
Q_{c}
if and only if
c=c(t)
. The portrait of the
Q_{c}
-orbit
Z
of this common landing point is non-trivial and its
characteristic arc
, i.e., the shortest arc among all components of
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\smallsetminus A_{w}
as
w
runs through
Z
, is bounded by
t,\hat{t}
. Conversely, if the portrait of a periodic orbit
Z
of some
Q_{c}
is non-trivial, it has a well-defined characteristic arc bounded by a pair
t,\hat{t}
of
\mathcal{M}
-partners, and
c\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}(t,\hat{t})
.

Every periodic orbit
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
under
\mathbf{m}_{2}
with period
q>1
contains at most one pair of
\mathcal{M}
-partners. In other words, if
\hat{t}_{i}
denotes the
\mathcal{M}
-partner of
t_{i}
, then the relation
\hat{t}_{i}=t_{j}
can hold for at most one unordered pair
i,j
. Let
c\in\mathcal{M}
and consider the set
Z
of the landing points of
R_{c}(t_{1}),\ldots,R_{c}(t_{q})
. If
c\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}(t_{i},\hat{t}_{i})
for some
i
, the dynamic rays
R_{c}(t_{i}),R_{c}(\hat{t}_{i})
co-land and the portrait of
Z
is primitive if
t_{i},\hat{t}_{i}
belong to different
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbits and is satellite if
t_{i},\hat{t}_{i}
belong to the same
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit. Conversely, if the portrait of
Z
is non-trivial, then
c\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}(t_{i},\hat{t}_{i})
as we pointed out above.

We summarize the above observations in the following

Corollary 6.1
.

Let
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
be a periodic orbit of
\mathbf{m}_{2}
with period
q>1
and for each
1\leq i\leq q
let
\hat{t}_{i}
be the unique
\mathcal{M}
-partner of
t_{i}
. Take
c\in\mathcal{M}
and consider the set
Z
of the landing points of the dynamic rays
R_{c}(t_{1}),\ldots,R_{c}(t_{q})
. Then the portrait of
Z
is

\bullet
primitive if
c\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}(t_{i},\hat{t}_{i})
for some
i
and
t_{i},\hat{t}_{i}
belong to different
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbits;

\bullet
satellite if
c\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}(t_{i},\hat{t}_{i})
for some
i
and
t_{i},\hat{t}_{i}
belong to the same
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit;

\bullet
trivial otherwise.

6.2.
External angles of the co-landing orbit

For each
P\in\mathcal{L}(t)
the two cycles of external rays with angles in
\mathcal{O}_{k}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\}
and
\mathcal{O}_{k-1}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}\}
co-land on the orbit
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
. It is known that the number of distinct cycles of external rays that land on a periodic orbit of a polynomial of degree
D
is at most
D
(in fact, the sharp bound is one plus the number of critical values of the polynomial; see
[
M3
]
for the quadratic case already encountered in §
6.1
, and
[
K
]
for the higher degree case). It follows that in our cubic case there can be at most one additional cycle of rays landing on
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
. The following lemma describes when such third cycle exists for centrally renormalizable cubics:

Theorem 6.4

(Third cycle comes from central primitive portraits)
.

Let
P\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\operatorname{cen}}(t)
and
\chi_{\operatorname{cen}}(P)=c
. Take any hybrid equivalence
\varphi
between
P:U\to U^{\prime}
and the quadratic
Q_{c}
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

There is a third cycle of rays landing on
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
.

(ii)

The orbit
\{\varphi(z_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(z_{q})\}
of
Q_{c}
has a primitive portrait.

(iii)

c\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}(t_{i},\hat{t}_{i})
for some
1\leq i\leq q
, where
t_{i},\hat{t}_{i}
do not belong to the same
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit.

The condition (iii) puts
c
in the union of either
q-1
or
q
Mandelbrot limbs according as the
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
contains a pair of
\mathcal{M}
-partners or not (see §
6.1
). Note also that these equivalent conditions imply that
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
has exact period
q
, so no merging in the co-landing orbit can occur.

Proof.

As the equivalence (ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii) is part of Corollary
6.1
, it suffices to verify (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii). Consider the semiconjugacy
\Pi:I\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
of Theorem
5.4
associated with
P:U\to U^{\prime}
and normalized by
\Pi(0)=0
. By (the proof of) Lemma
5.5
,
\Pi(x_{i})=\Pi(y_{i})=t_{i}
and
R_{c}(t_{i})
lands at
w_{i}:=\varphi(z_{i})
for every
1\leq i\leq q
.

Let us first prove (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). Suppose the portrait of
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{q}\}
is primitive. Then every
w_{i}
is the landing point of exactly two external rays
R_{c}(t_{i})
and

, where the
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbits
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
and
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{q}\}
are disjoint (notice that this choice of labeling no longer guarantees that the

are in positive cyclic order). Choose

and define
u_{\sigma^{i}(1)}:=\mathbf{m}_{3}^{\circ i}(u_{1})
, so
\Pi(u_{i})=s_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq q
. Then
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{q}\}
is an
\mathbf{m}_{3}
-orbit of period
q
disjoint from
\mathcal{O}_{k}\cup\mathcal{O}_{k-1}
. By Theorem
5.4
, for every

the external ray
R(u_{i})
lands at
z_{i}
.

Let us now prove (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). Suppose there is a third cycle of rays
R(u_{1}),\ldots,R(u_{q})
such that
R(u_{i})
lands at
z_{i}
for every

. Setting
s_{i}:=\Pi(u_{i})
, it follows from Theorem
5.4
that
R_{c}(s_{i})
lands at
w_{i}
(again, keep in mind that the
u_{i}
or

are no longer in positive cyclic order). We need to check that
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{q}\}
is an
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit of period

disjoint from
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
.

Set
\mathcal{O}:=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{q}\}
. First we observe that

(6.1)

\mathcal{O}\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}\,]x_{i},y_{i}[=\emptyset.

In fact, if this fails, by taking a suitable iterate under
\mathbf{m}_{3}
we can find some

. As

. Thus

, which in turn shows that every

. It follows that

and

or

),

, which is a contradiction.

Now suppose the orbit

coincides with

. Then we can re-label the angles in

as

for every

Theorem 6.8

(Combinatorial characterization of merging)
.

There is a cubic
P\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\operatorname{cen}}(t)
whose co-landing orbit
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
merges into an orbit of period
<q
if and only if the combinatorics
\sigma\in S_{q}
of the
\mathbf{m}_{2}
-orbit of
t
is dynamically reducible.

Sketch of the proof.

As noted above, the existence of such
P
implies that
\sigma
is dynamically reducible. Conversely, suppose that for some proper divisor
p=q/r
of
q

\sigma^{p}
is the product
\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{p}
of unlinked rotation
r
-cycles with a common rotation number. We can then form the associated “formal portrait” in the sense of
[
M3
]
on the set
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{q}\}
in which the angles corresponding to each
\pi_{j}
are grouped in one class. The characteristic arc of this portrait determines a Mandelbrot limb in which the portrait is realized by the rays
R_{c}(t_{1}),\ldots,R_{c}(t_{q})

P\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\operatorname{cen}}(t)
with
\chi_{\operatorname{cen}}(P)
within this Mandelbrot limb (whose existence is guaranteed by Theorem

) will have a merged co-landing orbit.
∎

Remark 6.9
.

The decomposition of a dynamically reducible cycle into unlinked cycles of shorter lengths is unique. Hence the corresponding portrait (and therefore the pattern of merging) is uniquely determined by
\sigma
.

7.
Injectivity of straightening

The goal of this section is to prove the following

Theorem 7.1
.

The straightening map
\bm{\chi}:\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)\to\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}\times\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
is injective.

Given
P,\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
with
\bm{\chi}(P)=\bm{\chi}(\tilde{P})
, there are natural hybrid equivalences between the central and peripheral renormalizations of
P
and
\tilde{P}
that can be glued together to obtain a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\Phi_{0}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
on the entire plane which partially conjugates
P
to
\tilde{P}
. A standard pull-back argument then allows to promote
\Phi_{0}
to a sequence
\Phi_{n}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
of uniformly quasiconformal homeomorphisms which conjugate
P
to
\tilde{P}
on larger and larger domains. The limit
\Phi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi_{n}
is a global quasiconformal conjugacy and satisfies
\bar{\partial}\Phi=0
a.e. outside the
residual Julia set
of
P
defined by

K_{\infty}:=K\smallsetminus\bigcup_{n\geq 0}P^{-n}(K_{\operatorname{cen}}\cup K_{\operatorname{per}}).

The main technical point then is to show that
K_{\infty}
has measure zero, for that would imply
\Phi
is an affine map.

The construction of
\Phi
is standard. Take
P,\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
and let
\phi,\tilde{\phi}
and
G,\tilde{G}
denote their Böttcher coordinates and Green’s functions. If
\bm{\chi}(P)=\bm{\chi}(\tilde{P})
, the quadratic-like restriction pairs
P:U\to U^{\prime},\tilde{P}:\tilde{U}\to\tilde{U}^{\prime}
and
P^{\circ q}:V\to V^{\prime},\tilde{P}^{\circ q}:\tilde{V}\to\tilde{V}^{\prime}
are hybrid equivalent. Recall from §
5.2
that
U,U^{\prime}
and
V,V^{\prime}
are obtained by slightly thickening the open sets
U_{0},U^{\prime}_{0}
and
V_{0},V^{\prime}_{0}
. We have corresponding pairs of open sets
\tilde{U}_{0},\tilde{U}^{\prime}_{0}
and
\tilde{V}_{0},\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{0}
for
\tilde{P}
. There is a hybrid equivalence
\varphi:U_{0}^{\prime}\to\tilde{U}^{\prime}_{0}
conjugating
P
to
\tilde{P}
which we may assume satisfies
\phi=\tilde{\phi}\circ\varphi
on
\partial U_{0}^{\prime}
. Similarly, there is a hybrid equivalence
\psi:V_{0}^{\prime}\to\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{0}
conjugating
P^{\circ q}
to
\tilde{P}^{\circ q}
which satisfies
\phi=\tilde{\phi}\circ\psi
on
\partial V^{\prime}_{0}
. Define a homeomorphism
\Phi_{0}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\Phi_{0}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\phi}^{-1}\circ\phi&\quad\text{on}\ G^{-1}([s,\infty[),\\
\varphi&\quad\text{on}\ U^{\prime}_{0},\\
\psi&\quad\text{on}\ V^{\prime}_{0},\\
\tilde{P}^{-i}\circ\psi\circ P^{\circ i}&\quad\text{on}\ W_{\sigma^{-i}(k)}\cap G^{-1}([0,s[),\ 1\leq i\leq q-1.\end{cases}

Here
\tilde{P}^{-i}
in the last line is the inverse branch which maps the wake
\tilde{W}_{k}
of
\tilde{P}
to
\tilde{W}_{\sigma^{-i}(k)}
. Evidently
\Phi_{0}
is an
M
-quasiconformal homeomorphism for some
M>1
and satisfies
\bar{\partial}\Phi_{0}=0
a.e. on

G^{-1}([s,\infty[)\cup K_{\operatorname{cen}}\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{q-1}P^{\circ i}(K_{\operatorname{per}}).

Since
\Phi_{0}
conjugates
P
and
\tilde{P}
on their respective postcritical sets, we can define
\Phi_{n}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
inductively by lifting
\Phi_{n-1}\circ P
under
\tilde{P}
, so
\Phi_{n}
is
M
-quasiconformal, coincides with
\Phi_{n-1}
on the set

G^{-1}([3^{-n}s,\infty[)\cup\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{n-1}P^{-\ell}(K_{\operatorname{cen}}\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{q-1}P^{\circ i}(K_{\operatorname{per}})),

and satisfies
\bar{\partial}\Phi_{n}=0
a.e. there. Thus, after passing to a subsequence,
\{\Phi_{n}\}
converges to an
M
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
which conjugates
P
to
\tilde{P}
everywhere and satisfies
\bar{\partial}\Phi=0
a.e. on the set

(\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus K)\cup\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{\infty}P^{-\ell}(K_{\operatorname{cen}}\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{q-1}P^{\circ i}(K_{\operatorname{per}}))=\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus K_{\infty}.

Assuming
K_{\infty}
has measure zero, it follows that
\Phi
is conformal a.e. in
\mathbb{C}
, hence is an affine map. Since
\Phi
is tangent to the identity at
\infty
and
\Phi(0)=0
, we must have
\Phi=\operatorname{id}
and
P=\tilde{P}
.

We now address the measure zero statement that was essential in the above argument:

Theorem 7.2
.

For every
P\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
the residual Julia set
K_{\infty}
has Lebesgue measure
0
.

Compare
[
IK
, Lemma 6.1]
where a similar result is proved in a more general and abstract setting. Below we give a concrete independent proof based on hyperbolic geometry estimates in Lemma
7.5
. See also
[
EY
, Theorem 6.1]
for the special case of intertwining cubics with a restriction on their quadratic renormalizations.

Let
\Omega_{0}
denote the complement of the compact invariant set
K_{\operatorname{cen}}\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{q-1}P^{\circ i}(K_{\operatorname{per}})
. Evidently
\Omega_{0}
is a backward-invariant domain which avoids the postcritical set of
P
. For
n\geq 1
set
\Omega_{n}:=P^{-n}(\Omega_{0})
. Then
\Omega_{n}\subsetneq\Omega_{n-1}
and each
\Omega_{n}
is conformally isomorphic to the punctured disk, the puncture being at
\infty
. For simplicity we will write
\lambda_{n}
for the hyperbolic metric
\lambda_{\Omega_{n}}
,
B_{n}(w,r)
for the hyperbolic ball
B_{\Omega_{n}}(w,r)
, and
\operatorname{area}_{n}(\cdot)
for the hyperbolic area in
\Omega_{n}
. By the Schwarz lemma,
\lambda_{n}>\lambda_{n-1}
in
\Omega_{n}
and in particular
\lambda_{n}>\lambda_{0}
in
\Omega_{n}
. Since the restriction
P^{\circ n}:(\Omega_{n},\lambda_{n})\to(\Omega_{0},\lambda_{0})
is a covering map and therefore a local isometry, it follows that
P^{\circ n}:(\Omega_{n},\lambda_{0})\to(\Omega_{0},\lambda_{0})
is expanding (although not uniformly).

The residual Julia set
K_{\infty}
is the set of points in
J=\partial K
which stay in
\Omega_{0}
under the iterations of
P
:

K_{\infty}=J\cap\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\Omega_{n}.

Recall that for each
1\leq i\leq q
,
W_{i}
is the dynamical wake bounded by the pair
R(x_{i}),R(y_{i})
co-landing at
z_{i}
and
W^{\prime}_{k}
is the sub-wake of

bounded by the pair

. Observe that

.

Lemma 7.3
.

There are constants
0<\epsilon<r
such that for every

Proof.

Set
j:=\sigma(k)

\rho:=(P^{\circ q})^{\prime}(z_{j})

\psi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}

\psi(0)=z_{j}

\psi(\rho z)=P^{\circ q}(\psi(z))

\psi^{-1}(P(K_{\operatorname{per}})),\psi^{-1}(R(x_{j})),\psi^{-1}(R(y_{j}))

\Omega:=\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus E
and define

\Omega

\gamma_{x},\gamma_{y}

\Omega,W
as well as the rays
\gamma_{x},\gamma_{y}
are invariant under the linear map
z\mapsto\rho z
.

A small round disk
D

0
maps isomorphically under
\psi
to a small neighborhood of
z_{j}
, which in turn maps isomorphically under a local branch
g
of
P^{-1}
to a small neighborhood
D^{\prime}
of

g\circ\psi:D\to D^{\prime}

\Omega\cap D
to
\Omega_{0}\cap D^{\prime}
and

\Omega

, it suffices to prove the existence of constants

such that for every

B_{\Omega}(w,r)
contains a ball
B_{\Omega}(u,\epsilon)
disjoint from
\psi^{-1}(K)
. This is easy to see once we pass to the quotient space
\tilde{\Omega}:=\Omega/\langle z\mapsto\rho z\rangle
which is a conformal cylinder (of modulus less than

. The rays
\gamma_{x},\gamma_{y}
project to two homotopically non-trivial loops
\tilde{\gamma}_{x},\tilde{\gamma}_{y}

\tilde{\gamma}_{x},\tilde{\gamma}_{y}
have annular neighborhoods of modulus

of

\delta>0

\tilde{W}
in
\tilde{\Omega}

\tilde{W}
, hence it contains the ball

for every

.

For the proof of Theorem

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
X\subsetneq Y
be hyperbolic Riemann surfaces such that the inclusion
i:X\hookrightarrow Y
induces an injection
\pi_{1}(X)\to\pi_{1}(Y)
. Then
\log\|i^{\prime}\|=\log(\lambda_{Y}/\lambda_{X}):X\to\,]-\infty,0[
is a Lipschitz function (with constant
2
).

Proof.

Fix
x_{0},x_{1}\in X
, take holomorphic universal covering maps
p_{X}:(\mathbb{D},0)\to(X,x_{0})
and
p_{Y}:(\mathbb{D},0)\to(Y,x_{0})
, and lift
i
to a holomorphic map
f:(\mathbb{D},0)\to(\mathbb{D},0)
. By the assumption on the fundamental groups,
f
is injective. Take a minimal geodesic in
X
joining
x_{0}
to
x_{1}
and lift it under
p_{X}
to a geodesic in
\mathbb{D}
joining
0
to some
w
. Then
\operatorname{dist}_{X}(x_{0},x_{1})=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{D}}(0,w)=\log((1+|w|)/(1-|w|))
. Now use Koebe’s distortion theorem to estimate

\displaystyle\frac{\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|}{\|i^{\prime}(x_{1})\|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|f^{\prime}(0)\|}{\|f^{\prime}(w)\|}=\frac{\lambda_{\mathbb{D}}(w)}{\lambda_{\mathbb{D}}(f(w))}\ \frac{|f^{\prime}(0)|}{|f^{\prime}(w)|}=\frac{1-|f(w)|^{2}}{1-|w|^{2}}\ \frac{|f^{\prime}(0)|}{|f^{\prime}(w)|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-|w|^{2}}\ \frac{(1+|w|)^{3}}{1-|w|}=\left(\frac{1+|w|}{1-|w|}\right)^{2}=\exp(2\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{D}}(0,w)).

Taking logarithms gives

\log\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|-\log\|i^{\prime}(x_{1})\|\leq 2\operatorname{dist}_{X}(x_{0},x_{1}),

and the result follows by symmetry.
∎

Lemma 7.5
.

In the situation of Lemma
7.4
, fix
x_{0}\in X
and let

be smaller than the injectivity radius of
X
at
x_{0}
, so the ball
B_{X}(x_{0},r)

(i)

For every

there is a constant
C(r,\epsilon)>0
such that if
B_{X}(x,\epsilon)\subset B_{X}(x_{0},r)
, then

\frac{\operatorname{area}_{Y}(B_{X}(x,\epsilon))}{\operatorname{area}_{Y}(B_{X}(x_{0},r))}\geq C(r,\epsilon).\vskip 6.0pt

(ii)

The ball
B_{X}(x_{0},r)
has bounded geometry in

in the following sense:

\displaystyle r_{\operatorname{\,in}}

\displaystyle:=\max\{s:B_{Y}(x_{0},s)\subset B_{X}(x_{0},r)\}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\,(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2r})\,\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|,

\displaystyle r_{\operatorname{out}}

\displaystyle:=\min\,\{s:B_{Y}(x_{0},s)\supset B_{X}(x_{0},r)\}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\,(\mathrm{e}^{2r}-1)\ \ \|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|.

In particular,
r_{\operatorname{\,in}}/r_{\operatorname{out}}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-2r}
.

Proof.

We will use the following inequalities given by Lemma
7.4
:

\mathrm{e}^{-2\operatorname{dist}_{X}(z,x_{0})}\leq\frac{\|i^{\prime}(z)\|}{\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|}\leq\mathrm{e}^{2\operatorname{dist}_{X}(z,x_{0})}\qquad\text{for all}\ z\in X.

(i) We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{area}_{Y}(B_{X}(x,\epsilon))

\displaystyle=\iint_{B_{X}(x,\epsilon)}\lambda_{Y}^{2}(z)\,|dz|^{2}=\iint_{B_{X}(x,\epsilon)}\|i^{\prime}(z)\|^{2}\lambda_{X}^{2}(z)\,|dz|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-4r}\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|^{2}\operatorname{area}_{X}(B_{X}(x,\epsilon)),

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{area}_{Y}(B_{X}(x_{0},r))

\displaystyle=\iint_{B_{X}(x_{0},r)}\lambda_{Y}^{2}(z)\,|dz|^{2}=\iint_{B_{X}(x_{0},r)}\|i^{\prime}(z)\|^{2}\lambda_{X}^{2}(z)\,|dz|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{4r}\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|^{2}\operatorname{area}_{X}(B_{X}(x_{0},r)).

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{area}_{Y}(B_{X}(x,\epsilon))}{\operatorname{area}_{Y}(B_{X}(x_{0},r))}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-8r}\ \frac{\operatorname{area}_{X}(B_{X}(x,\epsilon))}{\operatorname{area}_{X}(B_{X}(x_{0},r))}=\mathrm{e}^{-8r}\ \frac{\operatorname{area}_{\mathbb{D}}(B_{\mathbb{D}}(0,\epsilon))}{\operatorname{area}_{\mathbb{D}}(B_{\mathbb{D}}(0,r))}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-8r}\,\frac{\cosh(\epsilon)-1}{\cosh(r)-1}=:C(r,\epsilon).\vskip 6.0pt

(ii) Find
p,q\in\partial B_{X}(x_{0},r)
such that
\operatorname{dist}_{Y}(x_{0},p)=r_{\operatorname{\,in}}
and
\operatorname{dist}_{Y}(x_{0},q)=r_{\operatorname{out}}
. Take the
Y
-geodesic
\gamma:[0,r^{\prime}]\to\overline{B_{Y}(x_{0},r_{\operatorname{\,in}})}
with
\gamma(0)=x_{0},\gamma(r^{\prime})=p
, parametrized so that
\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|_{X}=\lambda_{X}(\gamma(t))\,|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|=1
for all
t
. Then
\operatorname{dist}_{X}(x_{0},\gamma(t))\leq t
, and in particular
r\leq r^{\prime}
. Hence,

\displaystyle r_{\operatorname{\,in}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r^{\prime}}\lambda_{Y}(\gamma(t))\,|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\ dt=\int_{0}^{r^{\prime}}\|i^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\|\ dt\geq\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|\int_{0}^{r^{\prime}}\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\,dt=\frac{1}{2}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2r})\,\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|.

Similarly, take the
X
-geodesic
\eta:[0,r]\to\overline{B_{X}(x_{0},r)}
with
\eta(0)=x_{0},\eta(r)=q
, parametrized so that
\|\eta^{\prime}(t)\|_{X}=\lambda_{X}(\eta(t))\,|\eta^{\prime}(t)|=1
for all

. Then
\operatorname{dist}_{X}(x_{0},\eta(t))=t
and

\displaystyle r_{\operatorname{out}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{r}\lambda_{Y}(\eta(t))\,|\eta^{\prime}(t)|\ dt=\int_{0}^{r}\|i^{\prime}(\eta(t))\|\ dt\leq\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{2t}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{2r}-1)\,\|i^{\prime}(x_{0})\|.\hfill\qed

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

Assume by way of contradiction that
K_{\infty}
has positive measure. Since the forward orbit of a.e. point in
J
converges to the postcritical set of
P
(see for example
[

, Theorem 3.9]
), we can find a Lebesgue density point
w_{0}\in K_{\infty}
whose orbit
\{w_{n}:=P^{\circ n}(w_{0})\}
converges to the postcritical set. Evidently the orbit
\{w_{n}\}
must visit the sub-wake
W^{\prime}_{k}
infinitely often. Since the postcritical set can meet
\overline{W^{\prime}_{k}}
only at
z^{\prime}_{k}
, it follows that some subsequence of
\{w_{n}\}
converges to
z^{\prime}_{k}
, i.e., there is an increasing sequence
S\subset\mathbb{N}
such that
w_{n}\to z^{\prime}_{k}
as
n\in S
tends to
\infty
. By Lemma
7.3
there are constants
0<\epsilon<r
such that for all large
n\in S
the ball
B_{0}(w_{n},r)
contains a ball of radius
\epsilon
disjoint from
K
. Note that
B_{0}(w_{n},r)
is embedded since
\Omega_{0}
is a disk punctured at
\infty
. Lifting under the covering map
P^{\circ n}:(\Omega_{n},\lambda_{n})\to(\Omega_{0},\lambda_{0})
then shows that the ball
B_{n}(w_{0},r)
is embedded and contains a ball
B_{n}(u_{n},\epsilon)
disjoint from
K
. By Lemma
7.5
(i) (applied to
X=\Omega_{n},Y=\Omega_{0}
),

\frac{\operatorname{area}_{0}(B_{n}(u_{n},\epsilon))}{\operatorname{area}_{0}(B_{n}(w_{0},r))}\geq C(r,\epsilon).

On the other hand, the inclusion
i_{n}:\Omega_{n}\to\Omega_{0}
satisfies
\|i^{\prime}_{n}(w_{0})\|=\lambda_{0}(w_{0})/\lambda_{n}(w_{0})\to 0
since the Euclidean distance between
w_{0}
and
\partial\Omega_{n}
tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
. By Lemma
7.5
(ii),
B_{n}(w_{0},r)
is squeezed between two balls
B_{0}(w_{0},s_{n})
and
B_{0}(w_{0},r_{n})
, where
s_{n}/r_{n}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-2r}
and
0<s_{n}\leq r_{n}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{2r}-1)\,\|i^{\prime}_{n}(w_{0})\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{area}_{0}(B_{n}(u_{n},\epsilon))}{\operatorname{area}_{0}(B_{0}(w_{0},r_{n}))}

\displaystyle\geq C(r,\epsilon)\,\frac{\operatorname{area}_{0}(B_{0}(w_{0},s_{n}))}{\operatorname{area}_{0}(B_{0}(w_{0},r_{n}))}=C(r,\epsilon)\,\frac{\cosh(s_{n})-1}{\cosh(r_{n})-1}

\displaystyle=C(r,\epsilon)\,\frac{s_{n}^{2}(1+O(s_{n}^{2}))}{r_{n}^{2}(1+O(r_{n}^{2}))}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-4r}C(r,\epsilon)

for all large
n\in S
. Since
\lambda_{0}
is asymptotically a multiple of the Euclidean metric near
w_{0}
, it follows that the ball
B_{0}(w_{0},r_{n})
is uniformly round, i.e., has Euclidean area comparable to (Euclidean diameter)
2
, and that

\frac{\operatorname{area}(B_{0}(w_{0},r_{n})\smallsetminus K)}{\operatorname{area}(B_{0}(w_{0},r_{n}))}\geq\frac{\operatorname{area}(B_{n}(u_{n},\epsilon))}{\operatorname{area}(B_{0}(w_{0},r_{n}))}\geq\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{e}^{-4r}C(r,\epsilon)

for all large
n\in S
. This contradicts the hypothesis that
w_{0}
is a density point of
K_{\infty}
.
∎

8.
Continuity of the inverse straightening

So far we have seen that
\bm{\chi}:\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)\to\bm{\chi}(\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t))
is a continuous bijection. The goal of this section is to prove continuity of the inverse of this map:

Theorem 8.1
.

If
P_{n},P_{\infty}\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
and
\bm{\chi}(P_{n})\to\bm{\chi}(P_{\infty})
, then
P_{n}\to P_{\infty}
.

The proof of Theorem
8.1
has two main ingredients. The first is the fact that the combinatorial rotation number of a repelling orbit controls its multiplier. Suppose a degree
d
polynomial map with connected Julia set has a repelling fixed point
\zeta
of multiplier
\lambda
at which
N
cycles of external rays of common combinatorial rotation number
0\leq p/q<1
land. Then, according to
Yoccoz’s inequality
(see
[
Hu
]
and
[
P
]
), there is a suitable branch of
\log\lambda
that satisfies

\frac{|\log\lambda-2\pi\mathrm{i}p/q|^{2}}{\operatorname{Re}(\log\lambda)}\leq\frac{2\log d}{Nq}.

This means that
\log\lambda
belongs to the closed disk of radius
\log d/(Nq)
tangent to the imaginary axis at
2\pi\mathrm{i}p/q
. It follows from this inequality that if
\lambda\to 1
, then
p/q\to 0
. Conversely, if
p/q
remains non-zero and
p/q\to 0
, then
\lambda\to 1
. We invoke this observation in the following

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
f_{n}
be a sequence of degree
d
polynomials with quadratic-like restrictions
f_{n}:U_{n}\to U^{\prime}_{n}
and with both
f_{n},f_{n}|_{U_{n}}
having connected Julia sets. Take a hybrid equivalence
\varphi_{n}
between
f_{n}|_{U_{n}}
and a quadratic polynomial
Q_{a_{n}}
. If
\zeta_{n}
is the
\alpha
-fixed point of
f_{n}|_{U_{n}}
and the multiplier
\lambda_{n}:=f^{\prime}_{n}(\zeta_{n})
tends to
1
as
n\to\infty
, then the same is true of the multiplier
\mu_{n}:=Q^{\prime}_{a_{n}}(\varphi_{n}(\zeta_{n}))
, hence
a_{n}\to 1/4
.

Proof.

As the multiplier of a non-repelling fixed point is invariant under a hybrid equivalence, we may assume that
\zeta_{n}
and therefore
\varphi_{n}(\zeta_{n})
is repelling for every
n
. It follows from
\lambda_{n}\to 1
and Yoccoz’s inequality that the combinatorial rotation number of
\zeta_{n}
tends to
0
, so the same must be true of the
\alpha
-fixed point
\varphi_{n}(\zeta_{n})
of
Q_{a_{n}}
. This combinatorial rotation number cannot be equal to zero since
a_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
. Another application of Yoccoz’s inequality now shows
\mu_{n}\to 1
.
∎

If

\zeta_{n}\to z_{k}
, so
(P_{n}^{\circ q})^{\prime}(\zeta_{n})\to 1
and therefore
\nu_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\nu_{n}=1/4
by Lemma
8.2
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Remark 8.6
.

The proof of Lemma
8.4
shows that for every cubic
P\in\overline{\mathcal{L}(t)}\smallsetminus\mathcal{L}(t)
exactly one of the rays
R(x_{k}),R(y_{k})
lands at a parabolic fixed point
\zeta
of
P^{\circ q}
of multiplier
1
. The other ray necessarily lands at a repelling fixed point of
P^{\circ q}
on the boundary of an immediate basin
B
of
\zeta
having exact period
q
. Moreover, the first-return map
P^{\circ q}:B\to B
is proper of degree
3
or
4
depending on whether both
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
belong to
B
or they belong to different basins in the orbit of
B
. In Milnor’s terminology
[
M4
]
, these cases correspond respectively to
P
being on the boundary of an “adjacent” or “bi-transitive” hyperbolic component in
\mathcal{C}(3)
. The failure of co-landing of
R(x_{k}),R(y_{k})
is caused by the creation of a heteroclinic arc in the Hausdorff limit of one of these rays. This mechanism can be forced, for example, by taking the limit of suitable sequences
P_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
with
\chi_{\operatorname{cen}}(P_{n})\to c(t_{i})
for some
1\leq i\leq q
and
\chi_{\operatorname{per}}(P_{n})\to 1/4
. It seems reasonable to speculate that all cubics of the form described above are in fact in
\overline{\mathcal{L}(t)}\smallsetminus\mathcal{L}(t)
.

9.
Surjectivity of straightening

The final piece in the proof of Theorem
B
is the following

Theorem 9.1

(Surjectivity of
\bm{\chi}
)
.

For every pair
(c,\nu)\in\check{\mathcal{M}}_{t}\times\check{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
there is a cubic polynomial
P\in\mathcal{L}_{\operatorname{ren}}(t)
such that
\chi_{\operatorname{cen}}(P)=c
and
\chi_{\operatorname{per}}(P)=\nu
.

The proof of this theorem is achieved in three steps that can be loosely described as follows:

Step 1.
Start from the quadratic polynomial
Q_{c}
and the unique cubic polynomial
P_{a}\in\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)
which is peripherally renormalizable around the second critical point
\omega_{2}
with a quadratic-like restriction hybrid equivalent to
Q_{\nu}
(Corollary
4.4
). Construct a new holomorphic map
f:\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\overline{\Lambda}\to\mathbb{C}
by changing the external class of
Q_{c}
(i.e., the map
Q_{0}:z\mapsto z^{2}
) to the external class of the quadratic-like map obtained from the central renormalization of
P_{a}
around its superattracting basin
B_{a}
. Here
\Lambda
is the “hole” corresponding to the component
B^{\prime}_{a}
of
P_{a}^{-1}(B_{a})
other than
B_{a}
.

Step 2.
Change the conformal structure on
\Lambda
and all its iterated preimages under
f
if necessary to create a large enough modulus necessary for quasiconformal interpolation.

Step 3.
Restrict to a topological disk bounded by a suitable equipotential curve and perform a quasiconformal interpolation on the sub-wake
W_{k}^{\prime}\subset W_{k}
of
f
containing
\Lambda
to obtain a quasiregular polynomial-like map of degree
3
with a central renormalization hybrid equivalent to
Q_{c}
and a peripheral renormalization hybrid equivalent to
Q_{\nu}
. This quasiregular map has an invariant conformal structure of bounded dilatation, so we can apply the straightening theorem to obtain the desired cubic polynomial
P
.

The full details of the above steps are given in §
9.2
through §
9.5
. The short section §
9.1
recalls some definitions and relevant known facts on quasiconformal interpolation that are needed in the proof of Theorem
9.1
.

9.1.
Dynamical cylinders and interpolation on sectors

We begin with some generalities. Let
f
be a degree
d
polynomial with connected filled Julia set
K
. Suppose
z_{0}
is a repelling point of
f
of period
p
and combinatorial rotation number
s/r
. The external rays landing at
z_{0}
have a common period
q=pr
under

and fall into
N
cycles for some
1\leq N\leq d-1
, forming
Nr
rays altogether. There are
Nr
connected components of

whose intersections with a small neighborhood of

are permuted around by

with combinatorial rotation number

. We may label these rays and components as

and

in positive cyclic order, so

lies between

and

Now take a small Jordan domain

containing

and the biholomorphism

, where

. The quotient

. In particular, the conformal isomorphism class of

is independent of the choice of

. For

the ray tails

and the intersections

and compact sets

in

containing

is a conformal cylinder

of modulus
\operatorname{mod}(C_{j})=\pi/(q\log d)
having
\tilde{R}_{j}
as its core geodesic. All the

(hence the

) are in the same non-trivial isotopy class in

.

. As

.

Let
f,\Sigma
and
\hat{f},\hat{\Sigma}
be as above and
h:\partial\Sigma\to\partial\hat{\Sigma}

\Sigma
diffeomorphically to the corresponding boundary arc of
\hat{\Sigma}
. If

\Sigma\to\hat{\Sigma}
.

9.2.
Modifying the external class of
Q_{c}

Let us return to the three steps of the proof of Theorem
9.1
outlined in the beginning of this section. Step 1 can in fact be performed for any choice of
c\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
P_{a}\in\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)
. As before let
B_{a}\cong\mathbb{D}
be the immediate basin of attraction of

for

and let

denote the connected component of
P_{a}^{-1}(B_{a})
other than
B_{a}

[

. This will also allow us to setup the notation.

The fundamental annulus
\overline{U^{\prime}}\smallsetminus U

on

as follows: On

let

be the pull-back under

of the standard conformal structure on

, and on

be the standard conformal structure. Then

and satisfies

on

and the identity map on
\hat{\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus\overline{B_{a}}
. Notice that the change of coordinates

is biholomorphic by the construction. Evidently
\mathcal{Y}

\Phi:\mathcal{Y}\to\hat{\mathbb{C}}
normalized by the conditions

\Phi\circ h_{a}\circ\Phi^{-1}&\quad\text{on}\ \Phi(\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus P_{a}^{-1}(\overline{B_{a}})).\end{cases}

C_{\Lambda}:=(\Delta\cap\Lambda)/g\Subset C^{\prime}
. By the construction

is conformally isomorphic to the cylinder
(D_{a}\cap B_{a})/P_{a}^{\circ q}

of common modulus

.

We remark that
\operatorname{mod}(C^{\prime})
can be made as large as we wish by changing the conformal structure on

and its preimages under

(this fact will be used later in our quasiconformal interpolation step). Indeed, since

, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{mod}(C_{\Lambda})
can be made arbitrarily large. The idea is of course standard. Let
m>0
and

be any cylinder of modulus

. Take a quasiconformal homeomorphism

. Let
\mu
be the conformal structure on

obtained by pulling back the standard conformal structure of

under

and then lifting under

. Extend

to the union of the iterated
f

and define it to be the standard conformal structure in the rest of the plane. Evidently
\mu
has bounded dilatation, so there is a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\phi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
which integrates
\mu
and is normalized by the conditions
\phi(0)=0,\phi(\infty)=\infty
and

can now be replaced with

which still has the central and peripheral renormalizations hybrid equivalent to

and

, but for which the corresponding cylinder

(and therefore

) for our map

Completion of the proof of Theorem
9.1

The set where

is contained in the union

. Each forward orbit of
F
can meet

at most once because
F({E^{\prime}}_{\!\!{\textrm{odd}}})=E_{\textrm{odd}}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\Sigma_{2i-1}}
, where
E_{\textrm{odd}}\cap{E^{\prime}}_{\!\!{\textrm{odd}}}=\emptyset
and all further iterates of
E_{\textrm{odd}}
under
F
are disjoint from

and contained in the escaping set
\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus K_{f}
. Define a conformal structure
\mu
on
U^{\prime}
by taking the standard conformal structure on
E_{\textrm{odd}}
and extending it to the union
\bigcup_{i\geq 0}F^{-i}(E_{\textrm{odd}})
by iterated pull-backs. On the rest of
U^{\prime}
let
\mu
be the standard conformal structure. Notice that
\mu
is well-defined since the branches of
F^{-q}=f^{-q}
that map

to itself are conformal. Moreover, the iterated branches
F^{-i}
can contain at most one non-conformal branch of

by the above observation. Thus,
\mu
is
F
-invariant and has bounded dilatation.

Let
\Psi:U^{\prime}\to\mathbb{D}
be a quasiconformal homeomorphism that integrates

with say
\Psi(0)=0
. Then

is a polynomial-like map of degree

. Moreover, by the construction of

in

, the map

has a quadratic-like restriction around the critical point

and

Appendix. On the intersection of lemon limbs

Although distinct lemon limbs are disjoint, there could be plenty of non-trivial intersections between a limb
\mathcal{L}(t)
and a rotated limb
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(s)
. This appendix provides some relevant results and examples; they will be mostly independent from the rest of this paper.

Throughout the following discussion we assume
t,s
are periodic angles under
\mathbf{m}_{2}
with periods
q,p\geq 2
and combinatorics
\sigma\in S_{q},\tau\in S_{p}
, respectively. As usual, let
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q}\}
and
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}\}
be the simulating orbits for
t=t_{k}
with simulating angles
x_{k},y_{k}
. Similarly, let
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{p}\}
and
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\}
be the simulating orbits for
s=s_{\ell}
with simulating angles
u_{\ell},v_{\ell}
. Set
I_{i}:=[x_{i},y_{i}]
for
1\leq i\leq q
and
J_{j}:=[u_{j},v_{j}]
for
1\leq j\leq p
. Recall from §
3.2
that
I_{\sigma(k)}
and
J_{\tau(\ell)}
are the shortest intervals in their respective collections with
|I_{\sigma(k)}|=1/(3^{q}-1)
and
|J_{\tau(\ell)}|=1/(3^{p}-1)
. Similarly,
I_{k}
and
J_{\ell}
are the longest intervals in their respective collections with
|I_{k}|=3^{q-1}/(3^{q}-1)
and
|J_{\ell}|=3^{p-1}/(3^{p}-1)
.

Theorem
.

\mathcal{L}(t)\cap\mathcal{L}(s)=\emptyset
whenever
t\neq s
.

Proof.

Assume by way of contradiction that there is a cubic
P\in\mathcal{L}(t)\cap\mathcal{L}(s)
and
t\neq s
. For
1\leq i\leq q
let
z_{i}
denote the co-landing point of the rays
R(x_{i}),R(y_{i})
and for
1\leq j\leq p
let
w_{j}
denote the co-landing point of
R(u_{j}),R(v_{j})
. Setting
y^{\prime}_{k}:=y_{k}-1/3<1/2<x^{\prime}_{k}:=x_{k}+1/3
as before, the rays
R(y^{\prime}_{k}),R(x^{\prime}_{k})
co-land at a preimage of
z^{\prime}_{k}
of
z_{\sigma(k)}
other than
z_{k}
. Since
w_{\ell}
is periodic but
z^{\prime}_{k}
is not, the arcs
R(u_{\ell})\cup R(v_{\ell})\cup\{w_{\ell}\}
and
R(x^{\prime}_{k})\cup R(y^{\prime}_{k})\cup\{z^{\prime}_{k}\}
are disjoint. The length comparison
|J_{\ell}|>1/3>|[y^{\prime}_{k},x^{\prime}_{k}]|
then shows that

(9.4)

u_{\ell}<y^{\prime}_{k}<1/2<x^{\prime}_{k}<v_{\ell}.

As the intervals
I_{k}
and
J_{\ell}
both contain
1/2
, there are only two possibilities:

(i)
I_{k}
and
J_{\ell}
are not nested. Then, by (
9.4
),

(9.5)

x_{k}<u_{\ell}<y^{\prime}_{k}<1/2<y_{k}<v_{\ell}\qquad\text{or}\qquad u_{\ell}<x_{k}<1/2<x^{\prime}_{k}<v_{\ell}<y_{k}.

In either case the arcs
R(x_{k})\cup R(y_{k})\cup\{z_{k}\}
and
R(u_{\ell})\cup R(v_{\ell})\cup\{w_{\ell}\}
necessarily meet, so
z_{k}=w_{\ell}
. Since the ray
R(x_{k})
landing at
z_{k}
is fixed under
P^{\circ q}
, every ray landing at
z_{k}
(such as
R(u_{\ell})
) must be fixed under
P^{\circ q}
. This shows
\mathbf{m}_{3}^{\circ q}(u_{\ell})=u_{\ell}
, so
p|q
. A similar argument shows
q|p
, and we conclude that
p=q
. In particular, every
u_{j},v_{j}
has period
q
under
\mathbf{m}_{3}
. Now (
9.5
) together with the fact that
\mathbf{m}_{3}
maps each of
[x_{k},y^{\prime}_{k}]
and
[x^{\prime}_{k},y_{k}]
homeomorphically onto
I_{\sigma(k)}
shows that either
u_{\tau(\ell)}
or
v_{\tau(\ell)}
lies in the interior of
I_{\sigma(k)}
. This is a contradiction since the endpoints
x_{\sigma(k)}
and
y_{\sigma(k)}
of
I_{\sigma(k)}
, being
1/(3^{q}-1)
apart, are adjacent period
q
points of
\mathbf{m}_{3}
.

(ii)
I_{k}
and
J_{\ell}
are nested. Assume without loss of generality that
q\leq p
. If
q=p
, then
|I_{k}|=3^{q-1}/(3^{q}-1)=|J_{\ell}|
, so
x_{k}=u_{\ell},y_{k}=v_{\ell}
and therefore
t=s
, contradicting our hypothesis. Let us then assume that
q<p
so
|I_{k}|>|J_{\ell}|
. By (
9.4
),

x_{k}\leq u_{\ell}<y^{\prime}_{k}<1/2<x^{\prime}_{k}<v_{\ell}\leq y_{k}.

Since the iterate
\mathbf{m}_{3}^{\circ q}
is an expanding diffeomorphism from each of the intervals
[x_{k},y^{\prime}_{k}]
and
[x^{\prime}_{k},y_{k}]
onto
I_{k}
, it follows that
x_{k}\leq u_{\ell}\leq u_{\tau^{q}(\ell)}<y_{k}
and
x_{k}<v_{\tau^{q}(\ell)}\leq v_{\ell}\leq y_{k}
, so
J_{\ell}
and
J_{\tau^{q}(\ell)}
meet. This is a contradiction since by
q<p
the intervals
J_{\ell}
and
J_{\tau^{q}(\ell)}
must be disjoint.
∎

It follows from the above theorem and symmetry that the rotated limbs
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(t),\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(s)
are also disjoint whenever
t\neq s
. However, the intersection
\mathcal{L}(t)\cap\mathcal{L}^{\ast}(s)
may well be non-empty in both cases
t=s
and
t\neq s
.

For example, suppose
t,s
are
complementary angles
in the sense that they belong to the same
