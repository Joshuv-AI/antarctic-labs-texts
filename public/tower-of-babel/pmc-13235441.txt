Comparison of the efficacy of different doses of omalizumab (150 mg vs 300 mg) in patients with seasonal allergic rhinitis and low total IgE: A randomized, double-blind, controlled study☆
Authors: Lisha Li, Yingyang Xu, Sainan Bian, Zhouxian Pan, Kai Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study compares the efficacy of monthly subcutaneous injections of 150 mg versus 300 mg omalizumab in patients with seasonal allergic rhinitis (AR) caused by cypress pollen allergy and with total immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels below 100 IU/ml, aiming to provide a basis for individualized treatment in the population with low total IgE.MethodsThis single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial enrolled 60 patients with seasonal allergic rhinitis induced by cypress pollen and total IgE levels below 100 IU/mL. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive a single subcutaneous injection of either 300 mg omalizumab or 150 mg omalizumab plus placebo within 5 days prior to the pollen season onset. The endpoints included daily symptom and medication scores (dSS, dMS, CSMS), Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RQLQ) score, Allergic Rhinitis Control Test (ARCT) score, and nasal resistance.ResultsAmong the 54 patients ultimately included in the analysis (27 in each group), the 300 mg group demonstrated significantly better outcomes than the 150 mg group in the CSMS (1.1 ± 0.1 vs 1.6 ± 0.1, P= 0.007), dSS (0.6 ± 0.1 vs 0.8 ± 0.1, P= 0.016), and ocular symptom score (0.5 ± 0.1 vs 0.7 ± 0.1, P= 0.003). The total RQLQ score in the 300 mg group was significantly lower than that in the 150 mg group during both the peak and late phases of the pollen season (P< 0.05). Furthermore, the ARCT score during the peak phase was significantly better in the 300 mg group (P= 0.011), and nasal resistance was significantly reduced (P= 0.020). No drug-related adverse reactions occurred in either group.ConclusionsIn patients with seasonal AR and total IgE levels of less than 100 IU/ml, a monthly dose of 300 mg omalizumab provided greater improvement in symptoms, quality of life, and nasal ventilation function compared with the 150 mg dose. This suggests that a higher individualized dosing strategy should be considered for such patients.Trial registrationNational Medical Research Registration and Filing Information System (MR-11-25-016855), registered 27 February 2025.Keywords: Rhinitis, Allergic, Seasonal, Omalizumab, Immunoglobulin E, Precision medicine

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a common respiratory allergic disease. Multiple global, multicenter epidemiological studies have shown that the prevalence of AR in children is 8.5%–14.6%, while in the 20- to-44-year-old population, the prevalence ranges from 11.8% to 46%.1 Pollen is a major allergen responsible for AR, causing sneezing, nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, and ocular itching during the spring and autumn, which significantly impairs quality of life for patients.2,3 Common symptom-relieving medications for AR include oral or topical antihistamines, oral leukotriene receptor antagonists, and intranasal corticosteroids.4 However, for patients with severe symptoms, the efficacy of the aforementioned drugs is suboptimal, with response rates below 40%.5 Targeted monoclonal antibody biologics that precisely block key allergic mediators have emerged as new therapies for AR. Omalizumab acts by binding free immunoglobulin E (IgE), thereby preventing activation and degranulation of effector cells and reducing allergic inflammation. Multiple clinical trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated that omalizumab effectively alleviates rhinoconjunctivitis symptoms in patients with seasonal AR due to pollen allergy and improves their quality of life.6

Currently, the approved indications for omalizumab in China include moderate-to-severe allergic asthma and chronic urticaria.7 For allergic asthma, dosing is determined by the patient's body weight and total IgE level, with each dose ranging from 150 to 600 mg and administered every 2–4 weeks.8 Given the close association and similarity in pathogenesis between AR and allergic asthma, the clinical dosing of omalizumab for AR is generally referenced from the asthma dosing table. However, in our research team's previous study administering omalizumab to patients with cypress pollen-induced allergic rhinitis following this rule, no significant difference in rhinoconjunctivitis quality of life was found between the omalizumab group and traditional medication controls. Additionally, a negative correlation was observed between the dose of omalizumab and the patient's peak season symptom score, suggesting better efficacy with higher doses.9 A large-scale study in the United States on seasonal AR also showed that 300 mg omalizumab was more efficacious than placebo, while 150 mg was not different from placebo.10 In our previous study, more than half of the cypress pollen-allergic patients received the monthly 150 mg dose due to generally low total IgE levels, which may explain the suboptimal outcomes in the omalizumab group and warrants further investigation.

Therefore, this study intends to compare the efficacy of monthly subcutaneous injections of 150 mg versus 300 mg omalizumab in patients with cypress pollen-induced AR and total IgE levels below 100 IU/ml, in order to provide reference data for developing individualized and precise treatment strategies for seasonal AR.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study recruited patients with AR induced by cypress pollen who attended the outpatient clinic of the Department of Allergy, Peking Union Medical College Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) age between 18 and 65 years, with no gender restriction; 2) typical symptoms of rhinorrhea, sneezing, and ocular pruritus occurring in March and April during the past 2 years, and poor response to oral antihistamines; 3) skin prick tests performed in Peking Union Medical College Hospital and showing positive result to cypress pollens with diameter of wheal 3 mm larger than negative control (Allergen Manufacturing Laboratory of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Beijing, China); 4) serum specific immunoglobulin E (sIgE) tests conducted in Peking Union Medical College Hospital and showing positive sIgE to cypress pollens of ≥0.7 KUA/L, and total IgE level between 30 and 100 IU/ml (ImmunoCAP, ThermoFisher Scientific Inc. Waltham, MA, USA); 5) negative skin prick test and serum sIgE <0.7 KUA/L to other common aeroallergens (including house dust mites, molds, other tree pollens, weed pollens, and animal dander). The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients with asthma; 2) allergy to omalizumab, excipients of therapeutic agents, or other biosimilars, or a history of severe drug allergy or anaphylactic shock; 3) patients with severe cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, or other systemic diseases that may affect the study outcomes or increase treatment risks; 4) patients currently receiving, or planning to receive during the study period, other therapies that may affect immune function (such as immunosuppressants or biologics); 5) pregnant or lactating women; and 6) individuals with psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment who are unable to understand or comply with the study protocol. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Ethics Approval No.: I-25PJ0499). All participants provided written informed consent and the project was registered in the National Medical Research Registration and Filing Information System (MR-11-25-016855).

Study design

This study is a single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. Per protocol, eligible patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio using block randomization to receive subcutaneous injections every 4 weeks: the 150 mg group received 150 mg omalizumab plus 150 mg placebo, and the 300 mg group received 300 mg omalizumab. However, as the cypress pollen season lasts approximately 1 month (4 weeks), the treatment period coincided with the entire pollen season, requiring only a single injection. Consequently, all patients received a single subcutaneous injection of the assigned study drug (omalizumab [Enitan®, CSPC Pharmaceutical Group, China] or matched placebo) within 5 days of the pollen season onset. During the study period, the use of systemic glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, or other biologics was prohibited; however, patients experiencing intolerable rhinoconjunctivitis symptoms were permitted to use oral antihistamines, intranasal corticosteroids, and ophthalmic antihistamine drops for symptomatic relief as needed. Patients recorded daily rhinoconjunctivitis symptom scores and symptomatic medication scores throughout the cypress pollen season. Every 2 weeks during the pollen season, patients completed assessments of Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RQLQ) score and Allergic Rhinitis Control Test (ARCT) score. All adverse events associated with omalizumab were observed and documented throughout the study.

Sample Size Calculation: Based on the results reported in similar foreign studies, the nasal conjunctival symptom scores for patients with seasonal AR treated with omalizumab of different doses were 0.61 ± 0.05 (300 mg group) and 0.68 ± 0.05 (150 mg group), respectively. Using these data, and employing the sample size calculation formula with α = 0.05, β = 0.20, δ = 0.07, and σ = 0.05, the calculated sample size per group is n = 8. Taking into account differences in study population and pollen allergens compared to the previous research, as well as potential patient dropout rates, the sample size for each group in this study is set at 30 patients, yielding a total of 60 patients across both groups.

Evaluation indicators

Daily Symptom Score (dSS) for rhinoconjunctivitis: Each day, patients recorded the severity of 6 major symptoms—nasal itching, sneezing, rhinorrhea, nasal obstruction, ocular itching, and tearing, using a 0–3 scale for each symptom (0 = none, 3 = most severe). The scores from all 6 symptoms were averaged to generate the daily symptom score for rhinoconjunctivitis.

Daily Medication Score (dMS): Patients recorded their daily use of oral/topical antihistamines, intranasal corticosteroids, and systemic corticosteroids according to the following scale: no medication, 0 points; oral or topical antihistamines, 1 point; intranasal corticosteroids, with or without antihistamines, 2 points; oral/intravenous/intramuscular corticosteroids, with or without antihistamines, 3 points.

Combined Symptom and Medication Score (CSMS): This score is calculated by summing the daily symptom score (dSS) with the daily medication score (dMS), resulting in a range of 0–6 points.11

Rhinoconjunctivitis quality of life score: Assessed using the standardized Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RQLQ), which includes 7 domains—activities, sleep, non-nasal/ocular symptoms, practical problems, nasal symptoms, ocular symptoms, and emotional function—with a total of 28 questions. Scoring criteria are: 0 = not troubled at all; 1 = hardly troubled; 2 = slightly troubled; 3 = moderately troubled; 4 = very troubled; 5 = notably troubled; 6 = extremely troubled. The overall RQLQ score is the average of all question scores, while subscores for each domain are calculated as the mean of scores for the questions within that domain.12

Allergic Rhinitis Control Test (ARCT) score: The ARCT is used to assess the level of control of AR. It comprises 5 questions, with each question scored on a scale from 1 to 5 (1 indicating the worst outcome and 5 indicating the best outcome), resulting in a total score range of 5–25. A total score of ≥20 indicates well-controlled AR, while a total score of <20 indicates poorly controlled AR.

Nasal resistance: Nasal resistance was measured using the nasal resistance component of the MasterScreen™ pulmonary function testing system. Subjects were seated in a resting state and instructed to breathe through their mouth with a normal respiratory rhythm. Appropriately sized nasal olive probe was securely attached to unilateral anterior nares to ensure an airtight seal. Using the integrated technique of active anterior rhinomanometry, the system simultaneously recorded transnasal pressure and nasal airflow while the subject performed quiet, unassisted tidal breathing. Measurements were taken for each nostril separately over a minimum of 3 consecutive, valid respiratory cycles during both the inspiratory and expiratory phases. The system's software automatically calculated nasal airway resistance (in Pa/cm3/s), and the mean value of bilateral nasal resistance was used for final analysis.

Statistical methods

Qualitative data were presented as frequencies (percentages); quantitative data were assessed for normality, and if normally distributed, were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, otherwise as median (interquartile range). The comparisons of daily symptom score of rhinoconjunctivitis, daily medication score, and combined symptom and medication score between the 150 mg group and the 300 mg group were conducted using linear mixed models; group comparisons of the RQLQ score and ARCT score were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 software (ver. 23; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and a two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 60 patients were enrolled in this study and randomized in a 1:1 ratio using block randomization to either the 300 mg omalizumab group or the 150 mg omalizumab group. The assigned dose of omalizumab was administered within 5 days preceding the pollen season, followed by a follow-up period of 4 weeks during the pollen season. During the follow-up, 3 patients from the 300 mg group and 3 patients from the 150 mg group withdrew from the study due to poor compliance and inability to consistently record symptoms and medication use. Ultimately, 27 patients in the 300 mg group and 27 patients in the 150 mg group were included in the efficacy analysis. All patients in both groups presented with only rhinoconjunctivitis symptoms, without asthma. Comparisons of age, gender, weight, duration of AR, visual analogue scale (VAS) score for symptom severity during the previous pollen season, and total IgE level did not reveal any statistically significant differences between the 2 groups (Table 1).

Symptom and medication scores

The cypress pollen season in 2025 lasted from March 5 to April 5, with a peak pollen concentration reaching 4090 grains/1000 mm2. As shown in Fig. 1, during the cypress pollen season, the mean Combined Symptom and Medication Score (CSMS) in the 300 mg group was 1.1 ± 0.1—significantly lower than 1.6 ± 0.1 in the 150 mg group (P= 0.007). As detailed in Table 2, the 300 mg group also had significantly lower daily symptom scores (dSS) for rhinoconjunctivitis and ocular symptom score compared to the 150 mg group. The 300 mg group showed a trend toward lower nasal symptom scores and lower daily medication scores (dMS) during the pollen season relative to the 150 mg group; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Quality of life score

As shown in Table 3, during the peak cypress pollen season, the total RQLQ score in the 300 mg omalizumab group was 1.77 (0.92, 2.74), which was significantly lower than the 2.40 (1.28, 4.55) score in the 150 mg group, P= 0.047. In the late pollen season (4 weeks after the beginning of the season), the total RQLQ score in the 300 mg group was 1.22 (0.91, 2.00), also significantly lower than the 2.13 (1.15, 3.91) in the 150 mg group, P= 0.035. Comparison of the individual domains of quality of life showed that, in terms of improving sleep quality and controlling ocular symptoms, the 300 mg group outperformed the 150 mg group at both time points, and its effect on alleviating nasal symptoms during the peak season was superior as well, with all P values less than 0.05.

ARCT score

During the cypress pollen peak season, the ARCT score in the 300 mg group was 20.0 (17.0, 21.0), significantly higher than the 17.5 (13.3, 20.0) score in the 150 mg group, P= 0.011; in the late pollen season, the ARCT score in the 300 mg group was 20.0 (18.0, 22.0), which also tended to be higher than the 18.5 (15.0, 21.0) in the 150 mg group, P= 0.071.

Nasal resistance

During the peak pollen season, we measured nasal resistance in both groups. The nasal expiratory flow rate in the 300 mg group was 268.46 ± 83.26 ml/s, which was significantly higher than that in the 150 mg group (202.27 ± 80.09 ml/s, P= 0.015); nasal resistance in the 300 mg group was 0.58 (0.45, 0.82) Pa/cm3/s, significantly lower than 0.91 (0.65, 1.36) Pa/cm3/s in the 150 mg group, P= 0.020.

Adverse reactions

No immediate or delayed drug-related adverse reactions were observed in either group following administration of different doses of omalizumab.

Discussion

This study enrolled seasonal AR patients with total IgE≤100 IU/ml and compared the effects of monthly injections of 150 mg versus 300 mg omalizumab. The results demonstrated that during the pollen season, the 300 mg group had significantly better outcomes with respect to daily rhinoconjunctivitis symptom scores, especially ocular symptom scores, as well as combined symptom and medication scores and RQLQ scores, compared to the 150 mg group; additionally, ARCT scores during the pollen season peak were higher and nasal resistance was lower in the 300 mg group versus the 150 mg group, indicating that monthly administration of 300 mg omalizumab was more effective than 150 mg omalizumab in this group of AR patients with low total IgE levels.

Pollen is a major allergen responsible for AR. In the grassland regions of northern China, the prevalence of AR is as high as 32.4%, with approximately half of these cases triggered by weed pollens.13 In addition to autumn pollen, spring tree pollen is also an important cause of seasonal AR. From 2010 to 2013 in urban Beijing, cypress pollen accounted for 16% of the annual pollen count—a four-fold increase compared to 30 years prior.14 Identifying optimal treatment approaches for pollen-induced seasonal AR is crucial for alleviating patient symptoms and improving quality of life.

Omalizumab is a recombinant anti-IgE monoclonal antibody that suppresses allergic inflammation by blocking the action of free IgE.15 Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that omalizumab can effectively relieve rhinoconjunctivitis symptoms in patients with seasonal AR.6 In Chinese populations, we have used omalizumab to treat autumn weed pollen-induced AR and found that omalizumab can effectively control symptoms, improve quality of life, and is superior to conventional drugs.16,17 A 2025 study conducted in Japan further reported that omalizumab significantly alleviated AR symptoms caused by spring cedar pollen exposure and improved eosinophilic inflammation in the nasal passages.18

Currently, in clinical practice, the omalizumab dose for AR is selected according to the dosing table for allergic asthma. However, many patients with cypress pollen-induced AR have low total IgE levels (≤100 IU/ml), and are thus typically prescribed a monthly dose of 150 mg omalizumab. In a prior retrospective study by our group on cypress pollen-allergic patients, 56% received the 150 mg monthly dose. However, the results showed no significant difference in quality of life scores between the omalizumab group and controls; moreover, there was a significant negative correlation between the omalizumab dose and patients’ peak season symptom scores.9 These observations suggest that for AR patients with low total IgE but severe symptoms, strict adherence to the asthma dosing table may not be appropriate, and a more individualized, disease-specific treatment approach should be explored. Accordingly, we conducted this study to compare the efficacy of 150 mg versus 300 mg monthly omalizumab in seasonal AR patients with total IgE ≤100 IU/ml. As described above, the findings indicated that the 300 mg dose was superior in relieving rhinoconjunctivitis symptoms, improving quality of life, increasing control of rhinitis, and reducing nasal resistance compared to the 150 mg dose. Therefore, in seasonal AR patients with low total IgE, a monthly dose of 300 mg omalizumab may be more effective, particularly when response to 150 mg has proven inadequate, and consideration should be given to increasing the dose.

The correlation between omalizumab dose and efficacy has been reported in previous studies. A randomized controlled trial recruited 536 patients with ragweed pollen-induced AR, administering omalizumab in doses of 50 mg, 150 mg, 300 mg, or placebo. For patients with total IgE between 30 IU/ml and 150 IU/ml, omalizumab was administered every 4 weeks; for those with total IgE between 150 IU/ml and 700 IU/ml, dosing was every 3 weeks. The study found that the 300 mg omalizumab dose resulted in superior control of nasal symptoms and quality of life compared to placebo, while the 150 mg and 50 mg doses showed no statistical difference from placebo for the same indicators.10 Additionally, Chinese researchers reported that pre-seasonal administration of a uniform dose of 300 mg omalizumab in autumn weed pollen-induced AR resulted in lower symptom-medication scores and quality of life scores than traditional drugs.16 These findings consistently support the robust efficacy of 300 mg omalizumab, in agreement with the present study. Notably, the recommended dose for chronic urticaria is 300 mg monthly. Whether omalizumab dosing for seasonal AR should follow asthma protocols—determined by total IgE and body weight19—or adopt the same monthly 300 mg regimen as for chronic urticaria20 remains an open question for future investigation.

About the paradox of low serum IgE levels yet severe clinical manifestations requiring high dose of omalizumab to treat in cypress pollen-allergic patients, we hypothesize that the low serum IgE levels observed in cypress pollen-allergic patients may reflect the transient, explosive nature of pollen exposure—peaking for merely 2 weeks for cypress pollen—coupled with potentially high local IgE production in nasal and conjunctival mucosa that is not captured by peripheral blood measurements. Consequently, the abrupt, massive allergen challenge during the pollen season necessitates rapid and potent IgE neutralization, explaining the superior efficacy of higher omalizumab doses in this population despite modest systemic IgE levels. We acknowledge that these explanations remain speculative and require further validation through local IgE quantification and tissue pharmacokinetic studies.

The results of this study suggest that in patients with seasonal AR and total IgE ≤100 IU/ml, monthly administration of 300 mg omalizumab is more effective than 150 mg. This provides reference data for developing individualized and precise treatment strategies for seasonal AR. However, this study has several limitations. First, there was no control group (either placebo or active comparator) receiving standard pharmacotherapy such as intranasal corticosteroids alone or combined with antihistamines administered preseason; therefore, the difference in efficacy between omalizumab and conventional treatments could not be assessed, nor could the absolute treatment effect be determined beyond the dose-response comparison. Second, differences in efficacy between the monthly 300 mg dose and higher dosing strategies (eg, 450 mg or 600 mg every 2–4 weeks) were not investigated. Third, the sample size was relatively small and the study was single-center, limiting generalizability and external validity. Future multi-center randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes should therefore incorporate a control group receiving standard preseason pharmacotherapy, and additionally evaluate multiple dosing regimens (including higher doses and varied administration intervals) to enable direct comparison with conventional treatments, optimize individualized dosing strategies, and enhance the generalizability of findings in seasonal AR.
