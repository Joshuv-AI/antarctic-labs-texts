Five states of reduction in OTA/AO A1.3 intertrochanteric fractures of the femur a biomechanical study
Authors: Shu Li, Yong-Gang Bao, Rong-Hua Tian, Chun-Yang Meng, Hai-Bin Wang, Bin Wu, Xian-Min Bu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to analyze the differences in mechanical stability of OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fractures under various reduction conditions.MethodsTwenty standard synbone artificial femur test bones were selected for the OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fracture model. The models were divided into five groups according to their reduction state: positive support, neutral support, negative support, varus fixation, and valgus fixation, with four specimens in each group. All models were fixed using PFNA intramedullary fixation and subjected to static axial compression tests. The subsidence displacement of the proximal femur under different loads and the axial stiffness of the model were measured to verify the mechanical stability of the OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fracture under different reduction conditions.ResultsAfter the static axial compression test, the proximal femoral subsidence displacement in the positive support and neutral support groups was lower than that in the negative support, valgus fixation, and varus fixation groups (p < 0.001). The axial stiffness of the model was highest in the positive support group. Significant differences in subsidence displacement and axial stiffness were found between the groups (p < 0.001). The positive support group demonstrated the best mechanical stability, while the varus fixation group showed the poorest performance.ConclusionPositive support of the medial cortex can be regarded as the best reduction state for OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fractures, suggesting that this approach should be preferred during surgery to enhance mechanical stability and improve clinical outcomes. Conversely, varus fixation should be avoided due to its inferior stability.Keywords: Femur, Intertrochanteric fracture, Cortical support, Internal fixation, Angulation

Introduction

31A1.3 intertrochanteric fractures are more common in clinical practice, typically affecting patients over 60 years old who often have osteoporosis and sustain injuries due to torsional forces [1]. During the extended rehabilitation period, these patients are required to remain bedridden for prolonged periods, increasing the risk of complications such as pneumonia and deep vein thrombosis, with a one-year mortality rate as high as 36% [2]​​. With advancements in internal fixation materials, surgical techniques, and rehabilitation concepts, internal fixation has become the preferred treatment for patients with 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fractures [3, 4].

Intramedullary fixation generally yields satisfactory results for these fractures. However, some patients experience clinical issues such as fixation failure and the need for revision surgery after the initial reduction and fixation [5]. The clinical success of surgical fixation is closely related to the reduction state of the medial cortex. Therefore, Zhang Shimin first proposed the theory of positive support reduction for the anterior medial cortex of 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fractures [6, 7]. According to this theory, under positive support, the head and neck bones can contact the medial cortex of the femoral shaft through limited sliding, resulting in secondary stability​​​​. Several clinical studies and biomechanical tests have validated the effectiveness of this theory. However, there is a lack of comprehensive reports on the biomechanical stability of various fracture reduction states [8].

Materials and methods

Test materials and fracture models

Materials: Twenty standard test bones of fourth-generation artificial bone femurs (Malans Synbone, Switzerland) were used. All materials had the same characteristics and geometric shape: full length of 337 mm, femoral head diameter of 48 mm, neck shaft angle of 130°, and anteversion angle of 15°.

Model: The distal metaphysis of the model bone was removed According to the OTA/AO classification standard for femoral intertrochanteric fractures and approximately 27 cm of the proximal end was retained. The 40° oblique and downward from the greater trochanter of the femur was cut off with a pendulum, and the posterior medial lesser trochanter was cut off to create an intertrochanteric fracture model (OTA/AO 31A1.3) with a medial cortical defect and a complete lateral wall (OTA/AO 31A1.3) [9]. The model was generated by the same senior orthopaedic trauma specialist and reduced and fixed by the same orthopaedic trauma team.

Experimental grouping

Based on the alignment relationship between the medial cortex of the head and neck and the medial cortex of the femoral shaft, patients were divided into a positive support group (superior cover), neutral support group (anatomical reduction), negative support group (lower cover), varus fixation group (10° varus), and valgus fixation group (10° valgus) [7]. Twenty fracture models were used in each group. The head and neck bone mass moved inward and downward by one cortical thickness (approximately 3 mm) as positive support, while the head and neck block moving outward and upward by one cortical thickness was considered negative support.

Model fixation and biomechanical testing

Model fixation: 31A1.3 intertrochanteric femur fractures involve a fracture line affecting the medial cortex of the proximal femur. When there is a defect in the medial cortex and destruction of the calcar femorale, there is a lack of effective support on the compression side, leading to stress concentration at the nail-plate junction, which easily causes fatigue fracture of the internal fixation [10]. In response to this situation, PFNA is widely used in the surgical treatment of intertrochanteric femur fractures due to its high stiffness, superior resistance to bending and compression, and smaller torque, making it more biomechanically suitable [11]. PFNA brings the fracture ends closer together through rotational action at its head, generating axial pressure to promote the alignment and healing of the fracture ends.

All fracture models in this study were fixed with a PFNA intramedullary nail (model: 9 × 170 mm standard proximal intramedullary nail; Weiman Company, China), and the fracture model was subsequently embedded and fixed with denture powder to maintain approximately 15° of adduction in the coronal plane (see Fig. 1).

Five reduction states of femoral intertrochanteric fracture model (OTA/AO A1.3). From left to right, the reduction states were medial cortical positive support, medial cortical neutral support, medial cortical negative support, varus fixation, and valgus fixation. Based on the medial cortical neutral support (anatomical support), the head and neck bone mass moved one cortical thickness (approximately 3 mm) inward and downward as the positive support [5]. In comparison, the head and neck moved one cortical thickness (approximately 3 mm) as the negative support. Valgus fixation and valgus fixation had an angle of 10° respectively

Biomechanical test: This study selected the Newton Laboratory of Tianjin Weiman Biomaterials Co., Ltd. (this laboratory was approved by the China National Conformity Assessment Commission [CNAS]) and included corresponding qualifications and conditions. Biomechanical tests use a microcomputer-controlled electronic universal testing machine (MTS, USA) for load loading, which is loaded to 200 N continuously 1–2 min before testing so that the model is in close contact with the biomechanical testing machine and reduces the error caused by the elastic creep effect [12]. Then, we selected the static axial compression test (see Fig. 2), set the loading rate as 5 mm/min, the disc spacing as 50 mm, the data acquisition frequency to 10 Hz, gradually load from 0 N to 1500 N, and recorded the subsidence displacement of the proximal femur under 400, 800, 1200, and 1500 N loads. According to the load‒displacement curve, the overall axial stiffness of the model under 1500 N is calculated using the linear regression equation, and the formula is as follows: axial stiffness = axial load/longitudinal displacement [13]. During the test, the applied load is plotted against the displacement of the model. The yield load corresponds to the inflection point where the load-displacement curve shifts from linear to nonlinear, indicating that the material is transitioning from elastic deformation to plastic deformation (i.e., the material begins to undergo permanent deformation) [14]. The test starts at 0 N, with the load gradually applied at a rate of 5 mm/min. A high-frequency data acquisition system (data acquisition frequency of 10 Hz) is used to record the load-displacement data of the model. The test continues until the model undergoes plastic deformation. The yield point is determined from the load-displacement curve as the position where the curve starts to deviate from the linear portion, at which point the model begins to experience plastic deformation. The load corresponding to this point is the yield load.

Single-factor analysis of variance was used for the overall comparison between groups. If the difference was statistically significant, Bonferroni method was used for multiple comparisons after the event. The differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

The results of one-way ANOVA showed that under the same load, the proximal femoral subsidence displacement in the positive support group was the smallest (p < 0.001), and the positive support group had the most potent ability to resist axial compression in OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fractures (see Fig. 3). Post hoc two-by-two comparisons were made using the Bonferroni method, and the results showed that there was a statistically significant difference between the groups (p < 0.001), and there was no significant difference in the proximal femoral subsidence displacement between the neutral support group and the valgus fixation group under 400 N, 800 N, or 1500 N loads (p > 0.05). However, the proximal femoral subsidence displacement was the largest in the varus fixation group, with a significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.001).

Compare the axial stiffness values of five models

The axial stiffness of the model reflects the ability of the model structure to resist elastic deformation under stress, that is, the force required to cause unit displacement, and it varies with the support state of the inner cortex [15]. The axial stiffnesses of the individuals in the positive support group, neutral support group, negative support group, varus fixation group, and valgus fixation group were 231.83 ± 7.19 N/mm, 185.40 ± 10.22 N/mm, 136.96 ± 3.44 N/mm, 114.41 ± 6.92 N/mm, and 169.39 ± 7.45 N/mm, respectively.

The results of the one-way ANOVA showed that axial stiffness was greatest in the positive support group, indicating that the reduction between the medial cortex of the head and neck and the medial cortex of the proximal femoral shaft in the positive support group was proper (see Fig. 4). The boundary support was reasonable such that the medial cortex of the proximal femoral shaft only bears axial force and can have greater stiffness. The greater its ability to resist axial stress, the greater the axial stability [6, 16]. After Bonferroni correction, a significant difference was observed between the two groups (p < 0.001).

Yield load of the model

During the biomechanical test of the OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fracture fixation model, the plastic deformation load of the fracture fixation model begins to appear, and the structural failure load is called the yield load [17]. The greater the yield load, the stronger the ability of the fixation model to resist the load, and plastic deformation is difficult, as shown in Table 2. The yield loads of the positive support group, neutral support group, negative support group, varus fixation group, and valgus fixation group were 4183.75 ± 138.14 N, 3804.75 ± 104.39 N, 2927.75 ± 81.42 N, 1037.25 ± 49.74 N, and 3079.50 ± 114.33 N, respectively. The yield load of the positive support group was the highest, and the positive support group had the most robust ability to resist plastic deformation in the OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fracture (see Fig. 5). There was no significant difference in yield load between the negative support and valgus fixation groups (p > 0.05). The yield load was lowest in the varus fixation group, and the model in the varus fixation group was the most prone to plastic deformation during the test. The Bonferroni method was used to compare and analyze the results. The two groups were significantly different (p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the mechanical stability of OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fractures under various reduction conditions using artificial femur models. Our findings indicate that the positive support of the medial cortex provides the best reduction state for these fractures, significantly enhancing the mechanical stability compared to other reduction states such as neutral support, negative support, varus fixation, and valgus fixation.

The proximal femur functions as a cantilever structure, primarily bearing downward compressive stress and varus forces during weight-bearing [18]. The compressive trabeculae of the femoral neck and the calcar femorale play a critical role in load transmission. Notably, the anterior-medial cortex and its deep structures are sturdier, enabling them to bear more load. The PFNA screw passes through the femoral neck and is positioned centrally in the femoral head, providing optimal resistance to rotational and shear forces [19]. Additionally, slight sliding of the screw can further compress the fracture ends, enhancing fracture stability.

Under positive fixation, the PFNA generates axial compressive forces through head rotation and slight sliding, which gradually closes the fracture gap and enhances the mechanical stability of the fracture [20]. As the load increases, the sliding of the screw further reduces the fracture gap, creating compression at the fracture line and increasing the mechanical stability at the anteromedial cortex of a 31A1.3 intertrochanteric femoral fracture.

Our research presents several innovative aspects that distinguish it from previous studies on intertrochanteric fractures. Firstly, we utilized a biomechanical approach to quantify the stability provided by different reduction states, which has not been extensively explored in the literature. By systematically comparing the proximal femoral subsidence displacement and axial stiffness across five reduction states, we provide robust evidence that positive support is superior in maintaining fracture stability [21].

The positive support group’s proximal femoral subsidence displacement was significantly lower under all tested loads (400 N, 800 N, 1200 N, and 1500 N), indicating a more stable fixation. Additionally, the axial stiffness was highest in the positive support group, further confirming its effectiveness in resisting elastic deformation under stress. These findings support Zhang Shimin’s positive support reduction theory and demonstrate its practical applicability in enhancing the mechanical stability of intertrochanteric fractures [7, 9]. Another novel aspect of our study is the comprehensive comparison of varus and valgus fixation, which are often encountered in clinical practice but rarely subjected to detailed biomechanical analysis [22]. Our results showed that varus fixation had the poorest performance in terms of proximal femoral subsidence displacement and axial stiffness, suggesting that this reduction state should be avoided during surgery.

Previous studies have primarily focused on the clinical outcomes of different fixation methods for intertrochanteric fractures without a detailed biomechanical comparison of various reduction states [23, 24]. Our study fills this gap by providing quantitative data on the mechanical stability of different reduction conditions. For instance, Gotfried’s concept of non-anatomical reduction in femoral neck fractures emphasized the importance of cortical support but did not specifically address the biomechanical differences between positive, neutral, and negative support in intertrochanteric fractures​​ [25, 26]. Our findings extend this concept by demonstrating that positive support not only enhances stability but also significantly reduces the risk of subsidence and deformation under load. Furthermore, clinical studies by Zhang Shimin and others have suggested that positive support can lead to better functional outcomes and lower complication rates​​ [7]. Our biomechanical data provide a mechanistic explanation for these clinical observations, showing that positive support offers superior mechanical stability, which is crucial for successful fracture healing and long-term function.

Although our study has certain advantages and innovations, we must also acknowledge its limitations. First, while the use of artificial femur models provides highly repeatable results in mechanical performance testing of fractures and internal fixation techniques, the materials and structure of these models are relatively uniform, making it difficult to fully reflect the individual differences in real human bones, such as age, gender, and degree of osteoporosis [27]. These differences may affect fracture behavior and the healing process.

Moreover, artificial femur models cannot replicate the dynamic stress conditions that human bones experience during daily activities [28]. After PFNA fixation of an OTA/OA A1.3 intertrochanteric femur fracture, the fracture site is subjected to dynamic load pressure. The application of dynamic loading may influence the proximal femur to slide along the axis of the lag screw in the fracture fixation model. The sliding movement of the lag screw converts one fixation state to another (e.g., varus fixation and valgus fixation might change to positive support or neutral support). This change in state, therefore, affects the positioning of the screw fixation in patients with OTA/OA A1.3 intertrochanteric femur fractures in real-life situations. Our current experiments use static axial compression tests, whereas human bone is subjected to dynamic stress, implying that the experimental results may be insufficient to accurately predict the actual fracture healing state in real-life scenarios [29]. Given the limitations of the current experiments, future research should consider using cadaveric bones to validate the results in a context that is more dynamically biologically relevant.

While we have confirmed that mechanical stability improves under positive support conditions in different reduction states, anatomical reduction remains the gold standard for treating OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric femur fractures [30]. Anatomical reduction helps restore the bone’s normal structure, providing a natural load transmission path and enhancing mechanical stability, which is crucial for complex intertrochanteric fractures. Future studies should further validate different reduction techniques in more complex biological models or clinical cases. Additionally, cyclic loading tests and fatigue performance assessments under different reduction conditions will provide a more comprehensive evaluation of mechanical performance.

Finally, the sample size in this study is relatively small, with only 4 samples per group. Although the results are statistically significant, increasing the sample size would enhance the robustness of the findings and allow for more detailed subgroup analysis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that positive support of the medial cortex provides superior mechanical stability for OTA/AO 31A1.3 intertrochanteric fractures compared to other reduction states. These findings have significant clinical implications, suggesting that surgeons should aim for positive support during the reduction of intertrochanteric fractures to optimize mechanical stability and improve patient outcomes. Future research should address the limitations of our study by using cadaveric bones, incorporating dynamic loading conditions, and increasing sample sizes to further validate and extend our findings.
