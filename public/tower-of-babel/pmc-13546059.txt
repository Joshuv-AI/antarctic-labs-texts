Prevalence of Generalized Anxiety Disorder in Psychiatric Outpatients (PRADO Study): A Multicenter Cross-Sectional Study in Japan
Authors: Keisuke Nomoto, Tempei Otsubo, Shingo Higa, Satoshi Matsuyama, Takeshi Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGeneralized anxiety disorder (GAD) impairs quality of life (QoL) and daily function; however, it remains under-recognized in Japan due to symptom overlap and a lack of approved treatments. This study estimated GAD prevalence among psychiatric outpatients using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM (SCID), examined associated demographic and clinical factors, assessed health status and QoL, and evaluated the diagnostic validity of the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) and the Kessler 6-item Psychological Distress scale (K6) versus SCID.MethodsThis multicenter, cross-sectional study analyzed 407 patients across 22 sites. Prevalence was determined through SCID-based structured interviews. Demographics were obtained from paper questionnaires and clinical characteristics from medical records. Patient-reported outcomes quantified burden and screening accuracy.ResultsEighty-three of 407 patients (20.4%) met DSM-5 criteria for GAD using SCID. Prevalence was higher in females versus males (68.7% vs 31.3%) and more common among younger individuals. Comorbid depressive disorders were frequent (63.4%), with major depressive disorder being the most prevalent. Patients with GAD were more likely to have lower income, be students, and exhibit higher symptom severity on both the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology Self-Report (QIDS-SR) and the GAD-7. Patients with GAD (n=83) had higher QIDS-SR, GAD-7, and K6 scores than non-GAD patients, indicating greater depressive symptoms, anxiety, and psychological distress. Health-related QoL and functional impairment measured through EuroQol 5 Dimensions 5 Levels and the Sheehan Disability Scale indicated worse outcomes in GAD. GAD-7 and K6 demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance versus SCID.ConclusionGAD was prevalent among Japanese psychiatric outpatients (20.4%), suggesting the potential under-recognition in clinical practice, with some patients receiving alternative diagnoses. The lack of approved treatments and recognized diagnostic procedures may exacerbate the patient’s burden. Developing GAD-specific therapies and improving diagnostic awareness are critical to addressing this unmet need.Keywords: generalized anxiety disorder, Japan, prevalence, DSM-5, structured interview, PRO

Introduction

Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) is a chronic psychiatric condition marked by excessive, pervasive worry and associated psychological symptoms such as restlessness, fatigue, and sleep disturbances.1,2 GAD significantly impairs quality of life (QoL), affecting work productivity, interpersonal relationships, and health care utilization. Emotional dysregulation and reduced physical activity further exacerbate QoL outcomes.3 Although the etiology remains unclear, GAD is thought to result from a complex interaction of biological and genetic predisposition and environmental stressors, including adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), substance use, and comorbid somatic illnesses.4 Thus, unlike other anxiety disorders, the prevalence of GAD increases with age, with lower rates noted in adolescents and young adults and higher rates in older adults.5,6 However, more recent epidemiological evidence suggests that GAD can also be highly prevalent in younger cohorts.7 Women are disproportionately affected, with a prevalence of 3.4% versus 1.9% for men.8–10 Socioeconomic status also plays a role, with higher prevalence observed in high-income countries.7

A global epidemiological study from 2017 found the prevalence of GAD to be 3.7% according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria.7 Despite the high prevalence, GAD is often overlooked as excessive anxiety and worry. The hallmark symptoms of GAD are nonspecific and frequently co-occur with other psychiatric conditions, notably major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BD), panic disorder (PD), social anxiety disorder, and other anxiety disorders. Comorbidity rates are substantial: for example, a study from China reported that 71.7% of patients with MDD were also diagnosed with GAD, with GAD prevalence in BD types I and II at 14% and 16.6%, respectively.11,12 Another study from Japan similarly indicated a strong link between MDD and GAD, with an odds ratio (OR) of 11.5.13 These overlapping symptoms and comorbidities complicate timely and accurate diagnosis, underscoring the need for comprehensive assessment strategies.

In Japan, a recent study found the burden of GAD to follow global trends, indicating both a high prevalence rate and high humanistic burden, with probable patients with GAD indicating lower QoL.14 Moreover, even if patients are diagnosed with GAD, there are currently no officially recognized treatments that can receive insurance coverage. Consequently, patients may be treated with off-label products under alternative diagnoses. Some indications suggest that patients with GAD may visit psychiatric departments for other symptoms and remain undiagnosed. Hence, the actual prevalence of the disorder might be underestimated. Importantly, however, real-world data on the prevalence, clinical profile, and diagnostic recognition of GAD specifically among psychiatric outpatients in Japan remain limited, particularly when using standardized diagnostic tools such as the SCID in routine clinical settings.

The primary aim of this study was to estimate the prevalence of GAD among outpatients in Japanese psychiatric departments, according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental-5 (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria, as confirmed by the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Disorders (SCID). In addition, this study sought to characterize the clinical and humanistic burden of GAD within the context of Japanese psychiatric practice and to evaluate the performance of commonly used screening tools against a structured diagnostic standard, thereby providing context-specific evidence to inform clinical recognition and management of GAD in Japan.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted across psychiatric departments of 22 medical facilities in Japan to assess the prevalence of GAD among outpatients. Data were collected through structured interviews conducted at a single study visit, complemented by retrospective extraction of clinical information from patient medical charts. In addition, paper-based questionnaires were used to assess patient demographics and patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures.

Eligibility Criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were adults aged ≥18 to <80 years, were receiving outpatient psychiatric care (excluding first visit), and had a principal diagnosis within the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) code of F30–F39 (mood [affective] disorders) or F40–F48 (neurotic, stress-related, and somatoform disorders) categories. All patients provided written informed consent and received only standard clinical care.

Participants were excluded if they had a diagnosis of mental and behavioral disorders due to psychoactive substance use, including addiction diagnosed within 12 months prior to enrollment; current or past history of schizophrenia, delusional disorder, or other psychotic disorders; current diagnosis of dementia; or mood or anxiety disorders due to physiological conditions. Participants were also excluded if they had a history of convulsive disorders (excluding single febrile seizures in childhood), were deemed to be at imminent risk of suicide by the investigator, or were considered unsuitable for study participation based on the investigator’s clinical judgment.

Study Endpoints and Analysis Populations

The primary endpoint was the prevalence of GAD, determined according to DSM‑5 diagnostic criteria confirmed by the SCID.

The SCID diagnoses were summarized for two assessment time frames: lifetime (met DSM-5 criteria at least once in the participant’s lifetime) and current (met DSM-5 criteria at the study visit). Among current diagnoses, investigators additionally identified the primary diagnosis, defined as the single most clinically significant current condition. In addition, ICD‑10 diagnoses recorded prior to study enrollment were summarized from medical records. Psychiatric comorbidities at study visit (current diagnosis) and investigator‑assigned primary diagnoses were also evaluated among participants with SCID‑confirmed GAD.

Other outcomes included participant demographic and clinical characteristics and their association with GAD prevalence, health status, QoL quality of life, functional impairment, and symptom severity, as well as the diagnostic performance of the GAD‑7 and Kessler 6-item Psychological Distress scale (K6) instruments compared with the SCID.

The primary analysis was conducted on the Primary Analysis Population Set (PAS), which included all participants who met the inclusion criteria and did not meet any exclusion criteria. Exploratory analyses were conducted on the Exploratory Analysis Population Set (EAS), which was a subset of the PAS, excluding individuals who did not meet any current diagnostic criteria for psychiatric disorders according to the SCID. All outcomes were analyzed in the PAS. Outcomes related to patient‑reported measures were additionally evaluated in the EAS to provide a clearer characterization of the symptom burden and functional impact among participants with a current SCID‑confirmed psychiatric disorder.

Results were summarized overall for the total PAS population and stratified by GAD status (GAD vs, other psychiatric disorders [non‑GAD]) to assess the distinct burden associated with GAD in comparison with other psychiatric disorders in outpatient settings.

Diagnosis and Rating Scales

Structured interviews were conducted by investigators for the diagnosis of GAD. Specifically, the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders Research Version (SCID-5-RV), which is currently accepted as the gold standard for many psychiatric diagnoses, was used.15

The validated Japanese versions of rating scales were administered to assess symptom severity, functional impairment, and QoL. Generalized anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7), a participant measure of symptom frequency.16 Functional impairment was evaluated with the Sheehan Disability Scale (SDS), which assesses disruption in work, social, and family life.17,18 Health-related QoL was measured using the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L), which includes 5 dimensions and a visual analog scale (VAS) for self-rated health.19 ACEs were assessed using the ACE questionnaire, covering abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction.20,21 Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology Self-Report (QIDS-SR). Psychological distress was measured with the K6 scale. Clinician-rated severity of illness was assessed using the Clinical Global Impression-Severity (CGI-S) scale. CGI-S was applied only to patients diagnosed with GAD by SCID.22

Statistical Analysis

A GAD prevalence of approximately 6.5% was assumed based on prior SCID-based data derived from a comparable population.23 The sample size was calculated using a standard approach for estimating a population proportion with a two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) and a target precision of ±2.5%. Based on these assumptions, a minimum of 355 participants was required. As this was a multicenter study with individual-level enrollment and no cluster-based sampling, no design effect was applied. To account for potential variability and ensure adequate precision, the target sample size was set at 400 participants.

Analyses were performed using SAS software for Windows, version 9.4 or higher (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). No imputation was applied for missing or unknown data. Descriptive statistics were calculated based on available cases. The prevalence of GAD was estimated using descriptive statistics, and 95% CIs were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method. Summary statistics (means, standard deviations [SD], minimums, maximums, and first and third quartile values) were calculated for each PRO scale, viz, GAD-7, EQ-5D-5L, QIDS-SR, SDS, ACE calculator, and K6. The diagnostic performance of GAD-7 and K6 was assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and unweighted kappa coefficients. To explore potential risk factors associated with GAD, multiple logistic regression analyses were conducted with GAD diagnosis as the dependent variable. Independent variables included demographic and baseline characteristics. Additional sensitivity analyses using alternative multivariable logistic regression models were also performed: one model included a predefined set of selected covariates, while a second model employed a stepwise covariate selection approach. Odds ratios, 95% CIs, and p-values were reported for each covariate.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with local regulations and approved by the Medical Corporation TOUKEIKAI Kitamachi Clinic ethics review board prior to patient enrollment (approval no. QTN09944). Written approval for the protocol, informed consent form, and related documents was obtained before study initiation at each site, and investigators retained all ethics committee correspondence.

The study was registered with the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT1030230621).

Prevalence of GAD and Its Severity Levels Among Outpatients

In the PAS (N=407), 83 participants (20.4%) met DSM‑5 criteria for GAD and confirmed by SCID at the study visit. CGI‑S was administered to 53 participants with SCID‑confirmed GAD. Among those assessed, the mean CGI‑S score was 3.5 (SD 0.91). Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

The mean age in the overall PAS was 43.9 years, and the majority of participants were female (60.9%). Participants with GAD were younger than those without GAD (mean age 41.3 vs 44.6 years) and more likely to be female (68.7% vs 59.0%). Indicators of lower socioeconomic status were more frequent among participants with GAD, including lower household income (<4 million yen in 67.5% vs 53.1%) and lower representation among company employees or civil servants (24.1% vs 34.6%). Developmental disorders were more prevalent in the GAD group (20.5% vs 9.6%), particularly attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (Table 2). Participants with GAD also reported higher adverse childhood experience (ACE) scores (mean 2.3, SD 2.3 vs mean 1.6, SD 1.9) and a greater prevalence of at least one physical comorbidity (73.5% vs 60.2%) (Table 3). Psychotropic medication use was common in both groups (94.0% vs 84.6%) (Supplemental Table 1).

Other factors, including age and sex, were not significant in the adjusted model. These findings were supported by sensitivity analyses using alternative regression model specifications, which demonstrated similar overall trends (data not shown). These associations reflect cross‑sectional relationships and do not imply causality.

Prevalence of Psychiatric Disorders Among Outpatients

SCID diagnoses are summarized as lifetime prevalence and current prevalence (both overall and primary diagnosis) (Table 4). Depressive disorders were the most frequently observed major diagnostic classification across lifetime and current SCID assessments and were also the most commonly assigned primary diagnosis. For current overall diagnoses, MDD was the most frequent (33.9% at study visit, and 32.2% as the primary diagnosis), followed by GAD (20.4% at study visit, and 6.9% as the primary diagnosis). These trends were similar for lifetime prevalence, with 63.4% and 28.3% of participants having had MDD and GAD, respectively.

ICD‑10 primary diagnoses recorded prior to study enrollment in relation to SCID‑confirmed GAD at the study visit are summarized in Table 5. A total of 175 participants (43.0%) had a prior diagnosis of a depressive episode (F32) before study enrollment, and 37 of these (21.1%) were subsequently diagnosed with GAD using SCID during the study period. Recurrent depressive disorder (F33) was recorded in 63 participants (15.5%), of which 17 (27.0%) subsequently diagnosed with GAD. MDD corresponding to ICD-10 codes F32 and F33, was the most common diagnosis in our sample, affecting 238 participants (58.5%). Discussion

This study describes the burden associated with GAD among outpatients in Japanese psychiatric departments. The observed prevalence was higher than in comparable international studies (10.3% [Ozcan et al]24 and 13. 7% [Bobes et al]25), which may reflect both methodological differences and the exclusion of patients with psychotic disorders, potentially enriching the sample with mood and anxiety cases. Notably, this study adds to the limited body of evidence from Japan by providing SCID-based prevalence estimates and a detailed characterization of comorbidity patterns and patient-reported burden in a real-world psychiatric outpatient setting. Many patients who met DSM-5 criteria for GAD had not previously received a formal GAD diagnosis, which may indicate under-recognition in routine psychiatric care in this setting.

A notable diagnostic gap was identified in this study. Although 20.4% of patients met DSM-5 criteria for GAD via structured interviews, the majority had not previously received a formal GAD diagnosis. Instead, many had been diagnosed and treated for other psychiatric conditions, most commonly depressive episodes (ICD-10 F32). Overall, 43.0% (175/407) of all patients had a prior diagnosis of depression, and among these, 21.1% (37/175) were subsequently found to meet criteria for GAD in this study. This finding aligns with broader evidence indicating that GAD is frequently under-recognized in routine practice. A 2023 national survey in the United States found that over half of adults experiencing significant GAD symptoms had never been formally diagnosed.26 Moreover, the World Health Organization reports that globally only about 1 in 4 people with an anxiety disorder receive treatment, implying that roughly 75% of cases go untreated or undiagnosed.27 One contributing factor is symptom overlap with other disorders: GAD’s chronic worry and somatic complaints are often misattributed to conditions such as MDD, leading to misdiagnosis. In fact a recent review noted that nearly 30% of patients diagnosed with depression did not actually meet diagnostic criteria, and in half of those cases, an anxiety disorder (such as GAD) was the more appropriate diagnosis upon re-evaluation.28 These findings underscore the need for improved diagnostic vigilance; clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for GAD in patients with persistent anxiety or mood symptoms, especially when standard treatments yield suboptimal response. Enhancing screening (eg, use of GAD-7 in primary care) and increasing awareness of GAD’s distinct clinical profile can help close this recognition gap and ensure that patients receive appropriate, targeted interventions. This under-recognition underscores the need for clinicians to reassess patients with persistent mood or anxiety symptoms for possible GAD, particularly when symptoms evolve or do not respond to standard treatment.24,25 In this context, the present findings extend existing global evidence by illustrating how healthcare system characteristics in Japan, such as limited availability of approved GAD-specific treatments and reliance on symptom-based diagnostic pathways, may influence both recognition and management of GAD in clinical practice.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with GAD in this study were broadly similar to the literature. GAD was more prevalent among females and individuals in lower socioeconomic groups. Specifically, part-time or contract employees exhibited higher rates of GAD compared to full-time company employees or civil servants, and a greater proportion of patients with GAD reported household incomes below 4 million yen. While causality cannot be established, it is possible that symptoms of GAD may contribute to difficulties in maintaining stable employment. This interpretation is supported by PRO findings, which revealed that patients with GAD experienced significantly more underproductive days and absenteeism, as measured by the SDS.7,14,29

The clinical burden of GAD extended beyond employment challenges. Patients with GAD reported worse health-related QoL, greater psychological distress, and anxiety symptoms compared to non-GAD patients. These findings were consistent across multiple validated PRO instruments, including the EQ-5D-5L, SDS, QIDS-SR, GAD-7, and K6. Additionally, our study found that more patients with GAD had received treatment for another psychiatric condition (not GAD), with a notably higher proportion reporting a history of psychotropic medication use. This suggests that while patients with GAD are engaged in psychiatric care, their core condition may not be adequately addressed.

These findings were further supported by exploratory analyses conducted on the EAS, which excluded patients without current psychiatric symptoms. Within this subset, patients diagnosed with GAD consistently demonstrated significantly worse scores across all PRO instruments, including when compared with patients diagnosed with other psychiatric disorders such as MDD, BD, or social anxiety disorder. This reinforces the notion that GAD is associated with a uniquely high-level of functional impairment and symptom severity.30,31

In this study, GAD commonly co-occurred with other medical conditions. The majority of patients with GAD had at least one additional psychiatric diagnosis, most commonly depressive disorders. This overlap complicates diagnosis and management, as GAD symptoms may be misattributed to other conditions. These findings align broadly with previous national and international studies that have documented high rates of GAD–MDD comorbidity.7,12,32 However, associations observed in the cross-sectional analyses (including regression estimates) should not be interpreted as causal or mechanistic, particularly where confidence intervals are wide. Improving diagnostic precision and awareness of GAD’s distinct clinical profile is essential to ensure that patients receive appropriate, targeted interventions. This diagnostic under‑recognition has important clinical implications in the Japanese context. In Japan, pharmacological treatments approved specifically for GAD are limited, and there are no GAD‑specific treatment pathways currently covered by insurance. As a result, even when GAD is identified, management may default to treatments targeting comorbid conditions, most commonly depressive disorders, rather than addressing GAD as a distinct clinical entity. In this setting, failure to recognize and label GAD appropriately may further contribute to unmet treatment needs and an underestimation of the disease burden.

Finally, the study evaluated the diagnostic performance of the GAD-7 and K6 screening tools against the SCID gold standard. While GAD-7 demonstrated reasonable specificity, its sensitivity was limited, indicating that it may be more effective in ruling out GAD than in confirming it. K6 showed poor diagnostic performance overall. These results suggest that while screening tools can aid in initial assessment, structured interviews remain essential for accurate diagnosis.33

In summary, this study underscores the high prevalence, significant burden, and frequent under-recognition of GAD in Japanese psychiatric outpatient settings. Enhancing diagnostic practices and expanding access to GAD-specific treatments are critical steps toward improving outcomes for affected patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, although the study was designed as a cross-sectional assessment with data collected at a single study visit, it incorporated both prospectively collected data (structured interviews and patient-reported outcomes) and retrospectively extracted information from patient medical records. As such, the study design may be subject to inherent limitations associated with retrospective data collection, including incomplete documentation and potential misclassification. In addition, potential confounding factors cannot be fully ruled out given the observational nature of the study. Furthermore, given that the prevalence of GAD in the study population was relatively high (~20%), the use of odds ratios in the regression analyses may overestimate the strength of associations compared with prevalence ratios. Therefore, these estimates should be interpreted with caution. While the present findings suggest potential gaps between structured diagnostic assessment and routine clinical diagnosis, the study was not designed to directly compare SCID-based diagnoses with routine clinical diagnoses, and therefore firm conclusions regarding underdiagnosis cannot be drawn. Secondly, although the selection criteria covered illnesses that account for a large portion of psychiatric outpatient cases, restricting the study population to specific diagnostic categories may have affected representativeness, for example, the exclusion of individuals with psychotic disorders may have skewed the sample toward patients with mood and anxiety disorders. Thirdly, even though participants were continuously enrolled, this study included only those who consented and whom investigators judged eligible for participation, and this reliance on investigator discretion and patient willingness to participate may have introduced selection bias, potentially influencing both the observed prevalence estimates and the characterization of clinical and PRO outcomes. Finally, as the study population was restricted to psychiatric outpatients from selected clinical sites, the findings may have limited generalizability to the broader population, including patients managed in other healthcare settings (eg, inpatient or primary care) and those not engaged in specialist psychiatric care. In addition, cultural and healthcare system–specific factors in Japan may further limit extrapolation of these findings to other geographic regions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that GAD is relatively common among psychiatric outpatients in Japan, with a prevalence of 20.4%. However, it may be under-recognized in routine clinical practice. Despite structured assessment identifying GAD in a substantial proportion of patients, it was often not assigned as the primary diagnosis, particularly in the presence of comorbid depressive disorders. Patients with GAD experienced considerable symptom burden, functional impairment, and reduced QoL, underscoring the clinical significance of potentially missed or delayed recognition. In the Japanese context, where treatment options approved specifically for GAD are limited, improving diagnostic precision and awareness of GAD as a distinct clinical entity is essential. Future efforts should focus on strengthening diagnostic practices and developing comprehensive strategies to better address the unmet needs of patients with GAD in Japan.
