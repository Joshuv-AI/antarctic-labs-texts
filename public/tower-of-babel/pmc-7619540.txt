Ultrasound-guided renal artery administration of angiopoietin-1 RNA therapy slows the progression of preclinical WT1 glomerular disease
Authors: Saif N Malik, Jennifer C Chandler, Ruhina Maeshima, Maria Kolatsi-Joannou, William J Mason, Lauren G Russell, Shunping Han, Yanis Harfouche, Daniyal J Jafree, Emily A E Moore, Hanna Gautier, Christina Katsiva, Reem Al-Saadi, Kathy Pritchard-Jones, Daniel J Stuckey, Andrew V Benest, David O Bates, Paul J Winyard, Aoife M Waters, Luigi Gnudi, Stephen L Hart, Tammy L Kalber, David A Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mutations in the transcription factor Wilms Tumour 1 (WT1) are one of the leading causes of congenital glomerular disease, characterised by severe urinary protein loss and glomerular scarring. Currently no disease modifying therapies exist for WT1 glomerulopathies and affected children rely on dialysis or kidney transplantation. We evaluated a new treatment in a mouse model with an orthologous human mutation in Wt1 (Wt1+/R394W) that replicates the pathology of WT1 glomerulopathy. Lipid nanocomplexes were engineered to target integrin αvβ3 for efficient delivery of mRNA to primary podocytes and glomerular endothelial cells in vitro. A minimally invasive ultrasound-guided renal artery injection enabled precise and specific kidney localization in vivo, a finding not replicated by systemic administration. Nanocomplex-derived protein localized in glomeruli for up to seven days in healthy and diseased mice. This platform was then used to perform an interventional preclinical trial in Wt1+/R394W mice, delivering angiopoietin-1 (Angpt1) mRNA, a vascular growth factor critical for glomerular health which is reduced in Wt1+/R394W podocytes. Angpt1 nanocomplex therapy reduced albuminuria, preserved glomerular endothelial integrity, prevented podocyte loss, and alleviated glomerulosclerosis in Wt1+/R394W mice. These findings demonstrate the therapeutic potential of this targeted approach for WT1 glomerulopathy and provide a foundation for clinical translation to improve outcomes in children with glomerular disease.

Introduction

Congenital glomerular diseases are among the most common diagnoses in childhood chronic kidney disease (CKD) (1). Over 80 monogenic causes of congenital glomerular disease have been identified, the majority of which implicate epithelial podocytes (2). Together with glomerular endothelial cells (gECs) and the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), podocytes form the glomerular permeability barrier, preventing molecules such as albumin from entering the urine (3). Variants in the podocyte transcription factor Wilms Tumour 1 (WT1) account for around 15% of congenital glomerular diseases, with patients commonly presenting with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome and diffuse mesangial sclerosis (DMS) (4). This includes a cohort with Denys-Drash syndrome (DDS, OMIM: 194080) that have a high risk of Wilms’ Tumour, with some patients presenting with disorders of sex development (5).

There are no disease-modifying therapies for WT1 glomerulopathies. This reflects not only the lack of treatment targets capable of slowing glomerular damage, but also the inadequacy of systemic administration routes that result in minimal kidney uptake (6, 7), necessitating higher therapeutic dosing and increasing the risk of adverse events. Additionally, the cellular complexity of the kidney means it is difficult to direct treatments to specific cells in the nephron (8). Glomerular therapies also need to overcome possible size and charge exclusion due to the presence of gEC fenestrations and podocyte slit diaphragms, as well as the negative charge of podocytes (9) and the GBM (10).

Glomerular structure and function are maintained by molecular signalling between podocytes and gECs (11). This communication is disrupted in adult glomerular diseases (11), as well as in Wt1+/R394W mice (12), a genetically orthologous model of DDS which harbours the most commonly diagnosed WT1 mutation (WT1 c.1180C>T; p.R394W). Wt1+/R394W mice replicate DDS glomerular pathology, with albuminuria and DMS but do not present with genital abnormalities nor Wilms’ Tumour (13). In the early stages of disease in Wt1+/R394W mice, podocyte-gEC signalling is disrupted, prior to gEC loss, indicating that therapies targeting the vasculature may be beneficial (12). Angiopoietin-1 (ANGPT1) is a podocyte vascular growth factor (14), critical for glomerular health (15), through its maintenance of endothelial structure and permeability through tyrosine kinase with immunoglobulin-like and EGF-like domains 2 (TIE2) receptor activation (11, 16), and its support of perivascular-endothelial cell interactions (17). Reduced podocyte ANGPT1 expression occurs in preclinical models of adult glomerular disease (14, 18, 19). Therefore, considering the disruption in podocyte-gEC communication observed in Wt1+/R394W mice, we hypothesised that ANGPT1 expression may be reduced in Wt1+/R394W glomeruli, and that targeted Angpt1 RNA therapy could slow disease progression in this context.

To test this, we designed a therapeutic approach to deliver Angpt1 mRNA to glomeruli. Firstly, we predicted that local delivery would be more effective than systemic administration and evaluated this using a minimally invasive ultrasound-guided renal artery injection, with proven success in the administration of stem cells to mouse kidneys (20). Secondly, although studies incorporating glomerular cell-specific promoters (21) or targeting peptides (22) into adeno-associated viruses (AAV) have shown therapeutic promise, we adopted a non-viral approach, with the intention of reducing the risk of glomerular size or charge exclusion of our therapy. To do so, we encapsulated Angpt1 mRNA into cationic lipid nanocomplexes that protect and stabilise negatively charged nucleic acids (23) and self-assemble into particles of different sizes (23). Finally, we reasoned that Angpt1 therapy would be more effective if our nanocomplexes contained peptides which enhanced cellular uptake in podocytes and gECs. By incorporating these components, we sought to determine whether a glomerular targeted Angpt1 nanocomplex, delivered locally through the renal artery, could slow progression of glomerular disease in Wt1+/R394W mice.

Results

Nanocomplexes containing integrin αvβ3 targeting peptides improve podocyte and gEC transfection

Prior studies have shown nanocomplexes containing an integrin targeted cyclic Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) peptide facilitate podocyte uptake (24). However, RGD binds multiple integrins (25) found in non-glomerular nephron cells (26), limiting the specificity of these nanocomplexes for treating glomerular disease. We reasoned that nanocomplexes containing targeting peptides binding to glomerular enriched integrins would be more effective. We identified integrin αvβ3 as a promising target reflecting its reported expression profile in podocytes and gECs (27–29).

First, we determined the localization of integrin αvβ3 within healthy human kidneys. Kidney sections from two healthy pediatric donors were stained for integrin β3, selected to represent αvβ3 expression as integrin αv subunits form additional heterodimers in the kidney besides αvβ3, whereas β3 does not (29). β3 expression was enriched in healthy glomeruli compared with non-glomerular regions (Fig. 1A). This β3 glomerular enrichment was replicated (Fig. 1B) in biopsy tissue from a patient with the WT1p.R394W mutation, with DMS on histology (12). Glomerular enrichment of β3 was also observed in kidney sections of 8-week-old wild-type Wt1+/+ (Fig. 1C) and Wt1+/R394W (Fig. 1D) mice, when glomerular scarring was present (12). Immunofluorescent co-staining for synaptopodin found β3 expression in close proximity to glomerular podocytes in healthy pediatric and WT1p.R394W patient biopsy tissue (Fig. 1A and B), a finding replicated in mouse kidneys (Fig. 1C and D). To examine this at the cellular level, β3 localization was assessed in wild-type primary WT1+/synaptopodin+ (fig. S1A to D) mouse podocytes (Fig. 1E) and cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31)+ gECs (Fig. 1F). In both cell types, β3 showed expression on the plasma membrane (Fig. 1E and F). β3 expression was also detected on the plasma membrane of diseased Wt1+/R394W primary podocytes (fig. S2A) and significantly elevated mRNA expression was apparent in Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W primary podocytes compared with the whole kidney (fig. S2B; P = 0.003 and P = 0.008 respectively for Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W podocytes versus whole kidney), with no difference between genotype (P = 0.96). Together, these data demonstrated glomerular localization and enrichment of integrin β3 in podocytes and gECs, providing rationale to examine the efficacy of targeting αvβ3 for specific transfection of both cell types.

We manufactured lipid nanocomplexes based on formulations successfully used for delivery of nucleic acids to tumour and lung cells (23). We compared the transfection efficiency of nanocomplexes incorporating peptides (30) containing either cyclic RGD or RWrNM targeting motifs, on primary podocytes and gECs. The RWrNM motif, an RGD peptidomimetic, was chosen as it has higher affinity and specificity for αvβ3 compared to RGD when assessed in tumour cells (31). As an additional control, we included nanocomplexes with a RWRNM motif to evaluate the importance of the central arginine chirality (D- or L-Arginine) on integrin αvβ3 binding affinity. Each peptide was complexed with cationic liposomes, encapsulating luciferase mRNA (Fig. 1G). Initial experiments identified DOTMA:DOPE (C18) as the most efficient liposome in primary podocytes, and this was used for all subsequent experiments (fig. S3). RGD and RWrNM luciferase mRNA nanocomplex formulations had a respective mean size ± standard deviation of 202.1 nm ± 7.0 and 220 nm ± 1.0 (fig. S4A and B); polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.29 ± 0.01 and 0.25 ± 0.02 and zeta potential (ZP) of 38.1 ± 0.4 mV and 56.3 ± 0.5 mV (table S1).

RWrNM nanocomplexes elicited 2- and 1.5-fold significantly higher transfection rates in wild-type primary murine podocytes than RGD and RWRNM nanocomplexes respectively (Fig. 1H; RGD = 6.35 × 105 ± 1.04 × 105, RWrNM = 1.26 × 106 ± 1.33 × 105 and RWRNM = 8.27 × 105 ± 1.28 × 105 RLU/mg of protein, P = 0.004 and P = 0.04 for RWrNM versus RGD and RWRNM respectively). In primary murine gECs, RWrNM nanocomplexes elicited 2.1- and 3.7-fold significantly higher transfection rates than RGD and RWRNM respectively (Fig. 1I; RGD = 9.06 × 105 ± 1.93 × 105, RWrNM = 1.95 × 106 ± 1.60 × 105 and RWRNM = 5.21 × 105 ± 4.26 × 105 RLU/mg of protein, P = 0.01 and P < 0.001 for RWrNM versus RGD and RWRNM respectively). We also tested whether RWrNM nanocomplexes improved transfection in mutant Wt1+/R394W podocytes isolated at 8 weeks of age during advanced disease (12) and found 6- and 3-fold significantly higher transfection efficiency than RGD and RWRNM nanocomplexes respectively (fig. S5A; RGD = 1.77 × 105 ± 4.23 × 104, RWrNM = 1.09 × 106 ± 2.21 × 105 and RWRNM = 3.23 × 105 ± 1.71 × 105 RLU/mg of protein, P = 0.0013 and P = 0.0054 for RWrNM versus RGD and RWRNM respectively). Finally, to determine whether the Wt1 mutation and its associated phenotype affected nanocomplex uptake, we directly compared RWrNM nanocomplex uptake between wild-type and Wt1+/R394W primary podocytes isolated at 8 weeks of age and found no significant difference (fig. S5B; Wt1+/+ = 2.69 × 106 ± 9.40 × 105 and Wt1+/R394W = 3.03 × 106 ± 1.06 × 106, P = 0.81). Collectively, this demonstrates the efficacy of RWrNM for αvβ3-mediated transfection in primary podocytes and gECs in vitro.

Ultrasound-guided renal artery injection facilitates kidney localization of RWrNM nanocomplexes in vivo

Next, we aimed to assess the in vivo biodistribution of RWrNM nanocomplexes containing 20 μg of luciferase mRNA (97.24 ± 2.19 % encapsulation efficiency) delivered through a minimally invasive, ultrasound-guided renal artery injection (20). Color doppler mode visualized kidney blood vessels allowing for direct injection into the renal artery without surgical intervention. The right kidney of 5-week-old female CD1 nude mice was injected and live whole-body, bioluminescent imaging (BLI) scans were taken 18 hours post injection, followed by ex vivo assessment of the injected kidney, non-injected control kidney, lungs and liver.

Whole body scans showed specific localization in the injected right kidney with no apparent signal from the rest of the body (Fig. 2A). Quantitative analysis of the total bioluminescence in each mouse revealed that 80.67 ± 6.48 % of the mean percentage signal was accounted for by the injected kidney (fig. S6). To validate live kidney localization, we combined three dimensional (3D) BLI with micro computed tomography (micro-CT) following renal artery injection of luciferase mRNA RWrNM nanocomplexes. The 3D BLI reconstruction superimposed onto a micro-CT scan of the mouse showed the BLI signal localized to the injected right kidney (Fig. 2B). Injected kidneys showed a significantly higher luminescent signal ex vivo (photons/second) than either the non-injected kidney (36.9-fold), lungs (37.6-fold) and liver (21.2-fold) (Fig. 2C; injected kidney = 1.31 × 105 ± 2.15 × 104, non-injected kidney = 3.55 × 103 ± 1.01 × 103, lung = 3.48 × 103 ± 2.74 × 102, liver = 6.18 × 103 ± 8.67 × 102, injected kidney versus all other organs P < 0.001).

To compare the biodistribution of locally and systemically delivered RWrNM nanocomplexes, we injected luciferase mRNA nanocomplexes at the same dose into the tail vein of female 5-week-old CD1 nude mice. Luminescent signal was not observed in any organ following whole body in vivo dorsal and ventral BLI scans. No signal was found in either kidney ex vivo (Fig. 2D) with bioluminescence only observed in the liver of one mouse (5.52 × 104 photons per second) and spleen of both mice (8.68 × 104 ± 6.13 × 103 photons per second).

We also assessed biodistribution following renal artery injection in 8-week-old male Wt1+/R394W mice. In all animals, the luminescent signal localized to the injected kidney in vivo and ex vivo (Fig. 2E). Although fur coverage restricted quantitative analysis of whole body bioluminescence, ex vivo measurements showed the injected kidney to have significantly higher luminescence than the non-injected kidney (12.1-fold), lung (23.3-fold) and liver (14.0-fold), as observed in the CD1 mice (Fig. 2F; injected kidney = 7.48 × 104 ± 7.97 × 103, non-injected kidney = 6.48 × 103 ± 7.41 × 102, lung = 3.36 × 103 ± 3.35 × 102, liver = 5.60 × 103 ± 4.90 × 102 photons/second, injected kidney versus all other organs P < 0.001). Together, this confirmed a suitable delivery technique to localize RWrNM nanocomplexes to the kidney in health and glomerular disease.

Nanocomplex cargo is detected in kidney glomeruli up to 7 days post injection in Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W mice

Once kidney localization of the RWrNM nanocomplexes was evident, we aimed to determine glomerular uptake and the longevity of protein expression in vivo. To do so, we administered mCherry mRNA (20 μg) RWrNM nanocomplexes (mean size = 227.4 ± 5.9 nm (fig. S4C); mean PDI = 0.264 ± 0.015; mean ZP = 41.4 ± 0.7 mV; encapsulation efficiency = 97.68 ± 2.54 %) (table S1) into the right kidney of Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W mice at 4 and 8 weeks of age. These time points represent early and advanced disease in Wt1+/R394W mice, with albuminuria but no overt histological damage at 4 weeks and elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) alongside glomerular scarring at 8 weeks (12).

Mice of each genotype and age were euthanized at 24 hours, 3 days or 7 days post injection and the injected kidneys were stained for mCherry and the podocyte marker synaptopodin. Representative micrographs for all groups showed mCherry positive staining within glomeruli, indicating the efficacy of the RWrNM peptide in localizing nanocomplexes to the glomerulus up to 7 days post injection in Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W animals at 4- and 8- weeks of age (Fig. 3A to D). At the cellular level, mCherry showed minimal colocalization with synaptopodin, suggesting the primary site of uptake to be gECs (Fig. 3A to D).

The percentage of mCherry positive glomeruli at 24 hours post injection was 75.3% ± 3.71 and 81.3% ± 2.90 in 4-week-old and 66.0% ± 16.8 and 76.3% ± 12.6 in 8-week-old Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W mice respectively (Fig. 3E and F). For animals at 4 weeks of age, this was significantly reduced to 25.3% ± 4.80 (Wt1+/+) and 38.7% ± 8.96 (Wt1+/R394W) by 3 days post injection (Fig. 3E; P ≤ 0.001 for both genotypes 24 hours versus 3 days post injection). Similarly, in 8-week-old animals the number of mCherry positive glomeruli was reduced by 3 days post injection to 34.7% ± 8.4 (Wt1+/+) and 35.3% ± 19.2 (Wt1+/R394W) compared with 24 hours, although this did not reach significance (Fig. 3F; P = 0.30 and P = 0.13 for 8-week-old Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W mice respectively between 24 hours and 3 days). From 3 to 7 days, there was no further reduction in mCherry positive glomeruli, with 40.0% ± 4.2 and 37.7% ± 7.3 for 4-week-old and 38.3% ± 4.2 and 53% ± 5.7 for 8-week-old Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W mice respectively (Fig.3 E and F; P = 0.26 and P > 0.99 for 4-week-old and P = 0.99 and P = 0.79 for 8-week-old Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W mice respectively between 3 and 7 days).

There was no difference between the percentage of mCherry positive glomeruli in Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W mice at 24 hours, 3 days or 7 days post injection (fig. S7A and B, P = 0.85, P = 0.33 and P = 0.98 for 4-week old and P = 0.93, P > 0.99 and P = 0.86 for 8-week old Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W mice at 24 hours, 3 days and 7 days respectively), indicating that disease state does not affect glomerular RWrNM nanocomplex uptake. Additionally, we pooled Wt1+/+ and Wt1+/R394W animals at each time point post injection and found no significant differences between 4- and 8-week animals (fig. S7C, P = 0.83, P = 0.98 and P = 0.93 for 24 hours, 3 days and 7 days respectively between 4- and 8-week-old animals), suggesting that glomerular uptake is similar across both analysed ages.

Angpt1 nanocomplexes are stable for one month and their delivery enhances TIE2 kidney phosphorylation in Wt1+/R394W kidneys

Having demonstrated the efficacy of our nanocomplex formulation in podocytes and gECs in vitro, alongside an effective delivery route facilitating glomerular uptake in vivo, we aimed to assess whether this approach could be used to administer an Angpt1 RNA therapy. Firstly, we performed CiiiDER analysis, which identified predicted WT1 binding motifs within both the human and mouse Angpt1 proximal promotor regions, suggesting transcriptional regulation of Angpt1 by WT1 (fig. S8A). We then assessed Angpt1 transcript expression in Wt1+/R394W primary podocytes (cultured ex vivo) and found a significant reduction compared with Wt1+/+ controls at 4- (P = 0.011) and 8-weeks of age (P≤0.001) (Fig. 4A), akin to findings in mouse models of adult glomerular diseases (14, 18, 19), as well as in patients with CKD (fig. S8B), and corresponding with the loss of gECs observed by 8 weeks of age in Wt1+/R394W mice (12). Thus, considering the protective role of ANGPT1 on the endothelium (11), we sought to replenish glomerular Angpt1 expression using mRNA nanocomplexes as a therapeutic strategy for Wt1+/R394W glomerulopathy.

Angpt1 nanocomplexes were visualized by negative stain transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 4B), displaying spherical particles 50 -100 nm in diameter. Colloidal stability was assessed by measuring hydrodynamic size (fig. S9A) and PDI (fig. S9B) over 28 days at 4°C and 25°C, reflecting clinically accessible storage conditions. At 25°C, nanocomplexes exhibited a 37.9% reduction in mean size (fig. S9A) and a 17.5% rise in PDI (fig. S9B) over 28 days, indicating structural collapse and loss of uniformity. Over the same timeframe, nanocomplexes at 4°C maintained greater integrity and homogeneity, showing a 9.2% reduction in size (fig. S9A) and 9.9% decrease in PDI (fig. S9B). Guided by our previous experiments using 20 μg of luciferase or mCherry mRNA per kidney, pilot studies were performed in which 4-week-old Wt1+/R394W mutant mice were administered RWrNM nanocomplexes carrying either 10 μg (total 20 μg) or 20 μg (total 40 μg) Angpt1 into each kidney via the renal artery. Twenty-four hours post injection, animals that received 20 μg of Angpt1 mRNA per kidney had significantly increased Angpt1 expression in the kidney cortex, a finding not replicated in animals treated with 10 μg (fig. S10; P = 0.052 and P = 0.033 for 10 μg and 20 μg respectively versus non injected Wt1+/R394W animals). Therefore a 20 μg dose per kidney was selected for further studies. Based on our longevity data (Fig. 3), we decided to provide three doses of Angpt1 nanocomplexes (mean size = 153.6 ± 5.2 nm, fig. S4D; mean PDI = 0.212 ± 0.014; mean ZP = 40.1 ± 0.5 mV; encapsulation efficiency = 97.92 ± 1.77 %) (table S1) into each kidney of Wt1+/R394W mice between the ages of 4 and 8 weeks.

The design of our interventional preclinical trial consisted of 4-week-old Wt1+/R394W mice treated with 20 μg mCherry mRNA nanocomplexes delivered via the renal artery of each kidney (RA vehicle), 40 μg Angpt1 mRNA nanocomplexes delivered via the tail vein (IV Angpt1), or 20 μg Angpt1 mRNA nanocomplexes delivered via the renal artery of each kidney (RA Angpt1) (Fig. 4C). To ensure that neither the procedure nor the therapy had a negative effect on overall health, mice were weighed at baseline and five days post each injection. Across all groups weight gradually increased with age (fig. S11), suggesting that therapeutic administration was well tolerated.

At the termination of the experiment at 8 weeks of age, our first aim was to determine the bioactivity of our treatment by assessing the phosphorylation of the ANGPT1 receptor, TIE2 (pTIE2) by Western blot (Fig. 4D). Total protein isolated from the kidney cortex of animals in the IV Angpt1 group (0.45 ± 0.18) showed pTIE2 expression that was 2.5-fold higher than the RA vehicle group (0.18 ± 0.03), but this did not reach significance (P = 0.16). In contrast, the RA Angpt1 group, had significantly increased expression of pTIE2 (0.59 ± 0.07), 3.3-fold higher than (P = 0.01) the RA vehicle group (Fig. 4E). Analysis of total TIE2 in RA Angpt1 kidney cortex found this significant increase was due to changes in pTIE2 rather than elevated expression of total TIE2, as there was no alteration in total TIE2 protein between any of the groups (Fig. 4F; RA Vehicle 1.76 ± 0.23, IV Angpt1 1.37 ± 0.19 and RA Angpt1 1.23 ± 0.16, P = 0.16). The pTIE2:total TIE2 ratio corroborated this, with the RA Angpt1 pTIE2:total TIE2 ratio (0.60 ± 0.13) significantly higher than RA Vehicle animals (0.10 ± 0.01), whereas the IV Angpt1 group did not reach significance (0.31 ± 0.09) (Fig. 4G; P = 0.01 and P = 0.48 for RA Angpt1 and IV Angpt1 versus RA vehicle respectively).

We then examined if pTIE2 was localized within glomeruli using immunohistochemistry. In accord with the Western blot data, there was a 1.6-fold increase in mean percentage pTIE2 glomerular coverage in the RA Angpt1 group (11.56%) compared with both the RA vehicle (7.36%, P = 0.094) and IV Angpt1 (7.62%, P = 0.174) groups, although these increases were not significant over 50 glomeruli analysed/animal (Fig. 4H). Representative micrographs support this trend, with more pTIE2 staining in the glomerular tuft of RA Angpt1 animals (Fig. 4I).

Angpt1 nanocomplex therapy improves glomerular structure and function in Wt1+/R394W mice

We then examined the glomerular structure and function of Wt1+/R394W mice following Angpt1 RNA therapy. As a measure of glomerular function, urinary albumin:creatine ratio (ACR) was monitored throughout the treatment period and showed an average significant decline of 21.4% from 4 to 8 weeks of age in RA Angpt1 animals (Fig. 5A; log10 ACR of RA Angpt1 4 week 3.14 ± 0.20 versus RA Angpt1 8 week 2.64 ± 0.17 μg/mg, P = 0.0018). The ACR in the RA Angpt1 was reduced to values comparable to that observed in our historical Wt1+/+ measurements at 8 weeks of age (log10 2.21 ± 0.12 μg/mg). In contrast, there was no change in ACR from 4 to 8 weeks in both RA vehicle (4 week 3.43 ± 0.23 versus 8 week 3.38 ± 0.22 μg/mg; P = 0.98) and IV Angpt1 treatment groups (4 week 3.38 ± 0.27 versus 8 week 3.16 ± 0.30 μg/mg; P = 0.52). Coomassie blue staining was also performed on terminal urine samples from the RA Vehicle and RA Angpt1 groups (Fig. 5B). The RA Angpt1 treated group exhibited bands with significantly smaller pixel area at 67 KDa, the size of albumin, compared with RA Vehicle mice (Fig. 5C; P = 0.04). As an overall indication of kidney health, BUN was measured at 4 and 8 weeks of age (fig. S12A). Paired analysis showed no significant change in BUN for any group (RA vehicle, P = 0.99; IV Angpt1, P = 0.99; RA Angpt1, P = 0.57). Plasma creatinine was assessed in terminal blood plasma at 8 weeks of age by mass spectrometry and showed no significant difference between any group (fig. S12B; P = 0.19).

Due to the anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, and stabilising effect of ANGPT1 on endothelial cells (14, 18) and the significant loss of gECs observed in Wt1+/R394W mice (12) by 8 weeks, we next assessed gEC coverage across treatment groups at 8 weeks of age using CD31 immunostaining (Fig. 5D). We found that glomerular CD31+ percentage coverage was significantly increased in the RA Angpt1 animals (51.2% ± 2.3) compared with either RA vehicle (29.9% ± 2.2) (Fig. 5E; P < 0.001) or IV Angpt1 (36.4% ± 4.3) groups (P = 0.004), a percentage similar to that seen in our historical samples of Wt1+/+ animals (52.39 ± 4.0% (12)). In contrast, there was no significant difference between RA vehicle and IV Angpt1 groups (P = 0.45).

To determine if Angpt1 therapy was impacting podocytes we quantified the number of WT1+ cells within the glomerular tuft area. In the RA Angpt1 group, we found a significant increase in the number of WT1+ cells per tuft area (2.7 × 10-3 ± 1.3 × 10-4) compared with RA vehicle (1.2 × 10-3 ± 1.8 × 10-4; P < 0.001), and IV Angpt1 (1.9 × 10-3 ± 2.1 × 10-4; P = 0.009) groups (Fig. 5D and F), a number similar to that observed in our historical Wt1+/+ animals (3.2 × 10-3 ± 3.9 × 10-4 (12)). A significantly higher number of WT1+ cells was also observed in the IV Angpt1 group compared to RA vehicle group (P = 0.002).

One of the pathological hallmarks of WT1 glomerulopathy is glomerulosclerosis, the deposition of ECM components in the mesangium which leads to irreversible glomerular scarring, reducing its filtration capacity. In the Wt1+/R394W model, similar to gEC loss, glomerulosclerosis is present by 8 weeks of age, defining an irreversible stage of disease (12). Therefore, we assessed glomerulosclerosis, measured by a pathological severity score index (32), to determine whether RA Angpt1 therapy resulted in reduced scarring. Representative micrographs of the RA vehicle group (Fig. 5G) show matrix deposition and sclerosis, with few capillary loop structures. In IV Angpt1 animals, glomeruli showed more open capillary loop structures and less sclerosis, but with some regions of matrix deposition (Fig. 5G). This matrix deposition was visibly reduced in RA Angpt1 glomeruli, with an increase in the number of visible open capillary loops (Fig. 5G). Blinded quantitative analysis showed RA Angpt1 animals had a significantly lower glomerulosclerosis score (1.47 ± 0.14) than either RA vehicle (2.84 ± 0.07; P < 0.001) or IV Angpt1 (2.14 ± 0.07; P = 0.007) groups (Fig. 5H). Similar to that observed with podocyte number, IV Angpt1 treatment improved glomerulosclerosis compared with the RA vehicle group (Fig. 5H, P = 0.008).

Collectively, these clinically-relevant metrics showed a significant improvement in ACR, rescued gEC and podocyte number, and prevention of glomerulosclerosis after three doses of Angpt1 RWrNM nanocomplex therapy via renal artery administration in Wt1+/R394W mice. In contrast, only partial prevention of glomerulosclerosis and podocyte number was achieved through IV administration, highlighting the importance of local kidney delivery.

Discussion

Our study describes a glomerular-targeted Angpt1 nanocomplex that, when delivered through the renal artery, slows disease progression in a genetically orthologous WT1 glomerulopathy model. This work establishes a targeted RNA therapeutic approach for a congenital glomerular disorder lacking effective treatments and provides a foundation for clinical translation to improve outcomes in affected children.

To localize our Angpt1 therapy to the glomerulus we adopted a lipid nanocomplex approach allowing for small peptide targeting to integrin αvβ3. We used the targeting motif RWrNM to increase uptake by two key cells implicated in WT1 glomerulopathy, podocytes and gECs (12). Compared with cyclic RGD, RWrNM improved transfection efficiency of both cell types in vitro, aligning with findings in tumour cells (31) and rat primary podocytes (33). This is the first use of the RWrNM peptide in combination with cationic lipid nanocomplexes (23) to deliver nucleic acids, providing a strong foundation for future RNA therapies.

Systemic administration for renal therapies is limited by reticuloendothelial system (RES)-mediated clearance, causing high liver and spleen accumulation (34). Consistent with this, we found no kidney localization of luciferase RWrNM nanocomplexes following IV injection, in accord with a prior study using integrin αvβ3-targeted RWrNM micelles (33), underscoring the need for alternative delivery routes even for glomerular-targeted vehicles. Renal artery injection bypasses the RES, improving kidney uptake, highlighted by microbubble (35), microcapsule (36), extracellular vesicle (37) and stem cell (38) delivery. However, these studies utilise surgical approaches like aortic ligation (37) or catheter-based delivery (36), posing risks such as hematoma (39). By contrast, ultrasound-guided renal artery injection is minimally invasive, requires no surgery (20) and achieves approximately 80% kidney-specific localization of luciferase nanocomplexes.

Our non-viral strategy facilitated glomerular retention of nanocomplexes in wild-type and diseased mice, consistent with findings in other murine glomerular disease models (40). This contrasts with smaller AAVs, which are detected in the urine of mice (41) and non-human primates (42) post IV injection, with a further report showing a 104 fold increase in urinary AAV9 concentration in CKD mice compared with wildtype controls, a finding associated with increased glomerular permeability (43). The absence of colocalization between mCherry and the podocyte marker synaptopodin suggests that our nanocomplexes primarily interact with gECs, likely reflecting their larger size compared with other delivery vectors, reducing the chances of urinary loss. In this context, gEC localization is not expected to limit therapeutic efficacy as ANGPT1 is a secreted molecule that acts locally to maintain gEC health (11). Moreover, Angpt1 nanocomplexes were smaller than the mCherry nanocomplexes used for cell localization analysis, increasing the likelihood of podocyte uptake and potential enhancement of natural podocyte-gEC crosstalk. Overall, local provision to the glomerulus is sufficient for ANGPT1 to elicit a protective effect in Wt1+/R394W mice.

Podocyte overexpression of Angpt1 in adult diabetic mice also reduces albuminuria (14) and prevents gEC proliferation. Although providing a strong proof of concept for Angpt1 therapy, this transgenic approach is not clinically feasible. In a more translational strategy, IV delivered COMP-ANGPT1 adenoviruses also reduced albuminuria in diabetic mice, alongside attenuation of mesangial matrix deposition and GBM thickening, slowing progression of glomerulosclerosis (44). Despite using an untargeted vector and systemic delivery, the efficacy of this approach may reflect the use of COMP-ANGPT1, a more potent ANGPT1 variant, with enhanced TIE2 activation. Our data adds to these observations, showing that Angpt1 therapy is also protective in congenital glomerular disease, improving albuminuria and preventing glomerulosclerosis. Angpt1 expression is also reduced in rodent models of glomerulonephritis (18), and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (19)colloidal stability and reduced aggregation. Additionally, we report ANGPT1 is decreased in human CKD, a finding confirmed by others (45). Furthermore, an ANGPT1 mimic is protective in a unilateral urethral obstruction murine model of CKD (45). Therefore, our nanocomplex platform, which has the advantage of being inherently scalable to treat large populations and enables glomerular targeting of Angpt1, may have broader therapeutic applications in acquired glomerular diseases and in the wider context of CKD.

RA Angpt1-treated mice had higher gEC coverage and reduced vascular permeability compared with RA vehicle and IV Angpt1 groups. This aligns with the known protective effects of ANGPT1, shown to enhance endothelial cell survival (46), reduce vascular hydraulic conductivity and increase albumin retention (16), as well as improve peri-vascular cell (17) and endothelial glycocalyx (16) coverage, all of which act to prevent vascular leakage (47). In the kidney, Angpt1 overexpression prevents microvascular loss in preclinical models of kidney injury induced by either ureteral obstruction (48), ischemia (49) or toxins (50). In the latter study (50), although systemic Angpt1 adenoviral delivery prevented microvascular loss in a folic acid induced nephropathy model, this was accompanied by a pro-inflammatory response, likely mediated by TIE2 expression on macrophages and neutrophils (51). This observation highlights the importance of localized Angpt1 delivery which may limit potential confounding effects seen following systemic administration.

We also found that RA delivered Angpt1 nanocomplexes increased WT1+ cells per tuft area, more than IV Angpt1 and RA vehicle groups. IV Angpt1 also increased the number of WT1+ cells versus vehicle, suggesting circulating nanocomplexes reached the glomerulus, albeit to a lesser extent. One explanation for this is gEC uptake and secretion of ANGPT1 across the GBM to reach podocytes, also known to express TIE2 (14, 52). Alternatively, smaller Angpt1 nanocomplexes may traverse gEC fenestrae to reach podocytes directly. DLS showed that Angpt1 nanocomplexes were, on average, 73.8 nm smaller than mCherry complexes, likely because Angpt1 mRNA was prepared in water, whereas mCherry used citrate buffer, which can promote salt formation and aggregation (53). TEM confirmed the presence of smaller Angpt1 nanocomplexes ranging from 50 to 100 nm in dry-state. Either way, it is likely that the restoration of either cell type would mediate a positive feedback loop, normalising the cellular crosstalk that is vital for both podocyte and endothelial function (11). Thus, rescued podocyte number could be partially attributed to the improvement in gEC health. In turn, the increased podocyte number may also protect against glomerulosclerosis, as podocytes prevent GBM bulging and subsequent extracellular matrix deposition that leads to scarring (54).

Our therapeutic strategy shows promise for translation into the clinic to treat human WT1 glomerulopathy. Ultrasound-guided injections are part of clinical practice for steroid (55) and anticoagulant (56) delivery. Furthermore, cationic lipid nanoparticles have shown safety worldwide in COVID-19 vaccine recipients (57). Our Angpt1 nanocomplex formulation also remained stable for up to one month at 4°C, an accessible storage condition that supports wide patient access. This stability is consistent with the high ZP (+40.1 ± 0.5 mV) of our nanocomplexes, as values above +30 mV are associated with strong colloidal stability and reduced aggregation (58). Importantly, treatment was initiated after the onset of increased albuminuria, reflecting a clinically relevant interventional design. We speculate that ANGPT1 therapy could complement existing treatment strategies for congenital glomerular disease, including inhibitors of the renin-angiotensin system, which can modulate ANGPT1 expression (59), to provide a synergistic approach for the management of WT1 glomerulopathy and potentially other glomerular diseases.

This study has several limitations. From our longevity analyses, mCherry protein expression dropped at 3 days post injection and despite modifications to the Angpt1 mRNA such as codon optimisation (60) and uridine substitution (61), we still reasoned that three therapeutic doses over a 4-week period were required for our preclinical trial. Further work is required to determine the minimal dosing regimen, as repeated dosing, although clinically feasible, may pose challenges for long-term implementation, introducing risks of procedural burden and patient compliance. Despite this, renal arterial access is routinely performed for diagnostic angiography and is used repeatedly in pediatric patients undergoing angioplasty (62). Furthermore, the transient expression of mRNA enabled treatment discontinuation if required, a limitation of viral approaches which can result in sustained, uncontrollable transgene expression (63). In the present study, ultrasound-guided renal artery injection of Angpt1 nanocomplexes was well tolerated, enabling three administrations over four weeks without procedure-related mortality. A second limitation reflects the use of plasma creatinine and BUN as kidney function markers in mice and the lack of differences observed with Angpt1 treatment. In mice, creatinine undergoes substantial tubular secretion (64), which can mask reductions in glomerular filtration and thereby confound values until global nephron dysfunction occurs. BUN is influenced by tubular urea reabsorption, as well as systemic urea production (65), limiting its sensitivity and specificity as a marker of early glomerular injury. Thus a longer duration of therapeutic intervention may be required for structural recovery to translate into detectable improvements in plasma creatinine and BUN, and future studies should incorporate additional metrics such as real-time monitoring of FITC-sinistrin clearance or plasma Cystatin C to provide a more comprehensive assessment of kidney function. Further work is also required to understand the molecular pathways by which ANGPT1 exerts its therapeutic effect. Finally, toxicology was not assessed in this current study but is important for future work as mRNA therapies can trigger immunogenicity (66).

In conclusion, we have developed an αvβ3-targeted lipid nanocomplex platform for delivering mRNA to glomeruli, achieving effective transfection in vitro and localized administration in vivo using an ultrasound-guided delivery technique. Using this bespoke technology, we delivered Angpt1 and demonstrated therapeutic benefit in Wt1+/R394W mice, reducing albuminuria and glomerulosclerosis severity, as well as rescuing podocyte and gEC loss. Together this work provides a basis for the further clinical development of this therapy, to slow disease progression and to prolong the lives of patients with glomerular disease.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This study was designed to test Angpt1 lipid nanocomplex therapy in a murine model of WT1 glomerulopathy. Lipid nanocomplexes with integrin αvβ3-targeting peptides were tested for uptake in primary murine podocytes and gECs in vitro using luciferase assays (mean of six replicates per group: three independent experiments). In vivo biodistribution and longevity of expression experiments with RWrNM nanocomplexes were carried out with at least two biological replicates. Specific sample numbers for each experiment are detailed in the figure legends. The nanocomplexes used self-assemble on mixing of three components: nucleic acids (mRNA), liposomes and targeting peptides in a 1:3:4 weight ratio.

All animal experiments and procedures were approved by the U.K. Home Office in accordance with the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986. In our preclinical therapeutic trial, animals were administered either via the tail vein or renal artery under ultrasound-guidance with three doses of Angpt1 mRNA (encapsulated in RWrNM nanocomplexes) from 4 to 8 weeks of age. mCherry mRNA was used as a vehicle control. Sample size was determined by power calculations based on previous data (12) of log ACR in Wt1+/R394W mice at 8 weeks of age (n = 18), the mean of which was 3.57 with a standard deviation of 0.69. We sought to achieve a 30% decrease in line with UK Renal Association guidelines, which would reclassify a patient with severely increased ACR (category A3; at 30 mg/mmol) down to moderately increased (A2; at 21 mg/mmol); at 80% power, 0.05 two-tailed significance, this required a minimum n of 7. Animals were assigned randomly to each experimental group, monitored and weighed throughout the experiment. Our primary outcome, urinary ACR, was measured every 10 days from 4 to 8 weeks of age. Blood was collected at 4 weeks and 8 weeks of age to assess BUN and plasma creatinine. Tissues were harvested at 8 weeks and processed for Western blot, histology, and immunofluorescence. Animals were excluded from a specific analysis if there was insufficient urine, plasma, or tissue. Glomerulosclerosis scoring was conducted blinded and all other analyses were not blinded

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM), unless otherwise indicated and analysed using GraphPad Prism v10. Normality was assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test. When comparing 2 groups, data were analysed using an unpaired t-test (normally distributed) or a Mann-Whitney U test (not normally distributed). When comparing 3 or more groups, a one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison (normally distributed) was used. When comparing paired data, i.e., from the same biological entity at two different time points, a paired t-test was carried out for 2 groups and a 2-way matched ANOVA, with Sidak’s multiple comparisons, for 3 or more groups. *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001 was considered significant. All individual level data for n < 20 are presented in data file S1.
