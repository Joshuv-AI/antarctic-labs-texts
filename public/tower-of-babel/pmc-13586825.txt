A rare guest in the hernia sac: Preoperatively diagnosed Amyand’s hernia with gangrenous appendicitis
Authors: Carolina Baz, Kevin Connors, Selma Siddiqui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmyand’s hernia is a rare clinical entity defined by the presence of the vermiform appendix within an inguinal hernia sac, with acute appendicitis occurring in this setting being exceptionally uncommon. We report the case of a 57-year-old male who presented with pain in a known right inguinal hernia. Clinical evaluation demonstrated an incarcerated right inguinal hernia, and contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed an inflamed appendix herniating into the right inguinal canal, consistent with acute appendicitis within an Amyand’s hernia. The patient underwent urgent laparoscopic exploration, which confirmed an acutely inflamed, gangrenous appendix partially contained within the hernia sac. A laparoscopic appendectomy was successfully performed, and the appendix was reduced from the hernia sac. Given the gangrenous nature of the appendix and concerns regarding microperforation and potential infection, inguinal hernia repair was deferred. The patient recovered without immediate complications.This case highlights the diagnostic value of preoperative imaging, the feasibility of a minimally invasive approach, and the importance of individualized surgical decision-making for Amyand’s hernia. In selected patients with acute appendicitis and suspected perforation within the hernia sac, laparoscopic appendectomy without concurrent hernia repair can be considered, minimizing operative risk while allowing staged definitive hernia management.Keywords: Amyand’s hernia, Acute appendicitis, Inguinal hernia, Appendectomy, Hernia repair

Introduction

Amyand’s hernia is defined as the presence of the appendix within an inguinal hernia sac, with or without associated inflammation. It represents less than 1% of all inguinal hernias, while the incidence of acute appendicitis occurring in an Amyand’s hernia is estimated at approximately 0.07–0.13% of all cases of appendicitis [1]. The condition is named after Claudius Amyand, who on December 6th, 1735, performed the first documented appendectomy in an 11-year-old patient with a perforated appendix contained within a right inguinal hernia [2].

Preoperative diagnosis remains difficult due to nonspecific clinical features that often mimic an incarcerated or strangulated inguinal hernia [1,3]. With the increasing use of cross-sectional imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT), preoperative identification has become more frequent, enabling better surgical planning [3]. Management strategies vary depending on the condition of the appendix and the degree of contamination; furthermore, the indication for primary hernia repair remains a subject of ongoing debate [1,4]. We present the case of a 57-year-old male with a preoperatively diagnosed Amyand’s hernia complicated by gangrenous appendicitis, managed laparoscopically with appendectomy and without concomitant hernia repair.

Case report

A 57-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a 4-day history of progressively worsening pain localized to a known right inguinal hernia. The patient reported a long-standing right inguinal hernia that suddenly became painful and irreducible. He denied nausea, vomiting, or changes in bowel habits. His medical history included active smoking, with no prior abdominal surgeries.

The patient was hemodynamically stable and afebrile. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, nondistended abdomen without signs of generalized peritonitis. The right groin demonstrated a tender, irreducible right inguinal hernia with some erythema overlying the scrotal skin. Laboratory values were within normal limits. A CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated the appendix within the right inguinal hernia sac, with a dilated tip measuring up to 1.1 cm, surrounding inflammatory stranding, and fluid within the hernia sac extending into the scrotum. The appendix appeared partially contained within the sac; it showed mural enhancement, with its base in the abdominal cavity. Imaging did not show an appendicolith, and there was no evidence of bowel involvement. Given the absence of free air and the dilated portion of the appendix being contained within the hernia sac, the evidence pointed more toward obstruction rather than ischemia. On imaging, it was clear that the hernia sac contained only the appendix, since bowel was not seen within the sac, was located at a considerable distance from the inguinal area, and the patient was not exhibiting obstructive symptoms. These findings established a preoperative diagnosis of Amyand’s hernia complicated by acute appendicitis (Fig. 1A–B).

Contrast CT scan axial section (A) and sagittal section (B), and coronal section (C) showing a dilated and inflamed appendix (red circles) within the hernia sac (A) and entering the right inguinal area (B–C).

The patient was taken to the operating room. A diagnostic laparoscopy confirmed an inflamed appendix extending into the right inguinal canal. A laparoscopic appendectomy was performed, with the decision to divide the appendiceal base first due to the initial difficulty reducing the appendiceal tip from the inguinal hernia, as well as concerns for avulsion if traction of the appendix continued. The entire appendix was removed. The appendix tip was evidently dilated, exhibiting serosal discoloration and pus on its surface, but there was no clear perforation or evidence of an intra-abdominal abscess. Nevertheless, considering the presence of gangrenous appendicitis and the associated risk of surgical site infection, no attempt was made to repair the inguinal hernia during the same operation (Fig. 2A–D). Pathology reported a 7.3 cm appendix in length, with purulent material in the serosal surface. No stones or perforation identified.

Intraoperative pictures showing the appendix extending into the right inguinal canal (A), and the division of the appendix at its base (B).

The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful. He resumed oral intake on postoperative day one and was discharged home in stable condition with good recovery and no evidence of surgical site infection, fever, or abdominal pain at follow-up, excluding the possibility of an ongoing intraabdominal infection or abscess formation. He underwent elective robotic hernia repair on 3/2026.

Discussion

Amyand’s hernia is an uncommon variant of an inguinal hernia, [1] usually caused by extraluminal obstruction of the appendix due to pressure on the hernia neck rather than intraluminal obstruction [5]. Reports indicate that the condition may affect patients ranging from 3 weeks to 92 years of age, with a threefold higher prevalence in children, attributable to the patency of the processus vaginalis [6]. While the appendix may be normal in most cases, the presence of acute appendicitis is rare, with an incidence of a perforated appendix incarcerated within an inguinal hernia reported at only 0.1% of all cases of appendicitis [1].

Several complications of Amyand’s hernia have been reported in the literature. Lyass et al [7] described an abdominal abscess secondary to a perforated appendix in an inguinal hernia, whereas Serrano et al [8] reported an incarcerated right inguinal hernia containing a perforated appendix, along with an inflamed right testicle and spermatic cord; all of which required removal. Additionally, necrotizing fasciitis of the anterior abdominal wall secondary to bowel perforation has also been documented [9].

The preoperative diagnosis of Amyand’s hernia is challenging and rarely established prior to surgical intervention, as physical examination often cannot identify the hernia sac contents, and the condition is often mistaken for an incarcerated or strangulated inguinal hernia [10]. The low incidence and non-specific presentation frequently lead to delayed or intraoperative diagnosis, contributing to increased morbidity when complications are present [1,3]. This may also account for the mortality rate associated with Amyand’s hernia, which, according to some authors, can range from 14% to 30%, primarily due to peritoneal dissemination of sepsis [11].

CT is the most frequently used imaging modality for assessing the acute abdomen and abdominal hernias. Luchs et al [12] were the first to diagnose Amyand’s hernia on CT, which allows direct visualization of the appendix within the inguinal canal. This imaging modality plays an important role in the preoperative evaluation and diagnosis of Amyand’s hernia, particularly because its clinical presentation can closely mimic an incarcerated or strangulated inguinal hernia, making diagnosis based on physical examination alone challenging. CT can directly visualize the vermiform appendix within the inguinal hernia sac, typically appearing as a blind-ending tubular structure originating from the cecum. It also facilitates the identification of associated findings such as appendiceal wall thickening, periappendiceal fat stranding, ischemia, perforation, or abscess formation. Multiplanar reformatted images, especially sagittal and coronal views, enhance the visualization of the appendix and its relationship to the hernia sac. Recognition of these findings before surgery can facilitate appropriate operative planning, including selecting the surgical approach and anticipating the need for appendectomy and hernia repair based on the inflammatory status of the appendix [4,13]. Although CT scan aid accurate diagnosis, it is generally not included in the standard diagnostic workup when a straightforward inguinal hernia is suspected [1,14].

The Losanoff and Basson classification remains the most widely cited framework for guiding management [15]. According to this system, Amyand’s hernia with acute appendicitis without abdominal sepsis is classified as Type II, whereas appendicitis with local or systemic sepsis is classified as Type III. Our patient’s presentation with gangrenous appendicitis without peritonitis aligns with a Type II. Management is tailored according to the classification. Appendectomy is recommended for Types II–IV, whereas prophylactic appendectomy in Type I remains controversial. Supporters of appendectomy claim that the appendix is prone to reherniate and that in younger patients, it might cause future appendicitis [4].

Moreover, the role of hernia repair in the setting of appendicitis is also debated. While mesh repair is standard for uncomplicated inguinal hernias, its use in contaminated or potentially contaminated fields is associated with increased rates of infection, mesh explanation, and hernia recurrence [1,15]. Consequently, most authors advocate deferring hernia repair in cases of complicated appendicitis, with consideration of delayed elective repair once infection has resolved. This strategy was implemented in our case to prioritize source control and minimize postoperative infectious complications.

The first laparoscopic repair of an Amyand’s hernia without mesh was performed by Vermillion in 1999 [16]. Since then, minimally invasive approaches, including laparoscopic appendectomy and hernia repair, have gained increasing acceptance, particularly when the diagnosis is established preoperatively [11]. Laparoscopy offers several advantages, including excellent visualization of intra-abdominal structures, diagnostic confirmation, reduced postoperative pain, and faster recovery [11,16]. Several case reports and small series have demonstrated favorable outcomes with laparoscopic appendectomy alone in complicated Amyand’s hernia, supporting its use in selected patients [10,11,16].

Despite increasing recognition, there is no universal consensus on optimal management, primarily owing to the infrequency of the condition and the predominance of evidence derived from case reports and small retrospective series. Personalized treatment strategies, based on the severity of appendiceal inflammation, the degree of contamination, and the patient’s comorbidities, remain essential.

This case supports the existing literature advocating a tailored approach based on the appendix’s condition and the degree of contamination. Appendectomy and non-mesh hernia repair, or the decision to forego concomitant hernia repair, are generally recommended in cases of appendicitis. It also highlights the value of laparoscopy in both diagnosis and treatment. Reporting additional cases will contribute to refining diagnostic strategies and optimizing surgical management.
