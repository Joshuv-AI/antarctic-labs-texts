Comparing the digital dynamometer and Martin vigorimeter for handgrip strength: reliability, agreement, and prognostic value
Authors: Shuyue Luo, Xiaoyan Chen, Rongna Lian, Huiyu Tang, Zecong Chen, Wenhua Jiang, Jiaojiao Jiang, Ming Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHandgrip strength (HGS) measurement is vital for clinical assessment in older adults, yet comparative performance of different devices in Asian populations remains understudied. Prior validation focused mainly on healthy adults, but this is the first comprehensive comparison in institutionalized older adults with cognitive impairment and ADL disability. To compare the measurement properties and prognostic value of a digital dynamometer and Martin vigorimeter in Chinese nursing home residents, especially those with ADL disability and cognitive impairment.MethodsA multicenter longitudinal study of 772 residents (502 men, 270 women; median age 71.0 vs. 80.5 years) from 15 nursing homes. HGS was measured with both devices per standardized protocols. Test-retest and inter-rater reliability were evaluated using ICC, CV, SEM, MDC, and Spearman’s ρ. Device agreement was assessed via Bland-Altman, and prognostic value for 30-month mortality was analyzed with Cox regression, adjusting for multiple covariates.ResultsBoth devices showed excellent test-retest reliability (digital ICC: 0.945; Martin ICC: 0.943). The Martin vigorimeter performed better in participants with ADL disability (CV: 10.48% vs. 14.01%; SEM: 8.6% vs. 11.6%; MDC: 23.0% vs. 25.7%). Inter-rater reliability favored the Martin device (ICC: 0.790 vs. 0.664). Strong correlations were observed (R = 0.60–0.62). Both devices had comparable prognostic value for mortality (adjusted HR: digital 4.50; Martin 2.77), despite different measurement principles.ConclusionsBoth devices are reliable mortality predictors in older adults, but the Martin vigorimeter offers superior properties in those with ADL disability. Background

Handgrip strength (HGS) is a well-established biomarker of muscular function and overall health, serving as a reliable predictor of various health outcomes [1, 2]. Low HGS has been consistently associated with increased mortality risk, and various chronic conditions across different healthcare settings [3, 4]. Recently, HGS has been incorporated as a key diagnostic criterion in several clinical conditions, including sarcopenia, sarcopenic obesity, and malnutrition, making accurate measurement crucial for clinical decision-making [5–7].

The implementation of accurate HGS measurement depends on reliable and validated instruments. While the Jamar dynamometer is traditionally considered the gold standard, it faces several limitations, including high cost, lack of digital capabilities, and potential measurement difficulties in certain populations [8, 9]. Digital dynamometers have emerged as promising alternatives, offering advantages in data storage, measurement precision, and cost-effectiveness [10, 11]. However, both Jamar and digital dynamometers may present challenges for older adults, particularly those with hand conditions or reduced HGS [12].

The Martin vigorimeter represents a unique alternative, utilizing a pneumatic-based measurement system with a compressible rubber bulb interface. This design offers several potential advantages for older adults and those with functional limitations, including enhanced comfort, adaptability to different hand sizes, and reduced risk of measurement-related discomfort [13, 14]. Initial studies have suggested promising results for the vigorimeter, particularly in individuals requiring frequent monitoring or those with hand conditions [15]. However, comprehensive validation studies in Asian populations, where hand anthropometry may differ significantly from Western populations, remain limited.

The associations of HGS with cognitive impairment and activities of daily living (ADL) disability have been widely studied[16, 17]. However, measuring HGS in individuals with these conditions, especially among the oldest-old, cognitive impairment or ADL disability, presents unique challenges that warrant special consideration. For these vulnerable populations, concerns about their ability to comprehend and follow measurement instructions, maintain proper positioning, and exert maximal effort consistently are particularly relevant[3, 18]. The reliability of measurements may be compromised by factors such as fluctuating attention, reduced comprehension, and day-to-day variations in cooperation levels. Despite the widespread use of various dynamometers, there is limited evidence regarding their measurement properties, reliability, and clinical utility in these vulnerable groups [3, 16–18]. Previous validation studies have primarily focused on healthy adults or specific patient groups, with limited research on populations with cognitive impairment or ADL disability [19]. Moreover, the prognostic value of HGS measurements using different devices for mortality prediction remains largely unexplored in Asian populations, particularly among those with impairments.

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically compare the measurement properties and prognostic value for mortality prediction of a digital dynamometer and a vigorimeter in Chinese institutionalized older adults. This research is the first of its kind to evaluate both the technical aspects and clinical applications of these devices in an Asian population with varying levels of cognitive and functional capacity.

Methods

Study design and participants

This longitudinal study was conducted and reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement[20]. The baseline investigation was conducted at 15 nursing homes in Zigong City, China, between September 2021 and July 2022[21]. All residents aged 60 years and older at the participating nursing homes were screened for eligibility and consecutively recruited. Participants were excluded if they had any of the following conditions:

Presence of implants (pacemakers, implantable cardioverter defibrillators, or dental implants).

Acute illness (trauma, infection, or fracture), recent surgery (within three months), or hand arthritis.

The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2021 − 965), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

A total of 870 older adults were initially included. After excluding 60 participants due to missing handgrip strength (HGS) measurements, 772 older adults (502 men and 270 women) were included in the baseline analysis. Furthermore, 38 participants (4.4%) were excluded from the survival analysis due to missing death data. Sample size calculation

Sample size calculation was based on two primary objectives. For reliability analyses, following Walter et al.‘s method [22], a minimum of 46 subjects per group was required to achieve 80% power to detect an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) difference of 0.10 (α = 0.05, β = 0.20, ICC0 = 0.80, ICC1 = 0.90). For survival analyses, based on previous studies reporting a mortality rate of approximately 25%, expecting to detect a hazard ratio of 2.5 between handgrip strength quartiles, and considering an anticipated 20% loss to follow-up, a minimum of 680 participants was required to achieve 80% power at α = 0.05.

HGS measurements

A trained nurse measured HGS using two devices: a digital dynamometer (Camry EH101, Zhongshan, China) and a Martin vigorimeter (Martin, Tuttlingen, Germany). Measurements were performed following a standardized protocol: participants were seated on a chair with their shoulders adducted and neutrally rotated, elbow flexed at 90°, and wrist positioned between 0° and 30° of dorsiflexion[23]. The digital dynamometer was held in a neutral position with a comfortable fit and 90° flexion of the index finger[23]. The Martin vigorimeter was held in a neutral position with a comfortable fit[13]. For each device, three consecutive measurements were taken on the dominant hand, which was chosen for its clinical practicality and to minimize participant burden among this vulnerable population. A minimum 60-second rest interval was provided between trials. The maximum value of these three trials was used for all subsequent statistical analysis. Additionally, our methodology aligns with the AWGS 2019 consensus, which recommends using the dominant hand for grip strength measurements[24]. No verbal encouragement was provided during the measurements to ensure standardization. The digital dynamometer measures force in kilograms (kg, digital HGS) while the Martin vigorimeter measures pressure in kilopascals (kPa, Martin HGS). Both devices were calibrated before testing.

Moreover, to assess the inter-rater reliability of digital dynamometer and Martin vigorimeter, we randomly selected 10% of the participants to measure HGS by another trained nurse using both devices after one week from the baseline measurement.

Body composition

Body composition was evaluated using a multi-frequency segmental bioelectrical impedance analysis device (InBody 770, Biospace, Seoul, Korea), which measured key parameters including skeletal muscle mass (SMM), fat mass (FM), fat-free mass (FFM) of left arm, FFM of right arm, phase angle (PA) of left arm, PA of right arm, and PA of whole body (PA-WB), a potential indicator of muscle quality[25]. PA has been shown to be a potential indicator of muscle quality[24]. Addtionally, height was measured using a portable stadiometer (0.5 cm precision), and weight was measured using a digital scale (0.1 kg precision). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²). Skeletal muscle index (SMI) was calculated as SMM divided by height squared (kg/m²)[5].

Other variables

Demographic and clinical data were collected through face-to-face interviews, encompassing age, sex, education level, smoking status, alcohol consumption patterns, and presence of chronic conditions (including hypertension, diabetes, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]). Activities of daily living (ADL) were evaluated using the Katz Index [26], which assesses independence in six fundamental functions: bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, and feeding. Each function is scored dichotomously (independent/dependent), with a total score of ≤ 5 indicating ADL disability. Cognitive function was assessed using the Global Cognitive and Clinical Assessment Scale (GCCAES) [27], where scores below 24 indicated cognitive impairment. Nutritional status was evaluated using the Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF) [28], with scores of 12–14 indicating normal nutritional status, 8–11 indicating risk of malnutrition, and ≤ 7 indicating malnutrition. Additionally, gait speed was measured by timing participants walking at their usual pace over a 4-meter course, using a standing-start protocol and ensuring steady walking throughout the measurement.

Follow-up

The participants were followed up annually through telephone interviews to assess their vital status. For participants who could not be contacted directly, survival status was obtained through their family members or nursing home staff. Vital status was also confirmed by cross-referencing information with official facility records and death certificates. The follow-up period extended from baseline until death or the end of the study in April 2024.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data, and as median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test for normally distributed data, while the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test was used for non-normally distributed data. Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between groups. Scatter plots with linear regression lines were created to examine the correlation between measurements from the two devices. Due to the different units of measurement between devices (kg vs. kPa), measurements were standardized to z-scores before comparing agreement using Bland-Altman plots.

Test-retest reliability of each device was evaluated using ICC (excellent: >0.90, good: 0.75–0.90, moderate: 0.50–0.75, poor: <0.50), coefficient of variation (CV, acceptable: <15%), Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ, strong: >0.70, moderate: 0.40–0.70, weak: <0.40), standard error of measurement (SEM), minimal detectable change (MDC), relative SEM (acceptable: <10%), and relative MDC (acceptable: <30%), all with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subgroup analyses were conducted by age groups (< 80 vs. ≥ 80 years), sex, ADL disability, and cognitive impairment. Additionally, inter-rater reliability was evaluated using these metrics and Bland-Altman plots.

The correlations of muscle strength (digital HGS and Martin HGS), muscle mass (SMM and FFM of dominant arm [FFM-arm]), muscle quality (phase angle of dominant arm [PA-arm] and PA-WB), muscle function (gait speed), and BMI were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Subgroup analyses were conducted by age groups (< 80 vs. ≥ 80 years), sex, ADL disability, and cognitive impairment.

The distribution of HGS measurements was visualized using ridge plots, stratified by age groups, sex, ADL disability, and cognitive impairment status. Correlation analyses between HGS and other clinical variables were performed and visualized using heatmaps, with stratification by sex and ADL disability status.

For survival analysis, we employed a systematic approach beginning with univariate Cox regression for potential risk factors. Variables yielding P < 0.1 in univariate analysis were considered for multivariate modeling. Prior to multivariate analysis, we assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF > 5 indicating collinearity) and verified the proportional hazards assumption through Schoenfeld residuals and log-minus-log plots. The final multivariate Cox model incorporated HGS (categorized by quartiles) with adjustment for significant confounders (age group, sex, current smoker, hypertension, SMI, phase angle of whole body, cognitive impairment, and ADL disability). We did not include gait speed in the model because it was collinear with HGS. Survival patterns across HGS quartiles were visualized using Kaplan-Meier curves. To compare the prognostic performance of both devices, we constructed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and compared their areas under the curve (AUC) using DeLong’s test.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics by sex. Women were significantly older than men (median age 80.5 vs. 71.0 years, P < 0.001). The majority of participants were at risk of malnutrition (men: 67%, women: 65%), with no significant difference in nutritional status between sexes (P = 0.627). In terms of physical and functional status, women demonstrated slower gait speed (0.74 vs. 0.87 m/s, P < 0.001) and higher prevalence of ADL disability (23% vs. 13%, P = 0.002). Cognitive impairment was highly prevalent in both groups but more common in men (85% vs. 76%, P < 0.001). Most participants were right-hand dominant (men: 95%, women: 92%, P = 0.107). For HGS measurements, men demonstrated significantly higher values than women on both devices. The median digital HGS was 32.5 kg (IQR: 28.4–36.8) for men and 20.3 kg (IQR: 17.2–23.5) for women (P < 0.001). Similarly, the median Martin HGS was 98.7 kPa (IQR: 85.3–112.4.3.4) for men and 62.8 kPa (IQR: 53.1–72.6) for women (P < 0.001). Although BMI was similar between groups (men: 23.7 kg/m², women: 23.5 kg/m², P = 0.256), body composition measurements revealed significant sex differences, with men having higher values for FFM of both arms (right arm: 2.1 vs. 1.6 kg; left arm: 2.0 vs. 1.6 kg; both P < 0.001), SMI (6.4 vs. 5.5 kg, P < 0.001), and phase angles of arms and whole body (all P < 0.001).

Subgroup analyses revealed several notable patterns. In terms of functional status, the Martin vigorimeter showed superior performance in participants with ADL disability, demonstrating substantially lower CV (10.48% vs. 14.01%) and better relative SEM (8.6% vs. 11.6%) compared to the digital dynamometer, while both devices maintained excellent ICC values (Martin: 0.943, digital: 0.932). For participants with cognitive impairment, the Martin vigorimeter also demonstrated better measurement properties, with lower CV (10.01% vs. 11%) and relative SEM (8.8% vs. 9.7%) compared to the digital dynamometer, while maintaining slightly lower ICC values (Martin: 0.933, digital: 0.941). Moreover, the Martin vigorimeter demonstrated better precision in older participants (≥ 80 years), with lower CV (10.70% vs. 12.74%) and relative SEM (8.8% vs. 11.0%), although both devices maintained excellent reliability across age groups (all ICC > 0.93).

Inter-rater reliability analysis

We randomly included 72 participants in the inter-rater reliability analysis. As shown in Table S1, the inter-rater reliability analysis revealed superior performance of the Martin vigorimeter compared to the digital dynamometer across multiple metrics. The Martin vigorimeter demonstrated higher ICC (0.790, 95% CI: 0.620–0.889 vs. 0.664, 95% CI: 0.490–0.792) and Spearman’s correlation coefficient (0.784, 95% CI: 0.622–0.882 vs. 0.650, 95% CI: 0.483–0.779). Notably, the Martin vigorimeter showed substantially better precision with a lower CV (16.02%, 95% CI: 10.49–22.67) compared to the digital dynamometer (25.45%, 95% CI: 18.61–30.73). The measurement error indicators also favored the Martin vigorimeter, with lower relative SEM (16.2% vs. 23.6%) and relative MDC (44.8% vs. 65.5%).

As shown in Figure S1, Bland-Altman analysis showed a mean percentage difference between repeat and baseline measurements of 1.2% for the digital dynamometer (95% LoA: −26.8% to 29.2%) and − 7.2% for the Martin vigorimeter (95% LoA: −32.1% to 17.7%). The scatter of points around the mean difference line appeared relatively uniform across the range of measurements for both devices, with the Martin vigorimeter showing slightly narrower limits of agreement, particularly in the higher strength ranges.

Correlation and agreement between devices

The correlation between digital dynamometer and Martin vigorimeter measurements was examined through scatter plots and standardized difference analysis (Fig. 3). Strong positive correlations were observed for both men (R = 0.62, P < 0.001) and women (R = 0.60, P < 0.001). The scatter plot revealed a linear relationship between the two devices across the entire range of HGS values, with similar correlation patterns for both sexes (Fig. 3A).

When examining the agreement between devices using z-scores (Fig. 3B), the measurements showed good agreement across the strength spectrum, with most differences falling within ± 1.96 standard deviations (indicated by dashed lines) of the mean difference. The distribution of differences was symmetric around zero for both men and women, suggesting no systematic bias between the devices regardless of HGS level. The spread of differences remained relatively constant across the range of standardized mean values, indicating consistent agreement between devices across different strength levels.

Distribution of HGS measurements by subgroups

The distribution of HGS measurements across different subgroups revealed distinct patterns for both devices (Fig. 4). Men had higher HGS values than women for both devices. Older participants (≥ 80 years) showed lower HGS compared to younger ones (< 80 years). Participants with ADL disability had lower HGS than those without disability. Those with cognitive impairment showed slightly lower HGS values compared to those without impairment.

Interestingly, the shape of the distributions varied between devices. The digital dynamometer showed more right-skewed distributions, particularly in the higher-functioning groups (younger age, without ADL disability, without cognitive impairment), while the Martin vigorimeter demonstrated more symmetrical distributions across all subgroups, suggesting potentially better measurement properties across the functional spectrum.

Correlations between HGS and muscle-related parameters

Correlation analysis revealed consistent patterns across different subgroups (Fig. 5). Both HGS measurements showed moderate to strong correlations with muscle-related parameters. The Martin vigorimeter and digital dynamometer demonstrated strong correlations with each other across all subgroups (r ranging from 0.57 to 0.72, all P < 0.001), indicating good concurrent validity.

Correlation coefficients are displayed in each cell, with blank cells indicating non-significant correlations (P > 0.05)

In the sex-stratified analysis, both devices showed stronger correlations with muscle mass indicators (FFM-arm and SMI) in men (r = 0.39–0.46) compared to women (r = 0.36–0.39). Phase angle measurements (PA-arm and PA-WB) were moderately correlated with HGS in both sexes (r = 0.31–0.41). Gait speed showed consistent correlations with both devices across sex groups (r = 0.34–0.38).

Age-stratified analysis revealed stronger correlations in the younger group (< 80 years) compared to the older group (≥ 80 years), particularly for muscle mass parameters. Similarly, participants without ADL disability showed stronger correlations between HGS and other parameters compared to those with ADL disability. Cognitive status had minimal impact on the correlation patterns, with similar correlation coefficients observed in both cognitive groups.

Notably, BMI showed weak or non-significant correlations with HGS across all subgroups, suggesting that body mass alone may not be a good indicator of muscle strength in older adults.

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significant associations between HGS and 30-month mortality for both devices (Fig. 6). The survival curves showed clear stratification across HGS quartiles (log-rank test, P < 0.0001 for both devices). Participants in the lowest HGS quartile (Q1) demonstrated markedly lower survival probability compared to those in higher quartiles, with this pattern being consistent for both the digital dynamometer and Martin vigorimeter measurements.

The adjusted HR for the lowest quartile was 4.50 (95% CI: 1.68–12.1, P = 0.002) for digital HGS and 2.77 (95% CI: 1.25–6.15, P = 0.030) for Martin HGS.

ROC curve analysis demonstrated comparable discriminative ability for mortality prediction between the two devices (Figure S2). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.671 for the digital dynamometer and 0.667 for the Martin vigorimeter, with no significant difference between the two devices (DeLong’s test, P = 0.839). These findings support the equivalent prognostic utility of both devices in predicting mortality risk among older adults.

Discussion

This multicenter longitudinal study provides the first systematic comparison of measurement properties between a digital dynamometer and Martin vigorimeter in Chinese nursing home residents. Our findings align with recent research by Gränicher et al. [29], who demonstrated excellent intrasubject reliability (ICC: 0.91–0.97) across multiple grip strength measurement devices. Our study extends these findings to a more vulnerable population of nursing home residents.

The superior performance of the Martin vigorimeter in participants with functional limitations may be attributed to its pneumatic measurement system and ergonomic design, which allows for more comfortable grip positioning. This advantage is particularly relevant for older adults with reduced hand strength or joint problems, as recently highlighted in Gränicher’s study [29], which noted the challenges that traditional hydraulic dynamometers (e.g., Jamar dynamometer) present for individuals with painful finger joints or low grip strength.

Our findings regarding test-retest reliability align with previous validation studies. The excellent ICC values (> 0.90) for both devices in our study are comparable to those reported by Sipers et al. [13], who found high reliability (ICC = 0.93) for the Martin vigorimeter in geriatric patients. For the Camry digital dynamometer, our results (ICC = 0.945) are consistent with recent validation studies by Sánchez-Aranda et al.[10], who reported excellent reliability (ICC = 0.98) in adults, though their study was conducted in a younger population with better functional status.

The slightly lower ICC in our study might reflect the challenges of measuring HGS in older adults with varying functional capabilities, particularly those with cognitive impairment. Recent studies have demonstrated a significant association between grip strength and cognitive function [16], with Chai et al. [17] showing that functional limitations mediate this relationship. Alencar et al. [18] found that while grip strength measurements remained reliable in those with mild to moderate cognitive impairment, the reliability decreased significantly in those with severe impairment. This is further supported by Bai et al. [30], who demonstrated that incorporating HGS assessment into cognitive risk evaluation significantly improved the predictive discrimination for both incident dementia and all-cause mortality. Our findings of maintained measurement reliability in participants with cognitive impairment suggest that both devices can provide valid assessments in this population, though the Martin vigorimeter’s simpler grip action may offer advantages for cognitively impaired individuals.

Notably, our inter-rater reliability analysis revealed superior performance of the Martin vigorimeter compared to the digital dynamometer (ICC: 0.790 vs. 0.664). This finding aligns with previous research showing good inter-rater reliability of pneumatic devices in older adults. The better inter-rater reliability of the Martin vigorimeter might be attributed to its pneumatic measurement system and ergonomic design, which allows for more comfortable grip positioning and reduces measurement variability across different assessors. However, it should be noted that the one-week interval between measurements may have introduced variability due to potential changes in participants’ physical condition and grip strength. The advantages of the Martin vigorimeter’s pneumatic system are particularly relevant for Asian populations, who typically have smaller hand sizes compared to Western populations, as demonstrated by recent validation studies in Korean older adults[30].

The moderate correlation between devices (R = 0.60–0.62) observed in our study is similar to that reported by Neumann et al. (R = 0.67) in their comparison between the Martin vigorimeter and Jamar dynamometer14. Interestingly, Sánchez-Aranda et al. [10] reported higher correlations (R = 0.96) between the Camry and Jamar dynamometers, suggesting that the lower correlation in our study might be specifically related to the different measurement principles between pneumatic and strain gauge systems. This difference might be particularly pronounced in Asian populations, where Desrosiers et al. have shown that grip strength measured by the Jamar dynamometer is even more dependent on hand anthropometry than measurements with the Martin vigorimeter. [15]

Our analysis of the relationships between HGS and muscle-related parameters revealed that these correlations were generally stronger in men and participants without functional limitations, consistent with previous findings by Akamatsu et al. [23] The observed sex differences in these correlations align with earlier studies suggesting that the relationship between muscle mass and strength may be influenced by sex-specific factors[5].

Regarding mortality prediction, our results extend previous findings by demonstrating the prognostic value of both devices in an Asian institutional population. While earlier studies have established the predictive value of HGS for mortality using traditional dynamometers, such as Jamar dynamometer and Camry digital dynamometer [2, 3, 31], our study provides novel evidence that both digital dynamometers and the Martin vigorimeter offer similar prognostic utility in institutional settings. This finding supports the use of Martin vigorimeter, as an alternative to traditional dynamometers, in institutional settings.

Our findings have several important clinical implications for the measurement of HGS in institutional settings, particularly for older adults with functional limitations. First, while both devices demonstrated excellent test-retest reliability, the Martin vigorimeter showed superior measurement properties in participants with ADL disability and the oldest-old, suggesting it might be the preferred choice for these vulnerable populations. Second, the better inter-rater reliability of the Martin vigorimeter has practical implications for clinical settings where multiple healthcare providers may be involved in patient assessment. The pneumatic measurement system’s reduced sensitivity to positioning variations makes it more suitable for routine clinical use, especially in settings with high staff turnover or where standardization of measurement technique across multiple raters is challenging[15]. Third, the comparable prognostic value of both devices for mortality prediction supports their interchangeable use in risk assessment. This is particularly important for institutional settings where cost considerations may influence device selection. The digital dynamometer, being more affordable, might be preferred for large-scale screening programs, while the Martin vigorimeter might be more suitable for detailed clinical assessment of vulnerable older adults[10, 13]. Finally, our results support the need for population-specific reference values and measurement protocols[32, 33]. The differences in measurement properties across functional status groups and the potential influence of hand anthropometry suggest that a one-size-fits-all approach may not be appropriate. Healthcare providers should consider their patient population’s characteristics when selecting measurement devices and interpreting results.

Our study has several limitations. A key limitation is the potential for selection bias, as a comparison of baseline characteristics revealed significant differences between included and excluded participants (see Table S1). While we adjusted for these variables in our multivariable models, this potential bias should be considered. Second, while we demonstrated good reliability and validity of both devices, although we randomly included participants in the inter-rater reliability analysis, the cross-sectional nature of the reliability analyses limits our understanding of their long-term stability, particularly in participants with progressive conditions. Third, although we included participants with varying levels of cognitive function, those who could not collaborate with the measurement were excluded, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to this population. Fourth, while we found comparable prognostic value between devices for mortality prediction, the follow-up period was relatively short (30 months). Longer follow-up periods might reveal differences in predictive ability between devices. Additionally, our study was conducted in institutional settings, and the results may not be fully generalizable to community-dwelling older adults. Our study’s primary limitation is that handgrip strength was only measured on the dominant hand, precluding the assessment of potential bilateral strength asymmetries. Finally, we did not assess the impact of different hand sizes or grip patterns on measurement accuracy, which might be particularly relevant given the anthropometric differences between Asian and Western populations[30, 34].

Conclusions

This comprehensive longitudinal study provides novel evidence regarding the measurement properties and prognostic value of digital dynamometer and Martin vigorimeter in Chinese nursing home residents. Both devices demonstrated excellent test-retest reliability, with the Martin vigorimeter showing superior performance in vulnerable populations, particularly those with ADL disability and cognitive impairment. The comparable prognostic value of both devices for mortality prediction supports their interchangeable use in risk assessment, while their distinct advantages suggest complementary roles in different clinical scenarios.

Our findings have important implications for future research and clinical practice. First, the development of device-specific, population-based reference values is essential, particularly for Asian populations where hand anthropometry may differ significantly from Western populations. Second, longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to evaluate the devices’ responsiveness to functional changes and their long-term prognostic value. Third, investigation of measurement protocols optimized for specific populations (e.g., those with severe cognitive impairment or hand conditions) could enhance the clinical utility of HGS assessment. Finally, the integration of HGS measurement with digital health technologies presents opportunities for improved clinical monitoring and research applications.
