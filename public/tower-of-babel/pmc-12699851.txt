Associations of life course social determinants of health, social mobility with cognitive function and roles of social participation and lifestyle: a multicohort study
Authors: Xiang Li, Zhongxuan Wang, Qi Wang, Chunying Fu, Bo Xie, Luyi Zhang, Xiaoyu Zhang, Dongshan Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial determinants of health (SDOH) throughout life are key factors affecting cognitive aging. The long-term effects of life course SDOH and social mobility on cognitive function are not well understood. We aimed to examine how stage-specific and cumulative SDOH and social mobility related to cognitive function and decline, considering the roles of lifestyle and social participation.MethodsWe pooled individual-level data (2010–2021) from three nationally representative longitudinal cohorts in the USA, China, and England. Ten SDOH indicators spanning four dimensions (education, financial circumstances, healthcare access, social context) across childhood, early adulthood, and middle-late adulthood were used to evaluate stage-specific and cumulative SDOH levels (high, medium, low). Twenty-seven social mobility patterns were identified based on life course SDOH transitions. Global and domain-specific (memory, executive function, orientation, verbal fluency) cognitive function were evaluated using Z-scores. Linear mixed-effects models were applied to investigate the associations of life course SDOH and social mobility with cognitive function and rate of decline. Mediation analyses evaluated the contributions of lifestyle and social participation to the link between SDOH levels and cognition. Combined effects of SDOH with lifestyle or social participation were also examined.ResultsAmong 23,750 participants (mean age 63.5 years), medium (β − 0.319, 95% CI − 0.344 to − 0.293) and low (− 0.705, − 0.735 to − 0.676) cumulative SDOH were linked to poorer global and domain-specific cognitive function than high SDOH. Low cumulative SDOH predicted faster decline in global cognition (− 0.013 SD/year, − 0.019 to − 0.007), executive function (− 0.007 SD/year, − 0.012 to − 0.002), and orientation (− 0.019 SD/year, − 0.027 to − 0.011). Participants with persistently low SDOH exhibited the greatest decrease in global cognitive function (− 1.520, − 1.601 to − 1.439), while downward mobility accelerated cognitive decline (− 0.027 SD/year, − 0.040 to − 0.013). Lifestyles and social participation mediated 12.1% to 28.3% of the SDOH-cognition associations. At equivalent SDOH levels, unhealthy lifestyles and inactive social participation independently predicted poorer cognitive function.ConclusionsLifelong social disadvantage and downward mobility were associated with lower cognitive function and faster decline. While promoting healthy lifestyles and social engagement may partly mitigate theses effects, comprehensive interventions addressing adverse SDOH across the lifespan are still important for reducing cognitive aging inequities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04510-3.Keywords: Life course, Social determinants of health, Social mobility, Cognitive function, Cognitive decline

Background

Social determinants of health (SDOH) are non-medical factors influencing health, including the conditions of birth, growth, work, living, aging, and broader societal forces [1]. Emerging evidence indicates that SDOH served as fundamental drivers of health disparities, exerting a more profound influence than downstream moderators, such as health behaviors and health conditions [2, 3]. Low socioeconomic status (SES) has been linked to an increased risk of late-life cognitive impairment [4], dementia [5], Alzheimer’s disease [6], and dementia-related mortality [7]. Nevertheless, current evidence regarding the associations between SDOH levels and cognitive function, including specific cognitive domains, remains limited.

Previous research has identified SDOH as key predictors of cognitive aging [8]. From a life course perspective, unfavorable SDOH at different stages independently and synergistically impair cognitive function, as multiple exposures—like low wealth, poor education, and adverse neighborhoods—interact to worsen cognitive function [9, 10]. Moreover, individuals underwent dynamic changes in SDOH levels over time [11], and these mobility patterns may also relate to cognitive performance and neurodegenerative progression [12, 13]. However, research exploring the effects of SDOH across the life course and the impact of social mobility across different life stages on cognitive function is sparse and has yielded inconsistent findings. Although some studies have shown that SDOH in childhood and adulthood were associated with poorer cognitive function or faster cognitive decline in later life [9, 14, 15], others have found no significant links between SDOH at certain periods (e.g., childhood) or social mobility and the rate of cognitive decline [16, 17].

Moreover, elucidating mediating pathways and moderating factors is crucial for understanding how SDOH are associated with cognitive function. The link between SDOH levels and cognition might partly be explained and moderated by modifiable factors, such as lifestyles and social participation [5, 18]. Identifying and quantifying the contributions of these actionable mechanisms are crucial given the lack of effective treatments to alter the progression of cognitive decline in aging populations.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study aimed to investigate the associations of stage-specific and cumulative levels of SDOH across the life course, alongside social mobility from childhood through early adulthood to late adulthood, with cognitive function and the rate of cognitive decline. Furthermore, we examined the mediating roles of lifestyles and social participation in the relationship between SDOH levels and cognitive function, as well as the combined effects of SDOH with lifestyles and social participation on cognitive function.

Methods

Study design and participants

This pooled study utilized individual-level data from three nationally representative longitudinal cohorts within the Program on Global Aging, Health, and Policy [19]: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). These cohorts shared a similar study design and survey protocols. Detailed descriptions of each cohort were available elsewhere [20–22]. All studies adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent were obtained from all participants. Data from waves 10–15 of HRS (2010–2021), waves 1–5 of CHARLS (2011–2020), and waves 7–9 of ELSA (2014–2019) were utilized for analyses to ensure comparability across cohorts. Surveys were conducted at 2-year, with occasional 3-year intervals. The exclusion criteria for study population were as follows: (1) aged less than 45 years at baseline; (2) with missing data on any SDOH indicator at baseline; (3) missing complete cognitive scores at baseline; (4) having a diagnosis of dementia at baseline; (5) without any repeated cognitive assessments during follow-up. This study was reported in compliance with the STROBE guidelines (Additional file 1: Table S1).

SDOH levels across life course and social mobility

This study systematically assessed social determinants of health (SDOH) across three critical life-course stages: childhood, early adulthood, and middle to late adulthood. A total of ten SDOH indicators were selected, informed by prior research [10, 23, 24] and the availability of harmonized data across cohorts. These SDOH indicators act as proxies for SDOH levels at different life stages. Childhood SDOH were represented by parental educational attainment, categorized into three levels: no formal education, primary or lower secondary education, and upper secondary education or higher. This measure served as a proxy for early-life socioeconomic conditions and intergenerational support, with higher parental education reflecting more favorable early-life environments [25, 26]. In early adulthood, SDOH were characterized by participants’ educational attainment and the occupation of their primary caregiver. Educational attainment was standardized into three levels based on the 2011 International Standard Classification of Education: less than lower secondary education, upper secondary education or vocational training, and tertiary education [27]. The primary caregiver’s occupation was classified as blue-collar or non-blue-collar, capturing disparities in access to health and social resources during this stage [12]. In middle to late adulthood, six SDOH indicators were evaluated across three domains: financial circumstances (total non-housing wealth and employment status), healthcare access (health insurance), and social context (marital status, loneliness, and psychological disorders) [23]. Total non-housing wealth, encompassing financial assets such as vehicles, investments, and savings, was stratified into quartiles. Employment status was dichotomized as unemployed or currently working/retired. Health insurance coverage was dichotomized into insured (including governmental, public, private, or life insurance) and uninsured. Marital status was categorized as either single or married/partnered. Loneliness and psychological disorders were assessed as binary variables (yes/no) based on participants’ self-reports. To minimize the missingness of childhood and early adulthood SDOH at baseline, we performed imputation based on participants’ responses to relevant SDOH indicators collected either before the baseline survey or during follow-up. Detailed definitions and harmonization strategies for these SDOH indicators are provided in Additional file 1: Table S2.

Each SDOH indicator was assigned a score corresponding to its level, with higher values representing more advantaged social conditions. A one-point increment was applied between levels to calculate the score for each indicator. For instance, maternal educational attainment was scored as follows: no formal education = 0, primary or lower secondary education = 1, and upper secondary education or higher = 2. The total SDOH score for each life-course stage was derived by summing the scores of all indicators within that stage. Specifically, the SDOH scores ranged from 0 to 4 in childhood, 0 to 3 in early adulthood, and 0 to 8 in middle to late adulthood, resulting in a cumulative score spanning 0 to 15 across the entire lifespan (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Participants were stratified into low, medium, and high SDOH groups within each cohort based on cumulative SDOH scores using tertile thresholds. Individuals with identical scores were assigned to the same group, allowing for minor variations in group sizes. The distribution of participants across strata demonstrated consistent proportions across the three cohorts and in the pooled population, with approximately 25% classified into the low SDOH level group and one-third categorized into the high SDOH level group (Additional file 1: Table S4).

Social mobility was assessed based on SDOH levels at three life stages and their transitions over time. Childhood SDOH were categorized as low (no formal education for both parents), medium (primary education or higher for one parent), and high (primary education or higher for both parents) levels. In early adulthood, SDOH were also classified into low (0 points), medium (1 point), and high (2–3 points). For, middle-late adulthood, SDOH levels were defined as low (0–4 points), medium (5–6 points), and high (7–8 points) levels based on the score distribution (Additional file 1: Table S5). Each participant’s SDOH level (low, medium, high) was determined at childhood, early adulthood, and middle-late adulthood. By combining these levels across the three stages (3 stages × 3 levels per stage = 27 patterns), we constructed 27 distinct social mobility patterns. For instance, the “High-High-High” pattern indicates consistently high SDOH from childhood to late adulthood.

Cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed for all study participants at baseline and at least one follow-up visit. Cognitive function was evaluated across four domains: memory, executive function, orientation, and verbal fluency. Memory was measured using immediate and delayed recall of ten random words, with one point awarded for each correct response. The sum of correct responses represented the memory performance (0–20 points). Executive function was evaluated using the Serial Sevens test, in which participants sequentially subtracted 7 from 100 up to five times, receiving one point for each correct answer (0–5 points). Orientation was measured through four questions regarding the year, month, date, and day of the week (0–4 points). Verbal fluency, assessed specifically in HRS and ELSA, was measured by asking participants to list as many animal names as possible within 1 min, with scores ranging from 0 to 100 points. These cognitive assessments have been widely used in multicohort studies and demonstrated strong reliability and validity [4, 28].

To compare the three studies with different cognitive assessments and scoring systems, we standardized domain-specific scores using Z-score transformation by subtracting the baseline mean and dividing by the standard deviation. This puts all scores on a common scale for direct comparison and pooled analyses. The global cognitive function Z-score was then derived by averaging the scores of memory, executive function, and orientation, followed by restandardization to the baseline [29, 30].

Covariates, lifestyles, and social participation

Covariates included age (continuous variable), sex (male or female), birth cohort (born in 1928 or earlier, 1929–1938, 1939–1945, 1946 or later), cohort (HRS, CHARLS, ELSA), body mass index (BMI) category (normal or abnormal), and the number of comorbidities (0–8). The abnormal BMI category comprised participants with a BMI of less than 18.5 or exceeding 25.0 kg/m2. Comorbidities were self-reported, physician-diagnosed conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, heart diseases, stroke, arthritis, lung diseases, cancer, and depressive symptoms assessed by the CESD scale [31, 32].

Drawing on existing literature [5, 18], lifestyle factors and social participation were examined as potential mediators and moderators in the association between SDOH levels and cognitive function. Lifestyle factors, including alcohol consumption, smoking status, and physical activity, were analyzed as binary variables. Alcohol consumption was defined as consuming alcohol at least once per week, while smoking status was categorized into current smokers and non-smokers. Physical activity was classified into active and inactive groups, with physical inactivity characterized by rarely or never engaging in moderate or vigorous physical activities. We defined unhealthy lifestyles as non-moderate alcohol consumption, current smoking, or physical inactivity. Social participation referred to engagement in community activities and interactions, while its absence defined social disengagement. Detailed descriptions of covariates, lifestyles, and social participation across cohorts are provided in Additional file 1: Table S6.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants in the pooled sample and each cohort were summarized. Continuous variables were presented as means with SD and categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages.

Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were employed to examine the relationships between SDOH levels at distinct life stages—childhood, early adulthood, and middle-late adulthood—and cumulative SDOH levels across the lifespan, as well as social mobility, with cognitive function scores and the rate of cognitive decline. Coefficients (β) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. The models incorporated random intercepts with an unstructured covariance matrix, facilitating the full utilization of repeated measures both within and between individuals, while also accommodating unbalanced data. Covariates were fully adjusted in the analyses for the associations between SDOH levels, social mobility, and cognitive function.

To assess the rate of cognitive decline, the model incorporated baseline age, SDOH levels or social mobility, time (years since baseline), an interaction term (SDOH levels × time or social mobility × time), and additional covariates. The coefficients of the interaction term represented the rate of cognitive decline (SD per year) over the follow-up period.

The difference method was used to estimate the mediation proportion attributed to lifestyle factors and social participation in the relationship between low cumulative SDOH levels across the life course and cognitive function. Total, indirect, and direct effects of SDOH on cognitive function were calculated by comparing estimates derived from models with and without mediators [24, 33]. To examine the combined effects of SDOH, lifestyle factors, and social participation on cognitive function, participants were categorized into six groups based on the combination of stage-specific and cumulative SDOH levels (high, medium, and low) and individual lifestyle factor (healthy and unhealthy) or social participation status (active and inactive). Cognitive function was compared across groups by estimating coefficients and 95% CIs, with those having high SDOH and healthy lifestyles or active social participation serving as the reference group.

Sensitivity analysis

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of findings. First, cohort-specific estimates for global cognitive function and its decline rate were calculated to test the robustness of our main findings. Second, participants aged 50 years or younger at baseline were excluded to ensure age-related relevance. Third, individuals reporting dementia during follow-up were removed from analyses. Fourth, to further substantiate the relationship between SDOH levels and neurodegeneration, Cox regression models with time-varying covariates were used to examine associations of cumulative SDOH levels and social mobility with incident dementia, estimating hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. Participants were censored at dementia diagnosis, death, or loss to follow-up.

A cutoff value of p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 17.0 and R version 4.4.2.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 23,750 individuals (HRS 9702, CHARLS 8017, ELSA 6031) with complete baseline SDOH and cognitive assessments, plus at least one follow-up cognitive assessment, were included in the final analyses (Fig. 1). The pooled sample had a mean (SD) baseline age of 63.5 (10.4) years, with 12,533 (52.8%) females. Among the participants, 41.8% had a primary level of education, one-fifth were classified as having low household wealth, over a quarter were single, 60% had abnormal BMI levels, and approximately a quarter self-rated their health status as poor (Table 1). Both global cognitive scores and specific cognitive domain scores at baseline declined as SDOH levels decreased, a trend consistent across cumulative SDOH levels and SDOH at different stages of life course (p < 0.001) (Additional file 1: Table S7). Compared to the retained population, participants excluded from the main analyses were more likely to be born in 1928 or earlier, experience greater social deprivation throughout life, and report more comorbidities and poorer health conditions (Additional file 1: Table S8).

Social mobility and cognitive function and rate of cognitive decline

A total of 27 social mobility patterns were constructed. Participants with sustained low SDOH levels across life stages exhibited the greatest decrease in global cognitive function score (− 1.520, − 1.601 to − 1.439) compared to those with persistently high SDOH. In contrast, individuals who had high SDOH levels during early and middle-late adulthood demonstrated less cognitive dysfunction, with coefficients of − 0.278 (− 0.511, − 0.004) for the “Low–High-High” pattern (Fig. 2). These trends remained largely consistent across cognitive domains (Additional file 1: Figs. S1–S4).

Association between 27 social mobility patterns and global cognitive function. All β estimates were based on linear mixed-effects models adjusting for age, sex, birth cohort, cohort, BMI category, and the number of comorbidities

Regarding the rate of cognitive decline, compared to the stable high level group, individuals whose SDOH levels declined from high in childhood to low in early adulthood exhibited a faster rate of global cognitive decline. The “High-Low-Low” and “High-Low-Medium” patterns showed decline rates of − 0.027 SD/year (− 0.040, − 0.013) and − 0.024 SD/year (− 0.036, − 0.012), respectively (Fig. 3). Similar patterns were observed in cognitive domains of memory, executive function, and orientation (Additional file 1: Figs. S5–S7), but not in verbal fluency (Additional file 1: Fig. S8).

Mediating and combined effects of SDOH, lifestyle, and social participation on cognition

Alcohol consumption explained 3.8% (95% CI: 3.4%, 4.2%) of the association between low cumulative SDOH level and global cognitive function, whereas social participation mediated 12.7% (11.9%, 13.5%). The mediating effect was most pronounced for orientation, with lifestyle factors (alcohol consumption, smoking, and physical inactivity) and social participation collectively accounting for 28.3% (24.2%, 32.3%) of the association (Table 4).

Sensitivity analyses

Cohort-specific estimates for global cognitive function and its decline rate were consistent with the main results (Additional file 1: Table S9). After excluding participants aged 50 or younger at baseline and those reporting dementia during follow-up, results were consistent with the main analyses (Additional file 1: Tables S10–S17). In analyses of SDOH and dementia risk, 1247 dementia cases occurred over a mean follow-up of 7.8 years. Compared to the high SDOH level group, medium (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.04 − 1.49) and low (1.71, 1.41–2.07) SDOH groups had higher dementia risk (Additional file 1: Table S18). Furthermore, those transitioning to low SDOH level in early and middle-late adulthood showed increased dementia risk compared to consistently high SDOH individuals (Additional file 1: Table S19).

Discussion

Summary of findings

In this pooled analysis of three nationally representative cohorts, we found that lower cumulative SDOH levels across the lifespan were associated with poorer cognitive function and a more rapid rate of cognitive decline. Compared to individuals who maintained high SDOH levels throughout life, those with persistently low SDOH levels exhibited the greatest cognitive dysfunction. Notably, a downward transition from high SDOH levels in childhood to low levels in later life was associated with an accelerated rate of cognitive decline. Lifestyle factors and social participation collectively mediated 12.1% to 28.3% of the association between low SDOH levels and cognitive function. Social participation mediated 9.1% to 19.7% of this association, while individual lifestyle factors accounted for 1% to 9.1%. At the same SDOH level, individuals with physical inactivity, current smoking, non-regular moderate alcohol consumption, or inactive social participation demonstrated poorer cognitive function.

Life course SDOH levels and cognitive function

Previous research indicates that SDOH levels at different life stages show varying associations with cognitive function in later life. Among individuals aged 65 years and older, Krueger et al. reported that higher lifetime SDOH (i.e., cumulative SDOH) and higher adulthood SDOH levels were associated with better baseline global cognitive function, whereas childhood SDOH level showed no association with cognitive function [34]. In individuals with a mean baseline age of 80.4 years, Krasnova et al. found that after a 6-year follow-up, high SDOH levels in early and middle-late adulthood were associated with better global and domain-specific cognitive function. However, no such association was observed with high SDOH in childhood [9]. In contrast to previous studies that found no link between childhood SDOH and cognitive function, Cermakova et al., in their study involving European participants with a median baseline age of 71 years, observed that low childhood SDOH level was associated with lower baseline cognitive function [16]. Utilizing categorized levels of SDOH, our study found that among individuals with a mean baseline age of 63.5 years, lower SDOH levels at each life stage were all associated with poorer global and domain-specific cognitive function.

Life course SDOH levels and rate of cognitive decline

Previous research on the association between SDOH level and rate of cognitive decline has yielded inconsistent results, potentially due to variations in the life stages at which SDOH indicators were selected, such as childhood or adulthood or lifetime, differences in cognitive assessment methodologies, and population heterogeneity [9, 34–37]. Krueger et al. reported that higher lifetime SDOH (per SD increase) was not associated with rate of cognitive decline over time (estimate 0.003, 95% CI − 0.001 to 0.006, p = 0.10) [34]. Krasnova et al. observed that none of the childhood, young-adulthood, and middle-late adulthood SES related to rate of global or domain-specific decline, however, this study had a follow-up period of only 6 years, with participants having an average age of 80.4 years at baseline [9]. Wang et al. found that individuals with low SDOH level during late adulthood and unfavorable lifestyles experienced a faster rate of global cognitive decline compared to those with high SDOH and favorable lifestyles [5]. Aartsen et al. found that advantaged socioeconomic conditions in childhood were associated with higher cognitive functioning but accelerated cognitive decline (1.6 times faster in the most advantaged group vs. the most disadvantaged group) in older age [35]. Also, one study found that childhood SES was not associated with cognitive decline in late life [36]. Using categorized cumulative SDOH level across the life course, we found that low SDOH levels were associated with a faster rate of global cognitive decline, particularly in executive function and orientation. Consistent with Aartsen et al., low SDOH levels in childhood were linked to a slower rate of global cognitive decline [35]. One explanation is cognitive resilience. Adverse childhood SDOH may foster compensatory strategies and stress resilience, helping preserve later-life cognitive function. Early challenges might also drive neurobiological adaptations, protecting against cognitive decline. Additionally, our study adds to existing evidence by showing that low SDOH in early adulthood is linked to accelerated declines in overall cognition, memory, executive function, and orientation.

Social mobility and cognitive outcomes

Social mobility refers to changes in an individual’s SES throughout the life course, often resulting in improved or diminished health outcomes [38]. Previous studies had found that compared with consistently low SES status, consistently high status was linked to the best cognitive function and a slower rate of cognitive decline [9, 10]. However, previous studies have not adequately explored the relationships between all patterns of social mobility and cognitive function and their declines. In our study, 27 mobility patterns were identified. Consistent with previous studies, we observed lifelong maintenance of low SDOH levels was associated with the worst cognitive function [9, 10, 12]. Cognitive function progressively improved with increasing SDOH levels from childhood to adulthood, suggesting that enhancements in adulthood SDOH may mitigate the negative impacts of early-life SDOH disadvantage.

Regarding the rate of cognitive decline, we observed that a downward transition from high childhood SDOH levels to lower adulthood SDOH levels was associated with accelerated deterioration in both global and domain-specific cognition. This finding suggests that the health advantage gained from strong intergenerational support during childhood may be undermined by insufficient educational attainment in early adulthood and subsequent psychosocial disadvantage. Downward social mobility or loss of social status can lead to chronic stress, which activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and may result in its dysregulation [39]. This process increases levels of stress hormones like cortisol, promotes systemic inflammation, and impairs neural plasticity [40]. These biological changes can negatively affect brain regions involved in cognition, providing a plausible pathway linking social mobility to cognitive decline.

Roles of lifestyles and social participation

Health behaviors accounted for 20%–30% of the socioeconomic inequities in health outcomes [41]. While lifestyles and social participation are well-established modifiable risk factors of health [42], their roles in the relationship between SDOH levels and cognitive function remain underexplored. Wang et al. reported that healthy lifestyles and active social contact jointly explained 10.4% of the association between SES and dementia [5]. Similarly, we found that lifestyles and social participation collectively accounted for 14.9% of the association between low SDOH levels and global cognitive function. Notably, lifestyles accounted for less than 4% of the observed association, indicating that promoting healthy behaviors in adulthood alone may be insufficient to mitigate cognitive impairment without addressing lifelong adverse SDOH accumulation. Our findings highlight the essential role of SDOH as determinants of cognitive well-being. Nevertheless, individuals with disadvantaged SDOH and unhealthy lifestyles or social disengagement exhibited poorer cognitive function compared to those with more favorable SDOH and healthy behaviors. These findings underscore the critical need for implementing integrated interventions that address social inequities across the life course while simultaneously promoting healthy lifestyles to alleviate cognitive dysfunction among older adults.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, by utilizing longitudinal cognitive data from three nationally representative cohorts, we examined life course SDOH levels, including stage-specific and cumulative, in relation to both cognitive function and the rate of cognitive decline. Second, in assessing SDOH levels at each stage, we considered multidimensional SDOH, addressing the limitations of previous studies with fewer SDOH selections. Third, comprehensive social mobility patterns (27 in total) were constructed to thoroughly analyze the relationship between social mobility across the life course and cognition.

We also acknowledged several limitations. First, the self-reported and retrospective nature of SDOH may introduce recall bias. Nonetheless, prior studies have demonstrated the reliability of recalled basic socio-demographic information [35, 43]. Second, some important SDOH factors like neighborhood environment, early-life nutrition, and housing conditions were not included due to unavailable data. Childhood SDOH was assessed primarily by education, which may not fully reflect its complexity. Nevertheless, the selected indicators in this study serve as reliable proxies for SDOH level across different life stages. Third, we measured social mobility using proxy indicators instead of tracking changes in the same variable, like wealth, over time. This may not fully capture the complexity of mobility. Fourth, we included psychological disorders as indicators of middle-late adulthood SDOH to capture psychosocial adversity within the social context, consistent with previous research [23]. This approach is somewhat controversial, as such conditions are typically considered downstream health outcomes or potential mediators. Fifth, the excluded participants were more likely to be socially disadvantaged and have poorer health, making them more susceptible to neurodegenerative conditions. This may introduce selection and survivor bias, potentially underestimating the associations between SDOH level and cognitive outcomes. Sixth, the absence of APOE ε4 genotype or polygenic risk scores in our data may lead to residual confounding. In addition, it is possible that unrecognized early mild cognitive decline had already influenced participants’ social participation and lifestyle behaviors at baseline, introducing the possibility of reverse causation. Another limitation is imputing missing social and financial data, which are likely missing systematically rather than randomly. This violates key assumptions of most imputation methods and may introduce bias. Last, inherent heterogeneity across cohorts was inevitable due to social and cultural differences among countries. Nevertheless, we harmonized diverse measurements and definitions across cohorts and adjusted for cohort effects in our models to mitigate this heterogeneity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, both stage-specific and cumulative SDOH levels throughout life are linked to cognitive function and the rate of cognitive decline. Low SDOH levels in adulthood and accumulating across the life course are associated with poorer cognitive function and accelerated cognitive decline. In contrast, low childhood SDOH levels are related to poorer cognitive function but a slower rate of cognitive decline. Individuals who experienced downward social mobility from high SDOH levels in childhood to low levels in adulthood were more susceptible to accelerated cognitive decline. Lifestyles and social participation collectively accounted for 15% of the association between low SDOH levels and global cognitive function. Our findings indicate that intervention strategies should span the entire life course. Early-life measures such as educational support, poverty reduction, and enhancing parental resources can lay the groundwork for cognitive health. In middle to late life, structured social participation, lifelong learning, and volunteering can further support cognitive resilience. Notably, an integrated approach across varying life stages is likely more effective than interventions focused only on late life.
