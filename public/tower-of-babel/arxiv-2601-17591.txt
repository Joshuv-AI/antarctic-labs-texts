Exact Recovery in the Geometric Hidden Community Model
Julia Gaudio, Andrew Jin

Abstract
Hidden community problems, such as community detection in the Stochastic Block Model (SBM), submatrix localization, and ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} synchronization, have received considerable attention in the probability, statistics, and information-theory literature. Motivated by transitive behavior in social networks, which tend to exhibit high triangle density, recent works have considered hidden community models in spatially-embedded networks. In particular, Baccelli and Sankararaman proposed the Geometric SBM, a spatially-embedded analogue of the standard SBM with dramatically more triangles. In this paper, we consider the problem of exact recovery for the Geometric Hidden Community Model (GHCM) of Gaudio, Guan, Niu, and Wei, which generalizes the Geometric SBM to allow for arbitrary pairwise observation distributions. Under mild technical assumptions, we find the information-theoretic threshold for exact recovery in the “distance-dependent” GHCM, which allows the pairwise distributions to depend on distance as well as community labels, thus completing the picture of exact recovery in spatially-embedded hidden community models.



1
Introduction

Community detection is the problem of identifying hidden dense communities in networks. The Stochastic Block Model (SBM), introduced by Holland, Laskey, and Leinhardt in 1983
[
12
]
, is considered the canonical model for generating random graphs with community structure. In its simplest version, the SBM comprises two communities of vertices of equal size. Pairs of vertices in the same community are connected with probability
p
, while pairs of vertices in opposite communities are connected with probability
q
. If
p>q
, then the graph exhibits assortative community structure; that is, typical instances of the SBM have two dense communities with sparse connections between them. Depending on the values of
p
and
q
, it may be possible to recover the communities exactly, almost exactly, or partially. These inference tasks have been the subject of a large body of research, much of it summarized in the survey of Abbe
[
4
]
.

An emerging line of work studies spatially-embedded versions of community detection problems, which lead to
transitive
community structure; namely, if
(u,v)
and
(u,w)
are edges, then
(v,w)
is likely to be an edge as well. Recently, Baccelli and Sankararaman proposed the Geometric Stochastic Block Model (GSBM)
[
14
]
, a spatially-embedded analogue of the SBM in which vertices are placed in a bounded region of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
according to a homogeneous Poisson process, and vertices are connected by edges with probabilities that depend on both their communities as well as their distance. An important special case of the GSBM is one where vertices may only be connected by an edge if they are sufficiently close to each other (i.e., if the distance between
u,v
satisfies
\|u-v\|\leq r_{n}
); in that case, they are connected with probability
a
if they are in the same community and with probability
b
otherwise. We refer to this as the
constant-probability
GSBM. In this special case, the GSBM has dramatically more triangles than the standard SBM. Fixing the average degree to be the same in the two models, the GSBM with
\text{Pois}(\lambda n)
vertices has order
n
times more triangles than the SBM with
n
vertices.

Following the introduction of the GSBM, subsequent works focused on the exact recovery problem in the constant-probability GSBM. Gaudio, Niu, and Wei
[
11
]
determined the exact recovery threshold in the logarithmic-degree regime of the constant-probability GSBM, complementing the impossibility result that had been established by Abbe et al
[
1
]
. Furthermore,
[
11
]
proposed a linear-time algorithm for exact recovery which achieves the information-theoretic (IT) threshold. Gaudio, Guan, Niu, and Wei
[
9
]
then proposed the Geometric Hidden Community Model (GHCM), a generalization of the GSBM which allows for multiple, possibly unbalanced communities, and considers pairwise distributions that are not necessarily Bernoulli-valued (i.e. “edge” or “no edge”). The GHCM thus extends prominent hidden community models like submatrix localization and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization to the spatially-embedded setting, capturing settings where pairwise observations are available only for vertices which are “close” in terms of their physical distance or their feature embedding. For example, consider the problem of sensor calibration in a physical network, in which relative measurements are available only for pairs of sensors that are close to each other. A variation of the algorithm of
[
11
]
was shown to achieve the IT threshold in the constant-probability GHCM, under some technical assumptions. Subsequently, Gaudio and Guan
[
8
]
showed that one of these assumptions, which requires a certain “distinctness of distributions,” is not required in the two-community setting, thus allowing for a spatially-embedded analogue of the planted dense subgraph and submatrix localization problems, among other hidden community problems.

Moving away from the constant-probability assumption, Avrachenkov, Kumar, and Leskelä
[
5
]
considered the setting of two communities, where edge probabilities are governed by a pair of functions
f_{\text{in}}(\cdot)
and
f_{\text{out}}(\cdot)
: if
u,v
are in the same community, they are connected with probability
f_{\text{in}}(\|u-v\|)
; otherwise, they are connected with probability
f_{\text{out}}(\|u-v\|)
. The authors considered only the case where
d=1
and the functions differ by a constant factor. Subsequently, Gaudio and Jin
[
10
]
handled the case of arbitrary, constant dimension, where
f_{\text{in}}(\cdot)
and
f_{\text{out}}(\cdot)
only need to satisfy some regularity conditions but otherwise can be quite general.

In this paper, we complete the picture for the exact recovery problem in the GHCM by allowing for distance dependence in pairwise distributions. We determine the IT threshold for exact recovery and show that an extension of the algorithms proposed by
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
achieves the IT threshold. Intuitively, the IT threshold is determined by the probability that a vertex can be accurately labeled when all other labels are accurately given. Since there are
\Theta(n)
vertices, the IT threshold occurs when the probability of misclassifying a given vertex is
\Theta(1/n)
. That is, if the error probability is
\omega(1/n)
, exact recovery is impossible, while if it is
o(1/n)
, then exact recovery is possible.

While these asymptotics are intuitive, they are not immediate. The asymptotics of the impossibility result are established in
[
1
]
, and lower-bounding the error probability is accomplished by employing a Cramér lower bound from large deviations theory, following the analysis of
[
9
]
. On the other hand, the achievability result comes from adapting the algorithm proposed in
[
9
]
. The algorithm has three main steps: (1) label an initial seed set of
\Theta(\log n)
vertices using the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator; (2) propagate labels among “blocks” of vertices to achieve almost exact recovery; and (3) refine the almost exact labeling to turn it into an exact labeling. Step 3 is where the IT threshold is invoked. The main challenge in adapting the algorithm to the current, more general setting is in Steps (1) and (2). By allowing the observation distributions to be distance-dependent, there are pairs of vertices
(u,v)
such that their pairwise observation, given by
X_{uv}
, has a similar distribution no matter what communities
u
and
v
belong to. In this way, some distances are more informative than others. While the IT threshold accounts for the heterogeneity of information, accounting for this hurdle in Steps (1) and (2), where the IT threshold is not invoked, is the main technical challenge in this paper.

We use
D_{+}
to denote the Chernoff-Hellinger (CH) divergence, which was first introduced by Abbe and Sandon in
[
2
]
, then extended in
[
9
]
and
[

]
. We use the definition from
[
10
]
, which goes as follows. Let
p(y)=(p_{1}(\cdot;y),\ldots,p_{k}(\cdot;y))
and
q(y)=(q_{1}(\cdot;y),\ldots,q_{k}(\cdot;y))
be vectors of probability distributions depending on a parameter
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, where
p_{i}(\cdot;y)
and
q_{i}(\cdot;y)
are probability mass functions (in the discrete case) or probability density functions (in the continuous case). In addition, let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k})
be a vector of prior probabilities, and let
g(y)
be a density on
\mathcal{Y}
. Then, we define the CH-divergence between
p
and
q
with respect to
\pi
and
g
as

\displaystyle D_{+}(p\|q;\pi,g)=1-\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pi_{i}\int_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}g(y)\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{i}(x;y)^{t}q_{i}(x;y)^{1-t}\>dy

(1)

when
p
and
q
are discrete, and

\displaystyle D_{+}(p\|q;\pi,g)=1-\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pi_{i}\int_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}g(y)\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{i}(x;y)^{t}q_{i}(x;y)^{1-t}\>dx\>dy

(2)

when
p
and
q
are continuous.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
formally defines the GHCM model and presents our main results establishing an information-theoretic threshold for exact recovery under certain assumptions. Section
3
describes the algorithm for exact recovery and provides a proof sketch for our achievability result. Section
4
provides a proof sketch for our impossibility result. We discuss potential future research directions in Section
5
. Appendix
B
presents the full details of the exact recovery algorithm and Appendix
C
proves that the algorithm achieves exact recovery, except for the special one-dimensional case where there’s a unique “permissible labeling”. Appendix
D
handles that special case, providing a modified algorithm to achieve exact recovery and the corresponding proof. Finally, Appendix
E
proves the impossibility of exact recovery below the information-theoretic threshold.

2
Model and Main Results

We consider a generalization of the GHCM
[
9
]
by allowing the edge weight distribution between two vertices to depend on their distance, in addition to their community labels.

Definition 1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a scaling parameter. Let
\lambda,r>0
be constants,
d\in\mathbb{N}
be the dimension, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of communities. Let
Z
with
|Z|=k
be a set which contains the label of each community. Let
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
represent the prior probabilities of each community. For any
i,j\in Z
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
, let
P_{ij}(y)
be the edge weight distribution between two vertices of community
i
and
j
at a distance
y
. Let
p_{ij}(\cdot;y)
denote the probability mass function (PMF) or probability density function (PDF) of
P_{ij}(y)
. Then, we sample
G\sim\text{GHCM}(\lambda,n,r,\pi,P(y),d)
as follows:

1.

The locations of the vertices are generated by a Poisson point process with intensity
\lambda
over
\mathcal{S}_{d,n}:=[-n^{1/d}/2,n^{1/d}/2]^{d}
, the cube of volume
n
centered at the origin. Let
V
be the set of generated vertices.

2.

Each vertex
v\in V
is independently assigned a community label, denoted
\sigma^{*}(v)
, with
\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{*}(v)=i)=\pi_{i}
for each
i\in Z
.

3.

Conditioned on the locations and community labels, edge weights are generated independently between each pair of vertices. Let
\overline{P}(y):=P(y/(\log n)^{1/d})
and
\overline{p}(\cdot;y)
be the PMF/PDF of
\overline{P}(y)
. For each pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
where
u\neq v
, the edge weight
X_{uv}
is sampled from

X_{uv}\sim\overline{P}_{\sigma^{*}(u)\sigma^{*}(v)}(\|u-v\|),

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean toroidal metric. We denote the observed edge weight as
x_{uv}
. We assume that
r:=\inf\{y>0:P_{ij}(y)=0\>\forall y>r\}<\infty
, and call
r(\log n)^{1/d}
the
visibility radius
. In addition, we say that
u
is
visible
to
v
if
\|u-v\|\leq r(\log n)^{1/d}
, which we denote
u\leftrightarrow{}v
.

Our goal is to achieve exact recovery, which is a particular notion of recovering the community labels. As discussed in
[
9
]
, note that if there are symmetries in the prior probabilities and edge weight distributions, then it is only possible to recover the correct labeling up to a permutation. Consequently,
[
9
]
introduced the definition of a permissible relabeling to account for such symmetries, which we modify to account for distance-dependent edge weight distributions.

Definition 2

(Permissible Relabeling)
.

A permutation of communities
\omega:Z\to Z
is called permissible if
\pi_{i}=\pi_{\omega(i)}
and
P_{ij}(y)=P_{\omega(i)\omega(j)}(y)
for all
i,j\in Z
and almost all
y\in[0,r]
. We denote the set of permissible relabelings as
\Omega_{\pi,P}
.

Now, we define exact recovery. Let
\sigma^{*}_{n}
be the true labeling and
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
be an estimated labeling. We define the agreement of
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
and
\sigma^{*}_{n}
as

A(\tilde{\sigma}_{n},\sigma^{*}_{n})=\frac{1}{|V|}\max_{\omega\in\Omega_{\pi,P}}\sum_{u\in V}\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{\sigma}_{n}(u)=\omega\circ\sigma^{*}_{n}(u)\}

which is the proportion of vertices that
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
labels correctly, up to a permissible relabeling. Then, we say that
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
achieves

•

exact recovery
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(A(\tilde{\sigma}_{n},\sigma^{*}_{n})=1)=1
,

•

almost-exact recovery
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(A(\tilde{\sigma}_{n},\sigma^{*}_{n})\geq 1-\epsilon)=1
, for all
\epsilon>0
, and

•

partial recovery
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(A(\tilde{\sigma}_{n},\sigma^{*}_{n})\geq\alpha)=1
for some constant
\alpha>0
.

We now state our impossibility result for exact recovery. Let
\theta_{i}:=(p_{i1},p_{i2},\ldots,p_{ik})
be the vector of edge weight distributions from community
i
to all other communities, and
g(y):=dy^{d-1}/r^{d}
be a density on
\mathcal{Y}=[0,r]
. Let
\nu_{d}
be the volume of the d-dimensional unit ball, and
D_{+}
be the CH-divergence defined in (
1
) and (
2
).

Theorem 1

(Impossibility)
.

Any estimator
\tilde{\sigma}:V\to Z
fails to achieve exact recovery for
G\sim\text{GHCM}(\lambda,n,r,\pi,P(y),d)
when either

1.

\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)<1
, or

2.

d=1,\lambda r<1,
and
|\Omega_{\pi,P}|\geq 2
.

Next, we present our achievability result for exact recovery. We require the following assumptions, which are similar to those in
[
9
]
. These assumptions are used to achieve almost-exact recovery in Steps (1) and (2) outlined in the introduction.

Assumption 1

(Identifiability of pairwise distributions)
.

If
\{i,j\}\neq\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
as sets, then either

•

P_{ij}(y)\equiv P_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}(y)
for almost all
y\in[0,r]
, or

•

P_{ij}(y)\not\equiv P_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}(y)
for almost all
y\in[0,r]
.

We abbreviate the cases as
P_{ij}\equiv P_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
and
P_{ij}\not\equiv P_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
, respectively.

Assumption 2

(Bounded log-likelihood difference)
.

We assume that
P_{ij}
have the same support
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}
for all
i,j\in Z
, and that there exists
\eta>0
such that

\log\left(\frac{p_{ij}(x;y)}{p_{ab}(x;y)}\right)<\eta

for any
i,j,a,b\in Z
, any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
and almost all
y\in[0,r]
.

Assumption 3

(Distinctness)
.

For any community
i\in Z
, we have
P_{ia}(y)\not\equiv P_{ib}(y)
for any
a,b\in Z
where
a\neq b
and almost all
y\in[0,r]
.

For exact recovery when
d=1
and
|\Omega_{\pi,P}|=1
, we need a stronger distinctness assumption.

Assumption 4

(Strong Distinctness)
.

For any
i,j,a,b\in Z
, we have
P_{ij}(y)\not\equiv P_{ab}(y)
for almost all
y\in[0,r]
.

Under these assumptions, we establish the following achievability theorem.

Theorem 2

(Achievability)
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that achieves exact recovery for
G\sim\text{GHCM}(\lambda,n,r,\pi,P(y),d)
if
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)>1
and either

1.

d\geq 2
, or

2.

d=1,\lambda r>1
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
and
4
, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that achieves exact recovery for
G\sim\text{GHCM}(\lambda,n,r,\pi,P(y),d)
if
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)>1
,
d=1
, and
|\Omega_{\pi,P}|=1
.

Exact Recovery Algorithm

3.1
The Algorithm

In this section, we describe our algorithm for exact recovery for the
d\geq 2
and
d=1
,
\lambda r>1
cases. We note that our exact recovery algorithm is the same as the exact recovery algorithm in
[
9
]
; our main contribution is proving that this algorithm achieves exact recovery even in the distance-dependent GHCM under suitable assumptions. To describe the algorithm, we need the following definitions from
[
9
]
.

Definition 3

(Occupied Block)
.

Given
\delta>0
, we call a block
B_{i}

\delta
-occupied if
B_{i}
contains at least
\delta\log n
vertices.

Definition 4

(Mutually Visible Blocks)
.

We call two blocks
B_{i}
and
B_{j}
mutually visible, which we note
B_{i}\leftrightarrow{}B_{j}
, if

\sup_{u\in B_{i},v\in B_{j}}\|u-v\|\leq r(\log n)^{1/d}.

Definition 5

(Block Visibility Graph)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on
\mathcal{S}_{d,n}
and consider a partition of
\mathcal{S}_{d,n}
into blocks of volume
v(n)
. Then, we define the
(v(n),c(n))
-block visibility graph of
G
as the graph
H=(V^{\dagger},E^{\dagger})
, where
V^{\dagger}:=\{i\in[n/v(n)]:|V_{i}|\geq c(n)\}
is the set of all blocks with at least
c(n)
vertices and
E^{\dagger}:=\{\{i,j\}:i,j\in V^{\dagger},B_{i}\leftrightarrow{}B_{j}\}
is the set of all pairs of blocks in
V^{\dagger}
that are mutually visible.

We now provide an overview of the algorithm. First, we divide
\mathcal{S}_{d,n}
into cubes of volume
r^{d}\chi\log n
, which we call
blocks
. The parameters
\chi
and
\delta
are chosen so that the
(r^{d}\chi\log n,\delta\log n)
-block visibility graph is connected with high probability.
If
d=1
, fix
\chi_{0}<(1-1/(\lambda r))/2
and
\delta^{\prime}
such that Proposition 1 in
[
10
]
is satisfied. Then, we can take any

\chi_{0}/2<\chi<\chi_{0}\>\text{ and }\>\delta^{\prime}\chi/2<\delta<\delta^{\prime}\chi,

(3)

where
\delta^{\prime}
, and thus
\delta
, can be made arbitrarily small. If
d=2
, fix
\chi_{0}
such that

\lambda r^{d}\bigg(\nu_{d}\Big(1-\frac{3\sqrt{d}}{2}\chi_{0}^{1/d}\Big)^{d}-\chi_{0}\bigg)>1\>\text{ and }\>1-\frac{3\sqrt{d}}{2}\chi_{0}^{1/d}>0

and
\delta^{\prime\prime}
such that Proposition 1 in
[
10
]
is satisfied. Then, we can take any

\chi_{0}/2<\chi<\chi_{0}\>\text{ and }\>\delta^{\prime\prime}\chi/(2\nu_{d})<\delta<\delta^{\prime\prime}\chi/\nu_{d},

(4)

where
\delta^{\prime\prime}
, and thus
\delta
, can be made arbitrarily small. Since the block visibility graph is connected, we can then label the vertices block-by-block, for all
\delta
-occupied blocks. In particular, we find a rooted spanning tree of the block visibility graph, ordering
V^{\dagger}=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{|V^{\dagger}|}\}
in breadth-first order. For any
i_{j}\in V^{\dagger}
, we denote its parent block as
p(i_{j})
.

First, we label
V_{i_{0}}
(a subset of vertices in the initial block
V_{i_{1}}
) using the MAP estimator, which computes the labeling that maximizes the posterior likelihood of the observed graph
G
(i.e. the vertex locations and pairwise edge observations). That is,

\hat{\sigma}_{V_{i_{0}}}=\argmax_{\sigma:V_{i_{0}}\to Z}\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{*}=\sigma\mid G).

(5)

We remark that we only label
V_{i_{0}}
instead of the entire initial block to maintain runtime efficiency, since the MAP estimator is computed through an exhaustive iteration of all possible labelings.

Then, we iterate through each
\delta
-occupied block
V_{i_{j}}
for
j=1,\ldots,|V^{\dagger}|
, labeling each block using the estimated labels of the parent block. We call this technique
propagation
, which was first developed in
[
11
]
. For a given block
V_{i_{j}}
, we label each vertex
v\in V_{i_{j}}
with the community that maximizes the posterior likelihood of the observed edge weights between
v
and the vertices
V_{p(i_{j})}
in the parent block. That is,

\hat{\sigma}(v)=\argmax_{a\in Z}\sum_{u\in V_{p(i_{j})}:\hat{\sigma}(u)=b}\log(p_{ab}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)).

(6)

Due to the connectivity of the block visibility graph, we can label all
\delta
-occupied blocks via propagation. This produces an almost-exact labeling
\hat{\sigma}:V\to Z

Finally, we compute the exact labeling
\tilde{\sigma}:V\to Z
iteratively for each vertex
v\in V
. Given the initial labeling
\hat{\sigma}
, we compute
\tilde{\sigma}(v)
by choosing the community which maximizes the posterior likelihood of the observed graph
G
with respect to the labeling
\hat{\sigma}
. That is,

\tilde{\sigma}(v)=\argmax_{j\in Z}\sum_{u\in V:u\leftrightarrow{}v}\log(p_{j,\hat{\sigma}(u)}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)).

(7)

This procedure is inspired by the genie-aided estimator, which labels a vertex knowing the true labels of all other vertices.

We leave the full description of the algorithm for the
d=1,|\Omega_{\pi,P}|=1

. In summary, the algorithm is quite similar—we first divide
S_{1,n}
into blocks (though a different number of them), then label the initial blocks using the MAP estimator, label the remaining blocks via propagation, and compute the exact labeling as in (
7
). The main difference is that we have multiple initial blocks because the block visibility graph may be disconnected.

3.2
Proof Sketch

First, we discuss the proof of exact recovery in the
d\geq 2
and
d=1,\lambda r>1
cases. Our proof mainly follows the strategy used to prove exact recovery in
[
9
]
, with several adaptions to handle distance-dependent edge weight distributions. First, we note that due to Proposition 1 in
[
10
]
, the choice of
\chi
and
\delta
in (
3
) and (
4
) ensure that the
(r^{d}\chi\log n,\delta\log n)
-block visibility graph is connected with high probability.

Next, we show that the MAP estimator correctly labels all vertices in
V_{i_{0}}
with high probability, following the strategy of
[
9
]
. Rather than analyzing the MAP estimator directly, we analyze the restricted maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) because the analysis is more tractable. To define the restricted MLE, let

\displaystyle X_{0}^{*}(\epsilon):=

\displaystyle\Big\{\sigma:V_{i_{0}}\to Z\text{ such that }|\{u\in V_{i_{0}}:\sigma(u)=j\}|\in\Big((\pi_{j}-\epsilon)|V_{i_{0}}|,(\pi_{j}+\epsilon)|V_{i_{0}}|\Big)\>\forall j\in Z\Big\},

(8)

which is the set of all labelings such that the proportion of vertices in each community is close to the expected proportion. Then, the restricted MLE
\bar{\sigma}
chooses the labeling within
X_{0}^{*}(\epsilon)
that maximizes the likelihood of the observed graph. That is,

\bar{\sigma}=\argmax_{\sigma\in X_{0}^{*}(\epsilon)}\sum_{u\in V_{i_{0}}}\sum_{v\in V_{i_{0}},v\neq u}\log\Big(\overline{p}_{\sigma(u),\sigma(v)}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)\Big).

(9)

We then show that for sufficient small
\epsilon
, the restricted MLE labels all vertices in
V_{i_{0}}
correctly with high probability. Due to optimality of the MAP estimator, the MAP labels all vertices in
V_{i_{0}}
correctly with high probability.

To accomplish this, we fix
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small and upper-bound the probability that there exists an incorrect labeling (up to a permissible relabeling) in
X_{0}^{*}(\epsilon)
having a higher posterior likelihood than the ground truth labeling
\sigma^{*}
—this is the error probability of the MAP estimator. We adapt an idea of Dhara, Gaudio, Mossel, and Sandon
[
7
]
, dividing the proof into two cases depending on the
discrepancy
between the incorrect labeling
\sigma
and
\sigma^{*}
, which is the number of vertices on which
\sigma
differs from
\sigma^{*}
. In the low-discrepancy case, the proof relies on concentration inequalities to directly obtain a high probability upper bound over all low-discrepancy labelings. Meanwhile, in the high-discrepancy case, we first use concentration inequalities to upper-bound the probability for any given high-discrepancy labeling and then use the union bound. We note that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
are used here.

Then, we show that during propagation,
\hat{\sigma}
makes at most
M
mistakes in each
\delta
-occupied block
V_{i_{j}}
for
j=1,\ldots,|V^{\dagger}|
, for some constant
M
. We follow the strategy used in
[
9
]
and first use concentration inequalities to show that
\hat{\sigma}
makes more than
M
mistakes on any given block with probability
o(1/n)

Finally, we show that labeling

4
Proof Sketch of Impossibility

In this section, we discuss the impossibility proof, starting with the case where
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)<1
. We split the proof into two further cases:
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}<1
and
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\geq 1
. For the first case where
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}<1
, we use Theorem 7.1 from Penrose
[
13
]
to show that there is an isolated vertex with high probability. Consequently, there is a constant nonzero probability that the MAP estimator fails, since it fails to label the isolated vertex correctly with a constant nonzero probability. Since the MAP is optimal, any other estimator fails as well. For the second case where
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\geq 1
, we use Proposition 8.1 from
[
1
]
.

Lemma 1

(Proposition 8.1 in
[
1
]
)
.

We call a vertex

“Flip-Bad” if there exists a community

such that

. Let
F_{v}^{H}
denote the event that vertex

is Flip-Bad in graph
H
. Now, suppose that the graph
G
satisfies the following two conditions:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathbb{E}^{0}[\mathbbm{1}_{F_{0}^{G\cup\{0\}}}]=\infty,\text{ and }

(13)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\int_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{d,n}}\mathbb{E}^{0,y}[\mathbbm{1}_{F_{0}^{G\cup\{0,y\}}}\mathbbm{1}_{F_{y}^{G\cup\{0,y\}}}]\>m_{n,d}(dy)}{n\mathbb{E}^{0}[\mathbbm{1}_{F_{0}^{G\cup\{0\}}}]^{2}}\leq 1

(14)

where

is the Haar measure,
\mathbb{E}^{0}
represents the expectation with respect to the graph
G\cup\{0\}
, and
\mathbb{E}^{0,y}
is the expectation with respect to the graph

To prove (
13

[
9
]
. We lower-bound the probability that the vertex

is Flip-Bad; in particular, we show that
\mathbb{P}(0\text{ is Flip-Bad in }G\cup\{0\})\geq n^{-1+\beta}
for some constant
\beta>0
, which implies (
13
). To compute this lower bound, we use Cramér’s Theorem from large deviations theory. We note that the condition
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)<1
(i.e. being below the information-theoretic threshold) is used here. To prove
(
14
)
, we use a straightforward calculation that is essentially identical to that of Lemma B.5 in
[
9
]
.

We now discuss the impossibility proof when
d=1,\lambda r<1,\text{ and }|\Omega_{\pi,P}|\geq 2
. We define the
vertex visibility graph
of
G
as the graph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
where
E^{\prime}=\{\{u,v\}:u,v\in V,\|u-v\|\leq r(\log n)^{1/d}\}
, i.e. the graph formed from taking all vertices of
G
and adding an edge between any pair of vertices which are visible to each other. From Lemma 15 in
[
10
]
, we obtain that the vertex visibility graph of
G
is disconnected with high probability. This makes exact recovery impossible because we can permute the labels in one connected component (but not other components) via a non-trivial permissible relabeling, which yields an incorrect labeling with the same posterior likelihood as
\sigma^{*}
. Since the MAP estimator cannot differentiate between this incorrect labeling and
\sigma^{*}
, the MAP will be incorrect with constant probability.

5
Discussion

In this paper, we identified the information-theoretic threshold for exact recovery in the distance-dependent GHCM under some technical conditions. In addition, we provided an efficient algorithm which achieves exact recovery above this threshold.

One limitation of this paper is that we do not handle the case where two pairwise distributions
P_{ij}
,
P_{ab}
are equal for some distances and not equal for other distances. This case would be challenging for the MAP estimator and for propagation if, for example, the distributions coincide nearby.
Another related direction for future work is considering the case where the pairwise distributions have different “radii”. For instance, labels
(i,j)
could have information up to a larger radius
r_{1}
while
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
only have information up to a smaller radius
r_{2}
.

In addition, we assume that the vertex locations and parameters of the model are known. It would be interesting to consider the question where the distribution family for the pairwise distributions is known, but the parameters are not; this question has been studied in the SBM, for instance by Abbe and Sandon
[
3
]
, but not for spatially-embedded community models such as the GSBM or GHCM.

In this section, we will show that the Algorithm

Lemma 5
.

\phi_{t}(P_{ij}(y),P_{ab}(y))

P_{ij}\not\equiv P_{ab}\end{subarray}}\{y\in[0,r]:\phi_{t}(P_{ij}(y),P_{ab}(y))\geq 1-\gamma\}.

(18)

Then, for any
\epsilon>0
and
t\in(0,1)

\gamma>0

|S(\gamma;t)|<\epsilon

First, we show that for any

, we have
\lim_{\gamma\to 0}|S^{\prime}(\gamma;P_{ij},P_{ab},t)|=0
. Let

. Observing that

\bigg|\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}S^{\prime}(\gamma_{n};P_{ij},P_{ab},t)\bigg|=\lim_{n\to\infty}|S^{\prime}(\gamma_{n};P_{ij},P_{ab},t)|

\displaystyle\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}S^{\prime}(\gamma_{n};P_{ij},P_{ab},t)

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\{y\in[0,r]:\phi_{t}(P_{ij}(y),P_{ab}(y))\geq 1-\gamma_{n}\}

\displaystyle=\{y\in[0,r]:\phi_{t}(P_{ij}(y),P_{ab}(y))\geq 1-\gamma_{n}\>\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\}

\displaystyle=\{y\in[0,r]:\phi_{t}(P_{ij}(y),P_{ab}(y))=1\}

Fact 1

Let
Y

X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{Y}
be
Y

X
independent of

. Then,
\Lambda_{S}(t)=\Lambda_{Y}(\Lambda_{X}(t))
,

We first upper-bound the probability that the MAP estimator comes “close” to making an error on any given vertex

correctly, where the posterior likelihood is computed with respect to the true labeling

Lemma 9
.

If
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)>1
, then for a fixed
0<\epsilon\leq(\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)-1)/2
, we have that

\mathbb{P}(\ell_{i}(v,\sigma^{*})-\ell_{j}(v,\sigma^{*})\leq\epsilon\log n\mid\sigma^{*}(v)=i)=n^{-(1+\Omega(1))}

where

\ell_{i}(v,\sigma)=\sum_{v\in V:u\leftrightarrow{}v}\log(\overline{p}_{i,\sigma(u)}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)).

Proof.

Fix a vertex
v\in V
with
\sigma^{*}(v)=i
. We construct a random variable
X
such that

X\sim\ell_{j}(v,\sigma^{*})-\ell_{i}(v,\sigma^{*}).

Observe that

\displaystyle\ell_{j}(v,\sigma^{*})-\ell_{i}(v,\sigma^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in V:u\leftrightarrow{}v}\log\bigg(\frac{p_{j,\sigma^{*}(v)}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)}{p_{i,\sigma^{*}(v)}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in Z}\bigg(\sum_{u\in V:u\leftrightarrow{}v,\sigma^{*}(u)=s}\log\Big(\frac{p_{js}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)}{p_{is}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)}\Big)\bigg)

Now, we construct
X_{s}
for each
s\in Z
such that

X_{s}\sim\log\bigg(\frac{p_{js}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)}{p_{is}(x_{uv};\|u-v\|)}\bigg).

Let
D
be a random variable with density

f_{D}(x):=\begin{cases}dx^{d-1}/r^{d}\text{ if }0\leq x\leq r\\
0\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

which represents the distance between
v
and a vertex
u
placed uniformly at random in the neighborhood of
v
. Let
X_{is}
be a random variable coupled to
D
, such that conditioned on
D=y
, we sample
X_{is}
from
P_{is}(y)
. Note that
X_{is}
represents the edge weight between
u
and
v
. Then, we define

X_{s}:=\log\bigg(\frac{p_{js}(X_{is};D)}{p_{is}(X_{is};D)}\bigg).

Now, letting
N_{s}\sim\text{Pois}(\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\pi_{s}\log n)
and
\{X_{s}^{(m)}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}X_{s}
, we can define

X=\sum_{s\in Z}\bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{N_{s}}X_{s}\bigg).

By the Chernoff bound, we have that for any fixed
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\ell_{i}(v,\sigma^{*})-\ell_{j}(v,\sigma^{*})\leq\epsilon\log n\mid\sigma^{*}(v)=i)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X\geq-\epsilon\log n)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]e^{t\epsilon\log n}

\displaystyle=\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]n^{t\epsilon}.

(34)

Thus, we need to compute the moment-generating function (MGF) of
X
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(t\sum_{s\in Z}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{s}}X_{s}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\prod_{s\in Z}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(t\sum_{m=1}^{N_{s}}X_{s}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\prod_{s\in Z}\exp\Big(\Lambda_{N_{s}}(\Lambda_{X_{s}}(t))\Big).

(35)

Computing the MGF of
X_{s}
using the law of total expectation, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{tX_{s}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(t\log\Big(\frac{p_{js}(X_{is};D)}{p_{is}(X_{is};D)}\Big)\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(t\log\Big(\frac{p_{js}(X_{is};D)}{p_{is}(X_{is};D)}\Big)\bigg)\mid D=y\bigg]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(t\log\Big(\frac{p_{js}(X_{is};y)}{p_{is}(X_{is};y)}\Big)\bigg)\bigg]\frac{dy^{d-1}}{r^{d}}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\phi_{t}(P_{js}(y),P_{is}(y))\frac{dy^{d-1}}{r^{d}}dy

\displaystyle=\overline{\phi}_{t}(P_{js},P_{is}).

Hence,
\Lambda_{X_{s}}(t)=\log\overline{\phi}_{t}(P_{js},P_{is})
. Now, combining the fact that
\Lambda_{N_{s}}(t)=(\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\pi_{s}\log n)(e^{t}-1)
with Fact
1
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N_{s}}(\Lambda_{X_{s}}(t))

\displaystyle=(\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\pi_{s}\log n)(\overline{\phi}_{t}(P_{js},P_{is})-1)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\pi_{s}(1-\overline{\phi}_{t}(P_{js},P_{is}))\log n.

(36)

Substituting (
C.3
) into (
C.3
) yields the MGF of
X
, which is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{s\in Z}\exp\bigg(-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\pi_{s}(1-\overline{\phi}_{t}(P_{js},P_{is}))\log n\bigg)

\displaystyle=\prod_{s\in Z}n^{-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\pi_{s}(1-\overline{\phi}_{t}(P_{js},P_{is}))}

\displaystyle=n^{-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\big(1-\sum_{s\in Z}\pi_{s}\overline{\phi}_{t}(P_{js},P_{is})\big)}.

(37)

Finally, substituting (
C.3
) into (
34

\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\ell_{i}(v,\sigma^{*})-\ell_{j}(v,\sigma^{*})\leq\epsilon\log n\mid\sigma^{*}(v)=i)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{t\in[0,1]}n^{-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\big(1-\sum_{s\in Z}\pi_{s}\overline{\phi}_{t}(P_{js},P_{is})\big)+t\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\big(1-\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\sum_{s\in Z}\pi_{s}\overline{\phi}_{t}(P_{js},P_{is})\big)+\epsilon}

\displaystyle=n^{-\big(\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)-\epsilon\big)}.

Since

to obtain that

\tilde{\sigma}:V\to Z
fails to achieve exact recovery for
G\sim\text{GHCM}(\lambda,n,r,\pi,P(y),d)
when
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)<1
.

We will split the proof into two cases:
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}<1
and
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\geq 1
.

Case 1:
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}<1
. In this case, we will show that the vertex visibility graph contains an isolated vertex with high probability. Consequently, there is a constant, nonzero probability that the MAP estimator fails since it fails to label the isolated vertex correctly with a constant, nonzero probability.

To accomplish this, we note that the vertex visibility graph is a
random geometric graph
. We define the random geometric graph
RGG(d,n,x)
as follows: First, we distribute vertices within the
d
-dimensional unit torus by a Poisson point process of intensity
n
. Then, for any pair of vertices, we form an edge if and only if the distance between the vertices is less than
x
.

The vertex visibility graph of
G
is an
RGG(d,\lambda n,r(\frac{\log n}{n})^{1/d})
because points are distributed on the volume
n
torus by a Poisson point process of intensity
\lambda
with visibility radius
r(\log n)^{1/d}
. Rescaling the volume by
1/n
yields a Poisson point process of intensity
\lambda n
on a unit torus with visibility radius
r(\frac{\log n}{n})^{1/d}
.

Now, we use the following result from the literature on random geometric graphs.

Lemma 13

(Theorem 7.1 in
[
13
]
)
.

Let
M_{n}:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\{\text{RGG}(d,n,x)\text{ has no isolated vertex}\}
. Then,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\nu_{d}M_{n}^{d}}{\log n}=1\quad\text{a.s.},

which implies that

M_{n}\sim\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n\nu_{d}}\bigg)^{1/d}\quad\text{a.s.}

Lemma
13
considers a Poisson process of intensity
n
; for a Poisson process of intensity
\lambda n
, we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda n\nu_{d}M_{\lambda n}^{d}}{\log\lambda n}=1.

Thus, there exists an isolated vertex on the vertex visibility graph with high probability if the visibility radius is less than
M_{\lambda n}
. Since the visibility radius is
r(\frac{\log n}{n})^{1/d}
and
M_{\lambda n}\sim(\frac{\log(\lambda n)}{\lambda n\nu_{d}})^{1/d}
, we obtain that there exists an isolated vertex with high probability if
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}<1
. Consequently, the MAP estimator fails with a constant, non-zero probability when
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}<1
. Therefore, any estimator fails to achieve exact recovery in Case 1.

Case 2:
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\geq 1
. In this case, Lemma
1
gives a sufficient condition for the MAP estimator to fail. As mentioned in Section
4
, we will adapt the proofs of Lemma B.4 and Lemma B.5 in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
G\sim\text{GHCM}(\lambda,n,r,\pi,P(y),d)
, and suppose that
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\geq 1
and
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)<1
. Then, (
13
) is satisfied.

Proof.

It is sufficient to show that
\mathbb{P}(0\text{ is Flip-Bad in }G\cup\{0\})\geq n^{-1+\beta}
. Observe that 0 is Flip-Bad if and only if the posterior likelihood of an incorrect label (with respect to the true labels of all other vertices) is larger than the posterior likelihood of the correct label. Therefore, letting
\sigma^{*}(0)=i
, we would like to show that there exists
j\neq i
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\log(\pi_{j})+\sum_{v\in V,v\sim 0}\log p_{j,\sigma^{*}(v)}(x_{0,v};\|v\|)\geq\log(\pi_{i})+\sum_{v\in V,v\sim 0}\log p_{i,\sigma^{*}(v)}(x_{0,v};\|v\|)\bigg)\geq n^{-1+\beta}.

(48)

Fix
j
such that
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)<1
. Rearranging
(
48
)
, we would like to lower bound

P_{\text{Flip-Bad}}:=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{v\in V,v\sim 0}\log\Big(\frac{p_{j,\sigma^{*}(v)}(x_{0,v};\|v\|)}{p_{i,\sigma^{*}(v)}(x_{0,v};\|v\|)}\Big)\geq\log\Big(\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{j}}\Big)\bigg).

(49)

We now construct a random variable
X
such that

X\sim\log\bigg(\frac{p_{j,\sigma^{*}(v)}(x_{0,v};\|v\|)}{p_{i,\sigma^{*}(v)}(x_{0,v};\|v\|)}\bigg)

where
v
is a vertex placed uniformly at random in the neighborhood of 0 and assigned a community label
\sigma^{*}(v)
according to the probability vector
\pi
. Let
D
be a random variable with density

f_{D}(x):=\begin{cases}dx^{d-1}/r^{d}\text{ if }0\leq x\leq r\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

which represents the distance between 0 and a vertex
v
placed uniformly at random in the neighborhood of 0. Let
X
be a mixture distribution of
X_{s}
for
s\in Z
, with probability
\pi_{s}
, which represents the community of
v
. Let
X_{is}
be a random variable coupled to
D
, such that conditioned on
D=y
, we sample
X_{is}
from
P_{is}(y)
; note that
X_{is}
represents the edge weight between
v
to 0. Finally, let

X_{s}:=\log\bigg(\frac{p_{js}(X_{is};D)}{p_{is}(X_{is};D)}\bigg).

as in the proof of Lemma
9
. Letting
N(0)\sim\text{Pois}(\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\log n)
and
\{X_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\overset{iid}{\sim}X
, from (
49
) we obtain that

\displaystyle P_{\text{Flip-Bad}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{N(0)}X_{\ell}\geq\log\Big(\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{j}}\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(N(0)=m)\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}X_{\ell}\geq\log\Big(\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{j}}\Big)\bigg)

(50)

Now, we lower-bound the probability of each term in (
E
). Fix
\alpha>0
and
\delta>0
, which we will determine later. Using Cramér’s theorem (Lemma
4
), we have that for any fixed
\epsilon>0
, there exists
M
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}X_{\ell}\geq m\alpha\bigg)\geq\exp\Big(m(-\Lambda^{*}_{X}(\alpha)-\epsilon)\Big)\quad\text{for all }m\geq M

For sufficiently large
n
, we have that
\alpha\delta\log n\geq\log(\pi_{i}/\pi_{j})
. Thus, for any
m\geq\delta\log n
, we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}X_{\ell}\geq\log\Big(\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{j}}\Big)\bigg)\geq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}X_{\ell}\geq m\alpha\bigg)\geq\exp\Big(m(-\Lambda^{*}_{X}(\alpha)-\epsilon)\Big)

Furthermore, we also have that
\delta\log n\geq M
for sufficiently large
n
. Hence, we can write (
E
) as

\displaystyle P_{\text{Flip-Bad}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}X_{\ell}\geq\log\Big(\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{j}}\Big)\bigg)\mathbb{P}(N(0)=m)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m=\delta\log n}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}X_{\ell}\geq\log\Big(\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{j}}\Big)\bigg)\mathbb{P}(N(0)=m)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m=\delta\log n}^{\infty}\exp\Big(m(-\Lambda^{*}_{X}(\alpha)-\epsilon)\Big)\mathbb{P}(N(0)=m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\exp\Big(m(-\Lambda^{*}_{X}(\alpha)-\epsilon)\Big)\mathbb{P}(N(0)=m)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{m=0}^{\delta\log n-1}\exp\Big(m(-\Lambda^{*}_{X}(\alpha)-\epsilon)\Big)\mathbb{P}(N(0)=m)

\displaystyle\geq\bigg[\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\exp\Big(m(-\Lambda^{*}_{X}(\alpha)-\epsilon)\Big)\mathbb{P}(N(0)=m)\bigg]-\mathbb{P}(N(0)<\delta\log n)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(N(0)(-\Lambda^{*}_{X}(\alpha)-\epsilon)\Big)\Big]-\mathbb{P}(N(0)<\delta\log n)

\displaystyle=n^{\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\big(\exp\big(-\Lambda^{*}_{X}(\alpha)-\epsilon\big)-1\big)}-\mathbb{P}(N(0)<\delta\log n).

(51)

where last equality follows from the moment-generating function of the Poisson random variable
N(0)
.

Now, let
t_{ij}
be defined as in (
15
) and define
\Lambda(t):=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]
with derivative
\Lambda^{\prime}(t)
. In
[
9
, Lemma B.4]
, the authors showed that for any
\eta>0
, taking
0<\alpha<\Lambda^{\prime}(t_{ij}+\eta)
yields that
\Lambda^{*}_{X}(\alpha)\leq(t_{ij}+\eta)\alpha-\Lambda(t_{ij})
. Combining this result with (
E
), we obtain that for any
\eta>0
, there exists
\alpha>0
such that

P_{\text{Flip-Bad}}\geq n^{\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\big(\exp\big(-(t_{ij}+\eta)\alpha+\Lambda(t_{ij})-\epsilon\big)-1\big)}-\mathbb{P}(N(0)<\delta\log n).

(52)

Now, we show that there exist
\epsilon>0
,
\eta>0
,
\alpha>0
, and
\beta>0
such that

\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\Big(\exp\big(-(t_{ij}+\eta)\alpha+\Lambda(t_{ij})-\epsilon\big)-1\Big)>-1+2\beta.

(53)

By (
15
) and (
16
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\Big(\exp\big(-(t_{ij}+\eta)\alpha+\Lambda(t_{ij})-\epsilon\big)-1\Big)

\displaystyle=\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\bigg(\exp\bigg(-\epsilon-(t_{ij}+\eta)\alpha+\log\Big(\sum_{a\in Z}\pi_{a}\overline{\phi}_{t_{ij}}(P_{ia},P_{ja})\Big)\bigg)-1\bigg)

\displaystyle=\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\bigg(\Big(\sum_{a\in Z}\pi_{a}\overline{\phi}_{t_{ij}}(P_{ia},P_{ja})\Big)\cdot\exp\big(-\epsilon-(t_{ij}+\eta)\alpha\big)-1\bigg)

\displaystyle=\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\bigg(\big(1-D_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})\big)\cdot\exp\big(-\epsilon-(t_{ij}+\eta)\alpha\big)-1\bigg)

For any
\gamma>0
, observe that there exist
\epsilon>0
,
\eta>0
, and
\alpha>0
sufficiently small such that
\exp(-\epsilon-(t_{ij}+\eta)\alpha)\geq 1-\gamma
. Hence,

\displaystyle\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\Big(\exp\big(-(t_{ij}+\eta)\alpha+\Lambda(t_{ij})-\epsilon\big)-1\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\geq\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\bigg(\big(1-D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)\big)\cdot(1-\gamma)-1\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad=-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\bigg(\big(D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)\big)\cdot(1-\gamma)+\gamma\bigg)

(54)

Observe that (
E
)
\to-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)
as
\gamma\to 0
. Hence, let
\beta:=(1-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g))/3
and note that
\beta>0
because
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)<1
. Then, we obtain that for sufficiently small
\gamma
, we have

-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\bigg(\big(D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)\big)\cdot(1-\gamma)+\gamma\bigg)>-1+2\beta.

Therefore, there exist
\epsilon>0
,
\eta>0
,
\alpha>0
, and
\beta>0
such that (
53
) holds. Taking these values of
\epsilon
,
\eta
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
, from (
52
) we obtain that

P_{\text{Flip-Bad}}\geq n^{-1+2\beta}-\mathbb{P}(N(0)<\delta\log n).

(55)

Finally, we show that for any fixed
0<c<2\beta
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(N(0)<\delta\log n)<n^{-1+c}
. Since
N(0)\sim\text{Pois}(\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\log n)
, the Chernoff bound (Lemma
2
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N(0)<\delta\log n)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigg(\delta\log n-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\log n-\log\Big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}}\Big)\delta\log n\bigg)

\displaystyle=n^{\delta-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}-\delta\log(\delta/(\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}))}.

Let
f(\delta):=\delta-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}-\delta\log(\delta/(\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}))
. Observe that
f(\delta)\to-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}
as
\delta\to 0
and also that
f(\delta)
is strictly increasing on
0<\delta<\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}
. Therefore, we can let
\delta>0
sufficiently small such that
f(\delta)<-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}+c
. Furthermore, since
-\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\leq-1
by hypothesis, we obtain that
f(\delta)<-1+c
. As a result, we obtain that

\mathbb{P}(N(0)<\delta\log n)\leq n^{-1+c}

which combined with (
55
) implies that

\mathbb{P}(0\text{ is Flip-Bad in }G\cup\{0\})\geq n^{-1+2\beta}-n^{-1+c}>n^{-1+\beta}.

Therefore, (
13
) is satisfied.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
G\sim\text{GHCM}(\lambda,n,r,\pi,P(y),d)
, and suppose that
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\geq 1
and
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)<1
. Then, (
14
) is satisfied.

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof of
[
10
, Lemma 13]
, which itself is an adaption of
[
9
, Lemma B.5]
, apart from the different IT threshold. Concretely, we invoke Lemma
14
to obtain
\mathbb{E}^{0}[\mathbbm{1}_{0\text{ is Flip-Bad in }G\cup\{0\}}]\geq n^{-1+\beta}
for some constant
\beta>0
when
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\geq 1
and
\lambda\nu_{d}r^{d}\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i}\|\theta_{j};\pi,g)<1
.
∎

Combining Lemmas
14
and
15
with Lemma
1
, we conclude that exact recovery is impossible in Case 2. This completes the proof of Proposition
6
.
