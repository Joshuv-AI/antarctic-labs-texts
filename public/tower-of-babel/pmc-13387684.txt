Triglyceride Glucose Index as a screening tool for subclinical heart failure with preserved ejection fraction in type 2 diabetes mellitus in resource-limited settings
Authors: Balachandra Routhu, Ravi Kant, Barun Kumar, Meenakshi Khapre, Vinay Tulsian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Diabetic cardiomyopathy is an entity with a multifactorial pathophysiology, in which insulin resistance plays a key role. It increases the risk of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). The Triglyceride-Glucose Index (TGI) is a novel measure of insulin resistance which is simple to use and may serve as an accessible marker for subclinical HFpEF.Aims:To evaluate the association between TGI and subclinical HFpEF in asymptomatic patients with T2DM and assess its diagnostic performance.Methods:This was a cross-sectional observational analytic study in which 274 asymptomatic T2DM patients aged 18–60 years were enrolled. Patients with known cardiovascular disease, hypertension, macro- and microvascular complications, and other major comorbidities were excluded. TGI was calculated, and all participants underwent 2D echocardiography to assess left ventricular diastolic function.Results:LV diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) was detected in 62.4% of participants. TGI was significantly higher in patients with LVDD (mean 9.69 ± 0.49) versus those without (mean 9.23 ± 0.63), P < 0.001. ROC analysis identified a TGI cutoff of 9.34 as optimal for detecting LVDD, yielding a sensitivity of 80%, a specificity of 63%, and an AUROC of 0.734. To achieve high sensitivity (100%) and NPV (100%), the cutoff of 8.32 is best for screening for LVDD.Conclusions:TGI demonstrates fair diagnostic accuracy for identifying subclinical HFpEF in T2DM. Given its simplicity and cost-effectiveness, it may be considered a valuable screening tool in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) represents a significant global health challenge, with its prevalence increasing rapidly worldwide, including in developing nations. Globally, India ranks second in the number of diabetes cases.[1] It is estimated that 89.8 million individuals aged over 20 years are currently living with diabetes in India. The number is estimated to increase to approximately 156.7 million by the year 2050.[1] T2DM and associated metabolic disturbances lead to multiple adverse effects on various organ systems, of which the cardiovascular system is particularly susceptible.

Diabetes mellitus independently increases the risk of new onset heart failure. Patients with diabetes mellitus were found to be at 2.14 times higher risk of new onset heart failure. An increase of 1% in glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) is associated with an 8% increased risk for heart failure in people with T2DM.[23]

Cardiovascular disease is also the leading cause of death among diabetics as it impacts nearly 32.2% of all individuals with T2DM and accounts for half of all deaths within this population.[4]

Stage B heart failure (HF), or subclinical heart failure, represents a critical phase in the heart failure continuum.[5] It is marked by structural heart disease without any signs or symptoms of heart failure. T2DM causes direct toxic effects on the myocardium, leading to an entity called diabetic cardiomyopathy. This condition encompasses a subclinical phase marked by structural and functional abnormalities, such as left ventricular hypertrophy, fibrosis, and cell signaling irregularities. These alterations of cardiac fibrosis and stiffness, along with associated subclinical diastolic dysfunction, have been demonstrated to progress toward heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and eventually lead to systolic dysfunction and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).[6]

The clinical significance of identifying Stage B HF lies in the opportunity for early intervention to mitigate risk and potentially prevent or delay the onset of symptomatic disease.

The Triglyceride-Glucose Index (TGI) is a novel measure of insulin resistance (IR) which is easily accessible and simple to use. It is a composite measure that assesses insulin resistance by analyzing the fasting triglyceride and fasting plasma glucose levels. It has been established as a reliable marker of insulin resistance in multiple studies.[7891011] As compared to other measures of IR, such as hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic glucose clamp technique, HOMA-IR, QUICKI, and Adipo-IR, the TGI is simpler to use and more accessible, especially in resource-limited settings.

As insulin resistance is closely linked with the development of diabetic cardiomyopathy, we hypothesized that indices used to measure insulin resistance may be used to predict heart failure in asymptomatic diabetic patients. For clinicians, a simple biomarker like TGI can facilitate early risk stratification, especially in limited resource settings where echocardiography is often unavailable. For patients, early risk identification could translate into earlier appropriate interventions, potentially preventing symptomatic disease.

The absence of such accessible screening tools for subclinical HFpEF in India underscores the need for cost-effective markers. While previous studies have linked the TGI to IR and cardiovascular disease, there remains limited evidence on its utility in predicting subclinical HFpEF in Indian diabetic populations. This study aims to address this research gap by evaluating the diagnostic performance of TGI.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study was a cross-sectional analytical study, conducted in the Department of Medicine, at a tertiary care teaching hospital in North India over a period of 18 months between January 2024 and June 2025. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (DHR Reg No. EC/NEW/Inst/2022/UA/01801, Letter No. AIIMS/IEC/24/36), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study participants

A total of 274 participants aged 18–60 years with T2DM without any history of current heart failure symptoms (NYHA Class I) were recruited. The sample size (n = 254) was calculated assuming a 22% prevalence of heart failure in T2DM,[12] 71% sensitivity, 60% specificity, and 90% confidence interval, using Buderer’s method.[13]

Data collection process

Eligible participants underwent a detailed history, physical examination, and laboratory evaluation. After at least 8 hours of fasting, the peripheral venous blood sample was collected for fasting blood glucose, fasting lipid profile, and HbA1c before administering the morning dose of antidiabetic medication. TGI was calculated using the formula:

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS v30. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as frequencies or percentages. Group differences were analyzed using independent t-tests, ANOVA, and Chi-square tests. Multivariate logistic regression was performed with LVDD as the dependent variable and age, sex, HbA1c, and TGI as independent variables. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to identify an optimal cutoff value as well as determine the overall diagnostic accuracy of TGI for detecting subclinical HFpEF in T2DM patients. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

Prevalence and key findings

The study population had a high prevalence (62.4%) of subclinical HFpEF. The prevalence data for heart failure in diabetes mellitus vary globally. Faden et al.[15] found a prevalence of asymptomatic LVDD in T2DM patients to be 16%. Indian data are also quite variable on the prevalence of subclinical LVDD with the estimated prevalence ranging between 55% and as high as 91.2%.[1617] Direct comparisons with these studies are difficult as different methods have been used to define heart failure in the studies.

The high observed prevalence of subclinical HFpEF in our study may be attributable to several factors. First, our study defined the diagnostic criteria for subclinical HFpEF based on any grade of LVDD on echocardiography, while other studies have used other indicators such as biomarkers of heart failure (BNP/N-terminal proBNP) or composite scores such as HFA-PEFF or H2FPEF. It is possible that our definition captured a broader spectrum of early diastolic abnormalities, potentially including individuals who may not meet stricter criteria for established HFpEF but are nevertheless at risk of or on the trajectory toward it. Second, the population characteristics may have also played a role. Our study population had a mean (SD) HbA1c level of 9.49 (2.56), indicating suboptimal glycemic control, which itself is a strong risk factor for subclinical HFpEF. Additionally, studies focusing on South Asian populations, especially Indian populations, have reported higher rates or earlier onset of cardiovascular complications associated with diabetes.[1819] It may thus be postulated that certain genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors prevalent in the region may contribute to the high burden of early cardiac dysfunction. Irrespective of these precise comparisons, the findings of our study underscore the substantial burden of underlying, asymptomatic cardiac structural and functional changes present in a significant majority of T2DM patients, thus highlighting the critical need for effective screening strategies.

The primary objective of the study was to evaluate the association between TGI and its feasibility as a diagnostic tool for detecting subclinical HFpEF. Our findings demonstrate a strong, significant, and independent association between TGI and subclinical HFpEF. Patients with LVDD exhibited significantly higher mean (9.69 versus 9.23) and median (9.64 versus 9.16) TGI values. Additionally, each unit increase in TGI corresponded to nearly a fivefold increase in the odds of having LVDD (OR 4.76). This association further intensified to almost eightfold upon adjusting for multiple confounders (OR 7.71).

Comparison with previous studies

The findings of our study corroborate with existing literature linking the TGI as a surrogate for insulin resistance, to various facets of cardiovascular disease and specifically to heart failure and its precursors. Wang et al.[20] demonstrated an independent association between TGI and subclinical HFpEF in asymptomatic T2DM patients in China. Albeit, in their study, subclinical HFpEF was defined using the HFA-PEFF score. Their study also found a higher TGI in patients with higher HFA-PEFF scores, representing suspicious and positive HFpEF groups.

The clinical utility of TGI was evaluated by assessing its diagnostic performance. ROC curve analysis yielded an AUC of 0.734 (95% CI: 0.671–0.798, P < 0.001), indicating fair discriminating ability for detecting LVDD. Our result was comparable to values reported in other studies. Wang et al.[20] reported an AUC of 0.706 for TGI predicting subclinical HFpEF. Zhang et al.[21] used multivariate logistic regression models to evaluate the association between TGI and the risk of HF, reporting an AUC of 0.602 for TGI predicting heart failure. Sánchez-Íñigo et al.[22] followed up 5014 patients over a period of 10 years, reporting an AUC of 0.719 for the model utilizing TGI for predicting cardiovascular disease.

We also evaluated the sensitivity and specificity of the TGI and attempted to determine an optimal cutoff from our ROC analysis. The Youden index was calculated at various cutoff values, and the optimal cutoff derived was 9.34. At this threshold, the TGI was found to have a sensitivity of 80.0%, a specificity of 63.3%, a positive predictive value of 76.7%, and a negative predictive value of 67.6%. Our findings indicated a slightly better performance of the index and a higher cutoff value when compared to previous studies. In their study, Wang et al.[20] reported an optimal cutoff of 9.0067 and a sensitivity and specificity of 72.8% and 60.6%, respectively, which may be attributable to the possible variations in genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors prevalent in the different regions.

Clinical implications

At the primary care level and resource-limited settings such as rural India, echocardiography is often not available. The priority in such scenarios is usually to not miss cases. This means we want a high sensitivity and good NPV to confidently rule out the disease in those who test negative. Thus, the cutoff of 8.32 is the best for screening LVDD among diabetic populations [Table 4]. It is generally preferable to overidentify potential cases (and then use a more specific, perhaps more invasive or costly, confirmatory test) rather than missing true cases. The high number of false positives would necessitate further investigation, but the benefit of not missing any true LVDD cases outweighs this drawback at this initial screening stage. Similar proposals for stepwise screening have been advocated in recent global studies as well.[23]

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind to evaluate the clinical utility of TGI in an Indian setting and identifying a cutoff for detection of subclinical heart failure. Strengths of the study include robust exclusion of confounding comorbidities and standardized echocardiographic definitions to clearly define the study population. Limitations include cross-sectional design, single-center setting, and a lack of longitudinal follow-up. Future longitudinal studies would be needed to validate TGI’s predictive value for clinical HFpEF progression in Indian cohorts.

Conclusion

This study provides significant evidence demonstrating a strong, independent association between the TGI and the presence of subclinical HFpEF in asymptomatic patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.

As a simple, inexpensive, and fairly accurate biomarker, the TGI holds promise as a valuable tool for initial screening and risk stratification and facilitating earlier identification of asymptomatic T2DM patients at increased risk of subclinical HFpEF, particularly in resource-limited settings, as is the case across most of India. Future multicentric longitudinal studies are needed to confirm its predictive role and integration into routine diabetic risk assessment.
