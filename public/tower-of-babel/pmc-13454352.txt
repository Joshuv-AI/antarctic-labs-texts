Investigating active dynamics of contractile actomyosin gels with micro particle image velocimetry (micro-PIV) analysis
Authors: Sakshi Choudhary, Subhaya Bose, Yuval Amit, Daniel Sevilla Sanchez, Gefen Livne, Kinjal Dasbiswas, Anne Bernheim-Groswasser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicro-particle image velocimetry (micro-PIV) is a powerful imaging method for resolving flow fields in microscopic systems, but its application to rapidly deforming gels requires key modifications. Here, we adapt micro-PIV to quantify contraction dynamics of active actomyosin gels without invasive tracer beads by tracking local myosin-generated actin inhomogeneities. We show that accurate displacement measurements depend critically on optimizing the time interval over which displacements are computed and the number of frames used in ensemble correlation, relative to the poroelastic timescale and stage of contraction. To assess reliability, we quantify four complementary metrics: displacement vector fields, displacement-magnitude maps, radial profiles of radial and tangential displacement components, and ensemble-averaged orientation with rotational measures. Incorporating the elastic response of the gel, we extract radial strain profiles under an axisymmetric approximation and demonstrate robustness for irregular geometries and off-center contractions. Across conditions, we observe common radial signatures consisting of inward radial contraction with peak displacement at intermediate radii and effective radial stretching near the boundary. These deformation fields provide a basis for inferring spatial and orientational distributions of motor-generated active stresses using appropriate constitutive models. Our approach advances quantitative analysis of active poroelastic materials and has broad applications in biomaterials design, cytoskeletal dynamics, and morphogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54334-1.Keywords: Micro particle image velocimetry, Ensemble correlation, Contractile actomyosin gels, Cytoskeleton, Complex fluid dynamicsSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biophysics, Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Cell shape changes are essential for biological processes such as cell division and cell motility during embryonic development, wound healing and immune responses1. In animal cells, such shape changes are typically driven by the dynamic reorganization of their actomyosin cytoskeleton, a network of actin filaments, myosin molecular motors, and accessory proteins. Myosin motors consume ATP to slide actin filaments according to their polarity2, leading to active contractility and self-organization of the cytoskeletal network3–7. These active and chemo-responsive mechanical forces enable cellular functions such as shape changes and movement8–10, and are an example of active, adaptive gels that exhibit both viscous and elastic response11–13.

However, the complexity of the cytoskeleton, with its interconnected chemical and mechanical processes spanning diverse spatial and temporal scales, poses significant challenges for the complete understanding of its structural changes during cell motility and morphogenesis. In vitro reconstituted systems allow for controllable investigations using a minimal set of components, actin filaments, myosin II motors, ATP, and an actin crosslinker (such as fascin), which together form contractile elastic networks5,7,14–18. These actomyosin gels, which mimic the mechanical properties of the cellular cytoskeleton18–22, provide valuable insight into active force generation and contractility observed in processes such as shape changes, motility, and tissue mechanics23,24. Myosin motor-induced active forces have been shown to drive complex 3D shape changes in active gel sheets18,25, and identifying the spatiotemporal distribution of these forces requires tracking the gel dynamics. Notably, these dynamics exhibit poroelastic effects, where motor-driven contraction of the elastic actin network leads to the outflow of interstitial fluid18, consistent with observations in single cells26 and multi-cellular tissues8,27. Consequently, probing such complex two-phase dynamics requires modifications to conventional methods of tracking microscale flows.

Conventional particle image velocimetry (PIV) is a well-established, non-intrusive technique for measuring velocity fields in macroscopic flows, widely used in turbulence, aerodynamics, and engineering applications28–30. In a typical PIV experiment, the fluid is seeded with tracer particles ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers. A laser sheet illuminates a thin slice of the flow, and high-speed cameras record successive images. By using cross-correlation algorithms on these image pairs, the displacement of the material elements, and thus the displacement vectors, are calculated31. In contrast, micro-PIV extends this methodology to microscale flows using microscope-based imaging and volume illumination enabling measurements in microfluidic and biomedical systems32–34. The need for high spatial resolution dictates that the tracing particles must be small, typically sub-micrometer length scales. For such small particles, noise due to Brownian motion can significantly hinder accurate velocity measurement, making ensemble (multi-image) correlation averaging32,33 frequently used for extracting statistically reliable velocity fields.

Micro-PIV has been widely used to quantify velocity fields in reconstituted actomyosin and active cytoskeletal networks, enabling analysis of contractile flows, spatiotemporal patterns and organization dynamics at various length scales3,14,17,18,35–39. Traction Force Microscopy (TFM) implementations also typically involve a Micro-PIV step to measure the initial material displacement field, from which the force fields can be deduced40. This technique is also useful for measurement of velocity gradients within microchannels and complex microfluidic networks, useful for lab-on-a-chip applications41–43. Understanding these flows is crucial not only for the development and optimization of microscale technologies for biomedical application44,45 but also for resolving the dynamics of complex biological systems, which remain poorly understood.

In this study, Micro-PIV combined with multi-image ensemble correlation was employed to analyse the contraction dynamics of actomyosin gels of controlled composition and elastic properties. We adapt conventional micro-PIV to a system with poroelastic contractile dynamics that significantly reduces in size during the period of observation18,25,46. Prior studies of these actomyosin gels establish that they undergo poroelastic dynamics, where contraction results from solvent efflux through the pores in the elastic actin network18. Unlike a purely viscous fluid, the characteristic timescale of contraction is therefore a stress diffusion time scale that depends on system size and network pore size. Specifically, we show how the micro-PIV analysis parameters pertaining to time interval over which material displacement is considered, and the number of image frames to be correlated need to be optimally chosen in relation to the relevant poroelastic time scale that governs the rate of gel contraction, and the stage of contraction. Instead of placing invasive beads, we track the local inhomogeneities in gel density, caused by self-organization of actin by myosin (actin foci) that appear as bright spots in network fluorescence signal which we utilize for PIV analysis18,39. We extend our analysis of material deformation to non-axisymmetric shapes which advance upon prior work on these systems. Our analysis reveals how circular and non-circular gels share common features in their deformation profiles. Specifically, the gels exhibit an inner region with isotropic contraction and an outer, aligned region with radial stretching. Future studies involving physical modeling of the poroelastic dynamics will reveal the underlying mechanical processes that result in these deformation profiles. The methods reported here can be generally applied to study strongly deforming materials.

Methods

Preparation of contractile poroelastic actomyosin gels

We extend this methodology to explore the material and flow properties of contractile actomyosin gels of various shapes. The gels are formed by mixing 5µM actin monomers, 16.7 nM myosin motors (introduced as large aggregates of ~ 150 myosin dimers each), 280 nM of the passive crosslinker fascin, and 2mM ATP and an ATP regenerating system that maintains a constant ATP level constant18. The protein mixing time defines the experiment starting time \:t=0. A drop of solution is confined between two PEG-passivated glass coverslips. The drop volume and the spacing between the coverslips set the initial actomyosin gel radius \:R18,25,46. Network formation begins with actin filament polymerization and bundling by fascin47, followed by active crosslinking by myosin motors which localize to the bundles’ intersection points (bright actin foci), to create an interconnected active network that dynamically coarsens with time5. This initial forming phase is followed by network contraction, dominated by myosin-induced filament sliding4 as inferred from comparisons between bundle contour lengths and end-to-end distances18,46. These processes can be followed by labelling the actin monomers or the myosin aggregates using fluorescent markers5,18,46. We utilize the local inhomogeneities in gel density (i.e., actin foci) that appear as bright actin spots in network fluorescence signal18,39 for Micro-PIV analysis. The area and mean intensity of the individual actin foci remain grossly unchanged during contraction18, unlike during the initial network forming and coarsening phases5,18.

Image acquisition

Actomyosin gels labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 were imaged 1–2 min after protein mixing using an Olympus IX-71 inverted fluorescence microscope. Imaging was performed at low-magnification to capture the entire gel area throughout contraction while minimizing depth-of-field limitations associated with higher magnifications. Images were acquired with an Andor DV887 EMCCD camera controlled by MetaMorph software in streaming mode at 100 ms per frame. This temporal resolution was sufficient to resolve the dynamics of gel contraction while maintaining adequate signal-to-noise. The excitation intensity was kept as low as possible to prevent signal saturation at later stages of contraction. The effective pixel size at the sample plane was 6.4 μm pixel− 1. A 512 × 512 pixels image encompassing the full gel disc (see for instance the gel shown in Fig. 1) was acquired, while preserving sufficient spatial resolution to resolve actin-density inhomogeneities (actin foci), which serve as tracers for micro-PIV analysis.

Image pre-processing

Raw images were pre-processed in ImageJ/Fiji48 prior to PIV analysis to improve data quality and reliability. Background subtraction was performed using a rolling-ball algorithm (radius = 50 pixels)49 to remove static background signal and enhance contrast of the actin-density features. When necessary, image sequences were stabilized using image registration (StackReg, ImageJ/Fiji)50 to correct for translational drift between frames. These steps ensured that measured displacements reflect gel deformation rather than stage motion. We also evaluated image deconvolution to enhance image sharpness, using PSFGenerator51 and DeconvolutionLab252 in ImageJ/Fiji. However, in our system, deconvolution introduced artifacts in the computed velocity field. Therefore, all results presented in the main text were obtained without deconvolution. Further details are provided in Supplementary Information, Section S1.

Masking of the contracting gel

Following pre-processing, the gel region was isolated by masking in MATLAB (as shown in Fig. 1(c)) prior to PIV analysis53. The mask excludes the surrounding background solution and the drop edge, preventing non-relevant regions from contributing to the correlation calculation. Importantly, the mask was updated dynamically to follow the contracting gel boundary, defined by the instantaneous gel radius . This adaptive masking ensures that the computed displacement and velocity fields correspond exclusively to the deforming gel throughout the contraction process. The procedure used to adapt the mask to the changing gel area, along with representative MATLAB code snippets, is described in Supplementary Information, Section S2. The resulting masked image sequences were then used as input for the main PIV analysis pipeline.

Workflow of micro-PIV analysis

PIV analysis was performed on the masked image sequence in MATLAB using ensemble correlation in PIVLab53 to estimate local displacement vectors of actin-density features (bright actin spots) across selected image pairs. From the resulting displacement fields (Fig. 1d), velocity fields were computed over a chosen time interval , and then the local deformation field (strain rate, Fig. 1e) was derived from the Jacobian of the velocity field. Further implementation details of the PIV analysis are provided in Supplementary Information, Section S3.

Adaptive ensemble-correlation Micro-PIV for shrinking poroelastic contractile gels

To improve the accuracy of displacement estimation in the presence of noise and evolving structure, we employed ensemble correlation across multiple image pairs, enhancing signal-to-noise and stabilizing displacement estimates32,33. The number of image pairs being correlated, \:N, the time interval between successive image pairs, and the initial frame from which correlation begins, , all play significant roles in determining the reliability of the correlation and of the resulting velocity data.

Adapting the interrogation windows size for ensemble correlation

To resolve deformation across spatial scales while maintaining robust correlation, we implemented a three-pass interrogation scheme with progressively smaller windows and 66% overlap between adjacent windows (step size equal to 1/3 the window size)53, ensuring smooth spatial sampling and reducing discretization artifacts. Crucially, interrogation window sizes were defined relative to the instantaneous gel radius , determined from the automated masking procedure, allowing the analysis to adapt to the shrinking domain while maintaining a consistent spatial resolution relative to gel size. This yields a balance between spatial resolution and vector density, constrained by the need to preserve reliable correlation while resolving deformation at the scale of the gel. Further implementation details of the PIV analysis are provided in Supplementary Information, Section S4.

Displacement and strain analysis between frames

To quantify material deformation, we compute strain between consecutive frames. This is equivalent to calculating the strain rate times The actomyosin gel is a poroelastic material comprising an elastic network permeated with solvent. The timescale over which the gel displacement vectors are measured in our experiments range between (for of \:2-10)\: frames) which is much shorter than the poroelastic stress relaxation time scale given by the timescale of contraction of the gel disk which is in the order of tens of seconds. The gel deformation between two frames is thus small, and can be described by the linearized strain rate tensor, constructed from the displacements measured in PIV54. The displacement vectors obtained from micro-PIV analysis are located at regularly spaced lattice points (Fig. 2), from which the gel strain can be calculated using finite differences.

Obtaining radial profiles in displacement by azimuthal averaging

The gel in Fig. 1 which we analyze here appears to contract towards the centroid with all displacement vectors oriented radially. Hence, we expect the local azimuthal displacements to be very small and can approximate the gel contraction to be axisymmetric. We consider circular annuli of equal thickness, from the gel boundary to the centre (a total of 25 annular bins) (Fig. 2). The bin width is calculated at every frame and is given by In each of these annular bins, there are multiple PIV lattice points. We calculate the mean and standard deviation of the radial and azimuthal displacements in each of these annular bins. To validate our assumption of axisymmetric contraction, we consider azimuthal displacement as a function of the normalized distance from the centre of the gel (see below Figs 6, 9, 11). It fluctuates close to zero, suggesting no apparent average azimuthal displacement, consistent with axisymmetric contraction.

Results

We now present our key results from analyzing the spatio-temporal contraction dynamics of poroelastic actomyosin gels using the micro-PIV methods described above.

Gel contraction dynamics

Figure 3 depicts the characteristic time-dependent gel edge radial velocity of the actomyosin gel depicted in Fig. 1. The corresponding radial contraction velocity indicates an initial rapid contraction, characterized by a linear increase in contraction velocity up to maximal value . This initial linear phase is followed by an exponential decay phase, , where τ is the characteristic relaxation time18. This relaxation time was shown to be proportional to an effective friction constant, inversely proportional to the gel elastic modulus, and to depend on system size18,26,56. This is consistent with poroelastic models that account for the permeation of water through the actin gel pores. At the end of gel contraction, the gel reaches a mechanically stable state where the active contractile stresses exerted by myosin motors in the gel are balanced by the elastic stresses developed due to gel deformation18.

Quantitative analysis for micro-PIV parameter selection: number of correlated image pairs and frame interval

The number of image pairs \:N is a crucial parameter as it directly impacts the statistical reliability and accuracy of velocity measurements. Specifically, for steady flows, it is expected that increasing the number of image pairs for a given frame interval will reduce random errors and enhance measurement accuracy32,33. However, for strongly contracting gels where velocities change significantly and nonlinearly with time (Fig. 3), the choice of optimal \:N at given is not straightforward and should depend on the stage of contraction, at which velocities are being measured. Overall, the total time over which image pairs are correlated, , should be smaller than the relevant physical timescale over which the material velocities significantly change. In the following results, we use several quantitative metrics to establish the optimal choice of these parameters.

Optimizing image pre-processing for micro-PIV analysis

Before examining the effects of the values of \:N and , we first evaluated how different pre-processing steps affected the accuracy and stability of the displacement fields. We compared raw, background-corrected, and deconvolved images by examining correlation peak quality, vector yield, and the spatial consistency of the resulting displacement fields (Fig. 4a-c) and rotational flows (vorticity) (Fig. 4e-f). Raw images showed uneven illumination and residual background structures, which weakened the correlation peaks and led to noisy or missing displacement vectors. Deconvolution improved visual sharpness but also introduced artifacts, such as unusually small displacement vectors of material points within the bulk and a few large, randomly oriented displacement vectors near the boundary leading to artificially large vorticity (Fig. 4f). In contrast, background-corrected images without deconvolution generated the most spatially coherent displacement fields, which are primarily radial14,17,18, as supported by the absence of rotational flows (Fig. 4e), and which we use in all subsequent PIV analysis.

Strain analysis as measure of gel deformation in time

After optimizing the micro-PIV process, we now investigate how the displacement data can be used to obtain insights into the material deformation. The physical quantity that describes deformation is the strain or strain rate, which is obtained from spatial gradients of the displacement or velocity, respectively. It is natural to ask how the strain values are distributed in space, i.e. at different positions within the gel, and how they are oriented, i.e. along radial and azimuthal directions. The methods for calculating the components of the strain from the displacement data are detailed in SI Supplementary Note 5. Importantly, we consider small strains occurring over one frame interval ( frame) and so these values are just the strain rate times .

Bulk or area strain maps as a measure of local contraction

The representative 2D heatmap of bulk or area strain (shown in Fig. 12) is obtained by tracing filled contour plots of the trace of the strain tensor ( + ) at each lattice point. The latter are computed by taking finite differences of the PIV displacement vectors on the lattice shown. This area measures the net area deformation (contraction or expansion) at a given location in the gel. Positive (negative) values correspond to expanding (contracting) regions. In Fig. 12, we show both the strain values directly (Fig. 12c), as well as after normalizing each individual positive or negative area strain by the maximum positive and negative values in the whole field of view at that specific time. The former shows that strain values are typically much smaller than unity, justifying the linearized strain calculation. The latter emphasizes common features between strain maps at different times in the contraction. Analyzing gels with non-axisymmetric displacement field

Some gels exhibit rigid body motion, that is displacement of their center of mass, as they contract. The displacement of the gel is no longer axisymmetric about its center of mass which we can verify by constructing concentric annuli around this point. We see for example in Fig. 14c-d that some of the displacement vectors are not oriented radially inward. We can instead identify an apparent centre of displacement (CoD) for such gels from the displacement vectors and estimate the radial profiles of displacements and strains with respect to this special point (CoD). We note that all deformation of the gel is encoded in the strains, which are gradients of the displacement. The displacement field, by itself, does not provide any fundamental insights into gel contraction. Nonetheless, by subtracting the rigid body or center of mass (CoM) motion, and subsequent averaging, we show that we recover universal trends in the radial gel displacement profiles. In general, it is possible that a gel also undergoes some rigid body rotation, but this is not the case in our gels where the tangential displacement is negligible.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we establish and validate a methodology for adapting conventional micro-PIV to the dynamics of strongly contractile active poroelastic gels. A central advance is the identification of optimal analysis parameters, specifically the time interval over which displacements are computed and the number of image frames included in ensemble correlation, which must be chosen in relation to the poroelastic timescale of contraction and its stage18,25–27,46,56. We further demonstrate that reliable displacement fields can be obtained without invasive tracers by tracking local myosin-generated actin inhomogeneities. The robustness of the inferred fields is assessed using multiple quantitative metrics, including displacement vector fields, displacement-magnitude maps, radial profiles of radial and tangential displacement components, and ensemble-averaged orientation and rotational measures.

Analysis of strain fields and radial profiles reveals consistent features across gels of diverse geometries, including isotropic contraction in the interior, peak inward displacement at intermediate radii, suppressed motion near boundaries, and azimuthally aligned principal strains at the edge, consistent with actin fiber alignment observed in fluorescence microscopy25. Importantly, the approach remains effective even for non-axisymmetric and irregularly shaped gels. While micro-PIV is commonly used to quantify local velocity and contraction rates in cytoskeletal networks3,14,17,18,35–39, many prior studies rely on correlation between single image pairs. In contrast, our use of ensemble correlation across multiple image pairs enables robust extraction of global displacement fields in rapidly contracting gels.

Several limitations should be noted. The optimization of micro-PIV parameters must be performed separately for each gel, as contraction dynamics can vary qualitatively across samples. In fast-contracting gels, the number of frames that can be correlated to reduce noise is inherently limited, whereas slower-contracting gels exhibit higher intrinsic noise levels. Furthermore, our analysis is based on two-dimensional displacement fields obtained from fluorescence imaging; however, this approximation is appropriate for our system, as significant out-of-plane buckling occurs only at later stages of contraction18,25.

Future work will extend the analysis to multiple gels that lead to diverse three-dimensional shapes25. It will leverage the strain inhomogeneities quantified here during early and intermediate stages of contraction to inform three-dimensional mechanical models that describe out-of-plane deformation driven by in-plane stress imbalances. In summary, this research highlights the necessity of modified micro-PIV approaches for studying complex fluids, specifically poroelastic active materials with large, autonomous contraction at the microscopic scale, and advances our understanding of cytoskeletal dynamics and self-organization. While this work is in vitro on a purified protein system, it shows how aligned structures may arise spontaneously through the interplay of active contractility and poroelasticity also in cytoskeletal or tissue systems. The findings have broad implications for biomaterials design, cell motility, morphogenesis, and active matter physics.
