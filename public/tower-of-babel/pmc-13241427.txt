Insights into the mitochondrial genomes of Taenia tapeworms
Authors: Abraham Landa, Ricardo Miranda-Blancas, Elisa Heredia-Gómez, Laura A Velázquez-Villegas, Oscar Rodríguez-Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe genus Taenia belongs to the class Cestoda and comprises a diverse group of species of medical, veterinary, and ecological importance. These tapeworms exhibit complex life cycles involving carnivorous definitive hosts and herbivorous or omnivorous intermediate hosts. Taenia solium, T. saginata, and T. asiatica are particularly important due to their impact on human health, notably through taeniasis and cysticercosis. Mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) have emerged as powerful tools for taxonomy, diagnostics, and evolutionary studies of these parasites. Complete mitogenome sequences are available for several Taenia species, revealing conserved structures and a compact genome, including 12 protein-coding genes, 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs, along with species-specific variations in initiation and termination codons. Beyond taxonomy, mitochondrial targets offer a promising avenue for antiparasitic drug development, especially genes related to oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis.Keywords: Taenia spp., Mitochondrial genomes, Cestodes, Neglected tropical diseases, Tapeworm

Introduction

Neglected tropical diseases comprise a group of infections closely associated with social and economic conditions in developing countries, particularly affecting impoverished populations in tropical regions [1]. These diseases are caused by a wide range of pathogens, including viruses [2], bacteria [3], and parasites [4], such as those of the Taenia genus [5].

Taenia spp. can cause taeniasis or cysticercosis depending on the stage of their life cycle, infecting various hosts such as felids [6], canids [7], and humans [8]. These organisms may serve as intermediate or definitive hosts within the complex ecological interactions and biological cycles characteristic of these parasites [9]. As a result, infections caused by Taenia spp. represent an important yet often overlooked public health problem. Their impact is especially significant in developing regions with poor sanitation, where they also affect livestock and companion animals, generating economic losses and zoonotic risks [10].

A better understanding of Taenia spp. biology, combined with effective sanitary measures, is essential for improving diagnosis, treatment, and zoonotic control in endemic areas [11–13]. In this context, the present study aims to analyze mitochondrial genome (mitogenome) characteristics and differences among Taenia species, providing new insights into their taxonomy and contributing to the identification of potential mitochondrial targets for drug development.

Taenia spp

The genus Taenia comprises a diverse group of parasitic flatworms within the family Taeniidae. A recent review identified 44 reported species of Taenia [11]. These tapeworms exhibit complex life cycles involving definitive hosts—typically carnivorous or omnivorous mammals, including humans—and intermediate hosts, commonly herbivorous or omnivorous animals [12]. Their broad host range and adaptability reflect a high degree of evolutionary success and ecological versatility. Adult worms inhabit the small intestine of definitive hosts, where they can reach several meters in length and produce numerous gravid proglottids containing infective eggs capable of surviving in the environment for extended periods.

Several species within the genus, including Taenia solium, Taenia saginata, Taenia asiatica, and Taenia multiceps, have significant medical, veterinary, and ecological importance (Table 1). These parasites infect both humans and animals, affecting public health, food safety, and livestock production systems [13, 14].

The global distribution of Taenia spp. is influenced by dietary habits, livestock management practices, and sanitary conditions. Effective control requires a One Health approach that integrates human medicine, veterinary surveillance, and public health education to disrupt transmission cycles and reduce the burden of disease [15, 16].

Medical importance

Among Taenia species, T. solium, T. saginata, and T. asiatica are of major medical importance because they infect humans (Table 1). T. solium is particularly significant since it can cause both intestinal taeniasis in humans and cysticercosis in pigs and humans. Cysticercosis occurs after ingestion of T. solium eggs, leading to the development of larval cysts (cysticerci) in various tissues, especially in the central nervous system. This condition results in neurocysticercosis, a major cause of adult-onset epilepsy in endemic regions [17].

In contrast, T. saginata and T. asiatica cause only taeniasis, which is frequently asymptomatic or associated with mild symptoms but remains relevant from a public health perspective due to its contribution to parasite transmission [18]. Although morphologically similar to T. saginata, T. asiatica differs in its tissue tropism in the intermediate host (cattle and pigs, respectively) and its geographic distribution, being mainly confined to East and Southeast Asia [14]. Because these species share strong morphological similarities, molecular methods are often required for accurate differentiation [19].

These species also exhibit similar indirect life cycles involving humans as definitive hosts and mammals as intermediate hosts. Adult tapeworms reside in the human small intestine, producing gravid proglottids that release infective eggs into the environment through the feces. Intermediate hosts become infected by ingesting these eggs in contaminated food or water. Cattle serve as the main intermediate host for T. saginata, whereas pigs are the intermediate hosts for T. solium and T. asiatica. After ingestion, oncospheres hatch in the intestine, penetrate the intestinal wall, and migrate via the bloodstream to different tissues where they develop into cysticerci. In T. saginata and T. solium, these larvae are mainly located in skeletal muscle, while T. asiatica shows a preference for visceral tissues, particularly the liver. Humans acquire infection by consuming raw or undercooked meat or viscera containing viable cysticerci, which develop into adult tapeworms in the intestine. Notably, T. solium poses an additional health risk because humans can also act as accidental intermediate hosts after ingesting parasite eggs, resulting in cysticercosis.

Veterinary impact

The veterinary importance of Taenia spp. extends beyond animal health, as these parasites generate substantial economic losses in livestock production and represent a persistent challenge for food safety and zoonotic disease control (Table 1). Infected animals frequently exhibit reduced productivity, lower commercial value, and organ condemnation during meat inspection, directly affecting the livestock industry [20, 21].

For instance, Taenia hydatigena and T. multiceps utilize canids (primarily dogs) as definitive hosts, and intermediate hosts including sheep, goats and pigs [22, 23]. T. hydatigena causes hepatic cysticercosis, leading to liver damage and organ condemnation at slaughter, thereby reducing meat market value [20, 24]. T. multiceps is the causative agent of coenurosis, a severe neurological disease in sheep and goats, resulting from larval migration to the central nervous system [22]. Clinical signs include ataxia, depression, and often death, causing major losses in small ruminant farming. Taenia pisiformis, cycling between canids and lagomorphs (rabbits, hares), poses concerns in both commercial and companion animal contexts [25, 26].

The close interaction among domestic animals, wildlife, and humans facilitates parasite transmission and maintenance in endemic areas. Therefore, understanding the biology, transmission dynamics, and host associations of Taenia spp. is essential for improving veterinary surveillance, implementing effective control strategies, and reducing the economic and public health burden associated with taeniid infections.

Ecological relevance

From an ecological standpoint, Taenia spp. exemplify complex host-parasite dynamics, involving predator-prey interactions and biodiversity maintenance [6]. Understanding these cycles is crucial for wildlife management and zoonotic risk assessments (Table 1).

Some Taenia spp. illustrate the ecological significance of this genus across different biomes and host systems. Taenia crassiceps, for example, circulates primarily in sylvatic cycles involving canids as definitive hosts and rodents as intermediate hosts. This species is particularly notable for its capacity for asexual proliferation in the larval stage, which can lead to high parasite burdens and significant impacts on rodent population dynamics, with potential cascading effects on predator–prey interactions [27].

The recently described species Taenia tianguangfui and Taenia caixuepengi have been identified in the intermediate hosts Neodon fuscus and Ochotona curzoniae (respectively) in China, indicating that the genus’s true diversity is yet to be fully characterized. Mitogenome analyses have confirmed the validity of these two new Taenia species and provided insights into their evolutionary origins [28].

In northern ecosystems, species such as Taenia arctos [29, 30] and Taenia laticollis [31, 32] are associated with wildlife hosts including large carnivores, reflecting adaptation to colder environments and distinct biogeographic histories. These species provide valuable models for understanding how environmental conditions and host availability shape parasite distribution and evolution. Collectively, these examples demonstrate that Taenia spp. are not only taxonomically diverse but also ecologically versatile, occupying a wide range of niches and contributing to the complexity of host–parasite interactions across ecosystems [33].

Taxonomic considerations

The identification and differentiation of Taenia species have evolved from purely morphological methods to advanced molecular techniques. Mitochondrial genes, such as Cox1 and Nad1, have proven effective for phylogenetic studies and species delimitation [34]. These methods are crucial not only for taxonomy but also for diagnostics, epidemiology, and control strategies.

Molecular techniques have clarified distinctions between cryptic species like T. saginata and T. asiatica, which are morphologically similar but genetically distinct [35]. Additionally, genetic analyses have facilitated the discovery and characterization of new Taenia species from wild animals, enriching our understanding of parasitic biodiversity [28].

Mitochondrial markers have become widely used in the taxonomy and phylogenetic analysis of different species due to their relatively high mutation rate, lack of recombination, and the availability of complete mitogenome sequences for many species [36]. However, several limitations should also be considered when interpreting mitochondrial data for species delimitation [37]. Because mitochondrial DNA is maternally inherited, it represents only a single genealogical history corresponding to the maternal lineage and therefore reflects the time since the most recent common female ancestor rather than the full evolutionary history of the species [38]. As a consequence, mitochondrial divergence does not necessarily correspond directly to speciation events [39]. In addition, as observed in many animal taxa, the range of intraspecific mitochondrial variation may overlap with the divergence observed between very closely related species [40]. This overlap can complicate the distinction between conspecific isolates and members of closely related taxa when mitochondrial markers are used alone [41]. For these reasons, mitochondrial markers are most informative when interpreted in combination with additional lines of evidence, including nuclear genetic markers, host associations, morphology, and life-cycle characteristics [42].

Mitogenomes

Mitogenomes are a distinct and intriguing subset of an organism’s genetic material, separate from the nuclear genome found in the cell’s nucleus. They are essential components of eukaryotic cells, including those in humans, animals, plants, and fungi. Mitogenomes consist of small, circular, double-stranded DNA molecules. Each mitochondrion within a cell can contain multiple copies of this circular genome [43]. One of the most intriguing features of mitogenomes is their mode of inheritance. In sexually reproducing organisms like humans, mitochondrial DNA is typically inherited solely from the mother. During fertilization, the sperm usually contributes no mitochondria to the offspring, making the mitogenome a maternal genetic marker often used in genetic and anthropological research [44].

Mitochondria are commonly referred to as the “powerhouses” of the cell because they are responsible for producing adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the cell’s primary energy currency. Mitogenomes encode a small set of essential genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and the electron transport chain, which are crucial for ATP production [45]. Mitogenomes are highly susceptible to mutations due to their proximity to the oxidative processes occurring within mitochondria. These mutations can accumulate over time, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, energy production deficits, and various health disorders [46]. Some well-known mitochondrial diseases result from such mutations. Mitochondrial DNA is often used for identifying individuals in forensic investigations, tracing maternal lineages in human populations, and shedding light on human migration and evolution.

The mitogenomes of Taenia spp. range from 13,387 to 13,747 bp and contain 22 tRNAs (tRNATyr, tRNALeu(CUN), tRNASer(UCN), tRNALeu(UUR), tRNAArg, tRNAGly, tRNAHis, tRNAGln, tRNAPhe, tRNAMet, tRNAVal, tRNAAla, tRNAAsp, tRNAAsn, tRNAPro, tRNAIle, tRNALeu, tRNASer(AGN), tRNATrp, tRNAThr, tRNACys, tRNAGlu), 2 rRNAs (srRNA and lrRNA), 12 protein-coding genes (Atp6, Cytb, Nad4L, Cox1–3 and Nad1–6), a short non-coding region (SNR, between tRNATyr and tRNALeu(CUN) genes) and a long non-coding region (LNR, between Nad5 and tRNAGly genes), but lack the Atp8 gene as usual in cestode mitogenomes (Fig. 1). All genes are encoded in the same direction, have no introns and in some cases, there is gene overlap (for example, in T. asiatica and T. saginata, the Nad4L and Nad4 genes overlap). Also, there is a bias towards A-T content in all Taenia species, ranging from 70.9% to 74.2%.

Mitogenome map of Taenia spp. showing size and organization

Most of the protein-coding genes have an AUG initiation codon (Tso: 92%, Tmu: 83%, Thy: 83%, Tpi: 83%, Tas: 100%, Tsa: 83%, Tta: 92%, Tca: 75%, Tcr: 100%, Tse: 100%, Tov: 100%, Tma: 100%, Tla: 100%, Tmr: 100%, Ttw: 100%, Tcro: 100%, Tar: 100%, and Tre: 100%). However, several alternative initiation codons are also present. The GUG codon occurs as an alternative start codon in T. solium Nad4; T. multiceps Nad2 and Nad6; T. hydatigena Cox3 and Nad3; T. pisiformis Cox3; T. saginata Nad4 and Atp6; T. tianguangfui Nad5; and T. caixuepengi Cox3 and Nad3. The uncommon GUU initiation codon has been observed in T. pisiformis Atp6, similar to E. granulosus [57] and Hymenolepis diminuta Cox3 [58]. Also, the uncommon AUU initiation codon was observed in T. caixuepengi Atp6, previously detected in Ascaris suum and Caenorhabditis elegans [59]. On the other hand, the most common termination codon is UAA (Tso: 42%, Tmu: 75%, Thy: 75%, Tpi: 58%, Tas: 67%, Tsa: 50%, Tta: 50%, Tca: 83%, Tcr: 75%, Tse: 75%, Tov: 75%, Tma: 75%, Tla: 75%, Tmr: 75%, Ttw: 67%, Tcro: 67%, Tar: 67%, and Tre: 67%), followed by UAG (Tso: 50%, Tmu: 25%, Thy: 25%, Tpi: 25%, Tas: 42%, Tsa: 33%, Tta: 50%, Tca: 17%, Tcr: 25%, Tse: 25%, Tov: 25%, Tma: 25%, Tla: 25%, Tmr: 25%, Ttw: 33%, Tcro: 33%, Tar: 33%, and Tre: 33%). Interestingly, an abbreviated U termination codon has been found in T. solium Nad1, T. pisiformis Cox3 and Nad3, where the complete UAA termination codon is created post-transcriptionally by polyadenylation [47, 60]. An in-depth analysis in cestode mitogenome genetic code shows that AUA codon, which in mitochondria of other metazoans encodes a methionine, in cestodes corresponds to an isoleucine. AAA corresponds to asparagine in cestodes, instead of lysine as observed in mitochondria in other organisms and in the universal nuclear code. UGA specifies tryptophan (together to UGG) as it does in mitochondria of other organisms and not stop as in the universal nuclear code. UAA is used as stop codon in cestodes, as it is in most other organisms, but it encodes tyrosine in planarian organisms [61, 62].

The mitogenomes of Taenia spp. encode the canonical oxidative phosphorylation subunits constituting the catalytic cores of complexes I, III, IV, and V, which are embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane [51]. These complexes exhibit strong evolutionary conservation, reflecting the stringent structural and functional constraints imposed by proton translocation and electron transfer mechanisms that depend on precise residue geometry and multisubunit coordination [63]. Among these, COX1 and NAD1 display exceptionally high levels of selective pressure across taeniid lineages, consistent with their essential roles in the catalytic architecture of complexes IV and I, respectively [64, 65]. The slow evolutionary rate of Cox1, in comparison with other mitochondrial genes, renders it one of the most robust mitochondrial loci for species discrimination and for resolving higher-level phylogenetic relationships within the genus Taenia and related cestodes (Fig. 2) [66].

Intraspecific genetic variation in the mitochondrial genes of Taenia spp. displays contrasting patterns across species. While T. asiatica exhibits minimal diversity (0-0.2%) in Cox1, T. saginata shows a higher degree of polymorphism (0.2–0.8%) [49, 67]. In T. solium, variability is characterized by clean geographic stratification, differentiating Asian and African-American genotypes through markers such as Cox1 and Cytb [68, 69]. On the other hand, species like T. multiceps and T. hydatigena reveal high haplotype richness combined with low nucleotide diversity, suggesting recent population expansion or selective pressure [70–72].

Mitochondrial sequences can be analyzed at both the nucleotide and amino acid levels for phylogenetic reconstruction, and each approach has particular advantages [73, 74]. Analyses based on nucleotide sequences often provide greater phylogenetic resolution among closely related taxa because they retain more information about synonymous substitutions [75, 76]. However, nucleotide sequences may also be more affected by substitution saturation and compositional bias, particularly in mitogenomes with strong A + T bias such as those of cestodes [77]. In contrast, analyses based on inferred protein sequences are generally more conserved and can reduce the impact of synonymous substitutions and nucleotide compositional bias, which may improve phylogenetic inference at deeper evolutionary levels [73]. For this reason, many phylogenetic studies of cestode mitogenomes employ both nucleotide and amino acid datasets to evaluate the robustness of inferred relationships [75, 78].

The mitogenome of Taenia spp. contains 22 tRNAs, of which 18 adopt the typical clover-leaf shape with paired DHU arms. Nonetheless, tRNAArg, tRNACys, tRNASer(UCN) and tRNASer(AGN) have unpaired DHU arms of 7–16 bp length. The prediction also shows that 21 tRNAs have 5-bp anticodon stem and only tRNAAsn has a 4-bp anticodon stem [50, 51, 53, 61, 79].

Recently, Jiménez-Avalos et al. [68] identified specific mitochondrial point mutations that consistently differentiate the T. solium African-American genotype from the Asian genotype, and explored whether the African-American lineage is itself composed of distinct genetic subgroups or sublineages that had not yet been fully recognized. The study identified a total of 31 novel non-synonymous point mutations that were consistently present across all the analyzed African-American isolates and that differentiated them from the Asian genotype. These mutations were distributed across multiple mitochondrial genes, specifically Atp6, Cox1, Cox2, Cox3, Cytb, Nad2, Nad4, Nad4L, Nad5, and Nad6. Remarkably, none of these mutations appeared to affect highly conserved regions of the mitochondrial proteins. The authors suggested that these mutations likely have minimal or no detrimental impact on protein structure or function. This indicates that the mitochondrial variations observed may be the result of neutral genetic drift rather than adaptive evolution [68].

While significant strides have been made in understanding these genomes, there are still gaps, particularly regarding the functional aspects of non-coding regions. Since many Taenia species are parasitic and of medical and veterinary importance, understanding their genetics is crucial for developing effective control measures. Continued exploration of mitochondrial genomes is necessary, particularly in the context of their role in parasite biology and host-parasite interactions.

Mitochondrial targets studied in other parasites for therapy development

Mitochondrial proteins remain an underexplored yet promising class of drug targets in helminths, with several studies highlighting vulnerabilities in complexes of the electron transport chain (ETC). Atovaquone, a ubiquinone analog, has shown efficacy against Echinococcus multilocularis primary cysts through inhibition of CYTB (complex III), underscoring the feasibility of targeting mitochondrial respiration in cestodes [80]. Similarly, nafuredin, a natural product isolated from Aspergillus, selectively inhibits complex I in helminth mitochondria, confirming the differential sensitivity of helminth NADH dehydrogenase compared to mammalian counterparts [81]. More recently, rationally designed endochin-like quinolones and combination approaches have been explored to simultaneously inhibit ETC complexes and mitochondrial dehydrogenases, enhancing efficacy and reducing the likelihood of single-target resistance [82].

Population genetic surveys in Echinococcus granulosus have revealed variability in the mitochondrial Cytb and Nad5 genes, documenting polymorphisms that may provide raw material for selection under drug pressure, though no causal resistance mutations have yet been functionally validated [83]. This lack of direct evidence contrasts with apicomplexan parasites, where Cytb point mutations in the Qo pocket, notably Y268S in Plasmodium falciparum, are well-established determinants of high-level atovaquone resistance [84]. Similarly, Eimeria tenella develops resistance to decoquinate via distinct non-synonymous mutations in Cytb, further supporting the notion that ETC inhibitors are prone to resistance via single amino acid changes at inhibitor-binding sites [85].

Beyond Cytb, other mitochondrial loci have received limited attention in helminths, though their homologs are drug-sensitive in related taxa. Mutations in Atp6 and Nad genes have been implicated in resistance phenotypes in protozoa and arthropods, suggesting that selective pressure in helminths could yield parallel adaptations. While niclosamide, an anthelmintic with pleiotropic effects, disrupts oxidative phosphorylation and has been repurposed for diverse biomedical applications [86], its precise mitochondrial targets in helminths remain to be clarified. Taken together, these studies point toward a need for systematic surveys of mitochondrial sequence variation across drug-exposed helminth populations to elucidate unknown aspects of mitochondrial druggability and its potential as a drug target.

Exploiting comparative genomics of mitochondrial targets for novel Taenia therapies

Looking ahead, the exploration of mitochondrial targets in Taenia spp. offers a promising and largely untapped avenue for antiparasitic drug development. Future research should prioritize the functional characterization of taxon-specific sequence variants of mitochondrial genes, particularly those involved in oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis, since mitochondrial metabolism represents an attractive drug target. Comparative mitochondrial genomics among Taenia species could reveal conserved elements that serve as broad-spectrum drug targets, as well as species-specific variations that might be exploited for precision therapeutics. For example, mitochondrial protein-coding genes such as Cox1, Nad1, and Cytb are highly conserved across cestodes, reflecting strong functional constraints and making them potential universal drug targets, whereas other genes such as Nad2, Atp6, or Nad6 exhibit higher variability and may provide species-specific signatures for targeted interventions [77]. Additionally, studies on cyclophyllidean tapeworms have shown that mitochondrial genes can provide high-resolution among closely related taxa, supporting their use in identifying lineage-specific molecular differences relevant for selective targeting [75].

Integrating bioinformatics, structural biology, and high-throughput screening will be key to identifying lead compounds that selectively disrupt mitochondrial function without harming host cells. For instance, comparative mitogenomic analyses combined with phylogenetic methods have revealed that nucleotide composition bias and codon usage differences can affect protein structure and function, highlighting the importance of integrating computational and evolutionary analyses when identifying druggable targets [73, 74]. Moreover, mitochondrial functional profiling has been proposed as a tool to evaluate bioactive compounds and their selective biological effects, supporting its application in drug discovery pipelines [87].

Additionally, studies should investigate the mechanisms of drug delivery to mitochondrial compartments and assess the potential for resistance development. Mitogenomes in parasitic helminths often exhibit strong nucleotide biases and rapid evolutionary changes, which can influence drug sensitivity and facilitate adaptive responses, underscoring the importance of evaluating resistance mechanisms [78, 88]. These findings highlight that both conserved mitochondrial functions and lineage-specific adaptations must be considered when designing effective and durable antiparasitic therapies. Establishing robust in vitro and in vivo models for evaluating mitochondrial inhibitors will also be essential. As these efforts progress, mitochondrial biology in Taenia spp. could shift from being a subject of descriptive study to a foundation for rational drug design, opening new frontiers in the control of taeniasis and cysticercosis.

Concluding remarks

Mitogenome analyses of Taenia spp. provide critical insights into parasite bioenergetics, evolutionary adaptation, and molecular vulnerabilities. The compact architecture of the Taenia mitogenome, encompassing protein-coding genes such as Cox1–3, Cytb, Atp6, and Nad1–6, encodes essential components of the oxidative phosphorylation machinery that are increasingly recognized as druggable targets. Comparative studies across helminths and protozoan parasites demonstrate that mutations in CYTB or NAD subunits can confer resistance to ETC inhibitors, underscoring the translational relevance of mitochondrial genomics in anticipating resistance mechanisms. For Taenia spp., systematic characterization of sequence polymorphisms, structural modeling of mitogenome-encoded proteins, and functional validation of putative resistance-conferring variants will be essential to establish causality and guide rational drug development. Ultimately, integrating high-resolution mitogenome datasets with biochemical and pharmacological approaches promises to accelerate the identification of selective inhibitors targeting Taenia spp. mitochondria, thereby bridging fundamental mitochondrial genomics with translational anthelmintic discovery.
