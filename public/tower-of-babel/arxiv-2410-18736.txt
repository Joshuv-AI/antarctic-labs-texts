Error convergence of quantum linear system solvers
Matias Ginzburg, Ugo Marzolino

Abstract
We analyze the performance of the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd algorithm (HHL algorithm) for solving linear problems and of a variant of this algorithm (HHL variant) commonly encountered in literature. This variant relieves the algorithm of preparing an entangled initial state of an auxiliary register. We prove that the computational error of the variant algorithm does not always converge to zero when the number of qubits is increased, unlike the original HHL algorithm. Both algorithms rely upon two fundamental quantum algorithms, the quantum phase estimation and the amplitude amplification. In particular, the error of the HHL variant oscillates due to the presence of undesired phases in the amplitude to be amplified, while these oscillations are suppressed in the original HHL algorithm. Then, we propose a modification of the HHL variant, by amplifying an amplitude of the state vector that does not exhibit the above destructive interference. We also study the complexity of these algorithms in the light of recent results on simulation of unitaries used in the quantum phase estimation step, and show that the modified algorithm has smaller error and lower complexity than the HHL variant. We supported our findings with numerical simulations.

Introduction

Quantum computers are machines made of physical systems ruled by the laws of quantum mechanics. The possibility to explore the whole Hilbert space allows for computational improvements over known classical algorithm
[
55
,
9
]
.
It is conjectured that quantum algorithms can solve in polynomial time (with respect to the problem size) problems than cannot be solved polynomially by classical algorithms, like the factorization problem
[
65
]
, and even problems whose solutions are not thought to be polynomially verifiable by classical computers, like the boson sampling problem
[

[
70
,
5

Among computational problems that are ubiquitous in computer science and big data
[
64
,
30
]
and attract attention in quantum computation,
there are solutions of linear systems.
The inputs of a linear problem are a vector

with components

satisfying

(1)

In the following analysis, we consider
s

s
of non-vanishing elements in each row or column, and define the condition number as
\kappa=\max_{j}|\lambda_{j}|/\min_{j}|\lambda_{j}|
.

The understanding of the potentialities of quantum algorithms for linear problems is then of great importance for several potential applications.
The first quantum algorithm for linear systems was derived by Harrow, Hassidim and Lloyd
[

(henceforth HHL algorithm).
The HHL algorithm exploits two fundamental subroutines, namely the quantum phase estimation

,

,

,

[

]
.

In literature, the HHL algorithm is sometimes applied using a simpler, fully factorized initial state of an auxiliary quantum register
[
23
,
7
,
56
,
27
,
29
,
53
]
.
This simplification allows to reduce the complexity of the initial state preparation that is entangled in the original HHL algorithm.
In the following, we refer to this version of the HHL algorithm as

These algorithms are also used as subroutines of more complicated algorithms, like those for solving linear and non-linear differential equations
[
42
,
41
]
.
The simpler state preparation of the HHL variant is advantageous
to design near-term experimental realisations
with noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) technologies
[
60
,
18
]
.

Within this framework, it is important to understand how the performances of these algorithms scale with the amount of resources, namely the number of qubits and the number of gates.
We show that the computational error of the HHL variant does not approach zero for large registers when the condition numbers
\kappa
is large or using a poor estimation of

With minimal modifications to the algorithm structure,
we derive an improved algorithm which provides solutions up to small errors with a lower complexity than the HHL variant.
We also show that the original HHL algorithm does not suffer from the above convergence issue and always provides better performances than the HHL variant. The different convergence behaviour relies on the aforementioned state preparation that is simpler in the HHL variant.

Our analysis then sheds light on the theoretical structure of quantum algorithms for linear problems, and can be generalized to algorithms exploiting recently developed alternatives the quantum phase estimation

2
HHL algorithm

The linear problem (
1
) has a solution if and only if
\mathbf{b}
lies in the support of
A
. In this case, given the eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{j}
of
A
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}\neq 0
, there is a decomposition of the input vector
\mathbf{b}=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}
for some
\beta_{j}\in\mathbbm{C}
and the solution is
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{j}(\beta_{j}/\lambda_{j})\mathbf{u}_{j}
. An alternative decomposition of the input state is
\mathbf{b}=\sum_{j}b_{j}\mathbf{e}_{j}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
are basis vectors with elements
\big(\mathbf{e}_{j}\big)_{l}=\delta_{j,l}
.

For later convenience, assume that
\mathbf{b}
has unit
L^{2}
-norm,
\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}=1
. If this condition is not met, the linear problem (
1
) can be mapped to an equivalent problem by dividing
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{x}
by the norm
\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}
.
Assume also that
A
is Hermitian, as the problem with non-Hermitian
A
can be embedded in
\mathbbm{C}^{2d}
with an Hermitian matrix.
Consider indeed the following problem

\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\
A^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbf{y}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{b}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2)

whose solution is the solution of the original problem embedded in
\mathbbm{C}^{2d}
:
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}\oplus A^{-1}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}\oplus\mathbf{x}
where
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbbm{C}^{d}
is the vector with all components set to zero. Solving the new problem, then solves the original problem at the cost of doubling the dimension of the solution space.
Finally, assume without loss of generality that the eigenvalues of
A
satisfy
0<\lambda_{j}\leqslant 1
. The conditions
\lambda_{j}\leqslant 1
can be achieved by dividing
A
and multiplying
\mathbf{x}
by a rough estimate (rounded up) of the largest eigenvalue of
A
.
We will show in Section
7
how to relax the condition
\lambda_{j}>0
.

The HHL algorithm and its variant use three quantum registers.

•

The register
r
consists of
n=\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil
qubits used to encode the vector
\mathbf{b}
in the pure state
|b\rangle_{r}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}b_{j}|j\rangle_{r}
, where
\{|j\rangle_{r}\}_{j=0,\dots,d-1}
is the computational basis, i.e. the eigenvectors of the Pauli matrix
\sigma_{z}
for each qubit.
This is the reason for requiring
1=\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}=\langle b|b\rangle
.
This pure state can also be expressed as
|b\rangle_{r}=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}|u_{j}\rangle_{r}
, where
|u_{j}\rangle_{r}=\sum_{k}V_{k,j}|j\rangle_{r}
and
V=[V_{k,j}]
is the unitary matrix that diagonalises
A
,
V^{{}^{\dagger}}A\,V=\textnormal{diag}\,(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
.

•

The register
c
, called
clock
, is used to implement the quantum phase estimation and
to store the binary representation of eigenvalues of
A

as elements of the
computational basis.
It consists of
n_{c}
qubits, so that the states in the computational basis represent up to
T=2^{n_{c}}
different values and consequently
the maximum precision for eigenvalue estimations is
2^{-n_{c}}=T^{-1}
.
When
n_{c}
and
T
increase, the eigenvalues of
A
are discriminated with better precision leading to a smaller error in the final output.
For this reason, we use
T
as the parameter to analyse the convergence towards the exact solution.

•

The algorithm also uses an additional ancillary qubit
a
.

The solution
\mathbf{x}
is encoded in the amplitudes of the output state vector of the
r
-register. Nevertheless, it is impossible to generate a state whose amplitudes are the components of
\mathbf{x}
, namely
\sum_{j}x_{j}|u_{j}\rangle_{r}
with
x_{j}=\beta_{j}/\lambda_{j}
, because it is not normalized in general.
Instead, the HHL algorithm aims at producing a state as close as possible to
\sum_{j}x_{j}|u_{j}\rangle_{r}/\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
.
Unfortunately, the amplitudes can be precisely measured with state tomography which is not efficient in the worst case
[
57
,
33
,
32
,
31
]
. Therefore, the HHL algorithm does not compute the exact solution
\mathbf{x}
, but it can be used to compute the norm
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
and expectation values
\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}M\mathbf{x}

of matrices
M

that can be implemented efficiently on the quantum register.

3
Error analysis

In this section, we discuss how close the output states of the HHL algorithm and of its variant are to the ideal solution of the linear problem (
1
).
The ideal output of the algorithm is the embedding of the vector
\mathbf{x}
in the
r
-register as follows

|x\rangle=|1\rangle_{a}|0\rangle_{c}\,\frac{C}{\sqrt{p}}\,\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}\,|u_{j}\rangle_{r}\,,

(13)

where we wrote the normalization constant as
C/\sqrt{p}
in order to make the expression more similar to (
12
), and the normalization of the state (
13
) entails

p=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(\frac{C\,|\beta_{j}|}{\lambda_{j}}\right)^{2}\,.

(14)

Since
\sin\big(\theta(k)\big)=Ct_{0}/(2\pi k)
is an amplitude of the state in (
7
), the constant
C
satisfies
C\leqslant 2\pi k/t_{0}
for all
k\geqslant k_{\textnormal{min}}
.
We then choose the largest of the allowed values
C=2\pi k_{\textnormal{min}}/t_{0}\simeq 1/(2\kappa^{\prime})
that maximizes the ideal and the actual probability of measuring
|1\rangle_{a}
, respectively
p
and
p_{0}
.

Before proceeding with our analysis, we stress that
the precision of the eigenvalue estimation
in the phase estimation subroutine is improved by increasing
t_{0}
. The reason is that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
are amplified and can be better distinguished. On the other hand, the phase estimation cannot differentiate phases modulo
2\pi
, and if
\lambda_{j}{t_{0}}/{T}>2\pi
the protocol fails.
Therefore, under the assumption
0<\lambda_{j}\leqslant 1
for all

t_{0}

T

t_{0}=tT
for some

.
This scaling implies
p=\mathcal{O}(C/\lambda_{\textnormal{min}})^{2}=\mathcal{O}\big((2\pi k_{\textnormal{min}})^{2}/(\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}tT)^{2}\big)=\mathcal{O}(\kappa/\kappa^{\prime})^{2}
.

The states
|x\rangle

|x_{0}\rangle
are close to each other if their squared overlap, also called fidelity
|\langle x|x_{0}\rangle|^{2}
, is close to
1

from the ideal state

Indeed, the square root of the infidelity satisfies the properties of a distance between pure states

[

63

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{2}}{\sqrt{p\,p_{0}}}\,\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}\,\sum_{k=k_{\textnormal{min}}}^{T-1}\big|\alpha_{k|j}\big|^{2}\,\frac{t\,T}{2\pi k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}\,\sum_{k=k_{\textnormal{min}}}^{T-1}\big|\alpha_{k|j}\big|^{2}\,\frac{t\,T}{2\pi k}}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{|\beta_{l}|^{2}}{\lambda_{l}^{2}}\,\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}|\beta_{j}|^{2}\,\sum_{k=k_{\textnormal{min}}}^{T-1}\left(\big|\alpha_{k|j}\big|\,\frac{t\,T\,}{2\pi k}\right)^{2}}}\,.

d\big(|x\rangle,|x_{0}\rangle\big)=\sqrt{\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j,l=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}\,|\beta_{l}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}\,\lambda_{l}^{2}}\left(\epsilon_{2,j}-\epsilon_{1,j}-\epsilon_{1,l}-\epsilon_{1,j}\,\epsilon_{1,l}\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j,l=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}\,|\beta_{l}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}\,\lambda_{l}^{2}}\left(1+\epsilon_{2,j}\right)}}\,.

(18)

The algorithm provides reliable solutions of linear systems only if

approaches zero, namely for small
\epsilon_{1,j}
and
\epsilon_{2,j}
.

We have computed numerically

Given

(19)

For the HHL variant,
\epsilon_{1,j}^{\textnormal{var}}

Recalling

4
Improved HHL variant

We have shown that the HHL variant is not useful when the condition number is poorly estimated.
An improved HHL variant, that is accurate event without any bound
\kappa^{\prime}
,
can be implemented with the amplitude amplification
[
19
,
20
]
of the component
|1\rangle_{a}|0\rangle_{c}
, instead of
|1\rangle_{a}
, after the circuit that leads to the state (
8
).
The probability
\tilde{p}
of measuring
|1\rangle_{a}|0\rangle_{c}
from the state (
8
) is

\tilde{p}=\sum_{j}|\beta_{j}|^{2}\left(\sum_{k=k_{\textnormal{min}}}^{T-1}\big|\alpha_{k|j}^{\textnormal{var}}\big|^{2}\,\frac{CtT}{2\pi k}\right)^{2}.

(24)

The amplitude amplification will require an average number of calls to the circuit in figure
1
of order
\sqrt{1/\tilde{p}}
.
We are more restrictive on the state component to be amplified,
\tilde{p}<p_{0}
, but
the bound (
20
) and
C=2\pi k_{\textnormal{min}}/t_{0}\simeq 1/(2\kappa^{\prime})
prove that
\tilde{p}
and
p_{0}
are both of order
\mathcal{O}(\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\,\kappa^{\prime})^{-2}=\mathcal{O}(\kappa/\kappa^{\prime})^{2}
.
Therefore, the HHL variant and the improved algorithm have the same scaling of the running time.

After the amplitude amplification of the state
|1\rangle_{a}|0\rangle_{c}
, the output state is

|\tilde{x}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tilde{p}}}\sum_{j}\sum_{k=k_{\textnormal{min}}}^{T-1}\beta_{j}\,\big|\alpha_{k|j}^{\textnormal{var}}\big|^{2}\,\frac{CtT}{2\pi k}\,|1\rangle_{a}|0\rangle_{c}|u_{j}\rangle_{r}\,.

(25)

In analogy to the state (
13
) that
encodes the exact solution
\mathbf{x}
in the Hilber space of quantum registers, the state (
25
) encodes the approximate solution
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\sum_{k=k_{\textnormal{min}}}^{T-1}\big|\alpha_{k|j}^{\textnormal{var}}\big|^{2}\,\frac{tT}{2\pi k}\mathbf{u}_{j}
.
We then measure the error of the estimate through the fidelity between
|\tilde{x}\rangle
and
|x\rangle
,

\displaystyle\langle x|\tilde{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{2}}{\sqrt{p\tilde{p}}}\,\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\big|\alpha_{k|j}^{\textnormal{var}}\big|^{2}\,\frac{tT}{2\pi k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\big|\alpha_{k|j}^{\textnormal{var}}\big|^{2}\,\frac{tT}{2\pi k}}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\sum_{l}|\beta_{l}|^{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\big|\alpha_{k|l}^{\textnormal{var}}\big|^{2}\,\frac{tT}{2\pi k}\right)^{2}}}\,.

(26)

The difference between equation (
26
) and equation (
15
) is that the same sum appears in both the numerator and the denominator of (
26
). Then, the convergence of the distance
d\big(|x\rangle,|\tilde{x}\rangle\big)
depends only of the quantity
\epsilon_{1,j}^{\textnormal{var}}
(
16
) and on its bound (
20
). Using the notation
1/\tilde{\lambda}_{j}:=\sum_{k=k_{\textnormal{min}}}^{T-1}\big|\alpha_{k|j}^{\textnormal{var}}\big|^{2}tT/(2\pi k)
, the computational error is given by

d\big(|x\rangle,|\tilde{x}\rangle\big)=\sqrt{\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\sum_{l}\frac{|\beta_{l}|^{2}}{\lambda_{l}\,\tilde{\lambda_{l}}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{l}}{\tilde{\lambda_{l}}}-\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\tilde{\lambda_{j}}}\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\sum_{l}\frac{|\beta_{l}|^{2}}{\tilde{\lambda_{l}}^{2}}}}\,.

(27)

Since equation (
20
) implies that
\lambda_{j}/\tilde{\lambda_{j}}=1+\mathcal{O}\left(\ln(\lambda_{j}\,t\,T)/(\lambda_{j}\,t\,T)\right)
, the error is bigger if the eigenvalue is smaller. Then, recalling
\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}=\min_{j}\lambda_{j}
, the error is bounded by

d\big(|x\rangle,|\tilde{x}\rangle\big)\leqslant\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\ln(\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\,t\,T)}{\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\,t\,T}}\right)\leqslant\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{t\,T}\,\ln\frac{t\,T}{\kappa}}\right)\,.

(28)

Equation (
28
) shows that the error can be
arbitrary small by choosing a big enough number of qubits
n_{c}
in the
c
register, with
T=2^{n_{c}}
, provided that the condition number
\kappa
is not as large as
T
.

The quantum states
|\tilde{x}\rangle
and
|x\rangle
have unit normalization, therefore, vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbbm{C}^{d}
that differ only in the normalization have the same encoding
|\tilde{x}\rangle
. In this sense, the states
|x\rangle
and
|\tilde{x}\rangle
encode only the direction of the vectors
\mathbf{x}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
respectively. Nevertheless, the norms of the vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
and
\mathbf{x}
defined above are

||\tilde{\mathbf{x}}||_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{p}}}{C}\,,\qquad||\mathbf{x}||_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{p}}{C}\,.

(29)

The probability
\tilde{p}
can be measured implementing the amplitude estimation algorithm (a combination of the amplitude amplification and the quantum phase estimation algorithms)
[
19
,
20
]
that uses an average number of calls to the circuit of figure
1
of order
1/\sqrt{\tilde{p}}
.
In order to prove that
||\tilde{\mathbf{x}}||_{2}
well approximates
||\mathbf{x}||_{2}
, use equation (
20
) and
\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}=\min_{j}\lambda_{j}
to compute

\frac{\big|\,||\mathbf{x}||_{2}^{2}-||\tilde{\mathbf{x}}||_{2}^{2}\,\big|}{||\mathbf{x}||_{2}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left|1-\frac{\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\tilde{\lambda_{j}}^{2}}\right|}{\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{|\beta_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ln(\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\,t\,T)}{\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\,t\,T}\right)\,.

(30)

The bound (
30
) proves that the norm of the solution can be measure with
arbitrary small error
and with the same running time scaling required for the amplification of the amplitude of state
|\tilde{x}\rangle
.

The components of
\mathbf{x}
cannot be computed efficiently using this algorithm, as discussed in section
2
. Nevertheless, the algorithm allows to efficiently compute expectation values
\mathbf{x}^{T}M\mathbf{x}
if
M
is a matrix that can be efficiently implemented on a quantum computer. In order to prove it, assume without loss of generality that
M
is Hermitian with eigenvalues
\{m_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,d}
and eigenvectors

\{\mathbf{m}_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,d}
, otherwise apply the following argument to the Hermitian and the anti-Hermitian part of
M
independently.
Perform then a projective measurement on the output of the algorithm,
|\tilde{x}\rangle
, with projectors

\big\{|m_{j}\rangle\langle m_{j}|\big\}_{j=0,\dots,T-1}
.
After
\mu
of such measurements, the statistical frequency of obtaining the outcome
m_{j}
approximates the probability
|\langle\tilde{x}|m_{j}\rangle|^{2}
up to an error
\delta
with probability
1-\mathcal{O}(e^{-2\mu\delta^{2}})
from the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound
[
24
,
40
]
. When the sampling error
\delta
is comparable with the computational errors (
28
),
one requires
\mu>\mathcal{O}\big(\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\,t\,T/\ln(\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\,t\,T)\big)
repetitions for a good success probability in the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound. These repetitions can however be parallelized, without then increasing the computational time.

From the frequency of obtaining the state
|m_{j}\rangle
and from the meaurement of the norm discussed above, one computes

\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\,\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}m_{j}|\langle\tilde{x}|m_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\,\langle\tilde{x}|M|\tilde{x}\rangle=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{T}M\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}^{T}M\mathbf{x}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ln(\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\,t\,T)}{\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\,t\,T}\right)\right)\,,

(31)

achieving a good estimate of
\mathbf{x}^{T}M\mathbf{x}
for large
T
.

5
Complexity

We analyse and compare here the complexity of the above algorithms.
The relevant parameters for this analysis are
the computational error
\epsilon
, the condition number
\kappa=\max_{j}|\lambda_{j}|/\min_{j}|\lambda_{j}|
of the matrix
A
and its sparsity
s
that it the maximum number of non-vanishing elements in each row or column.

The simulation of the controlled gates
(
6
) within error
\epsilon^{\prime}
in the quantum phase estimation can be done with a number
\mathcal{O}\big(s\,T\max_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|+\frac{\ln(1/\epsilon^{\prime})}{\ln\ln(1/\epsilon^{\prime})}\big)
of queries to the matrix elements of
A

[
47
,
48
]
and additional
\mathcal{O}\big((\ln d)\,s\,T\max_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|+\frac{(\ln d)\ln(1/\epsilon^{\prime})}{\ln\ln(1/\epsilon^{\prime})}\big)
one- and two-qubit gates.
Note that the assumption
0<\lambda_{j}\leqslant 1
implies
\max_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|\leqslant 1
. The implementation of the quantum Fourier transform requires a number
\mathcal{O}(\log_{2}^{2}T)
of one- and two- qubit gates

[
55
,
9
]
and the controlled rotations (
7
) require
\mathcal{O}(T\log_{2}^{2}T)

of such operations (see appendix
A
).
Moreover, the circuit in figure
1
has to be executed
\mathcal{O}\big(1/\sqrt{\tilde{p}}\big)=\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa)
times to implement the postselection.
The number of sequential queries is called
query complexity
,
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{O}(s\,T\,\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa)
,
and the number of consecutive additional gates is called
gate complexity
,
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{O}\big((s\log_{2}d+\log_{2}^{2}T)\,T\,\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa\big)
.
We focus on the gate complexity as the query complexity is proportional to
\mathcal{G}
up to logarithmic corrections.

The gate complexity can be expressed in terms of the computational error quantified by the distance between the output state and the ideal state (see equations (
18
) and (
27
)).
From equation (
19
), consider
the computational error
\epsilon=\kappa/T
, and the gate complexity becomes
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{O}\big((s\log_{2}d+\log_{2}^{2}(\kappa/\epsilon))\,\kappa^{\prime}/\epsilon\big)
.
Near term experimental realizations and applications of HHL algorithms as subroutines
[
23
,
7
,
56
,
27
,
29
,
53
]

could benefit from avoiding the preparation of the initial entangled state of the clock (
4
). We then analyse the gate complexity of the HHL variant and of the improved HHL variant.

For the HHL variant, we consider the best computational error, namely the scaling of the lower bound (
22
), while the computational error of the improved HHL variant is given by the upper bound (
28
) which captures the exact numerical scaling.
The computational error is then
\epsilon=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{T}\,\ln\left(\frac{T}{\kappa}\right)}
for HHL variant with
\kappa^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{T\kappa\ln T/\kappa}\big)
and for the improved HHL variant for any
\kappa^{\prime}
. Inverting this relation, we obtain
T=-\frac{\kappa}{\epsilon^{2}}\,W_{-1}(-\epsilon^{2})
where
W_{-1}(z)
is the branch of the Lambert function defined as the real solution of
We^{W}=z
with largest absolute value
|W|

[
50
]
.

The gate complexity in this case becomes

\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{O}\left(-\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\epsilon^{2}}\,W_{-1}\big(-\epsilon^{2}\big)\left(s\log_{2}d+\log_{2}^{2}\left(-\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\epsilon^{2}}\,W_{-1}\big(-\epsilon^{2}\big)\right)\right)\right)\,.

(32)

Using the notation
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
which denotes orders
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
up to multiplicative powers of the logarithm of its argument, the gate complexity (
32
) reads
\mathcal{G}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\kappa^{\prime}\,s\,\epsilon^{-2}\log_{2}d)
.
The condition
\kappa^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{T\kappa\ln T/\kappa}\big)
for the HHL variant is compatible with
\kappa\leqslant\kappa^{\prime}
only if
\kappa\leqslant\chi\sqrt{T\kappa\ln T/\kappa}
for some constant
\chi
. This inequality imples
\kappa\leqslant T\,e^{-W_{0}(1/\chi^{2})}=T\,\chi^{2}\,W_{0}(1/\chi^{2})
, where
W_{0}(z)
is the principle branch of the Lambert function defined as the unique real solution of
We^{W}=z
for positive
z
(for instance
W_{0}(1)\simeq 0.57
)
[
50
]
.

Equation
(
22
)
also imples that the computational error of the HHL variant with
\kappa^{\prime}
as large as
T
(
\kappa^{\prime}\sim T
) is a non-negligible constant, and the algorithm output is not reliable.
If, instead,
\kappa^{\prime}\gg\sqrt{T\kappa\ln T/\kappa}
then
\kappa\ll\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big((\kappa^{\prime})^{2}/T\big)

and the gate complexity is lower bounded by
\mathcal{G}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(T\kappa^{\prime}\kappa^{-1}s\,\log_{2}d\big)\gg\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(T^{2}(\kappa^{\prime})^{-1}s\,\log_{2}d\big)

where, given the computational error

\epsilon\sim\kappa^{\prime}/T
as in (
22
),
the bound equals the gate complexity
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\kappa^{\prime}\,s\,\epsilon^{-2}\log_{2}d\big)
in (
32
).
In conclusion,
for
\kappa^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{T\kappa\ln T/\kappa}\big)
the HHL variant and the improved algorithm have comparable computational error, query and gate complexity, while
for
\kappa^{\prime}\gg\sqrt{T\kappa\ln T/\kappa}
the improved algorithm is more efficient in terms of these figures of merit.

In this section, we discuss how to apply the proposed algorithm to the case of non-positive eigenvalues of

(33)

(

8
Conclusions

We have analysed the HHL algorithm and a commonly used variant of the HHL algorithm for solving linear systems (
1
) on quantum computers.
Both these algorithms consist in two main subroutines, the quantum phase estimation and the amplitude amplification.
The quantum phase estimation is used for computing the eigenvalue of the matrix
A
in (
1
), and the amplitude amplification is required for increasing the success probability of the matrix inversion.
The HHL variant replaces the initial entangled state (
4
) of the auxiliary register used in the quantum phase estimation, called clock, with the fully factorizable state (
5
).

We analysed how the performances of the HHL algorithm and of its variant scale with the number of qubits
n_{c}
of the clock. This parameter is relevant as it gives the precision of the quantum phase estimation for eigenvalue computations and the number of required gates.
We proved that, the error of the HHL variant, unlike that of the original HHL algorithm, does not converge for large
n_{c}
but instead oscillates around a constant value,
when the condition number of the matrix
A
is large or when a poor estimation of it is used.
The reason is the presence of oscillating terms in the amplified amplitude. To solve this problem we proposed a modified algorithm, based on the amplification of a smaller amplitude without detrimental oscillating terms.
We confirmed this result with numerical simulations.

The simulation of unitary exponentials of
A
in the quantum phase estimation contribute significantly to the gate counting in previous discussions of the original HHL algorithm and of its variant.
We have therefore studied the complexity of these algorithms in the light of recent results on unitary similations

[
15
,
68
,
17
,
12
,
16
,
13
,
10
,
11
,
47
,
48
]
.
Our improved algorithm has a lower complexity than the HHL variant
not only when the prior estimation of the condition number is so large that the error of the HHL variant does not converge, but also at smaller condition numbers.

Our results are relevant for applications of the HHL algorithm and its variants as subroutines of more complicated algorithms and for next term experimental realisations
with noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) technologies

]
.

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{j}tT}{2}\right)}{k_{\textnormal{min}}^{2}T^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left(\frac{\lambda_{j}tT}{2\pi}-k_{\textnormal{min}}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda_{j}tT}{2\pi}+k_{\textnormal{min}}\right)}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{T}\left(\frac{\lambda_{j}tT}{2\pi}-k_{\textnormal{min}}\right)\right)}
