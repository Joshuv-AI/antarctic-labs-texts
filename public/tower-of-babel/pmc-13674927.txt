Outcomes of early amniotic membrane transplantation in acute ocular thermochemical injuries caused by carbide guns in India
Authors: Aafreen Bari, Shifa Ahmed, Purnamita Saras, Rajesh Sinha, Tanuj Dada, Radhika Tandon, Tushar Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To analyze the clinical features, management, and short-term outcomes of acute ocular thermochemical injuries caused by carbide gun explosions, and to evaluate the role of amniotic membrane transplantation (AMT).Design:Retrospective observational study.Methods:This study included 20 eyes from 18 patients presenting to a tertiary eye care center in India with acute thermochemical burns caused by carbide guns during Diwali. All patients underwent immediate ocular irrigation, detailed ophthalmic evaluation, and injury grading using Dua’s classification. Following assessment of clinical parameters—including visual acuity, corneal epithelial defect, corneal haze, limbal and conjunctival involvement, intraocular pressure, and pain scores—all eyes received standardized medical therapy followed by overlay AMT. Outcomes were analyzed descriptively.Results:The mean age was 20.25 ± 10.58 years; all patients were male. Most injuries were unilateral and associated with direct carbide gun use. The median presenting injury grade was Dua’s grade III. Mean presenting visual acuity was logMAR 1.77 ± 0.65, improving to logMAR 0.77 ± 0.62 at three months. Complete epithelialization was achieved in all eyes, with a mean healing time of 22.52 ± 22.4 days. Pain scores significantly improved from a mean of 6.05 to 1.16. Repeat AMT was required in 21% of eyes. Cicatricial complications were infrequent, and no case developed secondary glaucoma. One eye with delayed presentation progressed to phthisis bulbi.Conclusion:Early aggressive management with AMT yields favorable short-term outcomes in carbide gun–related ocular thermochemical injuries.

Acute ocular chemical and thermal injuries constitute true ophthalmic emergencies, often resulting in significant visual morbidity if not managed promptly and appropriately. These injuries can lead to a wide spectrum of ocular surface damage, ranging from mild conjunctival irritation to severe limbal stem cell deficiency, corneal opacification, secondary glaucoma, and even permanent blindness.[1] The prognosis depends on multiple factors, including the nature of the offending agent, duration of exposure, depth of penetration, and timeliness of intervention. Despite advances in acute care and reconstructive strategies, ocular surface burns continue to pose major therapeutic challenges, particularly in developing countries where access to immediate specialized care may be limited.[2]

Globally, chemical injuries are among the most common causes of ocular burns, with alkali injuries being the most frequent and severe due to their rapid penetration into ocular tissues. Alkali agents such as lime, ammonia, and caustic soda cause liquefactive necrosis, leading to extensive damage to the cornea, limbus, and anterior segment structures. Acid burns, while also common, generally result in coagulative necrosis that can limit deeper tissue penetration. In both developed and developing nations, alkali burns account for the majority of ocular chemical injuries.[3] In India, ocular chemical injuries are likewise predominantly alkali in nature, often associated with industrial accidents, household cleaning agents, agricultural chemicals, and construction materials such as lime.[4]

In contrast to these well-documented causes, ocular thermochemical injuries resulting from carbide guns represent a rare and underreported etiology. Carbide guns are improvised devices commonly used in certain parts of India during festivals, weddings, and celebrations to produce loud explosive sounds. These devices typically use calcium carbide, which reacts with water to produce acetylene gas. Ignition of this gas generates a sudden explosion accompanied by intense heat, pressure waves, and dispersal of chemical residues. When such explosions occur in close proximity to the face, they can result in combined thermal and chemical injury to the eyes and periocular tissues.[56]

The ocular damage caused by carbide gun explosions is unique in its mechanism, as it involves both thermal burns from the heat of ignition and chemical burns from alkaline residues of calcium hydroxide formed during the reaction. This dual insult can cause severe damage to the ocular surface, including corneal epithelial defects, stromal haze, limbal ischemia, conjunctival necrosis, and eyelid burns. The unpredictable nature of these injuries, along with variability in exposure intensity and delay in presentation, makes standardized management challenging. Furthermore, awareness regarding the potential ocular hazards of carbide guns remains low, contributing to delayed treatment and poorer outcomes.

In the Indian context, carbide gun–related ocular injuries are particularly relevant due to their cultural and social use, especially in rural and semi-urban settings. However, published literature on the clinical presentation, severity spectrum, and long-term outcomes of such injuries is scarce.[78] Most existing studies on ocular burns focus on industrial or household chemical exposures, leaving a significant gap in understanding the consequences of unconventional thermochemical injuries such as those caused by carbide guns.

Management of acute ocular surface burns aims to minimize inflammation, promote epithelial healing, preserve limbal stem cell function, and prevent long-term sequelae. Among the evolving treatment modalities, amniotic membrane transplantation (AMT) has gained prominence as an effective adjunct in the acute phase of ocular surface burns. The amniotic membrane possesses anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and epithelialization-promoting properties, making it particularly beneficial in reducing pain, accelerating surface healing, and improving visual outcomes.[91011] While the role of AMT has been well studied in conventional chemical burns, its application in rare thermochemical injuries such as those caused by carbide guns has not been extensively explored.

This study aims to evaluate the clinical outcomes of acute ocular thermochemical injuries caused by carbide guns in India. By analyzing the presentation, severity, management strategies, and visual outcomes, this study seeks to highlight an uncommon but preventable cause of ocular morbidity. Additionally, the role of AMT in the management of these rare injuries is explored, contributing to the limited existing literature and providing insights into optimal therapeutic approaches for such unique ocular emergencies.

Methods

A retrospective observational study was planned after approval from the institute’s ethics committee (AIIMSA5566/09.01.2026). The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. As a tertiary apex referral center, all patients presenting to the eye casualty with firecracker- and carbide-related ocular injuries are routinely documented in institutional records. Although these injuries are commonly associated with the Diwali and Chhath festival period, presentations continued over 2–3 months during the extended festive season. After Institutional Ethics Committee approval, data of patients presenting between November 1 and 30 were retrospectively analyzed, with clinical outcomes assessed over a three-month follow-up period.

Detailed history, including demographic details, mode of ocular injury, and duration from trauma, was noted. Eyewash was done using normal saline till pH reached normal value. The visual acuity and intraocular pressure were assessed using Snellen’s chart and non-contact tonometer respectively. The peri-ocular and ocular adnexal of both eyes were examined followed by detailed slit lamp examination. Based on the conjunctival and limbal involvement, the cases were graded according to Dua’s classification for ocular surface burns.[1] The clinical parameters were assessed as described.

Corneal epithelial defect

The corneal epithelial defect was assessed using fluorescein staining. The maximum diameter of corneal epithelial defect (EDD) was measured in millimeters. The area of epithelial defect (EDA) was calculated by multiplying EDD with dimension perpendicular to EDD.

Corneal haze grading

The corneal haze was graded using grading defined by Sharma et al.[12] The cases with no anterior segment structure visible were graded as zero, those with severe corneal haze obscuring pupil was graded as one. The cases with corneal haze in which pupil was seen were graded as grade two. The cases with slight corneal haze and in which the details of iris were visible were graded as grade three, while cases with a clear cornea were graded as grade four [Table 1].

Pain was assessed using a numeric rating scale ranging from 0 to 10, where 0 indicated no pain. Scores of 1–3 represented mild pain, 4–6 indicated moderate pain, and scores of 7 or higher denoted severe pain [Table 2].[13]

Medical therapy, including topical moxifloxacin hydrochloride 0.5% (Vigamox; Alcon, Fort Worth, Texas, USA) four times per day, homatropine sulfate 2% four times per day, sodium citrate (10%) and sodium ascorbate (10%) every 2 hours, preservative free lubricants, topical prednisolone acetate 1% (Predforte; Allergan, Bangalore, India) four times a day, and oral vitamin C (500 mg) three times a day, was given.

All cases were explained regarding the nature of their ocular injury. After obtaining informed consent from the patients or their legal guardian, they underwent amniotic membrane grafting (AMG). Amniotic membrane was collected from seronegative donors (Hep B, Hep C, HIV, syphilis). AMT was done using the overlay technique under local anesthesia in adults and general anesthesia in children. Intra-operatively, it was ensured that after peeling the membrane from nitrocellulose paper, it covered the entire ocular surface with the stromal surface facing the ocular surface. Six interrupted vicryl sutures were passed circumferentially around the limbus to ensure stability of the membrane. The eye was then bandaged after surgery, and topical medications were continued in the post-operative period.[14]

Clinical photographs were taken for all cases pre-operatively and at all follow-ups. Patients were followed up on post-operative day one, week 2, week 4, week 6, and 3 months. Repeat AMT was considered in eyes with persistent epithelial defects and was typically planned 7–10 days after the previous AMT procedure. All pre- and post-operative clinical parameters were entered in an Excel sheet and analyzed.

The statistical analysis was done using SPSS software 22.0 (International Business Machines Corp., New York, United States). For descriptive purposes, non-parametric data were expressed as median (range), while parametric qualitative data were expressed as percentage and quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

A total of 20 eyes of 18 patients with acute thermochemical ocular burns caused by carbide guns were included and followed up for a period of three months. All patients completed the follow-up.

Baseline demographic results

The mean age of the included cases was 20.25 ± 10.58 years, and the median age was 16 (2.36) years. All cases were males. Two patients (2/18; 11.11%) had bilateral ocular involvement, while the rest had unilateral ocular injury. One eye of one of these cases presented with corneal perforation with endophthalmitis and underwent therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty and injection of intravitreal ceftazidime (2.25 mg/0.1 mL) and vancomycin (1 mg/0.1 mL). It was considered an outlier case and excluded from the analysis of other ocular thermochemical injuries.

Fifteen cases (15/18; 83.33%) gave a history of trauma from primary usage of a carbide gun, while the remaining three (3/18; 16.67%) were bystanders of their relative or friend, who was primarily using the carbide gun. The mean and median period from injury to presentation were 6.4 ± 5.57 and 2 (1.24) days.

Clinical results

The mean presenting visual acuity of the cases was log MAR 1.77 ± 0.65. The median grade of presenting ocular chemical injury was Dua’s grade III. Six eyes had Dua’s grade I-II, seven had grade III, and six had grade four or higher grade of ocular chemical injury [Figs. 1-3]. Ten patients (10/18; 55.56%) had periocular burns or bruises [Fig. 4]. None of the cases presented with ocular perforation except the one that presented with endophthalmitis. All cases underwent amniotic membrane graft for ocular thermochemical injury. Four eyes (4/19; 21.05%) underwent AMG multiple times for persistent epithelial defect. The maximum number of amniotic membrane grafts done for a particular case was thrice. The mean post-operative visual acuity at three-month follow-up was log MAR 0.77 ± 0.62. The mean intraocular pressure was 16.63 ± 2.93 mm Hg. None of them had secondary glaucoma or cataract.

At presentation, the mean EDD was 6.94 ± 4.93 mm and the mean EDA was 64.73 ± 61.32 mm2. The mean time to complete epithelization was 22.52 ± 22.4 days, and the median time for the same was 21 (5.13) days. The median pre-operative corneal haze was grade 1, which improved to grade 3 on the last follow-up. Conjunctival epithelial defects were observed in 11 cases (11/19; 57.9%) with involvement of on an average 1.72 ± 0.9 quadrants (range: 1–3).

The mean pre-operative pain score on presentation was 6.05 ± 2.43, which improved to 1.16 ± 2.0 at three months. Symblepharon formation was observed in two cases (2/20; 10%). One case developed a pyogenic granuloma at the superior limbus at three months. Granuloma excision with cauterization of the conjunctiva was done.

Outcomes

At the three-month follow-up, 11 eyes (11/20; 55%) were managed conservatively using lubricants and refraction. Five eyes (5/20; 25%) were planned for anterior lamellar or penetrating keratoplasty, two eyes (2/20; 10%) were planned for ocular surface reconstruction (pannus excision with simple limbal epithelial stem transplantation), and one (1/20; 5%) was planned for corneal tattooing with optical iridectomy. The eye with corneal perforation and endophthalmitis (1/20; 5%), which underwent therapeutic keratoplasty, developed phthisis bulbi at three months follow-up. Discussion

This study describes the clinical characteristics, management strategies, and short-term outcomes of acute ocular thermochemical injuries caused by carbide guns, a relatively uncommon yet preventable cause of ocular morbidity in India. The cohort predominantly comprised young male patients, reflecting patterns seen in other trauma-related ocular injuries from developing nations. This demographic trend likely relates to greater participation of males in outdoor activities and celebratory practices involving carbide guns, highlighting an important public health concern.[15] In this study, normal saline was used for ocular irrigation in all cases because of its immediate availability in the emergency setting, facilitating prompt copious irrigation, which is the cornerstone of acute ocular burn management. However, other solutions, such as Ringer lactate, owing to their more physiological osmolarity and electrolyte composition, may offer certain advantages over normal saline.

When compared with other thermochemical and chemical ocular injuries reported from India, carbide gun–related injuries demonstrate a distinct pathophysiology and clinical behavior. Conventional alkali injuries caused by substances such as edible lime (chuna), cement, or household chemicals are known to penetrate deeper ocular tissues, often resulting in extensive limbal ischemia, secondary glaucoma, and poor visual outcomes.[16] Early AMT was employed for its established anti-inflammatory and anti-scarring properties, along with its ability to promote epithelial healing and reduce corneal haze and subsequent ocular surface sequelae. As carbide-related ocular injuries represent a relatively uncommon entity with an uncertain clinical course, an early proactive approach was adopted in all acute cases. Interestingly, despite a substantial proportion of eyes in the present series presenting with moderate-to-severe ocular surface burns, more than half could ultimately be managed conservatively at the three-month follow-up. This observation suggests that carbide gun injuries, when treated early and appropriately, may carry a comparatively favorable short-term prognosis.

The unique mechanism of injury associated with carbide guns provides insight into this clinical pattern. Calcium carbide reacts with water to generate acetylene gas and calcium hydroxide (lime) through an exothermic reaction, resulting in a combined thermal, chemical, and mechanical insult. The rapid expansion and momentum of acetylene gas cause mechanical trauma to the eyelids and ocular surface, while the heat and alkaline environment contribute to epithelial damage and stromal injury [Fig. 5]. Unlike pure alkali burns, mechanical lime debris is typically not retained within the corneal tissue in carbide gun injuries.[1718] Consequently, although focal areas of stromal opacification may be observed, adjacent regions of the cornea often remain relatively clear and close to normal in appearance. This focal pattern of injury may partly explain the relatively preserved visual outcomes seen in several eyes.

(b) Exothermic reaction of calcium carbide with water producing acetylene gas, calcium hydroxide and heat leading to combined thermal and alkali ocular surface injury

The forceful impact of heated acetylene gas on the cornea can lead to stromal opacification, limbal damage, and a risk of limbal stem cell deficiency. Superior limbal involvement was more commonly noted, possibly due to the upward trajectory of the gas plume and blink-related protective mechanisms, which may preferentially expose the superior limbus. This distribution differs from that seen in typical alkali burns, where limbal involvement is often more diffuse and circumferential.[17]

Arora et al. evaluated the outcomes of carbide-related ocular injuries and adopted a grade-based management approach.[7] Thermochemical injuries up to Dua’s grade III were managed conservatively, whereas higher-grade injuries were treated with AMG. The median visual acuity in the conservatively managed group was 20/160.[7] In contrast, in our study, AMG was performed in all cases irrespective of the grade of ocular injury, resulting in a better median visual acuity (logMAR 0.77 ± 0.62). This difference may be attributed to variations in patient clinical profiles; however, our findings suggest that the use of amniotic membrane is beneficial even in lower grades of oculo-thermal injury by reducing corneal haze, alleviating ocular pain, and improving final visual outcomes.

Another study from India reported complete epithelialization in 66.7% of cases at a mean follow-up of 14 weeks, wherein AMG was not performed universally.[8] In contrast, all patients in our study received AMG, resulting in complete ocular surface epithelialization in every case, with a mean epithelialization time of 22.52 ± 22.4 days. These findings further underscore the potential role of AMG in promoting faster epithelialization and improved recovery, even in lower-grade oculo-thermal injuries.

AMG played a pivotal role in the acute management of these injuries. AMG promotes epithelial healing through its anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and anti-angiogenic properties and acts as a biological scaffold rich in growth factors and cytokines.[1920] While a single application was sufficient in most cases, repeat AMG was required in some eyes, reflecting the variable severity of injury and the dynamic healing process characteristic of thermochemical burns. The low incidence of cicatricial complications such as symblepharon and the absence of secondary glaucoma during follow-up further support the effectiveness of early ocular surface reconstruction. Similar favorable outcomes in ocular surface stabilization and epithelial healing have also been reported with large-diameter lamellar keratoplasty in severe ocular alkali burns.[21]

Pain was a prominent presenting symptom, with high baseline pain scores reflecting acute inflammation and epithelial disruption. The significant reduction in pain scores over the follow-up period in the majority of patients underscores the benefit of prompt intervention, intensive ocular surface lubrication, and AMG in reducing inflammation and promoting surface healing.

One eye in the present series progressed to phthisis bulbi. However, this patient presented late with corneal perforation and endophthalmitis and was considered non-salvageable at presentation, emphasizing the importance of early referral and timely management in determining visual and anatomical outcomes. The overall globe survival in the remaining eyes highlights the potential for meaningful recovery even in eyes presenting with severe initial injury.

The ready availability of calcium carbide as an over-the-counter substance poses a serious public health concern, as it facilitates the improvised manufacture of carbide guns capable of causing severe facial and ocular injuries. These findings underscore the need for regulatory control, public awareness campaigns, and preventive strategies to reduce the incidence of such avoidable injuries. In addition, safety advisories during festive seasons and promotion of protective eyewear may further help minimize the risk of ocular thermochemical burns.

The strengths of this study include its focused evaluation of a rare and underreported injury mechanism, detailed clinical characterization, and uniform management protocol with complete follow-up for all patients. The study provides novel insights into the pathophysiology, clinical behavior, and outcomes of carbide gun–related ocular injuries, contributing valuable information to the existing literature.

However, the study is limited by a relatively small sample size and a short follow-up duration of three months, which may not capture long-term visual outcomes, late-onset complications such as progressive limbal stem cell deficiency, or the need for secondary surgical interventions. The single-center design may also limit generalizability. Larger, multicentric studies with longer follow-up are warranted to better define the long-term prognosis and optimize management strategies for these injuries.

Conclusion

Carbide gun–related ocular thermochemical injuries represent a distinct clinical entity differing from conventional alkali burns in both mechanism and course. Despite severe presentation, early recognition and prompt ocular surface–directed therapy, particularly with AMG, can result in satisfactory short-term anatomical and functional outcomes. Preventive measures, regulation of calcium carbide availability, and public education remain essential to reduce the burden of this preventable cause of ocular morbidity.
