de Winter-like anterior electrocardiographic changes coexisting with inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction: a case report and literature insight

Background

The de Winter electrocardiographic (ECG) pattern is considered a ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) equivalent that is most commonly associated with acute occlusion of the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery. It is characterised by upsloping ST-segment depression at the J-point, with tall, symmetric T-waves, and often lacks the classic ST-segment elevation, which may lead to underrecognition and delayed reperfusion therapy, resulting in inferior clinical outcomes [1, 2]. However, de Winter-like anterior ST-T morphology may also be observed in the setting of severe non-occlusive LAD disease or multivessel ischaemia; therefore, its interpretation should remain cautious and be integrated with angiographic evidence. Accordingly, de Winter-like morphology should be treated as an ECG phenotype that warrants urgent evaluation but should not be used in isolation to infer acute LAD plaque rupture/occlusion when angiography does not confirm an LAD culprit lesion. When inferior ST-segment elevation is present (leads II, III and aVF), it independently establishes the diagnosis of inferior STEMI and warrants emergent coronary angiography/reperfusion evaluation. Accordingly, the coexistence of de Winter-like anterior morphology should not be framed as a cause of delayed management. Rather, it may serve as an additional clue suggesting extensive ischaemia or multivessel coronary artery disease, prompting comprehensive coronary assessment without altering the urgency dictated by the inferior STEMI criteria [3]. In such settings, the anterior ST-T morphology does not change the indication for emergent coronary angiography but mainly refines ECG interpretation and supports consideration of a broader ischaemic burden and multivessel/high-risk coronary anatomy. Reports describing such coexistence are scarce, and there is currently no consensus on optimal recognition and management strategies for this atypical presentation [3, 4]. Here, we present a case of inferior STEMI with concomitant de Winter-like anterior changes in a patient who initially declined urgent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and was managed conservatively, with subsequent successful delayed intervention. Rather than proposing a new mechanism, this case underscores a pragmatic point: de Winter-like anterior morphology should not be overinterpreted as proof of LAD occlusion, and its significance should be framed based on angiographic correlation while maintaining immediate STEMI-directed care.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old male with a 10-year history of type 2 diabetes presented to the emergency department on day 0 (approximately 2 h after symptom onset) with a 2-hour history of exertional chest discomfort, dizziness, diaphoresis and fatigue, without palpitations. On arrival, his vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.2 °C, pulse 56 bpm, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min and blood pressure 67/47 mmHg. He was alert and oriented. Cardiac and pulmonary auscultation was unremarkable. His extremities were cool to the touch with prolonged capillary refill time (> 3 s), indicating poor peripheral perfusion.

The initial 12-lead ECG (Fig. 1) showed sinus bradycardia with upsloping ST-segment depression of 0.1–0.3 mV and tall symmetrical T-waves in leads V2–V6, horizontal ST-segment depression of 0.05–0.1 mV in leads I and aVL, and ST-segment elevation of 0.05–0.15 mV in leads II, III and aVF, along with poor R-wave progression in the precordial leads. These findings were consistent with inferior STEMI with concomitant de Winter-like anterior ST-T changes [1], supporting an emergent reperfusion evaluation based on the inferior STEMI criteria and raising concern for possible multivessel coronary artery disease, rather than implying LAD occlusion based on ECG alone. Transthoracic ECG revealed mild left atrial enlargement, mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, and abnormal left ventricular diastolic function with a preserved ejection fraction of 57%. A detailed regional wall motion assessment was not explicitly documented in the initial ECG record available for this report.

Initial laboratory tests revealed mildly elevated myoglobin (136 ng/mL; normal 23–112), troponin I within the normal range at presentation (0.014 ng/mL; normal 0.01–0.023) and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) (2.6 ng/mL; normal 2–7.2), with N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) at 347 ng/L (normal 300–450). Blood count and electrolytes showed mild abnormalities as follows: sodium 133 mmol/L (normal 137–147), potassium 3.2 mmol/L (normal 3.5–5.1), haemoglobin 118 g/L and WBC 10.5 × 10⁹/L. Emergency management included establishing intravenous access, administration of one ampoule of dopamine (200 mg) and 500 mL normal saline for volume resuscitation, along with continuous ECG and haemodynamic monitoring. Given the initial ECG findings and clinical presentation consistent with acute coronary syndrome requiring emergent reperfusion evaluation, immediate medical therapy was initiated, including dual antiplatelet therapy and statin therapy, with close observation and serial reassessment (repeat ECGs and cardiac biomarkers). Emergent coronary angiography/PCI was recommended at presentation. The patient’s chest discomfort completely resolved, and blood pressure improved to 85/57 mmHg, with no recurrence of chest discomfort during subsequent in-hospital monitoring before coronary angiography. He was admitted with a diagnosis of acute coronary syndrome (Killip class I). Emergency PCI was advised but declined by the patient; conservative therapy was initiated, including dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg/day, clopidogrel 75 mg/day), statin therapy (rosuvastatin 10 mg/day), a proton pump inhibitor (rabeprazole 20 mg/day) and antihyperglycaemics including dapagliflozin 10 mg/day and acarbose 50 mg three times daily. Additional cardiac support medications included creatine phosphate sodium, 1 g twice daily.

On day 1, repeat cardiac biomarkers showed the following: myoglobin 253 ng/mL, troponin I > 25 ng/mL and CK-MB 239 ng/mL, confirming myocardial infarction. On day 4 after symptom onset, biomarkers had declined (myoglobin 24 ng/mL, troponin I 4 ng/mL, CK-MB 3 ng/mL), though NT-proBNP rose to 2230 ng/L. The ECG on the same day (Fig. 2) continued to show inferior ST-segment elevation with reciprocal ST-segment depression in the anterior leads, with prominent anterior T-wave morphology (de Winter-like appearance) in V2–V6. Overall, the ECG remained most consistent with inferior STEMI, and the anterior morphology was described as an ECG phenotype to be interpreted in conjunction with angiographic findings, rather than as standalone evidence of acute LAD occlusion. The patient agreed to coronary angiography following repeated consultation. Angiography revealed 80% stenosis in the proximal LAD (Fig. 3a), diffuse plaque infiltration in the LCX, and near-total occlusion in the mid-right coronary artery (RCA) (Fig. 3b). A drug-eluting stent was successfully implanted in the RCA (Fig. 3c). Given that angiography did not demonstrate complete LAD occlusion and the culprit vessel was the mid-RCA, the revascularisation strategy in this admission was guided by culprit-lesion identification, with LAD intervention planned as a staged procedure after stabilisation and outpatient reassessment. The patient’s overall condition, particularly fatigue, significantly improved post-procedure, and chest discomfort did not recur, and he remained haemodynamically stable.

On day 7, follow-up ECG (Fig. 4) showed sinus rhythm, persistent poor R-wave progression in V1–V4, abnormal Q-waves in leads III and aVF, as well as T-wave inversions in II, III, aVF and V1–V6. These findings were consistent with a completed inferior myocardial infarction and post-ischaemic (reperfusion/post-infarction) repolarisation abnormalities in the setting of severe proximal LAD stenosis, rather than evidence of ongoing anterior myocardial injury or active ischaemia based on T-wave inversion alone. The patient was maintained on standard dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg/day, clopidogrel 75 mg/day) and optimal medical therapy, including statin therapy (atorvastatin 40 mg/day). Intravenous tirofiban was administered for one day to enhance platelet inhibition to reduce the risk of recurrent ischaemic events. Hepatoprotective medications, including bicyclol and tiopronin, were also prescribed due to mild liver function impairment. Given the patient’s haemodynamic stability, the resolution of chest discomfort after RCA PCI, and the absence of angiographic evidence of acute LAD occlusion, revascularisation of the LAD was deferred in accordance with current guideline-based recommendations, which support staged PCI in stable patients with multivessel disease. A specific timeline for the staged LAD intervention was to be determined after discharge, based on outpatient reassessment; however, within the 1-month follow-up covered by this report, a definitive procedure date/interval was not documented.

By day 8, the patient reported no further chest discomfort. Serial ECG showed no new ST-segment changes but continued to demonstrate poor R-wave progression and persistent abnormal Q-waves, indicating established myocardial necrosis. At the 1-month follow-up, ECG (Fig. 5) remained similar, with persistent mild ST-segment elevation in V1–V4 with anterior QS complexes/poor R-wave progression, which was considered more consistent with a post-infarction persistent ST-elevation pattern rather than acute recurrent ischaemia, and low-amplitude or inverted T waves in II, III, aVF, and V4–V6, suggesting post-ischemic repolarisation changes and post-infarction remodelling, as well as persistent myocardial electrical instability. Transthoracic ECG revealed segmental wall motion abnormalities, mild-left atrial enlargement, trace mitral and tricuspid regurgitation and mildly impaired diastolic function, while left ventricular systolic function was preserved with an ejection fraction of 58%. These ECG findings reflected the structural and functional consequences of prior myocardial infarction, particularly in the inferolateral and anterior walls.

Discussion and conclusion

The de Winter ECG pattern is increasingly recognised as a high-risk but easily overlooked equivalent of STEMI, typically associated with acute proximal LAD occlusion in its classic form. In the present case, angiography showed severe but non-occlusive proximal LAD disease, and the culprit lesion was a near-total mid-RCA occlusion. Although its prevalence is estimated at approximately 2% among patients with acute LAD occlusion [1], its lack of classic ST-segment elevation makes it prone to misdiagnosis, especially in emergency and non-specialist settings. Nonetheless, de Winter-like anterior ST-T morphology may be encountered in broader ischaemic settings and should be described and interpreted in close correlation with coronary angiography, particularly when angiographic findings do not demonstrate complete LAD occlusion. From a triage perspective, this supports avoiding ECG-only culprit-vessel assignment based on de Winter-like morphology and prioritising ECG–angiography correlation. Clinically, this supports an ECG–angiography correlation approach rather than ECG-only culprit-vessel assignment when de Winter-like morphology is present.

Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the underlying electrophysiological mechanism of this ECG pattern. One explanation involves delayed endocardial conduction due to anatomical variation in Purkinje fibres [5]. Another mechanism suggests that the absence of ST-segment elevation may be linked to impaired activation of adenosine triphosphate-sensitive potassium channels under ischemic conditions, thereby altering repolarisation patterns [6, 7]. The tall, symmetrical T-waves observed in the de Winter-like pattern may reflect transmural gradients in myocardial ischaemia, where the sub-endocardium is severely affected, while the sub-epicardium remains partially perfused, consistent with sub-total rather than total coronary occlusion [2].

Initially described as a static ECG pattern that does not evolve into classic STEMI, subsequent studies and case series have demonstrated that the classic de Winter pattern can be a transient phenomenon. It may precede, coexist with or evolve into ST elevation, and in some cases, patients have experienced rapid haemodynamic deterioration or even sudden cardiac death [3, 7]. These findings support the current guideline recommendations that classify the classic de Winter pattern as a STEMI equivalent, warranting immediate reperfusion therapy, preferably via primary PCI [8]. In contrast, de Winter-like anterior morphology without angiographic evidence of complete LAD occlusion should be interpreted cautiously, with attention to the overall clinical and angiographic context. When diagnostic STEMI criteria are already met in another territory, the co-existing de Winter-like phenotype should be framed as an additional high-risk ECG feature rather than a determinant of the culprit vessel.

The present case demonstrates inferior STEMI coexisting with de Winter-like anterior changes, a rare but an ECG interpretation challenge with respect to anterior ST-T morphology and culprit-vessel localisation. Importantly, inferior ST-segment elevation alone mandates immediate STEMI activation and emergent coronary angiography; therefore, management urgency should be driven by the STEMI criteria, and no delay should be attributed to the concurrent anterior morphology. In our patient, coronary angiography confirmed RCA sub-total/near-total occlusion as the culprit lesion, together with severe proximal LAD stenosis without complete occlusion. The initial delay in reperfusion therapy occurred because the patient declined immediate PCI, rather than due to ECG misclassification. The co-existence of inferior STEMI with de Winter-like morphology may signal extensive ischaemia or high-risk coronary anatomy. Importantly, in patients who already meet STEMI criteria, this co-existing morphology does not alter the need for emergent angiography; rather, it highlights the importance of avoiding ECG-only culprit-vessel localisation and of interpreting the anterior ST-T phenotype in conjunction with angiographic findings. Accordingly, this case reinforces a triage principle: once STEMI criteria are met, clinicians should proceed with standard emergent coronary angiography, while treating concomitant de Winter-like anterior morphology as an adjunctive marker of possible broader ischaemic burden and not as standalone proof of LAD occlusion or a second independent STEMI-equivalent event. Following RCA PCI, the absence of recurrent symptoms or dynamic ischaemic ECG changes, together with angiographic confirmation of non-occlusive LAD disease, supported a staged approach to LAD revascularisation. From a prognostic perspective, leaving a severe proximal LAD stenosis untreated after culprit-lesion PCI represents incomplete revascularisation and may maintain a residual ischaemic substrate, which has potential implications for recurrent ischaemia, reinfarction, and longer-term adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Accordingly, a staged strategy should be paired with structured follow-up and timely reassessment to minimise prolonged exposure to residual risk. Additionally, the persistent T-wave inversions were interpreted as post-ischaemic repolarisation changes rather than evidence of ongoing anterior ischaemia. Overall, in this case, the de Winter-like anterior morphology was best understood as a high-risk ECG phenotype, contextualised by culprit-vessel angiographic assessment, rather than as proof of an acute LAD occlusion or a separate anterior STEMI-equivalent event requiring immediate LAD PCI.

Several case reports have documented similar dual-territory involvement in de Winter-like syndrome [3, 9], often highlighting diagnostic difficulty and the need for heightened ECG awareness. Unlike typical STEMI evolution, the dynamic changes in this case (persistent upsloping ST-depression and hyperacute T-waves) were present over multiple days, further emphasising the variable temporal profile of the de Winter-like pattern. Taken together, these reports suggest that this co-presentation is uncommon and can be clinically confusing. This supports triage/interpretation protocols that (i) activate STEMI pathways based on diagnostic ST-elevation criteria when present, and (ii) record concomitant de Winter-like morphology as a cue to consider multivessel/high-risk anatomy without relying on ECG alone to localise the culprit vessel.

In conclusion, the classic de Winter ECG pattern is widely regarded as a critical STEMI equivalent. However, de Winter-like anterior morphology should be interpreted cautiously and described in a way that remains consistent with angiographic findings. When it coexists with inferior STEMI, clinicians should proceed with standard emergent reperfusion pathways based on STEMI criteria, and the presence of an additional anterior de Winter-like phenotype should not be assumed to represent a separate anterior STEMI-equivalent event in the absence of angiographic confirmation. Accordingly, this case is presented as an instructive ECG–angiography correlation scenario, rather than as definitive evidence that the observed anterior morphology mandates LAD occlusion. In the present patient, inferior STEMI was attributable to a mid-RCA culprit occlusion with concomitant de Winter-like anterior ST-T changes in the setting of severe but non-occlusive LAD disease; primary PCI was, therefore, directed to the angiographically confirmed culprit RCA lesion, while LAD revascularisation was planned as a staged strategy after clinical stabilisation. For triage, the key message is to maintain STEMI-driven urgency when criteria are met, while using de Winter-like morphology as a cautionary signal to avoid ECG-only culprit assumptions and to ensure comprehensive angiographic assessment.
