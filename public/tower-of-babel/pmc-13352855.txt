Hypoxia-pretreated exosomes promote enterocutaneous fistula healing via regulating inflammation and tissue repair
Authors: Yankui Liu, Beini Zhu, Hongbin Liu, Qingmei Yuan, Cong Yuan, Yijun Meng, Hong Zhou, Luming Sun, Beiyin Liu, Zhaoxiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnterocutaneous fistula (ECF) is an abnormal pathological passage. This study aims to investigate the role and mechanism of action of gelatin sponges loaded with hypoxic exosomes (GS-Hypo-Exos) in the treatment of enterocutaneous fistulas.MethodsA rat ECF model was established and subjected to intervention therapy, with the following groups: control group, gelatin sponge loaded with PBS group (GS-PBS), gelatin sponge loaded with hypoxia-exosomes group (GS-Hypo-Exos), and gelatin sponge loaded with normoxic exosomes group (GS-Nor-Exos). After 21 days of intervention, rat serum and perifistulous tissue were collected. Inflammatory response and collagen were analyzed via ELISA and histological staining. Flow cytometry determined macrophage phenotype proportions in fistula tissue. Immunofluorescence staining assessed angiogenesis. In vitro experiments further examined the effects of GS-Hypo-Exos on epithelial cell, fibroblast, endothelial cell, and macrophage function.ResultsThe GS-Hypo-Exos group demonstrated the most significant fistula healing effect. This group exhibited the lowest serum IL-1β levels and the highest IL-10 levels. Histopathological analysis revealed reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and increased collagen deposition in fistula tissues following GS-Hypo-Exos treatment. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in the proportion of M2 macrophages within the GS-Hypo-Exos group. Enhanced signaling of angiogenesis-related markers indicated increased mature vascular structures. In vitro experiments confirmed that GS-Hypo-Exos promoted HaCaT cell proliferation and migration, enhanced fibroblast collagen synthesis capacity, improved endothelial cell tube formation ability, and drove macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype.ConclusionsThe GS-Hypo-Exos suppress inflammatory responses, enhance the function of key repair cells, promote collagen deposition and neovascularization, thereby creating conditions for fistula tissue repair.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12860-026-00592-w.Keywords: Enterocutaneous fistula, Exosomes, Hypoxia, Macrophage polarization

Introduction

An enterocutaneous fistula (ECF) is an abnormal communication between the intestine and skin most commonly due to surgery, inflammatory bowel disease, an intra- abdominal infection, or trauma [1–3]. A distinctive characteristic of ECF is the extrusion of gut contents through the fistula tract onto the skin surface, which results in loss of nutrients and electrolytes, and increased risk of infection [4–6]. The primary focus of ECF management currently is on managing the infection, optimizing nutritional status and preparing for the surgery conditions [7–9]. The initial line of treatment is usually conservative, which may comprise total parenteral nutrition or fistula feeding to alleviate metabolic imbalance, antibiotic therapy to check infection, and anti-inflammatory drugs [10–12]. The surgical option is reserved for obstinate cases [13, 14].

Exosome therapy, as an emergent biological therapy, is characterized by core advantages of high biocompatibility as well as low toxicity [15]. Exosomes contain multiple cytokines that can simultaneously regulate apoptosis, oxidative stress, and angiogenesis processes [16–18]. Besides, through engineering modifications, exosomes can achieve accurate tissue targeting that enhances therapeutic efficacy while minimising side effects [19]. Exosomes have been shown to promote epithelial regeneration and angiogenesis, as well as inhibit inflammation and scar formation in wound healing [20–23]. Exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells have important anti-inflammatory impact and intestinal barrier repair [24]. At the same time, exosomes treated with hypoxia have demonstrated greater efficacy in tissue repair [25]. However, direct studies on the use of hypoxia-treated exosomes for fistula treatment remain scarce, warranting further exploration. However, because exosomes have a short half-life and are easily cleared from the body, gelatin sponges are a relatively safe biomaterial and serve as an ideal carrier for exosomes.

In this study, a rat model of ECF was established to investigate the molecular mechanism by which gelatin sponge loaded with hypoxia-preconditioned exosomes (GS-Hypo-Exos) exerts its therapeutic effect on ECF, aiming to provide novel insights for the clinical management of ECF.

Materials and methods

Animal model

All animals were purchased from Hunan SLAKE Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Twenty-four male SPF SD rats (200 ± 20 g) were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 6). After one week of acclimatization, all rats underwent surgical cecostomy to establish an ECF model (Supplementary file 1). Group A served as the control with no treatment. Groups B–D received fistula tract implantation of gelatin sponge loaded with lyophilized PBS, hypoxia-preconditioned exosomes, and normoxic exosomes, respectively. After 21 days of treatment, rats were anesthetized with propofol (10 mg/kg) and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Serum was collected for ELISA analysis of inflammatory cytokines, and fistula tract tissues were harvested for TUNEL staining.

Cell culture

The HaCaT, human dermal fibroblasts, human dermal microvascular endothelial cells, and RAW 264.7 cells were obtained from the Kunming Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Kunming, China). HaCaT cells, human dermal fibroblasts, and RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Thermo Fisher, Shanghai China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Human dermal microvascular endothelial cells were cultured in ECM medium containing 10% FBS and growth factors. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. For the experiment, the control group of cells received no treatment, while the treatment group was incubated with gelatin sponge-loaded lyophilized hypoxia-preconditioned exosomes (GS-Hypo-Exos). After 24 h of treatment, functional assays were performed to evaluate cellular responses.

Tissue staining

To observe the pathological features of fistula tissues, including morphology and inflammatory infiltration, hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining was performed. To assess collagen levels in the tissues, Masson’s trichrome staining was performed. Histological evaluation was performed under an optical microscope.

Cell proliferation and migration

The viability of HaCaT cells was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Remove the 96-well plate, aspirate the original medium, and add 100 µL of basal medium to each well. Set up a blank control group. Add 10 µL of CCK-8 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) solution to each well. Place the culture plate in a 37 °C incubator and incubate in the dark for 2 h. Measure the optical density (OD) of each well at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

To evaluate the effect of hypoxic pretreated exosomes on the migration capacity of HaCaT cells, a cell scratch assay was performed. Digest and collect cells using 0.25% trypsin, then adjust the cell density to 2.5 × 10⁴ cells/mL. Add 100 µL of cell suspension to the left and right wells of the Ibidi scratch assay insert, with 3 replicate wells per group. After overnight culture until approximately 95% confluence, remove the insert, gently rinse once with basal medium, then add fresh basal medium for continued culture. Photograph scratch closure every 12 h under a microscope.

Tube formation assay

In order to explore the effect of hypoxic pretreated exosomes on the tube forming ability of human skin microvascular endothelial cells, tube forming experiments were carried out. Cells were harvested by 0.25% trypsin digestion. After counting, the cell concentration was adjusted to 2 × 10⁵ cells /ml. Subsequently, 500 µ L of cell suspension was seeded into the chamber containing Matrigel. After thorough mixing, continue to incubate for five hours.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using a lysis buffer containing 100× protease inhibitor. The proteins were separated by 12.5% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was incubated sequentially with specific primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Target protein bands were detected using Clarity Western ECL substrate for chemiluminescent development. β-Actin was used as the internal reference protein.

Flow cytometry

Cells from RAW 264.7 and those treated with lyophilized hypoxic exosomes loaded on gelatin sponge were collected. After Fc blocking, label and stain the cells with antibodies. Prior to analysis on a BD LSRFortessa™ X-20 Cell Analyzer, the cells were resuspended in 500 µL of PBS buffer containing 3% FBS.

Immunofluorescence

Tissue sections were deparaffinized at 60 °C for 30 min, rehydrated via xylene and graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by microwave heating. RAW 264.7 cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. Both samples were blocked with goat serum for 1 h, incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, and then with secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 1 h. After DAPI staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China), sections were mounted and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Results

GS-Hypo-Exos promote wound healing and reduce inflammatory response

HE staining results demonstrated that groups C and D exhibited reduced inflammatory cell infiltration compared to groups A and B (Fig. 1a-d). Masson staining revealed extensive blue-stained collagen fiber proliferation in the exosome-treated groups (Fig. 1e-h). TUNEL results indicated abundant brown-positive signals in groups A and B, while the apoptotic index in group C was significantly lower than that in groups A and B (P < 0.001, Fig. 1i). Exosome therapy effectively promotes tissue repair and reduces inflammation and apoptosis.

GS-Hypo-Exos treatment most effectively enhances collagen deposition, reduces inflammatory infiltration, and inhibits apoptosis, thereby significantly promoting tissue repair in ECF. GS-Hypo-Exos mediated modulation of inflammation and macrophage polarization

ELISA was used to measure the expression levels of IL-1β and IL-10 in serum. The results showed that group C exhibited the lowest level of IL-1β and the highest level of IL-10 (Fig. 2a-b). To determine whether exosome treatment influenced macrophage phenotype switching in fistula tissues, flow cytometry was employed to identify cells based on M1 macrophage markers (CD86, iNOS) and M2 macrophage markers (CD206, ARG-1) (Fig. 2c-d). The results demonstrated that compared with the control group, group C had the lowest proportion of M1 macrophages and the highest proportion of M2 macrophages, indicating that exosome treatment effectively promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 anti-inflammatory/reparative phenotype.

Hypoxia-preconditioned exosomes modulate the expression of inflammatory factors and drive macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. A: Group A (Blank), B: Group B (GS-PBS), C: Group C (GS-Hypo-Exos), D: Group D (GS-Nor-Exos). (a) Detection of inflammatory factors (IL-1β) in serum by ELISA; (b): Detection of inflammatory factors (IL-10) in serum by ELISA; (c) Flow cytometry detection of macrophage (M1) markers; (d) Flow cytometry detection of macrophage (M2) markers. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, ns represents not significant

Hypoxic exosomes promote angiogenesis

Results of immunofluorescence staining showed that in groups A and B, abundant CD31-positive signals (red) were observed in the peri-fistula tissues, while α-SMA-positive signals (green) were sporadically distributed (Fig. 3a). In the exosome-treated groups (C and D), numerous α-SMA-positive signals (green) were detected, and the majority of CD31 signals (red) were closely surrounded by α-SMA signals (green) (Fig. 3a). In the positivity rate analysis, the CD31 positivity rates in Groups C and D were significantly lower than those in Groups A and B (Fig. 3b), while the α-SMA positivity rate in Group A was significantly higher than those in Groups C and D (Fig. 3c).

The exosome-treated group exhibited more mature vascular characteristics compared to the control group. A: Group A (Blank), B: Group B (GS-PBS), C: Group C (GS-Hypo-Exos), D: Group D (GS-Nor-Exos). (b) Positive rate of CD31 in immunofluorescence detection; (c) Positive rate of α-SMA in immunofluorescence detection. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, ns represents not significant

GS-Hypo-Exos promote HaCaT proliferation and migration

To evaluate the effect of GS-Hypo-Exos on the proliferation of HaCaT cells, a CCK-8 assay was used to measure cell viability in each group. The results demonstrated that the cell viability of HaCaT cells in the GS-Hypo-Exos treated group was significantly higher than that in the control group (Fig. 4a). To further assess cell migration ability, a scratch wound assay was conducted. The results showed that cell migration rate in the GS-Hypo-Exos group reached 100% after 24 h (Fig. 4b), and within 48 h, the migration rate of HaCaT cells in the GS-Hypo-Exos treated group was significantly greater than that in the control group (Fig. 4c).

GS-Hypo-Exos enhance HaCaT viability and migratory capacity. (a) Cell proliferation by CCK8 assay, **P < 0.01; (b) 24-hour cell migration observation; (c) Cell migration assessment, The horizontal axis represents time, and the vertical axis represents cell mobility

Effects of GS-Hypo-Exos on fibroblast and endothelial cell functions

To evaluate the effect of GS-Hypo-Exos on fibroblast function, WB analysis was performed to detect the expression levels of collagen in fibroblasts treated with GS-Hypo-Exos compared to control group fibroblasts. The results showed that the expression of both Collagen I and Collagen III was significantly higher in the GS-Hypo-Exos treated fibroblasts than in the control group (Fig. 5a-d). To assess the impact of hypoxic exosomes on the tube-forming ability of endothelial cells, endothelial cells were treated with GS-Hypo-Exos. The results revealed that, compared to the untreated group, the GS-Hypo-Exos treated endothelial cells exhibited denser branch connections and mesh-like structures(Fig. 5e-f).

GS-Hypo-Exos drive macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype

To evaluate the effect of GS-Hypo-Exos on macrophage polarization, flow cytometry with dual-color antibody staining and double immunofluorescence staining were employed to analyze phenotypic changes in RAW 264.7 cells treated with GS-Hypo-Exos compared to untreated RAW 264.7 cells. Analysis of M1 phenotype by flow cytometry showed that, compared to the untreated group, the proportion of CD86⁺ iNOS⁺ double-positive cells was significantly reduced in the GS-Hypo-Exos group (Fig. 6a). Evaluation of the M2 phenotype revealed that the percentage of CD206⁺ ARG-1⁺ double-positive cells was markedly higher in the exosome-treated group than in the untreated group (Fig. 6b).

GS-Hypo-Exos treatment significantly suppressed M1 macrophage polarization while promoting M2 polarization. (a) Detection of macrophage (M1) markers by flow cytometry; (b) Detection of macrophage (M2) markers by flow cytometry; (c-d) Immunofluorescence staining of macrophage (M1); (e-f) Immunofluorescence staining of macrophage (M2). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, ns represents not significant

Immunofluorescence analysis of the M1 phenotype demonstrated scattered CD86 (green) and iNOS (red) signals in the untreated group, with a small number of cells exhibiting yellow co-localization signals. In the exosome-treated group, the fluorescence intensities of both CD86 and iNOS were significantly weakened, and clear yellow co-localization signals were rarely observed under high-power fields (Fig. 6c). Quantification of CD86⁺ iNOS⁺ double-positive cells across multiple fields confirmed that their proportion was significantly lower in the exosome-treated group (Fig. 6d).

Analysis of the M2 phenotype indicated weak and scattered CD206 (green) and ARG-1 (red) signals in the untreated group (Fig. 6e), with a small population of cells in a baseline M2 state. In contrast, the GS-Hypo-Exos group exhibited significantly enhanced fluorescence intensities of both CD206 and ARG-1. In the merged channels, these signals highly overlapped, producing bright yellow fluorescence—a characteristic feature of M2 macrophages. The proportion of CD206⁺ ARG-1⁺ double-positive cells was significantly increased in the GS-Hypo-Exos group (Fig. 6f).

Discussion

The standard therapy of ECF consists of nutritional support, controlling the infection, and surgical intervention but the process is protracted and carries high surgical failure [26, 27]. The goal of this research is to explore the effects and mechanisms of GS-Hypo-Exos preconditioning on ECF healing.

The occurrence of ECF may occur spontaneously or can arise as a complication of surgical trauma, malignancy or inflammatory bowel disease [28, 29]. According to the data, tissues around the fistula will have significant inflammatory infiltration which is characterized by bleeding, swelling, pain as well as exudative skin lesion formation [30–33]. Leakage of gut content across the fistula causes soiling of the peritoneal cavity or subcutaneous tissue inducing local or systemic infection [34, 35]. According to a study conducted in 2020, sepsis has grown to be among the leading causes of death in patients of ECF whose mortality may even go high as 15% to 20% [36]. Inflammatory mediators produced by pathogens also lead to metabolic dysfunctions and malnutrition, continuing the cycle. Hence, regulation of the inflammatory response must be done to manage ECF. Our experimentation has demonstrated that both serum tests and histopathology proven that the GS-Hypo-Exos possessed more anti-inflammatory power as they lower inflammatory states the most.

As key regulators of the innate immune system, macrophages play a vital role in tissue repair [37, 38]. During the formation of a fistula, the site of injury gets inflamed due to the recruitment of large numbers of macrophages (M1). A wide range of matrix metalloproteinases and inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, are highly expressed by these cells to clear necrotic tissue and pathogens [39–42]. The polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype is crucial for tissue repair. According to our findings, in both in vivo and in vitro studies, the GS-Hypo-Exos group exhibited lower levels of M1 macrophages compared to the control group, confirming that GS-Hypo-Exos possesses potent anti-inflammatory effects. At the same time, the increase in M2 macrophages suggests that GS-Hypo-Exos exerts its tissue-repairing effects through macrophage polarization.

The closure of the abdominal wall is a protective factor against ECF recurrence. The mechanism of this protective effect is related to the deposition of fibrofblast-mediated collagen [43–45]. Research conducted on teduglutide (a GLP-2 analog) for ECF treatment has shown reduced fistula drainage and spontaneous closure [46]. GLP-2, which promotes intestinal angiogenesis and mucosal repair, suggests that angiogenesis may play an indirect role in enhancing fibroblast function by improving local blood supply and nutrient delivery, which may enhance fistula closure [47, 48]. Likewise, in ECF, PRP can also stimulate angiogenesis and collagen synthesis, which occurs through a release of growth factors and modulation of fibroblasts that promote granulation and vascularization [49–52]. Thus, fibroblast-mediated collagen deposition and angiogenesis monitoring helps assess tissue repair progress during ECF treatment and may provide a theoretical basis for dissecting novel therapies. Based on our study’s findings, GS-Hypo-Exos significantly enhance the collagen synthesis capacity of fibroblast, modulate angiogenesis, and tissue repair. In in vivo studies, Masson’s staining revealed distinct collagen staining in fistula tissue from the GS-Hypo-Exos group. In vitro experiments showed that GS-Hypo-Exos significantly promoted collagen expression in fibroblasts.

Exosomes that are hypoxic have major benefits in therapeutic tissue repair. Exosomes generated under hypoxic preconditioning promote endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and capillary-like structures formation to speed healing-related vascular regeneration at wound sites, with superior efficacy than exosomes from normoxic culturing [53–55]. Hypoxic preconditioning enhances exosome yield and internalization efficiency, but it may also be combined with a gel-based sustained-release system to prolong the duration of action of the exosomes [55]. Hypoxic exosomes have shown promise in skin wounds repair, nerve regeneration, cardiac repair and dental pulp angiogenesis in various types of tissue repair [56, 57]. In our study, GS-Hypo-Exos significantly promoted HaCaT cell proliferation and facilitated endothelial cell tubule formation. Hypoxic exosomes overcome the limitations of conventional exosomes, such as a short lifespan and low yield in vitro, and are highly biocompatible, supporting the advancement of cell-free therapies.

Conclusions

The findings of this research validates that GS-Hypo-Exos significantly enhance healing of the ECF. The principal mechanism consists of the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, leading to the inhibition of local inflammation, as evidenced by decreased IL-1β and increased IL-10 levels. Exosomes further stimulate fibroblasts to synthesize and deposit collagen, enhancing the formation of granulation tissue and tissue remodelling. It also helps to improve the ability of endothelial cells to form tubular structures to generate mature vascular networks. Also, the epithelial cells’ proliferation and migration were induced by exosomes, thus enhancing healing of tissue. To sum up, GS-Hypo-Exos give rise to a promising biological strategy for ECF treatment through the multi-pronged mechanisms that they employ, including synergistic regulations of the immune microenvironment, angiogenesis promotion, and tissue repair enhancement.
