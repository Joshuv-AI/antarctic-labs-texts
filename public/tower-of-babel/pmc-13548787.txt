Material consequences: an initial assessment of the influence of flooring type on lactating sow thermoregulation in summer heat
Authors: Jay S Johnson, Ashley Rodman, Caitlyn R Sullivan, Carley Hopkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat stress (HS) impairs lactating sows’ ability to provide adequate nutrition for their litters, prompting the development of management practices and new housing systems to alleviate its effects. However, few have investigated materials used in farrowing crate construction and their influence on sows’ ability to remain euthermic. Therefore, the study objective was to assess the association of metal (MTL) versus plastic (PLS) crate flooring with sow euthermia and litter performance under natural summer HS conditions. Landrace x Large White lactating sows and their litters (N = 23) were housed in individual crates in a single barn containing one room with MTL (n = 12) and one room with PLS (n = 11). Temperature and relative humidity were measured in 5 min intervals and were similar between the MTL (25.7 ± 1.8°C and 88.0 ± 5.9%, respectively) and PLS (25.4 ± 1.9°C and 86.2 ± 6.2%, respectively) rooms. All sows were provided ad libitum feed and water and piglets were cross fostered to standardize litter sizes (12.0 ± 1.4 piglets/litter). Sow vaginal temperature (TV) was assessed in 10 min intervals daily, and respiration rate (RR; breaths per minute [bpm]) and ear, shoulder, rump, and tail skin temperatures were measured at 0800, 1200, 1600, and 2000 h daily during the last 7 d of lactation. Sow average daily feed intake (ADFI) was measured weekly and litter weights were determined on d 1, 7, 14, and at weaning (d 19.7 ± 1.8) to assess average daily gain. Piglet mortality was monitored starting at d 2 of lactation immediately post-crossfostering and continued throughout the trial. The sow was considered the experimental unit for sow-based measures, and litter was considered the experimental unit for litter-based measures. Parity was similar (P = 0.60) between MTL (2.25 ± 0.86) and PLS (2.88 ± 0.90) sows. Sow ADFI was increased (P < 0.01; 0.98 kg/d) for MTL versus PLS sows. Greater TV (P < 0.01; 0.08°C) was detected for MTL versus PLS sows. Respiration rate was increased (P < 0.01; 10 bpm) at 2000 h for MTL versus PLS sows. Ear and shoulder skin temperatures were greater (P < 0.01; 0.34 and 0.65°C, respectively) in MTL versus PLS sows. Average daily gain was similar (P = 0.55; 249.3 ± 11.6 g/d) for MTL and PLS litters. Mortality was greater overall (P < 0.01) for PLS (20.25 ± 5.14%) versus MTL (10.77 ± 5.44%) litters. In summary, MTL increased sow ADFI which may have influenced TV, RR, and skin temperatures, and was associated with lower pre-weaning mortality in litters.

Introduction

Heat stress (HS) continues to be one of the most persistent and costly challenges in modern swine production, particularly during lactation when sows are under high metabolic demand and produce substantial body heat to support milk production (Johnson et al. 2022). When environmental conditions limit heat dissipation, lactating sows rely on a combination of behavioral and physiological mechanisms to maintain thermal balance, including increasing respiration rate, heat dissipation through the skin, and interacting more with waterers (Johnson et al. 2022; Byrd et al. 2025). Although these responses can help limit total heat load, they often come at the expense of productivity, with HS commonly reducing feed intake and negatively affecting piglet growth and litter survival (Johnson et al. 2022).

Producers have adopted a range of strategies to mitigate HS in lactating sows, including ventilation systems (Godyń et al. 2020; Johnson and Stewart 2025), floor cooling (Godyń et al. 2020; Johnson et al. 2022), and diet formulations (Zhang et al. 2020). However, one factor that has received far less attention is the role of farrowing crate materials in balancing heat production with heat loss in the lactating sow. Because crate flooring serves as a direct interface between the sow and her environment, it may represent an important pathway for conductive heat loss. Flooring type, therefore, may directly influence how effectively a sow can dissipate heat, with downstream consequences for sow physiology, piglet survival, and overall litter performance.

Metal (MTL) flooring, because of its greater thermal conductivity than plastic (PLS) flooring (Ghasemi et al. 2014), may facilitate greater conductive heat transfer away from the sow. Anecdotal observations suggest that, relative to PLS flooring, MTL flooring may improve sow thermoregulation and help maintain productivity during periods of HS. Although previous studies have examined the effects of flooring materials on piglet behavior, comfort, preweaning survival (Lewis et al. 2005; de Oliveira Possagnolo et al. 2024), as well as sow feed intake, body condition, and reproductive efficiency (Johnston et al. 1987), no studies to our knowledge have evaluated whether farrowing-crate flooring influences sow thermoregulatory capacity during the summer.

To better understand this relationship, this study evaluated whether MTL versus PLS farrowing crate flooring directly under the lactating sow would alter the sow’s ability to maintain thermal balance under natural summer heat load in Missouri, USA. Based on previous reports that sows housed on concrete flooring consumed more feed than those housed on plastisol-coated metal flooring (Johnston et al. 1987), we hypothesized that more thermally conductive MTL flooring would enhance conductive heat dissipation and help sows maintain euthermia relative to PLS flooring during late lactation, when metabolic heat production is higher and susceptibility to HS is increased (Johnson et al. 2019, 2022).

Materials and methods

Animals and treatments

The University of Missouri Animal Care and Use Committee approved all procedures involving live animals (#65243). Animal care and use standards were based upon the Guide for the Care and Use of Agricultural Animals in Research and Teaching (Federation of Animal Science Societies 2020). Landrace x Large White sows and their litters (N = 23) were housed in individual crates in a single farrowing barn at the University of Missouri Swine Research Complex (Columbia, MO USA) from June 19, 2025 to July 11, 2025. The farrowing barn contained two identical rooms with 12 farrowing crates in each room and were both mechanically ventilated with evaporative cooling pads set to the same high ventilation temperature setpoint (27.22°C). In one room, farrowing crates (1.52 × 2.13 m) had MTL flooring (cast-iron slatted flooring plates) placed directly under the lactating sow (n = 12; parity distribution: 1 [n = 9], 3 [n = 1], 6 [n = 1], 9 [n = 1]) and one room had PLS flooring (plastic grates) directly under the lactating sows (n = 11; parity distribution: 1 [n = 7], 2 [n = 1], 5 [n = 1], 7 [n = 1], and 11 [n = 1]) as illustrated in Fig. 1. The remaining flooring material within the crates was constructed from PLS, and piglets were provided with supplementary heat using heat lamps (Fig. 1).

Schematic of the flooring dimensions and materials in MTL and PLS farrowing crates.

All sows received a diet consisting primarily of corn, soybean meal, distillers dried grains with solubles, and corn oil (1.5%), which was formulated to meet or exceed nutrient requirements for lactating sows and fed ad libitum (NRC 2012). Sows were provided with water ad libitum via a nipple waterer. Furthermore, piglets were cross fostered within 48 h of birth to standardize litter size across both treatment rooms yielding 12.0 ± 1.4 piglets/litter.

Measurements

Environmental conditions including dry bulb temperature (TDB) and relative humidity (RH) were monitored in 5 min intervals 24 h per day using two data loggers (Hobo logger model UX100-003; accuracy = 3.5%; resolution = 0.024°C at 25°C; Bourne, Maine, USA) mounted at sow head height within each room. Throughout the trial, TDB within the MTL and PLS rooms were 25.7 ± 1.8°C and 25.4 ± 1.9°C, respectively, and RH was 88.0 ± 5.9% and 86.2 ± 6.2%, respectively (Fig. 2). Litter characteristics including total born, live born, still born and mummies were recorded on a per sow basis at farrowing. Litters were standardized through crossfostering within the first 48 h post-farrowing. Sows were hand fed and feed disappearance was assessed weekly throughout lactation by subtracting the amount of feed weighed back from the amount offered and was used as an indicator of average daily feed intake (ADFI). Feeders were checked twice daily (0800 and 2000 h), and any wet or soiled feed was removed, weighed, and discarded before fresh feed was provided. Piglet body weight (BW) was determined post-farrowing on d 1, 7, 14, and 19.7 ± 1.8 (weaning) to calculate average daily gain (ADG). Pre-weaning mortality was recorded continuously throughout the study on a litter basis and was calculated by subtracting the number of piglets weaned from the post-cross-fostering litter size on d 2 and dividing the difference by the post-cross-fostering litter size beginning on d 2 of lactation. This approach was used to specifically evaluate mortality occurring during the lactation period after litter standardization, rather than mortality associated with farrowing, congenital abnormalities, low birth weight, or other factors occurring before cross-fostering. Additionally, weekly mortality was calculated as the number of piglets that died during each lactation week divided by the number of piglets alive at the beginning of that week, thereby accounting for changes in the population over time.

The a) TDB within the MTL room, b) TDB within the PLS room, c) RH within the MTL room, and d) RH within the PLS room by study day. The dashed line represents the period of time when the TMP occurred.

In the last 7 d of lactation prior to weaning, thermoregulatory measures were recorded on all sows. This period will herein be referred to as the thermoregulatory measurement period (TMP) and was selected to represent the phase in which sows were likely to be most HS sensitive due to an increase in total heat production as a result of increased milk production (Johnson et al. 2019, 2022). Thermoregulatory measures including vaginal temperature (TV), respiration rate (RR), and skin temperature of the ear (TEar), shoulder (TShoulder), rump (TRump), and tail (TTail) were determined in accordance with our previously described protocols (Johnson et al. 2022; Byrd et al. 2025). Briefly, TEar, TShoulder, TRump, TTail, and RR were recorded daily at 0800, 1200, 1600, and 2000 h. Skin temperatures were measured with an infrared temperature gun (Raytek model ST61; accuracy = ± 1%; emissivity = 0.98; resolution = 0.10°C; Raytek Corporation, Fluke Process Instruments, Everett, WA, USA) held at a consistent 1 m distance from a dry, clean, and shaved skin location. Respiration rates were measured by counting flank movements for 15 s and multiplied by four to determine breaths per minute (bpm) in sows laying sternally or laterally. Finally, TV was recorded in 10 min intervals using a data logger (DST milli-T; manufacturer calibrated accuracy ± 0.10°C; resolution = 0.032°C; Star Oddi; Gardabaer, Iceland) attached to a blank controlled internal drug-releasing device (Eazi-Breed CIDR Sheep; Zoetis U.S.; Parsippany, NJ, USA).

Statistical analyses

All data were analyzed using PROC GLIMMIX in SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA). Sow was the experimental unit for sow parity, all thermoregulatory measures, and sow ADFI. Litter was the experimental unit for litter ADG, BW, and pre-weaning mortality measures. Assumptions of residual normality, homogeneity of variance, and linearity were evaluated post hoc. Residual normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and observations identified as outliers during residual diagnostics were removed when appropriate. Flooring treatment (MTL vs. PLS), week, and their interaction were included as fixed effects for sow ADFI and litter ADG. Flooring treatment, study day, hour, and their interactions were included as fixed effects for sow thermoregulatory measures. Flooring treatment was included as the fixed effect for litter characteristics and overall post-cross-fostering pre-weaning mortality. Overall mortality was calculated as the number of piglets that died from d 2 of lactation through weaning relative to the post-cross-fostering litter size. Weekly mortality was calculated relative to the number of piglets alive at the beginning of each week and was analyzed with flooring treatment, week, and their interaction as fixed effects. Sow parity was included as a covariate in all statistical models. Total born was included as a covariate in the analysis of piglet d 1 BW. Piglet d 1 BW was included as a covariate when analyzing piglet ADG, piglet BW, and piglet weaning BW. Covariates were removed from the final models if identified as non-significant (P > 0.05). Study day or week were used as repeated measures, and covariance structure was selected based on goodness of fit criteria using the structure with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion value (Littell et al. 1998). Effects of week, day, and hour are only presented or discussed when they interact with flooring treatment and when differences were detected within the same week, day, or hour. This approach was used because temporal changes in litter growth and lactation performance were expected and were not central to the study objective; therefore, emphasis was placed on time effects only when they modified the response to flooring treatment. Lactation day was considered as a random effect for sow thermoregulatory measures. Data are presented as least squares means (LSmeans) ± SE. Statistical significance was considered at P ≤ 0.05 and a tendency was defined as 0.05 < P ≤ 0.10.

Discussion

Continuously recorded TDB and RH were similar between flooring treatment rooms throughout the study, suggesting that farrowing room environment differences were unlikely to explain the observed sow and litter responses. Additionally, sow parity and litter characteristics at farrowing and after cross-fostering, including piglet d 1 BW, also did not differ statistically between treatments, indicating that sows and litters were generally comparable at the beginning of the lactation measurement period. It should be mentioned that installation required each flooring system to be evaluated in a separate room, so unmeasured room-level influences cannot be completely excluded and flooring effects cannot be fully separated from room effects. However, interspersing flooring treatments within a single room was not technically feasible, and the absence of differences in the measured environment and initial biological characteristics reduces evidence that these factors accounted for the subsequent responses. Accordingly, the data should be interpreted as treatment-associated responses under the specific conditions of the present study.

One of the most apparent flooring treatment effects was observed for sow ADFI, as MTL sows consumed more feed than PLS sows overall throughout lactation. Greater feed intake has similarly been reported in lactating sows housed on concrete compared with plastisol-coated metal flooring, supporting the possibility that flooring materials with greater thermal conductivity may improve feed intake during periods of elevated heat load (Johnston et al. 1987). This response may imply that MTL flooring could have improved conductive heat transfer from the sow, thereby allowing greater nutrient intake during summer heat load, as lactating sows typically reduce feed intake under higher temperatures to limit metabolic heat production and the associated thermal burden (Williams et al. 2013; Cecil et al. 2025). Therefore, it is possible that improved heat loss capacity may explain the greater ADFI of MTL sows during lactation under the conditions of the present study.

The greater feed intake in MTL sows was likely accompanied by increased heat production associated with digestion, nutrient absorption, and metabolism, and may have also supported greater lactogenesis (Strathe et al. 2017; Cecil et al. 2025). Consequently, although increased ADFI is favorable from a production standpoint, it was also likely accompanied by a greater internal heat load. This increase in ADFI, and likely metabolic heat production, may explain the TV response, which was greater in MTL than PLS sows across the TMP. Although the magnitude of this difference was moderate, core temperature in endotherms is tightly regulated, with even small increases impacting biological capacity under hotter conditions (Curtis 1983; Blatteis 1998). Therefore, the greater TV observed in MTL sows may have reflected the thermoregulatory cost of maintaining higher ADFI during lactation. Rather than demonstrating a net cooling effect of MTL flooring during the last week of lactation, these results could suggest that any conductive heat loss provided by the flooring may have been counterbalanced by the greater internal heat load generated from increased ADFI.

In addition to modestly increased TV, RR and skin temperature responses may support the interpretation that greater ADFI resulted in an increased thermal load for MTL sows. Specifically, MTL sows exhibited greater RR at 2000 h, when room TDB was declining, as well as greater overall TEar and TShoulder, indicating that these animals likely mounted a greater physiological response to dissipate excess body heat. In pigs, increased RR is one of the primary mechanisms used to increase evaporative heat loss during thermal challenge due to a lack of functional sweat glands (Ingram 1967). Likewise, increased skin temperature is generally interpreted as evidence of increased blood flow to the body surface to facilitate heat loss from the core (Blatteis 1998). Taken together, the greater RR, TEar, and TShoulder in MTL sows could suggest that the increase in TV stimulated a stronger heat dissipation response in an attempt to offset the additional internally generated heat from increased ADFI. However, because metabolic heat production and the timing of feed consumption were not directly measured, this mechanism remains speculative. An alternative explanation for the greater TEar and TShoulder of MTL relative to PLS sows is that PLS sows may have experienced greater evaporative cooling through behavioral wetting of the head and shoulders. The PLS sows were anecdotally observed to be interacting more frequently with their waterers during the hottest periods of the day (eg 1200 and 1600 h), although waterer use was not quantified. Consequently, behavioral wetting cannot be identified as the direct cause of the skin temperature differences and should be evaluated in follow-up studies. Based on the available data, the thermoregulatory profile of MTL sows may therefore reflect a physiological response to greater internally generated heat, potentially associated with increased ADFI, rather than necessarily indicating greater HS.

Despite these sow-level differences, piglet BW and ADG did not differ between flooring treatments throughout lactation, indicating that the greater ADFI of MTL sows was not associated with a detectable increase in piglet growth rate. These data are consistent with Johnston et al. (1987), who reported greater feed intake by lactating sows housed on concrete compared with plastisol-coated metal flooring without corresponding differences in litter growth. However, the absence of a flooring effect on piglet growth should be considered alongside the lower overall preweaning mortality percentage observed in MTL litters. Although the flooring treatment by week interaction was not significant, the numerical separation between treatments was most apparent during the first 2 wk of lactation, when preweaning losses were greater in PLS litters. Thus, the significant overall treatment effect appeared to be driven primarily by losses occurring during early lactation, although a week-specific flooring effect cannot be established statistically. The number of piglets weaned did not differ between flooring treatments, indicating that MTL litters achieved similar piglet growth and weaning litter size with a lower overall proportion of preweaning losses. The greater ADFI of MTL sows was therefore not expressed as increased piglet growth, but it occurred alongside improved piglet survival. Because milk production, sow body weight, and backfat depth were not measured, whether greater ADFI contributed directly to the mortality response or influenced maternal energy balance cannot be determined.

The greater preweaning mortality observed in PLS litters was a practically important production finding. However, the study was designed and powered primarily to detect differences in sow thermoregulatory responses rather than litter-level preweaning mortality; therefore, the mortality response should be considered a secondary finding that warrants confirmation in studies specifically designed to evaluate piglet survival. Sow parity, litter characteristics, and piglet d 1 BW did not differ between flooring treatments, providing little evidence that the mortality response was attributable to measured sow- or litter-level differences present immediately after farrowing and before the measurement period began. While the flooring treatment by week interaction was not significant, the numerical separation between treatments was most apparent during the weeks 1 and 2 of lactation, likely indicating that much of the overall treatment difference occurred during early lactation. Because flooring can influence piglet comfort, sow behavior, and piglet access to the sow, differences in the physical or thermal characteristics of the farrowing environment represent potential reasons by which piglet survival could have been affected (Lewis et al. 2005). However, the flooring contact in the present study was limited to the portion of the crate directly beneath the sow rather than extending across the sow and piglet areas, as occurred in previous flooring studies (Johnston et al. 1987; Lewis et al. 2005; de Oliveira Possagnolo et al. 2024). Consequently, any treatment-related influence may have occurred through localized conditions beneath the sow or through the sow-piglet interface, including the potential effects on sow posture or behavior and piglet udder access. However, these mechanisms remain speculative as sow posture or behavior was not directly measured and cannot be distinguished in the present data set. Moreover, because each flooring treatment was installed in a separate room, an influence of unmeasured room-level factors cannot be completely excluded, although measured environmental conditions, initial litter characteristics, and post-cross-fostering litter size did not differ between rooms. Taken together, the results demonstrate that the PLS condition was associated with reduced maternal ADFI and greater overall post-crossfostering preweaning mortality under summer conditions, but the mechanism underlying the mortality response remains to be determined.

Taken together, these results demonstrate concurrent differences in sow ADFI and thermoregulatory responses under summer conditions. The MTL flooring did not reduce physiological indicators of thermal load. Instead, MTL sows consumed more feed and exhibited modestly greater TV, RR, TEar, and TShoulder. This thermoregulatory profile is consistent with greater heat-dissipation effort, potentially in response to additional internally generated heat associated with greater ADFI. Additionally, MTL litters maintained similar piglet growth and weaning litter size while experiencing a lower overall percentage of preweaning mortality from the post-crossfostering period (d 2 of lactation) to weaning. Thus, the practical implication of MTL flooring may not be improved sow euthermia, but rather the maintenance of greater ADFI and favorable litter outcomes despite modestly greater physiological heat-dissipation responses.

An important consideration in interpreting these data is that the study was designed and powered primarily to detect differences in sow thermoregulatory responses rather than litter-level preweaning mortality. Although the lower mortality observed in MTL litters represents a practically important secondary finding, the magnitude and temporal pattern of this response should be confirmed in studies specifically powered to evaluate piglet survival. In addition, because each flooring treatment was installed in a separate room, unmeasured room-level influences cannot be completely excluded, although measured environmental conditions, sow level characteristics, and initial litter characteristics did not differ between rooms. Within these considerations, this study indicates that flooring beneath the sow may influence both maternal and litter responses and support further evaluation of flooring systems as a strategy for maintaining lactational performance and piglet survival during periods of summer heat load.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the flooring treatment beneath the sow was associated with differences in maternal and litter outcomes during lactation under summer heat load. Compared with the PLS treatment, the MTL treatment was associated with greater sow ADFI, modestly greater TV, RR, TEar, and TShoulder, and lower overall post-cross-fostering preweaning mortality, while piglet growth and the number of piglets weaned were similar. The thermoregulatory profile of MTL sows (eg greater RR, TEar, and TShoulder) was consistent with greater heat-dissipation efforts, potentially associated with the additional internally generated heat accompanying greater ADFI, rather than necessarily indicating greater HS. Future studies specifically powered to confirm flooring-related differences in preweaning mortality should also incorporate sow behavioral assessments, direct measurements of conductive heat transfer and flooring surface temperature, and detailed evaluation of feeding behavior, metabolic heat production, and sow–piglet interactions to clarify possible mechanisms linking flooring treatment with maternal thermoregulation and piglet survival.
