Quantum tunneling and anti-tunneling across entropic barriers
Paolo Malgaretti, Francesco Petiziol, Alexander Schnell

Abstract
We study the dynamics of a quantum particle in a constricted two-dimensional channel and analyze how the onset of quantum corrections impacts the (semi-)classical high-temperature behaviour, as temperature is lowered. We characterize both equilibrium and non-equilibrium (transport) properties of the system, considering the case of a narrow and disorder-free channel. Counterintuitively, we find that quantum corrections do not monotonically enhance the particle current as the temperature is lowered, as naively expected from the activation of coherent tunnelling, but they rather inhibit transport at intermediate temperatures, increasing the effective free-energy barrier. We illustrate this “anti-tunnelling” effect numerically by computing the non-equilibrium steady-state of a quantum master equation describing the system, and confirm it analytically by adopting the Quantum Smoluchowski limit.



The transport of molecules, polymers, and colloids across constrictions and porous materials has attracted considerable attention due to both the theoretical challenges that it poses as well as to its relevance for technological application and bio-medical scenarios

From a theoretical perspective, describing transport across porous materials is a tremendous challenge, since it requires one to solve at the same time the microscopic interactions with the solid matrix as well as the mesoscopic entropic effects caused by the sequence of enlargements and constrictions along the porous material.

Theoretical effective models have been developed, where the porous material is modelled as a set of channels with varying cross-sections.

Here we aim at bridging the two scenarios – classical and quantum – by investigating how quantum corrections affect equilibrium and transport properties around the semi-classical high-temperature limit for a particle in a two-dimensional constricted channel. In particular, we study an ideal gas particle which is freely diffusing in a potential well whose stiffness gently varies along the longitudinal direction (see Fig.

In order to compare to classical systems (for which the dynamics is governed by the local equilibrium free energy) we compute the local equilibrium free energy,
\mathcal{F}
and, in particualr, the free energy difference,
\Delta\mathcal{F}\equiv\mathcal{F}(L/2)-\mathcal{F}(0)
between the maximum and minimum of the free energy.
Interestingly, when the potential is varying smoothly, our results show that the first quantum corrections at high temperature lead to an increase in the free energy barrier
\Delta\mathcal{F}
. This is counterintuitive, since one would expect that quantum corrections may decrease the effective barrier due to the activation of coherent tunneling, which we indeed observe eventually at lower temperatures.
We capture this effect, which we will refer to as “anti-tunneling”, analytically by extending the Fick-Jackobs approximation to the case of a Quantum Smoluchowki equation accounting for the leading correction at high temperature. The success of this analytical approach is benchmarked against numerical solution of the quantum equation of motion of the open quantum systems.
The non-monotonic behavior of the free energy persists also in the case of transport as we observe by both numerical and analytical results.

Model

(2)

The small parameter of the expansion is

\displaystyle\Lambda=\frac{1}{48\pi}\frac{\lambda_{T}^{2}}{L_{y}^{2}}\,,\quad\quad

The equilibrium solution of Eq. (

According to our ansatz, the

Substituting the ansatz into

\displaystyle\dfrac{\dot{\rho}(x,t)}{L_{y}}\dfrac{e^{-\beta\psi(x,y)}}{e^{-\beta A(x)}}=

\displaystyle D_{cl}\nabla\cdot\left[\nabla\cdot\left[\mathbf{D}_{qm}(x,y)\frac{\rho(x,t)}{L_{y}}\dfrac{e^{-\beta\psi(x,y)}}{e^{-\beta A(x)}}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\rho(x,t)}{L_{y}}\dfrac{e^{-\beta\psi(x,y)}}{e^{-\beta A(x)}}\beta\nabla U(x,y)\right].

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}(x,t)=D_{cl}\partial_{x}\left\{\partial_{x}\rho(x,t)+\rho(x,t)\beta\partial_{x}\mathcal{F}(x)\right\},

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\mathcal{F}(x)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(x)=\beta\partial_{x}A_{0}(x)L_{y}^{2}\int dx\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{-\beta U}}{e^{-\beta A_{0}}}\partial_{y}^{2}\beta U\frac{dy}{L_{y}}+

\displaystyle+L_{y}^{2}\!\!\int\!dx\!\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\dfrac{e^{-\beta U}}{e^{-\beta A_{0}}}\left[\left(\beta\partial_{y}U\right)^{2}-\beta\partial^{2}_{y}U\right]\beta\partial_{x}U\frac{dy}{L_{y}}

II
Equilibrium

At thermodynamic equilibrium, the current vanishes,
J=0
. Accordingly, the steady state solution reduces to

\rho(x)=\Pi e^{-\beta\mathcal{F}(x)},

(18)

where
\Pi
is determined by the normalization condition

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}\rho(x)dx=\rho_{0}Le^{\beta(\mu-\mu_{0})},

(19)

where
\mu
is the chemical potential and
\rho_{0}
is the value of the density for
\mu=\mu_{0}
.

In order to further inspect the dependence of the effective free energy on
\Lambda
, in the following we specialize to the case of a modulated harmonic potential of the form

\displaystyle U(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}k(x)y^{2},

(20a)

\displaystyle k(x)

\displaystyle=k_{0}\left[1+k_{1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right)\right],

(20b)

where
L
is the period of the potential. In the case of harmonic potential, it is natural to introduce a (finite-temperature) transverse length scale
L_{y}=\sqrt{2k_{B}T/k_{0}}
, which captures the effective range of values explored due to thermal fluctuations.
For a harmonic potential, up to first order in
\Lambda
,
\rho(x)
reads

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\rho_{0}e^{\beta(\mu-\mu_{0})}(\rho_{cl}(x)+\Lambda\rho_{\Lambda}(x))

(21)

with

\displaystyle\rho_{cl}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta A_{0}(x)}=\sqrt{\pi\frac{k_{0}}{k(x)}},

(22)

\displaystyle\rho_{\Lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=-\!\int_{0}^{L}\!\dfrac{e^{-\beta U(x,y)}}{e^{-\beta A_{0}(x)}}L_{y}^{2}\partial_{y}^{2}\beta U(x,y)dy=-2\frac{k(x)}{k_{0}}.

(23)

Similarly, after some algebra (see Appendix
C
), for the free energy we get

\displaystyle\beta\mathcal{F}^{hrm}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{k(x)}{k_{0}}+2\Lambda\frac{k(x)}{k_{0}}\,.

(24)

Equation (
24
) shows that the free energy has two contributions: the classical (entropic) contribution plus a linear correction in
\Lambda
.

In order to validate our model, in Fig.
2
, we compare the density profile obtained from Eqs. (

The quantum master equation is derived by first discretizing the Schrödinger equation (see Appendix

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\psi(x,y,t)=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})+U(x,y)\right]\psi(x,y,t),

(x_{i},y_{j})=(\Delta_{x}(i-1/2),\Delta_{y}j)
with spacing

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{i,j}=(\Delta_{x}\Delta_{y})^{-1/2}\int\limits_{x_{i}-\Delta_{x}/2}^{x_{i}+\Delta_{x}/2}\mathrm{d}x\int\limits_{y_{j}-\Delta_{y}/2}^{y_{j}+\Delta_{y}/2}\mathrm{d}y\,\hat{\psi}(x,y)\,.

(26)

Here,
\hat{\psi}(x,y)

As discussed in

We then compute the thermal density matrix

For the discretized case, this corresponds to

\displaystyle\varrho(x_{i})=\Delta_{x}^{-1}\sum_{j}\mathrm{tr}(\hat{a}_{i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,j}\hat{\varrho})\,.

(29)

Fig.

Interestingly, in agreement with Eq. (

In order to further assess the reliability of the model on a wider range of parameters, we focus on the free energy barrier, i.e. the difference between the free energy maximum (at

Surprisingly, Eq. (

To better understand the physical origin of the growth of the free energy barrier upon increasing
\Lambda

\displaystyle\psi_{ent}(x)=U(x)+\frac{\lambda^{2}_{T}}{48\pi}\partial_{x}^{2}U(x)\,.

\displaystyle U(x)=U_{0}\cos(2\pi x/L),

we have that

\displaystyle\Delta\psi_{ent}\equiv\psi(0)-\psi(L/2)=2U_{0}\left(1-\frac{\pi}{12}\frac{\lambda^{2}_{T}}{L^{2}}\right)

(33)

and hence, as expected, the quantum corrections reduce the effective potential barrier.
Therefore the enhancement of the free energy barrier in Eq. (

III
Transport

In the following, we fix the magnitude of the chemical potential,
\mu_{1,2}
, at the ends (located at
x_{1}=0
,
x_{2}=L
) of a period of the external potential.
Accordingly, for the particle density we have

\displaystyle p(x_{1,2},y)=e^{-\beta\psi(x_{1,2},y)}e^{\beta\mu_{1,2}}

(37)

and for the
1\mathrm{D}
projected density, at linear order in
\Lambda

\displaystyle\rho_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\int e^{-\beta\psi(x_{1,2},y)}e^{\beta\mu_{1,2}}dy

\displaystyle\simeq e^{\beta\mu_{1,2}}\!\!\int\!e^{-\beta U}\left[1-\Lambda L_{y}^{2}\left(2\partial_{y}^{2}\beta U-(\partial_{y}\beta U)^{2}\right)\right]dy.

Accordingly, we have

\displaystyle\Pi

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}e^{\beta\mathcal{F}(x_{1})}\simeq\rho_{1}e^{\beta A_{0}(x_{1})}\left(1+\Lambda\mathcal{F}(x_{1})\right),

(39a)

\displaystyle\frac{J}{D_{cl}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\rho_{1}e^{\beta\mathcal{F}(x_{1})}-\rho_{2}e^{\beta\mathcal{F}(x_{2})}}{\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}e^{\beta\mathcal{F}(x)}dx}.

(39b)

In the case of periodic potentials, for which we have
A_{0}(x_{2}=L)=A_{0}(x_{1}=0)
, and at linear order in
\Lambda
the last expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\frac{J}{D_{cl}}\simeq\frac{J_{cl}}{D_{cl}}\left[1+\Lambda J_{\Lambda}\right],

(40)

with

\displaystyle\frac{J_{cl}}{D_{cl}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{\beta\mu_{1}}-e^{\beta\mu_{2}}}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\beta A_{0}(x)}dx},

(41a)

\displaystyle J_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\beta A_{0}(x)}\beta\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(x)dx}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\beta A_{0}(x)}dx}.

(41b)

Finally, for a harmonic potential, this reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{J_{cl}^{harm}}{D_{cl}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{\beta\mu_{1}}-e^{\beta\mu_{2}}}{\int_{0}^{L}\sqrt{{k(x)}/{k_{0}}}dx},

(42a)

\displaystyle J_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-2\dfrac{\int_{0}^{L}\left({k(x)}/{k_{0}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}dx}{\int_{0}^{L}\sqrt{{k(x)}/{k_{0}}}dx}.

(42b)

We remark that the dependency of the quantum correction on the magnitude of the potential, encoded in
k_{0}
, is only in
\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(0)
whereas, recalling the definition of
k(x)
in Eq. (

is independent of

.
Fig.

V
Conclusions

We have studied the equilibrium and transport properties of a quantum particle in a
2\mathrm{D}
harmonic confinement potential. We have extended the Fick-Jakobs approximation to the case of the Quantum Smoluchowski equations and identified the effective free energy profile along the longitudinal direction. Interestingly, in the presence of a length scale separation between the longitudinal and transverse direction, our analytical results show that the effective free energy barrier
\Delta\mathcal{F}

grows
upon decreasing the temperature, i.e., upon increasing the quantum corrections (up to linear order in
\Lambda
). We thus observe an anti-tunneling phenomenon, which contrasts with the intuitive expectation that the barrier would monotonically decrease as one enters the quantum regime due to the onset of tunneling effects. The barrier enhancement is absent for purely enthalpic potentials.
We have supported the analytical derivation by numerical solutions of the quantum master equation describing the open system in contact with thermal baths. Our numerical results not only confirm the analytical predictions but also show that for larger values of
\Lambda
(i.e., lower temperatures) or in the absence of length scale separation, the reduction of the free energy barrier upon increasing
\Lambda
is retrieved, consistently with expectations. This holds for both the equilibrim free energy barrier,
\Delta\mathcal{F}
, as well as for the flux of particles in the case in which a single period of the potential is putting in contact reservoirs of particles kept at different chemical potentials.

In future work, especially with regard to quantum gas experiments, it would be interesting to study how these effects are generalized to the case of a
3\mathrm{D}
trap geometry. Furthermore, it would be interesting to investigate the effects of quantum statistics and interactions on the anti-tunneling–tunneling transition.

Appendix A
Microscopic open system description

A.1
System Hamiltonian

Let us discuss the quantum dynamics of the isolated system (the 2D channel) first. Here, in first quantization, the dynamics is given by the 2D Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\psi(x,y,t)=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})+U(x,y)\right]\psi(x,y,t),

(54)

where we assume
0\leq x\leq L
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us first transform this equation to second-quantized form (to later allow for particle exchange with a bath). This gives rise to the second-quantized system Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}=\int_{0}^{L}\mathrm{d}x\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\,\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(x,y)\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})+U(x,y)\right]\hat{\psi}(x,y),

(55)

with field operator
\hat{\psi}(x,y)
. Since we want to describe single-particle physics, the quantum statistical properties of the particle field are irrelevant, so we choose a bosonic field
\hat{\psi}(x,y)
for convenience.
In order to solve the dynamics numerically, let us introduce a discrete grid of spatial points

\displaystyle(x,y)\rightarrow(x_{i},y_{j})=(\Delta_{x}(i-1/2),\Delta_{y}j)

(56)

with
\Delta_{x}=L/M_{x}
, and
i=1,\dots,M_{x}
, as well as
\Delta_{y}=2\tilde{L}_{y}/M_{y}
, with
j=-M_{y}/2,-M_{y}/2+1,\dots,M_{y}/2
and some cutoff length
\tilde{L}_{y}
that should be chosen such that the steady state real space density can be neglected for
|y|>\tilde{L}_{y}
.

This allows us to introduce the new field operators on this grid,

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{x}\Delta_{y}}}\int_{x_{i}^{-}}^{x_{i}^{+}}\mathrm{d}x\int_{y_{j}^{-}}^{y_{j}^{+}}\mathrm{d}y\,\hat{\psi}(x,y),

(57)

where we define
x_{i}^{\pm}=x_{i}\pm\Delta_{x}/2
, and
y_{j}^{\pm}=y_{j}\pm\Delta_{y}/2
.
Note that with this definition we indeed recover discrete bosonic field operators since

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{i,j},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}]=\frac{1}{\Delta_{x}\Delta_{y}}\int_{x_{i}^{-}}^{x_{i}^{+}}\mathrm{d}x\int_{y_{j}^{-}}^{y_{j}^{+}}\mathrm{d}y\int_{x_{i^{\prime}}^{-}}^{x_{i^{\prime}}^{+}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\int_{y_{j^{\prime}}^{-}}^{y_{j^{\prime}}^{+}}\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\,[\hat{\psi}(x,y),\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})]=\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta_{jj^{\prime}}.

(58)

Finally, by replacing the second order derivative with the correponding discrete operator, we find

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}=\sum_{ij}\biggl[-

\displaystyle J_{x}\left(\hat{a}_{i+1,j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,j}+\hat{a}_{i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i+1,j}-2\hat{a}_{i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,j}\right)-J_{y}\left(\hat{a}_{i,j+1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,j}+\hat{a}_{i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,j+1}-2\hat{a}_{i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,j}\right)+U(x_{i},y_{j})\,\hat{a}_{i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,j}\biggr],

(59)

with
J_{x}={\hbar^{2}}/({2m\Delta_{x}^{2}})
and
J_{y}={\hbar^{2}}/({2m\Delta_{y}^{2}})
.

A.2
Coupling to thermal particle reservoirs

We now couple to the system to two thermal reservoirs with Temperature
T_{l/r}
and chemical potential
\mu_{l/r}
on each of the leftmost and the rightmost sites. This leads to the total Hamiltonian of system and baths

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}+\sum_{j}\left(\hat{H}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{SB},l}+\hat{H}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{SB},r}+\hat{H}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{B},l}+\hat{H}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{B},r}\right).

(60)

Here enter the bath Hamiltonians

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{B},l}=\sum_{k}E^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k,j}\hat{c}_{k,j},\qquad\hat{H}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{B},r}=\sum_{k}E^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{k,j}\hat{d}_{k,j},

(61)

with bath energies
E^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
and corresponding bosonic bath annihilation operators
\hat{c}_{k,j},\hat{d}_{k,j}
. We assume the system-bath Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{SB},l}=\sum_{k,j}g_{k}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k,j}\hat{a}_{1,j}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1,j}\hat{c}_{k,j}\right),\qquad\hat{H}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{SB},r}=\sum_{k,j}g_{k}\left(\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{k,j}\hat{a}_{M_{x},j}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{M_{x},j}\hat{d}_{k,j}\right),

(62)

with coupling coefficients
g_{k}
to mode
k
to the left bath or to the right bath, respectively.

Using standard Born- and Markov approximation
[
34
]
, we find the Redfield master equation for the reduced density matrix of the system,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\varrho}(t)=-i[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}},\hat{\varrho}(t)]

\displaystyle+\sum_{j}\left([\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}_{1,j}\hat{\varrho}(t),\hat{a}_{1,j}]+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)+\sum_{j}\left([\hat{\tilde{a}}_{1,j}\hat{\varrho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1,j}]+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)

(63)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j}\left([\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}_{M_{x},j}\hat{\varrho}(t),\hat{a}_{M_{x},j}]+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)+\sum_{j}\left([\hat{\tilde{a}}_{M_{x},j}\hat{\varrho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{M_{x},j}]+\mathrm{h.c.}\right).

(64)

Herein occur the jump operators

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}_{1,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1,j}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}\sum_{k}g_{k}^{2}\,\langle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k,j}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}\hat{c}_{k,j}\rangle

(65)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{a}}_{1,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}\hat{a}_{1,j}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}\sum_{k}g_{k}^{2}\,\langle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}\hat{c}_{k,j}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k,j}\rangle.

(66)

Analogous expressions hold for the jump operators on the right hand side of the system. Here we calculate

\displaystyle\langle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k,j}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}\hat{c}_{k,j}\rangle=e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}E^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\tau}n_{l}(E^{\mathrm{B}}_{k})

(67)

as well as

\displaystyle\langle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}\hat{c}_{k,j}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{B}}\tau}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k,j}\rangle=e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}E^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\tau}(1+n_{l}(E^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}))\overset{n_{l}(E)\ll 1}{\longrightarrow}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}E^{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\tau},

(68)

and in the second expression we have used the classical limit in which the population of individual modes is very small,
n_{l}(E)\ll 1
.
Herein occurs the Bose-Einstein occupation function for the left bath, where we again perform the classical limit

\displaystyle n_{l}(E)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(E-\mu_{l})}-1}\overset{n_{l}(E)\ll 1}{\longrightarrow}e^{-\beta(E-\mu_{l})}=n_{\mathrm{cl},l}(E).

(69)

We introduce the spectral density of the baths (we assume equal spectral densities for both baths)

\displaystyle J(E)=\sum_{k}g_{k}^{2}\delta(E-E^{\mathrm{B}}_{k})=\gamma,

(70)

which we choose to be constant.
It also enter the single-particle eigenstates of the system
\varphi_{k}(i,j)
and corresponding system eigenenergies
E_{k}
, such that the field operators

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{k}=\sum_{i,j}\varphi^{*}_{k}(i,j)\hat{a}_{i,j},\qquad\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{k}=\sum_{i,j}\varphi_{k}(i,j)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i,j}

(71)

diagonalize the system Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}=\sum_{k}E_{k}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k}.

(72)

With this, we have

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1,j}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}=\sum_{k}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{k}\varphi^{*}_{k}(1,j)e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_{k}\tau}=\sum_{i,l}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i,l}\sum_{k}\varphi_{k}(i,l)\varphi^{*}_{k}(1,j)e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_{k}\tau},

(73)

and

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}\hat{a}_{1,j}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}\tau}=\sum_{k}\hat{b}_{k}\varphi_{k}(1,j)e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}E_{k}\tau}=\sum_{i,l}\hat{a}_{i,l}\sum_{k}\varphi^{*}_{k}(i,l)\varphi_{k}(1,j)e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}E_{k}\tau}.

(74)

By using the Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem and neglecting the imaginaries of the Fourier transform, we finally have

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{a}}^{\dagger}_{1,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,l}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i,l}f^{i,l}_{1,j},\qquad\hat{\tilde{a}}_{1,j}=\sum_{i,l}\hat{a}_{i,l}g^{i,l}_{1,j}.

(75)

Herein we define the coefficients

\displaystyle f^{i,l}_{m,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\varphi_{k}(i,l)\varphi^{*}_{k}(m,j)J(E_{k})n_{\mathrm{cl}}(E_{k}),

(76)

\displaystyle g^{i,l}_{m,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\varphi^{*}_{k}(i,l)\varphi_{k}(m,j)J(-E_{k}).

(77)

This gives rise to the master equation in explicit form with respect to local creation and annihilation operators, reading

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\varrho}(t)=-i[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}},\hat{\varrho}(t)]

\displaystyle+\sum_{ilj}\left(f^{i,l}_{1,j}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i,l}\hat{\varrho}(t),\hat{a}_{1,j}]+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)+\sum_{ilj}g^{i,l}_{1,j}\left([\hat{a}_{i,l}\hat{\varrho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1,j}]+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)

(78)

\displaystyle+\sum_{ilj}\left(f^{i,l}_{M_{x},j}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i,l}\hat{\varrho}(t),\hat{a}_{M_{x},j}]+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)+\sum_{ilj}g^{i,l}_{M_{x},j}\left([\hat{a}_{i,l}\hat{\varrho}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{M_{x},j}]+\mathrm{h.c.}\right).

(79)

From this master equation, we can derive a kinetic equation for the single-particle density matrix

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij,kl}=\mathrm{tr}(\hat{a}_{k,l}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,j}\hat{\varrho}),

(80)

which in the continuum limit leads to the distribution
p(x,y)
of the main text via

\displaystyle p(x_{i},y_{i})=\frac{\sigma_{ij,kl}}{\Delta_{x}\Delta_{y}}

(81)

By rewriting Eq. (
59
) with single particle matrix elements,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}=\sum_{ij,kl}h_{ij,kl}\hat{a}_{i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k,l}

(82)

we finally have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma_{ij,kl}=-i

\displaystyle\sum_{nm}\left(h_{ij,nm}\sigma_{nm,kl}-\sigma_{ij,nm}h_{nm,kl}\right)+(\mathcal{L}_{l}[\sigma])_{ij,kl}+(\mathcal{L}_{r}[\sigma])_{ij,kl},

(83)

with left dissipator

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{l}[\sigma])_{ij,kl}=

\displaystyle\delta_{k1}\left[f^{i,j}_{1,l}+\sum_{nm}\sigma_{ij,nm}\left(f^{n,m}_{1,l}-(g^{n,m}_{1,l})^{*}\right)\right]+\delta_{i1}\left[(f^{k,l}_{1,j})^{*}+\sum_{nm}\left((f^{n,m}_{1,j})^{*}-g^{n,m}_{1,j}\right)\sigma_{nm,kl}\right],

(84)

and right dissipator

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{r}[\sigma])_{ij,kl}=

\displaystyle\delta_{kM_{x}}\left[f^{i,j}_{M_{x},l}+\sum_{nm}\sigma_{ij,nm}\left(f^{n,m}_{M_{x},l}-(g^{n,m}_{M_{x},l})^{*}\right)\right]+\delta_{iM_{x}}\left[(f^{k,l}_{M_{x},j})^{*}+\sum_{nm}\left((f^{n,m}_{M_{x},j})^{*}-g^{n,m}_{M_{x},j}\right)\sigma_{nm,kl}\right].

(85)

We solve this master equation to solve for the steady state

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma^{\mathrm{SS}}_{ij,kl}=0.

(86)

The particle current into the left bath
is then given by

\displaystyle J=\mathrm{tr}[(\partial_{t}\varrho)_{\mathrm{left}}N]=\sum_{ij}\mathrm{tr}[(\partial_{t}\varrho)_{\mathrm{left}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{ij}\hat{a}_{ij}]=\sum_{ij}(\mathcal{L}_{l}[\sigma^{\mathrm{SS}}])_{ij,ij}.

(87)

Note that we choose the chemical potentials
\mu_{l}
and
\mu_{r}
such that the density on the left is and the right is according to
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
as assumed in the Smoluchowski equation.

Appendix C
Derivation of Eq. (
24
)

We can further simply the last expression in the case of a harmonic
potential of the form

\displaystyle\beta U(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\beta k(x)y^{2}

(97)

\displaystyle k(x)

\displaystyle=k_{0}\left(1+k_{1}\cos\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right)

(98)

where
L
is the period of the potential. Recalling that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta k(x)y^{2}}\frac{dy}{L_{y}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}}}

(99)

For such a potential we have

\displaystyle e^{-\beta A_{0}(x)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}}}

(100)

\displaystyle L_{y}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{-\beta U}}{e^{-\beta A_{0}}}\partial_{y}^{2}\beta U\frac{dy}{L_{y}}

\displaystyle=L_{y}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}}{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta k(x)y^{2}}\beta k(x)\frac{dy}{L_{y}}=\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}

(101)

\displaystyle\beta\int\limits\partial_{x}A_{0}(x)L_{y}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{-\beta U}}{e^{-\beta A_{0}}}\partial_{y}^{2}\beta U\frac{dy}{L_{y}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\beta L_{y}^{2}\int\limits\partial_{x}k(x)dx=\frac{1}{2}\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}

(102)

and

\displaystyle L_{y}^{2}\int dx\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{-\beta U(x,y)}}{e^{-\beta A_{0}(x)}}

\displaystyle\left(\left(\beta\partial_{y}U(x,y)\right)^{2}-2\partial_{y}^{2}\beta U(x,y)\right)\beta\partial_{x}U(x,y)\frac{dy}{L_{y}}=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}L_{y}^{2}\int\limits\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\sqrt{\frac{\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}}{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta k(x)y^{2}}\left[\left(\beta k(x)y\right)^{2}-2\beta k(x)\right]\partial_{x}\beta k(x)y^{2}\frac{dy}{L_{y}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}L_{y}^{2}\int\limits\sqrt{\frac{\beta k(x)L^{2}}{2\pi}}\partial_{x}\beta k(x)\left[\beta^{2}k^{2}(x)\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta k(x)y^{2}}y^{4}\frac{dy}{L_{y}}-2\beta k(x)\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta k(x)y^{2}}y^{2}\frac{dy}{L_{y}}\right]dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}L_{y}^{2}\int\left[3-2\right]\partial_{x}\beta k(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}

(103)

Accordingly, we have

\displaystyle\beta\mathcal{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta A_{0}(x)+\Lambda\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}}{2\pi}+\Lambda\beta k(x)L_{y}^{2}

(104)

Finally, using
L_{y}^{2}=2k_{B}T/k_{0}
and disregarding terms which do not depend on
x
we get

\displaystyle\beta\mathcal{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{k(x)}{k_{0}}+2\Lambda\frac{k(x)}{k_{0}}

(105)

which is Eq. (
24
) of the main text.

Appendix D
Harmonic potential: regime of validity

In our derivation we have requested

\displaystyle\partial_{x}U(x,y)\ll\partial_{y}U(x,y)

(106)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{x}U(x,y)\ll\partial^{2}_{y}U(x,y)

(107)

This implies:

\displaystyle y_{1}\ll L\frac{1+k_{1}\cos(2\pi x)}{k_{1}\pi\sin(2\pi x/L)}

(108)

\displaystyle y_{2}\ll L\sqrt{\frac{1+k1\cos(2\pi x)}{2k_{1}\pi^{2}\cos(2\pi x/L)}}

(109)

In order to fulfill this requirement we impose that when the above mentioned conditions are not fulfilled the probability distribution should be exponentially damped. This implies:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\beta ky_{1}\gg 1

(110)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\beta ky_{2}\gg 1

(111)

Hence we can derive conditions on
k_{0}

\displaystyle\beta k_{0}L_{y}

\displaystyle\gg 2\frac{L_{y}}{L^{2}}\frac{\left(k_{1}\pi\sin(2\pi x/L)\right)^{2}}{\left(1+k_{1}\cos(2\pi x/L)\right)^{3}}\simeq 2\pi^{2}\frac{L_{y}^{2}}{L^{2}}k_{1}^{2}

(112)

\displaystyle\beta k_{0}L_{y}

\displaystyle\gg 4\frac{L_{y}}{L^{2}}\frac{k_{1}\pi\cos(2\pi x/L)}{\left(1+k_{1}\cos(2\pi x/L)\right)^{2}}\simeq 4\pi\frac{L_{y}^{2}}{L^{2}}\frac{k_{1}\pi}{1-k_{1}}

(113)

whose solution is
\beta k_{0}L_{y}^{2}\gg 4\pi L_{y}^{2}/L^{2}k_{1}/(1-k_{1})
.

Appendix H
Classical Fick-Jacobs

In a nutshell the Fick-Jacobs approximation deals with non-self-interacting systems whose density,
p
, is governed, in the overdamped regime by the Smoluchowski equation:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\dot{p}(x,y,t)=\nabla\cdot\left[D\nabla p(x,y,t)+Dp(x,y,t)\beta\nabla U(x,y)\right]

(127)

where
D
is the diffusion coefficient and

\displaystyle U(x,y)=\begin{cases}W(x,y)&|y|\leq h(x)\\
\infty&|y|>h(x)\end{cases}

(128)

encodes for a conservative potential,
W
, and for the confinement.
The Fick-Jacobs approximation relies on the following ansatz:

\displaystyle p(x,y,t)=\rho(x,t)\dfrac{e^{-\beta W(x,y)}}{e^{-\beta A(x)}}

(129)

with

\displaystyle e^{-\beta A(x)}=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\beta W(x,y)}dy

(130)

where
L_{Y}
is a characteristic length (that is irrelevant for any physically meaning quantity).
Substituting the ansatz into the Smoluchowski equation leads

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}(x,t)=\partial_{x}\left[D\partial_{x}p(x,y,t)+Dp(x,y,t)\beta\partial_{x}A(y)\right]

(131)
