Type A spinal epidural arteriovenous fistula initially classified as a spinal dural arteriovenous fistula: Persistent shunting after intradural disconnection and salvage
Authors: Domenico Napoletano, Austin Reed, Aaron Higginbotham, Brandon Sharkey, Ari Williams, Lane Fry, Koji Ebersole, Ifije Ohiorhenuan, Jeremy Peterson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Spinal epidural arteriovenous fistulas (SEDAVFs) are rare lesions characterized by shunting between arterial feeders and the epidural venous plexus. Type A SEDAVFs demonstrate secondary intradural drainage and may produce congestive myelopathy resembling a spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (SDAVFs). We report a right L3 Type A SEDAVF initially classified and treated as a SDAVF, resulting in persistent epidural shunting and recurrent symptoms.Case Description: A 73-year-old male presented with progressive gait instability, lower-extremity spasticity, and paresthesia. Conventional spinal angiography demonstrated a right L3 arteriovenous fistula with intradural perimedullary drainage, initially classified as a SDAVF. Three-dimensional rotational angiography (3DRA) was not performed initially. The patient underwent intradural coagulation and transection of the arterialized draining veins, with temporary improvement. Symptoms recurred 1 year later, and progressed over 6 months. Repeat angiography demonstrated persistent shunting with initial epidural venous plexus drainage and secondary retrograde intradural reflux, consistent with a Type A SEDAVF. 3DRA localized the fistulous point and showed no spinal cord arterial contribution from the embolized pedicle. Transarterial embolization with 33% n-butyl cyanoacrylate achieved complete occlusion. Gait, strength, and spasticity improved, with occlusion maintained at 6-month angiographic follow-up.Conclusion: Type A SEDAVFs may mimic SDAVFs when secondary intradural reflux dominates the angiographic appearance. Intradural draining vein interruption without elimination of the epidural shunt may leave persistent fistulous flow. Careful localization of the fistulous point and drainage sequence is essential, and 3DRA should be considered when conventional angiography does not clearly distinguish a dural from an epidural shunt.

INTRODUCTION

Spinal epidural arteriovenous fistulas (SEDAVFs) are uncommon spinal vascular lesions characterized by abnormal shunting between arterial feeders and venous plexus or pouch.[2] They may be supplied by segmental, radicular, or paravertebral arteries and are classified according to their venous drainage patterns.[2,6] Type A SEDAVFs in the RangelCastilla classification, also described as Type Va spinal arteriovenous shunts in the Takai classification, demonstrate secondary intradural venous drainage.[6,8] Retrograde reflux into the perimedullary venous system may produce venous hypertension, spinal cord congestion, and progressive myelopathy.[2,5]

This intradural drainage pattern may closely resemble that of a spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (SDAVF), the most common type of spinal arteriovenous fistula that accounts for approximately 60–70% of all spinal vascular lesions.[3] SDAVFs typically arise within the dural sleeve of a spinal nerve root, where a radiculomeningeal artery connects directly to an intradural radiculomedullary vein.[1,3] Although both lesions may cause congestive myelopathy through perimedullary venous hypertension, their fistulous origins and initial venous drainage pathways are anatomically distinct.[1,5] Key distinguishing features between SDAVFs and SEDAVFs are summarized in Table 1.

The clinical consequences of this resemblance are substantial. In a multicenter angiographic review of thoracolumbar SEDAVF, 29/59 (49%) of lesions had been incorrectly diagnosed as SDAVFs at the individual treating centers.[5] A comparable rate was observed in a separate multicenter surgical and endovascular series, in which 29/81 (36%) were misdiagnosed at the participating institutions.[9]

Distinguishing these lesions on conventional angiography may be difficult when intradural venous reflux is prominent.[1,5] A characteristic feature of SEDAVFs is an epidural venous pouch or plexus that opacifies before secondary intradural venous drainage.[1,5] This epidural component may be difficult to identify on conventional two-dimensional angiography, particularly when overshadowed by extensive perimedullary venous reflux.[1] The distinction has direct therapeutic implications: Interruption of the intradural draining vein is often curative for SDAVFs but may leave the primary epidural shunt intact in a SEDAVF.[6,9] We present a Type A SEDAVF initially classified as an SDAVF on conventional spinal angiography. Interruption of the intradural venous drainage pathway produced temporary clinical improvement but did not eliminate the underlying epidural shunt, which was subsequently delineated on repeat angiography with 3DRA. Definitive occlusion was achieved through transarterial embolization with n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA).

CASE PRESENTATION

A 73-year-old man with history of cervical spondylotic disease treated with decompression and instrumented fusion presented with progressive neurological symptoms. He reported several months of dysesthesia and numbness beginning in the mid-thoracic region and extending into the lower abdomen, greater on the left than the right and partially alleviated by gabapentin. During this time, his symptoms progressed with worsening gait instability, impaired balance, and episodic lower-extremity cramping and spasticity, including involuntary hamstring tightening during activity and at rest.

Neurological examination demonstrated hyperreflexia and other findings consistent with myelopathy. Computed tomography myelography of the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine demonstrated prominent perimedullary vascularity at the T8–T9 levels, raising concern for a spinal arteriovenous fistula. Diagnostic spinal angiogram performed approximately 1 month after presentation demonstrated arteriovenous shunting supplied at the right L3 level with intradural perimedullary venous drainage. Based on the available conventional two-dimensional angiographic projections, the lesion was initially classified as an SDAVF. Three-dimensional rotational angiography (3DRA) was not performed during initial examination, and the epidural fistulous point and its relationship to the intradural drainage pathway were not definitively delineated. Given the patient’s worsening clinical course despite preserved motor strength, surgical treatment was recommended.

The patient underwent an L2–L4 laminectomy with microsurgical intradural disconnection of the presumed SDAVF. Following durotomy and arachnoid dissection, two venous structures were identified at the right L3 radicular level, including one thrombosed vein and a second arterialized draining vein. Intraoperative indocyanine green (ICG) angiography confirmed early filling of the arterialized vein. Both veins were coagulated and divided, and the dural sleeve along the involved nerve root was treated. Repeat intraoperative ICG angiography demonstrated no residual early filling within the exposed intradural venous structures.

The patient recovered without immediate complication and initially experienced improvement in gait and lower-extremity symptoms. This improvement was sustained for approximately 1 year. He subsequently developed gradual recurrence of lower-extremity weakness, spasticity, and gait instability over the following 6 months. Given concern for persistent or recurrent arteriovenous shunting, repeat spinal angiography was performed approximately 18 months after the original operation.

Repeat angiography demonstrated persistent arteriovenous shunting at the right L3 level. The lesion drained initially into the epidural venous plexus, with secondary retrograde reflux into the intradural and perimedullary venous system. Compared with the original angiogram, inferior epidural venous outflow had decreased, whereas communication with the perimedullary venous system persisted. A cross-midline arterial contribution from the contralateral segmental circulation was more clearly visualized, and the right L3 feeding artery appeared enlarged relative to the prior angiogram. 3DRA more clearly localized the epidural fistulous point and demonstrated the sequence of epidural and secondary intradural venous drainage. The lesion was therefore reclassified as a Type A SEDAVF rather than a SDAVF.

Given the recurrent symptoms and favorable endovascular anatomy, definitive embolization was pursued. Transfemoral access was obtained, and the right L3 segmental artery was selectively catheterized. 3DRA further delineated the epidural fistulous connection and demonstrated no angiographic evidence of a spinal cord arterial contribution from the selected embolization pedicle [Figure 1]. A microcatheter was advanced to a position immediately proximal to the fistulous point [Figure 2A-C]. After confirmation of a safe embolization position, 33% NBCA was injected under roadmap guidance. The embolic agent penetrated the fistulous connection and proximal epidural venous plexus. Post-embolization angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of arteriovenous shunting [Figure 2D].

Within 1 week of embolization, the patient reported improved gait stability and increased lower-extremity strength, particularly in the left leg. He regained the ability to stand on one leg and dress independently while standing, tasks he was unable to perform preoperatively. His gait instability and spasticity continued to improve over the ensuing months. Standardized neurological and functional outcome scores were not prospectively recorded; therefore, clinical improvement was assessed using serial neurological documentation and these specific functional milestones.

Follow-up spinal angiography performed 6 months after embolization demonstrated stable complete occlusion without residual dural or epidural arteriovenous shunting. No longer-term angiographic study was available to assess occlusion beyond this interval. The patient subsequently underwent a minimally invasive left L5–S1 hemilaminectomy for compressive radiculopathy. During the dissection, complex epidural venous trabeculations were visualized [Figure 3]. The larger venous channels were without increased turgor or erythema to suggest residual fistulous arterialization.

DISCUSSION

Diagnostic distinction between SDAVFs and SEDAVFs

SDAVFs and Type A SEDAVFs may produce nearly indistinguishable clinical syndromes because both can result in retrograde perimedullary venous drainage, venous hypertension, and congestive myelopathy.[1,5] Their underlying angioarchitecture, however, differs fundamentally.[1,2] An SEDAVF originates in the epidural space and initially drains into an epidural venous pouch or plexus.[1,2] Intradural venous reflux occurs secondarily in Type A lesions.[6,8]

Several angiographic findings may help differentiate these entities. SEDAVFs are more likely to demonstrate an epidural venous pouch, multiple or bilateral segmental arterial contributions, circuitous or delayed filling of intradural veins, and a bridging vein traversing the dura between the epidural and intradural venous systems.[1,5,9] The epidural component may be overlooked when intradural venous reflux is prominent.[1,5] Clinical presentation and conventional spinal imaging may also be similar between SDAVFs and SEDAVFs, including prominent perimedullary vessels and manifestations of venous congestive myelopathy.[1] Consequently, determining the precise site of shunting and the sequence of venous opacification is more important than identifying intradural venous drainage alone.[1,5]

In the present case, the lesion was initially classified as a SDAVF based on conventional two-dimensional angiography. Repeat angiography with three-dimensional rotational reconstruction subsequently demonstrated an epidural fistulous point, initial drainage into the epidural venous plexus, and secondary intradural venous reflux. These findings supported reclassification as a Type A SEDAVF. Because 3DRA was not obtained during initial evaluation, it cannot be determined with certainty whether the epidural component was discernible but unrecognized or inadequately resolved by the original imaging protocol. We, therefore, described the lesion as having been initially classified as SDAVF rather than definitively misdiagnosed.

Persistent epidural shunting after intradural disconnection

The original operation successfully interrupted the visibly arterialized intradural venous drainage pathway, as demonstrated by the absence of residual early filling within the exposed intradural field on repeat ICG angiography. This interruption likely reduced perimedullary venous hypertension and may account for the patient’s temporary clinical improvement.

Intraoperative ICG angiography, however, evaluates only the surgically exposed vascular structures and would not necessarily demonstrate persistence of an epidural fistulous point outside the intradural operative field. The subsequent angiographic findings therefore most strongly support a persistent epidural shunt with recurrent intradural venous reflux rather than recurrence of a previously eliminated fistula.

At least two mechanisms could explain the delayed clinical deterioration. First, the previously interrupted intradural draining vein may have recanalized. Alternatively, and perhaps more likely, the persistent epidural shunt may have recruited or enlarged another intradural drainage pathway.[5,9] The enlarged arterial feeder and more conspicuous contralateral arterial contribution on repeat angiography suggest interval vascular recruitment, although the precise venous mechanism cannot be established definitively from the available imaging.

Closure of one venous outflow route without elimination of the primary epidural shunt may alter pressure gradients within the epidural venous plexus and promote drainage through residual or alternative intradural pathways.[5,9] Once perimedullary reflux is re-established, recurrent spinal venous hypertension and progressive myelopathy may follow. The most accurate characterization of this case is therefore persistent epidural shunting with recurrent clinical symptoms rather than proven recanalization or recurrence of the entire fistula.

Treatment implications

The distinction between SDAVFs and SEDAVFs has direct implications for treatment. Microsurgical treatment of a SDAVF ordinarily requires disconnection of the intradural draining vein near its dural origin and can result in high rates of definitive fistula obliteration.[3] Applying the same intradural operation to a SEDAVF may interrupt secondary intradural reflux without eliminating the epidural fistulous point.[6,9]

Treatment of a Type A SEDAVF must instead be directed toward definitive elimination of the epidural shunt and its pathological venous outflow. This may be achieved through microsurgical interruption, endovascular embolization, or a combined approach, depending on the arterial supply, number of feeding pedicles, accessibility of the fistulous point, extent of the epidural venous component, and relationship to arteries supplying the spinal cord.[9]

In a multicenter study of 81 SEDAVFs with intradural venous drainage, Takai reported initial treatment failure in 31% of patients treated endovascularly compared with 7.5% of those treated microsurgically.[9] Importantly, the microsurgical procedures in that cohort were directed toward definitive treatment of the epidural fistulous connection, with or without coagulation of the associated epidural venous plexus.[9] These procedures differ fundamentally from isolated interruption of an intradural draining vein, as initially performed in the present case. The reported failure rates, therefore, should not be interpreted as a comparison between endovascular embolization and isolated intradural venous disconnection. Rather, they emphasize that successful treatment of a Type A SEDAVF requires elimination of the epidural shunt itself.[9]

These findings suggest that microsurgery may provide reliable elimination of the epidural shunt in anatomically complex lesions, particularly when multiple arterial feeders or an extensive epidural venous pouch limit effective embolic penetration.[9] They do not establish that microsurgery is preferable in every case or preclude endovascular treatment in patients with favorable anatomy. Treatment selection should be based on the arterial supply, accessibility of the fistulous point, extent of the epidural venous component, and relationship of the feeding vessels to arteries supplying the spinal cord.

In the present case, 3DRA identified a discrete fistulous point that was accessible through the right L3 arterial pedicle without an angiographically evident spinal cord arterial contribution from the planned embolization route. This anatomy permitted advancement of the microcatheter immediately proximal to the shunt. NBCA was selected to achieve penetration across the fistulous connection and into the proximal pathological epidural venous compartment. Post-embolization angiography demonstrated complete elimination of arteriovenous shunting, which remained absent at 6-month angiographic follow-up.

Comparative data on NBCA and Onyx derive from studies of SDAVFs rather than SEDAVFs; in a systematic review and meta-analysis of SDAVF, Onyx was associated with higher odds of initial treatment failure or late recurrence than n-butyl cyanoacrylate.[4] These results cannot be directly extrapolated to Type A SEDAVFs, and no conclusion regarding the relative efficacy of these embolic agents in SEDAVFs can be drawn from the available evidence or from this single case.[4] The relevant technical principle in the present patient was successful penetration of the embolic agent across the fistulous point and into the proximal pathological venous compartment.

Role of advanced angiographic imaging

3DRA was instrumental in defining the fistulous anatomy and planning embolization during the repeat evaluation. It clarified the relationship between the arterial feeder, epidural fistulous point, epidural venous plexus, and secondary intradural drainage while allowing selection of an embolization projection that minimized vessel overlap.

Ryu et al. demonstrated that combining 3DRA with heavily T2-weighted volumetric magnetic resonance fusion imaging improved image quality and interobserver agreement for classifying spinal arteriovenous shunt subtypes compared to 3DRA alone.[7] These results relate to spinal arteriovenous shunts broadly and should not be interpreted as evidence that rotational angiography alone will prevent every SEDAVF misclassification. Nevertheless, 3DRA should be considered when conventional angiography does not clearly establish whether the fistulous point is dural or epidural.

Because 3DRA was not performed during the initial examination, the later imaging cannot establish that specific findings were definitively overlooked during the original interpretation. Rather, the repeat examination provided additional anatomical detail that was not available during the initial evaluation. This distinction avoids attributing the original classification exclusively to interpretive error when differences between the imaging protocols may also have contributed.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, the initial angiographic examination was performed using conventional two-dimensional projections without 3DRA. The later classification as a Type A SEDAVF was therefore informed by a more detailed imaging technique than was available during the original evaluation. Although repeat angiography demonstrated an epidural fistulous point and secondary intradural venous reflux, it cannot be determined with certainty whether these features were present but unrecognized on the original study or were insufficiently resolved by the initial imaging protocol.

Second, the initial and repeat angiograms were interpreted retrospectively in the context of the patient’s recurrent symptoms and treatment course and were not evaluated by independent blinded readers. Confirmation and hindsight bias therefore cannot be excluded. Although independent review could strengthen the interpretation, the classification in this report is based on the drainage sequence and epidural fistulous anatomy demonstrated during the repeat angiographic examination.

Third, standardized neurological and functional outcome scores were not prospectively recorded at each treatment stage. Clinical outcomes were instead derived from serial neurological documentation and specific functional milestones, including gait stability, lower-extremity strength, spasticity, and the ability to dress independently while standing. Although these observations indicate clinically meaningful improvement, they limit quantitative comparison with other reported cases.

Fourth, paired pre- and post-embolization spinal magnetic resonance imaging examinations were not available to correlate angiographic occlusion with changes in spinal cord signal abnormality or perimedullary flow voids. Consequently, radiographic improvement in venous congestive myelopathy could not be independently assessed, and concurrent structural contributors to the patient’s symptoms cannot be completely excluded.

Finally, angiographic follow-up was limited to 6 months. Complete fistula occlusion was maintained during this interval, but the long-term durability of embolization beyond 6 months remains unknown. As a single-patient report, this case also cannot establish the comparative effectiveness of microsurgical and endovascular treatment or of specific embolic agents.

CONCLUSION

Type A SEDAVF may closely resemble SDAVF because both lesions produce intradural venous reflux and congestive myelopathy. The defining distinction is the location of the fistulous point: SDAVFs arise at the dural sleeve and drain directly into an intradural vein, whereas SEDAVFs originate within the epidural space and drain initially into an epidural venous plexus or pouch.

In this case, interruption of the intradural drainage pathway produced temporary clinical improvement but left the underlying epidural shunt intact. Repeat angiography with three-dimensional rotational imaging clarified the epidural origin and permitted targeted endovascular occlusion. Complete angiographic occlusion was maintained at 6-month follow-up and was accompanied by sustained functional improvement. Persistent or recurrent symptoms after treatment of a presumed SDAVF should prompt repeat angiography and reconsideration of the original fistula classification.
