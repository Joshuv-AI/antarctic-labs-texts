First development of a 100 K SNP array for small yellow croaker and its application in genomic selection for growth and disease resistance
Authors: Feng Liu, Rui Wang, Ting Ye, Jiajie Zhu, Dandan Guo, Wei Zhan, Bao Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall yellow croaker, Larimichthys polyactis, is a marine fish species of significant commercial importance in China and Korea, yet it suffers from various challenges including genetic degradation and disease problem. Strains with good performance traits such as fast growth and strong disease resistance are needed in aquaculture.ResultsIn the present study, a 100 K SNP array with exceptional genotyping quality was developed based on genomic resequencing, achieving a remarkable detection rate of 98.75% and concordance rate of 98.49%. A total of 946 individuals were genotyped for body weight, body length, and disease resistance (quantified by the survival time of fish injected with pathogen Pseudomonas plecoglossicida) using the SNP array. Genomic selection (GS) was conducted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) and genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) methods. The heritability estimates for body weight, body length, and survival time were 0.47 ± 0.05, 0.43 ± 0.05, and 0.33 ± 0.05, respectively. Notably, Ten-fold cross-validation using genomic BLUP gave a genomic prediction accuracy of 0.70 ± 0.04 for body length, and 0.43 ± 0.09 for body weight, exceeding that for disease resistance trait (0.40 ± 0.13). Evaluation results further indicated that implementing a 30% selection ratio translated into a relative genetic gain (ΔG′) of 17.62% for body weight and 4.28% for disease resistance per generation.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the effectiveness and efficiency of the SNP arrays for applications in GS and breeding. The array and accompanying GS pipeline provide a practical for accelerating genetic improvement in small yellow croaker and can serve as a template for other aquaculture species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12343-w.

Background

The small yellow croaker (Larimichthys polyactis) is an important marine fish species in aquaculture. It inhabits the temperate regions of the Bohai Sea, Yellow Sea, and East China Sea [1]. Belonging to the Sciaenidae family, it shares a close evolutionary relationship with large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea) and Collichthys croakers (Collichthys lucidus). Since its successful artificial breeding was achieved in 2015, it has been widely farmed along the coasts of Zhejiang and Jiangsu provinces in China [2]. However, the rapid development of the small yellow croaker industry has faced severe challenges, particularly from slow growth rate and diseases caused by pathogenic Pseudomonas plecoglossicida. With the increased intensity of aquaculture, the disease problems are intensifying.

Selective breeding for resistance to infectious diseases provides an effective and promising solution to reduce mortality associated with infectious disease outbreaks in aquaculture [3]. Traits related to disease resistance are complex and genetically polygenic in nature, making it difficult to achieve desired outcomes using traditional selective breeding techniques. However, recent advances in cost-effective sequencing technologies have facilitated the use of genomic selection (GS) in breeding aquatic species for disease-resistant traits. GS has increasingly been applied in the breeding of disease-resistant aquatic animals and has demonstrated higher accuracy compared to traditional selective breeding techniques, such as traditional pedigree-based methods [4–6]. Despite these advancements, the relatively high cost of genotyping relative to the market value of farmed fish remains a major economic barrier to the widespread application of GS technology in fish breeding. To address this issue, low-cost, efficient, and robust SNP arrays have been developed for genome-scale genotyping. These arrays have been designed for several important aquaculture species, including Rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) [7], Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) [8], Japanese flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus) [6], Chinese tongue sole (Cynoglossus semilaevis) [9], and large yellow croaker [10]. The implementation of these SNP arrays has significantly improved the accuracy and efficiency of selective breeding through GS. However, no SNP arrays are yet available for small yellow croaker.

Despite its significant economic importance in China and its ecological role in marine biodiversity, the development of genomic and genetic resources for the small yellow croaker has been limited. Recent transcriptomic studies have identified genes associated with adaptations to temperature variations and low-oxygen conditions in small yellow croaker, shedding light on the molecular mechanisms underlying thermal and hypoxic stress responses in euryhaline teleosts [11, 12]. Furthermore, several genetic tools and genomic resources have been established, including genetic maps [13, 14] and two reference genomes [15, 16]. The above research findings provide a valuable foundation for identifying SNP loci and developing SNP arrays. In the present study, a 100 K SNP array specific to small yellow croaker was designed, characterized, and validated. Furthermore, genetic evaluations of the disease resistance trait related to P. plecoglossicida and various growth-related traits were conducted using large-scale genotype data obtained from the SNP array. The findings underscore the promising applications of SNP arrays in genomic breeding programs aimed at improving growth performance, disease resistance, and other economically important traits.

Methods

Identification of SNPs

The SNPs used for the SNP array were obtained from whole-genome resequencing data of 1,511 L. polyactis individuals, including three groups: (i) 120 offspring from a full-sib family, along with their two parents, established in April 2022 at Xiangshan Gangwan Aquatic Seed Co., Ltd., Ningbo, Zhejiang Province, China. The family was produced using a cultured male and a wild female caught in Ningbo coastal, and was analyzed for heat tolerance using quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [17]; (ii) 200 offspring from another full-sib family, also established in April 2022 at the same location using a similar cross (cultured male × wild female), along with their two parents. This group was used for QTL analysis related to muscle nutrient components (Liu et al., unpublished); and (iii) 1,187 wild individuals sampled from the coastal areas of Zhejiang (n = 1,139), Jiangsu (n = 28), and Shandong (n = 20) Province. Genomic DNA was extracted from fin tissues using standard protocols. High-quality DNA (A₂₆₀/A₂₈₀ = 1.8–2.0) was used to construct paired-end libraries with ~ 350 bp inserts and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were filtered via QC-Chain (version 1.0) [18] to remove adaptors, low-quality sequences, and ambiguous nucleotides. Clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome (GenBank NO. GCA_040670005.1.) using BWA (version 0.7.17) [19]. The mapping rate of clean reads ranged from 84.6% to 99.92%. PCR duplicates were removed from the aligned files using SAMtools (version 1.4) [20]. SNP calling was performed with GATK (version 4.2.6.0) [21], applying the following filtering criteria: mapping quality >20, base quality >30, SNP quality >20, and coverage depth >8.

Development of the SNP array

To ensure high-quality variant selection, initial SNP were filtered using VCFtools (version 0.1.14) [22]. Only SNPs with Minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05, missing rate < 0.1, and no significant deviation from Hardy-weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) (p ≥ 0.0001) were retained. For the SNP array design, SNPs were further selected based on MAF >0.15, missing rate < 0.01, and biallelic status. Linkage disequilibrium (LD)-based pruning was then conducted using a 50-SNP sliding window (step size = 5 SNPs), removing SNPs with pairwise r² >0.2 to retain independent loci. Flanking sequences (80–150 bp) were evaluated for GC content (30–70%) to minimize amplification bias in the PCR process. Additionally, Probe design was optimized to minimize secondary structures and off-target binding: hairpin formation and dimerization were avoided by filtering sequences with predicted free energy (ΔG) ≥ −10 kcal/mol (hairpins) and ≥ −5.0 kcal/mol (dimers) using OligoAnalyzer. GC content was balanced (40–60%) and consecutive G/C runs (>4 bp) were avoided to enhance specificity. High specificity was ensured by designing probes in non-repetitive regions. Finally, SNPs were filtered to ensure relatively even coverage across the genome via in-house scripts. All qualifying SNPs were subjected to the probe design program using genotyping by pinpoint sequencing of captured targets technology (Higentec, China) for a 100 K liquid SNP array development.

Assessment of SNP array performance

To evaluate the genotyping performance of the developed 100 K SNP array, a total of 23 individual from a cultured population in 2022 were genotyped. To assess genotyping repeatability, 11 of these samples were independently genotyped twice. Genotyping quality were evaluated based on several key metrics, including genotype call rate, MAF, and concordance between technical replicates. Chromosomal SNP distribution was visualized using the CMplot R package v4.5.1 [23].

Application of the SNP array in genomic selection

The individuals used for GS were offspring of 495 selectively bred broodstocks (377 females and 118 males), provided by Xiangshan Gangwan Co., Ltd. (Ningbo, China). Broodstocks ovulation was induced via artificial injection of chorionic gonadotropin-releasing hormone analog A2, followed by natural mating and fertilization in April 2022. Approximately 85,000 larvae were reared in a 36 m3 indoor pond (6.0 m × 4.0 m × 1.5 m). Subsequently, in November 2022 (approximately 7.5 months post-hatching), 950 individuals sampled from this population were subjected to an artificial bacterial challenge experiments. Briefly, after a two-week acclimatization period, fish were intraperitoneally injected with P. plecoglossicida at a dose corresponding to 5 × 10² colony-forming units (CFU), as determined by a preliminary median lethal dose (LD₅₀) test. Fish were then evenly distributed into 10 tanks (2.5 m3 each) and monitored hourly. During the experiment, the experimental seawater temperature was maintained at 17 ± 0.5 °C. Additionally, an additional 100 individuals injected with sterile medium served as the control group and were maintained under identical conditions. The fish were considered dead if they lost balance and did not recover within 10 s. Survival time (h), body weight (g), body length (cm), and sex were recorded for deceased fish. Survival time was used as the phenotypic trait indicative of resistance to P. plecoglossicida. Muscle tissues were collected and preserved in absolute ethanol for subsequent SNP array-based genotyping at Higentec Co., Ltd. Genotype data were subjected to quality control using Plink software [24], applying filters for individual call rate >0.9, MAF >0.05, and SNP call rate >0.9.

Results

SNP selection and array design

SNP filtering was performed based on MAF ≥ 0.05, detection rate ≥ 0.9, heterozygosity ≤ 0.35, and sequencing depth ≥ 10X, yielding 8.02 million SNPs. After removing non-unique alignments and repetitive sequences in flanking regions, 1.68 million SNPs with optimal probe design were retained. Further filtering for MAF ≥ 0.15, narrowed the selection to 538,024 SNPs. Finally, 100,031 SNPs evenly distributed across 24 chromosomes were selected for probe synthesis and liquid chip preparation (Fig. 1; Table S1).

Genotyping performance of the SNP array

DNA from 23 samples was genotyped using the developed SNP array. All samples exhibited high detection rates, varying between 98.47% and 99.02%, with an average of 98.75%. The average MAF across loci was 0.22. In addition, Technical replicates (11 samples genotyped twice) showed genotype concordance rates ranging from 98.09% to 98.68%, with an average of 98.49% (Fig. 2), indicating high genotyping quality and accuracy of the array.

Phenotype collection in the test group

The P. plecoglossicida challenge test lasted 6 days, with all challenged individuals succumbing within this period. Excluding non-experimental deaths, survival time, body weight, body length, and sex from 946 individuals (487 females and 459 males) were analyzed. Kinship reconstruction based on genotyping data validated 894 individuals (94.5%), assigning them to three full-sib (FS) families of two individuals each and 42 half-sib (HS) families comprising 888 individuals; the remaining 52 samples could not be assigned to any family, indicating high genetic diversity within the test population. Phenotypic analysis indicated that body weight (21.49 ± 7.04 g) and body length (10.91 ± 1.20 cm) exhibited coefficients of variation (CV) of 32.77% and 11.00%, respectively (Table 1).

Statistical analysis of phenotype traits

Body weight and body length exhibited near-normal distributions, consistent with quantitative trait characteristics. During the challenge period, mortality onset occurred ~ 48 h post-injection, with peak mortality observed between 70 and 90 h. Maximum survival time reached 138.5 h (Fig. 3). Survival time distribution approximated a normal distribution, reflecting phenotypic variations in the disease resistance among small yellow croaker individuals, a prerequisite for selective breeding. Post-mortem analysis of randomly selected deceased individuals revealed white nodules in viscera organs (spleen and liver), consistent with P. plecoglossicida-induced pathology. In addition, control group individuals (n = 100) remained asymptomatic throughout the experiment, confirming that experimental group mortality resulted from P. plecoglossicida infection.

Genotyping and population structure

Quality filtering retained 82,901 high-quality SNPs from 946 individuals for further genetic analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a homogeneous population, with the first two principal components explaining 14.1% (PC1), and 12.32% (PC2) of genetic variation (Fig. 4), indicating minimal population stratification.

The variance components and heritabilities estimates for the three traits are presented in Table 2. Genetic gains under different selection pressures

To assess the relative genetic gains (ΔG’) achieved under different selection intensities, the genetic coefficient of variation (GCV), genetic gains (ΔG) and ΔG′ values for body weight, body length, and disease resistance traits in small yellow croaker were predicted based on the estimated additive variance components and heritabilities. Table 4 presents the analyzed results for these traits under selection ratios of 30%, 20%, and 10%, which correspond to selection intensities of 1.16, 1.40, and 1.76, respectively [28]. The results indicate that ΔG and ΔG’ increased with selection intensity increases. Among the traits, the ΔG′ values of body weight were 17.62%, 21.26%, and 26.73% which were greater than those of body length and survival time. Specifically, disease resistance trait (survival time) showed lower ΔG′ values: 4.28%, 5.17%, and 6.50%, under selection pressures of 1.16, 1.40, and 1.76, respectively.

Discussion

High-density SNP arrays and genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) are widely used for population-level genotyping studies [29]. Compared to GBS, SNP arrays offer advantages in efficiency, repeatability, cost-effectiveness, and user accessibility [30], making them particularly suitable for GS applications. With the expansion of genomic resources, there is a growing demand for low-cost, high-throughput SNP arrays in aquaculture breeding programs. To date, medium- to high-density SNP arrays (1.5–690 K) have been developed for multiple aquaculture species including common carp (Cyprinus carpio) [31], large yellow croaker [32], Japanese flounder [6] and tiger pufferfish (Takifugu rubripes) [33]. In the current study, the first 100 K SNP array with 10,031 SNPs of small yellow croaker was developed on the basis of genome resequencing data from both cultured and wild populations.

Chromosomal SNP coverage exhibited uneven distribution, primarily due to probe design constraints arising from regions of low assembly quality in the reference genome. Poorly assembled regions (e.g., centromeres, telomeres, or repetitive sequences) lack uniquely mappable probes, leading to their exclusion during array design. An evaluation of the SNP array’s performance revealed an exceptionally high SNP call rate of 98.75%, surpassing those reported for the 50 K SNP Japanese flounder array (74.7%) [6], the 600 K large yellow croaker array (97.4%) [32], and the 40 K Pacific abalone (Haliotis discus hannai) array (97.41%) [34], and approaching the performance of the 20 K leopard coral grouper (Plectropomus leopardus) array (>99%) [35], and tiger pufferfish array (99.82%) [36]. These results highlight the superior genotyping accuracy of our array for L. polyactis, positioning it as a useful tool for genetic studies and GS breeding.

Disease resistance traits present a considerable challenge for selective breeding due to measurement complexity, low heritability, and the necessity for destructive sampling [37]. Traditional pedigree-based methods yield limited improvements in disease resistance, whereas GS has demonstrated higher prediction accuracy and faster genetic progress [38, 39]. GS relies on a reference population with phenotypic and genotypic data, with SNP effects estimated through various statistical models [40]. Therefore, accurate phenotyping and genotyping are indispensable for effective GS. In the present study, disease resistance trait was quantified as survival time post-injection, a method validated in tilapia [41]. Furthermore, high-quality genotypes were obtained via the developed SNP array, and the moderate heritability of survival time (0.33 ± 0.05) suggests feasible genetic improvement through GS.

The accuracy of genomic prediction depends on population size, statistical methods, and SNP quantity/composition. In this study, a high genomic prediction accuracy of 0.73 ± 0.04 was achieved for body length, using 82,901 SNPs from 946 individuals and the GBLUP model, underscoring GS’s potential to accelerate genetic gain in animal [42].

It is important to note that the GBLUP method assumes that all SNPs have equal weight and variance. However, this assumption may not always be valid, particularly for traits influenced by major genes or quantitative trait loci (QTLs) with large effects. To address this, various alternative genomic evaluation approaches have been developed, including Bayesian methods (e.g., BayesA, BayesB, and BayesC) [42–44], weighted GBLUP, and single-step GBLUP (ssGBLUP) [45, 46]. These models incorporate prior information (e.g., SNP-specific weight or QTL position) to assign contributions to SNPs in high LD, harboring causal mutations, or associated with large-effect QTLs [47], thereby improving GEBV prediction accuracy. Additionally, expanding the reference population size is another effective strategy to enhance the accuracy of genome predictions [48–50]. Moreover, incorporating potential causal markers preselected by GWAS into the GS model has demonstrated significant improvements in predictive performance [51, 52]. These approaches leverage functional annotations or trait-specific associations to refine SNP weighting, offering a promising avenue for optimizing GS in aquaculture breeding programs.

Selective breeding aims to pinpoint superior animals as progenitors to promote favorable genetic gains [53]. Therefore, accurately predicting the impact of selection on economically important traits is crucial for designing efficient breeding programs. While increased selection intensity can enhance genetic gains, but may reduce genetic diversity through inbreeding [54]. Strategic selection intensity is critical to balancing these outcomes [55]. In the present study, a moderate selection intensity (1.16, corresponding to a selection ratio of 30%) was employed, aligning with recommendations to minimize genetic diversity loss in early generations [56]. This approach yielded a ΔG′ of 4.28% for disease resistance trait, lower than values reported for African catfish (Clarias gariepinus) (7.3–42.0%) [57, 58], suggesting a trade-off between genetic gain and diversity preservation. Future studies may explore optimizing selection strategies to maximize gains while maintaining diversity.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated the first 100 K SNP array for L. polyactis, comprising 10,031 high-quality loci. Using a reference population of 946 individuals with 82,901 SNPs, GS analysis employing REML revealed moderate heritability for body weight (0.47 ± 0.05), body length (0.43 ± 0.05), and survival time (0.33 ± 0.05). Genomic prediction accuracy for these traits ranged from 0.40 to 0.73, with body length demonstrating the highest accuracy (0.73 ± 0.04). Application a selection ratio of 30% (selection intensity = 1.16) yielded genetic gains 3.79% (ΔG) and 17.62% (ΔG’) for body weight, and 3.49% (ΔG) and 4.28% (ΔG’) for disease resistance. These findings confirm the efficacy of the SNP array and GS methods in improving growth and disease resistance traits in L. polyactis. This resource provides a robust foundation for future genetic improvement programs targeting economically important traits in aquaculture breeding systems.
