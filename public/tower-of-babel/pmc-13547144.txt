It’s Time to Measure Lp(a): Recognizing a Neglected Cardiovascular Risk
Authors: Hayato Tada, Masayuki Takamura
Published: 2026

See article vol. 33: 1148-1156

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a lipoprotein in which apolipoprotein(a) is covalently linked to an LDL‑like particle via a disulfide bond, and it is an independent risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) which is distinct from LDL 1 - 3) . Lp(a) particles are estimated to possess approximately sixfold greater atherogenicity than LDL particles 4) , and the pharmacologic therapies targeting elevated Lp(a) levels have recently attracted considerable attention. It has long been recognized that the Lp(a) levels and their distribution vary substantially across ethnic groups, and their epidemiology in Japan remains unclear. However, a recent large-scale, multicenter LEAP study conducted in Japan clarified the distribution of the Lp(a) levels and their association with ASCVD in high-risk Japanese populations 5) .

Against this background, the development of Lp(a)-lowering agents, including multiple nucleic acid–based therapeutics and several small‑molecule compounds, has accelerated. The anticipated roles of these emerging therapies include potent Lp(a) reduction, favorable safety profiles, and ultimately, the prevention of ASCVD events. Various agents are currently in different stages of development ( Table 1 ) . In this context, the present study by Fok et al. reports the results of a phase 1 trial evaluating the pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety of the siRNA therapeutic zerlasiran in Japanese patients with elevated Lp(a) levels. In the high-dose group (300 mg), zerlasiran produced a marked and sustained reduction in the Lp(a) levels for up to 150 days, with no major safety concerns 6) .

This raises the question as to how these numerous Lp(a)-lowering agents differ, and if all eventually become available, how might clinicians differentiate their use? Although many of these agents are nucleic acid–based injectable drugs and are expected to be costly, they differ in several clinically relevant aspects, including the dosing intervals (ranging from monthly administration to once every six months or even annually), the magnitude of Lp(a) reduction, and safety. Moreover, some agents already have ongoing clinical outcome trials evaluating ASCVD event reduction, whereas others are still in the early stages, with differences in both their target populations and primary endpoints.

Considering these factors comprehensively—and recalling our clinical experience that patients who develop adverse effects with one statin may tolerate another—having multiple agents with similar mechanisms, but distinct characteristics is likely to be beneficial for both clinicians and patients. We strongly hope that these investigational therapies will successfully achieve their intended endpoints and eventually become available for patients with elevated Lp(a) levels in Japan.

Until such therapies are accessible, clinicians should continue to focus on comprehensive risk reduction through lifestyle modifications (such as smoking cessation and weight reduction), LDL cholesterol management, and blood pressure control. A holistic approach to ASCVD risk management is therefore essential 7 , 8) .

Another important question is whether Lp(a) should be routinely measured in daily clinical practice when assessing patients’ risk for ASCVD 9) . One reason many clinicians remain reluctant to measure Lp(a) is the perception that “there is no available therapy to lower it.” However, Lp(a) measurement is valuable for risk stratification and for guiding interventions on other modifiable risk factors—actions that are already feasible in current practice. Therefore, it is essential to promote a broader awareness of the clinical utility of Lp(a) testing, given that we have a useful tool to harmonize the Lp(a) level assessed by different assays 10) and to increase the recognition and diagnosis of elevated Lp(a) levels.
