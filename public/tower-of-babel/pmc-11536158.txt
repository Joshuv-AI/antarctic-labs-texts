Caution urged for work with newly constructed human coronavirus
Authors: Paul S. Masters
Published: 2024

Hu et al. (1) report the construction of a chimeric coronavirus (rOC43-CoV2-S) in which the spike (S) protein of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) replaces that of the common cold coronavirus HCoV-OC43. Early and conscientious inclusion of institutional biosafety monitoring mechanisms in the course of this work resulted in the designation of rOC43-CoV2-S as a biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) agent. The recombinant was demonstrated to be highly attenuated in tissue culture and in vivo. It has thus been proposed as a safe surrogate for SARS-CoV-2 (a BSL-3 agent) in immunoassays and as a candidate live-attenuated vaccine. Nevertheless, rOC43-CoV2-S is a totally novel human coronavirus, and this raises significant concerns.

First, the impaired viability of rOC43-CoV2-S provides a very strong selective pressure for it to evolve to greater fitness. It has long been known that assembly of S protein into virions is mediated by interactions between the S endodomain and the membrane (M) protein endodomain (2–5). Consequently, the optimal way to engineer coronavirus S protein substitutions is to swap ectodomains (3, 4), not entire ORFs, as in the reported work (1). The S and M endodomains of SARS-CoV-2 and HCoV-OC43 are highly divergent (Fig. 1). There is thus a strong likelihood that the greatly reduced replication of rOC43-CoV2-S relative to either of its parental viruses results from the aberrant pairing of noncognate endodomains. This creates the real possibility that extended passage of rOC43-CoV2-S in tissue culture, in infected animals, or in vaccinated humans could select pseudorevertants harboring second-site suppressor mutations that strengthen the S–M interaction. Such faster-replicating pseudorevertants have been isolated from other constructed chimeric protein substitutions in coronaviruses, based on their ability to rapidly outgrow the original virus (6, 7).

Second, unlike SARS-CoV-2, HCoV-OC43 (and rOC43-CoV2-S) possesses an additional surface glycoprotein, the hemagglutinin-esterase (HE). Generally, coronavirus HE proteins bind to 9-O-acetylated-sialoglycans, to assist cellular entry, and hydrolyze them upon exit, thereby facilitating progeny virion passage through extracellular mucosa. While HCoV-OC43 HE has lost lectin-binding activity compared to that of closely related betacoronaviruses, it retains esterase activity (8). It is entirely unknown whether HE could potentiate the already altered tissue and organ tropism that has been made available to rOC43-CoV2-S through its acquisition of the SARS-CoV-2 S protein. Additionally, HCoV-OC43 encodes its own set of nonstructural proteins that contribute to evasion of innate and adaptive immunity; some of these have no counterparts in SARS-CoV-2. Again, it is unknown whether these could augment in vivo infection by rOC43-CoV2-S or its evolved variants.

HCoV-OC43 exhibits relative genetic stability because it has had over 100 y to optimally adapt to its human host (9). By contrast, SARS-CoV-2 began such an adaptation only 5 y ago, and it continues to more rapidly acquire mutational changes in S and many other genes to increase its fitness and transmissibility in the human population (10). The evolutionary trajectory of rOC43-CoV2-S has not yet begun, and it cannot be readily predicted. Therefore, its projected utilization as a BSL2 reagent or as a live-attenuated vaccine warrants exceptional caution.
