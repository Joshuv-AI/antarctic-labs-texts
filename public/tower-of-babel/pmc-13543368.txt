Real-world efficacy and safety of sofosbuvir/velpatasvir/voxilaprevir in treatment-naive and treatment-experienced chronic hepatitis c patients: a single-center study
Authors: Alper Tahmaz, Ülkü Üser, Merve Safa Keçe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough international guidelines primarily position the sofosbuvir/velpatasvir/voxilaprevir (SOF/VEL/VOX) combination as a salvage therapy for treatment-experienced patients, specific national reimbursement policies in Türkiye have uniquely positioned this regimen as a first-line treatment for treatment-naive individuals. Because international protocols largely restrict this combination to salvage settings, there remains limited real-world evidence regarding its efficacy and safety as a first-line therapy. This study aims to evaluate the real-world clinical performance of the SOF/VEL/VOX regimen in both treatment-naive and treatment-experienced patients with chronic hepatitis C (CHC), thereby providing additional real-world evidence for this underexplored clinical setting.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center observational longitudinal cohort study included 124 non-cirrhotic patients with CHC treated with the SOF/VEL/VOX regimen. The cohort comprised 86 (69.3%) treatment-naive and 38 (30.6%) treatment-experienced patients. Treatment-naive patients received the regimen for 8 weeks, whereas treatment-experienced patients received it for 12 weeks. The primary endpoint was sustained virologic response at 12 weeks post-treatment (SVR12). Liver fibrosis was evaluated using the aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI).ResultsThe mean age was 42 years, with a male predominance (81.4%) and a substantial proportion of individuals from the incarcerated population (44.3%). The most prevalent clinical comorbidities within the cohort were hypertension (8.1%) and diabetes mellitus (8.1%). Genotype 1 (58.1%) and genotype 3 (40.3%) were the most common variants. Regarding baseline differences, treatment-experienced patients were younger (mean age: 37.4 vs. 44.1 years) and exhibited a higher prevalence of genotype 1a infection compared to the treatment-naive group. At SVR12, hepatitis C virus ribonucleic acid (HCV RNA) clearance was achieved in 100% of both treatment-naive and treatment-experienced patients, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 1.000). Concomitant with virological clearance, levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) significantly decreased (p < 0.001), reflecting the resolution of hepatic necroinflammation. Furthermore, the median APRI score decreased from 0.28 at baseline to 0.14 at SVR12 (p < 0.001), reflecting the rapid resolution of hepatic necroinflammation following viral clearance rather than definitive structural fibrosis regression in this predominantly non-cirrhotic cohort. The regimen was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events or treatment discontinuations. Minor transient adverse events (3.2%) resolved completely during follow-up.ConclusionThis real-world study demonstrate that the SOF/VEL/VOX combination achieved a 100% SVR12 rate with excellent tolerability in both treatment-naive and treatment-experienced CHC patients. These findings support the use of SOF/VEL/VOX as a first-line option for treatment-naive patients, particularly in settings where alternative pangenotypic regimens are unavailable, while providing high virological efficacy and promoting rapid resolution of hepatic necroinflammation.Keywords: Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir/Voxilaprevir, Chronic hepatitis C, Real-world data, Sustained virological response, Treatment-naive

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection remains a major global public health concern due to its potential to progress to chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. According to 2022 data from the World Health Organization (WHO), it is estimated that approximately 50 million individuals worldwide are living with chronic HCV infection, with 1 million new cases occurring annually and approximately 242,000 deaths attributed to HCV-related liver diseases [1, 2].

The advent of pangenotypic direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) has transformed chronic HCV treatment, achieving sustained virologic response (SVR) rates exceeding 95% and serving as the cornerstone of the WHO’s 2030 global hepatitis elimination targets [3]. Accordingly, the fixed-dose combination of SOF/VEL/VOX has been approved as an effective pangenotypic regimen for chronic HCV infection across all major genotypes [4].

While guidelines from the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) and the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) primarily position SOF/VEL/VOX as a salvage therapy for patients who have failed prior DAA treatment, regional reimbursement policies significantly shape its real-world application [5, 6]. Because the dual sofosbuvir/velpatasvir (SOF/VEL) combination is unavailable in Türkiye, the national reimbursement policy has supported the use of the triple SOF/VEL/VOX regimen in June 2022 for both treatment-naive (8 weeks) and treatment-experienced (12 weeks) patients [7]. This reimbursement policy has effectively positioned the regimen as a first-line treatment option in Türkiye, differing from current international treatment algorithms [8, 9].

Although Phase 3 clinical trials (e.g., POLARIS) have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of the SOF/VEL/VOX regimen in treatment-naive patients [10], real-world evidence remains largely limited to treatment-experienced cohorts [11, 12]. Within the current reimbursement framework in Türkiye, SOF/VEL/VOX is utilized as a first-line treatment, independent of international salvage-therapy guidelines. This unique reimbursement setting provides an opportunity to evaluate the real-world effectiveness of a regimen generally reserved for salvage therapy when used as first-line treatment, thereby contributing additional evidence to an area that remains relatively underrepresented in the current literature. Accordingly, the present study compares the real-world efficacy and safety of SOF/VEL/VOX between treatment-naive and treatment-experienced patients within this unique reimbursement setting.

Materials and methods

The study was designed as a single-center, retrospective observational longitudinal cohort study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Antalya Training and Research Hospital (Decision no: 9/27, Date: May 7, 2026). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective design of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

The study population consisted of treatment-naive and treatment-experienced patients over 18 years of age, diagnosed with CHC, defined as persistent HCV RNA positivity for at least six months, who received SOF/VEL/VOX (400/100/100 mg) therapy. Patients were classified as ‘treatment-experienced’ if they had a documented history of prior antiviral therapy (e.g., DAAs or interferon-based regimens). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the fact that many patients received their initial treatments at external centers, definitive historical documentation to numerically stratify true virological non-response (failure) versus HCV reinfection following a previous cure was unavailable. Accordingly, this subgroup was analyzed as a single heterogeneous cohort. Patients with other non-HCV-related chronic liver diseases (such as autoimmune hepatitis or primary biliary cholangitis), as well as those with advanced chronic liver disease, specifically compensated or decompensated cirrhosis, or hepatocellular carcinoma, were excluded from the study to maintain cohort homogeneity. To improve diagnostic consistency, the absence of cirrhosis was confirmed using a combined clinical, radiological, and biochemical approach. Specifically, exclusions were verified by baseline abdominal ultrasonography (confirming the absence of nodular liver surfaces, portal hypertension, splenomegaly, or ascites) in conjunction with the non-invasive aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI). Patients were required to have APRI scores well below the established threshold indicative of cirrhosis (APRI < 2.0) [13, 14]. This study included 124 consecutive patients diagnosed with CHC who presented to the Department of Infectious Diseases and Clinical Microbiology of our hospital between June 1, 2022, and December 31, 2025, and met the predefined inclusion criteria. To preserve the integrity of the longitudinal analysis, we employed a complete-case analysis approach; thus, patients were included only if they had complete medical records, including all requisite baseline, treatment, and SVR12 follow-up data. Patients with incomplete documentation were excluded to minimize the potential for bias. The sample size reflects the total population of eligible patients who completed the treatment and follow-up protocol within the study period.

The following data were extracted from the patients’ medical records upon admission to the infectious diseases and clinical microbiology outpatient clinic: demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, and potential risk factors for HCV acquisition), laboratory parameters, history of CHC treatment, adjustments to concomitant medications due to drug-drug interactions, and adverse events. Routine complete blood counts, biochemical, and molecular tests (including HCV genotyping and quantitative HCV RNA assessment) were obtained from existing medical records for the initial visit, at month one of treatment, at the end of treatment, and at month three post-treatment. To evaluate liver fibrosis, the non-invasive APRI was utilized. Although Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) provides greater diagnostic accuracy for fibrosis staging, APRI was selected because it is a dynamic surrogate marker that is particularly sensitive to changes in AST following viral clearance, allowing assessment of early biochemical improvement. Furthermore, while serial AST levels alone reflect pure hepatocyte injury, utilizing APRI as a composite index normalizes AST against the institutional upper limit of normal (ULN) and incorporates platelet counts. This provides a standardized metric that simultaneously accounts for underlying portal hemodynamics and the hepatic microcirculatory status. The APRI score was calculated using the standard formula (APRI = (aspartate aminotransferase (AST) [U/L] / upper limit of normal AST [U/L]) x 100 / platelet count [10⁹/L]) [15]. For all APRI calculations, in this study, the upper limit of normal for AST was defined as 50 U/L, in accordance with our institutional laboratory reference standards.

In accordance with the recommendations of the manufacturer and the national guidelines mandated by the Social Security Institution of Türkiye (Health Implementation Communiqué - SUT), patients received one tablet of SOF/VEL/VOX once daily for 8 weeks in treatment-naive patients, and for 12 weeks in treatment-experienced patients [7, 16]. An HCV RNA level below the lower limit of quantification after the first month of treatment was defined as early virologic response (EVR). The presence of undetectable HCV RNA at the end of treatment was designated as the end-of-treatment response (ETR). Undetectable HCV RNA at 12 weeks following the completion of treatment was classified as sustained virologic response 12 week post-treatment (SVR12). The primary endpoint was the achievement of SVR12. Secondary endpoints were defined as the virologic responses at week four and at the end of treatment (weeks 8 or 12).

Quantitative analysis of HCV RNA levels across all longitudinal follow-up time points (including baseline, month one, end of treatment, and SVR12) was performed using the fully automated Abbott Alinity m (Abbott Molecular, Illinois, USA) system and the Alinity m HCV Assay kit; the lower limit of detection (LoD) of the assay was defined as 12 IU/mL. Conversely, the genotype distribution of the baseline samples (genotypes 1–6) was performed on the ELITe BeGenius® (ELITechGroup, Turin, Italy) platform using the HCV ELITe MGB® Kit, which has a lower limit of detection of 26 IU/mL. Both systems operated on a fully automated ‘sample-to-result’ principle, with nucleic acid extraction and real-time PCR steps completed in integrated modules according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

This study is a descriptive, retrospective observational longitudinal cohort analysis designed to evaluate clinical outcomes in a real-world setting. Given its observational design, it was not powered for formal superiority or non-inferiority comparisons with established clinical trial benchmarks (such as the POLARIS trials). The statistical framework focused on characterizing the clinical and virological experience within our study population, rather than testing a formal hypothesis of equivalence. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0). The normality of continuous variables was formally assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables that were normally distributed, specifically patient age, were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Longitudinal changes in biochemical parameters were evaluated across four predefined longitudinal time points: baseline (pre-treatment), week 4 of therapy, end of treatment (EOT), and SVR12. Given that most clinical laboratory parameters (alanine aminotransferase (ALT), AST, gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), APRI, Platelets, Creatinine, GFR, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP)) were non-normally distributed, they were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) rather than mean ± SD. Therefore, the non-parametric Friedman test was utilized as the repeated-measures ANOVA equivalent to evaluate dynamic changes over time for these variables. For clinical parameters evaluated at exactly two distinct time points—specifically, the pre-treatment (baseline) and 12 weeks post-treatment (SVR12) visits— paired comparisons were performed. Because these variables were non-normally distributed such as the APRI score, AFP, and platelet counts, the non-parametric Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test was utilized in place of the paired t-test to compare these specific milestones. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The mean age of the 124 patients included in the study was 42.0 ± 14.9 years (range: 20–87 years), of whom 81.5% were male. Of the patients, 44.4% (n = 55) were from the incarcerated population. Regarding treatment history, 69.4% (n = 86) of the cohort were treatment-naive, while 30.6% (n = 38) were treatment-experienced patients. Based on ultrasonography findings, 63.7% of the patients had no evidence of steatosis, whereas Grade 1 and Grade 2 hepatic steatosis were detected in 27.4% and 8.9% of the cases, respectively. No pathological findings other than hepatic steatosis were observed on ultrasonography in any of the patients. Regarding potential risk factors for HCV infection, isolated substance use (34.7%) and substance use combined with tattooing (22.6%) were the most frequently reported risk factors.

An evaluation of clinical comorbidities revealed hypertension in 8.1% (n = 10) and diabetes mellitus in 8.1% (n = 10) of the overall cohort. Coinfections with hepatitis B (2.4%, n = 3) and HIV (0.8%, n = 1) were observed at lower rates. Notably, none of the 3 HBV-coinfected patients experienced an HBV flare or spontaneous HBsAg seroconversion during or after SOF/VEL/VOX therapy, confirming the clinical stability of these individuals. Regarding HBV serological assessment, while all patients were screened for active HBV (HBsAg) as part of routine clinical evaluation, routine testing for resolved HBV infection (anti-HBc) was not consistently available across all retrospective clinical files. Among the treatment-experienced group (n = 38), glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB) (55.3%) and ombitasvir/paritaprevir/ritonavir plus dasabuvir (PrOD) (10.5%) were the most frequently used previous treatment regimens. The proportion of males was observed to be higher in the treatment-experienced group (94.7%) compared to the treatment-naive group (75.6%). Baseline virological and biochemical evaluation revealed a median HCV RNA level of 674,812 IU/mL (interquartile range [IQR]: 150,580–2,292,985 IU/mL) for the overall cohort. Analysis of the virological profile demonstrated that genotype 1 (58.1%) was the most frequently observed variant across the overall population. Upon evaluation of genotype 1 subtypes, genotype 1a and genotype 1b strains were detected in 36.3% and 21.8% of the patients, respectively. The second most frequently encountered variant in the overall cohort was genotype 3 (40.3%). Stratification by treatment history revealed that the predominance of genotype 1 was maintained in both the treatment-naive (57.0%) and treatment-experienced (60.5%) groups. However, a subtype-level analysis highlighted that while the proportions of genotype 1a (31.4%) and genotype 1b (25.6%) were relatively comparable in the treatment-naive group, genotype 1a (47.4%) was more common than genotype 1b (13.2%) in the treatment-experienced group. The rare genotype 2 and genotype 4 strains were detected in only a small proportion of the treatment-naive patients (1.2% each) (Table 2).

The biochemical and hematological profiles showed that the hepatic synthetic function of the patients was generally preserved, with no evidence of significant structural dysfunction. Median ALT and AST levels, serving as indicators of hepatic necroinflammation, were elevated as anticipated [52 (IQR: 29–88) and 38 (IQR: 26–55) U/L, respectively], whereas renal function remained optimal across both groups [median estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR): 98 (IQR: 88–111) mL/min]. Consistent with the absence of advanced liver disease, the median platelet count was within normal ranges [250 (IQR: 206–297) × 10³/µL], and coagulation parameters (median PT: 11.80 [IQR: 11.40–12.40] sec and INR: 1.06 [IQR: 1.01–1.10]) alongside the oncological marker (AFP: 2.69 [IQR: 2.19–4.18] ng/mL) remained within the reference range. Notably, the low median APRI score in the overall cohort (0.29 [IQR: 0.20–0.55]; 0.30 [IQR: 0.20–0.58] in treatment-naive and 0.27 [IQR: 0.18–0.45] in treatment-experienced patients) was consistent with the study population predominantly consisting of non-cirrhotic patients without significant fibrotic damage (Table 2).

An analysis of the HCV RNA clearance rates among the total 124 patients receiving SOF/VEL/VOX therapy revealed that viral clearance was achieved in 83.9% (104/124) of the patients at month one of treatment, 98.4% (122/124) at the end of treatment (EOT), and 100% (124/124) at the SVR12 assessment. This longitudinal increase in virologic response was found to be statistically significant (p < 0.001). When patients were stratified into subgroups based on their treatment history, the month one clearance rate in the treatment-naive group (n = 86) was 86.0%, increasing to 97.7% at EOT and reaching 100% at SVR12. Similarly, the treatment-experienced group (n = 38) demonstrated a 78.9% clearance rate at month one, which reached 100% at EOT and was maintained at SVR12. Overall, intergroup comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences in viral clearance rates between the naive and experienced cohorts at any of the evaluated time points: month one (p = 0.427), EOT (p = 1.000), and SVR12 (p = 1.000). These findings demonstrate that SOF/VEL/VOX therapy exhibits high efficacy independent of prior treatment experience, with all patients achieving SVR12 across (Fig. 1). Notably, two patients who did not achieve complete viral clearance at the EOT visit due to minor deviations in treatment adherence (i.e., 3–5 doses remaining at the final assessment) reached undetectable HCV RNA levels by the SVR12 milestone. These patients, characterized by near-threshold viremia at EOT, were captured as observed without data imputation, and their subsequent SVR12 status was consistent with the sustained virological success of the regimen despite these minor deviations. Concurrent with this virologic success, significant improvements were also reflected in hepatic physiological parameters. The APRI score, a widely used non-invasive surrogate marker influenced by both hepatic injury and fibrosis, significantly decreased from a median of 0.29 (IQR 0.20–0.55) before treatment to 0.14 (IQR 0.11–0.18) at SVR12 (p < 0.001). Given the short follow-up period, this decline most likely reflects rapid resolution of hepatic necroinflammation accompanying viral eradication rather than definitive regression of hepatic fibrosis. Furthermore, while no significant difference was detected regarding platelet counts (baseline median: 250 vs. week 12: 250 × 10³/µL, p = 0.640), the levels of AFP, a marker for hepatocellular carcinoma, exhibited a subtle but statistically significant decrease following viral clearance (baseline median: 2.69 [IQR: 2.19–4.18] vs. week 12: 2.40 [IQR: 1.83–3.64] ng/mL, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

An evaluation of the temporal changes in the laboratory parameters of patients receiving SOF/VEL/VOX therapy (Table 3) revealed a progressive improvement in markers of hepatic necroinflammation and cholestasis from the early stages of treatment. The median ALT level, which was 52 (IQR: 29–88) U/L prior to treatment, exhibited a marked decline to 17 (IQR: 14–26) U/L at week 4 of therapy, and further decreased to 15 (IQR: 12–19) U/L at 12 weeks post-treatment, when SVR12 is evaluated, demonstrating a statistically highly significant improvement (p < 0.001). A similar dynamic, in parallel with virological clearance, was also observed in AST values; baseline median AST levels of 38 (IQR: 26–55) U/L showed a gradual decline, decreasing to 17 (IQR: 15–21) U/L by the end of the follow-up period (p < 0.001). Additionally, a statistically significant regression was detected in the levels of GGT, a marker of cholestatic liver injury and oxidative stress, at 12 weeks post-treatment (median: 19, IQR: 15–25 U/L) compared to pre-treatment levels (median: 38, IQR: 24–71 U/L) (p < 0.001). Regarding the cholestatic marker ALP, although a statistically significant variation was observed across the four time points analyzed (Friedman test, p = 0.021), a pairwise comparison between the pre-treatment and 3-month post-treatment (SVR12) levels revealed no significant difference (Wilcoxon test, p = 0.789). This indicates that ALP levels showed minor temporal variation but essentially returned to baseline values by the end of the follow-up, demonstrating an overall stable profile.

Upon evaluating the parameters reflecting hepatic synthetic function, a statistically significant improvement was detected in PT compared to the baseline value (13.28 ± 4.85 s) (12 weeks post-treatment: 11.71 ± 0.87 s, p < 0.001). Concurrently, INR values remained stable and within the normal reference range from baseline (1.07 ± 0.12) through the 12-week post-treatment follow-up (1.06 ± 0.08), with no statistically significant variation observed (p = 0.129). The APRI score, a widely used non-invasive surrogate marker of hepatic parenchymal damage and fibrosis, showed a significant decline, declining from a pre-treatment level of 0.28 (0.04–1.43) to 0.14 (0.06–0.51) during the SVR12 period (p < 0.001). Conversely, no significant difference was detected between the pre- and post-treatment periods regarding platelet counts (baseline and week 12: 250.00 × 10³/µL, p = 0.640) (Table 3).

Renal function remained stable throughout the course of the antiviral regimen. The pre-treatment median creatinine [0.95 (IQR: 0.84–1.04) mg/dL] and glomerular filtration rate [median eGFR: 98 (IQR: 88–111) mL/min/1.73 m²] values of the patients were preserved without requiring treatment modification, and no statistically significant alterations in renal functions were recorded throughout the study period (Friedman test, p > 0.05). These findings suggest that the administered pangenotypic salvage therapy exhibits a favorable renal safety profile from a renal standpoint (Table 3).

Following the initiation of treatment, a marked improvement in biochemical parameters was observed across all patients. Baseline median ALT, AST, and GGT levels were determined to be 71.90 U/L, 47.32 U/L, and 53.46 U/L, respectively. A rapid and statistically significant decline in liver enzymes was recorded as early as the first month of therapy (p < 0.001 for all parameters). These reduced levels were maintained until the end of treatment (EOT) and exhibited a stable course during the 12-week post-treatment follow-up (Fig. 2A-C). Furthermore, the APRI score, a non-invasive marker of fibrosis, demonstrated a significant reduction from a baseline value of 0.28 to 0.14 at SVR12 (p < 0.001, Fig. 2D). These findings indicate a marked reduction in hepatic inflammation alongside an improvement in non-invasive surrogate markers throughout the study duration.

Safety analyses demonstrated that the SOF/VEL/VOX salvage therapy was well tolerated within the study cohort (n = 124). Throughout the treatment course, no serious adverse events (SAEs) or treatment modifications or discontinuations due to side effects were reported. During the first month of therapy (early phase), mild adverse events were observed in only 3.2% (n = 4) of the patients, with the most frequently reported symptoms being nausea (n = 3), headache (n = 2), diarrhea (n = 2), and fatigue (n = 1). All of these treatment-related adverse events were transient in nature and did not require treatment interruption while patients continued medication. No patients reported adverse events at the end of treatment (EOT) visit (n = 0). By the SVR12 follow-up period, all adverse events previously reported during the first month of therapy had resolved (n = 0). These findings suggest that the SOF/VEL/VOX regimen, in addition to its high virological clearance rates, offers a favorable safety profile in both treatment-naive and treatment-experienced patients.

Discussion

This retrospective observational longitudinal cohort study evaluated the real-world efficacy and safety of the pangenotypic SOF/VEL/VOX combination regimen in treatment-naive and treatment-experienced CHC patients. International guidelines (EASL and AASLD) primarily recommend the SOF/VEL/VOX regimen as a “salvage therapy” for patients who have previously failed DAA therapy [5, 6]. Although the Phase 3 POLARIS-2 and POLARIS-3 trials demonstrated the high efficacy of this medication in treatment-naive patients, current guidelines advocate dual combinations (SOF/VEL or GLE/PIB) as the first-line preference for naive cohorts [5, 6, 10, 17]. As noted earlier, Turkish reimbursement policies have created a unique cohort of treatment-naive patients receiving SOF/VEL/VOX [7]. This reimbursement framework has generated a cohort profile that differs from standard international practice, providing real-world evidence on the first-line use of SOF/VEL/VOX in a predominantly treatment-naive population (69.4%). Furthermore, the presence of a substantial proportion of incarcerated individuals (44.4%) in our cohort is a significant demographic characteristic that deserves particular attention. This specific setting likely contributes to the marked male predominance (81.5%) and heavily influences the overall distribution of risk factors for HCV acquisition observed in our study, as the penal system and the associated potential risk factors for HCV infection (namely isolated intravenous drug use [34.7%] and concurrent drug use with unsterile tattooing [22.6%]) were more frequently reported among male participants. While incarcerated populations are traditionally considered marginalized and “hard-to-reach” in civilian healthcare settings, the structured medical environment within the penal system may offer certain practical advantages for HCV management. The strict supervision and closely monitored medication administration inherent to prison healthcare facilities may have contributed to high treatment adherence to the daily SOF/VEL/VOX regimen. This structured treatment setting may have contributed to the high SVR12 rate observed in our cohort. Consequently, demonstrating such optimal virological outcomes with this highly tolerable pangenotypic combination provides additional real-world evidence supporting the inclusion of penitentiary systems among the settings targeted for HCV micro-elimination strategies.

The 100% SVR12 rate achieved in our study across both the treatment-naive and treatment-experienced arms is consistent with other phase and real-world studies in the literature. In the Phase 3 POLARIS-2 trial conducted by Jacobson et al., an SVR12 rate of 95% was reported following an 8-week SOF/VEL/VOX regimen in treatment-naive patients. Similarly, among recent real-world data reported from Türkiye, Damar Çakırca et al. documented a 100% SVR12 rate in a cohort of 23 naive patients, while Binay et al. reported a 98.5% SVR12 rate in a study comprising 75 naive patients [8, 9]. Comprising data from 86 treatment-naive patients, our research represents one of the larger real-world cohorts of treatment-naive patients treated with SOF/VEL/VOX reported after the phase 3 trials. Regarding treatment-experienced cohorts, Degasperi et al., in a multicenter study in Italy, reported that 162 out of 169 patients (96%) achieved SVR12; Liu et al., in a multicenter study in Taiwan, observed SVR12 in all 104 (100%) per-protocol patients; and a meta-analysis by Xie et al. demonstrated a 96% SVR12 rate among 1,796 per-protocol patients across 15 studies [18–20]. In our study, the 38 treatment-experienced patients predominantly consisted of individuals with prior exposure to GLE/PIB, followed by PrOD. The SVR12 rate in this subgroup was 100%, yielding outcomes that are consistent with the upper range of SVR12 rates reported in the existing literature. The fact that our real-world data (100% SVR12) appears slightly higher than the rates reported in some phase 3 trials can likely be attributed to the exclusion of cirrhotic and decompensated patients from our cohort, coupled with high patient adherence to the treatment. Importantly, while international EASL and AASLD guidelines predominantly reserve the SOF/VEL/VOX regimen as a salvage therapy to optimize drug stewardship, our 100% clearance rate demonstrates that its application as a first-line therapy is highly effective [5, 6]. The high virological efficacy observed in treatment-naive patients appears comparable to the efficacy of standard first-line pangenotypic regimens (such as dual SOF/VEL or GLE/PIB) recommended globally [5, 6]. Therefore, these data support that the strategy of utilizing SOF/VEL/VOX as a “first-line therapy in treatment-naive patients” within Turkish clinical practice is not only an administrative consequence of the reimbursement policy but also a clinically feasible treatment approach from a clinical and virological standpoint.

Concurrent with virological clearance, significant improvements were observed in biochemical and structural hepatic parameters. ALT and AST levels, the primary indicators of hepatic necroinflammation, exhibited a marked and statistically significant (p < 0.001) decline compared to their baseline values as early as the first month of therapy, and this reduction was maintained during the SVR12 period. As reported in the extensive Egyptian cohort by Omar et al. (n = 9515) and a large German registry study, the detection of elevated pre-treatment ALT and AST levels is an expected finding in chronic hepatitis C patients and serves as an early indicator of mild-to-moderate hepatocellular damage [21–23]. This rapid decline in aminotransferase levels, facilitated by DAA therapy, is a clinical manifestation of the rapid reduction in hepatic inflammatory activity within hepatocytes concomitant with the suppression of viral replication. Correspondingly, the study conducted by Atta et al. on patients receiving SOF/VEL/VOX reported a statistically significant reduction in ALT and AST levels at week 24 [24]. Indeed, studies by Hsu et al. and Huynh et al. have demonstrated that ALT and AST values normalize within weeks immediately following the initiation of DAA therapy and remain stable throughout the post-treatment period; our findings are consistent with these previous reports [25, 26]. Additionally, the significant regression (p < 0.001) observed in our study regarding the levels of GGT, a marker of cholestasis and oxidative stress, supports the improvement in hepatic inflammatory and cholestatic activity.

Regarding the synthetic functions of the liver (albumin, bilirubin, INR) and other laboratory parameters (platelets, AFP), certain variations exist in the literature. In our study, no statistically significant difference was observed in INR values throughout the follow-up period, and no prolonged INR abnormality was detected. This finding is consistent with the results of Shousha et al., who did not detect prolonged INR in patients receiving SOF/VEL/VOX, as well as the data of Atta et al., who observed no significant differences in albumin and INR [24, 27]. Conversely, different post-treatment patterns were observed concerning platelet counts and AFP levels. Platelet counts remained stable with no statistically significant alterations following therapy. While this stability diverges from the findings of Atta et al., who reported significant increases in platelet counts, it aligns closely with the data of Elhammady et al., who similarly reported no significant alterations in platelets. In contrast to platelet stability, we detected a subtle yet highly significant decrease in AFP levels (p < 0.001) during the SVR12 period. Although Atta et al. reported a paradoxical post-treatment elevation in AFP, our observation of a significant decline is biologically plausible and consistent with the broader consensus in DAA literature. This reduction in AFP—a well-established surrogate marker for hepatocellular carcinoma risk—may reflect the reduction of hepatic necroinflammation, decreased virus-related hepatocyte turnover, and the subsequent decrease in oncogenic drive secondary to viral eradication [24, 27, 28]. Ultimately, the limited variation in structural and synthetic parameters such as platelets and INR can be primarily attributed to the fact that hepatic functions were already well-preserved at baseline (compensated phase) within our predominantly non-cirrhotic cohort.

One of the notable biochemical findings in our study was the significant decline of the APRI score (APRI: 0.14, p < 0.001), a non-invasive marker of liver fibrosis, during the SVR12 period within our cohort, which predominantly comprised patients with compensated and early-stage disease (baseline APRI: 0.28). As emphasized by Hsu et al., this rapid decline indicates an early resolution of hepatic necroinflammation and microcirculatory dysfunction secondary to viral clearance, rather than a slow-developing structural regression of fibrosis [29]. In this clinical context, utilizing APRI provides a distinct methodological advantage over monitoring serial AST levels alone; by integrating platelet counts, APRI captures not only the cessation of acute hepatocyte necrosis but also reflects early improvements in portal hemodynamics and the hepatic microenvironment. Indeed, as demonstrated in the comprehensive review by Orasan et al. and the real-world data of Tufan et al., this cellular recovery observed in the APRI score following DAA therapy has been reported predominantly in individuals with F0-F2 early-stage liver disease and is closely associated with the normalization dynamics of aminotransferases (ALT and AST) [30, 31]. Furthermore, consistent with the long-term follow-up data of Huynh et al., the median APRI score of 0.14 achieved at SVR12 in our cohort fell below the safe threshold of 0.5 (< 0.5), suggesting a low likelihood of clinically significant fibrosis [32]. When these findings are evaluated holistically in the context of current literature, they demonstrate that the administered SOF/VEL/VOX regimen not only achieves virological cure and is associated with early improvement in hepatic inflammatory activity in the early phase by substantially reducing the inflammatory burden within the hepatic parenchyma, thereby driving the early improvement in APRI scores before any potential long-term structural changes can be determined.

The safety and tolerability profile of the SOF/VEL/VOX regimen in our study is consistent with global real-world evidence. The complete absence of serious adverse events (SAEs) or treatment discontinuations in our cohort is comparable to the low discontinuation rates (0.66%) reported in the extensive meta-analysis by Xie et al. [20], as well as corroborating findings from large multicenter cohorts by Yoon et al., Heo et al., and Gheorghe et al. [12, 33, 34]. Furthermore, the non-specific, mild, and transient symptoms (e.g., headache, nausea) observed in only 3.2% of our patients are similar to the manageable adverse event spectrum reported in local and international literature [8]. These findings suggest that this triple is not associated with unexpected toxicity and appears to be a well-tolerated therapeutic option, even in complex patient groups such as incarcerated individuals with polypharmacy risks.

Several methodological limitations must be considered when evaluating the findings of our study. First, the single-center and retrospective nature of our research, coupled with a relatively small sample size (n = 124), limits the statistical power and generalizability of the data. Furthermore, reflecting a common challenge in retrospective real-world studies involving marginalized populations, incomplete historical medical records from external centers prevented the precise numerical stratification of the treatment-experienced cohort into strict virological failures versus reinfections. Second, the strong male predominance (81.5%) and the specific distribution of reported risk factors—driven heavily by the large proportion of incarcerated individuals and male-dominated risk factors for HCV acquisition, such as intravenous drug use—creates a significant demographic and epidemiological skew. Consequently, while our findings suggest high efficacy within this specific high-risk subgroup, they may not be entirely generalizable to the broader, more gender-balanced civilian HCV population (e.g., female patients infected via historical nosocomial routes). Third, the evaluation of liver fibrosis relied exclusively on the non-invasive APRI score without histological confirmation via liver biopsy. Although APRI is widely validated, its strong reliance on transaminase levels makes it highly reflective of early necroinflammation, thereby limiting our capacity to definitively confirm true, long-term structural fibrosis regression. Fourth, the study design involved the deliberate exclusion of patients with cirrhosis or decompensated liver disease, as well as those with incomplete medical records. While this approach is a limitation regarding the broader generalizability of our findings—specifically concerning patients with advanced liver disease—it was a necessary design choice to ensure internal validity. By focusing on a well-defined non-cirrhotic cohort and employing a complete-case analysis, we reduced the confounding effects of advanced structural liver damage and avoided the potential statistical biases associated with data imputation. Consequently, our findings are particularly relevant for the non-cirrhotic population, though they should be extrapolated with caution to patients with advanced liver disease, for whom the biochemical response dynamics may differ. Fifth, the inability to perform baseline resistance-associated substitution (RAS) testing due to regional laboratory infrastructure constraints could be considered a limitation; however, contemporary real-world data suggest that the presence of RASs may not substantially affect the overall SVR12 success of the SOF/VEL/VOX regimen. Finally, our assessment of clinical and virological response was limited to the SVR12 period. The absence of long-term follow-up beyond SVR12 limits our ability to determine the persistence of liver fibrosis regression or to exclude the risk of de novo hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) development—reported in the literature even after successful HCV treatment—which necessitates large-scale and long-term prospective follow-up studies. Sixth, we acknowledge the absence of comprehensive anthropometric data, including body weight, height, and body mass index. While these metabolic factors are increasingly recognized as important modifiers of hepatic fibrosis and disease progression, the retrospective nature of our clinical documentation—compounded by the logistical challenges in routine data collection within the incarcerated subgroup—precluded a reliable systematic analysis. Future prospective investigations should integrate these metabolic indices to provide a more detailed characterization of the study population and further elucidate their impact on sustained virological response. Seventh, while APRI served as a sensitive and reliable tool to monitor the rapid resolution of hepatic necroinflammation in our study, we acknowledge that FIB-4 generally offers superior diagnostic granularity for staging chronic liver fibrosis by incorporating age. Future prospective investigations should integrate FIB-4 to better characterize the baseline fibrotic burden and provide more comprehensive longitudinal prognostic data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this real-world study demonstrates that the SOF/VEL/VOX combination achieved an excellent SVR12 rate of 100% in both treatment-naive (8 weeks) and treatment-experienced (12 weeks) chronic hepatitis C patients, while demonstrating a favorable safety profile and showing rapid improvements in biochemical parameters and surrogate indices of hepatic necroinflammation. Although international guidelines primarily position this regimen as a “salvage therapy,” our findings suggest it is an effective and well-tolerated strategy even when utilized as a first-line treatment in naive patients, within the context of the current reimbursement policies in Türkiye. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the study’s retrospective, single-center design and the strict exclusion of cirrhotic cases. Within these methodological boundaries, our data support the operational feasibility of this regimen in mixed, non-cirrhotic patient groups—including underserved populations with potential adherence challenges. Therefore, within similar clinical settings, the SOF/VEL/VOX regimen serves as a reliable pharmacological tool to support targeted, local HCV micro-elimination efforts. To build upon these foundational findings, future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter prospective studies that include patients with advanced cirrhosis. Furthermore, extended longitudinal follow-up beyond the SVR12 milestone would be valuable to better assess the durability of fibrotic regression and to evaluate the long-term risk of de novo hepatocellular carcinoma following viral clearance.
