Re-emergence of Bundibugyo Ebola virus disease in Central Africa: A critical threat to regional health security and the imperative for cross-border surveillance and epidemic preparedness in East Africa
Authors: Yusuf Abdullahi Hubow, Abas Nor Abdi, Nasteho Osman Hassan
Published: 2026

Letter to the Editor

The re-emergence of Bundibugyo Ebola virus disease (BDBV) in Central Africa is an under-recognized but urgent threat to regional health security. Unlike the more widely publicized Zaire ebolavirus, BDBV has caused significant outbreaks with high case fatality rates, notably in Uganda and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), and its resurgence now poses acute risks for East Africa due to porous borders, intense population mobility, trade routes, refugee flows, weak surveillance systems, and fragile health infrastructure [1,2]. The interconnectedness of Central and East African regions marked by frequent cross-border movement and shared vulnerabilities demands immediate attention to prevent localized outbreaks from escalating into regional epidemics.

Ebola virus disease (EVD) continues to exert a heavy public health burden across Africa. Recent outbreaks have demonstrated that all four human-pathogenic ebolaviruses including BDBV can cause severe disease with substantial morbidity and mortality [2,3]. While Zaire ebolavirus remains the most lethal, BDBV's case fatality rate is still considerable (32.8%) and its outbreaks are characterized by delayed detection, rapid spread, and significant disruption of health systems [2,4]. The 2012 BDBV outbreak in DRC challenged assumptions about single-source spillover events, highlighting complex transmission dynamics that complicate containment efforts [2]. Lessons from West Africa's devastating 2013–2016 epidemic underscore the catastrophic consequences of delayed action: economic losses exceeding $50 billion, widespread social disruption, and erosion of public trust [1].

Central and East Africa remain highly vulnerable due to persistent weaknesses in cross-border surveillance, laboratory confirmation capacity, case detection, contact tracing, infection prevention and control (IPC), community trust, and emergency response systems [3]. National-level preparedness alone is insufficient for a pathogen with clear regional transmission potential. The risks of delayed or fragmented action include uncontrolled spread across borders, health system collapse, border closures, economic downturns, misinformation proliferation, and further loss of community confidence [1]. Conflict-affected areas face additional barriers: population displacement disrupts surveillance networks; insecurity impedes humanitarian access; and fragile governance undermines coordinated response.

Given these realities, Bundibugyo Ebola virus disease demands urgent prioritization by ministries of health, WHO, Africa CDC, donors, researchers, and regional bodies. Low-resource border communities often highly mobile or affected by conflict are especially at risk. Strengthening cross-border surveillance is not merely a technical necessity but a moral imperative for regional solidarity. Real-time information sharing between countries is essential for early warning and rapid containment [3]. Joint outbreak preparedness plans must be established between Central and East African nations to ensure harmonized protocols for detection, isolation, referral, IPC measures, contact tracing, risk communication, community engagement, logistics support, genomic surveillance, vaccine research specific to BDBV (for which no licensed vaccine currently exists), and emergency financing mechanisms [2].

Recommendations include:

Expanding rapid diagnostic testing networks with mobile laboratories capable of deployment at outbreak epicenters.

Training frontline health workers in Ebola recognition and safe management;

Investing in sustainable emergency response teams;

Enhancing genomic surveillance for real-time tracking of viral evolution;

Supporting vaccine/therapeutic research tailored to BDBV;

Improving coordination among ministries of health, WHO/Africa CDC/EAC/humanitarian agencies;

Prioritizing risk communication strategies that build community trust while countering misinformation;

Institutionalizing joint simulation exercises at border crossings to test operational readiness.

Ensuring equitable resource allocation for fragile border populations.

In conclusion: The re-emergence of Bundibugyo Ebola virus disease is a clarion call for urgent regional action. Without decisive investment in cross-border surveillance and epidemic preparedness grounded in evidence-based policy and multisectoral collaboration localized outbreaks will continue to threaten the entire region's health security. Now is the time for governments and partners to act collectively before another preventable crisis unfolds.
