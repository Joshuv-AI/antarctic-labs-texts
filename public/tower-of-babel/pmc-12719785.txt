An RbohC and RbohE-mediated ROS oscillatory circuit regulates root cap turnover in Arabidopsis
Authors: Ye-Sol Shin, Woo-Taek Jeon, Hoon Jung, Thanh Ha Thi Do, Yuree Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAs the primary interface between the growing root and the soil, the root tip experiences continuous mechanical and environmental stress. The root cap protects this region through dynamic turnover, in which outer cells are regularly shed and replaced by new cells derived from inner initials. This turnover proceeds via two distinct pathways: programmed cell death in the lateral root cap and detachment of the outermost columella root cap. Focusing on the latter, we show that reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels oscillate within the columella during its developmental cycle in Arabidopsis thaliana. The disruption of these oscillations through chemical inhibitors or exogenous ROS impairs columella initial division and detachment. We identify RbohC and RbohE as major ROS producers, negatively regulated by the NAC transcription factor ANAC33/SOMBRERO. ROS oscillations also affect auxin distribution by modulating the transporter PIN-FORMED2. These findings reveal a redox-based mechanism integrating ROS and auxin signaling to coordinate columella root cap turnover.Subject areas: molecular biology, plant biology, plant histology, Plant cell biology

Introduction

Roots rely on their ability to sense and respond to diverse environmental cues while maintaining their structural integrity under challenging conditions. At the forefront of this adaptive mechanism is the root cap, a structure located at the outermost tip of the root,1 which plays a crucial role in protecting the root from biotic and abiotic stress.2 The root cap also transmits environmental signals to inner root tissues, allowing the root to adjust its directional growth through processes such as gravitropism,3,4 hydrotropism,5,6,7 and halotropism.8

The root cap undergoes continuous cycles of shedding and replenishment to maintain its protective and functional roles at the root tip. The root cap comprises central columella cells containing gravity-sensing statoliths and lateral root cap (LRC) cells surrounding the columella. Through the coordinated division of columella initials and the timely detachment of the outermost layer, the root cap maintains a consistent number of layers throughout root development.9,10,11 As the outermost layer is frequently exposed to mechanical stress and environmental stimuli, its timely removal and replacement with a new protective layer are essential for sustaining optimal root growth.12,13,14,15

Root cap development, encompassing the supply of new cells and their subsequent maturation leading to detachment, is regulated by distinct processes that require precise coordination between these two phases. Root cap development primarily involves the proliferation of columella stem cells, which establish and generate the root cap. In Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), this phase is regulated by the transcription factor ANAC009 (FEZ), which promotes division in the columella initial layer. ANAC33/SOMBRERO (SMB) antagonizes FEZ in daughter cells, limiting further division and initiating the transition to maturation.11,16 Maturation, in turn, involves programmed cell death, subcellular reorganization, cell wall modification, and cell separation, all of which are crucial for the functional turnover of the root cap. SMB, along with the Class IIB NAC transcription factors BEARSKIN1 (BRN1) and BRN2, regulates these processes, ensuring the proper maturation of the root cap.2,11,17,18,19,20

While the molecular basis of the transition from development to maturation remains incompletely understood, emerging evidence suggests that this process is tightly regulated by hormonal cues—particularly auxin.18,21 A spatial auxin gradient, with a maximum at the quiescent center (QC) and a minimum at the detaching layer, appears essential for maintaining root cap size by balancing cell division, differentiation, and detachment.21 Although the precise mechanisms are still unclear, auxin transporters such as PIN-FORMED (PIN) proteins are likely involved in regulating this transition.21,22

The final stage of root cap maturation involves autophagy-mediated cellular clearance and cell wall modification, both of which are crucial for the actual separation of the outermost layer of the root cap. Autophagy-mediated clearance facilitates the vacuolization and shrinkage of amyloplasts in the outermost detaching layer.19,23,24 Key autophagy genes, such as ATG2, ATG5, and ATG7, regulate this process, resulting in subcellular reorganization and programmed cell death.20,25 Mutants of these genes exhibit incomplete clearance and defective root cap detachment. Additionally, cell wall modification plays a critical role in final detachment. CELLULASE3 (CEL3) and CEL5 hydrolyze cellulose, while QUASIMODO1 (QUA1) and ROOT CAP POLYGALACTURONASE (RCPG) promote pectin degradation, collectively facilitating the separation of the outermost root cap layer.17,18,26

Another important aspect of the root cap cycle is its precise regulation, with its timing being finely tuned in response to root tip damage and environmental stimuli. However, the mechanisms through which these processes are integrated with environmental signals remain poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate that reactive oxygen species (ROS), acting as second messengers in various environmental signal transduction pathways,27,28 play a crucial role in fine-tuning the timing of the detachment cycle in Arabidopsis. ROS accumulation follows an oscillatory pattern that aligns with the root cap cycle, influencing the timing of root cap detachment. RbohC and RbohE are key to this oscillation, and their expression is negatively regulated by the transcription factor SMB. Furthermore, we show that ROS oscillations are linked to local auxin flux via modulating the PIN2 level at the root apex. This regulation of auxin dynamics affects the establishment of an auxin minimum, which plays a crucial role in regulating the timing of root cap differentiation and eventual separation.

Results

Reactive oxygen species oscillations regulate root cap detachment and columella initial division

Roots undergo periodic root cap shedding throughout their growth.21 For phenotypic quantification, we classified the patterns of root cap detachment into the following stages: 1) Stage 1: Root cap cells remain intact but have cleared amyloplasts; 2) Stage 2a: The outermost root cap layer initiates an organized detachment process, starting with LRC cells; 3) Stage 2b: Non-canonical detachment, characterized by the independent separation of individual cells, begins at the root tip; 4) Complete detachment: Root cap cells have fully detached, marking the completion of the last layer’s shedding and the initial stage for the next root cap detachment cycle (Figure S1A).

The first root cap detachment occurs at 4 days after germination (DAG), as indicated by the physical separation of the outermost layer visible in bright-field (BF) images. Notably, the degradation of the amyloplast in this layer is already apparent by 3 DAG, preceding the detachment event (Figure 1A). This initial detachment, referred to as Cycle I, is marked by a cuticle layer on the outermost root cap cells,29 visualized using Fluorol Yellow 088 (FY) staining (Figure 1A). This cuticle layer is unique to Cycle I and is not re-formed in subsequent cycles. After Cycle I, detachment proceeds in a cyclic manner, with Cycle II initiating the next round of shedding (Figure 1B). At 4 DAG, 59.29% (±6.12) of seedlings had completed root cap detachment and transitioned to the next phase of the cycle (Figure 1C). Subsequent detachments occurred at ∼2.5-day intervals. Detachment patterns showed slight variations between the first and second rounds. In the first round, the rate of disorganized shedding reached 14.75% (±2.93), but by 6 DAG, this rate had decreased to 3.33% (±7.14), with most layers separating in an organized manner.

To explore the mechanism underlying the timing of the root cap cycle, we investigated the relevance of ROS, which acts as signaling mediators in various developmental and stress-responsive pathways.30,31 ROS levels at the root apex were visualized using cell-permeant fluorescent indicator 2ʹ,7ʹ-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA).32,33 Localized ROS accumulation was observed in the outermost columella and adjacent LRC cells. Quantification in the outermost columella layer revealed a gradual increase in ROS levels, peaking just before detachment at 3 DAG, followed by a sharp decline at 4 DAG when detachment occurred (Figure 1A). This rise-and-fall pattern was repeated during the next detachment cycle (Figure 1D), suggesting a potential role for ROS in regulating the root cap cycle. To ensure this pattern was not an artifact of vacuolization during cell differentiation, we used constitutive cytoplasmic markers (pUBQ10::GFP and pUBQ10::4xYFP) as controls. These markers showed stable signal intensities throughout the cycle (Figures S1B–S1D).

To independently validate ROS fluctuations, we employed the ratiometric HyPer3 construct driven by the UBQ10 promoter. Consistent with DCFH-DA results, HyPer3 signals declined at the detachment point and re-accumulated thereafter (Figures S1E and S1F). To distinguish true ROS responses from potential pH artifacts, we used the HyPer3-C199S variant, which is ROS-insensitive but pH-sensitive. This control showed no signal change throughout the cycle (Figure S1G). Further supporting ROS specificity, treatments with H2O2 and DTT (a ROS scavenger) induced dynamic signal changes only in the HyPer3 line, while HyPer3-C199S remained unresponsive (Figures S1H–S1J), confirming that the observed fluorescence changes represent genuine ROS dynamics.

To test whether ROS actively regulates root cap detachment, we perturbed ROS levels chemically. We briefly sprayed 1 mM H2O2 solution on top of the agar plates and removed excess solution after ∼10 s, resulting in an estimated final exposure below 0.2 mM (see STAR Methods). Under this treatment, root cap detachment was delayed: at 4 DAG, the completion rate decreased from 41.1% (±7.30) in mock to 25.09% (±3.18), with most root caps remaining at the intermediate separation stage (54.60% ± 2.88; Figure 1E). Importantly, this treatment did not affect cell viability, as PI uptake remained below 1%, indicating that membrane integrity was largely preserved (Figures S1K and S1L). In contrast, treatment with the NADPH oxidase inhibitor VAS2870 accelerated detachment, increasing the completion rate to 66.59% (±5.42; Figure 1E).

The detachment cycle must be precisely coordinated with the division of columella initials to maintain a consistent number of columella layers. Using a Click-iT 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) cell proliferation assay at the root tip, we observed an oscillatory pattern in EdU incorporation, leaking at 4 DAG—coinciding with the timing of root cap detachment (Figures 1A and 1F). Perturbing ROS levels altered this division pattern: exogenous ROS application reduced EdU-positive cells, whereas ROS inhibition by VAS2870 significantly increased them (Figures 1G and S1M). This mirrors the effect of ROS on root cap detachment. Together, these findings suggest that the oscillatory ROS pattern at the root cap—reaching its lowest point at the time of detachment—serves as a regulatory signal that synchronizes both detachment and columella initial division.

RbohC and RbohE generate reactive oxygen species oscillations that regulate root cap detachment

To identify the sources of ROS oscillations during the root cap detachment cycle, we examined the expression and function of NADPH oxidases. Several Rboh genes are expressed during the seedling stage (Figures S2A and S2B)34,35,36,37,38; however, at the root tip, only RbohC and RbohE exhibited detectable promoter activity (Figures S2C and S2D). RbohC was broadly expressed across the columella layers and expanded its domain over time, whereas RbohE remained confined to the outermost layers (Figure 2A; Figures S2E and S2F). Both proteins localized to the plasma membrane (Figure S2G). Although RbohC and RbohE are strongly expressed in the primary root, the rbohC rbohE-2 double mutant showed no significant change in primary root length compared to the WT (Figure S2H), suggesting a specific role in the root cap. RT-qPCR confirmed altered transcript levels in both mutant and overexpression lines (Figure S2I).

The rbohC rbohE-2 mutant showed reduced ROS levels and precocious root cap detachment, whereas the overexpression of RbohC or RbohE delayed detachment and increased ROS levels at 4 DAG (Figures 2B–2E; Figures S3A and S3B). These phenotypes were recapitulated using an rbohE-1 allele (Figures S3C and S3D). To assess the effects of overexpression, we expressed RbohC and RbohE under the constitutive UBQ10 promoter. Interestingly, despite ubiquitous transcriptional activation, both proteins preferentially accumulated at the root tip, especially in the outermost layer (Figures S3E and S3F), suggesting that their spatial enrichment may be regulated at the post-transcriptional or protein stability level. Correspondingly, ROS levels were elevated in the same region (Figure 2B). Overexpression abolished the developmental oscillation of ROS, with both protein abundance and ROS levels remaining constant over time (Figures 2C and S3F). GFP fluorescence in the overexpression lines did not interfere with ROS detection (Figures S3G and S3H).

Together, these findings indicate that RbohC and RbohE generate local ROS at the root cap to regulate the timing of its detachment. Although our manipulations were performed at the whole-plant level and potential systemic effects cannot be fully excluded, these observations led us to test whether such ROS dynamics also influence cell proliferation at the root tip. To this end, we performed an EdU proliferation assay. In the rbohC rbohE-2 mutant, the number of EdU-positive cells increased at 3 DAG, whereas in the overexpression lines, EdU-positive cells were significantly reduced at 4 DAG (Figures 2F and 2G). These results suggest that RbohC- and RbohE-dependent ROS oscillations influence both root cap detachment and the division activity of columella initial cells by maintaining local ROS homeostasis at the root apex.

Sombrero modulates RbohC and RbohE expression to control reactive oxygen species levels and root cap detachment

To regulate ROS oscillations in the root cap effectively, the expression and/or activity of RbohC and RbohE must be precisely coordinated by the stages of columella differentiation. Notably, the overexpression of each gene not only delayed the timing of root cap detachment but also led to the irregular shedding of individual cells rather than coordinated layer-wise separation (Figure 2D). This cell-by-cell detachment pattern adds complexity to the phenotype and was similarly observed in the sombrero (smb) mutant (Figure S3A), which is known for its delayed root cap detachement.17 The resemblance between the overexpression lines and smb suggests that both conditions may involve a disruption in the spatial coordination of detachment signals required for organized separation.

Given this phenotypic similarity, we hypothesized that SMB, a transcription factor essential for root cap maturation,17 might regulate the expression of RbohC and RbohE. To test this, we monitored SMB protein dynamics in the outermost root cap layer (Figures 3A and 3B). SMB abundance peaked at 4 DAG—coinciding with root cap detachment—and subsequently declined, a pattern mirrored at the transcriptional level (Figures S4A and S4B). Notably, this oscillatory profile was inversely correlated with ROS levels in the same region (Figure 1A), suggesting that SMB may negatively regulate ROS production via RbohC and RbohE. Supporting this notion, RT-qPCR revealed elevated RbohC and RbohE transcript levels in the smb mutant (Figure 3C). Reporter analysis further showed expanded expression domains of pRbohC::nls-GFP and pRbohE::nls-GFP in smb, with RbohE notably extending into inner columella layers compared to its restricted expression in WT (Figures 3D and 3E). Together, these results indicate that SMB restricts the spatiotemporal expression of RbohC and RbohE.

We next examined whether this upregulation of RbohC and RbohE in the smb mutant leads to increased ROS accumulation. Compared to WT, smb exhibited elevated ROS levels at 3 DAG that persisted at 4 DAG, a stage when ROS levels normally decline in WT (Figures 3F and 3G). ROS levels in smb decreased at 5 DAG, coinciding with the delayed root cap detachment, suggesting a temporal shift in ROS dynamics rather than a complete failure of the turnover program. In the smb rbohC rbohE-2 triple mutant, ROS levels were moderately reduced compared to smb, indicating that RbohC and RbohE contribute to the elevated ROS in smb (Figures 3F and 3G). Consistent with these ROS dynamics, root cap detachment was delayed in smb at 4 DAG but partially restored in the triple mutant (Figures 3H, 3I, S3A, and S3B). By 5 DAG, the triple mutant had progressed further, with amyloplast degradation in the detaching layer already underway, whereas smb remained delayed, accentuating the phenotypic difference (Figures 3I and S3A). Furthermore, EdU assays revealed reduced cell division in smb at 4 DAG, which was alleviated in the smb rbohC rbohE-2 background (Figures 3J and S4C).

Collectively, these findings indicate that SMB acts upstream of RbohC and RbohE to negatively regulate ROS levels, thereby modulating both the timing of root cap detachment and the proliferation activity of columella initial cells.

Comparative transcriptomic analysis of the root cap detachment cycle in WT and sombrero plants

To identify the molecular links connecting changes in ROS levels to root cap detachment in the smb mutant, we performed transcriptome analysis using root tips of WT and smb plants sampled at 12-h intervals up to 5 DAG. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a distinct clustering pattern between WT and smb during the detachment cycle (Figure S5A), indicating significant transcriptomic differences between the two genotypes.

To identify genes with time-dependent differences in expression between WT and smb, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA),39 which revealed 11 modules of differentially expressed genes (Figures 4A and S5B–S5D; Table S2). To characterize each module, we analyzed their associated gene ontology terms. Given that root cap detachment involves cellular reorganization and cell wall remodeling, we focused on four modules (defined as yellow, brown, blue, and turquoise) that were enriched for genes related to hormone signaling and cell wall dynamics (Figure 4B; Table S3). The yellow and turquoise modules, which exhibited circadian oscillation patterns, were enriched for genes associated with the circadian rhythm and cell cycle. By contrast, the brown and blue modules, which showed notable upregulation in smb, were enriched for genes involved in cell wall organization, hydrogen peroxide catabolic processes, and auxin responses (Figure 4B).

Consistent with the elevated ROS levels observed in smb (Figures 3F and 3G), genes involved in ROS metabolism, including Rboh and peroxidase family genes, were upregulated in the mutant (Figure 4C). Interestingly, several circadian clock regulators implicated in ROS homeostasis—such as GIGANTEA (GI),40,41 LUX ARRHYTHMO (LUX), PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATOR 7 (PRR7), and PRR942—also showed increased expression in smb (Figure S6A), suggesting selective crosstalk between ROS-responsive circadian components and the transcriptional network regulating ROS metabolism. This coordinated regulation may underlie the cyclic nature of root cap detachment without broadly perturbing core circadian timing mechanisms. In parallel, genes encoding cyclin-related factors, particularly D-type cyclins that regulate the G1 phase of the cell cycle and influence the rate of cell division in plant,43,44 were downregulated in smb compared to WT (Figure S6B), consistent with the reduced cell proliferation observed in the mutant and potentially contributing to the delayed detachment phenotype.

Among plant-hormone-related genes, changes in the expression of auxin-related genes were particularly prominent in the smb mutant (Figure 4D). In addition to changes in auxin biosynthesis, the upregulation of AUXIN-RESPONSE FACTORS (ARFs) and Aux/IAA family genes involved in auxin-mediated transcriptional regulation suggests a potential role for auxin signaling in root cap detachment. ROS modulate primary root growth by influencing the root meristem through auxin signaling.45 Similarly, crosstalk between ROS and auxin may fine-tune the root cap detachment cycle.

Genes associated with cell wall remodeling were also upregulated in smb (Figure S6C), which contrasts with the delayed root cap detachment observed in this mutant (Figures 3H and 3I). Notably, the expression of IDA-LIKE1 (IDL1) and HAESA-LIKE2 (HSL2), encoding peptide-receptor signaling components that might regulate the expression of genes encoding cell wall enzymes,46 was also upregulated in the smb mutant (Figure 4E), suggesting that IDL1-HSL2 may act upstream of these upregulated genes. However, the delayed root cap detachment in the smb mutant suggests that additional layers of regulation beyond the expression of genes encoding cell wall enzymes are required to govern this process.

Notably, not all genes encoding cell wall enzymes were upregulated in smb. Among these, ROOT CAP POLYGALACTURONASE (RCPG), which is involved in root cap detachment and causes irregular detachment of individual cells when overexpressed and delayed detachment when absent,17 was downregulated in smb compared to the WT (Figure 4F). These findings suggest that multiple regulatory pathways are involved in controlling the expression of genes encoding cell wall enzymes. BRN1 and BRN2 are likely to form a key module in this process. These Class IIB NAC transcription factors function redundantly with SMB and are known to regulate cell wall modifications.11

Reactive oxygen species-mediated root cap detachment relies on a local auxin gradient

The upregulation of genes associated with auxin signaling in the smb mutant suggests that auxin signaling may act downstream of SMB (Figure 4D). A previous study showed that a local auxin minimum forms at the root cap and coincides with the site of cell separation, implying a potential role for auxin distribution in regulating detachment.21 To determine whether this auxin minimum is functionally required for root cap detachment, we treated seedlings with the auxin transport inhibitor N-1-naphthylphthalamic acid (NPA). NPA treatment disrupted the local auxin minimum (Figures 5A and 5B) and delayed root cap detachment (Figure 5C), indicating that proper auxin distribution is necessary for timely separation. While NPA does not necessarily abolish all auxin gradients, the observed disruption of the local minimum and delayed detachment suggests that auxin transport plays a key role in establishing the minimum required for this process.

Given that ROS levels were elevated in smb mutants—where auxin signaling is also upregulated—we hypothesized that ROS may act upstream of auxin, modulating the local auxin gradient to influence root cap detachment. To test this, we used the DII-Venus reporter system, which exhibits reduced fluorescence intensity in response to higher auxin levels (Figures S7A and S7B).47 Exogenous application of H2O2 led to a marked decrease in DII fluorescence, indicating an increase in auxin levels. Conversely, the inhibition of ROS production by VAS2870 increased DII fluorescence, consistent with reduced auxin accumulation (Figures 5D and 5E). These results suggest that ROS promotes the formation of a local auxin minimum at the root cap by modulating auxin levels.

The crosstalk between ROS and the auxin gradient was further validated using genetic backgrounds with altered ROS homeostasis. In the ROS-deficient rbohC rbohE-2 mutant, overall auxin levels at the root tip were markedly reduced, and the auxin minimum formed earlier than in the WT (Figures 5F and 5G). Conversely, in the smb mutant, which exhibited elevated ROS levels, auxin concentrations were generally higher, and the formation of an auxin minimum was severely impaired. This defect was partially rescued from the smb rbohC rbohE-2 triple mutant (Figures 5F and 5G). Together, these findings support a model in which oscillatory ROS signals modulate auxin dynamics to regulate the timing of auxin minimum formation, thereby coordinating the onset of root cap detachment.

Reactive oxygen species regulate the expression and trafficking of the auxin transporter PIN2

The disruptive effects of NPA on the auxin gradient (Figure 5A) imply that auxin transporters are essential for maintaining the local auxin gradient at the root cap. Among the PIN family members, PIN1,48,49,50 PIN2,51,52,53 PIN3,54 PIN4,50 and PIN748,55 were expressed in the root cap, each showing distinct spatial patterns (Figure 6A). To determine which PIN proteins contribute to the formation of the local auxin minimum, we examined root cap detachment phenotypes in various knockout mutants. Notably, the pin2 mutant displayed a significant delay in root cap detachment (Figures 6B and 6C), accompanied by a severe disruption of the local auxin gradient (Figures 6D and 6E). In the pin2 mutant, auxin remained highly enriched in the root cap layer at 4 DAG, when the auxin minimum is normally established in the WT. Instead, auxin levels began to decline at 6 DAG, indicating that minimum formation was delayed rather than completely abolished (Figure 6D). Root cap detachment also occurred at this later time-point. This temporal shift underscores the role of PIN2 not only in establishing the auxin minimum but also in coordinating its timing with root cap detachment. In contrast, the pin3 pin4 pin7 triple mutant has been reported to show no significant alterations in root cap organization,21 reinforcing that among the root cap-expressed PINs, PIN2 plays a uniquely pivotal role in directing auxin flow and establishing the auxin minimum.

Previous studies have shown that H2O2 can modulate PIN2 at the protein level,45,56 suggesting a potential link between ROS and auxin transport. To investigate this in the context of root cap detachment, we first tested whether H2O2 affects PIN2 transcript levels. RT-qPCR was performed using 3-day-old root tip samples enriched for the root cap after 1-day treatments with different concentrations of H2O2. PIN2 expression showed a slight but non-significant decrease at 0.17 mM H2O2, whereas a significant decrease was observed at 0.33 mM (Figure 6F), indicating a concentration-dependent response. We next assessed PIN2 expression on various mutant backgrounds (Figure 6G). In the rbohC rbohE-2 mutant, which exhibited accelerated root cap detachment, PIN2 expression was upregulated early (at 3 DAG) compared to the WT. By contrast, both the smb and smb rbohC rbohE-2 mutants, which showed delayed establishment of the auxin minimum, also exhibited delayed PIN2 induction. These results suggest that ROS regulate the timing of auxin minima and root cap detachment by modulating PIN2 expression.

PIN2-mediated regulation of auxin distribution relies not only on transcriptional control but also on the spatial dynamics of PIN2 protein localization.57,58 In the root cap, PIN2-GFP was localized to the plasma membrane in both the LRC and outermost columella cells (Figure S7C). Notably, apical polarity in the LRC, with signals enriched on the apical sides, was observed specifically at 4 DAG (Figures S7C and S7D), coinciding with the timing of auxin minimum formation and root cap detachment. This polar distribution may facilitate auxin efflux from the outermost columella cells, thereby contributing to the establishment of the auxin minimum.

Previous studies have shown that ROS modulate the subcellular distribution of PIN2 by promoting its internalization and degradation.45,56 To examine whether similar regulation occurs at the root tip, we treated seedlings with exogenous ROS. Upon ROS treatment, the PIN2-GFP signal at the plasma membrane was reduced, accompanied by a corresponding increase in intracellular puncta (Figures 6H, 6I, and S7E), consistent with enhanced internal trafficking. This effect was further amplified by the proteasome inhibitor MG132, suggesting the involvement of proteolytic pathways in ROS-mediated PIN2 relocalization in the root cap.

Finally, to explore how the ROS-auxin pathway regulates root cap detachment, we turned to downstream gene expression. RNA-seq analysis revealed that RCPG, a gene encoding a cell wall-modifying enzyme, was downregulated in the smb mutant. We hypothesized that this gene might be regulated via the ROS-auxin axis. Supporting this, RCPG expression was upregulated at 3 DAG in the rbohC rbohE-2 mutant (Figure 6J), coinciding with increased PIN2 levels. These findings suggest that ROS-auxin signaling controls the timing of cell wall enzyme gene expression, thereby orchestrating the detachment of the root cap.

Discussion

In plant roots navigating through heterogeneous soil environments, the root cap functions as both a protective barrier for the meristem and a sensory hub that perceives and integrates environmental signals to guide directional growth. Continuous exposure to mechanical and biotic stress necessitates a tightly regulated renewal program in which cell division, differentiation, and programmed shedding are coordinated. In this study, we identify ROS oscillations as central regulators of the root cap detachment cycle, with RbohC and RbohE acting as key NADPH oxidases responsible for localized ROS production. These oscillations, tightly regulated by SMB, occur in synchrony with the developmental cycle of detachment and replenishment. By linking ROS dynamics to the spatial auxin gradient via the regulation of PIN2 at both transcriptional and post-translational levels, we establish a mechanistic framework that connects intrinsic developmental programs with environmental stress responses. Given that abiotic and biotic stimuli broadly induce ROS production, this pathway positions the root cap as a strategic node where stress perception and developmental timing converge (Figure 7).

Schematic model of redox and hormonal regulation during root cap detachment

RbohC and RbohE drive ROS oscillations at the root cap, determining the timing of detachment. SMB acts upstream, repressing RbohC and RbohE transcription to fine-tune spatiotemporal ROS levels. These ROS dynamics influence PIN2 trafficking, modulating auxin minima formation and thereby controlling detachment onset.

A spatial auxin gradient, maximal at the QC and minimal at the detaching outermost layer, is essential for maintaining root cap size by balancing cell division, differentiation, and detachment.21 Such gradients are fundamental for many plant developmental processes that require clear spatial boundaries, including the transition zones between dividing and differentiating cells in the root meristem, lateral root initiation, and the specification of separating layers in the valve margin.22,59,60 Our findings reveal that ROS oscillations act upstream of this gradient: they modulate PIN2 expression, influence protein abundance, and shift PIN2 polarity in the LRC, collectively establishing the auxin minimum required for detachment. An inverse correlation between ROS levels and the PIN2 polarity index during the detachment cycle supports this regulatory relationship.

How ROS regulate PIN2 transcription remains an open question, but several molecular clues suggest that auxin signaling components, acting at multiple regulatory levels, are involved in this process. Transcription factors of the auxin response factor (ARF) family, which mediate auxin-responsive gene expression, also regulate PIN2 as one of their target genes.45 Recent studies have uncovered a redox-sensitive mechanism in which ROS promote the multimerization of the Aux/IAA repressor IAA3/SHY2, enhancing its interaction with the co-repressor TOPLESS (TPL) and thereby strengthening the ARF-mediated repression of target genes.61 Notably, different Aux/IAA proteins exhibit distinct redox sensitivities, forming disulfide-linked multimers to varying degrees, positioning them as potential ROS sensors that couple redox status to auxin-driven transcriptional control. Beyond transcriptional regulation, ROS may also affect PIN2 protein trafficking45 and modulate the expression of GRETCHEN HAGEN3 (GH3) auxin-conjugating enzymes,62 which adjust active auxin levels through conjugation and inactivation. Collectively, these mechanisms indicate that ROS can influence auxin signaling across transcriptional, post-translational, and metabolic layers, providing a versatile framework for integrating developmental and environmental cues during root cap turnover.

These findings place the ROS-PIN2–auxin axis at the core of root cap turnover and suggest that this regulatory framework extends beyond gradient formation to coordinate later stages of detachment, where cell wall remodeling and autophagy become dominant. The later phases of root cap detachment involve autophagy-mediated clearance20 and cell wall remodeling.63,64 Transcriptomic profiles of smb mutants reveal broad changes in genes encoding pectin methylesterases, polygalacturonases, and other wall-modifying enzymes, consistent with these processes and highlighting the extensive transcriptional reprogramming that accompanies layer-specific separation. Because the activities of these wall-modifying enzymes and autophagic processes depend on the surrounding biochemical conditions, understanding how this microenvironment is regulated becomes crucial. Both processes are strongly influenced by the apoplastic pH,65,66 which modulates enzyme activity and signaling at the cell wall-plasma membrane interface. Auxin, known to regulate many wall-related genes and to establish localized pH domains through proton flux regulation,67 may therefore contribute to these late-stage events through both transcriptional and microenvironmental control. Within this multilayered framework, ROS oscillations likely act upstream by dynamically shaping the auxin gradient that defines these pH domains, thereby integrating hormonal signaling with the physical events that execute root cap shedding.

Root cap detachment is a developmental process that rapidly responds to external cues. Our finding that ROS, key second messengers in both biotic and abiotic stress responses,31 regulate this process illustrates how intrinsic developmental programs are integrated with environmental stimuli. While we describe an auxin-mediated mechanism downstream of ROS, it is unlikely to act alone; clarifying the broader crosstalk between ROS and auxin remains a central challenge. In parallel, given that ROS-auxin interactions likely operate through diverse and interconnected pathways, the multilayered regulation of cell wall-modifying enzymes—spanning transcriptional control, protein secretion, and activity modulation—underscores the need for detailed studies to reveal how these processes collectively orchestrate layer-specific detachment of the root cap. Our transcriptomic data further raise the possibility that ROS-responsive circadian regulators, including GI, LUX, PRR7, and PRR9, may contribute to the cyclic nature of root cap detachment, providing an additional layer of temporal control. Together, these findings position ROS oscillations as a central integrator of developmental timing and environmental cues at the root tip and offer a conceptual framework for future studies exploring how this integration underpins root adaptability and resilience in dynamic soil environments.

Limitations of the study

This study provides insights into ROS dynamics during root cap detachment, though certain technical limitations should be considered. Many of the manipulations, such as exogenous H2O2 application and constitutive overexpression or knockout strategies, were performed at the whole-plant level rather than being restricted to the root cap, which could lead to systemic effects and reduce spatial resolution in functional analyses. In addition, DCFH-DA staining, used here as the primary method for ROS detection, has limited spatial and temporal resolution and is irreversible; this constrains its ability to monitor dynamic changes within the same tissue over time. Future studies incorporating root cap-specific genetic tools and high-resolution ROS sensors could address these issues and provide a more precise understanding of ROS-mediated root cap turnover.

Method details

Plant materials and growth conditions

All Arabidopsis mutants and marker lines were in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) background. The transfer (T)-DNA insertion lines rbohC (SALK_016593), rbohE (SALK_137898), smb (SALK_143526), pin3-5 (SALK_005544), and pin7-2 (SALK_044687) were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC) at The Ohio State University. The PIN2 mutants pin2/eir1-1 (CS8058) and pin2 (SALK_144447) were used in this study. The eir1-1 DR5::GUS (CS16706) and p35S::mDII-Venus (CS799174) lines were obtained from ABRC. UBQ10:4XYFP (CS2106130) was derived from the SAND line obtained from ABRC. The plants used for phenotyping and transcriptomic analysis were grown on half-strength Murashige & Skoog (MS) plates containing 0.1% sucrose under long-day conditions (16 h light provided by fluorescent light at 70 μmol m−2 s−1 photon flux density at 22°C and 8 h dark at 18°C) using seeds that had been sterilized by vapor-phase sterilization and stratified for 2 days.

Generation of transgenic plants

The constructs were generated using the MultiSite Gateway system (Invitrogen, 12537100). The genomic DNA sequences were initially introduced into pDONR vectors using primers listed in Table S3. The final entry clones underwent LR reactions to construct the expression vectors. Transgenic plants were generated by Agrobacterium tumefaciens–mediated transformation of Arabidopsis Col-0 plants with minor modification fromClough and Bent.81 Primary transformants (T1) were selected with phosphinothricin (PPT) or kanamycin (Kan).

mPS-PI staining of young Arabidopsis roots

Young Arabidopsis roots were subjected to mPS-PI staining as described by Truernit et al.82 with minor modifications. Whole seedlings were fixed in 50% methanol and 10% acetic acid (v/v) for at least 12 h at 4°C, rinsed at least twice in water, and incubated in 1% periodic acid diluted in water at room temperature for 40 min. The samples were washed at least three times with water and incubated in Schiff reagent with propidium iodide (100 mM sodium metabisulfite and 0.15 N HCl; propidium iodide to a final concentration of 50 μg/mL) for 3 min. The samples were washed at least twice with water and incubated in 50% Visikol (diluted in 70% ethanol, v/v) for 20 min prior to imaging.

Tissue staining and imaging

Confocal imaging was performed using an LSM 900 (Zeiss) confocal laser-scanning microscope with Plan-Apochromat 20× and 40× objectives. Counter-staining of the plasma membrane was performed by incubating roots for 15 min in 1 μM FM4-64. The FM dye was excited with a 515 nm laser and emission signal were collected at 640 nm. The cell walls were stained with 10 μM of PI by incubating the roots in the solution for 2 min. The excitation wavelength for PI-stained samples was 488 nm, and emission signals were collected at 550 to 720 nm.

Visualization of root cap cuticles

2 DAG to 6 DAG roots were stained with Fluorol Yellow 088 (0.01% in methanol) for 4 days at 4°C. Then, the roots were counterstained with aniline blue (0.5% diluted in water) for 1.5 h at room temperature. The samples were washed with water before proceeding to imaging. The confocal microscopy was used with 40× objectives.

Root cap sloughing assay

Roots from 5 DAG seedlings grown on the surface of solid medium were used for quantification. The boundaries of medium containing seedlings were cut, and each piece containing intact seedlings was transferred to a glass slide to facilitate quantification. For imaging, a small water droplet was placed near the root apex before placing the cover glass onto the intact sample in agar medium. The root tips were observed using Axio Observer 5 in brightfield mode and samples were counted according the phenotype categorized in Figure S1A.

Quantification of ROS levels

H2DCFDA (Sigma) dissolved in DMSO and was diluted to 10 μM as the working concentration before performing experiments to quantify ROS levels in roots. The roots were immersed in liquid half-strength MS medium for 15 min, transferred to half-strength MS medium supplemented with H2DCFDA solution and incubated for 13 min before quantification. For ROS quantification, Axio Observer 5 was set to an 8 ms exposure time with a GFP filter. The fluorescent intensities from obtained images were quantified using ImageJ.

For intracellular H2O2 assessment, Hyper379 and Hyper3-C199S80 (a pH-insensitive control) transgenic lines were used to monitor ROS level at the outermost layer. The samples were excited at 488 and 405 nm and emission was collected between 510 and 540 nm. The data are presented as the 488/405 ratio.

Pharmacological treatments

For pharmacological treatments, the seedlings were germinated and grown in half-strength MS then at 2 DAG, liquid half-strength MS containing H2O2 or VAS2870 was sprayed over the media containing seedlings. For H2O2 treatment, 10 mL of 1–3 mM H2O2 solution (sterilized through a 0.22 μm filter; Sartorius, Germany) was briefly sprayed over 60 mL of agar medium and excess solution was removed after approximately 10 s. This corresponds to an estimated final exposure below 0.2–0.3 mM, depending on the initial concentration (see also Figure S1). PI uptake assays confirmed that this treatment did not compromise cell viability (<1% PI-positive cells). VAS2870, a NADPH oxidase-specific inhibitor,83,84 was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). For auxin treatment, seedlings were germinated and grown in half-strength MS. At 2 DAG, the seedlings were transferred to medium containing N-1-naphthylphthalamic acid (NPA) to inhibit auxin transport; the NPA was dissolved in DMSO and diluted to a working concentration of 5 μM before use. MG132 was dissolved in DMSO and diluted to a working concentration of 30 μM 2 h before imaging.

Cell proliferation assay

Arabidopsis seedlings were incubated in half-strength MS medium supplemented with 10 μM 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) for 4 h. The seedlings were fixed for 1 h in 3.7% (w/v) formaldehyde solution in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) under low-level vacuum infiltration. The samples were washed at least twice with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS (pH 7.4), transferred to 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS, and incubated for 20 min. The samples were washed at least twice with 3% BSA in PBS and incubated in Click-iT reaction cocktail, prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen), for 30 min in the dark. The reaction cocktail was removed, the samples were rinsed at least twice with 3% BSA, and the solution was replaced with PSB. Hoechst 33342 (H3570) stain was diluted 1:2000 in PBS, and the samples were incubated in this solution for 1 h. The samples were then washed at least three times before imaging. Images were visualized using a stack of three z-plane images.

GUS histochemical staining of seedlings

Plants harboring GUS reporters driven by the DR5::GUS, pRbohC::GUS, and pRbohE::GUS reporter constructs were harvested and briefly washed in GUS staining buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.2, 0.2% Triton X-100, 2 mM potassium ferrocyanide, and 2 mM potassium ferricyanide). The solution was replaced with GUS staining buffer supplemented with fresh 10 mM X-Gluc (Duchefa Biochemie), and the samples were incubated in the solution for various amounts of time (DR5::GUS for 1 h; pRbohC::GUS and pRbohE::GUS for 30 min) in the dark at 37°C. The GUS enzymatic reaction was stopped and tissues were cleared by being submerged in a sequential series of ethanol washes (20, 30, 50, 70% ethanol) for 10 min each. The cleared tissues were treated in a sequential series of Visikol for Plant Biology: 20% Visikol (diluted in 70% ethanol, v/v) for 15 min and then 40% Visikol for 30 min. The cleared tissues were observed and imaged under an Axio Observer 5 inverted microscope in bright-field mode at 15 ms exposure time. GUS was quantified using Fiji/ImageJ as described in Béziat et al.85 with minor modifications.

RNA-seq data pre-processing

WT and smb roots were collected every 12 h from 2.5 to 5 DAG in three independent replicates (batches) of experiments. Thirty-six samples were collected, each consisting of 100–120 primary roots that were cut ∼0.5 cm from the root tip. RNA samples with RIN value > 9 were selected. After assessing quality, mRNA-sequencing libraries were prepared using a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The adaptor-ligated libraries were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina, USA). The indexed adapters were removed using Trimmomatic software v0.39,86 and the sequences were subjected to quality control using PRINSEQ v0.20.4.87 The clean reads were mapped to The Arabidopsis Reference Genome (TAIR10.1, GCF_000001735.4) using HISAT v2.2.1.88 The abundance of annotated reference genes was quantified using StringTie v1.3.4,89 and the reference genes were annotated using TAIR10 FASTA sequence and TAIR10 genome GTF annotation files (www.arabiodpsis.org). Then, the EdgeR90 package was used to normalize gene-level expression data using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method, fit negative binomial models to estimate dispersion across conditions. Finally, the Z score or log2(fold change) values were visualized as a heatmap using the pheatmap package v1.0.12 in R.91

WGCNA and gene ontology enrichment analysis

The R package WGCNA v1.7239 was employed to explore the significant modules among 18 different sample types. A network was constructed by incorporating the pairwise correlations between genes across samples. An adjacency matrix was constructed using the co-expression values (0.5 + 0.5 × correlation matrix) to the power of β = 6, serving as a soft threshold. For a robust and biological relevant measurement of network interconnectedness, a topological overlap matrix was generated based on the adjacency matrix. Average linkage hierarchical clustering grouped genes with similar co-expression patterns. Using the Dynamic Tree Cut algorithm, gene modules were depicted as branches with hierarchical order. Gene lists obtained from each co-expression module were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using DAVID.92

Quantification of mRNA levels

Root tips from 3-, 4-, 5-, 6-, and 7-day-old seedlings were harvested using a precision knife and subjected to total RNA extraction using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) unless otherwise noted. The total RNA was used to synthesize 500 ng of cDNA using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The final cDNA product was diluted 50-fold for quantification. Quantitative reverse transcription (RT)-qPCR was performed using QuantStudio I (Thermo Fisher) in 96-well plates with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR amplification was performed at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 58°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 20 s. A melting curve was generated for verification. Primer sequences are provided in the supplemental information. Relative quantification was performed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Three independent biological replicates and three technical replicated were performed.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Details of statistical analyses are found in the figure legends. Data were measured using ImageJ. Measurements were statistically analyzed in JMP18 and/or GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 for the phenotypic assays. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1.

This article does not report the original code. Any additional information required to reanalyze the data reported in this article should be directed to and will be fulfilled by the lead contact.
