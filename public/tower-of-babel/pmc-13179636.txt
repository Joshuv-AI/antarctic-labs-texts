The impact of nutrition education on food insecurity, food literacy, and dietary intake: a single-arm pre–post intervention study
Authors: Mahitab Hanbazaza, Lama Alaklabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoor food literacy behaviours may exacerbate food insecurity. Therefore, food literacy interventions have been increasingly utilized to improve food security and enhance dietary intake among low-socioeconomic adults. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a nutrition education intervention on food security, food literacy, and dietary intake among low-income Saudi families.MethodsThis single-arm pre–post intervention study was conducted between June and August 2024. Seventy-three adults completed the pre-survey and 67 completed the post-survey. The survey included questions on food security, food literacy, and dietary intake.ResultsThe intervention was significantly associated with improvements in both food insecurity and food literacy. The mean food insecurity score decreased from 4.06 ± 2.98 pre-intervention to 2.71 ± 2.58 post-intervention, with a mean reduction of − 1.35 ± 1.62 (P < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 0.83). In terms of food literacy, the mean score increased from 46.4 ± 10.9 to 58.4 ± 7.1, with a mean increase of 12 ± 9.07 (P < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 1.32). Dietary intake also improved following the intervention. After adjustment for multiple comparisons, significant improvements remained for fruit intake, red and white meat intake, legumes, and bread and cereals.ConclusionsThe intervention showed short-term improvement in food insecurity and food literacy, along with favorable changes in dietary intake among low-income Saudi families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01332-8.Keywords: Food insecurity, Food literacy, Dietary intake, Saudi Arabia, Adult

Introduction

Low-income adults are at higher risk of developing diet-related chronic diseases such as diabetes, obesity, hypertension, and heart disease [1–3]. Health disparities between lower- and higher-income individuals, such as restricted access to healthy foods, poor dietary quality, physical inactivity, and inadequate healthcare access, all contribute to an increase in disease risk [4]. Furthermore, because of its strong connection to socioeconomic status, food insecurity is a major problem for low-income families [5].

Around 821 million people worldwide experienced hunger and food insecurity in 2017 [6]. A study of low-income families in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, reported that 50% of the participants experienced food insecurity [7]. The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) defines food security as “access by all people to enough nutritionally adequate food for an active, healthy lifestyle [8].” The United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) introduced the concept of the four pillars of food security: food availability, food access, food utilization, and stability [8]. These pillars encompass knowledge, skills, and sociocultural aspects related to food literacy, which influence an individual’s ability to choose, prepare, and store food effectively [9].

Food insecurity has been linked to serious health and socioeconomic consequences, including a higher burden of chronic diseases and increased strain on healthcare systems [10]. Furthermore, food insecurity is associated with poor diet quality, characterized by a higher consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages, fewer fruits and vegetables, and increased intake of highly palatable foods such as high-fat dairy products and salty snacks [11]. Beyond its public health implications, food insecurity affects both individuals and communities, affecting children and adults [12]. Among adults, characteristics of the double burden of disease have been reported, such as the combination of dietary deficiencies (e.g., anemia) and excess diseases (e.g., diabetes, obesity) [13].

Food literacy is a relatively new and emerging concept; over the last decade, the term “food literacy” has become more prevalent in policy, practice, and research [14]. Food literacy is defined as a “collection of interrelated knowledge, skills, and behaviors required to plan, manage, select, prepare, and eat foods to meet needs and determine dietary intake [15].” There is a symbiotic relationship between food literacy and food security, where low food literacy contributes to food insecurity, and food insecurity limits one’s ability to adopt food literacy behaviors that ensure adequate diet quality [16]. Research has demonstrated positive associations between food literacy and healthy dietary intake, including increased consumption of fruits and vegetables [17, 18]. Food insecurity may also affect dietary intake because food-insecure individuals may change their eating habits, such as skipping meals or eating less than required due to financial constraints [8].

Previous research suggests that nutrition education and food literacy interventions may contribute to improvements in food security and dietary behaviors. By enhancing nutrition knowledge and practical food-related skills, these interventions may help individuals make more effective use of limited financial resources and improve diet quality [19, 20]. Several programs, such as the Nutrition Education and Skills Training (NEST) program and the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), have reported improvements in food-related behaviors and, in some cases, food security outcomes among participants [20, 21]. However, evidence examining the impact of such interventions in Saudi Arabia remains limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the impact of a nutrition education intervention on food literacy, food security, and dietary intake among low-income Saudi families.

Materials and methods

Design

This study was conducted using a single-arm pre–post intervention (Fig. 1). The study began between June and August 2024. The pre-survey was conducted to assess baseline food security and food literacy status while estimating the intake of food items among Saudi low-income families. In addition, post-intervention data were analyzed to assess changes in participants’ food security, food literacy, and dietary intake following the nutrition education intervention.

Study design

Participants

The study included a convenience sample of low-income individuals in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, who were recruited through charitable organizations, including families in need, widows, divorced women, orphans, families of prisoners, abandoned wives, and other financially disadvantaged individuals who reported a monthly household income of less than 4,000 Saudi Riyals (1,066 USD). All individuals invited to participate were registered beneficiaries of these organizations. The intervention was conducted without a control group as a single-arm pre–post intervention.

The sample size was estimated based on a previous single-arm pre–post food literacy intervention study that reported a reduction in mean food security scores from 2.33 ± 1.74 to 1.67 ± 1.71 [21]. This corresponded to an approximate standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.38. Assuming a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and 80% power for detecting a pre–post change, the required sample size was estimated to be 54 participants, increasing to approximately 72 participants for 90% power. The final baseline sample of 73 participants met this requirement.

Data collection

Pre-post-intervention

The survey was sent to family members through charity organizations, and data were collected from participants both before the intervention (baseline) and after the final session of the intervention. The data collection tool used in this study was an internet-based survey designed to get accurate information for each variable.

The Arabic version of the questionnaire was pilot-tested with ten individuals to assess its readability, feasibility, consistency in style and formatting, language clarity, and the average time required for completion. In addition, five nutrition experts evaluated the questionnaire for its suitability to the research aims, clarity, and understandability. Based on their suggestions, the questionnaire was subsequently modified to ensure face and content validity. The following sets of variables were considered.

Socio-demographic characteristics

Included age, gender, marital status, education level, occupation, monthly income, number of family members, number of children, housing structure, housing status, meal preparation, and receipt of benefits from charity organizations.

Food insecurity

Measured using the validated Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES) for households, [6], which has been validated for use in Arabic-speaking populations [22]. In the pre-survey (baseline), the past 12 months recall period was used to assess chronic exposure to food insecurity. At the post-intervention, a 30-day recall period was used to capture short-term changes in food insecurity experiences following the intervention. The questions are structured to measure the severity of food insecurity through self-reported conditions and behaviors [6]. The questionnaire consists of eight items that examine several levels of food insecurity, with each item relating to a specific situation and addressing concerns such as food worry, poor food quality, insufficient food amount, skipping meals, experiencing hunger, or going without food. Responses were categorized as Yes = 1, and No = 0. The total number of affirmative responses to the 8 items ranges from 0 to 8. Food security status was then classified into four categories: food-secure (0), mild food insecurity (1–3), moderate food insecurity (4–6), and severe food insecurity (7–8). The internal consistency of the food insecurity items in the current study sample was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.907).

Food literacy

Assessed using a valid short food literacy questionnaire (SFLQ) [23], which has been culturally adapted and validated for use in Arabic-speaking populations, including adults in Lebanon [24]. The questionnaire contains 12 self-rated items. Six of the 12 categories addressed functional skills such as evaluating nutritional information and creating a well-balanced menu. Two questions inquired about interactive abilities, including sharing nutrition-related knowledge with peers and family. The next four items focused on critical judgment skills, specifically assessing the long-term effects of food patterns on health. Respondents utilized four- or five-point Likert scales depending on the question, with options ranging from “very bad” to “very good,” “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” “very hard” to “very easy,” and “never” to “always.” For selected items, response options such as “not applicable,” or “I do not have experience were coded as 0.

Total SFLQ scores were calculated by summing responses across all 12 items. Due to variation in response scales (four- and five-point items) and inclusion of zero-coded options, the possible total scores ranged from 7 to 52, with higher scores indicating better food literacy. As no standardized cut-off values have been established for the SFLQ, participants were categorized into two groups based on the sample median score. The median score in the present study was 30. Scores below 30 were classified as indicating poor food literacy, whereas scores of 30 or above were categorized as adequate food literacy. This distribution-based approach has been used in a previous study [25]. The SFLQ was used in a study among Saudi adults, where the researcher modified some of the questions to reflect better Saudi recommendations and the needs of society [25]. Due to the sample’s homogeneity, the Arabic version of the Bookari, (2023) survey was employed in the present study [25]. The internal consistency of the SFLQ in the current sample was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.867), indicating good reliability.

Dietary intake

Measured using a self-administered food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). The questionnaire evaluated the consumption of selected food groups over the past week. Two sets of items were included, which were adapted from previously published dietary assessment instruments used in similar population-based studies. The first measured the frequency of consumption of essential food groups [26], while the second evaluated the frequency of consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages/soft drinks, salty snack foods and potato chips, sweets (sweet biscuits, cake, doughnuts, and chocolate), and fast foods [21]. Dietary intake frequency was assessed using categorical response options (never, 1–2 times/week, 3–4 times/week, 5–6 times/week, and daily). To allow quantitative analysis, responses were converted into estimated daily frequencies by dividing weekly responses by seven. Specifically, “never” was coded as 0; “1–2 times/week” as 1.5/7 (0.21 times/day); “3–4 times/week” as 3.5/7 (0.50 times/day); “5–6 times/week” as 5.5/7 (0.79 times/day); and “daily” as 1.0 time/day. These values represent estimated average daily consumption. These questions were effective in detecting dietary changes following behavioral interventions among low-income adults [27].

Intervention

Is a four-week educational program that consists of four online (Zoom) sessions, held once a week, along with three educational videos sent to the WhatsApp group after each session. Figure 2 provides program details. All sessions were recorded and sent to the WhatsApp group so that participants could watch the recordings at their convenience. Each session incorporates a sequence of nutrition information, activities, and low-cost healthy recipes. The educational program was developed to cover all of the aspects that were stated in the pre-post survey. The program was reviewed and evaluated by experts in the field of nutrition. Each session lasted approximately 60–90 min and was conducted by a trained facilitator, who was a university-qualified graduate student.

Program details

The sessions aimed to offer practical nutrition education through interactive discussions, educational videos, and hands-on activities like meal planning and preparing affordable, healthy recipes. The intervention was based on Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which emphasizes the dynamic interaction between personal, behavioral, and environmental factors in shaping health behaviors.

The intervention included four modules: (1) Eat for Variety, which explained healthy eating and essential nutrients while highlighting affordable nutrient-dense food choices; (2) Eat for Wellbeing, which focused on increasing dietary diversity within food groups using the Saudi Nutritional Palm dietary guide and strategies for improving eating habits on a limited budget; (3) Eat for Balance, which emphasized balanced meals using a MyPlate, identifying foods high in sugar and fat, and understanding nutrition labels; and (4) Eat for the Environment, which covered safe food handling, food storage practices, and strategies to reduce household food waste. A detailed outline of the session content is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Procedures

In June 2024, a link to an online pre-survey was sent through WhatsApp message to charitable organizations, who then forwarded it to their clients. In July 2024, a WhatsApp group was created with participants (n = 76) who had completed the pre-survey and provided their consent to participate in the intervention program; at this point, the program began. Following the intervention in August 2024, the post-survey was sent to participants. Participants who completed the post-survey received vouchers worth SR 50 (USD 13) in recognition of their participation.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 for Windows. Continuous data were presented as mean ± SD, or median and interquartile range, as appropriate, depending on data distribution. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. For pre-post within-subject comparisons, paired t-tests were used for normally distributed continuous variables and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Changes in categorical variables between pre- and post-intervention were assessed using the chi-square test with linear-by-linear association or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Associations between changes in food insecurity and food literacy scores and changes in dietary intake were assessed using Spearman’s rho test. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants’ sociodemographic characteristics

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the participants, comprising 67 individuals. The majority were female (86.6%), and most were aged between 35 and 44 years (38.8%). Most participants had completed high school (44.8%), whereas only 14.9% had attained a bachelor’s degree. Most participants were widowed (62.7%), with the majority being unemployed (92.5%). Regarding family structure, (55.2%) of participants lived in households with five or more people, and half (50.7%) reported a monthly family income of less than SR 3,000 (USD 799). Most participants were renters (68.7%), and 46.3% reported being responsible for preparing most of the household meals.

The impact of intervention on food insecurity and food literacy levels

The intervention showed significant short-term improvement in both food insecurity and food literacy. Table 2 describes the changes in food insecurity and food literacy before and after the intervention. The mean food insecurity score decreased significantly from 4.06 ± 2.98 pre-intervention to 2.71 ± 2.58 post-intervention, with a mean reduction of − 1.35 ± 1.62 (P < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 0.83). The proportion of food-secure participants increased from 23.1% to 38.5%, whereas severe food insecurity decreased from 30.8% to 9.2% (P = 0.008).

Changes in dietary intake after intervention

Table 3 describes the changes in dietary intake after the intervention. The intervention led to significant improvements in the consumption of various food items. Fruit consumption increased significantly from 0.212 ± 0.254 to 0.362 ± 0.313, with a mean increase of 0.151 ± 0.296 (P < 0.001). Vegetable consumption also increased significantly from 0.419 ± 0.327 to 0.563 ± 0.297, with a mean increase of 0.144 ± 0.349 (P = 0.007). Dairy product intake improved as well, increasing from 0.413 ± 0.348 to 0.567 ± 0.293, with a mean increase of 0.154 ± 0.395 (P = 0.011). The consumption of red and white meat increased significantly from 0.317 ± 0.328 to 0.455 ± 0.307, with a mean increase of 0.139 ± 0.308 (P = 0.001).

Correlation between changes in dietary intake and food literacy after intervention

Table 4 describes the correlation between changes in dietary intake and food literacy after the intervention. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between changes in food items and food literacy. There was a significant negative correlation between changes in bread and cereal intake and overall dietary intake (Spearman’s rho = − 0.269, P = 0.031) and food literacy (Spearman’s rho = − 0.294, P = 0.016). No other significant correlations were found between changes in the intake of fruits, vegetables, dairy products, meat, legumes, or other food items with either the dietary intake or food literacy (P > 0.05). This inverse relationship may reflect the common reliance on affordable, refined grains among low-income households, despite increased nutrition knowledge.

Discussion

This study was the first to examine the impact of a nutrition education intervention on food security, food literacy, and dietary intake among low-income families in Saudi Arabia. The findings indicated that most participants were female and predominantly aged between 35 and 44 years. Furthermore, half of the participants reported a monthly family income below SR 3,000. The findings indicate that the intervention was associated with a reduction in food insecurity and improvements in both food literacy and dietary intake.

This study’s findings align with the NEST (Nutrition Education and Skills Training) study, which aimed to evaluate the NEST educational program in promoting food security and food literacy among low-socioeconomic adults. The NEST educational program significantly improved food insecurity and food literacy [21]. Moreover, a study was conducted to determine the impact of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) in improving food security among US adults, who showed improvements in food security and food literacy skills such as planning, shopping, saving, and cooking that align with the current findings [20]. On the other hand, several studies have indicated that improvements in food literacy knowledge and skills did not necessarily lead to significant improvements in food security. According to a randomized controlled trial among low-income parents that aimed to assess the effectiveness of the Expanded Food and Nutrition Education Program on nutrition behaviors, the educational intervention revealed significant improvements in nutrition behaviors, but no significant changes were found in food security [27, 28]. Similarly, Morgan (2023) implemented a food literacy-based curriculum aimed at enhancing undergraduate students’ food literacy skills and self-efficacy while addressing food insecurity; the study increased food literacy behaviors, self-efficacy, and confidence, but food security did not significantly improve [29]. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in program design, duration, sample characteristics, and contextual socioeconomic factors. Furthermore, reducing food insecurity is complex because it requires addressing systemic, social, educational, and economic challenges that extend beyond nutrition education alone. The improvement in food security observed in the present study occurred despite the relatively high baseline level of food literacy among participants. One possible explanation is that the intervention provided practical guidance on preparing low-cost healthy meals, reading nutrition labels, and improving food storage practices, which may have helped participants manage available food resources more efficiently. Improvements in practical food-related skills may enable households to optimize limited food budgets and reduce food waste, thereby contributing to short-term improvements in food security. However, these improvements may reflect short-term changes rather than sustained reductions in chronic food insecurity.

After adjustment for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction, significant increases were observed in the consumption of fruits, red and white meats, legumes, and bread and cereals. Although decreases in the consumption of vegetables, dairy products, sugar-sweetened beverages, and salty snacks were observed, these changes did not remain statistically significant. These findings are consistent with previous nutrition education interventions aimed at improving food literacy and dietary behaviors among adults. For example, an Australian study, which provided an educational program to improve knowledge, skills, and motivation to purchase healthy foods on a limited budget, reported significant increases in fruit and vegetable consumption and reductions in fast-food consumption among low-income participants [30]. Similarly, the Food Sensations for Adults (FDA) nutrition program demonstrated significant improvements in vegetable and fruit intake after the intervention [31]. Improvements in diet quality following nutrition education interventions have also been reported among low-income women, where participation in a nutrition education program resulted in significant improvements in dietary quality and food security [32]. In addition, the NEST intervention program reported increased vegetable consumption while decreasing the intake of salty snacks and foods, sugar-sweetened beverages, after the intervention [21]. A systematic scoping review of several food literacy interventions and their effects on dietary habits across adults from different socioeconomic statuses indicated that improved food literacy was generally associated with healthier food choices and increased consumption of nutritious foods [33].

Despite these improvements, the association between changes in dietary intake and food literacy in the present study was generally weak, with the exception of bread and cereals. This may reflect their relatively low cost and widespread availability, making them more accessible to low-income participants compared with other healthier food options that may be more expensive. These findings highlight the complex relationship between nutrition knowledge and actual dietary behavior, particularly among low-income populations. Although participants may have improved their understanding of healthy eating, translating this knowledge into practice may be constrained by economic limitations, food affordability, and household food availability. Therefore, while the intervention improved food literacy, income-related constraints may have limited the extent to which participants were able to modify their dietary intake.

This study was the first to evaluate the impact of a nutrition education intervention on food security, food literacy, and dietary intake among low-income adults in Saudi Arabia, which constitutes its most significant strength. Another of its strengths is the use of pre- and post-assessments, which provide an accurate comparison of results and allow for an effective evaluation of the intervention’s success. Furthermore, the educational program included strategies that effectively expanded knowledge, and participants were able to use what they learned in practical situations, which improved dietary intake, food literacy, and food insecurity. However, the study has certain limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect smaller changes, particularly for some dietary outcomes, and may reduce the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of low-income families in Jeddah. Second, the data were collected using a self-reported questionnaire, which may be prone to misreporting and information bias that could reduce the accuracy of the results. In addition, different recall periods of the Food Insecurity Experience Scale were used at baseline (12 months) and post-intervention (30 days). Because these recall periods represent different temporal dimensions of food insecurity, direct comparability between baseline and post-intervention measures is limited. Also, the absence of a control group limits causal inference. Another limitation relates to the relatively high baseline level of food literacy observed among participants, with the majority already classified as having adequate food literacy before the intervention. This may have introduced a potential ceiling effect, limiting the ability to detect larger improvements in categorical classifications following the intervention. Consequently, although improvements were observed in the mean food literacy score, the magnitude of change in categorical outcomes should be interpreted with caution.

Future nutrition interventions in Saudi Arabia are expected to reduce food insecurity, enhance food literacy, and improve dietary intake among all age groups and socioeconomic backgrounds. Additionally, more research is needed to examine the long-term effects of nutrition interventions and to guide the development of large-scale educational programs. Collaborating with charitable organizations is essential for assessing the long-term impacts of nutrition intervention programs on larger populations.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the association of a nutrition education intervention with food insecurity, food literacy, and dietary intake among low-income families in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The intervention was associated with significant improvements in food security and food literacy, which were accompanied by improvements in selected aspects of dietary intake. However, the association between changes in food literacy and specific dietary behaviors was generally weak, with the exception of bread and cereals. These findings highlight the complex relationship between nutrition knowledge and actual dietary practices, particularly among low-income populations facing financial and food access constraints. Future research should incorporate longer follow-up periods (e.g., 6–12 months post-intervention) to assess the sustainability of these changes and explore opportunities for scaling similar interventions through partnerships with charity organizations or government platforms.
