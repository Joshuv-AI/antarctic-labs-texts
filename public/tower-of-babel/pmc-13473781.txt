The association of empirical treatment with oseltamivir with the outcome of critically ill patients admitted with severe acute respiratory illness (SARI)
Authors: Raymond M Khan, Linah Mosleh AlMarashi, Mohamad-Hani Temsah, Ghassan Ali Ahmed Al Ghamdi, Musharaf Sadat, Felwa Bin Humaid, Jesna Jose, Fahad Al-Hameed, Kasim Al Khatib, Mohammed AlObaidi, Mohammed Shaim Alanezi, Faisal Fouad Al-Basseet, Rasha Alanazi, Yaseen Arabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNeuraminidase inhibitors (NAIs) are widely used empirically in critically ill patients with suspected influenza; however, their effect on mortality remains uncertain. This multicenter study evaluates the association between empirical treatment with oseltamivir and the outcome of critically ill patients with Severe Acute Respiratory Infection (SARI) admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU).MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study conducted in the ICUs of four hospitals in Saudi Arabia, involving adult patients with SARI from September 2012 to December 2018. Data collected were: demographics, comorbidities, clinical presentation, and outcomes among patients treated with oseltamivir and those who were not. The primary outcome was 90-day mortality. The association of oseltamivir and mortality was evaluated adjusting for propensity score.ResultsDuring the study period, 456 patients with SARI were included in the study, 301 were treated with empirical oseltamivir within a median of 1 day from presentation (interquartile range, 0-1 day) and a median of 4 days after symptom onset, while 155 patients were not. No significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the two groups. Of the included patients, 334 (73%) were tested for influenza using PCR, and 87 (26%) had a confirmed diagnosis of influenza. Patients on oseltamivir were less likely to require rescue oxygen therapy (22.9% vs. 34.8%, p=0.007), and had shorter hospital stay (20 days vs. 27 days, p=0.01). Patients treated with oseltamivir had significantly reduced 90-day mortality on adjusted analyses (aOR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.81-0.94, p=0.0002). Subgroup analysis revealed that the association with reduced mortality extends to patients >70 years old (aOR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89-0.99, p=0.02) and those with negative influenza tests (aOR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.79, 0.84, p<0.0001).ConclusionAmong critically ill patients with SARI, empirical treatment with oseltamivir was associated with lower mortality. These results add to the body of evidence suggesting clinical benefits of oseltamivir in managing critically ill patients with influenza-like illnesses.Keywords: oseltamivir, severe acute respiratory infection (SARI), intensive care unit (ICU), influenza, antiviral therapy, clinical outcomes, empirical treatment, mortality

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines Severe Acute Respiratory Infection (SARI) as an acute respiratory infection with a history of fever (≥38°C) and cough, onset within the last 10 days and requiring hospitalization 1 SARI is considered the fifth leading cause of deaths globally, with an estimated mortality of approximately 4.2 million each year. 2 Viral SARI accounts for 17-53% of pathogens isolated from critically ill patients with respiratory illness, with rhinovirus (4-30%), influenza (3-24%) and RSV (1-7%) being the most common, while novel viruses like H1N1, MERs-CoV and COVID-19 being responsible for the most recent outbreaks.3,4 Viral SARIs impose a substantial economic burden on public healthcare systems, with reports suggesting that the estimated cost of the COVID-19 pandemic was more than $163 billion. 5 A recent cross-sectional study from Saudi Arabia found the financial cost of SARI to be 5020 million USD, with an average cost per patient of 10,182 USD. 6

Influenza is among the leading viral causes of SARIs with FDA approved antiviral agents. 1 The WHO estimates that the annual influenza epidemic results in 1 billion infections, with 3-5 million severe cases requiring medical attention, and up to 650,000 respiratory-associated fatalities annually. 7 The influenza virus can lead to critical illness requiring ICU admission, with a mortality rate of around 30%. 8 Neuraminidase inhibitors (NAIs) are considered the first-line option for prophylaxis and treatment of severe influenza.9–11 NAIs have been extensively studied in outpatient settings. A Cochrane review in 2014 on adults and children with confirmed or suspected exposure to influenza (20 RCTs, 9623 patients) revealed that oseltamivir decreased time to alleviation of symptoms by 16.8 hours, with no significant effect on hospitalization. 12 Dobson and colleagues’ 2015 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs using oseltamivir 75 mg twice a day in 4328 adults with influenza-like illness had a 44% lower risk of developing respiratory complications requiring antibiotics in the intention to treat population and fewer admissions to the hospital. 9

Data among hospitalized patients is less consistent. Among hospitalized patients, Muthuri et al. (2014) reviewed 78 studies (including RCTs and observational studies) involving 29,234 patients with influenza A H1N1. NAIs were associated with 19% reduction in mortality compared to no treatment. 13 Lytras et al., found that among 1330 ICU patients infected with influenza A, NAIs compared to no NAIs were associated with reduced mortality among patients with H3N2 (RR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.49-0.94), while there was no measurable mortality effect among patients with H1N1 and influenza B, suggesting that the benefit of oseltamivir may depend on the strain of the virus. 14 Moreno et al., observed in their multivariate analysis of 2124 ICU patients that early oseltamivir within 2 days of symptom onset was associated with reduced ICU mortality by 31% compared to later administration. 8 The pharmacological effect of NAIs might depend on the specific antiviral agent, viral strain, timing of administration, patient population genotype, and clinical setting, which may explain these conflicting findings. 15

The CDC and WHO recommend treatment with NAIs in high-risk or severely ill SARI patients with suspected or confirmed influenza and those presenting in the influenza season.15,16 It has been suggested that therapy should be initiated early and not be delayed awaiting laboratory results. 17 However, since hospitalized influenza patients often demonstrate prolonged viral replication, prescribing oseltamivir more than 48 hours from the onset of symptoms might also be biologically effective. 18 Thus, the 2019 IDSA guidelines suggest giving NAIs independently of the duration of illness. 11

This study aims to determine an association between empirical treatment with oseltamivir and 90-day mortality among ICU patients admitted with SARI. Furthermore, it assesses whether oseltamivir treatment effect varies across subgroups defined by age, presence of any comorbidity, influenza status, and duration of symptoms from onset to emergency room.

Method

Study design and eligibility criteria

This multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted in four adult ICUs in four cities in Saudi Arabia. The units were staffed with intensive care consultants. The study subjects included in the study were all adult patients with SARI from September 1, 2012, to December 31, 2018. We excluded patients: aged <14 years, diagnosed with Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), labeled as comfort care or Do-Not-Resuscitate status, or with incomplete medical records. The data for patients with SARI admitted to the ICU was analyzed, comparing those who received empirical oseltamivir versus those who did not.

Definitions

SARI was defined using the WHO Case definition: a patient with an acute respiratory infection that requires hospitalization, a history of fever or measured temperature of ≥ 38 °C, AND cough, with an onset within the last 10 days. 19

Rescue oxygen therapy among patients on mechanical ventilation included one or more of the following interventions: recruitment maneuvers, neuromuscular blockade, nitric oxide, prone positioning, high frequency oscillation ventilation, or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). 20

The oseltamivir group consisted of patients who were treated empirically with oseltamivir on admission to the Emergency Department (ER) or ICU. Decisions regarding testing for influenza, continuation or discontinuation of the drug after the test results, and the duration of treatment were made at the discretion of the treating team. The drug was continued as directed by the treating physicians, depending on the results of the real-time PCR for the influenza virus. Nasopharyngeal swabs, deep throat swab or lower respiratory secretions (tracheal aspirates) in intubated patients were collected for real-time PCR testing (GeneXpert, Cepheid, California, USA) at admission. Multiplex PCR molecular test (BioFire multiplex PCR, bioMerieux Lyon, France) were performed at the discretion of the treating teams.

Ethical approval

The Institutional Review Board of the Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (IRB Approval: IRB/2659/22-Dated 6 December 2022), and the other participating hospitals approved this study and waived the requirement for written informed consent because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Data collection

Data were obtained from the patients’ medical records using the standardized International Severe Acute Respiratory and Emerging Infection Consortium (ISARIC) case report forms for the analysis. 21 These data included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, symptom duration, source of ICU admission, the severity of illness on ICU admission assessed by Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), physiological parameters including Glasgow coma scale (GCS), 22 laboratory parameters, and the therapeutic intervention. Data on study outcomes were collected for each patient, including: tracheostomy, ICU and hospital mortality, hospital length of stay, and mechanical ventilator duration.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS Statistical software (SAS, version 9.0; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).) Patient’s demographic and clinical data were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), while the categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The baseline characteristics were compared between patients who received oseltamivir and those who did not using the Chi-squared test (or Fisher’s exact test if indicated) for categorical variables, and the Student t-test or ANOVA for continuous variables. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Because of imbalances in baseline characteristics between the two groups, propensity score for being treated with oseltamivir was developed with the following covariates used in the model development: age, SOFA score at admission to ICU, a history of chronic pulmonary disease including asthma, chronic cardiac disease, chronic neurological disease including hemiplegia or paraplegia or dementia, and diabetes with chronic complications. The overlap of the propensity score distributions between the two groups was visually checked using a butterfly plot (Figure 1). In addition, the association of oseltamivir versus no oseltamivir therapy with 90-day mortality was evaluated in subgroups defined by age (<70 years and >70 years), presence of any comorbidity, presence of influenza, and duration of symptoms from onset to emergency room (<2 days and> 2 days).

Results

The study cohort consisted of 456 patients; 301 (66%) receiving oseltamivir and 173 (57.5%) were males (Table 1). There were no significant differences between the two groups in age [median 69 years (Q1, Q3: 49, 78) vs 70 years (54, 79), p= 0.42], BMI 28 Kg/m2 (24,33.0) vs 27 (22.5,32), p= 0.07], SOFA score [7 (5, 9) vs 7 (5, 10), p= 0.4], duration of symptoms before admission to ER [3 days (2, 5) vs 3 (2, 6). p= 0.97 and ICU [4 days (3, 7) vs 5 (2, 10), p= 0.54]. Patients in the oseltamivir group were more likely to have had fever [164 (54.5%) vs 62 (40.0%), p=0.003], cough [242 (80.4%) vs 87 (56.1%), p < 0.0001], and less likely to have fatigue [64 (21.3%) vs 47 (30.3%), p=0.03] and altered sensorium [51 (16.9%) vs 53 (34.3%), p=<0.0001]. For the patients treated with oseltamivir, the antiviral was administered within a median of 4 days (3, 6) after symptoms developed and within 1 day (0, 1) of admission to the ICU.

The patients in the oseltamivir group (Table 2) had higher respiratory rates [25 (20, 32) vs 23 (18, 28), p=0.004], lower PaO2 [69 mmHg (53.0, 86.40) vs 75.3 (65.0, 97.3), p=0.001], oxygen saturation [93.0 % (87.8, 96.3) vs 94.4 (91.0, 98.0), p= 0.001] and PF ratio [150 (92, 224) vs 181 (115, 269), p=0.0014]. The laboratory values were similar, except the creatinine phosphokinase which was higher in those in the oseltamivir group [113 (47, 269) vs 51 (24, 113), p= 0.0002]. The most common viral pathogens identified in the SARI patients (Table 3) admitted to ICU were influenza (85/248 in oseltamivir group and 2/86 in the no oseltamivir group). In the oseltamivir group, the median duration for treatment in the influenza positive patients was 9 days (6, 12), while the median duration in the influenza negative patients was 3 days (2, 5).

Oseltamivir was associated with shorter hospital length of stay [20 (12, 35) vs 27 (12, 68), p=0.01], and a trend towards lower unadjusted hospital mortality [87 (28.9%) vs 57 (36.8%), p= 0.09]. On crude analysis there was no significant reduction in 90-day mortality between the two groups (OR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.54-1.26, p=0.36). After propensity score adjustment oseltamivir was associated with a significant reduction in 90-day mortality (OR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.81–0.94; p=0.0002). In addition, subgroup analyses showed that the association of oseltamivir with reduction in 90-day mortality extends to those >70 years old (aOR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.98-0.99, p=0.02) and in SARI patients with negative influenza PCR (aOR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.79, 0.84; p<0.0001). Discussion

The main finding of this study is that oseltamivir was associated with decreased 90-day mortality. Several studies, predominately observational, demonstrated that NAIs was associated with reduced symptom duration, complications and mortality.12,13,16,23,24 Muthuri et al. found in 29,234 patients hospitalized during the 2009 H1N1 pandemic that early therapy (within 2 days of symptom onset) was associated with a significant reduction in mortality (aOR: 0.48 95% CI 0.14-0.56, p<0.0001). 13 Viasus and colleagues, in the 2009 outbreak, demonstrated that patients given oseltamivir more than 24 hours after symptom onset had prolonged duration of fever (aOR: 1.67 CI 1.03-2.72, p=0.04), longer hospital length of stay (aOR: 1.67 CI 1.06-2.63, p= 0.001) and higher mortality (aOR: 4.29 CI 1.25-14.63, 0.02). 25 While, Hiba et al. found that patients who had oseltamivir therapy initiated ≥48 h after symptom onset experienced a significantly higher adjusted risk for complications [aOR: 2.20, 95% CI 1.47 –3.57]. 26 Furthermore, Rodriguez et al. examined 657 patients with influenza A/H1N1 who were admitted to the ICU. On multivariate analysis, early oseltamivir (<48 hours from symptom onset) was associated with a decreased risk of death in ventilated patients [OR: 0.44, CI 0.21-0.87, p=0.02]. 27

Our study found that oseltamivir therapy, initiated a median of 4 days after symptom onset and within 1 day of ICU admission, was associated with a significant reduction in mortality. While existing data advocates for early oseltamivir therapy, it is documented that viral shedding occurs beyond 7 days in up to 37% of infected individuals, 28 and in a study performed by the CDC, oseltamivir attenuated shedding, even if started after 48 hours from symptom onset. 29 Louie et al. reported similar findings to ours, in that when compared to patients with no treatment, those started on oseltamivir within 5 days after symptom onset had significantly decreased ICU mortality. 30 Additionally, a recent study done by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) from 11 EU countries analyzing data for 19, 937 patients hospitalized with influenza from 2010 to 2020, revealed that neuraminidase inhibitor initiated up to 7 days after symptom onset was associated with a lower risk of death compared with untreated cases. 31

The patients in our study who received oseltamivir required less rescue oxygen therapy (neuromuscular blockade, nitric oxide, prone positioning, high frequency oscillation ventilation, and ECMO) and had shorter hospital duration. Heneghan et al. in their study noted that oseltamivir therapy reduced symptomatic influenza and the risk of pneumonia. 32 A meta-analysis by Hernan et al. showed that oseltamivir treatment reduced the risk of lower respiratory tract complications requiring antibiotic treatment by 28%. 33 Moreno et al. in a multicenter study observed that prolonged therapy (median of 10 days, IQR: 7–12 days, similar to our data: median 9 days, IQR: 6-12 days) with oseltamivir was associated with decreased hazard ratio of pneumonia, need for invasive mechanical ventilation and incidence of multiorgan failure. 34 Recently published data suggest that patients treated with oseltamivir had a shorter duration of hospitalization,. 35 Additionally, Katzen et al. demonstrated that oseltamivir therapy was associated with a median reduction in LOS. Further, empiric treatment was associated with shorter LOS compared to delayed therapy. 16 These observations, along with other findings, indicate that oseltamivir may be able to reduce illness severity and decrease duration of hospitalization in patients infected with influenza.

There are no published RCTs conducted in critically ill patients with influenza to establish the efficacy of oseltamivir on mortality. However, multiple observational studies suggest that oseltamivir is associated with a decreased mortality rate.13,16,24,27,30,36 Rodriguez et al., in data from Spain, demonstrated in their multivariate model that oseltamivir was an independent variable associated with reduced ICU mortality. 27 Dominguez et al., investigating the predictors of NAI treatment in severe hospitalized influenza cases, found that administering oseltamivir up to 5 days post-symptom onset resulted in a mortality benefit, even in patients admitted to the ICU. 36 Moreover, empiric therapy (oseltamivir administered before documentation of a positive influenza result) was associated with a lower risk of mortality in multivariable analysis compared to non-empiric therapy. 16 This finding, along with ours, suggests that there might be clinical benefit even with late (more than 48 hours from symptom onset) initiation of antiviral therapy in critically ill patients; hence, it is prudent to prescribe oseltamivir in all ICU patients with suspected or confirmed influenza regardless of duration of symptom onset as indicated by the 2018 IDSA guidelines. 37

Our data suggest that there is a significant 90-day mortality benefit with oseltamivir that extends to SARI patients with negative influenza PCR. This could be related to misclassification of some influenza patients as influsnza-negative due to false negative testing. It could be also be related to the potential effect of oseltamivir on other viruses. Oseltamivir specifically inhibits influenza A and B neuraminidases by competitively and reversibly interacting with the active enzyme site. 38 However, there some data suggesting effectiveness in patients infected with other viruses. Moore et al., in a rat model showed that oseltamivir decreases GM1 expression on CD8+ T-cell in RSV-infected mice resulting in antiviral immunity. 39 Additionally, Aljuhani and colleagues found that COVID-19 patients treated with oseltamivir had shorter time to viral clearance (11 vs. 16 days, p=0.04), shorter mechanical ventilator duration (6.5 vs 8.5 days, p=0.02) and lower odds of hospital/ventilator-associated pneumonia (OR: 0.49, 95% CI: 0.28-0.86, p=0.01. 40 Further, Chen et al., showed that oseltamivir was able to bind to and inhibit the activity parainfluenza virus 3. 41 Also, oseltamivir is used as adjunctive therapy for hand-foot-and-mouth disease caused by enteroviruses. 42 Thus, oseltamivir may attenuate the replication of non-influenza virus, which could contribute to less secondary bacterial infections and hence better outcome. Second, the observed benefit could be related to confounding factors in our data, such as the use of statins, which are known to inhibit the prenylation of Rho and Ras GTPase, which may interfere with viral replication. 43 Finally, it is possible that patients on oseltamivir were given single negative pressure rooms, had better compliance to infection control practices (personal protective equipment) and adherence with ICU management guidelines, which may also affect outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has strengths and limitations. A strength of the study is its relatively large sample size spanning seven flu seasons. The detailed information extracted from our computerized SARI database contained various demographic characteristics, severity index, therapeutic interventions, and patient outcomes, allowing us to analyze clinical decision-making factors. Limitations include the retrospective observational design, and the potential unmeasured confounders. The decision to empirically treat with oseltamivir, without waiting for laboratory confirmation of influenza, might have introduced subjective indication bias based on clinical suspicion. Furthermore, relying on chart reviews for data collection could have led to inaccuracies due to incomplete or incorrect documentation. Due to the exploratory nature of the study, there was no formal calculation of sample size. Despite these limitations in addition to acknowledging the time lapse between data collection and publication, the core principles and the data presented in this study continue to have important implications for public health. The study offers valuable insights into the benefits of oseltamivir for critically ill SARI patients, underscoring the need for more rigorous, controlled research to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In the critically ill population, there remain many debates regarding the clinical benefit of oseltamivir based on initiation time, dosing, and duration of therapy. Our study adds to the existing observational data on the effectiveness of late initiation of oseltamivir in critically ill patients. These findings demonstrate that among patients with SARI admitted to the ICU, empirical oseltamivir was associated with lower mortality. Hence, it may be prudent to initiate oseltamivir therapy empirically in all critically ill patients with community-acquired pneumonia during the influenza season, regardless of symptom duration and without awaiting laboratory confirmation. Future research on the optimal timing of oseltamivir in SARI is warranted.
