Sub-ångström bond length tuning enhances photoluminescence quantum yield in copper nanoclusters
Authors: Li Tang, Wei Zhang, Qikai Han, Bin Wang, Meng Zhou, Shuxin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how atomic-scale structure dictates light emission in metal nanoclusters is central to designing efficient luminophores. Despite decades of intensive investigation into their photoluminescence, a clear quantitative link between metal-metal bonding and emission efficiency is still lacking. Here we show that quantitatively modulating Cu-Cu bond distances during crystallization of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters enables a direct correlation between structure and emission performance. By synthesizing a series of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters with quantitatively modulated Cu-Cu bond lengths, we reveal an exponential relationship between bond distance and photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY), and a linear correlation with emission energy. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and ultrafast spectroscopy demonstrate that the enhanced PLQY arises from reduced HOMO-LUMO overlap induced by extended Cu-Cu distances, which promotes greater orbital localization. Simultaneously, the associated widening of the electronic gap suppresses non-radiative decay via the energy-gap law, further contributing to the increase in PLQY. This work establishes a quantitative relationship between Cu-Cu bond distance and quantum yield in Cu clusters, providing a general design framework for achieving high-efficiency emitters through quantitative bond-length engineering.Subject terms: Chemistry, Chemical synthesis

Introduction

Metal nanoclusters (NCs) composed of a few to several dozen metal atoms exhibit distinct physiochemical properties distinct from their bulk counterparts due to their discrete electronic states and quantum size effects1–9. Particularly interesting is the PL phenomenon observed in ligand-protected noble metal NCs like Aun(SR)m, Agn(SR)m and Cun(SR)m (SR = thiolate ligand)10–17. Unlike non-luminescent bulk materials, these NCs often show strong emissions with PLQY ranging from 0.1% to several tens of percent18–23.

The distinctive features of noble metal NCs including their atomic-level dimensions, low toxicity, good photostability and biocompatibility make them promising luminescent nanomaterials with applications in cell labeling24, biosensing25, and phototherapy26. To enhance and customize the PL characteristics of these NCs, significant research efforts have focused to methods like alloying22,27–29, ligand modification23,30–32, aggregation-induced emission33–35, self-assembly36–38, metal-ion complexation38,39, and photoactivation40. Recent studies have shown that bond length adjustment can significantly affect the luminescence properties of nanomaterials41,42; for example, manipulating Cu-Cu interactions in copper nanoclusters through steric hindrance of ligands leads to a gradual shift in the emission wavelength from the near-infrared region to the red-light region43,44. Although previous studies, have highlighted the qualitative influence of Cu-Cu interactions on the photoluminescence behavior of Cu(I) nanoclusters, but lacks a more rigorous, quantitative description in part because synthesizing a series of NCs having the same cluster core geometry but variable intracluster Cu-Cu bond distances has been synthetically challenging and rarely achieved45. This is primarily due to the complexity of nanocluster systems, which makes it challenging to explore the optical properties of clusters by manipulating single-factor variables.

In this work, we continuously varied the Cu-Cu bond lengths in Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters, synthesized via a liquid-phase process, by incorporating guest molecules with different steric effects during crystallization process. The nanoclusters synthesized include Cu6(DPTU)6(CH2Cl2)4 (DPTU = N,N-diphenylthiourea, Cu6-DCM), Cu6(DPTU)6(C7H8)1.5 (Cu6-TOL), Cu6(DPTU)6(CH3COCH3)6 (Cu6-AC), Cu6(DPTU)6(CHCl3)3 (Cu6-TM), Cu6(DPTU)6(C4H8O)6 (Cu6-THF). Comprehensive optical measurements reveal that PLQY exponentially increases with lengthening Cu-Cu bond, an effect which is particularly pronounced at bond lengths exceeding 9.037 Å. To decipher the origin of this nonlinear optical behavior, we systematically investigated the structural, electronic, and excited-state properties of these nanoclusters using a combination of single-crystal X-ray diffraction, steady-state and time-resolved spectroscopy, DFT calculations, and ultrafast transient absorption measurements. These complementary studies reveal that the exponential PLQY enhancement originates from electronic decoupling induced by structural distortions, reducing the HOMO-LUMO orbital overlap integrals as the Cu-Cu bond distances increase, as the HOMO and LUMO become progressively more localized on distinct regions of the Cu6 core. Meanwhile, a widening of the energy gap suppresses non-radiative decay channels. Collectively, these findings establish a quantitative and predictive correlation between molecular geometry and luminescence efficiency and provide a structural blueprint for the rational design of high-performance luminescent nanomaterials with potential applications in optoelectronics.

Results

Synthesis and crystal structure anatomy of nanoclusters

The Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters were prepared in situ, by adding N,N-diphenylthiourea to a solution of CuI in a mixture of acetonitrile and tetrahydrofuran to give light yellow solution, which was then reduced with NaBH4. The resulting product was purified and crystallized to yield Cu6-DCM / Cu6-TOL / Cu6-AC / Cu6-TM / Cu6-THF nanoclusters at room temperature by liquid diffusion of n-hexane into respective DCM/TOL/AC/TM/THF solutions of the crude product. Details of synthesis are provided in the Methods Section. It is noteworthy that the variation in bond lengths occurs exclusively within the crystal lattice — the ultraviolet-visible absorption spectra of solutions of these crystals were highly consistent regardless of the solvent used (Supplementary Fig. 1), suggesting no structural difference in the solution state.

The XPS, EDX, PXRD, ESI-MS results (m/z = 1744.83) and UV-vis spectra of the above five Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters were all similar (see Supplementary Figs. 2–5), suggesting the molecular composition of the nanoclusters to be identical irrespective of the solvent molecules in the lattice — a finding consistent with the crystallographic data (vide infra).

According to the single-crystal parsing results, Cu6-DCM, Cu6-TOL, and Cu6-AC crystallized in space groups P21/n, Cc, and P21/n of the monoclinic system, whereas Cu6-TM and Cu6-THF belong to the triclinic crystal system in space groups P1 and P-1, respectively (see Supplementary Figs. 6–11 and Supplementary Tables 1–5). The differences in space group are due to the different solvent molecules in their lattice. Nuclear magnetic resonance measurements further confirmed the existence of different solvents in crystals (Supplementary Figs. 12–16). The total structures of the five crystal forms of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters resemble the overall framework of previously reported Cu6(SR)6, as depicted in Fig. 1a21. Each Cu6 octahedron is formed by six copper(I) atoms, with each N, S donor ligand acting as a η1(N) µ2(S) ligand coordinating to three copper(I) atoms, forming one face of the copper(I) octahedron. Cu6 nanoclusters stabilized by various ligands have emerged as a significant focus of research within the realm of Cu nanoclusters. Cu6 nanoclusters stabilized by monodentate or bidentate thiourea ligands have emerged as a model system, akin to Au25, to facilitate a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between structure and properties2,21,43,46–58. To avoid inter-cluster interactions, N,N-diphenylthiourea organic ligands containing the single S site of the diphenyl ring were selected, further simplifying the model and enabling a more comprehensive investigation of the optical behavior of individual, independent clusters21,55,57,58. Cu6-TOL, Cu6-AC, and Cu6-THF contain two types of Cu6 molecules in the unit cell, with slightly different bond lengths (Supplementary Fig. 6). Detailed comparisons of these bond-length variations are provided in the following section. These structural divergences are ultimately governed by the identity of the guest solvent molecules confined within the crystal lattice.

A comparison of Cu-Cu bond lengths among five crystal forms of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters is presented in Fig. 1b–d, Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Fig. 17. Among all Cu-Cu interactions, the greatest variability was observed in two symmetry-related triangular motifs: Cu1-Cu3-Cu4 (designated as Δ1) and Cu5-Cu6-Cu2 (∆2). Other Cu-Cu bond lengths remained largely invariant across the series (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 17). To capture the full range of structural variation, we defined the two specific triangle average perimeter (C∆) as the average value of sum of the three Cu-Cu bond lengths constituting triangles ∆1 and ∆2. Additionally, due to the presence of two independent Cu6 units in the Cu6-TOL, Cu6-AC, and Cu6-THF crystals, we further distinguish these as Cu6-TOL1, Cu6-TOL2, Cu6-AC1, Cu6-AC2, Cu6-THF1, and Cu6-THF2, respectively (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 17, and Supplementary Table 6). Both the C∆1 (Cu1-Cu3-Cu4) and C∆2 values (Cu5-Cu6-Cu2) range from 8.443 Å to 9.446 Å. The average values of C∆1 and C∆2 for each cluster, denoted as C∆, are 9.446 Å (Cu6-DCM), 9.248 Å (Cu6-TOL1), 9.131 Å (Cu6-TOL2), 9.205 Å (Cu6-AC1), 9.028 Å (Cu6-TM), 8.903 Å (Cu6-AC2), 8.578 Å (Cu6-THF1), and 8.443 Å (Cu6-THF2) (Fig. 1c), with the difference between the largest and smallest C∆ values being 1.003 Å. Averaging the C∆ values of the Cu6(SR)6 clusters containing two structural isomers gave a sequence: Cu6-DCM (9.446 Å) > Cu6-TOL (9.189 Å) > Cu6-AC (9.054 Å) > Cu6-TM (9.028 Å) > Cu6-THF (8.510 Å). In summary, this solvent-controlled crystallization strategy affords atomically precise, compositionally invariant Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters with tunable Cu-Cu bond lengths. The resulting library of eight discrete configurations provides a structurally rigorous platform for correlating geometric parameters with optical performance, as explored in subsequent sections.

Optical properties of the Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters in crystals

The critical role of a nanocluster’s structure on its chemical and physical properties is well established1,9,10. In this study, we systematically compare the optical properties of a series of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters in the crystalline state to understand how they correlate with Cu-Cu bond length.

Firstly, to determine whether luminescence behavior was specific to the crystalline state, we conducted additional photophysical measurements on nanoclusters dispersed in various solvents and as amorphous films (Supplementary Figs. 18–20). In solution, their emission spectra of all Cu6(SR)6 clusters were nearly identical across different solvents, excluding the possibility of solvent coordination. Similarly, the photophysical properties of film samples matched those of solution-phase clusters, indicating that guest molecules do not alter Cu-Cu bonding outside the crystalline lattice (Supplementary Fig. 20). In the solution state, the UV-vis absorption spectra, emission profiles, PL lifetimes (2.5 μs), and temperature-dependent PL features of these clusters were all mutually consistent. (Supplementary Figs. 1, 21, 22). These observations confirm that the Cu6(SR)6 core structure remains intact upon dissolution. Additionally, PL excitation spectra closely matched the absorption spectra (Supplementary Fig. 23), resembling the behavior of quantum dots and nanoclusters like Pt1Ag28, Ag32Cd12 and so on19,32.

The photophysical properties of the NCs in the crystalline state were next investigated. UV-vis absorption spectroscopy revealed progressive redshifts in the absorption band edges across the series, measured at 573 nm (Cu6-DCM), 591 nm (Cu6-TOL), 606 nm (Cu6-AC), 655 nm (Cu6-TM), and 664 nm (Cu6-THF) (Fig. 2a). Corresponding photoluminescence (PL) spectra under 405 nm excitation (Fig. 2b, c) exhibited emission maxima that systematically redshifted with increasing Cu-Cu bond lengths: 762 nm (Cu6-DCM), 766 nm (Cu6-TOL), 771 nm (Cu6-AC), 781 nm (Cu6-TM), and 797 nm (Cu6-THF). These shifts highlight a strong structure-property relationship between cluster geometry and optical response. The Stokes shifts, defined as the energy difference between the absorption and emission maxima, were 298 nm (Cu6-DCM), 306 nm (Cu6-TOL), 357 nm (Cu6-AC), 372 nm (Cu6-TM), and 233 nm (Cu6-THF). These relatively large shifts indicate dominant metal-centered transitions in the Cu6(SR)6 system, consistent with the redshift trend observed in the UV-vis spectra43. PLQYs decreased across the series: 18.9% (Cu6-DCM), 5.1% (Cu6-TOL), 3.1% (Cu6-AC), 2.8% (Cu6-TM), and 1.7% (Cu6-THF) (Fig. 2c). Importantly, the PL excitation spectra of all five nanoclusters closely resemble their respective absorption profiles, suggesting that the observed emission originates from direct excitation of the HOMO-LUMO transition within the Cu6 core16, rather than from ligand-to-metal charge transfer or other intermediate excited states (Supplementary Fig. 24). Time-resolved photoluminescence measurements revealed single-exponential decay kinetics for all five Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters, with emission lifetimes in the microsecond regime (Fig. 2d). This uniform decay behavior indicates that each crystal hosts a single emissive species in a homogeneous environment with a lifetime of 5.1 μs for Cu6-DCM, 4.8 μs for Cu6-TOL, 4.0 μs for Cu6-AC, 3.9 μs Cu6-TM and 2.9 μs for Cu6-THF as calculated by decay curve fitting. These microsecond-scale lifetimes are characteristic of phosphorescence and point to emission from a triplet excited state59.

To further understand the influence of structure on optical properties, we compared C∆ within the Cu6 octahedron, where structural variation is most pronounced and PLQY. We found that emission energy linearly increases (blue shifts) with increasing C∆, Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 7, and that PLQY displays a single-exponential decrease with decreasing C∆, ranging from 18.9% (Cu6-DCM) to 1.7% (Cu6-THF), following the order: Cu6-DCM (18.9%) > Cu6-TOL1 (6.99%) > Cu6-AC1 (5.85%) > Cu6-TOL2 (4.12%) > Cu6-TM (2.8%) > Cu6-AC2 (2.21%) > Cu6-THF1 (1.69%) > Cu6-THF2 (1.65%) (Fig. 2f, and Supplementary Table 7).

We estimated the emission energy and PLQY of Cu6-TOL1, Cu6-TOL2, Cu6-AC1, Cu6-AC2, Cu6-THF1, and Cu6-THF2 by inputting their respective C∆ values in these experimentally derived linear and exponential fitting functions (Supplementary Fig. 25, and Supplementary Table 7). An iterative fitting procedure was then employed to refine the model parameters60, which substantially improved predictive accuracy and yielded a high correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.999) for both energy and PLQY trends (Fig. 2e, f). The derived equations were subsequently validated by comparing predicted and experimental values, demonstrating the high reliability of the structure-property relationships. Using Cu6-DCM as an example, the C∆ value of 9.446 Å was substituted into the fitted C∆-dependent linear equation for emission energy and the exponential decay model for PLQY (Fig. 2e, f), yielding predicted values of 1.625 eV and 18.9%, respectively, both of which are exactly consistent with the experimentally measured data (1.627 eV for emission energy; 18.9% for PLQY). More validation results for other clusters can be found in Supplementary Table 8. These findings validate the linear and exponential equations as a quantitative framework for further theoretical modelling and experimental design based on precise geometric parameters.

Solving this validated exponential relationship between C∆ and PLQY, the characteristic point of the exponential curve C∆ of 9.037 Å was identified as the onset of rapid quantum yield enhancement. This indicates that when C∆ exceeds 9.037 Å, the PLQY enter an accelerated growth regime, defining a critical structural threshold for optimizing luminescence efficiency. Specifically, the point of significant acceleration was obtained by solving Eq. (1), where the exponential term becomes comparable to the constant offset, indicating the onset of exponential growth. This point corresponds to the inflection point where the second derivative is zero (see Methods section).

A similar abrupt change in quantum yield was noted for AgxAu25-x nanoclusters22, where it was attributed to the substitution of central Au atoms with Ag atoms. Computational analyses further revealed that this substitution led to a separation between the HOMO and LUMO — a phenomenon also observed in Au24 nanoclusters and the M29 series of nanoclusters16,27. These observations led us to posit that the separation of HOMO and LUMO is a critical consideration in the design of clusters with a high photoluminescence quantum yield. Our findings underscore the nuanced interplay between geometric structure, electronic configuration, and photoluminescent properties, providing a foundation for designing advanced nanomaterials with tailored optical characteristics.

To further probe the emissive behavior, temperature-dependent steady-state PL measurements were performed on crystalline Cu6(SR)6 samples over the range of 300 to 80 K (Fig. 3). All five nanoclusters exhibited gradual redshifts in emission maxima upon cooling: Cu6-DCM (762 → 780 nm), Cu6-TOL (766 → 795 nm), Cu6-AC (771 → 806 nm), Cu6-TM (781 → 803 nm), and Cu6-THF (797 → 817 nm). Concurrently, PL intensities increased by a factor of 1.15 to 1.39. These temperature-dependent trends suggest that lowering the temperature suppresses nonradiative decay pathways and stabilizes the excited states, likely due to reduced lattice vibrations43. As a result, lattice contraction reduces the Cu-Cu distances, resulting in a redshift of the PL. At the same time, weakened non-radiative decay channels make radiative recombination more favorable, leading to enhanced emission efficiency. Nonlinear fitting of the temperature-dependent PL intensity profiles yielded exciton binding energies of 24, 17.9, 30.1, 16.9, and 31.4 meV for Cu6-DCM, Cu6-TOL, Cu6-AC, Cu6-TM, and Cu6-THF, respectively, indicating that the excitonic states in these nanoclusters are moderately stable against thermal dissociation (Supplementary Fig. 26)61. To assess the environmental robustness of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters, we monitored their UV-vis spectra over a 30-day period under ambient conditions. No spectral degradation was observed (Supplementary Fig. 27), confirming that the clusters were stable in air over this time period and emphasizing their potential for real-world applications in optoelectronics and related technologies.

Building upon the comprehensive spectroscopic and crystallographic analysis described above, the visible emission of the Cu6(SR)6 nanosystem is unambiguously attributed to triplet cluster-centered (3CC) phosphorescence, supported by the following evidence: (i) microsecond-scale emission lifetimes; (ii) relatively large Stokes shifts; (iii) temperature-dependent enhancement and redshift; and (iv) strong correlation between emission energy and Cu-Cu interactions. Importantly, this emission behavior is largely independent of the surrounding organic ligands62–64. The 3CC triplet excited states involve a combination of ligand-to-metal charge transfer and metal-centered (3 d → 4 s) contributions63. Moreover, ligand-centered charge transition and metal-to-ligand charge transfer are not responsible for the emission in this work, as their emissions typically occur in the high-energy region, such as between 470 and 540 nm43.

Physicochemical background and computational insights

As described above, the PLQY and emission energy of the Cu6(SR)6 system exhibit linear and exponential growth relationships with C∆. To gain deeper insight into the nature of this relationship, theoretical calculations were performed to elucidate the luminescence origin and rationalize the crystal-dependent PLQY. It is well established that in Cu(I) clusters, the electronic energy gap is highly sensitive to Cu-Cu bonding distances59. Increasing C∆ results in a larger energy gap and a blue shift in emission, consistent with the experimentally observed linear increase in emission energy in the Cu6(SR)6 system. We also observed an increase in energy gap, a decrease in the non-radiative rate (from 3.7 × 104 to 5.9 × 103 s⁻¹), and an enhancement in the PLQY (Supplementary Table 9). This is consistent with the energy gap law, where larger energy gap suppresses non-radiative decay.

To further probe the electronic origins of these phenomena, we analyzed the HOMO and LUMO distributions. As shown in Fig. 4, the HOMO is primarily localized on the Cu atoms, while the LUMO is centered within the Cu6 core and core and is strongly bonding. The increasing Cu-Cu distance leads to the increase in the HOMO-LUMO gaps, whereas it also results in the reduction (from -0.73 to -1.18 eV) of the LUMO level. This trend arises from the bonding nature of the LUMO, which renders its energy highly sensitive to the Cu-Cu distances: shorter Cu-Cu bonds enhance bonding interactions, thereby lowering the LUMO energy. This relationship is clearly reflected in the calculated orbital energy levels. Systems such as Cu6-THF1 and Cu6-THF2 with shorter Cu-Cu bonds display lower LUMO energies (-1.31 and -1.18 eV, respectively), in contrast to Cu6-DCM (-0.73 eV) with longer Cu-Cu separations. As a result, the HOMO-LUMO gap narrows from 3.96 eV in Cu6-DCM to 3.38 eV in Cu6-THF1, highlighting a strong correlation between the structural contraction of the Cu6 core and electronic gap narrowing. Concurrently, an increase in C∆ induces greater localization of the HOMO and LUMO electron density, leading to a reduction in the orbital overlap integral from 0.588 (Cu6-THF) to 0.507 (Cu6-DCM) (Table 1). This reveals a clear inverse correlation between C∆ and the degree of orbital overlap. The reduction in orbital overlap, as indicated by the spectral data, led to an increase in the PLQY.

To quantitatively evaluate the HOMO-LUMO communication, we calculated the overlap integrals using the Multiwfn program65 (see Theoretical Methods). Our DFT calculations confirm that the LUMO in Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters exhibits strong bonding character. This electronic feature suggests a potential light-induced structural relaxation mechanism analogous to recent observations in photoexcited {[4-BMes2-3,5-(CH3)2Pz]Cu}3 systems44, where bonding-type LUMO excitation triggers Cu-Cu shortening to stabilize emissive excited state. Such dynamic structural adaptation would further enhance the emission efficiency through geometric confinement of excited states, providing additional support for the structure-dependent PLQY enhancement observed in our C∆-modulated system.

Fundamentally, the PLQY enhancement in Cu6(SR)6 systems originates from the orbital overlap modulation between HOMO and LUMO. As C∆ increases, greater spatial localization of electron cloud reduces the overlap integral, which inversely correlates with structural distortion. In parallel, an increased HOMO-LUMO gap suppresses non-radiative decay via the energy gap law, contributing to enhanced PLQY. These findings show that modulation of the Cu-Cu bonding interactions governs the quantum effects driving changes in photophysical properties, providing a theoretical basis to interpret the experimental optical behavior of copper clusters. To illustrate upper-regime behavior, we mathematically extrapolate Eq. (1) to PLQY = 100%, yielding CΔ = 9.736 Å (outside the domain; qualitative, see Supplementary Note).

To further evaluate the role of intracluster interactions, we examined the interaction energies between the two Cu3(SR)3 subunits in the Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters (Supplementary Table 10). Cu6-DCM shows the most negative interaction energy (-270.90 kcal mol-1) yet the most pronounced localization of the frontier orbitals (0.507, Table 1), while Cu6-THF1 has the least interaction energy (-252.36 kcal mol-1) together with relatively more delocalized HOMO-LUMO character (0.588, Table 1). This indicates that interaction energy contains multiple contributions, including orbital, electrostatic, and dispersion interactions, and thus it does not vary monotonically with cuprophilic orbital coupling. Consistent with the structural trend that a larger CΔ lengthens intracluster Cu-Cu contacts, the HOMO-LUMO overlap in Table 1 decreases with increasing CΔ, evidencing weakened cuprophilic interaction and enhanced electronic localization. The results in Supplementary Table 10 therefore track the overall thermodynamic stabilization of the fragment pair, whereas Fig. 4 and Table 1 capture how CΔ controls the cuprophilic orbital coupling; taken together, these data show that increasing CΔ weakens metal-metal orbital overlap and localizes the frontier orbitals even when the total interaction energy remains strongly negative due to non-orbital contributions.

Dynamics of excited states

To delve deeper into the origin of emissions and excited state dynamics of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters in the crystalline phase, we further conducted femtosecond time-resolved transient absorption (fs-TA) experiments. As discussed above, the five different crystals of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters exhibited similar properties when dissolved in DCM. Therefore, we performed fs-TA measurements of Cu6(SR)6 in DCM under 350 nm excitation. The initial fs-TA spectrum displayed a broad positive band across the entire visible region (Fig. 5a), consistent with the literature21. The kinetic traces are almost the same at all detected wavelengths, suggesting little spectral evolution occurs. The kinetic fitting at 500 nm (Fig. 5b) revealed two processes with lifetimes of 3.1 ps and > 1 ns. We ascribe the 3.1 ps to the intersystem crossing process and the > 1 ns to the relaxation to the ground state66–68. Subsequently, nanosecond time-resolved transient absorption (ns-TA) further employed to explore the triplet dynamics. It can be observed that the initial spectrum of ns-TA (Fig. 5c) is identical with the last spectrum from fs-TA, and there is no other spectral evolution during longer-time delay. By fitting the kinetic trace at 500 nm (Fig. 5d), a lifetime of 2.8 μs is obtained.

To understand how the excited-state dynamics correlate with the crystal structures, fs-TA kinetic traces at 500 nm were recorded for nanoclusters in different crystalline state (Fig. 5e–g), and the detailed fs-TA data is presented at Supplementary Fig. 28. A clear trend emerges: the decay of Cu6-THF (Fig. 5e) is significantly slower than that of Cu6-DCM (Fig. 5g), while Cu6-AC (Fig. 5f) shows intermediate behavior. This trend is in good agreement with the computed HOMO-LUMO overlap values (Table 1), which increase progressively from DCM to THF. These results highlight the critical role of crystal structure in modulating the excited-state dynamics and suggest that increased orbital overlap leads to slower decay rates and potentially enhanced stability of the excited states. Collectively, the TA results and theoretical orbital analysis establish a comprehensive structure-property relationship governing photoluminescence behavior in Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters.

Discussion

A series of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters having different Cu-Cu bond lengths has been synthesized and its optical properties analyzed using steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence spectroscopy, ultrafast dynamics, and DFT calculations. The results reveal a linear correlation between Cu-Cu bond length and emission energy, as well as an exponential relationship between bond length and PLQY. When the Cu-Cu bond length exceeds 9.037 Å, PLQY increases more than 11-fold, from 1.7% to 18.9%. A combined experimental-theoretical approach revealed that as the Cu-Cu bond expands, the HOMO and LUMO orbitals become increasingly localized on distinct atomic regions, decreasing their spatial overlap and enhancing PLQY. Concurrently, the increasing HOMO-LUMO energy gap reduces non-radiative decay pathways in accordance with the energy gap law. This dramatic enhancement highlights the critical role of Cu-Cu bond lengths in determining the luminescence efficiency of Cu6(SR)6 nanoclusters and provides insights into the design and synthesis of highly photoluminescent nanomaterials.

Methods

Materials

Copper(I) iodide (CuI, > 99.99% metal basis, Aldrich), N,N-diphenylthiourea (C13H12N2S, DPTU, 98%), sodium borohydride (NaBH4, Aldrich), tetrahydrofuran (C4H8O, THF, HPLC grade, ≥99.9%), methylene chloride (CH2Cl2, DCM, HPLC grade, ≥99.9%), acetone (CH3COCH3, AC, HPLC grade, ≥99.9%), toluene (C7H8, TOL, HPLC grade, ≥99.9%), trichloromethane (CHCl3, TM, HPLC grade, ≥99.9%), hexyl hydride (C6H14, n-hexane, HPLC grade, ≥99.9%) and Wahaha purified water were purchased and used as supplied, without further purification. All glassware was thoroughly cleaned with aqua regia (HCl/HNO3 = 3:1 vol.%) and rinsed with copious amounts of pure water before use, then dried in an oven.

Synthesis of the Cu6-DCM / Cu6-TOL / Cu6-AC / Cu6-TM / Cu6-THF nanoclusters

THF (10 mL) was added to a solution of copper(I) iodide (0.26 mmol, 50 mg) in 7 mL of acetonitrile (C2H3N). The resulting light-yellow solution was stirred at 1500 × g for 5 min. Subsequently, N,N-diphenylthiourea (0.43 mmol, 100 mg) was added to the solution, causing it to turn deep yellow. Finally, a solution of sodium borohydride (NaBH4) (0.8 mmol, 30 mg) in 2 mL of ice water was rapidly added to the solution, turning it red. The reaction was stirred for 4 h. The aqueous layer was then removed, and the organic phase concentrated using a rotary evaporator. Excess ligand was removed using n-hexane, and the precipitate was extracted with DCM multiple times. Crystals of Cu6-DCM / Cu6-TOL / Cu6-AC / Cu6-TM / Cu6-THF nanoclusters were obtained at room temperature by liquid diffusing the n-hexane into the DCM/TOL/AC/TM/THF solution of product, respectively. Yields: 13.2% for five Cu6(SR)6 systems based on Cu.

Interconversion of Cu6-DCM and Cu6-THF by solvent induction

A glass slide containing yellow crystal Cu6-DCM was placed into a 10 mL glass bottle containing THF, sealed, and left to stand at room temperature for 10 min. A red crystal of Cu6-THF was obtained. The reverse process was analogously performed, though required the standing time to be extended to 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 29).

Such an estimation is in line with established physicochemical considerations, as steric hindrance from ligands, electronic effects, and lattice constraints restrict the effective range of cuprophilic interactions to Cu-Cu distances of approximately 2.4–3.6 Å69. Furthermore, to further assess the feasibility of the extrapolated structure, we constructed a theoretical Cu6(SR)6 model structure with CΔ = 9.736 Å. The calculated HOMO-LUMO overlap integral (0.49) agrees closely with the value predicted (0.486) by inserting this CΔ into the experimentally derived linear relationship between CΔ and overlap (Supplementary Fig. 30), supporting the plausibility of such a structure. While experimental realization remains to be demonstrated, this analysis suggests a broader reasonable range for defining the applicability of Eq. (1), namely 8.446-9.736 Å. These extrapolated results are presented here to provide theoretical context and should be considered as indicative rather than experimentally validated.

Characterization

The liquid UV-vis absorption spectra of nanoclusters were measured using a UV-8000 spectrophotometer. Solid-state absorption spectra of nanoclusters crystallites were obtained using an Agilent cary5000. ESI-MS were recorded using a Bruker micro TOF-Q system. XPS measurements were performed at an analysis chamber base pressure of < 1 × 10−9 mbar using an ESCALAB XI+ photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a monochromated Al Kα (1486.8 eV) 150-W X-ray source, 0.5-mm circular spot size, a flood gun, to counter charging effects. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed using an */S-4800 microscope, operating within an accelerating voltage range of 0.1-30 kV. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements were performed on a Bruker Avance II spectrometer (500 MHz). For 1H NMR, CD2Cl2, CDCl3, and C3D6O were used as the solvent to dissolve all crystal samples. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) data were collected on a D/MAX/2500PC at ambient temperature. Photoluminescence spectra were recorded with an OceanOptics QE65000 spectrometer. Luminescence decay was measured on an F-6000 fluorescence spectrometer. Femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectra were measured as described in refs. 70,71. Briefly, the TOPAS Prime (Light Conversion) was used to generate the pump pulse with central at 350 nm (0.06 μJ), the probe pulse was delayed by a computer-controlled optical delay line. The polarization angle between the pump and probe pulses were set at 51.7° by placing a half-wave plate in the pump beam. Nanosecond transient absorption (ns-TA) spectra were measured using a commercial set-up (Time-Tech Spectra), in which the pump beam is the same as that in fs-TA, while the probe beam was generated from a supercontinuum laser (LEUKOS-DISCO, French) with the spectral region across 350 to 1800 nm. Steady-state absorption spectra of the nanoclusters were checked before and after the fs- and ns-TA measurements to rule out photodegradation. SC-XRD data of Cu6-DCM, Cu6-TOL, Cu6-AC, Cu6-TM and Cu6-THF was collected using a Bruker D8 QUEST X-ray single-crystal diffractometer irradiating with Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å) at 170 K. The nanocluster structures were solved using the ShelXT structure solution program via intrinsic phasing in Olex272,73. The full matrix least squares method was used to improve the structure of F2 using the SHELXTL software package73. The PLQY measurements were conducted using a 4-inch polytetrafluoroethylene integrating sphere coupled with an OceanOptics QE65000 spectrometer calibrated using a standard light source (Ocean Optics HL-3P-INT-CAL)74. Three replicate experiments were performed with samples synthesized in separate batches to ensure accuracy. PLQY was measured as follows: first, a blank spectrum was obtained by placing a blank sample in the integrating sphere and collecting the emission scan (repeated 5 times) over the wavelength range of 300-1090 nm with 405 nm excitation. The sample spectrum was subsequently obtained by replacing the blank with a crystalline Cu6(SR)6 nanocluster sample (40 mg) and repeating the emission scan (5 times) under the same conditions. PLQY is rigorously defined as the ratio of the number of photons emitted (Φem) to the number of photons absorbed (Φabs) by a material. This fundamental relationship is expressed as: PLQY = Φem/Φabs. All spectroscopic data, including PL and absorption spectra, are analyzed and fitted using Origin (OriginLab) software.

Theoretical methods

All the theoretical calculations were performed with the Gaussian 16 Reversion C.02 package75. Vertical excitation energies were calculated using time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) based on the crystal structures of the Cu6(SR)6 systems at the B3LYP76 functional and 6-311 G (d, p)76,77 basis set (LanL2TZ78 for Cu). Frontier molecular orbital calculations were performed using Multiwfn65 based on the results of Gaussian output. To evaluate the electronic communication between the HOMO and LUMO, we computed the orbital overlap using the Multiwfn program65. The spatial overlap was calculated by integrating the product of orbital densities Eq. (9). These integrals were evaluated numerically using Becke’s grid-based integration approach, where the radial and angular grid parameters were set to their default values as specified in the Multiwfn documentation65, ensuring sufficient integration accuracy. The HOMO and LUMO wavefunctions were extracted from the output of Gaussian. The interaction energy between the two Cu3(SR)3 units within the Cu6(SR)6 nanocluster was calculated using the Amsterdam Modeling Suite (AMS2025), formerly known as Amsterdam Density Functional (ADF) (Amsterdam Density Functional (ADF 2019) Code, Vrije Universiteit: Amsterdam, The Netherlands. Available at: (https://www.scm.com))79. All calculations were performed with the triple- zeta basis set including two polarization functions (TZ2P), employing the Becke exchange and Perdew correlation functionals (BP86)80,81, together with Grimme’s empirical dispersion correction with Becke-Johnson damping, D3(BJ)82.
