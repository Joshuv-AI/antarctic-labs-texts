Renormalization group analysis of color superconductivity revisited Preprint: N3AS-25-016, RIKEN-iTHEMS-Report-25
Yuki Fujimoto

Abstract
Color superconductivity in cold, dense quark matter is a key feature of the QCD phase diagram, whose present theoretical understanding relies predominantly on weak-coupling calculations. In this work, we revisit the evaluation of the color-superconducting gap using a renormalization group (RG) framework formulated in effective theory near the Fermi surface. By incorporating quark self-energy corrections into the RG equation, we reproduce the known weak-coupling results from the gap equation at the same perturbative order at O ⁡ ( g 0 ) O(g^{0}) . Within the RG approach, the angular momentum structure of the pairing channel becomes more transparent, allowing us to examine the size of the gap for various pairing patterns. We also compare our results with recent lattice QCD calculations at finite isospin density. Finally, we argue that the RG method potentially offers a simpler and more systematic route to higher-order computations of the gap, which are of order O ⁡ ( g ) O(g) and thus quantitatively important.

Introduction

Cold quark matter at finite baryon density is expected to be a color superconductor, induced by a diquark condensate (see Ref.
Alford et al. 2008
and reference therein).
Quark Cooper pairing is inevitable because there is always an attractive channel in quark-quark interaction;
whenever there is an attraction between particles on the Fermi surface, it leads to BCS instability and formation of Cooper pairs.
Onset of BCS instability can be seen transparently by using the renormalization group (RG) approach to the Fermi surface, which was pioneered in early 1990s
Benfatto and Gallavotti 1990a
;
Benfatto and Gallavotti 1990b
;
Benfatto and Gallavotti 1996

Feldman and Trubowitz 1991

Feldman et al. 1992
;
Feldman et al. 1993
;
Shankar 1991
;
Shankar 1994
;
Polchinski 1992
.
The same RG technology can be applied to relativistic quarks in cold and dense matter to study color superconductivity
Evans et al. 1999a
;
Evans et al. 1999b
;
Schäfer and Wilczek 1999a
;
Son 1999
;
Hsu and Schwetz 2000
.
In QCD at finite baryon density, there is a complication related to a statically unscreened chromomagnetic gluon, as first pointed out in Ref.
Son 1999
;
this long-range magnetic interaction gives rise to the amplification in the pairing gap compared to the short-range interaction case.

The current understanding of color superconductivity is grounded on the analysis at large quark chemical potential
\mu

\Delta

The pairing gap can either be obtained by solving the gap equation derived in perturbation theory

Currently, perturbative series of the logarithm of the pairing gap

The RG approach allows us to find the lowest-order term in the perturbative series of

Barrois 1979

Recently, there is a revived interest in color superconductivity, or quark Cooper pairing more in general.
This is especially in connection with the recent progress in astrophysics and first-principles calculation of QCD, which are specifically lattice QCD and perturbative QCD in this context.
On the astrophysical application side, in the recent work
Kurkela et al. 2024
, authors constrained the magnitude of the color-superconducting gap from observational data of neutron stars relying on the rigorous interpolation method between the two ab initio calculations at low and high densities
Komoltsev and Kurkela 2022

On the first-principles calculation side, there were remarkable advances in the lattice-QCD calculation of thermodynamic quantities at finite
isospin

\mu_{I}

Abbott et al. 2023
;
Abbott et al. 2025

QCD at finite
\mu_{I}
is free from the sign problem, and thus amenable to Monte Carlo calculation on the lattice.
In the latest lattice calculation in the continuum limit
Abbott et al. 2025

\mu_{I}\simeq 3\,\text{GeV}
, at which QCD becomes weakly coupled, so direct comparison with the weak-coupling calculation is possible
Fujimoto 2024a
;
Fujimoto 2024b
;
Fukushima and Minato 2025

In the weak-coupling result, the effect of the pairing gap is substantial because QCD at large
\mu_{I}

We find qualitative agreement between the lattice-QCD and weak-coupling calculations, but quantitatively, the pairing gap contribution in the weak-coupling calculation seems to be overestimated compared to the latest lattice calculation
Fujimoto 2024b
;
Fukushima and Minato 2025
.

In this work, we revisit the RG analysis of color superconductivity at weak coupling.
We demonstrate quantitatively that the RG analysis can also lead to the weak-coupling expression of the gap up to the currently known order
O(g^{0})
.
In particular, we include the self-energy correction into the RG equation, which can be incorporated by using the resummed quark propagator in hard dense loop (HDL) effective theory.
We also systematically re-derive the constant term in the beta function arising from the long-range interaction, which was first pointed out by Son
Son 1999
.

Although the current work does not push the limit of the existing calculation, there are several advantages of the RG approach over the other approaches to color superconductivity at weak coupling.
The RG equation decouples in each spin and orbital angular momentum channel, so the angular momentum state in which the pairing occurs becomes clear.
This is related to the accompanying paper
Fujimoto 2025a
in which we classify the possible spin and orbital angular momentum channels using the term symbols as in the nonrelativistic case.
This is possible because of the loss of Lorentz invariance at finite density.
Another advantage is that the RG approach provides good insight into higher-order calculation in perturbation theory.
All the perturbative corrections in the effective theory near the Fermi surface, for which we derive the RG equation, can be classified by whether they are relevant, marginal, or irrelevant, on top of the ordinary expansion in terms of the coupling constant.
The combination of these two hierarchies simplifies the organization of the higher-order corrections.

Renormalization group approach near the Fermi surface

To make the paper self-contained and to introduce the formulas necessary later in the text, we review the renormalization group (RG) approach to Landau Fermi liquid

Let us analyze interacting massless quark excitations near the Fermi surface using RG.
We consider the effective theory with the kinetic term of the action as

\displaystyle S_{0}=\int_{|\epsilon_{p}|<\Lambda}\frac{d^{4}p}{(2\pi)^{4}}\bar{\psi}(p)(p_{0}-\epsilon_{p})\psi(p)\,,

\displaystyle\qquad\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}(p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{3}-p_{4})\,,

(2)

III.2
Self-energy correction

In the derivation in the previous subsection, we neglected HDL resummation in the quark propagator.
However, one has to resum a self-energy contribution
\Sigma
in the quark propagator because both
k_{0}
and
\epsilon_{k}
in the propagator of the quasiparticle are equal or smaller than the soft scale
\sim g\mu
in the problem:
i/\not{k}\to i/(\not{k}-\Sigma)
.
This quark self-energy correction amounts to the wave function renormalization.
Namely, the effect of including the self-energy is to replace

k_{0}\to\frac{k_{0}}{Z(k_{0})}\,,

(59)

in the quark propagator.
The quark wave function renormalization factor
Z(k_{0})
up to the leading logarithm is obtained as
Brown et al. 2000b
;
Manuel 2000
;
Gerhold and Rebhan 2005

Z(k_{0})\equiv\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{18\pi^{2}}\ln\frac{48e^{2}m_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}{\pi^{2}k_{0}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\,,

(60)

where
m_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}=m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}/3
is the effective gluon mass at finite density, and
e
is the base of the natural logarithm.

III.2.1
Resummed quark propagator

Now, we include the wave function renormalization effect up to
O(g^{2})
in the RG equation.
This effect only appears through the integral
\mathcal{I}
defined in Eq. (
41
).
We replace
\mathcal{I}
with
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{d\Lambda}\frac{k^{2}\,dk}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk_{0}}{2\pi}\,\frac{1}{\left[k_{0}/Z(k_{0})\right]^{2}-\epsilon_{k}^{2}}\,.

(61)

We evaluate the
k_{0}
-integral by performing the Wick rotation
k_{0}\to ik_{4}
:

\displaystyle i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk_{4}}{2\pi}\,\frac{Z^{2}(ik_{4})}{\left(ik_{4}\right)^{2}+\left[Z(ik_{4})\epsilon_{k}\right]^{2}}=-\frac{iZ(\epsilon_{k})}{2|\epsilon_{k}|}\,.

(62)

Then, the integral
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{4}\int_{d\Lambda}d\epsilon_{k}\,\left[-\frac{iZ(\epsilon_{k})}{2|\epsilon_{k}|}\right]\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{iN}{4}\frac{9\pi^{2}}{g^{2}}\ln\left[\frac{1+\frac{g^{2}}{18\pi^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{48e^{2}m_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}{\pi^{2}\Lambda^{2}}\right)+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t}{1+\frac{g^{2}}{18\pi^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{48e^{2}m_{\mathrm{g}}^{2}}{\pi^{2}\Lambda^{2}}\right)}\right]\,.

(63)

Taking the derivative with respect to
t
, one gets

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}{dt}=\frac{iN}{4}\frac{1}{1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t+O(g^{2})}\,.

(64)

We see below that the
t
-independent term of
O(g^{2})
gives a higher-order contribution to the gap compared to the
t
-dependent term of
O(g^{2})
.

In the beta function, the density of states is replaced by

\displaystyle N\to Z_{\psi}(t)N\,,\quad Z_{\psi}(t)\equiv\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)^{-1}\,.

(65)

Although the term proportional to
t
contributes at
O(g^{2})
, this is important as the
t
-dependence changes the solution of the RG equation entirely.
We dropped the constant term at
O(g^{2})
as these corrections remain to be small.

III.2.2
Role of quark self energy in the gap

Let us now illustrate the role of the quark self energy in the pairing gap by the following simple argument.

The gap is given by the singularity in the solution of the RG equation.
Let us consider the simple RG equation without the tree-level beta function.

\frac{dG}{dt}=-NG^{2}\,.

(66)

Its solution at general
t>0
is

\displaystyle G(t)=\frac{G(0)}{1+NG(0)t}\,.

(67)

When
G(0)<0
, this solution has a singularity at

\displaystyle t=\frac{1}{N|G(0)|}\,.

(68)

The energy scale
\Lambda
at the singularity can be regarded as the pairing gap.
From the relation
t=-\ln(\Lambda/m_{\mathrm{D}})
, one finds

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\Delta}{m_{\mathrm{D}}}\right)=-\frac{1}{N|G(0)|}\,.

(69)

Now we do the same for the RG equation including the self-energy corrections:

\displaystyle\frac{dG}{dt}=-\frac{N}{1+c\epsilon+\epsilon t}G^{2}\,,

(70)

where
\epsilon\ll 1
is a small parameter related to the wave function renormalization.
The term
c\epsilon
in the denominator corresponds to the
t
-independent term of
O(g^{2})
in the denominator of Eq. (
64
).
Its solution is

\displaystyle G(t)=\frac{G(0)}{1+\frac{NG(0)}{\epsilon}\ln\left(\frac{1+c\epsilon+\epsilon t}{1+c\epsilon}\right)}\,.

(71)

When
G(0)<0
, this solution is singular at

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=1+\frac{NG(0)}{\epsilon}\ln\left(\frac{1+c\epsilon+\epsilon t}{1+c\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq 1-N|G(0)|\left[(1-c\epsilon)t-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon t^{2}\right]

(72)

One can solve

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{N|G(0)|}+\frac{c\epsilon}{N|G(0)|}+\frac{\epsilon}{2N^{2}|G(0)|^{2}}+O(\epsilon^{2})\,.

(73)

The gap is

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\Delta}{m_{\mathrm{D}}}\right)=-\frac{1}{N|G(0)|}-\frac{c\epsilon}{N|G(0)|}-\frac{\epsilon}{2N^{2}|G(0)|^{2}}\,.

(74)

Now, there is an additional suppression factor arising from the wave function renormalization appearing in the gap, which corresponds the second and third terms in the above expression.
As we see below, in the case of QCD with a magnetic interaction, the variables scale as
|G(0)|\sim g
and
\epsilon\sim g^{2}
so that the leading term scales as
1/(N|G(0)|)\sim O(1/g)
.
The subleading corrections scale as
c\epsilon/(N|G(0)|)\sim O(g)
and
\epsilon/(2N^{2}|G(0)|^{2})\sim O(g^{0})
.
The term dependent on
c
, which appears on the second term on the right-hand side, is one order higher in
g
compared to the third term.
Therefore, second term is subleading, and can be neglected if we only keep up to the
O(g^{0})
terms in the gap calculation, which is the reliable order in the current setup.
This justifies that we only keep the
t
-dependent term in the denominator in Eq. (
64
).

III.3
Renormalization of the tree-level amplitude

Now, we illustrate how tree-level amplitude for the unscreened magnetic field gives the nonzero beta function in the RG transformation.
In the context of dense QCD, this was first pointed out by Son in Ref.
Son 1999
.
Although we do not delve into the topic, the very existence of this term, which also survives in the forward scattering limit with
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{2}=\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{3}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{4}
, is responsible for the non-Fermi liquid nature of quark matter
Schäfer and Schwenzer 2004
.

III.3.1
Statically screened electric interaction

The tree-level beta function vanishes for the interaction that is screened statically.
From the chromoelectric gluon self-energy (
34
), the tree-level amplitude in the electric sector is

\displaystyle G^{0}(q)=-\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{q_{0}^{2}-q^{2}-m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}+i0^{+}}\,.

(75)

When one limits the mode near the Fermi surface
|\epsilon_{p}|<\Lambda
, then the energy transfer can only be limited to
|q_{0}|<\Lambda
.
That means, the tree-level amplitude at a given scale
\Lambda
is

\displaystyle G^{0}(\theta;\Lambda)\simeq-\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}-2\mu^{2}(1-\cos\theta)-m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}+i0^{+}}\,.

(76)

We expand the coupling function in the partial wave channel with angular momentum
L
,

\displaystyle G^{0}_{L}(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin\theta\,P_{L}(\cos\theta)G^{0}(\theta;\Lambda)\,.

(77)

When performing the RG transformation
\Lambda\to e^{-t}\Lambda
, then the rate of change in the tree-level amplitude in the partial wave channel can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\beta^{0}_{\mathrm{tree}}=\frac{dG^{0}_{L}(e^{-t}\Lambda)}{dt}=0+O\left(\frac{e^{-2t}\Lambda^{2}}{m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}}\right)\,.

(78)

There is only a contribution at
O(e^{-2t})
, which is only an irrelevant piece in the beta function.

III.3.2
Unscreened magnetic interaction

The tree level amplitude of unscreened magnetic sector is

\displaystyle G^{i}(q)=-\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{q_{0}^{2}-q^{2}+i0^{+}}\,.

(79)

Similar to the electric case, the tree-level amplitude at a given scale
\Lambda
is

\displaystyle G^{i}(\theta;\Lambda)\simeq-\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}-2\mu^{2}(1-\cos\theta)+i0^{+}}\,.

(80)

In the partial wave channel with angular momentum
L
,

\displaystyle G^{i}_{L}(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin\theta\,P_{L}(\cos\theta)G^{i}(\theta;\Lambda)\,,

(81)

the rate of change in the tree-level amplitude when performing the RG transformation
\Lambda\to e^{-t}\Lambda
is

\displaystyle\beta^{i}_{\mathrm{tree}}=\frac{dG^{i}_{L}(e^{-t}\Lambda)}{dt}=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}+O\left(\frac{e^{-2t}\Lambda^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\,.

(82)

We have suppressed the irrelevant piece in the beta function.
This beta function is dominated by the contribution from the integral boundary
\theta\simeq 0
in Eq (
81
).
Note that the marginal piece of the tree-level beta function does not depend on the angular momentum
L
.

III.3.3
Dynamical screening from Landau damping

In reality, magnetic interaction is also screened dynamically, depending on
q_{0}
, due to the Landau damping.
Therefore, the tree-level beta function, which is the rate of change in the magnetic interaction, of the dynamically screened interaction becomes smaller than that of the unscreened interaction.
When evaluating the tree-level beta function, incorporating the correct frequency dependence of the amplitude is important.
As we saw above, for
q_{0}\ll|\boldsymbol{q}|
and to leading order in perturbation theory, the gluon self-energy in the magnetic sector
\Pi^{\mathrm{M}}
is given by Eq. (
35
), which is proportional to
\sim q_{0}
.
Therefore, this term is dominant compared to the term
q_{0}^{2}
in the denominator of
G^{i}
.
Now, the tree-level amplitude of the magnetic sector becomes

\displaystyle G^{i}(q)=-\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{q_{0}^{2}-q^{2}+i\frac{\pi}{4}m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}\frac{q_{0}}{q}+i0^{+}}\,.

(83)

This equation shows that there is a dynamical screening with a frequency-dependent cutoff
q_{c}\simeq(\pi m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}q_{0}/4)^{1/3}
.
At a given renormalization scale
\Lambda
, the tree-level amplitude is

\displaystyle G^{i}(\theta;\Lambda)\simeq\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{2\mu^{2}(1-\cos\theta)-i\frac{\pi}{4}m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}\frac{\Lambda}{\sqrt{2\mu(1-\cos\theta)}}-i0^{+}}\,,

(84)

where we only kept the dominant
\Lambda
contribution in the denominator.
Because
\Lambda\simeq m_{\mathrm{D}}
, the cutoff is at
q_{c}\simeq(\pi/4)^{1/3}m_{\mathrm{D}}
.
In the partial wave channel, the tree-level beta function in the partial wave channel
L
is

\displaystyle\beta^{i}_{\mathrm{tree}}=\frac{dG^{i}_{L}(e^{-t}\Lambda)}{dt}=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{6\mu^{2}}+O\left(\frac{e^{-2t/3}\Lambda^{2/3}}{\mu^{2/3}}\right)\,.

(85)

Again, the marginal part of the tree-level beta function does not depend on the angular momentum
L
.

III.4
Full RG equation

By combining the results obtained in this section, the full RG equation for the linear combination of the coupling functions,
G^{0}+\alpha G^{i}
, can be schematically summarized as

\displaystyle\frac{d\left(G^{0}_{L\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}+\alpha G^{i}_{L\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\right)}{dt}=Z_{\psi}(t)\delta\beta\left[G^{0}_{L\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}+\alpha G^{i}_{L\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\right]+\alpha\beta^{i}_{\mathrm{tree}}\,,

(86)

where we obtained the one-loop beta function
\delta\beta
as a functional of
G^{0}(t)
and
G^{i}(t)
in Sec.
III.1
, the self-energy correction
Z_{\psi}(t)
in Sec.
III.2
, and the tree-level beta function
\beta^{i}_{\mathrm{tree}}
in Sec.
III.3
.

For the initial helicities
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=(+,+)
, the concrete expression of the RG equations is

\displaystyle\frac{d\left(G^{0}_{L++}+3G^{i}_{L++}\right)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N\left(G^{0}_{L++}+3G^{i}_{L++}\right)^{2}}{2\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)}+\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}\,,

(87)

\displaystyle\frac{d\left(G^{0}_{L++}-G^{i}_{L++}\right)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N\left(G^{0}_{L++}-G^{i}_{L++}\right)^{2}}{6\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)}-\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{6\mu^{2}}\,,

(88)

and for the initial helicities
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=(+,-)
, the RG equations are

\displaystyle\frac{d\left(G^{0}_{L+-}+G^{i}_{L+-}\right)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N\left(G^{0}_{L+-}+G^{i}_{L+-}\right)^{2}}{3\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)}+\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{6\mu^{2}}\,,

(89)

\displaystyle\frac{d\left(G^{0}_{L+-}-3G^{i}_{L+-}\right)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}\,.

(90)

The last equation does not lead to BCS instability in the solution.
This corresponds to the fact that the spin singlet state is missing in the helicity
+-
amplitude.

IV
Color-superconducting gap from the RG equation

In this section, we solve the RG equation as an initial value problem and find the pole in the solution; we identify such a pole as the energy scale at which BCS instability kicks in.
We impose the initial condition at
t=0
, which is set by the matching condition to perturbation theory (
31
) at a soft scale
\sim m_{\mathrm{D}}
, and solve for arbitrary
t=-\ln(\Lambda/m_{\mathrm{D}})>0
.
The solution at arbitrary
t
hits the singularity at a certain
t=t_{\mathrm{BCS}}
, which is the manifestation of BCS instability in the Landau Fermi liquid description of the dense matter.
The superconducting gap is the energy scale at which the Fermi liquid description breaks down, i.e.,
\Delta\simeq m_{\mathrm{D}}e^{-t_{\mathrm{BCS}}}
.
We obtain the perturbative series for logarithm of the pairing gap up to the next-to-leading order
O(g^{0})
.
We show the expressions for each color, flavor, and angular momentum channels as classified in Table
1
.

So far, we have discussed the RG equations in Minkowski space, but the RG equations remain the same in Euclidean space because the RG equation is derived in Sec.
II
from the consistency of the partition function, which remains the same after the Wick rotation.
Hereafter, we will work in Euclidean space by analytically continuing
p_{0}\to ip_{4}
so that the calculation related with the dynamical screening becomes cleaner.

For the sake of simplicity, let us redefine the coupling function as

\displaystyle G_{L}\to V_{L}\equiv NG_{L}\,,

(91)

by absorbing the density of states
N
into the definition of the coupling function.
Then, the RG equations become

\displaystyle\frac{dV^{\mathrm{(s)}}_{L++}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(V^{\mathrm{(s)}}_{L++}\right)^{2}}{2\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)}+\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\,,

(92)

\displaystyle\frac{dV^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}\right)^{2}}{6\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)}-\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\,,

(93)

where
V^{\mathrm{(s)}}_{L++}\equiv V^{0}_{L++}+3V^{i}_{L++}
and
V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}\equiv V^{0}_{L++}-V^{i}_{L++}
are the spin singlet and triplet combination of coupling functions, respectively.
And for the initial helicities
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=(+,-)
, the RG equations are

\displaystyle\frac{dV^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L+-}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L+-}\right)^{2}}{3\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)}+\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\,,

(94)

where
V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L+-}\equiv V^{0}_{L+-}+V^{i}_{L+-}
is the spin triplet coupling function.

IV.1
Initial condition

The initial condition at
t=0
is set by the matching condition (
31
).
In this section, we discuss how to obtain the initial condition in a given partial wave
L
.

The initial condition for the electric coupling function is

\displaystyle V^{0}_{L}(t=0)=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin\theta\,P_{L}(\cos\theta)\frac{1}{1-\cos\theta+2\Omega^{2}}\,,

(95)

where we define
\Omega^{2}\equiv m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}/(4\mu^{2})=N_{f}g^{2}/(8\pi^{2})
.
For the magnetic amplitude, first one has to find the cutoff for the angular integration.
The magnetic propagator reads

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{M}}(iq_{4},q)\simeq\frac{1}{q^{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}\frac{q_{4}}{q}}\,.

(96)

Because in the effective theory near the Fermi surface, the energy transfer
q_{4}
is limited to the scale
q_{4}\lesssim\Lambda
, and the scale parameter
t
quantifies the departure from the soft scale, i.e.
t=-\ln(\Lambda/m_{\mathrm{D}})
, in the above equation
q_{4}\simeq m_{\mathrm{D}}
has to be substituted for the initial condition at
t=0
.
Then, the initial condition for the magnetic coupling function is

\displaystyle V^{i}_{L}(t=0)\simeq\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin\theta\,P_{L}(\cos\theta)\frac{1}{1-\cos\theta+\frac{\pi\Omega^{3}}{\sqrt{2(1-\cos\theta)}}}\,.

(97)

For
L=0
channel to leading order, one finds

\displaystyle V_{L=0}^{0}(t=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\ln\left(1+\Omega^{-2}\right)\,,

(98)

\displaystyle V_{L=0}^{i}(t=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\ln\left(1+\frac{4}{\pi}\Omega^{-3}\right)\,.

(99)

For
L=1
channel to leading order,

\displaystyle V_{L=1}^{0}(t=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\left[-2+\ln\left(1+\Omega^{-2}\right)\right]+O(g^{4})\,,

(100)

\displaystyle V_{L=1}^{i}(t=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\left[-3+\ln\left(1+\frac{4}{\pi}\Omega^{-3}\right)\right]+O(g^{4})\,.

(101)

The initial conditions at
t=0
for the RG equations for the spin singlet and helicity
++
channel are

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{(s)}}_{0++}(t=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\ln\left[\left(1+\Omega^{-2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\frac{4}{\pi}\Omega^{-3}\right)\right]\,,

(102)

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{(s)}}_{1++}(t=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\ln\left[e^{-4}\left(1+\Omega^{-2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\frac{4}{\pi}\Omega^{-3}\right)\right]\,,

(103)

where the subscript
0
and
1
refers to the orbital angular momentum
L=0
and
1
.
For the spin triplet and helicity
+-
channel, they are

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{0++}(t=0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\ln\left[\left(1+\Omega^{-2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left(1+\frac{4}{\pi}\Omega^{-3}\right)\right]\,,

(104)

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{1++}(t=0)

\displaystyle=V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{0++}(t=0)\,.

(105)

Note that the sign of the coefficient is negative.
This means that the amplitude is attractive for color
\boldsymbol{6}
channel with
c_{R}=1/3
.
This was first noticed in Ref.
Schäfer 2006
.
As we will see below, much stronger attraction is powered by the unscreened magnetic interaction.
Finally, for the helicity
+-
channel, there is only spin-triplet channel:

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{0+-}(t=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\ln\left[\left(1+\Omega^{-2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(1+\frac{4}{\pi}\Omega^{-3}\right)\right]\,.

(106)

When
g
is small enough, the factor
1
in the above expressions may be neglected, i.e.,
1+\Omega^{-2}\simeq\Omega^{-2}
.
Nevertheless, we will keep this factor
1
for completeness.

IV.2
General solution of the RG equation

We solve the following differential equation, which has the identical structure as in Eqs. (
92
–
94
), under the initial condition
y(t=0)=-bg^{2}l/\pi^{2}
:

\frac{dy(t)}{dt}=-\frac{a}{1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t}y^{2}(t)-\frac{bg^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\,.

(107)

We rescale
\bar{g}=g/(18\pi\sqrt{ab})
so that the equation now becomes

\frac{dy(t)}{dt}=-\frac{a}{h^{2}(t)}y^{2}(t)-\frac{c^{2}}{4a}\bar{g}^{2}\,,

(108)

where we define the constant
c=36ab
and the function
h(t)=\sqrt{1+c\bar{g}^{2}t}
.
The solution is

y(t)=-\frac{c\bar{g}h(t)\mathcal{X}}{2a\mathcal{Y}}\,,

(109)

where the numerator
\mathcal{X}
and denominator
\mathcal{Y}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle=\left[c\bar{g}lJ_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)-2J_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]Y_{1}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)-\left[c\bar{g}lY_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)-2Y_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]J_{1}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)\,,

(110)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle=\left[c\bar{g}lJ_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)-2J_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]Y_{0}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)-\left[c\bar{g}lY_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)-2Y_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]J_{0}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)\,.

(111)

The Fermi liquid description breaks down when
\mathcal{Y}=0
, and we denote such
t
as
t_{\mathrm{BCS}}
.
We expand
\mathcal{Y}
by using asymptotic expansions for the Bessel function for large argument
x\gg 1
up to
O(1/x)
:

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\bigg[\cos\left(x-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)-\frac{4\nu^{2}-1}{8x}\sin\left(x-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\bigg]\,,

(112)

\displaystyle Y_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\bigg[\sin\left(x-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)-\frac{4\nu^{2}-1}{8x}\cos\left(x-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\bigg]\,.

(113)

Substituting these into the equation
\mathcal{Y}=0
, one obtains

\displaystyle\tan\left(\frac{h(t_{\mathrm{BCS}})-1}{\bar{g}}\right)\simeq\frac{2}{(-1+cl)\bar{g}}+O(\bar{g})\,.

(114)

Details of the computation is in Appendix
A.2
.
By taking
\arctan
in the both-hand sides, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{1+c\bar{g}^{2}t_{\mathrm{BCS}}}-1}{\bar{g}}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-cl)\bar{g}\,,

(115)

where we used the relation
\arctan(1/x)=\pi/2-\arctan(x)
and expanded as
\arctan(x)\simeq x
.
The singularity, which corresponds to BCS instability, is found at

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{BCS}}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\pi}{c\bar{g}}+\frac{1}{c}\left(1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\right)-l\,,

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\sqrt{ab}g}+\frac{\pi^{2}+4}{144ab}-l\,.

(116)

The magnitude of the first term is determined by the angular momentum structure, which is captured by the factor
a
, and the strength of the magnetic interaction, captured by the factor
b
.
The second term arises from the self-energy correction;
the factor
\pi^{2}+4
is common for the solution of the gap equation
Wang and Rischke 2002
.
The third term arises from the initial condition for the solution of the RG equation.
From the relation
\ln(\Delta/m_{\mathrm{D}})\simeq-t_{\mathrm{BCS}}
, the series expansion of the logarithm of the gap is

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\Delta}{\mu}\right)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\sqrt{ab}g}-\frac{\pi^{2}+4}{144ab}+l+\ln(2\Omega)\,.

(117)

This series expansion can be reliable up to
O(g^{0})
.
Beyond this order, there are other sources of the perturbative corrections that are not currently taken into account.

V
Comparison between solutions of the RG equation and the gap equation

Let us now focus on the leading coefficient in the perturbative series of
\ln(\Delta/\mu)
, and compare these values in the gap equation and the preceding RG equation.
We also discuss the overall coefficient of
\Delta
.

V.1
Leading coefficient of
\ln(\Delta/\mu)

The gap equation in
J=0
channel is

\displaystyle\Delta(p_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\int dq_{0}\int d\theta\,\sin\theta\bigg(\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cos\theta}{1-\cos\theta+\left[\Pi^{\mathrm{E}}+(p_{0}-q_{0})^{2}\right]/(2\mu^{2})}

(123)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\frac{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cos\theta}{1-\cos\theta+\left[\Pi^{\mathrm{M}}+(p_{0}-q_{0})^{2}\right]/(2\mu^{2})}\bigg)\frac{\Delta(q_{0})}{\sqrt{q_{0}^{2}+\Delta(q_{0})^{2}}}\,.

The
J=0
channel contains
{}^{1}S_{0}
and
{}^{3}P_{0}
channels, and we will exclusively extract the
{}^{1}S_{0}
contribution below.
One-gluon exchange amplitude in Eq. (
52
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{++}(\theta)=G^{0}_{++}\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}+G^{i}_{++}\frac{3-\cos\theta}{2}\,.

(124)

By contrasting this expression with the gap equation, one can clearly see that the chromoelectric and magnetic gluon propagators in the bracket correspond to
G^{0}_{++}
and
G^{i}_{++}
, respectively.
One can further rewrite the one-gluon exchange amplitude as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{++}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(G^{0}_{++}+3G^{i}_{++}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(G^{0}_{++}-G^{i}_{++}\right)\cos\theta\,.

(125)

From Eq. (
56
), the helicity amplitude in the
{}^{1}S_{0}
channel is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{++}^{{}^{1}S_{0}}

\displaystyle=G^{0}_{0++}+3G^{i}_{0++}\,,

(126)

and from Eq. (
57
), the helicity amplitude in the
{}^{3}P_{0}
channel is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{++}^{{}^{3}P_{0}}

\displaystyle=G^{0}_{1++}-G^{i}_{1++}\,.

(127)

As is evident from the comparison between these helicity amplitudes and the one-gluon exchange amplitude (
125
), one can clearly see that the
{}^{1}S_{0}
amplitude corresponds to the
\cos\theta
-independent term in Eq. (
125
).
Therefore, to extract the
{}^{1}S_{0}
component in the gap equation, we drop the
\cos\theta
-dependent term in the gluon propagators.
The resulting gap equation is

\displaystyle\Delta(p_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\int dq_{0}\int d\theta\,\sin\theta\bigg(\frac{1/2}{1-\cos\theta+\left[\Pi^{\mathrm{E}}+(p_{0}-q_{0})^{2}\right]/(2\mu^{2})}

(128)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\frac{3/2}{1-\cos\theta+\left[\Pi^{\mathrm{M}}+(p_{0}-q_{0})^{2}\right]/(2\mu^{2})}\bigg)\frac{\Delta(q_{0})}{\sqrt{q_{0}^{2}+\Delta(q_{0})^{2}}}\,.

By substituting the expression for
\Pi^{\mathrm{M}}
and carrying out the
\theta
-integral, we find to the leading logarithm

\displaystyle\Delta(p_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\int dq_{0}\ln\left(\frac{\mu}{|p_{0}-q_{0}|}\right)\frac{\Delta(q_{0})}{\sqrt{q_{0}^{2}+\Delta(q_{0})^{2}}}\,.

(129)

One can solve this by using the method in Refs.
Son 1999
;
Schäfer and Wilczek 1999b
.
The solution of this gives the leading coefficient

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\mu}\right)\simeq-\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}{g}\,.

(130)

This agrees with the solution of our RG equation.
This coefficient is
\sqrt{2/3}
times that of the solution of the gap equation in
J=0
channel.
This is owing to the difference in the coefficient of the magnetic gluon propagator.
The
\theta
-integral in the gap equation is dominant in the forward region
\theta\simeq 0
, so the coefficient of the magnetic gluon propagator in Eq. (
123
) is
3/2-\cos\theta/2\simeq 1
while the coefficient of that in Eq. (
128
) is
3/2
.
So, in
J=0
case, the prefactor in Eq. (
129
) is
g^{2}/(18\pi^{2})
, instead of
g^{2}/(12\pi^{2})
in
{}^{1}S_{0}
case.

Let us also compare our result with Son’s RG equation
Son 1999
.
Son’s RG equation only takes into account the magnetic sector.
The electric sector also has an attraction; they are screened at a small angle, but they also contribute to the pairing through a large angle scattering.
Neglecting the self-energy correction, as it was not taken into account in Ref.
Son 1999
, the RG equation with only the magnetic sector can be found by setting
G^{0}_{L++}=0
in Eq. (
92
):

\displaystyle\frac{dG^{i}_{L++}}{dt}=-\frac{3}{2}N\left(G^{i}_{L++}\right)^{2}+\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{6\mu^{2}}\,.

(131)

On the other hand, Son’s RG equation reads

\displaystyle\frac{dG^{i}_{L++}}{dt}=-N\left(G^{i}_{L++}\right)^{2}+\frac{c_{R}g^{2}}{6\mu^{2}}\,.

(132)

Again, the factor
3/2
difference in the coefficient of the one-loop beta function resulted in the factor
\sqrt{2/3}
difference in the leading expansion term of
\ln(\Delta/\mu)
.
This additional factor
3/2
arises from the combination of the electric and magnetic coupling functions.
One cannot neglect the electric sector even though the magnetic sector is dominant.

{}^{1}S_{0}
and
{}^{3}P_{0}

, the band corresponds to the scale variation in the running coupling.

J=0

{}^{3}P_{0}

{}^{1}S_{0}

{}^{1}S_{0}

.

Such difference arises from the difference in the electric and magnetic propagator integrated from small to large angles.
The ratio of the coefficients of the electric and magnetic sector is

in the
{}^{1}S_{0}

in the

{}^{1}S_{0}

In the
{}^{1}S_{0}

\displaystyle\ln\left[2\Omega\left(1+\Omega^{-2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\frac{4}{\pi}\Omega^{-3}\right)\right]\,.

(137)

The term
(1+\Omega^{-2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
is from the electric and
(1+\frac{4}{\pi}\Omega^{-3})

To compare with the
J=0

\displaystyle\ln\left[2\Omega\left(1+\Omega^{-2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(1+\frac{4}{\pi}\Omega^{-3}\right)\right]\simeq\ln\left(\frac{8}{\pi}\Omega^{-5}\right)\,.

(138)

On the right-hand side, we neglected the factor one in the brackets.
The right-hand side of this equation corresponds to the last term in the
J=0
gap (

).
This deficit in the electric sector by a factor
\Omega^{-2}
in the

channel makes the gap smaller compared to the

case.

V.3
Overall coefficient in
\Delta

Now, by comparing the third term in Eq. (

VII
Discussions

In this section, we mention two origins that can either suppress or enhance the magnitude of the gap.
Finally, we close this section by mentioning the convergence and the possible extension to higher order.

VII.1
Complication associated with the Meissner screening

We turn to the complication in the secondary gap.
The secondary pairing gap is among quarks that is left ungapped in the primary diquark pairing.
Let us consider an example of the 2SC phase in
N_{f}=2
case.
In the 2SC phase, only quarks with two out of three colors (e.g. red and green) undergo pairing in the gauge-fixed description, and quarks with the remaining color (e.g. blue) do not.
The remaining blue diquarks are in the color
\boldsymbol{6}
representation, which has been thought to have repulsive one-gluon exchange interaction.
However, as we have discussed above, this channel has an attraction and can form the Cooper pair (see cases IV and V in Sec.
IV.3
).

In the 2SC phase, the primary diquark condensate, which can also be regarded as a Higgs condensate, can “break” the gauge redundancy as
SU(3)_{c}\to SU(2)_{c}
.
The unbroken generator of
SU(2)_{c}
mediates the interaction between the red and green quarks.
The secondary pairing among blue quarks are mediated by the gluons corresponding to the broken generators, so the interaction is screened as it is subject to the color Meissner effect.
This color Meissner effect is the source of the complication as we will explain below.

Let the gap of the primary pairing be
\Delta_{1}
.
Then, the secondary pairing takes place at the energy scale
q_{0}
below the primary pairing gap
q_{0}\ll\Delta_{1}
.
At this energy scale, the magnetic interaction between the blue quarks are described by the gluon propagator (
32
) with the self-energy
Rischke 2000
;
Rischke and Shovkovy 2002

\Pi^{\mathrm{M}}(q_{0},q)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}m_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}\frac{\Delta_{1}}{q}\,,\quad(q_{0}\ll\Delta_{1}\ll q)\,.

(144)

This indicates that the screening becomes static, i.e., it does not depend on the energy
q_{0}
of the ultra-soft scale, so the tree-level beta function does not appear in the RG equation.
The tree-level beta function (
85
) in the RG equation (
86
) should be modified as

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}^{i}_{\mathrm{tree}}(t)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{c_{R}g^{2}}{6\mu^{2}}&(0<t<t_{1})\,,\\
0&(t>t_{1})\,,\end{cases}

(145)

where
t_{1}=-\ln(\Delta_{1}/m_{\mathrm{D}})
is the scale parameter associated with the energy scale at which the primary pairing occurs.

This Meissner screening may lead to the following.
The
\beta
function associated with the renormalization of the tree-level amplitude in the RG equation now gets modified as:

\tilde{\beta}_{\mathrm{tree}}(t)=\begin{cases}\beta_{\mathrm{tree}}&0<t<t_{1}\,,\\
0&t>t_{1}\,.\end{cases}

(146)

where we defined as
t_{1}\equiv-\ln(\Delta_{1}/m_{\mathrm{D}})
.
This means that we solve this RG equation from
t=0
to
t_{1}
:

\displaystyle\frac{dV^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}\right)^{2}}{6\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)}-\frac{g^{2}}{36\pi^{2}}\,,

(147)

and then from
t=t_{1}
to
t_{\mathrm{BCS}}
, namely, up to the scale at which the solution of the following RG equation hits the singularity:

\displaystyle\frac{dV^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}\right)^{2}}{6\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)}\,.

(148)

From this solution, the secondary gap
\Delta_{2}
can be read out as

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\Delta_{2}}{\Delta_{1}}\right)\simeq-\frac{6}{|V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}(t=t_{1})|}-\frac{2g^{2}}{\pi^{2}|V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}(t=t_{1})|^{2}}\,,

(149)

where
V^{\mathrm{(t)}}_{L++}(t=t_{1})
is obtained by integrating Eq. (
147
) from
t=0
to
t_{1}
.
The concrete expression can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

As we have seen in Fig.
4
, the secondary pairing in the color
\boldsymbol{6}_{c}
is already too small to be phenomenologically relevant.
The Meissner effect may further suppress the magnitude of the gap particularly at high density.

VII.2
Instanton effect

Let us turn to another effect that can possibly amplify the magnitude of gap particularly at low density.
This is the instanton effect.
Below we sketch how this might lead to an enhancement.
The interaction among massless quarks mediated by instanton is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{instanton}}=K^{\mathrm{S}}\det_{f}(\bar{\psi}_{+}\psi_{-})-K^{\mathrm{T}}\det_{f}(\bar{\psi}_{+}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\psi_{-})\,,

(150)

where
+
and
-
refer to the chirality of quarks, which matches with helicity in the massless limit.
This interaction is flavor-singlet.
Roughly speaking, the RG equation becomes

\displaystyle\frac{d\left(\tilde{G}\pm\tilde{K}\right)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\frac{N\left(\tilde{G}\pm\tilde{K}\right)^{2}}{\left(1+\frac{g^{2}}{9\pi^{2}}t\right)}+\alpha\beta^{i}_{\mathrm{tree}}\,,

(151)

where
\tilde{G}=G^{0}+\alpha G^{i}
collectively denotes the singlet or triplet combination of the electric and magnetic coupling functions, and
\tilde{K}=K^{\mathrm{S}}-K^{\mathrm{T}}
.
The coefficient
\gamma
a factor that depends on the helicity and spin.
One of the equation gives the enhanced gap, depending on the sign of
\tilde{K}
, because the net attraction is increased by the factor
\pm\tilde{K}
.
We also note that the quark operator in the instanton interaction (
150
) is flavor singlet, so this can only contribute to the case I and V in the classification in Sec.
IV.3
.

VII.3
Convergence and road map to the higher-order corrections

Finally, let us discuss the details related to the perturbative series of
\ln(\Delta/\mu)
, namely, its convergence and the possible road map to the higher-order corrections.

By substituting the values in Eq. (
118
) for the
N_{f}=2
case, one can evaluate the actual numbers as

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\Delta}{\mu}\right)\simeq-\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{2}\left(\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\pi}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[1+0.866\left(\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+0.511\left(\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\pi}\right)+\cdots\right]\,.

(152)

The coefficient of each term is smaller than unity.
This is the lowest order correction, so one cannot determine conclusively whether this series is converging well or not, nevertheless, a radius of convergence seems to be reasonable even compared to other perturbative series in finite-density QCD, see Eq. (
142
) for example.
That said, the leading correction starts at
O(\alpha_{s}^{1/2})
, and the higher-order corrections are still at
O(\alpha_{s})
, so they are likely to be important.

In the calculation of the gap, there are two hierarchy of organizing the series expansion.
The one is in terms of the ordinary expansion in terms of the coupling constant
g
.
The other is whether the beta function is relevant in the sense of the RG transformation.
In principle, the irrelevant beta function can also modify the gap (see, e.g., Ref.
Fujimoto 2025b
).
However, if the leading-order coefficient dominates the gap, corrections from the irrelevant beta functions in the RG equation turns out to be negligible.
This is confirmed by numerically solving the RG equation contrasting two cases with and without the irrelevant beta functions.
Particularly, when there is a constant term in the RG equation arising from the tree-level renormalization, the exponentially decaying irrelevant beta function are totally negligible.

The
t
-dependent term of the RG equations (
92
,
93
,
94
) should be unchanged with the higher order corrections because there are no new beta function coming in at higher order as we already explained above.
So, the form of the solution (
117
) remains the same when going higher order in
g
, and the only there are higher-order corrections to the parameters
a
,
b
, and
L
.
So, the only higher order correction comes in through the matching condition between the effective theory and QCD.
All one has to consider is just the one-loop correction to the tree-level diagram in HDL effective theory.
The technologies has already been developed in Refs.
Braaten and Pisarski 1990
;
Bellac 2011
;
Gorda et al. 2021
.
Such computations of the higher-order correction are under way.

VIII
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we have systematically revisited the calculation of the color-superconducting gap using the renormalization group (RG) approach.
Within this framework, Son’s beta function
Son 1999
emerges naturally, and the quark self-energy corrections can be consistently understood in the context of the hard dense loop (HDL) effective theory.
We have shown that the RG method reproduces, at the same perturbative order, the gap previously obtained by other approaches, and—crucially, as discussed at the end of Sec.
V
—allows the coefficient of the gap to be fixed unambiguously.

Traditionally, one-loop gap calculations have relied on solving the gap equation
Son 1999
;
Pisarski and Rischke 1999a
;
Pisarski and Rischke 1999b
;
Pisarski and Rischke 2000a
;
Pisarski and Rischke 2000b
;
Schäfer and Wilczek 1999b
;
Hong 2000a
;
Hong 2000b
;
Hong et al. 2000
;
Wang and Rischke 2002
or summing ladder diagrams in the two-particle correlation function to extract the singularity
Brown et al. 2000a
;
Brown et al. 2000b
;
Brown et al. 2000c
.
In our view, the RG equation offers the simplest route to this computation.
Whereas perturbation theory requires explicit summation of ladder diagrams as higher-order corrections, the RG framework performs this summation automatically via the solution of a differential equation.
Perturbative corrections enter only through the matching conditions between the effective theory and QCD, which reduce to tree-level quark-quark scattering diagrams.
This separation between perturbative corrections and ladder resummation significantly streamlines the analysis, and in the present case, the RG equation admits an analytic solution [Eq. (
109
)].
While higher-order corrections are challenging in other approaches, here they reduce to evaluating corrections to tree-level diagrams within the HDL effective theory under restricted kinematics.

Higher-order perturbative corrections are not merely of academic interest:
they are essential for improving the convergence of the expansion and reducing the renormalization-scale dependence.
Since the leading perturbative correction is of order
O(g)
rather than
O(g^{2})
, its quantitative impact is substantial.
From the perspective of applications to the QCD phase diagram and the equation of state, this improvement is highly relevant.
In the phase diagram, the competition between the stress induced by finite quark masses (which tend to suppress the gap) and the gap’s compensating effect is delicate.
As discussed in the accompanying paper
Fujimoto 2025a
, reducing the uncertainty in the gap allows for a more precise determination of the weak-coupling phase boundaries, which in turn constrains the nonperturbative, small-
\mu
region relevant for neutron-star physics.
From the equation-of-state perspective, it also facilitates more meaningful cross-checks between lattice QCD at finite isospin density and weak-coupling QCD results.
Although current comparisons rely on accidental cancellations of scale dependence between the gap and bulk thermodynamic quantities, as seen in Fig.
7
, the present approach may enable more unambiguous matching in the future.

The RG equations naturally decompose into independent channels labeled by color, flavor, helicity, and angular-momentum term symbols.
While our analysis has been restricted to the massless limit, finite quark masses induce mixing between different helicity channels.
In the
u
-
d
sector, such effects are expected to be small;
however, for strange quarks with masses of order
100\,\text{MeV}
, the mixing could be significant.
The computation of these mass effects, along with related higher-order corrections, is currently in progress and will be reported elsewhere.

A.2
Solution of the RG equation in perturbation theory

Let us discuss the solution of the differential equation (
108
) and obtain the solution in perturbation theory up to the next-to-leading order in perturbation theory.
The differential equation we consider is

\frac{dy(t)}{dt}=-\frac{a}{h^{2}(t)}y^{2}(t)-\frac{c^{2}}{4a}\bar{g}^{2}\,,

(
108
)

The solution for the initial condition
y(t=0)=-bg^{2}l/\pi^{2}
is

y(t)=-\frac{c\bar{g}h(t)\mathcal{X}}{2a\mathcal{Y}}\,,

(

)

where the numerator and denominator is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle=\left[c\bar{g}lJ_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)-2J_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]Y_{1}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)-\left[c\bar{g}lY_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)-2Y_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]J_{1}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)\,,

(
110
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle=\left[c\bar{g}lJ_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)-2J_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]Y_{0}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)-\left[c\bar{g}lY_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)-2Y_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]J_{0}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)\,.

(
111
)

Now we expand these Bessel functions in terms of
\bar{g}\ll 1
, using the asymptotic formulas (
112
,
113
).
We can approximate as

\displaystyle J_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\bar{g}}}{8\sqrt{\pi}}\left[(8-\bar{g})\cos\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)+(8+\bar{g})\sin\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]\,,

(171)

\displaystyle J_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\bar{g}}}{8\sqrt{\pi}}\left[(-8+3\bar{g})\cos\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)+(8+3\bar{g})\sin\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle Y_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\sqrt{\bar{g}}}{8\sqrt{\pi}}\left[(8+\bar{g})\cos\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)+(-8+\bar{g})\sin\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]\,,

(173)

\displaystyle Y_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\sqrt{\bar{g}}}{8\sqrt{\pi}}\left[(8+3\bar{g})\cos\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)+(8-3\bar{g})\sin\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]\,.

Also, because

\displaystyle J_{0}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)

\displaystyle J_{1}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\bar{g}}}{8\sqrt{\pi}}\left[(-8+3\bar{g})\cos\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)+(8+3\bar{g})\sin\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle Y_{0}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\sqrt{\bar{g}}}{8\sqrt{\pi}}\left[(8+\bar{g})\cos\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)+(-8+\bar{g})\sin\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle Y_{1}\left(\frac{h(t)}{\bar{g}}\right)

We further expand trigonometric functions in the above equations as

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}+\frac{h(t)-1}{\bar{g}}\right)

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\cos\left(\frac{h(t)-1}{\bar{g}}\right)-\sin\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h(t)-1}{\bar{g}}\right)\,,
