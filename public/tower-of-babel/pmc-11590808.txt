Physical activity interventions implemented for older people in Sub-Saharan Africa: a scoping review
Authors: Shane Naidoo, Samuel Otoo, Niri Naidoo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundSub-Saharan Africa mirrors global patterns with an expanding elderly demographic, frequently characterised by a high incidence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), manifesting as multimorbidity. Physical activity (PA) is a validated intervention for managing the increasing prevalence of NCDs in this demographic.ObjectiveThis review aimed to elucidate the extent of PA interventions implemented in Sub-Saharan Africa for managing NCDs in older people (OP).Eligibility criteriaThis review synthesised randomised controlled trials published in English since 2010, focusing on PA interventions for managing NCDs or associated risk factors in OP in Sub-Saharan Africa.Sources of evidenceThe Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews was implemented with searches in the following databases: PubMed, EBSCOhost (Academic Search Premier AfricaWide Information, CINAHL, Health Sources Premier Academic/Nursing), Scopus and ProQuest.Charting methodsExtraction and reporting adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses-Scoping Reviews framework, ensuring rigorous and systematic synthesis.ResultsA total of 3754 studies were initially identified, with 67 studies ultimately included after applying the exclusion criteria. The synthesised trials tested primarily supervised structured PA interventions (n=30), education on PA and lifestyle (n=30) and combined structured PA with lifestyle education (n=7). A structured supervised PA regimen combining aerobic exercise at 60–80% of maximum heart rate (three 45–60 min sessions per week) and resistance training starting at 50% of one-repetition maximum (3 weekly sessions of three sets with 10–12 repetitions per muscle group), progressively increasing to 60%, was the most effective in reducing risk factors associated with NCDs in this cohort.ConclusionThis analysis confirms that a structured PA regimen paired with lifestyle education significantly mitigates NCDs in Sub-Saharan Africa’s elderly population. Furthermore, it highlights the imperative for further investigation into non-pharmacological strategies, especially those targeting hypertension, diabetes and cognitive health disorders.Keywords: GERIATRIC MEDICINE, REHABILITATION MEDICINE, Health Services for the Aged

Introduction

There is a demographic change in population dynamics with the number of older people (OP) globally predicted to reach 2.1 billion by 2050.1 The WHO reported that in 2015, OP comprised 11% of the global population, but this figure is expected to double by 2050.2 This phenomenon will see OP accounting for a large percentage of the global population and is referred to as population ageing.3 Lower fertility rates and fewer births emanating from the global socioeconomic revolution have resulted in declines in the younger population.4 Population ageing in developing countries has recently shown an upward trajectory.5 Projections for 2050 indicate regional disparities in population growth, with increases of 66% in Asia, 71% in Latin America and 64% in Africa, contrasting with 23% in Europe and 41% in North America.1 Africa is still projected to have a young population by 2050, attributed to high fertility rates and a current younger age framework (median 18.8 years).6 However, it is estimated that the population of OP in Africa will quadruple between 2015 and 2050.3

One of the driving factors for Africa’s statistics is the high mortality rate in the younger population, which is plagued by the quadruple burden of the disease (infectious diseases, child/maternal health and nutrition, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and violence/injury).7 Sub-Saharan Africa is projected to contribute to more than 50% of the global growth of OP by 2050.8 OP have a high prevalence of multimorbidity,9 the most common being NCDs such as hypertension, musculoskeletal disorders, diabetes, cardiac disorders and depression.10 The increase in the prevalence of NCD in this cohort will place added strain on fragile health systems grappling to cope with the high burden of the disease. Furthermore, Sub-Saharan Africa has been labelled as the poorest region globally, accounting for two-thirds of the world’s extremely poor populations.11 Individuals with NCDs in lower socioeconomic countries have a 1.5 higher odds of premature mortality than the similar cohort in high-income countries.12 Low- and middle-income countries account for 77% of global NCD deaths and 86% of premature NCD mortality.13 The macro effect of population ageing presents countries with challenges in healthcare management, economic security and labour shortages.14

Physical inactivity is an adaptive risk factor that has direct correlations with the prevalence of NCDs.15,17 The WHO recommended levels of physical activity (PA) (online supplemental file 1) have proven to have positive effects on cognition, reducing the incidence of falls, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), body mass index (BMI), decreasing the incidence of cardiac mortality, delaying the onset of dementia and cardiac function in OP.18 Urbanisation and socioeconomic changes have seen a change in lifestyle behaviours in Sub-Saharan Africa, contributing to low levels of PA among OP.10 19 Amosun et al20 reported that interventions implemented in first-world countries may not be appropriate in Sub-Saharan Africa due to limited resources. The authors recommended that contextual prohabilitation is necessary for Sub-Saharan Africa to address the inadequate levels of PA in OP. A preliminary search of MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence Synthesis was conducted, and no current or underway systematic reviews or scoping reviews on the topic were identified. Furthermore, there is a dearth of literature that focuses on PA interventions to address low levels of PA and the high prevalence of NCDs in this cohort, although the demographic shift calls for an urgent response.

The objective of this scoping review was to assess randomised controlled trials conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa on OP where PA was used as an intervention to address clinical outcomes associated with NCDs. The following research questions were conceptualised:

Primary research question: What PA interventions have been implemented in Sub-Saharan Africa to address PA levels in OP?

Primary research question: What PA interventions have been implemented in Sub-Saharan Africa to address PA levels in OP?

Secondary research question: How effective have these interventions been on outcome measures of PA, BMI, blood pressure (BP), blood glucose levels, cognitive function, cardiac parameters and HRQoL?

Secondary research question: How effective have these interventions been on outcome measures of PA, BMI, blood pressure (BP), blood glucose levels, cognitive function, cardiac parameters and HRQoL?

Results from this study will assist in guiding key role players and stakeholders in choosing appropriate and contextual PA interventions when designing programmes to address NCDs. NCDs, which are largely correctable, are growing rapidly in the OP prevalence. This review will also identify and comment on gaps and possible direction of future planned studies in OP.

Methodology

Protocol and registration

The scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews,21 and reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).22 Details on the prospectively published protocol can be accessed on the British Medical Journal electronic platform (http://dx.doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-071127)23 and in onlinesupplemental files 2 3.

Patient and public involvement

This study is a review of published literature and did not involve any direct patient or public participation.

Synthesis of results

The PRISMA-ScR framework was followed to ensure transparency and rigor in the reporting of the review, focusing on data collection, analysis and presentation of findings. Data were collated and grouped into categories to fit the objectives of the research questions. Interventions were classified into three categories:

Supervised structured PA: studies employed supervised, structured PA interventions exclusively.

Supervised structured PA: studies employed supervised, structured PA interventions exclusively.

Structured PA with education on lifestyle changes: studies incorporated defined PA (supervised/unsupervised), alongside educational components addressing the participants’ conditions and associated risk factors.

Education on PA and lifestyle changes: these studies educated participants on their condition and risk factors and education on the importance of PA (undefined PA).

The studies examined were randomised controlled trials; thus, outcome measures were categorised based on their statistical significance post-intervention. Outcomes that exhibited no deviation from baseline in either the control or experimental groups were designated as Level 1. Outcomes showing minimal, statistically insignificant changes from baseline after the intervention were designated as Level 2. Outcomes demonstrating statistical significance (p<0.05) within groups alone were categorised as Level 3. Outcomes displaying significant inter-group differences were classified as Level 4. Changes reported were generally of positive clinical relevance unless specified otherwise. Results

Selection of sources of evidence

The full electronic search of the relevant databases yielded 6257 studies. The duplicates (1873) were removed automatically in Endnote (Version 20.3), and 4654 studies were subsequently loaded into Rayyan.24 Manual deletion of 900 duplicate studies were done in Rayyan,24 and the abstracts of 3754 studies were examined for eligibility for this review. Studies were excluded (n=3653) on the title and abstract examination (online supplemental file 4). Full-text electronic records were sought for 101 studies (three provided by contacting relevant authors), of which six were systematic reviews. Thirty-eight studies were excluded from the full-text review (online supplemental file 5), and we extracted ten additional studies (online supplemental file 6) from the systematic reviews. There were 67 randomised controlled trials (figure 1, online supplemental file 8) included for synthesis in this review.

Characteristics of analysed studies

Majority of studies selected for synthesis were conducted in Nigeria and South Africa (figure 2). Fifty-five percent of the studies were conducted between 2016 and 2020 (figure 3). There were 13 double-blinded, 38 single-blinded and 16 open-labelled randomised controlled trials conducted predominately in 2017. The total number of participants in the included studies was 37 024 (males n=15 253; females n=21 574; unspecified n=197). Studies were conducted in primary clinics (n=20), tertiary institutions/hospitals (n=32) and community-/home-based (n=15) settings.

Results of sources of evidence

In response to our first research question, we found seven studies25,31 that combined a structured PA intervention with education on lifestyle behaviours, 30 studies32,61 that used only a supervised structured PA intervention and 30 studies62,91 that trialled an intervention of educational PA and lifestyle behaviour.

Supervised structured PA interventions

A stationary bicycle was incorporated in 13 studies using different methodologies as part of the intervention. Two of the studies used a stationary bicycle to compare the effects of interval and continuous training with a sedentary activity control group on the effects of hypertension.53 57 Three studies compared interval training on a bicycle with a sedentary control group to effect outcome measures of hypertension50 and cardiac function.32 34 Five studies used the bicycle for aerobic/continuous training to effect outcome measures in BP,36 41 46 54 56 cardiac function,36 41 46 54 56 body composition,36 41 54 cognition46 and blood sugar.54 Three studies combined resistance exercise training with stationary cycling to effect outcome measures of BP,51 54 cognition,51 cardiac function,51 54 blood sugar,54 body composition54 and HRQoL.60

A study conducted in South Africa compared the effects of backward and forward gait on motor function in Parkinson’s disease.58 Three studies used dance as an intervention to effect outcome measures in HRQoL,39 cardiac function39 40 and BP.40 43 Pilates was used in two studies to effect outcome measures of BP,47 48 body composition,47 cardiac function48 and blood sugar.48 A novelty intervention of plastic cups stacking combined with a fun aerobic element was used to effect outcome measures of motor and cognitive function.38 Community healthcare workers were incorporated into a study conducted at the participants home to effect outcome measures of HRQoL and PA.44 In this study, the researcher designed a specific programme of resistance PA and walking activities, which were reinforced by weekly visits from two community healthcare workers.44 Gbiri et al42 used a task-orientated circuit training intervention to effect outcome measures in motor function, cognition and societal participation. Olaleye et al37 compared the effect of home and facility-based rehabilitation on stroke survivors. Combinations of aerobic and resistance PA were the interventions implemented in the other studies.3345 49 52 55 59,61

Education on PA and lifestyle changes

Educational interventions emphasised lifestyle modifications, with a particular focus on mitigating risk factors linked to hypertension,6367,74 79 81 88 diabetes6264,66 75 76 82 83 85 and obesity.80 The risk factors addressed were smoking, unhealthy diet, alcohol consumption and sedentary behaviours. Participants were educated on the importance of PA (no quantity/type or duration of PA) and healthy lifestyles changes to control/manage their conditions. There were 12 studies65 68 70 72 73 76 79 80 82 86 87 91 that incorporated text messages/phone calls to reinforce the intervention and/or remind participants of their appointment dates at the healthcare facility. Six interventions were conducted in the participant’s home/community and were reinforced by community healthcare workers66 77 78 80 84 and nurses.74 There were 14 studies that were conducted at primary healthcare facilities where the key role player/s were nurses,63,6569 71 72 81 88 research team,73 82 89 91 pharmacist79 and community healthcare workers.83 Ten studies were conducted at hospitals/tertiary institutions where key role players were paramedical providers (dietician, psychologist, pharmacist),7685,87 doctors,68 75 83 nurses62 67 75 85 90 and the research team.68 70

Structured PA and education on lifestyle changes

These studies combined a defined PA with an educational component on lifestyle changes (diet, reducing alcohol consumption, smoking cessation and the importance of PA). Three studies involved supervised exercise regimens with an educational component relative to diabetic,28 hypertensive29 and musculoskeletal disorder26 participants. One of the three studies was conducted by a nurse and dietician.29 In this study, the educational component covered information related to diabetes health management, combined with a stretching regimen and walking for 30–40 min. Van Rooijen et al25 also used walking by combining an educational component on diabetes management with a home walking prescription of 10 000 steps. The participants in this study were sent text messages as reminders to adhere to the walking prescription. Two studies combined the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension with exercises.30 31 One of the two studies recommended PA for 30 min, four times a week,27 while the other study recommneded PA activities five times a week for 30 min.28 Godlwana et al27 combined an educational component on lower limb amputation-related care and exercises to test the effectiveness of a home-based rehabiltation programme in amputees.

Discussion

This scoping review identified that supervised structured PA interventions yielded the most substantial improvements in BP, cardiac function, motor function, body composition, blood glucose levels, HRQoL and cognitive function among OP in Sub-Saharan Africa. The supervised PA could be home- or facility-based as both approaches produced similar clinical outcomes.37 A systematic review that compared home- and facility-based rehabilitation, in OP with musculoskeletal disorders, concurred with our results.92 While lifestyle-focused educational PA interventions showed positive outcomes, their effectiveness was less pronounced than that of other PA interventions synthesised in this review. Notably, studies that integrated structured PA with education on lifestyle modifications achieved particularly significant positive outcomes. Our analysis also corresponds with a study by Budreviciute et al,93 who reported that effective management of NCDs should incorporate lifestyle changes with effective PA. Buttar et al94 reported that 80% of cardiovascular disease occurrence can be reduced with a combination of lifestyle changes and PA. A systematic review also concluded that lifestyle interventions combined with a multicomponent PA regimen is the effective way of decreasing the prevalence of obesity in OP.95

Aerobic PA performed three times weekly for 45–60 min at 60–80% of maximum heart rate, significantly reduced BP in our synthesised studies. Our results concur with global studies on OP, reporting that moderate-vigorous-intensity aerobic PA performed for at least 30 to 60 min per session, 3 to 5 times a week, at 60–80% of maximum heart rate, was effective in significantly lowering BP.96 97 Resistance PA that decreased BP started at 50% of one-repetition maximum, with a 5% monthly increase, completing 12–15 repetitions, and conducted four times per week. Decrease in BP could be attributed to aerobic PAs’ ability to improve endothelial function,98 increase arterial compliance,99 reduce sympathetic nervous activity,100 lower inflammation/oxidative stress101 and improve cardiac function,102 which will inadvertently have effects on BP. Combination of resistance and aerobic PA also showed decreases in BP in the reviewed studies. Resistance PA improves body composition by reducing body fat, particularly visceral fat, which is associated with lower BP due to its role in systemic inflammation and vascular resistance.103 Our findings align with a systematic review from the United Kingdom, which concluded that both aerobic and resistance PA, individually or combined, are effective in lowering BP in OP.104

Aerobic PA, when paired with resistance training, produced significantly enhanced cardiac benefits compared with aerobic PA independently. These observations were also reported in studies done in developed countries.105 106 Physiologically, resistance PA improves cardiac function in OP by increasing muscle mass and oxygen demand,107 enhancing vascular function, reducing blood pressure,108 improving high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and decreasing total cholesterol and triglycerides.106 Aerobic PAs’ ability to improve VO2 max with sustained cardiovascular demands increased capillary density and mitochondrial efficiency,109 in combination with resisted PA produces robust results in cardiac outcomes in this cohort.

Aerobic PA that decreased blood sugar outcomes was predominately moderate to vigorous in intensity (60–80% maximum heart rate) with one study combining resisted PA in the intervention. The resistance PA was performed at 50–70% of one-repetition maximum, with three sets of 12–15 repetitions targeting each major muscle group. The resistance training regime was in alignment with global studies that were effective in reducing blood sugar outcomes.110 The combination of aerobic and resistance PA achieved a greater reduction in mean HbA1c levels (from 7.38 to 6.07%) compared with aerobic PA alone (from 6.84 to 6.26%) in our reviewed studies. These findings are consistent with global studies, which also demonstrate that while both aerobic and resistance PA can lower blood sugar, their combined implementation yields superior outcomes.111,113 Aerobic PA predominantly improves cardiovascular fitness and stimulates fat oxidation, while resistance PA increases muscle mass and strength.114 The integration of both PA modalities optimises glucose uptake in muscles,115 augments insulin sensitivity and substantially enhances glycaemic control,111 resulting in superior blood sugar regulation in OP compared with either PA alone.

Our synthesised studies demonstrated that moderate-to-vigorous PA performed three times weekly for 45–60 min significantly reduced body fat percentage, BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, waist circumference and fat mass. When supplemented with resistance PA, consisting of three sets of 10 repetitions per anatomical muscle group, beginning at 50% of one-repetition maximum and progressively increasing to 60%, significant hypertrophic adaptations were observed, resulting in enhanced muscle mass. Our results align with a systematic review of resistance and aerobic PA on outcome measures of body composition.114 Resistance PA enhances motor unit activation and neuromuscular coordination, thereby amplifying strength and power.116 Resistance PA also has the ability to increase muscle mass through increased protein synthesis,117 increase muscle capillarisation and blood flow,118 reduce muscle protein breakdown and increase satellite cell activation.119 Aerobic PA mitigates the decline in muscular strength and attenuates the phenotypic alterations that occur in muscle fibres during the ageing process.120 The ability of aerobic PA to reduce body fat outcomes through heightening caloric expenditure,121 optimising fat oxidation,122 improving insulin sensitivity123 and lean muscle preservation,124 coupled with resistance PA aimed at maintaining or enhancing muscle mass, is critical in OP. This synergistic approach mitigates the impact of sarcopenia, thereby decreasing fall risk and preserving functional mobility and independence.125 126

In our synthesised studies, aerobic activity consistently improved PA levels. When combined with progressive resistance PA, initiated at intensities ranging from 60–80% of one-repetition maximum over 12 weeks, significant enhancements in physical function were observed. This combination led to superior outcomes in strength, balance and overall motor function. Zhang et al127 conducted a comparison between a resistive-only and a combined aerobic-resistive intervention over 24 weeks, with sessions held five times per week. While aerobic PA alone improved motor function, the combination of resistance training significantly enhanced outcomes, particularly in balance. In a separate 26 week randomised controlled trial, participants engaged in a regimen of aerobic PA (starting at 65% of maximum heart rate, progressing to 85%) and resistance PA (beginning at 65%, advancing to 85% of one-repetition maximum for 8–12 repetitions across two sets), performed three times per week for 60–90 min. This combined protocol notably improved lower limb strength and gait speed.128 A 24 week intervention combining resistance PA (starting at 65% of 1 repetition maximum, progressing to 85–100%, with 1–2 sets of 8–12 repetitions) and aerobic PA (45–65% of maximum heart rate), conducted three times per week, yielded significant improvements in balance, timed up-and-go performance and frailty scores.129

Progressive task-orientated PA (combination aerobic and resisted) produced favourable outcomes for cognition and Parkinson’s scores in our synthesised studies. Moderate-intensity aerobic PA enhances the brain volume, particularly in the hippocampus, increases the blood flow to the brain and promotes neurogenesis, improving memory and executive function.130 131 Resistance PA enhances the production of brain-derived neurotrophic factor, which plays a critical role in promoting neuroplasticity and improving cognitive function.132 Task-oriented PA improves cognition in OP by promoting neuroplasticity, enhancing attention and executive function, reducing cognitive decline and supporting mental well-being.133,135 Systematic reviews demonstrated that integrating PA with cognitive tasks yielded significantly better outcomes compared with PA exclusively, suggesting a synergistic effect between physical and cognitive stimulation.136 137

Interventions can be adapted to local resources by following these principles; for instance, aerobic PA could include jogging/dancing/stepping, and resistance PA could involve the use of sandbags or water-filled objects, which suggests that there is flexibility in how PA can be structured, allowing for contextual interventions that can meet the specific needs and preferences of OP while still achieving significant health benefits. In Europe and the North America, cardiovascular diseases are prevalent among OP, with 77% in North America having hypertension and 25% diagnosed with diabetes, both of which significantly elevate cardiovascular risk, directing most of PA studies in that direction.138 139 In parts of Japan, while overall cardiovascular disease rates are lower, hypertension and stroke remain prevalent (43%), due to dietary factors, with diabetes affecting 12.1% of the population.140 141 Sub-Saharan Africa, hypertension prevalence varies between 22–90%, and diabetes affects up to 16% of OP in some regions.12 142 143 Therefore, PA interventions in these areas should largely focus on managing BP and diabetes to counter the rising burden of these conditions in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Limitations

One of the limitations was that our search included relevant articles in English only and published from 2010 onwards. A further limitation was that the studies of interest were randomised controlled trials in nature and there could be valuable information/data missed in other study designs. Our search methodology was rigorous, transparent and included all the relevant databases. However, we could have omitted some valuable studies while identifying keywords to build our search strategy. The quality of evidence has not been ascertained as is the characteristics of a scoping review; hence, a positive result may not necessarily mean statistical significance at respective randomised controlled trails. An interesting observation evident in figure 3 shows that coronavirus-2019 may have had an impact on the number of studies during the pandemic. This is alluded to by Tuttle et al and could have skewed the results of this review.144

Future research/recommendations

This comprehensive review highlights crucial insights and gaps in the implementation of PA interventions to manage NCDs, in OP in Sub-Saharan Africa. However, given the overwhelming prevalence of NCDs within the rapidly ageing population of this region, there is a conspicuous paucity of studies commensurating with the magnitude of the health crisis. This review found few studies that focused on mental/cognitive directed health, yet there is 4.1 ± 0.05% prevalence of depression in Sub-Saharan Africa.145 The authors stated that depression spiralled in older adults, from Sub-Saharan Africa, with a prevalence of 7.5% in males and 5.5% in females,145 and that there was a one in three occurrence of depression in patients who had an incidence of stroke. Stigma and shame146 could also affect under reporting of mental illness; hence, research interventions directed on education and defined PA is vital in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Conclusion

This review synthesises the implementation of PA interventions among OP in Sub-Saharan Africa, elucidating significant research gaps. It highlights the critical need for PA interventions in the region, given the burgeoning population of OPs and the escalating prevalence of NCDs. The substantial health benefits of PA in managing and mitigating NCDs in OP are unequivocal and warrant urgent attention. Comprehensive evaluations and implementations of PA interventions should encompass not only individual efforts but also involve policymakers and governance structures to effectuate sustainable and effective changes. This review provides empirical evidence underscoring the necessity for future research to focus on supervised structured PA programmes integrated with lifestyle educational components to effectively address the rising prevalence of NCDs among OP in Sub-Saharan Africa.
