Radiomic imaging models for predicting breast cancer prognosis based on Interleukin-18 (IL18)
Authors: Qian Zhou, Mie Chen, Xiahui Ye, Jie Wang, Xun Lu, Wenjie Shi, Cheng Lu, Hong Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOur study was aimed to established imaging models that can predict the prognosis of breast cancer patients based on IL18, so as to facilitate the formulation of more individualized treatment plans during the treatment.MethodWe established enhancing MRI models to predict the mRNA expression of IL18 in breast cancer tissues. Meanwhile, bioinformatics analysis was integrated to studied the expression of IL18 in breast cancer with its clinical significance to explore the potential molecular mechanisms and its association with the immune microenvironment.ResultIn breast cancer patients, high levels of IL18 expression are associated with improved overall survival (OS), and natural killer cells (NK cells) significantly increased (P < 0.001) with elevated IL18. Three radiomics models, RFE-GBM, Relief-GBM, and RFE_Relief-GBM were established in our study, each model demonstrated strong predictive consistency in the prognosis of breast cancer patients.ConclusionIL18 is a protective factor in breast cancer, our radiomics models can serve as valuable non-invasive tools for utilizing mRNA expression of IL18 by enhancing MRI and show promise in predicting prognosis of breast cancer patients.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-02012-5.

Introduction

Breast cancer is a widespread malignant tumor that impacts millions of women around the world [1]. It continues to pose a public health challenge due to its significant morbidity and mortality globally [1, 2]. Although the treatment of tumors is undergoing rapid and continuous changes [3, 4], the current screening methods of breast cancer are still physical examinations and traditional imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ultrasound (US), and mammography. Prognostic assessments primarily rely on pathological evaluations following biopsies, which can occasionally result in overdiagnosis and unnecessary medical procedures. In our daily work, the accuracy of MRI, US, and mammography varies due to the diverse presentations of the disease. Individual differences among breast cancer patients coupled with the limitations inherent in existing imaging modalities, complicates the accuracy of prognosis assessment of each patient. Therefore, there is an imperative to identify more precise and objective tools to enhance the prediction of prognosis, so as to facilitate the formulation of more individualized treatment plans.

Interleukin-18 (IL18), a member of the interleukin-1 (IL1) superfamily of cytokines, is expressed by a variety of human lymphoid and non-lymphoid cells and plays a crucial role in inflammatory processes [5]. The primary function of IL18 is mediated through its ability to induce the secretion of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) from T helper 1 (Th1) cells [6–8]. When combined with IL12, IL18 has the capacity to activate cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and natural killer (NK) cells, leading to IFN-γ production and contributing to tumor immunity [9–11]. IL 18 has shown both protumor and antitumor roles in different cancers [9], the involvement of IL18 in the immune system and host defense raises the prospect of its potential implications in tumorigenesis. It is an effective measure for breast cancer patients to get cancer immunotherapy treatment using irradiated, GM-CSF-secreting or tumor antigen-expressing allogeneic cancer cell line. The autologous cancer cells used for treatment exert anti-tumor effects by expressing multiple proteins and secreting various cytokines, including IL18 [12].

Radiomics, a field that focuses on the extraction and analysis of quantitative characteristics from medical imaging [13–15], has emerged as a promising approach for predicting disease outcomes and guiding personalized treatment strategies [16, 17]. MRI is recognized as a more precise imaging modality for breast cancer compared to mammography and ultrasound [18], offering a non-invasive method for detection and quantitative assessment of tumor characteristics. Previous studies have indicated that radiomic features can effectively capture underlying biological heterogeneity [19–21], providing insights that are often overlooked in conventional imaging analyses [22], for example, Chen et al.’s radiotranscriptomics work built a DCE MRI model for cholangiocarcinoma immunophenotypes [23]. To some extent, if radiomic features derived from preoperative staging MRI can accurately predict patient outcomes, they could prove especially advantageous, given that MRI is non-invasive and can avoid additional costs.

As the expression of IL18 is difficult to obtain through conventional detection methods, we innovatively constructed these radiomics models based on the enhanced T1WI sequence of MRI to predict the mRNA expression of IL18 and achieve non-invasive assessment of patient prognosis.

Materials and methods

Extract breast cancer-related information of IL18 in TCIA and TCGA

The medical imaging and transcriptomic sequencing information, encompassing both clinical and follow-up data, was sourced from the Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) (https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) respectively. MR image acquisition parameters in the datasets are as follows: Manufacturer: GE、SIEMENS, MagneticFieldStrength: 1.5T, Pixel_Spacing: median 0.7 mm, EchoTime: median 3.6ms. Information of breast cancer and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from the TCGA-BRCA dataset in conjunction with the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/), utilizing the UCSC XENA platform(https://xenabrowser.net/)as facilitated by Toil [24]. R package “survminer” was used to determine the cutoff value of IL18, and breast cancer patients included in this study were categorized into two distinct groups based on their IL18 expression levels.

Clinical analysis of breast cancer-related information of IL18

To facilitate expression comparisons among the samples, the RNA sequencing data, initially presented in transcripts per million (TPM) format, underwent a log2 transformation. Visualization of the data was accomplished using the R package “ggplot2” (version 3.3.3).

Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves were used to illustrate variations in survival rates in different groups. Univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to assess the influencing factors of OS. Multivariate analysis serves for investigating the association between various factors and survival outcomes. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to examine the relationship between IL18 clinical features in tumors. R package “survival”, “forestplot” and “cmprsk” were used in the analysis, whereas the “survminer” and “corrplot” were used to consolidate and present the findings of the analysis visually.

Bioinformatics analysis of IL18 in breast cancer

The gene expression matrix derived from breast cancer samples was uploaded from the ImmuCellAI database [25](https://guolab.wchscu.cn/ImmuCellAI/#!/) to assess the immune cell infiltration within each sample. Wilcoxon test was used to evaluate the differences in immune cell infiltration among the various subgroups of IL18, followed by the creation of box plots to depict the results.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted to elucidate the potential functions of the identified targets. GO serves as a widely recognized resource for annotating gene functions [26], encompassing molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and cellular component (CC). KEGG [27] is a prominent database that compiles information related to genomes, biological pathways, diseases, and pharmacological agents [28, 29].The visualization included the top ten significantly enriched pathways for BP, CC, and MF, in addition to the top thirty significantly enriched pathways for KEGG. R package “clusterProfiler” was utilized for both GO (BP/CC/MF) and KEGG enrichment analyses, applying a filtering criterion of qvalueFilter < 0.05.

Evaluating the consistency, dataset partitioning of radiomics models and selecting features for each model

Our study selected the phase with the most obvious lesion enhancement in the dynamic enhanced MRI T1WI images, and professional radiologists delineated the Region of Interest (ROI) of the entire tumor area as the basis for subsequent feature extraction. To reduce the influence of different scanning devices, imaging protocols, and lesion sizes on the extraction of radiomics features, we adopted the following standardized process in the image preprocessing stage:

N4 bias field correction: used to reduce signal drift caused by magnetic field inhomogeneity and improve the consistency of image signals;

Resampling to [1,1,1] mm³ voxel size: to ensure isotropy of the image and uniform spatial resolution;

Intensity normalization (normalizeScale = 100): to reduce the difference in pixel value distribution between different devices;

Pixel discretization (binWidth = 5): to reduce noise interference and enhance the stability of feature calculation.

In the feature selection stage, we jointly adopted the maximum correlation minimum redundancy (mRMR) algorithm, the recursive feature elimination (RFE) method, the Relevant Features (Relief) method and combined the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) [30] ≥ 0.75 as a stability criterion to select features with high repeatability and representativeness for modeling analysis. In our investigation, each methodology identified the top 20 features [31, 32], followed by the computation of their intersection. R packages utilized for this analysis were “caret” and “mRMRe.”

Cases that lacked critical information, including survival data, clinical data, or exhibited a survival duration of less than 30 days, as well as those identifying as male gender, were eliminated from the study. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training and validation subsets with a ratio of 6:4. Subsequently, an analysis was performed to assess the differences between the groups in both the training and validation sets. R packages “caret” and “survminer” were used in this analysis.

Construction, evaluation, analysis of each model and compare their AUC values

Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) algorithm is used for modeling the features identified through RFE, Relief, and RFE_Relief. During the training of the GBM model, Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) was implemented in the training set for model evaluation and hyperparameter tuning. LOOCV is a strict cross-validation strategy, especially suitable for medical studies with relatively small sample sizes, which helps to minimize overfitting and improve the generalization ability and stability of the model. R package “caret” was utilized for the evaluation of the radiomics models.

The efficacy of the radiomics model was assessed in both training and validation. An elevated area under the ROC curve (AUC) indicates superior model performance. A calibration curve was plotted to assess the calibration of the radiomics predictive model, accompanied by the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The overall performance of the model was quantified using the Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) to illustrate the clinical utility of them.

These radiomics models generate the Radiomics Score (RS), which indicates the probability of predicting gene expression levels. Wilcoxon tests were conducted to analyze differences in RS among various gene expression groups. R packages “pROC”, “measures”, “ResourceSelection”, “rms”, and “rmda” were utilized in this analysis. The Delong test was used to assess the AUC values of each model when comparing the training set to validation.

Result

Baseline data of breast cancer of IL18 in TCGA and TCIA

Our survival analysis included 962 breast cancer patients from TCGA. These patients were divided into two groups based on expression of IL18, with a cut-off value of 2.05387. Their clinical information is shown in Table 1 which lists the distribution of different factors in different groups. In our study, ER-negative samples comprised 22% (216/962) of the patients and ER-positive samples comprised 78% (746/962). In the low-expression group, ER-negative samples made up 17% (78/452) and ER-positive samples made up 83% (374/452). In the high-expression group, ER-negative samples constituted 27% (138/510) while ER-positive samples constituted 73% (372/510). Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in ER status distribution between different expression groups of IL18 (P < 0.001). The distribution of PR status (P = 0.009), the occurrence of metastasis (P = 0.016), and the various pathological subtypes (P = 0.003) exhibited significant disparities between the groups. However, such differences were not observed in subgroups classified by varying age (P = 0.492), HER2_status (P = 0.291), radiotherapy (P = 0.583), chemotherapy (P = 0.083) or N stage (P = 0.058).

High level of IL18 expression is associated with improved OS in breast cancer patients

Our study showed that IL18 is a protective factor for breast cancer patients. The expression of IL18 in tumor tissue was higher than that in normal tissue. In this study, we found that in the univariate Cox regression analysis, patients with high IL18 expression had a significantly better overall survival (OS) than those with low expression (HR = 0.648, 95% CI: 0.459–0.914, P = 0.013). In the multivariate analysis, after adjusting for covariates, IL18 still remained an independent prognostic protective factor (HR = 0.659, 95% CI: 0.456–0.952, P = 0.026). The KM curve also showed that the IL18 high expression group had a better survival prognosis (P = 0.013), indicating that IL18 is a protective factor for OS and correlates with a better prognosis. The expression level of IL18 can be a potential marker for a favorable prognosis of breast cancer. (Fig. 2)

Potential molecular mechanisms of IL18 and its association with immune microenvironment

The study on immune cell infiltration showed that NK cells were significantly increased in the high IL18 expression subgroup (P < 0.001), while no significant change was observed in DC cells. GO enrichment analysis indicated that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of IL18 were significantly enriched in nucleoside triphosphatase regulator activity and GTPase regulator activity pathways. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that DEGs were significantly enriched in the tumor necrosis factor signaling pathway, the mTOR signaling pathway and the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 3).

Establish radiomics models that based on IL18 levels in breast cancer patients

A total of 104 samples were retained for analysis (refer to the inclusion and exclusion criteria). In the bioinformatics analysis, we used the R package survminer to obtain the cutoff value of IL18 expression level at 2.0539, dividing the patients into high-expression group and low-expression group. Since this study is an exploratory study, there is no prior cutoff value for the binary score of IL18, so we chose the method based on the minimum P-value, gene expression threshold was established at 2.0539, consistent with the parameters utilized in the bioinformatics analysis. The most pronounced enhancement phase of the dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI T1WI imaging lesions was segmented for the purpose of extracting radiomic features, resulting in the standardization of 107 distinct features. Among numerous radiomic features, these features facilitate understanding and application of the model while ensuring its reproducibility across different datasets. They are more commonly used in clinical practice due to their clearer interpretability and ease of use.

These original radiomics features include: 14 shape features describing the three-dimensional geometric characteristics of volume of interst (VOI), 18 first-order statistical features describing the first-order distribution of intensity, and 75 texture features. The texture features are obtained through various matrix calculations, including the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), Gray-Level Run-Length Matrix (GLRLM), Gray-Level Size Distribution Matrix (GLSZM), Neighborhood Gray-Level Dependency Matrix (GLDM), and Neighborhood Gray-Level Difference Matrix (NGTDM), which reflect the spatial patterns and high-order intensity distribution characteristics of the image. Our consistency evaluation revealed a median intraclass correlation efficient (ICC) value of 0.951 for the radiomic features, with 97 of these features having an ICC value of 0.75 or higher. These features accounted for about 90.7% of all features, and all of them were included in the subsequent feature selection process. Next, we created the training and validation datasets. The training set included 63 cases, while the validation comprised 41, maintaining similar baseline characteristics (P > 0.05 for each variable). (Table 2)

Construct model of RFE-GBM

In the RFE-GBM, we intersected top 20 features chosen by mRMR with top 20 features selected by RFE, and filtered out 5 features (original_gldm_SmallDependenceLowGrayLevelEmphasis, original_ngtdm_Coarseness, original_ngtdm_Busyness, original_ngtdm_Strength, original_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformity). This radiomics model demonstrated predictive capabilities, as shown by the ROC curve, which revealed an AUC value of 0.856 in the training and 0.752 in the validation. This model passed the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (P > 0.05), had good calibration curve performance, and the decision curve analysis (DCA) also showed good clinical net benefit within a reasonable threshold range, demonstrating the applicability of the model in real world. The predicted Radiomics score (RS) was significantly higher in the IL18 high expression group in both the training set and the validation set (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.05), further verifying the feasibility of using imaging features as good indicators for IL18 expression. (Fig. 4)

Construct model of Relief-GBM

In the Relief-GBM, we identified the top 20 features selected by the mRMR and the top 20 features by Relief, subsequently take the intersection to obtain five common features intersecting (original_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformity, original_shape_SurfaceVolumeRatio, original_shape_Sphericity, original_glszm_LargeAreaLowGrayLevelEmphasis, original_ngtdm_Busyness). This radiomics model demonstrated commendable predictive capability, attaining an AUC value of 0.865 in training and 0.771 in validation. This model also passed the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (P > 0.05), had good calibration curve performance, and the decision curve analysis (DCA) showed good clinical net benefit within a reasonable threshold range. RS in this model was significantly higher in the IL18 high expression group in both the training and the validation (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.05), verifying the feasibility of using imaging features as good indicators for IL18 expression. (Fig. 5)

Construct model of RFE_Relief-GBM

In RFE_Relief-GBM, two features (original_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformity, original_ngtdm_Busyness) were left after the intersection of the top 20 features identified by the mRMR, Relief and RFE. This radiomics model demonstrated a predictive capability, as evidenced by the ROC curve, achieved an AUC value of 0.800 in the training and 0.746 invalidation. This model passed the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test too (P > 0.05), and the DCA also showed good clinical net benefit within a reasonable threshold range. The predicted Radiomics score (RS) showed the same trend as that in RFE-GBM and Relief-GBM. (Fig. 6)

Comparison of AUC values between the models

We used Delong test to evaluate AUC values between the training and validation datasets for each model. In training sets, the p-values between RFE vs. Relief, RFE vs. RFE_Relief and Relief vs. RFE_Relief are 0.84, 0.09, 0.12 respectively; in validation groups are 0.78, 0.92, 0.61. Disscussion

At present, in the field of biological indicators, there have been many advancements. For example, CENPA was revealed as a universal biomarker and therapeutic target [33]; Liu’s study revealed that genomic alterations and miRNA-mRNA network-mediated ectopic expression of cuproptosis genes, including 7 pro-cuproptosis genes, 3 anti-cuproptosis genes and 2 key copper transporters, were involved in the activation of other cancer-related pathways [34]; Liu’s review unveiled potential avenues for future investigation of TRPM7 in cancer [35]; Rasteh’s study showed that the protein kinases in mitotic DNA integrity checkpoint signaling may play a role in cancer development and could potentially be developed as biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and prognosis [36]. Other studies have found that RAD51 is a clinical valuable biomarker for diagnosis, prognosis and therapeutic prediction [37]; disulfidptosis-related gene sets are potentially involved in many cancer types and can be developed as candidates for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic biomarkers [38]. All these studies have revealed the broad prospects of cross-tumor biomarkers.

IL18 can severs as such a potential biomarker too. It is a single peptide chain cytokine expressed as an inactive precursor by nearly all healthy human cells, including blood macrophages, keratinocytes, osteoblasts, and endothelial cells [39]. Recent investigations have focused on the biological, genetic, and pathological roles of IL18 in various diseases [40, 41]. Numerous studies and clinical trials have been conducted to explore not only the antitumor properties of IL18 [42–44] but also its potential and efficacy for therapeutic applications.

In our study, in the initial feature extraction stage, we used the pyradiomics package to extract 107 radiomics features which is more commonly used in clinical practice and provides a clearer explanation of the specific features [45–47]. From the enhanced T1WI images, and through ICC analysis, we selected 97 features with relatively high consistency (ICC ≥ 0.75) as candidate features. Subsequently, we employed the mRMR algorithm to initially rank these features. This method aims for “maximum correlation + minimum redundancy”, comprehensively evaluating the correlation between features and the prediction target as well as the redundancy among features, with the evaluation metric being mutual information. This method can more comprehensively consider the importance of features and multicollinearity.

Then, we used RFE and Relief for further screening. The final model should retain the most important features and discard those without practical significance. Limiting the number of features to the top 20 is based on a common principle in radiomics research: after dimensionality reduction, a limited and stable subset of features is retained to reduce redundancy and avoid overfitting risks [31, 32]. This method builds models and gradually eliminates the least relevant features, based on their importance, through iterative rounds. This process continues until the optimal feature subset, defined by the best model performance, is selected [48]. Furthermore, all models passed the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with P > 0.05, indicating an adequate fit. They also demonstrated good performance in the calibration curve. Additionally, the DCA showed a significant clinical net benefit within a reasonable threshold range, further supporting the applicability of the model in real scenarios.

The two radiomics features obtained in the final intersection screening, original_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformity and original_ngtdm_Busyness, both belong to texture-based features and can reflect the heterogeneity and complexity of tumor tissues. GrayLevelNonUniformity originates from the gray level size region matrix (GLSZM) and is used to measure the uniformity of the distribution of different gray value regions in the image, indicating a more complex internal structure of the tissue; Busyness originates from the neighborhood gray difference matrix (NGTDM) and is used to measure the speed of gray level changes in the image, indicating a more complex microstructure of the tissue [49, 50]. In radiomics studies of tumors, texture features are often considered to reflect the histological heterogeneity of tumors to a certain extent [51]. Previous studies have shown that IL18 plays a significant role in the tumor immune microenvironment and inflammation regulation which is closely related to the heterogeneity of tumor cells and their microenvironment [52, 53]. As a result, we speculate that the expression level of IL-18 may have a connection with the heterogeneity features reflected by the radiomics-texture features of tumors. This provides a reasonable biological basis for applying radiomics to explain the immune-inflammatory phenotypes of tumors.

There are some limitations of our study. The models use only enhanced T1WI, if combining multiple MRI sequences (such as T2WI, DWI/ADC, etc.) they may provide complementary information that could further improve predictive performance. However, multi-sequence fusion often has high redundancy in data, and combining multiple sequences significantly increases the complexity of data acquisition, computational costs, and the challenges of image standardization and computational processing, which may affect the feasibility of clinical implementation. In our study, we designed the models based on the considerations of simplicity and clinical practicability, choosing to analyze only the enhanced T1WI sequence. In the following research, we can further explore multi-sequence fusion methods to seek the best balance between model performance and clinical applicability.

There are other limitations in our study. While our radiomics predictive models demonstrated promising performance in predicting breast cancer prognosis based on IL18 expression, the study was conducted on a relatively homogeneous patient cohort, which may not fully represent the diverse biological factors, such as tumor heterogeneity and clinical characteristics, including varied treatment responses, of the broader breast cancer population. Moreover, the reliance on MRI data introduces potential biases related to imaging quality and interpretation, which may affect the reproducibility of our models in different clinical settings. Furthermore, the models require validation on independent datasets to confirm their predictive accuracy and clinical utility. Future research should address these limitations by incorporating larger, more diverse cohorts and exploring the integration of multimodal data to enhance the accuracy, sensitivity, and overall predictive power of our models.

Conclusion

IL18 is a protective factor in breast cancer. Our radiomics models can serve as valuable non-invasive tools by integrating mRNA expression data of IL18 with MRI imaging. These models show promise in predicting the prognosis of breast cancer patients.
