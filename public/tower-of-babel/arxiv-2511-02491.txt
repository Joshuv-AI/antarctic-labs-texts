Abstract
. In syntax-guided synthesis, one of the challenges is to reduce the enormous size of the search space. We observe that most search spaces are not just flat sets of programs, but can be endowed with a structure that we call an oriented metric. Oriented metrics measure the distance between programs, like ordinary metrics do, but are designed for settings in which operations have an orientation. Our focus is on the string and the bitvector domains, where operations like concatenation and bitwise conjunction transform an input into an output in a way that is not symmetric. We develop several new oriented metrics for these domains. Oriented metrics are designed for search space reduction, and we present four techniques: (i) pruning the search space to a ball around the ground truth, (ii) factorizing the search space by an equivalence that is induced by the oriented metric, (iii) abstracting the oriented metric (and hence the equivalence) and refining it, and (iv) improving the enumeration order by learning from abstract information. We acknowledge that these techniques are inspired by developments in the literature. By understanding their roots in oriented metrics, we can substantially increase their applicability and efficiency. We have integrated these techniques into a new synthesis algorithm and implemented the algorithm in a new solver. Notably, our solver is generic in the oriented metric over which it computes. We conducted experiments in the string and the bitvector domains, and consistently improve the performance over the state-of-the-art by more than an order of magnitude.



1.
Introduction

The goal of inductive program synthesis is to synthesize a program from input-output examples that are given as the specification.
A prime example for inductive synthesis is the FlashFill
(
Gulwani 2011
;
Polozov and Gulwani 2015
;
Cambronero et al. 2023
)
feature in Microsoft Excel enabling millions of end-users to automate data manipulation tasks.
Other applications include superoptimization
(
Phothilimthana et al. 2016
)
,
program deobfuscation
(
Blazytko et al. 2017
;
David et al. 2020
)
,
and
synthesizing database queries
(
Liu et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2017a
)
.
In this context, the Syntax-Guided Synthesis (SyGuS) paradigm
(
Alur et al. 2015
)
received considerable attention.
Given a grammar
\mathcal{G}
and a specification
\Phi
,
the goal is to find a program
\texttt{p}\in L(\mathcal{G})
that satisfies
\Phi
.
Numerous tools
(
Reynolds et al. 2015
;
Alur et al. 2015
;
Alur et al. 2017
;
Lee et al. 2018
;
Barke et al. 2020
;
Lee 2021
;
Yoon et al. 2023
;
Ding and Qiu 2024
;
Ding and Qiu 2025
;
Hu et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2019
)
have been developed to tackle SyGuS problems.

Most successful SyGuS solvers implement a bottom-up enumeration, which constructs larger programs from smaller ones until a solution has been found.
While conceptually similar, the algorithms differ drastically when it comes to two parameters:
the search space of programs that are considered as possible solutions, and the enumeration order in which the search space is explored.
Most works aim to improve the enumeration order so as to find a solution quickly.
Learning
(
Barke et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2018
)
tries to understand which subprograms are likely to play a role in the solution, and therefore should be enumerated early on.
Deduction
(
Alur et al. 2017
;
Ding and Qiu 2025
;
Yoon et al. 2023
;
Lee 2021
;
Ding and Qiu 2024
)
tries to guide the bottom-up enumeration by information about the target values that has been computed top-down from program sketches.
Strategies to reduce the enormous search space have received less attention.
The standard technique is to factorize the search space along observational equivalence
(
Alur et al. 2015
;
Albarghouthi et al. 2013
;
Udupa et al. 2013
)
:
if two programs have the same outputs on the given inputs, it suffices to keep one of them.
The comparison has been weakened to an abstraction of the output values, and a refinement loop has been introduced to recover from imprecision and remain complete
(
Wang et al. 2018b
)
.

A drastically new approach to reduce the search space starts from the following consideration
(
Feser et al. 2023
)
.
Observational equivalence is a discrete judgment: programs may or may not be equivalent.
Metrics generalize this to a continuous notion of distance
(
Steen and Seebach 1978
)
: programs may be closer to each other or further apart.
Having a metric on the search space suggests a reduction strategy that we refer to as pruning: only consider programs that are close enough to the ground truth.
These programs form a ball around the ground truth whose radius is the threshold on the distance.
Pruning deliberately gives up completeness and trades it for performance.
The balance between completeness and performance, however, is under the control of the user: it is the radius of the ball.
Pruning can be combined with the aforementioned factorization techniques.

While metric search spaces and pruning are attractive conceptually, their applicability has been limited so far.
The problem is that metrics require symmetry,
\textit{m}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})=\textit{m}(\mathit{o},\mathit{i})
.
The operations on most data domains, however, are oriented.
Consider strings and concatenation.
If the string
\mathit{i}
is a substring of
\mathit{o}
, then
\mathit{i}
may help us produce
\mathit{o}
by concatenation.
If
\mathit{i}
is a superstring, then there is no chance to produce
\mathit{o}
by concatenation.
The situation is similar with bitvectors and the operations of bitwise conjunction, disjunction, and multiplication.
In short, the symmetric metrics cannot measure in a meaningful way the impact of operations that are oriented.

Quasimetrics
(
Steen and Seebach 1978
)
have been proposed as a generalization of metrics that does not require symmetry.
With quasimetrics, we can assign a meaningful distance to strings that are manipulated by concatenation:
if
\mathit{i}
is a substring of
\mathit{o}
, we take as distance the difference in length
|\mathit{o}|-|\mathit{i}|
; if
\mathit{i}
is a superstring, we take infinity.
Similar quasimetrics can be defined for the operations in the bitvector domain.
Unfortunately, quasimetrics do not work with example-based specifications.
When two programs have the same outputs on the given inputs,
their distance should be zero under the quasimetric (lifted to the space of input-output functions).
The definition of quasimetrics, however, requires equality for objects with distance zero.
This will not hold: the fact that programs agree on a number of inputs does not mean they agree on all inputs.

Contribution

We define a new notion of
oriented metrics (orimetrics)
that targets data domains whose operations are not symmetric.
Orimetrics only require reflexivity in the form of
\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{a})=0
.
We do not require that
\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})=0
implies
\mathit{a}=\mathit{b}
as in quasimetrics.
Orimetrics only require symmetry at zero,
\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})=0
implies
\textit{m}(\mathit{b},\mathit{a})=0
.
We do not require
\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})=\textit{m}(\mathit{b},\mathit{a})
as in metrics and pseudometrics.
We still have the triangle inequality,
\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})\leq\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{c})+\textit{m}(\mathit{c},\mathit{b})
.

We present new orimetrics for the
string
, the
bitvector
, and the
function domain
.
We give a principled way to derive these orimetrics that should carry over to other domains as well.
The idea is to let the orimetric measure the complexity of the inverse semantics of the operator
(
Polozov and Gulwani 2015
;
Lee 2021
)
.

Orimetrics are designed for pruning and factorization in bottom-up enumerative synthesis.
For
pruning
, we still limit the search to a ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathit{r}}(\mathit{gt})=\{\texttt{p}\mid\textit{m}(\texttt{p},\mathit{gt})<\mathit{r}\}
, as pioneered in
(
Feser et al. 2023
)
.
The difference, however, is that the direction in which we measure the distance matters.
For
factorization
, an important insight is that
every orimetric induces an equivalence relation
where
\mathit{a}\equiv_{\textit{m}}\mathit{b}
if
\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})=0
.
This generalizes all factorization strategies discussed above.
Depending on the orimetric, a distance of zero may mean the programs produce the same outputs on the inputs from all examples (observational equivalence), produce the same abstract outputs on the inputs from all examples (the equivalence used by Blaze
(
Wang et al. 2018b
)
), or produce similar outputs on some inputs.

Factorization makes it attractive to work with an orimetric that is rough in that it equates many programs.
At the same time, the orimetric should faithfully represent the distance to the ground truth.
To reconcile these desiderata, we work with
approximate orimetrics

\mathit{m}^{\sharp}
and introduce a refinement scheme.
Approximate here means that
\mathit{m}^{\sharp}(\texttt{p},\mathit{gt})=0
may hold although
\texttt{p}\neq\mathit{gt}
.
The reflexivity requirement for orimetrics plays a surprisingly important role for the refinement.
We can refine
\mathit{m}^{\sharp}
to any
{\mathit{m}_{\texttt{p}}}^{\sharp}
with
{\mathit{m}_{\texttt{p}}}^{\sharp}(\texttt{p},\mathit{gt})\neq 0
, and reflexivity will guarantee that the program will not be considered a candidate solution again.
In short, we obtain a stronger factorization at the expense of potentially finding spurious programs and having to refine.

What turned out surprisingly challenging is to harmonize the factorization with the bottom-up enumeration.
To see this, consider the set
\{\texttt{x},\texttt{y},\texttt{op}(\texttt{y}),\texttt{op}(\texttt{x})\}
on which we have the equivalence
\texttt{x}\equiv\texttt{y}
and
\texttt{op}(\texttt{x})\equiv\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})
.
Assume the bottom-up enumeration constructs the programs in the order given by the set, and the factorization maintains the first element in each equivalence class as a representative.
Then we obtain
\{\texttt{x},\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\}
, which is not bottom-up enumerable.
We give natural and easy to satisfy conditions under which the factorized set remains bottom-up enumerable.

We implemented our approach in a tool called Merlin
1

https://github.com/J4K0B/Merlin
and evaluated its performance against state-of-the-art SyGuS tools and domain-specific solvers.
In the bitvector domain, Merlin is 27 times faster than DryadSynth
(
Ding and Qiu 2024
)
, the current best SyGuS solver on the bitvector domain.
On Blaze’s
(
Wang et al. 2018b
)
string benchmarks, it is 75 times faster than Blaze.
Overall, Merlin is 42 times faster than a baseline implementation without our proposed techniques.
We give a careful evaluation of the impact of pruning and refinement.

In total, we make three contributions:

(1)

We define orimetrics for bottom-up enumerative synthesis.
Orimetrics allow us to prune, factorize, and refine the search space (
Sections

,
3
and
4
).

We define orimetrics for the string, the bitvector, and the function domain.

2.
Overview

A SyGuS problem takes as input a specification in the form of a function
\mathit{gt}
and a grammar for programs
\mathcal{G}
.
The task is to find a program
\texttt{p}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
that implements the function,
\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket=\mathit{gt}
.
We call
\mathit{gt}
the ground truth and
p
a solution to the synthesis task.
For simplicity, we assume
\mathit{gt}
is given as a finite set of input-output examples, but remark that our techniques carry over to more elaborate settings.
There are various strategies of how to solve a SyGuS problem.
The most successful solvers implement a form of bottom-up enumeration
(
Wang et al. 2018b
;
Alur et al. 2015
;
Alur et al. 2017
;
Lee 2021
;
Yoon et al. 2023
;
Ding and Qiu 2024
;
Ding and Qiu 2025
)
, where they try to find a solution by composing subprograms that have already been constructed.
While every new solver contributes a new technique that makes it faster than the state-of-the-art, there are two parameters that play a role in all solvers.
The
search space
\mathit{P}
contains the programs that are considered relevant to solve the synthesis task (they may form solutions or occur as subprograms in solutions).
The
enumeration order
\preceq
defines the order in which the search space should be explored.

Our first insight is that all search spaces of practical interest are not unstructured sets, but can be endowed with an

oriented metric

(orimetric)
m
that gives information about the distance between programs.
The purpose is to deal with data domains whose operations have an orientation.
Consider a grammar that supports
concat
, the concatenation of strings.
If
\mathit{i}
is a substring of
\mathit{o}
, it is easy to find a string
\mathit{i}^{\prime}
so that
\texttt{concat}(\mathit{i},\mathit{i}^{\prime})=\mathit{o}
.
This means
\textit{m}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})
should be small.
With
\mathit{o}
being a superstring of
\mathit{i}
, however, it is impossible to find a string
\mathit{o}^{\prime}
so that
\texttt{concat}(\mathit{o},\mathit{o}^{\prime})=\mathit{i}
.
The distance
\textit{m}(\mathit{o},\mathit{i})
should be infinity.
These considerations prompted us to drop the symmetry requirement in metrics.
The resulting object is an orimetric.

We present four techniques that capitalize on the information given by the orimetric to improve the efficiency of bottom-up enumerative synthesis.
A remarkable aspect is that our techniques are generic: they only refer to the orimetric and the enumeration order, but do not make assumptions on how these are defined.
This makes it possible to use the four techniques as enhancements in virtually any bottom-up enumerative solver.

Our first technique is called
pruning
.
Pruning limits the search to a ball around the ground truth, meaning it tries to build a solution solely from the programs contained in this ball.
Technically, the ball is the set of programs whose distance to the ground truth is smaller than a threshold
\mathit{r}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{(\mathit{P},\textit{m}),\mathit{r}}(\mathit{gt})\quad=\quad\{\ \texttt{p}\in\mathit{P}\ \mid\ \textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\mathit{gt})<\mathit{r}\ \}\ .

We refer to
\mathit{r}
as the radius of the ball, and just write
\mathcal{B}_{\mathit{r}}(\mathit{gt})
when the orimetric (search) space is understood.
The orimetric is required to have the mathematical properties described in the introduction (which will be made formal in
Section

4
).
What is surprisingly important in the context of SyGuS is reflexivity of the orimetric:

(reflexivity)

\displaystyle\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket=\mathit{gt}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\mathit{gt})=0\ .

When read in contraposition, reflexivity says that only programs at distance zero to the ground truth can solve the synthesis task.
This, however, does not mean we can just work with a ball of radius almost zero.
The purpose of the ball is to constrain the subprograms that can be used to build candidate solutions.
Reflexivity then applies to the candidate programs, but not to the subprograms.

The second insight is that the use of an orimetric not only allows us to limit the search to a ball, it also suggests identifying and removing duplicate elements from this ball.
We call this
factorization
.
Indeed, from the perspective of the orimetric, two programs are equivalent whenever they have a distance of zero.
The orimetric thus induces the equivalence

\displaystyle\texttt{p}_{1}\equiv_{\textit{m}}\texttt{p}_{2}\quad\text{if}\quad\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}_{1}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{p}_{2}\rrbracket)=0\ .

We remove duplicates by factorizing the ball along this equivalence, that is, searching
\mathcal{B}_{\mathit{r}}(\mathit{gt})/_{\equiv_{\textit{m}}}
.
In our implementation, we remove duplicates by considering representatives of equivalence classes.
Concretely, we use as representatives the minimal elements wrt. the enumeration order
\preceq
.
The reader will observe the similarity between factorization and observational equivalence
(
Udupa et al. 2013
;
Albarghouthi et al. 2013
)
.
There, programs are considered equivalent when they return the same outputs on the inputs from all examples, in which case one program will be discarded.
This significantly reduces the search space while leaving the search complete.
We emphasize that orimetrics are only able to capture factorization because they do not require objects to be equal when they have a distance of zero.

As discussed in the introduction, it would be attractive to have a coarse equivalence that equates many programs.
Our third technique is to work with
approximate orimetrics

\mathit{m}^{\sharp}
.
We say that an orimetric is
precise
, if the converse of reflexivity holds:

\mathit{m}^{\sharp}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\mathit{gt})=0
implies
\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket=\mathit{gt}
.
Otherwise, the orimetric is called
approximate
.
In approximate orimetrics, we may have
\mathit{m}^{\sharp}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\mathit{gt})=0
although
\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket\neq\mathit{gt}
.
We thus have to explicitly check whether a program solves the synthesis task.
If this is not the case — we call the program spurious — we have a
refinement
function that constructs from
\mathit{m}^{\sharp}
,
p
, and
\mathit{gt}
a new orimetric
{\mathit{m}_{\texttt{p}}}^{\sharp}
.
The idea is inspired by and can be combined with the abstraction-refinement approach from
(
Wang et al. 2018b
)
.
That work uses predicate abstraction on the output to equate programs.

Our fourth technique modifies the enumeration order on-the-fly by
learning
from spurious programs.
When we find a spurious program
p
, we know that
p
was promising from the perspective of the previous orimetric.
While it may not be a solution,
p
is likely to contain valuable subprograms.
We therefore update the enumeration order to list these subprograms early on.
To be precise, we only list them if they still reside in the ball that is formed with a precise orimetric
m
.
It is useful to have a precise orimetric at this point to eliminate as many programs as possible.
The approach is inspired by
(
Barke et al. 2020
)
, where operators are preferred that occur frequently in solutions to single examples.

Another approach to modify the enumeration order are deductive methods
(
Lee 2021
;
Yoon et al. 2023
;
Ding and Qiu 2024
;
Ding and Qiu 2025
;
Alur et al. 2017
)
.
We discuss them next, to make clear that they are orthogonal to the idea of using orimetrics and that the approaches can be combined.
One deductive method is case-splitting
(
Alur et al. 2017
)
.
When we enumerated a program for each of the given examples, we can build a decision tree to synthesize an if-then-else program that solves all examples.
Another deductive method

(
Lee 2021
;
Yoon et al. 2023
;
Ding and Qiu 2024
;
Ding and Qiu 2025
)

uses the inverse semantics of an operator.
Consider the program sketch
\texttt{concat}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
and the output "
POPL
".
Assume we already enumerated a program
\texttt{p}_{1}
that outputs "
PO
".
Given "
POPL
" and "
PO
", the inverse semantics is
\texttt{concat}^{-1}("\texttt{POPL}","\texttt{PO}")=\{"\texttt{PL}"\}
.
We check whether we already enumerated another program
\texttt{p}_{2}
that outputs "
PL
".
If so, we can complete the sketch to
\texttt{concat}(\texttt{p}_{1},\texttt{p}_{2})
and directly solve the synthesis problem.
So instead of having to enumerate all programs up to the size of
\texttt{concat}(\texttt{p}_{1},\texttt{p}_{2})
, we only need to enumerate programs until we find
\texttt{p}_{1}
and
\texttt{p}_{2}
.

We can leverage our understanding of an operator’s inverse semantics to construct orimetrics.
The idea is that the distance between an input and an output value should be inverse proportional to what may be considered the complexity of the inverse semantics.
Continuing on the above example, if
\mathit{i}
is a superstring of "
POPL
", the inverse semantics is
\texttt{concat}^{-1}("\texttt{POPL}",\mathit{i})=\emptyset
and hence the distance
\textit{m}(\mathit{i},"\texttt{POPL}")
should be infinity.
As a less extreme case, a prefix "
POP
" would leave us with
\texttt{concat}^{-1}("\texttt{POPL}","\texttt{POP}")=\{"\texttt{L}"\}
, which is likely easier to generate than
"\texttt{PL}"
,
and therefore
\textit{m}("\texttt{POP}","\texttt{POPL}")<\textit{m}("\texttt{PO}","\texttt{POPL}")
should hold.

Remarks

The above description is conceptual in that it separates the functions more than we do in our implementation.
The workhorse of our implementation is the search for a candidate solution.
Pruning and factorization run interleaved with it.
Concretely,
Search
constructs the programs one by one: given a list of programs that have already been constructed, it is able to determine the program
p
that should be constructed next according to the enumeration order
\preceq
.
If
p
does not belong to the ball of interest,
\mathit{m}^{\sharp}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\mathit{gt})\geq\mathit{r}
, it is discarded.
The same holds if we already have another representative
q
in the list, meaning

\mathit{m}^{\sharp}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket)=0
.
We already know
\texttt{q}\preceq\texttt{p}
, and thus
q
should be the representative.
If the program passes these tests, we append it to the list.

Why do we keep the enumeration order?
An alternative would be to just have an orimetric and imitate the enumeration order by going through the programs in the order of their distance to the ground truth.
First, we believe the enumeration order is such an integral part of the solving process that it deserves being a parameter on its own.
Second, our metrics are very coarse: they are made to define the ball but do not distinguish much between the programs inside the ball.
This will become clear in the next section where we illustrate our approach on an example.
Third, the orimetrics are not as flexible as the enumeration order.
An orimetric has to satisfy a few mathematical properties, whereas the enumeration order just has to be a total order on the search space.
This flexibility makes it easier to adapt the enumeration order based on learned information.

One may also ask whether the concept of orimetrics is needed after all, or whether we could have based our algorithmic improvements on more elementary mathematical notions.
One could try to prune the search space with a quasimetric
and factorize the search space with an equivalence.
Quasimetrics have the problem that a distance of zero should imply equality of the elements, which does not hold in example-based settings.
The elements are, however, observationally equivalent.
The discussion suggests we could endow the search space
\mathit{P}
with an equivalence
\equiv\ \subseteq\mathit{P}\times\mathit{P}
that should be used for factorization and with a quasimetric on the now factorized space
\mathop{\mathit{q}}\subseteq\mathit{P}/_{\equiv}{}\times{}\mathit{P}/_{\equiv}
that could be used for pruning.
To our surprise, this alternative definition
(\mathit{P},\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}})
is equivalent to our notion of orimetric search spaces
(\mathit{P},\textit{m})
: a combination of an equivalence and a quasimetric is enough to induce an orimetric, and vice versa.
At the same time, the alternative definition has disadvantages that orimetrics overcome:
(i) working with equivalence classes is cumbersome, we believe the symmetry at zero requirement for orimetrics is simpler,
(ii) one has to understand refinement for two objects, the equivalence and the quasimetric, and maintain both objects during computation,
(iii) there is no guidance on how to obtain the equivalence, while it is a derived concept for orimetrics.
All this indicates that the notion of orimetric search spaces is somewhat fundamental to bottom-up enumerative synthesis.

Using orimetrics, we can further reduce the search space.
The first step is to define an orimetric on programs.
The orimetric is chosen based on the data and data manipulations that should be supported.
For strings with replacement, it is beneficial to reward programs that produce superstrings of the outputs given in the examples and punish programs that do not.
We explain in a moment how this plays together with the fact that concatenation prefers substrings.
We begin by defining an auxiliary quasimetric
\widetilde{\textit{m}}
on strings:

\widetilde{\textit{m}}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})\ =\ \begin{cases}\mathit{len}(\mathit{i})-\mathit{len}(\mathit{o})&\ \text{if $\mathit{i}$ is a superstring of $\mathit{o}$}\\
100+\lvert\mathit{len}(\mathit{i})-\mathit{len}(\mathit{o})\rvert&\text{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

For example,
\widetilde{\textit{m}}("\texttt{PO}","\texttt{POPL}")=102
but
\widetilde{\textit{m}}("\texttt{POPL}","\texttt{PO}")=2
.
Note that
\widetilde{\textit{m}}
is not symmetric in general, but it is symmetric at distance zero.
We now lift the auxiliary quasimetric
\widetilde{\textit{m}}
on strings to an orimetric
m
on functions over strings.
The definition is as expected, we evaluate the given functions on the inputs from all examples and sum up the distances of the output values.
To make this formal, let
\mathit{J}\subseteq\mathit{I}
and define

\textit{m}_{\mathit{J}}(f,g)\ =\ \sum_{\mathit{i}\in\mathit{J}}\widetilde{\textit{m}}(f(\mathit{i}),g(\mathit{i}))\ .

Our orimetric is then
\textit{m}=\textit{m}_{\mathit{I}}
.
We will use other instantiations of
\mathit{J}
in a moment.
Note, that the lifting does not result in a quasimetric.
If two different programs
p
and
q
produce the same outputs on the given inputs, their distance is
0
.
A quasimetric would require
p
and
q
to be equal.
This need not be the case.
In fact, allowing
p
and
q
to have a distance of zero while being different is what enables factorization in the first place.

With the orimetric at hand, we restrict the search to programs in the ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathit{r}}(\mathit{gt})

\mathit{r}

100

This means we only keep programs that return a superstring of the output for every input example.
We admit that this is discrete, and does not make use of the fact that an orimetric yields continuous values in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

In our experiments, we will see more elaborate instantiations.

Note that focusing on superstrings does not mean we are not allowed to use the
concat

We can still use
concat

The superstring ball is even closed under concatenation: concatenating superstrings yields a superstring.
One may ask how this relates to the above argument that
concat
prefers substrings.
This argument was made for a comparison with the final value.
Since we use superstrings here, it means the final value cannot be produced by concatenation, and so

will not be the topmost operator in the solution to the synthesis task.
It can, however, still be a part of it.

Selecting an Orimetric

Coming up with a good orimetric is key to the success of our method.
In our example, had we defined an orimetric that rewards functions producing substrings of the output and punish those that produce superstrings,
we would have failed to generate the solution using bottom-up enumeration.

To construct good orimetrics, we derive them from the semantics of the operators in the grammar.
We already discussed how concatenation needs substrings to produce the desired output while replacement favors superstrings.
In practice, we
concurrently run a portfolio of solvers employing a different orimetric each.
We developed three orimetrics for the string domain and four orimetrics for the bitvector domain.
In
Sections

5.3
and
5.4
, we introduce a principled way of designing orimetrics.

Deduction

An aspect the above example did not highlight is the use of deduction.
Conceptually, deduction accelerates the search by modifying the enumeration order, as depicted in
Figure

4
.
Our tool Merlin implements the deduction technique from DryadSynth
(
Ding and Qiu 2024
)
.
To explain it, consider the sketch
\texttt{r}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
. After we found the programs

"␣
City
"
,
\epsilon
, and
\texttt{r}(\texttt{x},\text{"\textvisiblespace{Conference}"},\epsilon)
, we would enumerate

\texttt{r}(\texttt{r}(\texttt{x},\text{"\textvisiblespace{Conference}"},\epsilon),\text{"\textvisiblespace{City}"},\epsilon)
next,
which solves the synthesis problem.
Deduction thus understands a program sketch.
This relies on the inverse semantics of a sketch.
If one finds arguments such that the sketch filled with these arguments is a solution to the synthesis problem, the solution will be enumerated next.
This means, for any supported sketch, we only need to find the correct arguments.
Most orimetrics we define in this paper optimize the search for viable arguments of a sketch by approximating its inverse semantics.

The discussion shows that rather different techniques can be understood as (i) assuming an
orimetric

enumeration order
through learning.
These two ingredients are often left implicit in the related work.
By making them explicit and giving them rigorous definitions, we provide a framework in which SyGuS technology can be developed.

The discussion of the solvers is summarized in

ESolver
(
Alur et al. 2015
)
is the basis of enumerative SyGuS solvers.
It enumerates programs by size until it finds a program that works for all examples.
ESolver applies observational equivalence to factorize the search space.
The enumeration order is constant and does not use deduction.
EUSolver
(
Alur et al. 2017
)
advances ESolver by adding deduction.
If for every example a suitable program has been found, EUSolver tries to construct an if-then-else program that solves all examples.
Behind the construction is a decision tree classification of the examples.

Blaze
(
Wang et al. 2018b
)
was the first solver based on abstraction refinement
(
Clarke et al. 2018
, Chapter 13)
.
The focus is on matrix transformation and string problems, and
Blaze expects to have access to three pieces of information about the domain: the cost for each production in the grammar, a set of predicates that can be used to abstract data values
(
Clarke et al. 2018
, Chapter 15)
, and an abstract semantics for each operator.
Blaze then solves the synthesis problem in the abstract, and refines the abstract domain by adding predicates if the solution turns out to be spurious.
Blaze can be seen as an instance of the generic algorithm in
Figure

1
.
For an orimetric based on predicate abstraction, let
f^{\sharp}
be the abstraction of
f
and let
\alpha(\mathit{i})
be the abstraction of the input value
\mathit{i}
.
Both can be computed with the information assumed by Blaze.
One then defines

\textit{m}_{\mathit{Blaze}}(f,g)\ =\ \sum_{\mathit{i}\in\mathit{I}}\widetilde{\textit{m}}(f^{\sharp}(\alpha(\mathit{i})),g^{\sharp}(\alpha(\mathit{i})))\ .

Another novelty in Blaze is that the search space is represented and explored using automata-theoretic techniques.
This important algorithmic aspect is not reflected in our generic solver, which is more on the semantic level.
The enumeration order is constant, namely defined by the costs.

EUPhony
(
Lee et al. 2018
)
is the first solver that uses probabilistic information.
It translates the given grammar into a probabilistic variant, and then traverses the language using A
∗
.
These probabilities have been learned from solutions to a large collection of synthesis tasks.
The learning is offline, it happens before the translation and the probabilities are not adapted during the search.
The enumeration order, which is determined by A
∗
based on the probabilities, is therefore constant in our terminology.
EUPhony applies a weak version of observational equivalence that works on sentential forms to factorize the search space.
It adopts the deduction techniques from EUSolver to synthesize programs for benchmarks that require case-splitting.

Probe
(
Barke et al. 2020
)
uses just-in-time learning to train the grammar.
It assigns costs to each production, and the cost of a program is then the sum of the costs of the productions needed to derive it.
Probe enumerates programs in the order of increasing costs, and stops when a cost limit is reached or a solution is found.
When the cost limit is reached, the costs are updated as follows.
Operators which occur frequently in programs solving at least one example are assigned a lower cost than the other operators.
The technique immediately fits, and actually inspired, our refinement of the enumeration order.
Probe applies observational equivalence factorization and does not do deduction.

Duet
(
Lee 2021
)
, Simba
(
Yoon et al. 2023
)
, DryadSynth
(
Ding and Qiu 2024
)
, and Synthphonia
(
Ding and Qiu 2025
)
are similar when it comes to the following characteristics: they all use observational equivalence to factorize the search space, have a constant enumeration order, enumerate based on the program size (with sharing in
(
Ding and Qiu 2024
)
), and use a variant of the deduction technique from EUSolver to deal with synthesis tasks that need case-splitting.
What distinguishes them are their own new deduction techniques.

Duet
(
Lee 2021
)
solves SyGuS problems in the theories of bitvectors, Booleans, strings, and integers.
Its deduction is based on an inverse semantics for the operators in the grammar.
When one argument for an operator has been fixed, the inverse semantics provides subproblems for the remaining arguments.
These are solved by inserting an already enumerated term or by further decomposition.

Simba
(
Yoon et al. 2023
)
concentrates on bitvectors and advances Duet’s deduction process.
The deduction computes necessary preconditions for the arguments of a program sketch.
If a program satisfying the preconditions of an argument has been found, it is inserted at the corresponding place, the preconditions for the remaining arguments are refined, and the process repeats.

DryadSynth
(
Ding and Qiu 2024
)
solves SyGuS problems in the theory of bitvectors.
DryadSynth keeps a set of sketches that are hardcoded into the algorithm.
This results in a drastic increase in performance compared to Simba.
DryadSynth enumerates programs in order of their size (with sharing).
For each enumerated program it checks whether there are other previously enumerated programs with which a sketch can be completed.
To perform this search efficiently, DryadSynth maintains viable programs for each sketch in a separate data structure.

Synthphonia solves synthesis problems in the string domain and works on more expressive grammars than specified in the SyGuS format.
Synthphonia introduces a framework to perform deduction and enumeration concurrently instead of in an interleaved fashion, and has specialized data structures for the communication between the threads.
Moreover, the case-splitting deduction technique from EUSolver is also implemented in a concurrent fashion.

The general solver we propose in
Section

4
can be instantiated to the above solvers. While the above techniques mostly manipulate the enumeration order, our tool Merlin focuses on the search space (but can also update the enumeration order).
Merlin solves SyGuS problems in the bitvector and in the string domain.
Furthermore, Merlin uses abstraction refinement to factorize the search space more effectively.
Merlin also incorporates the deduction techniques from DryadSynth and extends them to more sketches as well as to sketches in the string domain.
The basis for these deduction techniques is to have an inverse semantics.
Orimetrics can capture the complexity of the inverse semantics and prune programs accordingly.

Definition 3.1.

A
SyGuS problem

(\mathcal{G},\Phi)
consists of a context-free grammar
\mathcal{G}
and a specification
\Phi

The task is to find a program

We briefly recall the ingredients.
A
context-free grammar

\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{S})
consists of finite sets of nonterminals
\mathcal{N}
, terminals
\mathcal{T}
, and
productions
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{N}\times(\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{N})^{*}
, together with a start nonterminal
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{N}
.
The term language of a nonterminal, denoted by
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A})
, consists of all words over
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{N}
that can be derived from
\mathcal{A}
using the productions.
The language of
\mathcal{A}
is limited to the terminal words,

L(\mathcal{A})=\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A})\cap\mathcal{T}^{*}
.
The language of the grammar is
L(\mathcal{G})=L(\mathcal{S})
.
The complete language of the grammar considers all nonterminals,
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})=\bigcup_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{N}}L(\mathcal{A})
.
Similarly, the complete term language of the grammar is

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G})=\bigcup_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{N}}\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A})
.

In SyGuS, the terminals are variables or operators.
Variables have arity zero, operators may have an arity greater than zero.
We expect the grammar to respect the arity, meaning the productions have to provide the expected number of arguments.
With this requirement, we can simply call terminal words programs, and write them as
\texttt{p}\in L(\mathcal{S})
.
A word over terminals and nonterminals is a program sketch
\texttt{s}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G})
.
The holes of a sketch are the unresolved nonterminals.
The arity of a sketch is the number of its holes, say
n
.
The program that results from replacing the holes by programs
\texttt{p}_{1}
to
\texttt{p}_{n}
is
\texttt{s}(\texttt{p}_{1},\ldots,\texttt{p}_{n})
.
We use
\sqsubseteq\ \subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})\times\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
for the subprogram relation where
\texttt{p}\sqsubseteq\texttt{q}
, if there is a sketch
s
so that
\texttt{q}=\texttt{s}(\texttt{p})
.
We use
\texttt{q}\mathop{\!\downarrow}=\{\texttt{p}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})\mid\texttt{p}\sqsubseteq\texttt{q}\}
for the set of all subprograms of
q
, including
q
itself.
This is the downward closure wrt.
\sqsubseteq
.

A domain
(\textit{D},\mathcal{I})
consists of a set of data values
D
and an interpretation
\mathcal{I}
.
The interpretation assigns a function

\mathcal{I}(\texttt{op})=\textit{D}^{k}\rightarrow\textit{D}
to each operator
op
of arity
k
.
The domain gives rise to a semantics for programs
\texttt{p}\in L(\mathcal{G})
.
The semantics is the function
\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket
of type
\mathcal{F}=(\mathit{Vars}\rightarrow\textit{D})\rightarrow\textit{D}
.
It takes as input a variable assignment
\mathit{i}:\mathit{Vars}\rightarrow\textit{D}
and returns a data value.
The definition is as expected:
\llbracket\texttt{x}\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathit{i}(\texttt{x})
and
\llbracket\texttt{op}(\texttt{p}_{1},\ldots,\texttt{p}_{k})\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathcal{I}(\texttt{op})(\llbracket\texttt{p}_{1}\rrbracket(\mathit{i}),\ldots,\llbracket\texttt{p}_{k}\rrbracket(\mathit{i}))
.
It is common in SyGuS to take the domain and the semantics of programs as defined by the SMT-LIB
(
Barrett et al. 2010
)
standard.
We follow this convention.

We consider example-based specifications where
\Phi\subseteq(\mathit{Vars}\rightarrow\textit{D})\times\textit{D}
consists of a finite set of input-output examples.
A program satisfies the specification,
\texttt{p}\models\Phi
, if

\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathit{o}
for all
(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})\in\Phi
.
The
ground truth

\mathit{GT}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
consists of all functions that satisfy the specification in this sense.

CEGIS

SyGuS problems that are not example based can still be solved with example-based techniques.
The idea, known as counterexample-guided inductive synthesis
(
Solar-Lezama et al. 2008
)
, is to let an SMT solver generate new examples should a candidate program not yet satisfy the specification.
With this argument, we focus on example-based specifications.

Equivalences

An equivalence
\equiv\ \subseteq\mathit{S}\times\mathit{S}
on a set
\mathit{S}
is a relation that is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
We write
[\mathit{a}]_{\equiv}=\{\mathit{b}\mid\mathit{b}\equiv\mathit{a}\}
for the equivalence class of
\mathit{a}\in\mathit{S}
.
We just write
[\mathit{a}]
if the equivalence relation is understood.
We lift the notation to sets
\mathit{G}\subseteq\mathit{S}
and define
[\mathit{G}]=\bigcup_{\mathit{g}\in\mathit{G}}[\mathit{g}]
.
We call this the closure of
\mathit{G}
under
\equiv
.
The equivalence is precise wrt.
\mathit{G}
, if the closure does not add any elements,
[\mathit{G}]\subseteq\mathit{G}
.
Note that the reverse inclusion always holds.
If the equivalence is not precise wrt.
\mathit{G}
, it is called approximate.
The equivalence is unambiguous wrt.
\mathit{G}
, if
[\mathit{G}]=[\mathit{g}]
for all
\mathit{g}\in\mathit{G}
.
This means all elements in
\mathit{G}
are equivalent.
Let
f:\mathit{S}\rightarrow\mathit{S}
be a transformer on
\mathit{S}
.
The equivalence is a congruence wrt.
f
, if
\mathit{a}\equiv\mathit{b}
implies
f(\mathit{a})\equiv f(\mathit{b})
.
The definition generalizes to functions in several arguments in the expected way.
Factorizing
\mathit{S}
along
\equiv
yields the set of equivalence classes
\mathit{S}/_{\equiv}\ =\{[\mathit{a}]\mid\mathit{a}\in\mathit{S}\}
.
A representative system for
\mathit{S}/_{\equiv}
is a set
R\subseteq\mathit{S}
that contains precisely one element
\mathit{c}\in C
for every class
C\in\mathit{S}/_{\equiv}
.

4.
Contribution I – Oriented Metric Search Spaces

We first define the main object of this paper, oriented metrics, and then turn to the search space and the enumeration order.
Finally, we describe the components of our CEGAR loop in detail.

4.1.
Oriented Metrics

It will be convenient to give the definition for arbitrary sets
\mathit{S}
.

Definition 4.1.

A function
\textit{m}:\mathit{S}\times\mathit{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is an
oriented metric (orimetric)
if, for all
\mathit{a},\mathit{b},\mathit{c}\in\mathit{S}
,

(reflexivity)

\displaystyle\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{a})\ =\ 0

(symmetry at zero)

\displaystyle\textit{m}(\mathit{b},\mathit{a})\ =\ 0\ \ \Rightarrow\ \

\displaystyle\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})\ =\ 0

(
\triangle
-inequality)

\displaystyle\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{c})\ \ \leq\ \

\displaystyle\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})\ +\ \textit{m}(\mathit{b},\mathit{c})\ .

The
equivalence induced by
m
is the relation
\equiv_{\textit{m}}\ \subseteq\mathit{S}\times\mathit{S}
where
\mathit{a}\equiv_{\textit{m}}\mathit{b}
if
\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})=0
, for all
\mathit{a},\mathit{b}\in\mathit{S}
.
The orimetric is
precise
,
approximate
, resp.
unambiguous
wrt.
\mathit{G}\subseteq\mathit{S}
, if the equivalence
\equiv_{\textit{m}}
has these properties.
The orimetric is a
congruence
wrt.
f:\mathit{S}\rightarrow\mathit{S}
, if this holds for
\equiv_{\textit{m}}
.
We generalize the congruence requirement to functions in several arguments where needed.
The
quasimetric induced by
m
is
\mathop{\mathit{q}_{\mathit{m}}}:\mathit{S}/_{\equiv_{\textit{m}}}\times\,\mathit{S}/_{\equiv_{\textit{m}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\mathop{\mathit{q}_{\mathit{m}}}([\mathit{a}],[\mathit{b}])=\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})
.

We also suggested an alternative to orimetrics.

Definition 4.2.

A
factorization and pruning structure

(\mathit{S},\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}})
consists of an equivalence relation
\equiv\ \subseteq\mathit{S}\times\mathit{S}
and a quasimetric
\mathop{\mathit{q}}:\mathit{S}/_{\equiv}\times\,\mathit{S}/_{\equiv}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on the factorized set. Recall that a quasimetric requires the triangle inequality and
\mathop{\mathit{q}}([\mathit{a}],[\mathit{b}])=0
if and only if
[\mathit{a}]=[\mathit{b}]

(
Steen and Seebach 1978
)
.
The
orimetric induced by the factorization and pruning structure
is
\mathit{m}_{\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}}}:\mathit{S}\times\mathit{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\mathit{m}_{\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}}}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})=\mathop{\mathit{q}}([\mathit{a}],[\mathit{b}])
.

The main finding is that these concepts are equivalent.

Theorem 4.3.

If
(\mathit{S},\textit{m})
is an orimetric space, then
(\mathit{S},\equiv_{\textit{m}},\mathop{\mathit{q}_{\mathit{m}}})
is a factorization and pruning structure.
If
(\mathit{S},\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}})
is a factorization and pruning structure, then
(\mathit{S},\mathit{m}_{\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}}})
is an orimetric space.

We split the proof into two lemmas.

Lemma 4.4.

Let
m
be an orimetric on
\mathit{S}
.
(i)
\equiv_{\textit{m}}
is an equivalence.
(ii) The orimetric is invariant under the induced equivalence,

\mathit{a}_{1}\equiv_{\textit{m}}\mathit{a}_{2}
and
\mathit{b}_{1}\equiv_{\textit{m}}\mathit{b}_{2}
imply
\textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{1},\mathit{b}_{1})=\textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{2},\mathit{b}_{2})
, which means the induced quasimetric is well-defined.
(iii)
\mathop{\mathit{q}_{\mathit{m}}}
is a quasimetric.

Proof.

(i) Reflexivity of the induced equivalence is by reflexivity for the orimetric. For symmetry, it suffices to have symmetry at zero.
Transitivity is by the triangle inequality.

(ii) Recall that
\mathit{a}_{1}\equiv_{\textit{m}}\mathit{a}_{2}
means
\textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{1},\mathit{a}_{2})=0=\textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{2},\mathit{a}_{1})
.
We can therefore calculate as follows:

\displaystyle\ \ \textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{1},\mathit{b}_{1})

\displaystyle\text{($\triangle$-inequality)}\quad\leq

\displaystyle\ \ \textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{1},\mathit{a}_{2})\ +\ \textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{2},\mathit{b}_{1})

\displaystyle\text{($\textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{1},\mathit{a}_{2})=0$)}\quad=

\displaystyle\ \ \textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{2},\mathit{b}_{1})

\displaystyle\text{($\triangle$-inequality)}\quad\leq

\displaystyle\ \ \textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{2},\mathit{b}_{2})\ +\ \textit{m}(\mathit{b}_{2},\mathit{b}_{1})

\displaystyle\text{($\textit{m}(\mathit{b}_{2},\mathit{b}_{1})=0$)}\quad=

\displaystyle\ \ \textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{2},\mathit{b}_{2})\ .

Repeating the argument with the indices swapped yields
\textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{2},\mathit{b}_{2})\leq\textit{m}(\mathit{a}_{1},\mathit{b}_{1})
.
Together, the desired equality follows.

(iii) The triangle inequality immediately follows from the orimetric.
For equality at zero, we have

\mathop{\mathit{q}_{\mathit{m}}}([\mathit{a}],[\mathit{b}])=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathit{a}\equiv_{\textit{m}}\mathit{b}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad[\mathit{a}]=[\mathit{b}]\ .\qed

Lemma 4.5.

Let
(\mathit{S},\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}})
be a factorization and pruning structure.
Then
\mathit{m}_{\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}}}
is an orimetric.

Proof.

Reflexivity and the triangle inequality carry over from the quasimetric.
Symmetry at zero is a consequence of the equality at zero property of quasimetrics:

\mathit{m}_{\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}}}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})=0\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \mathop{\mathit{q}}([\mathit{a}],[\mathit{b}])=0\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ [\mathit{a}]=[\mathit{b}]\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \mathop{\mathit{q}}([\mathit{b}],[\mathit{a}])=0\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \mathit{m}_{\equiv,\mathop{\mathit{q}}}(\mathit{b},\mathit{a})=0\ .\qed

With this equivalence in place, we concentrate on orimetrics and illustrate the above definitions.
We visualize an orimetric by a labeled and directed graph.
The nodes are the classes in the induced equivalence.
They are labeled by the elements they contain.
The graphs are complete, meaning we have a directed edge between every pair of nodes.
The edge is labeled by the distance between the nodes in the corresponding classes.
Here we use

Lemma

4.4
(ii).
The orientation of the edges matters,
which is what makes orimetrics more expressive than metrics.
The triangle inequality says that one cannot reduce the distance from one class to another by traveling a detour.

.

We discuss the motivation behind the properties we require of orimetrics.
Reflexivity is essential for the refinement.
It says that a distance different from zero is enough to rule out a program as a candidate solution.
Metrics and also the weaker pseudo-metrics are typically symmetric
(
Steen and Seebach 1978
)
.
By avoiding symmetry, we can assign a meaningful distance to relations that are oriented,
as explained in the overview.
We need symmetry at zero for symmetry of the induced equivalence.
The triangle inequality is important for transitivity of the induced equivalence and for
Lemma

(ii).
The three properties seem to be what is needed in the context of synthesis.
We have not found this definition in the literature, and chose to name the object oriented metrics.

In the overview, we considered the ground truth to be a single function.
This assumption is actually justified, but needs some discussion.
For now, all we know is that
\mathit{GT}
is a set.
The point is that an example-based specification defines the output only for some of the inputs, but leaves freedom for the remaining inputs.

This means if we picked the wrong ground truth function, we would not be able to show that we solved the synthesis task.

\textit{m}(f,\mathit{gt}_{1})=\textit{m}(f,\mathit{gt}_{2})
, whether it is zero or not!

Remark

We expect all orimetrics we work with to be unambiguous wrt. the ground truth
\mathit{GT}
.
With the above discussion, we can then concentrate on an arbitrary element

4.2.
Search Spaces and Enumeration Order

The two main parameters in bottom-up enumerative SyGuS solvers are the search space and the enumeration order.
We now give them formal definitions.

Definition 4.6.

A
search space
is a set of programs
\mathit{P}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
.
We call
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
the full search space.
The semantics of a program is a function from
\mathcal{F}=(\mathit{Vars}\rightarrow\textit{D})\rightarrow\textit{D}
.
If we have an orimetric
m
on this set
\mathcal{F}
, we speak of an
oriented metric search space
.
We also write
(\mathit{P},\textit{m})
to make both components explicit.
Since we are interested in bottom-up enumerative solving, the search space should be
bottom-up enumerable
: for every program
\texttt{p}\in\mathit{P}
we should also find all subprograms in the set,
\texttt{p}\mathop{\!\downarrow}\subseteq\mathit{P}
.
We use
\mathsf{BU}(\mathit{P})
to denote the largest bottom-up enumerable set contained in
\mathit{P}
.

Lemma 4.7.

The largest bottom-up enumerable set contained in
\mathit{P}
is well-defined:
the bottom-up enumerable sets are closed under arbitrary unions, and so

\mathsf{BU}(\mathit{P})\subseteq\mathit{P}
exists and is unique.

To give an example, consider the set
\mathit{R}=\{\texttt{x},\texttt{y},\texttt{op}(\texttt{x}),\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\}
from above.
This set is bottom-up enumerable,
\mathsf{BU}(\mathit{R})=\mathit{R}
.
If we remove the element
y
, we have
\mathsf{BU}(\{\texttt{x},\texttt{op}(\texttt{x}),\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\})=\{\texttt{x},\texttt{op}(\texttt{x})\}
.
We fail to construct the program
\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})
by bottom-up enumeration.

When is it sound to restrict the search to a subset of programs?
The following definition gives a sufficient condition.

Definition 4.8.

We call a search space
\mathit{Q}

complete
wrt. another search space
\mathit{P}
and orimetric
m
,
if for every program
\texttt{p}\in\mathit{P}
there is a program
\texttt{q}\in\mathit{Q}
with
\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket\equiv_{\textit{m}}\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket
.

In
Lemma

4.14
, we give termination guarantees for working with complete search spaces.
Pruning methods based on observational equivalence satisfy completeness.
Also, the abstraction method in
(
Wang et al. 2018b
)
is complete.
We will deliberately use
incomplete
search spaces.

Definition 4.9.

An
enumeration order
is a well-founded total order
\preceq\ \subseteq\mathit{P}\times\mathit{P}
on the search space.
It is a
bottom-up
enumeration, if it is compatible with the subprogram relation, for all
\texttt{p},\texttt{q}\in\mathit{P}
,
\texttt{p}\sqsubseteq\texttt{q}
implies
\texttt{p}\preceq\texttt{q}
.
For the factorization, it will be important that the enumeration order is a
precongruence
.
For every operation
op
, say of arity
1
, we expect that
\texttt{p}\preceq\texttt{q}
implies
\texttt{op}(\texttt{p})\preceq\texttt{op}(\texttt{q})
.
The definition generalizes to higher arities.

We define three enumeration orders on the set
\mathit{R}
:

\displaystyle\texttt{x}\preceq_{0}\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\preceq_{0}\texttt{op}(\texttt{x})\preceq_{0}\texttt{y}\quad\qquad\texttt{x}\preceq_{1}\texttt{y}\preceq_{1}\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\preceq_{1}\texttt{op}(\texttt{x})\quad\qquad\texttt{x}\preceq_{2}\texttt{op}(\texttt{x})\preceq_{2}\texttt{y}\preceq_{2}\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\ .

The enumeration order
\preceq_{0}
is not bottom-up, because
\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\preceq_{0}\texttt{y}
.
It is also not a precongruence, because
\texttt{x}\preceq_{0}\texttt{y}
but
\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\preceq_{0}\texttt{op}(\texttt{x})
.
The enumeration order
\preceq_{1}
is bottom-up, but fails to be a precongruence for the same reason.
The enumeration order
\preceq_{2}
is bottom-up and a precongruence.

Given an enumeration order, we can construct (a subset of) the search space
\mathit{P}
algorithmically.
We go through the programs as prescribed by the order, and only keep a program if the subprograms have already been listed.
Let this procedure return the set
\mathsf{Enum}_{\preceq}(\mathit{P})
.
On the running example, this yields
\mathsf{Enum}_{\preceq_{0}}(\mathit{R})=\{\texttt{x},\texttt{op}(\texttt{x}),\texttt{y}\}
,
\mathsf{Enum}_{\preceq_{1}}(\mathit{R})=\mathit{R}=\mathsf{Enum}_{\preceq_{2}}(\mathit{R})
.

Lemma 4.10.

If
\preceq
is bottom-up, then
\mathsf{Enum}_{\preceq}(\mathit{P})=\mathsf{BU}(\mathit{P})
.

4.3.
Components of the CEGAR Loop

Prune

The function computes a
ball around the ground truth element we have chosen.
We now define this ball.
We again give the definition in the abstract for flexibility, meaning we consider an arbitrary set
\mathit{S}
with orimetric
m
.
Let
\mathit{gt}\in\mathit{S}
be the element that should serve as the center of the ball.
Let
\mathit{r}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the radius.
The
ball
and the
closed ball
around
\mathit{gt}
of radius
\mathit{r}
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathit{r}}(\mathit{gt})\ \ =\ \ \{\ \mathit{a}\in\mathit{S}\ \mid\ \textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{gt})<\mathit{r}\ \}\qquad\text{resp.}\qquad\mathcal{B}_{\mathit{r}}[\mathit{gt}]\ \ =\ \ \{\ \mathit{a}\in\mathit{S}\ \mid\ \textit{m}(\mathit{a},\mathit{gt})\leq\mathit{r}\ \}\ .

The ball is of course monotonic in the radius: the larger the more.
To give an example, consider the set
\mathit{R}
with orimetric
\textit{m}_{1}
from above.
Then
\mathcal{B}_{0}(\texttt{x})=\emptyset
,
\mathcal{B}_{0}[\texttt{x}]=\{\texttt{x},\texttt{y}\}=\mathcal{B}_{1}(\texttt{x})=\mathcal{B}_{1}[\texttt{x}]
, and
\mathcal{B}_{2}[\texttt{x}]=\mathit{R}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\texttt{op}(\texttt{x}))=\{\texttt{op}(\texttt{x}),\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{1}[\texttt{op}(\texttt{x})]=\mathit{R}
.

Function
Prune
constructs the elements in the ball bottom-up, using
Lemma

4.10
.
This means the enumeration order should be bottom-up, and the result of the enumeration will not be the ball itself but
\mathsf{BU}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathit{r}}(\mathit{gt}))
, the largest bottom-up enumerable set that lives inside the ball.

Factorize

We factorize the search space
\mathit{P}=\mathsf{BU}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathit{r}}(\mathit{gt}))
that we have just determined along the equivalence induced by the orimetric,
\mathit{P}/_{\equiv_{\textit{m}}}
.
This means programs are put into an equivalence class if their distance is zero.
How do we represent the equivalence classes in a way that can be manipulated algorithmically?
The idea is to use a representative system that only keeps the
\preceq
-least program from each equivalence class.
Formally, the
Factorize
function is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\texttt{Factorize}(\mathit{P},\textit{m},\preceq)\ =\ \{\ \texttt{p}\in\mathit{P}\ \mid\ \forall\texttt{q}\in\mathit{P}:\texttt{q}\prec\texttt{p}\Rightarrow\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket\not\equiv_{\textit{m}}\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket\ \}\ .

We return to the set
\mathit{R}=\{\texttt{x},\texttt{y},\texttt{op}(\texttt{x}),\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\}
.
Recall that we defined the orimetrics
\textit{m}_{1}
and
\textit{m}_{2}
, but only the former is a congruence.
We also have the enumeration orders
\preceq_{1}
and
\preceq_{2}
, but only the latter is a precongruence.
Now
\texttt{Factorize}(\mathit{R},\textit{m}_{1},\preceq_{1})=\{\texttt{x},\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\}
,
\texttt{Factorize}(\mathit{R},\textit{m}_{2},\preceq_{2})=\{\texttt{x},\texttt{op}(\texttt{x}),\texttt{op}(\texttt{y})\}
, and
\texttt{Factorize}(\mathit{R},\textit{m}_{1},\preceq_{2})=\{\texttt{x},\texttt{op}(\texttt{x})\}
.
All sets are complete wrt.
\mathit{R}
and the given orimetric.
However, only the last set is bottom-up enumerable.
We see,
the correct use of
Factorize
is intricate.
If we use
Factorize
with an orimetric that is not a congruence or an enumeration order that is not a precongruence, we can lose bottom-up enumerability.
The following lemma states sufficient conditions
for
Factorize
to return a search space that is bottom-up enumerable.

Lemma 4.11.

(i) Let
\preceq
be an enumeration order on
\mathit{P}
.
Then
\texttt{Factorize}(\mathit{P},\textit{m},\preceq)
is complete wrt.
\mathit{P}
under
m
.
(ii) If
\mathit{P}
is bottom-up enumerable,
\preceq
is additionally a precongruence, and
m
is a congruence, then
\texttt{Factorize}(\mathit{P},\textit{m},\preceq)
is bottom-up enumerable.

The examples we have just given show that
Lemma

4.11
(ii) does not hold without the precongruence and the congruence requirements.
The proof of the result can be found in the appendix.
For
Lemma

4.11
(i), we use the well-foundedness of the enumeration order, which guarantees the existence of a least element in each equivalence class.

Search

The
Search
function iterates through the search space along the enumeration order until it finds the first program that, from the perspective of the orimetric, solves the synthesis task.
It starts with the
\preceq
-minimal program
\texttt{p}\in\mathit{P}
.
Then it sets
p
to the successor of
p
until

\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket\equiv_{\textit{m}}\mathit{gt}
holds,
upon which
p
is returned.
To compute the successor of
p
, we use the procedure
\mathsf{Enum}_{\preceq}(\mathit{P})
.
Assuming that
\preceq
is bottom-up,
Lemma
4.10
shows that
Search
explores
\mathsf{BU}(\mathit{P})
.
Hence, to make sure we inspect the entire search space,
\mathit{P}
must be bottom-up enumerable,
\mathsf{BU}(\mathit{P})=\mathit{P}
.
This is where we will use Lemma
4.11
(ii).

We illustrate how our theory of enumeration orders can be combined with deduction, more precisely, the deduction technique in DryadSynth
(
Ding and Qiu 2024
)
.
Consider a program sketch
s
of arity
n+1
.
If we find the last parameter of
s
that is needed to solve the synthesis task, then the instantiation of the sketch should be the next program to enumerate.
To make this formal, assume we currently explore program
p
and we have already enumerated
\texttt{p}_{1},\ldots,\texttt{p}_{n}\preceq\texttt{p}
.
If
\texttt{q}=\texttt{s}(\texttt{p}_{1},\ldots,\texttt{p},\ldots,\texttt{p}_{n})

solves the synthesis task,
\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket=\mathit{gt}
, then it should be the immediate successor of
p
, meaning
\texttt{q}=\mathit{succ}_{\preceq}(\texttt{p})
.
If we relax the requirement so that the filled sketch should be the successor of the last program of the same size as
p
, then we get the instantiations from Duet
(
Lee 2021
)
and Simba
(
Yoon et al. 2023
)
.
Checking whether there are
\texttt{p}_{1},\ldots,\texttt{p}_{n}\preceq\texttt{p}
so that
\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket=\mathit{gt}

can be done efficiently for some operators.
For the
xor
operator for example, only a lookup in a hashmap storing all programs with their values as keys is needed
(
Ding and Qiu 2024
)
.
For the
ite
operator, a decision tree can be learned, as done in EUSolver
(
Alur et al. 2017
)
.

This modification of the enumeration order may ruin the precongruence property,
and one may be concerned that it jeopardizes the guarantees given by
Lemma

4.11
.
The point is that we only see the subset of programs up to and including the first deduction step.
On these programs, precongruence and the guarantees hold.
Afterwards,
Search
terminates and so the guarantees are not needed for the remaining programs.

Refine

We leave the exact instantiation of the function to the user and present our instantiation in
Section

5
.
Here, we only give two properties that the refinement scheme must satisfy.
Let
\textit{m}^{\prime}=\texttt{Refine}(\textit{m},\texttt{p},\mathit{gt})
be the refined orimetric.
We want

(refinement)

\displaystyle\equiv_{\textit{m}^{\prime}}\ \subseteq\ \equiv_{\textit{m}}\qquad\qquad\mathit{and}\qquad\qquad\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket\not\equiv_{\textit{m}^{\prime}}\mathit{gt}\ .

The first property states that the new orimetric is more precise in that the induced equivalence relates fewer programs.
The second says that the new orimetric differentiates between
\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket
and
\mathit{gt}
.

Lemma 4.12 (Progress).

If function
Refine
guarantees the properties in (
refinement
), then the algorithm will never explore the same program in two different loop iterations.

Learn

The enumeration order is updated by
Learn
based on the programs seen in

.
Programs and operators which promise to be more valuable for a solution to
\mathit{gt}
are preferred in the updated enumeration order.
The exact instantiation is left to the user, and we present our instantiation in
Section

5
.

Putting everything together, we obtain the above CEGAR loop.
The following correctness guarantee immediately follows from the termination condition of the loop.

Lemma 4.13.

Consider the SyGuS problem
(\mathcal{G},(\mathit{I},O))
.
If the CEGAR loop (
Figure

\texttt{p}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})

We also have a termination guarantee. It puts together Lemmas

Assume

5.
Contribution II – Constructing Oriented Metric Search Spaces

We define new orimetrics and explain how these orimetrics can be refined.
Our focus will be on strings and bitvectors.
We do not have new instantiations for the enumeration order, but work with a standard size-based definition.
However, we enhance this order with new deduction strategies for strings and bitvectors.
As it turns out, the definition of orimetrics and the design of deduction strategies are somewhat related, so we discuss deduction together with the orimetrics.

5.1.
Lifting Oriented Metrics to Function Spaces

For SyGuS, we are interested in orimetrics on the function space
\mathcal{F}=(\mathit{Vars}\rightarrow\textit{D})\rightarrow\textit{D}
.
However, as we have seen in the overview, it is convenient to construct such orimetrics on functions by lifting orimetrics on the data domain.
This lifting is actually the reason why we defined orimetrics for arbitrary sets.
We now make the lifting explicit.

Let
\tilde{\textit{m}}
be an orimetric on the data domain
D
.
Our goal is to lift
\tilde{\textit{m}}
to the function space
\mathit{X}\rightarrow\textit{D}
.
The set
\mathit{X}
should be understood as the set of (inputs from) all examples, although the definition does not rely on this understanding.
We define the lifting parametric in a set
\mathit{Y}\subseteq\mathit{X}
.
Intuitively, this is the subset of examples we currently consider, and the refinement loop will then make
\mathit{Y}
larger and larger.
We define
\textit{m}_{\mathit{Y}}:(\mathit{X}\rightarrow\textit{D})\times(\mathit{X}\rightarrow\textit{D})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

\displaystyle\textit{m}_{\mathit{Y}}(f,g)\ \ =\ \ \sum_{\mathit{y}\in\mathit{Y}}\tilde{\textit{m}}(f(\mathit{y}),g(\mathit{y}))\ .

Lemma 5.1.

If
\tilde{\textit{m}}
is an orimetric on
D
, then

\textit{m}_{\mathit{Y}}
is an orimetric on
\mathit{X}\rightarrow\textit{D}
, for all
\mathit{Y}\subseteq\mathit{X}
.

Besides the triangle inequality,
a quasimetric satisfies
\tilde{\textit{m}}(a,b)=0
if and only if
a=b

(
Steen and Seebach 1978
)
.
When we lift from a quasimetric on the data domain using the set
\mathit{I}
of inputs from all examples, then the result will be precise and unambiguous wrt.
\mathit{GT}
.

Lemma 5.2.

For
\mathit{J}\subseteq\mathit{I}
and
\tilde{\textit{m}}
an orimetric on
D
, the orimetric

\textit{m}_{\mathit{J}}
on
\mathcal{F}=(\mathit{Vars}\rightarrow\textit{D})\rightarrow\textit{D}
is unambiguous wrt.
\mathit{GT}
.
If
\tilde{\textit{m}}
is a quasimetric and
\mathit{J}=\mathit{I}
, the orimetric

\textit{m}_{\mathit{J}}

is also precise wrt.
\mathit{GT}
.

Refine

As explained in the overview, we use
\mathit{m}^{\sharp}=\textit{m}_{\mathit{J}}
with
\mathit{J}\subseteq\mathit{I}
as our approximate orimetric.
We refine the orimetric by adding an example for which the spurious program
p
does not return the expected value.
Let
\mathit{i}\in\mathit{I}
be an input example for which

\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket(\mathit{i})\neq\mathit{gt}(\mathit{i})
holds. We define

\texttt{Refine}(\textit{m}_{\mathit{J}},\texttt{p},\mathit{gt})=\textit{m}_{\mathit{J}\cup\{\mathit{i}\}}\ .

This is a refinement indeed.
Blaze’s
(
Wang et al. 2018b
)
abstraction refinement can also be instantiated in
Refine
.
For this, we only need to update the abstraction
\alpha
and the abstract transformers
f^{\sharp}
.
Then we can calculate the orimetric as described in
Section

2.2
.

Learn

When
\texttt{Learn}(\preceq,\mathit{P},\mathit{gt})
is called,
the spurious program
p
is
\preceq
-maximal in
\mathit{P}
.
We use the precise metric
\textit{m}_{\mathit{I}}
to judge all subprograms
q
of
p
.
For every subprogram
q

whose semantics reside in

\mathcal{B}_{(\mathcal{F},\textit{m}_{\mathit{I}}),\mathit{r}}(\mathit{gt})
,
we update the enumeration order by pretending
q

has no children and size
1
.

5.2.
Recipe

All orimetrics we work with will be liftings from orimetrics on the data domain.
We therefore only discuss orimetrics on strings and bitvectors, but not on functions over these data domains.
Actually, the orimetrics on strings and bitvectors we define will be quasimetrics.
This guarantees us preciseness of the orimetrics that result from lifting,
Lemma

5.2
.
We stress, however, that the lifted objects are no longer quasimetrics, they are orimetrics. The definitions in this section are guided by the following

Rule of Thumb:

If we understand deduction for the operators on the data domain, then we understand which values should be close in the orimetric for the data domain.

5.3.
Oriented Metrics for Strings

For strings, we have three orimetrics, two of them defined with deduction in mind.

5.3.1.
An Oriented Metric for
concat

For deduction, we start from the sketch
\texttt{concat}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}^{\prime})
that consists of the concatenation operation.
If
p
returns
\mathit{i}
and
\texttt{p}^{\prime}
returns
\mathit{i}^{\prime}
, then
\texttt{concat}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}^{\prime})
will return
\mathit{o}=\mathit{i}.\mathit{i}^{\prime}
.
Deduction infers missing arguments from already given arguments and target values.
Concretely, if we want to obtain value
\mathit{o}
and we already have
\mathit{i}
, then the missing input must be
\mathit{i}^{\prime}
.
This has been captured by the inverse semantics in
(
Lee 2021
)
.

We define an orimetric that is optimized for concatenation.
We think of the arguments of the orimetric as the values that are given to the deduction engine, namely the output
\mathit{o}
and the input
\mathit{i}
that is already known.
The value
\textit{m}_{\texttt{concat}}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})
should then capture how far
\mathit{i}
is away from
\mathit{o}
when concatenation is applied next.
This amounts to judging what could be called the complexity of the inverse semantics.
How many values does the inverse semantics contain, and how easy is it to generate these values.
The distance should then be inverse proportional to this complexity.
In particular, when there are no values left in the inverse semantics, the distance should be infinity.
For concatenation, this would be the case when
\mathit{i}
is not a prefix or a suffix of
\mathit{o}
.

While these considerations are useful as guidance, implementing them strictly does not lead to a useful definition.
First, we want to determine the ball not only for the concatenation operation, but it should also work for the remaining operations.
Second, trying to define the complexity of the inverse semantics quickly gets out of hand mathematically.
How do we make sure the definition still satisfies the triangle inequality?

Our definition relaxes the requirement that the input should be a prefix or a suffix to just an infix.
We approximate the complexity of the inverse semantics as follows.
The number of elements in the inverse semantics is always one, we are just missing one string.
The string, however, is easier to generate the shorter it is.
With this, we use the orimetric

\displaystyle\textit{m}_{\texttt{concat}}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})\ =\ \begin{cases}\mathit{len}(\mathit{o})-\mathit{len}(\mathit{i})&\text{if $\mathit{i}$ is an infix of $\mathit{o}$}\\
c+\lvert\mathit{len}(\mathit{o})-\mathit{len}(\mathit{i})\rvert&\text{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

We select the constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
to be larger than the sum over the lengths of all output values in the examples, and use this constant to define the ball.
Alternatively, we could extend the reals with infinity and define
\textit{m}_{\texttt{concat}}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})=\infty
, if
\mathit{i}
is not an infix of
\mathit{o}
.
However, this would add infinity as an undesirable technicality to many places.

5.3.2.
An Oriented Metric for
substr

The second sketch our deduction should support consists of the substring operation.
It takes three arguments: the original string
\mathit{i}
, the starting index of the substring that should be determined as the output
\mathit{o}
, and the maximal length of that substring.
Our orimetric focuses on the relationship between
\mathit{i}
and
\mathit{o}
.
The only precondition we can derive is that the input must be a superstring of the output.
We define
\textit{m}_{\texttt{substr}}
just like
\textit{m}_{\texttt{concat}}
, but with the infix requirement flipped.
Actually, within Merlin we have implemented a full new deduction strategy for the substring operation.
It can be found in the appendix.

5.3.3.
Levenshtein

Our synthesizer runs with a portfolio of different orimetrics, and it has turned out beneficial to have a fallback that is not optimized for a deduction strategy.
We use the Levenshtein distance for this purpose
(
Levenshtein 1966
)
.
The Levenshtein distance between two strings is the number of substitutions, deletions, and insertions that is necessary to convert one string into the other.
To efficiently check whether a program lies inside a ball, we use Levenshtein automata
(
Schulz and Mihov 2002
)
.
A Levenshtein automaton for a target string
\mathit{o}
and a radius
\mathit{r}
is a finite automaton that accepts all strings
\mathit{i}
with
\textit{lvst}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})<\mathit{r}
.
In our implementation, we set the maximum radius to
\mathit{r}=4
.

5.4.
Oriented Metrics for Bitvectors

For bitvectors, we have three orimetrics tailored towards sketches, and
one additional orimetric.

5.4.1.
An Oriented Metric for
and

We consider the bitwise conjunction
\texttt{and}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}^{\prime})
.
It takes as input bitvectors
\mathit{i}
and
\mathit{i}^{\prime}
and produces the bitvector
\mathit{o}=\mathit{i}\&\mathit{i}^{\prime}
.
We again derive an orimetric by working out a deduction method.
The deduction method reasons over the output
\mathit{o}
and the input
\mathit{i}
that is already known.
The first step is to check whether
\mathit{o}
is bitwise smaller than
\mathit{i}
, denoted by
\mathit{o}\sqsubseteq\mathit{i}
.
If the check fails, the deduction aborts.
If the check succeeds, we calculate the requirements on
\mathit{i}^{\prime}
, for each bit
b
, as follows.
(R1) If
\mathit{o}[b]=1
, then
\mathit{i}^{\prime}[b]=1
.
(R2) If
\mathit{o}[b]=0
and
\mathit{i}[b]=1
, then
\mathit{i}^{\prime}[b]=0
.
There are no requirements if
\mathit{o}[b]=0
and
\mathit{i}[b]=0
.

The goal of the orimetric
\textit{m}_{\texttt{and}}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})
is to estimate the complexity of the inverse semantics.
The idea is to sum up all situations (R1) and (R2) in which the inverse semantics has no choice for the value of a bit:
\sum_{b\in[0,63]}\mathit{i}[b]\vee\mathit{o}[b]
.
This function, however, will not be reflexive.
If
\mathit{i}=\mathit{o}=1^{64}
, it will return
64
rather than
0
.
The solution is to drop requirement (R1).
The idea is to approximate the search for
\mathit{i}^{\prime}
from above.
We take for granted that we are in a space of values that are bitwise larger than the output.
Only summing up situations (R2) then leads to the final definition:

\displaystyle\textit{m}_{\texttt{and}}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})\ =\ \begin{cases}\sum_{b\in[0,63]}\neg\mathit{o}[b]\wedge\mathit{i}[b]&\text{if $\mathit{o}\sqsubseteq\mathit{i}$}\\
c&\text{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

The large constant is again for emptiness of the inverse semantics.
It is worth noting that under the assumption
\mathit{o}\sqsubseteq\mathit{i}
, the sum
\sum_{b\in[0,63]}\neg\mathit{o}[b]\wedge\mathit{i}[b]
is just the Hamming distance.
The Hamming distance is even a metric.
A similar reasoning yields an orimetric for bitwise disjunction.

5.4.2.
An Oriented Metric for
mul

For the multiplication among bitvectors
\texttt{mul}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}^{\prime})
, deduction has to start from
\mathit{o}
and
\mathit{i}
and determine
\mathit{i}^{\prime}
so that
\mathit{o}=\mathit{i}\times\mathit{i}^{\prime}
.
This amounts to finding the multiplicative inverse in the integer ring represented by the bitvectors.
Note that the inverse does not exist if the output is smaller than the input.

The corresponding orimetric again approximates the complexity of the inverse semantics, here the multiplicative inverse element.
We take this complexity to be the difference in the number of leading zeros between input and output:

\displaystyle\textit{m}_{\texttt{mul}}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})\ =\ \begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathit{i}=\mathit{o}$}\\
1+\textit{nlz}(\mathit{o})-\textit{nlz}(\mathit{i})&\text{if $\textit{nlz}(\mathit{i})\leq\textit{nlz}(\mathit{o})$}\\
c&\text{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

5.4.3.
Hamming

To relate two values directly, we use a modification of the Hamming distance.
The Hamming Distance between two bitvectors is the number of bits where the two bitvectors do not match.
Taking
HDist
directly as the quasimetric prunes programs that are good.
Consider a 64-bit bitvector
\mathit{i}
with
\textit{HDist}(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})=
64.
A single
not
operation would yield
\mathit{o}
.
With that in mind, we define a new quasimetric
\textit{m}_{\mathit{hd}}
that relates two bitvectors
\mathit{a}
and
\mathit{b}
as follows:

\textit{m}_{\mathit{hd}}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})=\begin{cases}\textit{HDist}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})&,\textit{HDist}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})\leq 32\\
64-\textit{HDist}(\mathit{a},\mathit{b})+1&,\textit{otherwise}\ .\end{cases}

5.5.
Hacks

Sharing Programs Between Concurrent Solver Instances

In our implementation, we run several solvers concurrently that utilize different orimetrics to prune the search space. When a solver with orimetric
m
finds a candidate solution, we also analyze it in the context of the other orimetrics
\textit{m}^{\prime}
.
The goal is to find subprograms that are valuable wrt.
\textit{m}^{\prime}
, and modify the enumeration order of the corresponding solver accordingly.
This way, valuable programs can be shared between the concurrent solvers.

Keeping Programs up to a Size Threshold

We do not want to apply pruning to small programs, otherwise the bottom-up enumerable portion of the ball may become too small to be useful.
We slightly modify the given orimetric
m
to create
\textit{m}^{\prime}
, which keeps programs of size up to a threshold
\mathit{s}
in the open ball of radius
\mathit{r}
.

6.
Contribution III – Implementation and Evaluation

We implemented our approach in a SyGuS solver called Merlin.
It is written in C++ and uses Z3
(
de Moura and Bjørner 2008
)

as an SMT solver backend for the CEGIS loop.
We evaluate the performance of Merlin to answer the following research questions:

Q1:

How does Merlin perform on SyGuS tasks of a variety of domains?

Q2:

How does Merlin compare against state-of-the-art SyGuS and domain-specific solvers?

Q3:

What is the effect of pruning using orimetrics?

Q4:

Does abstraction and learning from spurious programs enhance performance?

Q5:

What impact does the radius of the ball have?

Q6:

Which orimetrics are successful?

Q7:

What is the benefit of running multiple instances with different orimetrics concurrently?

We ran all experiments on an Apple M3 Max with 64 GB of RAM and used a 10-minute timeout.

6.1.
Implementation Details

For strings, we use a size threshold
\mathit{s}
of
3
and
start with orimetrics that consider one example only.
To implement Levenshtein automata, we use the Mata finite automaton library
(
Chocholatý et al. 2024
)
.
For Bitvectors, we use a size threshold
\mathit{s}
of
7

and start with orimetrics that consider two examples.
We concurrently run several instances of the solver using different orimetrics.
We also run an instance that does not prune and will refer to it as
\textit{m}_{\infty}
.
For each orimetric designed for a specific sketch, the solver only uses deduction for this sketch.
The other threads apply deduction on sketches for which we did not design a specific orimetric, e.g. the
\texttt{add}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
sketch.
That means, for each benchmark featuring strings (bitvectors),
Merlin runs solver instances for all string (bitvector) orimetrics in a portfolio.
In particular, even when a benchmark features multiple oriented operations, e.g.
concat
and
substr
, Merlin runs four threads each employing one of

\textit{m}_{\texttt{concat}}{}
,
\textit{m}_{\texttt{substr}}{}
,
lvst
, or
\textit{m}_{\infty}
.
If the learning mechanism finds a program that is valuable using a specific orimetric, we only change the enumeration order of the thread employing this orimetric and of the thread that does not prune.

The choice of the radius depends on the evaluation.
For the comparison with other tools, we conduct experiments with varying radii for
lvst
and
\textit{m}_{\mathit{hd}}
.
For the remaining orimetrics, we use
c
, which means we evaluate the given conditions and prune if they fail.
For the ablation studies, we use varying radii for all orimetrics.

Setup

Benchmarks

We use benchmarks from three domains: SyGuS bitvector benchmarks without conditionals, SyGuS string benchmarks without conditionals, and the Blaze string benchmark set.

In the Bitvector domain, we have 549 benchmarks:

7.
Related Work

Metrics in Synthesis

SyMetric
(
Feser et al. 2023
)
uses distance metrics for approximate observational equivalence.
They cluster programs whose output is within a given radius inside an equivalence class.
SyMetric also prunes the search space using a ball around
\mathit{gt}
.
Their resulting equivalence is not a congruence.
They rely on similarity between the inputs and the outputs for most operators.
Using this similarity, they are able to repair a spurious program given a predefined set of rewrite rules.

Syntia
(
Blazytko et al. 2017
)
incorporates metrics in top-down search.
For a given sketch, they randomly fill its holes and execute the resulting program.
Using metrics they assign each sketch a score based on similarity of the program’s output and the specified output.
This score is then used to guide search.

Pruning and Factorization

Most tools employ some form of OE factorization
(
Udupa et al. 2013
;
Albarghouthi et al. 2013
)
.
For this,
Version Spaces Algebras are used in FlashFill
(
Gulwani 2011
;
Polozov and Gulwani 2015
;
Cambronero et al. 2023
)
and
finite tree automata are used by Dace
(
Wang et al. 2017b
)
.
Absynthe
(
Guria et al. 2023
)
enables the use of OE for programs with local variables.
Morpheus
(
Feng et al. 2017
)
and Neo
(
Feng et al. 2018
)
use logical reasoning to prune parts of the search space for which they can prove that it does not contain a solution.
For this, Morpheus requires over-approximative specifications of program components.
It then generates sketches and uses an SMT solver to discard infeasible program sketches.
Neo extends this approach with conflict driven learning.
Having ruled out one sketch, it extracts the root cause of its infeasibility.
Then other sketches for which this root cause applies can also be pruned.
FlashFill++
(
Cambronero et al. 2023
)
proposes cuts to enable middle-out synthesis.
A cut creates a set of subproblems whose solutions can be combined to solve the whole synthesis problem.
This set of subproblems may be incomplete, i.e. not every possible way to dissect the synthesis problem is explored.
This effectively prunes the search space.

Approximation

To further prune the search space, a common approach is to use abstractions or type information to represent programs.
With this, one can decide the feasibility of sketches in order to prune infeasible ones.
NOSDAQ
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
synthesizes database queries.
Through abstraction of database collections, they can prove that a sketch cannot be completed to yield the correct output.
Similarly, Synquid
(
Polikarpova et al. 2016
)
, its extension
(
Eguchi et al. 2018
)
, and
\lambda^{2}

(
Feser et al. 2015
)
use type information to prune infeasible partial programs.
Scythe
(
Wang et al. 2017a
)
first searches for sketches that satisfy the specification in the abstract setting.
Then, it uses this set of sketches to search for an instantiation satisfying the specification in the concrete setting.
Absynthe
(
Guria et al. 2023
)
is similar in that it uses user-defined abstract semantics to create viable sketches.
For each a viable sketch, it fills the holes and executes the concrete program using a given interpreter.
If the program satisfies the specification, it is returned as the solution.

Learning

Similar to DryadSynth
(
Ding and Qiu 2024
)
, HySynth
(
Barke et al. 2024
)
and DeepCoder
(
Balog et al. 2017
)
use Large Language Models to instantiate an enumeration order.
LaSy
(
Perelman et al. 2014
)
and ReGuS
(
Cui et al. 2024
)
blend upfront and just-in-time learning.
They solve a suite of related synthesis tasks.
The solutions for easier tasks can then be used as a callable component to solve harder tasks.
In our framework, we can simulate these learning approaches by appropriately setting the initial enumeration order.
Atlas
(
Wang et al. 2018a
)
extends Blaze
(
Wang et al. 2018b
)
by learning useful predicates for abstraction from a set of training problems upfront.
As we have shown for Blaze, predicate abstraction can be seen as an instantiation of oriented metrics.
Bester
(
Peleg and Polikarpova 2020
)
motivates just-in-time learning by showing that for complex benchmarks, partial solutions are often part of the intended solution.
FrAngel
(
Shi et al. 2019
)
modifies partial solutions to solve the synthesis problem.

8.
Conclusion and Future Work

We presented oriented metrics as a foundation for pruning, factorization, refinement, and learning in syntax-guided synthesis.
We defined a synthesis algorithm that has these features and is parametric in the enumeration order and the initial orimetric.
We invented new orimetrics for the string and the bitvector domain that occur frequently in SyGuS problems.
We implemented our approach in a tool called Merlin, and obtained a speed-up of an order of magnitude compared to the state-of-the-art.
In the future, we will further explore the design of orimetrics and the choice of when to prune.

Lemma B.1.

An orimetric
m
induces an equivalence relation
\equiv_{\textit{m}}
with

f\equiv_{\textit{m}}g\quad\text{ if }\quad\textit{m}(f,g)=0\ .

Proof.

We first show reflexivity.
Because
\textit{m}(f,f)=0
by definition, we have
f\equiv_{\textit{m}}g
.

Next, we show transitivity.
We show
f\equiv_{\textit{m}}h
.
We have
f\equiv_{\textit{m}}g
and
g\equiv_{\textit{m}}h
.
This means that
\textit{m}(f,g)=0
and
\textit{m}(g,h)=0
.
Therefore,
\textit{m}(f,g)+\textit{m}(g,h)=0
.
Because
\textit{m}(f,h)\geq 0

and

\textit{m}(f,h)\leq\textit{m}(f,g)+\textit{m}(g,h)=0
,
we get
\textit{m}(f,h)=0
.
Thus,
f\equiv_{\textit{m}}h
holds.

Lastly, we show symmetry.
We show
g\equiv_{\textit{m}}f
.
We have
f\equiv_{\textit{m}}g
.
This means that

\textit{m}(f,g)=0
.
By definition, we get

\textit{m}(g,f)=0
.
And thus,

g\equiv_{\textit{m}}f

holds.
∎

Lemma B.2.

Given a search space
\mathit{P}

\preceq

Let
\mathit{Q}=\texttt{Factorize}(\mathit{P},\textit{m},\preceq)

\mathit{P}

[\llbracket\texttt{q}^{\prime}\rrbracket]=[\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket]

\texttt{p}\neq\texttt{q}

[\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket]=[\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket]

\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket\equiv\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket
.
Because
\texttt{p}\prec\texttt{q}

cannot be in
\mathit{Q}
.
∎

Size Hack

Lemma B.3.

Given an orimetric
m
, then
\textit{m}^{\prime}
also is an orimetric.

Proof.

The formal definition of
\textit{m}^{\prime}
is:

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)=\begin{cases}\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)&\text{, }\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)<\mathit{r}\\
\mathit{r}-\epsilon&\text{, }\textit{sz}(\texttt{p})\leq\mathit{s}\vee\exists\texttt{q}\in\mathit{P}:\textit{sz}(\texttt{q})\leq\mathit{s}\wedge\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket\equiv_{\textit{m}}\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket\\
\mathit{r}&\text{, otherwise.}\end{cases}

As
\epsilon
, we take half of the minimum distance between two functions, and the minimum difference between the distance of two functions and
\mathit{r}
.
For the minimum to exist, we restrict the function space to functions expressible by programs of whose size is less than an exorbitant size, say 1 million, plus
\mathit{gt}
.
We call the function space
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.
Then
\epsilon
is formally defined as:

\epsilon=\frac{\min(\{\ \textit{m}(f,g),\mathit{r}-\textit{m}(f,g)\ \mid\ f,g\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\ \}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0})}{2}

We first show reflexivity.
Because,
m
is an orimetric, we have

\textit{m}(f,f)=0
.
Because
0<\mathit{r}
, we have
\textit{m}^{\prime}(f,f)=0
.

Next, we show symmetry at zero.
If
\textit{m}^{\prime}(f,g)=0
,
then we know that

\textit{m}(f,g)=0
.
Because
m
is an orimetric, we have that

\textit{m}(g,f)=0
.
And therefore

\textit{m}^{\prime}(g,f)=0

holds.

Last, we show the triangle inequality.
Consider
\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)
.
We differentiate two cases.
The first case is when
\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)<\mathit{r}
.
The second case is when
\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\geq\mathit{r}
.

We start with the first case, so
\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)<\mathit{r}
.
Then, we have
\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)=\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)<\mathit{r}
, and thus we have

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\leq\mathit{r}-\epsilon
.
Consider now

\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket)+\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket,f)
.
If both addends are
<\mathit{r}
, we get the triangle equality directly from the triangle inequality of
m
.
If one addend is
\geq\mathit{r}
, then the result in
\textit{m}^{\prime}
is
\geq\mathit{r}-\epsilon
and thus also greater than or equal to

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)
.

In the second case we have
\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\geq\mathit{r}
.
Then,
\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\geq\mathit{r}-\epsilon
.
We have two subcases.
The first subcase is that
\textit{sz}(\texttt{p})\leq\mathit{s}
or there exists
\texttt{q}\in\mathit{P}
with
\textit{sz}(\texttt{q})\leq\mathit{s}
and
\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket\equiv_{\textit{m}}\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket
.
The second subcase is the negation of the first subcase.

We start with the first subcase.
We have that
\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)=\mathit{r}-\epsilon
.
That means we have

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\leq\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\leq\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket)+\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket,f)\ .

Again, if both addends are
<\mathit{r}
, the triangle inequality follows directly.
If one addend is
\geq\mathit{r}
, then the result in
\textit{m}^{\prime}
is
\geq\mathit{r}-\epsilon
and thus also greater than or equal to

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)
.

In the second subcase we have

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)=\mathit{r}

and

\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\geq\mathit{r}

.
This means

\mathit{r}=\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\leq\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\leq\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket)+\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket,f).

Again, if both addends are
<\mathit{r}
, the triangle inequality follows directly.
If not, consider the addends in
\textit{m}^{\prime}
:

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket)
and
\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket,f)

.
At least one of the addends is
\geq\mathit{r}-\epsilon
.
Since it also is
\leq\mathit{r}
, we have the two cases:
Either it is equal to
\mathit{r}-\epsilon
or it is equal to
\mathit{r}
.
In the latter case, we are done, because

\mathit{r}=\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)
.

In the former case,
towards a contradiction, assume the first addend is equal to
\mathit{r}-\epsilon
.
So, we have

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket)=\mathit{r}-\epsilon
.
For this to be true,

\textit{sz}(\texttt{p})\leq\mathit{s}
or there exists
\texttt{q}^{\prime}\in\mathit{P}
with
\textit{sz}(\texttt{q}^{\prime})\leq\mathit{s}
and
\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket\equiv_{\textit{m}}\llbracket\texttt{q}^{\prime}\rrbracket
.
Then we would not be in this case.
This is a contradiction.

Next, assume
the second addend is equal to
\mathit{r}-\epsilon
:

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket,f)=\mathit{r}-\epsilon
.
Then,

\textit{sz}(\texttt{q})\leq\mathit{s}
or there exists
\texttt{q}^{\prime}\in\mathit{P}
with
\textit{sz}(\texttt{q}^{\prime})\leq\mathit{s}
and
\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket\equiv_{\textit{m}}\llbracket\texttt{q}^{\prime}\rrbracket
.
For

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)\leq\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket)+\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket,f)

to be violated, we would need

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket)=0
because all other distances are greater than
\epsilon
.
This, would mean that
\textit{m}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,\llbracket\texttt{q}\rrbracket)=0
.
Because
\equiv_{\textit{m}}
is transitive,
we have
\llbracket\texttt{q}^{\prime}\rrbracket\equiv_{\textit{m}}\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket
.
This, however, has the consequence that there exists the same
\texttt{q}^{\prime}
with
\textit{sz}(\texttt{q}^{\prime})\leq\mathit{s}
.
Therefore,

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)
should have been
\mathit{r}-\epsilon
.
This is a contradiction to

\textit{m}^{\prime}(\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket,f)=\mathit{r}
.
∎

Appendix C
Details on Deduction

In the following, we describe how to efficiently check if a successor of a program is determined through deduction.
The explanation will reason about settings with one input-output example
(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})
only.
This is just to ease the notation and can easily be lifted to settings with multiple input-output examples.

For all deduction techniques, if we enumerated the program
p
, we first compute its output
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}=\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket(\mathit{i})
.
Also, in order to make the deduction efficient, we maintain sets of programs that have been enumerated.
They are usually indexed by the programs’ output values for easy lookup.

C.1.
Deduction for Bitvectors

In the following, we describe how to efficiently check if a successor of a program is determined through deduction.
The explanation will reason about settings with one input-output example
(\mathit{i},\mathit{o})
only.
This is just to ease the notation and can easily be lifted to settings with multiple input-output examples.
For all deduction techniques, if we enumerated the program
p
, we first compute its output
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}=\llbracket\texttt{p}\rrbracket(\mathit{i})
.
Also, in order to make the deduction efficient, we maintain sets of programs that have been enumerated.
They are usually indexed by the programs’ output values for easy lookup.

For bitvectors, we have nine sketches.
Four of them are also used by DryadSynth:

\texttt{and}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
,

\texttt{or}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
,

\texttt{add}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
,
and

\texttt{xor}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
.
The remaining five sketches are new:

\texttt{mul}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
,

\texttt{not}(\texttt{add}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?}))
,

\texttt{neg}(\texttt{add}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?}))
,

\texttt{not}(\texttt{xor}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?}))
,
and

\texttt{neg}(\texttt{xor}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?}))
.

\texttt{and}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})

For the
\texttt{and}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
sketch, we keep a set of programs
\mathit{P}_{1}
that return a bitvector which is bitwise greater than the output bitvector
\mathit{o}
.
If we now enumerate a program
p
that returns the bitvector
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}
, we first check whether the program is a viable candidate for the
and
operator by checking whether
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}
is bitwise greater than
\mathit{o}
.
If that is the case, we search through
\mathit{P}_{1}
to find a program
\texttt{p}_{1}
for which
\llbracket\texttt{and}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{1})\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathit{o}
holds.
If there is such a program,
\texttt{and}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{1})
is one of the immediate successors of
p
.

\texttt{or}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})

For the
\texttt{or}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
sketch, we keep a set of programs
\mathit{P}_{1}
that return a bitvector which is bitwise less than the output bitvector
\mathit{o}
.
The deduction procedure is analogous to the one for the
\texttt{and}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
sketch.

\texttt{mul}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})

For the
\texttt{mul}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
sketch, we keep a set of programs
\mathit{P}_{1}
that return a bitvector.
If we now enumerate a program
p
that returns the bitvector
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}
, we compute candidate inputs for the
mul
operator.
To get viable candidates, we compute all values
\mathit{o}_{1}

that solve the equation
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}\times\mathit{o}_{1}=\mathit{o}
.
This involves finding the multiplicative inverse of
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}
in the integer ring represented by the bitvectors using the extended euclidean algorithm.
For each candidate
\mathit{o}_{1}
, we check if
\mathit{P}_{1}
contains a program
\texttt{p}_{1}
whose output is
\mathit{o}_{1}
.
If there is such a program,
\texttt{mul}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{1})
is one of the immediate successors of
p
.

\texttt{add}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})

For the
\texttt{add}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
sketch, we also keep a set of programs
\mathit{P}_{1}
that return a bitvector.
If we now enumerate a program
p
that returns
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}
, we can directly compute the value
\mathit{o}_{1}
we need such that
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}+\mathit{o}_{1}=\mathit{o}
by calculating
\mathit{o}_{1}=\mathit{o}-\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}
.
Then, we look up whether there exists a program
\texttt{p}_{1}
in
\mathit{P}_{1}

whose output is
\mathit{o}_{1}
.
If there is such a program,
\texttt{add}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{1})
is the successor of
p
.

Remaining Sketches

The deduction for the remaining sketches is analogous to the deduction for the
\texttt{add}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
sketch.
For each sketch, we can compute the value
\mathit{o}_{1}
needed to complete the sketch.
Then, we look up if there is a program in
\mathit{P}_{1}
whose output is
\mathit{o}_{1}
.
If so, we fill the sketch holes with
p
and
\texttt{p}_{1}
and determine the resulting program to be one of the immediate successors.

C.2.
Deduction for Strings

For Strings, we have three sketches which use one operator each:

\texttt{concat}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
,
\texttt{replace}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
, and
\texttt{substr}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
.

\texttt{replace}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?},\mathord{?})

The operator
replace
takes as arguments three strings: the first is the string in which the replacement should happen, namely the first occurrence of the second argument, should it exist, will be replaced by the third argument.
We keep two sets of programs that are enumerated earlier:
\mathit{P}_{1}
and
\mathit{P}_{2}
.
In
\mathit{P}_{1}
, we keep all programs that return a string.
In
\mathit{P}_{2}
, we keep all programs that return a substring of the expected output
\mathit{o}
.
If there is a program
\texttt{p}_{1}\in\mathit{P}_{1}
whose output
\mathit{o}_{1}=\llbracket\texttt{p}_{1}\rrbracket(\mathit{i})
returns a substring of
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}

and there also is a program
\texttt{p}_{2}\in\mathit{P}_{2}
for which

\llbracket\texttt{replace}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{1},\texttt{p}_{2})\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathit{o}
holds,
then the one of the immediate successors in the enumeration order is

\texttt{replace}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{1},\texttt{p}_{2})
.
We check the same for the other positions in the sketch, i.e. we search programs
\texttt{p}_{1},\texttt{p}_{2}
with

\llbracket\texttt{replace}(\texttt{p}_{1},\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{2})\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathit{o}

or

\llbracket\texttt{replace}(\texttt{p}_{1},\texttt{p}_{2},\texttt{p})\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathit{o}
.

For the sketch
\texttt{concat}(\mathord{?},\mathord{?})
, one necessary precondition for the programs that fill the sketch holes is that they must produce substrings of the target output.
For strings that do not satisfy the precondition, we penalize them with a big constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Otherwise, the distance between two strings is the difference of their length.
Formally, we define the quasimetric
\widetilde{\textit{m}}
as follows:

\widetilde{\textit{m}}(\mathit{str},\mathit{str}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\mathit{len}(\mathit{str}^{\prime})-\mathit{len}(\mathit{str})&,\textit{substr}(\mathit{str},\mathit{str}^{\prime})\\
c+\lvert\mathit{len}(\mathit{str}^{\prime})-\mathit{len}(\mathit{str})\rvert&,\textit{else}\ .\end{cases}

The key insight from the perspective of oriented metrics is that the first program should produce a superstring of the output
We keep three sets.
In the first set
\mathit{P}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})\times\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
we keep all pairs of programs that we have explored, where the first program returns a superstring
\mathit{o}_{s}
of the output and the second program returns the indices of
\mathit{o}
in
\mathit{o}_{s}
.
In the second set
\mathit{P}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})\times\mathbb{N}
we keep programs that output a superstring
\mathit{o}_{s}
of
\mathit{o}
alongside with the starting index of
\mathit{o}
in
\mathit{o}_{s}
.
In the third set
\mathit{P}_{3}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
we keep all programs that return an integer.
We have one approach for programs that return strings and one for programs that returns integers:

If
p
returns a superstring
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}
of
\mathit{o}
,
then let
k
be the index of
\mathit{o}
in
\mathit{o}_{\texttt{p}}
.
Next, we look up whether there is a program
\texttt{p}_{k}\in\mathit{P}_{3}
that returns
k
.
If so, we look for a program
\texttt{p}_{l}\in\mathit{P}_{3}
which specifies the correct length, i.e.
\llbracket\texttt{substr}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{k},\texttt{p}_{l})\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathit{o}
.
If there is such a program,
\texttt{substr}(\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{k},\texttt{p}_{l})
is one of the immdediate successors of
p
.

If
p
returns an integer, we check if there is a program pair
(\texttt{p}_{s},\texttt{p}_{k})\in\mathit{P}_{1}
for which the current program returns the correct length,
i.e.
\llbracket\texttt{substr}(\texttt{p}_{s},\texttt{p}_{k},\texttt{p})\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathit{o}
holds.
In that case,
\texttt{substr}(\texttt{p}_{s},\texttt{p}_{k},\texttt{p})
is one of the successor programs.

Otherwise, we check if the current program returns a suitable starting index for a program we have enumerated before.
If
p
returns the integer
k
,
We look for a pair
(\texttt{p}_{s},k)\in\mathit{P}_{2}
.
If it exists, we search through
\mathit{P}_{3}
again to find a program
\texttt{p}_{l}
with

\llbracket\texttt{substr}(\texttt{p}_{s},\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{l})\rrbracket(\mathit{i})=\mathit{o}
.
If there is such a program,

\texttt{substr}(\texttt{p}_{s},\texttt{p},\texttt{p}_{l})

is the successor of
p
.

Appendix D
Example: Instantiation of ESolver and Observational Equivalence Factorization

As an example, we instantiate ESolver in our framework.
To recap, ESolver uses size based enumeration with OE factorization.
Size based enumeration discovers programs in increasing size.
Here, the size is measured as the number of nodes in the AST representing the program.

Following the instantiation in
Section

5.1
, we only need to define a quasimetric.
For bitvectors, one could use the hamming distance as the quasimetric, for example.
We call the resulting metric
\textit{m}_{\mathit{I}}
.

Now that we have a metric in place, the next step is to define an enumeration order
\preceq
.
We use a size based enumeration order.
As described in
Section

2
, ESolver only factorizes the search space with OE.
Thus, for pruning, we set the radius
\mathit{r}
of the ball to infinity.

Prune
therefore returns the identity:

\texttt{Prune}(\mathit{P},\textit{m},\mathit{gt})=\mathit{P}
.
The function
Factorize
is defined as above.

Since
\textit{m}_{\mathit{I}}
respects congruence, we have that if
the search space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
is bottom-up enumerable,
\texttt{Factorize}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}),\textit{m}_{\mathit{I}},\preceq)
is bottom-up enumerable and complete wrt.
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
and
\textit{m}_{\mathit{I}}
.

Because
\textit{m}_{\mathit{I}}
is precise and unambiguous,
Refine
and
Learn
will not be reached (
Lemma

4.14
).
Thus, we omit defining a
Refine
or a
Learn
function for this example.
The resulting instantiation is standard size based enumeration with OE factorization as is done in ESolver.
