Introduction

Transcatheter left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) is an important nonpharmacological strategy for preventing stroke and other thromboembolic events in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF). Current guidelines recommend LAAC for patients at moderate to high risk of stroke (CHA2DS2-VASc score ≥2) who have contraindications to long-term oral anticoagulation therapy.1 However, LAAC is an invasive procedure and carries a risk of serious procedural complications, including device-related thrombosis, left atrial appendage (LAA) perforation, and pericardial tamponade.2 Pericardial tamponade during LAAC often necessitates emergent surgical repair.3

Here, we report a case of LAA perforation occurring in the proximal portion of the appendage during LAAC, which was successfully managed using an imaging-guided percutaneous bailout strategy. Multimodality imaging, including transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and angiography, enabled precise localization of the perforation and facilitated targeted device deployment to achieve effective hemostasis, thereby avoiding emergent surgical intervention.

Case report

A 72-year-old man with symptomatic persistent AF, a high thromboembolic risk, and a high bleeding risk (CHA2DS2-VASc score 4; HAS-BLED score 4) was referred to our institution for concomitant AF ablation and LAAC. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with TEE and fluoroscopy guidance. The patient restored sinus rhythm after pulmonary vein and left atrial posterior wall isolation. After LAA angiography with a 6-F pigtail catheter in the right anterior oblique caudal projection, detailed assessment of the LAA was performed. The LAA ostial width was approximately 23 mm; the depth was 12 mm; and the landing zone width was 16 mm (Figure 1A). Based on these measurements and the LAA morphology, implantation with a Leftear 19/23 mm device (Pulse Scientific, Guang Dong, China) was planned.

After partial deployment of the distal umbrella, angiography and TEE demonstrated a suboptimal device position, and full recapture and redeployment were attempted. However, the anchor hook appeared to be engaged at the superior margin of the LAA (Figure 1B). Although the device was subsequently fully retrieved, injury to the superior LAA margin was suspected. Immediately thereafter, the patient developed cardiac tamponade with a systolic blood pressure of 70 mm Hg, and TEE revealed a rapidly increasing pericardial effusion (Figure 1C). Emergent pericardiocentesis was performed via a subxiphoid access, and a 7-F pigtail catheter was placed in the pericardial space. After approximately 120 mL of blood was initially drained from the pericardial space, the systolic blood pressure increased to around 100 mm Hg without vasopressor support, suggesting relative hemodynamic stabilization. A total of 600 mL of blood was subsequently aspirated and autotransfused. However, continuous drainage suggested ongoing bleeding from an LAA perforation. Both surgical and percutaneous bailout strategies were considered, and cardiac surgical backup was immediately available. Given the stabilized hemodynamics after drainage, the planned indication for LAAC, and the desire to avoid the morbidity associated with emergent surgical repair, a percutaneous bailout strategy was selected as the first-line approach, with surgical repair reserved as a rescue option in case of procedural failure or hemodynamic deterioration.

After careful evaluation with TEE and fluoroscopic angiography, the perforation site was located at the superior basal aspect of the LAA on the TEE short-axis view, corresponding to the 2 o’clock position (Figure 1D) and to the superior aspect of the LAA ostium on the right anterior oblique 30°/caudal 20° fluoroscopic view (Supplemental Video 1). We attempted closure of the perforation using a Watchman FLX device (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA). We hypothesized that the covered semipermeable membrane at the proximal part of the device could facilitate thrombus formation and promote hemostasis. Reassessment of the LAA showed that the ostial width was approximately 18 mm and the depth was 17 mm (Supplemental Figure 1), and therefore a 24-mm Watchman FLX device) was selected. After full deployment, all Position, Anchor, Size, Seal criteria were met. The abnormal blood signal at the proximal part of the LAA on TEE disappeared (Figure 2A), suggesting that the device might cover and successfully seal the perforation. No increase in pericardial effusion was observed (Figure 2B), and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for close hemodynamic monitoring. Oral anticoagulation with edoxaban (30 mg) was initiated 24 hours after stable hemodynamics were confirmed. The pericardial drainage catheter was removed after echocardiography confirmed the absence of reaccumulation of pericardial effusion. The patient was discharged on edoxaban 60 mg after repeat echocardiography showing no pericardial effusion (Figure 2C). During follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic. Transthoracic echocardiography at 1 and 3 months showed no pericardial effusion (Figure 3A). Cardiac computed tomography at 3 months demonstrated no peridevice leak or device-related thrombosis, although incomplete endothelialization was observed (Figure 3B).

Discussion

Cardiac tamponade due to pericardial effusion is one of the most serious complications during LAAC. It most commonly occurs during transseptal puncture, sheath manipulation, device deployment, or repeated retrieval and redeployment of the occluder. In early randomized controlled trials of LAAC, the incidence of pericardial tamponade exceeded 4% and approximately one-third of affected patients required surgical intervention.3 With increasing operator experience and advancements in device technology, the risk of this complication has significantly decreased. In the first US registry study of the Watchman 2.5 device, pericardial tamponade occurred in 39 of 3822 patients (1.02%), of whom 12 required surgical repair, 24 were managed percutaneously, and 3 died.4 In the National Cardiovascular Data Registry left atrial appendage occlusion Registry, which included 65,355 patients, pericardial tamponade was reported in 881 patients (1.35%) and 177 patients (20.1%) required surgical intervention. Importantly, patients who developed tamponade during LAAC had a more than 60-fold increased risk of in-hospital and 45-day mortality.5 Consistent with these findings, a recent single-center registry including 1023 patients reported early pericardial effusion in 25 patients (2.4%) within 6 hours of LAAC. Pericardial effusion was independently associated with the use of double-closure LAA devices, female sex, baseline oral anticoagulation therapy, and advanced age, underscoring the multifactorial nature of this complication.6 In the present case, the patient had several established risk factors for pericardial effusion, including advanced age, baseline oral anticoagulation therapy, and the use of a double-closure LAA device. This combination of risk factors highlights the importance of heightened procedural vigilance and individualized risk assessment during LAAC, particularly in patients with multiple risk factors.

For patients with pericardial effusion who remain hemodynamically unstable despite pericardiocentesis and drainage, early surgical repair via thoracotomy is generally recommended.7 However, surgical intervention is associated with increased invasiveness and a higher risk of early postprocedural mortality. As a result, percutaneous bailout strategies have been increasingly explored in selected cases. For distal LAA perforations, several percutaneous approaches have been described, including sealing the LAA ostium with an occluder device,8 in situ deployment of the device responsible for the perforation,9 deployment of a disc-type occluder extending toward the pericardial space,10 closure of the defect using a vascular plug,11 and combined approaches such as cryoballoon occlusion of the LAA ostium with the LARIAT epicardial LAA suture device.12 Although evidence is limited to case reports, these strategies demonstrate that percutaneous hemostasis may be feasible in carefully selected patients.

In the present case, the perforation was located at the proximal portion of the LAA. After device deployment, the abnormal flow signal at the proximal LAA disappeared immediately, and no further increase in pericardial effusion was observed on TEE, suggesting successful sealing of the perforation site. This case illustrates an imaging-guided percutaneous bailout strategy. Multimodality imaging enabled precise localization of the perforation, which facilitated targeted device deployment. The fabric-covered proximal portion of the Watchman FLX device may contribute to sealing of the defect through mechanical coverage and promotion of local thrombosis. However, this mechanism remains speculative and cannot be confirmed on the basis of a single case and therefore should be interpreted with caution.

Restarting anticoagulation after tamponade and LAA perforation requires balancing the risks of rebleeding and device-related thrombosis, particularly in patients with high thromboembolic risk.13 In the absence of direct evidence for LAAC-related tamponade, anticoagulation was resumed 24 hours after confirmed hemostasis, based on evidence from AF ablation studies suggesting that early resumption may be feasible.14 Given the bleeding concern, a reduced dose of edoxaban was selected, in line with prior evidence demonstrating maintained efficacy with lower bleeding risk.15

Conclusion

For perforations involving the proximal part of the LAA, surgical repair may not always be required in highly selected hemodynamically stable patients after adequate pericardial drainage. In such cases, percutaneous hemostasis with imaging-guided deployment of a fabric-covered occluder may represent an alternative strategy when performed by experienced operators.
