Growth, Carcass Quality and Gut Microbiota Responses to Extruded Rapeseed Meal With Protease in Broiler Chickens
Authors: Rohban Hameed, Muhammad Tahir, Rifat Ullah Khan, Sohail Ahmad, Luciana Rossi, Rasha K. Al‐Akeel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSoybean meal (SBM) is a major protein source in broiler diets, but its cost and dependence on a limited number of protein sources have increased interest in alternative feed ingredients. Rapeseed meal has considerable potential as an alternative protein source; however, its utilisation is constrained by antinutritional factors that can limit nutrient availability and broiler performance. Extrusion processing and exogenous enzyme supplementation may improve the nutritional value of rapeseed meal. We hypothesised that replacing part of dietary SBM with extruded rapeseed meal (ERSM), in combination with protease and non‐starch polysaccharide (NSP)‐degrading enzymes, would improve or maintain broiler performance and nutrient utilisation while supporting intestinal health.MethodsA total of 320 day‐old Ross 308 male broiler chickens were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments in a completely randomised design. During the finisher period (22–35 days), birds received a corn–SBM control diet or diets in which 5%, 7.5%, or 10% of SBM protein was replaced with ERSM. All diets contained identical levels of protease and NSP‐degrading enzymes. Growth performance, nutrient digestibility, carcass characteristics, intestinal morphology and caecal microbial populations were evaluated to determine the effects of increasing ERSM inclusion.ResultsDietary ERSM affected feed intake (p = 0.029), final body weight (p = 0.017), feed conversion ratio (p = 0.009) and mortality (p = 0.041), with the 5% and 7.5% ERSM treatments generally producing the most favourable performance. Apparent digestibility of dry matter, crude protein, crude fibre, fat and ash was improved in ERSM‐fed birds (p < 0.05). Carcass yield, breast weight and thigh weight increased with ERSM inclusion (p ≤ 0.025), whereas liver weight decreased at the highest inclusion level (p = 0.034). ERSM also improved intestinal morphology by increasing villus height and the villus height‐to‐crypt depth ratio and reducing crypt depth (p ≤ 0.016). In addition, ERSM increased beneficial caecal bacterial populations and reduced Escherichia coli and Clostridium counts (p ≤ 0.050).ConclusionsReplacing SBM protein with 5%–7.5% ERSM in diets supplemented with protease and NSP‐degrading enzymes improved growth performance, nutrient digestibility, carcass characteristics, intestinal morphology and caecal microbial balance in broilers. The findings indicate that appropriately processed rapeseed meal can partially substitute for SBM while maintaining or improving important productive and intestinal health outcomes.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of the global poultry industry has increased the demand for sustainable, cost‐effective and nutritionally adequate protein sources capable of maintaining broiler growth performance and meat quality while reducing reliance on soybean meal (SBM), the conventional protein source in poultry diets (Milczarek et al. 2022; Nurhayati et al. 2025). Although SBM provides a favourable amino acid profile and high protein digestibility, its increasing cost, dependence on global commodity markets, environmental footprint associated with cultivation and competition with other food and feed uses have stimulated interest in alternative plant protein sources (El‐Sabrout et al. 2024). Among these alternatives, rapeseed meal (RSM), a major co‐product of Brassica napus oil extraction, has considerable potential because it contains approximately 34%–38% crude protein and provides substantial amounts of essential amino acids (Dastar et al. 2025; Hafeez et al. 2025a). Thus, increasing the effective utilisation of RSM could contribute to diversification of protein sources and reduce dependence on SBM in poultry production.

Despite its nutritional potential, the inclusion of conventional RSM in broiler diets has traditionally been restricted because of its relatively high concentrations of antinutritional factors (ANFs), including glucosinolates, phytate and non‐starch polysaccharides (NSPs). These compounds can adversely affect feed intake, nutrient utilisation, intestinal function and ultimately growth performance (Hafeez et al. 2025a). Processing methods can reduce some of these constraints; however, their effectiveness depends on the type and intensity of processing, and excessive thermal treatment may negatively affect protein quality and amino acid availability (Duodu et al. 2018). Therefore, processing approaches that improve the nutritional value of RSM while preserving protein quality are required to facilitate its greater inclusion in broiler diets.

Extrusion is a promising processing technology for improving the feeding value of oilseed meals. During extrusion, the combined effects of temperature, pressure, moisture and mechanical shear can alter the physical structure of feed ingredients, disrupt cell matrices, modify protein structure and improve the accessibility of nutrients to digestive enzymes (Long et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2021). In RSM, extrusion has been reported to reduce residual glucosinolates and improve the availability and utilisation of nutrients, including amino acids (Elbaz et al. 2023). However, extrusion alone may not completely overcome all nutritional limitations associated with RSM, particularly those related to protein utilisation and NSP‐associated nutrient encapsulation. Therefore, combining extrusion with nutritional strategies such as exogenous enzyme supplementation may provide an additional means of improving the feeding value of RSM.

Exogenous enzymes are particularly relevant in this context because they can target specific nutritional constraints that remain after processing (Ciptaan et al. 2025). Proteases hydrolyse dietary proteins and may increase the release and absorption of amino acids, whereas carbohydrases can degrade NSPs and thereby improve nutrient accessibility and intestinal conditions (Woyengo et al. 2022; Hafeez et al. 2025a). Previous studies have demonstrated beneficial effects of enzyme supplementation in diets containing rapeseed‐ or canola‐derived ingredients, including improvements in nutrient digestibility, growth performance, carcass characteristics and intestinal development (Boonmee et al. 2024; Hafeez et al. 2025b). However, the response to enzyme supplementation may depend on the physical and chemical characteristics of the protein ingredient. Consequently, the effectiveness of protease supplementation when used with extruded, rather than conventionally processed, RSM remains insufficiently characterised.

Importantly, although previous research has investigated RSM, extrusion processing and exogenous enzymes individually, limited information is available regarding the combined use of ERSM and dietary protease in broilers, particularly when ERSM is included at levels exceeding the conventional 5% inclusion level. Moreover, it remains unclear whether the potential improvement in nutrient availability achieved through extrusion and protease supplementation is sufficient to maintain broiler performance when SBM is partially replaced by ERSM. Addressing this question is important because successful use of higher ERSM inclusion levels could increase the flexibility of protein formulation and reduce dependence on SBM.

Therefore, the present study was designed to investigate the effects of replacing part of dietary SBM with 5%, 7.5% and 10% ERSM in broiler diets supplemented with protease. These inclusion levels were selected to evaluate whether ERSM could be progressively increased beyond the conventional low inclusion range while maintaining biological performance. The study evaluated growth performance, nutrient digestibility, carcass characteristics, intestinal morphology and intestinal microbial populations to provide an integrated assessment of the nutritional and physiological consequences of ERSM utilisation. We hypothesised that extrusion processing combined with dietary protease supplementation would improve the utilisation of RSM sufficiently to permit its inclusion at levels up to 10% without adversely affecting broiler growth, nutrient utilisation, carcass characteristics, intestinal morphology or microbial balance.

Materials and Methods

Chemical Composition, Source and Preparation of Rapeseed Meal

RSM used in the present experiment was obtained from the local market. The RSM was derived from Brassica napus and was subjected to extrusion processing to produce ERSM. The extrusion process was performed at 120°C, moisture 18%, with a residence time of 30–60 min using a single‐screw extruder. After extrusion, the product was cooled to room temperature, dried to the desired moisture content and ground through a 2‐mm sieve before incorporation into the experimental diets. These processing conditions should be reported because temperature, moisture, pressure, shear and residence time can influence the nutritional characteristics and ANF content of RSM.

The chemical composition of the unextruded RSM was determined according to AOAC (2005) procedures. Standard gravimetric and Kjeldahl methods were used to determine dry matter, crude protein, ether extract, ash and crude fibre. Phytic acid was extracted using hydrochloric acid and sodium sulphate and quantified spectrophotometrically at 660 nm. Total tannins and phenolic compounds were determined using the vanillin–HCl colorimetric method at 500 nm. Glucosinolates were enzymatically hydrolysed using myrosinase, and the released glucose was quantified by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). For pH determination, 20 g of sample was homogenised with 200 mL of distilled water and measured using a calibrated pH meter (Model CG 804; Schott Geräte GmbH, Mainz, Germany). Birds, Housing, Management and Ethical Approval

The experiment was conducted at Global Protein Farm, Unit 9, Dunyapur, Punjab, Pakistan. All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of experimental animals and were approved by the (Given in the title page due to journal guideline).

A total of 320 day‐old Ross 308 male broiler chicks were obtained from a commercial hatchery and randomly allocated to floor pens. The experimental unit was the pen, with five replicate pens per dietary treatment and 16 birds per pen. Thus, the four dietary treatments comprised 20 experimental pens in total. Pens were thoroughly cleaned and fumigated before chick placement and bedded with fresh rice hulls.

During the first week, ambient temperature was maintained at approximately 35°C (95°F) and gradually reduced to approximately 24°C (75°F) by the third week. Thereafter, birds were maintained at room temperature. A lighting schedule of 23 h light and 1 h dark was maintained throughout the experimental period. Feed and clean drinking water were provided ad libitum. A routine vaccination program was implemented according to the farm's standard vaccination schedule; the vaccine types, administration routes and vaccination days should be reported to ensure complete reproducibility.

All feed ingredients, including ERSM, were procured from Sadiq Brothers Feed Mill, Rawalpindi, Pakistan, where the experimental diets were formulated and mixed. Diets were pelleted using a 3‐mm die. Representative feed samples were collected from each dietary treatment and analysed for dry matter, crude protein, ether extract, crude fibre and ash according to AOAC (2019) procedures. The ERSM‐containing diets were supplemented with a commercial alkaline microbial protease (Ronozyme ProAct; DSM Nutritional Products, Basel, Switzerland) at 200 mg/kg feed, whereas the control diet did not receive supplemental protease. A commercial NSP‐degrading enzyme preparation was included at 100 FXU/kg feed. Thus, the experimental comparison incorporated both ERSM inclusion and protease supplementation.

Extrusion Processing of Rapeseed Meal

RSM was extruded following standard high‐temperature, short‐time processing methods commonly used to improve the nutritional quality of oilseed meals (Liang et al. 2016). Whole rapeseed or solvent‐extracted meal was first cleaned to remove dust, stones and metallic particles, with optional dehulling, and then ground to a particle size <3 mm to ensure uniform heat penetration and smooth flow through the extruder. The ground material was conditioned to a moisture content of 15%–20% by the addition of steam or water, a critical step for starch gelatinisation and protein denaturation during cooking. Conditioned meal was then conveyed through a single‐ or twin‐screw feed extruder, where mechanical shear and injected steam rapidly increased the internal temperature to 110°C–140°C under pressures often exceeding 30–40 bar, with a residence time of 15–45 s. This high‐temperature, high‐pressure treatment effectively inactivates the enzyme myrosinase, thereby preventing the conversion of glucosinolates to toxic isothiocyanates, reduces NSP viscosity, lowers microbial contamination and enhances protein digestibility by denaturing storage proteins and rupturing cell walls (Khajali and Slominski 2012).

Upon exiting the die, the sudden drop in pressure caused instantaneous steam flashing, expanding and partially puffing the extrudate and further disrupting cell walls. The hot product was then dried and cooled to approximately 10%–12% moisture to prevent spoilage and ensure storage stability, after which it was ground or crumbled to the particle size required for incorporation into poultry diets. Properly controlled extrusion lowers glucosinolate activity, improves pellet quality and handling characteristics and increases metabolisable energy and amino acid availability without excessive loss of heat‐labile nutrients (Liang et al. 2016). These changes allow ERSM to be included at higher levels in broiler diets compared with unprocessed meal.

Growth Performance

Growth performance—including body weight, feed intake and mortality—was recorded at the end of the finisher phase. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as total feed intake divided by body weight gain and was corrected for mortality to account for any birds lost during the period.

Carcass and Organ Measurements

At Day 35, two birds per replicate (closest to the mean body weight of the pen) were fasted for 12 h, weighed and humanely slaughtered. Carcass yield, breast and thigh weights were expressed as a percentage of live weight. The liver, heart, gizzard and entire intestine were excised and weighed individually relative to live weight.

Nutrient Digestibility

On the 31st day of the experiment, five birds per replicate were randomly chosen and housed in individual metabolic cages for a 4‐day period to collect excreta. Throughout this collection phase, each bird's feed consumption and excreta output were carefully measured to determine nutrient utilisation. At the end of the collection, the same birds were humanely slaughtered, and their carcasses were opened to obtain ileal digesta. The ileum was removed promptly and kept chilled to maintain sample quality. The recovered digesta were then freeze‐dried and analysed for dry matter, crude protein, ether extract, crude fibre and apparent metabolisable energy using the analytical procedures outlined by Hafeez et al. Ileal Histology

At the end of the study, two birds per replicate were chosen and humanely slaughtered. A 2‐cm segment of the ilium was collected from each sampled bird immediately after slaughter. Tissues were gently rinsed with cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to remove digesta and immersed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin for at least 24 h to ensure complete fixation. Fixed samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin wax. Paraffin blocks were trimmed and sectioned at 5 µm thickness using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235, Leica Biosystems, Germany). Sections were floated on a warm water bath, mounted on glass slides and dried overnight before staining with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to visualise mucosal architecture. Slides were examined under a Nikon Eclipse E200 light microscope equipped with a calibrated digital camera and image‐analysis software. For each bird, 10 well‐oriented villi—those with intact tips and a clearly visible lamina propria—were selected, and both villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured. The VH:CD ratio was calculated from these measurements. In total, six birds per treatment group were evaluated, yielding 60 villi measurements per treatment (10 villi from each bird). The mean of the ten measurements for each bird served as the experimental unit for statistical analysis, ensuring reliable and reproducible assessment of intestinal morphology.

Caecal Microbiota

Fresh caecal digesta were collected aseptically from each sampled bird immediately after slaughter, and about 1 g of content was transferred into a sterile 15 mL polypropylene tube containing 9 mL of sterile PBS (pH 7.2) to achieve a 1:10 (w/v) initial suspension. The samples were thoroughly homogenised with a vortex mixer, and 10‐fold serial dilutions were prepared in sterile PBS up to 10− 7, depending on bacterial density. From each appropriate dilution, 0.1 mL aliquots were spread in duplicate on selective media to enumerate different bacterial groups: Lactobacillus spp. were cultured on De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar (Oxoid, UK) and incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 48 h in an anaerobic jar with a GasPak system; Bacteroides spp. were grown on Bacteroides Bile Esculin (BBE) agar (Difco, USA) under the same anaerobic conditions and temperature for 48 h; Clostridium spp. were enumerated on Reinforced Clostridial Medium (RCM) agar (Oxoid, UK) incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 48 h; and Escherichia coli were plated on MacConkey agar (Oxoid, UK) and incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24 h. After incubation, colonies showing the typical morphology of each bacterial group were counted using a digital colony counter, and results were expressed as log10 colony‐forming units (cfu) per gram of fresh digesta.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analysed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with dietary treatment (0%, 5%, 7.5% or 10% ERSM) as the fixed effect. The replicate pen was considered the experimental unit, with five replicate pens per dietary treatment. Prior to analysis, the data were examined for normality and homogeneity of variance using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests, respectively. When the ANOVA indicated a significant treatment effect, differences among treatment means were determined using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) multiple‐comparison test. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05. All results are expressed as least‐squares/observed means ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Discussion

Overall, moderate inclusion levels of ERSM plus protease conferred benefits in many parameters, while higher inclusion (10%) showed more mixed responses. In the finisher phase, feed intake, final body weight, FCR and mortality all showed statistically significant differences. The 5% and 7.5% ERSM + protease treatments yielded improved feed efficiency and reduced mortality compared to control or the 10% level. These results suggest that moderate inclusion levels of processed rapeseed can be tolerated or even advantageous when paired with proteolytic enzyme support (Akram et al. 2025). This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence that dietary protease supplementation can improve broiler growth and feed efficiency. Nguyen et al. (2018), for example, reported that supplementation with a cocktail containing 200 mg protease/kg and 100 FXU xylanase/kg increased body weight gain during both the starter and overall experimental periods. Similarly, Park and Kim (2018) found that protease supplementation improved body weight gain and FCR during the overall rearing period. Hu et al. (2020) further demonstrated that increasing exogenous acid protease supplementation improved body weight gain and reduced FCR, particularly at higher protease inclusion levels. Collectively, these findings support the possibility that the protease component of the present diets contributed to the favourable performance observed at moderate ERSM inclusion levels. In past work, RSM has often reduced growth performance in broilers, primarily due to its content of glucosinolates, fibre and other antinutritional components (Recoules et al. 2019). However, processing methods such as fermentation or extrusion can alleviate some of these limitations. For example, replacing SBM with fermented rapeseed meal (FRSM) mitigated the adverse effects on weight gain and FCR compared to raw RSM diets (Dastar et al. 2025). In those studies, FRSM diets improved beneficial microbial populations and intestinal morphology, which were linked to better nutrient absorption and growth. The favourable performance in our 5% and 7.5% groups may reflect two key synergistic effects: (1) extrusion partially inactivating ANFs (e.g., myrosinase, resistant cell walls) and improving energy release, and (2) protease facilitating digestion of encapsulated proteins and overcoming residual inhibitors. The latter interpretation is supported by Park et al. (2020), who observed progressively greater body weight and average daily gain and lower FCR as dietary protease increased from 0% to 0.09%, together with improvements in the digestibility of dry matter, crude protein, energy and amino acids. Indeed, exogenous protease has been reported to improve nutrient utilisation and gut morphology when added to diets containing “challenging” ingredients (Hafeez et al. 2021). Moreover, Mohammadigheisar and Kim (2018) demonstrated that protease supplementation could partially alleviate the adverse effects of reducing dietary crude protein, improving body weight gain and dry matter digestibility in broilers. This finding is particularly relevant to the present study because replacement of SBM with ERSM represents a nutritional challenge in which efficient utilisation of dietary protein becomes important for maintaining growth. The drop in advantage at 10% ERSM suggests a threshold beyond which remaining indigestible fibre or residual toxins counterbalance gains (Hafeez et al. 2025a).

Results show that dressing percentage and breast/thigh weights were improved under moderate ERSM levels. This aligns with greater nutrient availability and deposition of muscle mass. When birds are more efficient in nutrient use, more protein accretion to desirable muscles is expected (Hafeez et al. 2025b). Although the studies of Nguyen et al. (2018), Park and Kim (2018), Hu et al. (2020), Park et al. (2020) and Mohammadigheisar and Kim (2018) primarily focused on growth performance and nutrient utilisation rather than carcass responses, their findings provide a physiological basis for the improved carcass traits observed in the present study. In particular, improved protein and amino acid digestibility following protease supplementation could increase the availability of substrates for muscle protein deposition. In contrast, data indicated that only liver weight was depressed at the highest ERSM inclusion. A lighter liver at 10% ERSM may reflect reduced metabolic load or lower toxicity stress, but could also suggest a mild negative effect on hepatic growth or lipid deposition if substrate supply is limiting (Hafeez et al. 2025a). The absence of change in heart, gizzard and intestinal weights suggests that overall organ development was relatively unaffected by the treatments.

The digestibility data showed significant improvements in dry matter, crude protein, crude fat and fibre in ERSM + protease diets relative to control. These gains are consistent with the hypothesis that enzyme inclusion helps release nutrients trapped behind fibre matrices or antinutritional barriers (Akram et al. 2025). Strong support for this interpretation comes from Hu et al. (2020), who reported linear improvements in ileal digestibility of crude protein, energy and several essential amino acids, including lysine, methionine, threonine and leucine, with increasing exogenous acid protease supplementation. Park et al. (2020) likewise reported linear improvements in dry matter, crude protein, energy and amino acid digestibility when dietary protease increased from 0% to 0.09%. Protease digestion can break the protein–fibre matrix and reduce the physical encapsulation effect, increasing accessibility of enzymes and microbes (Khan et al. 2024). Moreover, residual NSPs in rapeseed may hamper digestion; combining protease with an NSP‐degrading enzyme (as in our design) could further reduce digesta viscosity and nutrient “stickiness.” This combined‐enzyme rationale is supported by Nguyen et al. (2018), who found that a protease–xylanase cocktail increased dry matter retention and nitrogen retention in broilers. Their study is particularly relevant because it demonstrates that simultaneous targeting of protein and NSP‐related constraints can improve nutrient utilisation. Indeed, in rapeseed‐based diets, use of NSP‐degrading enzymes improves carbohydrate digestibility and helps overcome the nutrient‐encapsulation effect of fibre (Wiśniewska et al. 2023). Similarly, Mohammadigheisar and Kim (2018) reported that protease supplementation improved dry matter digestibility in broilers fed diets with reduced crude protein, while Nguyen et al. (2018) observed improved nitrogen retention following protease–xylanase supplementation. These findings reinforce the interpretation that improved nutrient utilisation contributed to the favourable growth responses observed in the moderate ERSM treatments. Better digestibility is also reflected in improved energy and amino acid availability, leading to more efficient growth. Improvements in digestibility have been shown in other studies where unconventional or processed feed ingredients were fed along with enzyme supplementation (Elbaz et al. 2023).

Morphometric data revealed that ERSM + protease treatments improved villus height, reduced crypt depth and elevated the villus‐height‐to‐crypt‐depth (VH:CD) ratio, indicating a larger absorptive surface and slower epithelial turnover. Such structural enhancements are critical for maximising nutrient uptake and overall growth efficiency. Similar improvements have been reported when broilers were fed fermented or enzyme‐supplemented diets. For example, Hafeez et al. (2025a) demonstrated that feeding fermented guar meal enhanced nutrient digestibility through marked increases in villus height and width, while Feng et al. (2007) observed that fermented cottonseed meal reduced crypt depth, a hallmark of improved gut health. In particular, the enhanced crude protein and amino acid digestibility reported by Hu et al. (2020) and Park et al. (2020) suggests that protease supplementation can improve the availability of nutrients required for intestinal epithelial maintenance and growth.

The present results therefore align with a consistent body of evidence showing that either fermentation or targeted enzyme addition can remodel intestinal architecture to favour nutrient absorption. Mechanistically, these changes likely reflect several converging factors. First, extrusion of RSM reduces antinutritional compounds such as glucosinolates and NSPs, lessening mucosal irritation and antigenic stimulation (Elbaz et al. 2023). Second, protease supplementation decreases the amount of undigested protein reaching the hindgut, thereby limiting protein fermentation and the generation of toxic peptides and ammonia that can damage the mucosa (Akram et al. 2025). Third, improved digestibility supplies more amino acids and energy to enterocytes, supporting villus growth and maintenance (Hafeez et al. 2025b). Finally, the favourable shifts in caecal microbiota observed in this study—higher Lactobacillus and Bacteroides, lower Clostridium and Escherichia coli—likely reduce local inflammation and further promote epithelial integrity.

In the present study, diets containing ERSM with protease significantly increased the abundance of beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus and Bacteroides, while reducing populations of Clostridium and Escherichia coli (p < 0.05). This microbial modulation reflects an improved gastrointestinal environment and a lowered pathogen load. Similar outcomes have been documented when fermented or enzyme‐treated rapeseed products were fed to broilers. For example, Dastar et al. (2025) reported that fermentation of RSM elevated lactic acid–producing bacteria and suppressed Coliform counts compared with raw rapeseed diets.

Mechanistically, extrusion and protease supplementation likely enhance the availability of fermentable substrates and reduce ANFs, fostering the proliferation of lactic acid bacteria (LAB). Fermented or enzyme‐processed feeds are characterised by elevated lactic acid concentrations and naturally high LAB counts (Su et al. 2024). The lactic acid produced lowers gut pH, creating conditions that favour LAB colonisation while inhibiting pathogens such as Salmonella and Coliforms. LAB also generate short‐chain fatty acids that further acidify the intestinal milieu and exert direct antimicrobial effects, reinforcing a balanced, health‐promoting microbiota.

Conclusion

Partial replacement of SBM with ERSM supplemented with protease effectively enhanced broiler performance, nutrient digestibility, carcass yield, gut morphology and caecal microbial balance. Inclusion of 5% ERSM with protease consistently produced the most favourable outcomes, improving feed conversion, breast and thigh yield, villus height and beneficial Lactobacillus and Bacteroides populations while lowering Clostridium and Escherichia coli counts. Increasing ERSM beyond 7.5% provided no additional benefit and, at 10%, reduced liver weight and tended to reverse some improvements. These results indicate that ERSM at about 5% of the diet, combined with protease, is a sustainable and effective alternative protein source for broiler finisher diets, supporting both growth efficiency and gut health without adverse effects on carcass quality.
