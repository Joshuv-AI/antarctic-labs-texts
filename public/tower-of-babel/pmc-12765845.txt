Complete neutralizing antibody evasion by serodivergent non-mammalian AAVs enables gene therapy redosing
Authors: Ezra J. Loeb, Sophia A. Fergione, Vivian Yudistyra, Marco M. Fanous, Abigail R. Benkert, Delaney G. Fisher, Joshua A. Hull, Mai K. ElMallah, Aravind Asokan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe use of adeno-associated virus (AAV) as a gene therapy vector is significantly limited by pre-existing immunity. The high seroprevalence and broad antigenic cross-reactivity of primate-derived AAVs restrict patient eligibility and preclude therapeutic redosing. Here, we harness the phylogenetic diversity of non-mammalian dependoparvoviruses to engineer serologically distinct AAV capsids for immune evasion. A barcoded screen of divergent Dependoparvovirus isolates identifies AAV.div3A, a chimeric capsid with robust transduction, zero antigenic cross-reactivity, and undetectable seroprevalence. Derived from a phylogenetically distant Muscovy duck isolate, AAV.div3A fully evades neutralization in mice, even after passive immunization with NAb+ human serum or following initial vector dosing. Further engineering yields AAV.div3A-M1, a myotropic, liver-detargeted capsid with enhanced cardiac and diaphragm transduction. In a Pompe disease model, redosing with AAV.div3A or div3A-M1 significantly increases therapeutic GAA levels. Overall, our work leverages untapped dependoparvoviral diversity to overcome pre-existing and vector-induced immunity, enabling expansion of patient eligibility and effective redosing.

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is a small, non-enveloped DNA virus commonly used as a gene therapy vector.1,2 The AAV capsid consists of three overlapping proteins—VP1, VP2, and VP3—and packages a 4.7 kilobase genome, flanked by inverted terminal repeats (ITRs).2 The ITRs are the only cis-acting elements required for genome packaging and can be used to package transgenes into the AAV capsid, generating recombinant gene transfer vectors.1 Since 1996, AAV has been used for the treatment of over 180 diseases, across 350 clinical trials. Currently, AAV-based gene therapies have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for six diseases—Leber congenital amaurosis, spinal muscular atrophy, hemophilia A, hemophilia B, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase deficiency.1

The AAV genus, Dependoparvovirus, is a diverse taxon that infects many avian, mammalian, and reptile species.3,4 Despite infecting a wide range of vertebrate hosts, most AAV isolates currently undergoing clinical trials were derived from human or non-human primate tissues.5,6 With 30%–80% of adult humans producing neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) against endemic AAVs, pre-existing immunity poses a major obstacle in the gene therapy field.7,8,9,10 AAV redosing presents a similar challenge since patients develop high-titer and long-lasting NAbs following vector dosing.11,12 Administration of AAV into patients with pre-existing immunity can drastically reduce therapeutic efficacy due to vector neutralization and T cell infiltration in transduced tissue, while also significantly increasing the risk for severe adverse events.5 NAb titers ≥1:100 have been shown to induce a systemic inflammatory response, promote vector uptake by antigen-presenting cells, and induce complement activation, which can lead to severe sequelae such as thrombotic microangiopathy and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome.5,11

The most common approach to managing pre-existing immunity against AAV is excluding seropositive patients from eligibility.11 Given the high prevalence of AAV immunity in most populations, this strategy significantly limits patient access to gene therapy.7,8 A recent meta-analysis found that 45% of AAV trials excluded subjects with anti-AAV NAbs.5 For blood disorders, this figure exceeds 90%.5 Other strategies to mitigate pre-existing immunity include immunosuppressive regimens, such as rituximab for B-cell depletion and rapamycin for T cell suppression.5,11,13 However, these pharmacological agents can have significant and undesirable side effects.5 Moreover, such approaches do not fully mitigate the risk of immune-mediated toxicity, evidenced by several recent patient deaths.14

One approach to overcoming pre-existing immunity is vectorizing AAV isolates of non-primate and non-mammalian origin. These isolates exhibit substantial capsid divergence from primate-derived AAVs, potentially rendering them serologically orthogonal to the primate-derived isolates. Recent structural studies have shown that non-mammalian AAVs, such as snake or avian isolates, have only 8%–15% of surface amino acids conserved, with significant structural variability in surface-exposed loops.15,16 Despite binding to ubiquitous glycans such as sialic acid or galactose, these isolates show significantly reduced antibody binding.15,16 Previously, the applicability of non-mammalian AAV isolates was limited due to low transduction efficiency in human cells.17,18 Recently, however, we discovered that a capsid luminal N-terminal domain from the VP1 protein of primate AAVs, when swapped onto non-mammalian capsids, can unlock robust transduction in human cells and mouse tissue.18

Here, we apply this discovery to engineer immune-evasive AAV capsids for gene therapy applications. Through a barcoded screen of diverse, non-primate Dependoparvovirus isolates, we identified a top-performing chimeric capsid, AAV.div3A, derived from a phylogenetically distant Muscovy duck isolate. The chimeric capsid demonstrated broad tissue tropism and robust transduction in vivo while exhibiting complete serological orthogonality to mouse and human antisera. AAV.div3A further enabled the evasion of pre-existing immunity in mice passively immunized with human serum or previously dosed with AAV9. Rational engineering of AAV.div3A via peptide surface display yielded AAV.div3A-M1, a myotropic and liver-detargeted variant. Critically, in a Pompe disease mouse model, AAV.div3A-based vectors enabled effective redosing of a therapeutic transgene following an initial subtherapeutic AAV9 treatment. These results establish divergent chimeric AAVs as a promising platform for immuno-evasive gene delivery.

Results

Identification of divergent AAV capsids and generation of barcoded library

In order to identify serologically distinct capsids for immuno-evasive gene therapy, we generated a library of diverse and divergent Dependoparvovirus isolates (AAV.div) (Figures 1A and 1B). To determine which capsids to include in the library, we performed a principal-component analysis of the VP1 sequence of 435 dependoparvoviruses, followed by K-means clustering (Figure 1C). This analysis yielded three distinct clusters—primate isolates, anseriform isolates, and diverse isolates with little similarity to the other two groups (Figure 1C). To ensure broad phylogenetic and antigenic representation in our library, we examined the VP1 sequences from the non-primate clusters and algorithmically selected isolates to maximize the average distance between the candidates and against primate-derived AAVs. Our final 16 candidates have a maximum VP1 similarity of 60% to primate isolates and a maximum similarity of 73% to one another (Figures S1A and S1B). Next, we selected sequences to use for N-terminal domain swap. Previously, we had shown that non-mammalian AAVs cannot transduce human cells but that transduction can be rescued with a 40 amino acid N-terminal domain from primate AAVs.18 While transduction was similar using an AAV2 or AAV8 N-terminal domain, other N-termini, such as AAV5 or sea lion AAV, altered the properties of the chimera, allowing for transduction of GPR108-deficient cells.18 In order to capture greater sequence diversity and biological variation, we used a matrix approach for our library and evaluated all four of these N-terminal domains with each candidate capsid (Figure S1C).

Overall, we cloned 80 capsids for inclusion in our AAV.div library—each of the 16 candidate isolates with each of the four VP1 N-terminal domains, in addition to the cognate native N-termini (Figure 1D). Further, we cloned a unique barcoded ITR plasmid for each capsid, using self-complementary CBh-GFP with a 10-nucleotide barcode in its 3′ untranslated region (UTR). Our barcoded AAV candidates were then produced individually using triple transfection of HEK293 cells. Media were pooled and then purified, yielding a single AAV library prep.

Barcoded AAV library yields enrichment of capsid candidates

In order to evaluate our AAV.div library, C57BL/6J mice were injected intravenously (i.v.) with a dose of 5e13 viral genomes (vg) per kilogram (Figure 1D). After 2 weeks, mice were sacrificed, and heart, liver, and skeletal muscle tissues were collected. Concomitantly, three cell types—U87, HuH7, and iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (CMs)—were dosed with the capsid library using a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1e5 vg/cell. Cells were harvested after 48 h. RNA and DNA were then extracted from both murine tissue and cell lines. Successful administration as well as overall distribution and transgene expression of the library were confirmed by qPCR (Figures S2A and S2B).

Next, we assessed the performance of individual capsid candidates. By quantifying the relative abundance of each barcode in the 3′ UTR of viral transcripts, we can determine the relative transduction of every capsid in each tissue and cell type. Thus, we amplified the viral barcode from each RNA sample, as well as the parental library, and performed next-generation sequencing. We then deconvolved the sequencing data and calculated the enrichment of each capsid from the parental library (Table S1, Data S1). As expected, significant enrichment and depletion of AAV capsids were observed, following a log-normal distribution (Figures 1E and 1F).

To validate the results from our pooled library screen, the top candidate capsids were produced individually with a single-stranded luciferase genome (ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP) and screened for transduction efficiency. While titers varied substantially by capsid, our lead Muscovy duck capsid—AAV.div3A—was similar in yield and vector quality to an AAV9 benchmark (Figures 2C, S3A, and S3B). Notably, the relative transduction efficiencies of individual capsids mirrored the rank order from the pooled library screen, indicating strong concordance between library-based enrichment and recombinant vector performance (Figure S3C).

Among the individually assessed capsids, transduction was the greatest with AAV.div3A, the Muscovy duck/AAV2 chimera. This finding is particularly notable due to its substantial sequence divergence from other AAVs. The Muscovy duck isolate shares only 59% VP1 similarity to primate-derived isolates, 61% similarity to our previously studied avian DA-1 isolate, and 57% similarity to Muscovy duck parvovirus (MDPV) (Table S2). In silico structural modeling reveals that the Muscovy duck capsid displays extensive structural divergence from primate-derived capsids (Figures 2D and S4). Along the 5-fold axis of symmetry, the HI loop shows significant deviation in backbone conformation, driven by the substitution of hydrophilic side chains (Figure S4E). Substantial perturbations were also observed in the surface-exposed variable regions (VRs), especially those forming the 3-fold axis (Figures 2D and S4). These structural changes result in a blunted surface topology, primarily driven by deletions within VR4 and insertions within VR8 (Figure S4G). Additional remodeling is also observed along the base of the 3-fold axis—with insertions in VR6 and VR7—and along the 2-fold axis, with deletions in VR1 and insertions in VR3 and VR9 (Figures S4D–S4F). Notably, these surface perturbations overlap with regions known to mediate receptor engagement and NAb recognition in primate-derived AAV capsids.19

AAV.div3A displays robust transduction in vivo and in vitro

To further evaluate AAV.div3A, transduction was assessed in a panel of diverse cell lines, including both primary and transformed cells. AAV.div3A showed robust transduction in all of the tested cell types, across multiple time points and MOIs, suggesting that the capsid can utilize ubiquitous mechanisms for cell entry (Figures S5A–S5C). Previously, we had shown that chimeric avian DA-1 (chicken/AAV8) relies upon the canonical AAV receptor (AAVR) for transduction, an endosome escape factor required by most AAV isolates.18,20 AAV.div3A, however, does not appear to utilize AAVR for transduction (Figures S5D–S5F). To the contrary, a significant increase in transduction is observed when AAVR is knocked out, although the magnitude varies markedly by cell type—1.4-fold enhancement in HEK293 cells and 165-fold in HuH7 cells (Figures S5D–S5F).

To evaluate in vivo transduction, mice were injected i.v. with either AAV.div3A or AAV9 packaging ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP at a dose of 5e13 vg/kg and sacrificed 6 weeks later. Robust luciferase transgene expression was observed in heart, skeletal muscle, and liver tissue, with expression also observed in the brain, kidney, and spleen (Figure 2E). For most tissue types, there were no statistically significant differences between AAV.div3A and an AAV9 benchmark, although lower transduction was observed relative to AAV9 in heart tissue (Figure 2E). Trends were also similar at a lower dose of 5e12 vg/kg, with no statistically significant differences between AAV.div3A and AAV9 (Figure S6A). Notably, no transduction was observed with Muscovy duck AAV incorporating its wild-type N-terminus. Rather, AAV.div3A requires a chimeric N-terminal domain derived from mammalian capsids to transduce human cells or mouse tissue (Figures S6B and S6C). This further corroborates our earlier findings with avian AAV and highlights the key role that the VP1 N-terminal domain plays in restricting host range.18

AAV.div3A exhibits a distinct serological profile

To characterize the serological profile of AAV.div3A, we evaluated neutralization by antisera from mice immunized with commonly used primate-derived AAV capsids. Promisingly, AAV.div3A showed no neutralization or cross-reactivity with any antisera (Figures 3A and S7A–S7C). AAV.div3A was also serologically distinct from other non-mammalian chimeric AAVs (Figures S7D–S7F). On the other hand, commonly used primate isolates—such as AAV8, AAV9, rh10, and rh74—showed significant cross-reactivity with one another and were neutralized by heterologous serum samples (Figures 3A and S7A–S7C). To validate that AAV.div3A antibody escape is not limited to mouse serum, we additionally evaluated the cross-reactivity of AAV.div3A with serum from five seropositive non-human primates. To assess the human seroprevalence of AAV.div3A, serum from 20 donors was screened for NAbs via luciferase transduction assay (Figure 3B). While six of the donors were positive for NAbs against AAV9, all donors were negative for NAbs against AAV.div3A (Figures 3C–3E). In fact, with AAV9 NAb+ samples, AAV.div3A transduction was up to 10,000-fold higher than AAV9 (Figure S8E). Interestingly, the transduction of both AAV9 and AAV.div3A was significantly increased in non-neutralizing samples, relative to a no-serum control. As previously reported, we observed that the serum-mediated increase in transduction was caused by serum albumin.21 When albumin was removed by ultrafiltration, the serum-dependent increase in transduction was eliminated, whereas when albumin was supplemented into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), we observed a similar dose-dependent increase in transduction (Figure S8F).

AAV.div3A evades pre-existing neutralizing antibodies and vector-induced immunity

In order to assess the immune evasion of AAV.div3A in vivo, we performed a passive immunization experiment. Mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with serum from one of four AAV9 NAb+ donors or one of five AAV9 NAb− donors (Figures 4A and 4B). Seventy-two hours post-immunization, mice were dosed i.v. with 5e13 vg/kg AAV.div3A or AAV9 packaging ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP and sacrificed 6 weeks later. After passive immunization, there was a significant decrease in AAV9 transduction, with transgene expression dropping below background levels in heart, muscle, and brain tissues (Figures 4C–4E and S9A). In contrast, no change in transduction was observed for AAV.div3A, with robust transduction significantly above AAV9 observed in passively immunized mice (Figures 4C–4E and S9A). The results were strikingly different, however, after in vivo evaluation (Figures 6C–6F and S10E–S10L). Mice were injected i.v. with the peptide insert capsids at a dose of 5e13 vg/kg and sacrificed 6 weeks later. Unlike the cell culture studies, transduction was not significantly increased with div3A-M4 in vivo. However, we observed markedly enhanced transduction in heart and diaphragm tissue with a different peptide insert capsid—AAV.div3A-M1 (Figures 6C and 6D). Incorporating the RGD peptide from an AAV9-based capsid previously engineered by our group, AAV.div3A-M1 exhibited cardiac transgene expression 14-fold higher than AAV.div3A and diaphragm expression 12-fold higher (Figure 6G). Notably, AAV.div3A-M1 also displays significant liver detargeting, with a 93% reduction in liver transgene levels (Figure 6E). Liver detargeting was confirmed by qPCR analysis, which shows a 95% reduction in liver vector genome copies compared to AAV.div3A (Figure S11A). Cardiac expression using AAV.div3A-M1 was 13-fold higher than liver expression, suggesting a distinct myotropism (Figure 6H). Interestingly, the M1 peptide only conferred this myotropic effect when inserted into the VR8 region of AAV.div3A and had no effect when inserted into the VR4 region (Figure S11B). Moreover, RGD peptides derived from other published myotropic capsids, such as AAV-MYO (M1) and MyoAAVs 2A (M3), 4A (M4), and 4E (M5), showed no significant enhancement of transduction in muscle tissue when incorporated into AAV.div3A (Figures S10H and S10L).23,24

Further evaluation of non-RGD peptides, however, identified several additional capsids with altered in vivo tropism. AAV.div3A-M6 displayed 4-fold enhanced transduction in skeletal muscle, while AAV.div3A-Br3 showed 2-fold enhanced transduction across brain regions (Figures S11C and S11D; Table S2). When tested against a panel of antiserum samples from mice immunized with common clinical AAV vectors, AAV.div3A peptide insert capsids exhibited no neutralization or cross-reactivity (Figure S12A). They were neutralized, however, by AAV.div3A antiserum, indicating that peptide insertion alone does not confer antibody escape (Figure S12A).

AAV.div3A enables therapeutic redosing in a Pompe disease mouse model

Next, we investigated the use of AAV.div3A for therapeutic redosing in a Pompe disease model, a monogenic disorder caused by autosomal recessive mutations in the acid alpha-glucosidase gene (GAA).26 Redosing for Pompe disease is clinically important due to the early onset of the disease—AAV dosing in children can lead to loss of transgene expression due to episome dilution and transgene silencing.27,28,29 To model this scenario, juvenile GAA−/− mice were injected i.v. with 2e12 vg/kg AAV9, packaging ss-CBh-GAA (Figure 7A). After 4 weeks, mice received a second injection of 2e13 vg/kg AAV9, AAV.div3A, or div3A-M1 vector. Mice that received a secondary dose of AAV9 experienced a significant loss of transgene expression and a corresponding reduction in vector genome copy number (Figures 7B–7E and S12B). In contrast, noticeably higher levels of GAA were observed in mice administered AAV.div3A or div3A-M1 as a second dose following initial AAV9 treatment (Figures 7B–7E). Compared to the single-dose treatment, enzyme levels were most improved in diaphragm and heart tissue, with a modest increase in the liver. We also observed further enhancement of GAA expression with AAV.div3A-M1, corroborating the myotropic nature of this capsid. Redosing with AAV.div3A-M1 boosted GAA enzyme levels 9-fold in diaphragm tissue and 7-fold in cardiac tissue, compared to a single-dose AAV9 control (Figures 7B and 7C). Discussion

The genus Dependoparvovirus infects a wide range of vertebrate species, including mammalian, avian, and reptile hosts.3,4 These isolates display significant variability in capsid structure, receptor usage, dependence on helper viruses, and intracellular function. This genetic diversity, however, has remained inaccessible to the gene therapy field due to transduction barriers that restrict host range.18 Recently, we found that substituting a minimal N-terminal domain from primate-derived AAVs enables efficient transduction in non-mammalian isolates.18 This simple yet effective modification allows us to unlock the untapped diversity within the genus for gene therapy applications. Here, we leverage divergent Dependoparvovirus sequences to engineer serologically orthogonal chimeric AAV capsids capable of evading pre-existing immunity.

Using a barcoded library approach, we identified AAV.div3A as a lead candidate, which shows potent transduction across multiple tissue and cell types. While the primary receptor and glycan attachment factors utilized by AAV.div3A remain to be determined, our results demonstrate that the capsid does not rely upon AAVR—an endosome-to-Golgi trafficking receptor required for transduction by most AAV isolates.20,30 To our knowledge, Muscovy duck AAV is the first natural isolate outside of the AAV4 clade shown to be AAVR-independent.20,30 Further investigation of the cellular uptake mechanisms of AAV.div3A is ongoing.

Examining the immunological profile of AAV.div3A, we found that the capsid exhibits no antigenic cross-reactivity with commonly used primate-derived isolates and is capable of evading NAbs in vitro and in vivo. Given these properties, its potential for gene transfer was tested in mice immunized with an initial dose of AAV9, a clinically used AAV with high seroprevalence.9 Strikingly, AAV.div3A transgene levels were undiminished in immunized mice. In fact, increased transduction was observed in the mouse liver following immunization with AAV9. This observation mirrors previous findings on antibody-dependent enhancement of viral infection, which can occur directly through immune-complex-mediated uptake or indirectly via modulation of the immune environment.31 Prior studies demonstrate that AAV transduction is enhanced by pro-inflammatory signaling, which may be elevated in mice that have previously received AAV9, leading to increased transduction upon dosing with a serodistinct vector, such as AAV.div3A.32

The unique immunological profile of AAV.div3A arises from its evolutionary distance from mammalian AAVs. Originally derived from a Muscovy duck isolate, AAV.div3A has a highly divergent capsid sequence with only 59% VP1 similarity to primate AAVs and 57% similarity to Muscovy duck parvovirus (MDPV). Unlike MDPV, Muscovy duck AAV is helper-virus dependent, sharing key replication features with mammalian AAVs that enable vector production at high titers. Much like other avian AAVs, however, Muscovy duck AAV lacks inherent human tropism and must undergo N-terminal swap in order to unlock transduction.

Paradoxically, the inability of non-mammalian AAVs to transduce human cells without N-terminal modification is precisely what constitutes the main advantage of chimeric AAVs, such as AAV.div3A. The lack of natural human tropism implies that these dependoparvoviruses likely do not circulate in the population and have undetectable seroprevalence. While some studies have reported successful redosing of mice using capsids such as AAV3 and AAV5,33,34 these capsids still have high human seroprevalence as naturally circulating isolates—approximately 60% and 40%, respectively.10 Moreover, the capsids show high co-prevalence, with 85% of individuals positive for AAV3 NAbs also testing positive for AAV5 NAbs.10 This highlights how the use of endemic primate-derived isolates presents an inherent challenge with pre-existing immunity. Other groups have suggested using bovine, porcine, or caprine AAV isolates to evade NAbs.35 These capsids, while not primate-derived, possess high similarity to AAV5, a human-derived isolate, and have undergone recombination events with several other primate AAVs that have high seroprevalence.35

Prior work on capsid immune evasion has focused on mutating specific surface residues to avoid antibody recognition.36,37,38,39 While point mutations can enable escape from certain monoclonal antibodies or antisera at low titers in cell-based assays or passive immunization experiments, they are largely ineffective against the high titer, polyclonal antibody response observed upon AAV vector dosing in animal models and patients.36,37,38,39 With AAV.div3A, we demonstrate robust evasion of high-titer antiserum in vitro and vector-induced immunity in vivo.

In the context of cell-mediated immunity, AAV redosing can lead to a complete loss of transgene expression due to reactivation and expansion of T memory cells, generated by the initial AAV dose.13,29 Surface mutagenesis does not prevent T cell recognition of capsid peptides displayed by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules, which can mediate loss of transgene expression and toxicity. While a full characterization of cell-mediated immunity is ongoing, loss of transgene expression and a reduction in vector copy number was indeed observed after repeat dosing of AAV9 ss-CBh-GAA. In contrast, no loss of expression or vector copy number was observed when using AAV.div3A as a secondary dose, potentially indicating the absence of a cytotoxic T cell response. This aligns with recent findings that replacing the immunodominant AAV capsid epitopes can attenuate the cell-mediated immune response to vector dosing.40

In considering the cell-mediated response to AAV.div3A, a key question is whether the primate-derived N-terminal domain might be processed and presented by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I molecules, triggering recognition by pre-existing memory T cells. However, an analysis of published datasets of AAV capsid T cell epitopes suggests that this is unlikely. No reactive peptides corresponding to the 40 amino acid N-terminal segment used in AAV.div3A were identified in these studies.41,42 Furthermore, this region was not identified as an immunodominant HLA-I epitope—only 1 out of 65 presented capsid peptides mapped to the N-terminus.43 For HLA-II, peptides matching the N-terminal domain comprised just 46 out of 2,627 AAV peptides.44 In contrast, the full VP1/2 unique region contained 768 matching peptides, 29% of all presented from the AAV capsid.44 This stark disparity underscored the importance of using only the minimal N-terminus when engineering chimeric capsids, as opposed to the larger swap that contains a highly immunogenic phospholipase A2 domain.45 Recent work demonstrates that avian phospholipase A2 domains contain structural changes that may reduce immunogenicity, as well as enhance trafficking and nuclear import.46

Much like natural serotypes, AAV.div3A is amenable to improvements using rational engineering. Insertion of peptides into the capsid variable regions is a widely used strategy for conferring tissue-specific tropism.47 Previous studies have utilized RGD-containing peptides in order to promote integrin binding and enhance muscle-specific tropism.23,24,25 To this end, five RGD-containing peptides—engineered by our group and others—were inserted into the VR8 region of AAV.div3A for evaluation. One candidate, AAV.div3A-M1, showed a significant myotropic effect in vivo, while also detargeting liver transduction. Notably, no myotropic phenotype was observed when incorporating RGD peptides from other published myotropic capsids, such as AAV-MYO and MyoAAV. This suggests that the context in which a peptide insert evolved is critical to its overall effect on the capsid.23,24

Using previously published data on AAV-MYO as a benchmark,23 AAV.div3A-M1 shows a similar level of liver detargeting, while achieving increased transduction in the heart, relative to a parental baseline. Unlike AAV-MYO, AAV.div3A-M1 was engineered using an immuno-evasive parental capsid, allowing for the evasion of pre-existing antibodies in a myotropic context. To highlight this application, redosing of our engineered chimeric capsid was then evaluated in a clinically relevant Pompe disease model. After redosing with AAV.div3A, significantly increased GAA enzyme levels were observed, while enhanced expression in cardiac and diaphragm tissue was further achieved using the peptide-insert variant, AAV.div3A-M1.

It is important to note that peptide inserts, while useful from an application standpoint, are not capable of conferring antibody evasion when incorporated into AAV capsids. While valuable in altering AAV tropism by exploiting different receptors, peptide inserts do not alter capsid surface diversity and serodivergence to the robust extent required for redosing applications. This highlights the importance of performing peptide insertion mutagenesis on serodistinct, immuno-evasive capsids, such as AAV.div3A.

Taken together, our study illustrates how leveraging deep evolutionary divergence, coupled with rational capsid engineering, can yield serologically divergent AAV vectors with robust performance in vivo. In this study, we characterize AAV.div3A-based capsids as an initial proof of concept for vectorizing serodistinct capsids for the evasion of pre-existing and vector-induced immunity. Overall, our AAV.div platform shows potential for expanding patient access to gene therapies and offers a viable strategy for secondary dosing. This is particularly critical for patients who have already received gene therapy but have experienced sub-therapeutic effects or loss of therapeutic transgene expression due to growth, degeneration, immune-related vector loss, or transgene silencing.48 Ongoing work focuses on expanding the AAV.div portfolio, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying receptor usage, epigenetic regulation of vector genomes, and immune recognition in different species, with the ultimate goal of clinical translation.

Limitations of the study

It is noteworthy to reflect that the current study has certain limitations. Although serologically distinct, AAV.div3A does not exhibit decreased immunogenicity. As with other AAV vectors, this capsid elicits a de novo humoral response that prevents effective repeat dosing of the same capsid. Instead, AAV.div3A is best suited for therapeutic gene transfer in patients with pre-existing NAbs, who would otherwise be excluded from treatment, or potentially in individuals requiring secondary AAV dose following an initial dose of a different vector, which may have yielded a sub-therapeutic or waning outcome.48 In addition, our current results are based upon studies in mouse models. Additional evaluation of AAV.div3A-based capsids in large animal models, such as non-human primates, is warranted prior to clinical translation. Redosing of AAV.div vectors in non-human primates will help better model the impact of human-like immune responses following vector administration. The timing of AAV redosing against the backdrop of immune memory and cellular immunity also requires further investigation.

Experimental model and subject details

Cell lines and primary cells

Cell lines were authenticated by morphology prior to use and were subject to periodic mycoplasma testing. The sexes of utilized cell lines and primary cells are as follows: HEK293 (female), C2C12 (female), U87 (male), HuH7 (male), iPSC-derived human cardiomyocytes (male), RD (female), TH1 (unspecified), A549 (male). All cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cell lines were maintained using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (4.5 g/L D-glucose, 110 mg/L sodium pyruvate), supplemented with 5–10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. iPSC-derived human cardiomyocytes were maintained on Matrigel-coated dishes using RPMI-1640 with 1X B27 Supplement.

Mouse model experiments

Animal procedures were performed in accordance with protocol A186-22-10 under the Institutional Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Duke University, accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC). C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (#000664) and GAA (−/−) B6:129 mice were generously provided by Dr. Mai Elmallah (Duke University). All mice were maintained at the Duke University School of Medicine with the assistance of Duke’s Division of Laboratory Animal Resources (DLAR). Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled (18°C–23°C, 40–60% humidity) and enriched environment, with a 12h light/hour dark cycle. Mice were acclimated to the habitation environment for four to six weeks prior to experimentation. Standard food/water were available ad libitum. Mouse sacrifice was performed with cardiac exsanguination under anesthesia with tribromoethanol.

Method details

Phylogenetic analysis

Genomes for all available Dependoparvovirus isolates were obtained from NCBI GenBank. Isolates with complete Rep78 sequences (n = 294) were aligned using MUSCLE,50 and a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was inferred with IQ-TREE.51 Branches were subsequently collapsed into monophyletic clades based on sequence identity and host species.

Selecting library candidates

For isolates with complete VP1 sequences (n = 435), proteins were embedded using one-hot encoding. VP1 embeddings were dimensionally reduced via principal component analysis and clustered by k-means. Within the resulting non-primate clusters, VP1 sequences were then aligned using MUSCLE and pairwise similarity was calculated.50 Final candidates were selected using a farthest-first traversal algorithm to maximize both inter-candidate VP1 distance and distance from primate-derived AAVs. Starting from an arbitrary seed sequence, VP1 sequences were added to the set of candidates if they had the lowest maximum similarity to any member of the current set of candidates. This procedure was then repeated until 16 sequences had been selected.

Structure prediction and modeling

VP1 structures for AAV9, AAV.div3A, and peptide insert capsids were predicted with AlphaFold3 and visualized using ChimeraX.52,53 Full capsid structure for AAV.div3A was generated with CapBuild using AlphaFold3 VP3 structure and visualized using ChimeraX.52,53,54 VP3 and full capsid structure for AAV9 was derived from PDB 7MT0.55

Generation of iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes

The human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) line DM-03 was maintained under feeder-free conditions on Matrigel-coated plates (Corning) in mTeSR medium (Stemcell Technologies). DM-03 hiPSCs were passaged as small clusters of cells every 5 days. Differentiation into cardiomyocytes (CMs) was then performed via small molecule-based modulation of Wnt signaling. Briefly, DM-03 hiPSCs were plated into 10-cm Matrigel-coated dishes at a density of 2.84e6 cells/dish using mTeSR with 5 μM Y27632 (Stemcell Technologies).56 To induce cardiac differentiation, cells were incubated in 8 μM CHIR99021 (Stemcell Technologies) in RPMI-1640 (Thermo Fisher) with B27(−) insulin (Thermo Fisher). Exactly 24 h later, CHIR99021-containing media was replaced with RPMI-1640, 5 μM IWR-1 (Stemcell Technologies), 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid and B27(−) insulin. This was repeated again on day 3. On day 4, media was replaced with RPMI-1640 containing only 5 μM IWR-1 and B27(−) insulin. From day 6 onwards, cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 with B27(+) insulin (Thermo Fisher). Spontaneous contraction of CMs began on days 5–7 of differentiation. At the end of the differentiation period, cultures were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using Accutase (Stemcell Technologies) and re-plated onto fresh Matrigel-coated dishes to remove dead cells and debris. Cells were then maintained in RPMI-1640 with B27(+) insulin. CMs were then seeded in a Matrigel-coated 96-well plate at a density of 4e4 cells/well 24 h prior to experimental use.

Generation of AAVR knockout cell line

Recombinant lentivirus was produced by triple transfection of HEK293 cells. Cells were transfected with lentiCRISPRv2 plasmid, encoding guide RNAs against AAVR, psPAX2 packaging plasmid, and pVSVG envelope plasmid, using a DNA:PEI (Polysciences) ratio of 1:3.5. Lentivirus-containing media was harvested at 48 h post-transfection. HuH7 and HEK293 cells were seeded at 1e5-3e5 cells/well in a 6-well plate and transduced with lentivirus in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene for 10 min. Plates were then centrifuged for 30 min at 400 g for spinoculation and incubated for 2–4 h at 37°C. Media was then replaced with fresh lentivirus-containing media without polybrene. At 48 h post-transduction, cells were selected with puromycin (1–2 μg/mL) for 7 days. Single cell clonal lines were subsequently generated by serial dilution and expanded for downstream assays.

AAV vector production

Recombinant AAV vectors were produced by triple transfection of adherent HEK293 cells. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium with 5% fetal bovine serum. At 90% confluence, cells were transfected with 12 μg adenovirus helper plasmid (pXX680), 10 μg pLH-RepCap plasmid, and 6 μg ITR transfer plasmid, per 15-cm plate. Transfection was performed using polyethyleneimine (PEI) (Polysciences) at a ratio of 3.5 μg PEI per 1 μg DNA. Cells were maintained in a 37°C CO2 incubator and media was harvested on days 3 and 7 post-transfection and pooled. To precipitate AAV vector, PEG-8000 (40% PEG-8000, 2.5 M NaCl) was added to the collected media to a final concentration of 8%, followed by incubation at 4°C overnight. The PEG-treated media was centrifuged at 4,000 g for 1 h to pellet the virus-containing flocculate. PEG pellets were resuspended in 2 mL DNase buffer (10 mM tris-HCL, 10 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2) and treated with 20 μL DNase I (Sigma Aldrich; 10 mg/mL). For vector purification, an iodixanol density gradient was prepared by layering 3 mL 17% iodixanol, 3 mL 25% iodixanol, 4 mL 40% iodixanol, and 3 mL 60% iodixanol in a clear ultracentrifuge tube (Beckman-Coulter). Vector solution was layered on top the gradient and ultracentrifuged at 30,000 rpm for 15 h. Post-centrifugation, the top two layers were discarded and the 40% iodixanol fraction was collected and diluted into 45 mL formulation buffer (PBS with 0.001% Pluronic F68 and 1 mM MgCl2). AAV vector was then buffer-exchanged by diafiltration using a 100 kDa protein concentrator (Thermo Fisher) with additional formulation buffer. Final vector genome titers were determined by qPCR (see below).

AAV vector quantification

AAV samples were treated with DNase I, along with standards and negative controls, to remove non-encapsidated DNA. 10 μL of each sample was combined with 89 μL DNase I buffer (10 mM tris-HCL, 10 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2) and 1 μL DNase I (10 mg/mL; Sigma Aldrich) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. DNase I was inactivated by addition of 100 μL EDTA (20 mM). Samples were then diluted 1:100 in 0.05% Tween 20 (Sigma Aldrich). AAV standards were prepared by serial 3-fold dilution of a dPCR validated vector standard in 0.05% Tween 20. In a qPCR plate, 2 μL of each sample or standard was combined with 5 μL PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher), 2.5 μL nuclease-free water, and 0.5 μL ITR primer mix (10 μM each primer, see key resources table). Quantitative PCR was performed using a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems), with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min to disrupt capsid structure, followed by 45 amplification cycles with an annealing temperature of 62°C.

Mass photometry

Empty-full capsid ratios were calculated by mass photometry using the Refeyn SamuxMP system. For focus calibration, 10–18 μL of PBS were added to each well and then focus determined. After focusing, the volume of each well was brought to 20 μL with virus. A 1 min video was recorded on AcquireMP software (Refeyn) and analyzed using DiscoverMP software (Refeyn). For calibration of mass, AAV9 empty particles produced in a Rep-free system and used as an empty control (3800 kDa), while wild-type AAV9 virus was used as the full standard (5250 kDa). After calibration, gating was performed using an automatic protocol, based on transgene length.

Barcoded library production

To avoid cross-packaging or formation of mosaic capsids, each library candidate was transfected individually, with 12 μg adenovirus helper plasmid (pXX680), 10 μg pLH-RepCap plasmid, and 6 μg ITR plasmid (pTR-sc-CBh-NLS-GFP-barcode) per 15-cm plate. Transfection was performed using polyethyleneimine (PEI) (Polysciences) at a ratio of 3.5 μg PEI per 1 μg DNA. Two 15-cm plates of HEK293 cells were transfected per capsid, with each plate receiving the appropriate RepCap plasmid and corresponding barcoded ITR plasmid. Media from days 3 and 7 post-transfection were pooled across candidates and concentrated to 400 mL by tangential flow filtration prior to PEG precipitation. AAV was then purified as described above.

Barcoded library selection and processing

Three six-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were injected i.v. with 5e13 vg/kg pooled AAV library, packaging sc-CBh-GFP with a 10 nucleotide barcode in the 3′UTR. In parallel, HuH7 cells, U87 cells, and iPSC-derived human cardiomyocytes were dosed in triplicate with 1e5 vg/cell. Mice were sacrificed 14 days post-injection and cells were harvested 48 h after dosing. RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher) and DNA was isolated using back extraction buffer (4 M guanidine thiocyanate, 50 mM sodium citrate, and 1 M tris). Residual genomic DNA was degraded from RNA samples using the TURBO DNA-Free kit (Invitrogen). Purified RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Applied Biosystems). Viral barcodes were amplified from each cDNA sample, as well as the parental library, by PCR using primers in the 3′UTR (see key resources table) and sequenced using 2 × 250 bp paired-end Illumina sequencing.

Barcoded library data analysis

Library data processing and analysis was performed with a custom python script (Data S1). The frequency of each barcode was calculated independently for each tissue and cell type, and for the parental library with an error correction value of 1. Raw read counts were normalized to the total read depth to obtain the relative abundance of each capsid variant within each tissue. The enrichment score E for each variant was computed as:E=(Rselected,i÷Rselected,total)(Rparental,i÷Rparental,total)Where Rselected,i and Rparental,i represent the read counts for barcode i in the selected and the parental library, respectively. To minimize the effect of low-abundance barcodes exhibiting artificially inflated enrichment values, capsids representing less than 0.015% of total reads in the parental library were excluded from further analysis. Based on this criteria, three capsids were excluded from final analysis—brown rat AAV, Chinese rufous horseshoe bat AAV, and marsupial AAV. p-values were calculated for each capsid using an unpaired two-tailed t test, comparing the biological triplicates of each capsid’s relative abundance in the selected library to its input frequency in the parental library.

qPCR library analysis

Viral genomes and transgene RNA were quantified by qPCR of extracted gDNA and cDNA with primers against the GFP transgene (see key resources table). Expression levels were normalized to endogenous RPL13A per tissue using ΔΔCt normalization.

AAV titer screen

HEK293 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at a density of 6e5 cells/mL and grown in a 37°C CO2 incubator overnight. Each well was transfected with 300 ng adenovirus helper plasmid (pXX680), 250 ng pLH-RepCap plasmid, and 150 ng ITR transfer plasmid (pTR-ss-EF1α-TdTomato-WPRE-PolyA). Transfection was performed using 3.5 μg PEI (Polysciences) per 1 μg DNA. 24 h post-transfection, media was replaced to remove residual untransfected DNA. At day 5 post-transfection, cells and media were harvested. For cell lysis, 5 μL 10% Triton X-100 (Fisher Scientific), 10 μL Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher), 10 μL DNase I (10 mg/mL; Sigma Aldrich), and 10 μL RNase A (10 mg/mL; Thermo Fisher) were added per milliliter of cell-media suspension. Samples were vigorously vortexed and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Samples were then titered as described above, using primers against the WPRE element (see key resources table) and an annealing temperature of 62°C.

In vitro luciferase assays

Cells were seeded into opaque white 96-well plates at a density of 2e5 cells/mL and cultured overnight in 37°C CO2 incubator. AAV vector was diluted in formulation buffer (PBS, 0.001% Pluronic F68, 1 mM MgCl2) to the desired MOI. 20 μL of diluted vector was added to each well and plates were incubated at 37°C for 72 h. To measure luciferase activity, media was aspirated from each well and cells were treated with 20 μL 1X passive lysis buffer (Promega) for 30 min at room temperature. Following lysis, 100 μL Luciferase Assay Reagent (Promega) was added to each well, and luminescence was immediately measured using a Varioskan LUX plate reader (Thermo Fisher), with a duration of 1 s per well.

Time and dose-response curve

In vitro luciferase assays were performed, as described above, using 4-fold serial dilutions of each capsid, resulting in MOIs of 2.5e5, 5e4, 1e4, and 2e3 vg/cell. Luciferase activity was measured at 24, 48, and 72 h post-transduction. The resulting data were fit to a Gompertz growth model, assuming Y0 = 0.001 and K shared for all datasets.

Antigenic cross-reactivity

For each capsid, two eight-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were injected i.p. with 5e12 vg/kg AAV packaging ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP. Six weeks post-injection, mice were sacrificed and whole blood was collected by cardiac puncture. Blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 30–60 min and then centrifuged at 1,500 g for 15 min. Serum was then decanted and pooled between biological replicates. Each serum sample was diluted 1:2 using PBS and then mixed in equal volume with each AAV vector, resulting in a final dilution of 1:4 and a final MOI of 1.25e4 vg/cell. Samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 h and then U87 cells were then dosed in triplicate with the serum/virus mixture. Luciferase activity was measured at 72 h post-transduction, as described above. Luminescence values were normalized to a no-serum control for each capsid and averaged across replicates.

Neutralizing antibody assay

Serum samples from human donors were purchased from BioIVT (Woodbury, NY). Rhesus macaque serum samples were gifts from Alice Tarantal (UC Davis, CA). Serum samples were subjected to 2-fold serial dilution in PBS. Each diluted serum sample was mixed in equal volume with AAV vector packaging ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP, resulting in a final MOI of 1.25e4 vg/cell. Sample/virus mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Following incubation, U87 cells were dosed in triplicate with the serum/virus mixture. Luciferase activity was measured 72 h post-transduction, as described above. Luminescence values were normalized to the smallest dilution value (1:128) for each capsid and averaged across replicates. ID50 values were calculated by fitting normalized luminescence to a logistic model.

Serum albumin study

Fetal bovine serum was centrifuged in a 30 kDa protein concentrator to remove high-molecular-weight components, such as albumin. The resulting flow-through was collected (low-molecular-weight FBS). Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was then added to either PBS or low-molecular-weight FBS (LMW-FBS) to a final concentration of 30 mg/mL. The four experimental conditions—FBS, LMW-FBS, PBS with BSA, and LMW-FBS with BSA— were then each mixed with an equal volume of AAV vector, packaging ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP, resulting in a final MOI of 1.25e4 vg/cell. Samples were then incubated at 37°C for 1 h prior to transduction. Following incubation, U87 cells were dosed in triplicate and transduction was measured via luciferase assay at 72 h, as described above. Luminescence was then normalized to a PBS-only control.

In vivo luciferase assays

For in vivo evaluation of capsid transduction, six-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were injected i.v. with 5e13 vg/kg AAV vector, packaging ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP. Six weeks post-injection, mice were sacrificed, and tissues were harvested. Approximately 40 mg of each tissue was excised, weighed, and homogenized in 400 μL of 1X Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega), using 1–3 cycles on a FastPrep-24 homogenizer with Lysing Matrix D (MP Biomedicals). Homogenate was then centrifuged at 21,000 g for 5 min 50 μL of tissue supernatant was transferred to an opaque white 96-well plate. Luciferase activity was measured by adding 100 μL Luciferase Assay Reagent (Promega) to each well and immediately reading luminescence using a Varioskan LUX plate reader (Thermo Fisher), with a duration of 1 s per well. Luminescence values were then normalized to initial tissue mass and reported as relative luminescence units (RLU) per mg.

Passive immunization experiment

Human serum samples, previously screened for AAV9 NAbs, were used for passive immunization experiments. For each donor, 200 μL of serum—either AAV9 NAb+ (n = 4), or AAV9 NAb– (n = 5)—was injected i.p. into four C57BL/6J mice (2 male, 2 female). After 72 h, mice were injected i.v. with 5e13 vg/kg AAV9 or AAV.div3A packaging ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP. Six weeks post-injection, mice were sacrificed, tissues were harvested, and luciferase activity was measured using the methods described above. For each capsid and tissue, luciferase activity for mice receiving AAV9 NAb+ serum was normalized to the mean luciferase activity for mice receiving AAV9 NAb– serum.

Vector-induced immunity experiment

Female C57BL/6J mice were divided into immunized and unimmunized cohorts. Immunized mice were injected i.v. with 5e12 vg/kg AAV9 packaging a non-translated stuffer genome (ss-EF1α-stuffer) while unimmunized received a PBS vehicle injection. After six weeks, both cohorts received a subsequent i.v. dose of 5e12 vg/kg AAV9 or AAV.div3A packaging ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP. Six weeks later, mice were sacrificed, and transduction was measured by luciferase assay, as described above. For analysis, the luciferase activity of immunized mice was normalized to the luciferase activity for unimmunized mice.

Peptide insert engineering

Alignment of capsid sequences and AlphaFold3 predicted structures was used to identify the VR8 region of the AAV.div3A capsid. Using site-directed mutagenesis, three distal amino acids in the VR8 region were replaced with an RGD-containing 10-mer peptide. Capsids were then produced, using methods described above, packaging ss-CBh-Luc-P2A-GFP. In vivo and in vitro transduction was then evaluated, as described above.

Vector copy number analysis

Genomic DNA was purified from heart and liver tissue using the PureLink Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher). Vector genome copy number was determined by qPCR of gDNA (10 ng) using a plasmid standard and primers targeting the SV40 polyadenylation signal. In a qPCR plate, 2 μL of each sample or standard was combined with 5 μL PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher), 2.5 μL nuclease-free water, and 0.5 μL SV40 primer mix (10 μM each primer, see key resources table). Quantitative PCR was performed using a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) and 45 amplification cycles with an annealing temperature of 55°C. Vector genome copy numbers were then normalized per microgram of DNA.

GAA redosing study

At five weeks of age, B6:129 GAA (−/−) mice were injected i.v. with 2e12 vg/kg AAV9 packaging ss-CBh-GAA. Four weeks later, mice received a second i.v. injection with 2e13 vg/kg AAV9, AAV.div3A, or AAV.div3A-M1, packaging ss-CBh-GAA, or with a PBS vehicle (3 female, 2 male per capsid). After six weeks, mice were sacrificed, and tissues were harvested. Approximately 20 mg of each tissue was excised and homogenized in 400 μL molecular grade water with 1x Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher), using 1–3 cycles on a FastPrep-24 homogenizer and Lysing Matrix D (MP Biomedicals). Homogenates were then freeze-thawed 3 times and centrifuged at 21,000 g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and centrifugation was repeated. Supernatant was diluted 1:10 in molecular grade water. GAA activity was then quantified using a fluorometric substrate cleavage assay.57 Briefly, a master mix was prepared with 3 mM 4-methylumbelliferyl-α-D-glucoside (4-MUG) and 200 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.3). 40 μL of each homogenate was combined with 40 μL master mix in an opaque black 96-well plate. Plates were covered and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. While samples were incubating, a standard curve was prepared using 2.5-fold serial dilutions of 4-methylumbelliferone (4-MU), with concentration ranging from 1000 μM to 4 μM. 40 μL of each standard dilution was added to the 96-well plate, along with 40 μL 200 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.3). Following incubation, a stop solution of 500 mM sodium carbonate (pH 10.7) was then added to each well. Fluorescence was measured using a Varioskan LUX plate reader (Thermo Fisher), with 365 nm excitation and 445 nm emission for 0.1 s. GAA activity was calculated by fitting sample fluorescence to a four-parameter logistic curve, based on the 4-MU standards. GAA activity was then normalized to total protein content for each sample, determined by Bradford assay (Thermo Fisher), and reported as nmol/h/mg.

Quantification and statistical analysis

p-values for capsid enrichment were calculated using an independent two-sample t test, comparing the relative frequencies of each barcode between the selected and parental libraries. For non-library data, statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software. Comparisons between two groups were performed with an unpaired two-tailed t test. Comparisons between three or more groups were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) as appropriate. For non-parametric data, a Kruskal-Wallis (KW) test was used. Multiple comparisons were then performed, and statistical significance was determined using a Holm-Sidak posttest (ANOVA) or Dunn’s posttest (KW) with an alpha value of 0.05. The number of biological replicates (n) is specified in the corresponding figure legends. For in vivo studies, data are visualized as box-and-whisker plots, where whiskers represent minimum and maximum values (n ≥ 4) or without whiskers (n = 3). All reported replicates were taken from distinct samples. ns: p > 0.05, ∗: p ≤ 0.05, ∗∗: p ≤ 0.01, ∗∗∗: p ≤ 0.001, ∗∗∗∗: p ≤ 0.0001. Figure error bars represent one standard error.
