An Improved Inference for IV Regressions Thanks: We are grateful to Michal Kolésar for helpful comments. Any and all errors are our own.
Liyu Dou, Pengjin Min, Wenjie Wang, Yichong Zhang

Abstract
Researchers often report empirical results that are based on low-dimensional IVs, such as the shift-share IV, together with many IVs. Could we combine these results in an efficient way and take advantage of the information from both sides? In this paper, we propose a combination inference procedure to solve the problem. Specifically, we consider a linear combination of three test statistics: a standard cluster-robust Wald statistic based on the low-dimensional IVs, a leave-one-cluster-out Lagrangian Multiplier (LM) statistic, and a leave-one-cluster-out Anderson-Rubin (AR) statistic. We first establish the joint asymptotic normality of the Wald, LM, and AR statistics and derive the corresponding limit experiment under local alternatives. Then, under the assumption that at least the low-dimensional IVs can strongly identify the parameter of interest, we derive the optimal combination test based on the three statistics and establish that our procedure leads to the uniformly most powerful (UMP) unbiased test among the class of tests considered. In particular, the efficiency gain from the combined test is of “free lunch” in the sense that it is always at least as powerful as the test that is only based on the low-dimensional IVs or many IVs.

Introduction

Empirical applications of instrumental variables (IV) regressions in economics often involve multiple sets of candidate instruments.
For example, in their seminal paper,
Angrist and Krueger 1991
studied the effect of schooling on wages by using three dummies for the quarter of birth (QoB) as IVs. Then, in pursuit of potential efficiency gain, they further interacted the three QoB instruments with states and years of birth, resulting in a total of 180 instruments (including both QoB and interacted IVs). The estimation and inference results based on the 180 IVs were reported in
Angrist and Krueger 1991
along with those based on the three IVs.
Another example is the shift-share IVs,

Given that researchers often report empirical results that are based on low-dimensional IVs, such as the shift-share IV, together with many IVs, could we combine these results in an efficient way and take advantage of the information from both sides? In this paper, we propose a combination inference procedure to solve this problem. Specifically, we consider a linear combination of three test statistics: a standard cluster-robust Wald statistic based on the low-dimensional IVs, a leave-one-cluster-out Lagrangian Multiplier (LM) statistic, and a leave-one-cluster-out Anderson-Rubin (AR) statistic. The LM and AR statistics are based on many IVs, and the leave-one-cluster-out construction is employed to remove the many-IV bias under within-cluster error dependence. We first establish the joint asymptotic normality of the Wald, LM, and AR statistics and derive the corresponding limit experiment under local alternatives. Then, under the assumption that at least the low-dimensional IVs are able to strongly identify the parameter of interest, we derive the optimal combination test based on the three statistics and establish that our procedure leads to the uniformly most powerful (UMP) unbiased test among the class of tests considered. In particular, the efficiency gain from the combined test is of “free lunch” in the sense that it is always at least as powerful as the test that is only based on the low-dimensional IVs or many IVs. Simulation experiments confirm the good performance of our combined inference procedure.

The power improvement of our combined test originates from two factors: the non-centralities of the Wald, AR, and LM statistics, and their correlation structure.
The improvement from the correlation is important because even if the non-centralities of certain statistics among the three are zero, the statistics may still be useful, depending on the correlation structure. In particular, this implies that power improvement is possible even when the identification strength under the low-dimensional IVs differs (in terms of the order of magnitude of the concentration parameter) from that under many IVs.
For example, suppose the shift-share IV in
Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. 2020
provides strong identification for the parameter of interest while the corresponding many base IVs only provide weak identification, then power improvement over the standard Wald test (which is only based on the shift-share IV) is still possible by using our combined test, because of the correlation among the statistics.

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\Pi_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}+\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2}}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\Pi_{i}P_{ii}P_{W,ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{j}P_{ji}\tilde{e}_{i}\right)^{2}

The test statistic of interest, the leave-one-cluster-out jackknife Lagrangian Multiplier (LM) statistic, is defined as

\displaystyle LM(\beta_{0})=\frac{\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}X_{i}P_{ij}e_{j}(\beta_{0})}{\sqrt{\widehat{\Phi}_{2}}}.

Lastly, as pointed out by
Hausman et al. 2012
and
Lim et al. 2024a
, it is possible to use the jackknife AR statistic to further improve the efficiency of the jackknife LM statistic. In the case with clustered data, we define the leave-one-cluster-out jackknife AR statistic as

\displaystyle AR=\frac{\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\hat{e}_{i}P_{ij}\hat{e}_{j}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\Phi}_{3}}},

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}_{3}=2\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\hat{e}_{i}P_{ij}\hat{e}_{j}\right)^{2}

is a consistent estimator of the asymptotic variance of
\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}
, given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{3}=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)^{2}

Here we use
\hat{e}_{i}
instead of
e_{i}(\beta_{0})
to construct the AR statistic for at least two reasons: (1) the two choices are asymptotically equivalent under strong identification and local alternative, as pointed out by
Lim et al. 2024a
and (2) under strong identification and fixed alternative, using
e_{i}(\beta_{0})
can lead to non-monotonic power curve for the test combined by jackknife AR and LM statistics, as pointed out by
Lim et al. 2024a
, while this can be avoided by using
\hat{e}_{i}
.
We make the following assumptions on the
K
-dimensional many IVs.

Assumption 3
.

The following conditions hold almost surely:

1.

K\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
such that
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{K/n}\leqslant C<1
and
\sqrt{K}/\Pi^{\top}\Pi\rightarrow 0
;

2.

\text{rank}(P)=K
and
\max_{1\leq g\leq G}\lambda_{\max}\left(P_{[g,g]}\right)\leqslant C<1
for all
n
large enough;

3.

Let
\hat{\Pi}=M_{W}(P-\bar{P})\Pi=Q\tilde{\Pi}
and
\bar{\Pi}=(Q-\bar{Q})\tilde{\Pi}
, then

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq g\leq G}\left\|\hat{\Pi}_{[g]}\right\|_{2}^{2}=o(\hat{\Pi}^{\top}\hat{\Pi}),\;\;\max_{1\leq g\leq G}\left\|\bar{\Pi}_{[g]}\right\|_{2}^{2}=o(\bar{\Pi}^{\top}\bar{\Pi}),

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\Pi^{\top}\Pi\leqslant\hat{\Pi}^{\top}\hat{\Pi}\leqslant C\Pi^{\top}\Pi,\;\;\frac{1}{C}\Pi^{\top}\Pi\leqslant\Pi^{\top}(P-\bar{P})\Pi\leqslant C\Pi^{\top}\Pi

for all
n
large enough.

2.4
Consistent Estimation of
\beta

Recall
\hat{e}_{i}=Y_{i}-X_{i}\hat{\beta}
. The optimal combination of
(T(\beta_{0}),LM(\beta_{0}),AR)
crucially relies on the consistency of
\hat{\beta}
.
Throughout the paper, we maintain the assumption that at least one IV strategy (low-dimensional IVs or many IVs) can strongly identify
\beta
, implying that at least one of
(\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{2})
is consistent. However, the researchers may not know which one is consistent. Then, we will construct
\hat{\beta}
as a linear combination of
(\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{2})
where the combination weight depends on the identification strength of two IV strategies.

More specifically, let
\widehat{\Phi}_{1}^{pre}
and
\widehat{\Phi}_{2}^{pre}
be the variance estimators
\widehat{\Phi}_{1}
and
\widehat{\Phi}_{2}
, but where the estimated errors are now constructed using
\hat{\beta}_{1}
and
\hat{\beta}_{2}
respectively. Our combined estimator is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}=\hat{\omega}\hat{\beta}_{1}+(1-\hat{\omega})\hat{\beta}_{2},

with

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}=\frac{\sqrt{\widehat{\Phi}_{2}^{pre}/\left(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}X_{i}P_{ij}X_{j}\right)^{2}}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\Phi}_{1}^{pre}}+\sqrt{\widehat{\Phi}_{2}^{pre}/\left(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}X_{i}P_{ij}X_{j}\right)^{2}}}.

The intuition of the combined estimator is that
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}/\left(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\Pi_{i}P_{ij}\Pi_{j}\right)^{2}
are the asymptotic variance of
\hat{\beta}_{1}-\beta
and
\hat{\beta}_{2}-\beta
under strong identification, and thus they measure the identification strengths of
\hat{\beta}_{1}
and
\hat{\beta}_{2}
.

\widehat{\Phi}_{1}^{pre}
and
\widehat{\Phi}_{2}^{pre}/\left(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}X_{i}P_{ij}X_{j}\right)^{2}
are their consistent estimators, respectively, under strong identification.
If the identification for one of the two IV strategies is weak, but the corresponding variance estimator can be bounded away from zero in a proper sense, then the combination weight for that IV estimator will converge to zero, and thus
\hat{\beta}
will remain consistent. Below, we show formally this combination procedure is valid of at least one IV strategy can strongly identify
\beta
.

3
Inference with Strong Low-Dimensional IVs

In this section, we assume that the low-dimensional IVs are strong so that Assumption
2
holds. When Assumption
3
holds and the
K
-dimensional many IVs have the same (strong) identification strength as the low-dimensional IVs, we propose a combined test that improves the power of the Wald test that is only based on the low-dimensional IVs. Furthermore, we show that when the identification strength of the many IVs is weak, this combined test reduces to the Wald test, and thus there is no loss of efficiency.

3.1
Limiting Problem and Optimal Test

If the low-dimensional IVs and many IVs have the same identification strength, we can define the local alternative based on this identification strength, which enables us to derive the corresponding limiting problem and optimal test.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose there exists a deterministic sequence
d_{n}\downarrow 0
such that

\displaystyle d_{n}\Phi_{1}^{-1/2}\rightarrow a_{1}\neq 0,

\displaystyle d_{n}\frac{\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\Pi_{i}P_{ij}\Pi_{j}}{\sqrt{\Phi_{2}}}\rightarrow a_{2}\neq 0,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\beta-\beta_{0}=\delta d_{n}

for some fixed
\delta
. Assume that the following limits exist (almost surely)

\displaystyle\rho_{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Psi\Phi_{2}}}\sum_{g\in[G]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\acute{\Pi}_{i}\tilde{e}_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\hat{\Pi}_{i}\tilde{e}_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\rho_{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{2}{\sqrt{\Phi_{2}\Phi_{3}}}\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)\right]

and
\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}<1
. Then, under Assumption
1
-
3
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}T(\beta_{0})\\
LM(\beta_{0})\\
AR\end{pmatrix}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\delta\\
a_{2}\delta\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_{1}&0\\
\rho_{1}&1&\rho_{2}\\
0&\rho_{2}&1\end{pmatrix}\right).

Corollary 3.1
.

Suppose one observes
(\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{N}_{2},\mathcal{N}_{3})
, which follow the joint distribution of

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\delta\\
a_{2}\delta\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_{1}&0\\
\rho_{1}&1&\rho_{2}\\
0&\rho_{2}&1\end{pmatrix}\right)

with
\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}<1
, knows the values of
(a_{1},a_{2},\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and the fact that
\mathbb{E}\mathcal{N}_{3}=0
, and wants to test
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\delta=0
v.s.
\mathcal{H}_{1}:\delta\neq 0
, then the uniformly most powerful (UMP) unbiased level-
\alpha

\displaystyle\left(\frac{b_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}+b_{2}\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}+b_{3}\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{3}}{\sqrt{b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}+b_{3}^{2}}}\right)^{2}\geqslant\mathbb{C}_{\alpha},

where

is the

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}\\
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}\\
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_{1}&0\\
\rho_{1}&1&\rho_{2}\\
0&\rho_{2}&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1/2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{N}_{1}\\
\mathcal{N}_{2}\\
\mathcal{N}_{3}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\\
b_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho_{1}&0\\
\rho_{1}&1&\rho_{2}\\
0&\rho_{2}&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1/2}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

To implement the optimal test, we still need to estimate
(a_{1},a_{2},\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
. It turns out to be easier to estimate
\alpha_{1}=a_{1}/\sqrt{a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}}
and
\alpha_{2}=a_{2}/\sqrt{a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}}
, and we propose the following estimators

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\widehat{\Phi}_{1}/\left(\widehat{\Phi}_{2}/(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}X_{i}P_{ij}X_{j})^{2}\right)}},

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\widehat{\Phi}_{1}/\left(\widehat{\Phi}_{2}/(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}X_{i}P_{ij}X_{j})^{2}\right)}}{\sqrt{1+\widehat{\Phi}_{1}/\left(\widehat{\Phi}_{2}/(\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}X_{i}P_{ij}X_{j})^{2}\right)}}.

In addition, let
\hat{X}=M_{W}(P-\bar{P})X
and
\acute{X}=z\hat{A}_{n}z^{\top}X
, for
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
we propose the following estimators

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{\Psi}\widehat{\Phi}_{2}}}\sum_{g\in[G]}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\acute{X}_{i}\hat{e}_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\hat{X}_{i}\hat{e}_{i}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\widehat{\Phi}_{2}\widehat{\Phi}_{3}}}\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}X_{i}P_{ij}\hat{e}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\hat{e}_{i}P_{ij}\hat{e}_{j}\right)\right].

.

Suppose Assumptions

-
3

\displaystyle\phi_{n}=1\left\{\left(\frac{\hat{b}_{1}\widetilde{T}(\beta_{0})+\hat{b}_{2}\widetilde{LM}(\beta_{0})+\hat{b}_{3}\widetilde{AR}}{\sqrt{\hat{b}_{1}^{2}+\hat{b}_{2}^{2}+\hat{b}_{3}^{2}}}\right)^{2}\geqslant\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}\right\},

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{b}_{1}\\

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
3

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\phi:\textbf{R}^{3}\mapsto[0,1]\quad\text{the discontinuities of $\phi(\cdot)$ has zero Lebesgue measure}\\

where

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\phi}_{n}\right]\leqslant\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{n}\right]

3.2
Weak Identification of Many IVs

We now investigate the property of our combined test when Assumption
3
fails. To characterize the limiting behavior of many IVs under weak identification, we introduce the following assumption.

Assumption 4
.

The following conditions hold almost surely:

1.

K\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
such that
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{K/n}\leqslant C<1
;

2.

\Pi^{\top}\Pi/\sqrt{K}=O(1)
with

\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\Pi_{i}P_{ij}\Pi_{j}\rightarrow\gamma\in\textbf{R};

3.

The following limits exist

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{\tilde{V},\tilde{V}}&\Lambda_{1}&\Lambda_{2}\\
\Lambda_{1}&\Gamma_{\tilde{V},\tilde{e}}&\Lambda_{3}\\
\Lambda_{2}&\Lambda_{3}&\Gamma_{\tilde{e},\tilde{e}}\end{pmatrix}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{11}&\Gamma_{12}&\Gamma_{13}\\
\Gamma_{21}&\Gamma_{22}&\Gamma_{23}\\
\Gamma_{31}&\Gamma_{32}&\Gamma_{33}\end{pmatrix}\equiv\Gamma>0

in the matrix sense and
\Gamma
is symmetric, where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\tilde{V},\tilde{V}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{V}_{j}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{V}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{j}P_{ji}\tilde{V}_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\tilde{V},\tilde{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{j}P_{ji}\tilde{e}_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\tilde{e},\tilde{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{j}P_{ji}\tilde{e}_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{V}_{j}\right)+\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{j}P_{ji}\tilde{V}_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{V}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)+\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{V}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{j}P_{ji}\tilde{e}_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\Lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{g,h\in[G]^{2},g\neq h}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)+\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{V}_{i}P_{ij}\tilde{e}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\sum_{j\in I_{h}}\tilde{e}_{j}P_{ji}\tilde{e}_{i}\right)\right].

Note that
\Pi^{\top}\Pi/\sqrt{K}=O(1)
corresponds to the case of weak identification.
In particular, it implies that, for any test that can control size under the null regardless of
\Pi
, under any fixed alternative there exists
\Pi
such that this test is inconsistent, as shown in
Mikusheva and Sun 2022
.
In our context, however, we only consider
\Pi
such that at least one IV strategy (low-dimensional IVs or many IVs) has strong identification strength, and thus it is still possible for a consistent test to exist.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose there exists a deterministic sequence
d_{n}\downarrow 0
such that

\displaystyle d_{n}\Phi_{1}^{-1/2}\rightarrow a_{1}\neq 0,

\displaystyle\beta-\beta_{0}=\delta d_{n}

for some fixed

. Under Assumption

,
2
and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{n}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}_{1}^{2}\geqslant\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}\right),\quad\mathcal{N}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\left(a_{1}\delta,1\right).

Note that under the local alternative, the right-hand side is the asymptotic power of the two-sided test using

4
Monte Carlo Simulations

We consider the following linear panel IV regression

\displaystyle Y_{i,t}

\displaystyle=X_{i,t}\beta+W_{i,t}^{\top}\gamma+\alpha_{i}+e_{i,t}

\displaystyle X_{i,t}

\displaystyle=Z_{i,t}^{\top}\pi+W_{i,t}^{\top}\delta+\xi_{i}+V_{i,t},

where
\alpha_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
are cluster-level fixed effects. The fixed effects are generated by
\alpha_{i}=u_{1i}+i/G,i=1,\dots,G
and
\xi_{i}=u_{2i}+i/G,i=1,\dots,G
, where
u_{1i}
and
u_{2i}
are independent standard normal random variables. The fixed effects are partialled out from the model by demeaning at the cluster level. The control variables in
W
are generated by standard normal distribution, and the dimension of
W
is fixed at
d_{w}=10
. The instruments in
Z
are normally distributed with mean
0
and we allow for cluster-level dependence: for each cluster the covariance matrix is given by

\Omega_{1i}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\theta_{1}&\cdots&\theta_{1}\\
\theta_{1}&1&\cdots&\theta_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{1}&\theta_{1}&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}_{n_{i}\times n_{i}},\;\;i=1,\dots,G,

and across clusters these instruments are independent from each other; we set
\theta_{1}=0.5
in our simulation. Note that the number of clusters is equal to the number of individuals in this setting. Finally, the error terms are generated by

\tilde{e}_{i,t}=\rho\varepsilon_{i,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\sigma_{i,t}v_{i}

and

\tilde{V}_{i,t}=\rho\eta_{i,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\sigma_{i,t}v_{i},

where
\varepsilon_{i,t}
,
\eta_{i,t}
and
v_{i}
are independent standard normal random variables,
\sigma_{i,t}=\sqrt{(0.2+(W_{i,t}^{\top}\delta)^{2})/2.4}
generates heteroskedasticity,
\rho
controls the degree of endogeneity and we set
\rho=0.5
in our simulation. To generate more complicated error structure, we further collect
\tilde{e}_{i,t}
and
\tilde{V}_{i,t}
for each cluster and left multiply them by

\Omega_{2i}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
\theta_{2}&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{2}^{n_{i}-1}&\theta_{2}^{n_{i}-2}&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}_{n_{i}\times n_{i}},i=1,\dots,G,

to generate
e_{i,t}
and
V_{i,t}
; we set
\theta_{2}=0.7
in our simulation. For the parameters, we set
\beta=0.3
,
\gamma=\delta=(1/\sqrt{d_{w}})\times\iota_{d_{w}}
where
\iota_{d_{w}}
is a
d_{w}\times 1
vector of ones. For the value of
\pi
, we consider letting the coefficient decrease geometrically with

\displaystyle\pi=\left(\phi^{0},\phi^{1},\cdots,\phi^{d_{z}-1}\right),

where
\phi=\left\{0,0.95,1\right\}
; note that
\phi=0
corresponds to the case where only the first instrument has identification strength, and
\phi=1
corresponds to the case where each instrument has the same identification strength. We further normalize
\pi
to have
\left\|\pi\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{\psi\sqrt{d_{z}}/n}

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a combination inference procedure to solve the problem. Specifically, we consider a linear combination of three test statistics: a standard cluster-robust Wald statistic based on the low-dimensional IVs, a leave-one-cluster-out Lagrangian Multiplier (LM) statistic, and a leave-one-cluster-out Anderson-Rubin (AR) statistic. We first establish the joint asymptotic normality of the Wald, LM, and AR statistics and derive the corresponding limit experiment under local alternatives. Then, under the assumption that at least the low-dimensional IVs can strongly identify the parameter of interest, we derive the optimal combination test based on the three statistics and establish that our procedure leads to the uniformly most powerful (UMP) unbiased test among the class of tests considered. In particular, the efficiency gain from the combined test is of “free lunch” in the sense that it is always at least as powerful as the test that is only based on the low-dimensional IVs or many IVs. The power improvement of our combined test originates from two factors: the non-centralities of the Wald, AR, and LM statistics, and their correlation structure. The improvement from the correlation is important because even if the non-centralities of certain statistics among the three are zero, the statistics may still be useful, depending on the correlation structure.
