Soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor and perioperative complications: a systematic review
Authors: Ata B. Gungor, Prateek Pennathur, Hamza S. Guran, William Chu, Pavel S. Roshanov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBetter informative predictive tools are required to improve risk-stratification in patients undergoing surgery. Soluble urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) has the potential to predict postoperative complications. The primary objective of this systematic review was to evaluate the association between suPAR measured around the time of surgery and perioperative complications.MethodsWe systematically reviewed studies indexed in Embase or PubMed from inception up to October 8, 2024. We included studies evaluating the association between suPAR measured around surgery and perioperative complications captured up to 90 days after surgery. We excluded case reports, reviews, editorials, studies with non-human participants, and clinical practice guidelines. We used the Quality in Prognostic Studies tool to assess the risk of bias, and Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation to ascertain the certainty in the inference for reported associations.ResultsEighteen of the 19 included studies provided evaluable data from 6410 patients on the association between suPAR and perioperative outcomes. There was moderate certainty that suPAR associates with acute kidney injury, low certainty of association with mortality, very low certainty of association with composites of multiple complications, resource utilization outcomes, and infectious complications. However, we were highly uncertain about the magnitude of association because studies were heterogeneous, poorly reported, mostly retrospective, and too small to provide robust estimates.ConclusionsWe conclude that serum or plasma concentrations of suPAR measured around surgery is likely associated with perioperative complications, especially acute kidney injury. The magnitude of these associations and the clinical value of suPAR should now be compared to other clinical information and prognostic biomarkers in biobanks of large prospective studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03350-1.

Background

Over 300 million surgeries are performed around the world annually and this number is projected to increase [1]. Perioperative complications are common and associate with mortality and extensive resource utilization [2, 3]. Preoperative estimation of the risk of complications and death informs patient and clinician decisions about the appropriateness of surgery, approaches to medical optimization before surgery, and selection of the postoperative care setting (e.g., intensive care, hospital ward, or same-day discharge) and the required intensity of postoperative monitoring. The measurement of certain biomarkers can objectively inform perioperative risk estimation and some (e.g., natriuretic peptides and cardiac troponins) are now routinely recommended in clinical practice [4–6].

Serum or plasma soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) may help to predict perioperative complications. It has been associated with numerous health outcomes in acutely ill patients [7–9] and is involved in cell adhesion, migration, extracellular matrix remodeling, and inflammation [10, 11].

We conducted this systematic review to summarize the available evidence of association between suPAR measured around surgery and perioperative complications.

Methods

We report this systematic review according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement guidelines [12]. We did not register a protocol.

Study selection

We searched Embase and PubMed from inception to October 8, 2024. We included original studies published in any language that reported a pre-, intra-, or postoperative measurement of suPAR and reported intra- or postoperative outcomes captured up to 90 days after surgery (Supplemental Table S2). We excluded studies of non-human participants, case reports, reviews, editorials, or clinical practice guidelines. Four authors (PP, ABG, WC, HSG) working in duplicate independently screened titles and abstracts, and subsequently screened full texts of potentially relevant records. A separate reviewer (PSR) resolved conflicts.

Data extraction

Data from each study were extracted by one reviewer, then confirmed by a second reviewer, and reviewed by the entire author group at weekly meetings. We extracted data regarding the study setting, number of study centers, number of participants evaluated for each outcome, details of study design, participant characteristics, details of missing participant data, reported potential conflicts of interest of study authors, outcome definitions, statistical analysis methods, the suPAR assay used in the study, and any estimates of association between suPAR and perioperative complications.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

All reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias for the association between suPAR and each outcome in each included study using the Quality in Prognostic Studies (QUIPS) tool. The QUIPS tool uses 6 domains to assess the risk of bias: [1] study participation; [2] study attrition; [3] prognostic factor measurement; [4] outcome measurement; [5] study confounding; [6] statistical analysis and reporting [13]. We assessed study participation by the degree to which the samples reflected the population of interest. We assessed study attrition by whether more than 10% of participants were lost-to-follow-up or otherwise excluded from analysis for missing outcome or predictor data. We assessed prognostic factor measurement by whether the timing of suPAR measurements between patients was consistent and if the time frame of measurement was appropriate. We assessed outcome measurement by whether the outcome was adequately defined and systematically ascertained. We assessed potential for confounding if the models evaluating the association between suPAR and perioperative outcomes were adjusted for pre-operative kidney function. In the absence of adjustment in a model we considered whether studies restricted eligibility or stratified by kidney function. We focused on this because suPAR levels are closely associated with kidney function and risk may be falsely attributed to suPAR instead of kidney disease—a known strong predictor of perioperative complications [14, 15]. We rated risk of confounding as low if the study adjusted for pre-operative kidney function or the study restricted or stratified participants to normal kidney function, moderate if not restricted or adjusted and outcome was not acute kidney injury, and high if there was no restriction or adjustment for pre-operative kidney function and outcome was acute kidney injury or if the model was unadjusted for any covariates. Finally, we assessed the statistical analysis and reporting domain by calculating the events per variable (EPV) for studies with multivariable (i.e., adjusted) analysis (low risk if > 10 EPV, moderate risk if 5–10 EPV, and high risk if < 5 EPV) [16]. If the number of non-events were less than the number of events in a study, we applied these criteria using non-events per variable instead. For overall risk of bias, we rated studies as ‘low risk’ (i.e., low risk in ≥ 5 of 6 domains or moderate risk in 1 domain), ‘high risk’ (i.e., high risk in ≥ 1 of 6 domains or moderate risk in ≥ 3 domains), and ‘moderate risk’ for the remaining studies.

Data synthesis and analysis

We summarized estimates of association (i.e., odds ratios, risk ratios, and hazard ratios) in forest plots to facilitate interpretation. We did not perform meta-analysis due to heterogeneity in outcome measures. We summarized results qualitatively for each outcome. We used vote counting based on statistical significance to make inferences regarding the presence and direction of association between suPAR and each outcome. Each study could contribute information on multiple outcomes. Where studies reported estimates of association from several models, we relied on the model adjusting for the largest number of covariates. We only used unadjusted estimates in the absence of adjusted estimates. If a study reported suPAR as a categorical variable, we took the estimate of association reported for the contrast between the highest and lowest suPAR categories. If a study reported both a continuous and categorical expression of suPAR, we made inferences based on the continuous expression because we expect these to provide the most power and precision.

Grading certainty in inferences

We used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to grade our certainty in the inference about the presence of association between suPAR and each reported outcome. We rated the overall certainty in the inference for each outcome as ‘very low’, ‘low’, ‘moderate’, or ‘high’. Outcomes reported from only one study was rated as ‘very low’, and outcomes reported from more than one study started with an overall ‘high’ rating before we applied criteria for rating up or down. We applied all GRADE domains: [1] study limitations; [2] inconsistency; [3] indirectness; [4] imprecision; [5] publication bias; [6] effect size; [7] dose-response; [8] plausible confounding [17]. We judged the study limitations domain based on the overall risk of bias gathered from the QUIPS tool. We judged inconsistency by examining the overlap of confidence intervals and the variability of point estimates across studies. We judged indirectness based on how aligned the patient populations were with the aim of our review and whether outcomes were adequately defined. We graded the inference around the existence of an association and the direction of the association; we did not grade around magnitude. We, therefore, judged imprecision based on whether the reported confidence intervals for the estimates of association were confined in one direction. We judged likelihood of publication bias as high a priori for all studies because studies about prognostic factors are particularly susceptible to selective publication [18]. We did not rate up any given outcome for large effect size because we were unable to perform a pooled estimate and the magnitudes of association varied across studies. We judged dose-response based on whether most studies within an outcome found a graded relationship between suPAR and complication risk. Lastly, we judged that confounding was a plausible explanation of the association between suPAR and a perioperative outcome if authors failed to adjust for a confounder and that failure would increase the strength of apparent association between suPAR and the perioperative outcome.

Description of included studies

Supplemental Table S1 summarizes the characteristics of the included studies. Ten studies were retrospective [19–28] and nine were prospective [29–37]. Three studies were case-control [26–28], and the rest were cohort studies. Six studies were based in Denmark [19, 21–25], five in Germany [30–33, 37], two in China [27, 35], three in the USA [20, 29, 36], one in Italy [26], one in Greece [34], and one in Scotland [28]. All but four studies [20, 22, 26, 36] were single centered. Studies ranged in sample size from 30 to 2404 participants (median, 126; 25th percentile, 102; 75th percentile, 428). Four studies measured suPAR after surgery [26, 27, 35, 37] and the remainder measured suPAR before surgery. Some studies had important conflicts of interest as study authors were employed by the suPAR assay manufacturer [19, 20, 34].

Risk of bias within studies

Table 1 summarizes risk of bias assessments for the reported estimates of association between outcomes and suPAR in each study. Nine estimates of association were at low overall risk of bias [21, 22, 24, 25, 27–29], four estimates of association were at moderate overall risk of bias [19, 20, 23], and 20 estimates of association were at high overall risk of bias [19, 20, 22, 23, 26, 27, 30–37]. Most estimates of association were at low risk of bias for participation, attrition, prognostic factor measurement, and outcome measurement domains and a few were at moderate risk of bias [19, 20, 26, 30, 32, 34–36]. Many estimates were at high risk of bias in the confounding and statistical analysis and reporting domains.

Association between SuPAR and perioperative outcomes

Figure 2 displays 24 associations between suPAR measured around surgery and perioperative complications. Eight associations reported on acute kidney injury, five associations reported on mortality, five associations reported on different composite outcomes, one association reported on sepsis and surgical site infection, and one association reported on prolonged hospital stay, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and ventilation. Outcome definitions and time frames for ascertainment were heterogeneous. Studies evaluating composite outcomes examined outcome combinations that included sepsis, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, and reoperation.

Table 2 reports ratings of certainty in the inference of the 31 evaluable associations between suPAR and perioperative outcomes according to the GRADE criteria. Ten studies identified a significant positive association between perioperative suPAR levels and acute kidney injury (moderate certainty evidence) [24, 27, 29–33, 35–37]. Four out of five studies evaluating mortality identified a significant positive association with preoperative suPAR (low certainty evidence) [19, 21, 23, 25]. One study identified a positive association, but was not statistically significant [20]. Five studies evaluating composite outcomes identified a significant positive association with perioperative suPAR (very low certainty of evidence) [19, 20, 22, 23, 34]. One study evaluating resource utilization identified a positive association between preoperative suPAR and prolonged hospital stay, ICU stay, and prolonged ventilation but the association with prolonged ventilation was not statistically significant (very low certainty of evidence) [28]. One study evaluating sepsis, pneumonia, surgical site infection, urinary tract infection, and reoperation separately identified a significant positive association with preoperative suPAR (very low certainty of evidence) [19]. One study found a significant positive association with pneumonia, but a nonsignificant positive association with surgical site infection and preoperative suPAR (very low certainty of evidence) [22]. One study evaluated a decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and found no association with preoperative suPAR (very low certainty of evidence) [36].

Effect of SuPAR on prediction model performance

Several studies reported on the change in model discrimination performance (as measured using the C-statistic) when suPAR is added to a multivariable model [38]. The C-statistics for suPAR and other predictors reported in each study can be found in Supplemental Table S1. The addition of preoperative suPAR to EuroSCORE II, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, and C-Reactive Protein (CRP) showed a statistically significant increase in C-statistics for resource utilization including prolonged hospital length of stay, ICU stay, ventilation, and a composite outcome based on the comprehensive complication index [28, 34]. However, in two other studies that independently examined the addition of preoperative suPAR to EuroSCORE II and Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score reported a nonsignificant increase in C-statistics for 30 day mortality, and a composite outcome comprised of all-cause mortality, congestive heart failure, non-hemorrhagic or hemorrhagic stroke, and renal impairment [20, 25].

Discussion

We found evidence that suPAR measured around the time of surgery is associated with postoperative acute kidney injury (moderate certainty), mortality (low certainty), composite outcomes comprised of complications such as pneumonia, sepsis, blood transfusions, and urinary tract infections (very low certainty), resource utilization (very low certainty), and infectious complications (very low certainty). However, we could draw no conclusions about the magnitude of associations as studies ascertained and defined outcomes inconsistently (i.e., some assessed mortality at 30 days and others at 90 days post-operatively; some reported only AKI stage I and others reported stages I, II, and III). Studies were heterogeneous in methodology, and suffered from poor reporting of methodological details, retrospective analysis, and were limited by small samples sizes with few events. Most studies were at high or moderate overall risk of bias particularly due to risk of confounding and limitations of statistical analysis and reporting.

Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown the value of suPAR in predicting sepsis, predicting acute kidney injury, and predicting mortality in COVID-19 patients [7, 39, 40]. It is suggested that when urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA) binds to its receptor (uPAR) it becomes cleaved into the soluble version, suPAR. A sequence in the linker region between the first and second domain of suPAR has chemoattractant properties that help immune cells to migrate from the blood to tissues [41]. Once in the tissue, monocytes facilitate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-6) which are associated with complications including acute kidney injury [42]. Alternatively, Hayek et al., proposed a causative role of suPAR in the development of acute kidney injury. Human kidney proximal tubular cells (HK-2) treated with suPAR had increased oxidative stress compared to non-treated HK-2 cells, and suPAR overexpressing mice had increased incidence of acute kidney injury compared to wild type mice [29]. They observed an attenuation of oxidative stress and incidence of acute kidney injury when treated with an uPAR monoclonal antibody.

Limitations of this review

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review evaluating the association of suPAR with perioperative complications. We did not conduct meta-analysis due to the heterogeneity of the studies and measures of association presented. Instead, we used the limited methodology of vote-counting for data synthesis prone to overestimating certainty. Moreover, outcomes in the literature were ascertained and defined inconsistently, and pre- and post-operative suPAR measurements were included which limit construct validity and reduce generalizability. We were unable to perform a structured comparison of discrimination, calibration, or reclassification metrics due to limitations in the literature. We instead presented the effect of suPAR on prediction model performance (e.g., changes in C-statistics) in an exploratory manner, and thus, results should be interpreted with caution. We did not include conference abstracts or other grey literature in our analysis, but it is unlikely that these sources would affect our conclusions given our comprehensive search in the major databases likely to index original publications on this topic. There may exist an assay-related variability in suPAR measurements and their correlated outcomes, but only 3 of 19 studies measured suPAR using a different assay and the directionality for clinical outcomes remained consistent across assays. Due to the exploratory nature of this review, we did not prospectively register a study protocol which may limit transparency. We conducted searches in only two databases, but Embase and MEDLINE are the highest yield databases providing good coverage and our searches are unlikely to have missed studies that would change our conclusion [43]. Finally, assessing degree of certainty in inference regarding associations of biomarkers and clinical outcomes is a challenging practice that necessitates some subjective judgements, but we conducted transparent assessments in accordance with the GRADE approach.

Future direction

The clinical value of suPAR over and above that of prognostic information already captured by perioperative risk scores and other biomarkers is not yet established. There is weak evidence that suPAR may improve prediction of postoperative complications when added to EuroSCORE II, ASA score, and CRP [20, 26, 28]. The thresholds at which suPAR portends higher complication risk are not established, and even the magnitude of association is still unclear. In contrast, natriuretic peptides and postoperative cardiac troponins have been extensively studied in large prospective cohorts and have been shown to independently predict perioperative mortality and vascular complications [5, 6, 44]. Clinically relevant thresholds of these biomarkers are now established in noncardiac surgery [4–6, 45–47]. More recently, growth differentiation factor-15 demonstrated prognostic value when added to the Revised Cardiac Risk Index and preoperative measurements of N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide when evaluated in a large biobank of a prospective cohort study in noncardiac surgery [48]. This type of study is the appropriate next step in the evaluation of perioperative suPAR. Moreover, as a continuous variable, suPAR is likely to offer substantially more prognostic information than if it is dichotomized at any one threshold. Future research should focus on modeling it as a continuous variable [49].

Conclusion

Serum or plasma concentration of suPAR measured around the time of surgery is likely associated with a variety of perioperative complications, of which we are most confident in the association with acute kidney injury. However, the magnitude of these associations and the clinical value of suPAR remain to be clarified in large well-characterized cohorts of surgical patients.
