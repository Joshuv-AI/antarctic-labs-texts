Combinatorial action of regulatory systems generates colistin heteroresistance
Authors: Jacob E. Choby, Emily K. Crispell, Muqing Ma, Linda M. Vu, Mary-Kate Key, Robert K. Ernst, Minsu Kim, David S. Weiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The mechanism by which phenotypic heterogeneity is generated within a bacterial population and leads to colistin heteroresistance has been unclear. Heteroresistance is a form of antibiotic resistance in which a minor subpopulation of resistant cells coexists with a majority susceptible population. Colistin heteroresistance is common among Enterobacter isolates, threatens its utility as a therapeutic, and has become a model to study the fundamental bases of heteroresistance. Here, we identify the sigma factor σE as the source of heterogeneity in colistin resistance. Single-cell tracking revealed that σE is active in 1% of the population, and only those cells survive colistin exposure. However, σE expression and heterogeneity were insufficient for survival, as a mutant lacking the PhoPQ system controlling lipid A modifications necessary for colistin resistance retained heterogeneity but lost resistance. These findings lead to a paradigm in heteroresistance; the combinatorial action of regulatory systems, encompassing a heterogeneity generator and a distinct resistance generator, gives rise to colistin heteroresistance.

INTRODUCTION

Heteroresistance (HR) is a type of phenotypic heterogeneity in which cells in a population exhibit differences in relative antibiotic resistance; a low frequency subpopulation of cells is markedly more resistant than the majority susceptible population. The resistant subpopulation can be extremely rare (as few as 1 per 106 cells) and is thus often undetected by conventional antimicrobial susceptibility testing. The subpopulation is also dynamic: The resistant cells are enriched in the presence of a given antibiotic but return to their baseline frequency in its absence. For these reasons, HR can contribute to failure of antibiotic therapy, when a patient is treated with an antibiotic to which the infecting organism exhibits undetected HR (1–4).

Colistin is a last-resort antibiotic reserved for treatment of multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, but its usefulness is challenged by emerging resistance. A member of the polymyxin class, colistin is a cationic antimicrobial peptide (CAMP) that targets the Gram-negative outer membrane constituent lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and disrupts the outer and inner membranes, killing the bacterium (5). Gram-negative bacteria can resist colistin killing by changing the composition of the outer membrane, in particular the lipid A portion of LPS (6). In general, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales, a WHO Critical Priority (7) and CDC Urgent Threat (8), are designated colistin susceptible. However, conventional colistin resistance can occur when an isolate has intrinsic or horizontally acquired LPS-modifying enzymes. Equally or more common, but challenging to detect (9–12), is colistin HR (13).

Colistin HR among carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales is especially common in the Enterobacter genus (9, 12). The Enterobacter are diverse, and many pathogenic species are often collectively referred to as the Enterobacter cloacae complex (14). They are leading nosocomial, opportunistic pathogens and cause pneumonia, bloodstream infections, and urinary tract infections (15). A challenge for treating infections caused by E. cloacae complex isolates is the high rate of antibiotic resistance, especially to cephalosporins and carbapenems, making colistin an important last-line treatment option.

We previously described colistin HR in a cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacter clinical isolate (16). That work and analyses of other colistin HR Enterobacter isolates have resulted in colistin HR serving as a model for Gram-negative antibiotic HR. The resistant subpopulation in colistin HR Enterobacter harbors cationic 4-amino-4-deoxy-l-arabinose (l-Ara4N) modifications on the lipid A moiety of LPS, increasing cellular surface charge and thus reducing binding of cationic colistin and other CAMPs (12, 16–19). The arnBCADTEF gene cassette encodes the l-Ara4N modification machinery, and its expression is activated by the two-component system PhoPQ (6). Different alleles of phoP, phoQ, their promoter (12, 17), the PhoPQ-regulating mgrB (12), or ecr (20), affect PhoPQ/arn activation and the subsequent frequency of the resistant subpopulation. However, the underlying basis for the phenotypic heterogeneity exhibited within the cellular population of a colistin HR isolate has remained unresolved.

In this work, we used a transposon-based mutagenesis screen and single-cell tracking to identify the alternative sigma factor σE as the critical determinant of phenotypic heterogeneity in colistin HR of an Enterobacter clinical isolate. σE responds to envelope stress and activates transcription of a large stress response regulon (21). We observed that the resistant subpopulation had distinctly high σE activity which resulted in those cells surviving colistin treatment. σE increased l-Ara4N modification of lipid A and responded to antimicrobial peptide exposure. However, while σE was the critical determinant of phenotypic heterogeneity, it was insufficient to cause HR, since a phoPQ mutant strain maintained heterogeneity but lost HR. Thus, we have identified σE as the generator of heterogeneity in the population that, in tandem with the resistance conferred by PhoPQ and the Arn machinery, generates a rare, colistin-resistant subpopulation. This work establishes a mechanistic paradigm where the distinct but combinatorial activity of two regulatory systems drives HR, an enigmatic yet increasingly recognized source of antibiotic failure in the clinic.

RESULTS

Colistin HR requires lipid A modification

E. cloacae strain RS, a bloodstream isolate from a renal transplant patient, displays colistin HR and caused colistin treatment failure in a murine model of infection (16). Population analysis profile (PAP; fig. S1), the gold standard method for sensitively detecting HR, involves culturing bacteria in antibiotic-free broth followed by plating serial dilutions onto agar containing increasing concentrations of a given antibiotic. Resistant subpopulations form colonies that are quantified as colony-forming units (CFU) at each concentration, allowing calculation of the proportion of the initial population that survives across that antibiotic gradient. PAP revealed that ∼1% of the total RS population (−2 log) was resistant to colistin (4 μg/ml; the CLSI breakpoint concentration used to discriminate susceptible from resistant strains) (Fig. 1A and fig. S2). In contrast, the ColR2 strain demonstrated homogeneous resistance; every cell in the population survived at high colistin concentrations. ColS1 was susceptible to the colistin breakpoint concentration with no cells surviving (Fig. 1A and fig. S2). When exposed to colistin, the resistant subpopulation of cells in strain RS survived and was enriched (Fig. 1B). However, characteristic of HR, when colistin was removed, the frequency of the resistant subpopulation returned to the baseline level within one passage, demonstrating that the phenotypically resistant subpopulation can be transiently enriched and is not the result of stable genetic changes that confer resistance (Fig. 1B).

While the majority susceptible population of cells lacked colistin resistance–associated l-Ara4N modifications to lipid A, as determined by mass spectrometric lipid analysis (Fig. 1, C and D, and table S2), these modifications became prominent following enrichment of the resistant subpopulation in colistin-containing broth (Fig. 1E and table S2). Correspondingly, genetic deletion of arnB, which encodes UDP-4-amino-4-deoxy-l-arabinose-oxoglutarate aminotransferase that converts UDP-4-ketopentose to UDP-l-Ara4N and is required for l-Ara4N addition to lipid A (22), resulted in loss of the colistin-resistant subpopulation (Fig. 1F). Similarly, deletion of phoPQ, encoding a two-component system that activates expression of the arn operon (16, 17), resulted in elimination of the highly resistant subpopulation (Fig. 1F and fig. S3A). The deletion strains were complemented in cis, and the resistant population was restored in each case (fig. S3B). Further, mutants lacking arnB or phoQ exhibited no l-Ara4N modifications to lipid A (fig. S3, C and D). Thus, the presence of the colistin-resistant subpopulation is dependent on PhoPQ and l-Ara4N modification of lipid A.

Activation of the arn operon promoter is not bimodal

Since the arn genes are critical for the presence of the colistin-resistant subpopulation, we hypothesized that heterogeneous activation of the arn promoter might generate the phenotypic heterogeneity in colistin HR in strain RS. If so, we predicted that in the absence of colistin, RS would exhibit bimodal arn expression corresponding to its bimodal resistance phenotype. That is, we expected ∼1% of the population to have high arn expression and demonstrate resistance, while ∼99% of the cells would have low arn expression and be susceptible. To test this hypothesis, we generated a transcriptional reporter by fusing the arn promoter to gfp (Parngfp) and introduced this construct into the attB site of the RS chromosome (generating strain RS::Parngfp). We imaged RS::Parngfp grown in Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) without colistin on agar pads and measured green fluorescent protein (GFP) intensity per cell. While Parngfp expression varied among individual cells, the distribution of arn reporter fluorescence was continuous and unimodal (a single peak; Fig. 1G) rather than bimodal (two discrete populations), counter to our hypothesis. These data demonstrate that while arn expression is required for colistin HR, its expression in the population is not bimodal and therefore insufficient to explain the heterogeneity in colistin resistance in strain RS.

Activation of the alternative sigma factor σE pathway increases colistin resistance

To attempt to identify the factors that generate the phenotypic heterogeneity in colistin HR, we next tested whether the resistant subpopulation demonstrated gene amplification. For many instances of HR, an increase in copy number of genes that encode resistance determinants generates the resistant subpopulation (23). However, when the genome of the resistant subpopulation was compared to that of the majority susceptible population, no sequence variants were detected (16), and no gene copy number variation was observed (table S1), consistent with phenotypic differences between the resistant and susceptible cells that are not due to genetic changes.

We next sought to identify additional genes involved in colistin HR which could be the source of the phenotypic heterogeneity, performing a transposon screen for mutants that do not survive on colistin at wild-type levels. Of ∼16,000 colonies screened, we identified 42 transposon mutants that demonstrated reduced frequency of the colistin-resistant subpopulation. Validating our screen, we identified a mutant in arnD, part of the arnBCADTEF operon that is required for colistin resistance (table S3). We noticed that transposon mutants were identified in clpP, which encodes a protease adaptor involved in many cellular pathways, and sspB, encoding the stringent starvation protein B, which acts in conjunction with ClpP in the activation of the alternative sigma factor σE (encoded by rpoE) (Fig. 2A). We therefore pursued the hypothesis that σE may contribute to colistin HR. Clean deletion mutants of either clpP or sspB displayed reduced survival on colistin, further confirming the accuracy of the transposon screen, although the degree of colistin susceptibility of the clpP mutant was much greater than that of sspB (Fig. 2B and fig. S4A). An rpoE mutation was not identified in our screen, and we were unable to generate an rpoE deletion, consistent with its reported essentiality in Escherichia coli (24). To further evaluate the involvement of σE, we instead deleted rseA, encoding its antisigma factor which restrains its activity, and rseB, which encodes a periplasmic protein that enhances RseA inhibition of σE (25, 26). Inactivation of RseA and, to a lesser extent, RseB is known to increase σE activity (25, 26). The frequency of the colistin-resistant subpopulation increased in both the ΔrseA and ΔrseB mutants relative to wild type (Fig. 2C and fig. S4, B and C). In the case of the ΔrseA mutant, the frequency of the resistant cells reached almost 100% even when plated on agar containing colistin (100 μg/ml) (Fig. 2C). Together, the genetic inactivation of proteins which promote σE activation (ΔclpP and ΔsspB) resulted in a lower frequency of the colistin-resistant population, and inactivation of proteins which restrain σE activation (ΔrseA and ΔrseB) increased the frequency of cells resistant to colistin. These results demonstrate the role of the σE pathway in colistin HR.

σE promoter activation is bimodal

Having identified the σE pathway as an important regulator of colistin HR, we revisited our hypothesis that bimodal gene expression underlies this phenotype. To test this, we generated a chromosomally encoded reporter of σE activity using the promoter for a σE-regulated gene, micA (RS::PmicAgfp). MicA is among the most strongly and tightly σE-regulated promoters in the σE regulon, exhibiting rapid and uniform responsiveness to σE activation (27). We microscopically imaged individual cells of the RS::PmicAgfp strain in the absence of colistin and measured their fluorescence. We observed that σE activity demonstrated bimodality; two distinct peaks of fluorescence distribution including a majority population with low promoter activity and a minor subset of cells with high promoter activity. Approximately 1% of the total population exhibited high PmicAgfp expression (Fig. 2D), which matched the measured percentage of colistin-resistant cells when RS is grown in the absence of colistin (Fig. 1A). Together, these data suggest that active σE in a rare subpopulation of cells could generate the colistin-resistant subpopulation.

Rare σE high cells constitute the colistin-resistant subpopulation

To investigate the relationship between σE activity and survival on colistin, we next assessed the σE reporter strain (RS::PmicAgfp) before (Fig. 3A) and after addition of colistin (8 μg/ml) (Fig. 3, B and C), performing single-cell, time-lapse microscopy to track the fate of each cell upon colistin exposure (movie S1). We quantified promoter activity by measuring GFP levels in individual cells in the absence of colistin and then exposed the population to colistin and classified cells as susceptible or resistant based on their ability to replicate. Notably, cells with low PmicAgfp reporter expression failed to replicate in the presence of colistin, whereas only those with high preexposure reporter expression were able to grow and divide (Fig. 3D).

Further, when we encoded the PmicAgfp reporter in the ΔrseA strain, which is made up almost exclusively of colistin-resistant cells (Fig. 2C), we observed a corresponding and robust increase in PmicAgfp expression as compared to the parental wild-type RS strain (Fig. 3E). Together, these data suggest that the resistant subpopulation in RS is generated by preexisting, bimodal σE activity before colistin exposure.

Colistin HR conferred by σE activation requires l-Ara4N modification

It remained unclear how activation of the σE pathway promotes colistin HR and whether lipid A modifications are required. To address this, we performed PAP on a strain lacking both rseA (to constitutively activate σE) and arnB (to block l-Ara4N modification of lipid A) and found that deletion of arnB rendered the otherwise highly resistant ΔrseA strain susceptible to colistin (Fig. 4A and fig. S5). Next, we directly characterized the lipid A of the ΔrseA and ΔrseAΔarnB strains grown in the absence of colistin using mass spectrometry. Compared to wild-type RS, the ΔrseA strain was highly modified with l-Ara4N, consistent with its high frequency of colistin-resistant cells (Fig. 4B). However, these modifications were absent in the ΔrseAΔarnB strain (Fig. 4B). Thus, the colistin resistance conferred by constitutive activation of σE requires arn-mediated lipid A modification.

Since arn plays a critical role in lipid A modification and colistin resistance, we examined its expression at the single-cell level and its relationship with survival on colistin using the Parngfp reporter in wild-type RS. While arn expression overall was unimodal (Fig. 1G), we observed that cells exhibited graded differences in expression levels, and resistance occurred only in the subset whose arn expression exceeded a functional threshold, resulting in partial overlap between arn expression and resistance (Fig. 4C). This contrasted with the results for micA expression which was bimodal and where there was no overlap between the resistant and susceptible populations (Fig. 3D). Notably, a larger proportion of cells exhibited elevated Parngfp in the ΔrseA background, suggesting that constitutive σE activation enhanced arn transcription, consistent with increased l-Ara4N modifications (Fig. 4B) and colistin resistance.

The σE pathway is activated by LL-37 and confers resistance

Our previous work demonstrated that the colistin-resistant subpopulation in strain RS is cross-resistant to the human cationic α-helical antimicrobial peptide LL-37, that LL-37 exposure increases the frequency of this subpopulation, and that, even in the absence of colistin treatment, the resistant subpopulation becomes enriched during murine infection (16). We therefore tested whether our findings were consistent in a medium more similar to the host environment. We grew strain RS in M9 broth with glucose and low or high magnesium concentrations; low magnesium can increase PhoQ signaling to PhoP and induce colistin resistance, while high magnesium can repress PhoQ (28, 29). The frequency of colistin-resistant cells was similar regardless of the level of magnesium in the media (fig. S6, A and B). We additionally imaged RS::PmicAgfp and RS::Parngfp following growth in M9 with glucose and high magnesium (10 mM MgSO4) and observed similar fluorescence intensity of the susceptible and resistant populations to that observed in MHB (fig. S6, C and D). Therefore, we conclude that our findings in MHB that the colistin-resistant subpopulation is driven by high σE expression and generally higher arn expression extend to more host-relevant conditions.

Since strain RS harbors cells cross-resistant to colistin and LL-37, and the σE pathway is involved, we hypothesized that LL-37 treatment would activate the σE pathway. When strain RS was treated with LL-37, we observed an increase in transcript abundance of rpoE and rseA, consistent with activation of the σE pathway (fig. S7A). These conditions also activated the canonical PhoPQ response to LL-37, as measured by expression of the PhoPQ-activated mgtA transcript and arnB (fig. S7A). Consistent with σE pathway induction in response to LL-37, the ΔrseA strain was more resistant to LL-37 than wild type (fig. S7B). Thus, these data demonstrate that σE is involved not only in survival in colistin but also in response to a host antimicrobial peptide.

Many known regulators of LPS modification do not contribute to colistin HR in strain RS

Our findings highlight that σE controls cell to cell heterogeneity in lipid A modification in strain RS. σE has previously been shown to regulate population-wide shifts in sugar composition of other components of LPS, the core, and O-antigen. In a ΔrseA strain in the E. coli W3110 background, LPS glycoforms IV and V predominated, with a near absence of glycoform I, as compared to the wild type (30). Notably, the σE-regulated small RNA (sRNA) rybB was critical to regulating these σE-induced core and O-antigen changes, as deletion of rybB in the ΔrseA background resulted in a shift back to a broader mix of glycoforms, including glycoform I, as observed in the wild-type strain. The effect of σE was enhanced via WaaZ activity, which strain RS does not encode. We assessed the potential involvement of rybB on colistin HR in Enterobacter strain RS by generating a rybB deletion in wild-type and ΔrseA backgrounds and tested these strains by PAP. In RS, rybB did not affect colistin HR in the parental or ΔrseA backgrounds (fig. S8, A and B). Deletion of micA, another σE-regulated sRNA that has been linked to regulation of lipid A in E. coli (31), similarly did not affect colistin HR in strain RS (fig. S8, C and D).

We also tested for the potential involvement of PmrAB (BasRS), a two-component system known to contribute to polymyxin resistance in Enterobacterales (32), and previously shown by Raina and colleagues (30) to influence LPS modification in an E. coli ΔrseA strain. In strain RS, pmrB is a predicted pseudogene with a premature stop codon. Correspondingly, pmrAB deletion in wild-type and ΔrseA backgrounds had no effect on colistin HR (fig. S8, E and F). This is consistent with PmrAB not contributing to colistin HR in other E. cloacae strains (17).

In addition, Klein and colleagues (30) identified a role for the stringent response in controlling LPS core and O-antigen modifications; loss of ppGpp synthesis via deletion of relA and spoT in the constitutive σE background (ΔrelA spoT rseA) resulted in homogeneous LPS lacking glycoform IV and V derivatives and l-Ara4N modifications of lipid A. We were inexplicably unable to generate a strain lacking both relA and spoT (which would be unable to produce ppGpp) but nevertheless tested ΔrelA and ΔrseAΔrelA mutant strains and measured the effects on colistin HR by PAP. Deletion of relA had no effect on colistin HR (fig. S8, G and H), but we are unable to conclusively rule out a contribution of ppGpp because we could not generate a ppGpp null strain.

Further work identified transcriptional regulation of rpoE promoters by RpoD, RpoN, and RpoS (33). This raised the possibility that additional sigma factors, in addition to σE, might demonstrate bimodal activation in strain RS and therefore contribute to σE bimodality. To test this possibility, we generated gfp fusions to PrpoD, PrpoN, or PrpoS and integrated each reporter into the RS chromosome. All three promoter fusions demonstrated unimodal expression in the population before colistin exposure (fig. S9). In addition, the cells that survived colistin exposure were distributed across the overall unimodal expression range, indicating that expression of RpoD, RpoN, or RpoS does not correlate with or explain the presence of the colistin-resistant subpopulation, as does RpoE activation.

The σE pathway may be a common regulator of colistin HR in Enterobacter clinical isolates

We next tested whether our finding that σE regulates the frequency of the colistin resistant subpopulation in strain RS extends to other Enterobacter clinical isolates. First, we deleted rseA in an Enterobacter kobei isolate, strain Mu208 (16), which also exhibits colistin HR. Consistent with our observation in RS, genetic inactivation of rseA in Mu208 resulted in homogeneous resistance to high colistin concentrations (fig. S10). Next, we took advantage of the ability of zinc to activate σE (34, 35). We validated the ability of zinc to induce σE in strain RS by measuring PmicAgfp in MHB with zinc added (fig. S11A). In line with σE activation, zinc increased the frequency of the resistant subpopulation in strain RS (fig. S11B). We then performed PAP with two additional Enterobacter clinical isolates for which we are unable to perform genetic manipulation, Mu1197 and Mu930. Following growth in MHB supplemented with zinc, there was an increase in the frequency of the colistin-resistant subpopulation compared to growth without zinc (fig. S11, C and D). These results are consistent with σE regulating a colistin-resistant subpopulation across distinct E. cloacae complex isolates.

The σE pathway generates heterogeneity but is not sufficient for HR

PhoPQ is involved in arn induction and colistin HR in RS, but its impact on σE activation remained unclear. The heterogeneous activation of σE, as measured using the micA reporter, was maintained in the ΔphoPQ mutant (Fig. 5A compared to Fig. 2D), indicating that PhoPQ is not required. This finding is also supported by the PAP result of the ΔphoPQ mutant, where a subpopulation of roughly 1 in 100,000 cells with a higher minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) than the majority population was present in colistin (0.25, 0.5, and 1 μg/ml), demonstrating the maintenance of heterogeneity (Fig. 1F). However, none of the cells in the ΔphoPQ mutant were classified as colistin-resistant since none of them survived at the clinical breakpoint concentration of 4 μg/ml (Fig. 1F). This is consistent with vastly reduced arn reporter expression in the ΔphoPQ mutant (Fig. 5B compared to Fig. 1G). These data highlight that (i) overall population phenotypic heterogeneity (heterogeneous survival) and HR (resistance of a subpopulation at the clinical breakpoint) are separable traits in strain RS and (ii) that PhoPQ is not required for phenotypic heterogeneity but is required for the overall increase in MIC of a subpopulation to and beyond the clinical breakpoint, thus making RS heteroresistant to colistin. These results demonstrate that the σE pathway is the generator of phenotypic heterogeneity in RS and PhoPQ is a generator of resistance, with the combined activity of these two regulatory systems being required to generate colistin HR.

Using the ΔrseA mutant in which σE activity is constitutive, we observed that the σE pathway can also affect arn expression. In the ΔrseA mutant, the population shifted toward increased arn expression (Fig. 4D) and elevated colistin resistance (Fig. 2C), accompanied by higher arnB transcript levels by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) relative to wild type (fig. S12E). However, this increase in arn expression did not appear to result from σE activating PhoPQ, as expression of the PhoPQ-regulated gene mgtA was unchanged (fig. S12E). Moreover, phoPQ deletion in the ΔrseA mutant only led to a modest reduction in the frequency of the resistant subpopulation relative to ΔrseA (figs. S12F and S13) and did not notably reduce arn expression (fig. S12, G and H).

This contrasted with the ΔrseAΔarnB double mutant, in which no resistant cells were detected (Fig. 4A). We found no evidence that σE directly enhances arn expression, and the arn promoter lacks a clear σE binding motif. Instead, activation of the broader σE regulon may indirectly elevate arn expression. Together, these data highlight that in the setting of robust, constitutive σE activation, arn plays a more critical role in colistin HR than PhoPQ and that σE activation can induce arn expression, although it is unclear whether this is a direct or indirect effect.

DISCUSSION

Clinical isolates are conventionally categorized as susceptible or resistant to a given antibiotic, classifications which omit the phenotypic heterogeneity observed in HR. Colistin HR is a frequent feature of clinical Enterobacter isolates, is often undetected by conventional antibiotic susceptibility testing, and can cause treatment failure in murine models of infection. However, the mechanism that generates phenotypic heterogeneity in this system has remained unclear.

Here, we performed a transposon screen for loss of colistin HR. While identification of sspB and clpP led us to pursue the involvement of alternative sigma factor σE in colistin HR (Fig. 2), there were other transposon mutants of interest that have not yet been pursued. These include mutants in genes encoding predicted outer membrane proteins (FhuA, a ferrichrome transporter, and OmpW), as well as inner membrane proteins (MdfA, a multidrug transporter; YajC, a translocase subunit; ProY, a cryptic proline permease). Mutations of these membrane proteins could alter membrane homeostasis and potentially σE activation or arn activity, altering lipid A modification and colistin HR. In addition, the most frequently identified transposon mutant encoded the phosphoenolpyruvate synthetase regulatory kinase/phosphorylase PpsR, although we were unable to generate a corresponding deletion mutant. While several avenues remain to be pursued in the future, the screen identified a major regulator of colistin HR in σE.

We have demonstrated that in the Enterobacter isolates examined here, regulatory pathways do not always align with those described in E. coli or other Enterobacterales. In strain RS, pmrB is a predicted pseudogene, and genetic inactivation of pmrAB did not affect colistin HR (fig. S8, E and F), which was also observed for other Enterobacter HR isolates (17). While E. coli overexpression of micA leads to direct inhibitory base pairing of phoP (31), Enterobacter micA is divergent from that of E. coli and Salmonella (36), and we observed that deletion of micA resulted in no change to colistin HR in Enterobacter strain RS (fig. S8, C and D).

In a population of wild-type cells of strain RS, before colistin exposure, ∼1% of the population exhibited increased σE activity, making up a distinct second peak in the bimodal distribution of σE activity (Fig. 2D). Single-cell tracking revealed that these cells with high σE activity exclusively survived colistin exposure (Fig. 3D). Although PhoPQ has been strongly linked to colistin HR, we were surprised to find that this system is not the generator of phenotypic heterogeneity, which was maintained in a ΔphoPQ mutant strain (Fig. 5A). However, the heterogeneity was lost, as evidenced by a unimodal curve of σE activity, when σE was constitutively activated via deletion of the σE inhibitory antisigma factor, rseA (Fig. 3E), further demonstrating that the σE pathway is the generator of heterogeneity. It remains unclear why σE activity is bimodal in wild-type cells and what drives the emergence of a distinct σE-high subpopulation. Explanations could include certain cells having different activities in any of the components of the σE activation cascade: RseA, RseB, RseP, or ClpP and SspB, identified in our screen. In addition, stochastic differences in rpoE expression could generate a positive feedback loop and establish bimodality because σE is autoregulatory. These differences could occur at the level of binding to and activating the rpoE promoter or at the antisigma activity of RseA, among others. These possibilities including bimodal expression or bimodal protein abundance of rseA, clpP, or sspB remain to be explored.

While phenotypic heterogeneity was dependent on the activity of the σE pathway, HR to colistin nonetheless required PhoPQ as none of the cells of the ΔphoPQ mutant survived at the colistin clinical breakpoint (Fig. 1F). The cells surviving colistin exposure also had higher arn expression (Fig. 4C), which required PhoPQ (Fig. 5B). These data highlight that PhoPQ is not required for phenotypic heterogeneity but is required for the overall increase in MIC of a subpopulation to the clinical breakpoint, thus generating colistin HR. These data demonstrate that overall population phenotypic heterogeneity (heterogeneous survival) and HR (resistance of a subpopulation at the clinical breakpoint) are separable traits in strain RS. Further, these results reveal that the σE pathway is the generator of phenotypic heterogeneity in RS, and PhoPQ is a generator of resistance, with the combined activity of these two regulatory systems being required to generate colistin HR. Together, these data elucidate a mechanistic framework for HR via the combinatorial activity of distinct regulatory pathways (Fig. 6).

Proposed model for bimodality in σE generating a colistin-resistant subpopulation.

A proposed model for the activity of signaling pathways active in the subpopulation of cells with preexisting colistin resistance. Before colistin exposure, σE promoter activity is distinctly bimodal, while arn promoter activity follows a broad unimodal expression. High σE cells have higher arn promoter activation and are colistin resistant, while cells with moderately high Parn activity but low σE activation remain colistin susceptible. PhoPQ directly activates transcription of the arn operon, while activation of σE additionally directly or indirectly increases arn transcription, resulting in lipid A modification that prevents colistin entry, such that in ΔrseA mutant, arn expression is generally increased but remains unimodal, while nearly every cell has high σE and therefore colistin resistant. In ΔphoPQ, σE bimodality remains like wild type, but the absence of lipid A modifications results in an entirely susceptible population. WT, wild type.

The mechanism by which σE influences l-Ara4N modification of lipid A remains unclear, and the increased arn expression observed in the ΔrseA strain may reflect either a direct or indirect effect of σE (fig. S12, C to E). Constitutive activation of the σE regulon likely causes widespread membrane changes that might trigger envelope stress, resulting in lipid A remodeling through unidentified pathways. In E. coli, σE affects LPS biosynthesis through members of its regulon: plsB, bacA, and lpxA/B/D/P (37), as well as lipid A modification through complex regulation including sRNAs and two-component systems (38). As discussed directly above, however, whether these regulatory relationships hold true in Enterobacter, and whether these regulators affect colistin HR, remain to be assessed. In addition, altered LPS or LPS biosynthetic intermediates have been found to activate σE in E. coli (39, 40), suggesting a complex interplay between responding to LPS disruptions and regulating LPS modification. Hence, bimodal activation of σE may result in a small population of cells with higher rates of LPS synthesis and arn-mediated modifications.

We propose that σE and PhoPQ act independently but converge on arn expression, to generate σE (high)/arn (high), σE (low)/arn (high), and σE (low)/arn (low) cells, but not σE (high)/arn (low) cells. The σE (high) cells, which are distinctly resistant (fig. S12A), correspond to arn fluorescence intensities above 400 (fig. S12B); as all resistant cells fall within this range, indicating that they represent the σE (high)/arn (high) subpopulation. This precludes the existence of σE (high)/arn(low) cells. However, there are cells in the susceptible population with greater arn activity than resistant cells (see fluorescence intensity range of ∼400 to 550 in Fig. 4C and fig. S12B), evidence of the σE (low)/arn (high) population. There are likely additional arn-independent changes in the σE (high) subpopulation that contribute to colistin resistance because resistant cells must absolutely have high σE, but the requirement for high arn is not as strict (Figs. 3D and 4C).

In the absence of rseA, σE activity is elevated across the population; however, σE (high)/arn (high) remain colistin resistant, while a minor population of arn-high cells (∼400 to 550 gfp of Parn in Fig. 4D) is susceptible, corresponding to comparatively lower σE activity (∼300 to 600 gfp range of PmicA in Fig. 3E). Notably, σE (high)/arn(low) cells were again not observed (Fig. 4, C and D). Consistent with our hypothesis that PhoPQ and σE function independently, arn expression is similar between RS::Parngfp and ΔrseA ΔphoPQ::Parngfp [compare geometric mean fluorescence intensity (GMFI) in fig. S12 (B and H)], demonstrating that constitutive σE activity can overcome the contribution of PhoPQ on arn expression. However, when RseA is present and σE activity is at a nonconstitutive level, PhoPQ is critical to achieve arn expression levels required for a resistant subpopulation and there remains a possibility of σE affecting PhoPQ (Figs. 1F, 5B, and 6).

This combinatorial regulatory model of HR differs markedly from the best-characterized mechanism of HR, in which one event, an increase in copy number of a given single gene, generates HR. The most well-established example is tandem gene amplification of a DNA segment including a β-lactamase gene and leading to overproduction of the β-lactamase in the population with increased gene copy number. This mechanism can generate a broad continuum of cellular subpopulations resistant to a given β-lactam antibiotic (41). It should be noted that gene amplification can cause polymyxin HR as was observed in Salmonella Typhimurium (42) and E. coli (43). However, gene amplification conferring colistin HR does not appear to be a highly prevalent mechanism at least among Enterobacter isolates. In addition, a report of aminoglycoside HR in E. cloacae involves the two-component system CpxRA, which senses envelope stress, with some parallel to the involvement of regulatory systems in this work (44).

It remains unclear why lipid A is not constitutively modified with l-Ara4N in all cells, which would otherwise result in uniform resistance to colistin and other CAMPs. There is a fitness cost associated with constitutive σE activation, which is toxic in E. coli [reviewed in (21)]. Thus, HR may serve as a form of bet-hedging, in which a dynamic preexisting subpopulation can coexist with other phenotypically diverse subpopulations and can be transiently enriched when the selective pressure is present. The mammalian host is one such environment, as the colistin-resistant subpopulation in strain RS is enriched during murine infection by the selective pressures of the murine LL-37 ortholog mCRAMP, lysozyme, and NADPH oxidase, even in the absence of colistin treatment (16). Thus, bimodality in σE activation may confer on the population an advantage in resistance to antibiotic and host antimicrobial stresses, without a commitment of the entire population to costly LPS modifications.

This study does have limitations and open questions remain. We do not understand the mechanism by which σE responds to the presence of colistin, or how σE increases arn-mediated lipid A modifications. However, our model accommodates various levels of arn expression induced by PhoPQ signaling. Hence, genetic variation in PhoPQ or RseA/σE may affect the frequency of the resistant subpopulation by changing the baseline resistance that, when combined with σE activation, could result in high- or low-frequency resistant subpopulations (e.g., compare the PAP profiles of RS and Mu208 wild-type strains; Fig. 1A and fig. S10, A and B).

Our model of combinatorial regulatory signaling converging on the arn resistance determinant is consistent with work on colistin HR in other Enterobacter, where inactivating PhoPQ/arn eliminates the resistant subpopulation, and manipulating PhoPQ activity changes the frequency of the resistant subpopulation, but not the baseline heterogeneity in the population (12, 17). In sum, we have identified a transcriptional regulator that generates phenotypic heterogeneity to colistin in the population. This heterogeneity facilitates HR when the PhoPQ two-component system increases the overall resistance level of all the cells in the population. Thus, this combinatorial regulatory model is a paradigm in the mechanistic generation of HR, a phenomenon which is increasingly recognized as a cause of antibiotic treatment failure in the clinic.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Isolate information

E. cloacae complex strain RS was isolated from a blood sample from a renal transplant recipient at Emory University Hospital, Atlanta, GA and described previously (16, 45). The RS genome is available in National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank, accession numbers CP010512 and CP010513. E. cloacae ColS1 and ColR2 are clinical isolates and described previously (45). The Mu208 genome is available in NCBI GenBank, accession numbers CP182179, CP182180, and CP182181. Mu208, Mu1197, and Mu930 were collected as part of the Georgia Emerging Infections Program as part of the CDC’s EIP Multi-site Gram-negative Surveillance Initiative in Georgia, USA. Additional strains and plasmids in this paper are detailed in table S4.

Reagents

Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA; BD Difco) and MHB (BD Difco) were used throughout as indicated. For growth of E. cloacae RS before plating on MHA containing colistin, bacteria were grown at 37°C with shaking, 1.5 ml of volume in 12 mm–by–75 mm polypropylene aeration tubes (Globe Scientific, #110438). YESCA was made by autoclaving yeast extract (1 g/liter; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #BP1422) and casamino acids (10 g/liter; Acros, #61204) in water. YESCA agar included agar (20 g/liter; Sigma-Aldrich, #A1296). Antibiotics used were as follows: colistin sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich, #C4461), kanamycin sulfate (Teknova, #K2150; 90 μg/ml for strain RS and 35 μg/ml for strain Mu208), tetracycline (Alfa Aesar, #J61714; 20 μg/ml for RS), chloramphenicol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #BP904; 50 μg/ml for RS), and spectinomycin dihydrochloride pentahydrate (Sigma-Aldrich, #S4014; 50 μg/ml for E. coli and 300 μg/ml for RS). Medium gel pads used for cell imaging under a microscope were made with agarose (UltraPure agarose; Invitrogen, #16500100) and MHB to prepare MHB 1.5% agarose gel pad.

Population analysis profile

PAP was performed as indicated in fig. S1. A given strain was grown overnight from a single colony streaked to MHA from −80°C glycerol stocks. After ∼16 to 20 hours of growth, the culture was diluted 1:1000 (except for strains with rseA or clpP mutation, which were diluted 1:250 to account for diminished growth yield of these strains) into fresh media and grown until optical density (OD) reached ∼0.3. In experiments using zinc enrichment, cultures were diluted into fresh MHB containing 0.9 mM zinc chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, Z0152). In experiments using minimal media, cultures diluted 1:100 and grown in M9 media supplemented with 0.4% glucose and either 10 μM MgSO4 or 10 mM MgSO4. The cultures were serially diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) in a 96-well plate (Falcon), and 7.5 or 10 μl of each dilution was plated on MHA containing colistin as indicated. Colonies were enumerated after 24 to 48 hours of growth. For many experiments shown, the same protocol was followed, but the cultures were plated only on MHA containing 0 or a single colistin concentration indicated.

The surviving colonies are enumerated, and the isolate is classified as resistant if at least 50% of the total colonies grow at 1× or 2× breakpoint. An isolate is considered susceptible if less than 0.0001% (−6 logs) of the cells grow at any concentration shown. An isolate is considered heteroresistant if there is greater than 0.0001% survival at 1× and 2× breakpoint and an ≥8-fold difference between the resistance of the subpopulation and resistance of the main population. As an example for RS, <50% of the population survives at 0.125 μg/ml, thus the MICsusceptible population is 0.125 μg/ml. The subpopulation survives up to 100 μg/ml, so the MICresistant population of the resistant population is >100 μg/ml. For RS, the fold change of the MICresistant population relative to MICsusceptible population is >8. The limit of detection is approximately −5.5 to 6 logs but varies on the basis of the density of the culture, and in the figures, the y axis is set to the approximate limit of detection for each graph.

In defining the features of HR in the isolates of this work, based on guidelines set forth by the field (10, 13, 46):

1) Clonality: These isolates demonstrate monoclonal HR, and they are purified isolates and single colonies are used throughout experiments.

2) Level of resistance: The MIC of the resistant subpopulation in wild-type RS is ≥8× the MIC of the main population, when comparing the amount of killing by the lowest concentration of colistin used throughout, and the growth of the resistant subpopulation at 100 μg/ml.

3) Frequency of the resistant subpopulation: We consider the frequency of the subpopulation at 2× the CLSI breakpoint, and for wild-type RS, the frequency is ∼0.1 to 1%.

4) Instability in the frequency of the resistant subpopulation: Wild-type RS demonstrated unstable HR. As shown in the passage experiments after selection, there was a significant reduction in the resistant population frequency within a single passage (∼10 generations per passage) in antibiotic free media.

Copy number variation sequencing analysis

Illumina sequencing of samples of RS previously grown in Mueller Hinton broth containing colistin (0 or 100 μg/ml) (16) was accessed from NCBI SRA. Reads were mapped to the respective reference genome with CNV analysis. Quality control and adapter trimming were performed with bcl2fastq (47). Reads were mapped to their respective references via bwa mem (48). PCR and optical duplicates were marked and excluded from the analysis using PicardTools’ “MarkDuplicates” (49) functionality. Aligned read counts were imported into the R’s CNOGpro (50) package. CNV events were called via CNOGpro using a bootstrapping method, which calculates an average gene number event giving a possible upper and lower bound. Genome sequencing and analysis were performed by SeqCenter (seqcenter.com). Table S1 details the analysis results as well as SRA accessions for each sample.

Resistance stability assays

RS was streaked from −80°C glycerol stocks to MHA for isolation, and a single colony was used to start overnight cultures in 1.5 ml of MHB and grown 15 to 20 hours. Fifteen microliters of the cultures was diluted into 1.5 ml of MHB and grown until OD reached ∼0.3 to 0.4. These cultures were diluted and plated MHA containing colistin (0 or 100 μg/ml), the baseline. Fifteen microliters of the cultures was added to 1.5 ml of fresh MHB containing colistin (100 μg/ml) and grown for 20 hours. Following growth, the cultures were diluted and plated MHA containing colistin (0 or 100 μg/ml). A total of 1.5 μl of the cultures was added to 1.5 ml of fresh MHB and grown until OD reached ∼0.3 to 0.4. Following growth, the cultures were diluted and plated MHA containing colistin (0 or 100 μg/ml).

Lipid A mass spectrometry

For sample growth, strain RS and indicated mutants were grown overnight in 1.5 ml of MHB, and 120 μl was added to 12 ml of fresh MHB in a 50-ml conical tube and grown for 4 hours and then harvested by centrifugation. For RS grown in colistin, 100 μl of overnight culture was added to 10 ml of MHB containing colistin (100 μg/ml) in a 50-ml conical tube, grown for 9 hours, and then harvested by centrifugation. Cells were washed in PBS and stored in −80°C.

A fast lipid analysis technique (FLAT) (51) was adapted as follows: Bacteria were scraped from frozen pellets and spotted directly onto a steel matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) plate. One microliter of FLAT extraction buffer (0.2 M anhydrous citric acid and 0.1 M trisodium citrate dihydrate) was spotted on top of the plated bacteria. The MALDI plate was placed into a humidified 100°C “panini-press” chamber for 30 min. Afterward, the plate was rinsed with endotoxin-free water and air dried. One microliter of Norharmane matrix [10 mg/ml in 2:1 (v/v) chloroform-methanol solution] was spotted on each sample. Samples were analyzed in linear, negative ion mode on a Bruker Microflex LRF. Data were processed with flexAnalysis software.

Imaging cells and GFP expression

RS wild type or mutants encoding indicated promoter-gfp fusions were used to visualize population-level variation in promoter activation before colistin exposure and to assess viability following treatment. Imaging of cells and GFP expression was performed as previously described (52, 53). Cells were grown to an OD600 of approximately 0.1 in the indicated medium (MHB or M9 supplemented with 0.4% glucose and 10 mM MgSO4). Cells were then transferred onto a 35-mm glass-bottom petri dish (Cellvis) and overlaid with a 1.5% agarose gel pad prepared in the corresponding medium for imaging. Cells were imaged using an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope with a 60× oil immersion phase-contrast objective, housed within a temperature-controlled (37°C) incubation chamber (InVivo Scientific). GFP fluorescence was detected using a GFP filter cube. Snapshot imaging of cells was performed before exposure to colistin. Time-lapse images of cells were captured at 30-min intervals after adding colistin to the agarose pad covering cells.

Image analysis

Fluorescence intensity of GFP was measured using a plug-in for Fiji ImageJ software, named MicrobeJ (54) [version 5.13 I-22]. Reported fluorescence intensities represent intracellular GFP signals after subtracting both extracellular background and the autofluorescence of wild-type RS cells. Cell viability under colistin treatment was determined through time-lapse imaging. Continuous cellular division and colony formation represented survival. Killing of cells by colistin was defined as visible lysis or permanent arrest of cell growth.

Transposon screen

Seven hundred fifty microliters of a culture of a RS att::Parngfp+ was combined with 750 μl of E. coli BW19851 carrying pFD1, collected by centrifugation and resuspended in 50 μl of YESCA (yeast extract casamino acids broth). The suspension was plated on a 0.45 μM nitrocellulose membrane placed on top of a YESCA agar plate and incubated at 37°C for 2.5 hours. The nitrocellulose was washed in a 15-ml tube with YESCA containing 1 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside and spectinomycin (300 μg/ml), grown for 3 hours at 37°C, diluted, and plated on MHA containing spectinomycin (300 μg/ml) and kanamycin (90 μg/ml). After 24 hours of growth at 37°C, colonies were replica-plated using sterile velvet onto MHA containing kanamycin or kanamycin and colistin (100 μg/ml). Colonies that appeared on kanamycin but not kanamycin + colistin were restreaked for isolation. To identify the transposon insertion site, colony PCR was performed using primers 71/72 to yield the DNA flanking the transposase. Two microliters of the PCR product from JCP71/72 was the template for subsequent nested PCR with JCP73/74. PCR products were Sanger sequenced (Azenta) using JCP74 and aligned to the RS genome.

Strain construction: Gene deletions

Lambda red–based allelic exchange was used to replace the coding sequence of genes with a kanamycin resistance gene as indicated. The kanamycin resistance gene from pEXR6K_kanFRT was cloned using Promega GoTaq 2X master mix with Flp recognition sequence and homology to regions flanking the gene to be replaced using primers JCP171/172 (phoPQ), EKC202/203 (arnB), EKC301/302 (sspB), JCP59/60 (clpP), EKC309/310 (rseA), JCP56/57 (rseB), EKC313/314 (micA), JCP692/693 (rybB), JCP688/689 (pmrAB), and JCP700/701 (relA) detailed in table S5. Note that pmrB is predicted to be a pseudogene with premature stop codon. The purified PCR product was electroporated into competent RS (or mutant) carrying pKD46-tet (55), and transformants were selected on kanamycin. Transformants were restreaked to kanamycin for isolation and subject to PCR for successful allelic exchange as indicated by a product size change using primers flanking the gene to be replaced: primers JCP173/174 (phoPQ), EKC208/209 (arnB), EKC303/304 (sspB), JCP61/62 (clpP), EKC311/312 (rseA), JCP58/EKC311 (rseB), EKC315/316 (micA), JCP694/695 (rybB) JCP690/691 (pmrAB), and JCP702/703 (relA) detailed in table S5. For rseA::kan_FRT in Mu208, the protocol above was followed except that kanamycin (35 μg/ml) was used as selection. Mu208 pKD46-tet was transformed with PCR product from JCP662/663 and checked by PCR with JCP664/665.

As indicated, the kanamycin resistance gene was removed with Flp recombinase (56, 57) to create unmarked in-frame deletions. The mutants were then made electrocompetent and electroporated with pCP20 (57). Transformants were selected at 30°C on chloramphenicol, patched to chloramphenicol at 30°C, grown for 24 hours, and then patched to MHA and MHA + kanamycin. Kanamycin-sensitive mutants were streaked for isolation, and PCR was used with the same flanking primers to screen for the loss of the kanamycin resistance gene.

For PrmicAgfp, pCD13PSK was digested with Bam HI, the micA promoter region was an ultramer JCP41, and promoterless gfp+ was cloned with JCP31/42 from the vector pZEP07 (58) as template. pCD13PSK gfp+ was amplified with JCP556/557 and assembled with Hifi assembly for the following: arn promoter cloned with JCP558/559 for Parngfp, rpoS promoter cloned with JCP704/705 for PrpoSgfp+, rpoD promoter cloned with JCP706/707 for PrpoDgfp+, and rpoN promoter cloned with JCP708/709 for PrpoNgfp+. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction

For LL-37 treatment, RS was grown overnight (16 to 18 hours) in MHB, and subsequently, 6 μl of culture was added to fresh 3 ml of MHB and grown to OD600 of ∼0.3. Human cathelicidin LL-37 (50 μg/ml; Elabscience, E-PP-1466) was added for 30 min. One hundred microliters of aliquots was taken before and after LL-37 exposure and plated on MHA following serial dilution.

RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, #74104) and then treated with deoxyribonuclease (BioLab, #M0303S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was stored at −80°C. Reverse transcriptase qPCR was performed with the Power SYBR Green RNA to CT 1-Step Kit (Applied Biosystems, #4389986). cysG served as the housekeeping gene for normalization. cysG (JCP241/242), mgtA (mgtA-1F/R), arnB (arnB-3F/R), rseA (rseA-2F/R), and rpoE (JCP608/609) were detected by primers detailed in table S5. Fold change was determined for each biological replicate by comparison to the same replicate without LL-37 exposure. Fold change was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method (64).

LL-37 killing assay

Strains RS and the ΔrseA mutant were streaked to MHA from −80°C glycerol stocks. After overnight growth, a colony was inoculated into 1.5 ml of MHB. After approximately 16 to 20 hours of growth, the culture was diluted 1:1000 for RS and 1:500 for ΔrseA into 1 ml of fresh MHB, and an aliquot was taken, serially diluted, and plated on MHA for enumeration. After 3.5 hours of growth, an aliquot was taken for enumeration, and then LL-37 (100 μg/ml; Elabscience, E-PP-1466) was added. At 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 hours following treatment, aliquots were taken for CFU enumeration.

Broth culture reporter fluorescence assay

RS::PmicAGFP was grown overnight in MHB and diluted 1:100 in fresh MHB or MHB supplemented with 0.9 mM zinc chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, #Z0152) in a black clear-bottomed 96-well plate. The plate was incubated at 37°C with continuous shaking in a BioTek Synergy 2 plate reader. Over the course of 12 hours, GFP fluorescence and OD600 were measured every 30 min. Fluorescence was measured using 485/20 excitation and 528/20 emission wavelength settings. Reporter activity was calculated as GFP fluorescence normalized to optical density (GFP/OD600).
