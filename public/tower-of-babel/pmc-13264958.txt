Ultra-processed food and risk of gastric cancer: a systematic review
Authors: Abdur Rahman, Tahmid Hussain, Munia Afroz, Sakib Rahman, Malay Kanti Mridha, Abu Ahmed Shamim, Fahmida Akter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUltra-processed food (UPF) consumption has increased globally and has been linked to several non-communicable diseases including cancer. However, evidence regarding its association with gastric cancer (GC) remains limited and inconsistent. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the association between UPF consumption and GC risk.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024504965). PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar were searched for studies published from January 1991 to April 2024, with an updated search in February 2025. Eligible studies were observational studies assessing the association between UPF consumption, specific UPF categories or UPF-related dietary patterns and GC risk. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data and assessed methodological quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist. Due to heterogeneity in exposure definitions, outcome classification and effect estimates, findings were synthesised narratively.ResultsSeventeen studies were included, comprising 14 case–control studies and 3 cohort studies, with a total of 276825 participants. Eight studies reported positive associations between UPF consumption and GC risk, three reported no association and six showed mixed findings depending on UPF category, population subgroup or cancer subtype. Processed meats, fried or fast foods, sugary beverages, highly processed dairy products and Western-style dietary patterns were more frequently associated with increased GC risk. However, findings varied across studies due to differences in dietary assessment methods, UPF classification, exposure categories and confounder adjustment.ConclusionsThe available evidence suggests a potential association between UPF consumption and GC risk, particularly for processed meats, fried foods and UPF-rich dietary patterns. However, the evidence remains inconclusive because of methodological heterogeneity and reliance on observational studies. Further prospective studies using standardised UPF classifications and validated dietary assessment tools are needed.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024504965.Keywords: Systematic Review, Public Health, Food Safety

Introduction

Ultra-processed foods (UPFs), as categorised by the NOVA classification, are industrially manufactured products composed of food-derived ingredients and additives, often subjected to intensive processes such as hydrogenation, extrusion or chemical modification.1 2 These foods are generally characterised by high levels of added sugars, total fats and sodium, with low fibre and micronutrient content, resulting in a high energy density but poor nutritional quality.3,5 Their widespread accessibility and convenience have established them as an important component of modern diets, contributing up to 50% of daily caloric intake in urbanised and high-income countries such as Australia, USA6 7; and up to 30% in middle-income countries such as Chile and Mexico.8 9 In low and middle-income countries, UPF consumption is also notable. Studies have shown that in Delhi, India, UPFs contribute an average of 16.2% to total daily energy intake, while in rural Bangladesh, 83% of adolescents consume at least one ultra-processed or deep-fried item daily, with packaged confectioneries being the most consumed (53.5%).10 11 While UPFs are designed to be palatable, affordable and shelf-stable, increasing evidence highlights significant health risks associated with their consumption, driven by their poor nutritional quality and the presence of additives, emulsifiers12 13 and neo-formed contaminants like acrylamide14 and advanced glycation end products.15 The role of UPFs in the aetiology of chronic diseases, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, Crohn’s disease, symptoms of depression in adults and hypertension, is well documented.16,23 More recently, attention has turned to the potential role of dietary patterns in cancer development, including gastric cancer (GC).24,26

GC remains a significant global health burden, ranking as the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide.27 Projections indicate that, by 2040, the global burden of GC is expected to rise significantly, with an estimated 1.63 million new cases and 1.18 million deaths, driven primarily by population growth and ageing demographics.28 Dietary factors, including high salt intake, low consumption of fresh fruits and vegetables and the growing reliance on UPFs, have been implicated in its pathogenesis.29,31

While very few studies have specifically investigated UPFs in relation to GC, many studies linking dietary factors to GC risk have focused on foods that align closely with the definition of UPFs, underscoring the relevance of exploring this connection explicitly.2932,35 There is an urgent need for systematic reviews to elucidate this relationship more comprehensively and clarify the potential mechanisms involved.

Therefore, this review aimed to systematically evaluate the association between UPF consumption and GC risk by synthesising evidence from observational studies and examining specific UPF categories and dietary patterns reported in the literature.

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic literature review was carried out in accordance with the guidelines set by Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).36 The review protocol has been registered with PROSPERO (registration number CRD42024504965).

Two authors (AR and TH) independently conducted systematic searches across several databases, including PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar, covering data from January 1991 to April 2024. Each database was searched on the same dates (January 11, 2024, and updated February 19, 2025). The search utilised keywords, incorporating medical subject headings (MeSH) in Boolean operators: (“Stomach cancer” OR “gastric cancer” OR “Stomach neoplasms” OR “Gastric carcinoma” OR “Gastric adenocarcinoma” OR “Gastric Neoplasm” OR “Gastric cancer pathway” OR “Gastric adenoma”) AND (“Ultra-processed food " OR ”Junk food” OR “Dietary Factors” OR " Ultra-processed foods " OR “Processed food products” OR “Industrialized foods” OR “Highly processed foods” OR “Dietary Habit” OR “Ready-to-eat foods” OR “Fast food” OR “diet”). The detailed search terms are provided in online supplemental table 1 and table 2). Google Scholar count reflects the number of top-ranked records screened after relevance filtering, as recommended by methodological guidance for Google Scholar use in systematic reviews.37 38

The references of all included studies were identified during the database searches to include any potentially overlooked publications. To facilitate the referral process, all studies were stored and screened in Rayyan.39 Any discrepancies regarding the eligibility of studies were resolved through consensus and discussion with a third investigator (FA). Inclusion criteria

For this study, UPFs were defined by the NOVA classification system.40 In studies that reported association between several food items and GC, association with only UPF items was considered, and other food items were overlooked.33 Studies that reported association between any particular UPFs with GC were also considered.41 Additionally, studies that showed association between dietary patterns or dietary habits with GC were also considered and among the patterns, only ultra-processed-based dietary patterns were reported in this study and other patterns not related to UPFs were disregarded.29

Other inclusion criteria for this review were studies that were observational such as cross-sectional, case–control and cohort studies; studies published in English; studies that evaluated the association with GC and studies that examined the association (OR, risk ratio (RR), HR with 95% CI) between UPF consumption and GC.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) review articles and grey literature, including book chapters, letters and comments, and editorial and case reports; (2) studies conducted on animals; (3) studies reporting only descriptive results without statistical associations such as OR, RR or HR with corresponding 95% CIs; (4) studies that did not evaluate the direct consumption of UPFs or that did not employ the NOVA food classification system; (5) studies that did not mention GC as an outcome.

Selection criteria

After removing duplicate articles, two reviewers (AR and TH) independently screened all titles and abstracts using the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements at this stage were resolved by consulting a third reviewer (FA). Full-text assessments were then carried out independently by another pair of reviewers (MA and SR), and reasons for excluding studies were documented. Data extraction for the final set of eligible studies was completed using a structured, pilot-tested form developed by the research team. Any inconsistencies regarding study selection or data extraction were addressed through discussion, with input from the third reviewer (FA) when needed. All screening activities were managed through Rayyan to facilitate a systematic and streamlined review process. At all stages of screening and quality appraisal, reviewers worked independently and were blinded to each other’s decisions until reconciliation.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted individually by two authors (AR and TH) using an Excel spreadsheet. Any disagreements were either resolved through consensus or referred to a third investigator (FA) if needed. The extracted data from the original articles included the first author, year of publication, study design, the country of the study population, sample size, population, sex, study period covered, exposure, tools used for dietary data collection, methods employed to evaluate UPF intake, outcome, key findings (association of UPFs with GC, effect size measures and CIs).

Due to substantial heterogeneity across studies in terms of exposure definitions (individual food items vs dietary patterns), outcome classifications (cardia vs non-cardia GC) and reporting formats of effect estimates, a formal meta-analysis was not performed. Instead, a narrative synthesis approach was adopted to summarise findings.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies in this review was assessed using Joanna Briggs Institute’s (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for cross-sectional, cohort and case–control studies.42 The tool includes 10 assessment categories for cross-sectional studies: (1) well-defined inclusion criteria; (2) suitable study population and context; (3) accurate measurement of exposure; (4) valid and reliable measurement of outcomes; (5) control for confounding factors; (6) adequate follow-up duration; (7) proper statistical analysis; (8) sufficient sample size; (9) use of control groups and (10) clear presentation of results. For cohort studies, the tool has 11 assessment categories: (1) similar groups from the same population; (2) similar exposure measurement; (3) reliable exposure measurement; (4) confounding factors identified; (5) strategies for confounding stated; (6) participants are free of outcome at baseline; (7) reliable outcome measurement; (8) sufficient follow-up time; (9) completion of follow-up; (10) strategies for incomplete follow-up; (11) proper statistical analysis. The answer to each question was assigned a score, as follows: yes (1); NA (0), ie, not applicable or not mentioned; or no (0), unclear (0). Two independent reviewers (AR and TH) assessed the quality of the studies. A score greater than 6 or equal was considered high-quality study and included in this review, while a score less than 6 was considered poor quality study and excluded from the study. In the event that the reviewers disagreed about the scores, it was resolved through consensus and discussion in the presence of a third reviewer (FA).

Results

Study selection

The search strategy identified a total of 5577 potential articles. This number includes publications found through reference screening of the articles reviewed, with 2612 sourced from PubMed, 2854 from Scopus and 111 from Google Scholar. After removing 1641 duplicates and 3456 irrelevant articles based on their titles or abstracts, we retained 480 articles. Among them, 70 could not be retrieved in full text due to access limitations, such as availability only as abstracts or being behind paywalls. Despite efforts to obtain full texts through alternative means, including contacting authors, using university library services, and exploring open-access platforms, access could not be granted. As a result, these 70 articles were excluded, leaving us with 410 articles for inclusion in the review. On reviewing the full texts, we excluded 393 articles. Among these exclusions, 67 were review articles, 146 yielded irrelevant outcomes, 131 did not mention UPFs as the exposure and 49 were wrong study design. In the end, 17 articles were included in this study. Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flowchart detailing the study selection process.

Study characteristics

The general characteristics of included studies are outlined in online supplemental table 4. Of all the included studies, 14 were case–control and three had a cohort design, accounting for a total of 276 825 participants with an age range between 18 and 93 years. Among the included studies, 12 studies reported association directly with UPFs while five studies reported association with dietary patterns (table 1). All included studies were conducted in Brazil, Canada, China, Germany, Iran, Japan, Jordan, Korea, Mexico, Netherlands, Portugal, Russia, South Korea, Spain, Sweden and Uruguay.

Study results

Out of 17 studies, 8 studies (47%) found a positive association with GC, 3 studies (18%) reported a negative association and 6 studies (35%) showed both positive and no association depending on the UPF category. The results were divided into two categories: studies examining the direct association between individual food items and GC, and those exploring the relationship between dietary patterns and GC. UPFs in selected studies are categorised in online supplemental table 3. Below is a narrative synthesis of the findings for each category.

Association between specific foods and GC

Several studies have explored the direct link between the consumption of specific UPF items and the risk of GC.

Ultra-processed meats and GC

Seven studies3343,46 50 51 investigated the association between ultra-processed meat consumption and GC risk. Among these, five studies33 43 45 46 51 reported a positive association. Wang et al and Peres et al observed significant associations for sausages, bacon and processed meats, highlighting their role in elevating GC risk. Similarly, Ward et al and Boeing et al found strong links between processed meat consumption and a higher risk of GC. Larsson et al also noted significant associations for ham, salami, bacon and processed meats but not for sausages or hotdogs, indicating a varied impact depending on the type of meat products. In contrast, Tayyem et al and Keszei et al reported no significant association between red or processed meat consumption and GC. Keszei et al found null results across different cancer subtypes (gastric cardia and non-cardia) and gender, suggesting potential variability in risk based on individual factors.

Processed dairy products and GC

Five studies3043,46 assessed the association between processed dairy products and GC risk. Among these, four studies3044,46 reported varying associations depending on the type of dairy product. Tayyem et al and Somi et al found significant positive associations for certain processed dairy products, including buttermilk, processed cheese and specially cheeses like Koze and Khiki, indicating an increased risk of GC. Ward et al also identified a positive association with ice cream consumption. In contrast, Boeing et al observed an inverse association, suggesting that higher cheese intake may reduce GC risk. Additionally, Tayyem et al reported no significant associations for some dairy products, such as drained yoghurt and ice cream.

Fried foods, fast foods and convenience foods and GC

Three studies30 34 45 explored the association between fried foods, fast foods, convenience foods and GC risk. All three studies reported a positive association between the consumption of these food categories and an increased risk of GC. Peres et al identified a significant association with pizza, fried snacks and baked snacks, highlighting the risk posed by these popular convenience foods. Kwak et al similarly found a link between the consumption of noodles and dumplings and higher GC risk. Somi et al reported the strongest association, with high-fat foods such as processed yoghurt substantially elevating the risk.

Sweets and desserts and GC

Four studies33 43 45 46 investigated the relationship between sweets, desserts and GC risk. Two studies45 46 reported a significant positive association. Peres et al found that the consumption of sweets in general (cakes, whipped cream, candies, ice cream confectionery sweets cookie, chocolate, gelatin, chocolates) was associated with an elevated GC risk. Similarly, Ward et al identified an increased risk linked to soda and sweet (cakes and candy) consumption. In contrast, Wang et al observed no association between the consumption of cakes and candies and GC risk. Similarly, Boeing et al reported no significant link between cake consumption and GC.

Breads, refined grains and snacks and GC

Four studies30 34 43 46 examined the relationship between the consumption of breads, refined grains, snacks and GC risk. Two studies34 46 found a significant positive association. Kwak et al reported an increased GC risk associated with the consumption of noodles and dumplings, while Ward et al found that salty snacks were significantly linked to higher GC risk. In contrast, two studies30 46 reported no association. Somi et al observed no significant link between bread consumption and GC risk, and Ward et al found no association for breads, cereals and grains. Similarly, Boeing et al noted no significant association between pretzels or salty snacks and GC.

Sugary drinks and carbonated beverages and GC

Three studies45 46 52 explored the relationship between sugary drinks and carbonated beverages and the risk of GC. Khairan et al found no significant association with total GC risk for both males and females who consumed sugary drinks and carbonated beverages, but a significant inverse association was observed for cardia GC in males. In contrast, females showed no significant association for cardia cancer. For non-cardia GC, no associations were found for either gender. Peres et al suggested a higher likelihood of stomach adenocarcinoma associated with sugary drinks but did not provide further details on statistical significance. Ward et al reported a positive association between soda and sweet consumption and GC risk.

Distilled alcoholic beverages and GC

Two studies41 48 examined the association between distilled alcoholic beverages and GC risk. Zaridze et al found that vodka consumption was associated with an increased risk of total GC, with males showing a stronger association (OR: 2.0) compared with females (OR: 1.4). For cardia GC, a significant increase in risk was observed in males (OR: 3.4), but no significant association was found for females (OR: 1.2). Similarly, for non-cardia GC, males had an elevated risk (OR: 1.8), while females showed a weaker association (OR: 1.5). Stefani et al reported a positive association between hard liquor consumption and GC risk (OR: 2.1), with a statistically significant p value of 0.01.

Association between dietary patterns and GC

The association between dietary patterns and GC risk was explored in five studies.29 32 35 47 49 Wu et al reported a positive association for the fast-food pattern, characterised by high consumption of barbecue, fried foods, red meat and seafood. Similarly, Kim et al found that a non-staple food pattern, rich in snacks and carbonated drinks, was linked to an increased risk. While Castelló et al identified no significant association for the Westernised pattern (high in processed and UPFs), Campbell et al reported that the Western diet, featuring processed meats, high-fat dairy, refined grains and sweets, was positively associated with GC. Santiago et al observed an elevated risk with an unhealthy pattern characterised by UPFs. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential role of ultra-processed and Western-style diets in increasing GC risk, with some variation depending on specific dietary components.

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to explore association between UPF consumption and the risk of GC, looking at both individual food items and overall dietary patterns. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first systematic review to examine this association using a globally accepted classification system like the NOVA classification. Among the 17 studies included in this review, eight studies reported a positive association between UPF consumption and GC, three found no association, and six presented mixed results, highlighting the variability in findings across different study settings. The majority of studies were conducted in high-income and upper middle-income countries, where dietary transitions towards UPFs consumption have been well documented.

A majority of studies indicated a positive correlation between the intake of ultra-processed meats—such as sausages, bacon, ham and other processed meats—and the risk of developing GC.33 43 45 46 51 These results are in line with broader evidence suggesting that processed meats contribute to cancer risk, possibly due to the presence of nitrates, nitrites, and other toxic substances formed during the processing.53 54 Nonetheless, the variability in findings, especially in studies that did not show a significant association (Tayyem et al; Keszei et al) highlights the possible impact of individual factors such as cancer types, gender, and eating habits.

The relationship between processed dairy products and GC risk was complex, with some studies identifying increased risk from specific ultra-processed items, such as processed cheeses and buttermilk (Tayyem et al and Somi et al), and others noting protective effects, like higher cheese intake (Boeing et al); interestingly, no significant association was observed for certain items, such as drained yoghurt or ice cream in specific contexts (Tayyem et al). Ultra-processed dairy products often contain additives and preservatives1 that may contribute to GC risk, while less processed items may retain protective nutrients, such as bioactive compounds.

Consistent evidence pointed to a positive association between fried, fast and convenience foods and GC risk.30 34 45 Fried and fast foods can contain carcinogenic compounds like acrylamide, heterocyclic amines (HCAs) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).55 The high-fat content, carcinogenic compounds generated during frying and overall poor nutritional profile of these foods likely contribute to the increased risk. Notably, an existing systematic literature review on fried food intake and GC demonstrated a significant positive association between fried food consumption and the risk of GC, further reinforcing these findings.56

Findings about sweets and desserts were mixed, with some studies indicating a significant association with GC risk, particularly for sugary beverages and general sweet consumption,45 46 while others found no significant link.33 43 The variability may be attributed to differences in study designs, methodological approaches, population characteristics such as age, dietary habits and genetic predispositions as well as the types of sweets evaluated. Findings on bread and refined grains were inconclusive, with some studies reporting a positive association,34 46 particularly with noodles, dumplings and salty snacks, while others observed no significant links.30 43 An existing systematic review found that a high intake of refined grains was associated with an increased risk of colon and GC. This further supports the notion that the degree of refinement and processing is critical in influencing cancer risk.57

The evidence on sugary drinks and carbonated beverages was similarly inconclusive. While some studies found an increased risk of GC associated with soda consumption,45 46 others reported no significant effects.52 Previous research investigating the association between carbonated beverage consumption and GC reported no significant relationship between increased intake of carbonated soft drinks and the risk of GC.58 59 Consistent evidence linked the consumption of distilled alcoholic beverages, such as vodka and hard liquor, with an increased risk of GC, particularly among males.41 48 The stronger associations observed in males may reflect differences in consumption patterns or alcohol metabolism.60 However, a previous study reported no association between distilled alcoholic beverage consumption and increased GC risk, contrasting our findings.61

Several studies have examined the link between dietary patterns and GC risk, revealing consistent associations with certain eating habits. Wu et al found a positive link between a fast-food pattern, high in barbecue, fried foods, red meat and seafood, and GC risk. Kim et al observed a similar association with a non-staple food diet rich in snacks and carbonated drinks. While Castelló et al found no significant link with a westernised diet high in processed foods, Campbell et al reported a positive association with processed meats, high-fat dairy, refined grains and sweets. Santiago et al also found an elevated risk linked to an unhealthy diet dominated by UPFs. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses on dietary factors and GC reported that high adherence to Western diets and unhealthy dietary patterns are both associated with an increased risk of GC.62 63

The findings from this systematic review demonstrate mixed and inconclusive results regarding the relationship between UPF consumption and GC risk. Besides, our findings align with the broader evidence summarised by Coelho et al, although their analysis included multiple gastrointestinal cancers while our review focuses specifically on GC.24 Further research is required to better understand this relationship and to determine the UPF categories mentioned in this study and their associations with GC risk.

UPF consumption may contribute to GC through several interconnected biological pathways, including salt-related gastric mucosal injury and Helicobacter pylori interaction, nitrite-related and nitrate-related formation of N-nitroso compounds (NOCs) from processed meats, processing-derived contaminants from fried or heat-treated foods, obesity-related metabolic dysfunction and displacement of micronutrient-rich protective foods. These pathways may operate differently depending on the dominant UPF categories consumed in each population. UPFs often contain high salt levels, along with added sugar and saturated fat.64 One notable mechanism is the association between high salt content in UPFs and an increased risk of Helicobacter pylori infection, a well-established risk factor for GC.65,67 High salt intake has been associated with a greater likelihood of developing intestinal metaplasia, a recognised precursor to GC.66 68 Furthermore, excessive salt concentration in the stomach can cause mucosal injury, which may enhance mitotic cell activity when coupled with damage from H. pylori colonisation. This process leads to increased cell turnover and gastric epithelial hyperplasia, thereby elevating the risk of carcinogenesis.69 This pathway may help explain the associations observed for ultra-processed meats (such as sausages, bacon and ham), breads, refined grains and snacks, which were identified in this review and are often characterised by high sodium content. Additionally, UPFs are generally more energy-dense, high in refined carbohydrates and rich in saturated and trans-fatty acids while low in fibre. They also contain added sugars and sodium, contributing to obesity, another risk factor for GC.70 71 GC is recognised as an obesity-related cancer.72 73 Possible mechanisms linking obesity with GC encompass obesity-associated insulin resistance, abnormally elevated blood levels of insulin-like growth factor and associated levels of adipokines such as adiponectin, leptin, steroid hormones and cytokines.74 75 This mechanism is particularly relevant to processed dairy products, fried foods, fast foods and convenience foods, sweets and desserts, and sugary drinks and carbonated beverages, which were examined in several included studies and are typically energy-dense and nutrient-poor.

Moreover, UPFs often contain various additives, including preservatives and cosmetic agents, some of which have been suggested to possess carcinogenic properties in experimental studies.76,78 When nitrite, used to preserve processed meat, reacts with amines or amides in the meat, NOCs can be formed endogenously in the stomach, potentially increasing the risk of GC.79,81 This pathway provides a strong biological explanation for the consistent positive associations observed with ultra-processed meats (such as sausages, bacon and ham) in several included studies. Heat-treated processed foods can contain neo-formed contaminants, including substances like acrylamide, HCAs and PAHs, which have been shown to have carcinogenic properties.82 83 Accordingly, the increased risk observed for fried foods, fast foods and convenience foods in multiple studies may be attributed to the formation of these carcinogenic compounds during high-temperature processing. Furthermore, UPFs significantly impact the micronutrient content of the diet. Higher consumption of UPFs has been found to be inversely and significantly associated with vitamins B12, D and E as well as niacin, pyridoxine, copper, iron, phosphorus, magnesium, selenium and zinc.84 However, GC was found to be inversely associated with the consumption of vitamin A, beta-carotene, vitamins D, E, K, B2, B3, B6, B12 and C as well as folate, chromium, iodine and selenium.85 These findings suggest that higher intake of UPFs may lead to nutrient deficiencies, impacting health and increasing the risk of conditions like GC. This may further explain the associations observed for processed dairy products, sweets and desserts, breads, refined grains and snacks, and sugary drinks and carbonated beverages, where high consumption may displace protective micronutrient-rich foods.

In addition to these pathways, alcohol consumption, often associated with ultra-processed dietary patterns, may further contribute to gastric carcinogenesis. Ethanol is metabolised into acetaldehyde, a recognised carcinogen that can form DNA adducts, induce mutations and impair DNA repair mechanisms.86 87 Alcohol also promotes oxidative stress through the generation of reactive oxygen species, disrupts gastric mucosal integrity and increases the permeability of the gastric lining to carcinogens, thereby facilitating gastric carcinogenesis.88 89 This mechanism supports the findings of included studies that reported increased GC risk associated with distilled alcoholic beverages, particularly in populations with higher consumption levels.

The absence of significant associations in some studies may reflect methodological and contextual differences rather than clear evidence of no relationship. Several studies used broad dietary patterns or general food groups instead of NOVA-based UPF classification, which may have diluted associations with specific high-risk UPF categories. Null findings may also relate to weaker biological relevance of some exposures, limited exposure contrast, cancer subsite differences, sex-specific analyses, sample size and variation in confounder adjustment. Therefore, non-significant findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of exposure misclassification and study precision.

Interpretation of these findings must also consider the reliability of individual study estimates and the methodological differences across the included studies. Larger cohort studies with more comprehensive adjustment for confounders generally produced more stable and precise risk estimates, while several smaller case–control studies exhibited wider CIs, reflecting limited statistical power. Variations in exposure levels also contributed to inconsistency: studies from regions where UPFs are dominated by processed meats or high-salt products tended to report stronger associations, whereas those where UPFs mainly included confectionery or sweet snacks often showed null results. Additionally, differences in dietary assessment tools influenced reliability, studies using validated food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) and NOVA-aligned UPF classification provided more robust estimates compared with those relying on broad food group categories or retrospective recall, which increase the risk of exposure misclassification. These differences in study size, design, exposure patterns, geographic context and measurement quality likely contributed to the heterogeneity in the reported associations and highlight the need for cautious interpretation of individual study estimates.

A key strength of this review is the first GC-specific systematic review, synthesising evidence on UPF consumption, individual UPF categories and UPF-related dietary patterns. A rigorous and extensive literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies, ensuring a broad and detailed examination of the available evidence. Most eligible studies also controlled for major confounding variables, improving the reliability of the observed associations. Collectively, these strengths ensured the credibility of the findings from this review.

Despite these strengths, certain limitations should be acknowledged. One major limitation was the scarcity of studies directly investigating UPF consumption and GC risk. Only one study explicitly focused on UPFs and GC risk, while other studies examined dietary factors, habits or specific UPF categories. From these, we identified and categorised UPFs accordingly. Additionally, there was considerable heterogeneity among the selected studies regarding exposure measurement. Moreover, the absence of quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) limits the ability to estimate a pooled effect size and may reduce the objectivity of the findings. Different studies applied varying UPF classification systems or assessed only particular UPF categories, making direct comparisons challenging. Additionally, 70 potentially relevant studies could not be retrieved in full text despite repeated attempts through institutional subscriptions, author contact and alternative access routes. Many of these records appeared to be older publications or conference proceedings without publicly available digital versions. The exclusion of these studies raises the possibility of missing relevant data and may reduce the representativeness of the overall evidence base, particularly for regions where journal digitisation is limited or where non-English publications are more common. Together with the English-language restriction, this may have resulted in an incomplete capture of the global scientific literature and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Besides, all included studies were observational (case–control or cohort) and therefore susceptible to inherent sources of bias. These include residual confounding from lifestyle and socioeconomic factors that may not have been fully adjusted for, recall bias in self-reported dietary assessments, exposure misclassification linked to broad UPF definitions and selection bias in case–control recruitment. These methodological limitations may have contributed to heterogeneity in the reported associations and must be considered when interpreting the overall evidence.

Given these limitations, further research is necessary to address the existing gaps. Future studies should aim to provide more clarity on the association between UPF consumption and GC risk by employing consistent and well-defined UPF classification methods. Future studies should also aim for standardised reporting of effect estimates and exposure definitions to enable meta-analytic integration. More well-designed prospective cohort studies are necessary to establish clearer causal relationships between UPF consumption and GC risk. These studies should incorporate validated dietary assessment tools, such as FFQs combined with repeated 24-hour dietary recalls, to reduce measurement bias and improve exposure accuracy. Moreover, there is a lack of studies from low-income countries, where dietary patterns are also shifting due to urbanisation and globalisation. This gap in evidence limits a comprehensive understanding of the association between UPFs and GC across different socioeconomic contexts. Future research should prioritise strengthening evidence from low and middle-income countries to better capture regional dietary transitions and contextual differences in UPF consumption. Additionally, further research should focus on assessing the impact of the specific UPF categories identified in this review, as the current evidence remains inconclusive. Integrating advanced approaches, such as metabolomics and gut microbiome analyses, may further help elucidate the biological mechanisms linking UPF consumption to gastric carcinogenesis. Addressing these gaps will be essential for developing targeted dietary recommendations and public health strategies to mitigate GC risk.

Conclusions

This systematic review examines the relationship between UPFs consumption and GC risk. While evidence remains mixed, a significant number of studies suggest a positive correlation, particularly with processed meats, fried foods and highly processed dairy products. However, variations in study designs, dietary assessment methods and the lack of standardised UPF classifications complicate direct comparisons. Many studies focused on overall dietary patterns, making it challenging to isolate the specific role of UPFs in GC risk. Future research should prioritise well-designed cohort studies with standardised dietary assessments to clarify causal relationships. Public health initiatives should promote whole, minimally processed foods, while policymakers could consider stronger regulations on food additives and clearer labelling to support informed consumer choices.
