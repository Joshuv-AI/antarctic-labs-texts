Anticonvulsant effects of pentoxifylline on seizures induced by pentylenetetrazole and maximal electroshock in male mice: The role of the nitrergic pathway
Authors: Mohammad Keshavarzi, Moein Ghasemi, Mohammad Amin Manavi, Ahmad Reza Dehpour, Hamed Shafaroodi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEpilepsy remains a challenge, with one-third of patients experiencing refractory seizures despite current anti-seizure medications. The nitrergic system, which involves nitric oxide (NO) and NO synthase (NOS) enzymes, plays a complex role in seizure pathophysiology. Pentoxifylline (PTPh), an FDA-approved phosphodiesterase inhibitor, has anticonvulsant effects; however, its relationship with the pathway is unclear. This study focused at how the nitrergic system could be involved in PTPh’s anticonvulsant effects.MethodsSeizures were induced in male mice by intravenous pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) infusion (absence-like seizures), intraperitoneal PTZ injection, and maximal electroshock (generalized tonic-clonic seizures). PTPh was administered at various doses, alone or in combination with the NO precursor L-arginine, as well as non-selective (L-NAME) and selective NOS inhibitors (nNOS inhibitor 7-NI and iNOS inhibitor aminoguanidine). Seizure thresholds, latencies, incidence, and mortality were assessed. Moreover, in the next paradigm, using maximal electroshock model, we evaluate possible protective effects of PTPh against generalized tonic-clonic seizures and subsequent mortality.ResultsIn the intravenous PTZ model, PTPh (≥150 mg/kg) increased the seizure threshold, potentiated by L-arginine but reduced by L-NAME and 7-nitroindazole. In the intraperitoneal PTZ model, 150 mg/kg PTPh decreased tonic seizure frequency, which was mitigated by aminoguanidine. However, PTPh failed to prolong clonic seizure latency. In the maximal electroshock test, 100 mg/kg PTPh protected against tonic seizure incidence (reduced by aminoguanidine). Although PTPh could not reduce mortality, its combination with L-NAME or 7-nitroindazole increased mortality compared with the vehicle-treated group.ConclusionPTPh exerted anticonvulsant effects against absence-like and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, likely through modulation of the nitrergic system involving neuronal, endothelial, and inducible NOS isoform. These findings provide novel insights into the complex interplay between NO signaling and the anticonvulsant actions of PTPh, highlighting the potential therapeutic implications of targeting the NO pathway in epilepsy management.Keywords: Pentoxifylline, Nitric oxide, Pentylenetetrazole, Maximal electroshock, Dose-response

Introduction

About 65 million individuals worldwide suffer from epilepsy, a neurological disorder with a 1 % incidence (Martinet et al., 2017). Despite vast studies seeking novel anti-seizure medications (ASMs), epilepsy management remains unsatisfactory, with one-third of patients experiencing uncontrolled seizures with current ASMs (Ravizza et al., 2024). Seizures are frightening events that can vary from brief immobility to severe convulsions with loss of consciousness. It often leads to physical injury and even sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (Manavi et al., 2022, Willems et al., 2018), where different animal models have emerged to investigate the pathophysiology of each seizure type (Manavi et al., 2024). Seizures are mostly triggered by a disturbance of the fine balance between excitatory along with inhibitory neural activity (Manavi, 2022). However, it is difficult to specifically target certain pathways implicated in the onset and progression of epilepsy due to the complex functions that different neurotransmitters undertake in a variety of brain processes (Manavi et al., 2024, Masoumi et al., 2023).

The nitrergic system, which is made up of the chemical nitric oxide (NO) and the enzymes that produce it, is crucial in maintaining the nervous system's function. NO is an unconventional neurotransmitter and "retrograde messenger that is involved in neuronal hyperactivity, neuroinflammation (Almostafa et al., 2024), and synaptic transmission (Giachello et al., 2021, Goudarzi et al., 2024), all of which are critical to the pathophysiology of epilepsy and the occurrence of seizures. "The enzyme nitric oxide synthase (NOS) is responsible for the production of NO from L-arginine. There are three distinct NOS isoforms: neuronal NOS (nNOS), endothelial NOS (eNOS), and inducible NOS (iNOS)" (Jourian et al., 2023, Förstermann et al., 1991). The role of the nitrergic system in epilepsy is complex and multifaceted. Excessive NO production has been associated with neuronal excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and blood-brain barrier disruption, all of which can contribute to seizure generation and propagation (Ko et al., 2015). However, NO also plays a role in modulating synaptic plasticity (Aghili et al., 2024) and has been implicated in the anticonvulsant effects of certain ASMs (Murashima et al., 2000). Indeed, the dual effect of NO in seizure suggests an optimal level in favor of anticonvulsive, which may depend on the cellular environment and other involved pathways (Moradi et al., 2022).

Pentoxifylline (PTPh), commonly referred to as oxpentifylline, is an FDA-approved xanthine derivative that is used to treat claudication symptoms and muscular discomfort in patients with peripheral artery disease (McDermott, 2013, Liang et al., 2022). While inhibition of phosphodiesterase is the main defined mechanism of pentoxifylline action (El-Haggar et al., 2022), modulation of adenosine receptors (Khoury et al., 2023, DiNicolantonio and Barroso-Aranda, 2020), acetylcholinesterase (Mohamed et al., 2013, Elseweidy et al., 2023), and cAMP (Selli et al., 2022) are also involved. In addition, PTPh has exhibited neuroprotective (Salama et al., 2024) and anti-seizure effects in previous studies (Godlevsky et al., 2013a).

While phosphodiesterase inhibitors have revealed convulsive effects, PTPh favorably modulates seizures, suggesting that mechanisms other than phosphodiesterase inhibition, such as neuroinflammation modulation (Badawi et al., 2021, Kang et al., 2017), can overwhelm epileptogenesis (Mukhtar, 2025). In fact, Neuroinflammation plays a key role in facilitating seizures, with elevated levels of proinflammatory mediators like IL-1β and TNF-α contributing to hyperexcitability in the brain (Badawi et al., 2024). Pentoxifylline exerts anti-inflammatory effects by restricting these cytokines (Bahaa et al., 2024, Mohammad et al., 2024), which is thought to be a critical mechanism behind its antiseizure action. By reducing IL-1β and TNF-α, pentoxifylline may help prevent the inflammatory processes that promote seizure activity (Mukhtar, 2025). Furthermore, the modulation of NO levels following PTPh administration is well defined (Fadl et al., 2023, Abd Al Haleem et al., 2021). There is, however, no information about the potential contribution of the nitrergic system to the anticonvulsant properties of PTPh. Consequently, a more thorough investigation regarding how the nitrergic system contributes to pentoxifylline's anticonvulsant actions is required.

Using three seizure models comprising intravenous (i.v.) infusion and intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of pentylenetetrazole (PTZ, a negative allosteric modulator of GABAA receptor that widely has been used as a chemo-convulsant) as well as maximal electroshock (MES) indicative of different seizure types (Manavi et al., 2022), we investigated the effect of PTPh on seizures. The PTZ i.v. model mimics absence-like seizures, which are common in childhood epilepsy and involve brief episodes of impaired consciousness without convulsions. The i.v. PTZ model provides precise control over the dose-response relationship and offers high sensitivity to detect subtle anticonvulsant effects. It allows us to explore the threshold for seizure induction, giving insights into the potential of PTPh to raise seizure thresholds, which is valuable for preventive interventions. But the i.p. PTZ model induces generalized tonic-clonic seizures, representing severe forms of epilepsy that are resistant to treatment. Unlike the intravenous model, which focuses on seizure thresholds, the i.p. PTZ model allows the assessment of both tonic-clonic seizure frequency and mortality protection, reflecting the severity of seizures. The model helps identify drugs that may reduce seizure frequency and mitigate fatal outcomes. Last but not least, the MES test is a gold standard for evaluating drugs effective against generalized tonic-clonic seizures. It provides insights into anticonvulsant effects on seizure propagation through the motor cortex, which is highly relevant for drugs targeting tonic hindlimb extension (THLE). The MES model also simulates seizures caused by electric discharges, resembling refractory epilepsy. Given the complex and multifaceted effects of NO signaling in epilepsy, elucidating this potential interaction could provide valuable insights into PTPh’s mechanisms of action. Understanding the interplay between NO and key mediators, provides a comprehensive view of potential interactions with the NO pathway.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the anticonvulsant effects of PTPh using three complementary seizure models: i.p. and i.p. PTZ-induced seizures and MES test. Our goal was to explore the role of nitrergic pathway in mediating PTPh’s anticonvulsant activity, focusing on the involvement of different NOS isoforms. Specifically, we evaluated the ability of PTPh to alter seizure threshold, latency, incidence, and mortality. By employing these distinct models, we aimed to provide comprehensive insights into both the preventive and therapeutic potential of PTPh in epilepsy, with a particular focus on NO modulation.

Methods

Experimental animals

"The Razi Institute (Tehran, Iran) provided 249 male Naval Medical Research Institute (NMRI) mice weighing 23–29 g with standard housing conditions, which included a controlled temperature (23±1°C), a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00), a relative humidity of 45 %–55 %, and unrestricted access to food and water". Animals were allowed to acclimate in groups of five to six per cage for at least 1 week. Experiments were conducted between 9:00 AM and 2:00 PM, and the treated groups were compared with their respective control groups recorded on the same day. "Our work was approved by the Tehran University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee on Animal Experiments and adhered to the National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH publication no. 85–23; updated 1985)."

Chemicals

"The following drugs were used throughout the study: PTZ (p6500, Sigma), aminoguanidine hydrochloride (AG, a selective inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) inhibitor, 396494), L-arginine, a NO precursor (A5006, Sigma), Nω-Nitro-L-arginine methyl ester hydrochloride (L-NAME, a nonselective NOS inhibitor, N5751, Sigma), 7-nitroindazole (7-NI, a selective neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) inhibitor, N7778, Sigma)" (Shafaroodi et al., 2015), and pentoxifylline (PTPh, p1784, Sigma). All drugs were injected i.p. with a volume of 10 ml/kg body weight, except for PTZ in the intravenous-induced seizure method. In addition, 7-NI was suspended in a solution of 1 % Tween 80 (p8074, Sigma) in 0.9 % saline. Other drugs were dissolved in 0.9 % isotonic saline. Isovolumic 0.9 % saline was used as the vehicle wherever needed. The inhibitor and L-arginine dosages were chosen on the basis of prior investigations (Jourian et al., 2023).

Intravenous PTZ-induced seizures

To determine the PTZ-induced seizure threshold, we measured the PTZ (0.5 %) infusion time through the tail veins of freely roaming mice at a constant flow rate of 1 ml/min using a 30-gauge needle which was attached to a Hamilton microsyringe and a pump for infusion (NE 1000, New Era Pump System, Inc.) via a polyethylene tube. The clonic seizure threshold (CST), which is determined by the injection duration, is shown by the lowest dosage of PTZ (mg/kg) necessary to cause forelimb clonus motions followed by whole-body clonus.

Forty-eight mice in groups of 6 received a single vehicle or PTPh injection with different doses (10, 25, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250 mg/kg) 60 min before PTZ administration to assess the effect of PTPh on CST and identify the minimal dose with substantial anticonvulsive effect (Fig. 1A).

Experimental groups and timeline for treatment administration and behavioral tests.

We used a variety of mediators linked to the NO pathway to gain insight into the possible involvement of NO in the PTPh mechanism of action. Twenty-four mice were divided into groups of 6 with the following treatments: The minimal effective dose of PTPh, 150 mg/kg, along with (i) L-NAME (5 mg/kg) 75 min before CST determination, (ii) AG (100 mg/kg) 75 min before CST determination, (iii) 7-NI (40 mg/kg) 75 min before CST determination, and the last group (iv) received an ineffective dose of PTPh, 50 mg/kg, along with L-arginine (60 mg/kg) 75 min before CST determination (Fig. 1A). We refer to ineffective dosages as doses that, when administered alone, do not significantly alter the seizure threshold, latency, or seizure incidence compared to the vehicle group. However, these doses were used in combination with other agents (such as nitric oxide precursors or inhibitors) to explore potential synergistic or modulatory effects.

Intraperitoneal PTZ-induced seizures

To simulate clinical generalized tonic-clonic seizures, we administered a high dose of PTZ (85 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection. This dose corresponds to CD97, which induces clonic seizures in 97 % of the experimental animals in our laboratory settings. Seventy mice in groups of 10 received the following treatments before PTZ injection: (i) Vehicle (isovolumic 0.9 % saline); (ii) PTPh 150 mg/kg; (iii) PTPh 150 mg/kg + L-NAME 5 mg/kg; (iv) PTPh 150 mg/kg + AG 100 mg/kg; (v) PTPh 150 mg/kg + NI 40 mg/kg; (vi) PTPh 50 mg/kg; (vii) PTPh 50 mg/kg + L-arginine 60 mg/kg. Vehicle and PTPh were injected 60 min before PTZ administration, and L-NAME, AG, 7-NI, and L-arginine were administered 75 min before PTZ injection, with all injections being intraperitoneal (Fig. 1B). Of note, the dose selection of L-arginine and NOS inhibitors was based on our previous studies (Manavi et al., 2024, Jourian et al., 2023).

Once animals received PTZ, they were placed in an open field with a diameter of 50 cm and monitored for 30 min for the occurrence of convulsions or death. We observed autonomic symptoms, such as hypersalivation and defecation, as well as the involvement of each of the four limbs and the tail, frenzied running and leaping, and abrupt loss of upright posture, all indicative of a delay in generalized clonus. With no sign of clonus movements, 1800s was the recorded latency. Tonic seizure and death incidence were additional factors recorded. Tonic seizure implies full hindlimb extension when the angle of the hindlimbs to the torso exceeds 180°.

Maximal electroshock test

An electroconvulsive treatment device (Model 7800, Ugo Basile, Camerio, Italy) was used to induce tonic hindlimb extension via transcorneal electrical stimulation (50 Hz, 50 mA, 0.1 s). To increase conductivity, electrodes were soaked in regular saline before being affixed to the ears. The proportion of animals in each group that showed no hindlimb extension or died from electroshock was used to display the data. In the maximal electroshock (MES) model, the primary evaluation criterion was the presence or absence of tonic hindlimb extension (THLE), defined as hindlimb extension greater than 180° with respect to the torso. This endpoint is widely accepted as a reliable marker for generalized tonic-clonic seizures. The animals were also monitored for mortality and the protective effect of treatments, if any, against both seizure incidence and mortality.

To assess the effects of different doses of PTPh on tonic-clonic seizure induced by electroshock, in groups of 9–12 mice, we injected vehicle (isovolumic 0.9 % saline) and 50, 100, 200, 250, and 300 mg/kg PTPh (Fig. 1C). Considering the minimal effective dose of PTPh in the electroshock test, 100 mg/kg, we evaluated mediators of the NO pathway and the potential role of PTPh using the following groups (each 9–12 mice): (i) PTPh 100 mg/kg + L-NAME 5 mg/kg; (ii) PTPh 100 mg/kg + AG 100 mg/kg; (iii) PTPh 100 mg/kg + 7-NI 40 mg/kg; (iv) PTPh 50 mg/kg + L-arginine 60 mg/kg. Vehicle and PTPh were injected for 60 min, and L-NAME, AG, NI, and L-arginine were administered for 75 min before PTZ injection, with all injections being intraperitoneal (Fig. 1C). Similar to the PTZ paradigm, the dose selection of L-arginine and NOS inhibitors was based on our previous studies (Manavi et al., 2024, Jourian et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SEM or median ± IQR of 6–12 mice, and SPSS (Version 18.0) statistical software was used for analysis. The data were analyzed using Tukey-Kramer post hoc as well as Dunn's post hoc tests following one-way analyses of variance (ANOVA). Fisher’s exact test was employed to determine the protective effects against tonic seizures and mortality, and the Kruskal–Wallis’s test, followed by the post hoc Dunn’s test, was used for analyzing tonic seizure incidence. A P value less than 0.05 was regarded as significant in statistical terms.

Results

Intravenous PTZ-induced seizures

Dose-response determination

Acute PTPh treatment (150, 200, and 250 mg/kg, i.p.) revealed anticonvulsant effects stated by an increased CST in PTZ-induced seizure (Fig. 2) compared with isovolumic vehicle injection, with the 150 mg/kg dosage being the minimal effective dose (p<0.001). Also, PTPh exhibited the most significant impact at the doses of 200 and 250 mg/kg (p<0.0001). In our study, PTPh at the highest dose (250 mg/kg) increased CST from 37.85 mg/kg PTZ injection to 58.69 mg/kg, about a 55 % raise (p<0.0001).

Investigating the role of the nitrergic pathway

Co-treatment with an ineffective dose of L-arginine, as an NO precursor, and 50 mg/kg PTPh increased CST (Fig. 3, p<0.001). Moreover, an ineffective dose of the non-selective inhibitor of nitric synthase, L-NAME, diminished the anticonvulsive effect of PTPh (p<0.05) on CST to an amount comparable to the vehicle group (p=0.07 vs vehicle). Similarly, ineffective treatment with neuronal NOS, 7-NI, partially reduced CST (p<0.01) but was still significantly higher than the vehicle (p<0.01). In contrast, iNOS inhibition by AG failed to affect PTPh treatment (p=0.92).

Intraperitoneal PTZ-induced seizures

Considering the minimal effective dose of PTPh in PTZ-induced seizures, 150 mg/kg, we evaluated the tonic seizure and mortality protection, frequency of tonic seizure, and latency in the same groups as those administered intravenous PTZ. As indicated in Fig. 4, contrary to the intravenous injection method, 150 mg/kg of PTPh was ineffective in altering the latency of clonus movements (271 vs. 213 second, p=0.83). Co-administration of ineffective doses of L-arginine and PTPh significantly increased latency from 213 s to 546 (p<0.01). But L-NAME, 7-NI, and AG did not impact the latency compared with PTPh 150 alone (p>0.05).

Discussion

For the first time in our study, the anticonvulsant effect of PTPh through the NO pathway in PTZ-induced and maximal electroshock seizure models was proved as follows: First, in the PTZ-induced seizure following intravenous injection, an animal model corresponding to human absence seizures, PTPh administration at a dosage of 150 mg/kg and higher increases CST through nNOS but not iNOS; Second, in the high-dose PTZ-induced seizure following intraperitoneal injection, equivalent to generalized tonic-clonic seizures in humans, 150 mg/kg PTPh efficiently diminished the frequency of tonic seizures probably by inducible NO synthase, while failed to prolong the latency of clonic movements; Lastly in the maximal electroshock test as another model that is comparable to generalized tonic-clonic seizures, PTPh administration at a dosage of 100 mg/kg prohibited tonic seizures, but failed to protect against mortality, where tonic seizure and mortality protections were associated with NOS through inducible and neuronal NO synthase, respectively.

We demonstrated that PTPh at 150 mg/kg and higher dosages increased CST in PTZ-induced seizure, consistent with previous studies showing the anticonvulsive effects of PTPh in PTZ-kindling (Badawi et al., 2021) and lithium-pilocarpine (Kang et al., 2017, Ahmad et al., 2014) models in adult and young rats (Tariq et al., 2008). The neuroprotective and anticonvulsant potential of PTPh continues to be highlighted in recent studies, further supporting its promise as a therapeutic agent for epilepsy. Its ability to modulate neuroinflammatory pathways, particularly the HMGB1/TLR4/RAGE signaling cascade, has been demonstrated to play a key role in protecting against PTZ-induced kindling. By reducing the expression of amyloid-β peptide (Aβ), Tau, and pro-inflammatory mediators, and enhancing antioxidant defense mechanisms such as Nrf2 activation, PTPh has shown significant efficacy in preventing seizures and improving memory and cognitive functions in PTZ-kindled rats. This evidence underscores its dual role in modulating epilepsy progression and cognitive impairments associated with the condition (Badawi et al., 2021). Moreover, PTPh has been shown to potentiate the anti-seizure effects of diazepam in treatment-resistant focal epilepsy models. Combined administration of sub-effective doses of PTPh and diazepam revealed synergistic effects in reducing seizures, suggesting a potential role for PTPh as an adjunctive therapy in difficult-to-treat epilepsy cases (Godlevsky et al., 2013b).

In comparison to most models, intravenous injection of PTZ provides more clinical relevance and satisfactory sensitivity for behavioral investigation of anticonvulsive drugs. PTZ mimics a similar pattern to human absence seizures at low doses, which is common in childhood and adolescence periods (Zhang et al., 2023). The promising impact of drugs with anticonvulsive effects in this model in treating absence seizures highlights a potential similarity in the underlying pathophysiology. Studies suggest that PTPh acts through modulation of A2b adenosine receptors (A2bAR) (Kim et al., 2002), leading to increased levels of cAMP via adenylate cyclase activation (Uboldi and Savage, 2002, Vig et al., 2002) and simultaneously activating NOS (Fadl et al., 2023) through the Akt pathway (Zhang et al., 2007). In a study performed by Yildirim et al., adenosine reduced the frequency but not amplitude of epileptiform activity partly through the NO pathway, as L-NAME administration mitigated the adenosine effect, whereas results of adenosine or sodium nitroprusside (SNP), an NO precursor, alone or in combination were similar (Stosić-Grujicić et al., 1998). In our research, L-arginine along with an ineffective dose of PTPh resulted in an increased CST because the administration of ineffective doses of L-NAME and 7-NI reduced the CST, suggesting that PTPh, at least partly, serves through NO production by neuronal NOS. Although, in this study, we focused on NO modulation through the adenosine pathway, PTPh has proven neuromodulator effects through phosphodiesterase inhibition and acetylcholinesterase modification (Jowzi et al., 2016).

The significant difference between the PTPh 150+L-NAME 5 and PTPh 150+AG 100 groups may indicate a "double dissociation," which suggests different roles of L-NAME (a nonselective NOS inhibitor) and AG (an iNOS inhibitor) in modulating the anticonvulsant effects of PTPh through NO pathways. The findings could imply that L-NAME impacts PTPh’s efficacy more prominently than AG, hinting at distinct NOS isoforms being involved. L-NAME's impact on the nitrergic pathway, potentially through neuronal NOS (nNOS), may be critical in seizure modulation. The lack of effect from AG, which targets iNOS, might suggest that iNOS plays a less significant role in PTPh’s anticonvulsant mechanisms, or its role might be dependent on different conditions or seizure models.

In high-dose intraperitoneal PTZ-induced seizures, the effective dose of PTPh in CST failed to prolong the latency of clonic movements. Various doses of PTZ show distinct natures in the seizure types they induce and the effectiveness of convulsive modulators as L-NAME differently affected clonic seizure latency (Jelenković et al., 2002). In our study, co-administration of L-arginine prolonged latency clonic seizure in contrast to L-NAME and 7-NI. The preceding findings propose that NO features a dual effect in the context of PTPh-stimulated pathways, where a lower and higher activity of the NO pathway exhibited an anticonvulsive impact, although further investigations are required. Previous studies have shown that NO precursor and NOS inhibitors, when used in the dosages we employed, did not show any pro- or anticonvulsive effects on their own (Jourian et al., 2023). However, when combined with other drugs, they did exhibit both pro- and anticonvulsive effects, suggesting that the role of the NO pathway in the modulation of seizure depends on the cellular context and other activated pathways (Manavi et al., 2024). Therefore, we cannot consider the solitary impact of the pathway in the modulation of seizure. With regard to tonic seizure frequency, PTPh significantly reduced the number of tonic seizures, albeit failed to protect against tonic seizure incidence or mortality. Using NO precursor resulted in reduced seizure frequency, and concurrently, iNOS inhibitor mitigated the anticonvulsive effect of PTPh, suggesting a role for PTPh through NO pathway stimulation via inducible NOS enzyme (Gooshe et al., 2018). The observed failure of pentoxifylline to prolong the latency to clonic seizures in the intraperitoneal PTZ model, despite its effects on CST and tonic seizure frequency, suggests a complex and multifaceted role of NO in different phases of seizure pathogenesis (Sumanont et al., 2006).

Maximal electroshock test mimics generalized tonic-clonic seizure through pathways distinct from high-dose PTZ administration. PTPh demonstrated promising effects in protecting against tonic seizures at 100, 250, and 300 mg/kg doses, whereas 200 mg/kg was ineffective. NO precursor decreased seizures while increasing mortality. Although both effects were insignificant, they agree with the results of the high-dose PTZ model. In contrast, AG reduced PTPh-prompted seizure protection, which is consistent with the findings of high-dose PTZ administration. Moreover, L-NAME, in conjunction with 7-NI, lowered mortality protection, indicating the involvement of iNOS and nNOS. Seizure initiation and termination show distinct and sometimes opposing mechanisms. While adenosine prevents seizure initiation and adenosine burst release during neuronal firing assists seizure termination, excess adenosine can cause mortality vulnerability (Boison, 2013, Tescarollo et al., 2020). Likewise, although neuronal depolarization predisposes to seizure formation, a notable mechanism for seizure termination is the depolarizing blockade of Na channels (Agbo et al., 2023).

Therefore, the counter effects of drugs on protection against seizure incidence and mortality are not surprising. We demonstrated protection against tonic seizure and mortality by PTPh through inducible and neuronal NOS. Furthermore, the study’s findings underscore the potential benefits of combination therapy with NO modulators or the development of multi-target compounds that simultaneously modulate multiple pathways, such as the nitrergic systems (Lorigados Pedre et al., 2018). Such combinatorial or multimodal approaches could enhance therapeutic efficacy, particularly in cases of drug-resistant epilepsy (Khatibi et al., 2023).

Our study faces some limitations: first, assessing biomarkers and gene expression related to the NO pathway in various brain regions allows for a more assured investigation of the interaction between PTPh and the NO pathway. Also, investigation on both sexes could give us more precise insights.

Conclusion

As far as we are aware, this is the first study that shows pentoxifylline acts as an anticonvulsant on male mice's clonic seizures induced by PTZ via the neural NOS enzyme and tonic-clonic seizures induced by MES through nitrergic pathways, primarily via the inducible NOS enzyme.
