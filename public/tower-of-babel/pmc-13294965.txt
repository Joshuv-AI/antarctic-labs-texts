Innovative Interfacial Compatibilization and Blowing Strategies for Advancing PLA/PBAT Blown Films: Enhancing Film Blowing Stability, Mechanical Properties, and Barrier Properties
Authors: Qilin Wen, Haoran Cao, Ning Chen, Yinghong Chen, Dawei Xu, Bin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of biodegradable plastic films as an alternative to traditional plastic films is one of the important ways to address the problem of plastic pollution. However, producing high-quality blown films from polylactic acid (PLA) and polybutylene adipic acid/terephthalate (PBAT) is challenging due to their poor miscibility. In situ reactive compatibilization offers an effective solution to this problem. This study investigates the effects of epoxy equivalent weights (EEW) and molecular weights of an epoxy multifunctional chain extender on the rheological behavior, dynamic thermomechanical properties, film blowing stability, mechanical properties, phase morphology, and barrier properties of PLA/PBAT blends and blown films during reactive processing. The results indicate that ADR 4468, which has a low EEW and a high molecular weight, can significantly enhance interfacial adhesion and phase morphology. The tensile strength and elongation at break of the resulting blown film in the machine direction (MD)/transverse direction (TD) could reach 60.6/57.4 MPa and 608.5/458.3%, respectively, which were 29.8/21.1 and 158.8/89.0% higher than those of the pure PLA/PBAT blown film. Moreover, the oxygen permeability (OP) and water vapor permeability (WVP) are as low as 3.88 × 10–15 cm3·cm/(cm2·s·Pa) and 1.57 × 10–13 g·cm/(cm2·s·Pa), respectively. The compatibilized system also enables stable film blowing and retains acceptable visual transparency. These improvements demonstrate the potential of PLA/PBAT/ADR blown films as sustainable and versatile materials for commercial and food packaging applications, owing to their enhanced mechanical and barrier properties coupled with satisfactory transparency.

Introduction

Since plastics were first used as packaging materials, the packaging industry has remained one of the primary consumers of the plastic industry. Packaging materials are mainly made of nonbiodegradable petroleum-based plastics, which have the advantages of lightweight, low cost, high transparency, and excellent barrier properties, and have always been essential items in people’s daily production and life. , However, traditional plastic films are often discarded after single use, with low postconsumer value, difficulty in recycling, and resistance to degradation. This results in long-lasting plastic waste that pollutes the atmosphere, soil, and oceans. − In contrast, biodegradable plastics can be degraded by naturally occurring microorganisms or under specific environmental conditions into carbon dioxide, water, methane, and mineralized inorganic salts. Therefore, replacing traditional plastic films with biodegradable packaging films offers a promising solution to address plastic pollution.

Polylactic acid (PLA), a biodegradable aliphatic polyester derived from renewable plant resources, exhibits physical and mechanical properties comparable to those of conventional thermoplastics, such as polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET). Nevertheless, its broader development and application are hindered by inherent brittleness and relatively high production costs. , This makes it particularly unsuitable for packaging film applications requiring high flexibility. Blending with various flexible polymers such as polybutylene adipic acid/terephthalate (PBAT), polycaprolactone (PCL), poly­(butylene succinate) (PBS), propylene carbonate (PPC), epoxidized soybean oil (ESO), and polyurethane (PU) is an effective method to enhance the toughness of PLA. In practical applications, blending PLA with other biodegradable polymers serves as a simple and efficient approach to improving its toughness, while maintaining overall biodegradability. Among these, PBAT is composed of rigid segments (butylene terephthalate) and flexible segments (butylene adipate). Owing to its excellent toughness, film blowing stability, and low cost (relative to other biodegradable plastics), − PBAT is considered the optimal candidate for toughening PLA blends. Consequently, PLA/PBAT alloying can produce high-quality blown films with enhanced comprehensive properties. However, numerous studies have shown that due to the poor compatibility between PLA and PBAT, the film blowing stability and mechanical properties of the resulting blown films are often unsatisfactory. − Furthermore, the inevitable thermal degradation occurring during the melt processing of PLA and PBAT also leads to a decline in the mechanical properties of these materials. , Therefore, compatibilization modification is essential to fully leverage PBAT’s toughening efficiency in PLA and ensure stable film blowing.

For multicomponent blends, in situ reactive compatibilization is a widely adopted method that enhances the dispersion of the dispersed phase and improves interfacial adhesion through the addition of chain extenders or cross-linking agents. − Consequently, in situ reactive compatibilization typically leads to significant improvements in the overall properties of the composites and their processing behaviors (such as film blowing, injection molding, and 3D printing). − For instance, Niu et al. designed a biodegradable epoxy-based terpolymer (SHG) for the interface modification of the PBAT/PLA blends. Due to the enhanced interfacial bonding force and interface entangling of the blend, the tensile strength and elongation at break of the 0.7 wt % SHG-filled PBAT/PLA blend were enhanced by about 48.2 and 215.6%, respectively. Shen et al. used dicumyl peroxide (DCP) as an in situ reactive compatibilizer to improve the processing performance and toughness of PLA/PBAT (70/30) blends. The function of DCP is evidenced by the uniform dispersion of PBAT in the PLA matrix and the formation of rapidly cooled amorphous specimens. Ayan et al. used random ethylene–methyl acrylate–glycidyl methacrylate terpolymer (EMA-GMA) as a compatibilizer to improve the toughness of PLA/PBAT blend sheets. The optimized sample can enhance the toughness and ductility by up to 7100%. This improvement is attributed to the formation of a phase-separated microstructure that combines internal cavitation, interfacial cavitation, and strong adhesion features in an appropriate proportion. As a polyepoxy multifunctional material designed to prevent degradation and enhance processability, the epoxy multifunctional chain extender (ADR) can react with the carboxyl groups (−COOH) and/or hydroxyl groups (−OH) of PLA and PBAT to reduce the interfacial tension and enhance the melt strength of the blend. This represents a promising solution for improving the compatibility of PLA/PBAT blends and the stability of the blown films.

The compatibility effect of the ADR on PLA/PBAT is closely related to the epoxy equivalent weight (EEW) and the molecular weight. However, systematic research on the effect of the ADR’s EEW and molecular weight on the miscibility of PLA/PBAT is currently lacking. In this work, we selected three types of ADRs with different EEWs and molecular weights, aiming to clarify the influence of the ADR’s EEW and molecular weight on the compatibilization effect of PLA/PBAT as well as the processing performance, rheology, dynamic thermomechanical, optical, mechanical, and barrier properties from the perspective of molecular reaction. Additionally, we explored the influence of interfacial interactions on the performance of PLA/PBAT blown films from a microscopic perspective. This study aims to understand the effects of different structural ADRs on the miscibility and film blowing stability of the PLA/PBAT blend, providing an industrially feasible approach for producing biodegradable PLA/PBAT packaging films with balanced high strength and high toughness through the traditional extrusion-blowing molding process.

Experimental Section

Materials

Sample Preparation

The PLA and PBAT pellets were dried at 60 °C in a drying oven for 12 h before processing. The PLA/PBAT (70/30) mass ratio was determined based on the Supporting Information. The blends shown in Table S1 were prepared by reactive extrusion using a corotating twin-screw extruder (Φ: 25 mm, L/D: 44, TSSJ25/44, Angemei Company, China) with a screw speed of 100 rpm. The melt compounding temperature for each zone was independently controlled. The temperatures from the feeding zone to the die zone were sequentially set at 120, 150, 160, 170, 180, 180, 180, 180, 180, 180, and 175 °C. The water-cooled extrudates were cut into pellets and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. The blends are systematically labeled as PLA/PBAT, xADR 4370-S, yADR 4400, and zADR 4468, respectively, where x, y, and z represent the weight percentages of the ADRs with different structures.

The dried pellets were blown using a single-screw extruder (Φ: 25 mm, L/D: 28, FBSI-25/28, POTOP, China) with a screw speed of 25 rpm. The temperatures from the feeding zone to the die zone were sequentially set at 130, 150, 160, 170, and 170 °C, and the take-up speed was set to 2.5–4 m/min. Notably, given the significant variations in melt strength across different PLA/PBAT (6/4–4/6) ratios, the processing temperature was slightly adjusted to ensure smooth film blowing (see Table S1). The blown films are approximately 50 ± 10 μm in thickness. The general preparation process is illustrated in Scheme .

Characterization

Rheological Behavior Analysis

The 2 mm-thickness and 25 mm-diameter circular sheets were produced by hot-pressing at 180 °C for subsequent rheological testing. The rheological measurements were conducted on an AR2000ex rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) with two circular parallel plates (1 mm gap and 25 mm diameter) at a constant temperature of 180 °C, with an applied strain amplitude of 1% and an angular frequency sweep spanning from 0.1 to 100 rad/s.

Gel Content Measurement

The gel content in blown films was measured by a Soxhlet extraction method. The sample with a recorded weight of W 1 cut from the blown films was extracted by refluxing with CHCl3 at 70 °C for 24 h to dissolve all non-cross-linked components thoroughly. Then, the weight of the insoluble residues collected after drying at 80 °C for 6 h was recorded as W 2, and the gel content values were calculated using the following formula gelcontent=(W2/W1)×100%

Gel Permeation Chromatography (GPC) Measurement

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC, Alliance e2695, Waters, USA) measurements, using tetrahydrofuran as the eluent (0.5 mL/min) at 35 °C to maintain good solubility and a polystyrene (PS) standard for calibration, was used to get the number- and weight-average molecular weight (Mn and M w, respectively) and polydispersity index (PDI) to study the chain extension between the ADR and PLA or PBAT.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy

The FTIR spectra of epoxy chain extenders and blown films were obtained by using an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an ATR accessory at a range of 4000–400 cm–1.

Dynamic Mechanical Analysis (DMA)

The dynamic thermomechanical property of blown films (35 mm × 5 mm) in the machine direction (MD) was measured by using a dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA, Q850, TA Instruments, USA) in stretching mode at 1 Hz fixed frequency. The measurement was performed by heating from −60 to 120 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min.

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM)

The cryofractured surface morphology of blown films in MD was observed by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Apreo S HiVoc, FEI, USA) with an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV.

Mechanical Properties

The tensile properties of the blown films in both the MD and transverse directions (TD) were measured by using a long travel extensometer (DVX-1000, ROCINST, China) according to ASTM D882-2018 with a crosshead speed of 50 mm/min. Right-angle tear energy of the blown films both in MD and TD was measured by using a universal material testing machine (Instron 5567, Instron, USA) according to QB/T 1130-1991 with a tearing rate of 200 mm/min. Five specimens of each formulation were tested to obtain an average value.

Barrier Properties

The oxygen permeability (OP) of the blown films was evaluated using a differential pressure gas permeation tester (VAC-V1, Labthink, China) according to GB/T 1038.1-2022 with a temperature of 25 °C and a humidity of 50% RH. The water vapor permeability (WVP) of the blown films was evaluated using a water vapor resistance tester (C306H, Labthink, China) according to GB/T 1037-2021 with a temperature of 23 °C and a humidity of 90% RH. Three specimens of each formulation were tested to obtain the average value.

Results and Discussion

In Situ Reaction Compatibilization Mechanism of the PLA/PBAT/ADR

The predicted nucleophilic ring-opening reactions between the terminal −COOH and/or −OH of PLA and PBAT with epoxy groups of the ADR compatibilizer during the melt blending process are shown in Figure . , Interfacial tension drives these reactions to occur preferentially at the PLA/PBAT interface, resulting in the in situ formation of the PLA-grafted-PBAT copolymer. ,, Concurrently, it is also possible that a homopolymer grafting reaction occurs, resulting in a copolymer composed of PLA-grafted-PLA and PBAT-grafted-PBAT, thereby effectively improving the interfacial compatibility and the overall performance of the blend.

Predicted mechanism of in situ reactions for forming PLA-grafted-PBAT copolymers.

The existence of the in situ grafting reaction was confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy, as shown in Figure a,b. The characteristic peak at 920, 909, and 846 cm–1 corresponded to the epoxy group in the ADR with different structures. The characteristic peaks at 1750 and 1715 cm–1 were assigned to the CO stretching vibrations of the ester groups in PLA and PBAT, respectively. Compared with the pure PLA/PBAT blown film, the intensity of these peaks increased slightly upon introducing the ADR with different structures. These phenomena also appeared in the ester C–O vibration peaks (1270 and 1083 cm–1) and ether C–O vibration peaks (1045 cm–1) corresponding to the esterification or etherification reactions between −COOH or −OH and epoxy groups. Furthermore, similar epoxy group characteristic peaks in the blown film disappeared, indicating that the epoxy groups were consumed through in situ reactions.

(c) GPC curves of pure PLA/PBAT, 1ADR 4370-S, 1ADR 4400, and 1ADR 4468. (d) Gel content of pure PLA/PBAT and PLA/PBAT/ADR blends.

The FTIR spectrum initially confirmed that different structural ADRs could react with PLA and PBAT during extrusion blending. GPC and gel content analysis were employed to study the changes in the molecular weight of PLA/PBAT mixtures after adding different structural ADRs, as shown in Figure c,d and Table S4. After adding different structural ADRs, the Mn, Mw , and PDI follow the following order 1ADR 4468 > 1ADR 4400 > 1ADR 4370-S > PLA/PBAT. These changes in molecular weight and PDI further confirmed the chain extension reactions between the ADR and PLA/PBAT as well as the branching characteristics caused by the multiple reactive sites of the epoxy function in the ADR. The gel contents of blown films containing different structural ADRs exhibited a concentration-dependent effect. Additionally, the gel content shows a similar sequence to that of Mn , Mw , and PDI. However, it is worth noting that these results appear inconsistent with theoretical expectations, as ADR 4370-S possesses more active epoxy functional groups and should be more likely to form linear or branched copolymers through reactive blending, thereby demonstrating a superior chain extension effect. One possible explanation is that the steric hindrance effect of the epoxy groups in ADR 4370-S hinders full contact with the terminal groups of PLA/PBAT. Additionally, the relatively low EEW (indicating high reactivity) causes ADR 4370-S to undergo intramolecular reactions with polyester chains or to react with polyester degradation products, thereby resulting in poor chain extension effects. This is consistent with the observed changes in the torque.

The torque of the blend depends on the chain structure, molecular weight of the polymers, and the processing temperature. During melt processing, the in situ grafting reaction between terminal −OH groups and terminal −COOH groups of PLA/PBAT and the epoxy groups of the ADR usually results in changes in the torque value of the blend. Figure S3 shows the torque change over time during the melt blending of PLA/PLA/ADR blends. The equilibrium torque of neat PBAT/PLA was 4.1 N m. After adding different structural ADRs, the torque–time curve exhibited varying degrees of increases. Notably, when ADR 4468 was used as the chain extender, the equilibrium torque of the mixture reached 5.9 N m, which is 43.9% higher than that of pure PLA/PBAT. This torque increase arises from more efficient chain extension and branching reactions, which form a cross-linked network within the PLA/PBAT matrix, thereby increasing the viscosity of the mixture. , Additionally, the in situ grafting reaction increases the molecular weight of the PLA/PBAT blend, thereby reducing polymer decomposition during processing. The significantly enhanced torque is related to the improvement in the mechanical performance.

Thermodynamic Behavior of PLA/PBAT/ADR Blown Films

The thermodynamic behavior of the blown film and the improvement in PLA/PBAT compatibility induced by the ADR were analyzed by DMA, as shown in Figure . The storage modulus (E′) of the pure PLA/PBAT blown film at −60 °C was 1367.4 MPa (see Table S5). After adding different structural ADRs, the change sequence of the E′ at −60 °C was 1ADR 4468 > 1ADR 4400 > 1ADR 4370-S > PLA/PBAT. This result indicates that there is a stronger interaction between the ADR 4468 and the PLA/PBAT interface, significantly enhancing the interfacial adhesion and interphase layer strength between PLA and PBAT. The glass transition temperature (T g) of pure PLA and PBAT blown films is −21.3 and 64.0 °C, respectively (see Table S5). The significant temperature difference between the two indicates that this blend system exhibits typical thermodynamic incompatibility, resulting in microphase separation. After adding different structural ADRs, the T g of PLA and PBAT shifts to higher temperatures, and the difference in ΔT g decreases. This result is attributed to the graft copolymer formed by the chain extension reaction, which increases the degree of molecular chain entanglement in the mixed system and hinders chain segment mobility. The lower T g is attributed to improved interfacial compatibility between the two phases resulting from the in situ compatibility reaction. Furthermore, the decrease in the peak height of the Tan delta curve indicates that the elastic behavior of the polymer matrix has been enhanced. This result further indicates that the addition of ADR 4468 enables stronger compatibility to PLA/PBAT.

Rheological Behaviors of PLA/PBAT/ADR Blends

The rheological properties of the PLA/PBAT/ADR blends were systematically investigated to clarify the interaction between the in situ grafting reaction and the melt processability. Figure presents the storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), complex viscosity (|η*|), and Han curves (log G′ vs log G″) of PLA/PBAT/ADR blends. As shown in Figure a, the G′ values in the low-frequency region all show different increasing trends after adding different structural ADRs, indicating enhanced network formation via chain extension and chain branching. Compared to the pure PLA/PBAT (G′ = 37.4 Pa at 0.1 rad/s), 1ADR 4468 can increase the G′ value to 130.8 Pa at 0.1 rad/s, quantitatively verifying that ADR 4468 is more conducive to the formation of the melt internal penetration entanglement network. This entanglement network arises from physical entanglement between PLA and PBAT chains and the transient chemical cross-linked network mediated by the ADR. The trend of G″ is similar to that of G′ (see Figure b). The addition of different structural ADRs enhances the G″ of PLA/PBAT/ADR blends. This is because the ADR can form cross-linked structures within the PLA/PBAT matrix, thereby generating strong intermolecular forces between the polymers and restricting the relaxation behavior of the polymer chains.

Dynamic rheological properties of pure PLA/PBAT and PLA/PBAT/ADR blends: (a) storage modulus, (b) loss modulus, (c) compound viscosity, and (d) Han curves.

The film bubble stability of biodegradable polymers has a high demand for melt strength. Low melt strength results in a narrow processing window, making the film bubble prone to rupture and necking during blown film production and making it difficult to obtain films with uniform thickness and excellent performance. All the blends exhibit obvious shear thinning behavior (see Figure c). With the introduction of different structured ADRs, the |η*| of the blends shows different increasing trends. As the amount increases, this trend becomes more obvious. Notably, compared with pure PLA/PBAT, when 1 wt % ADR 4468 is incorporated, the |η*| of the blends can be increased by up to 167.8–52.5% within the tested angular frequency range (0.1–100 rad/s). This phenomenon is attributed to more effective chain extension and chain branching effects between ADR 4468 and PLA/PBAT. The significant improvement in |η*| for the zADR 4468 blend is expected to enhance film blowing stability more effectively than the other two chain extenders.

The nonlinear Han curve (see Figure d) presented by PLA/PBAT indicates the inherent immiscibility between PLA and PBAT. Introducing different structural ADRs will induce structural changes in the blend. The grafted products generated during in situ grafting impart higher elasticity to the blend, which is also a necessary result of the good interface interaction that restricts chain migration. Meanwhile, with the addition of different structured ADRs, the G′ and G″ at the terminal region increase significantly. This is attributed to the ADR-induced copolymer formation between PLA and PBAT, thereby enhancing the interfacial interactions between the two phases. Notably, when ADR 4468 exceeds 0.5 wt %, the slope of the curve decreases markedly. This evidently arises from the superior interfacial compatibilization induced by ADR 4468 between PLA and PBAT.

Crystallization Property of PLA/PBAT/ADR Blends

The second heating DSC curve of PLA/PBAT/ADR blends is shown in Figure . The relevant thermal parameters are summarized in Table S6. It can be seen that different structural ADRs have played a role in improving the interfacial compatibility of PLA/PBAT. The T g of pure PLA and PBAT is −29.2 and 62.9 °C, respectively. After adding different structural ADRs, the T g of PBAT shifts to higher temperatures, while the T g of PLA remains basically unchanged. Moreover, compared with pure PLA/PBAT, adding different structural ADRs leads to an increase in the T cc and a decrease in the X c of the blends. Further increasing the dosage of the ADR will exacerbate this increase and decrease the trend. This is attributed to the chain extension and chain branching effects promoted by the ADR-mediated in situ grafting reaction.

Blown Film Processability of PLA/PBAT/ADR Blends

The blown film processability of PLA/PBAT/ADR blends was systematically evaluated using melt flow rate (MFR), bubble stability, and optical transmission analysis. The MFR reflects the flow behavior of polymers in high-temperature fields. A higher melt strength (lower MFR) is associated with improved bubble stability during film blowing. As shown in Figure a, the MFR of pure PLA/PBAT is higher than 10 g/10 min, which is due to the degradation and breakage of PLA/PBAT molecular chains during thermoplastic processing. The introduction of the ADR reduces the MFR, indicating that the ADR inhibits the degradation of PLA/PBAT to a certain extent. Moreover, with the increase of the ADR content, this downward trend becomes more significant, further demonstrating the role of the ADR in inhibiting the degradation of PLA/PBAT and improving miscibility. The MFR results also explain the observed trends in the molecular weight and gel content of the blend.

(a) Melt flow rate, (b) digital picture of the blown film, and (c) ultraviolet–visible curve of pure PLA/PBAT and PLA/PBAT/ADR blown films.

The improvement in bubble stability and size during the film blowing process further validates these findings. Without an ADR, the PLA/PBAT blend exhibited an irregular bubble size and a limited blow-up ratio (see Figure b), attributed to insufficient melt strength. The addition of a small amount of ADR (0.5 wt %) improved bubble uniformity and size to some extent. At 1 wt % ADR, bubble stability and size were further enhanced. Notably, when the ADR 4468 content exceeded 1 wt %, a stable blown film could be achieved for extended periods, and the improvement in bubble stability and size was consistent with the trend of MFR reduction. These results emphasize that both chain extension and chain branching mediated by ADRs with different structures can enhance melt strength, among which ADR 4468 made the greatest contribution to the stable film blowing process.

The transparent film can directly display the appearance, color, and texture of the product. Figure c shows the ultraviolet–visible light transmittance of the blown film. The blown film is completely opaque in the UV–C (200–290 nm) and part of the UV–B regions (290–300 nm). This excellent ultraviolet shielding ability is closely related to the chemical structure of the PBAT component in the blown film. Within the visible light range, the transmittance of the blown film increases with increasing wavelength. Compared with the pure PLA/PBAT blown film, although adding different structural ADRs would reduce the transmittance of the blown film, the film still has an acceptable transmittance. The decrease is attributed to the chain extension and chain branching mediated by ADRs, which cause fluctuations in the internal density of the material, resulting in refraction and reflection when light passes through the blown film.

Mechanical Properties of PLA/PBAT/ADR Blown Films

The mechanical properties of pure PLA/PBAT and PLA/PBAT/ADR blown films with different structural ADRs in the machine direction (MD) and transverse direction (TD) were investigated, as shown in Figures and S2 and Tables S7 and S8. Because of the lack of compatibility between PLA and PBAT, the retention of strength and the increase in toughness are limited. Compared with PLA/PBAT blown films, the introduction of different structural ADRs has led to varying degrees of improvement in the mechanical properties of the blown films in both the MD and TD. The modest improvement with ADR 4370 is attributed to the steric hindrance effect and the high reactivity, resulting in a poor chain extension effect. The moderate improvement with ADR 4400 arises from insufficient multifunctional epoxy groups, which failed to generate sufficient PLA-grafted-PBAT copolymers at the PLA/PBAT interface. Notably, ADR 4468 contributes the most to the improvement of the mechanical properties of the blown films, and the mechanical properties of the blown film show a trend of increasing first and then decreasing with an increase in the amount of ADR 4468. When the dosage of ADR 4468 was 1.5 wt %, an optimal balance between rigidity and toughness was achieved. The tensile strength and elongation at break in the MD/TD could reach 60.6/57.4 MPa and 608.5/458.3%, respectively, which were 29.8/21.1 and 158.8/89.0% higher than those of pure PLA/PBAT blown films. These improvements are attributed to that (1) the elimination of microphase separation and the formation of uniform and finely dispersed micro/nanostructures facilitate the triggering of large-scale matrix plastic deformation; (2) the ADR-induced chain extension/branching enhances molecular weight and improves the entanglement between molecules; and (3) the in situ formed PLA-grafted-PBAT copolymer improves the interfacial compatibility. When the ADR 4468 load is 2 wt %, the decrease in tensile strength and elongation at break is attributed to excessive cross-linking. Furthermore, the blown film exhibits obvious anisotropy in its tensile properties. The properties in the MD are always superior to those in the TD. This anisotropy is attributed to the preferential parallel orientation of PLA and PBAT molecular chains along the MD. When stretched along the MD, the strong covalent bond polymer backbone is mainly damaged, while when stretched along the TD, the weaker van der Waals forces between molecular chains are mainly disrupted. Figure d presents a radar image demonstrating the mechanical performance advantages of the 1ADR 4468 blown film. Evidently, the 1ADR 4468 blown film exhibits balanced isotropic high tensile strength and considerable elongation at break in MD, while also having moderate tear strength. It meets most of the current standards for commercial and food packaging applications in the market. In general, ADR 4468 exerts a more notable effect on enhancing the mechanical properties of the PLA/PBAT systems.

Cryofractured Surface Morphology of PLA/PBAT/ADR Blown Films

The mechanical properties of the PLA/PBAT/ADR blown film are related to the phase morphology transformation and interfacial adhesion induced by different structural ADRs. The cryofracture surface morphology of the PLA/PBAT/ADR blown film in MD is shown in Figures and S4. The pure PLA/PBAT blown film presents a rough phase separation morphology, with large discrete ellipsoidal PBAT particles (mean domain size is 1.38 μm) distributed in the PLA matrix. At 0.5 wt % ADRs, elongated PBAT domains were observed, with decreased PBAT particle size and partial interfacial adhesion. When the ADR loading was 1 wt %, the PBAT particle size (mean domain size is 0.89 μm) further decreased and interfacial adhesion was significantly enhanced. Additionally, the introduction of different structural ADRs altered the phase morphology. For instance, as for yADR 4400, the PBAT particle size was smaller compared to that of xADR 4370-S, but partial interfacial detachment occurred. This is attributed to insufficient multifunctional epoxy groups in ADR 4400, resulting in an insufficient compatibility between PLA and PBAT. For the blown film containing ADR 4468, the particle size of the PBAT decreases significantly. With increasing ADR 4468 content, it is observed that the PBAT domain size continued to decrease, and the microphase separation structure gradually disappeared (see Figure S4). This enhanced interface adhesion is beneficial for the PLA matrix to efficiently transfer energy to the more energy-dissipating PBAT phase. When the loading content of ADR 4468 is 2 wt %, the phase morphology changes, and spherical PBAT small particles appear. Combined with the mechanical properties and cryofractured surface morphology, this indicates good consistency among high strength, toughness, and compatibility in the blown film.

Enhanced Compatibility Mechanism

Based on the above experimental analysis, we summarized the enhanced compatibility mechanisms of different structural ADRs on PLA/PBAT, as shown in Figure . Pure PLA/PBAT exhibits an obvious microphase separation due to poor compatibility. For ADR 4370-S, with its low EEW and short main chain, most of the epoxy groups on its main chain hinder sufficient contact with the terminal −OH/–COOH groups of PLA and PBAT due to their own steric hindrance effect. Moreover, its high reactivity may lead to intramolecular reactions with polyester chains or reactions with polyester degradation products, resulting in a limited reduction of the PBAT dispersion size. For ADR 4400, with its high EEW and longer main chain, sufficient in situ compatibility reactions promote the refinement of PBAT dispersion size, but due to the insufficient epoxy groups, the interfacial adhesion is relatively weak. For ADR 4468 with relatively lower EEW and longer main chain, most of the epoxy groups on its main chain can react with PLA and PBAT to form a large amount of PLA-grafted-PBAT copolymer, which eliminates microphase separation and leads to a homogeneous, finely dispersed micro/nanostructure with strong interfacial adhesion.

Barrier Properties of PLA/PBAT/ADR Blown Films

The oxygen and water vapor barrier properties of polymer films play a crucial role in commercial and food packaging applications. The oxygen and water vapor permeability properties of the blown films are shown in Figure . The pure PLA/PBAT blown film exhibited unsatisfactory barrier properties, with the oxygen permeability (OP) and water vapor permeability (WVP) being 11.21 × 10–15 cm3·cm/(cm2·s·Pa) and 2.64 × 10–13 g·cm/(cm2·s·Pa), respectively. After in situ compatibility modification of the PLA/PBAT system with different structural ADRs, the OP and WVP of the blown films decreased to varying degrees. This is because the ADR enhances the interfacial interaction by eliminating microphase separation and forming uniform and finely dispersed micro/nanostructures, reducing interface defects and free volume. Therefore, the voids for gas transportation in the blown film matrix decrease, and the gas diffusion path increases, resulting in a decrease in the OP and WVP. Furthermore, the consumption of hydrophilic terminal −OH and −COOH also contributed to a certain degree of reduction in WVP. When the content of ADR 4468 was 1.5 wt %, the OP and WVP values of the blown film were 3.88 × 10–15 cm3·cm/(cm2·s·Pa) and 1.57 × 10–13 g·cm/(cm2·s·Pa), respectively, which decreased by 65.39 and 40.53% compared to pure PLA and PBAT blown film. Its OP value is similar to that of the polypropylene flexible film (trade name: Bicor 70 HSH), indicating that it has excellent gas barrier properties. Further increasing the content of ADR 4468 leads to abnormal increases in OP and WVP, which might be attributed to the reduction in gas diffusion pathways arising from phase morphology changes.

Conclusions

In this work, an effective strategy for enhancing the comprehensive performance of biodegradable PLA/PBAT blown films via in situ interfacial compatibilization induced by ADRs with different structures is demonstrated. The realization of significant effects requires sufficient active epoxy groups and long backbones. In situ reaction compatibilization bridges PLA and PBAT through grafting reactions, increasing the molecular weight and melt strength of the blend, thereby effectively improving the film blowing process stability. The addition of an ADR promotes chain extension/branching and interfacial adhesion as well as uniform and fine dispersion of the PBAT phase. This structure is conducive to triggering large-scale matrix plastic deformation, achieving a balance between film strength and toughness. The reduction of interfacial voids and free volume, coupled with increased gas diffusion paths, significantly enhances the oxygen and water vapor barrier properties. This research successfully integrated the high strength of PLA and the high toughness of PBAT through a commercial ADR. The corresponding material–process–structure–property relationship provides strong guidance for designing practical biodegradable PLA/PBAT packaging films with both high strength and high toughness.
