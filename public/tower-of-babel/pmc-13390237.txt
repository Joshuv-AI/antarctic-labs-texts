Phenotype discovery and mortality prediction in sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction: a deep learning and stratified modeling approach
Authors: Yan Zhuang, Xin-yuan Zhang, Da-cheng Wang, Qiu-hua Chen, Xiao-huan Zhuang, Xiao-peng Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD) is a common and heterogeneous complication in patients with sepsis and is associated with increased mortality. This study aimed to identify distinct clinical phenotypes of SIMD using unsupervised deep learning and to develop an optimal short-term survival prediction model based on phenotypic stratification. Data from SIMD patients in the MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV databases were retrospectively analyzed. An autoencoder was used for feature compression, followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and K-means clustering to identify phenotypes, with a novel composite scoring system applied to ensure robust cluster selection. Prognostic differences among phenotypes were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses. XGBoost with SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) was used for phenotype prediction and model interpretability. Multi-strategy models (M1–M4) were further constructed to assess the predictive value of phenotypic stratification and determine the optimal modeling strategy for survival prediction. Three clusters (Cluster 0–2) with distinct prognostic profiles were identified. Cluster 2 (high-risk phenotype), characterized by metabolic acidosis and multiorgan dysfunction, showed the highest 90-day mortality (55.1% in the development cohort). SHAP analysis highlighted lactate, bilirubin, coagulation indices, and Glasgow Coma Scale as key drivers of phenotype differentiation. Phenotype-specific modeling (M3) significantly outperformed the global model (M1), achieving a validation AUC of 0.880 and a PR-AUC of 0.863 for Cluster 2. Further feature enhancement in M4 did not yield significant additional benefit, supporting M3 as the optimal modeling strategy. These findings delineate clinically actionable SIMD phenotypes using interpretable unsupervised learning and demonstrate that phenotype-specific modeling markedly improves mortality prediction, particularly for Cluster 2 (high-risk phenotype), underscoring the potential of phenotypic stratification to advance precision critical care in SIMD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03575-1.Keywords: Sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction, Phenotyping, Unsupervised learning, Explainable AI, SHAP, Mortality prediction, Precision medicine

Introduction

Sepsis remains a leading cause of mortality in critical care, with an estimated 48.9 million incident cases and 11.0 million deaths globally in 2017, accounting for 19.7% of all deaths [1, 2]. Sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD) is a frequent and severe complication, affecting 20–60% of ICU sepsis patients and associated with 28-day mortality rates up to 50% [3, 4]. SIMD is characterized by marked clinical heterogeneity, with patients exhibiting diverse patterns of cardiac injury, multiorgan dysfunction, and inflammatory profiles, leading to significant variability in prognosis [5, 6]. Conventional assessments, such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and cardiac biomarkers, are confounded by systemic inflammation and hemodynamics, failing to capture the full spectrum of SIMD pathophysiology and prognostic diversity [7–10].

Against this background, precise risk stratification is urgently needed. Unsupervised machine-learning approaches have advanced sepsis phenotyping by uncovering reproducible clinical subgroups with distinct outcomes in large-scale datasets [11, 12]. Notably, Seymour et al. delineated four sepsis phenotypes with differing mortality risks, and subsequent studies validated phenotype–outcome associations across heterogeneous ICU cohorts. However, most prior work has focused on unselected sepsis populations. A comprehensive SIMD-specific phenotypic framework—linking data-driven subtypes to prognosis and actionable prediction—remains underdeveloped, limiting individualized management strategies [13, 14].

To address this gap, a retrospective cohort study was conducted using the MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV databases, in which SIMD was operationally defined based on Sepsis-3 criteria and early cardiac biomarker elevation (e.g., NT-proBNP > 300 pg/mL). A deep learning pipeline was applied, integrating autoencoder-based feature compression, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), and K-means clustering optimized by a novel dynamic composite scoring system to identify distinct SIMD phenotypes. The resulting clusters were interpreted as phenotypic subgroups and characterized using SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) to elucidate key clinical drivers. We then evaluated prognostic differences via Kaplan–Meier and multivariable Cox models and developed phenotype-stratified mortality prediction strategies, with model interpretability provided by SHAP. We hypothesized that (i) patients with SIMD comprise reproducible phenotypes with distinct clinical and prognostic profiles; and (ii) phenotype-specific modeling would improve mortality prediction and conventional scores—particularly in higher-risk subgroups—thereby informing precision risk assessment and individualized ICU management [11–13, 15].

Methods

Study population and data sources

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the MIMIC-III v1.4 and MIMIC-IV v2.2 databases. Both databases are large, de-identified, and publicly available repositories of critical care data, with established ethical approval and credentialed access (PhysioNet; IRB and data use agreements) [16, 17]. Only patients with appropriate data access and research authorization were included. The investigator holds a valid CITI certification (Certificate ID: 55981932) in Biomedical Research Investigators – Refresher Course, ensuring compliance with ethical research standards.

Eligible patients were identified according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) first ICU admission during the study period; (3) fulfillment of Sepsis-3 criteria at ICU admission, defined by ICD-9/10 diagnostic codes in combination with suspected or confirmed infection and an acute increase in SOFA score ≥ 2; and (4) objective evidence of myocardial injury within the first 24 h after ICU entry, operationally defined as at least one cardiac biomarker exceeding predefined thresholds (NT-proBNP > 300 pg/mL, Troponin T > 0.01 ng/mL, or CK-MB > 5 ng/mL). This pragmatic definition was adopted based on commonly used criteria in large critical care database studies, given the lack of consistently available echocardiographic data [18, 19].

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a documented history of chronic cardiovascular disease, including heart failure, cardiomyopathy, ischemic heart disease, or valvular/congenital heart disease, or direct ICU admission following cardiac surgery (as determined by ICD codes or medical records); (2) active malignancy or AIDS at admission; (3) missing essential clinical data such as SOFA score, cardiac biomarkers, or vital signs within the first 24 h; (4) for patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU stay was retained for analysis; and (5) cases with more than 30% missing values among baseline variables were excluded.

Data preprocessing and variable extraction

Baseline data from the first 24 h of ICU admission were extracted, including demographics, comorbidities, vital signs, and laboratory results (e.g., WBC, lactate, hepatic and renal function, and coagulation indices). Variables with ≤ 30% missing data were imputed using k-nearest neighbor (KNN, k = 10). Continuous variables were subsequently scaled using robust scaling prior to model training.

Feature reduction and clustering analysis

To improve phenotype discovery in high-dimensional and potentially nonlinear clinical data, we adopted a sequential representation-learning and manifold-embedding pipeline rather than applying clustering directly to the original variables. Specifically, an autoencoder was first used to learn a compact latent representation from all candidate clinical features. This step was intended to reduce redundancy, denoise the input space, and capture nonlinear interactions among variables that may not be adequately preserved by linear methods. UMAP was subsequently applied to the autoencoder-derived latent representation, not as a redundant second compression step, but as a manifold-learning tool to reorganize the learned feature space by preserving local neighborhood structure and improving subgroup separability for downstream clustering and visualization. Thus, the two components served complementary purposes: the autoencoder performed nonlinear feature abstraction, whereas UMAP refined manifold organization within the learned latent space.

Because sequential transformation of high-dimensional data may introduce information distortion or alter inter-sample relationships, we did not assume the superiority or fidelity of this pipeline a priori. Instead, clustering robustness was evaluated systematically across candidate cluster numbers, clustering algorithms, random seeds, and bootstrap resampling. In addition, we formally benchmarked the main pipeline against simpler or reduced-complexity alternatives, including PCA + K-means, PCA + GMM, AE + K-means, and AE + GMM, to assess whether the sequential autoencoder–UMAP framework offered practical advantages in clustering structure, reproducibility, and downstream clinical interpretability.

All candidate clinical features were included as input for representation learning. High-dimensional data were first nonlinearly compressed using an autoencoder neural network (architecture: 100–64–16–2; BatchNorm, LayerNorm, LeakyReLU activation, dropout rate 0.3, Adam optimizer, MSE loss, with early stopping to reduce overfitting). Subsequently, UMAP was applied to the autoencoder-derived latent features to generate a three-dimensional manifold embedding (n_neighbors = 20, min_dist = 0.05, metric = euclidean), which served as the input space for downstream clustering [20, 21].

For unsupervised subgroup identification, both K-means and Gaussian mixture models (GMM, diagonal covariance) were performed on the 3D embedding. Candidate cluster numbers (k) ranged from 2 to 7, and each setting was repeated across 10 random seeds with 100 bootstrap iterations to assess robustness and reproducibility [22, 23]. The resulting clusters were labeled numerically and interpreted as phenotypic subgroups throughout the study.

Clustering evaluation and optimal k selection

Clustering performance was evaluated using a combination of internal validity metrics and cross-cohort structural consistency measures. Internal metrics included the silhouette coefficient, Calinski–Harabasz (CH) index, and Davies–Bouldin index. In addition, cluster-center correlation and the adjusted Rand index (ARI) were used as supplementary indicators to assess the reproducibility of clustering structure between the development and validation cohorts.

To assess cross-cohort structural agreement, clustering models were first trained in the development cohort. The same clustering framework was then independently refitted in the validation cohort to obtain validation-specific cluster assignments. In parallel, cluster assignments for the validation cohort were also generated by applying the clustering model learned in the development cohort (i.e., using centroids or model parameters derived from the development data). The adjusted Rand index (ARI) was calculated between these two sets of labels within the validation cohort, thereby quantifying the agreement between independently derived clustering structures without requiring ground truth labels. This use of ARI reflects cross-cohort structural reproducibility rather than true external validation cohort in a supervised sense.

To facilitate model selection across candidate cluster numbers, we used a dynamic composite scoring system based on the silhouette coefficient and CH index. Specifically, for each random seed and candidate k (ranging from 2 to 7), the silhouette coefficient was retained in its raw form, whereas the CH index was normalized within the same seed by dividing by the maximum CH value observed across candidate k values. For each clustering method and random seed, dynamic weights were assigned according to the standard deviations of the silhouette coefficient and CH index across candidate k values within that seed. The composite score was then calculated as a weighted sum of the silhouette coefficient and normalized CH index. The Davies–Bouldin index and ARI were reported as complementary indicators rather than components of the composite score (see Supplementary Methods for details).

The final clustering solution was not selected solely according to the highest composite score. Instead, model selection additionally considered parsimony, cluster-center reproducibility, and cross-cohort structural agreement. Preference was given to solutions showing: (1) only limited incremental gain in composite score with further increases in k; (2) cluster-center correlation coefficient ≥ 0.80; and (3) ARI-based cross-cohort structural agreement ≥ 0.70. Using these criteria, K-means with k = 3 was selected as the final clustering solution, and the resulting subgroups were labeled as Cluster throughout the study.

Phenotype prognostic analysis and interpretability

Phenotype-specific prognostic analyses were performed by plotting Kaplan–Meier survival curves for 28-day and 90-day mortality across the identified clusters, with between-group differences evaluated using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusted for age, sex, comorbidities, and SOFA score, was employed to assess the independent effect of each phenotype on mortality.

To further investigate the discriminative power and clinical interpretability of the identified phenotypes (Clusters 0–2), supervised machine learning models—including XGBoost, random forest, and multilayer perceptron (MLP)—were trained to predict cluster assignment. Model evaluation was conducted using stratified five-fold cross-validation in the development cohort and subsequently assessed in an independent temporal cohort (MIMIC-III). Performance metrics included overall accuracy, macro-averaged F1 score (Macro F1), weighted F1 score, precision, recall, log loss, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve calculated using the one-vs-rest (OvR) approach (AUC (OvR)). Macro F1 reflects the unweighted mean of F1 scores across all classes, while weighted F1 accounts for class imbalance by weighting each class-specific F1 by the number of samples per class. AUC (OvR) summarizes the model’s discriminative ability in multi-class classification by averaging the AUC for each class against the rest.

Model interpretability was addressed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which provided both global and local explanations of the key discriminative variables underlying phenotype assignment, offering clinically interpretable insights into the characteristics of each phenotype [29, 30]. Full details of feature selection, variable definitions, and validation procedures are provided in the supplementary appendix.

Phenotype-stratified modeling and feature enhancement

To optimize the prediction of 28-day mortality, we developed and systematically compared four modeling strategies, each designed to account for phenotype heterogeneity and improve predictive performance. All models employed the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to address class imbalance, applied only within the training data to avoid information leakage, and were subsequently evaluated in an independent temporal cohort [31].

M1: An overall baseline model trained on the entire cohort, with features selected using recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV), optimizing the number of variables based on the highest cross-validated AUC.

M2: Separate predictive models developed within each phenotype subgroup (Cluster 0–2), using the same set of features as in M1.

M3: Phenotype-specific feature re-selection and modeling; within each phenotype subgroup (Cluster 0–2), RFECV was re-applied to identify the most predictive variables and to optimize subgroup-specific models.

M4: An enhanced modeling approach integrating multi-source features from both the original clinical data and the unsupervised learning pipeline. Specifically, the input feature set consisted of: (1) original clinical variables selected through RFECV; (2) autoencoder-derived latent features (AE1–AE2); (3) UMAP embedding features (UMAP1–UMAP3); and (4) cluster membership probability features (KMeans_prob_0–KMeans_prob_2). Since K-means does not inherently provide probabilistic outputs, cluster membership probabilities were estimated based on the relative distances of each sample to cluster centroids and subsequently normalized across clusters to obtain a soft representation of cluster affiliation. All features were concatenated to form a unified feature matrix and standardized using z-score normalization prior to model training.

Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), area under the precision–recall curve (PR-AUC), F1 score, and other standard metrics. For benchmarking, the predictive performance of both the overall baseline model (M1) and the optimized subgroup-specific model was compared with the conventional Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score. All comparisons were conducted in the independent temporal cohort using AUC, PR-AUC, Brier score, and reclassification metrics.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in Python 3.9 using pandas, scikit-learn, PyTorch, umap-learn, and matplotlib. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA), while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median [interquartile range, IQR] and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (n) and percentages (%) and compared using Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Multiple comparisons were adjusted using Scheffé’s test, Nemenyi test, or Bonferroni correction as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A schematic overview of the study design and analytical workflow is provided in e-Fig. 1.

Results

Study cohort and modeling variables

A total of 2,906 patients with sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD) were included in this study, comprising 1,789 cases in the development cohort and 1,117 cases in an independent temporal cohort. All patients met the Sepsis-3 diagnostic criteria and had evidence of myocardial injury within 24 h of ICU admission. One hundred candidate variables—including demographic characteristics, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory findings, and treatment measures—were extracted for model development.

Key baseline characteristics of the development and temporal cohorts are summarized in e-Table 1. The proportion of male patients was 52.5% in the development cohort and 58.2% in the temporal cohort; median ages were 64 and 61 years, respectively. The median SOFA scores were 3 and 6, respectively, indicating differences in disease severity distribution between the cohorts (e-Table 1).

Identification of three clinical phenotypes

Based on the composite scoring system and overall clustering performance evaluation, K-means with k = 3 was selected as the final clustering solution. The resulting subgroups were labeled as Cluster 0–2 and interpreted as distinct clinical phenotypes.

As summarized in e-Table 2, this solution demonstrated favorable clustering performance, with a silhouette coefficient of 0.422 ± 0.033 and a composite score of 0.871 ± 0.073. Measures of stability and reproducibility were also satisfactory, including a bootstrap adjusted Rand index (ARI_boot) of 0.944 ± 0.036 and a cluster-center correlation of 0.880 ± 0.168. Cross-cohort structural agreement, assessed using the adjusted Rand index between independently derived and projected cluster assignments in the temporal cohort (ARI_ext), was 0.711 ± 0.141.

Prognostic differences among clusters

Significant differences in 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality were observed among the three clusters (Cluster 0–2) (Fig. 2A–B; e-Table 5). In the development cohort, 28-day mortality rates were 22.8% (Cluster 0), 37.8% (Cluster 1), and 50.1% (Cluster 2) (P < 0.001). In the independent temporal cohort, the corresponding rates were 14.9%, 29.5%, and 41.5% (P < 0.001), demonstrating consistent trends across cohorts. Similarly, 90-day mortality was highest in Cluster 2 (development: 55.1%; temporal: 47.0%).

Fully adjusted multivariable Cox regression analysis (Model 4) demonstrated that, compared with Cluster 0 (reference group), the risk of death was significantly higher in Clusters 1 and 2. Key discriminative variables among subgroups

Kruskal–Wallis tests identified the top ten variables most strongly associated with cluster assignment: anion gap (AG), base excess, PT, INR, PTT, creatinine, total bilirubin, lactate, platelet count, and ALT (e-Fig. 2). Most variables showed significant distributional differences across clusters in both cohorts, highlighting substantial heterogeneity in acid–base status, coagulation function, and organ dysfunction profiles (e-Table 8; e-Table 9).

Global SHAP importance analysis

Global SHAP analysis (Fig. 3A–B) confirmed the leading importance of PTT_max, AG, PT_max, base excess, total bilirubin, INR, creatinine, lactate, ALT, and platelet count in cluster assignment prediction. These findings are consistent with differences in coagulation status, metabolic balance, and organ function across clusters (Fig. 3A1–A4; B1–B4). Both distribution-based analyses and model-derived feature importance showed concordant patterns in the development and independent temporal cohorts.

Performance of overall modeling (M1)

For the unstratified overall modeling strategy (M1), the random forest model achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.831 and a PR-AUC of 0.694 in the independent temporal cohort (development cohort five-fold cross-validation: ROC-AUC 0.769 ± 0.033, PR-AUC 0.660 ± 0.039). The corresponding F1 score and recall in the temporal cohort were 0.599 and 0.669, respectively (e-Table 10; e-Fig. 7). The optimal number of features for the M1 model was determined using recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV; see e-Fig. 3 for the selection curve and variable importance ranking).

An enhanced version of the overall model (M1-Enhanced), which incorporated latent and cluster-related features without phenotype stratification, achieved a slightly higher ROC-AUC of 0.840 and recall of 0.687 in the temporal cohort, but showed limited improvement across other performance metrics (e-Table 10).

Comparative analysis indicated that the M1 model outperformed the conventional Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score in the independent temporal cohort, with higher ROC-AUC (0.831 vs. 0.730), as well as improvements in PR-AUC and reclassification metrics (Table 4). These findings provide a reference for subsequent phenotype-stratified modeling and support the potential advantage of machine learning–based approaches over conventional clinical risk scores.

Performance of cluster-stratified modeling (M2)

Cluster-stratified modeling (M2) revealed heterogeneity in predictive performance across subgroups. While models in Clusters 0 and 1 showed modest to moderate discrimination, performance in Cluster 2 (high-risk phenotype) consistently exceeded that of the overall M1 model, particularly in recall, PR-AUC, and F1 score (independent temporal cohort: ROC-AUC 0.857, PR-AUC 0.851, F1 score 0.731; e-Table 10).

Performance of subgroup-specific variable re-selection (M3)

After further optimizing feature selection within each cluster (M3 strategy) (e-Figs. 4, 5 and 6), model performance improved across all subgroups. In particular, Cluster 2 (high-risk phenotype) achieved the best overall performance, with ROC-AUC of 0.880, PR-AUC of 0.863, recall of 0.776, and F1 score of 0.797 in the independent temporal cohort.

A comprehensive evaluation—including ROC, precision–recall, calibration, decision curve, and risk stratification analyses—is presented in Fig. 5, demonstrating consistent model performance and potential clinical applicability for identifying high-risk patients (e-Table 10).

Importantly, in direct comparison, the M3 model for Cluster 2 substantially outperformed the SOFA score in the independent temporal cohort (ROC-AUC 0.880 vs. 0.737, PR-AUC 0.863 vs. 0.659, Brier score 0.132 vs. 0.249; Table 4), with significant improvements across all metrics.

Performance of feature-enhanced modeling (M4)

As a sensitivity analysis, we incorporated enhanced features—including cluster membership probabilities, autoencoder-derived latent representations, and UMAP embeddings—into each subgroup model (M4 strategy).

Model performance showed modest improvement in Cluster 0, whereas in Clusters 1 and 2, key metrics such as ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, and F1 score did not show meaningful improvement compared with the M3 strategy, and in some cases declined slightly (e-Table 10).

Similarly, the M1-Enhanced model, which incorporated latent and cluster-related features at the overall population level, demonstrated limited improvement over the baseline M1 model.

Comprehensive evaluation

Comprehensive evaluation indicated that the overall model (M1) provided moderate discrimination and calibration but had limited ability to identify high-risk patients. After phenotype-stratified optimization (M3 strategy), predictive performance improved notably in Cluster 2 (high-risk phenotype).

Compared with the SOFA score, the M1 model showed improved performance in the independent temporal cohort; however, the M3 model for Cluster 2 demonstrated greater improvement, particularly in ROC-AUC and PR-AUC (Table 4).

Overall, the integrated framework—combining unsupervised phenotyping and phenotype-specific modeling—was associated with stepwise improvement in outcome prediction, with the most pronounced gains observed in Cluster 2.

SHAP-based mechanistic interpretation for the high-risk subgroup (Cluster 2)

To further characterize the key variables associated with the high-risk phenotype (Cluster 2), we performed SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis of the corresponding mortality prediction model.

Global SHAP bar and beeswarm plots consistently identified lactate (minimum and maximum), bicarbonate, base excess, temperature, GCS motor score, respiratory rate, creatinine, and anion gap as the most influential variables. Elevated lactate, together with lower bicarbonate and base excess, was associated with higher predicted risk, reflecting differences in metabolic status. Additional contributions from GCS motor score, temperature, and respiratory rate were also observed, suggesting associations with neurological and respiratory function (Figs. 6A–B and e, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 and 14A–B).

At the individual level, SHAP beeswarm and waterfall plots demonstrated that predicted risk in Cluster 2 was primarily influenced by lactate, base excess, and markers related to neurological and respiratory status, consistent with the overall feature importance patterns (Figs. 6C–E and e, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 and 14C–E).

For comparison, SHAP analyses for Cluster 0 and Cluster 1 (e-Figs. 10, 11, 12 and 13) showed distinct feature patterns. In Cluster 0 (mild phenotype), model predictions were mainly associated with hepatic and renal function variables (e.g., bilirubin, creatinine, PT), whereas Cluster 1 (hepato-coagulopathic phenotype) was characterized by bilirubin, INR, and PTT, indicating differences in coagulation and hepatic profiles.

Discussion

Key findings and phenotypic insights

This study provides a comprehensive phenotyping framework for sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD), using unsupervised learning to identify three reproducible subgroups with distinct clinical features and prognoses. Cluster 0, characterized by relatively stable metabolic and coagulation parameters (e.g., median lactate 2.104 mmol/L, INR 1.2), was associated with the lowest mortality (28-day mortality: 22.8% in the development cohort and 14.9% in the temporal cohort; 90-day mortality: 28.3% and 18.1%, respectively), suggesting preserved compensatory mechanisms and lower intervention requirements. Cluster 1 showed elevated bilirubin and INR (e.g., median bilirubin 2.2 mg/dL, INR 1.8), consistent with hepato-coagulopathic dysfunction and intermediate risk. Cluster 2 represented a hypermetabolic high-risk phenotype with severe metabolic disturbance and multi-organ dysfunction, yielding the highest mortality. Notably, Cluster 2 remained independently associated with mortality in adjusted Cox models, and phenotype-specific modeling demonstrated improved predictive performance compared with global models and SOFA scores.

Mechanistic interpretations

These phenotypes may reflect distinct underlying pathophysiological states rather than directly observed mechanisms. Cluster 2 appears to represent a state of severe metabolic dysregulation, characterized by hyperlactatemia, metabolic acidosis, and multi-organ dysfunction. These features are consistent with impaired tissue oxygen utilization, mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered metabolic pathways described in prior sepsis research [32–34].

SHAP analysis identified lactate, base excess, and neurological status as key contributors to model predictions, which aligns with established clinical markers of disease severity in sepsis. However, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal mechanisms.

Cluster 1 may reflect a phenotype dominated by hepatic dysfunction and coagulopathy, consistent with inflammation-related impairment of liver function and coagulation pathways [35]. Cluster 0 likely represents a less severe phenotype with relatively preserved physiological regulation.

Comparison with existing literature

Our study extends prior phenotyping efforts by offering a cardiac-specific perspective on SIMD. While Seymour et al. defined four sepsis phenotypes, the δ-phenotype (marked by hyperlactatemia and organ failure) resembles our Cluster 2 but lacked cardiac focus [11]. Similarly, subclinical cardiac dysfunction in sepsis has been linked to increased vasopressor use and fluid needs, supporting the circulatory instability of Cluster 2 [4, 5, 36]. Sweeney et al. identified transcriptomic subtypes in bacterial sepsis, one associated with immune suppression and coagulation abnormalities, akin to our Cluster 1 [37]. This is reinforced by emerging studies implicating ferroptosis and NETs in myocardial injury and hypercoagulability in sepsis [38–40]. Methodologically, our dynamic scoring system enhances cluster reproducibility compared to traditional silhouette-based approaches [24–28]. The M3 strategy further improves predictive performance via phenotype-specific feature modeling, surpassing conventional severity scores. Together, these results validate the clinical and biological distinctiveness of SIMD phenotypes and establish a framework that bridges omics-derived insights with bedside applicability.

Clinical significance

Phenotype-driven stratification offers meaningful avenues for precision care. Cluster 2 may benefit from early metabolic support (e.g., bicarbonate, thiamine, levosimendan) [41–43]. In selected high-risk patients, levosimendan has been proposed as a potential adjunctive therapy for sepsis-related cardiac dysfunction; however, current evidence regarding its efficacy and safety remains inconsistent, warranting cautious and individualized application [44–46]. Cluster 1 may require liver and coagulation-focused monitoring with potential hepatoprotective or anticoagulant therapies; Cluster 0 patients may be safely managed with standard care, minimizing overtreatment. Importantly, phenotype-informed prediction outperformed SOFA (e.g., ΔPR-AUC = 0.204), highlighting its relevance for triage, ICU resource allocation, and enrollment in precision trials. SHAP-based interpretability addresses AI’s black-box issue, facilitating bedside adoption and clinical trust [47, 48].

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, SIMD in this study was defined primarily based on biomarker criteria without systematic echocardiographic confirmation. As a result, elevated cardiac biomarkers in critically ill patients may partially reflect non-cardiac mechanisms, such as renal dysfunction, volume shifts, or systemic inflammatory responses, rather than true myocardial dysfunction. This may have introduced misclassification and contributed to phenotypic heterogeneity within the study cohort.

Second, although two independent retrospective cohorts were used, both MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV originate from the same healthcare system. Therefore, the validation performed in this study should be interpreted as temporal or cross-cohort validation rather than true external validation across independent healthcare systems, which may limit broader geographic and institutional generalizability.

Third, although the sequential autoencoder–UMAP framework was benchmarked against simpler strategies, multi-step representation learning may still distort global data geometry or attenuate information from low-variance but clinically relevant features. In addition, the integration of latent, embedding, and cluster-derived features in the M4 model may introduce additional model complexity, which could affect generalizability in external populations. To mitigate these risks, we evaluated clustering consistency across repeated random seeds and bootstrap samples and conducted comparative analyses against simpler baseline pipelines; however, some degree of representation bias cannot be fully excluded.

Fourth, techniques such as KNN imputation and SMOTE may introduce bias, and method selection can influence model calibration and predictive performance [49, 50].

Finally, the lack of multimodal data, including genomics, metabolomics, and continuous hemodynamic monitoring, limited the mechanistic depth of the present study. Future studies should incorporate multi-center and multi-regional cohorts for broader validation, further compare alternative embedding frameworks in independent datasets, explore phenotype-targeted interventions, and develop real-time phenotype-driven decision-support tools for ICU care [15].
