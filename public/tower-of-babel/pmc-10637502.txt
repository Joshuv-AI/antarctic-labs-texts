Effect of an herbal medicine, Chunghyul-dan, on prevention of recurrence in patients with ischemic stroke: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Han-Gyul Lee, Seungwon Kwon, Seung-Yeon Cho, Seong-Uk Park, Woo-Sang Jung, Sang-Kwan Moon, Jung-Mi Park, Chang-Nam Ko, Hyeung-Jin Jang, Ki-Ho Cho
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Research indicates that Chunghyul-dan (CHD), a herbal medicine, has an inhibitory effect on stroke recurrence in small vessel disease. Recent studies have suggested that CHD might also act on large arteries. This study aimed to verify the preventive effect of CHD on strokes of all the Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) causative classifications. We retrospectively analyzed 2 years of medical records of patients with ischemic stroke treated with CHD, 600 mg once daily, in combination with antiplatelet or anticoagulant agents. The prevalence of stroke recurrence in 2 years was analyzed. Stroke recurrence was defined as new neurological symptoms with corresponding brain imaging results. Nine of the 202 patients (4.46%) had recurrent ischemic stroke. Four occurred within 180 days, 3 between 180 and 365 days, and 2 between 365 and 730 days. All had only 1 recurrence. The recurrence rates were 1.12%, 5%, and 5.48% for small vessel occlusion, cardioembolism, and large vessel atherosclerosis, respectively. There were no adverse effects. These results suggest that CHD could inhibit ischemic stroke recurrence of all TOAST causative categories. A randomized controlled trial is needed to confirm this hypothesis.

1. Introduction

Prevention of recurrence is an important factor in the management of patients with ischemic stroke.[1] Patients with ischemic stroke have a high risk of recurrence,[2] and a reduction in that risk has a significant impact on morbidity and mortality.[3] Antiplatelet agents are recommended for most patients to prevent recurrence.[4] However, the prevention rate of antiplatelet agents for ischemic stroke was found to be insufficient at 6% to 13%,[5–11] and the recurrence rate of stroke over the past few decades has not changed.[12] Antiplatelet agents have a high risk of adverse effects, such as a hemorrhagic tendency, headache, gastrointestinal disturbances, neutropenia, and purpura.[13] Therefore, an additional strategy for secondary stroke prevention and treatment is needed. Recently, circular RNAs have emerged as a potentially ideal treatment for ischemic stroke due to their excellent stability and conservation in brain tissue.[14] However, it is still not widely utilized in clinical practice.

Chunghyul-dan (CHD) is a complex herbal medicine, in capsule form, derived from an 80% ethanol extraction of Scutellariae Radix, Coptidis Rhizoma, Phellodendri Cortex, Gardeniae Fructus, and Rhei Rhizoma. CHD inhibits 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl (HMG) coenzyme A (CoA) reductase[15] and improves arterial stiffness.[16] It also has anti-inflammatory[17] and antilipidemic effects,[18] which are due to the activation of NO production and the inhibition of VCAM-1 expression in endothelial cells.[19] In addition, antihypertensive,[20] anti-oxidative[17] and anti-apoptotic effects[21] have been verified.

In particular, previous studies have suggested that CHD suppresses the progress of microangiopathy, which is a key factor in small vessel disease, thereby reducing the occurrence of ischemic stroke due to silent brain infarction or small vessel disease over 1, 2, and 5 years.[22–24] It has also been reported that CHD could have an effect on cerebral arteries in addition to small vessels. When CHD was administered to healthy participants and the hyperventilation-induced CO2 reactivity of the middle cerebral artery was measured, a significant improvement in CO2 reactivity was found 2 to 3 hours after administration.[25] The results suggest that CHD could be utilized not only for small vessel disease, but also for the prevention of large artery stenosis or occlusion. However, the effect of CHD on the prevention of recurrent ischemic stroke has not yet been determined directly for causes such as large-artery atherosclerosis (LAA). Therefore, the need for verification of its preventive effect on strokes of all Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) causative classifications has been raised.

This study aimed to verify the role of CHD as an adjunct preventive treatment for all subtypes of ischemic stroke by observing the recurrence rate of ischemic stroke after 2 years of administration of CHD.

2. Methods

2.1. Participant recruitment

The study protocol and methods were based on our previous studies.[23,24] From January 2017 to December 2020, patients who met the following criteria were recruited from the Stroke and Brain Disease Center of Kyung Hee University Korean Medicine Hospital, Seoul, Korea: 19 years of age or above; diagnosis of cerebral infarction by combining brain imaging (computed tomography [CT] or magnetic resonance imaging) and clinical symptoms; intake of CHD for at least 2 years with documented relevant follow-up results; and concomitant intake of antiplatelet or anticoagulant medication. Cases where medical records were insufficient to determine whether cerebral infarction recurrence occurred, or cases deemed inappropriate for the study analysis, were excluded.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Korean Medicine Hospital (KOMCIRB 2022-11-008, approved on December 2,2022) and the study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol is also registered on the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS, KCT0008052, 2022-12-30).

2.2. Materials

CHD is an 80% ethanol extract (400 mg per capsule) of S Radix, C Rhizoma, P Cortex, G Fructus, and R Rhizoma (Table 1). Extraction of each herbal medicine was conducted with 80% ethanol in boiling water for 2 hours. Herb extracts were filtered and evaporated in a rotary vacuum evaporator and finally lyophilized with a freezing dryer. To standardize the quality of CHD, berberine in C Rhizoma and P Cortex, baicalin in S Radix, geniposide in G Fructus, and sennoside A in R Rhizoma were quantitatively assayed according to previously reported methods.[26]

2.3. Procedures

In the present study, a retrospective observational cohort study design was used. Detailed information about concomitant diseases (previous stroke, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, other cardiac diseases, symptomatic carotid artery stenosis, and postmenopausal hormone therapy), social history (smoking history and family history of stroke), concomitant antiplatelet (aspirin, clopidogrel, cilostazol, ticlopidine, triflusal, etc) and anticoagulant therapy (warfarin, apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, etc), and TOAST classification (LAA, large artery atherosclerosis; cardioembolism, CE; small vessel occlusion, SVO; other determined; undetermined) were obtained from the medical records of all the patients. CHD was administered in a dose of 800 mg (2 capsules) per day and we retrospectively investigated medication compliance, occurrence of adverse effects, and stroke recurrence for a period of 2 years.

The primary outcome was the prevalence of ischemic stroke recurrence during the follow-up period (24 months). The secondary outcome was the occurrence of adverse effects due to CHD. Stroke recurrence was defined as new neurological symptoms (sudden clinical symptoms and loss of focal or global brain function) with corresponding brain imaging results (brain CT or magnetic resonance imaging).

2.4. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel (ver. 15.26). All patient data were expressed as number (%). Continuous data, such as age and body mass index, were summarized as means and standard deviations, and categorical data, such as adverse effects, were presented as presence or absence.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 202 patients with cerebral infarction who met the inclusion criteria of the present study. The mean age was 72.94 ± 10.63 years, and the most common comorbidity was hypertension (69.31%), followed by dyslipidemia (40.10%), and diabetes mellitus (31.19%). According to the TOAST classification, SVO (44.06%) was the most common etiology, followed by LAA (36.14%) and CE (9.90%); other determined etiologies was the least common at 1.98%. All patients were taking antiplatelet or anticoagulant agents based on the pathogenesis of the ischemic stroke, with aspirin as the most common antiplatelet agent (60.40%) and warfarin as the most common anticoagulant (4.46%) (Table 2).

3.2. Recurrence rate of ischemic stroke during the 2 year follow up

During the 2-year follow-up period, 9 patients (4.46%) on CHD with antiplatelets or CHD with anticoagulants experienced ischemic stroke recurrence. Four experienced recurrence within 180 days of follow-up, 3 between 180 and 365 days, and 2 between 365 and 730 days. All these cases had only 1 recurrence. The recurrence rate of ischemic stroke, according to the TOAST causative classification, was lowest with SVO with 1 recurrence (1.12%), followed by CE with 5%, and LAA with 5.48%. The patient with SVO experienced the recurrence on day 648 and the patient with CE had a recurrence on day 150. Of the patients with LAA, 4 had recurrences, 1 within 180 days and 3 between 180 and 365 days. Two patients with Other determined etiology experienced recurrences on days 72 and 103. One patient with Undetermined had a recurrence on day 578 (Table 3, Figure 1). There were no significant adverse effects among the total patients.

4. Discussion

This is the first study to investigate the recurrence rate of all ischemic stroke TOAST causative categories with CHD administration. We demonstrated that the 2-year ischemic stroke recurrence rate of CHD combination therapy was 4.46%. The recurrence rate, according to the TOAST categories, was lowest for SVO at 1.12%, followed by CE at 5%, and LAA at 5.48%. There were no adverse effects due to CHD administration.

Antiplatelet therapy is the current standard therapy for the prevention of recurrent ischemic stroke, with aspirin and clopidogrel being most commonly used.[4] In previous studies, the rate of recurrent ischemic stroke with aspirin ranged from 8.9% to 12.5% at 18 to 32 months,[7,8,10] and with clopidogrel alone or dual therapy with aspirin it was 8.0% at 18 months.[9] Cilostazol, which was developed relatively recently, showed a recurrence rate of 6.1% at 29 months.[10] Since the lowest ischemic stroke recurrence rate was the 4.46% for CHD combination therapy in this study over a similar period (24 months), it indirectly suggests that it has superior performance in the inhibition of ischemic stroke recurrence compared to conventional therapy alone. In addition, there were no significant adverse effects from CHD, which is consistent with previous studies,[22–24] providing further evidence that CHD is safe.

The recurrent ischemic stroke rate, according to the TOAST causative categories, was lowest in the SVO type at 1.12%, followed by CE at 5.0%, and LAA at 5.48%. CHD has been proven to have an excellent recurrence inhibitory effect on SVO-type ischemic stroke, with previous retrospective cohort studies showing recurrence rates of ischemic stroke of 2.0% at 2 years,[23] 2.6% at 3 years, 4.7% at 4 years, and 6.2% at 5 years.[24] The lower SVO-type recurrence rate in our study may be because, unlike previous studies that did not consider whether CHD was combined with antiplatelets or anticoagulants, this study only included patients who received combination therapy with CHD and antiplatelets or anticoagulants.

We suggest that the mechanism of SVO-type ischemic stroke recurrence inhibition in CHD might be due to biochemical effects on microangiopathy, which is closely related to cell cycle progression, hypertension, dyslipidemia, vascular inflammation, and oxidative damage. In support of this suggestion, a previous experimental study has shown that CHD works as an anti-apoptotic agent, a cell cycle progressive agent, and a cell-migration inducing agent.[21] Fas is a major receptor for cell death by apoptosis and shows high blood concentrations in patients with ischemic stroke, with higher levels being associated with higher mortality.[27] This supports the mechanism that anti-apoptosis in CHD inhibits recurrence of ischemic stroke. Other in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that CHD inhibits HMG-CoA reductase and pancreatic lipase,[15] has antioxidant effects by scavenging free radicals, and anti-inflammatory[17] and antihyperlipidemic effects[28] associated with NO production and inhibition of VCAM-1 expression in human endothelial cells.[19] Previous clinical studies have demonstrated its antihypertensive effect by 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring in hypertensive patients with stroke,[20] as well as its serum lipid lowering-effect in patients with hypercholesterolemia.[18]

The recurrence rate of LAA-type ischemic stroke on CHD was higher than that of the SVO-type, but lower than that of conventional antiplatelet therapy alone. A previous clinical study reported that CHD affected the cerebral artery. When hyperventilation-induced CO2 reactivity was measured after CHD intake by normal participants the CO2 reactivity of the middle cerebral artery was significantly enhanced 2 to 3 hours after intake, and cerebral blood flow velocity was also significantly improved 3 hours after taking CHD.[25] It has also been reported that CHD improves arterial stiffness in patients with elevated brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity.[16] Although additional studies are needed to understand the mechanisms, these results provide evidence that the combination of antiplatelet therapy and CHD should be actively considered for its additional benefits in suppressing recurrence of ischemic stroke in patients with LAA-type ischemic stroke.

Since there are no studies on the effectiveness of anticoagulants in preventing recurrent ischemic stroke in CE-type ischemic strokes, it is difficult to compare the CE-type recurrence rate results of this study with existing data. For atrial fibrillation, which accounts for the largest proportion of CE-type, warfarin was associated with a 4% annualized rate of recurrent ischemic stroke in trials with patients with a previous transient ischemic attack or minor ischemic stroke,[29] and novel oral anticoagulants had similar recurrence rates to warfarin.[30] Considering the recurrence rate of 5% over 24 months in this study, it might be inferred that additional ischemic stroke recurrence suppression benefit can be expected when CHD is used in combination with anticoagulants. However, since there were only 20 CE-type participants in this study, the interpretation of this data requires further validation. Furthermore, there has been no study on the heart disease of CHD to date, so evidence to support this is required in the future.

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, this study was not a randomized controlled trial and only analyzed data from patients with CHD combined with antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, so it lacks a control group for comparative analysis. Although we compared the results with historical data on conventional therapy in the discussion, there are limitations due to the demographic differences between the present study and historical data. Second, CHD was administered as a combination therapy in patients who were taking antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications. Therefore, the effectiveness of CHD alone in preventing recurrent ischemic stroke could not be determined. Third, this study design is retrospective, which means that the researchers relied on medical records and data that were collected for other purposes. This might limit the accuracy and completeness of the data, and there may be confounding variables that were not accounted for. Fourth, this study included a relatively small sample size of 202 patients, which may limit the generalizability of the findings and increase the risk of false negative results. Finally, as this was a retrospective cohort study, blinding could not be applied, which may have introduced bias and affected the outcomes of the study. In addition, the retrospective cohort study was not controlled for confounding, which may have affected the results. To compensate for these limitations, based on the results of this study, we are planning to follow up with a blinded, randomized controlled trial to evaluate the clinical efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness of inhibition of recurrent ischemic stroke by CHD. This will provide a basis for safer and more effective ischemic stroke prevention and treatment in the future.

5. Conclusions

The recurrence rate of ischemic stroke with combination conventional therapy and CHD was much lower than that of conventional therapy alone, and there were no adverse effects. The recurrence rates were lower in all TOAST causative categories, with the SVO-type being the lowest, followed by CE, and LAA. We suggest that CHD combination therapy is safer and more effective for the prevention of recurrent ischemic stroke for all TOAST categories. It is necessary to validate this hypothesis through follow-up randomized controlled trials in the future.
