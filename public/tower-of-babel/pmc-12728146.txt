SLC16A3 in lung adenocarcinoma regulates glycolysis and lactate release to facilitate M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages
Authors: Peirui Chen, Qiusha Qing, Hongxia Gao, Zheng Qin, Jing Lv
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSLC16A3 is considered to affect the malignant progression of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), but its mechanism remains elusive. Lactate secretion can facilitate the M2 polarization of macrophages, which are essential components of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME).MethodsBased on the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, differential expression analysis of SLC16A3 in LUAD was undertaken and the Pearson correlation analysis was on SLC16A3 and targets of M2 macrophages. Pathway enrichment analysis on SLC16A3 was achieved by utilizing the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). The expression of SLC16A3 in cells was examined by qPCR and Western blot (WB). The levels of glycolysis marker proteins in cells were tested by WB. The Glucose test kit, lactate test kit, Seahorse energy metabolism analyzer, and pHrodo™ Green AM intracellular indicator reagent kit were applied in assessing cellular glycolysis levels. CCK-8, scratch assay, Transwell assay, and flow cytometry were conducted to evaluate the malignant phenotype and apoptosis level of cancer cells. Flow cytometry and Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) were utilized to assess the polarization of macrophages. Finally, a mouse model of allograft tumors was created, and the effects of SLC16A3 on glycolysis and M2 polarization of macrophages in vivo were evaluated by tracking tumor growth and detecting related protein distribution through Immunohistochemistry.ResultsSLC16A3 was greatly upregulated in LUAD. Knocking down SLC16A3 remarkably repressed the malignant phenotype of LUAD cells and reinforced apoptosis. The results derived from GSEA manifested that SLC16A3 had a higher enrichment in the glycolysis pathway. SLC16A3 positively modulated the extracellular and intracellular levels of lactate and glycolysis. Pearson correlation analysis uncovered a positive linkage between SLC16A3 and M2 macrophage markers. According to the rescue experiment, glycolysis inhibitors were observed to greatly reduce the enhancement in M2 polarization of macrophages caused by overexpression of SLC16A3. The final mouse experiment demonstrated that SLC16A3 boosted tumor growth in vivo and enhanced tumor glycolysis level and M2 macrophage infiltration in the TIME.ConclusionSLC16A3 in LUAD modulates the glycolysis pathway to facilitate M2 polarization of macrophages.

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) has the highest global incidence and mortality rates, with a five-year survival rate of less than 20%. In some high-smoking-rate countries, the mortality rate is still rising [1]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent LC type, responsible for over half of LC cases. Therefore, further elucidating the development mechanisms of LUAD can aid in identifying more potential treatment options and elevating the overall survival rate of LC patients.

Tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) plays a crucial part in tumor development. Macrophages are generally considered to be multifunctional effector cells derived from monocytes. In TIME, macrophages are referred to as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) with complex and dual functions. TAMs can be simply categorized into M1-type and M2-type based on their activation status. M1-type TAMs mainly exert pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor effects, whereas M2-type TAMs reinforce tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune suppression by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β [2]. Enhanced M2 polarization of macrophages in TIME is also widely seen in multiple malignancies. M2 macrophages are generally induced by some cytokines, such as IL-4 or IL-13, and other non-cytokine cell metabolites can also induce macrophage recruitment and M2 polarization, such as lactate [3]. The function of lactate on M2 polarization has garnered much attention, with one project showing that lactate can bind to the key glycolytic kinase PKM2 at the k62 site of macrophages, leading to acetylation of PKM2, potentiating its activity, and facilitating M2 polarization of macrophages [4]. Tumor-derived lactate induces M2 macrophage polarization by activating the ERK/STAT3 signaling pathway in breast cancer [5]. Lactate is a key factor driving M2 macrophage polarization to promote the invasion of endometriotic stromal cells in vitro and in vivo, and Trib1 induces M2 macrophage polarization by activating the ERK/STAT3 signaling pathway [6]. Therefore, the regulation of phenotypic transformation of macrophages has become one of the therapeutic strategies to delay tumor growth.

Due to the unique growth characteristics of tumors, cancer cells often adjust their metabolism to better adapt to the internal environment and growth requirements. The mechanism of altered metabolism of carbohydrates as the main energy source for cells has been extensively studied. Unlike normal somatic cells, cancer cells tend to preferentially obtain energy through glycolysis even under aerobic conditions, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect [7]. As a group of membrane transporters, SLC family proteins have many members implicated in the generation and regulation of glycolysis. For example, the most classic glucose transporter 1, GLUT1, encoded by the SLC2A1 gene, is instrumental for glucose uptake and the maintenance of basic cell metabolism, emerging as one of the reference indicators that can reflect the level of glycolysis [8]. SLC16A3, also known as MCT4, is majorly located on the cell membrane to catalyze the transmembrane transport of lactate and pyruvate and influence the lactate expulsion in hyperglycolytic cells. SLC16A3 is upregulated in LUAD, affects adverse prognosis, reinforces immune suppressive cell and cytokine infiltration in gliomas, and facilitates the shaping of the TIME [9, 10]. The mechanism, however, remains elusive. Although previous studies have reported the regulatory effect of lactic acid on the polarization of macrophages M2 [3], the key upstream regulatory factors of lactic acid excretion in tumor cells and their specific mechanisms in LUAD remain unclear. This study is the first to reveal that SLC16A3 in LUAD promotes lactic acid excretion by enhancing glycolysis, thereby facilitating the M2 polarization of TAMs. It clarifies a new mechanism of the "metabolism-immunology" regulatory axis in LUAD, providing a theoretical basis for targeted SLC16A3-based combined immunotherapy.

Based on bioinformatics analysis, we herein discovered that SLC16A3 was upregulated in LUAD and was linked with glycolysis and M2 macrophage infiltration. Validation through cellular and in vivo experiments revealed that SLC16A3 enhanced glycolysis, boosted M2 polarization of macrophages, and accelerated the malignant progression of LUAD.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human bronchial epithelial cells HBE135-E6E7 (CL-0732), human LC cells (H1975 (CL-0298), H1693 (CL-0876), and H1792 (CL-0910)), mouse LC cells LLC (CL-0140), and human monocytic leukemia cells THP-1 (CL-0233) were bought from Pricella, China. PC-9 (SNL-152) was purchased from Sunncell Biotechnology. HBE135-E6E7 cells were cultivated in HBE135-E6E7-specific medium. LLC cells were kept in the DMEM medium. THP-1, H1975, H1693, H1792, and PC-9 cells were all placed in 1640 medium. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% double antibodies. The cells above were housed in a constant temperature and humidity incubator (Thermo Fisher, USA) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cell culture medium was purchased from Pricella (China), while FBS and the double antibodies were from Gibco (USA). The THP-1 cells were induced with PMA (MCE, USA) before use.

Cell transfection

pcDNA3.1 empty vector (oe-NC), pcDNA3.1-SLC16A3 overexpressed plasmid (oe-SLC16A3), pLKO.1-H-SLC16A3 shRNA interfering plasmid (sh-H-SLC16A3), pLKO.1-Puro empty vector (sh-NC), and pLKO.1-M-SLC16A3 shRNA interference plasmid (sh-M-SLC16A3) were offered by Genepharma (China). Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) and plasmids were diluted with 100 μL of serum-free medium and then were left undisturbed for 30 min in a 1-to-1 ratio. Cells were inoculated into 6-well plates. The lipo2000 and plasmid mixture were introduced drop by drop to the wells when the culture reached 80%. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in an incubator for 24 h. We then changed the medium into a serum-containing medium. The protein expression level was detected after 48 h of culture.

Bioinformatics analysis

mRNA expression data of LUAD were sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (Tumor: 535, Normal: 59). Differential analysis of SLC16A3 mRNA between the normal group and tumor group was undertaken by utilizing the edgeR package (|logFC|> 1.0, FDR < 0.05). The enriched pathway of SLC16A3 was revealed by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). CIBERSORT was employed to dig out the correlation between SLC16A3 and M2 macrophage infiltration. According to Pearson correlation analysis, the correlation between SLC16A3 and macrophage marker expression was unraveled.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

1 mL Trizol (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) was introduced into each well to fully lysate the cells, which were then collected into the centrifuge tube. Chloroform (200 μL) was put into the mixture to isolate the RNA, which was then centrifuged and the supernatant was transferred to isopropanol. The mixture was left undisturbed for 20 min, following the addition of 1 mL of anhydrous ethanol for resuspension. Then, centrifugation was carried out to remove the supernatant. Dissolved in DEPC water, RNA concentration was determined by nanodrop (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and adjusted to 1000 ng/mL. Immediate use of the reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, China) reversely transcribed it into cDNA. With β-actin as the internal reference gene and SYBR Green I (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) as the fluorescent dye, three replicates were prepared for each sample. The fluorescence signal intensity was tested by fluorescence quantitative PCR (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). The relative quantitative analysis of the gene expression results was completed by the 2−ΔΔt method. Western blot (WB)

The total protein lysates were extracted from cells or tissues by utilizing RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China), separated by SDS-PAGE by utilizing the Mini-Protean III system (Bio-Rad, China), and transferred to the PVDF membrane by utilizing the Trans-Blot Turbo transfer system (Bio-Rad, China). The membrane was incubated with the primary antibodies (anti-β-actin (AF5003, Beyotime, China), anti-SLC16A3 (ab308528, abcam, UK), CD163 (ab182422, abcam, UK), anti-GLUT1 (ab115730, abcam, UK), LDHA (ab52488, abcam, ABCAM, China), Anti-SLC16A3 (AB308528, ABCAM, UK), HK2 (ab209847, abcam, UK), and CD206 (ab64693, abcam, UK)) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequent incubation was carried out with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody (ab205718, abcam, UK). With the use of an enhanced chemiluminescence ECL assay kit (Amersham Life Science, USA), we assessed and visualized the target protein. Image acquisition and analysis were completed by utilizing the UVP BioDoc It system (Upland, USA).

Apoptosis detection

The cells of each group were placed on 6-well plates with a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. ANNEXINV-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Yeasen, China) was employed to treat the cells at room temperature for 10 min without light. Subsequently, the apoptosis levels of cells in each group were tested by a flow cytometer (Agilent, China).

CCK-8 assay

Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 2000 cells per well, and cultivated for 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. After incubation, 10% of the original volume of CCK-8 detection reagent (Beyotime, China) was put into each well for cell viability measurement. After 2 h of incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was examined by a microplate reader (Biotek, USA).

Scratch assay

The cells were inoculated in a 6-well plate. When they converged to 90%, the sterile 200 μL pipette tip was applied to scratch the well at the appropriate location. After 24 h, the scratches were observed under a 40 × optical microscope (Leica, German).

Transwell assay

A diluted solution of matrix gel (Solarbio, China) was coated onto the upper surface of the membrane and incubated at 37 °C overnight to form a gel. The cell suspension was put into the chamber. The lower compartment of the chamber was filled with medium enriched with 10% FBS. Once the setup was complete, the chamber was removed, the medium was discarded, and the cells were rinsed twice using PBS. After gently wiping off excess cells on the bottom surface of the small chamber with a cotton swab, we immobilized the cells with ethanol for 30 min and then stained them with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. They were rinsed two more times. Finally, we got the cell number by utilizing a microscope.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The secretion levels of TGFβ (Elabscience, China) and IL-10 (Elabscience, China) in the co-culture system were examined with an ELISA kit. The sample to be tested was put into each well of the ELISA plate and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. The biotinized antibody working solution of 100 μL was put into each well. The ELISA plate was coated with film and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. We added 100 μL of HRP-conjugated working solution to each well, covered the enzyme plate with a sealing film, and incubated it at 37 °C for 30 min. After the reaction was complete, we removed the liquid, rinsed the plate, and introduced 100 μL of substrate to each well for 15 min of room-temperature incubation. Finally, the stop solution was introduced to terminate the reaction and the absorbance of the samples at 450 nm was examined with a microplate reader.

Detection of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)

We took a 96-well plate, plated cells (5 × 103 cells per well) on it, and kept cells at 37 °C until the confluence reached 80–90%. We employed the Seahorse XF glycolysis Stress Test Kit (Agilent, USA) and Seahorse XF Pro Analyzer (Agilent, USA) to test real-time ECAR.

Detection of lactate content in cells

The lactate concentration in cells and cell culture supernatant was determined with the lactate assay kit (Abcam, UK). For the detection of intracellular lactate content, we resuspended cells in 4 times the volume of lactate assay buffer, mixed them well, and centrifuged them at 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was gathered. The supernatant after centrifugation or cell culture supernatant was deproteinized with a deproteinization sample kit (Abcam, UK) to remove lactate dehydrogenase. Then, we took 50 μL of the deproteinized product and put it into 50 μL of the reaction mixture, and mixed them well. The reaction lasted for 30 min at 37 °C. A UV spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA) was employed to test the absorbance at 530 nm.

Detection of intracellular pH

The intracellular pH was determined with the pHrodoTMGreen AM intracellular indicator kit (ThermoFisher, USA) and live cell imaging solution (LCIS). In simple terms, cells were seeded in a 96-well plate. After cultivation, the supernatant was discarded and the cells were rinsed with LCIS. A mixture containing 10 μL pHrodoTMGreen AM and 100 μL PowerLoad™ concentrate was introduced into the well, followed by 30 min of incubation at 37 °C. Cell fluorescence was examined by a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE, Japan) after incubation.

Flow cytometry

The expression level of the M2 macrophage marker on the tumor cell surface was tested by flow cytometry. The cells were diluted to the desired density. Anti-CD206 (E-AB-F1161C, Elabscience, China) or anti-CD68 (12-0681-82, Thermo Fisher, USA) was put into the cell suspension. Samples were analyzed with a flow cytometer (Agilent, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The fresh tissue was soaked in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then removed, cut to a suitable size, and completely soaked in paraffin solution. After the paraffin solidified, the wax block was cut into 4 µm slices, baked, and immobilized on the slide to form a paraffin section. The slices were then dewaxed and rehydrated with xylene and a series of graded alcohols, followed by EDTA antigen retrieval, 10 min of incubation with 3% H2O2 at room temperature after two PBS rinsings, and 30 min of blocking with 5% silk milk after three PBS washing. The section was incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies (anti-CD163 (ab182422, abcam, UK), anti-LDHA (ab52488, abcam, UK), anti-SLC16A3 (ab308528, abcam, UK), and ki-67 (ab15580, abcam, UK)). After rinsing with PBS three times, the secondary antibody was introduced and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min (ab205718, abcam, UK). Then DAB (Solarbio, China) was employed for color development and hematoxylin (Solarbio, China) was for re-staining. Finally, the section was dehydrated, transparent, and sealed with neutral resin. The slide was observed and photographed under an optical microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Animal experiment

Ten 6-week-old C57BL/6 female mice were obtained from Jiangsu Huachuang Sino (China) and raised in a standard environment (12 h light/dark cycle, 55% relative humidity, 23 ± 1 °C) with free access to food and water. The mice were acclimated to the feeding environment for one week and then were randomly sorted into two groups, with five mice in each group. All protocols have been approved by the Animal Ethics Committee. The construction of an allograft tumor mouse model was fulfilled by suspending LLC cells containing empty plasmids or sh-M-SLC16A3 plasmids in a mixture of DMEM and MatriGel (Corning, USA) (1:1 volume ratio) and injecting them subcutaneously into the back flank of the mice. Tumor volume was recorded every 5 days. The formula for calculating tumor volume is Volume = length × width2 × 1/2. All mice were euthanized after 30 days, and tumors were collected and weighed. The levels of SLC16A3, Ki-67, CD163, and LDHA in tumor tissues were assessed by IHC, and the surface markers CD11b (E-AB-F1081F, Elabscience, China) and CD206 (E-AB-F1135E) of M2 macrophages in tumor tissues were assessed by flow cytometry. ELISA was employed to test the levels of cytokine TGF β (Elabscience, China) and IL-10 (Elabscience, China).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were finished with a triplicate setup, with the outcomes displayed in the form of mean ± standard. The data and images in the study were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad, USA). One-way ANOVA was the method for comparing multiple groups, and the Student’s t-test and Wilcoxon were for comparisons between groups (P < 0.05: statistically significant).

We first clarified the expression of SLC16A3 in LUAD. Based on the analysis results of the TCGA database, SLC16A3 exhibited a higher level in the LUAD tissue (Fig. 1A). Validation was completed by undertaking qPCR and WB detection with human bronchial epithelial cells and four LC cell lines, yielding consistent results with bioinformatics analysis. The mRNA and protein expression of SLC16A3 were elevated in the LUAD cell line compared to normal cells (Fig. 1B, C). SLC16A3 in LUAD reinforces cell-malignant phenotype and represses cell apoptosis

To figure out the cellular functions and mechanisms that the abnormal expression of SLC16A3 may affect in LUAD, we then dissected the question of whether SLC16A3 affected the malignant progression of cancer. SLC16A3 was knocked down using the H1975 cell line with a high SLC16A3 level and was overexpressed using the H1693 cell line with a low expression level (Fig. 2A). Tested by CCK8, cell viability was found to be considerably dampened by SLC16A3 knockdown (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, knocking down SLC16A3 remarkably depressed the migration and invasion abilities of LUAD cells (Fig. 2C, D). In addition, SLC16A3 downregulation also greatly elevated the apoptosis rate of LUAD cells (Fig. 2E). The above results represented opposite trends in the overexpressed cell lines. SLC16A3 reinforces the generation of lactate mediated by glycolysis in LUAD

As a monocarboxylate transporter, SLC16A3 plays a major part in lactate release [11]. Through GSEA analysis, we also discovered that SLC16A3 obtained a high enrichment score in the glycolysis pathway (Fig. 3A), suggesting that SLC16A3 may expedite malignant progression through glycolysis in LUAD. To validate this hypothesis, we continued to launch glycolysis-related assays on the SLC16A3-knockdown H1975 cell line. The results showed that after knockdown of SLC16A3, the remaining glucose increased, indicating that the glucose uptake capacity of the cells was significantly reduced (Fig. 3B) indicated a reduced utilization of energy by the cells. The ECAR of cells in each group was analyzed by Seahorse XF Cell Metabolism Analyzer. Knocking down SLC16A3 dramatically downregulated the ECAR of LUAD cells (Fig. 3C). Through the detection of lactate content in the cell supernatant, we discovered that the downregulation of SLC16A3 reduced the release of lactate in the cells, while the intracellular pH declined and the fluorescence became stronger, indicating that the ability of the cells to produce lactate was weakened. The lactate produced by glycolysis resided in the cells (Fig. 3D, E). Additionally, WB detection manifested that the protein levels of key proteins HK2, LDHA, and GLUT1 in glycolysis were downregulated in the SLC16A3 knockdown group (Fig. 3F). SLC16A3 boosts M2 polarization of macrophages by modulating glycolysis

Glycolysis is proven to reinforce the secretion of lactate, and lactate further facilitates M2 macrophage polarization [12, 13]. Through CIBERSORT analysis, SLC16A3 was found to strengthen the infiltration of M2 macrophages (Fig. 4A). In Pearson correlation analysis, SLC16A3 was positively linked with M2 macrophage markers (CSF1R, CD163, MSR1, CLEC7A) (Fig. 4B). To figure out whether SLC16A3 influenced macrophage M2 polarization through glycolysis, we set up a co-culture system of LUAD cells and macrophages using H1693 cells with SLC16A3 overexpressed and M0 macrophages induced by THP-1 cells. We applied the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG in rescue experiments. Overexpression of SLC16A3 led to a decrease in remaining glucose, indicating that SLC16A3 overexpression promoted the uptake of glucose by cells and could be partially restored by 2-DG (Fig. 4C). Additionally, overexpression of SLC16A3 reduced the intracellular content of lactate and increased the release of lactate (Fig. 4D). The increased extracellular content of lactate led to a great rise in ECAR as well (Fig. 4E), which could be restored by the glycolysis inhibitor. Due to the large amount of lactate being excreted from the cells, the intracellular pH was elevated and the fluorescence weakened. After using the glycolysis inhibitor, the further reduction of intracellular lactate led to a further elevation in intracellular pH and a further weakening of fluorescence (Fig. 4D, F). Moreover, the levels of key glycolytic proteins also elevated (Fig. 4G). These results indicated that SLC16A3 facilitated the glycolysis of cells. In Flow cytometry, the overexpression of SLC16A3 was revealed to strengthen the infiltration of CD206-positive macrophages. CD206 was a marker of M2-polarized macrophages. Therefore, SLC16A3 enhanced the M2 polarization of macrophages (Fig. 4H). CD163 is also a marker of M2 macrophages. By WB detection, similar to CD206, the protein expression of CD163 was upregulated (Fig. 4I). TGFβ and IL-10 were anti-inflammatory factors secreted by M2 macrophages. ELISA revealed that overexpression of SLC16A3 markedly elevated the levels of TGFβ and IL-10 in the cell culture supernatant (Fig. 4J). Treatment with 2-DG lowered the level of M2 polarization markers and depressed the secretion of TGFβ and IL-10. In vivo validation of SLC16A3 reinforcing TAMs M2 polarization and tumor progression by altering glycolysis in LUAD

To determine whether SLC16A3 can influence the malignant progression of LUAD in vivo through glycolysis and M2-polarized macrophages, we knocked down the SLC16A3 gene in LLC cells and employed the knockdown cells to construct the allograft tumor model. Mice were continuously monitored for tumor volume changes within 35 days of feeding. As shown in Fig. 5A, knocking down SLC16A3 slowed the growth rate of tumor volume. When the tumors were weighed after 35 days, the tumor weight in the knockdown group was considerably lower than that in the non-knockdown group (Fig. 5B). The content of ki-67, a cell proliferation marker, in the tumor tissues in the knockdown group was also greatly reduced (Fig. 5C), indicating that the down-regulated expression of SLC16A3 hindered tumor growth. Moreover, the glycolytic marker LDHA in tumor tissues also decreased, suggesting that the glycolytic function of tumor cells was also repressed (Fig. 5C). Similarly, the M2 macrophage marker CD163 was reduced. Through flow cytometry, the number of CD206-positive macrophages was observed to be greatly reduced (Fig. 5C-D). As macrophage M2 polarization decreased, the secretion of TGF β and IL-10 also declined (Fig. 5E). Discussion

SLC16A3 was highly upregulated in LUAD and affected the glycolytic pathway and M2 macrophage infiltration. SLC16A3 in LUAD cells was beneficial for maintaining cell viability and proliferation. Mechanistically, SLC16A3 boosted extracellular lactate release in cells, lowering the extracellular pH level. The acidic environment drove macrophages into M2-like macrophages, resulting in strengthened secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines (TGFβ and IL-10). In addition, SLC16A3 in the body also favored tumor growth.

Our results indicated that SLC16A3 enhanced the function of glycolysis in cancer cells, as well as cell proliferation, cell viability, and other cellular phenotypes. Hypoxia is one of the characteristics of the TIME due to the large energy demand generated by rapid growth, and reprogrammed metabolism is an essential means for tumors to cope with this unfavorable factor. In tumor-associated fibroblasts, the hypoxic microenvironment elevates the levels of glycolysis and SLC16A3 expression in CAFs and upregulates glucose consumption in an SLC16A3-dependent manner [14]. In this research, SLC16A3 knockdown repressed the glucose uptake of LUAD cells, and the proliferation and vitality of the cells were greatly weakened, indicating that SLC16A3 also played a pivotal part in the process of cancer cells adapting to the environment and maintaining their vitality. As the oxygen content in the environment declines, the energy production way for cells gradually shifts from oxidizing phosphorylation to glycolysis, which is why cancer cells also tend to favor glycolysis respiration. The activation of SLC16A3 in cancer cells is triggered by the combination of hypoxic inducible factor 1 induced by the hypoxic microenvironment and hypoxic response elements in the SLC16A3 promoter region. Under hypoxic conditions, hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) can directly bind to the hypoxia response element (HRE) in the promoter region of SLC16A3 to activate its transcription [15]. In this research, although the activation degree of SLC16A3 was not directly investigated, according to the assessments of the lactate level in the supernatant and the intracellular pH level, the downregulation of SLC16A3 considerably repressed the lactate release of cells and reduced the ECAR, but also reduced the intracellular pH level. Furthermore, in combination with the rescue experiment, overexpression of SLC16A3 slightly upregulated the intracellular pH level, which was further repressed after treatment with the glycolysis inhibitor. For this phenomenon, we speculated that it may have an association with the protein function of SLC16A3. Chiche et al. [16] asserted that SCL16A3 is a potent pH regulator that helps cells maintain a higher intracellular pH and a lower extracellular pH, creating a great pH gradient that enhances tumor pathogenicity. Combined with our results, we speculated that after SLC16A3 is knocked down, the cellular lactic acid production is turned off, but at this time, the upstream glycolytic rate-limiting enzyme protein of lactate, without direct control of SLC16A3, still produces lactate normally, thus resulting in the accumulation of lactate in the cell and the decrease of pH. Although cancer cells tend to thrive in an acidic environment, excessively low pH can lead to toxicity for cancer cells. Therefore, lowering the intracellular pH prompts cancer cells to make adaptive changes in their metabolism, such as reducing their glycolysis function, including decreasing enzyme and protein expression as well as glucose uptake. However, after overexpression of SLC16A3, a large amount of lactate is excreted, and the content of intracellular lactate is greatly reduced, resulting in a rise in intracellular pH and a decline in extracellular pH. The glycolysis function of cancer cells is enhanced through a large intracellular and extracellular pH gradient. Our speculation coincides with Chiche’s conclusion. However, it should be pointed out that in this study, the explanations of these mechanisms are still theoretical speculations and lack direct experimental verification. This study has only confirmed the regulatory effect of SLC16A3 on intracellular pH for the time being, but its specific molecular mechanism still awaits further investigation.

As reported in the literature and observed in our work, lactate in cells majorly originates from glycolysis. The overactivation of glycolysis triggers a rise in extracellular acidification, which can also result in changes in components in TIME, such as enhanced macrophage M2 polarization [12, 13]. We then investigated whether SLC16A3 could affect macrophage phenotypes through this pathway, finding that overexpression of SLC16A3 considerably upregulated the proportion of M2 macrophages and the levels of related cytokines (TGFβ and IL-10) both in vivo and in vitro, which was restored by the glycolysis inhibitor, indicating that SLC16A3 may influence macrophages to undergo immunosuppressive phenotype transformation through glycolysis. The polarization of macrophages is largely influenced by extracellular pH. In breast cancer, higher levels of glycolysis are adversely linked with lower immune-active cells, such as M1 macrophage infiltration [17]. Exogenous lactate can enter macrophages and suppress their M1 polarization, thus downregulating pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-6 [17]. In LC, multiple genes induce M2 polarization of macrophages by reinforcing extracellular acidification through the efflux of lactate [18, 19]. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this acidification process remain poorly dissected. This investigation brought new insights into the influence of lactate release on macrophages in LUAD. Although this study revealed the mechanism by which SLC16A3 regulates glycolysis and lactate export in LUAD as well as the immune microenvironment, there are still some limitations. For instance, this study has not explored the specific molecular mechanism of SLC16A3 upregulation in LUAD. In the future, we will predict the potential transcription factor binding sites in the SLC16A3 promoter region through the JASPAR database and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) public data analysis. We will further analyze the impact of DNA methyltransferase inhibitors on SLC16A3 expression and explore the role of histone modifications in the transcriptional activation of SLC16A3. We will deeply elucidate the molecular mechanism of SLC16A3 upregulation in LUAD. Additionally, although this study found that high expression of SLC16A3 is associated with a malignant phenotype, it lacks clinical cohort analysis to explore the association between the expression level and patient prognosis or chemotherapy sensitivity. Future research will include the analysis of clinical samples to clarify the correlation between SLC16A3 expression level and the prognosis (DFS/OS) of LUAD patients, and explore its potential as an independent prognostic marker. In the future, we will also focus on the research of SLC16A3-targeted intervention strategies, conduct preclinical evaluation of SLC16A3 inhibitors, test their therapeutic effects in LUAD models, and evaluate their potential as a targeted intervention strategy for drug resistance.

In conclusion, we validated through in vitro and in vivo experiments that the high level of SLC16A3 in LUAD potentiated the glycolysis of cancer cells. Lactate induces macrophage infiltration and M2 polarization by lowering the pH of TIME, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that favors cancer cell viability and tumor growth and leading to malignant progression. This research elucidated the oncogenic mechanism of SLC16A3 in LUAD. We also proposed a hypothesis that SLC16A3, as a cell membrane channel protein, may not directly affect the expression of glycolysis protein, but influence the intracellular pH and affect the function of glycolysis by reinforcing the release of lactate. This hypothesis needs further confirmation. In addition, since lactate can also affect the infiltration of M1 macrophages in TIME, the impact of SLC16A3 on the remaining components in TIME is worth more discussions.
