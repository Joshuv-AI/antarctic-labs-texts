Early hearing improvement predicts complete recovery in sudden hearing loss
Authors: Cheng-Han Wu, Chin-Lung Kuo, Shang-Liang Wu, Wen-Huei Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Early prediction of recovery in sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) remains challenging. Early hearing improvement (EHI) may serve as a simple prognostic marker, but its quantitative relationship with complete recovery (CR) has not been well defined. We investigated the association between EHI and CR and explored whether this relationship differed according to pretreatment hearing severity.Methods:We conducted a retrospective case-control study of adults hospitalized for a first episode of unilateral SSNHL at a tertiary referral center in Taiwan between 2012 and 2022. Patients who achieved CR, defined as a final four-frequency pure-tone average ≤25 dB hearing level (HL), were matched by pretreatment hearing grade to patients without CR. EHI was defined as the change in pure-tone average from baseline to the first follow-up audiogram within 2 weeks. Multivariable linear probability models and logistic regression were used to evaluate the association between EHI and CR.Results:The study included 240 patients (120 with CR and 120 without CR). Younger age and a shorter onset-to-treatment interval were independently associated with a higher probability of CR (age: β per 1-year increase, –0.009; 95% CI, –0.013 to –0.005; p < 0.001 and onset-to-treatment interval: β per 1-day increase, –0.013; 95% CI, –0.018 to –0.009; p < 0.001). Mean EHI was 14.9 ± 19.1 dB and increased with pretreatment hearing grade. Each 1-dB increase in EHI was associated with a 1.5% higher probability of CR (β = 0.015; 95% CI, 0.012-0.017; p < 0.001). The strength of this association progressively decreased as pretreatment hearing severity increased. Findings were consistent in sensitivity analyses using logistic regression.Conclusion:EHI within 2 weeks is a readily obtainable and quantitatively informative predictor of CR in SSNHL. Its prognostic value is greatest in patients with less severe pretreatment hearing loss and diminishes with increasing hearing severity.

1. INTRODUCTION

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) is an otologic emergency with an annual incidence of 5 to 30 cases per 100 000 individuals,1,2 and constitutes a substantial proportion of otolaryngology emergency and outpatient visits. Approximately 32% to 65% of patients experience hearing improvement, with most gains occurring within the first 2 weeks, while others achieve only partial recovery or persistent hearing loss.1 Because SSNHL carries the risk of permanent hearing disability, it imposes considerable physical, psychological, and economic burdens.3–5 Early and precise prognostic prediction is therefore critically needed to ensure that clinicians can design the most effective treatment plan within the optimal therapeutic window.6,7 Accurate prognostic information also benefits patients by providing a clearer understanding of their condition, alleviating anxiety and distress, enhancing compliance, and strengthening physician-patient trust.

Traditionally, prognosis has been estimated from baseline characteristics such as hearing loss severity and audiometric pattern,7–10 patient age, the presence of vertigo or tinnitus, onset-to-treatment delay,6,7 metabolic syndrome,11 imaging findings,12–14 and biological parameters, including biomarkers of inflammation and hemogram.13,15,16 However, many of these approaches lack practical quantifiability for routine prognostic use, are costly, or require resources that may limit timely application during the early disease stage. Recently, machine learning has also been explored as a predictive tool, but its computational opacity and complexity often constrain its clinical applicability.17

Early hearing improvement (EHI), generally defined as measurable hearing gain within the first 1 to 2 weeks after treatment, has emerged as a potentially useful prognostic marker in SSNHL.18–20 Several previous studies have suggested that early hearing changes may be associated with subsequent hearing recovery. However, the clinical application of EHI remains limited by substantial heterogeneity in its definition and interpretation. Previous studies used predefined cutoff values or percentage-based criteria to categorize EHI, which may reduce interpretability and limit standardization across different baseline hearing severities. In addition, the modifying effect of pretreatment hearing severity on the prognostic value of EHI has not been systematically evaluated.

To address these gaps, we hypothesized that EHI exerts a quantifiable, per-decibel effect on the probability of complete recovery (CR), and that this association varies according to pretreatment hearing grades. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of EHI across different pretreatment hearing grades using a continuous per-decibel framework. By providing an interpretable estimate of recovery probability based on early hearing change, this approach may facilitate early prognostic stratification and individualized clinical decision-making in SSNHL.

2. METHODS

The present study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for case-control studies published in 2019 by the UK EQUATOR Center, University of Oxford, Oxford, United Kingdom, in terms of its design and structure.

2.1. Study design and participants

We carried out a retrospective case-control study at the Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taiwan, from February 2012 to June 2022. The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the hospital (IRB: 2022-03-003BC). All study procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

This study identified 1055 patients diagnosed with SSNHL, based on the diagnostic criteria of the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery Foundation (AAO-HNS), which defines SSNHL as a hearing loss of ≥30 dB affecting at least three consecutive frequencies within 72 hours. Inclusion criteria were adults aged ≥20 years experiencing a first episode of unilateral SSNHL. Exclusion criteria included age <20 years, a history of recurrent or chronic hearing loss, incomplete medical records, or loss to follow-up.

Among them, 120 patients who achieved CR were assigned to the CR group. Another 120 patients who did not achieve CR were selected for the non-CR group using frequency matching in a 1:1 ratio based on pretreatment hearing grade through stratified random sampling, thereby creating a frequency-matched 1:1 case-control design. Matching by pretreatment hearing grade was performed to balance baseline hearing severity between groups, as it was considered both a major confounder and a potential effect modifier. This design enabled evaluation of the per-decibel effect of EHI across different baseline severity strata while minimizing confounding by initial hearing severity. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were additionally calculated to assess post-matching balance between groups. Patient selection was conducted using a random number table in Excel to minimize selection bias.

All included patients were hospitalized to receive systemic corticosteroid therapy, with some additionally receiving intratympanic steroid injections or other commonly used adjunctive treatments, the use of which varied between the CR and non-CR groups. Patients were followed for up to 3 months after treatment initiation for outcome assessment. CR was defined as a hearing level (HL) of ≤25 dB HL on the final audiogram within the 3-month follow-up period.

2.2. Audiometry assessment and classification

Pure-tone audiometry hearing test was performed at regular intervals for all patients, including at the initial visit (pretreatment), the first and second weeks, the first and second months, and the third month. Pure-tone thresholds were measured at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz. HL was calculated as the average threshold across these four frequencies.

EHI was defined as the change in pure-tone average from baseline to the first follow-up audiogram obtained within 2 weeks after treatment initiation. The first follow-up audiogram was obtained at a mean of 4.9 ± 2.2 days after treatment initiation.

Pretreatment HLs were categorized using the modified Siegel’s criteria21 (Appendix, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A431). Grades ranged from 2 to 5, with grade 2 defined as 26 to 45 dB HL, grade 3 as 46 to 75 dB HL, grade 4 as 76 to 90 dB HL, and grade 5 as >90 dB HL. Pretreatment hearing grade was considered a potential effect modifier and served as the basis for patient matching and stratified analyses.

Hearing recovery outcomes were classified according to the modified Siegel’s criteria.21 CR, defined as a final HL ≤25 dB HL, was used as the primary outcome measure.

2.3. Data collection

Data were extracted by a dedicated research team according to predefined variables, with two investigators independently cross-checking pure-tone average results and other key parameters. Data consistency and completeness were verified according to predefined rules. The cleaned data were then imported into the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for statistical analysis.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Demographic and clinical variables were summarized using descriptive statistics: categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD, minimum and maximum values. Differences in EHI across pretreatment hearing grades were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). To quantify the effect of EHI on the probability of CR, linear regression analyses were conducted using CR status (0 = non-CR, 1 = CR) as the dependent variable to estimate the probability of CR at the population level. Covariates included in the multivariable analyses were selected based on prior clinical relevance and previously reported prognostic importance in SSNHL. Variables demonstrating clinically meaningful imbalance between groups were additionally considered to reduce residual confounding. The estimated per-decibel effect was interpreted as a population-level average association to facilitate clinical interpretation. Because CR was analyzed as a binary outcome, logistic regression analyses were additionally performed as sensitivity analyses to confirm the robustness of the observed associations identified in the primary linear regression models. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Regression diagnostics were assessed visually using residual distribution and residual-vs-fitted plots. Variance inflation factors were calculated to evaluate multicollinearity among predictors. Partial R2 values were calculated to estimate the proportion of variance in the probability of CR uniquely explained by EHI after adjustment for other covariates.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Baseline characteristics of patients with and without complete recovery

All 240 patients were included, comprising 120 in the CR group and 120 in the non-CR group (Table 1). The two groups were matched based on pretreatment hearing grades, with identical distributions across grades 2 to 5: 28.3%, 36.7%, 29.2%, and 5.8%, respectively. Compared with the non-CR group, patients in the CR group were younger (43.4 ± 11.8 vs 52.6 ± 14.9 years) and had a shorter onset-to-treatment interval (5.2 ± 4.1 vs 16.5 ± 17.2 days). The use of vitamin B6 was more frequent (51.7% vs 26.7%), whereas the use of plasma expander was less common (83.3% vs 91.7%).

3.2. EHI in all patients, stratified by pretreatment hearing grade

Among all 240 patients, the mean EHI was 14.90 ± 19.08 dB (Table 2). When stratified by pretreatment hearing grade, the mean EHI increased with greater hearing loss severity. It was 7.15 ± 12.15 dB in patients with grade 2 hearing loss (26-45 dB HL), 15.56 ± 17.32 dB in grade 3 (46-75 dB HL), 19.60 ± 22.81 dB in grade 4 (76-90 dB HL), and 24.91 ± 24.95 dB in grade 5 (>90 dB HL), indicating that patients with more severe hearing loss at baseline exhibited greater EHI.

3.3. Per-decibel effect of EHI on complete recovery, stratified by pretreatment hearing grade

Linear regression models were used to examine the association between EHI and the probability of CR (Table 3). Four confounding variables—age, onset-to-treatment interval, vitamin B6 use, and plasma expander use—were included in the adjusted model. Pretreatment hearing grade was additionally considered a potential effect modifier and was therefore used for stratified analyses.

In the overall model including all 240 patients, EHI was significantly associated with a higher probability of CR after adjustment. Specifically, for every 1 dB increase in EHI, the probability of CR increased by an average of 1.5 percentage points (β = 0.015; 95% CI, 0.012-0.017; p < 0.001), with a model explanatory power of partial R2 = 0.407.

Stratified analyses by pretreatment hearing grade showed that the positive association between EHI and the probability of CR remained significant in grades 2, 3, and 4, with partial R2 values ranging from 0.437 to 0.536, indicating consistently strong explanatory power across subgroups. Notably, the effect size was highest in grade 2 (β = 0.026; 95% CI, 0.018-0.033) and decreased with higher severity—grade 3 (β = 0.020; 95% CI, 0.015-0.024) and grade 4 (β = 0.012; 95% CI, 0.008-0.016). For grade 5, although the effect was positive (β = 0.010; 95% CI, –0.002 to 0.022), statistical significance was not reached because of the small sample size (n = 14).

Sensitivity analyses using logistic regression demonstrated directionally consistent associations between EHI and CR. In the overall analysis, a 1-dB increase in EHI was associated with higher odds of CR (odds ratio [OR], 1.138; 95% CI, 1.092-1.184; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A431). Similar associations were observed in the grade 2 to 4 subgroup analyses. However, estimates for grade 5 were not estimable because of sparse outcome data and model instability.

4. DISCUSSION

4.1. Main findings and clinical implications

This study demonstrates that EHI exerts a quantifiable per-decibel effect on the probability of CR in patients with SSNHL. Importantly, the prognostic contribution of EHI varied across pretreatment hearing grades, confirming baseline severity as an effect modifier. By framing EHI on a per-decibel scale, we provide clinicians with a transparent, interpretable, and clinically practical prognostic tool. This approach enables individualized counseling and facilitates early therapeutic decision-making within the critical treatment window, thereby strengthening both prognostic precision and physician-patient communication. In clinical practice, limited early improvement despite treatment may identify patients who require closer monitoring, more cautious prognostic counseling, or consideration of additional salvage therapies, depending on clinical context.

4.2. Comparison with prior literature on EHI

The prognostic role of EHI has been recognized in several prior studies, yet its definition and clinical application remain inconsistent. Ito et al.18 reported that improvement rates exceeding 50% within 1 to 2 weeks were associated with favorable long-term outcomes in patients with mild hearing loss, whereas lower improvement rates indicated a poorer prognosis. Ogawa et al.22 demonstrated a cure rate of 68% in patients achieving >20 dB hearing improvement within the first treatment week. Similarly, Shimanuki et al.19 identified a ≥10 dB hearing gain during days 6 to 7 as a predictor of better prognosis. Sofer et al.20 further showed that >30% EHI was associated with substantial or CR in over 80% of cases, while patients with <10% improvement rarely recovered. Collectively, these studies emphasize the prognostic value of EHI while highlighting several areas for refinement. First, the hearing outcomes were often categorized into broad recovery groups, which may be less intuitive for clinical use. Second, the operational definitions of EHI varied widely, ranging from absolute decibel gains to percentage improvements, limiting standardization. Third, baseline hearing severity was not systematically considered, despite its likely influence on both the magnitude of EHI and its prognostic interpretation; more severe initial hearing loss likely requires larger early improvement to predict recovery meaningfully.

Our study builds upon and extends prior work in several ways. We selected CR as the clinical endpoint, establishing a clear, clinically meaningful target. We also introduced the concept of a per-decibel effect, quantifying the incremental prognostic value of each decibel of EHI. Finally, by using a stratified case-control design based on pretreatment hearing grade, we explicitly examined baseline severity as an effect modifier. We estimated distinct per-decibel effects across different strata. This methodological approach was intended to reduce confounding related to baseline hearing severity and enhance the practical utility of EHI as a standardized, stratified prognostic indicator.

4.3. Potential biological mechanisms and clinical significance

The clinical relevance of EHI may be further understood through underlying biological mechanisms.1,23,24 Early auditory recovery likely reflects rapid resolution of reversible pathophysiological processes, including cochlear microcirculatory compromise, metabolic stress, or transient viral or inflammatory processes. In contrast, delayed or absent improvement may indicate more extensive or irreversible cochlear injury, such as hair cell or spiral ganglion neuron degeneration. This perspective provides a mechanistic rationale for the observed association between EHI and prognosis, offering clinicians insight into the trajectory of cochlear recovery. Future studies integrating EHI with imaging or biomarker data may further clarify these mechanisms and improve individualized therapeutic strategies.

4.4. Comparison with prognostic tools beyond EHI

Beyond EHI, previous prognostic approaches in SSNHL have included clinical characteristics,6–10,25 laboratory biomarkers,13,15,26 imaging findings,12,14 and machine learning models.17 Although these methods provide valuable insights, their clinical adoption is often constrained by cost, accessibility, delayed availability, or limited interpretability. Inflammatory markers may lack standardized thresholds and assay consistency, reducing generalizability, while imaging studies, although informative for etiological evaluation, are costly and not feasible for routine use. Machine learning models can achieve high predictive accuracy but often have limited interpretability, offering abstract risk estimates rather than directly applicable clinical guidance.

By contrast, an EHI-based framework offers several practical advantages. It relies solely on routine audiometric testing, requires no additional procedures beyond standard care, and avoids dependence on advanced laboratory or imaging modalities. Expressing EHI on a per-decibel scale further improves interpretability and facilitates stratified prognostic assessment for clinical decision-making and patient counseling.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings, and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded despite multivariable adjustment. Although matching was performed according to pretreatment hearing grade, imbalance in other baseline clinical variables may still have remained. Second, the estimated per-decibel effect of EHI should be interpreted as a population-level average association rather than a deterministic prediction for an individual patient. Because the linear probability model directly estimates absolute percentage-point changes in recovery probability, it was retained as the primary analysis for clinical interpretability despite the known limitations of probability modeling using linear regression. In addition, the linear probability model assumes an approximately linear relationship across the observed EHI range, which may be less stable in patients with profound baseline hearing loss or extreme EHI values. In particular, estimates in grade 5 were statistically unstable because of the limited sample size and sparse outcome distribution; therefore, the applicability of the observed association in this subgroup remains uncertain. Third, this study included only hospitalized patients receiving systemic corticosteroid therapy, which may represent a more selected population with closer monitoring and more standardized treatment exposure than patients managed in outpatient settings. Therefore, the applicability of the present findings to patients treated in ambulatory settings, with delayed treatment initiation, or with alternative therapeutic regimens should be interpreted cautiously.

Further validation in larger, multicenter, and more diverse populations is warranted. Future studies may evaluate whether early individualized estimation of recovery probability could support prognostic counseling and shared clinical decision-making in SSNHL. In addition, normalized or ratio-based EHI metrics may provide further insight into relative recovery potential across different baseline hearing severity levels.

In conclusion, EHI is a strong, quantifiable predictor of CR in patients with SSNHL. Our stratified analysis demonstrates that the per-decibel effect of EHI diminishes as baseline hearing severity increases, highlighting pretreatment hearing grade as a clinically meaningful effect modifier. These findings identify EHI as a robust prognostic marker and provide clinicians with a transparent, interpretable tool for early treatment decisions and patient counseling, facilitating individualized care and optimizing hearing outcomes.
