To guide or not to guide percutaneous dilational tracheostomy? broncoscope, ultrasound or nothing?
Authors: Filippo Sanfilippo, Luigi La Via, Cristina Santonocito, Mateusz Zawadka
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Nachshon et al. [1] showed that routine bronchoscopic guidance during percutaneous dilational tracheostomy (PDT) does not reduce major complications or mortality, while adding procedural time, complexity, and costs. However, the RCT focused only on short-term outcomes of PDT, and several considerations leave three dilemmas opened.

Bronchoscopy: the benefit may depend on who is operating

The PDT in the RCT by Nachshon et al. [1] was performed by experienced operators, hence its findings should not be generalized. A recent meta-analysis [2] suggested a signal of benefits for bronchoscopy guidance during PDT mostly in less-experienced operators, in patients with difficult or distorted anatomy, and in small-volume centers. A further point that should be considered regarding bronchoscopy is its value in withdrawing the endotracheal tube (ETT) under direct vision with the purpose to minimize the risk of ETT cuff damage during needle puncture. Bronchoscopy is more precise than direct laryngoscopy, especially when visualization of vocal cord is suboptimal. In the event of cuff damage and loss of airway seal during PDT, ETT should be replaced (eventually prolonging the procedure) and the event might cause immediate (desaturation) and delayed complications (aspiration, pneumonia). Nachshon et al. [1] did not report this aspect, which remains unaddressed.

Ultrasound as a complementary monitoring tool

Pre-procedural scanning with ultrasound screens for pre-tracheal vessels and defines a safe puncture window. Intra-procedural ultrasound guidance improves its accuracy, reducing midline deviation and increasing first-pass success compared to landmark technique [3]. Ultrasound-guided PDT has shown non-inferiority to bronchoscopy-guided PDT [4], but in general these two modalities are complementary rather than competing. Ultrasound secures the extraluminal approach and midline, whereas addition of bronchoscopy reduce the risk of ETT damage and confirms each step until checking the final cannula position.

Off-midline puncture and late complications: an unexplored question

Although strong evidence is lacking, it is reasonable to suspect that stomal complications (i.e. dehiscence) might increase when tracheostomy entrance is off- midline. Dehiscence is difficult to treat as intensive care unit (ICU) patients have multiple factors delaying wound healing [5], and tracheostomy traction (i.e. during mobilization for hygiene) may further impair it. With off-midline puncture, dilation forces act obliquely rather than perpendicularly on the tracheal wall predisposing to greater damage on the stoma. It is possible that damage on the stoma might contribute to late stenosis, yet no study has directly tested such relationship. Demonstrating this would be difficult: symptomatic post-tracheostomy stenosis is uncommon [6], and any true effect size is likely modest and difficult to study. Nevertheless, without bronchoscopy and/or ultrasound guidance, the operator cannot confirm whether puncture is truly central [7]. In the RCT by Nachshon et al. aspects as the entrance of the tracheostomy cannula and the long-term complications such as stoma dehiscence were not reported.

In summary, we applaud the authors and agree that multicentre RCTs are needed. We advocate that such trials consider the above aspects among their outcomes. Rather than a universal recommendation for or against bronchoscopy during PDT, we think that a multimodal, risk-stratified strategy including bronchoscopy and/or ultrasound is needed at least in selected patients (difficult anatomy, obesity, cervical immobilization) and for less-experienced proceduralists/centres, while accepting that experienced operators working in favourable anatomy may safely require neither.
