Association between the digestion-resistant bioactive peptide content in dairy products and the risk of sarcopenia in Iranian elderly: a case-control study
Authors: Fatemeh Keshavarz, Seyyed Mohammad Alavi, Sazin Yarmand, Amirhossein Nazarian, Marzieh Mahmoodi, Zainab Shateri, Nasrin Nasimi, Mehran Nouri, Mohammad Hossein Dabbaghmanesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia is a generalized and progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, which is associated with various adverse health outcomes, such as fractures, impaired mobility, and increased mortality. Bioactive peptides (BPs) are absorbable protein fragments that remain bioaccessible after digestion. In the present study, we investigated whether the intake of BPs derived from dairy products could reduce the risk of sarcopenia by analyzing the dietary habits of elderly individuals with sarcopenia.MethodsThis case-control study used data collected from a population-based cross-sectional study conducted at healthcare centers in Shiraz, Iran. A total of 80 individuals diagnosed with sarcopenia and 80 control subjects without sarcopenia were selected. Sarcopenia was defined according to the diagnostic criteria established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia. Dietary intake over the previous year was assessed using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire consisting of 168 items. The amount of digestion-resistant BPs in dairy products was estimated by multiplying the grams of dairy consumed by the peptide content present in these products. The association between the intake of digestion-resistant BPs from dairy products and the risk of sarcopenia was evaluated using logistic regression analysis.ResultsIn the unadjusted analysis, participants with higher total peptide consumption had markedly reduced odds of sarcopenia compared with those in the lower-intake reference group (odds ratio [OR ]= 0.163, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.082–0.323, P < 0.001). After adjusting for potential confounders, the inverse association between total peptide intake and the odds of sarcopenia remained statistically significant. Individuals with greater total peptide intake showed substantially lower odds of sarcopenia than those in the low-intake reference group (OR = 0.336, 95% CI: 0.149–0.897, P = 0.028).ConclusionsIn summary, our findings suggest that BPs derived from dairy products are inversely associated with the risk of sarcopenia. Given the cross-sectional and retrospective design, causality cannot be established, and further prospective or interventional studies are needed to confirm these associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01206-5.Keywords: Dairy products, Bioactive peptides, Digestion, Muscle mass, Sarcopenia

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a generalized and progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, which is associated with various adverse health outcomes such as fractures, impaired mobility, and increased mortality [1]. It is estimated to affect 10–16% of the elderly population worldwide [1]. Although few studies have investigated the prevalence of sarcopenia in Iran, existing data suggest a relatively high prevalence, ranging from 16.5% to 32.5% [2]. While sarcopenia commonly occurs with aging, certain conditions—including cancer [3], liver disease [4], diabetes [5], and kidney dysfunction [6]—can increase its risk. Additionally, lifestyle factors such as physical activity, smoking, sleep quality, and nutritional status have been linked to the development of sarcopenia [7, 8].

Research indicates that improving nutritional status can positively impact both the prevention and treatment of sarcopenia [9]. A healthier diet can reduce the risk of declines in muscle strength and physical performance while also enhancing muscle mass [9]. Several dietary factors—such as adequate intake of protein, antioxidants, vitamin D, and omega-3 fatty acids—have been demonstrated to exert beneficial effects against sarcopenia [10, 11].

Dairy foods, as a major component of the diet, provide high-quality protein and several nutrients essential for older adults [12–14]. Although considerable controversy exists in studies concerning the effects of dairy products on various diseases—mainly due to the diversity of dairy types and their varying impacts—research suggests that dairy consumption among the elderly may enhance both physical and mental health [15]. Proteins derived from dairy products could positively influence physical performance and muscle mass, thereby reducing the risk of sarcopenia [12].

Milk and dairy products, with their rich protein content, remain a vital source of beneficial peptides [16]. Bioactive peptides (BPs) are absorbable protein fragments that remain bioaccessible after digestion. Previous studies have highlighted the anti-diabetic [17], anti-hypertensive [18], antimicrobial [19], and antioxidant effects of native BPs [20]. These peptides can influence various physiological processes and are increasingly recognized for their potential to promote health and prevent disease [21].

In the current study, we investigated whether the intake of dairy product-derived BPs could reduce the risk of sarcopenia by assessing the dietary habits of elderly individuals with sarcopenia. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has addressed this issue, which prompted us to conduct this research to evaluate the association between dairy-derived BPs and sarcopenia. Methods

Study population

This case-control study was conducted using data obtained from a population-based cross-sectional study carried out between August 2017 and February 2018. Among 501 eligible participants, all 80 individuals diagnosed with sarcopenia were included as the case group. An equal number of controls (n = 80) were randomly selected from participants without sarcopenia [22].

Participant selection was guided by specific inclusion and exclusion criteria: individuals aged 65 years or older who lived independently and were capable of walking were included. In contrast, individuals were excluded if they had limb prostheses or a history of major cardiac, pulmonary, musculoskeletal, or neoplastic conditions, uncontrolled endocrine or metabolic disorders (e.g., type II diabetes or thyroid-related diseases), stroke, or severe neurological disorders (such as Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s disease). Participants who failed to complete the questionnaires or declined to participate were also excluded.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.SCHEANUT.REC.1402.121). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Some aspects of this study have been previously published [23, 24].

Baseline information

During enrollment, participants were interviewed to collect demographic and clinical information, including sex, age, educational background, income, smoking history, medical history, and medication use. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), a validated tool in previous studies [25].

Participants’ weight and height were measured using a digital scale with a precision of 100 g and a non-elastic tape measure, ensuring that they wore light clothing and no shoes during the measurement. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of the height in meters.

Diagnostic measures for sarcopenia

According to the diagnostic criteria established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), sarcopenia is defined by the presence of: (1) low muscle mass, (2) low muscle strength, and (3) low physical function. The AWGS guidelines classify older adults who exhibit a combination of these conditions as having sarcopenia [26].

Body composition was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) with the InBody S10 analyzer (BioSpace Co., Ltd. in South Korea). The skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) was calculated by dividing appendicular skeletal muscle mass (kilograms) by height squared (meters squared). Low muscle mass was defined as an SMI of less than 7 kg/m² for men and less than 5.7 kg/m² for women [22, 26].

Muscle strength was evaluated by measuring handgrip strength (HGS) in a seated position with the elbows flexed at 90 degrees. Each participant performed three maximal grip attempts using a hand dynamometer (model MSD, Sihan, Korea), with 15-second intervals between trials. A maximum HGS of less than 18 kg for females and less than 26 kg for males indicated low muscle strength [22, 26].

Physical performance was assessed via gait speed (GS), measured over a four-meter walk without assistance. GS was calculated as distance (meters) divided by time (seconds), and a value of less than 0.8 m/s was considered indicative of low physical function [22, 26].

All measurements were conducted in the morning. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual dietary and physical activity routines and to avoid intense exercise on the day prior to the evaluation.

Dietary assessment

A semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) comprising 168 items—validated and shown to be reliable in the Iranian population—was used to assess dietary intake over the previous year [27]. During face-to-face interviews, participants reported the frequency of consumption for various food items on a daily, weekly, monthly, or annual basis. These frequencies were then converted into average daily intake (in grams) using standardized household measures [28].

To enhance accuracy in portion size, interviewers used standardized food models and photographs of portion sizes. Daily nutrient intake was analyzed using a modified version of Nutritionist IV software adapted for the Iranian diet, allowing for the estimation of individual energy and macronutrient intake.

Digestion-resistant peptides

The calculation of BPs in dairy products was performed by multiplying the amount of dairy consumed (in grams) by the peptide concentrations reported for these products. Peptide content data were obtained from a previous in-silico study [16].

Briefly, the enzyme action tool available on the BIOPEP web server was used to stimulate the proteolytic activity on dairy proteins [29]. In-silico enzymatic digestion was carried out using chymotrypsin A, trypsin, and pepsin. Target proteins included α-lactalbumin, β-lactoglobulin, αS1-casein, αS2-casein, κ-casein, and β-casein. The resulting peptide fragments were analyzed using the BIOPEP database to identify bioactive sequences.

The sequences were subsequently classified based on their biological functions—such as anti-diabetic, immunomodulatory, anti-hypertensive, and antioxidant properties—as well as by total peptide count, hydrophobicity (high or low), and peptide length (e.g., dipeptides, tripeptides, tetrapeptides, and longer fragments) [30].

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between cases and controls using the independent samples T-test. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied, with results reported as median (25th−75th percentiles).

Categorical variables were summarized as frequency (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test. Associations between BP intake and the risk of sarcopenia were examined using logistic regression analysis. Both unadjusted (crude) and adjusted models were used, with the adjusted model controlling for potential confounders, including age (in years), energy intake (kcal/day), and protein intake (g/day).

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this case-control study, we compared the dietary intake of individuals with sarcopenia to that of a control group and found a significant inverse association between higher intake of total dairy-derived BPs and the risk of sarcopenia in the elderly. This association remained significant after adjusting for potential confounding factors.

Research on the association between BPs and various medical conditions is an emerging field that has attracted increasing attention in recent years [31–33]. However, no prior studies have specifically examined the relationship between the digestion-resistant and BP content of dairy products and the risk of sarcopenia. Given that dairy products are a rich source of protein—an essential nutrient for muscle maintenance [34]—their role in the development of sarcopenia has been evaluated in several investigations.

For instance, a randomized controlled trial by Nasimi et al. demonstrated that daily consumption of yogurt fortified with beta-hydroxy beta-methyl butyrate (HMB), a leucine-derived bioactive metabolite, over 12 weeks significantly improved sarcopenia-related outcomes in older adults. While yogurt consumption increased muscle mass in both the intervention and control groups, participants who consumed the fortified yogurt experienced greater improvements in muscle strength and functional performance [35].

Furthermore, a systematic review study by Cuesta-Triana et al. assessing the effects of dairy consumption on sarcopenia, frailty, and cognitive decline in the elderly found that regular intake of dairy products was associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia and frailty. One notable finding from the review was that the daily inclusion of 210 g of ricotta cheese—an amount exceeding the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for protein—helped prevent muscle loss and lowered the risk of sarcopenia in older adults [15].

Moreover, several randomized controlled trials investigating protein or amino acid supplementation have reported inconsistencies regarding their effects on muscle strength and mass, which may be attributed to variations in baseline protein intake or differences in study populations [36–38]. For example, a two-year randomized clinical trial conducted among women aged 70–80 found no significant changes in muscle mass, muscle area, or physical function between those who consumed a high-protein dairy drink (containing 30 g of whey protein) and those in the control group [39].

A key distinction between that study and the present research is that it employed only whey protein supplementation—a rapidly absorbed protein—administered to participants who were already meeting their daily protein requirements. This suggests that protein supplementation may have limited benefits for individuals who are not protein deficient.

The beneficial effects of milk and dairy products on muscle strength, muscle performance, and the prevention of sarcopenia are likely attributable to their rich nutritional profile and bioactive components [36, 40]. Milk provides high-quality protein—comprising approximately 20% whey and 80% casein—alongside a variety of bioactive components [41]. Whey proteins are particularly notable for their high concentration of essential amino acids (EAAs), which are crucial for stimulating muscle protein synthesis (MPS) in skeletal muscles [42]. Among these, leucine plays a central role as the most potent EAA for activating MPS, primarily through the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway [43, 44].

In addition to their protein content, the bioactive components found in dairy products have demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antihypertensive properties, which may contribute to the development of sarcopenia [45]. Age-related oxidative stress impairs cellular homeostasis and damages vital cellular biomolecules, thereby promoting the onset of sarcopenia. The antioxidant potential of dairy products can enhance dietary antioxidant capacity by neutralizing reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) in myofibers [45].

Furthermore, casein-derived BPs may mitigate age-related muscle inflammation by modulating cytokine activity. Specifically, these peptides have been shown to reduce levels of key pro-inflammatory markers, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [41, 46]. Possible biological explanations for this association include the anabolic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties of dairy-derived peptides reported in previous studies [45, 47, 48]. Peptides rich in branched-chain amino acids, such as leucine, have been shown to activate the mTOR signaling pathway and stimulate muscle protein synthesis in experimental and animal models [43, 44, 46, 47].

From a clinical perspective, the observed inverse association between dairy-derived BPs and sarcopenia may suggest potential dietary strategies for maintaining muscle health in older adults. Aging is accompanied by anabolic resistance, impaired muscle protein synthesis, and chronic low-grade inflammation—all of which contribute to sarcopenia progression. According to previous studies, leucine-rich proteins and peptides can stimulate muscle protein synthesis via the mTOR pathway and may exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [46, 48, 49]. These mechanisms could, in theory, explain the beneficial association observed in our study. However, it is important to emphasize that our study did not measure any molecular or mechanistic pathways (e.g., mTOR signaling, inflammatory biomarkers), and therefore these explanations should be considered speculative and based on existing literature.

This study, using a novel measurement approach, offers valuable insights into the potential role of dietary BPs in relation to various morbidities. One of the strengths of our research is the adjustment for several potential confounders. However, it is important to acknowledge that our models adjusted for age, energy, and protein intake but did not account for other potential confounders such as socioeconomic status, chronic diseases, inflammatory biomarkers, or physical activity. These omissions may introduce residual confounding, and future studies should include a broader range of variables to improve accuracy and validity.

While our method is significantly more time- and cost-efficient than traditional in vitro and in vivo approaches, several limitations should be considered. First, recall bias in self-reported dietary intake may have influenced the results; however, we attempted to minimize this bias by using standardized food models and portion size images during dietary assessments. We did not collect data on the types of dairy products consumed (e.g., fermented vs. non-fermented) or specific brands, which could influence the variability of peptide profiles. Moreover, we lacked information on the source of dairy products (e.g., industrial vs. local), which may affect peptide composition [16]. Second, the case-control design of our study limits the ability to infer causality, as the temporal relationship between exposure and outcome cannot be firmly established.

Another limitation is the relatively small sample size, which may have reduced the statistical power of the study. In addition, recruitment from a single city (Shiraz, Iran) limits the generalizability of the findings. Although the AWGS diagnostic criteria—originally developed for East and Southeast Asian populations—have been applied in several Iranian studies [50], their full validation in the Iranian population remains limited and requires further research.

The classification of identified peptides as anti-diabetic, antioxidant, or otherwise bioactive was primarily based on in silico prediction tools. While these methods are useful for initial screening, their predictive capacity is not absolute and may not reliably reflect actual biological activity in vivo. As noted by Mooney et al. [51], computational bioactivity predictions can suffer from low precision and should be interpreted with caution. Future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings indicate that BPs derived from dairy products are inversely associated with the risk of sarcopenia in the elderly population. This association suggests that these novel compounds may serve as complementary strategies for the prevention of sarcopenia, highlighting the potential benefits of incorporating dairy-derived proteins into the diets of older adults.

Promoting a diet rich in dairy products could play a key role in mitigating muscle loss and preventing sarcopenia in this vulnerable demographic. However, multiple statistical comparisons increase the risk of Type I errors; therefore, our findings should be considered exploratory. Although the results are exploratory, the inverse association observed for total dairy-derived BPs supports their potential role in maintaining muscle health. Given the cross-sectional nature of dietary assessment and the exploratory scope of the peptide analysis, our findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger sample sizes and predefined hypotheses are warranted to confirm these results.
