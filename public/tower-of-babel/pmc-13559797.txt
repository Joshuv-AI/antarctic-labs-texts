Current Helicobacter pylori Diagnostics: Established Methods, Resistance Testing, and Emerging Approaches
Authors: Tianhua Tang, Erwin A Paz, Kan Yu, Khui Hung Lee, Chin Yen Tay, Jingjing Wang, Barry James Marshall, Linli Hu, Wanting Fu, Ziyao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsAccurate diagnosis of Helicobacter pylori infection is required to detect active infection, confirm eradication, assess gastric pathology when indicated, and obtain antimicrobial resistance information that may guide treatment. This review evaluates the clinical roles, limitations, and implementation maturity of established, molecular, and emerging diagnostic approaches.Methods and ResultsA narrative review was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar, prioritizing clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, diagnostic accuracy studies, multicenter validation studies, and directly relevant method‐development research. Histology, rapid urease testing, and culture remain established biopsy‐based methods, whereas urea breath testing and validated monoclonal stool antigen testing are the leading non‐invasive options for detecting active infection and confirming eradication. Serology is useful only in selected settings because it cannot reliably distinguish current from previous infection. Culture provides phenotypic susceptibility results but is technically demanding. qPCR and targeted PCR provide faster, target‐specific genotypic information, while next‐generation sequencing may broaden resistance‐variant detection in specialist or surveillance settings. Genotypic findings require antibiotic‐specific interpretation because genotype–phenotype concordance varies. AI‐assisted endoscopic interpretation, biosensors, LAMP, CRISPR‐based assays, and string test‐based sampling may address specific workflow gaps, but most remain adjunctive, selectively implemented, early translational, or investigational.ConclusionDiagnostic selection should begin with the clinical question and integrate patient risk, treatment history, medication exposure, local resistance patterns, specimen access, laboratory capacity, turnaround time, and available resources. Wider adoption of newer technologies requires external validation, standardization, quality assurance, regulatory readiness, economic assessment, and evidence that results improve clinical management.Keywords: bacterial, diagnostic techniques and procedures, drug resistance, Helicobacter pylori , microbial sensitivity tests, molecular diagnostic techniques

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori causes chronic gastric infection and is implicated in peptic ulcer disease, gastric mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma, and gastric cancer. Its recognition reshaped the understanding of gastroduodenal disease and made reliable detection central to prevention and treatment [1, 2, 3, 4]. Diagnosis now serves several distinct purposes. These include documenting active infection, determining whether eradication has succeeded, assessing gastric pathology when endoscopy is indicated, and obtaining antimicrobial‐resistance information when it could alter treatment [5, 6].

Established invasive and non‐invasive tests answer these questions with different degrees of directness. Endoscopy permits biopsy‐based histology, rapid urease testing, culture, and molecular analysis when the procedure is otherwise clinically indicated. The urea breath test (UBT) and stool antigen test (SAT) are well‐established non‐invasive tests for active infection and post‐eradication confirmation. Serology detects the host antibody response and therefore cannot reliably distinguish current infection from previous exposure [7, 8, 9, 10]. Test choice consequently depends on the clinical question, medication exposure, specimen availability, patient acceptability, and local expertise rather than on analytical sensitivity alone.

The consequences of a wrong or mistimed result also differ by context. A false‐negative result may defer necessary eradication, while an unconfirmed positive result may expose a patient to avoidable antibiotics. After treatment, a test that detects persistent antibodies cannot answer whether viable infection remains. During endoscopy, failure to sample both infection and relevant mucosal pathology can leave separate clinical questions unresolved. Diagnostic pathways must therefore define the intended decision before a specimen is collected.

Antimicrobial resistance has added a second diagnostic dimension. Most conventional tests establish whether infection is present, but they do not indicate whether a proposed antibiotic is likely to remain active. Culture can recover viable organisms for phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing, although transport, growth requirements, and specialist laboratory capacity restrict access. Molecular assays can provide faster genotypic information from several specimen types, but predefined panels detect only the mutations they contain. Their clinical value therefore depends on antibiotic‐specific genotype–phenotype relationships and on whether the result changes regimen selection [5, 6, 11].

Specimen acquisition connects these diagnostic and therapeutic aims. A technically capable assay has little value if suitable gastric or fecal material cannot be obtained, preserved, and delivered within the required workflow. Emerging approaches seek to simplify imaging, sensing, amplification, or gastric sampling, but most remain adjunctive, selectively implemented, or investigational. This narrative review follows the pathway from detection of active infection through specimen acquisition and resistance testing to clinical selection and implementation. It balances established methods with molecular and emerging approaches, without presenting any single technology as universally preferable. Literature Search Strategy

This article was prepared as a narrative review. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar were searched from database inception to 12 July 2026. Search terms combined “Helicobacter pylori ” with terms related to established diagnostics, including “endoscopy,” “histology,” “rapid urease test,” “culture,” “urea breath test,” “stool antigen test,” and “serology.” Resistance‐related terms included “antimicrobial resistance,” “susceptibility testing,” “PCR,” “qPCR,” “targeted PCR,” and “next‐generation sequencing.” Emerging‐technology terms included “biosensor,” “immunosensor,” “LAMP,” “CRISPR,” “artificial intelligence,” and “string test.” International guidelines and consensus statements, systematic reviews and meta‐analyses, diagnostic‐accuracy studies, clinically relevant multicenter validation studies, and directly relevant method‐development studies were prioritized. Duplicate reports, conference abstracts without sufficient methodological or outcome data, non‐diagnostic studies, and method papers without direct relevance to H. pylori diagnosis were excluded. Reference lists of key guidelines and reviews were searched manually. Because this was a narrative review, no formal risk‐of‐bias assessment or meta‐analysis was undertaken.

Evidence was appraised in relation to specimen type, reference standard, intended clinical role, validation setting, and where reported, its capacity to inform treatment.

Established Diagnostic Methods in Current Clinical Practice

Endoscopy and Biopsy‐Based Methods

Endoscopy and Image‐Enhanced Endoscopy

When endoscopy is otherwise clinically indicated, it provides an important opportunity for biopsy‐based H. pylori testing and concurrent assessment of gastric pathology. Conventional white‐light examination may show diffuse redness, swelling, nodularity, enlarged folds, atrophy, or other patterns associated with gastritis. These findings are neither specific nor sufficiently reproducible to confirm infection on their own [12, 13]. Image‐enhanced methods, including narrow‐band imaging, linked color imaging, and blue‐laser imaging, may improve mucosal characterization and help target biopsies. Their contribution remains dependent on equipment, operator experience, and the population examined. Endoscopic appearance should therefore guide sampling and risk assessment, not replace microbiological or histological confirmation. Histology

Histology provides direct visual evidence of organisms in gastric biopsy tissue and simultaneously characterizes the host mucosal response. Routine hematoxylin and eosin and Giemsa stains can demonstrate bacteria and gastritis. Immunohistochemistry may improve recognition when organisms are sparse or morphology is equivocal, while fluorescence in situ hybridization can offer organism‐specific detection in selected laboratories [14, 15, 16]. The same specimens permit assessment of atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and malignancy, which is a major advantage when gastric risk stratification is part of the indication for endoscopy.

Histological yield depends on biopsy distribution, bacterial density, tissue processing, staining, and reader expertise. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), antibiotics, and bismuth can reduce or redistribute organisms. Advanced atrophy, active bleeding, patchy colonization, and inadequate sampling can also produce false‐negative results. A negative report should therefore be interpreted alongside biopsy sites, medication exposure, and the clinical indication. Immunohistochemical characterization of virulence determinants can be undertaken in research or selected pathological settings, but it is not equivalent to demonstrating active infection or antimicrobial susceptibility [17].

Rapid Urease Test

The rapid urease test (RUT) detects urease activity in gastric biopsy material. Hydrolysis of urea generates ammonia, producing a measurable change in pH. The method is simple, rapid, and generally highly specific, so a positive result is useful when sampling and reading are appropriate [18]. A negative result does not exclude infection. Low bacterial density, recent PPI, antibiotic, or bismuth exposure, gastrointestinal bleeding, biopsy location, and premature or delayed reading can reduce sensitivity. Sampling both the antrum and corpus may lessen false‐negative results when clinically appropriate. RUT is consequently a valuable established biopsy‐based test, but it should be combined with histology or another method when the pre‐test probability remains substantial despite a negative result.

Concordant results across complementary biopsy sites and methods generally provide a more secure basis for interpretation than an isolated negative result.

Culture and Phenotypic Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

Culture confirms viable H. pylori and creates an isolate suitable for phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Isolates recovered by culture can subsequently be characterized for phenotypic susceptibility, biochemical properties, virulence determinants, and strain‐level features [19, 20]. This information is particularly relevant after treatment failure, in complex refractory infection, or when local resistance surveillance is needed. Phenotypic testing also retains value when molecular mechanisms are incomplete or when a panel does not cover the relevant resistance determinant.

Successful recovery is technically demanding. Biopsies require appropriate transport medium, timely processing, selective culture media, a microaerophilic atmosphere, controlled incubation, and experienced staff. Recent antimicrobial or acid‐suppressive therapy, bleeding, low biomass, and delays in handling may reduce recovery. Culture also has a longer turnaround time than most direct tests, and results must arrive early enough to influence prescribing. These constraints explain why culture is selectively implemented rather than universally available. Its diagnostic strength is the recovery of viable organisms and phenotypic susceptibility data, not convenience.

Non‐Invasive Tests for Active Infection

Urea Breath Test

UBT is one of the best‐established non‐invasive tests for active infection. After ingestion of 13C‐ or 14C‐labeled urea, bacterial urease generates labeled carbon dioxide that is measured in exhaled breath. Analysis may use isotope‐ratio mass spectrometry, infrared spectroscopy, or another validated platform [21, 22]. The non‐radioactive 13C format is suitable for broad use, whereas availability and regulation can restrict 14C testing in selected settings [23, 24].

UBT is appropriate for initial diagnosis and for post‐eradication confirmation when preparation requirements are met. Testing for cure should occur after an adequate interval from treatment and after guideline‐directed withholding of PPIs, potassium‐competitive acid blockers, antibiotics, and bismuth [5, 6]. Incorrect washout, incomplete ingestion, poorly timed breath collection, or an unsuitable analytical threshold can yield misleading results. The method therefore combines strong clinical validation with a pre‐analytical workflow that must be followed. It does not provide resistance information and cannot assess gastric mucosal pathology.

Stool Antigen Test

SAT detects H. pylori antigen in feces and can document active infection without endoscopy or breath sampling. Validated monoclonal assays generally provide more consistent performance than older polyclonal tests, although accuracy remains product‐ and setting‐specific [25, 26, 27]. Laboratory enzyme immunoassays and rapid immunochromatographic formats differ in instrumentation, readout, and quality‐control requirements. Newer chemiluminescent and bioluminescent formats illustrate continued analytical development, but each requires clinical validation for its intended population and use [28, 29, 30].

SAT can be used for initial diagnosis and as a test of cure after appropriate medication washout and timing. Stool collection is convenient for some patients but unacceptable or difficult for others. Watery feces, delayed processing, unsuitable storage temperature, and heterogeneous antigen distribution may impair recovery. Recent PPIs, antibiotics, or bismuth can cause false‐negative results. The effect of upper gastrointestinal bleeding appears assay‐ and setting‐dependent rather than uniformly adverse [31]. Clinical services should therefore specify the validated assay, collection container, transport conditions, and reporting threshold.

Serology and Virulence‐Marker Assays

Serology‐based assays include enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays, latex agglutination, immunoblotting, chemiluminescent antibody platforms, and multiplex serology. They detect a host antibody response to H. pylori antigens rather than the organism itself [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. Serology may be accessible when bacterial density has been suppressed and may support selected epidemiological or screening applications. Its central limitation is antibody persistence after spontaneous loss or successful eradication. A positive result cannot reliably distinguish active infection from previous exposure, and a falling titer is not a dependable test of cure. Positive serology therefore requires confirmation with a test of active infection when the result will prompt treatment [5, 6].

Virulence‐marker serology, including anti‐CagA assays, can describe population exposure and support research on disease associations. However, the distribution and predictive meaning of these antibodies vary across regions and strain populations [38, 39, 40]. Virulence‐marker results should not independently determine eradication therapy or replace clinical risk assessment [41]. Stool‐antigen chemiluminescent assays belong with SAT because they detect bacterial antigen in feces; they should not be grouped with serum antibody tests.

Molecular Diagnostics for Resistance‐Guided Management

qPCR and Nested PCR

qPCR can detect H. pylori DNA while simultaneously interrogating selected resistance‐associated mutations. Assays most often target 23S ribosomal RNA substitutions associated with clarithromycin resistance and gyrA variants associated with fluoroquinolone resistance. They can be applied to gastric biopsies and, after suitable validation, to stool, gastric fluid, or string‐derived material [42, 43]. Faster access to a defined resistance result may be clinically useful when it changes the planned regimen. However, qPCR provides target‐specific genotypic information rather than a complete susceptibility profile.

Performance begins with the specimen and extraction process. Low bacterial biomass, abundant host DNA, inhibitors in feces or gastric material, inefficient lysis, target polymorphism, and poor primer binding can cause false‐negative or indeterminate results. Contamination can cause false positivity, while amplification of DNA from non‐viable organisms can complicate interpretation after therapy. qPCR should therefore not be presented as a universal substitute for culture. The methods answer related but different questions: molecular testing detects included genotypes, whereas culture permits phenotypic testing of recovered viable organisms.

Nested PCR uses sequential primer sets and may improve amplification from low‐copy or degraded DNA. It has been explored for H. pylori detection when conventional amplification is difficult [44, 45]. The additional amplification step also increases contamination risk and workflow complexity. Primer design must account for genomic diversity, and each assay requires specimen‐specific controls, analytical thresholds, and external validation.

Targeted Commercial PCR Assays

Commercial or near‐patient PCR platforms can integrate extraction, amplification, and interpretation for predefined targets. Evaluated examples include stool‐based clarithromycin‐resistance testing, a compact Smart Gene workflow, and assays using intragastric fluid [43, 46, 47]. Such systems may shorten hands‐on time and make resistance testing available outside reference sequencing laboratories. Their apparent simplicity does not remove the need to define eligible patients, specimen collection, medication effects, invalid‐result handling, and the action attached to each mutation report.

Coverage remains assay‐specific. A clarithromycin panel cannot be assumed to predict susceptibility to other antibiotics, and local variants may fall outside probe design. Commercial availability, regulatory status, instrument maintenance, reagent continuity, and cost also vary. Targeted assays are best described as selectively implemented tools for validated targets and specimen types. Their value should be judged by whether timely results lead to an appropriate change in therapy.

Next‐Generation Sequencing

Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) can identify a broader range of resistance‐associated variants than targeted PCR. Targeted panels, whole‐genome approaches, and metagenomic strategies can also provide strain‐level and epidemiological information [48, 49, 50, 51]. These capabilities suit specialist centers, surveillance programs, and selected treatment‐refractory cases in which wider profiling is justified. They do not make NGS a routine first‐line diagnostic test.

Sequencing is vulnerable to low biomass, host‐DNA interference, library bias, inadequate depth, contamination, and uneven locus coverage. Turnaround time includes specimen preparation, sequencing, bioinformatics, review, and reporting. Variant calls still require curated resistance catalogs and clinically meaningful thresholds. Detection of DNA does not establish bacterial viability. NGS can therefore broaden genotypic observation, but its incremental value over targeted PCR or culture must be demonstrated for each clinical use.

Genotype–Phenotype Discordance and Interpretive Limitations

Genotypic resistance should be interpreted separately for each antibiotic. In a whole‐genome sequencing study with paired phenotypic testing, clarithromycin and levofloxacin genotypes showed high concordance with their corresponding phenotypes. Relationships for metronidazole and amoxicillin were more complex, reflecting multiple mechanisms, uncertain contributions of individual variants, and challenges in phenotypic classification [52]. These findings support focused molecular testing for well‐characterized targets, but they do not justify a uniform genotype‐to‐susceptibility inference across all drugs.

Predefined PCR panels cannot detect mutations that were not incorporated during assay design. Mixed infection and heteroresistance can produce both wild‐type and resistant signals, whose treatment implications depend on analytical limits and the regimen under consideration. Sequencing may reveal minority variants, but their clinical threshold is not automatically defined. Low bacterial biomass, abundant host DNA, and non‐viable bacterial DNA further separate an analytical signal from a current, treatable phenotype [48, 49].

Reports should therefore state the organism target, specimen type, resistance locus, variants covered, detection threshold, and interpretive limits. A “mutation not detected” result means that the included mutations were not found; it does not prove phenotypic susceptibility. Bioinformatics pipelines and resistance catalogs require version control, quality assurance, and external evaluation. Where discordance would materially alter treatment, phenotypic testing or an alternative validated method may remain necessary.

Laboratories should also define how mixed signals, low‐level variants, and failed quality metrics are reported. An indeterminate category is preferable to an unsupported susceptible interpretation.

Emerging and Adjunctive Approaches

AI‐Assisted Endoscopic Interpretation

AI‐assisted endoscopic interpretation is an emerging adjunct to conventional and image‐enhanced endoscopy rather than an established stand‐alone diagnostic method. Models have been trained to classify images according to H. pylori status or gastritis patterns, with encouraging results in selected datasets [53, 54]. Potential clinical roles include reducing observer variation, highlighting suspicious mucosal regions, and supporting biopsy targeting when endoscopy is already indicated.

Performance can shift with image quality, processor and endoscope type, mucosal preparation, prevalence, previous eradication, and patient population. Retrospective image collections may contain selection effects or labels derived from imperfect reference standards. Wider use requires prospective evaluation, multicenter external validation, clear reference standards, monitoring for model drift, and integration that does not disrupt examination. AI output should not replace histological or microbiological confirmation, particularly when treatment or gastric‐risk decisions depend on the result.

Biosensors and Immunosensors

Biosensors convert recognition of bacterial antigens, host antibodies, DNA, or related targets into optical, electrochemical, or microfluidic signals. Their appeal lies in small sample volumes, compact readouts, and the possibility of rapid testing. The maturity of the evidence varies markedly between platforms. An optical fiber aptasensor and several electrochemical designs illustrate analytical proof of concept, whereas a photonic label‐free system has been assessed with human gastric and stool samples [55, 56, 57, 58]. Digital microfluidics has also been used for rapid multi‐target genotyping, showing how fluid handling and molecular detection might be integrated [59].

Low limits of detection under controlled conditions do not establish clinical superiority. Matrix effects, non‐specific binding, surface stability, calibration, reagent aging, sample preparation, and lot‐to‐lot reproducibility may change performance with real specimens. Human‐sample validation is distinct from prospective clinical workflow validation, and neither ensures commercial standardization. Wider implementation requires defined reference methods, external and multicenter testing, manufacturing quality systems, regulatory assessment, and evidence that the result changes care. Biosensors remain investigational or early translational for most routine H. pylori applications.

The intended measurand also matters. A sensor for antibody, antigen, bacterial DNA, or a resistance‐associated sequence answers a different clinical question, even when all platforms are described as rapid point‐of‐care tests.

LAMP and CRISPR‐Based Assays

Loop‐mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) amplifies DNA without conventional thermocycling and may simplify instrument requirements. H. pylori ‐specific assays have been evaluated using biopsy and stool specimens, including a hydrogel format designed to reduce extraction steps [60, 61]. CRISPR‐associated detection can add sequence recognition and a visual or instrument‐based readout. Direct CRISPR assays and one‐pot LAMP–CRISPR workflows have entered early methodological and clinical evaluation [62, 63].

Simpler heating does not make the entire workflow simple. Nucleic‐acid release, inhibitor removal, closed‐tube contamination control, target selection, result interpretation, and waste handling remain necessary. Multiplex resistance testing is harder than detecting a single organism target, and an assay can miss variants outside its design. One‐pot formats may reduce handling but require careful optimization to avoid incompatibility between amplification and detection reactions. Before routine use, these systems need multicenter validation, stable reagents, reproducible manufacturing, quality‐control materials, cost assessment, and regulatory review. They may be useful in selected settings after validation, but evidence remains insufficient for broad substitution of established tests.

String Test as a Minimally Invasive Sampling Strategy

The string test is a gastric sampling strategy, not an analytical assay. A swallowed capsule releases a string that contacts gastric contents and mucus; the retrieved material can then undergo culture or molecular analysis. Feasibility studies established collection and transport procedures, and later work paired string‐derived material with qPCR for detection of infection and selected clarithromycin‐ and levofloxacin‐associated mutations [64, 65, 66, 67, 68].

This approach may be useful for selected patients without an independent indication for endoscopy after guideline‐based clinical risk assessment. Relevant scenarios include persistent infection after treatment, resistance‐oriented testing, and surveillance where gastric material is desired but endoscopy is not otherwise indicated. It may also support decentralized collection when validated preservation and transport are available. The string test does not assess mucosal pathology, and it should not be presented as a replacement for endoscopy when alarm features or gastric‐risk assessment require direct examination and biopsy.

Implementation depends on tolerance, collection consistency, contact with heterogeneous mucus, oral contamination, transport time, and nucleic‐acid preservation. The diagnostic output is determined by the downstream assay, including its extraction efficiency and resistance‐target coverage. Comparative studies must establish performance against appropriate reference methods and define invalid or low‐biomass results. Patient acceptability, staff training, cost‐effectiveness, and the effect on treatment outcomes also require evaluation before broader adoption.

Clinical Selection of Diagnostic Strategies

Selection of an H. pylori diagnostic strategy should begin with the clinical question rather than the availability of a particular technology. In patients without an independent indication for endoscopy, UBT or a validated monoclonal SAT is appropriate for documenting active infection. The same methods can confirm eradication when guideline‐directed timing and medication washout are followed. Serology may retain a role in selected epidemiological or screening settings, but it cannot reliably distinguish active infection from previous exposure and should not establish eradication [5, 6, 10].

The initial and post‐treatment pathways should be kept distinct in ordering systems and reports. A test requested too soon after therapy can be falsely reassuring, whereas failure to arrange post‐eradication confirmation can leave persistent infection unrecognized. In children and adolescents, test selection should follow age‐specific guidance [69]. When UBT and SAT are both feasible in adults, selection may also reflect pregnancy, radiation policy, stool acceptability, local assay validation, and access to breath‐analysis equipment [5, 6, 70]. When endoscopy is clinically indicated because of alarm features, ulcer complications, or the need for mucosal risk assessment, biopsy‐based tests can be combined. Histology assesses infection and gastric pathology, while RUT can provide a rapid complementary result. Culture or molecular resistance testing may be added after previous eradication failure, when empirical treatment is unreliable because of local resistance, or when susceptibility information is expected to alter management. Endoscopic appearance and AI‐assisted interpretation may support biopsy targeting, but neither substitutes for microbiological or histological confirmation [14, 18, 19].

Resistance‐oriented testing becomes more relevant in persistent or treatment‐refractory infection. Culture provides phenotypic susceptibility information but depends on recovery of viable organisms. Targeted PCR can provide faster results for selected resistance‐associated mutations, provided that assay coverage is explicit. NGS may be considered in specialist or surveillance settings when broader profiling is justified. Its interpretation must account for antibiotic‐specific genotype–phenotype concordance, sequencing depth, host‐DNA interference, and detection of DNA from non‐viable organisms [42, 43, 48, 52].

Resources should influence, but not replace, clinical indication. UBT and SAT remain pragmatic non‐invasive options in many settings. Culture, qPCR, and sequencing require progressively different combinations of specimen access, laboratory infrastructure, and interpretive expertise. Simplified molecular platforms and string‐derived sampling may provide additional value where analytical and clinical validation match the intended workflow. Emerging technologies should be adopted only when they demonstrate incremental benefit over established tests in practice. Relevant outcomes include treatment selection, timely reporting, patient acceptability, cost, antibiotic exposure, and eradication.

Practical Barriers and Evidence Gaps

Analytical performance is necessary but does not establish clinical utility. A diagnostic pathway starts before the assay, with medication washout, patient preparation, specimen choice, collection, labeling, transport, storage, and accession. Failure at any pre‐analytical stage can outweigh a platform's laboratory accuracy. The result must also return in time to influence prescribing. Delayed resistance information may arrive after empirical treatment has started, while a rapid result without a clear interpretation may add complexity rather than value [5, 6, 8].

Implementation costs extend beyond the price of a test cartridge. Endoscopy requires facilities and staff; culture needs transport systems, selective media, controlled incubation, and experienced microbiologists. Molecular platforms require equipment, extraction reagents, controls, maintenance, software, and trained operators. Sequencing adds library preparation, bioinformatics, data governance, and specialist reporting. Economic evaluations should include repeat sampling, invalid results, downstream procedures, altered prescriptions, eradication outcomes, and avoided antibiotic exposure. Evidence for these broader effects remains limited for many emerging methods.

Quality assurance must cover the complete workflow. Internal controls, external quality assessment, interlaboratory reproducibility, proficiency testing, and documented acceptance thresholds are needed for culture and molecular assays. AI systems additionally require device compatibility, prospective monitoring, and management of model drift. Biosensors and isothermal platforms require manufacturing consistency and stable control materials. Regulatory approval does not replace local verification, and locally successful validation does not guarantee transferability to another population or specimen type. Reports must be interoperable with clinical systems and state limitations in language that prescribers can use [71, 72, 73, 74].

Local resistance prevalence, treatment history, gastric cancer risk, and patient preferences should shape adoption. Tertiary centers may support endoscopy, culture, qPCR, and NGS, whereas lower‐resource services may prioritize validated UBT, SAT, or referral pathways. Patient acceptability may differ for stool collection, breath testing, endoscopy, or a string device. These differences make clinical implementation context‐dependent rather than a simple hierarchy of technologies.

Referral and transport networks can sometimes deliver more reliable access than installing a complex platform with insufficient throughput, maintenance, or quality oversight.

Evidence gaps are substantial [71, 73, 74]. Many emerging studies are single‐center, small, retrospective, or designed primarily to show technical feasibility. Direct head‐to‐head comparisons with UBT, SAT, histology, RUT, or culture are uncommon. Studies often report diagnostic accuracy without testing whether results change therapy, improve eradication, or reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure. Multicenter external validation, prospective implementation studies, external quality assessment, and economic evaluation are therefore priorities. New methods may be useful in selected settings, but they require further validation against a clearly defined clinical need. Conclusion

Current H. pylori diagnostics should be viewed as a coordinated pathway rather than a contest between old and new technologies. UBT and validated monoclonal SAT remain leading non‐invasive tests for active infection and eradication confirmation. When endoscopy is independently indicated, histology and RUT provide complementary biopsy‐based evidence, while culture can add phenotypic susceptibility information in selected settings. Molecular assays contribute faster, target‐specific resistance data, but their interpretation must remain antibiotic‐specific and attentive to specimen quality, panel coverage, heteroresistance, and non‐viable DNA.

AI‐assisted endoscopy, biosensors, LAMP, CRISPR‐based assays, and string test‐derived workflows may address defined gaps in imaging, amplification, or sample access. Most still require prospective multicenter validation, standardization, quality assurance, economic assessment, and regulatory readiness. The most appropriate strategy begins with the clinical question, patient risk, treatment history, local resistance, and available resources. A new test should be adopted in routine care when it supplies reliable information in time to change management and offers demonstrable benefit over an established pathway.
