Pervasive convergent evolution of sperm conjugation across the Arthropoda tree of life
Authors: R. Antonio Gomez, Romano Dallai, David Mercati, Rita Sinka, Steve Dorus, Scott Pitnick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Theory suggests that ejaculates should evolve heightened functionality through integration of their component parts: spermatozoa, seminal fluid, and ejaculate structures. Here, we exhaustively review the vast literature on sperm ultrastructure in arthropods to examine sperm conjugation - a form of social cooperation among sperm - and its relationship with an ostensible antecedent: membrane-bound, extracellular sperm-associated material (SAM). Our reconstructions suggest that sperm conjugation first arose on the branch leading to insects and remipedes during the Cambrian or Ordovician Periods (i.e., 452.6 – 508.5 million years ago). Since then, arthropods and related ecdysozoans have spent an estimated two-thirds of their time with sperm conjugation, which has been evolutionarily lost and gained approximately 45 times each. We show that most evolutionary derivations of conjugation occurred following the origin of SAM, with SAM subsequent diversifying to facilitate the evolution of multiple conjugate types. Finally, comparative analyses of proteomes of the convergently-derived spermatostyles (specialized rods of SAM to which sperm attach) of true bugs and beetles indicate parallel utilization of a common genetic toolkit that may draw upon deep homology for the evolution of complex ejaculate adaptations. Our analyses reveal an ancient, pervasive and dynamic history of evolutionary experimentation with ejaculate form and function.

Introduction

The overarching goal of evolutionary biology is to explain patterns of biodiversity over time and phylogenetic space, as well as the processes giving rise to them. Myriad investigations of diverse somatic structures (e.g., tissues, organs, limbs, sensory structures, coloration) have contributed to our understanding of the origin of novel traits, trait diversification, and the propensity for parallelism and convergence1–4. The soma further serves as a vehicle for the expression of extended phenotypic traits, in which genes exert effects outside of the organism by modifying the environment or influencing other individuals5–7. These extended phenotypes can include a diverse array of adaptive traits, with many examples being the products of exocrine glands that function as molecular signals and/or form secretory structures7,8. Extended phenotypes that have likely contributed to the diversification of animal lineages include shells and other products of biomineralization9, mucus10, silk11,12, pheromones13, venom, toxins and other chemical deterrents14. For example, silk (i.e., semi-crystalline, extruded protein structures) has 23 estimated independent origins spanning spiders, mites, crustaceans, myriapods and insects11. Silk is credited with the adaptive radiation of spiders; despite employing a relatively consistent set of silk production glands, spiders produce a remarkable variety of silk types12. Similarly, chemical innovation of defensive secretions is believed to underlie the adaptive radiation of the largest metazoan family, the staphylinid beetles14. Given their central role in reproduction, it is surprising that reproductive secretions (e.g., seminal and ovarian fluid) have not been similarly examined to discern their evolutionary history and putative role in animal diversification (but see ref. 15).

The germline, which consists of the lineage leading from primordial germ cells to gametes, is distinct from the soma, and gametes have their own extended phenotypes. For animals with external fertilization, the extended phenotype of eggs is ovarian fluid, which attracts sperm and influences their behavior16–18 (for internal fertilizers, see ref. 19). For taxa with internal fertilization, the extended phenotypes of spermatozoa are comprised by seminal fluid, which is a complex cocktail of proteins, carbohydrates, lipids and inorganic compounds20,21. Seminal fluid supports the transfer of sperm during insemination and the stability, survival, metabolism and motility of sperm in the female reproductive tract (FRT). Seminal fluid proteins also mediate both competition with the ejaculates of other males and multifarious post-mating responses of females, including sexual receptivity, egg laying, immune function, feeding and sleep20–23. Because of this integrated functionality, seminal fluid is putatively subject to intense natural and sexual selection, including sexual conflict21,24. This selective landscape explains why both sperm and seminal fluid proteins are notorious for evolving rapidly24–27.

In addition to seminal fluid composition, sperm quantity, and intrinsic sperm quality (e.g., morphometry, metabolism, swimming speed), ejaculates can vary along two other axes. First, ejaculates of innumerable species include secretory structures, such as mating plugs28, spermatophores29, spermatostyles30 and/or small inclusions such as strands, rods, granules and vesicles31. Second, all components of the ejaculate (i.e., sperm, ejaculate structures and seminal fluid constituents) can interact with one another in complex ways to manifest emergent properties. In D. melanogaster, for example, eight seminal fluid proteins are known to bind to sperm and hence be transported into the females’ sperm-storage organs22,32. At least one of these proteins, “Sex Peptide,” is then released from sperm by proteolytic cleavage so that it can bind to its female receptor33 and, in turn, induce long-term post-mating changes in female behavior and physiology21,22.

Even higher-order adaptive integration of an ejaculate’s component parts is evinced by the phenomenon of sperm conjugation, in which sperm physically bind to one another, along with other male-secreted material, for the apparent social facilitation of motility through the FRT34–36. Sperm conjugation has been suggested to be taxonomically widespread, yet an evolutionarily rare occurrence36. Here, we exhaustively examine the vast descriptive literature on sperm ultrastructure to resolve the evolutionary history of sperm conjugation across the phylum Arthropoda. We additionally investigate a structural constituent of ejaculates that facilitates the evolutionary origin of sperm conjugation and the dramatic diversification in the size and form of conjugates. Our analyses reveal that sperm conjugation has a deep and dynamic evolutionary history. We use comparative proteomic analysis of convergently derived sperm conjugation structural elements to show that a common genetic “toolkit”37 may underlie the dramatic variation in ejaculate form observed across the Arthropoda tree of life.

Results

Evolution of sperm conjugation across the Arthropoda tree of life

Mapping sperm ultrastructural data for 621 Arthropoda and outgroup Ecdysozoa species (Supplementary Data 1) onto our time-calibrated phylogeny (Fig. 1; Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Data 3) revealed a taxonomically widespread distribution of sperm conjugation. Our analysis indicates that sperm conjugation occurs in all extant arthropod subphyla38 (1 of 2 classes of Chelicerata, 1 of 4 classes of Myriapoda, 4 of 11 classes of “Crustacea,” and in the Hexapoda) and in more than one-third of sampled orders (34 of 88, or 38.63%).Fig. 1Sperm conjugation and sperm associated material (SAM) have a deep and evolutionarily dynamic history across the Arthropoda tree of life.Ultrametric fan phylogeny of 621 species of arthropods and outgroups with presence/absence data for sperm conjugation and SAM plotted at the tips. We next examined the evolutionary history of conjugation by reconstructing ancestral character states and estimating transition parameters while treating conjugation as a binary trait (i.e., present or absent). Stochastic character mapping determined that unconjugated sperm was the ancestral state and present in the backbone of the Arthropoda with high posterior probability (pp > 0.99; Supplementary Fig. 1). Nevertheless, sperm conjugation exhibits a dynamic evolutionary history, having been independently gained from unconjugated sperm throughout the phylum 34 − 63 times (95% Highest Posterior Density (HPD) = 39 − 56 times). An informative measure of the evolutionary prevalence of a trait is the amount of time spent in a particular character state, which can be estimated by stochastic simulations of exact character histories39,40 (e.g., relative time spent per mapping can be calculated as the length of all branches with conjugation divided by the sum of all branch lengths in the entire tree of arthropods and outgroup species). Despite unconjugated sperm being the likely ancestral state, conjugation was estimated to have been present across approximately two-thirds of the evolutionary history of all sampled arthropod and outgroup species (64.5– 68.9% of the time conjugated (95% HPD); Supplementary Fig. 1; see Supplementary Data 3 and Supplementary Data 4 for the phylogeny and ancestral state estimates of binary conjugation data, respectively). This striking prevalence of conjugation is primarily attributable to its propensity for independent derivation rather than to its evolutionary persistence. Although there are clades for which conjugation is common (e.g., Odonata, Hymenoptera, adephagan beetles, auchenorrhynch true bugs, and spiders) or nearly universal (e.g., Lepidoptera, Isopoda, and Scorpiones), the number of estimated evolutionary losses of conjugation (29 − 77 times; 95% HPD = 34 − 54 times) is roughly equivalent to the number of gains.

Evolutionary origins and diversification of conjugation types

Across arthropod taxa, sperm conjugates exhibit dramatic variation in size (i.e., number of sperm per conjugate) and form, which are likely to have functional consequences for sperm motility, sperm storage and other interactions between sperm and the FRT25,36,41–44 (Supplementary Table 1). In light of the numerous independent origins of conjugation (Fig. 1), we reconstructed ancestral states and examined transitions across the arthropod phylogeny while treating conjugation as a trait with multiple discrete states (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 2–4). We refined the structure-function organizational framework of Higginson and Pitnick36 and classified all 621 sampled species with conjugation as exhibiting one of five discrete types (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3a–l; Supplementary Data 2): (1) aggregates (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3d–f), (2) pairs (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3a, g), (3) rouleaux (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3k, l), (4) spermatostyles (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c), and (5) ensheathed (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3h–j; see Supplementary Table 1 for descriptions and phylogenetic distributions of each conjugate type). All forms of conjugation require extracellular material to bind sperm together36,45. Structural variation in sperm-associated material (SAM) and its configuration with sperm accounts for most of the variation in conjugate form (Supplementary Fig. 3a–l). In aggregates, SAM typically appears as flocculent material forming a plug or cap into which sperm heads are embedded46,47 (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3d–f). With pairs and rouleaux, SAM forms a thin matrix between conjoined sperm heads (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3a, g, 3k–i; e.g., ref. 48,), which can co-occur with other specializations such as the precisely aligned rows of intramembranous particles found in the attachment point of some pairs49,50. In the case of spermatostyles, SAM forms slender hyaline rods (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c) to which sperm heads attach, and these can persist in the FRT after sperm dissociation30,44,51. Aggregates appear to be the least specialized form of conjugation as judged by the lack of either accessory conjugation structures (i.e., spermatostyles and sheaths) or apparent head shape adaptations for conjugation (as typically observed in pairs and rouleaux). Moreover, aggregate conjugates frequently exhibit the greatest within-male and within-species variation in conjugate size. All studied arthropods with conjugation possess SAM, presumably because it is required to bind together multiple sperm, and we predicted that the evolution of more specialized conjugate forms was achieved by remodeling of SAM and sperm in ancestral aggregates. We coupled ancestral state reconstruction with multistate character modeling to explore the evolutionary relationships among conjugation states while testing the related predictions that (1) aggregates will be the most common type of conjugation, (2) most independent origins of conjugation from unconjugated sperm will be of the aggregate type, and (3) most derivations of other, more structurally complex conjugate types will first pass through the aggregate conjugation state.

Our analyses reinforced the conclusion that the ancestral sperm of all arthropods, and ancestors of the Chelicerata, Myriapoda and Pancrustacea were unconjugated (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, the ancestor of the Hexapoda likely had conjugated sperm in the form of aggregates (pp = 55.9 by 508.5 mybp in the parental node of both hexapods and remipede crustaceans and pp = 93.6 by 452.6 mybp in the parental node of both winged insects and silverfish; Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2), which is consistent with aggregates being widespread in the backbone of the Insecta clade, including members of several early diverging taxa such as Diplura and Zygentoma, Palaeoptera, and Polyneoptera (such as orders Blattodea, Gryllobllattodea, Mantodea, Mantophasmatodea, Orthoptera, and Zoraptera)45,48. The multistate analysis also reiterated a dynamic evolutionary history of sperm form across the Arthropoda (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 2–4 and Supplementary Table 2). Stochastic mapping revealed an average of 161.2 character state changes per simulation across the entire tree, with 9 - 44 (95% HPD = 15-34) origins of conjugation, 45-148 (95%; HPD = 57-115) losses of conjugation, and 19-105 (95% HPD = 30-74) transitions among conjugate types. All five conjugate types were observed among distantly related taxa and thus are likely to have arisen independently through convergent evolution (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 2–4, Supplementary Table 2, see Supplementary Data 5 for ancestral state estimates of multistate conjugation data for all nodes in the tree).

We found strong support for our predictions regarding the role of sperm aggregates in the origin and diversification of conjugation. First, of the total accumulated time that the arthropod tree spent in a state of sperm conjugation, 49.00% of the time was spent as aggregates, whereas sperm were conjugated as pairs 6.98% of the time, as rouleaux 10.71% of the time, as spermatostyles 16.53% of the time, and as ensheathed 16.73% of the time. Second, estimates of transitions between conjugation states under optimal “all rates different” Maximum-Likelihood models (Supplementary Fig. 4) indicate that aggregates evolved from unconjugated sperm more often than all other conjugate types combined (55.90% of all independent origins of conjugation), with rouleaux and spermatostyles never directly evolving from unconjugated sperm (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2). Third, aggregates are the only form of conjugation with a propensity to evolve into every other conjugate form (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2). In addition, when all non-aggregate conjugate types undergo an evolutionary transition, they have a high probability of evolving into either aggregate conjugates or into unconjugated sperm; all of the possible transitions between different specialized forms of conjugation were rare-to-nonexistent (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 2–4 and Supplementary Table 2).

We closely examined one such rare transition, observed to have occurred in ground beetles (family Carabidae). Stochastic mapping supports spermatostyle conjugates as ancestral to this family, following an independent origin from aggregates around 250 mybp (pp = 1.0 for spermatostyles in the parental node of Carabidae with ages between 186.1 – 237.8 mybp (95% HPD); see * in Fig. 2). Within carabids, a transition from spermatostyle to ensheathed conjugation was indicated with high probability, as seen in some species of Clivina and Paraclivina (the rightmost tip in Fig. 3). In other words, carabids provide an example of the independent origin of ensheathed conjugates from spermatostyles, a character state transition from a distinct and highly derived starting point that is estimated to have occurred within the past 25 – 107 mybp (Supplementary Data 3). In C. fossor and P. bipustulata, the sperm are ensheathed within the cavity of a unique elongate structure51 (Fig. 3b). Clivina nest within the family Carabidae, and their nearest relatives, other Clivina species, make spermatostyle conjugates that vary in the extent to which the sperm flagella are enveloped by the spermatostyle material (e.g., the middle tips in Fig. 3 are representative of other Clivina species and more distantly related ground beetles from another genus, respectively51,52). Evolutionary relationships between conjugation and sperm-associated material (SAM)

SAM, when present, is readily observable and explicitly described in most ultrastructural investigations of sperm (Figs. 1 and 2 and Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 1). Notably, SAM is not limited to conjugated sperm, as numerous taxa across the arthropod tree have unconjugated sperm with SAM (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Moreover, even in the absence of conjugation, SAM exhibits dramatic variation in form among species (Supplementary Fig. 5). For example, it may appear as a dense acrosomal cap (Supplementary Fig. 5d), as a larger mass surrounding the sperm head (Supplementary Fig. 5h, k, l), or as a coating around the entire sperm cell53 (e.g., Supplementary Fig. 5c, e–g, i, j). Stochastic mapping could not resolve whether SAM was present or absent in deep nodes of the arthropod phylogeny, presumably due to its putative evolutionary lability (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 6).

Arthropod sperm conjugates invariably include SAM36,45,51 (Supplementary Data 1), and independent origins of SAM may have facilitated the repeated evolution of conjugation. Specifically, we hypothesized that the origin of SAM represents a requisite transitional state (i.e., a preadaptation or precursor trait that facilitates sperm interactions) to the evolutionary origin of sperm conjugation. Hence, we utilized joint reconstructions of both traits to explore transition models that require the presence of SAM for the evolutionary gain of conjugation (note: this prediction includes the simultaneous appearance of SAM and conjugation, as the time lag between the two may be too brief to resolve). Bayesian probabilistic modeling of the 621 arthropod species (see Methods) revealed statistically significant support (log Bayes Factor > 100; Supplementary Fig. 7) for SAM as a precursor trait in a majority of the tree (66% of nodes in the tree support a transition model (minimum 50 pp) with an order between conjugation and SAM gain; Supplementary Fig. 7a), with a minority of conjugation gains occurring directly from unconjugated sperm (i.e., SAM and conjugation arising simultaneously; Supplementary Fig. 7). In fact, reconstructions indicate that most occurrences of unconjugated sperm with SAM in living arthropods are the result of conjugation loss rather than SAM gain, and the former was approximately 4000x as probable as the latter (P = 5.7e-182; Supplementary Fig. 7b). There was also significant support for the loss of SAM from unconjugated sperm with SAM (rjMCMC never eliminated this parameter from the model; Z% = 0; Supplementary Fig. 7b). In summary, phylogenetic modeling supports our a priori prediction that most evolutionary derivations of conjugation from an ancestor with unconjugated sperm occurred following the origin of SAM. Models further suggest that conjugate diversification frequently involved structural modifications of SAM to form structures such as sheaths and spermatostyles.

Convergent molecular basis of spermatostyles with independent evolutionary origins

We leveraged the independent origins of spermatostyles (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2 and Fig. 3b, c) to determine whether these convergent evolutionary events were dependent upon the recruitment of similar molecular components (e.g., ‘common toolkit’ hypothesis37). Multi-state reconstructions of conjugate evolution (see above; Supplementary Figs. 2–4) provided robust support for spermatostyles as a derived character state in both true bugs and beetles (posterior probability of aggregates being ancestral to true bugs and beetles = 0.93 and 0.89, respectively; Fig. 4a), along with the complementary finding that the most recent common ancestor of beetles and bugs (~400 million years ago; 371.9 – 457.9 95% HPD; Supplementary Fig. 2) produced aggregate conjugates (posterior probability > 0.99; Fig. 4a). To test the common toolkit hypothesis37 in this context, we expanded upon our previous characterization of spermatostyles in whirligig beetles30 by similarly characterizing spermatostyle proteomes in four species of true bugs: two spittlebug species (Philaenus spumarius and Lepyronia quadrangularis; family Aphrophoridae), a leafhopper (Cuerna striata; family Cicadellidae) and a cicada (Neotibicen lyricen; family Cicadidae) (Fig. 4b–e, Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Data 6). Among true bugs, there was significant overlap among the spermatostyle proteomes of the leafhopper and the two spittlebug species (68.46% average overlap of protein families; P < 1e-16 in all pairwise comparisons). In contrast, the cicada spermatostyle proteome was distinct and included numerous high-abundance proteins without significant homology to any proteins in UniProt or GenBank. Given the nested phylogenetic position of the cicada relative to the other sampled true bug species (see asterisk in Fig. 4d), the most parsimonious explanation is dramatic, lineage-specific molecular evolution of the cicada spermatostyle (see Supplementary Note 1 for further details).

We next compared spermatostyle proteome composition between the three species of true bugs (excluding the cicada) and the six species of whirligig beetles to explore the common molecular toolkit hypothesis. Despite their independent origins, we observed striking commonalities in the molecular characteristics of their proteomes (Fig. 4b). On average, 21.7% of identified proteins were present in both beetle and true bug proteomes, which is significantly greater than expected by random simulation (P < 0.0001 for all pairwise comparisons between true bugs and beetles). We note, however, that this analysis does not account for the fact that proteins could be co-recruited as spermatostyle components based on shared patterns of expression or functionality. Moreover, the overlapping proteins contributed significantly more to total protein abundance than expected (P < 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons between true bugs and beetles). This pattern is largely attributable to sperm-leucyl aminopeptidases (S-LAPs), which were consistently among the most abundant proteins in spittlebug, leafhopper and whirligig beetle spermatostyles (Fig. 4c). Phylogenetic reconstruction of the S-LAP protein family revealed that spermatostyle S-LAPs in both true bugs and beetles are members of the same S-LAP subclass (Fig. 4c). Further, protein abundance in spermatostyles was significantly correlated between the two taxa (P = 2.2e-16 for protein families in the top 25% by abundance). Finally, true bug spermatostyles were significantly enriched for proteolytic functions (Supplementary Fig. 9), including metalloexopeptidase activity (GO:0008235; P = 0.003) and aminopeptidase activity (GO:0004177; P = 0.001), which further supports functional coherence with beetle spermatostyles (see Supplementary Information for additional details; Supplementary Fig. 10). Taken together, we conclude that the independent evolution of spermatostyles in beetles and true bugs involved the co-option of many of the same proteins and that these ejaculate structures possess a highly convergent molecular architecture.

In light of the parallel co-option of S-LAPs into both true bug (excluding cicada) and whirligig beetle spermatostyles, we investigated whether S-LAPs might also be present in SAM. To do so, we examined both Scolops pungens, a dictyopharid true bug that has unconjugated sperm with SAM and is closely related to true bugs with spermatostyles sampled in this study (Supplementary Fig. 8), and Gyrinus natator, a whirligig beetle with aggregate conjugates that is closely related to the examined Dineutus species with spermatostyles30. In both cases, immunohistochemistry using an S-LAP antibody confirmed that S-LAPs are present within SAM (Supplementary Fig. 10). Although it is not possible to directly assess the ancestral molecular composition of SAM, these results confirm the presence of S-LAPs in the SAM of both true bugs and beetles. We thus postulate that the presence of S-LAPs in SAM facilitated their parallel recruitment during the independent evolution of spermatostyles in these distinct lineages.

Discussion

With limited evidence for haploid selection on sperm or intra-ejaculate competition among sperm34,54,55, ejaculates are expected to function with highly evolved integration among component parts. Nevertheless, studies of ejaculate quality, functionality and competitiveness have almost exclusively restricted their focus to single-component ejaculate phenotypes, such as sperm number, sperm length, and the function of individual seminal fluid proteins, rather than more integrative components and emergent properties. An exception has been resolving the interactions between sperm, Sex Peptide and the female31,56,57. One of the only other exceptions is sperm conjugation, although our understanding of the developmental origins, functional biomechanics and adaptive value of this complex trait is still limited34–36,58.

Whereas it is intuitive that selection should persistently favor social cooperation among sperm within ejaculates, sperm conjugation was believed to be a relatively rare phenomenon (albeit widespread in particular taxonomic groups36,58). In contrast, our comparative analysis of the Arthropoda reveals that sperm conjugation has been a pervasive and dynamic aspect of evolutionary experimentation. Conjugation likely first arose in arthropods during the Cambrian or Ordivician Periods (Figs. 1 and 2; 452.6 – 508.5 million years ago; Supplementary Fig. 2) and was subsequently lost and gained approximately 45 times each. Moreover, we estimate that across its entire history, the arthropod and outgroup tree of life has spent an average of two-thirds of its time in a state of sperm conjugation (Supplementary Fig. 1). The common ancestor of all insects (and non-insect hexapods) is estimated to have had conjugated sperm. Throughout this history, conjugation has adopted several discrete types and massive variations in sizes, from sperm pairs to thousands of sperm precisely organized into rouleaux.

The adaptive value of conjugation is not known for any taxon, and the selective benefits and costs of conjugation may differ among lineages or over time36. Most authors have focused on enhanced swimming speed and its benefits in sperm competition34–36. The number of sperm per conjugate is expected to positively correlate with swimming velocity due to enhanced force generation with less than additive drag (due to sperm alignment), provided that flagellar beating is synchronous to minimize interference36,59. Indeed, a study of bull sperm, which lacks conjugation, showed that when sperm traveling in the same direction contact one another, the phase of beat frequency synchronizes and velocity increases beyond that of either individual sperm60. Hence, any novel origin of sperm adhering to one another by SAM (i.e., proto-conjugation) could immediately result in a motility benefit - a condition that best describes aggregate conjugates prior to more specialized adaptations of SAM and/or sperm morphology to facilitate conjugation. This hypothetical scenario is supported by our phylogenetic modeling showing that most evolutionary derivations of conjugation occurred following the origin of SAM. Unfortunately, few studies have quantified the motility of conjugated sperm, and those that have done so provide inconclusive or mixed results36. The “enhanced motility” hypothesis also cannot explain the evolution of immotile ensheathed conjugates, nor the widespread association of SAM and unconjugated sperm.

Results of the current study suggest a new adaptive hypothesis for conjugation that is premised on the benefits of enhancing the (1) location and (2) persistence of seminal fluid constituents within the FRT. Seminal fluid proteins support sperm and mediate myriad important post-mating interactions between the sexes21. Yet, the vast majority of the hundreds of seminal fluid proteins of D. melanogaster are undetectable in the FRT a few hours after insemination61. Only those few seminal fluid proteins that are physically associated with sperm accompany them from the site of insemination into the sperm-storage organs and exhibit longer persistence32. In the case of Sex Peptide, its efficacy can last for days because it is cleaved from sperm in a time-release manner33. As an alternative to seminal fluid proteins binding to sperm (with or without conjugation), selection may have favored males incorporating critical proteins into ejaculate structures to ensure their proper localization and/or to enhance their persistence. For example, spermatophores, spermatostyles and mating plugs can persist for days-months within the FRT28,29. However, the only way to ensure that ejaculatory structures are transported to the sperm-storage organs may be through their physical association with sperm, which the female requires for fertilization.

The “seminal fluid delivery” hypothesis contends that sperm conjugation represents (at least in part) sperm cooperation for the regulated localization and persistence of SAM in the female sperm-storage organs30. For example, rather than spermatostyles having evolved as a structural mechanism facilitating the conjugation of sperm, conjugation may have evolved as a mechanism of delivering spermatostyles to the female sperm-storage organs. This hypothesis is consistent with several observations. First, in many instances, the association between SAM and sperm can only serve to impede motility: (1) SAM can be bound to single sperm that transport it to the sperm-storage organs53 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 5); (2) the spermatostyles of some species have extensive bare regions without sperm head attachment, and so are longer than necessary to facilitate conjugation51; (3) ensheathed conjugates are immotile until the sheaths are shed, typically within the sperm-storage organs62. Further, in Lepidoptera, only the fertilizing sperm morph is ensheathed62. Second, SAM in the form of spermatostyles is not composed of inert structural material as expected if functioning purely as scaffolding for conjugation. Our proteomic analyses of spermatostyles has revealed an enrichment of proteases in all true bug and beetle species examined to date. Furthermore, in the case of the beetle D. assimilis, we have confirmed that at least some of these proteases retain biochemical activity as spermatostyle components30.

Throughout its more than 500-million-year history (545.9 – 621.9 mybp for the root of Arthropoda; 95% HPD), the Arthropoda have been remarkably successful, diversifying to occupy a vast array of habitats and ecological niches. Our study reveals that, across this time span, arthropods likewise explored a vast phylomorphospace of ejaculate design that frequently featured intra-ejaculate sperm cooperation through conjugation34–36. We found evidence for evolutionary trajectories repeatedly driving lineages to convergently innovate one of five generalized conjugate blueprints: aggregates, pairs, ensheathed, rouleaux and spermatostyles. Our proteomic analysis of independently derived spermatostyles in true bugs and beetles represents a new example of convergent evolution, putatively through the deployment of a common genetic toolkit37. It thus expands the relevance of deep homology to the evolution of social cooperation among gametes63. Both taxa recruited S-LAPs, which have been predicted to have gained a structural function in Drosophila64, as part of the developmental origin of spermatostyles. As is the case more generally of LAPs functioning outside of reproduction65, S-LAPs appear to have notable multifunctionality and may represent a broader class of “common toolkit” genes with high evolutionary potential. Alternatively, it is possible that S-LAPs are more likely to be recruited as spermatostyle proteins given their enhanced expression in the testis. We currently have a limited understanding of how expression may influence the co-recruitment of genes and gene regulatory modules underlying convergent phenotypes. Still, cicada spermatostyle composition revealed that alternative evolutionary solutions were also possible (see Supplementary Note 1). Additional comparative studies of the developmental basis and molecular composition of different conjugate forms and other ejaculate structures are required before more general conclusions can be drawn about the contribution of deep homology to the convergent evolution of ejaculate adaptations. In particular, SAM warrants intense investigation as a highly evolvable and multifunctional substrate for the development of ejaculate structures and the diversification of extended gamete phenotypes.

Methods

Taxon sampling and criteria for inclusion in comparative literature analyses

For an arthropod species to be included in the comparative analyses of data from the published literature, it had to fulfill three criteria: (1) an adequate description of sperm morphology, (2) sufficient molecular data for phylogenetic placement, and (3) contributing to the goal of resolving transitions among character states while minimizing pseudoreplication. With regard to the first criterion, we surveyed the vast literature on sperm morphology (e.g., refs. 25,26,45,), for which most studies have employed scanning and transmission election microscopy approaches to examine sperm ultrastructure. To identify relevant studies throughout the arthropod tree of life38, we used Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Semantics Scholar to search the literature using a combination of terms that included a clade-specific taxon name (e.g., every arthropod order) coupled with each of the following: ‘sperm’, ‘sperm morphology’, ‘sperm structure’, ‘ultrastructure’, ‘conjugation’, ‘ejaculate’, and ‘reproduction’. Additionally, SpermTree66 was searched for relevant references. Articles were retained for further consideration if they contained images and/or detailed descriptions of sperm structure. The second criterion for inclusion required restricting our sampling to only those species with molecular phylogenetic information. Species that have not been sampled for molecular phylogenetics but otherwise met the first and third criteria were included in our analyses only if their phylogenetic position could be determined from a congener or near relative as a substitute. In some cases, we relied on the taxonomic classification and phylogenetic evidence for the placement of higher-level clades (e.g., tribes or subfamilies) to inform the placement of species unsampled for molecular phylogenetics. In such cases, we never sampled more than two species per higher-level clade to maximize phylogenetic resolution without assuming further phylogenetic structure. Fulfilling the third criterion greatly reduced the number of included species by prioritizing sampling of lineages that capture evolutionary transitions in conjugation phenotype. Dense sampling of species within a lineage was a priority when their addition would likely help resolve character state transitions, whereas repeated sampling of species with limited potential to resolve transitions (i.e., additional species sampling within clades with identical character states) was avoided. For example, we did not include the hundreds of Lepidoptera that have been sampled for sperm and molecular phylogenetics because nearly all species studied to date have the same conjugation state36,62, and therefore, many species did not meet our third criterion. Following this strategy, we aimed for a representative sampling of the known morphological variation in conjugates from diverse arthropod groups as well as broad taxonomic coverage of Arthropoda. Finally, we collected new sperm morphological data for 10 species based on the protocol of Syed et al.27: the five true bug species used in the proteomic analysis (see below and Supplementary Data 1; Lepyronia quadrangularis, Philaenus spumarius, Neotibicen lyricen, Cuerna striata and Scolops pungens), three additional true bugs (Lycorma delicatula, Gyponana sp., and Acanalonia bivittata), and two beetles (Peltodytes edentulus and Amblycheila baroni).

Our literature survey and data collection resulted in the convergence of sperm morphological data, phylogenetic information, and a contribution to the inference of sperm trait evolution for 615 arthropod species distributed among every living arthropod subphylum and class, and with representatives in 87 of 129 taxonomic orders (~70% of extant arthropod orders38). We additionally included six species (two per phylum) from Tardigrada, Onychophora, and Nematoda as outgroups, for a total of 621 species. The 42 arthropod orders that we did not sample tend to be small (i.e., < 100 species per order; mean ± s.e = 94 ± 221; range = 2–1400) and little studied. To the best of our knowledge, most of these taxa have never been investigated for sperm ultrastructure and/or molecular phylogenetics.

Phylogeny

We assembled a phylogeny for the 621 species using a custom supertree approach described in detail below (see also Supplementary Information). We note that by combining multiple phylogenetic histories together into one tree for our sampling of species (i.e., via direct grafting of multiple topologies from different sources, often without shared molecular data among species), we address the need for a phylogenetic framework that describes relationships among all our sampled tips, but we also risk producing spurious internal relationships that are incongruent or inconsistent with the true evolutionary history. Inferring the arthropod tree of life is beyond the scope of this study, and we contend that our phylogeny has several strengths beyond its practical strength of including all studied species. Crucially, our tree’s topology is derived from state-of-the-art phylogenomic studies of diverse arthropods, and its divergence times come from biologically realistic evolutionary models. Specifically, the branch lengths for this topology were sought using Bayesian estimation of divergence times with 14 calibrated node priors based on well-vetted calibrations from the fossil literature and two calibrations from recent phylogenetic studies (see below). Hence, although we lack molecular sequence data upon which to infer branch lengths for all our sampled species (i.e., edge lengths in substitutions per site per lineage per million years), our phylogeny represents a high-resolution, biologically realistic evolutionary history of our sampling of arthropods.

The supertree’s topology was assembled by directly grafting topologies together to construct an initial tree within Mesquite v. 3.6167 based upon a large set of phylogenetic studies (Supplementary Data 2) with phylogenetic references for all 621 tips (Supplementary Data 3) for the complete supertree topology, which is available in Supplemental Data 7. The backbone topology was derived from the most comprehensive phylogenomic analysis to date of the Pancrustacea68 and is available online as a separate tree file with branches color-coded by taxonomic order. Deeper nodes reflect recent phylogenomic studies of the Chelicerata, Myriapoda and Insecta (see Supplementary Data 2 and references therein). Within the scaffolding of these well-supported higher-level clades, we directly grafted additional tips onto the tree based upon more targeted molecular phylogenetic studies, typically at the ordinal level or below, while prioritizing more recent comprehensive phylogenomic or multi-locus studies over older single-gene studies. All analyses are based on a phylogeny that was finalized on November 1, 2024, which is provided online with all files necessary to replicate our phylogeny (see Supplementary Data 3 and Supplementary Information). The topology was ultrametricized using a node-based calibration approach in BEAST v. 2.6.369 with a fixed tree analysis enabled by providing the input xml file as an initial Newick tree and setting the following operators to zero: Subtree Slide, Narrow Exchange, Wide Exchange, and Wilson Balding. By setting the previously specified operators to zero, the divergence time analysis was able to optimize the branch lengths for our sampling of species without rearranging their topology. To accommodate the need for an alignment within BEAST, we simulated 1000 DNA sites under a Jukes-Cantor model of evolution for the supertree topology in Mesquite (archived online with DataDryad 10.5061/dryad.79cnp5jbm, which was provided as input to BEAUti v. 2.6.369. The final BEAST analysis sought branch lengths for the supertree topology using a Jukes-Cantor substitution model, a log-normal relaxed clock, a Birth Death tree model, and multiple calibrations based on the fossil record (Supplementary Data 8).

Calibrated nodes included 14 high-quality fossil calibrations for Arthropoda70 in addition to crown-group priors for Coleoptera and Adephaga based on robust divergence time estimates from Baca et al.71 (see references for fossil details (e.g., species names); see Supplemental Fig. S2 for the location of calibrated nodes and their associated age priors and Supplementary Data 8 for BEAST xml file with priors). Nodes were calibrated with normal prior distributions based on minimum and maximum ages, and the divergence time analysis was performed on a fixed tree topology with initial branch lengths obtained with R ape and the chronos function72 based upon these same calibrations. The chain was run for 200 million iterations, sampling every 20,000 iterations, and with the first 20 million samples discarded as burn-in. The BEAST analysis was rerun three times to ensure consistency of results between runs. The resulting log file from BEAST analysis was examined using Tracer v. 1.7.173 to ensure proper mixing of chains, convergence, and sufficient sampling of run parameters (for log file see Supplementary Data 8). Adequacy of posterior distributions of Bayesian parameters from Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) were assessed using their Effective Sample Sizes (ESSs). We note that Bayesian analysis using MCMC can result in parameters that are persistently difficult to identify, and under certain circumstances, it can be appropriate to disregard certain parameters as ‘nuisance’ parameters when the most critical parameters have been adequately sampled (i.e., when the tree likelihood, prior, and posterior and the parameters of greatest relevance to the aims of the study have ≥ 200 ESSs)74,75. All parameters had ESS values of 200 or higher, with the exceptions of five parameters associated with the molecular clock (67 – 93 ESS) and parameters associated with three fossil priors (84 – 119 ESS; Verunipeltata, Cumacea, and Diplura; see Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 9). The ESS values for these parameters failed to improve after running the chain for longer and combining chains, which suggests that there are limited data and a complex model landscape. Despite the low ESS and consistent unidentifiability of this limited set of parameters, the remaining parameters are well sampled, including the most important parameters for the overall tree and nodal age estimates (Supplementary Data 9). We note that the use of a simpler clock model (i.e., a strict clock that assumes a constant rate for all branches) resulted in > 200 ESS for all parameters but is a significantly worse model compared to a relaxed clock according to Bayes factor. For this reason, we chose to use the branch lengths from the analysis with a relaxed clock despite the unidentifiability of a handful of parameters. The final phylogeny’s branch lengths are based on the median estimate of the posterior distributions of nodal heights from 10,000 posterior trees (95% HPD node ages reported in Supplementary Fig. 2). The final ultrametric phylogeny used for downstream analysis (Supplementary Data 3) is provided in the Supplementary Information in addition to the following: the BEAST xml file with all run settings including priors (Supplementary Data 8), the log file from the BEAST analysis (Supplementary Data 9), and the set of 10,000 posterior trees used to generate the median node age estimates in the final ultrametric tree.

Characterization of sperm traits

We assembled a character state matrix of sperm traits for each of the 621 sampled species that included (1) presence versus absence of conjugation, (2) type of conjugation, and (3) presence versus absence of sperm-associated material (SAM). Sperm conjugation occurs when two or more sperm physically bind to one another. Conjugates form within the male reproductive tract and involve species-specific morphological and secretory adaptations. The presence of conjugation, structural mechanism of conjugation, and often the precise number of sperm per conjugate are clearly discernable in ultrastructural studies (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3a–l). The presence or absence of SAM, which we operationally define as extracellular material that is bound to sperm, could also be unambiguously determined from ultrastructural studies (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 3a–l and 5). Because our understanding of trait variation (and hence morphological categorization; see below) became more refined as the study progressed, we reexamined every paper and reevaluated the tip data for every species at least once following an initial scoring of all 621 tips (Supplementary Data 1).

The manner by which sperm structurally bind to one another to form conjugates and the number of sperm per conjugate have functional implications for sperm performance within the FRT42,43. In one of the most modified forms, conjugates are formed when groups of sperm are entirely ensheathed by SAM, which precludes motility. Conjugates were categorized into the following types: (1) pairs (2) ensheathed, (3) aggregate, (4) rouleaux, and (5) spermatostyle36. This organizational system focuses on the most distinctive and diagnosable aspects of conjugate morphology (i.e., variation in the number of included sperm and the organization of sperm within a conjugate), and those characteristics most likely to dictate the functional biology of conjugates within the FRT. Paired conjugates comprise two sperm, typically with their heads joined together in a highly precise or crystalline fashion (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3a, g). Conjugates with three or more sperm were categorized depending on the nature of their association to each other and the degree of morphological specialization in sperm and/or binding material (i.e., SAM). Ensheathed conjugates occur when multiple sperm are completely enveloped together within a coat (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3h–j). Aggregates typically contain variable numbers of sperm joined together principally via their heads, which are aligned in register and embedded in secreted material (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3d–f). Rouleaux conjugates are orderly stacks of sperm, in which one sperm head nests within the head of another sperm in a repeated series that can include from a few to thousands of sperm42 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3k, l). Hence, rouleaux require morphological adaptations such as a concavity or hollow pocket in the sperm head. Spermatostyles occur when the secretory material joining multiple sperm adopts an elongate, rod-like form and typically involves very precise sperm attachment (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). The different conjugation states typically do not co-occur, but rare instances when they do so lend support to our recognition of discrete states (Supplementary Table 3). For example, the rouleaux conjugates of male Stictonectes optatus diving beetles also feature attached spermatostyle material (Supplementary Fig. 3k), and the complex rouleaux of some telemid spiders are ensheathed (Supplementary Fig. 3l). It should be noted that numerous papers mistakenly refer to ensheathed conjugates as spermatophores, which are an unrelated phenomenon in which entire ejaculates are encapsulated within secretory material29.

Comparative phylogenetic methods for analyses of sperm trait evolution

Analyses of phylogenetic trait distributions and ancestral state reconstructions of discrete sperm traits were conducted using the R packages phytools76 and corHMM77. Because of the large size of our arthropod phylogeny and variation in the distributions of conjugation and SAM across clades, we considered the possibility that evolutionary rates of both traits might vary through time within and among clades. For example, conjugation is completely absent or rare from some clades (e.g., flies, ticks), nearly ubiquitous in others (e.g., moths and butterflies, scorpions), and highly variable in expression in still others (e.g., beetles, isopods). Therefore, we compared time-homogeneous (i.e., single-rate category) transition models featuring separate parameters for trait gain and loss with models that incorporate rate heterogeneity using corHMM. We found marginally non-significant support for two-rate models for binary (i.e., presence or absence) conjugation (P = 0.059; Likelihood Ratio Test) and highly significant support for two-rate models for SAM (P = 1.58e-07; Supplementary Table 4). When reconstructing multistate conjugation data across the phylogeny, a model with a single rate category with separate parameters for all possible transitions among conjugate types was optimal (P = 1.85e-06; Likelihood Ratio Test between this model and a simpler model where transitions between any two states do not differ). Species with co-occurring character states (e.g., the ensheathed rouleaux of telemid spiders; Supplementary Table 3) were incorporated into multistate trait mappings through the use of tip priors. Briefly, the prior probability for species was split among their observed character states, which we note is not an explicit polymorphic trait model and has the drawback of treating this unusual variation in conjugation as uncertainty in the tip data. However, this applies to a very small fraction of our species sampling ( ~ 1%), and tip priors offer a reasonable compromise in terms of computational difficulty, model complexity, and data resolution on conjugates. Stochastic character mappings (N = 1000 simulations) were generated under preferred models and were summarized using ‘describe.simmap’. Estimates for the timing of independent origins of distinct conjugate types are based on median node ages from relaxed clock Bayesian analyses of divergence times (i.e., parameter-rich models) for when an ancestral node was reconstructed as having between ≥ 50 – ≥ 90 posterior probability of a given conjugate type. Ancestral character estimates are provided online for all nodes from N = 1000 mappings of both the binary and multistate conjugation data.

The relationship between conjugation and SAM (N = 621 tips) was examined by employing Bayesian probabilistic modeling within the program BayesTraits Discrete with reversible-jump MCMC (rjMCMC) to select viable models and integrate over the complex model landscape78. BayesTraits Discrete models either permitted direct transitions to conjugation from unconjugated sperm or required species with conjugation to first have evolved SAM from unconjugated sperm. The two-step transition from unconjugated to SAM followed by SAM to conjugation (and their reverse transitions) represent an ordered evolutionary model. The fit of these ordered models to our data was compared with the results of an unordered transition model approach, which allows direct transitions between any two character states. Models did not include the character state combination of conjugation without SAM as it was never observed, and the transitions towards and away from this state combination were removed from the analysis. To accommodate possible heterogeneity across the phylogeny in the joint evolutionary history of these complex traits, we also examined the fit of Discrete Covarion models, which allow for the occurrence of both ordered and unordered character state transitions78. Because support for the Discrete Covarion model can be spurious due to rate heterogeneity78, it was also compared to a two-rate unordered model. RJMCMC was conducted using 21 million iterations, sampling every 20,000 generations, discarding the first million samples as burn-in, and we repeated the analysis 3x from different starting seeds to ensure stability among runs. Bayesian model log likelihood was calculated using stepping-stone sampling, and posterior distributions from rjMCMC revealed very strong support for the Discrete Covarion model (Log Bayes Factor > 100).

Transcriptomics, comparative proteomics, and immunohistochemistry

The proteomes of spermatostyles were characterized for four species of true bugs from three families: two spittle bugs (Philaenus spumarius and Lepyronia quadrangularis; family Aphrophoridae), a leafhopper (Cuerna striata; family Cicadellidae), and a cicada (Neotibicen lyricen; family Cicadidae). To facilitate species-specific proteomic analyses, de novo transcriptome assemblies and gene model annotations were obtained following the methods of Gomez et al.30. Briefly, total RNA from whole males was used to prepare Illumina TruSeq mRNA libraries for sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq 550 with 50 or 75 base pairs, and paired-end reads (raw reads are available via the NCBI SRA; BioProject Accession PRJNA1270706). The transcriptomes were assembled de novo using Trinity v. 2.13.279. The Trinotate pipeline80 was used for de novo annotation with TransDecoder79 for protein prediction with homology searching.

Spermatostyle purification and tandem mass spectrometry was conducted using the methods of Gomez et al.30. Briefly, spermatostyles were isolated and pooled from seminal vesicles, vortexed gently (or sonicated for the cicada samples) to remove sperm, and then centrifuged on a fine cell strainer prior to solubilization in 2X Laemmli sample buffer with protease inhibitors and TCEP. Samples were then separated on a 1.5 mm 12% SDS-PAGE gel stained with colloidal Coomassie dye and divided into four slices. Slices were analyzed with a Dionex UltiMate 300 rapid separation liquid chromatography nanoUPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with a Lumos mass spectrometer. Raw spectral data were analyzed using the PEAKS Studio X software (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.) against species-specific protein databases using the following conservative statistical criteria: (1) robust Peptide-Spectrum Matches (PSMs; 1% false discovery rate using a decoy-fusion approach; Zhang et al.81) (2) significant protein scores (-10log p ≥ 20) and (3) a minimum of two unique peptides and six PSMs for inclusion. These strict criteria resulted in consistent depth of coverage across the four species with almost all identified proteins possessing PSMs across all replicates (average protein identification by at least one PSM per replicate = 98.8%; SD = 0.75). Protein abundance values were estimated from MS1 using label-free quantitation82, and these estimates were normalized following Wisconsin double standardization to enable among species comparisons. MS/MS data are available via the ProteomeXchange Consortium (accession identifier PXD064531).

Spermatostyle proteome composition was compared among true bug species and with six species of whirligig beetles30 (family Gyrinidae). Orthology among species was inferred using Orthofinder analysis of de novo transcriptomes from all ten species and the Uniprot Drosophila melanogaster reference proteome83,84. A random permutation approach was used to assess true bug spermatostyle proteome overlap with beetle spermatostyles. Simulated proteomes were generated for each true bug species through random sampling of their complete protein database (1000 iterations). Overlap of these simulated proteomes was then determined in relation to a ‘core’ beetle spermatostyle proteome (i.e., those proteins present in the spermatostyles of five out of six beetle species) to establish an exact P value. Multivariate phylogenetic regressions were conducted using the R package RPP85, permitting tests of significant covariation in proteomic composition among species. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were conducted using the R package goseq with multiple test correction86. The S-LAP protein family phylogeny for our sampled species was inferred using Maximum-Likelihood with RAxML-NG as implemented in Orthofinder87.

Visualization of S-LAP localization in beetle and true bug sperm preparations was achieved using a polyclonal mouse antibody against Drosophila melanogaster S-LAPs88 and the immunohistochemistry protocols of White-Cooper89. This approach was used to examine (1) spermatostyle conjugates of two spittlebugs: L. quadrangularis and P. spumarius, (2) the unconjugated sperm with SAM of a dictyopharid planthopper, Scolops pungens and (3) the sperm aggregates of the whirligig beetle, Gyrinus natator. Sperm were isolated into PBS from seminal vesicles onto subbed slides (N = 1 – 5 males examined per species), dried in a slide oven, and then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at a dilution factor of either 1:200 or 1:400. Alexa Fluor 568 (Thermo Fisher) was used for secondary antibody staining, and samples were imaged using an Olympus DP-71 camera mounted on an Olympus BX60 fluorescence microscope.
