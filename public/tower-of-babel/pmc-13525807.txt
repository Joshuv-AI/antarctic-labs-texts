Outcomes of Preoperative TACE vs Upfront Hepatectomy in Hepatocellular Carcinoma: A Single-Center Retrospective Study in Vietnam
Authors: Anh Gia Pham, Nghia Trung Bui, Thau Manh Cao, Son Hong Trinh, Huyen Trang Thi Vo, Lam Thanh Phan, Thu Hang Thi Nguyen, Tung Thanh Pham, Hang Thu Nong, Ngoc Thi Tran, Cong Tien Bui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis propensity score-matched study aimed to compare survival outcomes between preoperative TACE followed by hepatectomy and upfront hepatectomy in patients with resectable hepatocellular carcinoma.Patients and MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study at Viet Duc University Hospital, 304 consecutive patients with resectable HCC treated between January 2015 and October 2024 were analyzed: 168 underwent upfront hepatectomy and 136 received preoperative TACE followed by hepatectomy. Propensity score matching (PSM; 1:1 nearest-neighbor, caliper 0.01) was performed to balance pre-treatment covariates between groups. Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were compared using Kaplan–Meier analysis and the Log rank test. Independent prognostic factors were identified using multivariable Cox regression.ResultsMedian follow-up was 58.9 months. After PSM (113 matched pairs), median OS was not reached in the upfront hepatectomy group versus 62.5 months in the preoperative TACE group (P = 0.322), and median DFS was 32.0 versus 29.2 months (P = 0.056). In multivariable Cox analysis of the matched cohort, preoperative TACE was not independently associated with OS (HR 1.15, 95% CI 0.74–1.79; P = 0.526) or DFS (HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.92–1.75; P = 0.130), whereas advanced TNM stage (OS: HR 2.45, 95% CI 1.08–5.52, P = 0.032; DFS: HR 2.26, 95% CI 1.14–4.46, P = 0.019) and microvascular invasion (OS: HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.09–2.40, P = 0.007; DFS: HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.01–2.29, P = 0.035) remained independent prognostic factors. Within the TACE group, patients achieving complete pathological necrosis (n = 26) had OS and DFS comparable to upfront hepatectomy, whereas incomplete or partial necrosis was associated with significantly worse survival (P = 0.05 for OS and P = 0.01 for DFS).ConclusionIn this retrospective, propensity score-matched cohort, preoperative TACE was not associated with improved overall or disease-free survival in patients with resectable HCC and does not appear justified as a routine strategy in patients who are already surgical candidates. Because the analysis was retrospective and residual confounding cannot be excluded, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Any role for preoperative TACE in selected settings, such as borderline resectability or when complete tumor necrosis is achieved, requires prospective confirmation.Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, hepatectomy, microvascular invasion, TACE, preoperative

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is among the most common cancers worldwide. According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 report, there are more than 800,000 new cases annually, accounting for 4.3% of all cancers. In Vietnam, liver cancer ranks second in incidence and first in cancer-related mortality, with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection as the dominant etiology.1,2

Although substantial advances in screening and diagnosis have enabled detection at earlier stages, overall prognosis remains limited. Hepatectomy and liver transplantation remain the principal curative options that provide long-term survival, but the recurrence rates remain high, which negatively affects survival. To improve outcomes, many supportive strategies have been studied and applied in the preoperative setting, including preoperative TACE. Initially, TACE was mainly used as a palliative treatment for HCC patients who were no longer candidates for surgery or transplantation. Later, it was explored as a preoperative therapy intended to reduce tumor size and invasiveness and to limit tumor spread, with the aim of increasing the likelihood of curative resection and prolonging long-term survival.3–5

In theory, preoperative TACE may confer several benefits; however, the current clinical evidence remains conflicting. Some observational studies have suggested that preoperative TACE may improve survival compared with surgery alone in patients with resectable tumors, whereas more recent clinical trials and meta-analyses have failed to demonstrate a clear benefit in terms of OS or disease-free survival (DFS), and preoperative TACE also carries certain risks. The 2022 update of the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) strategy confines transarterial chemoembolization to intermediate-stage disease and recommends resection or ablation, without neoadjuvant locoregional therapy, for patients with early-stage technically resectable tumors,6 and the 2023 practice guidance of the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases likewise does not recommend neoadjuvant TACE before resection.7 By contrast, the China Liver Cancer (CNLC) guidelines regard preoperative transarterial therapy as an option that may be considered in selected patients with a large or multifocal but still resectable tumor burden.8 This discordance between guidelines, together with the continued use of preoperative TACE in practice throughout much of Asia, defines the clinical controversy that the present study addresses.

Notably, most high-quality evidence originates from Eastern Asian populations (primarily China and Japan), with limited data from Southeast Asian HBV-endemic regions such as Vietnam, where patterns of HCC presentation and treatment access differ. A notable limitation of the existing literature is that most studies have focused on treatment allocation (TACE vs no TACE) rather than treatment response. In particular, the degree of tumor necrosis following preoperative TACE, which may reflect both therapeutic efficacy and underlying tumor biology, has not been adequately incorporated into outcome analyses. The degree of necrosis is of particular interest because it provides a direct, patient-level measure of the biological effect actually achieved by the intervention. Residual viable tumor after arterial devascularization identifies a tumor that is intrinsically resistant to ischemic injury and that may therefore retain a greater capacity for vascular invasion and early recurrence, whereas complete necrosis indicates that the intended biological objective of TACE has been met. Pathological response may thus carry prognostic information that treatment allocation alone cannot capture, and may ultimately help define the subgroup in which preoperative TACE is worthwhile. This limits the ability to identify which patients, if any, may truly benefit from preoperative TACE. Previous propensity score-matched analyses and meta-analyses have compared preoperative TACE with upfront resection at the level of treatment assignment only. The present study differs in that it combines propensity score matching with a survival analysis stratified by the degree of pathological necrosis, in a consecutive cohort from a Southeast Asian center in which chronic HBV infection is the dominant etiology; its intended contribution is therefore biological and methodological as well as geographical. Therefore, this study aimed not only to compare survival outcomes between preoperative TACE and upfront hepatectomy using propensity score matching, but also to evaluate the prognostic significance of tumor necrosis within the TACE-treated population.

Patients and Methods

Ethics Statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Viet Duc University Hospital (approval number: IRB-VN01.021_2026.02), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived, given the retrospective nature of the study. All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects set out in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Design and Patient Selection

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at Viet Duc University Hospital and included patients diagnosed with HCC between January 2015 and October 2024. Initially, 380 patients were identified. After data review, 76 cases were excluded due to incomplete or unreliable medical records, resulting in a final study population of 304 patients. Patients were divided into two groups according to initial treatment strategy: 168 underwent upfront hepatectomy, and 136 underwent preoperative TACE followed by hepatectomy. Treatment allocation was determined at presentation by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) comprising hepatobiliary surgeons, interventional radiologists, medical oncologists, and hepatologists. Preoperative TACE was generally selected for patients with larger tumor burden, multifocal disease, borderline future liver remnant, or those not immediately eligible for surgery for logistical or medical reasons, provided the overall resectability criteria were still met.

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) preserved liver function (Child-Pugh A or B); (3) HCC diagnosed according to American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines or confirmed by postoperative pathology; (4) tumor assessed as resectable by the MDT; (5) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1; and (6) no contraindication to hepatectomy.

Exclusion criteria: (1) incomplete or unreliable medical records; (2) evidence of extrahepatic metastasis; (3) macrovascular invasion of the main portal vein or hepatic veins; and (4) prior treatment other than TACE for HCC. Patients who received preoperative TACE but did not subsequently undergo hepatectomy, because of disease progression, deterioration of liver function, or the development of other contraindications to surgery, were not included in the present analysis. The comparison reported here is therefore between patients who successfully completed each treatment strategy rather than between the strategies as initially intended; the consequences of this design are considered in the Discussion.

Preoperative TACE Protocol

TACE was performed under local anesthesia, typically via the femoral or radial artery. Under digital subtraction angiography (DSA) guidance, an interventional radiologist advanced a catheter into the hepatic arterial system to precisely identify the tumor-feeding vessels. A chemotherapeutic mixture of doxorubicin, epirubicin, and cisplatin combined with an embolic agent (lipiodol or drug-eluting beads) was injected into the tumor-feeding branches to induce tumor cell death and occlude the arterial blood supply. After completion, the catheter was removed and the puncture site was compressed. The procedure typically lasted 30–60 minutes. Patients who developed disease progression, deterioration of liver function, or other contraindications to surgery after TACE were excluded from subsequent hepatectomy and were not included in the present analysis.

Assessment of Efficacy

The timing of surgery was determined by the MDT based on objective clinical and imaging assessment. Imaging response was evaluated approximately 4–6 weeks after TACE according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST).

Surgical Procedure and Pathological Assessment

Hepatectomy was performed with curative intent using an open or laparoscopic approach at the discretion of the operating surgeon. Pathological tumor necrosis in the TACE group was assessed on the resected specimen by dedicated hepatobiliary pathologists and categorized as: complete necrosis (100%), incomplete necrosis (≥50% and <100%), or partial necrosis (<50%). Microvascular invasion (MVI), capsule invasion, tumor grade, and resection margin status were documented according to standard pathological criteria.

Follow-Up

Decisions regarding postoperative adjuvant therapy were based on pathology findings. Patients were followed through outpatient visits or telephone contact until February 2026 for recurrence and survival data collection. Follow-up was scheduled every 3 months with CT or MRI imaging and AFP testing during the first two years after surgery, and then every 6 months thereafter.

DFS was defined as the time from surgery to recurrence detection or death from any cause. OS was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves and compared with the Log rank test. Variables that were clinically meaningful or showed P < 0.10 on univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable Cox proportional-hazards model to identify factors independently associated with mortality and recurrence. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed for every covariate retained in the multivariable models, by visual inspection of log(−log) survival plots and by testing the correlation between scaled Schoenfeld residuals and survival time; no significant departure from proportionality was identified. All variables used for matching and for the multivariable models were complete for the 304 patients analyzed, because cases with incomplete or unreliable records had been excluded a priori during case selection; no imputation of missing data was therefore required. The robustness of the matched analysis was examined in two sensitivity analyses: re-estimation of the propensity score using a wider caliper (0.01), and covariate-adjusted Cox regression in the full unmatched cohort. Both produced effect estimates consistent in direction and magnitude with the primary analysis. No a priori sample size or power calculation was performed, since the study analyzed a fixed consecutive cohort; the resulting limitation in statistical power is addressed in the Discussion.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to reduce selection bias between groups. Covariates included age, sex, BMI, Child-Pugh class, Hepatitis B, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), TNM 8th edition stage, tumor number, and tumor size, with a caliper width of 0.01. After matching, balance was assessed using the standardized mean difference (SMD), with SMD < 0.1 considered well balanced.

Results

The mean number of TACE sessions per patient was 1.6 (range 1–4). The median interval from the last TACE session to surgery was 6 weeks (interquartile range 3.8–12.6).

Overall and Disease-Free Survival Rates

During follow-up, recurrence occurred in 180 patients (59.2%) and 113 patients died (37.2%). Before matching, median DFS was significantly longer in the upfront hepatectomy group than in the preoperative TACE group (34.0 vs 27.2 months; P = 0.008). After PSM, median DFS was 32.0 months in the upfront hepatectomy group and 29.2 months in the preoperative TACE group, showing no statistically significant difference although results numerically favored upfront hepatectomy (P = 0.056) (Figure 1A and C). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year DFS rates were 74.4%, 49.9%, and 39.0% in the upfront hepatectomy group, versus 74.0%, 40.4%, and 23.7% in the preoperative TACE group, respectively.

Median OS was not reached in the upfront hepatectomy group versus 62.5 months in the preoperative TACE group before matching (P = 0.063), with similar results after PSM (P = 0.322) (Figure 1B and D). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates were 83.9%, 71.4%, and 65.5% in the upfront hepatectomy group, compared with 90.4%, 66.1%, and 52.1% in the preoperative TACE group, respectively.

Survival According to the Degree of Tumor Necrosis

In the preoperative TACE group, tumor necrosis was assessed based on the proportion of necrotic area relative to total tumor volume. Complete necrosis was defined as 100% necrosis; incomplete necrosis as ≥50% and <100%; and partial necrosis as <50%. Patients achieving complete necrosis had overall survival comparable to the upfront hepatectomy group. In contrast, patients with incomplete or partial necrosis had significantly lower OS and DFS compared with both the upfront hepatectomy group and the complete necrosis subgroup (P = 0.05 for OS and P = 0.01 for DFS) (Figure 1E and F). After propensity score matching, the same pattern was observed: complete necrosis was associated with survival outcomes similar to upfront hepatectomy, whereas incomplete or partial necrosis was associated with significantly worse OS and DFS (Figure 1G and H). These subgroup comparisons should be interpreted with caution. The complete necrosis subgroup comprised only 26 patients (21 in the matched cohort), so the corresponding survival estimates are imprecise, and this analysis is exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Univariate and Multivariate Analyses of Overall and Disease-Free Survival

Variables included in survival analyses were: age, sex, preoperative AFP >400 ng/mL, tumor number and size, baseline cirrhosis, hepatitis B, microvascular invasion, capsule invasion, margin status, histologic grade, and preoperative TACE.

In univariable analysis, factors significantly associated with OS included multifocal tumors, tumor size >5 cm, microvascular invasion, capsule invasion, positive resection margins, advanced disease stage, and hepatitis B status; the corresponding univariable results before and after matching are presented in Table 2. Variables that were statistically significant on univariable analysis or clinically relevant were entered into multivariable Cox models (Table 3). In the matched cohort, advanced TNM stage (OS: HR 2.45, 95% CI 1.08–5.52, P = 0.032; DFS: HR 2.26, 95% CI 1.14–4.46, P = 0.019) and microvascular invasion (OS: HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.09–2.40, P = 0.007; DFS: HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.01–2.29, P = 0.035) were independently associated with both endpoints, whereas preoperative TACE was not an independent prognostic factor for OS (HR 1.15, 95% CI 0.74–1.79; P = 0.526) or DFS (HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.92–1.75; P = 0.130). Discussion

In this propensity score-matched study, preoperative TACE was not associated with improved overall or disease-free survival compared with upfront hepatectomy in patients with resectable HCC. Only patients who achieved complete tumor necrosis after TACE had outcomes comparable to surgery alone, whereas incomplete response was associated with significantly worse survival.

The theoretical rationale for preoperative TACE includes several factors. Because lipiodol preferentially accumulates within tumor tissue, TACE can improve staging accuracy by revealing small satellite nodules or occult lesions that were missed on pre-treatment imaging.9 It may also downstage tumors that are initially too large or involve major vessels, thereby converting unresectable or high-risk disease into candidates for R0 resection.10 In addition, TACE can serve as a bridging therapy, controlling tumor growth in patients for whom surgery cannot be performed immediately or who are awaiting liver transplantation.11 Although these mechanisms suggest a potential survival benefit, the clinical evidence remains inconsistent. In this study, we evaluated the effect of preoperative TACE on short- and long-term outcomes using retrospective single-center data. Most baseline clinical and pathological characteristics were similar between groups, except for tumor number.

Some earlier studies suggested a benefit of preoperative TACE compared with surgery alone, but these were often limited by retrospective single-center designs and the potential benefit was largely confined to subgroups with higher necrosis or larger tumors, thus providing insufficient evidence to change clinical recommendations.12–14 Later RCTs and meta-analyses failed to demonstrate positive effects of preoperative TACE compared with surgery alone. A 2024 meta-analysis by Jamtani et al (1,556 patients) found no significant differences in OS or DFS between neoadjuvant TACE and upfront resection.15 Similarly, a 2016 meta-analysis by Si et al based on five RCTs showed no improvement in OS (HR = 1.25) or DFS (HR = 0.95) with preoperative TACE.16 Some studies even reported worse OS, especially in patients with incomplete necrosis after TACE.17,18 Several mechanisms may underlie these findings. TACE-induced hypoxia activates HIF-1α and upregulates VEGF, promoting reactive angiogenesis that can revascularize residual tumor and enhance its aggressiveness.19,20 From a surgical standpoint, post-TACE inflammation often produces perihepatic adhesions that obscure anatomical planes, prolong operative time, and increase bleeding risk.21 Injury to non-tumorous liver parenchyma from the chemotherapeutic agents and embolic material can also reduce hepatic reserve, raising the risk of postoperative liver failure and perioperative mortality. Moreover, the recovery interval after TACE may permit silent disease progression and the emergence of new lesions, potentially causing patients to miss the window for curative resection.17,22 Our findings are generally consistent with these studies, showing numerically lower DFS and OS in the preoperative TACE group, possibly because patients receiving TACE tended to have a larger tumor burden.

Subgroup analysis revealed that patients achieving complete tumor necrosis after TACE had survival outcomes comparable to upfront surgery; those with incomplete or partial necrosis had significantly worse prognoses. This suggests that the persistence of viable tumor reflects an aggressive tumor biology for which locoregional therapy alone is insufficient. Complete necrosis may therefore reasonably be regarded as the therapeutic goal whenever preoperative TACE is undertaken. It must be emphasized, however, that the degree of necrosis can be established only on the resected specimen and is not available at the time the decision to operate is taken; it cannot at present be used prospectively to select patients for preoperative TACE. The observation that complete necrosis identifies a subgroup with outcomes equivalent to upfront resection should accordingly be treated as a hypothesis requiring prospective validation, ideally against non-invasive surrogates of pathological response such as an mRECIST-defined complete response or biomarker kinetics.

When, then, might preoperative TACE be appropriate? For early-stage HCC, there remains a subset of patients who are “technically or functionally unresectable” despite the absence of metastasis or vascular invasion. Common reasons include insufficient future liver remnant, tumors adjacent to major vessels or central bile ducts preventing safe margins, and special situations such as tumor rupture. In this context, preoperative therapy functions as a bridging or downstaging strategy to enable curative surgery, addressing a technical barrier rather than systemic disease, and it may be combined with liver hypertrophy techniques such as portal vein embolization or ligation. For more advanced disease that is initially unresectable, multimodal conversion strategies combining transarterial therapy with tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors have reported substantially higher conversion-to-resectability rates than TACE alone, and combinations of TACE with hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or radiofrequency ablation have also been explored.23–27

In multivariable analysis, microvascular invasion (MVI) and disease stage were independent risk factors for both mortality and recurrence. This aligns with global evidence that MVI is among the most important adverse prognostic factors in HCC due to its strong association with early postoperative recurrence.28,29 Therefore, strategies that can reduce MVI are key to improving outcomes. From a tumor biology standpoint, MVI reflects an invasive phenotype driven by dysregulated expression of oncogenes and tumor-suppressor genes. Some studies suggest that reduced MVI seen in conversion-therapy groups may reflect synergistic effects of systemic and locoregional treatments.30 Antiangiogenic agents and immunotherapy can improve antitumor immunity and normalize tumor vasculature, potentially reducing invasiveness; meanwhile, TACE may enhance local inflammatory response and strengthen the peritumoral barrier, limiting the spread of malignant cells into the vasculature, which is related to MVI.31,32

The principal strengths of this study included a relatively large single-institution cohort with a long median follow-up of 58.9 months, the use of propensity score matching to reduce measurable confounding, and a dedicated subgroup analysis of tumor necrosis after preoperative TACE, an aspect that has been underreported in real-world cohorts from HBV-endemic regions. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design inherently limits generalizability, and residual confounding by unmeasured variables (for example, surgeon experience, detailed imaging features, and patient preference) cannot be excluded despite the use of PSM. Second, patients who received preoperative TACE but did not subsequently proceed to hepatectomy because of disease progression or deterioration of liver function were excluded from the analysis. The study therefore compares patients who successfully completed each treatment strategy rather than the two strategies as initially intended. Because the patients lost between TACE and surgery are precisely those with the least favorable tumor biology, this selection bias would be expected to operate in favor of the preoperative TACE group. Third, treatment allocation was determined by the MDT rather than randomly assigned, which introduces confounding by indication; patients allocated to preoperative TACE generally had more aggressive disease at baseline. Fourth, the preoperative TACE regimens were heterogeneous in terms of chemotherapeutic agents, embolic materials, and the number of sessions, which may have influenced tumor necrosis and survival outcomes. Fifth, no a priori sample size or power calculation was performed, and the matched cohort of 113 pairs may have been underpowered to detect modest but clinically relevant differences in survival. This is particularly relevant to the borderline result observed for DFS (P = 0.056), which should be regarded as inconclusive rather than as evidence of equivalence. Finally, molecular and biomarker data (for example, AFP-L3, PIVKA-II, and circulating tumor DNA) were not routinely available during the study period. Future multicenter prospective studies and RCTs, particularly those evaluating TACE in combination with modern systemic therapies such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors, are needed to further define the role of preoperative locoregional therapy in resectable HCC.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that preoperative TACE should not be routinely used in patients who are already candidates for upfront resection. Whether it retains a role in patients with borderline resectability or a high tumor burden could not be determined from the present data, because such patients were not evaluated as a separate population here; its use in those settings should therefore be regarded as individualized practice pending prospective evidence rather than as a recommendation supported by this study. Similarly, the association between complete pathological necrosis and favorable outcomes should currently be regarded as a post-resection prognostic observation rather than a validated tool for surgical decision-making, given the limited availability of robust supporting evidence.

Conclusions

In this propensity score-matched analysis from a Vietnamese tertiary center, preoperative TACE did not provide superior overall or disease-free survival compared with upfront hepatectomy in patients with resectable HCC. Among patients treated with TACE, only those who achieved complete tumor necrosis had outcomes comparable to upfront surgery, whereas an incomplete response was associated with significantly worse survival; because this subgroup was small and pathological necrosis is assessable only after resection, this finding should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Advanced TNM stage and microvascular invasion remained the dominant independent prognostic factors for survival after hepatectomy. Given the retrospective design, the exclusion of patients who did not proceed to surgery after TACE, and the possibility of residual confounding, prospective studies are required before any recommendation can be made regarding the use of preoperative TACE in selected settings such as borderline resectability, inadequate future liver remnant, or incorporation within multimodal treatment strategies.
