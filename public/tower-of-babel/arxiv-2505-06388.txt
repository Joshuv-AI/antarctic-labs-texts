Fundamental Notions of Projective and Scale-Translation-Invariant Metrics in Coding Theory
Gabor Riccardi, Hugo Sauerbier Couvée

Abstract
Projective metrics on vector spaces over finite fields, introduced by Gabidulin and Simonis in 1997, generalize classical metrics in coding theory like the Hamming metric, rank metric, and combinatorial metrics. While these specific metrics have been thoroughly investigated, the overarching theory of projective metrics has remained underdeveloped since their introduction. In this paper, we present and develop the foundational theory of projective metrics, establishing several elementary key results on their characterizing properties, equivalence classes, isometries, constructions, connections with the Hamming metric, associated matroids, sphere sizes and Singleton-like bounds. Furthermore, some general aspects of scale-translation-invariant metrics are examined, with particular focus on their embeddings into larger projective metric spaces.

Our contribution

Although a few specific projective metrics and the subfamily of combinatorial metrics have been studied in-depth, very little literature has been written on the general theory of projective metrics after its introduction. Our contributions are the following.

First, we introduce the notion of quotient spaces in the context of translation-invariant

metrics or weights and describe some category-theoretical properties. Using quotients we extend the groundwork of the theory, show additional fundamental characteristics of projective metrics, and demonstrate their equivalence to metrics defined by sets of subspaces and to integral, convex, scale-translation-invariant metrics. We further show that any scale-translation-invariant metric can be embedded into a suitably larger projective metric space.

Second, we make the relation between projective weights and their parent codes (already briefly noted in
[
12
]
) more precise, showing a bijective correspondence between equivalence classes of projective metrics and classes of Hamming-isometric subspaces of suitably large vector spaces. Additionally, we give a characterization of the isometries of projective metrics as the stabilizer of their parent codes.

Third, we explore ways of constructing new metrics from old ones and consider bounds on codes for projective metrics. In particular, we provide a Singleton-like bound, and with the focus on sphere-packing bounds we investigate sphere sizes. We demonstrate that these are fully determined by an underlying matroid structure regarding
extended spanning families
, i.e. if two projective metrics have matroid equivalent extended spanning families, then they have equal sphere sizes.

Throughout the paper we provide numerous examples, counterexamples, and a Sage script to facilitate these constructions. We also consider various connections to other fields of mathematics such as graph theory and matroid theory, highlighting potential directions for future research.

2.1

Metrics and weight functions

Definition 1
.

A
metric
or
distance function
on
A
is a function
d(\cdot,\cdot):A\times A\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfying the following properties for all
x,y,z\in A
:

(M0)

Non-negativity:

d(x,y)\geq 0

(M1)

Identity of indiscernibles:

d(x,y)=0\;\Leftrightarrow\;x=y

(M2)

Symmetry:

d(x,y)=d(y,x)

(M3)

Triangle inequality:

d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)+d(z,y)

In the case that
A
is a module over a commutative ring
R
(e.g. a vector space over a field
\mathbb{F}
), a
scale-invariant metric
on
A
moreover satisfies the additional property

(M4)

Scale invariance:

d(\alpha x,\alpha y)=d(x,y)
for all
\alpha\in R\setminus\{0\}

Likewise, a
translation-invariant metric
is a metric satisfying (M0)-(M3) and additionally

(M5)

Translation invariance:

d(x+z,y+z)=d(x,y)

In coding theory, it is regularly needed to measure the distance from
x
to
x+e
for given
x,e\in A
. This value
d(x,x+e)
is called the
weight
of
e
with respect to
x
. Since one often deals with a translation-invariant metric
d
, the distance
d(x,x+e)
is in that case independent of
x
and equals
d(0,e)
. We then call the function
\operatorname{wt}(\cdot):=d(0,\cdot)
a
weight function
.

Definition 2
.

A
weight function
on
A
is a function
\operatorname{wt}(\cdot):A\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfying the following properties for all
x,y\in A
:

(W0)

Non-negativity:

\operatorname{wt}(x)\geq 0

(W1)

Positive definiteness:

\operatorname{wt}(x)=0\;\Leftrightarrow\;x=0

(W2)

Symmetry:

\operatorname{wt}(x)=\operatorname{wt}(-x)

(W3)

Triangle inequality:

\operatorname{wt}(x+y)\leq\operatorname{wt}(x)+\operatorname{wt}(y)

In the case that
A
is a module over a commutative ring
R
(e.g. a vector space over a field
\mathbb{F}
), a
scale-invariant weight function
on
A
moreover satisfies this additional property:

(W4)

Scale invariance:

\operatorname{wt}(\alpha x)=\operatorname{wt}(x)
for all
\alpha\in R\setminus\{0\}

Note that
(W0)
is automatically implied by
(W1)-(W3)
since
0=\operatorname{wt}(0)\leq\operatorname{wt}(x)+\operatorname{wt}(-x)=2\operatorname{wt}(x)
, and if
(W4)
is satisfied,
(W2)
is as well.
We can now easily describe a bijective relation between (scale-invariant) weight functions and (scale-invariant) translation-invariant metrics:

•

Given a translation-invariant metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
, its corresponding weight function is defined as

\operatorname{wt}(x):=d(0,x).

•

Given a weight function
\operatorname{wt}(\cdot)
, its corresponding translation-invariant metric is defined as

d(x,y):=\operatorname{wt}(y-x).

Another way to view this correspondence is by the principle that we can describe a metric ‘up to its symmetries’, in this case translations and/or scaling.
When we introduce a metric by defining a weight function, we assume (unless stated otherwise) that the corresponding metric is the translation-invariant metric as defined above.

When a metric or weight function takes values in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, it is called an
integral metric
or
integral weight function
respectively. Likewise, a metric or weight function is
finitely valued
if it takes values solely in
{\mathbb{R}}
(and not
\infty
).

2.2

Weighted vector spaces and contractions

Groups/modules/vector spaces/etc. endowed with a weight function are referred to as
weighted
groups/modules/vector spaces/etc. We refer the reader to
[
14
]
for an overview of these objects in a categorical context.
Of particular interest to us are
finite dimensional
weighted vector spaces over
finite fields
, although some of the theory in this section is also applicable to other vector spaces.

Let
(V,\operatorname{wt}_{V})
,
(W,\operatorname{wt}_{W})
be finite weighted vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. A linear map
f:V\to W
is called a
(weak) contraction
if
\operatorname{wt}_{V}(v)\geq\operatorname{wt}_{W}(f(v))
for all
v\in V
. In the special case that
\operatorname{wt}_{V}(v)=\operatorname{wt}_{W}(f(v))
for all
v\in V
, then
f
must be injective and we call
f
a
(linear)
embedding
. If a linear
f:V\to W
is both an embedding and a bijection, then
f
is a
(linear) isometry
or
isomorphism
, and the finite weighted vector spaces
(V,\operatorname{wt}_{V})
and
(W,\operatorname{wt}_{W})
are called
(linearly) isometric
or
isomorphic
indicated with the notation
(V,\operatorname{wt}_{V})\cong(W,\operatorname{wt}_{W})
.
Note that linear isometries coincide with the usual category-theoretic notion of isomorphisms, i.e., contractions
f:V\to W
for which there exists a contraction
g:W\to V
such that
g\circ f=\operatorname{id}_{V}
and
f\circ g=\operatorname{id}_{W}
, where
\operatorname{id}_{V}
and
\operatorname{id}_{W}
are the identity maps on
V
and
W
, respectively.

Whenever
V\leq W
is a linear subspace, we endow
V
(unless stated otherwise) with the weight
\operatorname{wt}_{W}|_{V}
of
W
restricted to
V
, and call
V
simply a
subspace
(in the metric sense) of
W
.

We highlight an important elementary construction of weight functions on finite vector spaces that are induced by surjective linear maps:

Definition 3
.

Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a surjective linear map from a finite weighted vector space
(X,\operatorname{wt}_{X})
to a finite vector space
Y
. The
quotient weight/metric
on
Y
(with respect to
\varphi
) is defined as

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi}(y):=\min\{\operatorname{wt}_{X}(w)\ :\ w\in\varphi^{-1}(y)\}

for all
y\in Y
.

Remark
.

We can naturally generalize to quotients with respect to any linear map
\varphi
by setting
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi}(y):=\infty
whenever
\varphi^{-1}(y)
is empty.

Observation 4
.

Note that the quotient weight satisfies the following easy-to-check properties:

1.

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi}
is a weight function satisfying
(W0) - (W3)
.

2.

If
\operatorname{wt}_{X}
is scale-invariant satisfying
(W4)
, then so is
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi}
.

3.

If
\operatorname{wt}_{X}
is integral, then so is
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi}
.

4.

\varphi

(X,\operatorname{wt}_{X})
to
(Y,\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi})

Moreover, Theorem

states that the quotient weight satisfies a universal property similar to quotients of other types of objects, e.g. groups, modules, vector spaces, topological spaces, etc.

Theorem 5
.

The quotient weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi}
with respect to a surjective linear map
\varphi:(X,\operatorname{wt}_{X})\twoheadrightarrow Y

1.

(Maximality)
The quotient weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi}
is the largest weight on
Y
making

a contraction: for any other weight

such that

is a contraction, the identity map

3.1

Why projective metrics?

As we will later see in the examples in subsection
3.3
, many of the classical metrics such as Hamming metric, rank metric, discrete metric, combinatorial metric etc. are projective metrics. In this section we further justify the study of projective metrics by showing that a metric endowed with some ‘natural’ properties are projective metrics: while metrics as defined in Definition
1
are very general, the definition of metrics used in practice often arises from the type of errors which occur on a specific communication channel.

For example, let
V
be a finite dimensional vector space over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and consider the transmission of vectors from
V
over some noisy channel that adds error vectors. For every vector
v\in V
, let
{\mathcal{E}}(v)\subset V
be the set of possible ‘elementary’ errors (e.g. a one symbol change) which may occur at
v\in V
. Any error vector added during transmission is a sum of these elementary errors, and we always get a metric on
V
by defining the distance between two vectors
v_{1},\;v_{2}\in V
as the shortest path of elementary errors needed to transform one vector into the other.

More precisely, a path of elementary errors from
v_{1}
to
v_{2}
is a sequence
(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n})
such that
e_{i}\in{\mathcal{E}}(v_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}e_{i})
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
v_{2}=v_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}
. A distance function or metric corresponding to these elementary errors is then defined as

d(v_{1},v_{2})\coloneqq\min\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid\text{ there exists an elementary error path from $v_{1}$ to $v_{2}$ of length $n$}\}.

Metrics defined this way will always be integral and, since any path of errors between
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
of length
d(v_{1},v_{2})
is minimal by definition of
d
, we have that
d
is an integrally convex metric:

Definition 7
.

An integral metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is
(integrally) convex
if one of the following equivalent conditions holds:

1.

For all
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
with
d(v_{1},v_{2})<\infty
:

\displaystyle d(v_{1},v_{2})=

\displaystyle\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ \mid\text{ there exists a sequence }(v_{1}=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=v_{2})\text{ with $x_{i}\in V$ }

\displaystyle\text{}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{ such that }\ d(x_{k},x_{k+1})=1\ \text{ for all }\ k=0,\ldots,n-1\}.

2.

For all
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
with
d(v_{1},v_{2})<\infty
and all
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,d(v_{1},v_{2})\}
, there exists an
x\in V
such that

d(v_{1},x)=i\quad\text{ and }\quad d(x,v_{2})=d(v_{1},v_{2})-i.

See Appendix
E
for the proof of the equivalence of these two conditions.

An alternative approach (see also
[
23
]
) to defining a metric in practice is to capture how much error can occur while still allowing correct decoding of a received vector, or at least detecting that an error has occurred beyond a recoverable threshold. We will briefly discuss some concepts informally here, and refer to Appendix
E
for all formal definitions, statements, and proofs.

The
correction capability

\tau(v_{1},v_{2})
(see Definition
E.1
) is a measure of the maximum error magnitude for which, given a received vector
v
, we can always determine whether the original transmitted vector was
v_{1}
or
v_{2}
. For many metrics
d
there is a direct relationship between
\tau(v_{1},v_{2})
and
d(v_{1},v_{2})
: we say that
d
is
correction-normal
if for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in V

\tau(v_{1},v_{2})=\left\lfloor\frac{d(v_{1},v_{2})-1}{2}\right\rfloor

holds. Although well-known metrics are often correction-normal, there are useful metrics (e.g.
[
1
]
) for which the above equality does not hold.

We are also interested in the number of errors
\sigma(v_{1},v_{2})
for which we can detect that an error has occurred but cannot determine whether the original vector was
v_{1}
or
v_{2}
. Specifically, given a metric
d(v_{1},v_{2})
associated with the error,
\sigma(v_{1},v_{2})
is exactly equal to the minimal
d(v,v_{1})
, where
v\in V
is such that
d(v,v_{1})=d(v,v_{2})
, provided such a
v
exists. Analogous to the Euclidean case, if the points equidistant from
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
lie on the intersection of spheres centered at
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
with radius equal to half the distance between them (when the distance is even; otherwise, the intersection is empty since the distance is an integer), then we say that the metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is
equal-detection normal
. For any greater number than
\sigma(v_{1},v_{2})
of errors,
v
may become closer to
v_{2}
even if the original vector was
v_{1}
. Conversely, for any smaller number of errors,
v
would remain within the ball of radius
\sigma(v_{1},v_{2})
centered at
v_{1}
, allowing for correct decoding. We refer to
\sigma(v_{1},v_{2})
as the
equal-detection threshold
(see Definition
E.2
). Furthermore, a metric is
equal-detection normal
if the distance between vectors satisfies

d(v_{1},v_{2})=2\sigma(v_{1},v_{2})

whenever
\sigma(v_{1},v_{2})
is defined.

As a bridge between the first approach, where a metric measures shortest paths of elementary errors, leading to the notion of convexity, and the second approach, where a metric measures the correction and detection of errors, leading to correction- and equal-detection normality, the following proposition states that these two approaches in fact coincide (see Appendix
E
):

Proposition 8
.

An integral metric is convex if and only if it is correction-normal and equal-detection normal.

While integrality and convexity follow naturally from a definition of a metric arising from elementary errors, the following two properties only hold for a subset of all possible integral convex metrics, but are nevertheless natural in many applications:

•

Equality of the elementary error sets for all vector
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
, i.e.
{\mathcal{E}}(v_{1})={\mathcal{E}}(v_{2})
. Then the corresponding metric is
translation invariant
,
(M4)
;

•

The elementary error sets being closed under change of symbols, i.e., if
e\in{\mathcal{E}}(v_{1})
, then
\alpha e\in{\mathcal{E}}(v_{1})
for any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{0\}
. The corresponding metric is
scale invariant
,
(M5)
.

The following proposition, proved in Appendix
E
, comes directly from the equivalent definition
7
of convexity and justifies the study of projective metrics:

Proposition 9
.

A metric is projective if and only if it is integral, convex, scale- and translation-invariant.

Furthermore, in Section
5
, we show as a corollary of a more general result, that even metrics with weaker assumptions, namely those that are not convex, can be seen as submetrics of a projective metric on a larger ambient space.

Corollary 10
.

Any integral scale-translation-invariant metric space can be linearly embedded in a larger projective metric space.

We interpret this as follows: among all possible integral scale-translation-invariant metrics, the convex ones, i.e. projective metrics, can be used for good ambient spaces. The remaining metrics are then subspaces or codes in these ambient spaces.

3.2

Projective weight functions and projective metrics

In this subsection, we present the main definitions regarding projective metrics. For the remainder of this section, let
V
be a finite dimensional vector space over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Definition 11
.

Given a subset of pairwise linearly independent vectors
\mathcal{F}\subset V
, the
projective weight

\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)
of
x\in V
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
is defined as the minimum cardinality of a subset
I\subset\mathcal{F}
that satisfies
x\in\left\langle I\right\rangle
, where
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle
denotes the linear span in
V
.
Otherwise, if
x\notin\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle
, we set
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x):=\infty
as convention. In short,

\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x):=\min\left(\{\left|I\right|\ :\ I\subset\mathcal{F},\,x\in\left\langle I\right\rangle\}\cup\{\infty\}\right).

The function
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}:V\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
sending
x
to
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)
, is called the
projective weight function
.

Remark
.

Note that we only require
pairwise
linear independency, i.e.
0\notin\mathcal{F}
and if
x,y\in\mathcal{F}
are distinct, then
x\neq\lambda y
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Yet any three or more vectors in
\mathcal{F}
may be linear dependent.

The term ‘projective weight’ arises from the fact that replacing any element
f\in\mathcal{F}
with
\lambda f
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
, results in the same weight function. Consequently, a projective weight function can equivalently be defined by a subset of points
\mathcal{F}
in the projective space

\mathbb{P}(V)=(V\setminus\{0\})/\sim\quad\text{ with }\quad x\sim y\,\Leftrightarrow\,x=\lambda y\quad\text{for some }\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}

for
x,y\in V\setminus\{0\}
. This projective space is equivalently described by the set
\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V)
of 1-dimensional subspaces
\left\langle x\right\rangle\subset V
, since
x\sim y\Leftrightarrow\left\langle x\right\rangle=\left\langle y\right\rangle
. For this reason we will sometimes refer to
\mathcal{F}
as a subset of
\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V)
.

The set
\mathcal{F}
is called the
projective point family
of
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
. In general we will assume that
\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle=V
, in which case we call
\mathcal{F}
a
spanning family
. The reason for this assumption is that the weight function
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
is finitely valued whenever
\mathcal{F}
is a spanning family. Moreover, the family
\mathcal{F}
is uniquely determined by the projective weight function
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
:

Observation 12
.

Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\subset\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V)
be such that
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\cdot)=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{G}}(\cdot)
, then
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{G}
.

This follows directly from the fact that for every
x\in V\setminus\{0\}
we have

\left\langle x\right\rangle\in\mathcal{F}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)=1\;\Leftrightarrow\;\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)=1\;\Leftrightarrow\;\left\langle x\right\rangle\in\mathcal{G}
.

Gabidulin and Simonis in
[
12
]
first introduced the concept of projective weight functions as a special case of weight functions generated by families of subspaces (referred to as
\mathcal{F}
-weights).

Definition 13
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\}\subset{\mathcal{P}}(V)
be a family of subsets of
V
. Then, if
x\in\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\left\langle F\right\rangle
, the
\bm{\mathcal{F}}
-weight

\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)
of a vector
x\in V
is defined as the minimum cardinality of a set
I\subseteq[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}]
that satisfies

x\in\sum_{i\in I}\left\langle F_{i}\right\rangle.

Otherwise, if
x\notin\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\left\langle F\right\rangle
, we define
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)
as
\infty
. The function
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\cdot):V\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
sending
x
to
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)
is called the
\bm{\mathcal{F}}
-weight function
or the weight function generated by
\mathcal{F}
.

A projective weight associated to a family
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V)
is also trivially an
\mathcal{F}
-weight. The converse is also true; in Appendix
C
we show that projective weights and
\mathcal{F}
-weights are, in fact, equivalent. This is summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 14
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(V)
be a family of subsets of
V
. Then the
projective point form
of
\mathcal{F}
, defined as

\mathsf{ppf}(\mathcal{F}):=\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(\left\langle F\right\rangle)\;\;\subseteq\;\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V),

spans the same subspace spanned by
\mathcal{F}
, that is:
\sum_{G\in\mathsf{ppf}(\mathcal{F})}G=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\left\langle F\right\rangle
and generates the same weight function as
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.

\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathsf{ppf}(\mathcal{F})}(x)

for all
x\in V
.

We observe that a projective weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
with spanning family
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\}
can also be seen as a quotient weight (see Definition
3
) of the Hamming weight on the larger space
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
, given by the linear map
\varphi:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}\to V
sending the
i
-th basis vector
e_{i}
to
f_{i}
. This aspect of projective weight functions will be further discussed in depth in section
4
.

The distance function
d_{\mathcal{F}}(x,y):=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y-x)
corresponding to a projective weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
is called a
projective metric
. Since
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies all the axioms
(W0)
-
(W4)
of a scale-invariant weight function, any projective metric is a scale-translation-invariant metric. It turns out that for a scale-translation-invariant metric, integral convexity is necessary and sufficient for the metric to be projective.

See
9

Proof.

Consider a projective metric
d_{\mathcal{F}}(\cdot,\cdot)
with spanning family
\mathcal{F}\subset V
, and let
x\in V
with
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)<\infty
. Let
a
be any integer between
0
and
d:=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)
. Due to translation-invariance, it suffices to show the existence of a
y\in V
with
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y)=a
and
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x-y)=d-a
to prove convexity.

By definition there is a minimum size subset
I\subset\mathcal{F}
of size
d
such that
x=\sum_{f\in I}a_{f}f
for some coefficients
a_{f}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
J\subset I
with
|J|=a
. Let
y=\sum_{f\in J}a_{f}f
and
y^{\prime}=\sum_{f\in I\setminus J}a_{f}f
. Then
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y)\leq a
and
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y^{\prime})\leq|I\setminus J|=d-a
, and
d=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y+y^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y)+\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y^{\prime})\leq a+d-a=d
. Thus
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y)=a
,
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y^{\prime})=d-a
. From this we have
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y)=a
and
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(x-y)=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y^{\prime})=d-a
.

For the converse, consider an integral convex, scale- and translation-invariant metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
with corresponding weight function
\operatorname{wt}(\cdot)
. Let
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
with
d(v_{1},v_{2})<\infty
. Then
\operatorname{wt}(\cdot)
is projective with spanning family equal to
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\{v\in V\mid\operatorname{wt}(v)=1\}
, since by definition of integral convexity, there is a minimal length path
S=(v_{1}=x_{0},\ldots,x_{d(x_{1},x_{2})}=v_{2})
with
1=d(x_{i},x_{i+1})=\operatorname{wt}(x_{i+1}-x_{i})
for all
i=0,\ldots,d(x_{1},x_{2})-1
. Thus
x_{i+1}-x_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
and a minimal path
S
is equivalent to a minimal linear combination of elements in
\mathcal{F}
adding to
v_{2}-v_{1}
.
∎

Recall that two weight functions
\operatorname{wt}_{1}(\cdot)
,
\operatorname{wt}_{2}(\cdot)
on
V
are linearly isometric or isomorphic, denoted
\operatorname{wt}_{1}(\cdot)\cong\operatorname{wt}_{2}(\cdot)
, if there exists an invertible linear map
L\in\mathsf{GL}(V)
such that
\operatorname{wt}_{1}(x)=\operatorname{wt}_{2}(L(x))
for all
x\in V
. As convexity and scale/translation invariance are defined solely in terms of the weight function, addition, and scaling, all of which are preserved by linear isometries, it is not surprising that the property of ‘being a projective metric space’ is well-defined with respect to isometries, a simple yet necessary requirement for this property to be natural. This is summarized in the following proposition, with a short proof provided in Appendix
B
:

Proposition 15
.

Let
\operatorname{wt}(\cdot)
be a weight function and
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\cdot)
a projective weight function on
V
with
\mathcal{F}
its projective point family. Then

\operatorname{wt}(\cdot)\simeq\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\cdot)

if and only if
\operatorname{wt}(\cdot)
is also a projective weight function with projective point family

L(\mathcal{F}):=\left\{L(\left\langle f\right\rangle)\;\,|\;\left\langle f\right\rangle\in\mathcal{F}\right\}

for some invertible linear map
L\in\mathsf{GL}(V)
.

Observation 16
.

Seeing
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\subset\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V)
as sets of points in the projective space, then
w_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}\cong w_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}
if and only if there exists a homography mapping
\mathcal{F}_{1}
to
\mathcal{F}_{2}
.

Lastly, to any projective metric
d_{\mathcal{F}}
on
V
with family
\mathcal{F}
we can naturally define the
associated Cayley graph

\Gamma(V,S_{\mathcal{F}})
with vertex set
V
and generating set
S_{\mathcal{F}}:=\{\lambda f\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\},\,f\in\mathcal{F}\}
. We can then view projective metrics in the context of distances on Cayley graphs:

Proposition 17
.

The projective metric
d_{\mathcal{F}}
on a vector space
V
is equal to the graph distance on the associated Cayley graph
\Gamma(V,S_{\mathcal{F}})
.

3.3

Examples

In this section, we provide several examples of projective metrics on the vector space
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}
and introduce various operations on the spanning family to observe how more complex metrics can be obtained from simpler ones. Most of these examples were already noted in
[
11
]
; we report them here for ease of reference. Let
\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}\}
be a basis of
V
.

In many cases, the set
\mathcal{F}
defining a projective weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
is derived from a given set of vectors
F\subset V
, which may not necessarily be pairwise linearly independent. The set
\mathcal{F}
is then chosen as a maximal subset of
F
such that all its vectors are pairwise independent.

For simplicity, we adopt a slight abuse of notation by identifying
\mathcal{F}
with
F
. However, it is important to note that the size of the weight obtained from
F
can be strictly smaller than
|F|
, as non-independent vectors are excluded to ensure pairwise independence.

Example 18
.

Hamming Metric.
The Hamming metric
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}
can be shown to be a projective metric by simply taking the spanning family to be the basis
\mathcal{B}
itself.
Here, the weight of a vector
x\in V
is the number of nonzero coordinates respect to the basis
\mathcal{B}
.

Example 19
.

Discrete Metric.
The discrete metric is defined as:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Dis}}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x=0,\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This is a projective metric with the spanning family equal to
\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V)
, the set of all one-dimensional subspaces of
V
. The discrete metric assigns weight
1
to any nonzero vector, satisfying the projective metric definition.

Example 20
.

Combinatorial Metrics (over finite fields).
(See
[
20
]
). A combinatorial weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Combinatorial}}
is an
\mathcal{F}
-weight (see Definition
13
), where
\mathcal{F}
is a family of subspaces
\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}\}
with each subspace
F_{j}
of the form

F_{j}=\langle I_{j}\rangle\leq V

for some
I_{j}\subset\mathcal{B}
, where
\mathcal{B}
is a basis of
V
. Since by Proposition
14
all
\mathcal{F}
-metrics are projective metrics, combinatorial metric are as well.

Example 21
.

Phase Rotation Metric.
(See
[
7
]
). The phase rotation metric is a projective metric with spanning family:

\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}\cup\{w\},

where
w\coloneqq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}v
.

Example 22
.

Rank Metric.
Consider
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times m}
, the vector space of
n\times m
matrices. The rank metric of a matrix
A
is defined as:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Rank}}(A)\coloneqq\operatorname{rank}(A).

Any matrix of rank
r
can be written as the sum of
r
rank-1 matrices, thus the rank metric is a projective metric with spanning family

\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\{A\in V\mid A\text{ has rank equal to }1\}.

Example 23
.

Sum-Rank Metric.
Let
V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}\times m_{i}}
. The sum-rank metric is defined as:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Sum-rank}}(A)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Rank}}(A_{i}),

where
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})
and each
A_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{i}\times m_{i}}
. This metric is projective with spanning family
\mathcal{F}=\{A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})\in V\mid A\text{ has sum-rank weight equal to }1\}
.

Example 24
.

Row Metric
and
Column Metric
. For
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times m}
, the row metric assigns weight based on the minimum number of rows needed to cover all nonzero entries of a matrix. Specifically, the row weight is defined as:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Row}}(A)=\min\{|R|\mid R\subseteq[n]\text{ such that if }A_{ij}\neq 0,\text{ then }i\in R\}.

The row metric is a projective metric with spanning family consisting of matrices having all zero entries except for those in a single row. The column metric is defined as
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Column}}(A)\coloneqq\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Row}}(A^{T})
.

Example 25
.

Cover Metric (or Term-Rank Metric
[
8
]
).
For
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times m}
, the cover metric assigns weight based on the minimum number of rows or columns needed to cover all non-zero entries of a matrix. Specifically, the cover weight is defined as:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Cover}}(A)=\min\{|R|+|C|\mid R\subseteq[n],C\subseteq[m],\text{ such that if }A_{ij}\neq 0,\text{ then }i\in R\text{ or }j\in C\}.

The cover metric is a projective metric with the spanning family:

\mathcal{F}=\{A\in V\mid\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Cover}}(A)=1\},

which consists of matrices having all zero entries except for those in a single row or a single column. The cover metric is an example of a non-trivial combinatorial metric, where the sets
I_{j}
in Example
20
are sets of coordinates in single rows or columns.

Example 26
.

Tensor Rank Metric
. (See
[
2
,
21
]
).
Let
\mathcal{T}
be a
d
-tensor in the space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
. The tensor rank weight of
\mathcal{T}
, denoted as
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Rank}}(\mathcal{T})
, is defined as the minimum number
r
of rank-1 tensors needed to express
\mathcal{T}
as their sum:

\mathcal{T}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}u^{(1,k)}\boldsymbol{\otimes}u^{(2,k)}\boldsymbol{\otimes}\cdots\boldsymbol{\otimes}u^{(d,k)},

where
u^{(h,k)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n_{k}}
for
h=1,2,\ldots,d
, and
\boldsymbol{\otimes}
denotes the tensor (outer) product given by

(u^{(1,k)}\boldsymbol{\otimes}u^{(2,k)}\boldsymbol{\otimes}\cdots\boldsymbol{\otimes}u^{(d,k)})_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}}=\prod_{h=1}^{d}u_{i_{h}}^{(h,k)}.

Clearly the tensor rank metric is a projective metric having as spanning family the set of rank-1 tensors. The tensor rank metric for
d=2
, i.e. for 2-tensors, coindices with the rank metric for matrices.

We now define the following operations on projective metrics:

Definition 27

(
Union of Weights
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset V
and
{\mathcal{G}}\subset V
define the projective weights
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
\operatorname{wt}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
. The union of their weights is defined as:

\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}\cup\operatorname{wt}_{{\mathcal{G}}}:=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}\cup{\mathcal{G}}}.

which is a projective metric on
V

A particular case of the union of projective weights is given as follows. Let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be two vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, with two projective metrics given by
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subset V_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}\subset V_{2}
. Define
V\coloneqq V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
. By considering the natural inclusions
i_{1}:V_{1}\to V
and
i_{2}:V_{2}\to V
, we can define the weights
\operatorname{wt}_{i_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{1})}
and
\operatorname{wt}_{i_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{2})}
on
V
.

We then define the
disjoint union
or
sum
of the projective weights
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}
and
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}
as:

\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}\sqcup\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}\coloneqq\operatorname{wt}_{i_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{1})}\cup\operatorname{wt}_{i_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{2})}.

(1)

This construction provides a useful method to create simple projective weights in various dimensions by combining weights from different vector spaces.
An other useful operation on projective weights is given by the following:

Definition 28

(
Tensor Product of Weights
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N_{1}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N_{2}}
be two families defining projective weights
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
\operatorname{wt}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
. The tensor product of their weights is defined as:

\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}\boldsymbol{\otimes}\operatorname{wt}_{{\mathcal{G}}}:=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}\boldsymbol{\otimes}{\mathcal{G}}},

where

\mathcal{F}\boldsymbol{\otimes}{\mathcal{G}}:=\{\langle f\boldsymbol{\otimes}g\rangle\mid\langle f\rangle\in\mathcal{F},\langle g\rangle\in{\mathcal{G}}\}.

For the tensor product we regularly use the
outer
tensor product
\boldsymbol{\otimes}_{\operatorname{out}}
, for two row vectors
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N_{1}}
,
w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N_{2}}
given by
v\boldsymbol{\otimes}_{\operatorname{out}}w\coloneqq v^{\top}w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}}
, or the
Kronecker
tensor product
\boldsymbol{\otimes}_{\operatorname{Kron}}
, for two matrices
A\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n_{1}\times m_{1}}
,
B\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n_{2}\times m_{2}}
given by

A\boldsymbol{\otimes}_{\operatorname{Kron}}B:=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}B&A_{12}B&\cdots&A_{1m_{1}}B\\
\\
A_{21}B&A_{22}B&\cdots&A_{2m_{1}}B\\
\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\\
A_{n_{1}1}B&A_{n_{1}2}B&\cdots&A_{n_{1}m_{1}}B\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n_{1}n_{2}\times m_{1}m_{2}}.

We observe that the more “complex” metrics introduced previously can be constructed from simpler ones using the following operations:

Proposition 29
.

The following relations hold:

1.

Rank Weight:

Consider the discrete weights
\operatorname{wt}^{n}_{\operatorname{Dis}}
and
\operatorname{wt}^{m}_{\operatorname{Dis}}
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
, respectively, and the rank weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Rank}}
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times m}
. Then:

\operatorname{wt}^{n}_{\operatorname{Dis}}\boldsymbol{\otimes}_{\operatorname{out}}\operatorname{wt}^{m}_{\operatorname{Dis}}=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Rank}}.

2.

Sum-Rank Weight:

Let
V_{1}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{l}
with Hamming weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}
and
V_{2}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times m}
with rank weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Rank}}
. The Sum-Rank weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Sum-rank}}
on
V={\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n\times lm}
satisfies:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}\boldsymbol{\otimes}_{\operatorname{Kron}}\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Rank}}=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Sum-rank}}.

3.

Row Weight:

Let
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}
be the Hamming weight on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Dis}}
the discrete weight on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
. The Row weight on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n\times m}
satisfies:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}\boldsymbol{\otimes}_{\operatorname{out}}\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Dis}}=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Row}}.

4.

Column Weight:

Let
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Dis}}
be the discrete weight on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}
the Hamming weight on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
. The Column weight on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n\times m}
satisfies:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Dis}}\boldsymbol{\otimes}_{\operatorname{out}}\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Column}}.

5.

Cover Weight:

The cover weight, combining row and column weights, satisfies:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Row}}\cup\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Column}}=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Cover}}.

Proof.

We provide the proof for the relation
\operatorname{wt}_{\text{Dis}}\boldsymbol{\otimes}\operatorname{wt}_{\text{Dis}}=\operatorname{wt}_{\text{Rank}}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
and
\mathcal{F}_{m}=\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m})
be the spanning families inducing the discrete metrics on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
, respectively. Let
\mathcal{F}=\{A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times m}\mid\operatorname{rank}(A)=1\}
be the spanning family inducing the rank metric.

For all
\left\langle v\right\rangle\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\left\langle w\right\rangle\in\mathcal{F}_{m}
, the tensor product
v\boldsymbol{\otimes}w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times m}
is a rank-1 matrix. Thus,
\mathcal{F}_{n}\boldsymbol{\otimes}\mathcal{F}_{m}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. Conversely, any rank-1 matrix
A
can be written as
v\boldsymbol{\otimes}w
for some
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and
w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{F}_{n}\boldsymbol{\otimes}\mathcal{F}_{m}=\mathcal{F}
, which implies
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Dis}}\boldsymbol{\otimes}\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Dis}}=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{Rank}}
.

The proofs for the other relations follow similarly by demonstrating that the operations on the spanning families on the LHS correspond to the resulting spanning family for the metric on the RHS of the equation.
∎

Projective metrics as quotients

In this section we go in-depth into how projective metrics can be seen as quotient weights, and we will see that this induces many of their properties. The corresponding family of maps inducing projective weights were introduced in
[
11
]
as
parent functions
. In the following, let
V
be an
N
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\mathcal{F}=\{\left\langle f_{1}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left\langle f_{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\right\rangle\}\subset\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V)
a projective point family. Recall that we use the shorthand notation
[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}]
for the integer set
\{1,\ldots,{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\}
.

Definition 30
.

(Parent functions and Parent codes of
\mathcal{F}
).
A
parent function
of
\mathcal{F}
is a linear map
\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}\to V
such that

\left\{\left\langle\varphi(e_{i})\right\rangle\mid i\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}]\right\}=\mathcal{F}

where
e_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
.
I.e., there is a permutation
\sigma
of
[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}]
and scalars
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
such that for all
i\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}]

\varphi(e_{i})=\lambda_{i}f_{\sigma(i)}.

A
parent code
of
\mathcal{F}
is a code
\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
such that
\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)
where
\varphi
is a parent function of
\mathcal{F}
.

The main importance of parent functions is their connecting role between projective metrics and the Hamming metric. Namely, a projective weight function is equal to the quotient weight induced by a corresponding parent function, which follows by direct calculation.
Moreover, a parent code has minimum Hamming distance
\geq 3
, a direct result from the fact that the vectors in a projective point family are pairwise linear dependent.

Proposition 31
.

Consider the space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
endowed with the Hamming metric. If
\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\to V
is a parent function of
\mathcal{F}
, then

\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi},

and the parent code
\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)
has minimum Hamming distance

d_{\operatorname{H}}(\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}):=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\scalebox{0.8}{\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\{d_{\operatorname{H}}(x,y)\}\geq 3.

As often happens with projective metrics, a notion for metrics corresponds to an ‘equivalent’ notion for (parent) codes.
For example, as we will see, knowing the sphere sizes or weight distribution of a projective metric is equivalent to knowing the coset weight distribution of a parent code; a code is perfect for a projective metric if its inverse image under a parent function is a perfect code containing a parent code; decoding algorithms for a parent code are equivalent to an algorithm that calculates the projective weight, and so on.

4.1

Parent codes and parent functions

Observe that a function
\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}\to V
is a parent function of a projective weight if and only if
\left\{\varphi(e_{i})\mid i\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}]\right\}
are pairwise linearly independent. Fixing a basis in
V
, we get a correspondence between parent functions from
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
to
V
and matrices of size
{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\times N
with pairwise linearly independent rows.
Since any non-trivial projective point set has more than one parent function, and thus parent code, it is natural to ask what relationship is there between different parent codes associated to the same projective point set.

As briefly noted in
[
12
]
we see that they are Hamming equivalent: the set of parent codes of a projective metrics is equal to the Hamming equivalence class of any of the parent codes. We say that
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}\subset V
are
Hamming equivalent
,
\mathcal{C}_{1}\equiv_{\operatorname{H}}\mathcal{C}_{2}
, if there exists
L:V\to V
such that
L(\mathcal{C}_{1})=L(\mathcal{C}_{2})
and
L
is a linear isometry respect to the Hamming metric. It is a known fact that if
L
is a linear isometry respect to the Hamming metric then it can be written as the product of a permutation matrix
P
and an invertible, diagonal matrix
\Lambda
.

Observation 32
.

All parent codes of a projective weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
are Hamming equivalent.

Proof.

Let
\varphi,\psi
be two parent functions of
\mathcal{F}=\{\langle f_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle f_{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}\rangle\}
, and assume without loss of generality that
\varphi(e_{i})=f_{i}
for all
i\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}]
. Then there exists a permutation
\sigma\in S_{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
(the symmetric group), and scalars
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
for
i\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}]
such that
\psi(e_{i})=\lambda_{i}f_{\sigma(i)}=\varphi(\lambda_{i}e_{\sigma(i)})
. Thus
\psi=\varphi\circ\Lambda\circ P
, where
P
and
\Lambda
are the linear applications associated respectively to the permutation of coordinates induced by
\sigma
and the scaling of the coordinates by the
\lambda_{i}
’s. Thus,
\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)=\Lambda P\operatorname{Ker}(\psi)
and so
\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)\equiv_{\operatorname{H}}\operatorname{Ker}(\psi)
.
∎

Viceversa, another natural question to ask is: in what relationship are two projective metrics such that the corresponding parents codes coincide? It turns out that this happens precisely when two projective metrics are linearly isometric or isomorphic (see also Proposition
15
).
We now show that there is a natural bijection between the following sets of equivalence classes:

•

The set
\overline{Pr}_{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}(V)
of isomorphism classes of projective metrics
\overline{\operatorname{wt}}
on
V
induced by projective point families of cardinality
{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
;

•

The set
\overline{\mathsf{Gr}}_{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}-N}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}})_{d_{\operatorname{H}}\geq 3}
of Hamming-equivalence classes of
({\bm{\mathsf{N}}}-N)
-dimensional subspaces
\overline{\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
with minimum Hamming distance
d_{\operatorname{H}}\geq 3
.

This was already noted
[
12
]
; here we formalize the result.

Proposition 33
.

There exists a natural bijection between isomorphism classes of projective metrics and Hamming-equivalence classes of subspaces:

\displaystyle\overline{\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}}\qquad\

In conclusion, isomorphic projective metrics are obtained from quotient maps with equivalent kernels with minimum Hamming distance at least 3. These kernels, the parent codes, constitute the linear dependencies in the corresponding projective point family. Namely, if
\mathcal{F}=\{\langle f_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle f_{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}\rangle\}
is a family and
\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\to V
a parent function with
\varphi(e_{i})=f_{i}
and parent code

\wp

, then a codeword
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{\bm{\mathsf{N}}})=\sum_{i=1}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}c_{i}e_{i}\in\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}
implies
\sum_{i=1}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}c_{i}f_{i}=0
.

Example 34

(
Phase rotation metric
)
.

Let
N\geq 2
and consider the projective point family
\mathcal{F}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{N},-\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}
. Then the linear map
\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N+1}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}:e_{i}\mapsto e_{i}\text{ for }i\in[N]\text{ and }e_{N+1}\mapsto-\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}

represented by the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
-1&-1&\cdots&-1\\
\end{pmatrix}

is a parent function for
\mathcal{F}
with parent code equal to the repetition code
\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}=\left\langle(1\,1\,\ldots\,1)\right\rangle\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N+1}
.

Finally, we consider quotients of linear maps whose kernels have minimum Hamming distance less than 3. Interestingly, this does not yield any other types of metrics.
We show that for any map
\xi
coming from the Hamming metric inducing a quotient weight, there exists a parent function
\varphi
inducing the same quotient weight, and with
d_{\operatorname{H}}(\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi))\geq 3
; that is, any quotient weight of the Hamming metric is a projective metric.

In the following, given two finite vector spaces
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}
with standard bases
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}\}
and
\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}\}
respectively, a
weakly row monomial map
from
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}
is a linear map
\psi
such that for every
i\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}]

\psi(e_{i})=\lambda_{i}e^{\prime}_{j_{i}}

for some (possibly zero)
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
j_{i}\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}]
. Such a map can be represented by a
weakly row monomial matrix
, i.e. a matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}\times{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}
whose rows each contain at most one non-zero element.

Our interest in these maps comes from the following factorization property of matrices:

Proposition 35
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}\times N}
be any matrix. Then

M=R\cdot P,

with
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}\times{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}
a weakly row monomial matrix and
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}\times N}
a matrix whose rows are all pairwise linear independent (and thus non-zero), for some
{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}\leq{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}
.
Equivalently, any map
\xi:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}\to{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{N}
is equal to the decomposition
\xi=\varphi\circ\psi
for some
\psi
weakly row monomial map from
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}\to{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}
and a parent function
\varphi
from
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}\to{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{N}
.

Lemma 36
.

Consider the finite vector spaces
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}
with standard bases
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}\}
and
\{e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}\}
respectively and
{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}\leq{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}
, both spaces endowed with the Hamming weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}
.
If
\psi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}
is a surjective weakly row monomial map, then

(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}},\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\psi})=(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}},\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}).

Proof.

By surjectivity, for each basis vector
e^{\prime}_{j}
there is some
e_{i}
and
\lambda\neq 0
such that
\psi(e_{i})=\lambda e^{\prime}_{j}
.
As permutation and scaling of the basis vectors preserves the Hamming metric, let us assume without loss of generality that for all
i\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}]

\psi(e_{i})=e^{\prime}_{i}
and
{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}>{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}
. Now, let
w=\sum_{i=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}w_{i}e^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}
be any vector and
v\in\psi^{-1}(w)
with
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(v)
minimal. We can write
v=\tilde{w}+r
with
\tilde{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}w_{i}e_{i}\in\psi^{-1}(w)
for some
r=\sum_{i=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}r_{i}e_{i}\in\operatorname{Ker}(\psi)
. We claim that
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(\sum_{i=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}r_{i}e_{i})\leq\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(\sum_{j={\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}+1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}r_{j}e_{j})
, which implies that

\displaystyle\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(v)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\tilde{w}+\sum_{i=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}r_{i}e_{i}\right)+\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\sum_{j={\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}+1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}r_{j}e_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\tilde{w}\right)-\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}r_{i}e_{i}\right)+\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\sum_{j={\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}+1}^{N_{1}}r_{j}e_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}\left(\tilde{w}\right)=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(w)

with equalities for
r=0
, and hence
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\psi}(w)=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(\tilde{w})=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(w)
as desired.

To prove our claim above, note that
\psi(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}r_{i}e^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{j={\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}+1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}r_{j}\psi(e_{j})=0
. By linear independence of the
e^{\prime}_{i}
’s, for each
i\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}]
with
r_{i}\neq 0
there must be at least one
j\in\{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}+1,\ldots,{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}\}
such that
r_{j}\neq 0
and
\psi(e_{j})=e^{\prime}_{i}
. So
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(\sum_{i=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}}r_{i}e_{i})=\left|\{i\in[{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}]\mid r_{i}\neq 0\}\right|\leq\left|\{j\in\{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}+1,\ldots,{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}\}\mid r_{j}\neq 0\}\right|=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(\sum_{j={\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{2}+1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}_{1}}r_{j}e_{j})
which concludes the proof.
∎

4.2

Projective isometries

4.3

Decoding of the parent code and projective weight algorithms.

For general families
\mathcal{F}
, it is computationally hard (NP-hard) to calculate the
\mathcal{F}
-weight of a vector. For example, in the context of tensor codes (see Example
26
and
[
2
,
21
]
), calculating the tensor rank of a
d
-tensor for
d\geq 3
is known to be NP-hard. However in this subsection we show that if a fast decoding algorithm for the parent code is known, it can be used to easily calculate the weight in the corresponding projective metric.

Definition 41
.

(Decoding algorithm)
Let
\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
be a linear code. A minimum distance decoder (for

\wp

in the Hamming metric) is an algorithm
f
that for any input
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
outputs a vector
f(y)\in\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}
satisfying
d_{\operatorname{H}}(f(y),y)=\min_{c^{\prime}\in\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}}d_{\operatorname{H}}(c^{\prime},y)
.

Definition 42
.

Let
C
be a subspace of a weighted vector space
(X,\operatorname{wt}_{X})
and
y\in X
. The
coset leader weight
of the coset
y+C
is given by

\operatorname{wt}_{X}(y+C):=\min\left\{\operatorname{wt}_{X}(x)\mid x\in y+C\right\}.

An element
x\in y+C
attaining this minimum is called a
coset leader
.

Observation 43
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V)
be a spanning family with corresponding parent function
\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\to V
and parent code
\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
, with
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
endowed with the Hamming weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}
.
Then an element
x\in y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}
is a coset leader of
y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}\in{\mathbb{F}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}_{q}}/{\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}}
if and only if
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(x)=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi(x))
. This follows directly from the interpretation of projective metrics as quotient weights:
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi(x))=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi(y))=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\varphi}(\varphi(y))=\min\{\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(x^{\prime})\mid x^{\prime}\in y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}\}=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})
.

This shows a direct relation between coset leader weights and projective weights. Together with minimum distance decoding, the problems of calculating these weights are equivalent, as descibed and often used in the works of Gabidulin and coauthors
[
11
,
12
,
10
]

Proposition 44
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots\}\subset\mathsf{Gr}_{1}(V)
be a spanning family with corresponding parent function
\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\to V
and parent code
\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
. Then the following problems are polynomial-time (in
{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
) reducible to each-other:

1.

Minimum distance decoding
: given
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
, find
c\in\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}
satisfying
d_{\operatorname{H}}(c,y)=\min_{c^{\prime}\in\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}}d_{\operatorname{H}}(c^{\prime},y)
;

2.

Coset leader finding
: in a given coset
y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
, find a coset leader
x
with respect to the Hamming weight.

3.

Projective weight calculation and representation
: given
v\in V
, find a linear combination
v=\alpha_{1}f_{i_{1}}+\alpha_{2}f_{i_{2}}+\ldots
of length
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(v)
with
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

1\Longleftrightarrow 2
. This equivalence is immediate under the correspondence
x=y-c
.

2\Longrightarrow 3
. Given a coset
y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}
, let
v:=\varphi(y)=\varphi(y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})
. If
v=\alpha_{1}f_{i_{1}}+\alpha_{2}f_{i_{2}}+\ldots
is a minimum linear combination of length
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(v)
, then
x=\sum_{j=1}^{\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(v)}\alpha_{j}e_{i_{j}}
is a coset leader in
\varphi^{-1}(v)=y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}
, where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
.

3\,\Longrightarrow 2
. Conversely, given
v\in V
, let
y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}:=\varphi^{-1}(v)
. If
x=\sum_{j=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}\alpha_{j}e_{i}
is a coset leader, then
v=\varphi(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}\alpha_{j}f_{i}
is a minimum linear combination with
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(v)
non-zero terms.

For both directions we used the above Observation
43
that the coset leader weight and the projective weight are equal.
∎

This proof also shows how to directly obtain a projective weight calculation algorithm from a minimum distance decoder
f
: given
v\in V
, let
y\in\varphi^{-1}(v)
. Then
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(v)=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(y-f(y))
.

5.2

Bijection between Hamming subspaces and projective metric spaces

Next, we demonstrate the natural bijection between equivalence classes of Hammming metric subspaces with
(V,\operatorname{wt}_{V})
as quotient, and equivalence classes of projective metric subspaces with
(V,\operatorname{wt}_{V})
as subspace.

Lemma 49
.

Let
\rho:(X,\operatorname{wt}_{X})\to(Y,\operatorname{wt}_{Y})
be an embedding and
\operatorname{\psi}:(Y,\operatorname{wt}_{Y})\to(Z,\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\operatorname{\psi}})
be a surjective linear map such that
\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{\psi})\subset\operatorname{Im}(\rho)
. Then, the quotient weight satisfies:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\operatorname{\psi}\circ\rho}|_{\operatorname{\psi}(\rho(X))}=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{quot},\operatorname{\psi}}|_{\operatorname{\psi}(\rho(X))}.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{\psi})\subset\operatorname{Im}(\rho)
, it follows that

\rho(\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{\psi}\circ\rho))=\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{\psi}).

Let
z\in\operatorname{\psi}(\rho(X))
, and let
x\in X
be such that
z=\operatorname{\psi}(\rho(x))
. Then, by the definition of the quotient weight:

\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{\psi}}(z)=\min_{y\in\rho(x)+\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{\psi})}\operatorname{wt}_{Y}(y)=\min_{x^{\prime}\in x+\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{\psi}\circ\rho)}\operatorname{wt}_{X}(x^{\prime})=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{\psi}\circ\rho}(z)

which completes the proof.
∎

.

Sphere sizes and matroids

The study of sphere sizes in projective metrics is fundamental to understanding error correction capabilities and their relationships with classical coding bounds. Sphere sizes play a crucial role in determining the packing efficiency of a code and its ability to correct errors.
A first relation between sphere sizes in projective metric and coset weight distributions of their parent codes has been found in
[
12
]
.

One of the most well-known theoretical bounds in coding theory is the
sphere-packing bound

[
22
]
or
Hamming bound
(
[
15
]
,
[
18
]
) providing a limit on the number of codewords a code can contain while still maintaining a certain minimum distance. Codes attaining the Hamming bound are called
perfect codes
. In subsection
6.2
, we discuss perfect codes in projective metrics, referencing the foundational work of Gabidulin and others on such constructions.

In this section, we give some basic results on sphere sizes and relate sphere sizes to the coset weight distribution of
parent codes
: for any code

\wp

there exists a projective weight whose sequence of sphere sizes is equal to the coset weight distribution of

\wp

and viceversa. Additionally in subsection
6.3
, we show how a
matroid
structure defined from the spanning family
\mathcal{F}
univocally describes sphere sizes of the projective metric
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Definition 55
.

Given a projective weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
on
V

and vector

, the

and

radius

\bm{t}

centered at

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{F}}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle:=\{x+v\ :\ v\in V\ \text{ and }\ \operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(v)=t\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\mathcal{F}}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle:=\{x+v\ :\ v\in V\ \text{ and }\ \operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(v)\leq t\}.

The following lemma says that the restriction of the parent function to the ball of radius
r
equal to half of the minimum distance of the parent code, behaves like an isometry:

Lemma 56
.

Let
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
be a projective weight on
V
with
\mathcal{F}=\{\langle f_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle f_{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\rangle\}
and
\varphi:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\to V
a parent function with parent code
\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)
. Let
r:=\left\lfloor\frac{d_{\operatorname{H}}(\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})-1}{2}\right\rfloor
and
x\in V

\varphi
is injective on the ball
\mathcal{B}^{H}_{r}(x)\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
, i.e.
if

are two vectors in
\mathcal{B}^{H}_{r}(x)
such that
\varphi(y)=\varphi(z)
, then
y=z
.
Moreover, if
y\in\mathcal{B}^{H}_{r}(0)
, then
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi(y))=\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(y)
.

Proof.

Let
y,z\in\mathcal{B}^{H}_{r}(x)
such that
\varphi(y)=\varphi(z)
. Then
y-z\in\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}
, but
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y-z)=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}((y-x)-(z-x))\leq\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y-x)+\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(z-x)\leq 2r=d_{\operatorname{H}}(\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})-1
, so
y=z
.

To show the second statement, let
y\in\mathcal{B}^{H}_{r}(0)
and suppose
y=\sum_{i=1}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}y_{i}e_{i}
.
Then we have
\varphi(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}y_{i}\varphi(e_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}y_{i}f_{i}
and thus
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi(y))\leq\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(y)
.
By definition of projective metrics there exists a
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{\bm{\mathsf{N}}})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
such that
\varphi(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}z_{i}f_{i}=\varphi(z)
with
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(z)=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi(y))
. We obtain
\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(z)=\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi(y))\leq\operatorname{wt}_{\operatorname{H}}(y)\leq r
, so by the first statement it follows that
y=z
and thus the inequalities above are in fact equalities.
∎

\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(v_{1}+v_{2})=w_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}(v_{1})+w_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}(v_{2})

S_{r}^{\mathcal{F}}(0)=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1}+l_{2}=r\\
l_{i}\leq R_{i}\end{subarray}}(S_{l_{1}}^{\mathcal{F}_{1}}\oplus S_{l_{2}}^{\mathcal{F}_{2}})
.

Thus, given the sphere sizes of

and

. Taking the cardinality of the set in Equation (

|S_{r}^{\mathcal{F}}|=\sum_{l=0}^{r}|S_{r-l}^{\mathcal{F}_{1}}||S_{l}^{\mathcal{F}_{2}}|

(8)

We conclude this subsection with a connection between sphere sizes distributions and coset weight distribution. The theory of coset leader weights (see definition

6.2

Perfect codes

A code is
perfect
if there exists an integer
t
such that the union of all balls of radius
t
centred at the codewords partitions the ambient space.

Definition 62

(Perfect codes)
.

Given a distance function
d(\cdot,\cdot)
on
V
and a code
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq V
, we say that
{\mathcal{C}}
is
perfect
with respect to
d(\cdot,\cdot)
if there exists a
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that:

\bigsqcup_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}}B_{t}(c)=V

(9)

where
B_{t}(c):=\{x\in V:d(x,c)\leq t\}
are disjoint balls of radius
t
centered around codewords.

Observation 63
.

If
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a convex metric (e.g. a projective metric),
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq V
is perfect with balls of radius
t
and
|{\mathcal{C}}|\geq 2
, then
t=\frac{d({\mathcal{C}})-1}{2}
with
d({\mathcal{C}}):=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in{{\mathcal{C}}}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\left(d(x,y)\right)
. This follows from the fact that for any
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
there exists a different
c^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
B_{t+1}(c)\cap B_{t}(c^{\prime})
is nonempty, containing some vector
v
. Then
d(c,c^{\prime})\leq d(c,v)+d(v,c^{\prime})=2t+1
. On the other hand, for two distinct codewords
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
the distance
d(x,x^{\prime})
cannot be less then
2t+1
, otherwise, by convexity, the balls
B_{t}(x)
and
B_{t}(x^{\prime})
wouldn’t be disjoint.

For clarity we often refer to perfect codes respect to the Hamming metric as
Hamming perfect
and with respect to a projective metric with spanning family
\mathcal{F}
as
\bm{\mathcal{F}}
-perfect
.

Perfect codes for projective metrics were first investigated by Gabidulin and Simonis in
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
.
Part of their analysis relies on the following statement
[
12
, Proposition 2]
:

Remark
.

For every non-zero linear code
0\neq{\mathcal{D}}\subset V
we have

d_{\operatorname{H}}\!\bigl(\varphi^{-1}({\mathcal{D}})\bigr)=\min\{d_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{D}}),d_{\operatorname{H}}(\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})\}.

(10)

In particular, when
d_{\operatorname{H}}(\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})\geq\max_{x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}\{d_{\operatorname{H}}(x,\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})\}=\max_{y\in V}\{\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y)\}
, then

d_{\operatorname{H}}\!\bigl(\varphi^{-1}({\mathcal{D}})\bigr)=d_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{D}}).

(11)

As a direct consequence
[
12
, Corollary 1]
, there is a bijection between optimal codes in the Hamming metric space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}
containing

\wp

and optimal codes in the projective metric space
(V,\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}})
. Here, optimality is with respect to cardinality and minimum distance. In the rest of this subsection we extend this result and describe a correspondence between perfect linear codes in projective metrics and perfect linear codes in the Hamming metric.

Proposition 64
.

Given a projective weight
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
on
V
, a parent function
\varphi:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}\to V
with parent code

\wp

. Suppose that
d_{\operatorname{H}}(\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})\geq\max_{y\in V}\{\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}(y)\}
. Then a linear code
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
containing the parent code
\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}\subset\hat{\mathcal{C}}
is Hamming perfect if and only if
{\mathcal{C}}\coloneqq\varphi(\hat{\mathcal{C}})
if
\mathcal{F}
-perfect. In such case we have that
d_{\operatorname{H}}(\hat{\mathcal{C}})=d_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{C}})
.

Proof.

Let
t=\left\lfloor\frac{d_{\operatorname{H}}(\hat{\mathcal{C}})-1}{2}\right\rfloor
. We assume that
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
is Hamming perfect, so

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}=\bigsqcup_{x\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}}B_{t}^{H}(x).

Let
y\in\hat{{\mathcal{C}}}
be any codeword. Since
y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$}\subseteq\hat{\mathcal{C}}
we have that
d_{\operatorname{H}}(\hat{\mathcal{C}})\leq d_{\operatorname{H}}(y+\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})=d_{\operatorname{H}}(\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})
. Hence we can use Proposition
57
to find
\varphi(B_{t}^{H}(x))=B_{t}^{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi(x))
for any
x\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}
and thus

V=\varphi({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\bm{\mathsf{N}}})=\bigcup_{x\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}}\varphi(B_{t}^{H}(x))=\bigcup_{v\in{\mathcal{C}}}B_{t}^{\mathcal{F}}(v).

Also note that if
v,w\in{\mathcal{C}}
are codewords with
d_{\mathcal{F}}(v,w)=d_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{C}})
, then

d_{\operatorname{H}}(\hat{\mathcal{C}})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\hat{{\mathcal{C}}}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\left(d_{\operatorname{H}}(x,y)\right)\leq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\varphi^{-1}(v)\\
y\in\varphi^{-1}(w)\end{subarray}}\left(d_{\operatorname{H}}(x,y)\right)=d_{\mathcal{F}}(v,w)=d_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{C}}).

This implies that the balls
B_{t}^{\mathcal{F}}(v)
for
v\in{\mathcal{C}}
are all disjoint and thus

V=\bigsqcup_{v\in{\mathcal{C}}}B_{t}^{\mathcal{F}}(v).

As a consequence,
d_{\operatorname{H}}(\hat{{\mathcal{C}}})=2t+1=d_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{C}})
.

We now assume that
{\mathcal{C}}
is
\mathcal{F}
-perfect, let
t=\left\lfloor\frac{d_{\operatorname{H}}(\hat{{\mathcal{C}}})-1}{2}\right\rfloor\leq d_{\operatorname{H}}(\scalebox{1.1}{$\wp$})
. We already know that the Hamming balls of radius
t
centered at the codewords of
\hat{{\mathcal{C}}}
are disjoint; it remains to show that their union covers the entire space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
. Since
{\mathcal{C}}
is
\mathcal{F}
-perfect, there exists
y\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi(x),y)\leq t
. This implies that

d_{\operatorname{H}}(x,\varphi^{-1}(y))\leq t,

i.e., there exists
x^{\prime}\in\hat{{\mathcal{C}}}
such that
x\in B_{t}^{\operatorname{H}}(x^{\prime})
. Therefore,
\hat{{\mathcal{C}}}
is Hamming-perfect.

∎

6.3

Matroids and Projective Metrics

The notion of equivalence between projective metrics based on weights is quite restrictive.
Given two spanning families
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
in an
N
-dimensional space
V
, a projective equivalence is univocally determined by assigning
N+1
elements of
\mathcal{F}
to the same number of elements of
\mathcal{G}
.
However, this may not induce an actual equivalence if the resulting linear isomorphism does not map
\mathcal{F}
to
\mathcal{G}
.
Furthermore, many very “similar” projective metrics, sharing important characteristics such as ball sizes, etc., are not equivalent.
For this reason, in this subsection, we explore how projective metrics behave when we consider their natural matroid structure. With this structure, all equivalent projective metrics are also equivalent as matroids. However, the opposite implication is not true; therefore, matroid equivalence of projective metrics is a less strict equivalence.
For an introduction to matroid theory, refer to
[
19
]
.

Definition 65

(Matroid associated to
\mathcal{F}
)
.

Given a projective metric defined by
\mathcal{F}\subset V
. Let
A_{\mathcal{F}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{N\times{\bm{\mathsf{N}}}}
be the matrix that has as columns the vectors in
\mathcal{F}
. The
matroid associated to

\mathcal{F}
is
M(\mathcal{F})\coloneqq M(A_{\mathcal{F}})
.

Remark
.

1.

Changing the order of the columns of
A_{\mathcal{F}}
gives equivalent matroids.

2.

\mathcal{F}\equiv{\mathcal{G}}\implies M(\mathcal{F})\equiv M({\mathcal{G}})

3.

For every cycle
c\subset\mathcal{F}
exist
a_{1},\ldots,a_{|c|}\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{|c|}a_{i}c_{i}=0
.

For a general field
{\mathbb{F}}
, equivalence as projective weights and equivalence as matroids are not equivalent. This is because if we have a matroid equivalence
\varphi
between
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, we may define the linear isomorphism
\Phi
mapping basis
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
to the basis
\varphi(\mathcal{B})
, but this does not guarantee that
\Phi
will map
\mathcal{F}
to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. We must note that this is indeed the case for
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}
, to see this, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{0\}
consider the smallest
I\subset\mathcal{B}
such that
c:=I\cup\{f\}
is linearly dependent. Then
c
is a cycle respect to
M(\mathcal{F})
, and
f=\sum_{g\in I}g
. Then
\varphi(c)
is also a cycle respect to
M(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
and thus
\varphi(f)=\sum_{g\in I}\varphi(g)=\sum_{g\in I}\Phi(g)=\Phi(\sum_{g\in I}g)=\Phi(f)
. Thus
\Phi
maps
\mathcal{F}
to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and is thus a projective weights equivalence. In summary:

Remark
.

For
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}
matroid equivalence and projective equivalence are equivalent.

To understand how the sizes of the balls relate to the matroid equivalence classes, we consider the inclusion-exclusion principle, which allows us to compute the cardinality of the intersections of various subspaces generated by subsets of
\mathcal{F}
. In particular, we say that a hyperplane in
V
is generated by vectors in
\mathcal{F}
whenever it is the span of
N-1
linearly independent vectors in
\mathcal{F}
. Although the matroid structure suffices to describe the intersection between any two such subspaces (see Appendix
F
), it does not provide sufficient information to describe these intersections in general. Indeed, there exist matroid-equivalent projective metrics that have different ball sizes (see
[
17
]
). However, the matroid associated with an extension of
\mathcal{F}
contains enough information to fully describe the intersections between subspaces generated by
\mathcal{F}
. In this subsection we see that when the matroid associated to the extend families of two projective metrics are equivalent, then the sphere sizes of the two metrics also coincide. Following the ideas of
[
17
]
on derived codes, we consider an extension of spanning families that contains intersections of hyperplanes generated by vectors in
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 66

(Extended Projective Metric)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset V
be a spanning family. The
extended projective metric
of
\mathcal{F}
is the set
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
containing all vectors
v\in V
such that
\langle v\rangle
is the intersection of hyperplanes generated by vectors in
\mathcal{F}
. If
\mathcal{F}=\overline{\mathcal{F}}
, we say that
\mathcal{F}
is closed under extension.

Example 67
.

Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
N
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
. Let
\mathcal{B}\subset V
be a basis of
V
, and define

w\coloneqq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}v.

Consider the projective metric on
V
induced by the spanning family
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}\cup\{w\}
. This metric corresponds to the Phase Rotation metric (
21
) in the space
\mathbb{F}^{N}
, where vectors are expressed in the basis
\mathcal{B}
.

The extended family
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
of
\mathcal{F}
is

\overline{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\,w-\sum_{g\in I}g\;\Bigm|\;I\subset\mathcal{B}\Bigr\},

i.e., the set of all vectors in
V
whose coordinates (in the basis
\mathcal{B}
) take at most two values, one of which is zero.

For a proof of this statement, see Appendix
G.1
.

The following proposition motivates the name
extension
of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proposition 68
.

\mathcal{F}\subset\overline{\mathcal{F}}

Proof.

This is true since for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
there exists a basis
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
such that
f\in\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
, and

\left\langle f\right\rangle=\cap_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in I\subset\mathcal{B}\\
|I|=N-1\end{subarray}}\left\langle I\right\rangle.

∎

By definition, any one-dimensional intersection of hyperplanes generated by a vector in
\mathcal{F}
, is generated by a vector in
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
, the following proposition shows that any intersection of such hyperplanes can be generated by vectors in
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
.

Proposition 69
.

If
\mathcal{F}
be a spanning family with extension
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
.
If
W\coloneqq\cap_{j=1}^{N-w}H_{j}
where
H_{j}
are hyperplanes generated as vectors in
\mathcal{F}
, and
\dim{W}=w
, there exist
f_{1},\ldots,f_{w}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}
such that

W=\left\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{w}\right\rangle

Proof.

Let
h_{1},\ldots h_{N-w}\in V
be the vectors orthogonal to the corresponding hyperplane
H_{j}
. Add
h_{N},\ldots,h_{N-w+1}
, vectors orthogonal to hyperplanes generated by vectors in
\mathcal{F}
to obtain a basis of
V
(this is always possible because
\mathcal{F}
is a spanning family), and
H_{1},\ldots,H_{N}
be the corresponding hyperplanes. Then the vectors
\left\langle f_{i}\right\rangle\coloneqq\cap_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq N-w+i\end{subarray}}H_{j}
, for

are linearly independent, generate

and are in
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
.
∎

The following proposition shows that the matroid associated with the extended family of
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
fully describes the sphere sizes of
\mathcal{F}
. It can be interpreted as the matroid on
\mathcal{F}
, to which specific vectors have been added to describe the intersections of hyperplanes generated by
\mathcal{F}
.

Proposition 70
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
,
{\mathcal{G}}
be two spanning families with extensions
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
and
\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}
.
If there is a matroid isomorphism
F:\overline{\mathcal{F}}\to\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}
such that
F(\mathcal{F})={\mathcal{G}}
, then the metrics
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{F}}
,
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{G}}
have equal sphere sizes.

Proof.

Let
M_{t}(\mathcal{F})\subset M(\mathcal{F})
denote the family of subsets of
\mathcal{F}
of cardinality
t
that are linearly independent. The ball of radius
t
is given by:

B_{t}^{\mathcal{F}}=\bigcup_{J\in M_{t}(\mathcal{F})}\left\langle J\right\rangle.

By the inclusion-exclusion principle, the size
|B_{t}^{\mathcal{F}}|
can be expressed in terms of the cardinalities of the intersections
\left|\bigcap_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\left\langle J\right\rangle\right|
for all
\mathcal{J}\subset M_{t}(\mathcal{F})
. Since
F(M_{t}(\mathcal{F}))=M_{t}(F(\mathcal{F}))
and each
\left\langle J\right\rangle
can also be seen as the intersection of hyperplanes generated by subsets of
\mathcal{F}
. Therefore,
\left|\bigcap_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\left\langle J\right\rangle\right|
can be viewed as the intersection of
k
hyperplanes generated by subsets of
\mathcal{F}
.

Thus, we need only to show that for all hyperplanes defined by
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\in M(\mathcal{F})
, we have:

\left|\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle I_{i}\right\rangle\right|=\left|\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle F(I_{i})\right\rangle\right|.

Since
F^{-1}
is also a matroid isomorphism, it suffices to show that:

\left|\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle I_{i}\right\rangle\right|\leq\left|\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle F(I_{i})\right\rangle\right|.

By Proposition
69
there exist
f_{1},\ldots,f_{w}\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}
linearly independent vectors such that
\left\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{w}\right\rangle=\cap_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle I_{i}\right\rangle
, then
F{(f_{1})},\ldots,F{(f_{w})}
are still linearly independent, we only need to show that
\left\langle F{((f_{1}))},\ldots,F{(f_{w})}\right\rangle\subset\cap_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle F(I_{i})\right\rangle
.
We show that
F(f_{i})\in\left\langle F(I_{j})\right\rangle
for all
j=1,\ldots,k
and for all
i=1,\ldots,w
.
Let
I_{j}\in M(\mathcal{F})
generate
H_{j}
.
If
f_{i}\in I_{j}
, then
F(f_{i})\in F(I_{j})\subset\left\langle F(I_{j})\right\rangle
.
Otherwise, if
f_{i}\notin I_{j}
, let
c_{j}
be the cycle such that
{f_{i}}\in c_{j}\subset\{f_{i}\}\cup I_{j}
.
Since
F(c_{j})
is also a cycle,
F(f_{i})\in F(c_{j})\subset F(I_{j})\cup\{F(f_{i})\}
, and thus
F(f_{i})\in\left\langle F(I_{j})\right\rangle
.
∎

The following provides a trivial counterexample to the natural question: If two projective matroids have extended families that are matroid equivalent, are they necessarily linearly equivalent? Thus matroid equivalence of the extended families of two projective metrics is a strictly weaker condition than linear equivalence.

Example 71
.

Consider
V={\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{2}
. Any projective metric is trivially closed under extension. Hence, any two projective metrics that are matroid equivalent also have matroid equivalent extended families. However, not all such projective metrics are linearly equivalent. For instance, consider the projective metrics defined by the families
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1),(1,2)\}
and
{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1),(1,3)\}
for
q=7
. These projective metrics are matroid equivalent but not linearly equivalent.

A natural question is then if knowing the structure of the extended matroid is sufficient to know the ball sizes of a projective metric. The following result shows that this is the case.

Proposition 72
.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
be the extended family of
\mathcal{F}
. Then, we have:

|B^{\mathcal{F}}_{t}|=\sum_{\emptyset\neq H\subseteq M^{t}_{\mathcal{F}}}(-1)^{|H|+1}q^{|\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}|}

(12)

where
M^{t}_{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\{I\in M_{\mathcal{F}}\mid|I|=t\}
and
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}
is a maximal independent set in
M_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}
such that for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}
and
I\in H
, the set
\{f\}\cup I
forms a cycle respect to
M_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}
.

Proof.

By the inclusion-exclusion principle, it suffices to show that

|\cap_{I\in H}\left\langle I\right\rangle|=q^{|\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}|}.

Specifically, we demonstrate that:

\cap_{I\in H}\left\langle I\right\rangle=\left\langle\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}\right\rangle.

Since for all
f\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}
and
I\in H
, the set
\{f\}\cup H
forms a cycle, it follows that
f\in\left\langle I\right\rangle
for every
I\in H
. Therefore,
f\in\cap_{I\in H}\left\langle I\right\rangle
, implying
\left\langle\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}\right\rangle\subset\cap_{I\in H}\left\langle I\right\rangle
.

By Proposition
69
, there exist elements
f_{1},\ldots,f_{w}\in M_{\overline{\mathcal{F}}}
such that:

\left\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{w}\right\rangle=\cap_{I\in H}\left\langle I\right\rangle.

We proceed by contradiction. Assume that
\left\langle\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}\right\rangle\subsetneq\cap_{I\in H}\left\langle I\right\rangle
. Then, there exists an element
f_{k}
in
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{w}\}
such that
f_{k}\notin\left\langle\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}\right\rangle
. However, since
f_{k}\in\left\langle I\right\rangle
for all
I\in H
, adding
f_{k}
to
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{H}
would contradict its maximality, thus leading to a contradiction.
∎

The following proposition can be useful to construct high dimensional examples of spanning family extensions.

Proposition 73
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset V_{1}
and
{\mathcal{G}}\subset V_{2}
be projective point families in finite-dimensional vector spaces
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, respectively. Let
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\subset V_{1}
and
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{2}\subset V_{2}
denote their respective extensions. Then the extension of the direct sum family satisfies:

\overline{\mathcal{F}\oplus{\mathcal{G}}}=\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{2}.

Proof.

The extension
\overline{\mathcal{F}\oplus{\mathcal{G}}}
is defined as the minimal set of additional projective points needed to fully describe all hyperplane intersections among the elements of
\mathcal{F}\oplus{\mathcal{G}}
. Since
\mathcal{F}\subset V_{1}
and
{\mathcal{G}}\subset V_{2}
span disjoint coordinate spaces, any linear dependency among elements of
\mathcal{F}\oplus{\mathcal{G}}
must occur entirely within either
V_{1}
or
V_{2}
.

Consequently, the circuits (i.e., minimal dependent sets) of the matroid
M_{\mathcal{F}\oplus{\mathcal{G}}}
decompose as the disjoint union of the circuits in
M_{\mathcal{F}}
and
M_{{\mathcal{G}}}
. Thus, in order to consider all intersections in the direct sum, it suffices to include exactly the extensions required for each component independently.

Hence, the extended family of
\mathcal{F}\oplus{\mathcal{G}}
is exactly the direct sum of the extensions:

\overline{\mathcal{F}\oplus{\mathcal{G}}}=\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{2}.\qed

Singleton bound

A generalization of the Singleton bound for translation invariant metrics, such as projective metrics, can be obtained by introducing the concept of anticodes.

Definition 74
.

Let
V
be a vector space over
{\mathbb{F}}
. Let
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
be an integer and
d:V\times V\to{\mathbb{R}}
a distance function. A
t
-anticode is a non-empty subset
{\mathcal{A}}\subset V
such that
d(x,y)\leq t
for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{A}}
. We say that
{\mathcal{A}}
is linear if it is a subspace of
V
.

Lemma 75

(Code-Anticode Bound)
.

Let
d
be a translation-invariant distance function and

8

Conclusion

In this work, we developed a comprehensive framework for
projective metrics
or, equivalently, for integral convex scale–translation–invariant metrics on finite–dimensional vector spaces over finite fields.
Starting from the notion of
quotient weights
, we have shown several elementary tools and used them to streamline many proofs and existing constructions. This provided the technical backbone for all subsequent sections.

The main contributions can be summarized as follows.

•

We established new fundamental properties of projective metrics and showed that they coincide with the class of integral, convex, scale-translation-invariant metrics.

•

We exhibited an explicit bijection between isomorphism classes of projective metrics on a space
V
and Hamming–equivalence classes of linear subspaces (“parent codes”) of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}
, thereby characterizing their full isometry groups.

•

We proved that
every
scale–invariant metric embeds isometrically into a suitable projective metric, revealing projective metrics as natural ambient spaces for the study of general translation– and scale–invariant codes.

•

We analyzed the behavior of sphere sizes under metric constructions such as disjoint union and, via the notion of
extended spanning families
, showed that the associated matroid completely determines the volume of a ball.

•

We derived a Singleton–type bound for projective metrics by adapting the code–anticode technique to the present setting.

Beyond these theoretical advances, we supplied numerous examples, counterexamples, and a SageMath script that automates many of the computations introduced in the paper.

Future directions.

We hope that the unifying perspective offered and the tools developed in this paper will serve as a foundation for the study of projective metrics, further bridging the research on different metrics, and more broadly between coding theory, graph theory, matroid theory, and finite geometry.

E

Correction, detection and convexity

In this section we will introduce the notions of
correction-capability

\tau
and the
equal-detection threshold

\sigma_{\operatorname{eq}}
.
Suppose that from two distinct vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
one is selected (say
v
) and transmitted over a channel.
The numbers
\tau,\sigma_{\operatorname{eq}}
then indicate for which radii
\rho\in\mathbb{N}
, after receiving a vector
x\in V
with
d(v,x)=\rho
, a minimum-distance decoder will always decode
x
correctly to
v
, or if it could encounter a tie with
d(v_{1},x)=d(v_{2},x)=\rho
.

Secondly, two notions of
normality
are introduced that capture if
\tau
and
\sigma_{\operatorname{eq}}
only depend on the distance
d(v_{1},v_{2})
in a natural way. Finally it is shown that a metric being normal for both notions is equivalent to being convex, and give two equivalent definitions of convexity.
See
7

Proof.

Assume that property (1) holds for
d
. Let
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
and consider a sequence

S=\{x_{0}=v_{1},x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}=v_{2}\}\subset V

such that
d(x_{k},x_{k+1})=1
for all
k=0,\ldots,s-1
, and
d(v_{1},v_{2})=s-1
.

We aim to show that for all
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,d(v_{1},v_{2})\}
, we have:

d(v_{1},x_{i})=i\quad\text{and}\quad d(x_{i},v_{2})=d(v_{1},v_{2})-i.

For the boundary cases: When
i=0
, we have
x_{0}=v_{1}
, so
d(v_{1},x_{0})=d(v_{1},v_{1})=0
and
d(v_{2},x_{0})=d(v_{2},v_{1})-0
, which holds trivially. When
i=d(v_{1},v_{2})=s-1
, we have
x_{s}=v_{2}
, so
d(v_{1},x_{s})=s-1
and
d(x_{s},v_{2})=0
, which also holds trivially.

Now, consider the case where
0<i<s-1
. We first show that
d(v_{1},x_{i})\leq i
. By applying the triangle inequality along the sequence:

d(v_{1},x_{i})\leq\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}d(x_{j},x_{j+1})=i.

Now, assume for contradiction that
d(v_{1},x_{i})=n^{\prime}<i
. Then, there exists a shorter sequence:

S^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime}_{0}=v_{1},x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n^{\prime}}=x_{i}\}

such that
d(x^{\prime}_{j},x^{\prime}_{j+1})=1
for all
j
. However, if we form the sequence
S^{\prime\prime}=S^{\prime}\cup\{x_{k}\}_{k>i}
, we would obtain a sequence shorter than

connecting
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, contradicting the minimality assumption of
S
. Thus, our assumption that
d(v_{1},x_{i})<i
must be false, meaning
d(v_{1},x_{i})=i
.

Similarly, applying the same reasoning to
d(x_{i},v_{2})
, we conclude
d(x_{i},v_{2})=s-1-i
, completing the proof. Now assume that (2) holds. Given any two elements

, we want to show that the minimum number of steps of distance 1 to move from
v
to
w
corresponds to the metric
d(v,w)
.

We proceed by induction on
d(v,w)
. Base case: If
d(v,w)=1
, then there is a direct path from
v
to
w
, which satisfies condition (1) trivially. Assume that for all distances less than
n
, there exists a sequence of elements satisfying (1). Consider
d(v,w)=n
. By (2), there exists an element

such that:

d(v,x)=1\quad\text{and}\quad d(x,w)=n-1.

By the induction hypothesis, there exist sequences
S_{1}=\{v,x_{1}\}
and
S_{2}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}=w\}
, both of which satisfy condition (1). Concatenating these two sequences, we obtain a sequence of length

that satisfies (1) for
d(v,w)=n
, completing the proof.
∎

Let
V
be a finite vector space endowed with an integral metric

. Recall that for any

and

, the ball and sphere with radius

Definition E.1
.

(
[
23
]
)
Given a finite vector space
V
with an integral metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
, the
(distance)-correction capability

\tau(v_{1},v_{2})
of a pair of distinct vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
is the integer
\tau
satisfying one of these equivalent statements:

•

\tau=\min_{x\in V}\max\{d(v_{1},x),d(x,v_{2})\}-1;

•

Moreover,

is said to be

if for all

Informally, if the correction capability is

, this means that

and

can ‘distinguish’ any vector that is at most distance

away from one of
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
. If an integral metric

is correction-normal, it fully describes the error correction capability between pairs of vector in a natural way, as the triangle inequality ensures that
\tau(v_{1},v_{2})\geq\left\lfloor\frac{d(v_{1},v_{2})-1}{2}\right\rfloor
is always satisfied.

Definition E.2
.

Given a finite vector space
V
with integral metric

, the

of a pair of vectors

that, if such

exists, satisfies one of these equivalent statements:

•

s=d(B_{s}^{d}(v_{1}),v_{2})=d(B_{s}^{d}(v_{2}),v_{1});

•

s=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in V\\
d(v_{1},x)\leq s\end{subarray}}\{d(x,v_{2})\}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in V\\
d(y,v_{2})\leq s\end{subarray}}\{d(v_{1},y)\};

•

B_{s}^{d}(v_{1})\cap B_{s-1}^{d}(v_{2})=B_{s-1}^{d}(v_{1})\cap B_{s}^{d}(v_{2})=\emptyset\quad\text{ and }\quad S_{s}^{d}(v_{1})\cap S_{s}^{d}(v_{2})\neq\emptyset.

.

in a natural way.

\langle v_{c_{1}},\ldots,v_{c_{d-k}},F\cap G\rangle=\langle F\rangle\cap\langle G\rangle
. We refer to
\{v_{c_{i}}\}_{i\in[d]}
and to
\{c_{i}\}_{i\in[d]}
as a
bases of circuits
.

Further more we can chose a bases of circuits such that for all
i=1,\ldots,d-k
exists
e_{i}\in c_{i}\setminus\cup_{j\neq i}c_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
C
be the set of circuits contained in
F\cup G
. For all
c\in C
pick
v_{c}\in\langle c\cap F\rangle\cap\langle c\cap G\rangle
. We only need to prove that
\langle v_{c}\mid c\in C\rangle+\langle F\cap G\rangle=\langle F\rangle\cap\langle G\rangle
. Clearly
\langle v_{c}\mid c\in C\rangle+\langle F\cap G\rangle\subset\langle F\rangle\cap\langle G\rangle
. Viceversa, let
v\in\langle F\rangle\cap\langle G\rangle\setminus\langle F\cap G\rangle
and
\bar{c}\subset F\cup G
be a cycle with minimum cardinality
k=|\bar{c}|
such that
v\in\langle\bar{c}\cap F\rangle\cap\langle\bar{c}\cap G\rangle
. We prove that
v\in\langle v_{c}\mid c\in C\rangle+\langle F\cap G\rangle
by induction on
k
. If
k=2
then
\bar{c}
is a circuit therefore
v\in\langle\bar{c}\cap F\rangle\cap\langle\bar{c}\cap G\rangle\subset\langle v_{c}\mid c\in C\rangle
. For the induction step. Since
v\notin\langle F\cap G\rangle
, there exists
e\in\bar{c}\setminus(F\cap G)
and
e\in c\subset\bar{c}
such that
c
is a circuit. If
c=\bar{c}
we finished. Otherwise let
a_{f},b_{f}\neq 0
such that
v=\sum_{f\in\bar{c}\cap F}a_{f}f
and
v_{c}=\sum_{f\in c\cap F}b_{f}f
. Then
0\neq w\coloneqq v-a_{e}b_{e}^{-1}v_{c}\in\langle F\rangle\cap\langle G\rangle\neq 0
because
c\neq\bar{c}
and then
w\in\langle\bar{c}\setminus\{e\}\cap F\rangle\cap\langle\bar{c}\setminus\{e\}\cap G\rangle\neq\{0\}
. If
w\in\langle F\cap G\rangle
we finished. Otherwise,
\bar{c}\setminus\{e\}
is a cycle and
|\bar{c}\setminus\{e\}|=k-1
. By induction we have
w\in\langle v_{c}\mid c\in C\rangle+\langle F\cap G\rangle
. Thus
v=w+a_{e}b_{e}^{-1}v_{c}\in\langle v_{c}\mid c\in C\rangle+\langle F\cap G\rangle
and this conclude the first part of the proof.

We now want to prove that we can chose a bases of circuits such that for all
i=1,\ldots,d-k
exists
e_{i}\in c_{i}
such that
e_{i}\notin\cup_{j\neq i}c_{j}
. Let
S\subset F\cup G
be maximal among the elements of
M(\mathcal{F})
contained in
F\cup G
. For all
e\in F\cup G\setminus S

c_{e}
be the only circuit contained in
S\cup\{e\}
. Clearly
\langle v_{c_{e}}\mid e\in F\cup G\setminus S\rangle+\langle F\cap G\rangle\subset\langle v_{c}\mid c\in C\rangle=\langle F\rangle\cap\langle G\rangle
. Viceversa consider
v\in\langle F\rangle\cap\langle G\rangle\setminus\langle F\cap G\rangle
. There exists
a_{f},b_{g}\in{\mathbb{F}}
for all

and
m\in\langle F\cap G\rangle
such that

v^{\prime}:=v-m=\sum_{s\in S\cap F}a_{s}s+\sum_{f\in F\setminus S}a_{f}f=\sum_{s\in S\cap G}b_{s}s+\sum_{g\in G\setminus S}a_{g}g.

Since
S\in M(\mathcal{F})
, then
\langle S\cap F\rangle\cap\langle S\cap G\rangle=\{0\}
. If
a_{f}=0,b_{g}=0
for all
f\in F\setminus S,\,g\in G\setminus S
then
v\in\langle S\cap F\rangle\cap\langle S\cap G\rangle=\{0\}
thus
v=0
. Otherwise we ”eliminate” the non null coefficients in
G\setminus S)
of
v

v_{c_{g}}\in\langle F\rangle
:

v^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq v-m-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in G\setminus S\\
b_{g}\neq 0\end{subarray}}b_{g}\pi_{g}(v_{c_{g}})^{-1}v_{c_{g}}\in\langle G\cap S\rangle\cap\langle F\rangle

Where
\pi_{g}(v_{c_{e}})\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{0\}

v_{c_{e}}=\sum_{g\in G\cap c_{e}}\pi_{g}(c_{c_{e}})g
.Then we ”eliminate” the non null coefficients in
F\setminus S
of

, since for all
f\in F\setminus S

v_{c_{e}}\in\langle G\cap S\rangle

v^{\prime\prime\prime}\coloneqq v^{\prime\prime}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in F\setminus S\\
a_{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}a_{f}\pi_{f}(v_{c_{f}})^{-1}v_{c_{f}}\in\langle G\cap S\rangle\cap\langle F\cap S\rangle=\{0\}

Thus
v^{\prime\prime\prime}=0
and

v=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in F\setminus S\\
a_{f}\neq 0\end{subarray}}a_{f}\pi_{f}(v_{c_{f}})^{-1}v_{c_{f}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in G\setminus S\\

Extended Family of Phase Rotation Metric Example

Example G.1
.

Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
N
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
. Let
\mathcal{B}\subset V
be a basis of
V
, and define

w\coloneqq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{B}}v.

Consider the projective metric on
V
induced by the spanning family
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}\cup\{w\}
. This metric corresponds to the Phase Rotation metric (
21
) in the space
\mathbb{F}^{N}
, where vectors are expressed in the basis
\mathcal{B}
.

We claim that the extended family
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
of
\mathcal{F}
is

\overline{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\,w-\sum_{g\in I}g\;\Bigm|\;I\subset\mathcal{B}\Bigr\},

i.e., the set of all vectors in
V
whose coordinates (in the basis
\mathcal{B}
) take at most two values, one of which is zero. The proof proceeds by induction on
N
. The base case (N=1) is trivial, since
V
is one-dimensional and
\mathcal{B}
has only one vector.
For the inductive step, assume the statement holds for all dimensions less than
N
. All the vectors in
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
are such that
\langle v\rangle=\bigcap_{i=1}^{N-1}\langle G_{i}\rangle
, where
G_{i}\in M(\mathcal{F})
and
\lvert G_{i}\rvert=N-1
. We must show that

\left\langle v\right\rangle\;=\;\left\langle\,w-\sum_{g\in I}g\,\right\rangle\quad\text{for some }I\subset\mathcal{B}.

Case 1:
Suppose
w\in G_{i}
for all
i
. Then

\langle v\rangle\;=\;\bigcap_{i=1}^{N-1}\left\langle G_{i}\right\rangle\;=\;\left\langle w\right\rangle.

Case 2:
There exists some
G_{i}
with
w\notin G_{i}
. That is
G_{i}=\mathcal{B}\setminus\{g^{\prime}\}
for some
g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
.
Set
V^{\prime}\coloneqq\langle G_{i}\rangle
. Since
G_{i}
is a set of
(N-1)
linearly independent vectors, it forms a basis
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=G_{i}\subset V^{\prime}
. Define

w^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{g\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}g=w-g^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\cup\{w^{\prime}\}.

We aim to show that for all other
j
, the intersection

\langle G_{j}\rangle\,\cap\,\langle G_{i}\rangle

is a hyperplane in
V^{\prime}
generated by a subset of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. Then, by the induction hypothesis (applied to the space
V^{\prime}
of dimension
(N-1)
), we conclude that

\langle v\rangle
is spanned by a vector in
\overline{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}\subset\overline{\mathcal{F}}
, of the form
w^{\prime}-\sum_{g\in I^{\prime}}g=w-g^{\prime}-\sum_{g\in I^{\prime}}g
. Translating this back to the original family
\mathcal{F}
, we get the desired vector
w-\sum_{g\in I}g
, where
I=I^{\prime}\cup\{g^{\prime}\}
.

Subcase 2a:
If

\lvert G_{i}\cap G_{j}\rvert=N-2
, then

H_{ij}\coloneqq\langle G_{j}\rangle\,\cap\,\langle G_{i}\rangle=\langle G_{i}\cap G_{j}\rangle,

is a hyperplane in
V^{\prime}
generated by elements of
G_{i}\subset\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Subcase 2b:
Otherwise, if
\lvert G_{i}\cap G_{j}\rvert=N-3
, we have
w,g^{\prime}\in G_{j}
.
Furthermore, since
w^{\prime}\in\left\langle G_{i}\right\rangle
we have
w^{\prime}=w\;-\;g^{\prime}
is also in
\left\langle G_{j}\right\rangle
. Thus,

\langle G_{j}\rangle\,\cap\,\langle G_{i}\rangle=\langle G_{i}\cap G_{j}\rangle+\langle w^{\prime}\rangle,

which is also a hyperplane in
V^{\prime}
generated by elements in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

By induction, every one-dimensional intersection
\langle v\rangle
is spanned by some vector of the form
w-\sum_{g\in I}g
. Hence

\overline{\mathcal{F}}=\bigl\{w-\sum_{g\in I}g:I\subset\mathcal{B}\bigr\}
, as claimed.
