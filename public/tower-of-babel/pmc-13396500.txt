In vivo dynamic hotspot-enhanced Raman spectroscopy via reconfigurable swarming nanoprobes
Authors: Dongfang Zhao, Hui Chen, Dongdong Jin, Hanyu Cao, Xiaojia Liu, Yong Wang, Xingzhou Du, Jiangfan Yu, Jinhong Guo, Li Zhang, Xing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) offers exceptional sensitivity but faces a critical trade-off in living systems: rigid substrates lack biological adaptability, while colloidal nanoprobes suffer from poor signal reproducibility. Herein, we present a bioadaptive SERS platform using magnetically guided swarming nanoprobes. These probes integrate a magnetic core, plasmonic gold/silver layers, and a biocompatible silica coating, enabling programmable assembly under magnetic fields into chain-like nanostructures with interparticle gap-dependent hotspots, followed by coordinated reconfiguration into dynamically stable swarms. Multiphysics simulations reveal that cyclic assembly-disassembly generates transient electromagnetic hotspots while inducing convective flows to actively recruit analytes. This dual mechanism achieves reproducible enhancement factors exceeding 2.9×107, an order of magnitude higher than colloidal systems. In vivo, swarming nanoprobes deployed in rabbit models demonstrate over 10.3-fold Raman signal amplification during intravascular detection. By leveraging active matter physics to synergize nanoscale sensing, this work establishes a new paradigm for in vivo molecular diagnostics.

Introduction

Raman spectroscopy, a powerful analytical technique based on inelastic light scattering, has emerged as a cornerstone in the fields of biochemical sensing owing to its ability to provide molecular fingerprint information with unparalleled specificity1,2. The non-destructive nature, aqueous environment-compatibility and minimal sample preparation requirement render Raman spectroscopy particularly suitable for real-time monitoring of in vivo physiological processes3,4. However, the inherently weak Raman scattering cross-section and strong fluorescence interference in biological samples necessitate signal enhancement, especially when detecting subtle molecular changes in living organisms2,5. To overcome this limitation, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) has been developed as a powerful enhancement technique, which can amplify the Raman signals of analytes adsorbed near metallic nanostructures by several orders of magnitude6–8. This significant signal enhancement, often reaching factors of 102 to 106, enables the detection of analytes at low concentrations9–11. The synergy between Raman spectroscopy’s molecular specificity and SERS’s enhanced sensitivity has unlocked transformative applications in biosensing, such as early disease diagnosis, pharmacological assessment, in vivo imaging, and real-time health monitoring12–16.

Considerable efforts have been devoted to the development of SERS strategies for the amplification of Raman signals, which are broadly classified into two categories based on the design of plasmonic substrates and their enhancement mechanisms17,18. The first strategy employs nanostructured solid substrates fabricated via top-down approaches, such as electron-beam lithography, nanoimprinting, and anodic oxidation19–22. These substrates incorporate precisely engineered plasmonic architectures (e.g., periodic nanogap arrays, nanopillars) that generate uniform electromagnetic hotspots through localized surface plasmon resonance23,24. While the nanostructured solid substrates exhibit high reproducibility and stable enhancement factors (EFs) under controlled conditions, their macroscopic geometries (millimeter-to-centimeter scale) and mechanical rigidity preclude deployment in vivo25–28. Implanting such substrates into deep tissues or microlesions remains impractical due to invasiveness and spatial mismatch with biological microenvironments. Furthermore, their static architecture impedes adaptive interactions with dynamic biological systems, such as cellular interfaces and circulating biomarkers, thereby diminishing detection sensitivity in living systems.

Colloidal nanoparticle-based systems, synthesized via bottom-up methods (e.g., seed-mediated growth, and particle self-assembly), constitute the second SERS strategy by utilizing plasmonic nanoprobes, such as gold nanospheres, silver nanocubes, or plasmonic nanocomposites29–34. These systems exploit solution-phase dispersion to generate intense plasmonic coupling at the sharp edge or interparticle gap-dependent hotspots, achieving EFs over 106. Notably, recent advances have employed external magnetic fields to aggregate and enrich magnetic-plasmonic nanocomposites (e.g., templated or core-shell structured magnetic probes) in solution, thereby increasing interparticle gaps and creating denser hotspots for Raman signal amplification35–37. The nanoscale dimensions of colloidal probes enable penetration into biological matrices and passive targeting to specific tissues, positioning them as superior candidates for biosensing inside living bodies. However, critical challenges persist in the realization of in vivo applications, mainly due to the uncontrollable aggregation dynamics of nanoprobes in physiological environments38–43. The biological fluids and physiological factors may alter the interparticle spacing and destabilize plasmonic coupling, which disrupt hotspot formation and lead to stochastic signal variability. Moreover, off-target retention and heterogeneous biodistribution reduce nanoprobe bioavailability at desired sites for SERS amplification, compromising detection sensitivity.

Herein, we develop an adaptive SERS platform that enables robust in vivo biosensing through reconfigurable swarming nanoprobes capable of dynamically generating uniform electromagnetic hotspots. By addressing the inherent limitations of static aggregation, the platform enables robust in vivo biosensing through reconfigurable swarming nanoprobes capable of dynamically generating uniform electromagnetic hotspots. The nanoprobe features a multilayer architecture comprising a magnetic iron oxide core, plasmonic gold/silver intermediate layers and a bioinert silica shell (Fig. 1a). In contrast to conventional strategies that use magnets to accumulate nanoprobes into static aggregations, we employ programmable magnetic fields to guide their assembly into chain-like structures, with interparticle gaps optimized for intense electromagnetic hotspots via plasmonic coupling. These structures are then reconfigured into dynamically stable swarms that maintain continuous signal enhancement while adapting to biological environments (Fig. 1b). Multiphysics simulations (particle dynamics, electromagnetic field distribution and fluid behavior) reveal that continuous assembly-disassembly reorganization of nanoprobes in swarms creates spatially uniform transient hotspots, overcoming the fixed hotspot limitation inherent to conventional approaches. Meanwhile, collective nanoprobe motion induces convective microflows that actively transport analytes toward enhancement zones, bypassing reliance on passive diffusion. This synergistic effect achieves reproducible Raman signal amplification, with enhancement factors exceeding 2.9×107 and 3.4×107 when detecting crystal violet and rhodamine 6 G solutions (10−4 M), respectively, outperforming signal levels from static aggregations by 3.3- and 8.5-fold. Besides, magnetic steering enables swarm to traverse complex biological geometries (e.g., tortuous, branched, and confined networks) through reconfigurable morphological transformation, allowing precise targeting while maintaining hotspot integrity. Finally, in vivo validation demonstrates successful ultrasound-guided navigation of swarming nanoprobes (Fig. 1c), with intravascular detection in rabbit model showing >10.3-fold Raman signal amplification. Results

Design, synthesis, and characterization of magnetic SERS nanoprobes

To achieve multi-functionality of magnetic manipulation and Raman signal enhancement, we engineered a core-shell SERS nanoprobe through a layer-by-layer synthesis strategy (Fig. 2a). The architecture integrates: (i) a Fe₃O₄ core for magnetic responsiveness; (ii) a plasmonic Au/Ag intermediate layer for SERS amplification; and (iii) a protective SiO₂ shell for biostability. The synthesis commenced with monodisperse Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles prepared via hydrothermal synthesis, followed by polydopamine (PDA) coating via dopamine self-polymerization in alkaline buffer. The PDA layer enhanced colloidal stability while providing amine-rich surfaces for subsequent electrostatic immobilization of Au nanoseeds. A conformal Ag layer was then deposited through ascorbic acid-mediated reduction of Ag⁺, where the Au nanoseeds acted as catalytic sites to ensure uniform Ag growth, thereby forming a continuous plasmonic surface for broadband Raman enhancement. Finally, a silica shell was introduced via hydrolysis of tetraethyl orthosilicate, ensuring biostability by shielding the plasmonic layer from biofouling.Fig. 2Fabrication and characterization of magnetic SERS nanoprobe.a Schematic of the fabrication process of Fe3O4@PDA@Au@Ag@SiO2 as the SERS nanoprobe. b TEM images of nanoparticles at each fabrication stage. Figure 2b presents the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images that track the structural evolution across fabrication stages, confirming layer-by-layer assembly. Pristine Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles exhibited smooth surfaces, while PDA coating introduced a ~ 10 nm uniform organic layer on Fe₃O₄@PDA. Further immersion in an acidic buffer endowed the nanoparticles with a positive surface charge of +43.8 mV (Figure S1), enabling electrostatic adsorption of negatively charged Au nanoseeds (-99.3 mV). The spontaneous assembly of Au nanoseeds (~4.7 nm, Figure S2) onto Fe₃O₄@PDA@Au formed discrete nucleation sites, visible as discrete protrusions in TEM image, playing a critical role in subsequent Ag deposition. The ascorbic acid-driven Ag reduction produced a continuous metallic layer, while the final SiO₂ coating retained structural integrity with a ~ 2 nm shell. Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy confirmed the spatially resolved elemental distribution of Fe (core), Au/Ag (intermediate), and Si (shell) in Fig. 2c, validating the designed architecture. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Figure S3) further quantified the progressive size increase across synthesis stages (from 428 nm for Fe₃O₄ to 513 nm for final probe), demonstrating precise control over nanoscale dimensions.

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy further monitored the chemical modifications at each synthesis step in Fig. 2d. Fe–O vibrations (588 cm⁻¹) and hydroxyl groups (1650, 3450 cm⁻¹) confirmed Fe₃O₄ core formation, while subsequent PDA/Au modifications preserved these signatures. Ag deposition introduced a C–N stretching peak (1290 cm⁻¹), attributable to polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) surfactants used in Ag⁺ reduction, and SiO₂ coating generated characteristic Si–O–Si vibrations (800, 1110 cm⁻¹), verifying shell completeness. X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. 2e) identified the distinct crystalline phases of magnetite (Fe₃O₄), face-centered cubic Au, and face-centered cubic Ag. Complementary ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy (Figure S4) revealed a broad plasmonic absorption band spanning 350-450 nm, endowing the prepared nanoprobes with localized surface plasmon resonance properties essential for SERS. Magnetic characterization via vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) in Fig. 2f demonstrated ferrimagnetic behavior across all stages, with saturation magnetization decreasing progressively (from 82 emu/g for Fe₃O₄ to 40 emu/g for final probe), due to the incorporation of nonmagnetic components. Crucially, the final nanoprobe retained rapid magnetic response to a permanent magnet, enabling controlled swarm dynamics. At last, as shown in Fig. 2g, SERS efficacy was validated using crystal violet (CV) and rhodamine 6 G (R6G), where nanoprobe dispersion amplified characteristic Raman peaks by more than 51-fold versus analyte-only controls. This enhancement arises from electromagnetic field confinement at Au/Ag nanogaps on nanoprobes, demonstrating the nanoprobe’s capability for SERS applications. The above results collectively validate the fidelity of the layered synthesis approach, ensuring that each key attribute (magnetic maneuverability, structural stability, and plasmonic enhancement) is as designed, paving the foundation for in vivo biosensing.

To this end, we engineered a magnetic field-programmed swarm system capable of on-demand morphological transitions between isotropic and anisotropic configurations (Movie S1). Initially dispersed nanoprobes underwent magnetic alignment under static field (BS), forming precursor chains for swarm assembly (Fig. 3a, left). Upon applying rotating magnetic field (BR) or oscillating magnetic field (BO), two distinct swarm morphologies emerged: (i) vortex pattern exhibiting rotational symmetry under BR, and (ii) elongated ribbon assemblies under BO (Fig. 3a, right). Precise control over swarm morphology requires understanding phase transitions governed by magnetic field parameters. For vortex swarm, stability map (Fig. 3b, Movie S2) revealed three distinct regimes: spindly structure with uncontrollable elongation at high frequencies (region I), fragmented and disordered chain aggregation under excessive field strengths (region III), and dynamically stable vortex in the optimal regime (region II). This threshold-dependent behavior originates from competing magnetic torques and hydrodynamic drag forces, a balance critical for maintaining structural integrity. Ribbon swarm exhibited analogous amplitude ratio/frequency-dependent phase behaviors, where increasing γ and f progressively triggered the transitions from massive chain-like structures to compact ribbon pattern and final uncontrollable slender filaments (Fig. 3c, Movie S3). Within stable regimes, swarm dimensions scaled with field parameters: at fixed |BR | = 4 mT, vortex area increased by ~24.8% as f rose from 10 to 20 Hz (Fig. 3d, Figure S6). Higher |BR| enhanced pattern stability, suppressing frequency-induced fluctuations. Meanwhile, at fixed |BO | = 15 mT, ribbon elongation correlated positively with γ and f, achieving maximal aspect ratio of ~7.9 at γ = 4.0, f = 20 Hz (Fig. 3e, Figure S7). This dependence remained consistent when |BO| was set to 10 mT and 20 mT (Figure S8), confirming that the ribbon swarm constitutes a robust and readily achievable collective state across a range of operational parameters. These quantitative relationships establish a foundation for predictive swarm engineering, enabling the tailored design of collective morphologies suited to specific vascular geometries. Such quantitative relationships enable predictive swarm engineering, allowing to tailor small-scale collectives for specific vascular geometries. It was worth noting that swarm morphology would revert to initial states upon parameter reset, ensuring operational robustness for repeated targeting cycles.

Locomotion of swarming nanoprobes was activated by tilting the field alternating plane (grey plane in Fig. 3a), introducing translational asymmetry. We further imported two angular parameters, direction angle α (in-plane angle between field projection and Bx) and pitch angle β (out-of-plane tilt), to provide multiaxial control over swarm motion. Velocity modulation was achieved by varying the pitch angle β: increasing β from 1° to 5° accelerated velocities from around 7.5 to 26.1 μm s−1 for both swarms (Fig. 3f). Exceeding β over 6° disrupted swarm integrity due to excessive hydrodynamic shear forces, establishing operational limits. Subsequently, coordinated α and β control enabled directional navigation of swarms along arbitrary trajectories, including square loops in Fig. 3g (Movie S4), and other complex paths in Figure S9. Such swarming nanoprobes combining reconfigurable morphologies with steerable motility were capable of achieving adaptive navigation through simulated vasculature (Figure S10, Movie S5), thereby providing a platform for targeted therapy requiring coordinated nanoscale action, from drug delivery to in vivo biosensing.

Generation of dynamic hotspots in swarming SERS nanoprobes for enhancing Raman signals

Following validation of magnetic swarming control, we systematically evaluated the Raman detection performance of SERS nanoprobes in three distinct states: dispersion, aggregation, and dynamic swarming states (including vortex swarm and ribbon swarm as shown in Fig. 4a). Using CV and R6G solutions (10⁻⁴ M), baseline measurements of pure analytes exhibited negligible Raman signals (Fig. 4b). Dispersed nanoprobes enhanced characteristic analyte peaks marginally (~one orders of magnitude), attributable to the surface plasmon resonance effect of the Au/Ag intermediate layer on nanoprobe. Static magnetic aggregation amplified signal intensities by more than 65-fold, owing to the formation of numerous interparticle gap-induced electromagnetic hotspots. Remarkably, dynamic magnetic fields inducing vortex and ribbon swarm configurations further improved signal amplitudes by nearly 298% over static aggregates. This enhancement hierarchy—swarm > aggregation > dispersion—was corroborated by R6G spectra (Fig. 4c), confirming the universality of swarm-enhanced detection. To elucidate the enhancement mechanism, we performed coupled electromagnetic and hydrodynamic simulations on 16-nanoprobe systems under rotating and oscillating magnetic fields. For vortex swarm, initial dispersion (Fig. 4d) showed minimal field enhancement (|E/E₀ | 4 < 15) and laminar flow. Magnetic actuation induced chain formation with interparticle gaps <5 nm, generating intense hotspots (|E/E₀ | 4 > 106, the red-white regions in the electromagnetic simulation) through plasmonic coupling47–49. Further dynamic chain reorganization occurred under magnetic field through two synchronized processes: (i) magnetic dipolar interactions drove adjacent chains (length: 2–3 particles) to merge into extended structures (length: 4–5 particles), creating new interparticle gaps with |E/E₀ | 4 > 2×106; (ii) concurrently, fluid shear forces fragmented overgrown chains into shorter segments (2–4 particles), eliminating part of existing gaps while redistributing nanoprobes. This cyclic assembly-disassembly process regenerated transient hotspots every period (0.125 s), maintaining dynamic equilibrium. Moreover, chain rotation simultaneously created microflows by disturbing fluidic field surrounding nanoprobes (the green-red regions in the hydrodynamic simulation), promoting analyte transport to hotspot regions. This spatiotemporal hotspot evolution, combined with advection-enhanced mass transport, increased analyte-hotspot collision frequency, and thus amplified effective Raman excitation events over static systems. Similar electromagnetic-hydrodynamic synergy was observed in ribbon swarm (Fig. 4e), where oscillatory nanoprobes motion generated pulsatile hotspots synchronized with directional analyte convection.

Large-scale analysis of 2256 nanoprobes in vortex and ribbon swarms revealed critical statistical advantages. As shown in Fig. 5a, Figure S11, and Movie S6, despite continuous chain reorganization, hotspot density remained spatially uniform (variation <8.1% across 100 μm² regions in Fig. 5b). By dividing each period of magnetic actuation into 30 frames, temporal analysis in Fig. 5c showed stable global hotspot counts (1842 ± 14 gaps/frame) through dynamic equilibrium of gap formation (~129 gaps/frame) and dissociation (~129 gaps/frame). This self-regulating behavior translated to exceptional signal reproducibility: 25 random spatial Raman detection within vortex swarms exhibited <13.9% relative standard deviation (RSD) in the collected spectra of CV (Fig. 5d). Ribbon swarms demonstrated analogous uniformity despite distinct swarm kinematics (Fig. 5e-h, Figure S12, Movie S7). Hotspot density varied by <13.8% spatially, with temporal hotspot number fluctuations (±28.1% over one field cycle) arising from periodic chain realignment. Notably, 25 random Raman measurements in ribbon swarms maintained <16.8% RSD across all characteristic peaks, outperforming traditional aggregated SERS substrates (typical RSD > 30%50). Such statistical consistency, unachievable in conventional SERS substrates, stems from swarm-intrinsic self-organization that homogenizes both electromagnetic enhancement and analyte distribution. Moreover, signal variability could be further reduced by improving the size uniformity of the constituent nanoprobes. Systematic parameter sweeps quantified swarm-enhanced SERS performance across operational variables. Figure 5i&, Figure S14 demonstrates swarm superiority across Raman bands: vortex and ribbon swarms amplified CV signals at 917/1178/1373/1619 cm⁻¹ by ~3.3- and ~12.3-fold over aggregation and dispersion states of nanoprobes, respectively. Figure S15&S16 indicates a similar signals enhancement of R6G. Cyclic Raman tests alternating between the swarm and aggregated states (Figure S17) further confirmed the reproducible and reversible signal modulation intrinsic to the active system: the SERS intensity consistently and significantly increased upon switching to the dynamic swarm state and decreased when the field was removed and static aggregates reformed. This provides direct evidence that the additional enhancement observed in the swarm state (‘swarm > aggregation’) is attributed to the active, field-driven reconfiguration of the nanoprobes. When investigating the influence of magnetic field parameters on SERS performance, frequency emerged as the dominant control variable. For vortex swarm, increasing frequency from 6 to 22 Hz did not alter the swarm pattern area obviously, but enhanced CV Raman signal at 917 cm⁻¹ by ~355% (Fig. 5j& Figure S18), attributable to the increased hotspot regeneration rate and enhanced analyte mass transport under rotating magnetic field with high frequency. Contrastingly, swarm geometry alterations at fixed frequency (e.g., ribbon swam in Fig. 5k&, Figure S19) caused <7.5% signal variation, confirming dynamic characteristic over equilibrated morphology as the enhancement determinant. This quantitative framework establishes magneto-hydrodynamic swarm engineering as a paradigm for adaptive SERS platforms, enabling real-time optimization of enhancement through tunable field parameters rather than fixed nanostructure design. Finally, enhancement factors (EFs) were calculated using CV (Supplementary Note 1) and reached ~2.9×107 for swarming states (Fig. 5l), which was 333% and 1237% of the factors achieved by aggregation and dispersion, respectively. EFs calculated using R6G was shown in Figure S20 ( ~ 3.4×107), where swarming states also outperformed aggregation and dispersion by 8.5- and 21.5-fold, respectively, verifying the superiority of swarming nanoprobes over conventional colloid-based SERS systems. Moreover, we investigated the detection limit of swarming nanoprobes using CV model molecule. As shown in Figure S21, Raman signal intensity exhibited a monotonic decrease when gradually decreasing the concentration of CV solution. The characteristic Raman peaks remained distinguishable until the CV concentration was as low as 10 nM. While the optimal detection window varies for different analytes depending on their intrinsic Raman cross-section and binding affinity to the probe, the demonstrated nM-level sensitivity for CV positions our platform favorably for a range of in vivo molecular sensing use-cases.

Enhanced Raman spectroscopy in reconfigurable swarming nanoprobes for adaptative, remote and multiplex detection

Beyond static performance metrics, we evaluated the environmental adaptability of swarming SERS nanoprobes in a dynamic capillary flow system (inner side: 0.8 mm × 0.3 mm, length: 10 cm) with programmable analyte exchange (Fig. 6a). A vortex swarm was magnetically stabilized at the capillary midpoint while a syringe pump alternated analyte inputs between deionized water and 10⁻⁴ M CV solution at 2.5 mm/s flow velocity. Raman spectra were acquired every 2 s through a 10× objective focused on the swarm centroid. Initial analyte switching (water → CV) induced time-dependent signal evolution (Fig. 6b): CV-specific Raman bands (1373, 1590, 1621 cm‒1) emerged within 20 ± 5 s, reaching 90% signal saturation at 300 ± 5 s. Reverse switching (CV → water) triggered rapid signal decay at 917 cm‒1, with complete baseline recovery (<5% residual intensity) after 400 ± 10 s (Fig. 6c), demonstrating reversible analyte adsorption/desorption kinetics. Multiplex detection was tested by sequential introduction of R6G and CV solutions. Spectral deconvolution in Fig. 6d, e revealed synchronized signal transitions: R6G intensity at 613 and 1510 cm‒1 decreased by 80% over 80 s, while CV signals at 917 and 1621 cm‒1 rose to 70% of maximum intensity. Combined with active mobility and on-demand deformability, swarming nanoprobes achieved intelligent navigation through microfluidic networks (Fig. 6f) via field-programmed morphological reconfiguration. In the open region at origin, vortex swarms (diameter: ~560 μm) navigated at 26.2 μm/s under rotating magnetic field, gathering the nanoprobes into a compact pattern. Upon encountering constricted channel (width: 500 μm), real-time switching from rotating to oscillating field induced a vortex-to-ribbon transition within ~3 s. The resultant ribbon swarm (width: 176 μm, aspect ratio 5.7) traversed narrow passages with 94% velocity retention (24.6 μm/s), maintaining structural integrity. Post-constriction reversion to vortex swarm enabled efficient navigation of subsequent curved paths, followed by arrival at the target position with CV solution via repeating these procedures. After fully collecting CV molecules, the swarming nanoprobes were magnetically navigated back to the origin for SERS testing. As shown in Fig. 6g and Movie S8, swarm maintained morphological stability throughout navigation even under prolonged operation, demonstrating robust adaptation to heterogeneous terrains. The SERS spectra in Fig. 6h showed CV-specific Raman signals at origin increased from undetectable level to distinct peaks after round-trip traversal, confirming the target acquisition capability. Moreover, multiplex detectability was demonstrated through parallelized sub-swarm operations. In the bifurcated channels (Fig. 6i), magnetic field modulation split swarming nanoprobes into two independent ribbon swarms, which simultaneously navigated divergent paths at matched velocities (22.1 μm/s) and collected CV and R6G at two different targets. Afterwards, sub-swarms were moved back and merged into one entity again for SERS testing at the origin. Post-mission spectral analysis confirmed dual-analyte acquisition: merged swarm exhibited unambiguous CV (1362 cm‒1: 2121 counts) and R6G (613 cm‒1: 1555 counts) signatures (Fig. 6j). These results verify the capabilities of swarming nanoprobes in shape-shifting navigation, remote analyte acquisition and parallelized sampling, positioning themselves as a transformative platform for in vivo biosensing.

In the above experiments, the swarming nanoprobes exhibited dual capabilities: facile analyte exchange in a flow-through capillary and effective analyte capture within a static microfluidic chip. This apparent functional duality does not represent a fundamental contradiction but rather highlights the adaptable nature of the swarm, which is governed by a key experimental parameter—the accessible fluid volume governing mass transport between the swarming probes and their environment. This intrinsic adjustability constitutes a pivotal strength of our active microswarm, enabling its function to be tailored to specific physiological sensing tasks. For scenarios requiring high temporal resolution in dynamic fluidic environments (e.g., pharmacokinetic monitoring), surface engineering of the nanoprobes to minimize nonspecific adsorption—through PEGylation or other inert coatings—can further accelerate exchange kinetics and enhance real-time monitoring performance. Conversely, for detection in quiescent or confined regions (e.g., pinpointing low-abundance biomarkers in a tumor microenvironment), functionalizing the nanoprobe surface with high-affinity capture ligands (e.g., antibodies or aptamers) can improve active capture efficiency and specificity. Such programmable adaptability underscores the versatility and broad potential of our platform for in vivo biosensing applications.

Dynamic hotspot-enhanced Raman spectroscopy for in vivo biosensing

Prior to in vivo deployment, we validated the efficacy of swarming SERS nanoprobes in undiluted rabbit blood. Programmed magnetic fields successfully generated vortex and ribbon swarms within blood (Fig. 7a), confirming swarm integrity despite physiological viscosity. Raman spectra acquired across states revealed critical performance hierarchies in Fig. 7b-c: dispersed nanoprobes enhanced blood-specific bands (1586/1638 cm⁻¹) by 89%, while swarming configurations amplified signals by 215% over dispersion. Additional twenty independent replicate Raman detection experiments in Figure S22 indicated both vortex and ribbon swarms maintained <8% RSD for the characteristic peaks of blood at 1586 and 1638 cm‒1, demonstrating the performance of swarming nanoprobes was robust and reproducible within complex biological fluid. By tuning magnetic field parameters, swarming nanoprobes demonstrated precise navigation in a 3D-printed vascular network (Figure S23 and Movie S9). Magnetic steering enabled traversal of all branches (n = 4), followed by full return to origin within 60 s. Subsequently, target analyte acquisition was validated in a blood-perfused microfluidic chip (Fig. 7d and Movie S10), in which, swarm navigated 15 mm path to CV injection site, collected CV molecules, and then returned to the origin for SERS testing (Figure S24). Post-mission Raman spectra in Fig. 7e exhibited unambiguous CV signatures (917/1373/1619 cm⁻¹) superimposed on blood backgrounds, with signal-to-background ratios >155:1. Multivariate analysis (Fig. 7f) confirmed swarm-specific enhancement across five diagnostic bands (917–1619 cm⁻¹), exhibiting 5.9-fold intensity elevation over blood controls. The in vivo feasibility of swarming nanoprobes was further validated in a rabbit model using a custom-integrated multimodal platform (Fig. 7g). Nanoprobes (200 μL suspension, 1 mg/mL) were administered into the marginal ear vein via a 22-gauge indwelling needle and then magnetically controlled using a six-axis robotic arm integrated with a rotating permanent magnet. Real-time swarm tracking inside blood vessel was achieved through high-resolution ultrasound imaging. As shown in Fig. 7h and Movie S11, the indwelling needle could be clearly recognized from surrounding blood vessel, followed by the deployment of SERS nanoprobes. Under the application of rotating magnetic field, nanoprobes formed a dynamically stable swarm pattern and propagated bidirectionally at 714.3 μm/s against venous flow (~5 cm/s, Figure S25), maintaining structural integrity through field-programmed vorticity modulation51–53. Post-procedure retrieval of nanoprobes was also achievable via reverse magnetic field actuation and indwelling needle withdrawal to ensure nanoprobe clearance from living body, addressing potential biosafety concerns. During the swarming manipulation of nanoprobes in the blood environment of rabbit ear vein, spectral acquisition was performed using a portable Raman system with a 785 nm excitation laser coupled to a fiber-optic probe. This wavelength was selected to overcome the strong optical attenuation of visible light (e.g., the 532 nm radiation used in prior SERS experiments) in biological tissue, which results in shallow penetration and significant competing autofluorescence. Shifting to the near‑infrared optical window is therefore essential for retrieving usable signals from deep within living subjects. Since the plasmonic enhancement mechanism underpinning our swarming nanoprobes is broadly effective and not confined to a specific excitation wavelength (Figure S26), the 785 nm laser could be successfully adopted. This choice provides the combined benefits of reduced tissue scattering, greater penetration depth, and minimal autofluorescence interference, enabling the acquisition of clear SERS spectra from intravascular environments.

As shown in Fig. 7i, baseline spectra showed broad features (400–2000 cm‒1) with peak intensities less than 48 counts, owing to the intrinsic complex components of blood vessel and signal interference from biological tissue, while swarming nanoprobes enhanced characteristic signals over tenfold through dynamic hotspots regeneration. Through a detailed literature review on SERS of blood components54–57, we performed the corresponding peak assignments as shown in Figure S27 and Table S1. The major peaks observed in in vivo SERS spectra corresponded to the characteristic vibrational modes of ubiquitous blood and vascular wall constituents, such as proteins (e.g., hemoglobin), lipids, and amino acids. The key observation is that the spectral features of these intrinsic biological components were consistently amplified upon the action of the swarming nanoprobes compared to control measurements, directly evidencing the platform’s operational efficacy in a living system. Quantitative band analysis further demonstrated swarm superiority across wide Raman shift range in Fig. 7j. For instance, the intensity of Raman band at 1452 cm‒1 reached 290 ± 30 counts for swarming nanoprobes, surpassing ear vein background (22±11counts) by 13.2-fold. This in vivo validation—combining precision navigation, real-time spectral acquisition, and retrievable operation—establishes a paradigm for minimally invasive molecular monitoring in living systems.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a novel approach for in vivo Raman detection through rationally designed swarming SERS nanoprobes. The engineered nanoprobes exhibit triple functionality: rapid magnetic response, plasmonic enhancement, and biocompatibility. Through precisely programmed magnetic fields (rotating and oscillating magnetic fields), we achieved spatiotemporal control of nanoprobe swarm dynamics, enabling both collective migration and adaptive pattern reconfiguration. Crucially, the swarm’s self-organization generates transient interparticle gaps that produce high-density SERS hotspots with exceptional spatial uniformity. Concurrent microflow formation enhances analyte transport to these hotspots, synergistically amplifying detection sensitivity by ~20-fold compared to dispersed nanoprobes. Validation in whole blood and in vivo rabbit models establishes the platform’s clinical potential. By bridging active matter physics with SERS biosensing, this work pioneers a frontier in intravital molecular diagnostics, offering unprecedented opportunities for real-time disease monitoring and personalized therapeutic interventions. The methodology’s inherent adaptability suggests broad applicability across cardiovascular, oncological, and neurological diagnostics, marking a significant leap toward clinical implementation of intelligent nanoscale biosensors.

To advance this technology from a promising proof-of-concept in rabbit models toward a practical diagnostic tool, future work must address key challenges in specificity, sensitivity, and clinical integration. A primary focus will be extending the platform’s biosensing capability to specific intravascular analytes—such as heavy metal toxins, tumor biomarkers, and atherosclerotic plaque components—by stably integrating molecular recognition elements (e.g., antibodies, aptamers) onto the nanoprobe surface. This functionalization must preserve colloidal stability, magnetic responsiveness, and dynamic self-assembly properties. Concurrently, detecting trace-level targets within complex biological backgrounds will require deploying advanced computational methods, including AI-driven spectral analysis, to deconvolute subtle spectral signatures from dominant matrix interferences. Furthermore, the nanoprobe architecture itself can be optimized. Reducing the average probe diameter from several hundred nanometers to the sub-100 nm scale is expected to allow for finer control of interparticle gaps, thereby exploiting more intense plasmonic coupling and potentially amplifying Raman signals by at least an order of magnitude beyond the current enhancement factors (>10⁷)17,58. Besides, this reduction in physical dimensions can lower the propensity for recognition and clearance by macrophages and other immune cells, thereby favoring the intravascular deployment and utility of the swarming nanoprobes.

To overcome the limited penetration depth of laser light in tissues, we envision integrating interventional medical techniques with Raman spectroscopy—for instance, by navigating a fiber-optic Raman probe59 through intravascular catheters or endoscopic devices to directly interrogate nanoprobe swarms at deep-seated lesions, thereby bypassing tissue interference. This hybrid approach could enable transformative applications in atherosclerosis mapping and early gastrointestinal cancer detection. Alongside these technical developments, the long-term biocompatibility, potential immune responses, degradation pathways, and clearance kinetics of the nanoprobes require comprehensive investigation beyond acute studies to ensure safety for chronic or repeated use. Finally, scaling up the reproducible synthesis of monodisperse, multi-layered nanoprobes and seamlessly integrating magnetic navigation with Raman detection into a user-friendly, clinically compatible workflow present important engineering and regulatory hurdles. Successfully addressing these aspects will establish a versatile and robust platform for precision biosensing across multiple clinical scenarios.

Methods

Materials

The chemicals of iron (III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O; 99%), ethylene glycol (EG; 99%), sodium acetate (NaAc; 99%), polyethylene glycol (PEG, Average Mn 2000), dopamine hydrochloride (98%), Tris hydrochloride (Tris-HCl; 99%), gold(Ⅲ) chloride trihydrate (AuCl4·3H2O; 99%), Sodium borohydride (NaBH4, 98%), Citric acid (99.5%), Silver nitrate solution (AgNO3; 0.1 N), Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP; Average Mw 8000), Ascorbic acid (99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 97%), Sodium silicate (Na2O(SiO2)x·yH2O, powder), Crystal violet (CV; 90%), Rhodamine 6 G (95%) were purchased from Aladdin Chemicals. The rabbit whole blood was purchased from Hongyang Biotech Corporation.

Preparation of SERS nanoprobes

Fe3O4 nanoparticles were prepared via a solvothermal method60. Briefly, 1.35 g FeCl3·6H2O and 3.6 g NaAc were successively dissolved in 40 mL EG by magnetic stirring for 30 min. Then 1 g PEG was added, followed by ultrasonication for 30 min to obtain a russet and turbid mixture. The mixture was sealed in a 50 mL autoclave, heated at 200 °C for 10 h, and then naturally cooled to room temperature. Finally, the product was washed with deionized (DI) water for 3 times with a permanent magnet and stored in water for further use.

The PDA layer was first coated on the surface of Fe3O4 via an in-situ polymerization process. Typically, 0.12 g Tris was dissolved in 100 mL DI water and 1 m HCl was added dropwise to adjust the pH value to 8.5. The obtained buffer solution was poured into a 250 mL beaker together with 50 mg Fe3O4 nanoparticle, followed by mechanical stirring and ultrasonication for 30 min. Next, 0.02 g dopamine hydrochloride was added to the mixture, which was further mechanically stirred and sonicated for 5 h in ice-water bath to obtain Fe3O4@PDA nanoparticles. Subsequently, the nanoparticles were washed with DI water for 5 times.

Fe3O4@PDA@Au nanoparticles were prepared via an electrostatic adsorption method. Firstly, 600 μL chloroauric acid solution (1%) and 600 μL sodium citrate solution (25 mM) were poured into a 100 mL beaker with 58.8 mL DI water, followed by magnetic stirring for 10 min. Then, 900 μL NaBH4 (200 mM) solution was added to the mixture and continued magnetic stirring for 2 h, and the AuNP solution was stored in dark conditions. Secondly, 0.158 g citric acid was dissolved in 50 mL DI water and 1 m HCl was added dropwise to adjust the pH value to 2.0. Then, 20 mg Fe3O4@PDA nanoparticles were added to the buffer solution followed by mechanical stirring and ultrasonication for 30 min. Next, the AuNP solution was poured into the Fe3O4@PDA nanoparticles solution and continued mechanical stirring for 10 min. Subsequently, the nanoparticles were washed with DI water for 5 times.

The Ag shell was coated on the surface of Fe3O4@PDA@Au nanoparticles via an in-situ reduction reaction61. Briefly, 20 mg Fe3O4@PDA@Au nanoparticles were dispersed in 50 mL DI water with 1 g PVP. Next, 1 mL AgNO3 solution was added to the mixture and followed by mechanical stirring and ultrasonication for 10 min. Then 0.5 mL 0.1 M ascorbic acid was added. The reaction was kept for 2 min followed by adding 2 mL NaOH solution (0.1 M). Finally, the nanoparticles were washed with DI water for 3 times.

The SiO2 shell was finally coated on the surface via hydrolysis reaction29. Typically, 0.1 g Na2SiO3 was dissolved in 50 mL DI water, and 20 mg Fe3O4@PDA@Au@Ag nanoparticles were added into the solution. After ultrasonication for 10 min, the mixture was heated to 90 °C, the reaction was kept for 3 h with mechanical stirring. The nanoparticles were washed with DI water for 5 times.

Actuation and control of swarming SERS nanoprobes

For swarm generation, parameter influence experiment, and swarm translation experiment: Typically, 2 μL SERS probes (1 mg/mL, dispersed in DI water) were added into a plastic chamber (22 mm*22 mm*3 mm) with 1 mL DI water. The SERS probes were initially gathered with a permanent magnet, and then the chamber was placed in the 3-axis Helmholtz electromagnetic coil setup. Subsequently, programmed magnetic fields were applied to actuate and control the swarm behaviors.

For swarm navigation experiment: Firstly, the printed channel model was filled with DI water, followed by adding 2 μL SERS probes (1 mg/mL, dispersed in DI water) to the initial region. Then, the printed model was covered with a cover glass to ensure clear imaging. The SERS probes were gathered with a permanent magnet and then the model was placed in the 3-axis Helmholtz electromagnetic coil setup. The swarm was controlled by programmed magnetic fields and recorded by a CCD camera.

Multiphysics simulation

Simulations of vortex and ribbon swarms formed by nanoprobes were performed using the Particle Tracing Module in COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1. For rotating magnetic fields, the field strength and frequency were set to 8 mT and 8 Hz, respectively. For oscillating fields, the magnetic field strength, frequency, and field ratio were set to 15 mT, 10 Hz, and 2.5, respectively. The fluid density and viscosity were defined as 1 × 103 kg/m3 and 8.9 × 10‒4 Pa·s, respectively. After finishing particle dynamics simulation, the coordinates of 2256 particles within a defined region (50 μm × 50 μm) were exported and analyzed by MATLAB R2022b. Through investigating the relative distance between two particles before and after a frame, the formed and separated interparticle-gaps could be identified. Then the positions and numbers of gaps at each frame were output for further analysis. Specifically, we defined a transient “hotspot” whenever this gap closed to within 5 nm, a threshold known to enable strong plasmonic coupling and significant SERS enhancement. In a dynamic swarm, these narrow gaps were not fixed in location; they continuously formed, broke, and reformed at new positions as the swarm rotated or oscillated. To quantify this spatiotemporal phenomenon, we superimposed a fine grid (40 × 40) over the simulation area, effectively dividing it into 1600 small observation blocks. Over 5 complete swarm motion cycles, we counted how many times a hotspot (i.e., a sub-5 nm gap) appeared within each specific block. The resulting count for a block, normalized by the total counts generated by swarms during observation period, represented the hotspot distribution probability at that location.

The surface electric field distribution of nanoprobe with two different gaps (2 nm and 100 nm) was simulated using COMSOL electromagnetic waves module. For the SERS probe nanoparticle model, the diameter of Fe3O4 was 430 nm. While the thickness of PDA layer, Au layer, Ag layer, and SiO2 layer was 8 nm, 5 nm, 23 nm, and 2 nm, respectively. The wavelength of the incident light was 532 nm. Simulation of the electric field distribution around nanoprobes was performed by the COMSOL frequency domain module.

SERS detection experiments

For the Raman enhancement mechanism research: Typically, 2 μL SERS probe (1 mg/mL, dispersed in DI water) was added into a plastic chamber (22 mm*22 mm*3 mm) with CV or R6G solution with a certain concentration (10‒4 mol/L for mechanism research). The SERS probes were initially gathered with a permanent magnet, and then the chamber was placed in the 3-axis electromagnetic coil setup combined with confocal Raman spectrometer. Subsequently, programmed magnetic fields were applied to generate specific swarms and the Raman spectrums were tested simultaneously.

For dynamic SERS detection experiment: Firstly, a square capillary tube (internal dimensions 0.8 mm*0.3 mm, length 10 cm) was filled with SERS probe dispersion liquid (0.1 mg/mL). The SERS probes were initially gathered with a permanent magnet. Then a silicone tube (inside diameter 1 mm) was used to connect the injector and capillary tube. A rotating magnet sphere was placed under the capillary to keep a dynamic swarm. Then, CV solution (0.1 mg/mL) was injected into the capillary tube, while the Raman spectrum of the swarm was continuously acquired with an interval of 2 s. The velocity of the CV solution was controlled by a syringe pump (1 mm/s in silicone tube).

For sampling detection experiment: Firstly, the printed channel models were filled with DI water, followed by adding 2 μL SERS probes (1 mg/mL) to the initial region and adding 10 μL CV or R6G solution (1 mg/mL, dispersed in DI water) to target region. Then, the printed models were covered with a cover glass to reduce liquid convection. The SERS probes were initially gathered with a permanent magnet, and then the chamber was placed in the 3-axis electromagnetic coil setup combined with confocal Raman spectrometer. Subsequently, programmed magnetic fields were applied to control the swarm motion and transformation in channel models. The Raman spectrum was obtained before and after sampling.

SERS detection in whole blood

For the SERS effect verification experiment in whole blood: Typically, 10 μL SERS probe (1 mg/mL, dispersed in DI water) was added into a chamber (9 mm*9 mm*1 mm) with 75 μL whole blood. The SERS probes were initially gathered with a permanent magnet, and then the chamber was placed in the 3-axis electromagnetic coil setup. Subsequently, programmed magnetic fields were applied to control the swarm and the Raman spectrums were tested simultaneously.

For swarm motion and sampling detection experiment: Firstly, the printed channel model was filled with whole blood, followed by adding 10 μL SERS probe (1 mg/mL, dispersed in DI water). The SERS probes were initially gathered with a permanent magnet. A rotating magnet sphere fixed on a 4-axis robotic arm was used for controlling swarm. The motion path of the magnetic ball was pre-programmed in the software. A CCD camera was used to record the swarm motion in models.

In vivo SERS detection experiment

The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee and Licensing Office of the Shenzhen Top Biotechnology Limited Company (TOPGM-IACUC-2023-0218). The animal was handled in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals provided by Topbiotech Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. A male New Zealand rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus, outbred, ~3 kg, ~8-month-old, Topbiotech Biotechnology Co.,Ltd., China) was used for in vivo detection experiment. Sex was not considered as a biological variable in the study design, as the primary objective was to demonstrate the physical feasibility of magnetic swarm navigation and SERS signal amplification, mechanisms that are independent of the animal’s sex. After the rabbit was anesthetized, 200 μL SERS probe (1 mg/mL, dispersed in 0.9% NaCl solution) was injected into the ear blood vessel through an indwelling needle. A rotating magnet sphere fixed on a 6-axis robotic arm was used for swarm holding and motion controlling. While an ultrasound imaging system was applied to track the location of micro swarm robot. Finally, a portable Raman spectrometer (785 nm laser) with a fiber probe was used to obtain the Raman spectrum.

Characterization techniques

The surface morphology and corresponding EDX spectroscopy of nanoparticles were captured by scanning electron microscope (SEM, GeminiSEM560, Carl Zeiss) and transmission electron microscope (TEM, G2 F20 S-TWIN, Talos). The modifications layer by layer were characterized by FTIR (Nicolet6700, Thermo Fischer Scientific) and XRD (MiniFlex600, Bruker). The optical absorption characteristics were tested by UV-Vis (UV2600, Shimadzu). The magnetic properties were evaluated by VSM (LakeShore7404, LakeShore). The Raman spectrums were obtained with a confocal Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR, HORIBA), while the Raman spectrums in the animal experiment were obtained with a portable Raman spectrometer (ATR3110, OPTOSKY).
