Enhanced recovery after surgery turnkey order set for periprocedural care of the patient undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement
Authors: Daniel T Engelman, Gorav Ailawadi, Mark J Russo, Keith B Allen, Pinak B Shah, Molly Szerlip, Adnan K Chhatriwalla, Patrick M Wieruszewski, Subhasis Chatterjee, ERAS Cardiac Working Group

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTranscatheter aortic valve replacement is now a leading treatment for severe symptomatic aortic stenosis across the risk spectrum. The choice between transcatheter and surgical aortic valve replacement remains a shared decision, particularly for patients aged 65 to 80 years. Despite extensive device-level randomized evidence and procedural standardization, periprocedural care pathways remain variable across programs. Turnkey order set standardizing periprocedural TAVR care toward safe next-day discharge.

Central Message

Integration of contemporary guideline recommendations into a standardized turnkey order set facilitates practical, comprehensive periprocedural care for transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

Perspective

Multiple evidence-based guidelines and consensus documents address transcatheter aortic valve replacement. Standardized adoption of best practices remains inconsistent across programs. This turnkey order set consolidates these recommendations into a practical bedside tool to support a streamlined and safe pathway from referral through 30-day follow-up.

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has transformed the management of severe symptomatic aortic stenosis. Once reserved for patients at prohibitive surgical risk, TAVR is now indicated for intermediate- and low-risk patients and represents the majority of isolated aortic valve replacements performed in the United States. The 2020 American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) Guideline for the Management of Patients with Valvular Heart Disease1 and the 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC)/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS) Guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease2 provide complementary frameworks for patient selection, procedural conduct, and follow-up. Although TAVR is percutaneous, several surgeon-led domains remain central to the pathway and justify its inclusion in a surgical Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) series. These domains include Heart Team oversight of patient selection and valve strategy, as well as surgical and alternative access approaches such as transaxillary, transcarotid, transcaval, and transapical routes. They also encompass lifetime aortic valve management, including preservation of coronary re-access, commissural alignment, and planning for future redo surgical replacement or TAVR-in-TAVR. Institutional review board review and consent were not required for this work, because there were no patients.

Despite substantial procedural maturation, periprocedural care pathways for TAVR remain heterogeneous. Standardized minimalist pathways such as the Vancouver and 3M Clinical Pathways3,4 have demonstrated that next-day discharge home is feasible and safe. In the multicenter 3M study, which spanned low-, medium-, and high-volume centers, the 30-day composite of all-cause mortality or stroke was less than 3%. Building on this foundation, multicenter fast-track interventions, including the FAST-TAVI II cluster randomized trial5 and the BENCHMARK study,6 have shown that protocolized periprocedural care safely shortens length of stay and increases early discharge after TAVR without increasing 30-day mortality or readmission. Single-center7 and multicenter8 experience further support that even same-day discharge home is feasible and safe in highly selected recipients of transfemoral TAVR who meet strict procedural and clinical criteria. These findings were consolidated in a contemporary systematic review and meta-analysis.9 Although cerebral embolic protection10,11 has not reduced stroke in randomized trials, antithrombotic regimens informed by dedicated randomized trials12,13 and structured pathways for the management of conduction disturbances14,15 have each contributed to refined periprocedural practice. Although individual components of periprocedural care have been evaluated in isolation, no previous resource has systematically consolidated the full spectrum of Class I and IIa recommendations into a phase-of-care framework spanning the preprocedural period through 30-day follow-up. The purpose of this document is therefore 2-fold. The first aim is to provide a structured narrative synthesis of the guideline, consensus, and randomized trial evidence relevant to each phase of periprocedural TAVR care. The second is to translate that evidence, through multidisciplinary expert consensus, into a concrete, implementable order set. Because the consolidated pathway is intentionally broader than any single protocol prospectively tested to date, it should be regarded as a framework for local adaptation and prospective evaluation. This limitation is addressed in the Limitations and Future Directions section.

This turnkey order set (TKO) is part of a series developed by the ERAS Cardiac Society.16 Companion documents within the series address patient blood management,17 surgical-site infection prevention,18 postoperative atrial fibrillation,19 perioperative pain management,20 preoperative medication management,21 and cardiac surgery-associated acute kidney injury.22

Methods

Subject matter experts from cardiac surgery, interventional cardiology, anesthesiology, nursing, and periprocedural quality improvement were consulted to review existing peer-reviewed society guidelines, expert consensus documents, and randomized trial evidence relevant to the periprocedural care of patients who undergo TAVR. Recommendations were translated into a sample TKO. Orders derived from recommendations that received Class I, Class IIa, or equivalent grading consistently across referenced sources appear in the TKO in bold type. Elements with inconsistent Class I or IIa grading, Class IIb grades, or that were supported by peer-reviewed evidence appear in italic type. A small number of orders appear in bold type on the basis of consistent randomized evidence in the absence of a specifically graded guideline recommendation. The intent was not to recapitulate the evidence base supporting individual recommendations, as that work was undertaken by the original guideline and consensus committees. Decisions regarding order inclusion were made on the basis of estimated benefit, risk, cost, implementation complexity, and generalizability. Each order should be considered in the context of local institutional priorities, resources, practices, and expertise. The order set underwent iterative review by the full ERAS Cardiac Working Group, with final approval obtained before submission, ensuring broad stakeholder input and generalizability across institutional contexts.

The supporting literature was identified through a structured, although not formally systematic, review framed by a PICO (population, intervention, comparator, outcome) question. The population was adults undergoing TAVR, with emphasis on the transfemoral approach. The intervention comprised individual evidence-based periprocedural care elements spanning referral through 30-day follow-up, and the comparator was usual or unstandardized care. Outcomes were length of stay, mortality, stroke, vascular and bleeding complications, conduction disturbance and permanent pacemaker implantation, acute kidney injury, readmission, and patient-centered recovery. PubMed/MEDLINE was searched through May 2026, when the original manuscript was submitted, for English-language society guidelines, expert consensus documents, randomized controlled trials, registry analyses, and systematic reviews using combinations of the terms transcatheter aortic valve replacement, TAVR, TAVI, enhanced recovery, minimalist, periprocedural care, conscious sedation, vascular closure, conduction disturbance, antithrombotic therapy, length of stay, and discharge. Reference lists of retrieved documents were hand searched. Formal dual-reviewer screening, quality scoring, and meta-analysis were not performed because the objective was translation of existing graded recommendations rather than de novo evidence synthesis. When recommendations conflicted, priority was given to the most recent guideline grading and to randomized evidence.

Standardized adjudication of periprocedural outcomes referenced throughout this document, including stroke, bleeding, acute kidney injury, vascular complications, and new conduction disturbance, follows the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 (VARC-3) framework.23 Investigators and quality teams are encouraged to adopt VARC-3 definitions for institutional outcome tracking and reporting to facilitate benchmarking across centers.

Core Program Safety Resources

This order set presumes, and does not replace, the base institutional safety infrastructure defined in the AATS/ACC/SCAI/STS Systems of Care document. Core elements include an interdisciplinary Heart Team with cardiac surgery and interventional cardiology, a catheterization laboratory or hybrid operating room meeting imaging standards, and on-site cardiac surgery with the capability for emergency open conversion, including immediately available perfusion support. Additional requirements are vascular surgery and endovascular rescue capability for access complications, anesthesiology support, echocardiography, and an intensive care unit experienced with patients recovering from transcatheter structural heart procedures.25 Programs must also ensure continuous, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week capability for urgent temporary pacing and timely permanent pacemaker implantation, whether provided by on-site electrophysiology or by credentialed surgical or interventional operators. This capability is required because high-grade atrioventricular block may develop or recur unpredictably after the procedure.14,15 Centers without continuous pacing coverage should not adopt the abbreviated monitoring and early discharge elements of this TKO without first defining escalation pathways, transfer agreements, and default extended telemetry.15

Preprocedural

Heart Team Evaluation and Risk Stratification

Major valvular and TAVR guidance recommends interdisciplinary Heart Team evaluation of all candidates for TAVR.1,2,24,25 The team should include interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, imaging, anesthesiology, and structural heart nurse coordination, with input from geriatrics, heart failure, or palliative care as needed. Frailty and cognition should be assessed with structured tools rather than clinical impressions alone. The Essential Frailty Toolset is favored, with gait speed, Katz ADL (activities of daily living), Mini-Cog, or the Montreal Cognitive Assessment used as adjuncts.26 Multidetector computed tomography (CT) of the aorta, great vessels, and iliofemoral system is essential for annular sizing, access planning, and coronary obstruction risk assessment, with inferior vena cava assessment performed when transcaval access is considered.24,25 Image acquisition should follow a dedicated TAVR-specific protocol as defined by the Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography. This protocol comprises electrocardiogram (ECG)-synchronized imaging of the aortic root with systolic-phase annular measurement, combined with CT angiography of the aorta and iliofemoral vessels, ideally with a single contrast administration.E1 The value of this acquisition extends beyond annular sizing, which itself reduces more-than-mild paravalvular regurgitation compared with echocardiographic sizing (5.3% vs 12.8%, P = .032).E2 The TAVR-specific dataset also provides coronary ostial heights and sinus dimensions for coronary obstruction risk assessment, leaflet and left ventricular outflow tract calcification, and membranous septum length, an anatomic predictor of high-grade atrioventricular block and permanent pacemaker requirement that complements electrocardiographic risk stratification.E3 It also defines predicted coplanar deployment angles and access-vessel caliber, calcification, and tortuosity. The same acquisition can screen for obstructive coronary artery disease. Meta-analysis demonstrates 97% sensitivity with high negative predictive value, albeit modest specificity in heavily calcified vessels.E4 In a pragmatic US series, more than one half of candidates for TAVR were exempted from invasive coronary angiography without excess coronary events at 1 year.E5 The 2025 ESC/EACTS guidelines now recommend coronary CT angiography before valve intervention in patients with moderate or lower pretest likelihood of coronary disease, and omission of invasive angiography should be considered when the planning CT is of sufficient quality to exclude significant disease. Randomized TAVR-specific comparisons of a CT-first versus invasive-first strategy are lacking.2 In current US practice, many programs already omit invasive angiography when the planning CT shows no suggestion of significant coronary artery disease, particularly in elderly patients without angina.E5 Transfemoral access remains preferred. Alternative access is selected by Heart Team review of vascular imaging.27 Because pre-TAVR CT often reveals incidental extracardiac findings, especially pulmonary nodules, programs should define communication and follow-up pathways beyond the TAVR team.28 Streamlining the preprocedural evaluation may reduce time from referral to treatment, a barrier identified by national quality initiatives such as Target: Aortic Stenosis.29

Conduction Risk Stratification

Conduction disturbance after TAVR remains a key driver of length of stay and long-term outcome. Permanent pacemaker rates vary by valve platform, from about 5% to 10% with contemporary balloon-expandable valves to 10% to 20% with self-expanding valves, although optimized implantation techniques have narrowed this gap. Risk also depends on implantation depth and patient substrate.30, 31, 32 In the STS/ACC Transcatheter Valve Therapy (TVT) Registry, the overall in-hospital pacemaker rate was approximately 11%, with wide institutional variation and decline over time.33 Baseline ECG should be reviewed for right bundle branch block, left bundle branch block (LBBB), first-degree atrioventricular block, bifascicular block, and PR or QRS prolongation, as these findings guide monitoring duration and pacemaker thresholds under ACC/AHA/Heart Rhythm Society and ACC consensus guidance.14,15

Preexisting right bundle branch block is the strongest baseline electrocardiographic predictor of high-grade atrioventricular block and permanent pacemaker implantation after TAVR.15 It is independently associated with increased cardiovascular mortality, and patients who do not receive a pacemaker are at elevated risk of cardiac death early after discharge.34 Because baseline conduction abnormalities lack sufficient specificity to justify preemptive pacing, the 2020 ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway recommends individualized management rather than universal monitoring.15 Discharge rhythm surveillance is triggered by a postprocedural change in conduction rather than by baseline findings alone. They should continue guideline-directed medical therapy for coronary and heart failure indications despite this risk and are generally not appropriate candidates for same-day discharge.15

Anemia, Iron Deficiency, and Patient Blood Management

Preprocedural anemia and iron deficiency are common in older patients who undergo TAVR and are associated with worse outcomes, including greater long-term mortality.37 Patient blood management guidance supports preprocedural screening and treatment.17 Screening begins with hemoglobin assessment, with iron, vitamin B12, and folate testing when anemia is present. Iron deficiency should be treated with intravenous iron.17 Because evidence is largely extrapolated from surgical aortic valve replacement and transfemoral TAVR usually involves minimal blood loss, screening intensity should be individualized. After iron treatment, hemoglobin should be rechecked before the procedure to confirm response, typically within about 2 weeks.

Medication Management

Preprocedural medication management follows established cardiac preoperative principles.21 For TAVR, antiplatelet and anticoagulant plans should be individualized to patient and procedural risk, including continued dual antiplatelet therapy when recent coronary stenting warrants it, given procedural unfractionated heparin use. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors should be held 3 to 4 days before the procedure to reduce euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis risk. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists may usually be continued with aspiration-risk mitigation, such as a 24-hour clear-liquid diet before sedation.38 Both classes are resumed once the patient is hemodynamically stable and tolerating a normal diet.21 Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers are generally continued, although same-day omission may be considered for hypotension or acute kidney injury risk. Beta-blockers, statins, and antiarrhythmics are continued through the morning of the procedure.

Fasting and Day-of-Procedure Care

Updated American Society of Anesthesiologists guidance permits clear fluids up to 2 hours before anesthesia induction. Solids should stop 6 hours preprocedure, or 8 hours for fatty meals.35 Carbohydrate-containing clear oral fluid 2 hours preprocedure may be considered for patients without diabetes.16 Weight-based cefazolin (2 g intravenously; 3 g if greater than 120 kg) should be given within 60 minutes of vascular access, with vancomycin substituted for beta-lactam allergy or known methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus colonization.18 Because Enterococcus faecalis is a leading cause of TAVR-associated infective endocarditis and is linked to transfemoral groin access, meticulous groin antisepsis and access-site hygiene are emphasized, although routine enterococcal-specific prophylaxis is not established.39 Antibiotic prophylaxis before transfemoral TAVR is extrapolated from sternotomy and surgical-site infection evidence and rests on weaker support than for open cardiac surgery. It is therefore presented as reasonable practice rather than an established Class I recommendation.

Intraprocedural

Anesthetic Strategy

Procedural sedation or monitored anesthesia care has emerged as the preferred strategy for the majority of transfemoral TAVR cases at experienced centers, with safety comparable with general anesthesia and, in large registries, shorter hospital length of stay.3,4,24,36,40,E14 Consistent with multisociety procedural sedation guidance, the intended depth of sedation should be explicitly defined and titrated using a validated sedation scale. Continuous monitoring should include capnography, with the capability to rescue a patient from a deeper-than-intended level of sedation.E6 In the 3M TAVR study, procedural sedation was a foundational element of the minimalist pathway that achieved next-day discharge in approximately 80% of patients.4 Subgroup analysis of the same cohort suggested that local anesthesia alone, without procedural sedation, was feasible for selected patients.E15 General anesthesia remains appropriate for alternative access, anticipated need for transesophageal echocardiography, patients who are uncooperative, or planned concomitant procedures such as leaflet mitigation, to prevent coronary obstruction. To facilitate communication with the patient during procedural sedation, eyeglasses, hearing aids, and dentures may be left in place when feasible.

Sedation staffing models vary widely across programs and often differ between high- and low-volume centers. In the 2022 European TAVI Pathway Registry, 27% of centers reported performing routine transfemoral TAVR without an anesthesia team continuously present in the laboratory.E16 Observational series of nurse-administered sedation directed by the implanting team suggest that this model can be safe in appropriately selected patients.E17,E18 These series include 2000 consecutive patients at a high-volume center in the United Kingdom (conversion to general anesthesia 1.2%; 30-day mortality 1.3%) and a smaller comparative series from Europe. No randomized or multicenter comparison of nurse-administered versus anesthesiologist-led sedation exists, and a 2026 interdisciplinary consensus from Italy rated nurse-administered sedation inappropriate across scenarios, a divergence that likely reflects differing national credentialing and regulatory frameworks more than conflicting outcome data. The practice therefore remains dependent on the institution, credentialing, and jurisdiction.E7 Where nurse-administered moderate sedation is permitted and credentialed, it should conform to multisociety sedation standards, including a dedicated trained observer distinct from the operator, continuous capnography, immediately available personnel capable of airway rescue, and a defined pathway for rescue from deeper-than-intended sedation. US hospital regulations reserve deep sedation for anesthesia-qualified providers.E6 This TKO therefore specifies sedation targets, monitoring, and rescue capability while leaving the provider model to institutional policy, patient complexity, and immediate anesthesia backup availability.

Vascular Access

Transfemoral access is preferred whenever anatomically feasible, consistent with the ACC/AHA, ESC/EACTS, ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway, and AATS/ACC/SCAI/STS Systems of Care recommendations.1,2,24,25 Ultrasound-guided femoral access reduces the risk of major vascular complications and is a core element of the minimalist pathway.5,E8 Where feasible, a single arterial and venous access site may be shared between the anesthesia and procedural teams to reduce the number of access procedures and procedural time. Alternative access (transaxillary, transcarotid, transcaval, or transapical) is selected on the basis of imaging review by the Heart Team according to individual anatomy and institutional expertise. Because vascular and access-site complications are among the most closely scrutinized quality metrics in the STS/ACC TVT Registry and a frequent source of interinstitutional variation, meticulous access and closure technique is paramount.

Closure technique directly affects hemostasis, time to ambulation, and discharge, and randomized data now inform device selection. In the pilot MASH trial, which was not powered for superiority, plug-based (MANTA [Teleflex]) closure was associated with a numerically greater access-site vascular complication rate than suture-based (dual Perclose ProGlide [Abbott]) closure (10% vs 4%, P = .16).E9 In contrast, the larger CHOICE-CLOSURE (CatHeter-based Strategies fOr Interventional ACcess SitE CLOSURE during Transfemoral Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation) trial found more access-site or access-related vascular complications with a pure plug-based than with a primary suture-based strategy (19.4% vs 12.0%, P = .029) despite faster hemostasis.E10 An individual patient-level meta-analysis of both trials favored suture-based closure (odds ratio, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.35-0.82 for access-site vascular complications).E11 The ACCESS-TAVI trial further demonstrated that a hybrid strategy combining one suture-based device with a collagen-plug device halved the VARC-3 composite of major or minor access-site vascular complications relative to dual suture-based closure (27% vs 54%, P < .001), with shorter time to hemostasis; the difference was driven by minor complications (23.5% vs 47.3%), with no significant difference in major complications (3.5% vs 6.5%, P = .125).E12 Immediate, secure hemostasis is the gateway to protocolized ambulation at 4 to 6 hours and to next-day or same-day discharge. A randomized comparison of 3 versus 6 hours of bed rest after transfemoral TAVR found no increase in access-site complications with earlier mobilization.E13 Programs should therefore standardize a primary closure strategy, a bailout algorithm (crossover balloon occlusion, covered stent availability, and vascular surgery backup), and structured predischarge access-site surveillance. Taken together, these data do not support a pure plug-based strategy as a program default, whereas a primary suture-based strategy and a hybrid suture-plus-plug strategy are both defensible.

Cerebral Embolic Protection

The PROTECTED TAVR trial of 3000 patients and the larger BHF PROTECT-TAVI trial both found no significant stroke reduction with routine SENTINEL (Boston Scientific) cerebral embolic protection during transfemoral TAVR.10,11 Given the lack of proven benefit and added cost, routine cerebral embolic protection is not recommended. Selective use may be considered at operator discretion for patients judged at high embolic risk, such as those with heavily calcified or bicuspid valves, valve-in-valve procedures, or degenerated leaflets with mobile debris on preprocedural imaging.

Procedural Anticoagulation, Contrast, and Hemodynamics

Procedural unfractionated heparin (60-100 U/kg) targeting activated clotting time of at least 250 seconds is standard. Selective protamine reversal at procedure end can facilitate early mobilization and reduce access-site bleeding. Careful anticoagulation management is important because femoral complications are closely tracked quality metrics. Renal protection is supported by contrast minimization, including low- or iso-osmolar agents, contrast-sparing techniques, and standardized prehydration, as recommended by the POSEIDON (Prevention of Contrast Renal Injury With Different Hydration Strategies) trial,E19 for patients with reduced glomerular filtration rate. Mean arterial pressure should be maintained at or above 65 mm Hg outside brief rapid pacing windows, and normothermia preserved with forced-air warming.

Postprocedural

Disposition, Monitoring, and Early Mobilization

Admission to the intensive care unit is not routinely required after uncomplicated transfemoral TAVR. Step-down or structural heart unit recovery with continuous telemetry is supported by the AATS/ACC/SCAI/STS systems of care document.25 Telemetry duration should reflect procedural and conduction risk per the 2020 ACC expert consensus decision pathway,15 from short observation for selected same-day discharge candidates to extended monitoring for new conduction abnormality. Core elements include a predischarge 12-lead ECG compared with baseline and ambulation within 4 to 6 hours after vascular hemostasis.3,4 Routine urinary catheterization is avoided, and arterial or central venous catheters, when used, should be removed as soon as clinically feasible to support mobility and reduce infection risk. Noninvasive blood pressure monitoring usually suffices, given central pressure measurement from the aortic root pigtail catheter. Central venous access is not routine, and the femoral pacing sheath can be upsized when larger-bore infusion access is needed.

Antithrombotic Therapy

The POPular TAVI trial randomized patients without an indication for oral anticoagulation to aspirin alone versus aspirin plus 3 months of clopidogrel after TAVR. Aspirin alone significantly reduced all bleeding (15.1% vs 26.6%) and nonprocedural bleeding (15.1% vs 24.9%) at 12 months, with no excess in thromboembolic events.12 The GALILEO trial, which evaluated low-dose rivaroxaban in patients without an oral anticoagulation indication after TAVR, was terminated early because of increased death, thromboembolic events, and bleeding in the rivaroxaban arm.13 Together, these trials support single antiplatelet therapy with aspirin in patients without an indication for oral anticoagulation and oral anticoagulation alone in patients with an existing indication. Both the 2020 ACC/AHA and 2025 ESC/EACTS guidelines have aligned with this evidence.1,2

These recommendations apply to standard native-valve TAVR. POPular TAVI excluded valve-in-valve procedures, and randomized antithrombotic evidence specific to valve-in-valve TAVR and redo TAVR-in-TAVR is lacking. In these populations, and in patients with recent or complex percutaneous coronary intervention, antithrombotic strategy should be individualized by the Heart Team on the basis of leaflet thrombosis, ischemic, and bleeding risk rather than applying the simplified pathway by default.E20

Rhythm Monitoring and Management

Patients with new high-grade atrioventricular block, complete heart block, or new LBBB with PR prolongation should be managed according to contemporary bradycardia and TAVR conduction guidance, which inform pacemaker timing and telemetry duration.14,15 Isolated new LBBB without PR prolongation does not by itself mandate permanent pacing. Most programs observe these patients on telemetry for at least 24 hours and exclude them from same-day discharge. Temporary pacing access should reflect anticipated need. Internal jugular access is preferred when postprocedural pacing retention is likely, whereas femoral access is often used in low-risk patients when the lead is removed at procedure end. Ambulatory rhythm monitoring should be considered at discharge for new conduction abnormalities that do not meet pacemaker criteria. When used, monitoring should be continuous, sensitive to progressive conduction disturbance, support adherence, and provide timely notification of clinically significant events. Mobile cardiac telemetry is preferred over intermittent or patient-triggered devices for at least 14 days. Programs should also ensure transmitted data are reviewed and actionable findings reliably communicated.E21

Postprocedural Atrial Fibrillation, Pain, and Kidney Injury

Postprocedural atrial fibrillation is less common after TAVR than after surgical aortic valve replacement but remains clinically relevant. Management should follow established postoperative atrial fibrillation guidance.19 Multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia should center on as-needed acetaminophen with judicious nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use in selected patients.20 Acute kidney injury prevention should emphasize avoidance of nephrotoxins, hemodynamic optimization, and serial creatinine and urine output monitoring.22

Discharge Readiness Assessment and Follow-Up

Discharge readiness should be confirmed with a structured assessment. Access sites should be examined for bleeding, hematoma, expanding ecchymosis, pulsatile mass, or bruit, with distal pulses compared with baseline. Any new asymmetry, pulse loss, or concerning finding should prompt the use of Doppler and duplex ultrasound to exclude vascular complications. A predischarge 12-lead ECG should be compared with baseline for new LBBB, atrioventricular block, PR or QRS prolongation, or atrial fibrillation, with new high-grade conduction abnormalities managed per contemporary guidance.14,15 The 24-hour telemetry record should be reviewed for arrhythmias not seen on ECG. Predischarge transthoracic echocardiography is not routine after uncomplicated transfemoral TAVR but should be obtained for clinical indications such as hemodynamic instability, suspected complication, or more than mild paravalvular regurgitation. Thirty-day echocardiography remains required for STS/ACC TVT Registry participation.

Laboratory criteria include stable hemoglobin, creatinine at or trending toward baseline, and no new electrolyte abnormalities. Patients should be hemodynamically stable without intravenous vasoactive medications, off oxygen or at baseline, afebrile, ambulating at baseline, tolerating oral intake, voiding, and at baseline cognition. Discharge medications should be reconciled, including antithrombotic therapy and restart timing for held agents. When criteria are met, next-day discharge is feasible and safe after transfemoral TAVR and may be considered for nonthoracic alternate access by expert consensus, although evidence is limited.3,4 Thirty-day Heart Team follow-up should include transthoracic echocardiography, ECG, access-site reassessment, antithrombotic review, and cardiac rehabilitation referral when eligible.

Trajectory Toward Same-Day Discharge

The minimalist transfemoral approach has progressively reduced length of stay after TAVR, with accumulating evidence supporting next-day discharge and, in carefully selected patients, same-day discharge home (Table 3). Across published series, eligibility criteria are consistent. These include uncomplicated transfemoral access under procedural sedation or monitored anesthesia care, no new conduction abnormality requiring monitoring or pacing, and no major vascular or bleeding complication. Additional criteria include hemodynamic stability without vasoactive support, patient acceptance of the discharge plan, and reliable home support with timely follow-up. Contemporary machine-learning risk models, such as a single-center model derived in transfemoral conscious-sedation patients that has not been externally validated, may complement these consensus-based criteria by providing individualized estimates of postprocedural risk.E23 The order set in Table 2 is designed to support both next-day and same-day discharge pathways when institutional protocols, resources, and patient selection are aligned. Same-day discharge should therefore be viewed as a program-dependent option rather than a universal target, with adoption guided by local expertise, case mix, operational capacity, and reimbursement considerations.

Limitations and Future Directions

This TKO has an important limitation: it is a consensus translation of existing evidence, not a prospectively validated care bundle. Most individual elements are supported by randomized trials or consistently graded recommendations. Structured pathways such as the Vancouver and 3M pathways, FAST-TAVI II, and BENCHMARK have demonstrated that protocolized periprocedural care safely shortens length of stay.3, 4, 5, 6 The composite order set presented here, however, is intentionally more comprehensive than any pathway evaluated in those studies, and its aggregate effect on outcomes has not been tested. Programs adopting the TKO should therefore implement it as a quality improvement framework, with local audit against VARC-3 outcome definitions and iterative adaptation.23 The supporting literature review, although structured, was not a formal systematic review, and order selection necessarily involved judgment regarding benefit, risk, cost, and implementation complexity. Coverage and site-of-service policy for transcatheter aortic valve replacement also continues to evolve; the order set is deliberately written to be independent of any single coverage framework, because Heart Team review, on-site surgical capability, and the surgeon-led domains the pathway presumes remain prerequisites under any of them.

Several areas of periprocedural TAVR care merit ongoing investigation. The optimal antithrombotic strategy for patients with subclinical leaflet thrombosis or hypoattenuated leaflet thickening detected on follow-up imaging is not established. The role of cerebral embolic protection in subgroups potentially at greater risk for cerebrovascular events, such as valve-in-valve procedures or bicuspid anatomy, remains uncertain despite neutral randomized trial results in overall study populations. Patient-level criteria for safe same-day and next-day discharge home require ongoing refinement, particularly with respect to conduction risk stratification, ambulatory rhythm monitoring, and remote monitoring after discharge. Standardized strategies for patients on chronic oral anticoagulation, and the integration of left atrial appendage occlusion with TAVR in selected patients, also warrant further study. Although alternate access use has declined with device evolution, the preferred nonthoracic alternate-access route lacks randomized trial evidence and remains guided by local Heart Team expertise.

Finally, programmatic implementation science approaches are needed to translate this and related ERAS Cardiac TKOs into measurable improvements across the full care pathway. National quality initiatives such as Target: Aortic Stenosis29 have identified delayed referral and protracted time-to-treatment as key barriers along the aortic stenosis pathway. Timely treatment (definitive therapy within 90 days of a Class I indication) is now established as a performance metric. Evidence should ideally be derived from well-designed multicenter randomized controlled trials. Future work should integrate preprocedural workflow efficiency and time from referral to treatment with the downstream periprocedural outcomes targeted by this order set, including length of stay, readmission, and patient-reported outcomes across centers of differing volume and resource.

Conclusions

The periprocedural care of patients undergoing TAVR has matured into an interdisciplinary pathway informed by contemporary valvular heart disease guidelines, dedicated randomized trials, and structured consensus documents. This TKO consolidates Class I and IIa recommendations into actionable, phase-of-care orders spanning patient blood management, infection prevention, atrial fibrillation, pain management, medication management, and acute kidney injury prevention. Local adaptation, audit, and continuous quality improvement remain essential to realize the demonstrated benefits of standardized pathways.
