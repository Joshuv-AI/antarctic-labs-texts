Reassessing Murinae phylogeny: integrated mitogenomic and codon usage evidence challenges the sister relationship of Micromyini and Vernayini
Authors: Zhongsong Wang, Di Zhao, Wenyu Song, Wenge Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Murinae subfamily, one of the most diverse and widely distributed rodent groups, is an important model in ecology, evolutionary biology, and biomedical research. However, deep-level phylogenetic relationships, especially the evolutionary status of key tribes, remain highly contentious. Although mitogenomes are widely used in mammalian evolutionary studies, an integrated analytical framework that combines comparative genomics, selection pressure, codon-usage dynamics, and morphological evidence is currently lacking. Here, we aim to reassess the deep phylogenetic relationships within Murinae, with a particular focus on verifying the proposed sister relationship between the tribes Micromyini and Vernayini.ResultsWe sequenced and assembled the complete mitogenomes of a single individual of Leopoldamys neilli and Micromys pygmaeus. Through a systematic comparison of mitogenomes from 117 Murinae species—representing 13 tribes and 50 genera, we identified both conserved and lineage-specific genomic features. Murinae mitogenomes were highly conserved in structure and gene order, with length variation primarily attributable to changes in the control region. Nucleotide composition was largely uniform across most lineages, though Micromyini and Vernayini displayed distinct compositional profiles, suggesting possible differences in evolutionary pressures. All protein-coding genes were under strong purifying selection, yet evolutionary rates varied nearly tenfold among genes (e.g., ATP8 versus COX1). Codon-usage bias was predominantly influenced by natural selection and correlated with phylogenetic relationships. Critically, our large‑scale phylogenomic analyses did not support the previously proposed sister‑group relationship between Micromyini and Vernayini. Instead, we recovered Vernayini as the sister group to the remaining Murinae excluding Hapalomys—a topology that received maximal statistical support (PP = 1.00, BS = 100%). This revised phylogenetic hypothesis is supported by multidimensional evidence: (i) principal component analysis of morphological traits reveals significant divergence between the two tribes; (ii) COX1‑based genetic distances (18.93 ± 0.01%) substantially exceed both the generic (12.61%) and subfamilial (18.08%) thresholds reported for Murinae; and (iii) distinct codon‑usage patterns further differentiate these tribes at the molecular level.ConclusionThis study provides the first large‑scale, integrated analysis of comparative mitogenomic data, codon usage bias, and morphological data in Murinae. We supply new genomic resources, reassess the phylogenetic framework of Murinae, definitively clarify that Micromyini and Vernayini are not sister groups, and demonstrate the power of combining genomic, compositional, and phenotypic data in systematic research. These findings offer a foundation for future phylogenomic studies while highlighting the importance of validating mitochondrial‑based hypotheses with additional data sources, such as nuclear markers or morphological traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12999-y.Keywords: Tribe, Mitogenome, Comparative genomics, Phylogenomic relationships, Codon-usage patterns, Morphological integration

Background

Rodents (order Rodentia) are among the most diverse and widely distributed mammals, playing pivotal roles in terrestrial ecosystems and serving as key models in biomedical and evolutionary research [1, 2]. Within this order, the subfamily Murinae exhibits exceptional diversity, comprising approximately 135 genera and 656 species with striking morphological and ecological adaptability [1]. Their near-global distribution—from tropical rainforests to temperate grasslands—and diverse life histories (from fossorial to arboreal) make murines a valuable system for studying adaptive evolution and speciation [3–5]. Furthermore, many murine species are significant agricultural pests, disease vectors, or important laboratory models, underscoring their dual relevance to socioeconomic issues and fundamental research [6]. Despite this diversity, deep-level phylogenetic relationships within Murinae, particularly at the tribal level, remain poorly resolved and often contentious [7]. Establishing a robust phylogenetic framework is therefore crucial for understanding the evolutionary history and adaptive radiation of this group.

Mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) are widely used in phylogenetic studies due to their compact structure, maternal inheritance, absence of recombination, and relatively high evolutionary rate [8, 9]. Compared to single genes or short sequences, complete mitogenomes provide richer phylogenetic information and allow comparative analyses of evolutionary patterns such as nucleotide composition, codon-usage bias, and selective constraints, thereby offering improved resolution for deep evolutionary questions [10, 11]. Indeed, mitogenomic data have contributed to resolving several longstanding phylogenetic controversies within Rodentia [12–14]. However, as uniparentally inherited markers represent a single genealogical history, phylogenetic inferences based solely on mitogenomes can be confounded by processes such as incomplete lineage sorting or hybridization [15, 16]. Thus, integrating mitogenomic data with multidimensional evidence—such as nuclear genes and morphological data—is essential for reconstructing a reliable and well-supported phylogenetic framework.

Despite the increasing availability of murine mitogenomes, most previous studies have focused either on restricted taxonomic subsets or on analyses of individual genes [4, 17–19]. A comprehensive comparative framework that examines mitogenome architecture, evolutionary dynamics, and codon-usage patterns across the subfamily is still lacking. This gap limits our ability to discern general patterns of molecular evolution and to fully exploit mitogenomic data for phylogenetic inference. A prominent example of these unresolved relationships involves the tribes Micromyini and Vernayini. Early morphological and molecular studies, often based on limited data, yielded conflicting hypotheses regarding the composition and relationships of Micromyini [4, 20–22]. Recent genomic-scale studies focused on Micromyini have provided more stable phylogenetic hypotheses, some of which are supported by fossil evidence [23, 24]. Concurrently, integrated molecular and morphological data led to the recognition of Vernayini as a distinct tribe and proposed a sister-group relationship between Vernayini and Micromyini despite their marked morphological divergence [14]. However, this sister-group relationship has not yet been tested with a large-scale mitogenomic dataset nor with a multifaceted analytical approach.

To address these gaps, we conducted the first large-scale comparative mitogenomic analysis of the subfamily Murinae, encompassing 117 species from 13 tribes and 50 genera—substantially expanding taxonomic coverage compared to previous studies. Our specific objectives were to: (1) systematically characterize Murinae mitogenomes by assessing their structural features, nucleotide composition, evolutionary rates, and codon-usage patterns to infer the underlying evolutionary forces; (2) reconstruct a robust, genome-scale phylogeny for Murinae to evaluate the proposed sister-group relationship between Micromyini and Vernayini; and (3) adopt an integrative approach that combines phylogenetic signals from mitogenomes with codon-usage patterns, DNA barcode divergences, and quantitative morphological data to provide a cross-validated perspective on murine evolutionary history. By integrating these multiple lines of evidence, we aim to resolve long‑standing controversies and establish a more reliable phylogenetic framework for this ecologically and biomedically important group.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA sequencing

The specimens of Leopoldamys neilli and Micromys pygmaeus analyzed in this study were collected from Weixi Lisu Autonomous County, Yunnan Province, China (27.18° N, 99.28° E; elevation: 3,300 m) by Wenge Dong. Following capture, the two individuals (one per species) were humanely euthanized in the field via cervical dislocation to minimize stress and pain. All procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Dali University (Approval No. 2021-P2-162) and were performed in strict accordance with the committee’s guidelines. Immediately after euthanasia, tissue samples (muscle, liver, and spleen) were collected aseptically using sterile instruments. Samples were promptly immersed and fixed in 95% ethanol, and subsequently stored at − 80 °C in the biospecimen repository of the Institute of Pathogens and Vectors, Dali University, China, until DNA extraction. Voucher specimens (voucher numbers: GS2504003, E210006) were deposited at the same institute.

Species identity was verified by amplifying and sequencing the mitochondrial CYTB gene using primers L14725_hsw1 (5′-ATGACATGAAAAATCATCGTTGT-3′) and H15915_hsw1 (5′-TCYCCATTTCTGGTTTACAAGACC-3′) [25], with reference to current taxonomic treatments [1, 26]. Total genomic DNA was extracted from ethanol-preserved muscle tissue using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Redwood City, CA, USA). Library preparation and 150 bp paired‑end sequencing were performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) by Shanghai Winnerbio Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). In addition to the newly sequenced mitogenomes, a total of 121 publicly available mitogenomes were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/). These included 115 Murinae species representing 13 tribes and 50 genera, as well as six outgroup species from Gerbillinae. Only three tribes (Vandeleurini, Pithecheirini, and Phloeomyini) of Murinae were not included due to the unavailability of complete mitogenome sequences in GenBank. Accession numbers and species information are provided in Table S1.

Genomic assembly and annotation

Raw sequencing data were assessed using FastQC v0.12.1 (https://data.pankbase.org/software-versions/fastqc-v0.12.1) for multiple quality metrics, including per‑base quality scores, GC content, duplication levels, and adapter contamination. Based on the FastQC reports, reads with mean Phred score < 30 were discarded using Prinseq v0.20.4 [27], and Illumina adapter remnants were trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.36 [28] using default parameters and a minimum read length of 50 bp. The mitogenomes of L. neilli and M. pygmaeus were assembled using a hybrid approach: (1) de novo assembly with MITOZ v2.3 [29] with default parameters (k-mer size: 39, minimum overlap: 20 bp), and (2) reference-guided assembly in Geneious Prime v11.1.5 [30] using complete mitogenomes of two closely related Murinae species, Micromys minutus (NC_027932.1) and Leopoldamys edwardsi (NC_025670.1), as templates (minimum overlap identity: 95%, minimum overlap length: 50 bp). The independent assemblies were validated by mapping the original reads back to the contigs with BWA v0.7.17 [31] and calculating coverage depth using SAMtools v0.1.20 [32]. This validation step confirmed ≥ 100× average coverage across each mitogenome, with an estimated per-base error rate of < 0.01%.

Structural annotation of protein‑coding genes (PCGs), ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and the control region (D‑loop) was initially performed with MITOS2 [33] with NCBI Translation Table 2 (vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code, RefSeq release 89). tRNA predictions were further verified using tRNAscan‑SE v2.0 [34] (search mode: vertebrate mitochondrial) and ARWEN v1.2 [35] (genetic code: 2). All automated annotations were manually refined by comparing them to homologous Murinae sequences retrieved from NCBI via BLASTn (e‑value < 0.001, identity > 95%) and by adjusting start/stop codon boundaries to conform to conserved motifs. The final annotated mitogenome sequences of L. neilli (accession PQ824082) and M. pygmaeus (accession PV670005) have been deposited in GenBank. Circular mitogenome maps were generated using Proksee [36] for visualization.

Characterization of Murinae mitogenomes

Sequence feature analysis and nucleotide composition

Sequence length and nucleotide composition were calculated for the mitogenomes from 117 Murinae species. AT‑skew [(A–T)/(A + T)] and GC‑skew [(G–C)/(G + C)] were used to quantify strand‑specific compositional bias [37]. All sequence metrics were computed using MEGA v11.0.13 [38]; results were visualized using ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online) and organized in Microsoft Excel 2021.

To examine variation in nucleotide usage across codon positions, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on aligned PCG sequences from all 117 species. For each sequence, the relative frequencies of adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), and cytosine (C) were computed separately at the first, second, and third codon positions, yielding 12 compositional variables per sample. PCA was conducted in R v4.3.1 using the ade4 v1.7 package [39] to identify major axes of variation in codon‑position‑specific base composition.

Selection pressure and nucleotide diversity

Evolutionary selection pressures acting on the PCGs were assessed by calculating the ratios of non-synonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitution rates (Ka/Ks). Values > 1, < 1, and ≈ 1 were interpreted as evidence of positive selection, purifying selection, and neutral evolution, respectively [40]. The 13 PCGs were extracted from the 117 species, aligned using MEGA v11.0.13, and analyzed in DnaSP v6.12.03 [41] with six species from Gerbillinae designated as outgroups. Nucleotide diversity (π) was estimated across the aligned PCGs using a sliding-window approach (window length: 100 bp; step size: 25 bp) [42] implemented in DnaSP.

Analysis of codon-usage patterns

Codon usage bias analysis

Codon usage bias (CUB) was evaluated by calculating the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) for the 13 PCGs across all mitogenomes from 117 Murinae species using MEGA v11.0.13 [38]. RSCU values quantify the relative preference for a codon within its synonymous group [43]; values > 1, < 1, and = 1 indicate over‑representation, under‑representation, and no bias, respectively [44]. Codons with RSCU > 1.6 or < 0.6 were classified as significantly over‑ or under‑represented [45]. Start/stop codon assignments and codon‑usage frequencies were compiled in the same step, and results were visualized as correlation heatmaps and bar plots using ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online, accessed 24 July 2025).

Correspondence analysis

Major trends in codon‑usage variation across species were examined with correspondence analysis (COA) [46]. The analysis was performed on RSCU values mapped into a multidimensional space defined by synonymous codon positions (60 dimensions for vertebrate mitochondrial codons, excluding stops). The first two principal axes (Axis 1 and Axis 2), which captured the highest proportion of variance in codon-usage preferences, were extracted to visualize phylogenetic divergence and selective patterns [47]. COA was implemented in R v4.3.1 with the ggplot2 v3.5.0 package.

Effective number of codons

The effective number of codons (ENC, also denoted Nc) was calculated to quantify the degree of CUB [48]. ENC values range from 20 (maximum bias, only one codon used per amino acid) to 61 (no bias, with all synonymous codons used equally); for the vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code (NCBI Translation Table 2), the maximum is 60. Values < 35 are considered to reflect substantial bias [49]. ENC‑plots were generated by plotting Nc against GC content at synonymous third codon positions (GC3s). Points falling on or near the theoretical neutral curve are interpreted as mutation‑driven, whereas points below the curve suggest selection‑driven codon usage [48]. The analysis and visualization were implemented in R v4.3.1 using the ggplot2 v3.5.0 package.

Parity rule 2 analysis

Parity rule 2 (PR2) analysis was used to assess base‑composition bias at synonymous third positions independent of overall GC content. We plotted AT‑bias [A3/(A3 + T3)] against GC‑bias [G3/(G3 + C3)], where A3, T3, G3, and C3 are the base frequencies at third codon positions of four‑fold degenerate amino acids (Ala, Gly, Pro, Thr, Val, Leu, Ser, Arg) [50]. The central point (0.5, 0.5) indicates no bias (A3 = T3, G3 = C3), reflecting purely mutational pressure; deviations from this point signal the influence of natural selection [50]. Positional base frequencies were computed and the PR2 plot generated using R v4.3.1 with the Biostrings v2.68.1 package.

Neutrality plot analysis

To quantify the relative contributions of mutation and selection to CUB, a neutrality plot was constructed by regressing GC content at first and second codon positions (GC12) against GC content at synonymous third positions (GC3s) [51]. Each point represents a species‑level coding sequence (CDS). A regression slope close to 1 indicates that codon usage is largely shaped by mutational pressure, whereas a slope near 0 implies dominant natural selection [52]. GC12 and GC3s were calculated with Python v3.10.12 using SciPy v1.11.1; the plot and linear regression were generated in R v4.3.1 with ggplot2 v3.5.0 package.

Morphometric analysis of Micromyini and Vernayini

Morphological variation between Micromyini and Vernayini was assessed using principal component analysis (PCA). Ten morphological traits were included: head-body length, tail length, ear length, hind foot length, greatest length of the skull, skull basal length, zygomatic breadth, upper toothrow length, upper molar row length, and lower toothrow length. Detailed measurements are provided in Supplementary Table S2. The dataset combined measurements from a newly examined voucher specimen (E210006) with published data from references [18, 53]. For species with multiple individuals available, trait values were averaged to obtain species means, because individual-level data were not fully available from the published sources and sample sizes varied considerably among species. Standard deviations (SD) for each trait are also provided in Supplementary Table S2 to reflect intraspecific variation. The species included in this analysis were Micromys minutus, M. erythrotis, and M. pygmaeus (Micromyini), and Vernaya fulva, V. foramena, V. meiguites, and V. nushanensis (Vernayini). Prior to PCA, all measurements were log-transformed and centered to unit variance using the scale function in R. PCA was performed using the dudi.pca function in the ade4 v1.7 package for R v4.3.1.

Genetic distance analysis of Micromyini and Vernayini

Genetic divergence between Micromyini and Vernayini was quantified based on the mitochondrial COX1 barcode region. We compiled a dataset of seven sequences, encompassing all publicly available sequences for the two tribes as well as the newly generated sequences from this study. All COX1 sequences were extracted from the mitogenomes used in this study, and their GenBank accession numbers correspond to the mitogenome accession numbers listed in Table S1. The species used in this analysis were the same as those in the morphometric analysis. Sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm implemented in MEGA11 v11.0.13 [38]. Pairwise interspecific genetic distances were calculated under the Kimura-2-parameter (K2P) model, a standard metric for DNA barcoding studies. The analysis was performed in MEGA11 with uniform rates among sites and pairwise deletion of gaps/missing data.

Phylogenetic reconstruction

Phylogenetic relationships within Murinae were reconstructed based on concatenated sequences of 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs) and two ribosomal RNA (12 S and 16 S rRNA) genes obtained from 117 Murinae species and six outgroup species from Gerbillinae (Table S1). Gene sequences were extracted and concatenated using PhyloSuite v2.0 [54] with default parameters to generate a combined PCG + rRNA (PCGRNA) alignment. Multiple sequence alignments were performed for each gene using MAFFT v7.313 [55] under the auto strategy, followed by secondary refinement using MACSE v2.60 [56] to optimize the alignment of coding sequences. Ambiguously aligned regions were removed using Gblocks v0.91 [57] for PCGs and trimAl [58] for rRNA genes with default parameters. The trimmed sequences were then concatenated to construct the final dataset for subsequent phylogenetic analyses. Summary statistics of the concatenated alignment before and after trimming, including total alignment length, conserved sites, variable sites, parsimony‑informative sites, and GC content, are provided in Table S3. Substitution saturation of the complete alignment was assessed with Xia’s test [59] implemented in DAMBE v7.3.32 [60]; the absence of significant saturation supported the suitability of the dataset for phylogenetic inference (Table S4).

To determine the optimal partitioning scheme and optimal substitution models for ML and BI analyses, ModelFinder [61] was used under the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Partitioning was performed separately for PCGs and rRNAs: PCGs were partitioned by codon position (1st, 2nd, and 3rd positions), and rRNAs were partitioned by gene (12 S and 16 S). The best-fit substitution model was selected independently for each partition (Table S5). Phylogenetic reconstructions were performed using both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods to cross-validate the results. ML analysis was conducted in IQ-TREE v2.2.0 [62]. The best-fit substitution model for each partition was independently selected by ModelFinder (Table S5), and the analysis was performed with default parameters unless otherwise specified. Branch support was evaluated using the standard nonparametric bootstrap with 1,000 replicates as implemented in IQ‑TREE (the -b option). Bootstrap support (BS) values ≥ 70% were considered significantly supported [63, 64]. BI analysis was performed in MrBayes v3.2.6 [65] with the best-fit models independently identified by ModelFinder (Table S5). Two independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs were executed for 10 million generations, sampling every 1,000 generations, with each run containing four chains. Convergence was assessed by checking the average standard deviation of split frequencies (< 0.01) and by visualizing trace plots using Tracer v1.7 [66]. The first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in, and the remaining samples were used to construct a 50% majority-rule consensus tree, with node support evaluated by posterior probability (PP). Nodes with PP ≥ 0.95 were considered strongly supported [67]. All phylogenetic trees were rooted using the six outgroup species from Gerbillinae. The resulting consensus trees were visualized and annotated using the iTOL v7.5 (https://itol.embl.de/), and final figures were prepared for publication using Adobe Illustrator 2023 without altering the tree topology.

Divergence time estimation

To estimate divergence times among major Murinae lineages, we performed divergence time analyses using a Bayesian molecular clock approach based on the phylogenetic topology recovered from the BI analysis. The analysis was conducted in PhyloSuite v2.0 [54] using the MDGUI interface, which integrates the MCMCtree module of the PAML package [68].

Input files for the analysis comprised the concatenated PCGRNA alignment used in the phylogenetic analyses and a rooted tree with stable topology (the BI consensus tree). Six fossil calibration points were incorporated (Table S6), selected based on the comprehensive fossil calibration scheme of Aghová et al. (2018) [22]. Calibrations were applied to the corresponding nodes using the visual tree editor in MDGUI, with soft bounds defined as minimum and maximum age constraints (e.g., between-bound). The root node (crown age of Muridae) was calibrated following the same study, with a soft-bound constraint of 17.28–22.81 million years ago (Ma) [22].

A correlated rates model (clock = 2) was applied to allow evolutionary rates to vary among lineages in a correlated manner, implemented under the approximate likelihood (FAST) mode in MCMCtree [54]. The same substitution models and partitioning scheme employed in the Bayesian phylogenetic inference were applied in the divergence time analysis. Four independent Markov chains were run for 20 million generations, sampling every 100 generations. To ensure convergence, the “Repeat for convergence” option was enabled, performing two independent replicate runs, with the first 15% of generations discarded as burn‑in. Convergence was assessed using Tracer v1.7 [66], with effective sample size (ESS) values > 200 considered acceptable for all parameters. Results from the two independent replicates were combined to generate a maximum posterior probability tree. The final chronogram was visualized using TVBOT v2.6.1 (https://www.chiplot.online/tvbot.html), with geological time scales annotated according to the International Commission on Stratigraphy (https://stratigraphy.org/chart/). Divergence times are presented with 95% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals for each node.

Results

Mitogenomic characteristics of Murinae

Genome structure and nucleotide composition bias

The complete mitogenomes of L. neilli (16,301 bp) and M. pygmaeus (16,236 bp) were sequenced and annotated (GenBank accessions PQ824082, PV670005). Comparative analysis of mitogenomes from 117 Murinae species confirmed conserved gene order, with ND6 and eight tRNAs on the light-strand (L) and remaining genes on the heavy-strand (H) (Fig. 1, Table 1). Total mitogenome length ranged from 15,975 bp (Leopoldamys sabanus) to 16,351 bp (Vernaya foramena) (mean ± SD: 16,279 ± 41 bp), with length variation largely attributable to differences in the D‑loop region (874 ± 38 bp). In contrast, the lengths of PCGs (11,394 ± 9 bp), tRNAs (1,501 ± 7 bp), rRNAs (2,524 ± 10 bp), and the origin of light-strand replication (OL, 31 ± 1 bp) were relatively constant (Fig. 2A, Table S7). Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between D-loop length and total mitogenome length (y = 0.992x + 15400, R² = 0.8332, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Notably, species of the tribe Vernayini possessed consistently longer D‑loops (mean = 930 bp) and correspondingly larger mitogenomes (mean = 16,340 bp) compared with other Murinae tribes (mean total length ~ 16,276 bp), whereas Micromyini exhibited the shortest average mitogenome length (mean = 16,235 bp) among the tribes examined (Fig. 2C, Table S7).

Furthermore, a significant negative correlation was observed between A + T content and AT‑skew across the entire Murinae dataset (P < 0.05), with most data points clustering tightly along the regression line; this trend was consistently validated within all seven tribes (Micromyini, Vernayini, Murini, Rattini, Praomyini, Apodemini, and Arvicanthini) (Fig. 3C, Table S7). Similarly, a significant negative correlation between G + C content and GC‑skew was identified in six of the seven tribes, with the exception of Apodemini (Fig. 3D, Table S7).

Principal component analysis of base composition

PCA of base frequencies across the three codon positions revealed that most variation occurred at the third position (see Fig. S1 and Table S8 for the full PCA plot and detailed statistics). Although most Murinae species clustered tightly in the PCA space, several lineages showed marked divergence. Notably, members of the tribe Vernayini exhibited extreme A3 frequency (49.45 ± 4.36%) and minimal C3 frequency (22.28 ± 4.04%), indicating substantial compositional distinctions from Micromyini and the main Murinae cluster. This compositional divergence is consistent with the distant phylogenetic relationship between Vernayini and Micromyini recovered in our phylogenomic analyses (see below).

Selection pressure and nucleotide diversity

All 13 mitochondrial PCGs exhibited strong purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 0.5), although the strength of selection varied markedly among genes (Fig. 4A). ATP8 showed the highest evolutionary rate (Ka/Ks = 0.327 ± 0.060), followed by ND2 (0.311 ± 0.041). In contrast, the COX gene family was the most constrained (Ka/Ks = 0.022 ± 0.004), with CYTB being the next most conserved (0.061 ± 0.008). Nucleotide diversity across PCGs was generally low (Fig. 4B). ND2 displayed the highest π (0.227), consistent with its relatively relaxed selective constraint. Conversely, COX genes and CYTB showed notably low diversity, with COX1 being the most conserved (π = 0.148).

Codon-usage patterns in Murinae mitogenomes

Start and stop codon usage

Analysis of start and stop codon usage across the mitogenomes from 117 Murinae species revealed both conserved and lineage‑specific patterns (Fig. 5, Table S9). Five start codon types were identified: ATG, ATA, ATT, ATC, and GTG (Fig. 5A). The canonical start codon ATG was highly conserved across 11 PCGs (excluding ND2 and ND3) and served as the exclusive start codon in seven genes (ND4L, COX1, COX2, COX3, ATP6, ATP8, and CYTB). The non‑canonical start codon GTG was less frequent, occurring in ND1 of 79.5% (93/117) of species; additionally, GTG was used in ND6 of Leggadina lakedownensis and in ND4 of Dasymys incomtus. Start codon diversity varied among genes: ND1, ND2, and ND5 each exhibited four start codon types—ND1 used GTG, ATG, ATA, and ATT; ND2 and ND5 used ATA, ATT, ATC, and ATG. ND3 displayed three types (ATA, ATT, and ATC), whereas the remaining PCGs showed limited start codon diversity (Fig. 5A, Table S9).

Comparison of start codons (A) and stop codons (B) of 13 PCGs in the mitogenomes from 117 Murinae species. The names of PCGs are shown on the X-axis, and number of species are shown on the Y-axis

Five stop codon types were identified, including three canonical forms (TAA, TAG, AGA) and two truncated variants (TA‑ and T--). The canonical stop codon TAA was widely distributed across 12 PCGs, although its frequency varied among genes (Fig. 5B). The truncated T-- was the exclusive stop codon for ND4 in all 117 species. The rare stop codon AGA was observed only three times: in ND1 of Micromys erythrotis, and in ND2 of Niviventer confucianus and Dacnomys millardi. The truncated TA‑ was detected only in COX3 of 26 species and in ND1 of Dasymys incomtus, whereas the truncated T-- occurred frequently across seven PCGs (ND1, ND2, ND4, ND5, ND6, COX3, and CYTB) (Fig. 5B, Table S9).

Codon usage bias

Total codon numbers (excluding stop codons) of Murinae mitogenomes ranged from 3,705 to 3,793 (mean ± SD: 3,789 ± 9) (Table S10). Leucine (Leu1) and isoleucine (Ile) were the most frequent amino acids (441 ± 31 and 377 ± 11 occurrences, respectively), whereas cysteine (Cys) and serine (Ser1) were the least frequent (29 ± 1 and 54 ± 3) (Fig. 6A, Table S10). Preferred codons predominantly ended in A or U at the third position (Fig. 6B), consistent with the high A + T content observed at third codon positions across PCGs (Table S11). RSCU analysis confirmed strong codon-usage bias: 24 of the 60 synonymous codons showed preference (mean RSCU > 1), with 12 NNA-type codons being over-represented (RSCU > 1.6) and 14 NNG-type codons under-represented (RSCU < 0.6) (Fig. 7, Table S12). Codon-usage profiles were highly conserved across species, though tribe-specific signatures were evident (Fig. 7). For example, Micromyini displayed obviously lower RSCU for proline CCA (1.69 ± 0.03) than other Murinae (2.32 ± 0.31), whereas Vernayini exhibited a distinct RSCU for leucine UUA (1.93 ± 0.04 vs. 1.38 ± 0.21) (Fig. 7, Table S13).

Divergence dating

To obtain robust divergence time estimates for Murinae and to avoid systematic underestimation of the crown age, we conducted molecular dating analyses based on six rigorously evaluated fossil calibration points from Aghová et al. (2018) [22] (Table S6). The estimated divergence times are presented in Fig. 12. The split between Gerbillinae and Murinae was inferred to have occurred in the Early Miocene. The most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of Murinae was dated to 15.40 Ma (95% highest posterior density [HPD]: 14.49–16.33 Ma) in the Middle Miocene. Successive divergence events at the base of Murinae sequentially separated Clade A (Hapalomyini), Clade B (Vernayini), Clade C (Micromyini), and Clade D (core murines). The Vernayini (Clade B) diverged at 13.53 Ma (95% HPD: 12.79–14.30 Ma), with intratribal diversification within the crown group of Vernayini initiated at 5.23 Ma (95% HPD: 3.43–7.07 Ma). Similarly, the Micromyini (Clade C) diverged from the lineage leading to the core murines at 12.89 Ma (95% HPD: 12.21–13.58 Ma).

Within Clade D (core murines), Rattini was inferred as sister to the remaining core murines, with this divergence dated to 11.96 Ma (95% HPD: 11.39–12.52 Ma). Crown group diversification within Rattini was initiated at 10.65 Ma (95% HPD: 10.05–12.26 Ma). This period (~ 10.7–7.0 Ma) represents a major phase of species diversification across the core murines. Within the sister group comprising Chiropodomyini and Hydromyini, crown group diversification was initiated at 10.20 Ma (95% HPD: 9.55–10.80 Ma) and 7.00 Ma (95% HPD: 5.54–7.74 Ma), respectively. Within the clade comprising Millardini, Otomyini, and Arvicanthini, Millardini was sister to the clade comprising Otomyini and Arvicanthini, with the Millardini diverging at 10.42 Ma (95% HPD: 9.97–10.89 Ma). Otomyini and Arvicanthini were recovered as reciprocally sister, with crown lineages diverging at 9.60 Ma (95% HPD: 9.13–10.08 Ma) and 9.00 Ma (95% HPD: 8.53–9.50 Ma), respectively. The MRCA of the clade comprising Malacomyini, Murini, Apodemini, and Praomyini was dated to 10.60 Ma (95% HPD: 10.14–10.99 Ma) in the Late Miocene. Malacomyini and Murini formed a sister group, diverging at 10.01 Ma (95% HPD: 9.58–10.44 Ma), with subsequent crown group diversification in Murini initiated at 7.61 Ma (95% HPD: 7.29–8.02 Ma). Similarly, Apodemini and Praomyini were reciprocally sister to each other, with their MRCA dated to 10.24 Ma (95% HPD: 9.84–10.64 Ma). Crown group diversification was initiated at 9.27 Ma (95% HPD: 8.93–9.58 Ma) in Apodemini and 7.26 Ma (95% HPD: 6.54–7.95 Ma) in Praomyini.

Discussion

Structural conservation and evolutionary dynamics of Murinae mitogenomes

In this study, we sequenced and assembled the complete mitogenomes of L. neilli and M. pygmaeus, providing new genomic resources for Murinae. Comparative analysis of all mitogenomes from 117 Murinae species confirmed strong structural conservation, retaining the typical eutherian gene content, order, and organization without rearrangement [70–72]. The total mitogenome length ranged from 15,975 bp to 16,351 bp (mean ± SD: 16,279 ± 41 bp), with length variation largely attributable to differences in the D‑loop region (mean 874 ± 38 bp). In contrast, the lengths of PCGs, tRNAs, rRNAs, and the OL region were highly conserved, showing minimal variation (< 10 bp). This pattern aligns with observations in other vertebrates [9, 73] and supports the view that length variation in mammalian mitogenomes is driven largely by expansion and contraction of tandem repeats in the control region [74, 75].

Base composition analysis revealed a pronounced AT bias across Murinae mitogenomes (mean A + T content: 63.65 ± 1.14%), consistent with patterns widely reported in mammals [76]. Elevated A + T content may be linked to strand‑specific deamination during mitochondrial DNA replication and could influence genomic stability and evolutionary rate [77, 78]. Nucleotide skewness analysis indicated consistently negative GC‑skew in complete genomes, PCGs, rRNAs, and the D‑loop, while positive AT‑skew was observed across most regions, with the highest value in the OL region. Notably, we observed significant negative correlations between A + T content and AT‑skew, as well as between G + C content and GC‑skew, across all major murine tribes—including Micromyini, Vernayini, Murini, Rattini, Praomyini, and Arvicanthini. This universal pattern strongly supports the pervasive influence of strand‑specific mutation pressure on mitochondrial base composition [76]. However, lineage‑specific differences in the slope and strength of these regressions (Fig. 3C and D) likely reflect variation in selective constraints or the relative efficacy of genetic drift [79].

PCA of nucleotide composition across PCGs revealed that most variation occurred at the third codon position. The distribution patterns observed are consistent with those reported in other vertebrate mitogenomes [80, 81], characterized by balanced base frequencies at first codon positions, low G but elevated T at second positions, and a marked under‑representation of G at third positions—a configuration that may reflect optimization for translational efficiency [82]. While most Murinae species clustered tightly in the PCA space, Vernayini and Micromyini occupied distinct positions, implying distinct evolutionary trajectories and deep genetic divergence. Furthermore, considerable variation was observed within Apodemini, Arvicanthini, and Hydromyini, suggesting high intratribal heterogeneity in mutational or selective pressures [52].

Evolutionary analysis indicated that all PCGs were under strong purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 0.5), in line with patterns observed in other metazoans [83]. However, the strength of selective constraint varied considerably among genes. ATP8 evolved most rapidly (Ka/Ks = 0.327 ± 0.060), whereas COX1 was most constrained (0.022 ± 0.004), representing a > 10-fold difference in selective pressure. Nucleotide diversity analysis supported these findings: COX1 and CYTB exhibited both the lowest Ka/Ks and π values, reinforcing their established utility as DNA barcoding markers [84]. In contrast, ND2 displayed the highest nucleotide diversity (π = 0.227) along with relatively elevated Ka/Ks ratio, suggesting a regime of relaxed purifying selection [85]. The distinctive evolutionary pattern of ATP8—high Ka/Ks but low π—likely arises from its specific biological attributes. Functionally, as an ATP synthase subunit not directly involved in proton translocation, ATP8 may accommodate greater amino acid variation [86, 87]. Structurally, as the shortest gene in the mammalian mitogenome, its limited length may promote the fixation of mutations while constraining the total sequence space for variation [88]. Together, these functional and structural features help explain the unique evolutionary trajectory of ATP8 within Murinae mitogenomes.

Codon-usage patterns and evolutionary constraints in Murinae mitogenomes

Analysis of start and stop codon usage across the mitogenomes from 117 Murinae species revealed distinct evolutionary patterns in translation initiation and termination. The near‑ubiquitous use of ATG as the start codon across most PCGs reflects strong evolutionary conservation of translation initiation in Murinae mitogenomes. However, the high frequency of GTG in ND1 (79.5% of species) and the exceptional start codon diversity in ND5 suggest that certain genes experience relaxed selective constraints on initiation, possibly due to alternative translation mechanisms or compensatory mutations [9, 89, 90]. Similarly, stop codon usage is largely conserved (TAA and T-- dominate)—consistent with trends in mammalian mitogenomes [72]. Rare codons such as AGA occurred only in specific gene-species combinations (e.g., ND1 of Micromys erythrotis, ND2 of Niviventer confucianus and Dacnomys millardi), suggesting potential lineage-specific molecular adaptations or stochastic evolutionary outcomes [91].

Codon usage across Murinae mitogenomes was characterized by strong evolutionary constraints. The narrow range of total codon numbers (3,789 ± 9) and the overrepresentation of leucine and isoleucine highlight strong compositional constraints linked to the structural and functional requirements of mitochondrial membrane proteins [73, 92]. Preferred codons predominantly ended with A or U, corresponding to the overall AT‑rich composition of Murinae mitogenomes and likely reflecting the underlying mutational bias of mitochondrial DNA [93, 94]. RSCU analysis confirmed non-random codon usage across all amino acids [45, 48], and phylogenetically related species generally share similar RSCU profiles. Notably, Micromyini and Vernayini exhibit the most distinct tribe-specific codon‑usage signatures: Micromyini shows reduced usage of proline CCA, while Vernayini displays elevated usage of leucine UUA. These differences likely reflect their phylogenetic divergence.

Multiple analytical approaches consistently indicated that natural selection, rather than mutation pressure, is the dominant force shaping codon usage bias in Murinae mitogenomes. The distinct COA positions of Micromyini and Vernayini suggest lineage‑specific selective pressures linked to their phylogenetic divergence, whereas the high intratribal heterogeneity in Apodemini, Arvicanthini, Hydromyini and Murini implies weaker constraints, possibly due to broader ecological or functional niches [49]. ENC‑plot results, with all species below the neutral curve, confirm selection as the primary driver. The marked contrast between Vernayini (strongest bias) and Micromyini (weakest bias) is striking and likely reflects differences in evolutionary histories, translational efficiency demands or metabolic strategies [49, 95]. PR2‑plot analysis reinforces selection dominance, with all species deviating from parity‑rule center and showing a pronounced A/C bias [52]; again, Micromyini and Vernayini showed the most pronounced inter‑tribal differences in base-composition bias. Neutrality plot analysis estimated that natural selection accounted for approximately 86.5% of codon‑usage variation, with mutational pressure contributing only about 13.5%. These results suggest that natural selection outweighs mutation pressure in shaping codon‑usage patterns in Murinae mitogenomes, likely reflecting functional constraints related to mitochondrial translation efficiency [95]. Nevertheless, we note that mitogenomes are characterized by high mutation rates and AT bias [8, 9, 76], and the relative contributions of selection and mutation can vary across genes and lineages [49, 95]; thus, the general dominance of selection should be interpreted within the context of mitogenome‑specific evolutionary dynamics.

Phylogenetic framework and evolutionary history of Murinae

As one of the most species-rich mammalian groups, Murinae is central to understanding adaptive radiation in rodents. Building on the foundation of ten major lineages initially established by Lecompte et al. (2008) [96], subsequent studies [4, 7, 97] progressively refined this framework. Wilson et al. (2017) [1] further delineated Murinae into 15 tribes, establishing the classification system widely accepted today, albeit with several genera (e.g., Nilopegamys, Hadromys, and Vernaya) remaining incertae sedis. However, intertribal and intergeneric relationships remain contentious, especially in rapidly radiating lineages [14, 17, 98, 99].

Our phylogeny, based on 117 mitogenomes, strongly supports the monophyly of Murinae, with all 13 tribes exhibiting robust monophyly, consistent with concatenated nuclear-mitochondrial datasets [4, 22, 100]. We recovered L. neilli as sister to L. edwardsi, and this pair was sister to Dacnomys [101]. Divergence dating places the MRCA of Murinae at 15.40 Ma (95% HPD: 14.49–16.33 Ma) in the Middle Miocene—substantially older than estimates from some studies based on limited gene data or relaxed fossil calibrations (12–14 Ma) [3, 69]. This earlier origin time coincides with a series of paleoenvironmental events in the Middle Miocene across the African‑Eurasian landmass (sea-level decline, plateau uplift, monsoon intensification, tropical forest contraction) [100, 102], suggesting that environmental changes likely created ecological opportunities for the early diversification of Murinae.

Four major clades were identified within Murinae. Hapalomyini was recovered as sister to all remaining murine taxa (PP = 1.00, BS = 100%), consistent with nuclear gene-based studies [4, 7, 22]. Divergence time estimation indicated that Vernayini split from its sister clade (Micromyini + core murines) at 13.53 Ma (95% HPD: 12.79–14.30 Ma), and Micromyini subsequently diverged from the core murines at 12.89 Ma (95% HPD: 12.21–13.58 Ma). Our results strongly rejected the sister‑group relationship between Vernayini and Micromyini (PP = 1.00, BS = 100%), contradicting Liu et al. (2025) [14] but agreeing with Zhou et al. (2025) [19]. Interspecific relationships within Vernayini are fully congruent with those of Zhao et al. (2023) [53] based on combined mitochondrial and nuclear markers, indicating that mitogenomes alone are sufficient to resolve intratribal relationships in this group. Within Micromyini, M. erythrotis is sister to the remaining species of the tribe—differing from Chen et al. (2023) [18] possibly due to dataset and taxon sampling differences.

Clade D (core murines) comprises the remaining 10 tribes. Its internal topology—Rattini sister to the clade containing the other nine tribes, Chiropodomyini and Hydromyini as sisters, and Millardini sister to Otomyini + Arvicanthini—aligns with multi‑locus nuclear analyses [4, 7, 22, 69], indicating that mitogenomes retain reliable phylogenetic signal for resolving deep relationships. However, internal relationships within the clade comprising Malacomyini and three other tribes (Murini, Apodemini, and Praomyini) are incongruent with multi-locus nuclear genomic results—a discrepancy also observed in other mitogenomic studies [14], potentially attributable to mitochondrial DNA introgression, incomplete lineage sorting, or hybridization [103, 104]. Diversification within core murines concentrated in the Late Miocene (~ 10.7–7.0 Ma), suggesting climatic cooling, aridification, and habitat fragmentation drove rapid radiation [105]. Within Rattini, intergeneric nodes for Maxomys received low support, implying possible rapid radiation or complex evolutionary history for this genus [106]. The pronounced intrageneric divergence within Rattus, separating Asian-Southeast Asian island and Australian-Papuan lineages, was consistent with Robins et al. (2014) [107], revealing complex biogeographic evolutionary patterns.

Inter‑generic relationships within Arvicanthini were inconsistent between BI and ML trees (Figs. 11 and 12), with several nodes receiving moderate to low support. As noted by Mikula et al. (2021) [98], mitogenomes likely exhibit limited resolving power for rapidly radiating, complex lineages; accordingly, the ML topology only partially resembled nuclear genomic results [98] and support remains low (BS < 70%). Nevertheless, mitogenomes retain reference value for this group, though results require cautious interpretation. In contrast, phylogenetic relationships and divergence time estimates for Praomyini support the new generic (e.g., Montemys, Ochromyscus) classification proposed by Nicolas et al. (2021) [17] based on anchored phylogenomic data, resolving long‑standing paraphyly issues and ensuring monophyly for all constituent genera.

New insights into the phylogenetic relationship between Micromyini and Vernayini

Our analyses provide mitogenomic evidence for the phylogenetic position of M. pygmaeus, consistent with previous studies [19]. Although the non‑sister relationship between Micromyini and Vernayini was already established in the phylogeny above, we here provide multiple independent lines of evidence that reinforce this conclusion and explain why our mitogenome‑based result is more reliable than earlier conflicting hypotheses. Previous studies that placed Micromys close to Rattus or proposed a sister relationship between Micromyini and Vernayini were often based on limited genetic data (e.g., single genes or short fragments) [4, 14, 100], which are particularly problematic for rapidly radiating groups like Murinae [23, 108]. In contrast, our study utilized a large‑scale mitogenome dataset (117 species, 13 tribes), a fine‑grained partitioning scheme that accounts for rate heterogeneity, and explicit correction of systematic biases (e.g., base composition and codon‑usage heterogeneity). These methodological improvements provide a more robust phylogenetic estimate.

Divergence time estimates show that Vernayini split from its sister clade (Micromyini + core murines) at 13.53 Ma (95% HPD: 12.79–14.30 Ma), and Micromyini subsequently diverged from the core murines at 12.89 Ma (95% HPD: 12.21–13.58 Ma). The topology recovering Vernayini as sister to the clade comprising Micromyini and the core murines received maximal support (PP = 1.00, BS = 100%), whereas the alternative hypothesis—Vernayini and Micromyini as sister groups—was statistically unsupported (BS = 61%) [14]. This temporal sequence, with ~ 0.64 Ma separating the divergence of Vernayini and Micromyini, is inconsistent with the synchronous origin expected for sister groups, providing independent evidence against the hypothesis of Liu et al. (2025) [14].

Quantitative assessment of phenotypic variation via PCA reveals significant separation of Vernayini and Micromyini in morphospace (Fig. 9; PC1 + PC2 explaining 92.7% of variance), driven by differences in tail length, ear length, and zygomatic width (P < 0.001; Table S2). This morphological divergence aligns with earlier descriptive accounts [14, 18, 53] and is further supported by reported differences in M1 molar morphology between Micromyini and Rattini [14], which may signal deeper evolutionary separation. Notably, the magnitude of morphological differentiation between Vernayini and Micromyini exceeded that observed between some congeneric species pairs within the core murines, further undermining the sister group hypothesis. At the molecular level, K2P genetic distances calculated from the COX1 barcode gene averaged 18.93 ± 0.01% (Table 2)—markedly exceeding the generic threshold (12.61%) and even the subfamilial threshold (18.08%) proposed for Murinae [109]. This pronounced genetic divergence is incompatible with a recent common ancestry, as expected for sister groups, and instead indicates deep evolutionary separation consistent with their non-sister phylogenetic positions. While acknowledging that mitochondrial genes can evolve at variable rates and that distance thresholds are context-dependent, we note that this genetic divergence was corroborated by distinct mitogenomic architectures between the two tribes: significant differences in genome length, base composition, and codon usage bias. These concordant patterns across multiple molecular strata provide robust, multi-dimensional evidence against a close sister relationship.

The convergence of phylogenetic, temporal, morphological, and molecular evidence collectively argues against the sister group hypothesis of Vernayini and Micromyini, instead supporting their status as separately diverging crown lineages within the early radiation of Murinae. This finding highlights the insufficiency of limited mitogenomic representation (40 mitogenomes and 17 CYTB genes) [14] in inferring phylogenetic relationships, particularly for rapidly diversifying lineages where convergent or divergent morphological evolution may obscure true evolutionary affinities. Our results align with Zhou et al. (2025) [19] based on mitogenomes, but challenge the analysis of Liu et al. (2025) [14]—suggesting that phylogenetic signal from mitogenomes, when sufficiently sampled, can resolve relationships that remain ambiguous in smaller datasets. Notably, future validation through phylogenomic analyses of nuclear genomic data will be essential to further corroborate the deep evolutionary separation of these two tribes and to elucidate the potential role of incomplete lineage sorting or ancient hybridization in generating phylogenetic discordance [98].

Limitations and future perspectives

Although this study provides an integrated mitogenomic and morphological framework for reassessing the phylogeny and evolutionary history of Murinae, several limitations warrant consideration.

First, although our mitogenomic dataset includes representatives from 13 tribes, 50 genera, and 117 species—a significant advance for Murinae—three tribes (Vandeleurini, Pithecheirini, and Phloeomyini) remain unsampled due to the absence of complete mitogenome sequences in public databases. Additionally, several included tribes (e.g., Hapalomyini, Chiropodomyini, Malacomyini, and Millardini) are represented by limited numbers of species, potentially constraining phylogenetic resolution. Future efforts to fill these sampling gaps are essential for a more complete phylogenetic framework. Second, mitogenomes represent a single, maternally inherited locus and may not fully reflect species histories shaped by incomplete lineage sorting, introgression, or sex-biased dispersal [15, 16, 98]. Validation using genome‑wide nuclear markers will be critical to corroborate the proposed framework, particularly the phylogenetic placement of Vernayini and its non‑sister relationship with Micromyini. Third, while morphological data were integrated to complement molecular inferences, the available trait datasets remain limited in scope and consistency across studies. Standardized phenotypic scoring and expanded morphological sampling would strengthen future integrative analyses. Finally, although codon-usage patterns were interpreted in light of selection pressures [49], direct functional validation of adaptive hypotheses lies beyond this study’s scope. Experimental approaches (e.g., assessments of translational efficiency or functional assays) could provide deeper insights into the evolutionary mechanisms driving the observed patterns.

Despite these limitations, this work establishes a foundational mitogenomic reference for Murinae and demonstrates the value of integrating sequence, compositional, and morphological data in systematic research. Collectively, such integrative efforts will advance a more comprehensive understanding of murine diversification and adaptation.

Conclusion

This study presents an integrated analysis of mitogenomic architecture, codon-usage patterns, and morphological differentiation across Murinae, revealing patterns of deep evolutionary conservation alongside lineage-specific divergence. We sequenced the complete mitogenome of L. neilli and M. pygmaeus and, through comparative analysis of mitogenomes from 117 Murinae species, confirm that structural gene content and organization are highly conserved. Genome length variation is primarily driven by expansion and contraction of the control region, with Vernayini possessing the longest mitogenomes among the tribes examined. Nucleotide composition is largely uniform across most lineages, but Micromyini and Vernayini exhibit distinct profiles, suggesting divergent evolutionary pressures. Protein-coding genes are under strong purifying selection overall, yet evolutionary rates and nucleotide diversity vary substantially among genes, with COX and CYTB being the most conserved and ATP8 and ND2 evolving under relatively relaxed constraints. Codon-usage bias is shaped predominantly by natural selection and reflects phylogenetic conservation, with pronounced tribe-specific divergence between Micromyini and Vernayini. Critically, our phylogenomic reconstruction does not support the previously proposed sister‑group relationship between Micromyini and Vernayini. Instead, Vernayini is recovered as the sister group to the remaining Murinae lineages excluding Hapalomys, a topology receiving maximal statistical support. This finding is reinforced by significant morphological differentiation between the two tribes and by COX1‑based genetic distances that exceed typical subfamilial thresholds reported for Murinae. Collectively, these results establish a robust, multi-evidence framework for understanding murine evolution and underscore the value of integrating mitogenomic, compositional, and morphological data in phylogenetics research. We note, however, that inferences based on mitochondrial data alone may not fully capture evolutionary histories influenced by incomplete lineage sorting or introgression. Future studies incorporating genome‑wide nuclear datasets and expanded taxonomic sampling—particularly for currently unsampled tribes such as Vandeleurini, Pithecheirini, and Phloeomyini—will be essential to validate the phylogenetic relationships proposed here and to further elucidate the evolutionary mechanisms driving diversification in this ecologically and biomedically important group.
