Capnography-guided blind nasal intubation under conscious sedation for airway management in a plunging ranula
Authors: Vikash Bansal, Sony Sony, Shivam Shekhar, Kausiki Poddar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 22-year man with human immunodeficiency virus ((HIV)-positive status for excision of huge, painless swelling in the floor of the mouth was admitted. The swelling occupied the anterior part of the neck; intraoral part of the swelling was extended downward and anteriorly in the sublingual space deviating the tongue. Fiberoptic intubation has helped immensely in handling many difficult airway scenarios. In low-resource setting, often multiple-use fiberoptic is used due to which fear of risk of cross-contamination from blood, secretions, or suctioning is there. The challenges were to secure the airway with difficult bag-mask ventilation, difficult laryngoscopy, difficult video-laryngoscopy and nonavailability of single-use fiberoptic bronchoscope and to avoid tracheostomy. This case report illustrates how blind nasal intubation (BNI) under conscious sedation can help avoid tracheostomy or retrograde endotracheal intubation.

INTRODUCTION

A plunging ranula is a salivary gland cyst. It occurs when mucous extravasates through or around the mylohyoid muscle, reaching into the submandibular space. It can cause potential airway obstruction, leading to a catastrophe. Nevertheless, with the advent of fiberoptic, we are most often able to manage these situations meticulously. Cases have been reported where multiple attempts with a bronchoscope have also failed to secure the airway.[1] Lack of single-use fiberoptic forces for elective tracheostomy in serology-positive patients was observed. Old techniques now abandoned, like blind nasal intubation (BNI), can still be helpful in some of these difficult situations with proper planning and training. So, we present a case of difficult airway management in an HIV-positive patient having a large plunging ranula posted for excision under general anesthesia.

CASE REPORT

A 22-year-old male (weight 40 kilograms) was admitted to our hospital with a painless large swelling on the floor of the mouth. It extended to the floor of the mouth, occupying the sublingual region in the anterior part of the neck. The intraoral part of the swelling extended in the sublingual space, deviating the tongue posteriorly and superiorly, obliterating the view of the uvula and the soft palate (Mallampati grade 4). Thyromental distance was ≥6.5 cm [Figure 1]. Nasal patency was checked with a cotton wisp and was patent on both sides.

Difficult airway showing intraoral mass with Mallampati grade-4

The patient had HIV-positive status and was on a tab Tenofovir 300 mg, tab Lamivudine 300 mg, tab Dolutegravir 50 mg once daily each. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography neck showed a well-defined non-enhancing cystic lesion measuring 4.6 cm antero-posteriorly × 3.5 cm medio-laterally × 4.3 cm superior-inferiorly in the sub-lingual space in the floor of mouth with tongue displaced postero-superiorly [Figure 2]. There was no deviation or compression of the trachea or larynx. This showed that naso-pharynx and laryngo-pharynx space was intact. Excision of the ranula was planned under general anesthesia. BNI was chosen as the initial plan (plan A). Awake fiberoptic intubation with a reusable scope, under the hood of the endotracheal tube (ETT), was plan B to avoid soiling the reusable instrument. We did not wish to subject our patient to tracheostomy until necessary (plan C). The difficult airway cart was kept ready with a tracheostomy set during the management of this case, for emergency front of neck access.

CECT neck shows a well-defined non-enhancing cystic lesion measuring 4.6 × 3.5 × 4.3 cm (star) in the sublingual space of the floor of the mouth, tongue displaced postero-superiorly (arrow)

The patient was taken to the operation theater; all standard american society of anesthesiologists (ASA) monitors were attached. Injection glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg and injection midazolam 1 mg intravenous were given as premedication. Xylometazoline drops were instilled in both nostrils. The patient received 4% lidocaine, 3 ml nebulization using a nebulization mask kit (Romsons Pvt Ltd, India) with oxygen flow at 6 liters per minute for a total of 15 minutes. After serial dilatation, a nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) was inserted into the left nostril, connected to a universal connector, and a breathing circuit was attached to it with 100% oxygen. The patient was sedated using sevoflurane (minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of 0.4) via the NPA while being vigilant of saturation. The benefit was twofold as the NPA provided nasopharyngeal patency while sevoflurane, which can be titrated, provided a state of conscious sedation, where the patient remained cooperative and able to respond to commands. The surgical team was prepared for emergency tracheostomy in advance. For BNI, a well-lubricated 6.5-mm cuffed ETT with capnography attached to it was inserted gently through the right nostril while listening to a hissing sound. Capnography was used to identify the direction of tube insertion into the trachea while the patient’s head was extended, the neck was flexed, and tracheal cartilage was gently pressed down [Figure 3]. Nasotracheal intubation was confirmed via bilateral lung auscultation and capnography. General anesthesia was then given. Excision of the swelling was performed via both oral and cervical approaches.

DISCUSSION

The concern for cross-contamination in a hospital setting is very high and as pointed out in a safety communication by US food and drug administration, the risk is as high as 15%.[2] However, difficult airways are a major concern for anesthesiologists as they can greatly contribute to mortality and morbidity associated with anesthesia. BNI has demonstrated several clinical applications. An observational study found a 90% success rate of capnography assisted awake BNI in maxillofacial surgeries.[3] A case report highlighted the successful use of BNI in a patient experiencing acute respiratory failure and trismus, following the failure of other treatment methods.[4] Another retrospective analysis of 48 temporomandibular joint ankylosis cases reported a 92% success rate for BNI, suggesting its utility in resource-limited settings.[5]

Limitations: BNI is an art that is not practiced regularly; thus, many are not acquainted to it. It needs confirmation of tube placement via methods like capnography and five-point auscultation. The success rate also depends on operator skills. The potential risks and complications associated with nasal intubation remain, along with esophageal intubation, airway trauma due to multiple attempts, and obstruction due to pushing epiglottis on the laryngeal inlet.[6] Many modifications to provide assistance to this blind technique have been made, like inflating the ETT cuff, lighted stylet, etc., Recent efforts have been made to provide training at “difficult airway management” workshops with a breathing mannequin using the aid of capnography to participant in dying-art of BNI.[7]

Meticulous planning is the key to success in the anticipated difficult airway. The luxury of a disposable fiberoptic scope for every seropositive patient is unavailable in low-resource settings. Tracheostomy is an alternative, but for a day-care procedure, it is not desirable. BNI is a practical and effective alternative in airway management, especially when standard methods are unsuccessful or equipment is limited. It should be learned by the untrained for unforeseen circumstances.
