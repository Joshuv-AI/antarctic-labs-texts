Multiscale geographically weighted modeling of tuberculosis incidence in China: integrating geographic perspectives into epidemiological analysis
Authors: Zihao Wang, Jianchen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) is a major global health problem, and the pathogenesis of TB is determined by multiple variables. The complicated relationship between geographic determinants and incidence rates is poorly understood, and multicollinearity and spatial heterogeneity were not considered when exploring this relationship.MethodsIn this study, the factors influencing the incidence of TB in China were investigated, considering spatial heterogeneity, to develop a multidimensional TB indicator system that incorporates geographic factors. A comprehensive linear-nonlinear two-stage feature screening model was developed to identify key factors contributing to TB. The ordinary least squares model was constructed at the national scale using these key indicators to understand the macro-relationships between TB incidence rates and key indicators. A geographically weighted regression (GWR) model was constructed at a provincial scale, and a multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR) model was developed to conduct an in-depth comparative analysis of the fitting effects of the GWR and MGWR models on the TB incidence rates. The goal of this study is to investigate the impact of the GWR and MGWR models on TB incidence. The adjustable bandwidth mechanism of the MGWR model was compared with the fixed bandwidth mechanism of the GWR model to determine the best model for geographical analysis of TB incidence.ResultsThe MGWR model had the best fit (R2 = 0.942; AICc = 57.060) for TB incidence and provided unique bandwidths for important variables to improve model geographic analysis. The analysis of geographic components using the MGWR model revealed that the fitting coefficients of mean height, topographic relief, and average annual precipitation were spatially heterogeneous.ConclusionThese results provide the theoretical foundation for developing TB prevention and control measures.Keywords: Tuberculosis affecting factors, VIF, Random forest, Pearson's correlation coefficient, Multi-scale geographically weighted regression

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a contagious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis [1]. The primary symptoms of TB include chronic cough, chest discomfort, dyspnea, exhaustion, weight loss, fever, nocturnal night sweats, and altered breath sounds [2]. TB is airborne and is a hazard to public health around the world. Thus, the prevention and control of TB is challenging [3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the incidence of TB in China, which is among the world’s 30 high-burden TB countries, was estimated to be 52/100,000 in 2023, with approximately 741,000 new cases [4]. The incidence of TB in China had declined. However, an average annual decline of 14.5% is necessary to meet the WHO’s “Stop TB” goal of a 50% reduction in incidence by 2025 compared with 2015 (below 33/100,000).

Many studies have focused on the contributing variables and spatial distribution of TB. For example, Bai et al. [5] used Moran’s I model to study the impact of literacy rate, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) exposure, proportion of the population over 65 years old, proportion of males, poverty rate, and malnutrition rate on the global incidence of TB. Bai et al. demonstrated that the global incidence rate and spatial autocorrelation trend of TB are decreasing, but the spatial autocorrelation was still significant, and the literacy rate was the most prevalent factor contributing to TB. In a study using comparative genomics methods to integrate a large number of clinical indicators, Talarico et al. [6] demonstrated that social behavioral factors influenced TB transmission more than pathogen infectivity. In a study of four central urban areas of Guangzhou City, Ren et al. [7] demonstrated that the incidence of TB was closely related to socioeconomic variables and that environmental factors exhibited a weaker influence. The analysis of meteorological data by Zhang et al. [8] revealed that TB incidence was positively correlated with average rainfall but negatively correlated with other meteorological parameters. Studies on spatial distribution and other factors influencing respiratory diseases provide important references for geoepidemiological modeling. Principal component analysis (PCA) and multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR) models were used to identify differences in the prevalence of asthma in urban areas; geographic factors, such as population density and access to healthcare resources, were significantly associated with the spatial clustering of asthma incidence [9]. A spatial epidemiological study of lung cancer, using geographic variables, demonstrated the effects of urbanization and regional differences on the spatial and temporal distribution of lung cancer mortality [10]. A spatial epidemiologic study of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), integrating age, smoking status, and PM2.5, revealed the geographic disparity of COPD prevalence at the urban level in China and the regional heterogeneity of risk factors and key intervention areas and influencing factors using categorical regression tree modeling [11]. These previous studies indicate that geographic factors play an important role in the epidemiologic patterns of respiratory disorders. However, current TB studies do not adequately incorporate geographic features such as topography and elevation.

Many studies focused on specific topics and a few relevant elements. Although some studies included meteorology as a geographically relevant factor, few studies addressed the potential impact of other geographic factors, such as the degree of terrain relief and average altitude, on the incidence of TB. Geographic factors directly affect the living environment and transmission routes of M. tuberculosis and have a profound indirect impact on the incidence of TB via factors influencing population distribution and economic development. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation of geographic factors is essential to accurately understand disease transmission patterns and formulate effective scientific prevention and control strategies. Developing a set of factor screening techniques that span several dimensions is crucial. Earlier studies failed to account for the possibility of multicollinearity across variables when selecting TB-influencing factors [12]. Intrinsic correlations between variables may lead to skewed modeling and overestimates of the relevance of particular elements. Although several variables strongly correlate with the incidence of TB, the magnitude of covariance between influencing factors should be assessed [13]. A single analytical method cannot fully capture complex relationships. Therefore, a large number of factors should be tested for correlations and covariances, and this information should be incorporated into the analytical models. To address this problem, a comprehensive two-stage feature screening (TSFS) model was constructed to provide a scientific and precise basis for screening influencing factors.

Investigating the spatial heterogeneity of TB incidence and its affecting factors has recently emerged as an important direction in public health research. The traditional ordinary least squares (OLS) model is widely used to analyze the relationship between TB incidence and socioeconomic, environmental, and other factors at the national or regional level [14]. However, this model assumes global smoothness of the regression coefficients and does not adequately capture the data’s inherent spatial nonstationarity. A geographically weighted regression (GWR) model reveals spatial heterogeneity by estimating localized coefficients [15] and identifying region-specific risk factors such as medical accessibility and meteorological circumstances [12]. An MGWR model can specify distinct bandwidths for each key indicator and capture changes in the size of spatial effects caused by different factors [16]. Of note, the GWR model’s fixed bandwidth makes it difficult to fit to each key indicator contributing to the incidence of TB, whereas the MGWR model’s adaptive bandwidth reduces the spatial correlation between the key indicators, reducing the impact of the multiple covariance problem and yielding spatial results for the key indicators of TB incidence.

An analysis of data from 2005 to 2013 by Guo et al. [17] demonstrated obvious spatial aggregation and seasonal variations in the incidence of TB; winter to spring was the peak season, and meteorological and other factors were associated with TB. Yu et al. [18] combined a geographical and temporal weighted regression (GTWR) model with PCA, Spearman correlation, and multiple covariance tests to examine the spatiotemporal heterogeneity and key factors influencing the incidence of pulmonary TB in China from 2004 to 2021. Most current studies focus on improving and expanding the GTWR model in the temporal dimension, but in-depth exploration and enhancement of spatial heterogeneity have been neglected.

Oshan et al. [19] analyzed the factors influencing the obesity rate in the Phoenix metropolitan area of the United States using an MGWR model. Spatiotemporal heterogeneity was detected in the influence of socioeconomic indicators and the built environment, and the MGWR model was superior to the traditional GWR model. Lord and Odoi [20] used an MGWR model to determine the rate of diabetes-related hospitalizations in Florida and detected spatial differences in the influence of socioeconomic factors such as the proportion of the elderly population and unemployment rate, providing the basis for regional healthcare resource allocation. Li et al. [21] analyzed the distribution of elderly care institutions in China using point of interest data and an MGWR model. Their results demonstrated that the density of elderly care institutions is higher in the east than in the west, the economic level and proportion of the elderly population are the main driving factors, governmental inputs are critical for balancing resources in less developed regions, and environmental factors have a weaker influence. Thus, the MGWR model has already produced significant findings in other domains. However, research in the field of TB morbidity using this model is lacking.

In this study, factors influencing the incidence of TB were collected, machine learning methods were used to screen factors, and a TSFS process system was constructed. The aim of this study was to identify spatial relationships for the incidence of TB using the screened key indices. The screened key indicators were used to investigate the geographical relationship of TB incidence and close a gap in the MGWR model’s fitting effect on TB incidence. Furthermore, the index system was supplemented, and geographic factors were included in the comprehensive assessment to investigate the impact of geographic factors and new influencing factors on the incidence of TB. The results of this study will provide more comprehensive theoretical support and practical guidance for the prevention and control of global TB.

Study area and data sources

Overview of the study area

Because TB is an infectious disease that spreads across areas, the epidemiological characteristics of TB are influenced by population movement, socioeconomic inequalities, and environmental factors. Thus, understanding the geographical heterogeneity at the macro level is essential. Thus, the data for this study were collected from mainland China’s 31 provincial-level administrative entities, excluding Taiwan Province, Hong Kong, and Macao Special Administrative Regions.

Basic data

Data on the incidence rate of TB were extracted and sorted by province from the statistical information in the public health science data [22], and data on TB incidence rate across countries worldwide were sourced from the WORLD BANK GROUP [23]. Socioeconomic statistics, such as gross regional product, consumption level of the population, and per capita disposable income of the entire population, and data on individual health status, including HIV incidence rate, HIV mortality rate, incidence rate of diabetes mellitus, the number of health technicians per 10,000 people, and the number of healthcare institutions, were collected from statistical yearbooks. The data on geographic and environmental factors, including NOx emissions, SO2 emissions, precipitation, and annual average temperature, were collected from statistical yearbooks and combined with the ERA5-Land dataset released by the European Union and the European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts and the relevant data from the China Meteorological Data Network and the China Terrestrial Climate Data Dataset V3.0. The integration of data from multiple sources ensured the comprehensiveness and accuracy of the geographic and environmental data, providing strong support for in-depth research.

To ensure that the data provided an accurate, stable, and representative basis for subsequent analysis, the years 2018–2020 were selected as the time dimension for data processing. The average values of three years of TB incidence rate data were obtained separately before being substituted into the model for calculation due to possible data variations in different years. This approach reduced data loss.

Research methodology

Spatial autocorrelation test

Moran’s index analysis of TB incidence in 31 provincial administrative regions in mainland China using Geoda software revealed a global Moran’s index of 0.423 and a significance level of 0.01, indicating a significant positive autocorrelation between TB incidence and space. This result indicates spatial clustering in TB incidence; high-incidence areas are next to high-incidence areas, and low-incidence areas are clustered with low-incidence areas (Fig. 1). Thus, the OLS model, which assumes global smoothness of regression coefficients, cannot capture the geographical nonstationarity of the data. The GWR model may expose the heterogeneity of the main variables via spatial location weighting. As an extension of GWR, the MGWR model can assign independent bandwidths for many key indicators, allowing a more nuanced analysis of the spatial effects of factors contributing to the incidence of TB. The three models were compared.

Indicator selection and process construction

According to the Social Determinants of Health theory, the occurrence of disease is influenced by biological and social structural factors. For example, higher education can lower the risk of TB infection by raising public awareness about health and disease prevention [24]. Per capita disposable income influences illness risk indirectly via access to healthcare facilities and living conditions [25]. Provincial administrative areas are relatively independent and homogeneous geographic units and exhibit internal linkages and patterns of socioeconomic development levels, education penetration, and healthcare resource distribution [26, 27]. Provincial scales capture the relationship between socioeconomic characteristics and health more accurately than national scales. Furthermore, provincial scales show the overall characteristics of a region better than city- and county-level scales.

In China, geographic factors such as topographic relief and average elevation influence the allocation of healthcare resources [28] and exacerbate resource distribution inequities, which may influence TB transmission. Thus, geography should be considered when analyzing the incidence of TB. Provincial scales incorporate geographic data and the macroeconomic consequences of geographic issues. Provincial areas include various geomorphological units, reflecting the diversity and variance in geographic variables. Differences in geographic factors within a province may be disregarded at the national scale, and the impact of geographic factors on TB transmission at the municipal or county scale is difficult to understand from a macroscopic perspective. Thus, this study focused on terrain slope and average height.

Disease co-infection may also affect the incidence of TB. HIV infection severely decreases immunological function (particularly CD4 + T-lymphocyte activity), increasing vulnerability to M. tuberculosis. Furthermore, HIV progresses faster in individuals who are co-infected with TB, increasing the risk of death. Therefore, TB is a major cause of death among HIV-infected patients worldwide [29]. Understanding the mechanism of disease co-infection at the provincial level can provide a more comprehensive understanding of epidemiology to improve regional disease prevention and control. Provincial-scale analyses can more accurately locate high-prevalence areas of disease co-infection than national-scale analyses, and the provincial scale can summarize the laws on a larger scale than the municipal or county level, avoiding the limitations of conclusions based on small areas.

This study employed an interdisciplinary perspective as a theoretical framework to conduct systematic research to improve our understanding of the factors influencing the incidence of TB and optimize the effect of model fitting. First, data from various sources were combined and processed to develop a multidimensional indicator system that included demographic, geographic, and environmental traits. Data modeling was used to build the TSFS model, known as the “linear-nonlinear two-stage feature screening” model. The secondary indicators were initially screened using Pearson’s correlation coefficient test (p ≤ 0.05), and indicators with significant linear correlations were maintained. The variance inflation factor (VIF ≤ 10) was used to diagnose the covariance of strongly linearly correlated secondary indicators, excluding the interfering effects of multiple covariates on regression model parameter estimation. The secondary indicators with p > 0.05 in Pearson’s correlation coefficient test were screened for significant nonlinear correlation using random forest important selection. The results were pooled and summarized with the VIF diagnostic results. The Pearson correlation coefficient test, VIF diagnostic, and random forest importance selection were used to screen and summarize the relevant indicators, demonstrating the two-stage “linear-nonlinear screening” of the comprehensive TSFS model. The screened core variables were incorporated into the OLS, GWR, and MGWR models. The effectiveness of each model was compared using the goodness-of-fit (measured by , reflecting the proportion of variance in TB incidence explained by the model), Akaike information criterion (AICc), and adjusted (a modified accounting for the number of variables) to determine the optimal model. Construction of the indicator system

The data were preprocessed to thoroughly check for missing values using mean interpolation, followed by the construction of regression models for predicting and deleting missing proportions and variable features. Outliers were discovered and rectified using box-and-line charts. Three years of data were averaged to reduce the influence of chance on the outcomes. Finally, disparities in magnitude were eliminated using Z-score standardization.

TB incidence is affected by the interaction of natural, social, demographic, medical, and other factors. Thus, a variety of factors were considered when developing the TB indicator system. The multidisciplinary theoretical frameworks and the practical needs of TB prevention and control in China were considered, including the concept of full-cycle prevention and control [30], the cognitive-behavioral transmission mechanism [31], and other theories. The guidelines for developing an indicator system of macro-influencing elements of TB incidence were considered to create indicators based on the significance of reality from the perspectives of economics, culture, and urban and rural areas. Finally, indicators, including demographic characteristics, were developed. Five major primary indicators, namely demographic factors [32], geographical and environmental factors [33–35], socioeconomic factors [36], individual health factors [37, 38], and behavioral and cognitive factors [39], and the corresponding secondary indicator system were constructed to understand the factors influencing the original system. TSFS integrated model construction

The linear-nonlinear TSFS process has several advantages over existing variable selection methods. Compared with the LASSO regression, which captures only linear relationships and may over-compress coefficients [40], the TSFS combines Pearson’s tests with random forest tests to retain both linearly and nonlinearly associated variables. Unlike PCA dimensionality reduction, the TSFS retains the physical meaning of the variables and is better suited to elucidating the true mechanism of action of elements affecting TB. Thus, the TSFS was chosen as the primary method for variable screening in this study.

Model evaluation metrics

A higher value, which reflects the proportion of independent variables explaining the incidence of TB, implies that the model fits the data better. Thus, can be used to assess the model’s fundamental performance. The adjusted eliminates overestimation of R² due to the number of independent variables (seven core variables in this study) and reflects the actual explanatory power of the model better. AICc balances the complexity of the model with the fitting effect. A lower AICc value signifies a better balance between the explanatory power and the simplicity of the model. The AICc is well-suited for comparing the GWR and MGWR models and can effectively avoid the overfitting problem. The combination of the , adjusted , and AICc provides a comprehensive assessment of the performance of the model index systems.

The Gaussian kernel function generates continuous and smooth spatial weights via exponential decay and can accurately depict the “distance decay effect” of the geographic factors on the incidence of TB. In other words, the influence of neighboring regions decreases progressively with increasing spatial distance, which is highly consistent with the continuous and gradual characteristics of geographic effects in TB transmission. The “hard truncation” characteristic of the two-square kernel (the weight of the distance exceeds the bandwidth and then changes to zero) and the rapid decay pattern of the exponential kernel do not accurately reflect the spatial gradual change of disease transmission. The spatial heterogeneity of TB incidence is affected by multiple factors, including terrain, climate, and socioeconomic factors, and the local correlation exhibits complex continuity. The smoothing weight distribution of the Gaussian kernel function can reduce the interference of the extreme values on the local regression and is, thus, better suited to the spatial gradual change of this multifactorial-driven pattern than the other kernel functions. The Gaussian kernel function synergizes with the multi-scale bandwidth mechanism of the MGWR model. Furthermore, the sample size (31 provinces) at the provincial scale is limited. The continuous weighting property of the Gaussian kernel function can reduce fluctuations in model parameters due to small changes in the bandwidth and guarantee the stability of the local regression estimation. In contrast, truncated kernel functions, such as the two-square kernel, may amplify the local estimation error due to the sparsity of samples. Thus, we chose to use the Gaussian kernel function and AICc optimization to create the model.

The fit coefficients of the GWR and MGWR models are described by their mean, standard deviation, lowest value, median, and maximum values. To assess model superiority, the corrected AICc value was compared with the value for each model. A high value and a low AICc value indicate a stronger explanation of TB incidence by the selected key indicators and, thus, better model fit.

Variance inflation factor analysis

After correlation screening of secondary indicators, a covariance screening was conducted based on the magnitude of the VIF value (VIF >10 indicates strong covariance between variables, and VIF < 10 indicates weak covariance). Most secondary indicators substantially correlated with TB incidence (Table 3), indicating that these determinants have appropriate synergistic or interwoven effects on TB incidence. The global Moran’s I test was applied to the model residuals to confirm the elimination effect of the MGWR model on spatial correlation. Moran’s I value of the residuals was − 0.156 (P > 0.05), indicating that the residuals had no substantial spatial autocorrelation and a random distribution. No systematic spatial structure remained in the model residuals, bolstering the reliability of the MGWR model fitting results.

Factor analysis

The primary and secondary indicators in this study had significant impacts on TB incidence. Although previous studies showed that higher population density increases the risk of transmission [49], the regression coefficient between population density at the end of the year and TB incidence in this study was negative (Table 7). This negative correlation may be due to the rich medical resources and strong preventive and control measures in some high-population-density areas. These factors may weaken the positive effects of population density on TB incidence. The regression coefficient for disposable income per capita of all residents was negative due to the high level of consumption, a good public health system, good living conditions, and high health awareness, which reduces the risk of TB infection. The average number of students enrolled in higher education institutions and the number of graduates from higher education institutions were negatively correlated with the incidence of pulmonary TB. The number of people with higher education reflects the local education system and indicates a high degree of knowledge of the disease and strong awareness of disease prevention and control, which reduces TB incidence. The number of general tertiary schools reflects the local educational landscape [50].

The effects of geographical heterogeneity on TB incidence were particularly interesting (Fig. 3). The regression coefficients for average altitude varied greatly in space, revealing a decreasing spatial pattern from high-altitude areas in the west to low-altitude areas in the east. High-altitude areas have thin air and a fluctuating climate, which may decrease immunity, and medical services are sparsely dispersed and difficult to obtain in these regions [51], resulting in a spatially varied risk of TB incidence. The regression coefficients were higher in the northwestern and southwestern regions and lower in the central region. The population in the western region is sparse and dispersed, making it difficult to fully cover and efficiently deploy medical and health care resources, and the inconvenience of transportation also hinders the movement of people and the prevention and control of diseases. The population in the central region is relatively centralized, making resource allocation for the prevention and control of diseases more convenient. The regression coefficients for average annual precipitation were irregularly distributed regionally. Annual precipitation tended to decrease from south to north. Regions with high precipitation have a humid environment, which promotes the spread of M. tuberculosis and affects living and hygiene habits, contributing to an increased risk of TB compared with regions with low precipitation. We detected significant differences in TB incidence across precipitation zones, indicating geographical variability.

Spatial differences in regression coefficients for geographical factors

Other factors also affect the incidence of TB. The spatial heterogeneity of geographic factors was not emphasized because they are directly related to the natural environment. Focusing on the spatial heterogeneity of geographic factors provided a more in-depth understanding of the relationship between the incidence of TB and the geographic environment and laid the groundwork for future studies.

Discussion

Mechanistic analysis of model performance differences and comparison with existing studies

In this study, the fitting effects of the OLS, GWR, and MGWR models were compared. The MGWR model explained the spatial heterogeneity of TB incidence better than the other two models. MGWR exhibited higher goodness-of-fit (R2 = 0.942), adjusted coefficient of determination (adjusted R2 = 0.895), and AICc (57.060) than the GWR (R2 = 0.871; AICc = 72.704) and OLS (R2 = 0.610; AICc = 307.897) models. The MGWR model accurately depicts variations in the magnitude of action of distinct elements by adjusting the spatial bandwidth separately for each variable. Using terrain relief as an example, the regression coefficient was significantly higher in the Tibet Autonomous Region (0.6478) than in Hainan Province (− 0.16054), revealing an unbalanced effect of terrain relief on TB transmission. In contrast, the GWR model inadequately portrayed the local spatial relationships by using the same bandwidth for all variables, and the OLS assumes global smoothness, completely ignoring spatial heterogeneity.

The MGWR model (R2 = 0.942) outperformed the GTWR model (R2 = 0.88) of Yu et al. [18], indicating that the multiscale bandwidth mechanism successfully captures geographic variability. The MGWR model also outperformed the GWR model of Xiaodie Chen et al. [14]. The screening of variables by TSFS successfully decreased multicollinearity (all VIFs were less than 5) and improved model stability, supporting the benefits of the MGWR model for the spatial analysis of TB. Unlike Bai et al. [5], who screened variables using only linear regression analysis, we used the TSFS combined with linear (Pearson’s test) and nonlinear (random forest) screening strategies, which were more in line with the study by Talarico et al. [6] showing a nonlinear role of social behavioral factors. Thus, the screening was more comprehensive and accurate, providing a more reliable variable base for subsequent model fitting.

Importance of optimizing the feature selection process

The TSFS selection process played an important role in enhancing model correctness. During the initial stage of feature screening, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (p ≤ 0.05) and VIF (VIF ≤ 5) reduced variables with multicollinearity. In the second step, elements with nonlinear relationships, such as average annual precipitation, were mined using the random forest technique. To demonstrate the benefits of this screening method, a benchmark model without this screening method was used as a control. All secondary indicators were incorporated directly into the control model without filtering. The model fitting yielded = 0.959, adjusted = 0.751, and AICc = 400.634. Previous research showed that the value increases with the number of independent variables in the model. Thus, combining the value with the AICc index is important for comprehensive model evaluation. The pre-screening model AICc value was significantly higher than the post-screening AICc value. After screening, the number of secondary indicators in the model decreased significantly, but the value remained constant, confirming the necessity and correctness of the TSFS integrated model process. The model’s goodness-of-fit was maintained while optimizing the structure of the model’s independent variables using the TSFS screening method, thereby improving the model’s accuracy and reliability.

Policy implications

Targeted prevention and control recommendations can be made based on the regional heterogeneity indicated by the MGWR model and the influencing variables on TB incidence. The model’s primary policy benefit is its multi-scale bandwidth mechanism, which can match the specific geographical scales of diverse influencing elements, leading to policy strategies with “scale adaptation.” For elements with significant bandwidth (wide coverage), such as the degree of topographic relief, the strategy should focus on cross-regional synergy. For elements with limited bandwidth (strong local impacts), such as the average annual precipitation, strategies should concentrate on fine-tuning actions at the local level. This strategy design, based on differences in the scale of action of variables, improves the synergy of wide-area factors and the over-intervention of local factors compared with homogenized prevention and control policies based on the GWR model’s fixed bandwidth. Thus, policies based on the MGWR model significantly improve the scientific basis of TB prevention and control strategies and the efficiency of resource allocation.

In areas with high terrain relief (e.g., western provinces such as Xinjiang and Tibet), the substantial positive regression coefficient of terrain relief (> 0.3) indicated that difficult terrain increased the risk of morbidity by limiting access to medical services. Thus, a hierarchical prevention and control system of county sentinel hospitals and township mobile screening stations should be established, and mobile medical teams should conduct regular screening in high-altitude and high-relief areas to reduce the radius of patient access to medical care in remote areas and respond to the characteristics of large-scale impact. For areas with high average annual precipitation (e.g., Guangdong, Guangxi, and other provinces in South China), the positive impact coefficient (> 0.35) suggests that a humid environment supports the survival of M. tuberculosis. Thus, environmental interventions during the rainy season should be strengthened; regular disinfection systems should be implemented in rural settlements, urban villages, and other venues; and awareness should be raised. In areas with low levels of higher education (e.g., southwestern provinces such as Guizhou and Yunnan), the negative correlation coefficient (< − 0.05) between higher education enrollment per 100,000 population and TB incidence indicates that the level of health awareness plays a key role in TB prevention and control. Thus, knowledge of TB prevention and treatment should be included in the basic education curriculum, and scientific materials should be created in collaboration with local folk culture. The regression coefficient’s negative effects are more pronounced in western regions with lower per capita disposable income, indicating that economic restrictions limit access to medical resources. Financial transfers should be used to strengthen the construction of primary healthcare teams; allocate specialized TB prevention and treatment personnel based on population size; establish a mechanism for medical collaboration in the eastern, central, and western regions; and improve the diagnosis and treatment capacity of less developed regions using telediagnosis and treatment technology. These recommendations are based on the geographical differentiation rule established by the MGWR model, integrating the model findings with real regional features and providing an actionable path to precise and differentiated TB prevention and control.

Robustness tests

This section systematically evaluates the reliability of the main research findings from two perspectives: the temporal dimension and the spatial scale. By extending the time series to increase the sample size and shifting the analytical scale to increase the number of spatial units, we verify the stability of the MGWR model’s performance under different data conditions.

Robustness test based on extended time series

Although the study based on data from 2018 to 2020 had already yielded clear conclusions, we sought to test the model’s performance with a larger sample size. To this end, we collected tuberculosis incidence data and related influencing factors for 31 provincial-level administrative regions in China spanning 16 years from 2005 to 2020, constructing an extended dataset containing 496 observations (31 provinces × 16 years). We applied the same TSFS feature selection procedure and MGWR modeling approach to this dataset.

Building upon the clear conclusions drawn from the main study using 2018–2020 data, and to further test the model’s performance with a larger sample size, this study collected tuberculosis incidence data and related influencing factors for 31 provincial-level administrative regions in China spanning 16 years from 2005 to 2020. This process constructed an extended dataset containing 496 observations. The same feature selection procedure was applied to this dataset to identify the core influencing factors, and the modeling methodology was kept consistent.

The analysis results (as shown in Table 9) indicate that even with a significantly increased sample size, the MGWR model maintained the best fitting performance. The model achieved an R² of 0.889, an Adjusted R² of 0.796, and a corrected AICc value of 78.738. These metrics are notably superior to those of both the GWR and OLS models. The highest Adjusted R² suggests that the model’s superior performance is not due to over-parameterization. Simultaneously, its lowest AICc value further confirms that the model achieves the best balance between parsimony and goodness-of-fit. These results demonstrate that the MGWR model maintains stable performance across different time spans and sample sizes.

Robustness test based on global scale

To examine the general applicability of the research method across different spatial scales and with a greater number of spatial units, this study conducted a global-level analysis. We collected data on tuberculosis incidence rates and corresponding influencing factors for 193 countries worldwide from 2005 to 2020. After strict data cleaning, complete data for 130 countries spanning 16 consecutive years were retained, forming a global-scale dataset containing 2080 observations. The number of spatial units in this dataset significantly exceeds that of the original provincial-level analysis, providing a solid basis for verifying the model’s robustness across different spatial scales.

The modeling results on the global-scale data are highly consistent with the main findings of the study (as shown in Table 10). The MGWR model achieved an R² of 0.732, an Adjusted R² of 0.663, and a corrected AICc value of 267.415. These indicators are better than those of the GWR and OLS models. The model’s highest Adjusted R² value indicates its strong explanatory power, while its lowest AICc value confirms its high efficiency in handling complex spatial structures. This reproducible verification across different geographical extents and numbers of spatial units fully demonstrates the MGWR model’s strong capability and broad applicability in capturing spatial heterogeneity.

Research limitations and future perspectives

The feature screening approach proposed in this study is a methodological breakthrough for the examination of factors influencing TB, although further research into the definition of relevance levels for random forests should be conducted. Annual average precipitation was significantly more important than the other secondary variables, and the threshold is clearly defined in our model. However, the current academic community lacks a unified standard for determining the statistical significance of importance scores. Although some studies have used the top 30% as a threshold to balance model complexity and information completeness, the boundaries are fuzzy and may introduce subjective bias.

Future research can further optimize the method of determining the importance threshold of random forests. The variable values can be randomly disrupted to calculate the probability distribution of the importance scores using the substitution test. The upper limit of the 95% confidence interval can be used as the statistical significance threshold. Alternatively, the model can be combined with a Bayesian optimization algorithm to automatically search for the optimal thresholds with the goal of minimizing the model prediction error and reducing subjective bias. The spatial resolution of the data can be improved using municipal or county-scale data, integrating heterogeneous information from multiple sources, and investigating microscopic spatial heterogeneity using the MGWR model to compensate for the omission of local influence mechanisms at the provincial level.

Although this study improved our understanding of factors influencing the incidence of TB, there are several shortcomings. A variety of screening methods were used to construct the indicator system, the influence factors were supplemented from multiple levels, and specific influences at the macro-level of indicators could be demonstrated from secondary indicators. However, the incidence of TB is influenced by the interaction of complex factors, and some potential factors may not have been included in the study. Thirty-one provincial-level administrative regions were used as the spatial unit of analysis, despite a rather small sample size, raising concerns about the robustness of the spatial modeling. Lack of robustness may impair the model’s ability to capture modest spatial variability and diminish the reliability of statistical tests (e.g., bandwidth optimization and kernel function fitness). This study depended mostly on provincial data with poor spatial resolution, making it difficult to assess more specific regional differences within the province and potentially missing some localized mechanisms influencing the incidence of TB. Future studies should consider using higher-resolution spatial data, investigating a more scientific method for determining the importance threshold of random forests, improving the indicator system, and accurately quantifying behavioral and cognitive factors to reveal the pathogenesis of TB in a more comprehensive manner. Improved models will provide stronger support for the development of prevention and treatment strategies.
