Abstract
The canonical polyadic (CP) tensor decomposition has a long history. But it becomes challenging to find a tensor decomposition when the tensor’s rank is between the largest and the second-largest dimension. In such cases, traditional optimization methods, such as nonlinear least squares or alternative least squares methods, often fail to find a tensor decomposition. There are also direct methods, such as the normal form algorithm and the method by Domanov and De Lathauwer, that solve tensor decompositions algebraically. However, these methods can be computationally expensive and require significant memory, especially when the rank is high. This paper proposes a novel two-stage algorithm for the order-3 nonsymmetric tensor decomposition problem when the rank is not greater than the largest dimension. It transforms the tensor decomposition problem into two optimization problems which can be solved by algorithms such as the Levenberg–Marquardt-type methods. When the first-stage optimization is not fully solved, the partial solution will also be leveraged in the second-stage optimization problem. We prove the equivalence between tensor decompositions and the global minimizers of the two-stage optimization problems. Our numerical experiments demonstrate the proposed two-stage optimization method is very efficient and robust, capable of finding tensor decompositions where other commonly used state-of-the-art methods fail.

1.1
Contributions

To address the computational and memory limitations mentioned above, we propose a new two-stage optimization algorithm for solving the tensor decomposition problem in the Middle-Rank Case.
For the Low-Rank Case, our method is generally the same as the generating polynomial method given in
[
33
]
.

Given a Middle-Rank Case tensor
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
with
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq n_{3}
and rank
n_{2}<r\leq n_{1}
, our algorithm aims to find the tensor decomposition as in (
2
).
The high-level framework of the algorithm can be summarized as follows:

Step 1.

Preprocessing the tensor
\mathcal{F}
and get its reduced tensor slices
T_{2},\cdots,T_{n_{3}}
. (See Section 3.1.)

Step 2.

Try to find all the generalized left common eigenvectors of
T_{2},\cdots,T_{n_{3}}
by solving the first optimization problem.
If all the generalized common eigenvectors are found, a linear least squares is applied to get the tensor decomposition;
otherwise, go to Step 3. (See Section 3.2.)

Step 3.

Solve the second optimization problem as in (
3.3
) with the incomplete set of generalized common eigenvectors obtained from Step 2.
Then, a linear least square is applied to get the tensor decomposition. (See Section 3.3.)

In the ideal case, our method will successfully find the tensor decomposition after Step 2.
Otherwise, the method will construct and solve a second optimization problem in Step 3 based on the generating polynomials of the reduced tensor
\mathcal{T}
and the partial left common eigenvectors identified in Step 2.
The tensor decomposition can then be obtained by solving a linear least squares problem.
For more details on these steps, one may refer to Section
3
, Algorithms
7
and
9
.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the notation,
reviews the generating polynomials for tensors and presents some preliminary results.
In Section
3
, we describe how to reformulate the tensor decomposition problem into the first and second optimization problems and provide our optimization-based algorithms.
Section
4
presents numerical experiments comparing our algorithm with other often used and state-of-the-art algorithms for tensor decomposition.

Generating Polynomial and Tensor Decomposition

Generating polynomials are highly related to tensor decomposition. In this section, we review the definition of generating polynomials and how to relate the tensor decomposition problem to an optimization problem using generating polynomials.

For a tensor
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}

.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{F}
has rank

, we have

\mathcal{F}={U}^{(1)}\circ{U}^{(2)}\circ{U}^{(3)}

3
Equivalent optimization reformulation and two-stage algorithm

In this section,
we derive equivalent optimization formulations of tensor decomposition
and present our two-stage optimization algorithm.
We begin by obtaining a reduced tensor
\mathcal{T}
through preprocessing of a generic tensor
\mathcal{F}
.

3.1
Preprocessing for reduced tensor
\mathcal{T}

For a generic tensor
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
with
rank
n_{2}<r\leq n_{1}
, suppose
\mathcal{F}
has tensor decomposition

\mathcal{F}=U^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}
.
We would like to obtain a reduced tensor
\mathcal{T}
by preprocessing
a generic tensor
\mathcal{F}
.

First, in the generic case, we have
U^{(1)}_{1:r,:}
is nonsingular,

U^{(2)}
has full row rank and
U^{(3)}_{1,s}\neq 0
for all
s=1,\ldots,r
.
For any
\lambda_{s}\neq 0
,
s=1,\ldots,r
, we have the observation

U^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}=U^{(1)}\circ(U^{(2)}\mathrm{diag}(\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}\end{pmatrix}))\circ(U^{(3)}\mathrm{diag}(\begin{pmatrix}1/\lambda_{1},\cdots,1/\lambda_{r}\end{pmatrix})).

Hence, without loss of generality, we can assume that
U^{(3)}_{1,s}=1
for
s\in\{1,\cdots,r\}

Therefore, we have

\mathcal{F}_{1:r,:,1}=U^{(1)}_{1:r,:}\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{1,:})(U^{(2)})^{\top}=U^{(1)}_{1:r,:}(U^{(2)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}},

(16)

which has full column rank.
Therefore, there exists some matrix
C
such that

\widehat{F}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{1:r,:,1}&C\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}

is nonsingular.
So, denoting
P\coloneqq\widehat{F}^{-1}
, we will have

is nonsingular and

P\mathcal{F}_{1:r,:,1}=(I_{r})_{:,1:n_{2}}.

(17)

Now let the tensor
\mathcal{T}
be obtained by matrix-tensor product
on the tensor
\mathcal{F}_{1:r,:,:}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\coloneqq P\times_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1:r,:,:}.

(18)

Then, from tensor decomposition of
\mathcal{F}
and the property (
4
),
we would have

\mathcal{T}=\widehat{U}^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)},

(19)

where
\widehat{U}^{(1)}\coloneqq PU^{(1)}_{1:r,:}
.
Defining

,
1\leq k\leq n_{3}
,
it then follows from previous construction that

T_{1}=P\mathcal{F}_{1:r,:,1}=(I_{r})_{:,1:n_{2}}
.
Our first-stage optimization algorithm relates to finding the generalized left common eigenvectors of
\{T_{2},T_{3},\ldots,T_{n_{s}}\}

(See Definition
4
).

We now consider the linear systems (
13
) with
j=3
and
the tensor
\mathcal{F}
being replaced by
the reduced tensor

\mathcal{T}
.
Then, for all
2\leq k\leq n_{3}
, denoting
M^{3,k}[G]
as
M^{3,k}
,
the linear systems in (
13
) with
j=3
can be rewritten as

M^{3,k}T_{1}=T_{k}.

(20)

So, when
r>n_{2}
, the matrices
\{M^{3,k}\}_{2\leq k\leq n_{3}}
cannot be fully determined by the linear systems (
20
).
Fortunately, by Theorem
2
,

\{M^{3,k}\}_{2\leq k\leq n_{3}}
must mutually commute, that is,
for all
2\leq i<j\leq n_{3}
,

M^{3,i}M^{3,j}=M^{3,j}M^{3,i}.

(21)

Thus,
in addition to equations
(
2
), we also require
\{M^{3,k}\}_{2\leq k\leq n_{3}}
to be simultaneously diagonalizable in the generic case.
Now, for all
2\leq k\leq n_{3}
, by defining
P_{k}\coloneqq M^{3,k}_{:,n_{2}+1:r}
and plugging in
T_{1}=\mathcal{T}_{:,:,1}=(I_{r})_{:,1:n_{2}}
to (
20
),
we have

\displaystyle M^{3,k}=\begin{pmatrix}T_{k}&P_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

(22)

Since
\{T_{k}\}
is known , finding
\{M^{3,k}\}_{2\leq k\leq n_{3}}
turns out to be finding
\{P_{k}\}_{2\leq k\leq n_{3}}
.
With (
22
), for all
2\leq i<j\leq n_{3}
, the commuting equations (
21
)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M^{3,i}M^{3,j}-M^{3,j}M^{3,i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}T_{i}&P_{i}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}T_{j}&P_{j}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}T_{j}&P_{j}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}T_{i}&P_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

This gives the linear and quartic equations on unknowns
\{P_{i}\}_{2\leq i\leq n_{3}}
,

\begin{pmatrix}T_{i}&P_{i}\\
\end{pmatrix}T_{j}-\begin{pmatrix}T_{j}&P_{j}\\
\end{pmatrix}T_{i}=0,

(23)

and

\begin{pmatrix}T_{i}&P_{i}\\
\end{pmatrix}P_{j}-\begin{pmatrix}T_{j}&P_{j}\\
\end{pmatrix}P_{i}=0.

(24)

We would use these linear equations (
23
)
and nonlinear equations (
24
) for designing our second-stage optimization
algorithm.

3.2
The first-stage optimization algorithm

In this subsection, we propose the first reformulated optimization problem that is equivalent to the tensor decomposition problem for generic tensors with rank
r\leq n_{1}
.
We focus on the Middle-Rank Case with
n_{2}<r\leq n_{1}
.
For the Low-Rank Case with
r\leq n_{2}
, our method would essentially reduce to the generalized eigenvalue decomposition (GEVD) method
[
23
,
38
]
.

Recall from the decomposition
\mathcal{T}=\widehat{U}^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}

defined in (
19
).
Our first goal is to find the inverse of the first decomposition matrix
\widehat{U}^{(1)}

of
\mathcal{T}
.
Denoting
S\coloneqq(\widehat{U}^{(1)})^{-1}
and recalling the definition of

{T}_{k}={\mathcal{T}}_{:,:,k}
, for all
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
, we have

\displaystyle ST_{k}=(\widehat{U}^{(1)})^{-1}\widehat{U}^{(1)}\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{k,:})(U^{(2)})^{\top}=\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{k,:})(U^{(2)})^{\top}.

Then, it follows from
T_{1}=(I_{r})_{:,1:n_{2}}
and our assumptions

\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{1,:})=I_{r}
that
S_{:,1:n_{2}}=(U^{(2)})^{\top}
.
Moreover, denoting
D_{k}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{k,:})
,
we have

ST_{k}=D_{k}(U^{(2)})^{\top}=D_{k}S_{:,1:n_{2}}

(25)

for all
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
.
Note that
T_{k}
in (
25
) is a
r
by
n_{2}
matrix instead of a square matrix.
So, the rows of
S
can be considered as the generalized left eigenvectors of
T_{k}
.
Motivated by this observation, we propose the following definition of
the generalized left common eigenmatrix.

Definition 4
.

For a set of matrices
A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
with
m\geq n
, a full rank matrix
S\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is called the generalized left common eigenmatrix of
A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}
, if it satisfies

SA_{k}=D_{k}S_{:,1:n}~~\mathrm{for~}1\leq k\leq d,

(26)

where
D_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is a diagonal matrix.
For all
i=1,\ldots,m
,
s^{i}\coloneqq S_{i,:}
is called a generalized left common eigenvector of
A_{1},\cdots,A_{d}
, and
\lambda_{i,k}\coloneqq(D_{k})_{i,i}
s is called the generalized left common eigenvalue of
A_{k}
associated with
s^{i}
.

Since
T_{1}=(I_{r})_{:,1:n_{2}}
,
ST_{1}=S_{:,1:n_{2}}
naturally holds for all
S
.
So, our goal is to find the generalized left common eigenmatrix
S
of the reduced tensor
slices
\{{T}_{2},{T}_{3},\cdots,{T}_{n_{3}}\}
, i.e., find
S\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
and

\lambda_{i,k}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

S_{i,:}T_{k}=\lambda_{i,k}S_{i,1:n_{2}}~~\mathrm{for~all~}1\leq i\leq r\mbox{ and }2\leq k\leq n_{3}.

Of course, under different scenarios, the generalized left common eigenmatrix may not exist,
and even if it exists, it may not be unique. However, the following theorem shows that in
the Middle-Rank Case, the generalized left common eigenmatrix of the reduced tensor slices has a bijective relationship with the tensor decomposition,
which can help to find the tensor decomposition.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
be an order-3 tensor with
rank
n_{2}<r\leq n_{1}
.
Suppose
\mathcal{T}
is the reduced tensor of
\mathcal{F}
given in (
17
)
with
{T}_{k}={\mathcal{T}}_{:,:,k}
,
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
.
In the generic case, we have

(i)

for each nonsingular generalized left common eigenmatrix
S
of
T_{2},\cdots,T_{n_{3}}
,

\mathcal{F}
has a tensor decomposition given in (
29
);

(ii)

for each tensor decomposition, there is a nonsingular generalized left
common eigenmatrix
S
of
T_{2},\cdots,T_{n_{3}}
.

Proof.

We first prove
(i)
. Suppose
S
is a nonsingular generalized left common eigenmatrix of

T_{2},\cdots,T_{n_{3}}
, that is

ST_{k}=D_{k}S_{:,1:n_{2}}~~\mathrm{for~}2\leq k\leq n_{3}.

(27)

Let
\lambda_{i,k}=(D_{k})_{i,i}
for all
1\leq i\leq r
and
2\leq k\leq n_{3}
, and
let
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}=\widehat{U}^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{U}^{(1)}=S^{-1},~U^{(2)}=(S_{:,1:n_{2}})^{\top},U^{(3)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\\
\lambda_{1,2}&\lambda_{2,2}&\dots&\lambda_{r,2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{1,n_{3}}&\lambda_{2,n_{3}}&\dots&\lambda_{r,n_{3}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(28)

Then, by the construction of
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
and (
27
),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{:,:,k}=\widehat{U}^{(1)}\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{k,:})(U^{(2)})^{\top}=S^{-1}D_{k}S_{:,1:n_{2}}=S^{-1}(ST_{k})=T_{k}.

Hence, we have
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}=\mathcal{T}
.
Then, it follows from (
17
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1:r,:,:}=P^{-1}\times_{1}\mathcal{T}=P^{-1}\times_{1}\widehat{\mathcal{T}}=P^{-1}\widehat{U}^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}.

This gives a tensor decomposition for
\mathcal{F}_{1:r,:,:}
.
Based on Lemma
3
, the linear least squares system
AX=B

has a solution, denoted as
{U}^{(1)}
, where
A=U^{(2)}\odot U^{(3)}
,

B=\mathrm{Flatten}(\mathcal{F},1)^{\top}
.
Therefore, we have a tensor decomposition of

\mathcal{F}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}={U}^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}.

(29)

We now prove
(ii)
.
This essentially follows from the previous discussion on the motivations of the Definition
4
.
Since
\mathcal{T}
is the reduced tensor of
\mathcal{F}
given in (
17
), we have (
19
) holds. That is

\mathcal{T}=\widehat{U}^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}
,
where
\widehat{U}^{(1)}=PU^{(1)}_{1:r,:}
,
P
is given in (
17
)
and
U^{(i)}
,
i=1,2,3
, are matrices such that

\mathcal{F}=U^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}
.
Let
S=(\widehat{U}^{(1)})^{-1}
.
So, for all
1\leq k\leq n_{3}
, we have

T_{k}=PU^{(1)}_{1:r,:}diag(U^{(3)}_{k,:})({U}^{(2)})^{\top}=S^{-1}diag(U^{(3)}_{k,:})({U}^{(2)})^{\top}.

(30)

Then, it follows from
T_{1}=(I_{r})_{:,1:n_{2}}
,

\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{1,:})=I_{r}
and (
30
) that
S_{:,1:n_{2}}=(U^{(2)})^{\top}
.
Hence, by (
30
), for
2\leq k\leq n_{3}
we have

\displaystyle ST_{k}=diag(U^{(3)}_{k,:})({U}^{(2)})^{\top}=diag(U^{(3)}_{k,:})S_{:,1:n_{2}}.

(31)

Therefore,
S
is a nonsingular generalized left common eigenmatrix of

T_{2},\cdots,T_{n_{3}}
.
∎

Theorem
5
shows that when the tensor rank belongs
n_{2}<r\leq n_{1}
,
in the generic case, the reduced tensor slices would have a generalized left common eigenmatrix
S
,
which can be used to construct the tensor decomposition.
In the following, we propose an optimization-based approach to find the rows of
S
sequentially.
First, given an unitary matrix
Q\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
,
for any
x\in\mathbb{C}^{r-1}
denoting
\overline{x}=Q\begin{pmatrix}x^{\top}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
,
we define the function
f_{Q}(x)
with domain

\Omega\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{r-1}:\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}\neq 0\}
as

\displaystyle f_{Q}(x)\coloneqq Vec\big((I_{n_{2}}-\frac{\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}\overline{x}^{\top}_{1:n_{2}}}{\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}^{\top}\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}})(\overline{x}^{\top}\times_{1}\mathcal{T})\big).

(32)

Here,
\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}\overline{x}^{\top}_{1:n_{2}}/(\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}^{\top}\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}})
is a projection matrix that projects a vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}}
into the column space of
\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}
.
By the property of projection matrices, one can verify that
f_{Q}(x)=0
if and only if
all
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
, we have

(\overline{x}^{\top}\times_{1}\mathcal{T})_{:,k}=(\mathcal{T}_{:,:,k})^{\top}\overline{x}=\lambda_{1,k}\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}

(33)

for some
\lambda_{1,k}\in\mathbb{C}
.
Additionally, denoting
Z=I_{n_{2}}-\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}^{\top}/(\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}^{\top}\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}})
and
e_{i}
be the
i
-th
coordinate basis in
\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}}
,
one can derive the Jacobian matrix of
f_{Q}(x)
:

\displaystyle J_{f_{Q}}

\displaystyle=[\mathrm{Flatten}((\overline{x}^{\top}\times_{1}\mathcal{T})^{\top}\times_{2}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\overline{x}},3)+\mathrm{Flatten}(Z\times_{2}\mathcal{T},1)]^{\top}\frac{\partial\overline{x}}{\partial x},

where

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{2}}(e_{i}\otimes\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}\otimes e_{i}+\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{i})}{\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}^{\top}\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}}+\frac{2\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}\otimes\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}\otimes\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}}{(\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}}^{\top}\overline{x}_{1:n_{2}})^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\overline{x}_{n_{2}+1:r}}

\displaystyle=0\mathrm{~and~}\frac{\partial\overline{x}}{\partial x}=Q_{:,1:r-1}.

Remark 3.1
.

For a tensor
\mathcal{F}=U^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}
with rank
r
, we know all the columns of
U^{(2)}
are nonzero vectors.
Based on (
28
),
S_{i,1:n_{2}}=U^{(2)}_{:,i}
.
Therefore, for each generalized left common eigenvector
s^{i}
with
(s^{i})_{1:n_{2}}=U^{(2)}_{:,i}\neq 0
,
the global minimizer such that
\overline{x}=s^{i}
will be in
\Omega
.
This implies that solving
f_{Q}(x)=0
on
\Omega
will be generically sufficient to find all the generalized left common eigenvectors
in
S
of Theorem
5

(ii)
.

To find the first row of
S
, we start with a randomly generated unitary matrix
Q_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}

and formulate the optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\Omega}~\lVert f_{Q_{1}}(x)\rVert^{2}_{2}.

(34)

If we are able to find the global minimizer
x^{1}
of (
34
) such
that
f_{Q_{1}}(x^{1})=0
, we let

(s^{1})^{\top}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}(x^{1})^{\top}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}Q_{1}^{\top}
be the first row of
S
.
Now, suppose we have already found the first
p-1
rows of
S
for some
1<p\leq r
.
Let
S^{p-1}\coloneqq(s^{1},\ldots,s^{p-1})^{\top}
.
To determine the
p
-th row of
S
, which must be linearly independent to the first
p-1
rows,
we perform the QR decomposition of
(S^{p-1})^{\top}
, i.e., we find

(S^{p-1})^{\top}=Q_{p}R_{p}
for some unitary matrix
Q_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}

and
R_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times(p-1)}
with
(R_{p})_{1:p-1,:}
being an upper triangular matrix.
Then, we formulate the optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\Omega}~\lVert f_{Q_{p}}(x)\rVert^{2}_{2}.

(35)

If we are able to find the global minimizer
x^{p}
of (
35
) such
that
f_{Q_{p}}(x^{p})=0
, we let

(s^{p})^{\top}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}(x^{p})^{\top}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}Q_{p}^{\top}
be
the first
p
-th row of
S
; otherwise, we stop the process.
Suppose we have finally successfully determined the first
p
rows of
S
using the above process
with
S^{p}\coloneqq(s^{1},\ldots,s^{p})^{\top}
where
1\leq p\leq r
.
It then follows from (
33
) that for all
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
, we have

S^{p}T_{k}=D_{k}(S^{p})_{:,1:n_{2}},

(36)

where
T_{k}=\mathcal{T}_{:,:,k}
and
D_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}
is a diagonal matrix with
the diagonal element
(D_{k})_{i,i}=\lambda_{i,k}
.
In addition, the following lemma shows that the
p
rows sequentially found by the above process are
linearly independent.

Lemma 6
.

Given
1<p\leq r
, suppose
s^{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,p
are obtained by
setting
s^{i}=Q_{i}\begin{pmatrix}(x^{i})^{\top}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
, where
x^{i}
is the minimizer of

\min_{x\in\Omega}~\lVert f_{Q_{i}}(x)\rVert^{2}_{2}

and
Q_{i}
is constructed by the above process.
Then,
s^{p}\notin\mathrm{span}(s^{1},\cdots,s^{p-1})
.

Proof.

Since
Q_{p}R_{p}
is the QR decomposition of
S_{p-1}^{\top}=(s^{1},\ldots,s^{p-1})^{\top}
,
we have

\mathrm{col}((Q_{p})_{:,1:p-1})=\mathrm{span}(s^{1},s^{2},\dots,s^{p-1}).

(37)

On the other hand, by the process of obtaining
s^{k}
, we have

s^{p}=Q_{k}\begin{pmatrix}(x^{p})^{\top}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}^{\top}=(Q_{p})_{:,1:p-1}(x^{p})_{1:p-1}+(Q_{p})_{:,p:r}\begin{pmatrix}(x^{p})_{p:r-1}\\
1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Then, because
Q_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
is an unitary matrix, (
37
)
and
\begin{pmatrix}(x^{p}))_{p:r-1}^{\top}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\neq 0
,
we have
s^{p}\notin\mathrm{span}(s^{1},\cdots,s^{p-1})
.
∎

If we are able to determine all the
r
rows of the matrix
S
, i.e. find the entire matrix
S
,
based on Theorem
5
, we can obtain a tensor decomposition for
\mathcal{F}

as in (
29
).
In this case, the algorithm for finding
the tensor decomposition can be described as Algorithm
7
.
However, if only the first
p
rows
S^{p}
of
S
with
p<r
are available, we cannot
fully determine the tensor decomposition using this partial information.
Nonetheless, the relation (
36
) involving
S^{p}
will still be utilized in the second-stage optimization algorithm presented in the next section.

Algorithm 7
.

The First-Stage Algorithm for Tensor Decomposition

Input:
The tensor
\mathcal{F}
with rank
n_{2}<r\leq n_{1}
.

Step 1

Preprocess the tensor
\mathcal{F}
and get the new tensor
\mathcal{T}
as in (
18
).

Step 2

For
k=1,\ldots,r
, solve the optimization
\min_{x\in\Omega}~\lVert f_{Q_{k}}(x)\rVert^{2}_{2}

sequentially and obtain
s^{k}
as described in the above process.
If all the
r
optimization problems are successfully solved, form
the generalized left common eigenmatrix
S=(s^{1},\ldots,s^{r})^{\top}
and continue Step 3;
otherwise, if only
p<r
optimization problems are solved,
form partial left common eigenmatrix
S^{p}=(s^{1},\ldots,s^{p})^{\top}
and stop the algorithm;

Step 3

For
i=1,\ldots,r
and
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
,
let
\lambda_{i,k}
be the generalized common eigenvalue of
T_{k}
associated with
s^{i}
,

{w}^{i,2}\coloneqq(S_{i,1:n_{2}})^{\top}
and
w^{i,3}_{k}\coloneqq\lambda_{i,k}
.

Step 4

Solve the linear system

\sum_{i=1}^{r}{{w}}^{i,1}\otimes{w}^{i,2}\otimes{w}^{i,3}=\mathcal{F}

to get vectors
\{{w}^{i,1}\}
.

Output:
A decomposition of
\mathcal{F}
:

\quad\mathcal{F}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{w}^{i,1}\otimes{w}^{i,2}\otimes{w}^{i,3}

Our above process for finding tensor decomposition can in fact also be
analogously applied for tensors
\mathcal{F}
with rank
r\leq n_{2}
.
Suppose
\mathcal{F}
has decomposition given in (
29
).
In this case, the reduced tensor will be

\mathcal{T}=P\times_{1}\mathcal{F}_{1:r,1:r,:}
, where

P=(\mathcal{F}_{1:r,1:r,1})^{-1}=((U^{(2)}_{1:r,:})^{\top})^{-1}(U^{(1)}_{1:r,:})^{-1}.

(38)

Thus,
\mathcal{T}
will have decomposition

\mathcal{T}=\widehat{U}^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}_{1:r,:}\circ U^{(3)}
, where

\widehat{U}^{(1)}=PU^{(1)}_{1:r,:}=((U^{(2)}_{1:r,:})^{-1})^{\top}
.
Then, for
k=2,\ldots,n_{3}
, we would have

T_{k}=((U^{(2)}_{1:r,:})^{-1})^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{k,:})(U^{(2)}_{1:r,:})^{\top}.

The matrix
S
, as the common left eigenvectors of
T_{k}
for
k=2,\ldots,n_{3}
,
is
(U^{(2)}_{1:r,:})^{\top}
and
the generalized left common eigenvector reduces to the standard left common eigenvectors.
So,
S
can be found by determining the left eigenvectors of
T_{k}
, for instance, using the power method.
Finally, the tensor decomposition of
F
can be obtained by solving particular linear systems.
In this case, the approach is essentially similar to the generalized eigenvalue decomposition (GEVD) method
[
23
,
38
]
.

3.3
The second-stage optimization algorithm

In this section, we consider the scenarios where,
instead of the entire generalized left common eigenmatrix
S
, only
partial rows of
S
are obtained in Algorithm (
29
).
Recall that
\mathcal{T}
and
T_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times n_{2}}
represent the tensor and its matrix slices
produced by preprocessing the original tensor
\mathcal{F}
as in (
18
).
In this case, to find the tensor decomposition of
\mathcal{F}
, we reformulate the problem
by solving for
P_{k}
’s using the equations (
23
) and (
24
).
For convenience of the analysis, for
k=2,\ldots,n_{3}
, we denote that

T_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}(T_{k}^{1})^{\top}\\
(T_{k}^{2})^{\top}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{where }T_{k}^{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}}\mbox{ and }T_{k}^{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}\times(r-n_{2})}.

Therefore, from (
23
) we get

T_{i}(T_{j}^{1})^{\top}+P_{i}(T_{j}^{2})^{\top}=T_{j}(T_{i}^{1})^{\top}+P_{j}(T_{i}^{2})^{\top},

which then implies

\displaystyle P_{i}(T_{j}^{2})^{\top}-P_{j}(T_{i}^{2})^{\top}=T_{j}(T_{i}^{1})^{\top}-T_{i}(T_{j}^{1})^{\top}.

(39)

There are a total of
{n_{3}-1\choose 2}=(n-1)(n-2)/2
choices for the pair
(i,j)

in (
39
). Let

d_{1}=rn_{2}(n-1)(n-2)/2\quad\mbox{ and }\quad d_{2}=r(r-n_{2})(n_{3}-1).

We could reformulate (
39
) as a linear system

A\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(P_{2})^{\top}&\cdots&\mathrm{vec}(P_{n_{3}})^{\top}\\
\end{pmatrix}^{\top}=b,

(40)

where the coefficient matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}T^{2}_{3}\boxtimes I_{r}&-T^{2}_{2}\boxtimes I_{r}&0&\cdots&0\\
T^{2}_{4}\boxtimes I_{r}&0&-T^{2}_{2}\boxtimes I_{r}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{T^{2}_{n_{3}}}\boxtimes I_{r}&0&0&\cdots&-T^{2}_{2}\boxtimes I_{r}\\
0&T^{2}_{4}\boxtimes I_{r}&-T^{2}_{3}\boxtimes I_{r}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&T^{2}_{n_{3}}\boxtimes I_{r}&-T^{2}_{n_{3}-1}\boxtimes I_{r}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}},

(41)

and the right hand side

b=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(T_{2}^{1}(T_{3})^{\top}-T_{3}^{1}(T_{2})^{\top})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(T_{n_{3}-1}^{1}(T_{n_{3}})^{\top}-T_{n_{3}}^{1}(T_{n_{3}-1})^{\top})\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

Given any
p
rows of the generalized left common eigenmatrix
S
of the slices of the
reduced tensor
\mathcal{T}
,
the following theorem provides an important property to design our second-stage optimization.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
be an order-3 tensor with rank
n_{2}<r\leq n_{1}
.
Suppose
s^{1},\cdots,s^{p}
are linearly independent rows of the generalized left common eigenmatrix
S

of the reduced tensor slices
T_{2},\cdots,T_{n_{3}}
of
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
S^{p}=(s^{1},\cdots,s^{p})^{\top}
and
P_{k}=M^{3,k}_{:,n_{2}+1:r}
, where
M^{3,k}
is the generating matrix defined in (
11
).
We have

\displaystyle S^{p}\begin{pmatrix}T_{k}&P_{k}\\
\end{pmatrix}=D_{k}S^{p}~~~\mathrm{for}~2\leq k\leq n_{3},

(43)

where
D_{k}
is a diagonal matrix.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let us assume
S^{p}=S_{1:p,:}
.

S
is the generalized left common eigenmatrix of the slices
T_{2},\cdots,T_{n_{3}}
of
the reduced tensor
\mathcal{T}
.
Therefore, the proof of part (i) of Theorem
5
implies that

\mathcal{T}
has the tensor decomposition
\mathcal{T}=\widehat{U}^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}
.
Here
\widehat{U}^{(1)}
,
U^{(2)}
and
U^{(3)}
are given in (
28
).
Then, by (
27
), we have

S^{p}T_{k}=D_{k}S^{p}_{:,1:n_{2}}~\mathrm{where}~D_{k}=U^{(3)}_{k,1:p}.

(44)

We can rewrite (
14
) of Theorem
2
as

M^{3,k}=\widehat{U}^{(1)}\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{k,:})(\widehat{U}^{(1)})^{-1}.

(45)

From (
28
), we have
S=(\widehat{U}^{(1)})^{-1}
.
Hence, it follows from
P_{k}=M^{3,k}_{:,n_{2}+1:r}
, (
44
) and (
45
) that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S^{p}P_{k}~=&~S_{1:p,:}M^{3,k}_{:,n_{2}+1:r}\\
~=&~((\widehat{U}^{(1)})^{-1})_{1:p,:}\widehat{U}^{(1)}\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{k,:})(\widehat{U}^{(1)})^{-1}(I_{r})_{:,n_{2}+1:r}\\
~=&~\mathrm{diag}(U^{(3)}_{k,1:p})((\widehat{U}^{(1)})^{-1})_{1:p,n_{2}+1:r}\\
~=&~D_{k}S_{1:p,n_{2}+1:r}=D_{k}S^{p}_{:,n_{2}+1:r}.\end{aligned}

(46)

Finally, combining (
44
) and (
46
), we have (
43
) holds.
∎

From (
43
) of Theorem
8
, we have

S^{p}P_{k}=D_{k}(S^{p})_{:,n_{2}+1:r}
, which provides additional systems of linear equations for

P_{k}
for
2\leq k\leq n_{3}
.
These linear systems can be compactly written as

\widetilde{A}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(P_{2})^{\top}&\cdots&\mathrm{vec}(P_{n_{3}})^{\top}\\
\end{pmatrix}^{\top}=\widetilde{b}

where the coefficient matrix

\widetilde{A}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{r-n_{2}}\boxtimes S^{p}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&I_{r-n_{2}}\boxtimes S^{p}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&I_{r-n_{2}}\boxtimes S^{p}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{(r-n_{2})(n_{3}-1)p\times d_{2}},

(47)

and the right hand side

\widetilde{b}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(D_{2}(S^{p})_{:,n_{2}+1:r})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(D_{n_{3}}(S^{p})_{:,n_{2}+1:r})\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(48)

Combining it with the previous linear system (
40
), we can form a
larger linear system for
\{P_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n_{3}}
as

\widehat{A}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(P_{2})^{\top}&\cdots&\mathrm{vec}(P_{n_{3}})^{\top}\\
\end{pmatrix}^{\top}=\widehat{b},

(49)

where
\widehat{A}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{\top}&\widetilde{A}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
and
\widehat{b}=\begin{pmatrix}b^{\top}&\widetilde{b}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
.
In addition to linear system (
49
),

\{P_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n_{3}}
also satisfy the quadratic equations (
24
).
For
2\leq i<j\leq n_{3}
, let

g^{i,j}(P_{i},P_{j})\coloneqq\mathrm{vec}\left(\begin{pmatrix}T_{i}&P_{i}\\
\end{pmatrix}P_{j}-\begin{pmatrix}T_{j}&P_{j}\\
\end{pmatrix}P_{i}\right).

Then, to find
P_{k}
s, we propose to solve the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{P_{2},\cdots,P_{n_{3}}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times(r-n_{2})}}~~~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\begin{pmatrix}(g^{2,3}(P_{2},P_{3}))^{\top}\cdots&(g^{n_{3}-1,n_{3}}(P_{n_{3}-1},P_{n_{3}}))^{\top}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\rVert^{2}_{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\qquad\quad\quad

\displaystyle\widehat{A}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(P_{2})^{\top}\cdots&\mathrm{vec}(P_{n_{3}})^{\top}\\
\end{pmatrix}^{\top}=\widehat{b}.

(50)

Let
N\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}\times t}
b a matrix whose columns form a basis for the null space of
\widehat{A}
in (
49
)
and
\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(P_{2}^{0})^{\top}&\cdots&\mathrm{vec}(P_{n_{3}}^{0})^{\top}\\
\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
be a particular solution of (
49
).
Then, for
2\leq k\leq n_{3}
, we can parametrize the unknowns
P_{k}
in (
49
)
by
x\in\mathbb{C}^{t}
as

\mathrm{vec}(P_{k}(x))=\mathrm{vec}(P^{0}_{k})+N_{k}x,

(51)

where
N_{k}\coloneqq N_{(k-2)r(r-n_{2})+1:(k-1)r(r-n_{2}),:}
.
Then, by denoting

g(x)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}(g^{2,3}(P_{2}(x),P_{3}(x)))^{\top}&\cdots&(g^{n_{3}-1,n_{3}}(P_{n_{3}-1}(x),P_{n_{3}}(x)))^{\top}\end{pmatrix},

(52)

the constrained optimization problem (
3.3
) is equivalent to the following unconstrained optimization in terms of
x\in\mathbb{C}^{t}
:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{t}}~~~\frac{1}{2}\lVert g(x)\rVert^{2}_{2}.

(53)

By Theorem
2
, for a generic order-3 tensor
\mathcal{F}
,
the generating matrices
\{M^{3,k}\}_{2\leq k\leq n_{3}}
satisfy (
21
), which is equivalent to
(
23
) add (
24
).
Hence, by Theorem
8
and
our construction, in the generic case, the optimization problem (
53
) has a nonempty feasible set and
has a global minimizer
x
such that
g(x)=0
, which gives
P_{k}
’s by the parametrization (
51
).
Then, the tensor decomposition of
\mathcal{F}
can be recovered by using these
P_{k}
’s obtained
by solving (
53
).

To facilitate solving the optimization problems (
53
), we can derive the Jacobian of the function
g(x)
. Let
P_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{k}^{1}\\
P_{k}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times(r-n_{2})}
, where
P_{k}^{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}\times(r-n_{2})}
.
Then, the Jacobian of the function
g
can be given as

J_{g}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial g^{2,3}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{2})}&\frac{\partial g^{2,3}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{3})}&0&\cdots&0\\
\frac{\partial g^{2,4}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{2})}&0&\frac{\partial g^{2,4}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{4})}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial g^{2,n_{3}}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{2})}&0&0&\cdots&\frac{\partial g^{2,n_{3}}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{n_{3}})}\\
0&\frac{\partial g^{3,4}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{3})}&\frac{\partial g^{3,4}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{4})}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\frac{\partial g^{n_{3}-1,n_{3}}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{n_{3}-1})}&\frac{\partial g^{n_{3}-1,n_{3}}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{n_{3}})}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{2})}{\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{3})}{\partial x}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{n_{3}})}{\partial x}\\
\end{pmatrix},

(54)

where

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial g^{i,j}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{i})}=-I_{r-n_{2}}\boxtimes\begin{pmatrix}T_{j}&P_{j}\\
\end{pmatrix}+(P^{2}_{j})^{\top}\boxtimes I_{r},\\
\frac{\partial g^{i,j}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{j})}=I_{r-n_{2}}\boxtimes\begin{pmatrix}T_{i}&P_{i}\\
\end{pmatrix}-(P^{2}_{i})^{\top}\boxtimes I_{r},\\
\frac{\partial\mathrm{vec}(P_{i})}{\partial x}=N_{k}.\end{cases}

(55)

Given the Jacobian of
g
, we
could
seek for a minimizer
x
of
(
53
) using a Levenberg-Marquardt-type method.
If (
53
) is solved with a global optimizer
x
(i.e.,
g(x)=0
),
the
P_{k}(x)
for
2\leq k\leq n_{3}
can be computed using the
parametrization in (
51
).
Theorem
5
shows in the generic case the generalized
left common eigenmatrix
S
exists.
By Theorem
8
with
p=r
, the rows of
S

are just the left eigenvectors of
\begin{pmatrix}T_{k}&P_{k}\end{pmatrix}
for any
k\in\{2,\ldots,n_{3}\}
.
Hence, the matrix
S
can be obtained by solving for the left eigenvectors of
\begin{pmatrix}T_{k}&P_{k}\end{pmatrix}
for some
k\in\{2,\ldots,n_{3}\}
.
We simply take
k=2
in our numerical experiments.
With the matrix
S
, we can get a tensor decomposition of
\mathcal{F}
as in (
29
).
To summarize, we propose the following Algorithm
9
for finding the tensor decomposition.
We call Algorithm
9
the second-stage algorithm, as it utilizes partial
results from the first-stage Algorithm
7
when it could not find
the entire matrix
S
.

Algorithm 9
.

The Second-Stage Algorithm for Tensor Decomposition

Input:
Tensor
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

with rank
n_{2}<r\leq n_{1}
, the pre-processed tensor
\mathcal{T}
and
the partial generalized left common eigenvectors
s^{1},\cdots,s^{p}
of
T_{2},\cdots,T_{n_{3}}

given by Algorithm
7
with
p<r
.

Step 1

Construct the linear system (
49
) using
\mathcal{T}
and
s^{1},\cdots,s^{p}
.

Step 2

Construct the function
g(x)
as in (
52
) and solve the optimization problem
(
53
) with Jacobian (
54
) to find a global minimizer
x
.

Step 3

Compute
P_{k}(x)
for
2\leq k\leq n_{3}
as in (
51
) using the minimizer
x
.

Step 4

Compute
S=(s^{1},\ldots,s^{r})^{\top}
, whose rows are the left eigenvectors of
\begin{pmatrix}T_{2}&P_{2}\end{pmatrix}
. For
1\leq i\leq r
and
2\leq k\leq n_{3}
,
let
\lambda_{i,k}
be the left eigenvalue of
\begin{pmatrix}T_{k}&P_{k}\end{pmatrix}
associated with
s^{i}
. Then get a tensor decomposition of
\mathcal{T}
as in (
28
).

Step 5

Solve linear least squares and get tensor decomposition of
\mathcal{F}
as in (
29
).

Output:
A decomposition of
\mathcal{F}
:

\quad\mathcal{F}={U}^{(1)}\circ U^{(2)}\circ U^{(3)}

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel two-stage optimization algorithm to solve the
order-3 tensor decomposition problem with a tensor rank that does not exceed the largest dimension.
In the first stage, the algorithm preprocesses the tensor and focuses on finding the generalized left common eigenmatrix
S
of the slices of the reduced tensor. In the ideal case, all the generalized left common eigenvectors of the slices can be found
and a tensor decomposition can be subsequently derived based on
S
and linear least squares.
If not all the generalized left common eigenvectors are found in the first stage,
the second stage algorithm will use the partial rows
of the matrix
S
obtained from the first stage and the generating polynomials to
recover the entire
S
.
A tensor decomposition can then be constructed based on
S
by solving linear least squares.
By comparing with other commonly used and state-of-the-art methods, our proposed two-stage optimization algorithm is highly efficient and robust for solving the
order-3 Middle-Rank case tensor decomposition problems.
