Near-Optimal Clustering in Mixture of Markov Chains
Junghyun Lee, Yassir Jedra, Alexandre Proutière, Se-Young Yun

Abstract
We study the problem of clustering T T trajectories of length H H , each generated by one of K K unknown ergodic Markov chains over a finite state space of size S S . The goal is to accurately group trajectories according to their underlying generative model. We begin by deriving an instance-dependent, high-probability lower bound on the clustering error rate, governed by the weighted KL divergence between the transition kernels of the chains. We then present a novel two-stage clustering algorithm. In Stage I, we apply spectral clustering using a new injective Euclidean embedding for ergodic Markov chains—a contribution of independent interest that enables sharp concentration results. Stage II refines the initial clusters via a single step of likelihood-based reassignment. Our method achieves a near-optimal clustering error with high probability, under the conditions H = Ω ~ ​ ( γ ps − 1 ​ ( S 2 ∨ π min − 1 ) ) H=\widetilde{\Omega}(\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}^{-1}(S^{2}\vee\pi_{\min}^{-1})) and T ​ H = Ω ~ ​ ( γ ps − 1 ​ S 2 ) TH=\widetilde{\Omega}(\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}^{-1}S^{2}) , where π min \pi_{\min} is the minimum stationary probability of a state across the K K chains and γ ps \gamma_{\mathrm{ps}} is the minimum pseudo-spectral gap. These requirements provide significant improvements, if not at least comparable, to the state-of-the-art guarantee ( Kausik et al. 2023 ) , and moreover, our algorithm offers a key practical advantage: unlike existing approach, it requires no prior knowledge of model-specific quantities (e.g., separation between kernels or visitation probabilities). We conclude by discussing the inherent gap between our upper and lower bounds, providing insights into the unique structure of this clustering problem.

Clustering, or community detection for graphs, is a fundamental statistical problem with applications across diverse scientific disciplines, including social science, biology, and statistical physics

;
Kiselev et al. 2019
;

;

The precise statistical characterization of clustering has been rigorously investigated under various probabilistic frameworks, such as stochastic block models (SBMs;

However, the aforementioned models primarily focus on clustering static data points or nodes, where individual elements often lack inherent informative structure in isolation: the clustering signal typically emerges only when considering the entire data.
In contrast, many real-world applications involving multiple underlying processes, such as astronomy
(

)

(

)

The task is to cluster the trajectories based on their generating process or model.
The longer each trajectory (e.g., a user’s interaction sequence or a time series) is, the more information it potentially reveals about its generating model, facilitating clustering.

The
Mixture of Markov Chains (MCC)
provides a fundamental yet powerful probabilistic framework for the mentioned problem of trajectory clustering in the most basic scenario with no controllable actions.
In this model, we are given
T

K

This model has been studied in a variety of context, originally by

;

A more recent motivating example arises in personalized reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) in large language models (LLMs) interacting with diverse users possessing heterogeneous preferences
(

)

To provide the best personalized experiences for users of the same preference, LLMs must effectively group feedback data based on, e.g., users’ conversation traces, according to these underlying preferences, and then perform RLHF.

In

Successful clustering can significantly simplify the learning task by allowing for information aggregation within each identified group of trajectories.
Conversely, while learning each model accurately can indeed facilitate clustering, it is expected to necessitate stringent requirements on the trajectory length

Thus, beyond its intrinsic value as an exploratory data analysis technique, accurate clustering can lead to statistically more efficient inference than analyzing each trajectory independently.

As done in prior clustering literature, one can ask two critical questions regarding clustering in

:
(i) What is the fundamental limit on the misclassification rate?

(ii) Given sufficiently long (but not excessively so) trajectories, is there a computationally tractable, parameter-free

Instance-Specific High Probability Lower Bound

In this section, we present the first instance-specific high-probability lower bound on the number of misclassified trajectories in the MMC problem. We define an instance of the MMC as a tuple
\Phi_{T}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(({\mathcal{M}}^{(k)})_{k\in[K]},f,T)
, where
({\mathcal{M}}^{(k)})_{k\in[K]}
is a collection of
K
ergodic Markov chains,
f
is the decoding function, and
T\in\mathbb{N}
is the total number of trajectories to be clustered.
Note that each instance of
clustering in MMC
is fully described by
\Phi_{T}
.

To derive instance-specific lower bounds, we must consider algorithms that for a given instance, genuinely adapt to perturbations of the decoding function. Indeed, an algorithm that would return
\widehat{f}=f

denoting
\bm{\alpha}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\alpha_{k}(f))_{k\in[K]}\in\Delta([K])

\alpha_{k}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|f^{-1}(k)|/T

(Stable Clustering Algorithms)

Let

The proof proceeds by first constructing a large number of hypotheses, each corresponding to different cluster allocations of the observations using combinatorial arguments of

These hypotheses are slight perturbations of the given instance of clustering in MMC.
We then utilize the change-of-measure argument

Algorithm Design.

We utilize a variant of spectral clustering

The key novelty here is the data matrix on which we perform the spectral decomposition, which we describe here.

For an ergodic Markov chain

with transition probability matrix
{\bm{P}}

L\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{M}}\mapsto\vec\left(\mathrm{diag}(\pi)^{1/2}{\bm{P}}\right),\quad\widehat{L}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{T}}=(s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{H})\mapsto\left(\widehat{N}(s,s^{\prime})/\sqrt{H\widehat{N}(s)}\right)_{(s,s^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{S}}^{2}},

{\mathcal{T}}\sim{\mathcal{M}}

\widehat{N}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathds{1}[s_{h}=s]

\widehat{N}(s,s^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathds{1}[s_{h}=s,s_{h+1}=s^{\prime}]

Remark 4
.

To the best of our knowledge, such Euclidean embedding of ergodic Markov chain has not been reported in the literature yet.
Without the square root, this is precisely the doublet frequency
(
Wolfer and Kontorovich 2021
;
Vidyasagar 2014
)
, which is known to encode all information about the ergodic Markov chain.
This vaguely resembles the diffusion map
(
Coifman and Lafon 2006
)
or weighted Laplacian
(
Chung 1997
)
, but we could not establish a clear connection.

We show below that our
L
also encodes all information about the chain:

Proposition 4.1
.

Over the space of ergodic Markov chains with a common initial state distribution,
L
is an injective mapping, i.e.,
L({\mathcal{M}})=L({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime})\Longleftrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By definition, we must have that for all
s,s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
,
\sqrt{\pi(s)}p(s^{\prime}|s)=\sqrt{\pi^{\prime}(s)}p^{\prime}(s^{\prime}|s)
.
Summing over
s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, we have that
\pi(s)=\pi^{\prime}(s)>0
for all
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
, where the ergodicity of the chains implies the strict positivity.
This then implies that
p=p^{\prime}
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
.
∎

{\bm{W}}=\begin{bmatrix}L({\mathcal{M}}^{(f(t))})\end{bmatrix}_{t\in[T],\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}}\in[0,1]^{T\times S^{2}},\quad\widehat{{\bm{W}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{L}({\mathcal{T}}_{t})\end{bmatrix}_{t\in[T],\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}}\in[0,1]^{T\times S^{2}},

(5)

i.e., by row-concatenating the embeddings.
Then, the minimum (row-wise)

\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\left\lVert L({\mathcal{M}}^{(k)})-L({\mathcal{M}}^{(k^{\prime})})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\min_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left\lVert\sqrt{\pi^{(k)}(s)}p^{(k)}(\cdot|s)-\sqrt{\pi^{(k^{\prime})}(s)}p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

By Proposition
4.1

5
Discussions

(1) Gaps Between Upper and Lower Bounds.

By comparing our performance upper bound (Theorem
4.2
) and lower bound (Theorem
3.1
), we identify two key gaps.

The first concerns the clustering error rates: the lower bound scales as
T\exp(-2H{\mathcal{D}})
, while the upper bound scales as
T\exp(-C_{\eta}\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}H{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi})
, where
C_{\eta}
is a constant depending on the regularity parameters
\eta_{p}
and
\eta_{\pi}
. The upper bound thus contains an additional multiplicative factor of
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}
in the exponential term.
The second gap pertains to the requirement on the horizon length
H
. Specifically, the lower bound holds for any
H\geq 2
and
T\geq 1
, while the upper bound requires
H=\widetilde{\Omega}(\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}^{-1}(S^{2}\vee\pi_{\min}^{-1}))
and
TH=\widetilde{\Omega}(\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}^{-1}S^{2})
, omitting logarithmic and other secondary factors.

We believe these gaps indicate that the current lower bound may be loose. Both stem from the fact that the transition kernels
p
are not known
a priori
and must be estimated by the learner, whereas the lower bound analysis assumes full knowledge of the
p
’s.

The first gap arises from employing the Markovian Bernstein concentration inequality of
Paulin 2015
, which relies on decomposing the trajectories of the Markov chain into nearly independent blocks of length proportional to
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}H

(
Yu 1994
;
Janson 2004
;
Lezaud 1998
)
. Recent results confirm that this dependence on
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}
in the exponent is indeed unavoidable for (reversible) Markov chains: there exist chains for which the concentration rate matches this dependency
(
Jiang et al. 2018
;
Fan et al. 2021
;
Rabinovich et al. 2020
)
.
The second gap stems from the concentration of
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}
around
{\bm{W}}
, which in turn requires accurate estimation of the transition kernels.
Wolfer and Kontorovich 2021
show the following requirement on
H
: if
H=o\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\pi_{\min}}+\frac{S}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}}\right)
, then no estimator can achieve
\varepsilon
-accuracy in
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow\infty}
.
6
6

6

Their theorem is stated for
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1\rightarrow\infty}
, but the same argument applies to
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow\infty}
with minimal modification.

We leave to future work the development of a more comprehensive lower bound that explicitly accounts for the learner’s uncertainty about the transition kernels
p
. Broadly, this challenge can be framed as a nonparametric statistical question
(
Tsybakov 2009
)
: to what extent does uncertainty in the underlying probability measures degrade the power of hypothesis testing?

(2) Comparison with
Kausik et al. 2023
.

We first recall their result on exact cluster recovery:

Assumption 3

(Assumption 2 of
Kausik et al. 2023
)
.

There are
\alpha,\Delta>0
such that:
\forall k\neq k^{\prime}\in[K]
,

\ \exists s=s(k,k^{\prime})\ \text{ s.t. }\ \pi^{(k)}(s),\pi^{(k^{\prime})}(s)\geq\alpha\ \hbox{ and }\ \left\lVert p^{(k)}(\cdot|s)-p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right\rVert_{2}\geq\Delta.

(11)

Theorem 5.1

(Theorem 3 of
Kausik et al. 2023
)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
3
hold.
Set the algorithm hyperparameters, occurrence and histogram clustering thresholds, as
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{3}
and
\tau=\frac{3\Delta^{2}}{4}
, respectively.
Then, with
H\gtrsim K^{3/2}t_{\mathrm{mix}}\frac{\left(\log(T/(\alpha\delta))\right)^{4}}{\alpha^{3}\Delta^{6}}
and
T\gtrsim K^{2}S\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha^{3}\Delta^{8}}
, their Algorithm 2 attains exact clustering (i.e., no misclassifications) with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Kausik et al. 2023
treat
\alpha
and
\Delta
as constants, independent of the number of states
S
and the trajectory length
H
. This assumption allows their condition on
H
to avoid any polynomial dependence on
S
, and ensures that the requirement on
T
scales only linearly with
S
. In effect, they implicitly assume that for every pair
k\neq k^{\prime}
, there exists a highly visited state
s(k,k^{\prime})
with constant separation and from which clusters can be identified.
However, this assumption does not hold in general.
For example, in uniform-like Markov chains where
\pi(s)=\Theta(1/S)
, they require
H\gtrsim t_{\mathrm{mix}}S^{3}(\log T)^{4}
and
T\gtrsim S^{4}\log T
(with
\delta=1/T
), which are impractically large.
In the same regime, our algorithm requires only
H\gtrsim t_{\mathrm{mix}}(S^{2}\vee\pi_{\min}^{-1})\log T
and
TH\gtrsim t_{\mathrm{mix}}S^{2}
, a significant improvement.

Moreover, a major limitation of their algorithm is that it requires prior knowledge of
\alpha
and
\Delta
for Theorem
5.1
to apply.
In contrast, our algorithm is completely parameter-free (see Remark
1
for relaxing the knowledge of
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}
).
Also, our requirements of
H
and
T
, albeit worse by a factor of
\pi_{\min}^{-1}
in the ideal scenario mentioned above, are generally much better, and are believed to be minimax optimal as discussed in
(1)
.
An interesting future direction is whether an instance-wise optimal algorithm is possible, i.e., when the condition on
H
for cluster recovery is
H\gtrsim t_{\mathrm{mix}}
under Assumption
3
with
\alpha=\Omega(1)
, and
H\gtrsim\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}^{-1}(S^{2}\vee\pi_{\min}^{-1})
, or better, in the worst-case.

(3) Different gaps:
{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}
,
\Delta_{\bm{W}}
,
\Delta
and
\alpha
.

Throughout this paper, we have introduced different notions of separability: the KL gap
{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}
, the “aggregated”
\ell_{2}
gap
\Delta_{\bm{W}}
, and the separability quantities
\Delta,\alpha
from
Kausik et al. 2023
.
The following proposition, proved in Appendix
D.1
, shows the three-way relationship between these gaps:

Proposition 5.1
.

Define
p_{\max}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{k\in[K]}\max_{s,s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}p^{(k)}(s^{\prime}|s)
.
We then have the following:

(a)

p_{\max}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}\gtrsim\alpha\Delta^{2}
; (b)
p_{\max}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}+\sqrt{{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}/\pi_{\min}}\gtrsim\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}
;

(c)

under Assumption
2
,
\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}\gtrsim\alpha\Delta^{2}-{\mathcal{O}}((\sqrt{\eta_{\pi}}-1)^{2})
.

The inequality
(b)
holds up to uniform ergodicity contraction constants, see Appendix
D.1
for details. Note that due to our choice of
{\bm{W}}
(Eqn. (
5
)),
\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}
and the other gaps become comparable.
Indeed, for
\eta
-regular ergodic Markov chains with
\alpha\Delta^{2}\gtrsim(\sqrt{\eta_{\pi}}-1)^{2}
, we roughly have
\alpha\Delta^{2}\lesssim\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}\lesssim p_{\max}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}
.

Although our optimal clustering error rates depend only on
{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}
, ensuring the success of
\ell_{2}
-based spectral clustering in Stage I requires that
H\gtrsim\frac{1}{{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}}
. Note that
Kausik et al. 2023
’s algorithm also relies on a similar notion of
\ell_{2}
-separation, as reflected in their requirement
H\gtrsim\frac{1}{(\alpha\Delta^{2})^{3}}
. While our dependence on the separation gap is already clearly better, we make this comparison explicit by proving that the ratio between
\alpha\Delta^{2}
and
\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}
can be as large as
S
(see Appendix
D.2
for details).

Proposition 5.2
.

There exists an instance of
clustering in MMC
such that
\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}\asymp S\alpha\Delta^{2}\lesssim\frac{{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}}{S}
.

Intuitively,
Kausik et al. 2023
’s
\alpha\Delta^{2}
accounts for separation from a single “good” state, whereas our
\Delta_{\bm{W}}
aggregates separation information across all states.
Also, for uniform-like Markov chains where
p_{\max}\asymp\frac{1}{S}
, an additional gap of
S
pops up due to Proposition
5.1
(b).

It remains an interesting question whether the dependence of
H
on
\Delta_{\bm{W}}
is unavoidable or merely an artifact of our current algorithm design, which relies on
\ell_{2}
-based spectral clustering.
We believe that because the “natural” gap
{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}
requires knowledge of the transition kernels—which we do not assume—the use of
\Delta_{\bm{W}}
or a similarly weaker proxy is unavoidable.
Since spectral initialization is a standard Stage I for two-stage procedures
(
Lu and Zhou 2016
;
Gao et al. 2017
;
Yun and Proutière 2016
)
, we believe our dependency on
\Delta_{\bm{W}}
is optimal among
\ell_{2}
-based methods.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper investigates clustering in a mixture of ergodic Markov chains.
We derived an instance-specific lower bound on clustering error, which is, to our knowledge, the first of its kind in this literature.
We also proposed a computationally efficient, two-stage algorithm that delivers near-optimal error rates without requiring model-specific knowledge.
Among various technical novelties, our primary contribution is the design of Stage I, which introduces a new injective Euclidean embedding for ergodic Markov chains.
This novel embedding is crucial for achieving sharp concentration results and accurately clustering trajectories via spectral methods, reinforcing the growing body of literature on the efficacy of spectral algorithms in structured reinforcement learning environments
(
Jedra et al. 2023
;
Azizzadenesheli et al. 2016
;
Azizzadenesheli et al. 2017
;
Kausik et al. 2023
)
.

We present several future directions for this work.
First, our algorithm requires
{\mathcal{O}}(TS^{2})
space complexity, which is quite intensive.
Making the algorithm more space-efficient by considering streaming settings
(
Yun et al. 2014
)
and/or using tools from randomized linear algebra
(
Woodruff 2014
)
is important for large-scale applications.
It would also be interesting to explore the relationship between the distances considered here for Markov chains (KL divergence and our
L
-based
\ell_{2}
distance) and other information divergences beyond KL
(
Wang and Choi 2023
)
, as well as the recently studied bisimulation metric
(
Calo et al. 2024
;
Calo et al. 2025
)
.
Extending our algorithm to controlled Markov chains
(
Banerjee et al. 2025
;
Kausik et al. 2023
)
is another important direction, as having actions often presents unique challenges
(
Jedra et al. 2023
)
and naturally connects to latent MDPs
(
Kwon et al. 2021
;
Kwon et al. 2024b
;
Kwon et al. 2024a
)
with real-world applications like personalized RLHF
(
Park et al. 2024
)
.

Proof.

(Assume that
H>\frac{1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}}
)

Combining everything, we have that

{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}\left(\left\lVert p(\cdot|s)-\frac{\widehat{N}(s,\cdot)}{\widehat{N}(s)}\right\rVert_{2}>\varepsilon\right)\leq(H\pi(s)+1)\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}H\pi(s)}{2}\right)+2\exp\left(-\frac{H\pi(s)\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}}{104}\right),

and thus, with

, we have that

{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}\left(\left\lVert p(\cdot|s)-\frac{\widehat{N}(s,\cdot)}{\widehat{N}(s)}\right\rVert_{2}>\sqrt{\frac{2}{H\pi(s)}\log\frac{2(H\pi(s)+1)}{\delta}}\right)\leq\delta.

Here, we recall the following result that quantifies the price of non-stationarity due to the initial distribution

not necessarily being the stationary distribution

given

(27)

Combining everything.

(28)

As the exact constants for the

First, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{p}_{N}-p\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{p}_{N}(s)-p(s))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{Var}[\mathrm{Bin}(N,p(s))]

(

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}p(s)(1-p(s))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left(1-\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}p(s)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(1-\frac{1}{S}\right),

We now utilize the McDiarmid’s inequality
(
McDiarmid 1989
)
to turn this into a

For
{\bm{s}}_{N}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{N})
, let us define

\displaystyle f({\bm{s}}_{N})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\|\hat{p}_{N}-p\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{p}_{N}(s_{i})-p(s_{i}))^{2}}.

Now, let

for

and

be the empirical distribution using

.

Proof of Lemma
D.1
.

The second inequality is due to
Csiszar and Talata 2006
.
The first inequality is due to a
Stackoverflow post and user Ze-Nan Li’s answer
, whose proof we reproduce here for completeness.

Let us define
\eta(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{q(s)-p(s)}{p(s)}
and partition
{\mathcal{S}}
as follows:

{\mathcal{S}}=\underbrace{\left\{s\in{\mathcal{S}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\eta(s)>1\right\}}_{\triangleq{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}\dot{\cup}\underbrace{\left\{s\in{\mathcal{S}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\eta(s)\leq 1\right\}}_{\triangleq{\mathcal{S}}_{-}}.

(46)

Then it is easy to see that

(i)

For
s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}
,
1+\eta(s)\leq 2^{\eta(s)}=e^{\eta(s)\log 2}
, and

(ii)

For
s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{-}
,
1+\eta(s)\leq e^{\eta(s)-c\eta(s)^{2}}
, where
c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\log\frac{e}{2}
.

Then,

\displaystyle\KL(p,q)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}p(s)\log\frac{p(s)}{q(s)}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}p(s)\log(1+\eta(s))-\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{-}}p(s)\log(1+\eta(s))

\displaystyle\geq-\log 2\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}p(s)\eta(s)-\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{-}}p(s)(\eta(s)-c\eta(s)^{2})

\displaystyle=c\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}p(s)\eta(s)-\cancelto{0}{\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}p(s)\eta(s)}+c\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{-}}p(s)\eta(s)^{2}

\displaystyle=c\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}(q(s)-p(s))+c\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{-}}\frac{(q(s)-p(s))^{2}}{p(s)}

\displaystyle\geq c\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}q(s)\frac{q(s)-p(s)}{q(s)}+\frac{c}{p_{\max}}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{-}}(q(s)-p(s))^{2}.

Now, for
s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}
, we have that
0<\frac{q(s)-p(s)}{q(s)}\leq 1
, and thus,

\displaystyle\KL(p,q)

\displaystyle\geq c\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}q(s)\frac{q(s)-p(s)}{q(s)}+\frac{c}{p_{\max}}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{-}}(q(s)-p(s))^{2}

\displaystyle\geq c\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}q(s)\left(\frac{q(s)-p(s)}{q(s)}\right)^{2}+\frac{c}{p_{\max}}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}_{-}}(q(s)-p(s))^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{q_{\max}\vee p_{\max}}\underbrace{\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}(q(s)-p(s))^{2}}_{=L_{2}(p,q)}.

∎

Here, we provide the full statement of (b) and prove it:

Proposition D.1
.

We have

\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}\leq\min_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\left\{\frac{2p_{\max}}{\log\frac{e}{2}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}^{(k,k^{\prime})}+4H^{2}\left(\pi^{(k)},\pi^{(k^{\prime})}\right)\right\},

(47)

where

is the Hellinger distance:
H^{2}(p,q)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}(\sqrt{p(s)}-\sqrt{q(s)})^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Furthermore, we also have the following slightly more relaxed bound:

\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}\leq 7\left(p_{\max}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}+\left(\lceil\log_{\rho}M^{-1}\rceil+\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi_{\min}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}}\right),

(48)

where
\rho
and
M
are the contraction constants from the definition of uniformly ergodic Markov chains
8
8

8

Recall that the Markov chain with stationary distribution
\pi
and transition matrix
P
is uniformly ergodic if there exists
\rho\in(0,1)
and
M>0
such that
\max_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left\lVert P^{H}(s,\cdot),\pi\right\rVert_{1}\leq M\rho^{H}
for all
H\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
.

Proof.

For each
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sqrt{\pi^{(k)}(s)}p^{(k)}(\cdot|s)-\sqrt{\pi^{(k^{\prime})}(s)}p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sqrt{\pi^{(k)}(s)}\left(p^{(k)}(\cdot|s)-p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right)+\left(\sqrt{\pi^{(k)}(s)}-\sqrt{\pi^{(k^{\prime})}(s)}\right)p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi^{(k)}(s)\left\lVert p^{(k)}(\cdot|s)-p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\left(\sqrt{\pi^{(k)}(s)}-\sqrt{\pi^{(k^{\prime})}(s)}\right)^{2}\left\lVert p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi^{(k)}(s)\left\lVert p^{(k)}(\cdot|s)-p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\left(\sqrt{\pi^{(k)}(s)}-\sqrt{\pi^{(k^{\prime})}(s)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2p_{\max}}{\log\frac{e}{2}}\pi^{(k)}(s)\KL\left(p^{(k)}(\cdot|s),p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right)+2\left(\sqrt{\pi^{(k)}(s)}-\sqrt{\pi^{(k^{\prime})}(s)}\right)^{2}

(Lemma
D.1
)

We conclude the first part by summing over
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
and taking a minimum over
k\neq k^{\prime}
.

For the second part, we start by recalling Le Cam’s inequality

Tsybakov 2009
, Lemma 2.3)
:

H^{2}\left(\pi^{(k)},\pi^{(k^{\prime})}\right)\leq\mathsf{TV}\left(\pi^{(k)},\pi^{(k^{\prime})}\right).

We utilize the perturbation bound for Markov chains
(
Mitrophanov 2005
, Corollary 3.1)
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{TV}\left(\pi^{(k)},\pi^{(k^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\lceil\log_{\rho}M^{-1}\rceil+\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)\max_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mathsf{TV}\left(p^{(k)}(\cdot|s),p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\lceil\log_{\rho}M^{-1}\rceil+\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi_{\min}}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi^{(k)}(s)\KL\left(p^{(k)}(\cdot|s),p^{(k^{\prime})}(\cdot|s)\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\lceil\log_{\rho}M^{-1}\rceil+\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi_{\min}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}^{(k,k^{\prime})}}.

As

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{W}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 7\min_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\left\{p_{\max}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}^{(k,k^{\prime})}+\left(\lceil\log_{\rho}M^{-1}\rceil+\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi_{\min}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}^{(k,k^{\prime})}}\right\}

\displaystyle=7\left(p_{\max}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}+\left(\lceil\log_{\rho}M^{-1}\rceil+\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi_{\min}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\pi}}\right),

where the last equality follows from the fact that

.
