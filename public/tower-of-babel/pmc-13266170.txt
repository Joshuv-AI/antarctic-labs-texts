Preparedness of the Emergency Medical Services Educational System: Pandemic Barriers and Adaptations
Authors: Adrienne K. Kramer, Kim D. McKenna, Jonathan R. Powell, Juan March, William J. Leggio, Rebecca E. Cash, Ashish R. Panchal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe emergency medical services (EMS) educational infrastructure plays a critical role in maintaining a resilient and stable EMS workforce. Clear disaster preparedness strategies/initiatives are needed to maintain this pipeline during emergencies. Our objective was to build a basis for the EMS educational program’s disaster preparedness planning by identifying educational barriers and adaptations experienced through the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsWe held focus groups with EMS educational program directors (PDs) to identify educational barriers and the adaptations they used during the COVID-19 pandemic. PD participants discussed topics in small online groups of 3-6 PDs and 2 facilitators. Thematic analysis was performed to identify emerging themes. Initial coding of the transcribed conversations was performed with themes aggregated and categorized as barriers or adaptations.ResultsA total of 35 PDs were enrolled across 8 focus groups. Barriers and adaptations were grouped into 6 domains: regulatory, primary institution/program, faculty/educators, technology use, curriculum, and students. PDs and faculty noted increased workload, increased feelings of stress, and uncertainty in situations where online learning was not in place. New or enhanced technology implementation was essential to program continuation. Program, lab, and clinical structures changed substantially with a rapid increase in the use of simulation, scenarios, and distance learning to adapt to the lack of clinical and field sites.ConclusionAs EMS initial educational programs addressed the pandemic, significant barriers resulting in unique real-time adaptation were noted. To prepare for future educational interruptions, lessons learned from previous widespread system interruptions can be leveraged for informed educational system disaster planning.

The Bottom LineInitial emergency medical services education programs were heavily impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Program directors identified barriers it created, including regulatory challenges, lack of personal protective equipment, lab, field, and clinical experience disruptions, and student attrition. They also discovered adaptations to move forward and continue operating during this disruption that can be used as a guide for future emergency planning.

The Bottom Line

Initial emergency medical services education programs were heavily impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Program directors identified barriers it created, including regulatory challenges, lack of personal protective equipment, lab, field, and clinical experience disruptions, and student attrition. They also discovered adaptations to move forward and continue operating during this disruption that can be used as a guide for future emergency planning.

Introduction

Background

The emergency medical services (EMS) educational infrastructure in the United States is designed to produce entry-level clinicians who deliver competent prehospital care.1, 2, 3 Maintenance of this pipeline is critically important given the increasing burden of disease, estimated population increases, and the growth of prehospital clinicians needed to maintain population-level health.4 However, recent data have noted that in the face of known EMS workforce shortages there is a strong dependence on the entry of new clinicians to maintain a stable EMS workforce.5, 6, 7, 8, 9

Recognizing the critical role played by the EMS educational infrastructure, clear disaster preparedness strategies and initiatives are needed to maintain this pipeline during emergencies that could impact clinicians entering the workforce. Since January 2024, accreditation standards and guidelines for EMS educational programs require that program sponsors “have a preparedness plan that assures continuity of education services in the event of an unanticipated interruption.”10 Unfortunately, there is a paucity of research on how plans may be developed and on how EMS educational programs previously adapted to emergencies, particularly the COVID-19 pandemic, to maintain educational delivery. Understanding lessons learned by EMS programs may be critical to help inform future disaster preparedness strategies.

Importance

National organizations and stakeholders in the EMS community have recognized the need to better understand how EMS education was impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic (National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, 2020) to initiate pandemic and disaster preparedness initiatives.11 A national working group identified the top EMS educational research priorities during the COVID-19 pandemic as: prehospital internship access, impact of lack of field and clinical experience, student health and safety, and EMS educational program availability and accessibility.12 In addition, they identified the need to continue their initial work and prepare for future interruptions to EMS workforce development, program-specific resource needs, and lessons learned to evaluate effective response models.

Goals of This Investigation

The primary objective of this study was to build a basis for EMS educational program disaster preparedness planning by identifying educational barriers and adaptations experienced through the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Study Design

In depth, semistructured focus groups conducted with PDs from emergency medical technician (EMT)and paramedic educational programs identified barriers and adaptations collated using inductive thematic analysis. Participants consented to be included in the study, which was determined exempt by the Institutional Review Board at the American Institutes for Research.

PDs for both EMT and paramedic educational programs are responsible for all aspects of the educational program. Training occurs initially in classroom settings and transitions to clinical experiences with completion of the required EMS educational standards. EMT and paramedic educational program training lengths differ by certification level and associated scope of practice. EMT certification, in most states, requires completion of a state-approved EMT course (typically 120–150 hours) followed by completion of the National EMT Cognitive Examination as well as a state-approved psychomotor skills examination. Paramedic certification requires that the candidate is first certified as an EMT, completes an accredited paramedic course (typically 1200–1800 hours), and successfully completes the National EMS Certification examination or similar state-approved examinations. A key difference between these certifications is that paramedic programs are accredited by the Commission on Accreditation of Allied Health Education Programs after recommendation by the Committee on Accreditation of EMS Programs (CoAEMSP), whereas EMT programs are state-approved.13

Subject Selection, Data Collection, and Interviews

Focus groups were conducted with current EMS PDs from the National Certification Database.14 Participation was solicited at a National Registry Program Director Webinar that was available for all PDs to attend or view as a recording. CoAEMSP annual report data from 2020 contends that there were 612 paramedic programs, and the National Registry reports 2109 EMT programs that graduated students, forming the potential sampling frame of PDs from National Registry recruitment.15 To collect a diverse range of perspectives related to EMS program experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic, a purposeful process selected participants from diverse gender, program sizes (graduates/year), institutional types (eg, educational, hospital, private defined by CoAEMSP),13 geographic areas (defined by the National Association of EMS Officials16), and years of teaching experience. Prior research contends there are structural differences in outcomes (programmatic certification pass rates, attrition) based on these considerations; however, it was unknown how these may manifest in this evaluation until focus groups were conducted given that prior research is quantitative not qualitative.17, 18, 19

Focus groups were conducted across 2022 to give time for informed reporting surrounding not only pandemic operations but also the reflection on impact of actions on student outcomes. Focus groups continued until thematic saturation was achieved, defined as when the same themes are generated repeatedly rather than a predetermined number of focus groups or participants. EMT educational program focus groups were conducted first until thematic saturation followed by paramedic educational program focus groups with the concept that many of the programmatic challenges expressed by EMT educational programs may also be experienced by the more well-funded and structured paramedic educational programs.

An interview guide (Supplementary Appendix I) was developed by a group of EMS experts (RC, WL, WM, JM, AK, AP, JP) and piloted/cognitively debriefed with a separate group of 5 educators before the study began to ensure content validity. Focus group discussions were facilitated by 2 of the authors, with one serving as moderator (KM, EMS educator, female) and the other as facilitator (AK or JP, fire/EMS researchers, female and male), who kept detailed notes and recorded participant reactions in each session. Upon receiving their electronic invitation to the focus group containing overall goals of the research and researchers, participants were asked to provide information regarding their demographic and EMS educational program information. No previous relationships existed between research teams and participants. Moderator and facilitator began each discussion with introductions for visibility into interviewer characteristics.

Focus groups lasted 60 minutes; audio and video of each focus group was recorded with verbal participant permission via Zoom® Software (version 5.7.6 [1320]) and transcribed verbatim using Otter.ai (Otter.ai, 2022) to facilitate analysis. No additional parties were present, no field notes taken, nor were repeat interviews performed.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis to identify the dominant PD themes.20 Author expertise in EMS education, medical direction, and the PD role enabled informed review of themes provided by participants. Transcript data were uploaded into a shared, blinded file, and systematic initial coding was independently performed by 2 authors (KM, JP) to identify themes via an open inductive process without prior informed theme generation. Initial codes were purposely developed broadly but categorized as barriers and adaptations. Subsequently, 2 authors (AP, RC) evaluated the coded files for accuracy and conducted analysis to review patterns across the data set and collate initial inductive domains into overarching themes. Themes were refined and combined to reflect the overall data. Thematic analysis was reviewed by all authors and disputes resolved through author group discussion led by code evaluators (AP, RC) among independent code generators (KM, JP). Standardized consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research were followed.

For both EMT and paramedic educational programs, actions by regulatory authorities made program operations difficult in some situations and easier in others (Table 2 and 3). PDs found they were able to adapt to evolving certification requirements (eg, provisional certification process) while also being impacted by state regulatory changes (eg, suspension of in-person classes). Communication was often cited as a barrier, but then also featured as an adaptation when feedback from educational programs was presented.

The primary institution/program was often identified as a barrier (Table 2 and 3). Instructor staffing was a problem in several programs, with instructors, who were also practicing EMS clinicians, transitioning to emergency response rather than being available for teaching. Programs experienced delays or stoppages that interfered with program start and stop dates, often leading to structural delays with graduations or concurrent classes. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) shortages also impacted their ability to continue teaching, with institutions affiliated with hospitals putting out calls for PPE donations. Paramedic programs specifically felt pressure to change standards for enrollment and graduation during the pandemic. They further reported perceived differential restrictions based on their program type (ie, private vs academic), with private and off-campus programs reporting more flexibility than those tied to an academic institution. The move to remote learning also increased competition between regionally homogenous programs for student enrollment. Positively, some paramedic PDs reported that there was increased medical director involvement when programs partnered with local simulation labs, also used for physician education, to shore up lack of clinical opportunities.

Both EMT and paramedic programs reported common barriers related to their educators and faculty (Tables 2 and 3). PD stress was a concern that was expressed and characterized, resulting from increased workload, challenges constantly shifting to match operational realities, and increasing risk of personal and family exposure with return of in-person activities. Participants found peer support to be very helpful, with peer educator support groups organized at the local and state levels, allowing educators to feel a sense of community and belonging. Some of these groups were noted to have persisted past the height of the COVID-19 pandemic.

However, EMT PDs indicated there was worse continuity of instruction due to potential COVID-19 exposures or illness, again noting the dual nature of their faculty being active clinicians. PDs noted that they were able to identify other educational resources to aid their instruction, with heavy reliance on software and simulation that allowed for high-fidelity replacement for lost clinical activities. One paramedic PD reported an increase in allocation for educator/faculty staffing.

Both the availability of technology and the ability to adapt its use within programs was essential to program continuation (Tables 2 and 3). Many creative technology innovations were described, but others described the need for technology access to be enhanced for equitable opportunities for education. In some cases, technology was dependent on the instructor's comfort level across programs and even across classes within programs. Several PDs noted the need to remain flexible among technological offerings throughout the pandemic, allowing students to use modalities that fit their needs (eg, inperson and electronic offerings available at the same time within the same class). This model was also noted to be effective for traditional student issues that may restrict in-person learning (eg, childcare and sickness).

PDs identified barriers and adaptations that were distributed across curricula, including in laboratory settings, didactic teaching, and field and clinical experiences. Laboratory disruptions were acknowledged by all PDs with changes made to reduce infection potential (eg, fixed group and/or time rotations) and increase potential for tracing should infection occur (Tables 2 and 3). Even with these adaptations to curriculum delivery, PDs identified skills degradation that many associated with the length of program closure or lack of hands-on activities. The didactic portions of educational training programs also provided challenges (Tables 2 and 3). Several PDs noted that they had to administer tests online without robust security measures. These concerns were somewhat mitigated with formal contracts for online assessments, but the strength of this impact was anecdotal.

Field and clinical experiences suffered disruptions that required adaptations by all the programs (Tables 2 and 3). Many PDs described complete shutdowns of clinical operations, removing all hands-on patient contact from student experiences. Others described selective opportunities, in that rotation sites would allow rotations, if students were concurrently employed at that site. Some clinical rotation opportunities have yet to return, with paramedic programs that also housed EMT programs noting EMT access is still not reinstated. To overcome these barriers, the programs found expanded opportunities for placement at new clinical sites, but these relationships took time to find and were not readily available during the height of interruption.

There were common barriers and adaptations also found in the student domain (Tables 2 and 3). A paramedic PD noted that they expected students to be savvier with online programs, dispelling a common assumption of student technological expertise. EMT PDs reported worse continuity of learning due to COVID exposures or illness. Paramedic program PDs noted that students were graduating with less clinical experience but more simulation experience. They also noted that vaccine uncertainty was a barrier for placement at clinical sites.

A common theme was a lack of student confidence (Table 2, Table 3), with PDs recalling both students and employers questioning their readiness for competent, entry-level practice. Both EMT and paramedic programs’ enrollment numbers varied, with some experiencing increased enrollment and others decreased enrollment. EMT programs reported that there were more students leaving the program due to professional reasons (eg, activation with military, employer requirements) or personal reasons (eg, child/family care, online learning difficulty, exposure risk). Health worries also contributed to attrition, yet EMT PDs did note increased student motivation to join the EMS profession. Paramedic PDs generally remarked that their learners were performing worse on certification testing and not retesting. However, they found there were more EMS jobs available for graduating paramedic students.

Limitations

This study was limited to a sample of EMT and paramedic PDs at initial educational programs and did not include Advanced EMT PDs, EMS students, faculty, or medical directors. Results were not returned to participants to confirm coding to original intent. Thus, it is possible that some barriers/adaptations were not identified due to selection bias and that a differently constituted group may come to different conclusions, although we attempted to mitigate this through structured facilitation, consistent interview script, and a diverse group of PDs. These conclusions may also differ if the PD sample had increased demographic and program diversity, potentially providing different perspectives surrounding the impact of the pandemic on education through a nondominant lens. Due to the inherent heterogeneity of programs, every barrier and adaptation may not be applicable to all programs, if there is an emergency event disruption. Further, using a National Registry webinar to recruit participants may bias these results to nationally active PDs. This may skew results away from programs without time or resources for that national participation. Lastly, all discussions occurred remotely via Zoom®, and virtual group dynamics may have impacted willingness to discuss program-specific challenges.

Discussion

This evaluation identified educational barriers and adaptations experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic to build a basis for EMS educational program preparedness. The pandemic created significant disruptions at all levels of EMS initial education delivery, due to programs facing diverse barriers, of which almost all resulted in increased simulation replacing clinical experiences. As shown in prior research on EMS continuing education, this study suggests that initial EMS education during the pandemic had an increasing trend in the use of online education/distributed learning.21 In addition, this study identifies the importance of programs needing to preplan by having established professional networks and contingencies to shift educational delivery methods if necessitated by an emerging crisis.

Research related to health care education barriers and adaptations due to the pandemic have been limited to undergraduate and graduate medical education22, 23, 24, 25, 26 and other allied health educational programs.27, 28, 29, 30, 31 One study explored the work/life impact of the pandemic on nurses and paramedic students32 and another the effects on EMS continuing education,21 but none has examined the effects of the pandemic on initial EMS education strategies or identified barriers and adaptations to enhance preparedness.

Despite the barriers identified, most programs were able carry on perhaps because, as one EMT PD said, “We are EMS…adaptable.” A paramedic PD elaborated further, “it's been…very apparent that there are many different strategies that we can utilize to help make this work, even in those difficult times. And…our backgrounds of thinking on our feet, flexibility and how we worked as a clinician in our environment, probably gave us an advantage in being able to adapt and overcome much quicker than some of the institutions.” Those programs that had technology infrastructure or experience with distance education were more likely to report smoother transitions. “We already had the LMS <learning management system>, we already had the MS testing, we already had components in place…I was uncomfortable, but we didn't struggle as much as some of the others…we had the bones” (EMT PD). The programs’ adaptations to these challenges presented opportunities that are, in some cases, being carried forward. A paramedic PD noted, “we had never utilized our own School of Healthcare SIM Lab, which is typically excluded us. That's the nurses’ area. And we established some relationships there during COVID” and “a state EMS association…paid for my students to go to the SIM lab at the School of Medicine.”

Further, at the time of the focus groups, paramedic PDs reported continuing difficulty with clinical placements, which have been identified as a need prior to pandemic onset. This need underscores the priority for building and maintaining strong EMS educational programs, to serve as a base in the light of emerging system threats. Paired with qualitative data showing little impact on program outcomes, relative to prepandemic comparisons, this evaluation can assist educational PDs and relevant stakeholders to prepare for future system interruptions.15

In conclusion, we found the COVID-19 pandemic created significant barriers in initial EMS educational program administration and delivery among those active in the National Registry database, resulting in unique solutions being identified for real-time adaptation. As EMS educational programs prepare for future educational interruptions, lessons learned from previous widespread system interruptions can be leveraged for informed system planning.
