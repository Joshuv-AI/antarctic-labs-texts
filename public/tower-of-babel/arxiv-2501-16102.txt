Improved estimates of statistical properties in some non-uniformly hyperbolic dynamical systems
Péter Bálint, Ábel Komálovics

Abstract
Building upon previous works by Young, Chernov-Zhang and Bruin-Melbourne-Terhesiu, we present a general scheme to improve bounds on the statistical properties (in particular, decay of correlations, and rates in the almost sure invariant principle) for a class of non-uniformly hyperbolic dynamical systems. Specifically, for systems with polynomial, yet summable mixing rates, our method removes logarithmic factors of earlier arguments, resulting in essentially optimal bounds. Applications include Wojtkowski’s system of two falling balls, dispersing billiards with flat points and Bunimovich’s flower-shaped billiard tables.

Introduction

In this paper we consider statistical properties for a class of non-uniformly hyperbolic dynamical systems. Our primary motivation is to study some billiard-like systems with polynomial, yet summable mixing rates (see Section
5
for the specific examples) for which we manage to improve upon previous bounds on the rates of correlation decay and in the almost sure invariance. Nevertheless, we present a general scheme in the hope that it can be applied to many other models. This scheme reformulates and somewhat extends ideas of Chernov-Zhang (

Let us comment briefly on some previous results – it is important to emphasize that we do not aim for a complete survey of the rich literature on this subject, just mention works that are directly related to our approach. In her seminal papers
[
23
,
24

Young introduced a tower method which has turned out to be particularly efficient for studying statistical properties of non-uniformly hyperbolic systems. When modeling a non-uniformly hyperbolic system with a Young tower, a tower map is constructed that is either isomorphic, or is an extension of the studied dynamical system. Also, there exist one-sided and two-sided versions of Young towers appropriate for modeling non-invertible and invertible systems, respectively. Nonetheless, in all cases, the return process to the base of the tower is of Markovian character, and the statistics of the return times imply upper bounds on the mixing rates of the system with respect to a natural invariant probability measure.

Our main goal in this paper is to

Generality of the exposition.
As mentioned above, on top of the Chernov-Markarian-Zhang structure framework, some additional information are needed to show that the rates of the Young tower return process agree with the rates of the geometric return process. These information can be captured by the evolution properties of a class of invariant curves, and probability measures supported on these curves, which will be referred to as standard pairs. Standard pairs were introduced in the context of hyperbolic biliards by Chernov and Dolgopyat (see eg.
[
13
, Section 7.4]
) and have been widely used to study their statistical properties. Here we include only the amount of information on standard pairs that is needed for our exposition to work – we do not even explicitly assume full hyperbolicity of the dynamics – in the hope that our conditions, expressed in (C1)-(C5) of Section
2
, are flexible enough to be applied to many other situations.
We aim for generality also at the level of the decay rates treated. Indeed, the return time statistics does not have to be exactly of the form
\displaystyle n^{-\alpha}
, we can handle rates of the form
\displaystyle r(n)
which is an arbitrary regularly varying function of index
\displaystyle-\alpha

\displaystyle\alpha>0

Exact correlation asymptotics.
Concerning non-uniformly hyperbolic systems with polynomial decay of correlations, an important problem is to establish, on top of the upper bounds of

In the present paper we fill this gap and prove exact correlation asymptotics for such systems, which includes in particular the examples of Section

[

Rates in the almost sure invariance principle.
As mentioned above, in our main examples, although correlations decay polynomially, by summability of the rates, the Birkhoff sums obey a standard central limit theorem (CLT). Recently there has been much progress about the almost sure invariance principle (ASIP), a strengthening of the CLT stating that the process associated to the Birkhoff sums can be simultaneously realized and well approximated by Brownian motion, see
[
14
]
and references therein. In particular, in
[
14
]
it is proved that for systems modeled by Young towers, the rate of this approximation – that is, the rate of the ASIP – can be estimated in terms of the tails of the return times to the base of the tower. By better estimates on the return times, we also improve upon the rates of the ASIP specifically for the examples of Section
5
, as conjectured in
[
14
, Remark 3.3]
.

3.1
Necessary definitions

Decay rates.
To present our scheme for studying subexponential decay of correlations, first we specify the class of decay rates that our method can handle.

As we will study upper and lower bounds on the mixing rates, we want the bounding functions to have some structure. We will use the following class of functions, which have a substantial literature, see
[
5
]
for example.

Definition 2
.

A function
\displaystyle r:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is regularly varying with index
\displaystyle-\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, if

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{r(\lambda x)}{r(x)}=\lambda^{-\alpha}

holds for all
\displaystyle\lambda>0

Notations on hitting and return times.

We define the hitting time of
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}
and the first return time of
\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}
as follows.

Definition 3
.

For an
\displaystyle\hat{x}\in\hat{\Delta}

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}
is

h(\hat{x},\hat{F},\Delta_{0})=\min\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\;\left|\;\hat{F}^{n}\hat{x}\in\Delta_{0}\right.\right\}.

For
\displaystyle x\in\Delta

h(x,F,\Delta_{0})=\min\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\;\left|\;F^{n}x\in\Delta_{0}\right.\right\}.

Also,

R(\hat{x},F,\hat{\Delta})=R(\pi(\hat{x})).

With these functions, we can define the sets we need in order to state our main result.

Definition 4
.

D_{n}=\left\{\hat{x}\in\hat{\Delta}\;\left|\;h(\hat{x},\hat{F},\Delta_{0})>n\right.\right\}

H_{n}=\left\{\hat{x}\in\hat{\Delta}\;\left|\;R(\hat{x},F,\hat{\Delta})>n\right.\right\}

\displaystyle\rho<1

We call
\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{\Delta}}(H_{n})

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}

\displaystyle\hat{M}

\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A_{n})

satisfy (C1)-(C5). Then, for any
\displaystyle b>0
there exists a
\displaystyle q<1
and a
\displaystyle\delta>0
such that

\mu_{\hat{M}}(\widehat{R}_{k})\ll k^{-\delta}\mu_{\hat{M}}(R_{k}).

(3.3)

Heuristically, this means that for any point
\displaystyle\hat{x}
the probability, that before
\displaystyle b\log n
iterations of
\displaystyle\hat{F}
we find another point the return time of which is comparable with that of
\displaystyle\hat{x}
, is small. Let us mention that relations analogous to (
3.3
) are sometimes included among the assumptions of other works (see eg. assumption (H3’) in
[
22
]
) while here we derive this property from our conditions (C1)-(C5).

Recall, that for
\displaystyle x\in A_{n}\setminus B_{n,b}
,
\displaystyle I(x)>\frac{n}{b\log n}=n^{\prime}
denotes the length of the longest excursion of
\displaystyle x
before time
\displaystyle n
.
We separate two main cases.

If
\displaystyle I(x)\leq\frac{n}{10}
, then the second longest excursion must be at least
\displaystyle\frac{9}{10}\frac{h(x,F,\Delta_{0})}{b\log n}>\frac{9}{10}\frac{n}{b\log n}=\frac{9}{10}n^{\prime}>(n^{\prime})^{q}
long for any
\displaystyle q<1
and
\displaystyle n
large enough, meaning

\left\{x\in A_{n}\setminus B_{n,b}\;\left|\;I(x)\leq\frac{n}{10}\right.\right\}\subset\widehat{H}_{n^{\prime}}.

Hence, by Proposition
7

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{\Delta}}\left\{x\in A_{n}\setminus B_{n,b}\;\left|\;I(x)\leq\frac{n}{10}\right.\right\}\ll

\displaystyle\displaystyle(n^{\prime})^{-\delta}r(n^{\prime})=\left(\frac{n}{b\log n}\right)^{-\delta}r\left(\frac{n}{b\log n}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{n}{b\log n}\right)^{-\delta}\left(\frac{n}{b\log n}\right)^{-\alpha+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\displaystyle n^{-\delta+2\varepsilon}(\log n)^{\delta+\alpha-\varepsilon}r(n)

holds for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
. If we choose
\displaystyle\varepsilon<\delta/2
, we get

\mu_{\hat{\Delta}}\left\{x\in A_{n}\setminus B_{n,b}\;\left|\;I(x)\leq\frac{n}{10}\right.\right\}\ll r(n).

If
\displaystyle I(x)>\frac{n}{10}
, then we will use the following argument from the proof of
[
2
, Proposition 9.7]
.
For any
\displaystyle(x,0)\in\Delta_{0}
let
\displaystyle(x,l_{1}(x))\in\hat{\Delta}
be the starting point of the longest excursion, meaning
\displaystyle I(x,0)=R(x,l_{1}(x))
.
Then,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta_{0}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\{p\in A_{n}\;\left|\;I(p)>\frac{n}{10}\right.\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\asymp

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{\Delta}}\left\{(x,0)\in A_{n}\;\left|\;R(\hat{T}^{l_{1}(x)}x)>\frac{n}{10}\right.\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{\Delta}}\left\{(x,l_{1}(x))\in\hat{\Delta}\;\left|\;R(\hat{T}^{l_{1}(x)}x)>\frac{n}{10}\right.\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{\Delta}}\left\{(x,l_{1}(x))\in\hat{\Delta}\;\left|\;R\circ\pi(x,l_{1}(x))>\frac{n}{10}\right.\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{\Delta}}\left\{p\in\hat{\Delta}\;\left|\;R\circ\pi(p)>\frac{n}{10}\right.\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{\Delta}}(H_{\frac{n}{10}})\ll r(n/10)\ll r(n).

Proof of Proposition
7
.

We want to prove

\frac{\mu_{\hat{M}}(\widehat{R}_{k})}{\mu_{\hat{M}}\left(R_{k}\right)}\ll k^{-\delta},

for some
\displaystyle\delta>0
where

\widehat{R}_{k}=\left\{\hat{x}\in R_{k}\;\left|\;\exists m\leq b\log k:\;\hat{T}^{m}\hat{x}\in\pi H_{k^{q}}\right.\right\}=\bigcup_{m\leq b\log k}R_{k}\cap\hat{T}^{-m}\pi H_{k^{q}}.

It is enough to show that

\mu_{\hat{M}}\left(R_{k}\cap\hat{T}^{-m}\pi H_{k^{q}}\right)\ll k^{-\delta^{\prime}}\mu_{\hat{M}}\left(R_{k}\right)

(3.4)

holds with some
\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}>0
for all
\displaystyle m\leq b\log k
. Indeed, summation on
\displaystyle m
then gives the statement of the Proposition for any
\displaystyle\delta>0
with
\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}>\delta>0
, as the number of terms is
\displaystyle b\log k\ll k^{\delta^{\prime}-\delta}
for all such
\displaystyle\delta
.

To prove (
3.4
), we fix some
\displaystyle m\leq b\log k
and compute

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{\hat{M}}\left(R_{k}\cap\hat{T}^{-m}\pi H_{k^{q}}\right)}{\mu_{\hat{M}}(R_{k})}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{\hat{M}}\left(\hat{T}^{-m}\left(\hat{T}^{m}R_{k}\cap\pi H_{k^{q}}\right)\right)}{\mu_{\hat{M}}(\hat{T}^{-m}\hat{T}^{m}R_{k})}=\mu_{\hat{M}}\left(\pi H_{k^{q}}\;|\;\hat{T}^{m}R_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{M}}\Big(\bigcup_{l\geq k^{q}}R_{l}\;\left|\;\hat{T}^{m}R_{k}\right.\Big).

(3.5)

We may regard the conditional measure
\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{M}}|_{R_{k}}
and its push-forward by
\displaystyle\hat{T}^{m}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{M}}|_{\hat{T}^{m}R_{k}}
as standard families
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{0}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{m}
, respectively. Hence, we can estimate the conditional probability in (
3.5
) by using the unstable curves
\displaystyle\gamma
included in the standard family
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{m}
.
The measures
\displaystyle\mu_{\gamma}
on these unstable curves are uniformly equivalent to the Lebesgue (arclength) measure
\displaystyle m_{\gamma}
on
\displaystyle\gamma
, for each standard pair
\displaystyle(\gamma,\mu_{\gamma})
. Hence, we only have to compare the Lebesgue measures of
\displaystyle\gamma\cap(R_{l}\cap\hat{T}^{m}R_{k})
and
\displaystyle\gamma\cap\hat{T}^{m}R_{k}
.
Then, integration with respect to the factor measure
\displaystyle\lambda=\lambda_{\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{m}}
will provide an estimate of (
3.5
).
By our assumption (C4), the unstable size of
\displaystyle R_{l}
is uniformly bounded from above, which gives
\displaystyle\gamma\cap(R_{l}\cap\hat{T}^{m}R_{k})\ll l^{-t}
. Thus, for any
\displaystyle\gamma_{i}\in\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{m}

\frac{m_{\gamma_{i}}(\gamma_{i}\cap(R_{l}\cap\hat{T}^{m}R_{k}))}{m_{\gamma_{i}}(\hat{T}^{m}R_{k})}\ll\frac{l^{-t}}{m_{\gamma_{i}}(\hat{T}^{m}R_{k})}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{\hat{M}}\Big(\bigcup_{l\geq k^{q}}R_{l}\;\left|\;\hat{T}^{m}R_{k}\right.\Big)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{l\geq k^{q}}\left(\int_{I}\frac{m_{\gamma_{i}}(\gamma_{i}\cap(R_{l}\cap\hat{T}^{m}R_{k}))}{m_{\gamma_{i}}(\hat{T}^{m}R_{k})}\operatorname{d}\!\lambda(i)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{l\geq k^{q}}\left(\,l^{-t}\int_{I}(m_{\gamma_{i}}(\hat{T}^{m}R_{k}))^{-1}\operatorname{d}\!\lambda(i)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\sum_{l\geq k^{q}}\left(l^{-t}\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{m})\right)\ll\frac{\sum_{l\geq k^{q}}l^{-t}}{k^{p-t}}\ll\frac{k^{q(1-t)}}{k^{p-t}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle k^{(q-1)(1-t)+1-p}.

Here
\displaystyle q-1
,
\displaystyle 1-p
and
\displaystyle 1-t
are all negative. Yet, by choosing
\displaystyle q
sufficiently close to
\displaystyle 1
, in particular for
\displaystyle 1-\frac{p-1}{t-1}<q<1
, the above computation gives an estimate
\displaystyle\ll k^{-\delta^{\prime}}
on (
3.5
), thus providing (
3.4
) and completing the proof of Proposition
7
.

Corollaries and applications

4.1
Upper bound on the decay of correlation for general obervables

In Sections
4.1
and
4.2
we take a look at the decay of correlations of observables and ways of utilizing Theorem
5
to estimate it.
With this in mind, let us introduce the notations of the following sections.

For functions
\displaystyle f,g\in L^{1}(M)
, let

C_{T}(f,g,n)=\int_{M}f\cdot(g\circ T^{n})d\mu-\int_{M}fd\mu\cdot\int_{M}gd\mu

denote the correlation of
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle g
at time
\displaystyle n
.

It is well konwn that
\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A_{n})
gives an upper bound on the rate of decay correlations in
\displaystyle n
for sufficiently regular observables. Let us recall the standard notion of Hölder continuity.

Definition 8
.

Let
\displaystyle(M,d)
be a bounded metric space equipped with a Borel probability measure
\displaystyle\mu
. Fix
\displaystyle\eta\in]0,1[
.
For a function
\displaystyle f\in L^{\infty}(M)
define

||f||_{\eta}=|f|_{\infty}+|f|_{\eta},\quad|f|_{\eta}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in M\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d(x,y)^{\eta}}.

\displaystyle f
is Hölder continuous with exponent
\displaystyle\eta
if its Hölder norm,
\displaystyle||f||_{\eta}
is finite.

As in
[
7
]
, we consider the (more general) class of dynamically Hölder continuous functions. Recall from Appendix
A.2
, or
[
7
]
that if
\displaystyle T:M\to M
has a two-sided Chernov-Markarian-Zhang structure, then there is a separation time function
\displaystyle s
on
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}
. In the one-sided case it is separating, but in the two-sided case, given a non-trivial factor map
\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}
, it’s lift does not separate the points of the
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}
.

Definition 9
.

Suppose
\displaystyle T:M\rightarrow M
admits a two-sided Chernov-Markarian-Zhang structure, as in (C1). Fix
\displaystyle\vartheta<1
. For a function
\displaystyle f:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
define

||f||_{\mathcal{H}}=|f|_{\infty}+|f|_{\mathcal{H}},\quad|f|_{\mathcal{H}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Delta_{0}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\sup_{0\leq k<h(x)}\frac{|f(F^{k}x)-f(F^{k}y)|}{d(x,y)+\vartheta^{s(x,y)}}.

We say that f is dynamically Hölder, if
\displaystyle||f||_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty
and denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(M)
the space of such observables.
Write
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\hat{M})=\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}(M)\;\left|\;\operatorname{supp}f\subset\hat{M}\right.\right\}
.

The authors of
[
7
]
give sufficient conditions for
\displaystyle T:M\rightarrow M
to satisfy in order for the Hölder observables to be dynamically Hölder as well.

Proposition 10
.

[
7
, Proposition 7.3]

Let
\displaystyle\eta\in(0,1]
and let
\displaystyle d_{0}
be a bounded metric on
\displaystyle M
. Let
\displaystyle C^{\eta}(M)
be the space of observables that are
\displaystyle\eta
-Hölder with respect to
\displaystyle d_{0}
. Suppose that there exist
\displaystyle K>0
,
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

d_{0}(T^{\ell}x,T^{\ell}y)\leq K\big(d_{0}(x,y)+\gamma_{0}^{s(x,y)}\big)

for all
\displaystyle x,y\in\Delta_{0}
,
\displaystyle 0\leq\ell<h(x,T,\Delta_{0})
.

Then
\displaystyle C^{\eta}(M)\subset\mathcal{H}(M)
where we may choose any
\displaystyle\theta\in[\gamma_{0}^{\eta},1)
and
\displaystyle d=d_{0}^{\eta^{\prime}}
for any
\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}\in(0,\eta]
.

Hence, by inequality (
2.1
) assumed in condition (C1), Hölder observables with respect to the metric
\displaystyle d(x,y)
are also dynamically Hölder for all systems satisfying (C1).

Young proved in
[
24
, Theorem 3]
that if a one-sided Young tower
\displaystyle(\Delta^{\prime},F^{\prime},\mu_{\Delta^{\prime}})
satisfies
\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A_{n})\ll n^{-\alpha}
for some
\displaystyle\alpha>1
, then

|C_{F}(f,g,n)|\ll\sum_{k\geq n}k^{-\alpha}(\ll n^{-\alpha+1})

holds for all Hölder observables
\displaystyle f,g\in C^{\eta}(\Delta^{\prime})
.

[
16
, Theorem 2.10]
proves that the previous bound also holds for two-sided Young towers and Hölder observables. As noted in
[
7
, Section 7.2]
,
the proof works for dynamically Hölder observables as well. Indeed, the only statement that relies on the properties of the observables is
[
16
, Proposition 5.3]
, which holds in the dynamically Hölder case as well.
Moreover, here we argue that the proof also works if, instead an exact polynomial upper bound
\displaystyle n^{-\beta}
rate, we have an arbitrary regularly varying function
\displaystyle r(n)
with with index
\displaystyle-\alpha<-1
, see the statement of Theorem
12
below.
Indeed, the only two places where the authors rely on the attributes of the bounding function
\displaystyle n^{-\beta}
are the proofs of
[
16
, Theorem 2.10]
and
[
16
, Proposition 4.10]
(on which the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 2.10]
relies).
There, the only relevant property of polynomial functions - besides summability - is the fact that there is a
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\sum_{m\geq n}r\left(\frac{m}{k}\right)\leq k^{\gamma}\sum_{m\geq n}r(m)\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}

for all
\displaystyle n
large enough.
This holds for any regularly varying function
\displaystyle r
with index
\displaystyle-\alpha<-1
, since it is true for every element of the sum.

Hence, we get that
[
16
, Theorem 2.10]
also holds for dynamically Hölder observables and regularly varying bounding functions with index
\displaystyle-\alpha<-1
. Theorem
12
will also use the following simple Lemma (proof included in Appendix
A.3
).

Lemma 11
.

If
\displaystyle r:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a non-increasing regularly varying function with parameter
\displaystyle-\alpha<-1
, then

\sum_{k\geq n}r(k)\asymp\int_{n}^{\infty}r(x)\operatorname{d}x.

Altogether, we get the following result.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\displaystyle T:M\rightarrow M
satisfy (C1)-(C5). If

\mu_{\hat{\Delta}}(H_{n})\ll r(n)

holds for some regularly varying function
\displaystyle r
with index
\displaystyle-\alpha<-1
, then

|C_{T}(f,g,n)|\ll\sum_{k\geq n}r(k)\asymp\int_{n}^{\infty}r(x)\operatorname{d}x

for all
\displaystyle f,g\in\mathcal{H}(M)
.

4.2
Asymptotic bounds on the decay of correlation for special observables

If we restrict the support of the observables to the exponentially mixing
\displaystyle\hat{M}
, we can get asymptotic bounds on the correlations as well.

Recall from Appendix
A.2
, that

\overline{h}=\int_{\Delta_{0}}h\operatorname{d}\!\mu_{\Delta_{0}}.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\displaystyle T:M\rightarrow M
satisfy (C1)-(C5). If

\mu_{\hat{M}}(H_{n})\asymp r(n)

holds for some regularly varying function
\displaystyle r
with index
\displaystyle-\alpha<-1

(a)

|C_{T}(f,g,n)|\asymp\overline{h}\sum_{k\geq n}\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A_{k})\left|\int_{M}f\operatorname{d}\!\mu\int_{M}g\operatorname{d}\!\mu\right|\asymp\sum_{k\geq n}r(k)\asymp\int_{n}^{\infty}r(x)\operatorname{d}x

for all
\displaystyle f,g\in\mathcal{H}(\hat{M})
with
\displaystyle\int_{M}f\operatorname{d}\!\mu\int_{M}g\operatorname{d}\!\mu\neq 0
.

(b)

For any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
,

|C_{T}(f,g,n)|\ll||f||_{\mathcal{H}}||g||_{\mathcal{H}}n^{-\alpha+\varepsilon}\log n

holds for all
\displaystyle f,g\in\mathcal{H}(\hat{M})
with
\displaystyle\int_{M}f\operatorname{d}\!\mu=0
.

We included the
\displaystyle\log n
factor in
\displaystyle(b)
, since if we have a function
\displaystyle r
such that (
3.2
) holds with
\displaystyle-\alpha
instead of
\displaystyle-\alpha+\varepsilon
, then actually,

|C_{T}(f,g,n)|\ll||f||_{\mathcal{H}}||g||_{\mathcal{H}}n^{-\alpha}\log n

holds true.

Proof.

By Theorem
5
,
\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A_{n})\asymp r(n)
holds.
Since
\displaystyle f
has an index
\displaystyle-\alpha<-1
, we know that for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
,
\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A_{n})\asymp r(n)\ll n^{-\alpha+\varepsilon}
.
By
[
7
, Theorem 7.4
\displaystyle(a)
]
and
[
7
, Proposition 3.2]
,

\left|C_{T}(f,g,n)-\overline{h}\sum_{k\geq n}\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A_{k})\int_{M}f\operatorname{d}\!\mu\int_{M}g\operatorname{d}\!\mu\right|\ll||f||_{\mathcal{H}}||g||_{\mathcal{H}}(n^{-\alpha+\varepsilon}\log n+\zeta_{\alpha-\varepsilon}(n))

(4.1)

holds for all
\displaystyle f,g\in\mathcal{H}(\hat{M})
, where

\zeta_{a}(n)=\begin{cases}n^{-a}&a>2\\
n^{-2}\log n&a=2\\
n^{-2(a-1)}&1<a<2\end{cases}.

From Lemma
11
, it is clear that

\sum_{k\geq n}\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A_{k})\asymp\sum_{k\geq n}r(k)\asymp\int_{n}^{\infty}r(x)\operatorname{d}x\gg n^{-\alpha-\varepsilon+1}

Thus, in order to prove our statement, it is sufficient to check that both terms on the right side of (
4.1
) are decaying faster than
\displaystyle n^{-\alpha-\varepsilon+1}
. Let us take
\displaystyle\varepsilon<1/2
. Then,
apparently

n^{-\alpha+\varepsilon}\log n\ll n^{-\alpha-\varepsilon+1},

thus we only have to take a closer look at
\displaystyle\zeta_{a}
. It is easy to check that

\zeta_{\alpha-\varepsilon}(n)\ll n^{-\alpha-\varepsilon+1}

holds for all
\displaystyle\alpha-\varepsilon>1
. This proves
\displaystyle(a)
.
If
\displaystyle\int_{M}f\operatorname{d}\!\mu_{M}=0
, then the statement follows from
[
7
, Theorem 7.4
\displaystyle(b)
]
,
[
7
, Proposition 3.2]
and part
\displaystyle(a)
of Theorem
5
.

∎

If one does not have an asymptotic, only an upper bound on the tails of the return time to
\displaystyle\hat{M}

\displaystyle(a)
of Theorem

4.3
Almost sure invariance principle

We recall the following definition (see eg.
[
14
]
).

Definition 15
.

Let
\displaystyle\kappa:\mathbb{N}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
denote a function with
\displaystyle\kappa(n)=o(\sqrt{n})
, and
\displaystyle c^{2}
some positive constant. A random process
\displaystyle(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfies the almost sure invariance principle (ASIP) with variance
\displaystyle c^{2}
and rate
\displaystyle o(\kappa(n))
if
\displaystyle(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
can be redefined on some probability space supporting a Brownian motion
\displaystyle W_{n}
with variance

Wojtkowski’s system of two falling balls

Gibbs-Markov Maps

Suppose that
\displaystyle(\Delta_{0},\mu_{\Delta_{0}})
is a probability space with a countable measurable partition
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
, and let
\displaystyle F_{0}:\Delta_{0}\to\Delta_{0}
be an ergodic measure-preserving transformation.
Define the separation time
\displaystyle s(x,y)
to be

s(x,y)=\min\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\;\left|\;\exists A\in\mathcal{P}:\;F_{0}^{n}(x)\in A,F_{0}^{n}(y)\notin A\right.\right\}.

It is assumed that the partition
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
separates trajectories, so
\displaystyle s(x,y)=\infty
if and only if
\displaystyle x=y
.
Then
\displaystyle\theta^{s}
is a metric for any
\displaystyle\theta\in(0,1)
.

Let

\xi=\frac{d\mu_{\Delta_{0}}}{d\mu_{\Delta_{0}}\circ F_{0}}:\Delta_{0}\to\mathbb{R}.

We say that
\displaystyle F_{0}
is a (full-branch) Gibbs-Markov map if:

•

\displaystyle F_{0}|_{A}:A\to\Delta_{0}
is a measurable bijection for each
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{P}
, and

•

There are constants
\displaystyle C>0
,
\displaystyle\theta\in(0,1)
such that

|\log\xi(x)-\log\xi(y)|\leq C\theta^{s(x,y)}

for all
\displaystyle x,y\in A
,
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{P}
.

Hence, there is a constant
\displaystyle C>0
such that

\xi(x)\leq C\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A)\quad\text{and}\quad|\xi(x)-\xi(y)|\leq C\mu_{\Delta_{0}}(A)\theta^{s(x,y)},

for all
\displaystyle x,y\in A
,
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{P}
.

Return Maps

Let
\displaystyle(M,\mu)
be a probability space and
\displaystyle T:M\to M
an ergodic measure-preserving transformation.
Let
\displaystyle\hat{M}\subset M
be a measurable subset with
\displaystyle\mu(\hat{M})>0
. If
\displaystyle R:\hat{M}\to\mathbb{N}
is integrable and
\displaystyle T^{R(x)}x\in\hat{M}
for all
\displaystyle x\in\hat{M}
, then
\displaystyle R
is called a
return time
and
\displaystyle T^{R}:\hat{M}\to\hat{M}
is called a
return map
.

If
\displaystyle R
is the first return time of
\displaystyle\hat{M}
under
\displaystyle T
(i.e.,
\displaystyle R(x)=\inf\{n\geq 1\,|\,T^{n}x\in\hat{M}\}
), then
\displaystyle T^{R}:\hat{M}\to\hat{M}
is called the
first return map
and

\mu_{\hat{M}}=\frac{\mu|_{\hat{M}}}{\mu(\hat{M})}

is an ergodic
\displaystyle T^{R}
-invariant probability measure on
\displaystyle\hat{M}
.

Young Towers

Let
\displaystyle F_{0}:\Delta_{0}\to\Delta_{0}
be a full-branch Gibbs-Markov map on
\displaystyle(\Delta_{0},\mu_{\Delta_{0}})
with partition
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
and let
\displaystyle h:\Delta_{0}\to\mathbb{N}
be an integrable function constant on partition elements. The (one-sided) Young tower
\displaystyle\Delta=\Delta_{0}^{h}
and tower map
\displaystyle F:\Delta\to\Delta
can be defined the following way:

\Delta=\{(x,\ell)\in\Delta_{0}\times\mathbb{N}:0\leq\ell<h(x)\},\quad F(x,\ell)=\begin{cases}(x,\ell+1)&\text{if }\ell<h(x)-1,\\
(F_{0}x,0)&\text{if }\ell=h(x)-1.\end{cases}

\displaystyle\bar{h}=\int_{\Delta_{0}}h\,d\mu_{\Delta_{0}}
and let
\displaystyle\nu
be the counting measure on
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
.
Then there is an ergodic
\displaystyle F
-invariant probability measure

\mu_{\Delta}=\frac{\mu_{\Delta_{0}}\times\nu}{\bar{h}}

on
\displaystyle\Delta
.

Let
\displaystyle T:M\to M
be an ergodic measure-preserving transformation on a probability space
\displaystyle(M,\mu)
, and let
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}\subset M
be measurable with
\displaystyle\mu(\Delta_{0})>0
.
Suppose that
\displaystyle h:\Delta_{0}\to\mathbb{N}
is a return time, constant on partition elements, and that
\displaystyle F_{0}=T^{h}:\Delta_{0}\to\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta_{0}}
on
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}
.
Construct the tower
\displaystyle\Delta=\Delta_{0}^{h}
and tower map
\displaystyle F:\Delta\to\Delta
.

If the semiconjugacy
\displaystyle\pi_{M}:\Delta\to M
,
\displaystyle\pi_{M}(y,\ell)=T^{\ell}y
is measure preserving as well,
then we say that
\displaystyle T
is
modelled by a Young tower
.

Chernov-Markarian-Zhang Structure

Suppose
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}\subset\hat{M}\subset M
are Borel sets with
\displaystyle\mu(\Delta_{0})>0
.
Denote by
\displaystyle R:\hat{M}\to\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle\hat{T}=T^{R}:\hat{M}\to\hat{M}
the first return time and first return map of
\displaystyle\hat{M}

We assume that
\displaystyle\hat{T}:\hat{M}\to\hat{M}
is modelled by a Young tower
\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}=\Delta_{0}^{\sigma}
with return time
\displaystyle\sigma:\Delta_{0}\to\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle R

for all
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{P}
,
\displaystyle 0\leq\ell<\sigma(A)

Two-sided Chernov-Markarian-Zhang Structure

Suppose that
\displaystyle(M,d)
is a bounded metric space with Borel probability measure
\displaystyle\mu
and let
\displaystyle T:M\to M
be a mixing measure-preserving transformation.
Suppose that
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}\subset\hat{M}\subset M
are Borel sets satisfying
\displaystyle\mu(\Delta_{0})>0
.
Denote the first return time by
\displaystyle R:\hat{M}\to\mathbb{N}
and first return map by
\displaystyle\hat{T}=T^{R}:\hat{M}\to\hat{M}
.

We require that
\displaystyle\hat{T}:\hat{M}\to\hat{M}
is modelled by a two-sided Young tower
\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}=\Delta_{0}^{\sigma}
with return time
\displaystyle\sigma:\Delta_{0}\to\mathbb{N}
and return map
\displaystyle F_{0}=\hat{T}^{\sigma}:\Delta_{0}\to\Delta_{0}
, which is a two-sided Gibbs-Markov map with ergodic invariant probability measure
\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta_{0}}
and partition
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
such that
\displaystyle\sigma
is constant on partition elements.
We further assume that
\displaystyle R
is constant on
\displaystyle\hat{T}^{\ell}\pi^{\prime-1}A
for all
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{P}
,
\displaystyle 0\leq\ell<\sigma(A)
.

Then there is an integrable return time
\displaystyle h=R_{\sigma}:\Delta_{0}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
\displaystyle T:M\to M
is modelled by a Young tower
\displaystyle\Delta=\Delta_{0}^{h}
with the same two-sided Gibbs-Markov map
\displaystyle F_{0}=\hat{T}^{\sigma}=T^{h}
.

If
\displaystyle T:M\to M
satisfies these assumptions, it is said to have a
two-sided Chernov-Markarian-Zhang structure
.

Proof of some properties of regularly varying functions

Proof of Lemma
6
.

We will use Karamata’s integral representation theorem of regularly varying functions, see
[
5
, Theorem 1.3.1]
for example.

Theorem 18
.

A function
\displaystyle r:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is regularly varying, if and only if there exist measurable functions
\displaystyle c,a:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle\exists C=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}c(x)\in\mathbb{R}
,
\displaystyle\exists-\alpha=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}a(x)\in\mathbb{R}
and there is a
\displaystyle y>0
such that

r(x)=\exp\left(c(x)+\int_{y}^{x}\frac{a(s)}{s}\operatorname{d}s\right)

holds for all
\displaystyle x\geq y
.

With this, we can see that for any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists an
\displaystyle x_{0}>0
such that for all
\displaystyle x>x_{0}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log r(x)\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle C+\varepsilon+\int_{y}^{x}\frac{a(s)}{s}\operatorname{d}s\leq C+\varepsilon+\int_{y}^{x_{0}}\frac{a(s)}{s}\operatorname{d}s+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{a(s)}{s}\operatorname{d}s

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle C+\varepsilon+\int_{y}^{x_{0}}\frac{a(s)}{s}\operatorname{d}s+(-\alpha+\varepsilon)\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{1}{s}\operatorname{d}s

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle C+\varepsilon+\int_{y}^{x_{0}}\frac{a(s)}{s}\operatorname{d}s+(-\alpha+\varepsilon)(\log x-\log x_{0})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle C+\varepsilon+\int_{y}^{x_{0}}\frac{a(s)}{s}\operatorname{d}s-(-\alpha+\varepsilon)\log x_{0}+(-\alpha+\varepsilon)\log x

Hence,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\log r(x)}{\log x}\leq-\alpha+\varepsilon+\left(C+\varepsilon+\int_{y}^{x_{0}}\frac{a(s)}{s}\operatorname{d}s-(-\alpha+\varepsilon)\log x_{0}\right)(\log x)^{-1}

Which means that there is an
\displaystyle x_{1}>0
such that for all
\displaystyle x>x_{1}
,

\frac{\log r(x)}{\log x}\leq-\alpha+2\varepsilon

holds. By using similar calculations, one can arrive at the lower bound

-\alpha-2\varepsilon\leq\frac{\log r(x)}{\log x}

From this, we can see that

\exists\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log r(x)}{\log x}=-\alpha.

We can see that
\displaystyle-\alpha
is the index of
\displaystyle r

∎

Proof of Lemma

\sum_{k\geq n}r(k)\geq\sum_{k\geq n}\int_{k}^{k+1}r(x)\operatorname{d}x=\int_{n}^{\infty}r(x)\operatorname{d}x.
