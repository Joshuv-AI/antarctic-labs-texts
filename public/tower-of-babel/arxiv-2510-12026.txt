Mamba Can Learn Low-Dimensional Targets In-Context via Test-Time Feature Learning
Junsoo Oh, Wei Huang, Taiji Suzuki

Abstract
Mamba, a recently proposed linear-time sequence model, has attracted significant attention for its computational efficiency and strong empirical performance. However, a rigorous theoretical understanding of its underlying mechanisms remains limited. In this work, we provide a theoretical analysis of Mamba’s in-context learning (ICL) capability by focusing on tasks defined by low-dimensional nonlinear target functions. Specifically, we study in-context learning of a single-index model y ≈ g ∗ ​ ( ⟨ 𝜷 , 𝒙 ⟩ ) y\approx g_{*}(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle) , which depends on only a single relevant direction 𝜷 {\bm{\beta}} , referred to as feature . We prove that Mamba, pretrained by gradient-based methods, can achieve efficient ICL via test-time feature learning , extracting the relevant direction directly from context examples. Consequently, we establish a test-time sample complexity that improves upon linear Transformers—analyzed to behave like kernel methods—and is comparable to nonlinear Transformers, which have been shown to surpass the Correlational Statistical Query (CSQ) lower bound and achieve near information-theoretically optimal rate in previous works. Our analysis reveals the crucial role of the nonlinear gating mechanism in Mamba for feature extraction, highlighting it as the fundamental driver behind Mamba’s ability to achieve both computational efficiency and high performance.



1
Introduction

Mamba
(
Gu and Dao 2024
)
, a recently proposed state space model, has rapidly gained attention for its remarkable balance of computational efficiency and empirical performance. By replacing the quadratic-time attention mechanism of Transformers
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
with a selective state-space recurrence with nonlinear gating, Mamba enables scalable modeling of long sequences while maintaining competitive accuracy across a variety of tasks
(
Dao and Gu 2024
;
Waleffe et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Patro and Agneeswaran 2025
)
.
Despite Mamba’s remarkable computational efficiency, it remains unknown whether it can exhibit strong adaptability (often referred to as
feature learning
), a property widely recognized as critical to the success of deep learning neural networks
(
Girshick et al. 2014
;
Suzuki 2019
;
Damian et al. 2022
)
.

A key benchmark for test-time adaptability is in-context learning (ICL)
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, which has emerged as a canonical paradigm for understanding the adaptability of large language models and sequence architectures. By conditioning on context examples provided in the input prompt, a model can achieve strong performance on new tasks at test time without explicit parameter updates. While the empirical effectiveness of ICL is well documented, theoretical understanding of when and how different architectures exhibit this behavior remains limited
(
Xie et al. 2022
;
Garg et al. 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
. In particular, most existing theoretical analyses focus on Transformers
(
Ahn et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Mahankali et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Kim and Suzuki 2024
)
, whose quadratic attention mechanisms make them both powerful and computationally demanding. It remains unclear whether alternative architectures such as Mamba can offer comparable adaptability.

Recent works have investigated Mamba’s ICL capabilities, empirically demonstrating that Mamba performs competitively across various ICL benchmarks
(
Grazzi et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024c
)
.
However, our understanding of Mamba’s ICL capabilities remains lacking. This is due to its distinct inductive bias from the Transformer. The recurrent state-space model with nonlinear gating processes inputs through recurrent updates that maintain and transform hidden states over time, rather than relying on global attention over the entire context. This distinction motivates new theoretical questions:

Can Mamba provably achieve strong test-time adaptability like Transformers

with its recurrent state-space updates and nonlinear gating?

1.1
Summary of Contributions

In this paper, we study the ICL capabilities of Mamba, focusing on a single-index model—a widely adopted theoretical tool for studying adaptability. We summarize our contributions as follows:

•

We introduce a theoretical framework for analyzing Mamba’s ICL of single-index models, including input embeddings, the Mamba architecture, and a gradient-based pretraining algorithm. Under this framework, we characterize the optimization dynamics and establish the sample complexity in terms of the number of pretraining tasks and the number of context examples at pretraining and test time required to achieve strong performance (Theorem
3.3
).

•

Our analysis reveals that pretrained Mamba is capable of
test-time feature learning
, enabling it to extract task-relevant features directly from context examples (Proposition
4.1
). This result implies that Mamba can surpass the performance of kernel regression baselines and achieve adaptation at test time. Specifically, the gating mechanism enables Mamba to achieve test-time feature learning, thereby overcoming the limitations inherent to purely linear recurrent updates.

•

We provide a comparative analysis between Mamba and Transformer architectures, highlighting similarities and differences in their ICL mechanisms. Our results reveal that Mamba can achieve test-time feature learning via a qualitatively different mechanism—recurrent state-space updates with nonlinear gating—thus extending the theoretical landscape of in-context learning beyond attention-based models.

1.2
Related Works

Theory of In-Context Learning.

Theoretical investigations of ICL have predominantly centered on Transformers. Beyond initial results showing that Transformers trained on regression tasks can reproduce ordinary least squares solutions in-context
(
Akyürek et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Mahankali et al. 2024
;
Han et al. 2025
)
, subsequent analyses reveal their ability to emulate more complex procedures such as multi-step gradient descent
(
Ahn et al. 2023
;
Saunshi et al. 2025
)
, functional gradient descent
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
, and sparse regression
(
Bai et al. 2023
)
. Parallel works extend this line of inquiry to classification, where recent studies provide provable insights into how Transformers implement in-context classification
(
Li et al. 2024a
;
Bu et al. 2025
)
.

While the theoretical literature on ICL has dominantly focused on Transformers, a growing body of work is extending this theoretical analysis to linear-time sequence models. Recent works
(
Li et al. 2024b
;
Li et al. 2025b
)
prove that H3-like model
(
Fu et al. 2023
)
and gated linear attention
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
can implement weighted preconditioned gradient descent based on loss landscape analysis.
Bondaschi et al. 2025
study ICL of Mamba on Markov chains and show that it learns a Laplacian smoothing estimator in-context. However, these works do not provide optimization or generalization guarantees. Recent works by
Jiang et al. 2025
and
Li et al. 2025a
provide such guarantees for in-context learning of Mamba on linear regression and classification tasks with outliers, as we also do in this work.

Learning Low-Dimensional Target Function.

Low-dimensional target function classes, such as sparse parities
(
Barak et al. 2022
;
Suzuki et al. 2023
;
Glasgow 2024
)
, signal-noise models
(
Allen-Zhu and Li 2020
;
Cao et al. 2022
)
, are widely adopted as theoretical benchmarks for studying a neural network’s ability to perform feature learning.
This work specifically focuses on the single-index model. A line of theoretical work has analyzed the learning of these models and has established key results on sample complexity. The required sample complexity is governed by either the
information exponent
(for algorithms utilizing correlational information
(
Arous et al. 2021
;
Bietti et al. 2022
;
Damian et al. 2023
;
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2023
)
) or the
generative exponent
(for algorithms that employ suitable label transformations
(
Damian et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024
;
Arnaboldi et al. 2024
;
Joshi et al. 2024
)
). We discuss these sample complexity results in more detail in Section
3.1
.

Our work is most closely related to
Oko et al. 2024
;
Nishikawa et al. 2025
, which lie at the intersection of ICL and the single-index model. Specifically,
Oko et al. 2024
show that a pretrained linear Transformer can effectively learn a single-index model in-context. More recent work by
Nishikawa et al. 2025
establish an even smaller sample complexity and reveal that the nonlinear Transformer can perform test-time feature learning. A detailed comparison with these works is provided in Section
3.2
.

Problem Setting

In this section, we provide a formal description of the key components we focus on: the ICL data distribution, the Mamba model, and the gradient-based pretraining algorithm.

Notation.

We denote the
i
-th coordinate of a vector
{\bm{v}}
as
{\bm{v}}[i]
, and the
(i,j)
-th coordinate of a matrix
{\bm{M}}
as
{\bm{M}}[i,j]
. The matrix
\mathrm{diag}({\bm{v}})
represents a diagonal matrix with a vector
{\bm{v}}
on its main diagonal. We use
\odot
for the element-wise product.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the vectors with all entries equal to one and zero as
{\bm{1}}_{k}
and
{\bm{0}}_{k}
, respectively. We omit the subscript
k
when the dimension is clear from the context.

2.1
Data Distribution for In-Context Learning

In-context learning aims to solve the
task
of predicting the
label

y
of a
query

{\bm{x}}
by leveraging a sequence of input-label pairs
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
, which are referred to as
context examples
. The model then utilizes a prompt, which is a sequence
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{N},y_{N},{\bm{x}})
consisting of the context examples and the query, as its input. We focus on the case where prompts are constructed from the Gaussian single-index model, which is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Gaussian Single-Index Model)
.

Given a feature vector
{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we draw input-label pairs
({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\beta}}}
as

{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d}),\quad y=g_{*}(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)+\zeta,\quad\zeta\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{-\tau,\tau\}),

where
g_{*}
is a polynomial
link function
and
\tau>0
represents the noise level. For simplicity, we assume that
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}[g_{*}({\mathbf{z}})]=0,\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}[(g_{*}({\mathbf{z}}))^{2}]=1
and
\tau
is a small enough constant.

For each task, a prompt is constructed with a random choice of feature vectors.

Definition 2.2

(ICL Data Distribution)
.

For given a context length
N
, we define a data distribution
{\mathcal{D}}(N)
such that
({\bm{\beta}},\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in[N]},{\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N)
is constructed as follows.

1.

We draw the feature vector
{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from the
intrinsic feature space

S_{r}
, which is the unit sphere of a low-dimensional subspace with dimension
r
. This subspace is defined as:

{\bm{\beta}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{r}),\quad\text{where }S_{r}:=\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|\bm{\theta}\right\|=1,\bm{\theta}[j]=0\text{ for all }j\notin{\mathcal{I}}\right\},

for some unknown feature index set
{\mathcal{I}}
with
\left|{\mathcal{I}}\right|=r
.

2.

We sample
N
context examples
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
and a query-label pair
({\bm{x}},y)
from
{\mathcal{D}}_{\bm{\beta}}
.

Our task distribution exhibits a low-dimensional structure in two key aspects: (1) the label depends solely on the projection of the input onto the feature vector, and (2) feature vectors are supported on an
r
-dimensional subspace. We note that to achieve low prediction errors, it is crucial to extract both of these structures and estimate the link function
g_{*}
.

2.2
Prediction Model Architecture

Our prediction model for ICL is composed of three parts: input embedding, one-layer Mamba, multi-layer perceptron (MLP).

Input Embedding.

Given a prompt
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{N},y_{N},{\bm{x}})
with context length
N
and label
y
, we construct an input embedding
{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tilde{d}+1)\times(N+1)}
as

{\bm{Z}}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi({\bm{x}}_{1})&\phi({\bm{x}}_{2})&\dots&\phi({\bm{x}}_{N})&\phi({\bm{x}})\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\dots&y_{N}&0\end{bmatrix}=[{\bm{z}}_{1},\dots{\bm{z}}_{N},{\bm{z}}_{N+1}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tilde{d}+1)\times(N+1)},

where
\tilde{d}=\frac{d(d+1)}{2}+1
and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}}
is defined as

\phi(\bm{\theta})=\left[1,\bm{\theta}[1],\dots,\bm{\theta}[d],\frac{\bm{\theta}[1]^{2}-1}{\sqrt{2}},\dots,\frac{\bm{\theta}[d]^{2}-1}{\sqrt{2}},\bm{\theta}[1]\bm{\theta}[2],\dots,\bm{\theta}[d-1]\bm{\theta}[d]\right].

An input embedding similar to ours was also considered in the recent work by
Sun et al. 2025
, who studied the in-context learning of high-order polynomial target functions. They showed this embedding can be implemented with a simple version of Gated Linear Unit (GLU) and demonstrated its efficacy for enabling linear Transformers to learn these functions in-context. Unlike
Sun et al. 2025
, who repeatedly stacked a linear Transformer and a GLU layer, in our work, a single GLU-based embedding is sufficient due to the nonlinearity in Mamba and MLP layers. We discuss the efficacy of this input embedding in more detail in Section
4.1
.

Remark 2.3
.

Our input embedding is based on a basis for degree-2 polynomials in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Specifically, we use the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the construction of both the input embedding and the intrinsic feature space
S_{r}
. While extending our results to a general choice of
S_{r}
with an arbitrary basis may require additional techniques, our setting remains valuable for studying Mamba’s ability to learn low-dimensional structure. Furthermore, we emphasize that our result also holds with the standard choice of input embedding
\phi({\bm{x}})={\bm{x}}
with
\tilde{d}=d
, as considered in prior works including
Von Oswald et al. 2023
, for the case where link function
g_{*}
is not an even function. We refer to Section
4
for a more detailed discussion.

One-Layer Mamba.

Given an input embedding
{\bm{Z}}=({\bm{z}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{N+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tilde{d}+1)\times(N+1)}
, a one-layer Mamba model
{\mathsf{Mamba}}(\cdot;\bm{\Theta})
with parameters
\bm{\Theta}
has sequential outputs
{\bm{o}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{o}}_{N+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}+1}
and hidden states for
i
-th channel
{\bm{h}}_{1}^{(i)},\dots,{\bm{h}}_{N}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}
defined as below:

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}_{l}^{(i)}=\overline{{\bm{A}}}_{l}{\bm{h}}_{l-1}^{(i)}+\overline{{\bm{B}}}_{l}{\bm{z}}_{l}[i]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}},\quad{\bm{o}}_{l}[i]={\bm{C}}_{l}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{l}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{A}}}_{l}=\exp\left(\Delta_{l}{\bm{A}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}\times d_{h}},\quad\overline{{\bm{B}}}_{l}=\left(\Delta_{l}{\bm{A}}\right)^{-1}(\exp\left(\Delta_{l}{\bm{A}}\right)-{\bm{I}}_{d_{h}})\Delta_{l}{\bm{B}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}},

where
{\bm{h}}_{0}^{(i)}={\bm{0}}_{d_{h}}
and
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}\times d_{h}}
. Here, the components of the selection algorithm
{\bm{A}},{\bm{B}}_{l},{\bm{C}}_{l},\Delta_{l}
is chosen as

{\bm{A}}=-{\bm{I}}_{\tilde{d}+1},\quad{\bm{B}}_{l}={\bm{W}}_{B}{\bm{z}}_{l},\quad{\bm{C}}_{l}={\bm{W}}_{C}{\bm{z}}_{l},\quad\Delta_{l}=\mathrm{softplus}\left({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{z}}_{l}+b\right),

with parameters
{\bm{W}}_{B},{\bm{W}}_{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}\times(\tilde{d}+1)},{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}},b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, the
l
-th output can be expressed as

{\bm{o}}_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}G_{j,l}({\bm{Z}}){\bm{z}}_{j}{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}{\bm{W}}_{B}^{\top}{\bm{W}}_{C}{\bm{z}}_{l},

(1)

where
G_{j,l}({\bm{Z}})=\sigma\left({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{z}}_{j}+b\right)\prod_{k=j+1}^{l}\left(1-\sigma\left({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{z}}_{k}+b\right)\right)
with sigmoid function
\sigma(\cdot)
. It implies that Mamba involves two key mechanisms:
nonlinear gating

G_{j,l}({\bm{Z}})
and
linear attention
with projection matrices
{\bm{W}}_{B}
and
{\bm{W}}_{C}
.
Yang et al. 2024
refer the combination of these mechanisms as
gated linear attention
and recent recurrent models including Mamba, mLSTM
(
Beck et al. 2024
)
, and RWKV-6
(
Peng et al. 2024
)
can be viewed within this framework.

To ensure a tractable optimization guarantee, we further introduce the following simplifications to our model:

{\bm{W}}_{B}^{\top}{\bm{W}}_{C}=\mathrm{diag}({\bm{\gamma}},0),\quad{\bm{w}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{0}}_{\tilde{d}}\\
\rho^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}}
is a learnable parameter, while
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}+1}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
are fixed.
Our approach of merging the product of two learnable matrices into a single matrix and using sparse learnable parameters is a technique also adopted in the theoretical literature on optimization of attention mechanisms
(
Ahn et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Mahankali et al. 2024
;
Kim and Suzuki 2024
)
. Under this simplification, the last coordinate of the final output which serves as the input to the MLP can be expressed as follows:

{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}};{\bm{\gamma}})[\tilde{d}+1,N+1]=\sum_{j=1}^{N}G_{j,N+1}({\bm{Z}})y_{j}\phi({\bm{x}}_{j})^{\top}\left({\bm{\gamma}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\right).

Multi-Layer Perceptron.

We use a two-layer MLP with ReLU activation, width
m
and parameters
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined as follows:

{\mathsf{MLP}}(z;{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}}):=\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\bm{u}}[k]{\mathrm{ReLU}}\left({\bm{v}}[k]z+{\bm{a}}[k]\right).

We apply this MLP to the output of the Mamba layer, after normalizing it by its context length
N
. Then, the final output is given by

\displaystyle f({\bm{Z}};{\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}})

\displaystyle:={\mathsf{MLP}}\left(N^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}};{\bm{\gamma}})[\tilde{d}+1,N+1];{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\bm{u}}[k]{\mathrm{ReLU}}\left({\bm{v}}[k]N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G_{j,N+1}({\bm{Z}})y_{j}\phi({\bm{x}}_{j})^{\top}\left({\bm{\gamma}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\right)+{\bm{a}}[k]\right).

Remark 2.4
.

A similar structure to our models, which combines a sequence model with a MLP, has also been utilized in two closely related prior works. For example,
Nishikawa et al. 2025
follow a similar structure but use a softmax Transformer in place of Mamba. In contrast,
Oko et al. 2024
use a different architectural design, applying the MLP to the input embedding before a linear Transformer, rather than after the sequence model.

Our goal for ICL is to find parameters
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and context length
N
, achieving a small ICL test error, which is defined as

{\mathcal{R}}_{N}({\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}}):=\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N)}[\left|f({\bm{Z}},{\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}})-y\right|].

Here, we abuse notation and use
({\bm{Z}},y)
to denote the input embedding and label for a prompt sampled from the ICL data distribution
{\mathcal{D}}(N)
.
More precisely, we are interested in the sample complexity of context examples required for the parameters learned from pretraining to achieve a low ICL test error.

2.3
Pretraining Algorithm

Our prediction model is pretrained on a set of
T_{{\mathrm{pt}}}=T_{1}+T_{2}
tasks with context length
N_{\mathrm{pt}}
drawn from
{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})
. For each task
t\in[T_{\mathrm{pt}}]
, let we have input embedding
{\bm{Z}}^{t}
constructed from context examples
\{({\bm{x}}_{i}^{t},y_{i}^{t})\}_{i\in[N_{\mathrm{pt}}]}
and a query-label pair
({\bm{x}}^{t},y^{t})
with a feature vector
{\bm{\beta}}^{t}
. Then, our training losses can be written as

L_{l}({\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}}):=\frac{1}{T_{l}}\sum_{t=T_{l-1}+1}^{T_{l-1}+T_{l}}\left(f\left({\bm{Z}}^{t};{\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}}\right)-y^{t}\right)^{2},

for
l=1,2
and
T_{0}=0
. We employ a two-stage training procedure, as described in Algorithm
1
, using these objectives.

1.

In Stage I, we only train the Mamba layer parameter
{\bm{\gamma}}
, starting from proper initialization. Our training objective is
\ell_{2}
-regularized loss
L_{1}({\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{a}})+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\left\|{\bm{\gamma}}\right\|^{2}
. Due to the non-linearity introduced by the MLP, this objective is non-convex. To make the training dynamics tractable, we apply one-step gradient descent, following the approaches studied in the literature of feature learning
(
Ba et al. 2022
;
Damian et al. 2022
)
. As we describe in Section
4.1
, a single step update is sufficient to capture the low-dimensional structure of the feature vectors.

2.

In Stage II, we fix the Mamba layer parameter
{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}
obtained from Stage I and optimize the outer layer
{\bm{u}}
of MLP on
\ell_{2}
-regularized loss
L_{2}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|^{2}
with reinitialized inner layer parameters
{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*}
. This induces a convex problem that gradient-based methods can solve. As we show in Section
4.2
, the optimized MLP is capable of estimating the link function
g_{*}
.

3
Mamba Efficiently Learns Single-Index Models In-Context

In this section, we present our theoretical results on the ICL performance of our model. Our analysis focuses on the asymptotic dependencies on the input dimension
d
, with the assumption that the feature dimension
r
can scale with
d
, while the link function
g_{*}
is fixed. For our analysis, we let
N^{*}
and
T^{*}
be the maximum admissible context length and the number of pretraining tasks, respectively. We assume that
N^{*},T^{*}\leq d^{C^{*}}
for some large constant
C^{*}>0
. We use the standard asymptotic notation
{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot),\Theta(\cdot),o(\cdot)
to express dependencies on
d
, and
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(\cdot),\tilde{\Omega}(\cdot),\tilde{\Theta}(\cdot)
to hide logarithmic factors of
d
.

3.1
Preliminaries

We first provide backgrounds on learning Gaussian single-index models, which are essential for understanding our main result.
Let
\mathrm{He}_{i}(z)=(-1)^{i}e^{\frac{z^{2}}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{i}}{\mathrm{d}z^{i}}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}
denote the probabilist’s Hermite polynomials. Then, the set
\{\mathrm{He}_{i}(z)/\sqrt{i!}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
forms an orthonormal basis of the
L^{2}
space with respect to the Gaussian measure and serves as a key technical tool for the analysis of Gaussian single-index models. We now introduce two key terms relevant to the sample complexity of learning.

Definition 3.1
.

For any function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is
L^{2}
-integrable with respect to the Gaussian measure, we express its Hermite expansion as

h(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{H(h,i)}{i!}\mathrm{He}_{i}(z),\quad H(h,i):=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}[h({\mathbf{z}})\mathrm{He}_{i}({\mathbf{z}})].

We also define the following quantities:

•

We define
\mathrm{deg}(h)
as the degree of
h
, if it is a polynomial.

•

The
information exponent

(
Arous et al. 2021
;
Damian et al. 2023
)
of
h
is defined as

\mathrm{ie}(h):=\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}:H(h,i)\neq 0\}.

It implies that
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}[h({\mathbf{z}})\mathrm{He}_{k}({\mathbf{z}})]=0
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k<\mathrm{ie}(h)
.

•

The
generative exponent

(
Damian et al. 2024
)
of
h
is defined as the lowest possible information exponent after an
L^{2}
transformation. It is formally defined as:

\mathrm{ge}(h):=\min_{{\mathcal{T}}\in L^{2}(P_{h})}\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}:H({\mathcal{T}}\circ h,i)\neq 0\},

where
L^{2}(P_{h})

Sample Complexity of Learning Single-Index Models.

Previous works have established the sample complexity of various methods for learning a Gaussian single-index model. For example, kernel methods, which lack an adaptive basis, require at least

(

;

;
Damian et al. 2023

Dandi et al. 2024

(

4.1

Assuming a negative bias

{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}

\displaystyle{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}

\displaystyle\approx 2\eta\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})}[y\nabla_{\bm{\gamma}}f({\bm{Z}};{\bm{\gamma}}(0),{\bm{u}}(0),{\bm{v}}(0),{\bm{a}}(0))]

The Role of Gating and Input Embedding.

In the absence of a gating mechanism and with only a linear attention, the term

\mathrm{ie}(g_{*}\sigma(g_{*}/\rho+b))=\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})
. Combining Lemma
3.2
with our input embedding, we show that the gating enables the model to extract information. When

, as can be seen from this intuition. Reducing the information exponent to the generative exponent crucially affects the achievement of a test-time sample complexity below the CSQ lower bounds.
Nishikawa et al. 2025
shows that the softmax operator in the Transformer can also perform a nonlinear transformation on the label, which reduces the information exponent. This highlights a key difference in the mechanisms used by Mamba and the softmax Transformer to achieve this result.

Improved Sample Complexity of Pretraining.

If the indicator
\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]
inside the expectation is 1 with high probability, then the updated parameter

{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}\approx 2\eta\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\beta}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{r})}\left[\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\bm{\beta}})}[y{\bm{c}}_{\bm{\beta}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})]\right]

In our analysis of Stage II pretraining, we first construct an outer layer parameter

6
Conclusion

We investigated Mamba’s capability for in-context learning by focusing on a Gaussian single-index model. We proved that Mamba, when pretrained with gradient-based optimization, can efficiently learn in-context through a mechanism we termed test-time feature learning. Our derived test-time sample complexity is comparable to that of the softmax Transformer model, a result established by
Nishikawa et al. 2025
and also surpasses the CSQ lower bound. Our analysis reveals that Mamba’s gating mechanism is a key factor in enabling feature learning and strong performance. We also presented experimental results to support our findings.

We suggest several directions for future research. First, a valuable direction is to investigate whether our results can be extended to more general input embeddings by considering additional layers, which could help overcome our current limitations.
Second, while our analysis considers the case where “forgetting” in the gating mechanism is negligible, recent work by
Li et al. 2025a
reveals that this effect can be beneficial for tasks with outliers. Investigating the combination of this effect with our insight could be an interesting direction. Finally, studying how different choices of gating functions within the gated linear attention framework
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
lead to different behaviors is a possible direction for future work.

From our choice of

Lemma A.5
.

For any

The following two lemmas are crucial for our proof of Lemma

Lemma A.6

(Proposition 6 in

For any polynomial

•

If
P
is an even function, then there exists
i\leq C_{P}

Lemma A.7
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
z<-k-2
, we have
\frac{e^{z}}{2}\leq\sigma^{(k)}(z)\leq 2e^{z}
.

Proof of Lemma
A.7
.

For any
x<0
, we have

\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}=1-\frac{1}{1+\exp(x)}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j-1}e^{jx}.

Therefore, we have

\sigma^{(k)}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j-1}j^{k}e^{jz}=e^{z}+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{j-1}j^{k}e^{jz}.

For each
j\geq 2
, since
\frac{(j+1)^{k}e^{(j+1)z}}{j^{k}e^{jz}}\leq 2^{k}e^{z}
and
2^{k}e^{z}<\frac{1}{3}
, we have

\left|\sigma^{(k)}(z)-e^{z}\right|\leq\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}j^{k}e^{jz}\leq 2^{k}e^{2z}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(2^{k}e^{z}\right)^{j}=\frac{2^{k}e^{2z}}{1-2^{k}e^{z}}\leq\frac{e^{z}}{2}.

Hence, we have a desired conclusion.
∎

Proof of Lemma
A.5
.

By applying Taylor’s theorem for
\sigma(\cdot)
at points
\pm\tau/\rho+b
, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\quad 2\rho^{-1}A(z)

\displaystyle=(\bar{g}_{*}(z)+\tau/\rho)\sigma\left(\bar{g}_{*}(z)+\tau/\rho-b\right)+(\bar{g}_{*}(z)-\tau/\rho)\sigma\left(\bar{g}_{*}(z)-\tau/\rho-b\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{e_{p}(g_{*})-1}(s_{i}+\tilde{s}_{i})\bar{g}_{*}^{i+1}(z)+\big(R(z)+\tilde{R}(z)\big)\bar{g}_{*}^{e_{p}(g_{*})+1}(z)

\displaystyle\quad+\tau\left[\sum_{i=0}^{e_{p}(g_{*})-1}(s_{i}-\tilde{s}_{i})\bar{g}_{*}^{i}(z)+\big(R(z)-\tilde{R}(z)\big)\bar{g}_{*}^{e_{p}(g_{*})}(z)\right]

where

for

and

From the definition of
\bar{g}_{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=0}^{e_{p}(g_{*})-1}(s_{i}+\tilde{s}_{i})\bar{g}_{*}^{i+1}(z)+\tau\sum_{i=0}^{e_{p}(g_{*})-1}(s_{i}-\tilde{s}_{i})\bar{g}_{*}^{i}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{e_{p}(g_{*})-1}\rho^{-(i+1)}(s_{i}+\tilde{s}_{i})g_{*}^{i+1}(z)+\tau\sum_{i=0}^{e_{p}(g_{*})-1}\rho^{-i}(s_{i}-\tilde{s}_{i})g_{*}^{i}(z)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\sum_{i=0}^{e_{p}(g_{*})-1}\rho^{-(i+1)}(s_{i}+\tilde{s}_{i})g_{*}^{i+1}(z)+\tau\sum_{i=0}^{e_{p}(g_{*})-1}\rho^{-i}(s_{i}-\tilde{s}_{i})g_{*}^{i}(z)\right)\mathbbm{1}\left[\left|\frac{g_{*}(z)}{\rho}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\log d}\right].

For any

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}\left[g_{*}^{i}({\mathbf{z}})\mathbbm{1}\left[\left|\frac{g_{*}(z)}{\rho}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\log d}\right]\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}\left[g_{*}^{2i}({\mathbf{z}})\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}\left[\left|\frac{g_{*}({\mathbf{z}})}{\rho}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\log d}\right]

\displaystyle 2\rho^{-1}a_{p}

\displaystyle=\rho^{-e_{p}(g_{*})}(s_{e_{p}(g_{*})-1}+\tilde{s}_{e_{p}(g_{*})-1})H\left(g_{*}^{e_{p}(g_{*})},p\right)

\displaystyle\quad+H\left((R+\tilde{R})\cdot\bar{g}_{*}^{e_{p}(g_{*})+1},p\right)+\tau H\left((R-\tilde{R})\cdot\bar{g}_{*}^{e_{p}(g_{*})},p\right)\pm o\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{polylog}(d)}\right).

From our choice of

\Theta(d^{-C_{b}}(\log d)^{-C_{\rho}e_{p}(g_{*})})
.
Next, we bound the second term. For any

\displaystyle\quad\left|H\left((R+\tilde{R})\cdot\bar{g}_{*}^{e_{p}(g_{*})+1},p\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}\left[\mathrm{He}_{p}({\mathbf{z}})(R({\mathbf{z}})+\tilde{R}({\mathbf{z}}))\bar{g}_{*}^{e_{p}(g_{*})+1}({\mathbf{z}})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}\left[\left|\mathrm{He}_{p}({\mathbf{z}})(R({\mathbf{z}})+\tilde{R}({\mathbf{z}}))\bar{g}_{*}^{e_{p}(g_{*})+1}({\mathbf{z}})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2e^{b+1}\rho^{-(e_{p}(g_{*})+1)}}{(e_{p}(g_{*})+1)!}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}\left[\mathrm{He}_{p}({\mathbf{z}})^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}\left[g_{*}({\mathbf{z}})^{2e_{p}(g_{*})+2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

where we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the second inequality. Hence, the absolute value of the second term is
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(d^{-C_{b}}(\log d)^{-C_{\rho}(e_{p}(g_{*})+1)})

\Theta\left(d^{-C_{b}}(\log d)^{-C_{\rho}e_{p}(g_{*})}\right)

Using similar arguments, we can also obtain our conclusion for the case

1-{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d^{2}T_{1}N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-1/2}\right)
, the updated parameter
{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}
is given by:

\displaystyle{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}

\displaystyle=2\eta\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\beta}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{r})}\left[\psi({\bm{\beta}},a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})\odot\psi({\bm{\beta}},b_{0},b_{1},b_{2})\right]

(4)

\displaystyle+\eta{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\max\left\{d^{-2C_{b}+4},d^{-C_{b}+2}N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-1/2},d^{-C_{b}+1}T_{1}^{-1/2}\right\}\right),

with
a_{0},b_{0},b_{2}=\tilde{\Theta}(1)
and
a_{\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}=\tilde{\Theta}(1),a_{3-\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(1)
. Furthermore,
b_{1}=\tilde{\Theta}(1)
if
\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})=1
, and
b_{1}\lesssim 1/\mathrm{poly}(d)
otherwise.

B.3
Output of Updated Mamba Layer

Lastly, we characterize the output of the Mamba layer with the updated parameter
{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}
, which serves as the input to the MLP layer. This characterization is given in the following proposition, which is a formal statement of Proposition
4.1
.

Proposition B.5
.

Let
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{N},y_{N},{\bm{x}})
be a prompt with context length
N\leq N^{*}
and feature vector
{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and its embedding
{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tilde{d}+1)\times(N+1)}
. If
N=\tilde{\Omega}\left(r^{3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}\right)
, (
4
) holds, and
\eta=\Theta(d^{2C_{b}}(\log d)^{-C_{\eta}})
with some large enough constant
C_{\eta}>0
, then the following holds with high probability:

N^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}\left({\bm{Z}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}\right)[\tilde{d}+1,N+1]=P_{1}+P_{2}\left(\frac{\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle}{r}\right)^{\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}\!\!\!\!+o\left(P_{2}r^{-3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})/2}(\log d)^{-2\mathrm{deg}(g_{*})+2}\right),

where
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are independent of data and satisfies
P_{1}=o(1)
and
P_{2}=\Theta((\log d)^{-C_{P_{2}}})
with some constant
C_{P_{2}}>0
.

Proof of Proposition
B.5
.

Recall that
{\bm{c}}:=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\beta}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{r})}\left[\psi({\bm{\beta}},a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})\odot\psi({\bm{\beta}},b_{0},b_{1},b_{2})\right]
. From (
4
), we have

\displaystyle\quad(2\eta)^{-1}N^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}\left({\bm{Z}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}\right)[\tilde{d}+1,N+1]

\displaystyle=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G_{j,N+1}\left({\bm{Z}}\right)y_{j}\phi\left({\bm{x}}_{j}\right)^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi\left({\bm{x}}\right)\big)

\displaystyle\quad+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\max\left\{d^{-3C_{b}+4},d^{-2C_{b}+4}N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-1/2},d^{-2C_{b}+3}T_{1}^{-1/2}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}\sigma(y_{j}/\rho+b)\phi\left({\bm{x}}_{j}\right)^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi\left({\bm{x}}\right)\big)

\displaystyle\quad-N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[y_{j}\sigma(y_{j}/\rho+b)\left(1-\big(1-\sigma(b)\big)\prod_{i=j+1}^{N}\big(1-\sigma(y_{j}^{t}/\rho+b)\big)\right)\phi\left({\bm{x}}_{j}^{t}\right)^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\big)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\max\left\{d^{-3C_{b}+4},d^{-2C_{b}+4}N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-1/2},d^{-2C_{b}+3}T_{1}^{-1/2}\right\}\right).

Note that for each
j\in[N]
, with high probability,
y_{j}=\rho\bar{g}_{*}({\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}_{j})+\zeta_{i}
where
\zeta_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{-\tau,\tau\})
. It implies that

\displaystyle\quad\left|y_{j}\sigma(y_{j}/\rho+b)\left(1-\big(1-\sigma(b)\big)\prod_{i=j+1}^{N}\big(1-\sigma(y_{j}^{t}/\rho+b)\big)\right)\phi\left({\bm{x}}_{j}^{t}\right)^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\big)\right|

\displaystyle\leq N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\left|y_{j}\sigma(y_{j}/\rho+b)\right|\left(1-\big(1-\sigma(2b)\big)^{N^{*}}\right)\left\|\phi\left({\bm{x}}_{j}\right)\right\|\right]\left\|{\bm{c}}\right\|\left\|\phi({\bm{x}})\right\|

\displaystyle={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d^{(-3C_{b}+C^{*}+2)}\right).

In addition, with high probability, we have

\displaystyle\quad\phi\left({\bm{x}}_{j}\right)^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\beta}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{r})}\big[\left\langle\psi({\bm{\beta}},a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})\odot\phi({\bm{x}}_{j}),\psi({\bm{\beta}},b_{0},b_{1},b_{2})\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\right\rangle\big]

\displaystyle=a_{0}b_{0}+a_{1}b_{1}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\beta}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{r})}[\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}_{i}\rangle\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{a_{2}b_{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\beta}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{r})}\left[(\mathrm{He}_{2}(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)-1)(\mathrm{He}_{2}(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}_{j}\rangle)-1)\right].

In addition, combining with Lemma
A.3
, we have

\phi\left({\bm{x}}_{j}\right)^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi\left({\bm{x}}\right)\big)={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d^{-C_{b}}\right),

with high probability. Therefore, from our choice of
\eta=\Theta\left(d^{2C_{b}}(\log d)^{-C_{\eta}}\right)
, with high probability, we have

\left|\eta y_{j}\sigma(y_{j}/\rho+b)\phi({\bm{x}}_{j})^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\big)\right|\leq 1.

Therefore, with high probability, we have

N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta y_{j}\sigma(y_{j}/\rho+b)\phi({\bm{x}}_{j})^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\big)=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{j},

where

{\mathbf{z}}_{j}:=\eta y_{j}\sigma(y_{j}/\rho+b)\phi({\bm{x}}_{j})^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\big),\quad\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{j}:={\mathbf{z}}_{j}\mathbbm{1}[\left|{\mathbf{z}}_{j}\right|\leq 1].

By Höeffding’s inequality, with high probability, we have

\displaystyle N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}}[\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{1}]+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(N^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{1},y_{1}}[{\mathbf{z}}_{1}]+\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{1},y_{1}}\left[{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mathbbm{1}\left[\left|{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\right|>r^{2}\right]\right]+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(N^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{1},y_{1}}[{\mathbf{z}}_{1}]+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(N^{-1/2}\right),

where the last inequality holds since

\left|\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{1},y_{1}}\left[{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\mathbbm{1}\left[\left|{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\right|>r^{2}\right]\right]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{1},y_{1}}\left[{\mathbf{z}}_{1}^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\right|>r^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(d)}.

Therefore, with high probability, we have

\displaystyle\quad N^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}\left({\bm{Z}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}\right)[\tilde{d}+1,N+1]

\displaystyle=2\eta\mathbb{E}\left[y_{1}\sigma(y_{1}/\rho+b)\phi({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}\big({\bm{c}}\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\big)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(N^{-1/2}\right)+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\max\left\{d^{-C_{b}+6},d^{4}N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-1/2},d^{3}T_{1}^{-1/2}\right\}\right).

Lastly, the expectation can be calculated as

\displaystyle\quad\psi\left({\bm{\beta}},a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\right)^{\top}\left({\bm{c}}\odot\phi\left({\bm{x}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{r})}\Big[\big\langle\psi({\bm{\beta}},a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})\odot\psi({\bm{\beta}}^{\prime},a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}),\psi({\bm{\beta}}^{\prime},b_{0},b_{1},b_{2})\odot\phi({\bm{x}})\big\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=a_{0}^{2}b_{0}+a_{1}^{2}b_{1}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{r})}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{\beta}}[i]{\bm{x}}[i]{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}[i]^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{a_{2}^{2}b_{2}}{4}\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{\beta}}[i]{\bm{x}}[i]{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}[i]^{2}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{\beta}}[i]^{2}{\bm{\beta}}^{\prime}[i]^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a_{0}^{2}b_{0}-\frac{a_{2}^{2}b_{2}}{4}\right)+a_{1}^{2}b_{1}\left(\frac{\left\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle}{r}\right)+\frac{a_{2}^{2}b_{2}}{4}\left(\frac{\left\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle}{r}\right)^{2}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\quad N^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}\left({\bm{Z}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}\right)[\tilde{d}+1,N+1]

\displaystyle=2\eta\left(\left(a_{0}^{2}b_{0}-\frac{a_{2}^{2}b_{2}}{4}\right)+a_{1}^{2}b_{1}\left(\frac{\left\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle}{r}\right)+\frac{a_{2}^{2}b_{2}}{4}\left(\frac{\left\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle}{r}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\max\left\{d^{-C_{b}+6},d^{4}N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-1/2},d^{3}T_{1}^{-1/2}\right\}\right).

Our conclusion is reached by defining
P_{1}=2\eta(a_{0}^{2}b_{0}-a_{2}^{2}b_{2}/4)
and

\displaystyle P_{2}=\begin{cases}2\eta a_{1}^{2}b_{1}&\text{if $\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})=1$}\\
\eta a_{2}^{2}b_{2}/2&\text{if $\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})=2$}\end{cases}.

∎

Appendix C
Optimizing MLP Layer

In this section, we analyze the second stage of pretraining, which focuses on the MLP layer.

C.1
Construction of Approximating MLP Layer

First, we construct the infinite-width MLP layer approximating the link function
g_{*}
.

Lemma C.1
.

For given
{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\left\|{\bm{\beta}}\right\|=1
, suppose there exists a function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

h({\bm{x}})=P_{1}+P_{2}\left(\frac{\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle}{r}\right)^{\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}+n({\bm{x}}),

where
P_{1}=o(1),P_{2}=\Theta\left((\log d)^{-C_{P_{2}}}\right)
, and
\left|n({\bm{x}})\right|=o\left(P_{2}r^{-3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})/2}(\log d)^{-2\mathrm{deg}(g_{*})+2}\right)
with high probability over
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
. Then, there exists a function
\pi(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}),a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-1,1])}[\phi(v,a){\mathrm{ReLU}}(vh({\bm{x}})+a]-g_{*}(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)\right|=o(1),

with high probability over
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
. In addition,
\sup_{v,a}\left|\pi(v,a)\right|={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{2\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})})
.

Proof of Lemma
C.1
.

Since
\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})=2
implies
g_{*}
is even function, there exists a polynomial
\tilde{g}_{*}
such that
g_{*}(z)=\tilde{g}_{*}\left(z^{\mathrm{ge}(g_{*}))}\right)
. Let
\tilde{g}_{*}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\mathrm{deg}(\tilde{g}_{*})}s_{k}z^{k}
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, from Lemma 17 in
Damian et al. 2022
, there exists
\pi_{k}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for any
|z|\leq 1

\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}),a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-1,1])}\left[\pi_{k}^{\prime}(v,a){\mathrm{ReLU}}(vz+a)\right]=z^{k}\text{ and }\sup_{v,a}\left|\pi_{k}^{\prime}(v,a)\right|={\mathcal{O}}(1).

Let us define
\pi^{\prime}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\pi^{\prime}(v,a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\mathrm{deg}(\tilde{g}_{*})}s_{k}\frac{\pi_{k}^{\prime}\big(v,ap^{-1}(\log d)^{-2}\big)}{p(\log d)^{2}}(\log d)^{2k},

where
p:=P_{2}r^{-\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}
. Note that
\sup_{v,a}\left|\pi^{\prime}(v,a)\right|={\mathcal{O}}\left(p^{-1}(\log d)^{2\mathrm{deg}(g_{*})-2}\right)
and if
\left|z\right|\leq(\log d)^{2}
, then we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})\\
a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-p(\log d)^{2},p(\log d)^{2}])\end{subarray}}\left[\pi^{\prime}(v,a){\mathrm{ReLU}}\left(v(pz)+a\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\mathrm{deg}(\tilde{g}_{*})}s_{k}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})\\
a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-p(\log d)^{2},p(\log d)^{2}])\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{\pi_{k}^{\prime}(v,ap^{-1}(\log d)^{-2})}{p(\log d)^{2}}(\log d)^{2k}{\mathrm{ReLU}}\left(v(pz)+a\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\mathrm{deg}(\tilde{g}_{*})}s_{k}(\log d)^{2k}\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}),a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-1,1])}[\pi_{k}^{\prime}(v,a){\mathrm{ReLU}}(vz(\log d)^{-2}+a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\mathrm{deg}(\tilde{g}_{*})}s_{k}z^{k}=\tilde{g}_{*}(z).

Lastly, we define
\pi(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\pi(v,a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\mathbbm{1}[v=-1\wedge a\in[P_{1}-p(\log d)^{2},P_{1}+p(\log d)^{2}]]\pi^{\prime}(-1,b-P_{1})}{2p(\log d)^{2}}

(5)

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathbbm{1}[v=1\wedge a\in[-P_{1}-p(\log d)^{2},-P_{1}+p(\log d)^{2}]]\pi^{\prime}(1,b+P_{1})}{2p(\log d)^{2}},

then we have
\sup_{v,a}={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{2\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})})

With high probability,
\left|\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle\right|\leq(\log d)^{2}
and thus we have

\displaystyle\quad 2\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}),a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-1,1])}[\pi(v,a){\mathrm{ReLU}}(vh({\bm{x}})+b)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([P_{1}-p(\log d)^{2},P_{1}+p(\log d)^{2}])}\left[\pi^{\prime}(-1,b-P_{1}){\mathrm{ReLU}}(-P_{1}-p(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)^{\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})})-n({\bm{x}})+a\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([P_{1}-p(\log d)^{2},P_{1}+p(\log d)^{2}])}\left[\pi^{\prime}(1,b-P_{1}){\mathrm{ReLU}}(P_{1}+p(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)^{\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}+n({\bm{x}})+a)\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}),a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-p(\log d)^{2},p(\log d)^{2}])}\left[\pi^{\prime}(v,a){\mathrm{ReLU}}\left(vp\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle^{\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}+a\right)\right]+o(1)

\displaystyle=2g_{*}(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)+o(1).

Here, the second equality holds from the fact that
\sup_{v,a}\left|\pi^{\prime}(v,a)\right|={\mathcal{O}}\left(p^{-1}(\log d)^{2\mathrm{deg}(g_{*})-2}\right)
and
\left|n({\bm{x}})\right|=o\left(p(\log d)^{-2\mathrm{deg}(g_{*})+2}\right)
.

Therefore, we have the desired conclusion.
∎

Next, we prove that we can approximate the link function with a finite-width MLP.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
{\bm{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}^{m})
and
{\bm{a}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-1,1]^{m})
. Under the same condition of Lemma
C.1
, there exists
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{u}}^{\prime}[j]{\mathrm{ReLU}}({\bm{v}}[j]h({\bm{x}})+{\bm{a}}[j])-g_{*}(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)\right|=o(1)

with high probability over
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
. Furthermore,
\left\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\right\|^{2}={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(r^{3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}m^{-1}\right)
holds with high probability.

Proof of Lemma
C.2
.

We choose
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}
as
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}[j]=\pi({\bm{v}}[j],{\bm{a}}[j])/m
where
\pi(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is obtained from Lemma
C.1
. We will show that this choice satisfies the desired conclusions. Since
\left|h({\bm{x}})\right|\leq 1
with high probability and
\sup_{v,a}\left|\pi(v,a)\right|={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{2\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})})
, we can apply Höeffding’s inequality:

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{u}}^{\prime}[j]{\mathrm{ReLU}}({\bm{v}}[j]h({\bm{x}})+{\bm{a}}[j])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi({\bm{v}}[j],{\bm{a}}[j]){\mathrm{ReLU}}({\bm{v}}[j]h({\bm{x}})+{\bm{a}}[j])

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}),a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-1,1])}[\pi(v,a){\mathrm{ReLU}}(vh({\bm{x}})+a)]+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{2\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}m^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=g_{*}(\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{2\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}m^{-1/2})+o(1).

In addition, by applying Höeffding’s inequality, the following holds with high probability:

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=m^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi({\bm{v}}[j],{\bm{b}}[i])^{2}

\displaystyle=m^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}),a\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-1,1])}[\pi(v,a)^{2}]+{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{4\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}m^{-3/2}).

From (
5
),
\pi(v,a)
is nonzero with probability
{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{-\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})})
. Combining with
\sup_{v,a}\left|\pi(v,a)\right|={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{2\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})})
, we have desired conclusion.
∎

C.2
Characterization of Estimation Error on the Training Set

The following lemma characterizes estimation on the training set after pretraining.

Lemma C.3
.

There exists
\lambda_{2}>0
such that the following holds with probability at least
0.999
:

\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left|y^{t}-f({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right|=\tau+o(1)\text{ and }\left\|{\bm{u}}^{*}\right\|={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(r^{3\mathrm{ge}(g^{*})/2}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Proof of Lemma
C.3
.

From Proposition
B.5
, the condition in Lemma
C.1
is satisfied with probability at least
0.999
. Under this, let
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}
be the output layer parameter obtained from Lemma
C.2
. From the equivalence between
\ell_{2}
-regularization and norm-constrained optimization, there exists
\lambda_{2}>0
such that optimized parameter
{\bm{u}}^{*}
satisfies
\left\|{\bm{u}}^{*}\right\|\leq\left\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\right\|={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})/2}m^{-1/2})
and

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left|y^{t}-f({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left(y^{t}-f({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left(y^{t}-f({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\tau+o(1))^{2}.

∎

Appendix D
Test Error Analysis

In this section, we analyze the test-time estimation error:

{\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{test}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{test}})}[\left|f({\bm{Z}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-y\right|].

D.1
Test Error for Prompts with Pretraining Context Length

We first prove our conclusion for the case
N_{\mathrm{test}}=N_{\mathrm{pt}}
by establishing a generalization bound using Rademacher complexity.

We define a family of functions
{\mathcal{F}}_{U}
on inputs with context length
N_{\mathrm{test}}=N_{\mathrm{pt}}
as follows:

{\mathcal{F}}_{U}\coloneqq\left\{({\bm{Z}},y)\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{u}}[j]{\mathrm{ReLU}}\left({\bm{v}}^{*}[j]N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})+{\bm{a}}^{*}[j]\right)\middle|\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|\leq U\right\}.

In addition, the Rademacher complexity of
{\mathcal{F}}_{U}
for sample size
T_{2}
is defined as

\mathrm{Rad}_{T_{2}}({\mathcal{F}}_{U})=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})\\
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left(\{\pm 1\}^{T_{2}}\right)\end{subarray}}\left[\sup_{\tilde{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{U}}\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\bm{\epsilon}[t]\tilde{f}({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\right].

In the following lemma, we characterize the Rademacher complexity of
{\mathcal{F}}_{U}
.

Lemma D.1
.

It holds that

\mathrm{Rad}_{T_{2}}({\mathcal{F}}_{U})={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(Um^{1/2}T_{2}^{-1/2}\right).

Proof of Lemma
D.1
.

By sequentially applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathrm{Rad}_{T_{2}}({\mathcal{F}}_{U})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})\\
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left(\{\pm 1\}^{T_{2}}\right)\end{subarray}}\left[\sup_{\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|\leq U}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{u}}[j]\left(\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\bm{\epsilon}[t]{\mathrm{ReLU}}\Big({\bm{v}}^{*}[j]N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})+{\bm{a}}^{*}[j]\Big)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq A\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})\\
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left(\{\pm 1\}^{T_{2}}\right)\end{subarray}}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\bm{\epsilon}[t]{\mathrm{ReLU}}\Big({\bm{v}}^{*}[j]N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})+{\bm{a}}^{*}[j]\Big)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq A\left(\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bm{Z}}^{t},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})\\
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left(\{\pm 1\}^{T_{2}}\right)\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\bm{\epsilon}[t]{\mathrm{ReLU}}\Big({\bm{v}}^{*}[j]N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})+{\bm{a}}^{*}[j]\Big)\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=A\left(\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})}\left[\frac{1}{T^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}}\Big({\bm{v}}^{*}[j]N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})+{\bm{a}}^{*}[j]\Big)\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In addition, we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}}\Big({\bm{v}}^{*}[j]N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})+{\bm{a}}^{*}[j]\Big)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\left({\bm{v}}^{*}[j]N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})+{\bm{a}}^{*}[j]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\left({\bm{v}}^{*}[j]^{2}N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-2}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})^{2}+{\bm{a}}^{*}[j]^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(mT_{2}+mT_{2}\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})}\left[N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-2}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})^{2}\right]\right).

Let
({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})
and
{\bm{\beta}}
,
{\bm{x}}
be their feature vector and query data, respectively. From Lemma
A.1
, with high probability over
({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})
, we have

N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})=P_{1}+P_{2}\left(\frac{\langle{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{x}}\rangle}{r}\right)^{\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}\!\!\!\!+o\left(P_{2}r^{-\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}(\log d)^{-2\mathrm{deg}(g_{*})}\right)={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(1).

It implies
\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})}\left[N_{\mathrm{pt}}^{-2}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})^{2}\right]={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(1)
and it leads to our desired conclusion.
∎

Next, we obtain the following result on test error.

Lemma D.2
.

With probability at least
0.995
, it holds that
{\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-\tau=o(1)
.

Proof of Lemma
D.2
.

From Lemma
C.3
, with probability at least 0.999, we can choose
U={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})/2}m^{-1/2})
such that
{\bm{u}}^{*}\leq U
and we have

\displaystyle\quad{\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left|y^{t}-f\left({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left({\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left|y^{t}-f\left({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*}\right)\right|\right)-\tau

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{U}}\left({\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left|y^{t}-f\left({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*}\right)\right|\right)+o(1),

where
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{U}:={\mathcal{F}}_{U}\cup\{({\bm{Z}},y)\mapsto y\}
.
Using the standard symmetrization argument (Proposition 4.2 in
Bach 2024
), we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tilde{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{U}}\left({\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left|y^{t}-f\left({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*}\right)\right|\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{Rad}_{T_{2}}(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{U})

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{Rad}_{T_{w}}({\mathcal{F}}_{U})+\frac{2}{T_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})\\
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left(\{\pm 1\}^{T_{2}}\right)\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\left|\bm{\epsilon}[t]y^{t}\right|\right],

where the second inequality holds since
{\mathcal{F}}_{U}
contains zero function. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can also bound the second term as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})\\
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left(\{\pm 1\}^{T_{2}}\right)\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\left|\bm{\epsilon}[t]y^{t}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bm{Z}}^{t},y^{t})\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})\\
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left(\{\pm 1\}^{T_{2}}\right)\end{subarray}}\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\bm{\epsilon}[t]y^{t}\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{T_{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N_{\mathrm{pt}})}\left[\left(y^{t}\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Combining with Lemma
D.1
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\tilde{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{U}}\left({\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left|y^{t}-f\left({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*}\right)\right|\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}\left(r^{3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})/2}T_{2}^{-1/2}\right)=o(1).

Note that
\sup_{\tilde{f}\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{U}}\left({\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=T_{1}+1}^{T_{1}+T_{2}}\left|y^{t}-f\left({\bm{Z}}^{t},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*}\right)\right|\right)
is always non-negative due to
({\bm{Z}},y)\mapsto y\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{U}
. Therefore, by applying Markov’s inequality, we conclude that with probability at least
0.995
,

{\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-\tau=o(1).

∎

D.2
Test Error for Prompts with General Length

For the last step, we extend the result of the test error to a general test time context length
N_{\mathrm{test}}=\tilde{\Omega}\left(r^{3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})}\right)
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

To use the result of Lemma
D.2
, we bound the following quantity:

\displaystyle\quad\left|{\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{test}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-{\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N^{*})}\left[\left|y-f({\bm{Z}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right|-\left|y-f({\bm{Z}}_{N_{\mathrm{test}}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right|\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{Z}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}(N^{*})}\left[\left|f({\bm{Z}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-f({\bm{Z}}_{N_{\mathrm{test}}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right|\right].

Here,
Z_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}
and
Z_{N_{\mathrm{test}}}
are input embeddings consisting of the first
N_{\mathrm{pt}}
and
N_{\mathrm{test}}
context examples, respectively, along with the same query
{\bm{x}}
, when given an prompt
{\bm{Z}}
. From Lemma
A.1
, the following holds with high probability:

\left|N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}_{{\mathrm{pt}}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})-N_{\mathrm{test}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}_{\mathrm{test}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})\right|=o\left(r^{-3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})/2}(\log d)^{-2\mathrm{deg}(g_{*})+2-C_{P_{2}})}\right).

Combining with Lipschitz continuity of ReLU, this implies

\displaystyle\quad\left|f({\bm{Z}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-f({\bm{Z}}_{N_{\mathrm{test}}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|{\bm{u}}^{*}[j]\right|\left|N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}_{{\mathrm{pt}}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})-N_{\mathrm{test}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}_{\mathrm{test}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\bm{u}}\right\|m^{1/2}\left|N_{{\mathrm{pt}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}_{{\mathrm{pt}}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})-N_{\mathrm{test}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Mamba}}({\bm{Z}}_{\mathrm{test}};{\bm{\gamma}}^{*})\right|

\displaystyle={\tilde{\mathcal{O}}}(r^{3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})/2}m^{-1/2})\cdot m^{1/2}\cdot o\left(r^{-3\mathrm{ge}(g_{*})/2}(\log d)^{-2\mathrm{deg}(g_{*})+2-C_{P_{2}})}\right)

\displaystyle=o(1),

where we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the last inequality, and the last equality holds since we can make
C_{P_{2}}
arbitrarily large. Therefore, we have

\left|{\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{test}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})-{\mathcal{R}}_{N_{\mathrm{pt}}}({\bm{\gamma}}^{*},{\bm{u}}^{*},{\bm{v}}^{*},{\bm{a}}^{*})\right|=o(1),

and this implies that our desired conclusion holds with probability at least 0.99.
∎
