Prevalence and risk factors of asthma, allergic rhinitis, and atopic dermatitis among schoolchildren in Sri Lanka
Authors: Maneshka Vindesh Karunananda, Thushali Ranasinghe, Inoka Sepali Aberathna, Saubhagya Danasekara, Suranga Fernando, Gamini Jayakody, Segarajasingam Arulkumaran, Nayana Yasindu Samaraweera, Sarathchandra Kumarawansha, Subramaniyam Sivaganesh, Priyadarshanie Geethika Amarasinghe, Chintha Jayasinghe, Dilini Wijesekara, Manonath Bandara Marasinghe, Udari Mambulage, Helanka Wijayatilake, Kasun Senevirathne, Aththidayage Don Priyantha Bandara, Chandana Pushpalal Gallage, Nilu Ranmali Colambage, Ampe Arachchige Thilak Udayasiri, Tharaka Lokumarambage, Yasanayakalage Upasena, Wickramasinghe Pathiranalage Kasun Paramee Weerasooriya, Gathsaurie Neelika Malavige, Chandima Jeewandara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs the prevalence of asthma, allergic rhinitis (AR), and atopic dermatitis (AD) rises globally, it is important to determine the prevalence of these diseases in different geographic regions to identify potential modifiable risk factors.ObjectiveWe sought to determine the prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD in Sri Lankan children living in different climatic and geographical regions and to identify host and environmental risk factors.MethodsWe recruited 5043 children (51.9% girls) age 10 to 19 years from 9 districts representing Sri Lanka using stratified multistage cluster sampling. Data were collected using the International Society for Allergies and Asthma in Childhood questionnaire for children. Statistical significance was estimated using Fisher exact and χ2 tests for association.ResultsPrevalence of asthma, AR, and AD was 8.25%, 10.01%, and 2.8%, respectively. Children living in urban areas and children with higher body mass index were significantly more likely to have asthma. Children living in households that used cow dung for floors and clay/wattle and daub for walls were less likely to have asthma and AR, but not AD. The prevalence of asthma was higher in children living in houses with tiled floors (P = .03) and in which gas was used for cooking (P < .05), whereas prevalence of AR was significantly higher in households with domestic animals.ConclusionThe presence of other allergic diseases, BMI, and urbanicity together with certain environmental conditions was associated with the prevalence of these diseases. Therefore, environmental modification as well as lifestyle interventions may help to reduce the prevalence of these allergic diseases.

There has been a marked rise in chronic allergic diseases such as asthma, allergic rhinitis (AR) and atopic dermatitis (AD) globally. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 revealed that there was a 15.4% increase in the number of patients with asthma from 1990 to 2019, with the incidence and prevalence of asthma significantly increasing in countries including India and the United States.1 The number of individuals with asthma is predicted to increase by 5.5% from 2021 to 2050, although the age-standardized asthma prevalence is likely to remain stable due to the aging population, especially in the Asia-Pacific region.2 Many individuals with asthma are also affected by AR. The prevalence of self-reported AR in children has been shown to be 18.12%, with physician-diagnosed AR in children estimated to be 10.48%.3 The number of cases of AD have also risen, although the age-standardized incidence rate has declined.1 The number of individuals with AD is forecasted to rise by 15.3% from 2021 to 2050, with the disease prevalence rising predominantly in females (57.3%).2 Therefore, given that these chronic allergic diseases are likely to rise, it is important to determine the prevalence of these diseases in different geographic regions and identify potential modifiable risk factors to initiate possible countermeasures. These modifiable risk factors include passive smoking, indoor air pollution, exposure to indoor allergens, as well as childhood obesity.4,5

As asthma, AR, and AD are chronic diseases, they significantly affect the quality of life and education of children, especially in resource-poor settings. Indeed, the age-standardized disability-adjusted life years for asthma was found to be highest in South Asia, whereas for AD it was highest in high-income regions.2 Lower socioeconomic status has been associated with worse outcomes of asthma, including more frequent visits to the emergency department, hospital admissions, and frequent exacerbations.6 Sri Lanka is a lower-middle-income country with a nominal gross domestic product per capita of approximately US $4500, and nearly a quarter of the population lives below the poverty line, reflecting persistent socioeconomic challenges despite relatively high literacy and human development indicators.7 Although public hospitals offer free health care to all Sri Lankans, many public hospitals are not adequately funded to carry out all relevant tests and offer essential medicines such as inhalers free of charge to all patients.8 Further, because of significant delays and overcrowding of public hospitals, many Sri Lankans who can afford it seek private health care.9 The access to health care and medicines has worsened with the recent economic crisis in Sri Lanka, with a 40% increase in the price of some medicines.10 It has been shown that health inequalities influence the prevalence, diagnosis, management, and outcomes of allergic diseases.11 Accordingly, evaluating socioeconomic differences in the prevalence of chronic allergic diseases is essential for guiding targeted diagnostic and preventive strategies in Sri Lanka, a country markedly impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic and ensuing economic crisis.

Although Sri Lanka has higher incidence rates of asthma compared with other South Asian countries, age-standardized mortality rates are lowest in Sri Lanka.6 However, a recent study among children 13 to 14 years old in rural Sri Lanka showed that 29.9% of them had poorly controlled asthma, with the presence of concurrent AR being a significant risk factor.12 Another study reported that the presence of AR was associated with an increased risk of severe asthma in rural Sri Lanka.13 One important risk factor for asthma related to disability-adjusted life years and deaths was a high body mass index (BMI), along with occupational allergens and smoking.1 Because there is a marked rise of obesity in many developing countries including Sri Lanka,14 along with environmental pollution, it is important to assess the impact of these changing sociodemographic factors in the prevalence of asthma and other allergic diseases in Sri Lanka. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic disproportionately affected many communities, with studies reporting increases in both childhood wasting and obesity during the postpandemic period.15 In parallel, the subsequent economic crisis in Sri Lanka led to an approximately 40% increase in medicine costs, potentially limiting access to health care and appropriate treatment.10 Therefore, it is important to assess whether changes in nutritional status and access to health care have disproportionately influenced the prevalence and management of chronic allergic diseases among marginalized groups residing in urban, rural, and estate (tea and plantation worker) regions.

The International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC) was established in 1990 to describe the prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD in children living in different regions, enabling comparisons to be made between countries.16 This study uses standardized questionnaires so that comparisons between prevalence of these diseases and risk factors can be identified in different countries. Previous ISAAC studies were conducted among children age 6 to 7 and 13 to 14 in 2001 and 2013 in 9 provinces in Sri Lanka.17 Subsequent studies have been limited to only 1 or 2 districts in Sri Lanka.13,18 Thus, the prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD among schoolchildren in Sri Lanka has not been recently studied. Given the rapid socioeconomic and health system changes Sri Lanka has experienced in recent years, findings from earlier studies restricted to specific communities or regions are unlikely to accurately reflect the current burden of allergic diseases nationwide. Furthermore, prior studies in Sri Lanka have largely focused on asthma, with limited nationwide data on other allergic diseases such as AR and AD. To address this gap and inform more targeted prevention and management strategies, we conducted an island-wide study to assess the prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD across urban, rural, and estate communities and across different age groups in Sri Lanka.

Methods

Study participants and sampling technique

Sri Lanka has 9 provinces and 25 districts. To investigate prevalence rates for asthma, AR, and AD in Sri Lanka, 1 representative district from each province was randomly selected (see Fig E1 in this article’s Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org). Schools were selected randomly within each district using a stratified multistage cluster sampling approach. Of the 4.24 million total students enrolled in schools, more than 4.02 million (>95%) were enrolled in public schools, with the remainder in private and pirivena (monastic) schools.19 Children age 10 to 19 attending public schools were recruited from September 2022 to March 2023 following receipt of informed written consent from the parent(s)/guardian(s) and informed written assent from the children.

Demographic and clinical data on asthma, AR, and AD were collected by interviewers who were qualified medical professionals using the ISAAC Surveillance and Management Questionnaire16 developed by the Global Allergy Network. This questionnaire was previously translated into the local language and validated across several studies, which formed the basis for its adaptation and use in this study.20 Children who relocated into the districts within 12 months and children with comorbidities such as special needs, immunodeficiencies, and chronic diseases apart from allergies were excluded from the study.

A stratified multistage cluster sampling method was used to select the schools in each district, with a cluster size of 40 students at a selected school. Probability proportional to size sampling technique was used to select the sample size from each district, as the population size and urbanicity grade varied in different districts. Derived from the latest census for Sri Lanka,21 schools in urban areas were defined as those situated in areas administered by municipal and urban councils, whereas schools in estate areas were defined as those situated in plantations (extent of 20 acres or more and with 10 or more resident laborers). Schools situated in neither urban nor estate areas were classified as being in rural areas. The modifications carried out in the questionnaire, training and validation, and handling self-reported bias are described in the Methods section in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org.

Ethics approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the Faculty of Medical Sciences, University of Sri Jayewardenepura, Sri Lanka (COVID 13/21). Administrative clearance was obtained from the Ministry of Health and the Ministry of Education, Sri Lanka. Informed written consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian for all participants, with assent obtained from participants when developmentally appropriate, in accordance with International Committee of Medical Journal Editors guidelines.

Definitions of asthma, AR, and AD

According to the guidance provided in the ISAAC questionnaires,22 respondents who answered yes to “Have you (has your child) had wheezing or whistling noise coming from the chest in the past 12 months?” were defined as having asthma. Respondents who answered, yes to “In the past 12 months, have you (has your child) had a problem with sneezing or a runny or blocked nose when you (he/she) did not have a cold or the flu?” were defined as having AR. Respondents who answered yes to “Have you (has your child) had this itchy rash at any time in the past 12 months?” and yes to “Has this itchy rash at any time affected any of the following places: the folds of the elbows; behind the knees; in front of the ankles; under the buttocks; or around the neck, ears, or eyes?” were defined as having AD.

Patients were considered to have self-diagnosed asthma, AR, or AD if they responded “yes” to the presence of these diseases in the questionnaire, but not further diagnosed by a physician. In Sri Lanka, all patients who have a physician-diagnosed allergy carry with them a diagnosis card (if admitted to the hospital) or carry a clinical record book (if they received only outpatient care). Therefore, participants were considered to have physician-diagnosed asthma, AR, or AD if they had a diagnosis card or a clinic follow-up book.

Assessment of BMI

Anthropometric measurements were taken to understand the relationship between BMI and prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD. Height was measured by a stadiometer to within 0.5 cm, and weight was measured using a digital scale, which was calibrated regularly throughout the study. The BMI centile was derived by plotting the values on the World Health Organization BMI for age growth charts for boys or girls to acquire the percentile ranking, as this was shown to be the most suitable indicator for growth patterns in children.23

Statistical analysis

The World Health Organization defines ages 10 to 19 years as adolescents and 15 to 24 years as youth.4 Therefore, for the purpose of analysis, we divided the population into 2 age categories: 10 to 14 years (adolescents) and 15 to 19 years (youth).24 Data were entered using ArcGIS Survey 123 (https://survey123.arcgis.com/), and IBM SPSS Statistics 22 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY) and RStudio (Posit, Boston, Mass) software were used for statistical analysis. Statistical significance was estimated using Fisher exact and χ2 tests for association. The relative association of the exposure and the binary outcome was quantified using binary logistic regression analysis at the 5% significance level. Heat map symbology was obtained using ArcGIS Pro version 3.1.1. Associations between household/environmental factors and asthma were assessed using multivariable logistic regression. The outcome variable was asthma (yes/no), and predictor variables included wall material, floor material, roof material, cooking method, and other relevant household/environmental factors. To address potential multicollinearity, we evaluated correlations among predictors and calculated variance inflation factors, all of which were below or equal to 1, indicating acceptable levels of collinearity. We also used recursive feature elimination to select the most informative predictors and reduce redundancy. Multiple-testing correction was not applied, as logistic regression evaluates all predictors within a single model; therefore, interpretation was based primarily on odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs rather than P values alone.

Results

For this study, 5043 children, 2618 (51.91%) girls and 2425 (48.09%) boys, between the ages of 10 and 19 (mean [SD] age = 14 [2.80]) were recruited from 9 districts. The demographic characteristics of the population are shown in Table E1 (in the Online Repository at www.jaci-global.org).

The overall prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD was 8.25%, 10.01%, and 2.80%, respectively. Of children, 4.94% had asthma alone, 6.92% had AR alone, and 1.86% had AD alone (Fig 1). Asthma and AR were reported by 2.64%, asthma and AD were reported by 0.48%, and AR and AD were reported by 0.26% of participants. In 416 (8.25%) children, parents/children self-diagnosed asthma during the past 12 months, which was significantly higher (P < .001), than physician-diagnosed asthma in 222 (4.4%) participants (Table E2 in the Online Repository at www.jaci-global.org). Discussion

In this study, we assessed the prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD among children attending public school in Sri Lanka and found that the overall prevalence of asthma was 8.25%, prevalence of AR was 10.01%, and prevalence of AD was 2.80%. These prevalence rates are lower than reported rates in Sri Lanka in 2013, which were 18.1% of children age 6- to 7 and 17.5% of adolescents age 13- to 14 with a current wheezing condition.17 The overall prevalence of AR and AD were not described in the previous studies.17 Although the prevalence of asthma appears to have declined in Sri Lanka over time, different age groups were included in the 2013 study (approximately 50% of participants were age 6-7) study versus ours (participants were age 10-19). Furthermore, the prevalence of self-reported asthma, AR, and AD was substantially higher than that of physician-diagnosed disease. This discrepancy may reflect misclassification in self-reporting, limited health care utilization for formal diagnosis, or the use of alternative treatment pathways including traditional Sri Lankan medicine. These differences may also reflect economic constraints and inequitable access to health care services, which could limit opportunities for diagnostic evaluation and treatment. However, as this study was not designed to assess health-seeking behaviors, the underlying reasons for these discrepancies could not be examined.

Asthma symptoms are known to decline with age, with each additional year in children younger than 10 associated with an 18% reduction in symptom days and a 28% reduction in symptom nights.25 We observed that the prevalence of asthma was significantly less in youth age 15 to 19 compared with the younger age groups. Therefore, the difference in asthma prevalence between 2013 and 2025 in Sri Lanka is likely to be due to differences in age groups included in the study. Current asthma prevalence in Sri Lanka is similar to prevalence of asthma in India (7.9%)26 and many other Asian countries such as Malaysia, Pakistan, and Vietnam, but lower than the prevalence reported in Singapore, Bangladesh, some Latin American countries, the United States, and some European countries.27

The concurrent presence of AR, AD, and a higher BMI was significantly associated with an increase in prevalence of asthma as reported in many other studies.2 Obese children with asthma were more likely to have severe asthma, experience more frequent asthma exacerbations, and require hospitalization with reduced responses to inhaled corticosteroids.4,28 Given that the prevalence of childhood obesity is rising, especially in lower-middle-income countries such as Sri Lanka, public health education programs targeting childhood obesity would also have an impact on reducing the prevalence and severity of asthma.4

Although a meta-analysis showed a higher prevalence of asthma among males in Asia,29 in the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 for asthma and AD showed that the prevalence of asthma and AD was higher in females.2 Female sex has shown to be associated with a higher prevalence of asthma after puberty,30,31 which represented most children in our study.

The lower prevalence of asthma among male youth age 10 to 19 has been attributed to the increase in testosterone levels,32 which has been attributed to reduction in TH2 cells and cytokines mediating airway inflammation by higher testosterone levels.33 Studies in mouse models have shown that ovalbumin (OVA)-sensitized and OVA-challenged female mice had higher OVA-specific serum IgE levels, higher IL-5 and eosinophils, and increased airway remodeling.34,35 Therefore, sex hormones appear to play a significant role in modulating asthma and possibly other chronic allergic conditions.

Children in urban areas living in houses using tiles for floors and gas for cooking were significantly more likely to have asthma. Although many studies have shown that use of firewood was associated with asthma,36,37 our findings showed that using gas for cooking was associated with asthma. Gas cooking is an important indoor air pollutant and has been shown to increase the frequency of respiratory symptoms.38 However, some studies have shown that there has been certain bias in studies showing the association between gas cooking and occurrence of respiratory symptoms and asthma.39 In fact, many of the houses using firewood have stoves outside their houses and, in most instances, very open kitchens. In contrast, use of gas for cooking is more prevalent in urban areas, and in most instances, the kitchens are less ventilated than in rural areas. Therefore, the association of gas cooking with increased prevalence of asthma could be due to urbanicity rather than use of gas stoves itself.

The presence of domestic animals was associated with an increase in risk of AR, possibly due to sensitization to animal dander. Many studies had shown that early exposure to domestic pets was associated with a lower risk of developing allergies, attributed to exposure to diverse microbial products.40,41 Interestingly, we found that children living in houses using cow dung floors and clay/wattle and daub for walls were less likely to have asthma and AR, possibly due to exposure to diverse microbial products in clay and cow dung. This aligns with the observations of the initial studies describing the hygiene hypothesis, which showed that living on a farm reduced the prevalence of asthma, AR, and atopic sensitization.42 It was subsequently shown that diversity of environmental microbial exposure was inversely related to the risk of asthma.43 Furthermore, household dust in houses with pets, especially dogs, had a significantly more diverse microbiome than household dust in houses without any pets.44 Therefore, comparative analyses of household dust exposures and gut microbiome composition in children living in traditional households with cow dung floors and clay walls versus children living in urban households would be informative in elucidating differences in microbial diversity and composition. Improved understanding of early-life environmental and microbial factors associated with allergic disease development may help inform targeted strategies for allergy prevention.

This study was limited to participants age 10 to 19, preventing evaluation of allergic disease prevalence in early and late childhood. Moreover, although proportionate sampling was employed, logistical challenges during the economic crisis in Sri Lanka, including electricity and fuel shortages, may have contributed to recruitment bias in some communities.

Conclusions

We have studied the prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD in Sri Lanka using the ISAAC tools and identified risk factors for developing these chronic allergic diseases. The presence of another allergic disease was the strongest factor associated with the prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD, whereas urbanicity, type of material used for housing, method of cooking, and presence of domestic animals were associated with the prevalence of asthma, AR, and AD.Key messages•In Sri Lankan school-aged children, the overall prevalences of asthma, AR, and AD were 8.25%, 10.01%, and 2.8%, respectively.•Asthma prevalence was significantly associated with AR, AD, and increased BMI.•Urbanicity, type of housing material, cooking method, and presence of domestic animals were associated with atopic disease prevalence.
