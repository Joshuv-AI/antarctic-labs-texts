Impact of the COVID‐19 Pandemic on Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Related to Avian Influenza Among Poultry Workers in Cambodia
Authors: Malen Chan, Frida E Sparaciari, Sowath Ly, Kunthy Nguon, Kimsan Souv, Samantha Colquhoun, Claude Flamand, Erik A Karlsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLive bird markets (LBMs) are high‐risk environments for the amplification and transmission of avian influenza viruses (AIVs). In Cambodia, limited data exist on knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) among poultry workers, who represent a key occupational risk group. This study assessed KAP related to avian influenza (AI) among poultry workers in three major Cambodian LBMs, with a focus on changes before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey of 100 poultry workers was conducted using a structured questionnaire covering socio‐demographics, AI awareness, personal protective equipment (PPE) use, training participation, and preventive behaviors. Comparisons were made between the pre‐COVID‐19 pandemic and COVID‐19 pandemic periods using McNemar's test for dichotomous variables, and the symmetry chi‐square and marginal homogeneity tests for variables with more than two categories, to determine whether response distributions changed significantly over time.ResultsThe proportion of workers earning below USD 350 per month increased from 41% before the pandemic to 52% during the pandemic (p < 0.001). PPE use declined significantly: Face mask use dropped from 58% to 40% (p = 0.003), while reports of no PPE use rose from 21% to 49% (p < 0.001). Awareness of avian influenza decreased from 81% to 59% (p < 0.001), and participation in influenza‐related training fell from 30% to 10% (p < 0.001). Workers primarily relied on informal information channels, with veterinary officers identified as trusted but underutilized sources.ConclusionsDespite moderate baseline awareness of avian influenza, preventive practices among Cambodian poultry workers were limited and further declined during the COVID‐19 pandemic, reflecting broader economic and structural challenges. Supporting affordable access to PPE, strengthening market‐based training, and expanding culturally adapted, multiplatform risk communication strategies will be essential. Integrated interventions, combined with enhanced surveillance during peak poultry trade periods, can help reduce occupational exposure and interrupt zoonotic transmission pathways in LBMs.Keywords: Avian influenza viruses, knowledge attitude and practices, live bird markets and risk behavior, poultry workers

Introduction

Avian influenza viruses (AIVs) remain a significant global One Health concern. The first documented zoonotic transmission of highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) A(H5N1) occurred in Hong Kong in 1997 [1, 2]. Since then, AIVs have persisted as a recurrent health threat, causing outbreaks in poultry and occasionally spilling over into humans. Cambodia reported its first laboratory‐confirmed outbreak in poultry of HPAIV A(H5N1) to the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) on January 24, 2004 [3], followed by the first human case in 2005 [4]. Although human infections remain relatively rare, transmission is primarily linked to direct contact with infected poultry or contaminated environments. Sustained human‐to‐human transmission has not been documented [5, 6]. Live bird markets (LBMs) serve as a high‐risk environment for potential zoonotic transmission, particularly in poultry‐dense regions like Cambodia [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

LBMs are critical environments for the replication, persistence, amplification, and dissemination of AIVs [12, 13, 14]. In Cambodia, these markets are central to the poultry supply chain [15], but they also sustain continuous viral circulation, with peak detection occurring during festival seasons such as Lunar New Year and Khmer New Year, when poultry demand increases sharply. Previous studies in LBMs have reported AIV prevalence rates of up to 50% in ducks and 40% in poultry, indicating substantial risks of exposure for both humans and animals [8, 9]. Effective, active surveillance, safe handling practices, and targeted mitigation strategies are essential to reducing AIV risk in LBMs [13]. Poultry workers, such as sellers and slaughterers, are considered a high‐risk occupational group for AIV infection due to their frequent exposure and direct contact with infected birds or their body fluids [8, 12, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Poultry trade in Cambodia also relies heavily on middlemen (individuals who purchase live poultry and sell to secondary locations), facilitating trade between markets within Cambodia and across borders with Thailand and Vietnam [22, 23]. This movement of live poultry created interconnected trade hubs that can facilitate AIV dissemination [23]. Consequently, the large number of individuals in daily, direct contact with potentially infected birds creates frequent opportunities for human exposure to AIVs.

Cambodia reported its first confirmed case of Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) on January 27th, 2020 [24], 3 days before the World Health Organization (WHO) declared Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) a public health emergency of international concern [25]. In response, the Cambodian government implemented stringent public health measures, including lockdowns, physical distancing and travel restrictions. The government also implemented temporary market closures [26]. Cambodia fully reopened on November 1st, 2021, and WHO officially declared that COVID‐19 was no longer a public health emergency on May 5th, 2023 [27, 28].

Beyond its immediate health impacts, the COVID‐19 pandemic also reshaped zoonotic risk dynamics in LBMs in ways directly relevant to avian influenza, including market closures and disruptions to avian influenza training programs as resources were redirected towards the COVID‐19 response [29, 30]. Early in the pandemic, there was widespread adoption of personal protective equipment (PPE), particularly face masks; however, shortages and discomfort led to inconsistent use and declining adherence over time, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries where supply remained limited [31, 32]. In addition, market closures and economic disruptions altered the socioeconomics and lives of market sellers. In Bangladesh, survey data indicated that the most common negative impacts included increased living expenses (67%) and loss of employment (65%). Conversely, the pandemic was also associated with improvements in market hygiene, personal hygiene, and compliance with social distancing measures, each reported by 100% of respondents [33]. Similarly, in Cambodia, although there is limited evidence among poultry markers workers, the pandemic‐induced economic shocks that transformed the poultry sector, as many unemployed tour guides shifted to small‐scale backyard farming with limited biosecurity [34]. These activities often lacked formal training and included unsafe practices such as improper carcass disposal, thereby creating additional opportunities for avian influenza spillover. Taken together, these disruptions highlight the importance of assessing how poultry workers' knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) on avian influenza shifted during the pandemic.

Institut Pasteur du Cambodge (IPC) has conducted long‐term research and active surveillance on AIV prevalence and transmission risk factors in Cambodian LBMs, in close collaboration with market stakeholders. This study addressed an important knowledge gap by assessing poultry workers' KAP related to avian influenza in three major Cambodian LBMs. In the context of COVID‐19‐associated disruptions to market activities, training, and occupational practices, we examined reported changes before and during COVID‐19 and identified opportunities to strengthen occupational safety and zoonotic disease prevention within a One Health framework.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval

The study protocol was approved by the Cambodian National Ethics Committee for Health Research (Protocol No. 2022/303 NECHR) and the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Australian National University (Protocol No. 2022/669). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study adhered to principles of confidentiality, voluntary participation, and respect for participant autonomy.

Study Design and Study Sites

A cross‐sectional study with a retrospective comparative component was conducted between December 2022 and January 2023, at three distinct LBMs in Cambodia (Figure 1). The pre‐COVID‐19 period was defined as the time before January 2020. The selected LBMs included Orussey Market and Chbar Ampov Market in Phnom Penh, and Takeo Market in Takeo province. These markets were purposively selected based on high poultry trading volume, their importance within regional poultry distribution networks and their geographic distribution, with Orussey Market being the largest and most densely populated. IPC has conducted long‐term work in these specific LBMs and maintains strong relationships with market managers, sellers, transporters (people who deliver poultry from one to other places), and middlemen (people who buy and re‐sell poultry), which facilitated access and encouraged open participation. All three LBMs operate similarly, sourcing poultry from multiple regions and selling both live and slaughtered poultry.

Map of Cambodia showing the locations of the three live bird markets (LBMs) included in the study: Orussey Market and Chbar Ampov Market in Phnom Penh and Takeo Market in Takeo province.

Study Population and Data Collection

Poultry workers were approached during regular marketing hours and invited to participate in face‐to‐face interviews using a structured questionnaire. Eligible participants included those involved in poultry selling, slaughtering, transportation or intermediary trade activities within the markets. Additionally, workers with less than 1 year of experience were included, as they were working at a high‐risk site. Interviews were conducted individually and anonymously in Khmer by trained interviewers after obtaining written informed consent. The survey was designed to assess KAP related to AI, including self‐reported behaviors and perceived changes in risk awareness before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Participants were asked to retrospectively report their practices during the pandemic period. Individual interviews were conducted in private settings within the markets, when possible, to ensure confidentiality and minimize social desirability bias. Each interview lasted approximately 30 min.

Data collection was conducted using paper case report forms and subsequently entered into REDCap (v15.1.1) following standard quality control procedures to ensure data accuracy and completeness.

Questionnaire

A structured questionnaire was adapted from previously published KAP studies, with substantial modifications to meet the objective of this research [35, 36, 37, 38]. The final instrument consisted of 45 questions combining closed‐ and open‐ended items into two sections: (i) personal and demographic information (including age, gender, education, role, and years of experience) and (ii) avian influenza‐related KAP, including perceived impacts of COVID‐19 on income and working hours, vaccination status, health‐seeking behaviors and prevention practices. PPE use was defined as self‐reported use of any of the following items while working in the market: face masks, gloves, boots, or aprons. No other PPE items were assessed. Participants could report more than one PPE item, and respondents who reported using none of these items were classified as reporting “no PPE use.” Several questions explicitly distinguished between the period before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic to allow retrospective within‐participant comparisons. Open‐ended questions included participants to describe their perceptions of avian influenza (e.g., symptom recognition and perceived transmission routes), perceived risk reduction strategies, sources of information and training needs, and changes in work practices and COVID‐19 safety concerns.

The questionnaire was initially developed in English, then translated into Khmer, and pilot‐tested with a small number of poultry workers to ensure clarity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehension prior to data collection.

Quantitative Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize socio‐demographic variables and KAP responses. KAP scores were calculated as follows: The knowledge score was based on correct responses to 10 questions related to AI transmission and risk factors; the attitude score was derived from three questions on avian influenza risk perception; and the practice score was based on three items on preventive behaviors. A composite KAP score was generated by summing the individual components, and scores above the median were classified as adequate (Supporting Information). Because each participant reported their knowledge, attitudes and practices for both the pre‐COVID‐19 pandemic period and the current situation, the two time points were treated as paired, within‐participant observations. Changes between periods were assessed using McNemar's test for dichotomous variables. For categorical variables with more than two response categories, the Stuart‐Maxwell test of marginal homogeneity served as the primary test of whether the distributions of responses changed between periods and the Bowker test of symmetry was reported to describe the pattern of within‐participant transitions. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18 (StataCorp, Texas, USA), and a two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative Analysis

Responses to open‐ended questions were analyzed thematically using an inductive approach. Narrative responses were reviewed repeatedly and coded in Excel to identify patterns and recurring themes related to hygiene practices, poultry sourcing, disease prevention knowledge, and vaccination attitudes. Themes were compared across prepandemic and pandemic periods to capture changes in practices and perceptions.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics of Poultry Workers

A total of 100 participants were enrolled in the study: 37 from Orussey Market, 23 from the Chbar Ampov Market, and 40 from Takeo Market. The sample had an equal gender distribution (50% male and 50% female). Participants ranged in age from 18 to 68 years, with 57% between 18 and 43 years old. Regarding education, 9% reported no formal schooling (never attended any school), 7% had nonformal education (e.g., NGO‐run literacy programs or temple‐based classes), 35% completed primary school, and 40% completed secondary school. A smaller proportion reported higher educational attainment, with 6% completing high school diplomas, and 3% attended college/university or above (3%). In terms of employment status, 70% of participants identified as business owners, while 30% were employed as workers. Primary market roles included poultry sellers (72%), slaughterers (20%), middlemen (5%), and transporters (3%), noting that roles were not mutually exclusive, as many owners also engaged in selling activities. Nearly half of participants (49%) had more than 10 years of experience working in LBMs, while 25% reported 6–10 years, 19% reported 2–5 years, and 7% had less than 1 year of experience. The COVID‐19 caused major disruptions, with 70% of workers reporting that they temporarily stopped working at some point during the peak of the pandemic. Behavioral and Health‐Related Characteristics

Behavioral and health‐related characteristics before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic are presented in Table 2. Several indicators changed significantly during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Monthly income declined overall (p < 0.001), with a higher proportion of workers earning less than $350 USD (52% vs. 41%) and fewer earning more than $1000 USD (3% vs. 15%). Participation in influenza‐related training declined sharply (p < 0.001), from 30% before the pandemic to only 10% during the pandemic. Seasonal influenza vaccination coverage also decreased from 20% to 9% (p = 0.011). Daily poultry contact, interest in seasonal influenza vaccination, and healthcare‐seeking behavior for respiratory symptoms remained relatively stable. In both periods, pharmacies were the most common first point of care for respiratory symptoms (e.g., fever, sneezing, or coughing: 41% prepandemic and 35% during the COVID‐19 pandemic). About 2% of workers reported taking no action or being unsure how to respond to illness in both periods.

Knowledge of and Experiences Related to AI

Knowledge and awareness of avian influenza declined significantly during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The proportion of poultry workers who reported having heard of AI decreased from 81% before the pandemic to 59% during (p < 0.001), with those reporting no awareness increasing from 15% to 41%. Knowledge that AI transmits through contact with infected poultry decreased slightly (62%–57%, p = 0.010), while beliefs about airborne transmission and the seriousness of AI showed significant declines (both < 0.05). However, knowledge of potential transmission routes remained stable, including contact with infected humans (p = 0.249), cages (p = 0.072), and water (p = 319). Perceptions of disease severity and preventability showed little change over time: Most workers recognized avian influenza as a serious disease (59% vs. 52%) and believed that it could be prevented (82% vs. 86%). Concern about family or friends becoming infected was consistently high (67% vs. 69%). Detailed results are presented in Table 3.

Attitudes Toward Avian Influenza

Attitudes toward avian influenza are summarized in Table 4. Interest in learning more about avian influenza remained stable, with 53% of workers expressing a desire for additional information before the pandemic and 55% during the pandemic. Concern about future outbreaks also remained high (65% vs. 67%). Perceptions of personal risk showed little overall change, although the distribution shifted slightly. Around 20% reported not being worried in either period, while approximately one‐third were extremely worried (31% vs. 30%). However, there was a modest decline in those who were “unsure” (33%–25%) and a corresponding increase in those reporting moderate concern (10%–17%).

Preventive Practices Related to AI

Reported preventive practices are summarized in Table 5. Avoidance of contact with sick or dead poultry increased from 66% before the pandemic to 77% during the pandemic, although this change was not statistically significant (p = 0.109). Hand hygiene practices remained relatively stable, with roughly half of workers using soap or sanitizer (49% vs. 56%) and about 40% relying on water alone. In contrast, the use of PPE declined markedly during the pandemic. Face mask use fell from 58% prepandemic to 40% during the pandemic (p = 0.003), while the proportion reporting no PPE use more than doubled from 21% to 49% (p < 0.001). Use of gloves, boots, and aprons remained consistently low and did not change significantly between the two periods.

Qualitative Insights on Awareness, Prevention, and Work Practices

Open‐ended responses provided additional insights into changes in poultry sourcing, prevention practices and work routines before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic (Table 6 and Table 7). During the pandemic, notable sourcing of poultry from Takeo province increased (from 21% to 30%), as did sourcing from other markets (from 12% to 18%), while imports from Thailand remained uncommon (1%). Motivations for attending influenza‐related training shifted over time, moving from primarily wanting knowledge about infection prevention (60% previously) to more varied concerns such as disease detection (20%), preventative strategies (40%), and general knowledge of avian influenza (30%). Workers' reported areas of interest regarding avian influenza also shifted between the two periods. Before COVID‐19, workers most commonly wanted information on avian influenza symptoms or transmission and prevention or self‐protection (47% each), whereas during COVID‐19 responses included prevention and treatment methods (18%), transmission routes (17%), self‐protection and hygiene practices (3%), and personal or business safety concerns (2%). Among workers who expressed interest in seasonal influenza vaccination, the main reason remained disease prevention or self‐protection, reported by 91% before COVID‐19 and 90% during COVID‐19.

Frequent market closures and lockdowns led to 56% of workers remaining at home or shifting to local sales, while fear of infection and public health measures, such as mask‐wearing, affected work behaviors. More than half of the respondents (56%) reported increased use of hand sanitizer, primarily to prevent COVID‐19 and other illnesses, but also to maintain cleanliness, reduce exposure to viruses and bacteria, and comply with public health requirements. After working with poultry, 66% of workers reported showering, 21% used soap or alcohol, and 8% reported using face masks and hand sanitizer throughout the pandemic. Given the perceived risk of avian influenza, these hygiene precautions were also viewed as measures to protect household members. Recommendations given by the workers for the prevention and control of avian influenza included regular handwashing (17%), use of hand sanitizer (2%), wearing masks and gloves (12%), changing clothes and showering after work (8%), cleaning work environments (2%), and access to vaccination and medicine (6%).

Discussion

This study provides a comparative assessment of Cambodian poultry workers KAP related to avian influenza before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic. By examining these two periods, the findings highlight that preventive behaviors and awareness were dynamic rather than static, shifting in response to the pandemic context. Importantly, these changes occurred against a backdrop of widespread social and economic disruption, including lockdowns, temporary market closures, supply chain interruptions, and shifting worker roles. Declines in awareness, training participation, and use of PPE therefore reflect not only individual behaviors but also broader structural and occupational challenges faced during the pandemic.

These findings should be interpreted within the occupational exposure context of Cambodian LBMs, where previous studies have documented AIV circulation in poultry and market environments, serological evidence of subclinical A(H5N1) infection among poultry workers, and aerosol exposure to viable A(H5N1) and A(H9N2) viruses [8, 9, 13]. Although the contribution of market poultry workers to clinically detected human avian influenza cases in Cambodia remains incompletely defined, this exposure evidence supports the relevance of poultry workers as a target group for sustained risk communication, training, and feasible preventive measures.

Taken together, the findings indicate that declines in awareness, training participation, and PPE use during the COVID‐19 pandemic should not be interpreted as disengagement or low risk perception among poultry workers. Rather, the results point to a misalignment between formal prevention strategies and the lived realities of market‐based work during a period of sustained disruption. While institutional training and occupational safety measures weakened, workers continued to adapt pragmatically through persistent interest in vaccination, rapid uptake of hygiene behaviors in response to COVID‐19, and reliance on informal information and health‐seeking pathways such as pharmacies. This coexistence of declining formal prevention and adaptive individual behavior highlights a form of resilience operating within structural constraint, underscoring that system fragility, rather than individual inaction, shaped avian influenza risk in live bird markets during this period.

Economic and Structural Barriers to PPE Use

Economic constraints emerged as a major barrier to preventive practices. The proportion of workers earning less than USD $350 per month increased significantly during the pandemic (41% before the COVID‐19 pandemic compared with 52% during the pandemic, p < 0.001), underscoring heightened financial strain on market workers. Based on local market prices during the study period, (2020), the monthly cost of basic PPE totals approximately USD $16–17, including gloves (USD $9 per box), boots (USD $3), aprons (USD $3.2), and face masks (USD $1.5 per box). This cost represents approximately 5% of monthly income for many workers, making consistent PPE use difficult to sustain, particularly for those operating with narrow profit margins. These findings are consistent with evidence from China indicating that economic constraints constitute a major barrier to PPE adoption in LBMs, where nearly half of participants (49%) reported over a decade of occupational exposure, with more than 10 h of daily poultry contact with only limited PPE usage of aprons or waterproof footwear, providing insufficient protection against potential AIV exposure [39]. Taken together, these findings indicate that PPE use in LBMs is sensitive to affordability and access, rather than reflecting a lack of awareness or concern among workers. Notably, concern about avian influenza remained high across both periods, yet PPE use declined markedly as incomes fell and access to protective supplies was disrupted, reinforcing the sensitivity of prevention behaviors to economic and logistical conditions. This pattern suggests that knowledge and concern alone are insufficient to sustain protection in LBMs unless supported by affordable access and structurally enabling conditions. These constraints became particularly visible during the COVID‐19 pandemic, which brought limitations of existing prevention systems into focus, particularly those reliant on stable income, continuous training, and uninterrupted supply chains.

Impact of the COVID‐19 Pandemic

Beyond its effect on income, the COVID‐19 pandemic also affected the availability and affordability of PPE. Global supply chain interruptions and surging demand for masks, gloves, and disinfectants led to shortages and price volatility in Cambodia, particularly in the early months of the pandemic. National trade reports indicate a 130% increase (from $83.3 million to $191.3 million) in PPE exports from Cambodia in 2020, reflecting rapid expansion of domestic production largely oriented toward external markets rather than local informal sectors [40]. At the global level, the WHO estimated that the price of surgical masks rose sixfold and that of N95 respirators tripled within the first quarter of 2020, trends that further exacerbated affordability issues in low‐ and middle‐income countries [41]. For poultry workers already operating with constrained financial margins, these disruptions may have further limited access to protective equipment, providing important context for the observed decline in PPE use. Taken together, these findings reinforce that reduced adherence to preventive practices reflects broader structural and economic pressures, rather than individual choice alone.

Training, Awareness, and Health‐Seeking Behaviors

The decline in avian influenza awareness among poultry workers during the COVID‐19 pandemic (from 81% prepandemic to 59% during) raises important concerns when considered alongside the resurgence of reported AIV cases in Cambodia from 2023 onward [42]. Although causal links cannot be established, reduced awareness among frontline poultry workers may limit early recognition of avian influenza events, delay adoption of protective practices, and reduce reporting within market settings. Together, these factors could increase occupational exposure risk and create opportunities for wider spillover, without implying direct causation. Placed in the context of recent avian influenza activity, these findings underscore the importance of maintaining continuous risk communication and training in LBMs, even during periods when other public health priorities dominate.

Health‐seeking behaviors further contextualize these risks. Pharmacies remained the most common first point of care for respiratory illness, while a subset of workers reported taking no action when ill. Such patterns may reflect cost constraints, access barriers, or low perceived risk, with implications for surveillance sensitivity and delayed detection of zoonotic infections in high‐exposure market settings. These observations point to the potential value of community‐based surveillance approaches, including pharmacy‐linked detection and targeted outreach to individuals who do not engage with formal health‐care services.

Insights From Qualitative Responses and Risk Communication

Qualitative responses provide important context for interpreting the quantitative findings, particularly in relation to behavioral adaptability and unmet prevention needs. Interest in seasonal influenza vaccination emerged as a consistent theme, with disease prevention and self‐protection cited as primary motivations. This pattern aligns with findings from Nanchang, China, where farmers' market workers demonstrated similar motivations and positive attitudes toward vaccination [43]. Such convergence suggests that, if vaccines are accessible and appropriately communicated, uptake among poultry workers may be feasible, even in resource‐constrained settings. Qualitative responses also illustrate how the COVID‐19 pandemic reshaped daily routines and hygiene practices. Increased handwashing and sanitizer use were commonly described as measures to reduce the risk of COVID‐19 and other infections, reflecting broader public health messaging. These behaviors mirror observations from urban Bangladesh, where frequent hand hygiene was reported despite persistently low PPE use among individuals with high poultry exposure [44]. Together, these findings suggest that while hygiene behaviors may be responsive to acute public health threats and clear messaging, sustained adoption of occupational protective measures remains more challenging without structural support and consistent access.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its retrospective design relied on participants' recall of prepandemic practices, introducing potential recall bias. Second, the modest sample size (n = 100) may have limited statistical power to detect smaller associations, although effect sizes for key outcomes such as PPE use and awareness were substantial. Moreover, a small subgroup of participants (n = 7) had less than 1 year of experience at the LBM, precluding stratified analysis by duration of employment. Third, while the survey included open‐ended questions, a formal qualitative analytical framework was not employed; qualitative findings should therefore be interpreted as illustrative rather than exhaustive. In addition, reliance on self‐reported practices may be subject to social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive behaviors such as PPE use and health‐seeking.

Contextual factors related to the pandemic also complicate interpretation. Market closures, disruptions in training programs, and global PPE shortages likely contributed to the observed declines in training participation, vaccination uptake, and PPE use. These broader economic and structural pressures mean that reduced adherence to protective measures cannot be attributed solely to individual choices. Finally, although the study sites represent major trading hubs, the findings may not be generalizable to all live bird markets in Cambodia or the wider region.

Despite these limitations, the consistent patterns observed across multiple indicators provide important insights into the occupational risks faced by poultry workers and the challenges of sustaining avian influenza prevention during periods of broader public health disruption.

Conclusions

This study shows that avian influenza risk among poultry workers in Cambodian live bird markets is driven by intersecting economic, structural, and behavioral pressures that were further intensified during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Declines in awareness, training, and use of PPE illustrate how quickly preventive practices can weaken when livelihoods are disrupted and public health priorities shift. Importantly, these gaps do not reflect a lack of concern or willingness among workers. Persistent interest in training and vaccination points instead to constraints in access, affordability, and sustained engagement. Addressing avian influenza risk in live bird markets therefore requires approaches that extend beyond individual behavior change, integrating worker‐centered communication, affordable protective measures, and structurally informed surveillance within a One Health framework.
