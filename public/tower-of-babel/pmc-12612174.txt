Risk factors for gestational diabetes mellitus and postpartum glucose intolerance: a retrospective study from a hospital specializing in infertility treatment
Authors: Miyako Kishimoto, Shinichi Tamaru, Masato Odawara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is one of the most common complications of pregnancy and is associated with adverse outcomes for both mother and child as well as long-term maternal risk of type 2 diabetes. This study aimed to identify risk factors for the development of GDM and predictors of postpartum glucose intolerance. We conducted a single-center retrospective study at Sanno Hospital, Tokyo, including 11,694 pregnant women between 2015 and 2024. A total of 3177 women underwent a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) between 24 and 28 gestational weeks, and 1,025 were diagnosed with GDM (32.3%). The median maternal age at GDM diagnosis was 36 years, the mean prepregnancy body mass index (BMI) was 20.2 kg/m2, and the postpartum OGTT was performed at 6–12 weeks after delivery. As a result, women with GDM were more likely to have advanced maternal age, history of GDM, family history of diabetes, higher prepregnancy BMI, elevated hemoglobin A1c levels, higher 50-g glucose challenge test (GCT) results, and higher OGTT values at GDM diagnosis. Infertility treatment itself was not a risk factor, though age and obesity in this population likely contribute. Prepregnancy obesity had a stronger impact on GDM risk than maternal age, and combining both factors markedly increased risk. GDM was associated with a higher rate of large-for-gestational-age infants and neonatal hypoglycemia. Postpartum follow-up showed that 65.6% of women returned to normal glucose tolerance, 31.4% developed impaired glucose tolerance, and 3.0% developed diabetes mellitus. History of GDM, family history of diabetes, multiple positive points on 75-g OGTT, and 2-h 75-g OGTT results at GDM diagnosis were risk factors for postpartum glucose intolerance. The 50-g GCT results could significantly predict the risk of postpartum insulin deficiency. In conclusion, history of infertility treatment was not a risk factor for GDM; however, other factors such as prepregnacy BMI were consistent with previous report findings. These findings highlight the importance of prepregnancy weight management and postpartum monitoring, and emphasize the need for lifelong follow-up in women with GDM. A multidisciplinary approach from preconception through postpartum is recommended.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common glucose intolerance that develops during pregnancy1–3. Historically, GDM was defined as any degree of glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy, regardless of the severity of hyperglycemia4. The Hyperglycemia and Adverse Pregnancy Outcome study5 provided the basis for the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria for screening and diagnosing GDM6,7. However, these definitions and criteria have certain limitations, and various diagnostic criteria are still used worldwide, leading to wide variations in reported prevalence1,2,8–11. Despite this, the prevalence of GDM has been increasing globally across all racial and ethnic groups12–17, driven by rising rates of obesity, diabetes, and advanced maternal age10,18–21.

GDM negatively affects pregnancy outcomes. Even with modern management, women with GDM remain at increased risk of complications such as preeclampsia, cesarean section, and adverse neonatal outcomes7,8,10,17. In the long term, offspring are predisposed to obesity, impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), and type 2 diabetes5,10,22–27. Women with GDM themselves are also at elevated risk for IGT, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular or metabolic diseases compared with those without GDM28–41. A recent population-representative cohort study reported that women with prior GDM had an adjusted hazard ratio of 11.5 for developing type 2 diabetes, with only minor variation by race or ethnicity14.

Because of the above mentioned adverse outcome of GDM, we should reduce its incidence by identifying the risk factors for GDM. Modifiable factors, especially those related to lifestyle behaviors, should be addressed with effective interventions. Accurate prediction of future diabetes risk in the early postpartum period, including the evaluation of insulin function, is important because GDM often indicates underlying beta cell dysfunction42, which influences the development of IGT or diabetes later in life in women with GDM. In this study, we aimed to explore risk factors for GDM and to further evaluate postpartum insulin function, such as insulin secretion and insulin resistance, to appropriately identify women susceptible to developing diabetes later in life.

Material and methods

This retrospective single-center study was conducted at Sanno Hospital, a private hospital in Tokyo with multiple specialists in infertility treatment and pregnancy care, managing approximately 1,300 deliveries annually. We included pregnant women who delivered at the hospital between April 2015 and March 2024, underwent a 75-g OGTT during pregnancy for GDM screening, and had no prior diagnosis of pregestational diabetes. Exclusion criteria were overt diabetes at initial evaluation, absence of a 75-g OGTT despite GDM risk, and refusal to participate (opt-out).

Screening for GDM

Pregnant women without diabetes were screened for GDM using a two-step approach. Women were considered at risk of GDM if the random blood glucose level at an early stage of pregnancy (12–15 gestational weeks) was high; cutoff value ≥ 5.6 mmol/l (100 mg/dl)43,44, if glucose was present in the urine at any stage of pregnancy, or if the result of 50-g glucose challenge test (GCT) performed between 24 and 28 gestational weeks was positive; 1-h post-load value ≥ 7.8 mmol/l (140 mg/dl)43,45–48. Furthermore, participants with high-risk factors for GDM, such as those with obesity, family history of diabetes, or history of GDM, were considered at risk of GDM. Women at risk for GDM underwent a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at the appropriate and earliest timing, on average at 27 weeks of gestation, except those diagnosed with “overt diabetes during pregnancy”43. According to the IADPSG criteria6, patients are diagnosed with GDM if one or more glucose values reach or exceed the following thresholds in the 75-g OGTT: 5.1 mmol/l (92 mg/dl) for fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 10.0 mmol/l (180 mg/dl) for 1-h plasma glucose (1-h PG), or 8.5 mmol/l (153 mg/dl) for 2-h PG. Those who did not meet the criteria were classified into the non-GDM group.

Data collection

For women diagnosed with or without GDM, information such as age, height, body weight, body mass index (BMI) before pregnancy, family history of diabetes, parity, history of GDM, 50-g GCT results, gestational week at the time of 75-g OGTT, hemoglobin level, Glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level, determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an automatic glycohemoglobin analyzer (ADAMS A1c HA-8190V, ARKRAY, Kyoto, Japan), 75-g OGTT results, and infertility treatment details was retrieved from medical records. Additionally, delivery information, such as gestational week, vaginal delivery or cesarean section, complications at delivery, and infant weight, was collected. Blood glucose levels were measured in infants born to women with GDM or in those classified as large for gestational age (LGA, birth weight > 90th percentile for gestational age, according to sex-specific reference standards49). Neonatal hypoglycemia was defined as blood glucose level < 2.6 mmol/l (47 mg/dl)50,51.

GDM management

The fundamental treatment for most women diagnosed with GDM at our hospital consisted of lifestyle modification, including medical nutritional therapy, physical activity, patient education, and self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG). Women diagnosed with GDM received individualized dietary counseling from registered dietitians or physicians. If glycemic targets could not be achieved with diet and lifestyle modification, specifically when SMBG levels were frequently > 5.6 mmol/l (100 mg/dl) at fasting or > 6.7 mmol/l (120 mg/dl) at 2 h after a meal, insulin therapy was initiated at the discretion of the attending physician46,52. Women with GDM were followed at intervals of every two to four weeks, depending on their glycemic control and clinical needs.

Postpartum 75-g OGTT

At 6–12 weeks postpartum (mean 9.5 weeks), women with GDM underwent 75-g OGTT to assess glucose metabolism. Results were classified as normal glucose tolerance (NGT), IGT (FPG < 7.0 mmol/l [126 mg/dl] and 2-h OGTT 7.8–11.0 mmol/l [140–199 mg/dl]), or diabetes mellitus (DM: FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/l [126 mg/dl] or 2-h OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/l [200 mg/dl]), according to nonpregnancy criteria4,53.

Starting in 2022, in addition to FPG, 1-h PG and 2-h PG, plasma glucose at 30 min and insulin concentrations at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min were measured during the postpartum OGTT to evaluate insulin secretion and sensitivity (hereafter, FPI denotes fasting plasma insulin). Plasma glucose concentrations were measured using an automatic glucose analyzer (ADAMS Glucose GA-1172, ARKRAY, Kyoto, Japan) and serum immunoreactive insulin concentrations were measured by chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassay (Fujirebio Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Insulin secretion was assessed using the homeostasis model assessment for β-cell function (HOMA-β):(20 × FPI [mU/l])/(FPG [mmol/l] − 3.5)54,55, and the insulinogenic index:Δinsulin (mU/l, 30 min-0 min)/ ΔGlucose (mmol/l, 30 min-0 min)56,57. Insulin sensitivity was estimated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR): FPI (mU/l) × FPG (mmol/l)/22.554.

Statistical analysis

Variables are expressed as medians (interquartile ranges: IQR 25–75%) or as number of cases (percentages). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess whether variables had normal distributions. Continuous data were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis test. A Fisher’s exact test and the chi-square test were used to examine differences in categorical variables between the two groups, as appropriate. We used logistic regression analysis to examine associations between GDM incidence, age, and weight and to examine predictors of abnormal postpartum 75-g OGTT results and impaired insulin secretion. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 29.0.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Ethics declarations

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Board of Sanno Hospital (Approval Code: 23-S-11) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its subsequent amendments. The Medical Ethics Review Board waived the need for written informed consent from the individual participants because of the retrospective nature of the study. An opt-out procedure was conducted to allow patients to withdraw from the study by informing them about the study protocol on the hospital’s homepage.

Results

During the study period, 11,694 pregnant women were treated at our hospital. Among them, 3,177 women were at risk of GDM and underwent 75-g OGTT. Of these, 2,865 had a positive 50-g GCT result, 203 had high glucose levels at early-stage pregnancy, and 109 were recommended to undergo 75-g OGTT at the doctor’s discretion (glucose in the urine or high-risk factors for GDM). Analyses of advanced age and prepregnancy BMI

Prepregnancy BMI data were collected from 2,905 pregnant women. According to the Asian-specific prepregnancy BMI classification recommended by the World Health Organization58, 654 (22.5%) women were underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), 1,827 (62.9%) were normal weight (BMI: 18.5–22.9 kg/m2), 223 (7.7%) were overweight (BMI: 23.0–24.9 kg/m2), 166 (5.7%) were obese (BMI: 25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and 35 (1.2%) had a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. Compared to normal prepregnancy BMI, prepregnancy obesity was a greater risk factor for GDM, and the risk increased with increasing prepregnancy BMI (Table 2). Regarding age at GDM diagnosis, among the 3,177 pregnant women, 310 (9.8%) were aged ≤ 29 years, 2,237 (70.4%) were aged 30–39 years, and 630 (19.8%) were aged ≥ 40 years. Maternal age was a risk factor for GDM, with age ≥ 40 years being a significant risk factor (Table 2). When prepregnancy BMI and age were combined and classified into four categories—BMI < 25 kg/m2 and age < 35 years (category A), BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and age < 35 years (category B), BMI < 25 kg/m2 and age ≥ 35 years (category C), and BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and aged ≥ 35 years (category D). Factors associated with the number of positive points on OGTT at GDM diagnosis

A comparison of the groups classified by the number of positive points (FPG, 1-h PG, and 2-h PG) in the 75-g OGTT is presented in Table 4. Maternal age, history of GDM, and history of infertility treatment did not differ significantly between the groups, whereas family history of diabetes, prepregnancy BMI, HbA1c levels, and 50-g GCT results differed significantly. The values increased as the number of positive points on the 75-g OGTT increased. Regarding differences in obstetric outcomes, no significant group differences were observed in the gestational week at diagnosis, rate of cesarean section, prevalence of complications during pregnancy, birth weight of the infant, prevalence of LGA, or prevalence of neonatal hypoglycemia (Table 5). Postpartum 75-g OGTT results demonstrated significant differences in glucose tolerance status (NGT, IGT, and DM) across groups, with higher numbers of positive antepartum OGTT points associated with greater proportions of IGT and DM. Discussion

In this retrospective study, 32.3% of women at risk of GDM were diagnosed with the condition, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 8.8% among all pregnant women at our institution. This finding is consistent with the previously reported prevalence of 6.3–10.1% in Japan59. Risk factors identified in our study included advanced maternal age, history of GDM, family history of diabetes, higher prepregnancy BMI, elevated HbA1c, higher 50-g GCT values, and abnormal 75-g OGTT results. Women with GDM also had higher rates of LGA infants and neonatal hypoglycemia, and 34.4% developed postpartum glucose intolerance, with multiple positive points on the diagnostic 75-g OGTT, family history of diabetes, and higher 2-h PG values predicting abnormal glucose metabolism.

These findings are consistent with observations reported internationally, and they align with IADPSG and ADA guidelines, which emphasize maternal obesity, age, and family history as key risk factors6,7,60–64. Clinically, our results highlight the importance of preconception weight management and structured postpartum follow-up, as one-third of women with GDM developed glucose intolerance within 12 weeks of delivery. HbA1c should be interpreted cautiously during pregnancy as it is associated with physiological anemia, and HbA1c levels change during different trimesters65. The 50-g GCT results are not commonly recognized as a risk factor for GDM; however, our results indicated that 50-g GCT results may indicate risk of GDM. Moreover, 50-g GCT values increased with the number of positive points in the 75-g OGTT.

Japan is facing a declining birth rate (1.20 in 2023, the lowest since record-keeping began)66. In Tokyo, the birth rate was the lowest (0.99) among all prefectures in Japan66. Therefore, infertility treatment is becoming increasingly important. Our hospital specializes in infertility treatments, including fertility-enhancing drugs, artificial insemination, and assisted reproductive technology. Over the past 10 years (2013–2022), 8,563 artificial insemination procedures, 7,635 in vitro fertilization procedures, and 4,595 embryo transfers have been conducted at our hospital. In this study, 43.1% of the neonates were born as a result of infertility treatments, which is much greater than the national average in Japan in 2021 (8.6%)67. Infertility treatment has been reported as a potential risk factor for GDM68–77, with contributing factors such as maternal age, multiple pregnancy, obesity, polycystic ovary syndrome, and use of progesterone for luteal support. However, some studies have found no significant association after adjusting for age, parity, and BMI78. In our study, infertility treatment itself was not a significant risk factor for GDM, although the type of treatment may play a role71,74,76. These findings highlight the need for further investigation considering specific infertility treatment methods.

In Japan, the average childbearing age has recently increased due to economic pressures and the demand for women’s socioeconomic status, the average maternal age for the first, second, and third child in Japan in 2022 was 30.9, 32.9, and 34.1 years, respectively79. In our study, the average number of births and maternal age were 1.4 and 35.2 years, respectively, indicating that our participants were older than the national average age. This is partly because of patient characteristics, such as deliveries after long-term infertility treatments or living or working in urban areas. As reported previously60–64, advanced maternal age and high prepregnancy BMI were risk factors for GDM in our study. Combining these two factors resulted in the highest odds ratio for developing GDM. However, high prepregnancy BMI had a greater impact on the risk of GDM than maternal age, suggesting the need to provide appropriate education and care to this population before pregnancy.

Compared with non-GDM women, women with GDM had a greater risk of LGA and neonatal hypoglycemia in our study; however, the risk of these outcomes did not increase with an increase in the number of positive points in the 75-g OGTT. During pregnancy, GDM is characterized by excessive maternal glucose that crosses the placenta and consequent fetal hyperinsulinemia, resulting in LGA infants8,80–83. Neonatal hypoglycemia is a known complication in infants of women with GDM8,84. After delivery, the previous constant maternal glucose flow discontinues abruptly; however, if fetal hyperinsulinemia continues, infants develop hypoglycemia. Moreover, heavier infants are more likely to have hypoglycemia and hyperinsulinemia84. In our study, 4.2% of infants of mothers with GDM and 0.5% of infants of mothers without GDM exhibited hypoglycemia. The infants were promptly treated and exhibited no subsequent complications.

Regarding predictive factors for postpartum glucose intolerance, previous studies have reported higher fasting plasma glucose, higher HbA1c during pregnancy85, number of previous pregnancies, family history of diabetes86, use of insulin, 1-h PG values, and Asian origin87. Importantly, impaired insulin secretion has emerged as a major determinant of long-term glucose intolerance after GDM. Women with abnormal postpartum glucose tolerance were shown to have lower HOMA-β, insulinogenic, and disposition indexes than normoglycemic women86, highlighting the central role of β-cell dysfunction. Studies of Japanese women with GDM revealed that 10–39% subsequently develop abnormal glucose tolerance, with predictors including family history of diabetes, modest elevation of HbA1c, higher 1-h and 2-h OGTT values, and particularly lower insulinogenic index85,88–90. In our study, in addition to the abovementioned factors, GDM history and multiple positive points on the 75-g OGTT were risk factors for postpartum abnormal glucose tolerance. Moreover, multivariate analyses revealed that multiple positive points of the 75-g OGTT, family history of diabetes, and 2-h PG value of the 75-g OGTT were related to postpartum abnormal glucose tolerance. Of note, the 50-g GCT result was associated with both a lower insulinogenic index and postpartum insulin secretion deficiency, suggesting that poor β-cell compensation during pregnancy predicts future diabetes risk. A prior study also reported that elevated GCT values are linked with subsequent diabetes in both the general obstetric population and in women with GDM48. As the 50-g GCT reflects β-cell dysfunction in late gestation4,42,48, it may be a particularly useful and easily available predictor of women at risk for persistent β-cell dysfunction and type 2 diabetes. While insulin resistance is a physiologic feature of pregnancy, inadequate β-cell adaptation appears to be the key factor distinguishing women at highest risk of type 2 diabetes later in life91–93. Thus, markers of impaired insulin secretion during pregnancy may be particularly useful for identifying women who require close postpartum monitoring.

GDM recurrence rates are high (30–84%)94–99 and are influenced by ethnicity, parity, and other factors. Non-Hispanic whites and primiparous women show lower recurrence rates, while advanced age, higher prepregnancy BMI, family history of diabetes, prior insulin treatment, greater weight gain, and short interpregnancy intervals increase risk94–97. Unlike age, ethnicity, and family history of diabetes, body weight is a modifiable factor. Therefore, healthcare professionals should educate and support obese women with GDM to normalize their BMI before the next pregnancy to reduce the risk of GDM recurrence.

Compliance with postpartum 75-g OGTT follow-up is generally low28,100–103, and our study showed a rate of 55.8%. Barriers include time-consuming and inconvenience, poor communication, limited awareness, fear of diagnosis, and the burden of childcare34,100,102,104–106. Physician engagement also influences adherence. Patient-centered strategies, such as reminders and improved communication, may improve follow-up, though optimal methods remain unclear104.

Our study has several limitations, and the results may not apply to the general population for several reasons. First, the study was conducted at a single medical institute, and the participants were limited to those living or working in urban areas close to our hospital in a developed area of Tokyo. Second, the average age at delivery among the participants was higher than the national average due to the high number of patients who had undergone long-term infertility treatment. Third, the study was conducted with mostly Japanese women (96.8%). The Asian population, including the Japanese population, is considered a high-risk ethnic group for developing diabetes, partially owing to a genetic predisposition toward impaired insulin secretion89,107,108. Therefore, our results may not be universally applicable. Finally, the low follow-up rate of the postpartum OGTT might have affected our results. Further research with a larger number of participants from multiple medical facilities is required to confirm our findings.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that advanced maternal age, history of GDM, family history of diabetes, higher prepregnancy BMI, elevated HbA1c levels, and abnormal 50-g GCT results, but not history of infertility treatment, were risk factors for GDM. A high prepregnancy BMI had a more significant negative impact than advanced maternal age, underscoring the importance of effective weight management before and during pregnancy. For postpartum outcomes, multiple positive points on the 75-g OGTT, family history of diabetes, and higher 2-h PG values in 75-g OGTT were associated with abnormal glucose tolerance. Moreover, 50-g GCT values predicted impaired insulin secretion after delivery, suggesting a role as an early marker of long-term β-cell dysfunction. These findings emphasize the need for structured postpartum follow-up and lifelong preventive care to reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes in women with a history of GDM.
