Investigating an approach of robustly self-testing two-qubit entangled states
Chan-Ching Lien, Shin-Liang Chen

Abstract
In a recent paper [Quantum 5 , 552 (2021)], the authors proposed a framework for robustly self-testing steerable quantum assemblages. In this work, we apply their method to the scenario of self-testing two-qubit entangled quantum states. The new bounds on the fidelity with the reference states are compared with other methods.



I
Introduction

Since Bell’s seminal paper
Bell 1964
, quantum nonlocality
Brunner et al. 2014
plays an essential role in the field of quantum information science. Fundamentally, nonlocality changed our understanding of nature. In practice, it provides a powerful tool called
device-independent quantum certification

Acín et al. 2007
;
Scarani 2012
. The device-independent protocol certifies quantum properties without making assumptions on the underlying states nor the involving measurement devices. Such a certification includes entanglement certification
Wiseman et al. 2007
;
Moroder et al. 2013
, steering certification
Wiseman et al. 2007
;
Cavalcanti and Skrzypczyk 2016
;
Chen et al. 2016
, incompatibility certification
Wolf et al. 2009
;
Chen et al. 2021a
, dimension witnesses
Gallego et al. 2010
, etc.

The most extreme case in device-independent (DI) quantum certification is identifying the underlying states and measurements themselves. The so-called
self-testing
protocol was firstly introduced by Mayers and Yao
Mayers and Yao 1998
;
Mayers and Yao 2004
. Since then, many research results on self-testing have been proposed, ranging from self-testing entangled state
McKague and Mosca 2011
;
Yang et al. 2014
;
Kaniewski 2016
;
Coladangelo et al. 2017
, incompatible measurements
Yang et al. 2014
;
Kaniewski 2017
;
Bowles et al. 2018a
, steerable assemblages
Chen et al. 2021b
, preparation and measurements
Tavakoli et al. 2018
;
Miklin and Oszmaniec 2021
;
Chen and Eisert 2024
, entangled measurements
Renou et al. 2018
, and so on
(see Ref.
Šupić and Bowles 2020
for a comprehensive review of self-testing).

Self-testing has been studied mainly through the following approaches: The typical analytical approach
Mayers and Yao 1998
;
Mayers and Yao 2004
;
McKague and Mosca 2011
, the swap method
Yang et al. 2014
;
Bancal et al. 2015
, and the operator inequality method
Kaniewski 2016
. Different approaches have their features and benefits. The typical analytical approach is universal in general and can be generalized from simple system to multipartite system (e.g., Ref.
Bowles et al. 2018b
). The computation of the swap method is carried out by semidefinite programming and often provides more robust results than the analytical approach (e.g., Ref.
Yang et al. 2014
;
Bancal et al. 2015
). The operator inequality method is analytical, but the concept is totally different from the standard analytical approach. The operator inequality method often provides the best and remarkably robust results than the other two methods (e.g., Ref.
Kaniewski 2016
).

Recently, a new approach was introduced to self-test steerable quantum assemblages
Chen et al. 2021b
. Briefly speaking, the authors of Ref.
Chen et al. 2021b
follow the idea of the swap method, but they construct the local isometry from the viewpoint of channel-state duality. In this work, we apply the method to the scenario of self-testing two-qubit entangled quantum states and compare the results with other approaches. The overview picture is shown in Fig.
1
.

The article is organized as follows. First, we briefly review the Bell scenario. Then, in Section
III
, we give our general framework for self-testing of entangled states. After that, we consider a typical example of self-testing the two-qubit maximally entangled state in Section
IV
. We also consider a more general example of self-testing a family of pure entangled states in Sec.
V
. Finally, we address some open problems and future outlook in Sec.
VI
.

II
The Bell scenario

The Bell scenario considered in this work is composed as follows. A pair of particles are respectively sent to two parties, called Alice and Bob, who perform measurements on the received particle. Alice’s measurement choices and outcomes are labeled respectively by
x
and
a
while Bob’s are labeled by
y
and
b
. After repeating the experiment for many rounds, they can obtain probabilities of measurement outcomes conditional on the measurement choices, denoted as
P(a,b|x,y)
. The collection of
P(a,b|x,y)
is called the
correlation
, denoted as
\mathbf{P}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{P}:=\{P(a,b|x,y)\}
.

If Alice and Bob live in a world described by local causality, the correlation admits a local-hidden-variable model
Bell 1964
;
Brunner et al. 2014
. That is,

P(a,b|x,y)=\sum_{\lambda}P(\lambda)P(a|x,\lambda)P(b|y,\lambda)

(1)

for all
a,b,x,y
, where
\lambda
are hidden variables or the so-called
shared randomness
. In this case, we say that the correlation between Alice and Bob is
local
. A
Bell inequality
is a linear combination of
P(a,b|x,y)
and it is a way to demonstrate the limitation that all local correlations can achieve. In general, it is expressed as

\vec{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{P}:=\sum_{a,b,x,y}\beta_{a,b}^{x,y}P(a,b|x,y)\leq L,

(2)

where
\beta_{a,b}^{x,y}
are some real numbers, the set of which defines a Bell inequality.
L
is called the
local bound
for the Bell inequality.

In quantum world, Alice and Bob’s pair is described by a quantum state
{\varrho^{\text{AB}}}
. The measurements performed by them are described by positive operator-valued measures (POVMs)
E_{a|x}
and
E_{b|y}
. The correlation now is obtained by the Born rule:

P(a,b|x,y)=\tr(E_{a|x}\otimes E_{b|y}{\varrho^{\text{AB}}})

(3)

for all
a,b,x,y
. The correlation obtained in quantum world is called
quantum correlation
, and we can see that it is different from the local correlation in Eq. (
1
). In fact, such a difference can cause the violation of a Bell inequality (
2
). That is, there exists a quantum state
{\varrho^{\text{AB}}}
and measurements
E_{a|x},E_{b|y}
such that Eq. (
2
) does not hold. A correlation
\mathbf{P}
that yields the violation of a Bell inequality is referred to as a
nonlocal correlation
. Similar to the local case, quantum correlations also have limitations on achieving a Bell value. That is,

\vec{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{P}:=\sum_{a,b,x,y}\beta_{a,b}^{x,y}P(a,b|x,y)\leq Q.

(4)

In this paper, we are interested in the situation of
Q>L
. In self-testing scheme, one tries to identify the quantum systems (states, measurements, assemblages, etc.) themselves through the maximal quantum violation of a Bell inequality, i.e., when observing
\vec{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{\rm obs}=Q

FN1
. We will review the knowledge of self-testing in the coming section.

III
Self-testing of entangled states: Definition and Formulation

After reviewing the concept of nonlocality, we can see that the correlation
\{P(a,b|x,y)\}
and the state
{\varrho^{\text{AB}}}
can be connected via Eq. (
3
).

Namely, the correlation is obtained by performing Alice’s and Bob’s measurements on the shared state. The self-testing problem asks the reverse question: When observing a correlation
\{P(a,b|x,y)\}
, can we uniquely identify the underlying state? Since the correlation is the only known information and we do not assume the form nor the dimension of the underlying state, we cannot distinguish a state
{\varrho^{\text{AB}}}
and its isometry equivalence
(V\otimes W){\varrho^{\text{AB}}}(V^{\dagger}\otimes W^{\dagger})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tr(E_{a|x}\otimes E_{b|y}{\varrho^{\text{AB}}})=

(5)

\displaystyle\tr\Big[(VE_{a|x}V^{\dagger})\otimes(WE_{b|y}W^{\dagger})(V\otimes W){\varrho^{\text{AB}}}(V^{\dagger}\otimes W^{\dagger})\Big].

Therefore, what we actually certify is an equivalence-class state. Defining a
reference
(or
representative
) state
\rho^{\rm ref}
and associated optimal measurements
E_{a|x}^{\rm opt},E_{b|y}^{\rm opt}
yielding to the same correlation, we have

\displaystyle\tr

\displaystyle\Big[(VE_{a|x}V^{\dagger})\otimes(WE_{b|y}W^{\dagger})(V\otimes W){\varrho^{\text{AB}}}(V^{\dagger}\otimes W^{\dagger})\Big]

(6)

\displaystyle=\tr(E_{a|x}^{\rm opt}\otimes E_{b|y}^{\rm opt}\rho^{\rm ref})

Here,
(V\otimes W){\varrho^{\text{AB}}}(V^{\dagger}\otimes W^{\dagger})=\rho^{\rm ref}
, or equivalently, by the Stinespring dilation,
\Lambda(\rho)=\rho^{\rm ref}
, where
\Lambda
is a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map. Having all the ingredients above, we can define the self-testing of quantum state
Kaniewski 2016
;
Šupić and Bowles 2020
:

Definition (self-testing of entangled states)
We say that the observed correlation
P_{\rm obs}(a,b|x,y)
self-tests the reference state
\rho^{\rm ref}
if for each state
\rho
compatible with
P_{\rm obs}(a,b|x,y)
there exist CPTP maps
\Lambda^{\rm A}
,
\Lambda^{\rm B}
such that

\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho)=\rho^{\rm ref}.

(7)

From the resource theory of entanglement, the CPTP map has to be in the form of separable operation
\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}
since it cannot increase the degree of entanglement. Otherwise, a global CPTP map can always map the underlying state to the reference one.

In a practical situation, the underlying system is always suffered from environmental noise, which makes the observed correlation depart from the ideal value. Therefore, we are not able to draw a conclusion on the perfect self-testing. Nevertheless, we can still estimate how close the underlying state and the reference one are. Such a scheme is called
robust self-testing states

McKague et al. 2012
, which we define as follows:

Definition (robust self-testing of entangled states)
We say that the observed correlation
P_{\rm obs}(a,b|x,y)

robustly
self-tests the reference state
\rho^{\rm ref}
with the fidelity
f
if for each state
\rho
compatible with
P_{\rm obs}(a,b|x,y)
there exist CPTP maps
\Lambda^{\rm A}
,
\Lambda^{\rm B}
such that

F(\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho),\rho^{\rm ref})\geq f,

(8)

where
F
is the fidelity between two quantum states. For the reference state being pure (which is the case
in the conventional self-testing scheme
Šupić and Bowles 2020
), we have

F=\langle\psi^{\rm ref}|\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho)|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle

(9)

Note that one can treat the above definition as a special case of the definition proposed in Ref.
Kaniewski 2016
, where the author also considers the optimization over the CPTP maps
\Lambda^{\rm A}
and
\Lambda^{\rm B}
. Here, in contrast, we only consider the existence of CPTP maps.

Now, we use the channel-state duality to represent the CPTP maps as Choi matrix
Jamiołkowski 1974
;
Choi 1975
. Namely,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho^{\rm AB})=

(10)

\displaystyle\tr_{\rm AB}\Big[\Big(\Omega^{\rm AA^{\prime}}\otimes\Omega^{\rm BB^{\prime}}\Big)\Big((\rho^{\rm AB})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbb{1}^{\rm A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\Big)\Big],

where
\Omega^{\rm AA^{\prime}}
and
\Omega^{\rm BB^{\prime}}
are Choi matrices for the CPTP maps
\Lambda^{\rm A}
and
\Lambda^{\rm B}
, respectively. The action of transpose with respect to the computational basis is denoted as
\mathsf{T}
. The Choi matrix
\Omega^{\rm AA^{\prime}}
for
\Lambda^{\rm A}
is defined as
\Omega:=(\mathsf{id}\otimes\Lambda)|\phi^{+}\rangle\langle\phi^{+}|
with
|\phi^{+}\rangle:=\sum_{i}|i\rangle_{\rm A}\otimes|i\rangle_{\rm A}
being the subnormalized maximally entangled state and
\mathsf{id}
being the identity operation. A similar definition holds for the other Choi matrix
\Omega^{\rm BB^{\prime}}
. Because of the relation Eq. (
10
), the problem of searching for CPTP maps
\Lambda^{\rm A},\Lambda^{\rm B}
is equivalent to the problem of searching for positive semidefinite matrices
\Omega^{\rm AA^{\prime}},\Omega^{\rm BB^{\prime}}
.

In the following sections, we show that the method of self-testing assemblages in Ref.
Chen et al. 2021b
can be applied to the self-testing of entangled states. That is, we will show how to compute bounds on
f
in Eq. (
8
) using the method of Ref.
Chen et al. 2021b
.

IV
Self-testing of the two-qubit maximally entangled state

To make readers understand how our method works, we firstly consider the simplest self-testing scenario, i.e., self-testing the two-qubit maximally entangled state.
In this scenario, if Alice and Bob perform measurements in the following bases

\displaystyle A_{1}=Z,\quad B_{1}=\frac{Z+X}{\sqrt{2}},

(11)

\displaystyle A_{2}=X,\quad B_{2}=\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt{2}}

on the maximally entangled state

|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle,

(12)

then such a strategy can be used for violating the Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (CHSH) inequality
Clauser et al. 1969
:

I_{\rm CHSH}:=\langle A_{1}B_{1}\rangle+\langle A_{1}B_{2}\rangle+\langle A_{2}B_{1}\rangle-\langle A_{2}B_{2}\rangle\leq 2

(13)

and achieving the maximal quantum violation of
2\sqrt{2}
.

The self-testing issue asks the reverse: Can we identify that the underlying state is the maximally entangled state, up to some local isometries, through the observation of the maximal quantum violation of the CHSH inequality? Before solving this problem, we first apply a unitary on Bob’s side and redefine the optimal observables:

\displaystyle B_{1}^{\rm opt}=A_{1}^{\rm opt}=Z,\quad B_{2}^{\rm opt}=A_{2}^{\rm opt}=X.

(14)

The form of the maximally entangled state under such a local unitary becomes:

|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle^{\rm A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}:=\cos\frac{\pi}{8}|\Phi^{-}\rangle+\sin\frac{\pi}{8}|\Psi^{+}\rangle,

(15)

where

\displaystyle\left|\Phi^{-}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle_{\rm A^{\prime}}\otimes|0\rangle_{\rm B^{\prime}}-|1\rangle_{\rm A^{\prime}}\otimes|1\rangle_{\rm B^{\prime}}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle\left|\Psi^{+}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle_{\rm A^{\prime}}\otimes|1\rangle_{\rm B^{\prime}}+|1\rangle_{\rm A^{\prime}}\otimes|0\rangle_{\rm B^{\prime}}\right)

are two of the Bell states.

The method of Ref.
Chen et al. 2021b
is inspired by the so-called
swap method
proposed by Yang
et al.

Yang et al. 2014
. In Ref.
Yang et al. 2014
, to compute bounds on the fidelity, the authors choose a specific form of local isometries. Then, to obtain a device-independent result, they relax some characterized observables to unknown ones. In this work, with the method in Ref.
Chen et al. 2021b
, we firstly choose the identity maps
\mathsf{id}^{\rm A}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\rm A})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\rm A^{\prime}})
and
\mathsf{id}^{\rm B}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\rm B})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\rm B^{\prime}})
for both
\Lambda^{\rm A}
and
\Lambda^{\rm B}
. The choice has the same mathematical effect as the choice of the swap operator in Ref.
Yang et al. 2014
. Namely,

\displaystyle\tr_{\rm AB}\Big\{U_{\rm\tiny{SWAP}}\Big[|0\rangle^{\rm A^{\prime}}\langle 0|\otimes\rho^{\rm AB}\otimes|0\rangle^{\rm B^{\prime}}\langle 0|\Big]U_{\rm\tiny{SWAP}}^{\dagger}\Big\}

(17)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{id}^{\rm A}\otimes\mathsf{id}^{\rm B}(\rho^{\rm AB}),

where
U_{\rm\tiny{SWAP}}
is the swap operator mapping the state
\rho^{\rm AB}
from
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\rm AB})
to
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\rm A^{\prime}B^{\prime}})
.

The Choi matrix
\Omega
of identity operation
\mathsf{id}
is

\Omega=\mathsf{id}\otimes\mathsf{id}(|\phi^{+}\rangle\langle\phi^{+}|)=|\phi^{+}\rangle\langle\phi^{+}|.

(18)

Therefore, from Eq. (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{id}^{\rm A}\otimes\mathsf{id}^{\rm B}(\rho^{\rm AB})=

(19)

\displaystyle\tr_{\rm AB}\Big[\Big(|\phi^{+}\rangle^{\rm AA^{\prime}}\langle\phi^{+}|\otimes|\phi^{+}\rangle^{\rm BB^{\prime}}\langle\phi^{+}|\Big)\Big(\rho^{\rm AB,\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbb{1}^{\rm A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\Big)\Big].

The subnormalized maximally entangled states
|\phi^{+}\rangle^{\rm AA^{\prime}}\langle\phi^{+}|\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\rm AA^{\prime}})
and
|\phi^{+}\rangle^{\rm BB^{\prime}}\langle\phi^{+}|\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\rm BB^{\prime}})
can be represented by the optimal observables
A_{1}^{\rm opt},A_{2}^{\rm opt}
and
B_{1}^{\rm opt},B_{2}^{\rm opt}
, respectively:

\displaystyle|\phi^{+}\rangle^{\rm AA^{\prime}}\langle\phi^{+}|=

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{\openone+A_{1}^{\rm opt}}{2}\otimes\left.|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0|\right.+\frac{A_{2}^{\rm opt}-A_{2}^{\rm opt}A_{1}^{\rm opt}}{2}\otimes\left.|0\right\rangle\left\langle 1|\right.

\displaystyle+\frac{A_{2}^{\rm opt}-A_{1}^{\rm opt}A_{2}^{\rm opt}}{2}\otimes\left.|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0|\right.+\frac{\openone-A_{1}^{\rm opt}}{2}\otimes\left.|1\right\rangle\left\langle 1|\right.

and

\displaystyle|\phi^{+}\rangle^{\rm BB^{\prime}}\langle\phi^{+}|=

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{\openone+B_{1}^{\rm opt}}{2}\otimes\left.|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0|\right.+\frac{B_{2}^{\rm opt}-B_{2}^{\rm opt}B_{1}^{\rm opt}}{2}\otimes\left.|0\right\rangle\left\langle 1|\right.

\displaystyle+\frac{B_{2}^{\rm opt}-B_{1}^{\rm opt}B_{2}^{\rm opt}}{2}\otimes\left.|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0|\right.+\frac{\openone-B_{1}^{\rm opt}}{2}\otimes\left.|1\right\rangle\left\langle 1|\right.

In a DI setting, the measurements are uncharacterized, therefore we relax the optimal observables to unknown Hermitian operators, i.e.,

A_{x}^{\rm opt}\rightarrow A_{x},\quad B_{y}^{\rm opt}\rightarrow B_{y}.

(22)

Consequently, a DI description of the fidelity between the reference state
|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle
and
\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho^{\rm AB})
is

\displaystyle F^{\rm DI}=\langle\psi^{\rm ref}|\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho)|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle

(23)

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\rm ref}|\tr_{\rm AB}[(\openone^{\rm A^{\prime}}\otimes\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\openone^{\rm B^{\prime}})(\Omega^{\rm AA^{\prime}}\otimes\Omega^{\rm BB^{\prime}})]|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\rm ref}|\tr_{\rm AB}[(\openone^{\rm A^{\prime}}\otimes\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\openone^{\rm B^{\prime}})(|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|\otimes|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|)]|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,l}{\ \left\langle\psi^{\rm ref}\right|\left(|i\right\rangle_{\rm A^{\prime}}\left\langle j|\right.\otimes\left.|k\right\rangle_{\rm B^{\prime}}\left\langle l|)\left|\psi^{\rm ref}\right\rangle\right.\ }

\displaystyle\rm\displaystyle\cdot tr\mathit{\left(\rho^{\mathsf{T}}\left.|i\right\rangle_{\rm A}\left\langle j|\right.\otimes\left.|k\right\rangle_{\rm B}\left\langle l|\right.\right)}

The detailed derivation of the last equality can be found in Appendix
A
. Note that in the definition of robust self-testing, Eq. (
8
) holds for all states compatible with the observed correlation
\mathbf{P}_{\rm obs}
. Therefore, a lower bound
f
can be operationally defined as the fidelity between the reference state and the
worst
state
\rho^{\rm AB}
(up to some local CPTPs). Namely, a choice for
f
can be computed as:

\displaystyle f:=\min_{\rho^{\rm AB}}~F^{\rm DI}=\min_{\rho^{\rm AB}}~\langle\psi^{\rm ref}|\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho)|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle.

(24)

On the other hand, since the constraints of
F^{\rm DI}
are more relaxed than those of
F
, together with the minimization problem given in the above equation, we have

f:=\min_{\rho^{\rm AB}}F^{\rm DI}\leq F

(25)

Note that here we don’t consider the optimization over all the CPTPs
\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}
.

Now, we focus on solving the minimization problem of Eq. (
24
). The problem can be formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\rho^{\rm AB}}

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\rm ref}|\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho^{\rm AB})|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle

(26)

\displaystyle\text{such that}

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm AB}~~\text{is compatible with~~}\mathbf{P}_{\rm obs}.

Or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\rm ref}|\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho^{\rm AB})|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle

(27)

\displaystyle\text{such that}

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}_{\rm obs},

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{Q},

where
\mathcal{Q}
is the set of quantum correlations. The last constraint is hard to characterize, but we can use the technique of semidefinite programming relaxation for quantum correlations
Navascués et al. 2008
;
Doherty et al. 2008
;
Tavakoli et al. 2023
to characterize a
superset
:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\rm ref}|\Lambda^{\rm A}\otimes\Lambda^{\rm B}(\rho)|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle

(28)

\displaystyle\text{such that}

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}_{\rm obs},

\displaystyle\Gamma\succeq 0,

where

\Gamma=\Gamma(\rho,\mathcal{S}):=\sum_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j|\tr\Big(S_{j}^{\dagger}S_{i}\rho\Big)

(29)

is the moment matrix of the state
\rho
associated with the sequence
\mathcal{S}
, which is composed of POVMs
E_{a|x},E_{b|y}
and their products. The minimization is taken over all unknown variables in
\Gamma
.

In Eq. (
28
), the objective function is a polynomial of expectation values of POVMs and their products, such as
\tr(\rho E_{a|x}\otimes E_{b|y}),\tr(\rho E_{a|x}E_{a^{\prime}|x^{\prime}}\otimes E_{b|y}),\tr(\rho E_{a|x}\otimes E_{b|y}E_{b^{\prime}|y^{\prime}})
etc. (see Eq. (
23
)). The moment matrix
\Gamma
contains these terms. Therefore, Eq. (
28
) is a semidefinite program (SDP)
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
, which can be solved numerically by some computer packages
Löfberg 2004
;
K. C. Toh and Tütüncü 1999
. We implement the computation of the SDP and attach the result in Fig.

V
Self-testing of the two-qubit partially entangled states

In this section, we show that the method can be applied to self-testing of a family of two-qubit partially entangled states:

|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle=\cos\theta|00\rangle+\sin\theta|11\rangle.

(30)

The first few steps are the same as the previous section. That is, first, we identify a Bell inequality which can be maximally violated by
|\psi^{\rm ref}\rangle
. An instance is the so-called
tilted CHSH inequality

Acín et al. 2012
;
Yang and Navascués 2013
;
Bamps and Pironio 2015
:

\displaystyle I_{\rm tilted-CHSH}:=\alpha\langle A_{1}\rangle+

(31)

\displaystyle\langle A_{1}B_{1}\rangle+\langle A_{1}B_{2}\rangle+\langle A_{2}B_{1}\rangle-\langle A_{2}B_{2}\rangle\leq 2+\alpha.

where
0\leq\alpha<2
. The maximal quantum violation
\sqrt{8+2\alpha^{2}}
can be achieved by the above quantum state and the following measurements:

\displaystyle A_{1}^{\rm opt}=Z,\quad B_{1}^{\rm opt}=\cos\mu Z+\sin\mu X,

(32)

\displaystyle A_{2}^{\rm opt}=X,\quad B_{2}^{\rm opt}=\cos\mu Z-\sin\mu X,

where the relations between
\theta
,
\alpha
, and
\mu
are

\tan\mu=\sin 2\theta=\sqrt{\frac{4-\alpha^{2}}{4+\alpha^{2}}}.

(33)

In the previous section, we apply a unitary on Bob’s side to make the optimal observables the same as Alice’s. Here, instead, we introduce additional two observables
B_{3}^{\rm opt}
and
B_{4}^{\rm opt}
such that
Yang et al. 2014
;
Bancal et al. 2015

\displaystyle B_{3}^{\rm opt}\left(\frac{B_{1}^{\rm opt}+B_{2}^{\rm opt}}{\cos\mu}\right)\succeq 0,\quad B_{4}^{\rm opt}\left(\frac{B_{1}^{\rm opt}-B_{2}^{\rm opt}}{\sin\mu}\right)\succeq 0.

(34)

The idea behind is that given an operator
B
, there is a unitary operator
U
such that
UB
is positive semidefinite. Besides, if
B
is unitary, then
U=B^{\dagger}
. As a result, since
(B_{1}^{\rm opt}+B_{2}^{\rm opt})/\cos\mu
and
(B_{1}^{\rm opt}-B_{2}^{\rm opt})/\sin\mu
are unitary, we have
B_{3}^{\rm opt}=Z
and
B_{4}^{\rm opt}=X
. This allows us to keep
B_{3}^{\rm opt}
and
B_{4}^{\rm opt}
unitary when they are relaxed to known observables.

Now, we again choose the identity operations for Alice’s and Bob’s CPTP maps. The associated Choi matrix is the subnormalized maximally entangled state and can be represented as follows:

\displaystyle\Omega^{\rm AA^{\prime}}=

VI
Summary and Discussion

In this work, we consider the previous method of self-testing steerable quantum assemblages and generalize this method to self-test entangled quantum states. In each Bell scenario under consideration, we obtain the perfect self-testing result for the maximal quantum violation of the Bell inequality. We also obtain non-trivial fidelity even when the Bell inequality violation is not maximal, which means that the self-testing result is robust.

We list some open questions as follows. First, can this method be applied to the self-testing of complex-valued quantum states? Second, are there other forms of the Choi matrix that can be used to increase the bounds of the fidelity? Finally, we expect that the method can be applied to other self-testing tasks, such as self-testing of channels and measurements.
