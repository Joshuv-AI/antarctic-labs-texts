A new class of finite difference methods: The zigzag schemes
Lorenzo Poggioni, Didier Clamond & Yves D’Angelo

Abstract
. We introduce a novel class of finite difference approximations, termed zigzag schemes , that employ a hybrid stencil that is neither symmetrical, nor fully one-sided. These zigzag schemes often enjoy more permissive stability constraints and see their coefficients vanish as the order tends to infinity. This property permits the formulation of higher order schemes. An explicit formula is given for both collocated and staggered grids for an arbitrary order and a closed-form expression for the infinite-order scheme is also provided. A linear stability analysis indicates that the zigzag scheme offer a broader range of conditional stability compared to the centred and upwind schemes, sometimes being the only stable scheme. Additionally, the asymmetrical structure of the stencil of zigzag schemes prevents some issues such as the formation of “ghost solutions”. Moreover, implementing zigzag schemes is relatively easy when a code using classical finite differences is available, that is an important feature for well-tested legacy codes. Overall, zigzag schemes provide a compelling alternative for finite differences methods by enabling faster and more stable numerical simulations without sacrificing accuracy or ease of use.



1.
Introduction

Introduced by Taylor
[
23
]
and Euler
[
4
]
in the
eighteenth century, finite difference methods remain a cornerstone in the
numerical approximation of partial differences equations. These methods are
used in a wide array of domains, including the study of immersed boundary
problems
[
5
,
15
,
18
,
19
]

and the study of hyperbolic conservation laws
[
9
,
12
,
20
]
. Additionally, the local nature of finite difference
schemes make them an adapted tool regarding high performance computing, for
both CPU
[
1
,
22
]
and GPU
[
8
,
11
,
24
]
. Traditional schemes, most notably the centred
and upwind schemes, are well known for their effectiveness and their simplicity.
However, these schemes are often characterised by tight restrictions regarding
their stability. These restrictions also tend to worsen as the order of the
schemes increases. Thus, hybrid difference schemes have been proposed for
convection-diffusion problems
[
21
]
. The idea is to use
locally a centred or a upwind/downwind schemes according to the characteristics
of the problem, and thus to take advantage of the good properties of both schemes.
An alternative approach is described in the present paper.

In this work, we introduce a novel finite difference method, termed

zigzag schemes
, designed to relax the aforementioned restrictions
while retaining the simplicity of implementation enjoyed by the centred and
upwind schemes. This allows the zigzag schemes to be implementable into already
well established legacy codes based on finite difference methods such as the
direct numerical simulation (DNS) solver H-Allegro
[
3
]
.
Furthermore, the performance of this new general method does not rely on
properties inherent to a specific class of equation. Contrary to the upwind
schemes, the coefficients of the zigzag schemes vanish as the order tends to
infinity, which allows the use of higher-order schemes.
These zigzag schemes are characterised by stencils that are neither fully
centred nor fully one-sided, but instead represent a hybrid of both. We
present an explicit formula for the coefficients of these schemes for
arbitrary orders, as well as a closed-form expression for the infinite-order
case.

The paper is organised as follows. Sections
2
and
3
present,
respectively, the classical centred and onde-sided (upwind) schemes. In section

4
, we introduce the new collocated and staggered zigzag schemes. Section

5
is devoted to a linear stability analysis of the zigzag schemes,
as well as a comparison with the centred and upwind schemes
showcasing situations where the zigzag schemes are the only stable schemes.
In the section
6
, we highlight a situation where zigzag
schemes are able to avoid problems inherent to symmetrical schemes (such as
the formation of “ghost solutions”) without being limited to lower
orders.

2.
Classical centred finite differences

2.1.
Schemes definition

Consider a function
f
of one real variable
x
, discretised with a constant step size
\Delta x
.
The discrete abscissa are noted as
x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}i\Delta x
and the discrete value of
f
at
x_{i}
is
noted
f_{i}=f(x_{i})
for
i\in\{1,2,...,N\}
.
A
2N
-th order centred finite approximation of
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
can be written
[
6
]

(1)

\displaystyle f_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{N}^{j}\frac{f_{i+j}-f_{i-j}}{2j\Delta x},

(2)

\displaystyle f_{i}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,C_{N}^{j}\,\frac{f_{i+j}-2f_{i}+f_{i-j}}{(j\,\Delta x)^{2}},

where

(3)

C_{N}^{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\frac{2(-1)^{j+1}(N!)^{2}}{(N+j)!(N-j)!}.

Note that as
N\rightarrow\infty
, the centred coefficients become

[
7
]

(4)

C_{\infty}^{j}=2(-1)^{j+1},

an infinite-order scheme can then be considered. If the interval of definition
of
f
is discretised using
P\geqslant 2
equally spaced points,
then necessarily
1\leqslant N\leqslant\lfloor(P-1)/2\rfloor
(
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
the
rounding towards
-\infty
), and appropriate boundary conditions must be taken
into account to deduce values at virtual nodes outside the definition domain.
Note that the information contained in (
1
) and (
2
) is sufficient to approximate derivatives of any order, since higher–order derivatives can be expressed as combinations of first- and second-order derivatives.

2.2.
Staggered variant

Instead of using nodes at integer positions
x=j\/\Delta x
, one may advantageously
consider nodes at intermediate positions.
The generic staggered centred scheme of order
2N
then is
[
6
]
:

(5)

f_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,\breve{C}_{N}^{j}\,\frac{f_{i+j-1/2}-f_{i-j+1/2}}{(2j-1)\,\Delta x},

where the coefficients
\breve{C}_{N}^{j}
are defined by

(6)

\breve{C}_{N}^{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}\,4^{1-2N}\,(2N)!^{2}}{(2j-1)\,N!^{2}\,(N+j-1)!\,(N-j)!},\qquad\breve{C}_{\infty}^{j}=\frac{4\,(-1)^{j+1}}{(2j-1)\,\pi}.

Note that the staggered centred scheme coefficients decay faster than the coefficients
of the collocated centred scheme. The use of such staggered schemes leads to a
substantial gain in accuracy over the centred ones with identical
N
and
\Delta x

[
6
]
.

2.3.
Relation to Lanczos’
\sigma
-factors

For a pure frequency of wavenumber
k
, i.e.

f=\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}
(with
\text{i}^{2}=-1
not to be confused
with an index
i
), we have

(7)

\left[\left(\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}\right)^{\prime}\right]_{i}=\text{i}k\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{N}^{j}\frac{\sin(kx_{j})}{kx_{j}}=\sigma_{c}(k,N)\,\text{i}k\,\text{e}^{\text{i}kx},

where

(8)

\sigma_{c}(k,N)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{N}^{j}\text{sinc}\!\left(\frac{j\pi k}{k_{\text{max}}}\right)\qquad\text{with}\qquad\begin{cases}k_{\text{max}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\pi/\Delta x\\
\text{sinc}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\sin(x)/x\\
x_{j}&=j\Delta x=j\pi/k_{\text{max}}\end{cases}

Here,
\sigma_{c}(k,N)
is called the
centred
\sigma
–factor of order
2N

and
\sigma_{c}(k,1)
is the classical
\sigma
-factor of
Lanczos 1988
.
Note that
\sigma_{c}
satisfies the properties:
\sigma_{c}(k,N)\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sigma_{c}(k,N)=\sigma_{c}(-k,N)
,
\sigma_{c}(0,N)=1
and
\sigma_{c}(k_{\text{max}},N)=0
.

For a pure frequency, higher-order derivatives can concisely be defined as

(9)

\displaystyle\left[\left(\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}\right)^{\!(2n)}\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=(\text{i}k)^{2n}\,\text{e}^{\text{i}kx_{i}}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{N}^{j}\,\text{sinc}\!\left(\frac{j\,\pi\,k}{2\,k_{\text{max}}}\right)^{\!2n},

(10)

\displaystyle\left[\left(\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}\right)^{\!(2n+1)}\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=(\text{i}k)^{2n+1}\,\text{e}^{\text{i}kx_{i}}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{N}^{j}\,\cos\!\left(\frac{j\,\pi\,k}{2\,k_{\text{max}}}\right)\text{sinc}\!\left(\frac{j\,\pi\,k}{2\,k_{\text{max}}}\right)^{\!2n+1},

the elementary proof being left to the reader.

2.4.
Practical computation of high orders

In practice, the coefficients
C_{N}^{j}
are difficult to handle numerically for
the (not so) large
N
because of the factorials that appear within the formulas,
leading to large round-off errors.
To avoid both inaccuracies and integer overflows, certain computational
techniques should be used.

One trick is to rely on the logarithm of the
\Gamma
–function.
Using this approach, one can compute
C_{N}^{j}
for higher values of
N
faster
and more accurately using the optimised
\mathtt{Gammaln}
function
[
2
]

and exploiting the relation

\frac{(N!)^{2}}{(N+j)!(N-j)!}=\exp\!\left(2\/\log\!\left[\Gamma(N+1)\right]-\log\!\left[\Gamma(N+j+1)\right]-\log\left[\Gamma(N-j+1)\right]\right).

However, this approach merely delays the issue by a few orders. Indeed, even using this technique,
we found that it was sometimes impossible to go beyond a few hundred in order without overflow.
To overcome this limitation, we found that an accurate and robust way to compute
C_{N}^{j}

for any
N\in\mathds{N}
is to exploit the relation

(11)

\frac{(N!)^{2}}{(N+j)!(N-j)!}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{j}{N+l}\right)=\exp\!\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\text{log1p}\!\left(\frac{-j}{N+l}\right)\right],

where log1p
(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\log(1+x)
is specifically designed
[
10
]

to be accurate to machine precision as
1+x\approx 1
. To our knowledge, this
technique never appeared before in the literature (for computed the finite
difference weights). It allows to compute finite differences coefficients of
large orders accurately within a reasonable time, as illustrated in Table

for the computation of the staggered coefficients. Note
that this concise formula performs similarly to Fornberg’s efficient algorithm

3.
Classical forward/backward finite differences

It is well known that, in certain situations, centred schemes can be unstable.
An alternative is to consider stencils that are not symmetric. Fully non-centred (i.e. one-sided) schemes can then be considered.
Usual
N
-th order forward/backward schemes for
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
approximations can be written as

(12)

\displaystyle f_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,D_{N}^{j}\,\frac{f_{i\pm j}-f_{i}}{\pm j\,\Delta x},\qquad f_{i}^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,D_{N}^{j}\,\frac{f_{i\pm 2j}-2\,f_{i\pm j}+f_{i}}{(j\,\Delta x)^{2}},

where

(13)

D_{N}^{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}\,N!}{j!\,(N-j)!}.

Thus, for a pure frequency
f=\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}
, this yields

(14)

\displaystyle\left[\left(\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}\right)^{\prime}\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=\text{i}k\,\text{e}^{\text{i}kx_{i}}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,D_{N}^{j}\,\frac{\exp(\text{i}kx_{\pm j})-1}{\text{i}kx_{\pm j}}

(15)

\displaystyle=\text{i}k\,\text{e}^{\text{i}kx_{i}}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,D_{N}^{j}\,\left[\frac{\sin(kx_{\pm j})}{kx_{\pm j}}+\text{i}\frac{1-\cos(kx_{\pm j})}{kx_{\pm j}}\right].

The latter relation suggests the introduction of a
forward/backward
\sigma
-factor of
order
N
which, keeping the notation introduced in (
8
), is defined by

(16)

\sigma_{{{\textsc{f/b}}}}(k,N)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,D_{N}^{j}\,\left[\text{sinc}\left(\frac{j\pi k}{k_{\text{max}}}\right)\pm\text{i}\frac{1-\cos(j\pi k/k_{\text{max}})}{j\pi k/k_{\text{max}}}\right].

This
\sigma
-factor verifies
\sigma_{{{\textsc{f/b}}}}(k,N)\in\mathds{C}
,

\sigma_{{{\textsc{f/b}}}}(-k,N)=\overline{\sigma_{{{\textsc{f/b}}}}(k,N)}
,

\sigma_{{{\textsc{f/b}}}}(0,N)=1
,
\sigma_{{{\textsc{f/b}}}}(2\/k_{\text{max}},N)=0
and

\operatorname{Re}\{\sigma_{{{\textsc{f/b}}}}(k_{\text{max}},N)\}=0
, (
\overline{(.)}
denoting
the complex conjugate of
(.)
).

One can notice that, as
N\rightarrow\infty
, the coefficients
D_{N}^{j}
do not
converge since

(17)

\left|D_{N}^{j}\right|\sim N^{j}\,/\,j!\qquad\text{as}\qquad N\rightarrow\infty.

Consequently, this approach is restricted to relatively low orders
N
. To overcome these
limitations, we propose a novel non-centred scheme.

4.
Zigzag finite differences

Rather than solely relying on forward or backward schemes, we consider here a hybrid approach
combining both. More specifically, high-order non-centred schemes can be constructed alternating
forward and backward differences, starting with either a forward or backward difference.
We call these hybrid schemes
forward/backward-first zigzag schemes
.

4.1.
Definition of zigzag schemes for regular grids

The figures

where the coefficients

4.2.
Zigzag
\sigma
-factors

Considering a pure frequency
f=\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\/k\/x}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}\right)_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\text{i}k\,\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,Z_{N}^{j}\,\frac{\exp\left(\text{i}k\,x_{\mp j(-1)^{j}}\right)-1}{\text{i}k\,x_{\mp j(-1)^{j}}}

(27)

\displaystyle=\text{i}k\,\text{e}^{\text{i}kx}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,Z_{N}^{j}\,\left[\frac{\sin\left(k\,x_{\mp j(-1)^{j}}\right)}{k\,x_{\mp j(-1)^{j}}}+\text{i}\,\frac{1-\cos\left(k\,x_{\mp j(-1)^{j}}\right)}{k\,x_{\mp j(-1)^{j}}}\right],

which, once again using the notations introduced in (

\sigma

(28)

\sigma_{{{\textsc{z}}}}(k,N)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,Z_{N}^{j}\,\left[\text{sinc}\left(\frac{j\,\pi\,k}{k_{\text{max}}}\right)\mp(-1)^{j}\,\text{i}\,\frac{1-\cos\left(j\pi k\,/\,k_{\text{max}}\right)}{j\pi k\,/\,k_{\text{max}}}\right],

where the upper (resp. lower) sign corresponds to the forward-first (resp. backward-first) scheme.

A closed-form of the
\infty

\sigma

forward-first (the backward-first being the complex conjugate of the forward-first),
we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{{{\textsc{z}}}}(k,\infty)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{4(-1)^{\ell+1}(2\ell-2)!}{4^{\ell}(\ell-1)!^{2}}\frac{\exp((2\ell-1)\text{i}\pi k\,/\,k_{\text{max}})-1}{(2\ell-1)\text{i}\pi k\,/\,k_{\text{max}}}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}(2\ell)!}{4^{\ell}(\ell!)^{2}}\frac{\exp(-2\ell\text{i}\pi k\,/\,k_{\text{max}})-1}{2\ell\text{i}\pi k\,/\,k_{\text{max}}}

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\text{max}}}{\text{i}\pi k}\log\left(\frac{\exp(\text{i}\pi k\,/\,k_{\text{max}})+\sqrt{1+\exp(2\text{i}\pi k\,/\,k_{\text{max}})}}{1+\sqrt{1+\exp(-2\text{i}\pi k\,/\,k_{\text{max}})}}\right),

where we have exploited the two relations

(30)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\,\frac{4(-1)^{\ell+1}(2\ell-2)!}{4^{\ell}(\ell!)^{2}}=\text{arcsinh}(X)=\log\left(X+\sqrt{1+X^{2}}\right),

(31)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}(2\ell)!}{4^{\ell}(\ell!)^{2}}\frac{X^{2\ell}}{2\ell}=\log\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}{2}\right).

4.3.
Zigzag schemes for staggered grids

Zigzag schemes can be also considered for staggered grids,
the staggered variant of these zigzag schemes being obviously

(32)

f_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,\breve{Z}_{N}^{j}\,\frac{f_{i\mp(j-1/2)(-1)^{j}}-f_{i}}{\mp(-1)^{j}\,(j-1/2)\Delta x},

where the coefficients
\breve{Z}_{N}^{j}
are defined by

(33)

\breve{Z}_{N}^{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}\frac{(-1)^{\left\lceil 1+j/2\right\rceil}\,(2N-1)!}{(2j-1)\,8^{N-1}\,(N-1)!}\,\left(\left\lfloor\frac{N+j-1}{2}\right\rfloor!\,\left\lfloor\frac{N-j}{2}\right\rfloor!\right)^{-1}.

Again, this formula was inferred from numerically computed
coefficients, then verified using a computer algebra system
up to order one thousand.

5.1.
Euler explicit scheme

We start with the first-order explicit Euler scheme
\mathrm{d}\/U(t)/\mathrm{d}\/t\approx[U(t+\Delta t)-U(t)]/\Delta t
which, after substitution in (

\frac{U(t+\Delta t)-U(t)}{\Delta t}\/+\/\text{i}\,k\,c\,\sigma(k)\,U(t)\,=\,0.

With

(
a\in\mathds{R}
), one obtains

\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\/\omega\/\Delta t}=1-\text{i}k\,\Delta t\,c\sigma(k)

G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}U(t+\Delta t)/U(t)=\text{e}^{\text{i}\omega\Delta t}

[
14

||G||_{\infty}\leqslant 1
shows
that the solution is bounded if and only if (for any wavenumber

|1-\text{i}\Delta t\,ck\sigma(k)|\leqslant 1.

Let be

\kappa=k/k_{\text{max}}

4

considered,

(39)

(\pi\lambda\kappa\sigma)^{2}\leqslant 0,

which is never satisfied.
In other words, this means that centred schemes are unconditionally
unstable, a very well-known result
[
17
]
.

5.1.1.
Forward finite differences scheme of order 1

With the spatial derivative approximated by a first-order forward
finite difference scheme, the corresponding
\sigma
-factor is

(40)

\sigma(\kappa)=\frac{\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}-1}{\text{i}\pi\kappa}.

Substituting (
40
) into the condition (
38
),
we obtain the stability condition

(41)

\forall\kappa\in[-1,1],\quad|1+\lambda-\lambda\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}|\leqslant 1\quad\implies\quad\lambda(1+\lambda)\leqslant 0\quad\implies\quad-1\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 0.

In order to satisfy the above condition, the wave celerity
c
needs to
be negative (when the time increases). This means that the scheme must be
“upwind”
[
17
]
. Condition (
41
) also implies that
|\Delta x/\Delta t|\geqslant c
, meaning that the “numerical speed” must be greater
than the physical speed
c
. (If the celerity of the wave is positive, then
a backward scheme must be considered instead.) The corresponding stability
region is given in appendix
C
.

5.1.2.
Forward finite differences scheme of order 2

For this scheme, we have

(42)

\sigma(\kappa)=\text{i}\frac{\text{e}^{2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3}{2\pi\kappa},

and the substitution of (
42
) into (
38
) yields

(43)

\left\lvert 1+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\text{e}^{2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3\right)\right\rvert\leqslant 1.

After doing computations similar to those done in (
41
), we show that (
43
) implies
\lambda=0
.
Hence, we can conclude that the second-order purely forward scheme (with
first-order explicit Euler temporal scheme) is stable only for some wavenumbers,
but never for all wavenumbers. Therefore, it is unconditionally unstable.

5.1.3.
Zigzag finite differences scheme of order 2

Consider now the zigzag forward-first finite difference scheme of order
2

(recall that the first-order zigzag scheme coincides with the first-order
purely forward scheme); this scheme can be defined via its
\sigma
-factor

(44)

\sigma(\kappa)=\text{i}\frac{\text{e}^{-2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3}{6\pi\kappa}.

Substituting
\sigma
into (
38
) gives the condition

(45)

\left\lvert 1+\frac{\lambda}{6}\left(\text{e}^{-2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3\right)\right\rvert\leqslant 1.

After some computations we can show that this also implies
\lambda=0
.
Hence, as for the pure forward scheme, the zigzag scheme of order two
(with explicit Euler) is unconditionally unstable (c.f. Appendix
C
)
due to the lack of stability for low wavenumbers.

5.2.1.
Forward finite differences scheme of order 1

The
\sigma

(50)

\sigma(\kappa)=\frac{\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}-1}{\text{i}\pi\kappa},

substituted into (

\lambda

[-1,0]
. We note that, like in

5.2.2.
Forward finite differences scheme of order 2

In this case, the
\sigma
-factor

(53)

\sigma(\kappa)=\text{i}\frac{\text{e}^{2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3}{2\pi\kappa},

substituted into the von Neumann stability condition (

) yields

(54)

\left\lvert 1+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\text{e}^{2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\left(\text{e}^{2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3\right)^{2}\right\rvert\leqslant 1.

A study of the condition (
54
) shows that
\lambda\in[-\frac{1}{2},0]
.
Thus, the second-order forward finite differences scheme is conditionally
stable.
Similarly, for the first-order scheme, the strictest constraint on the stability-number
is imposed by the Nyquist wavenumber
(see Annex
D
).
Note that the stability strip is half as wide as that of the first-order.

5.2.3.
Forward finite differences scheme of order
3

For this scheme, the
\sigma
-factor is

(55)

\sigma(\kappa)=-\frac{\text{i}}{6\pi\kappa}\left(2\text{e}^{3\text{i}\pi\kappa}-9\text{e}^{2\text{i}\pi\kappa}+18\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}-11\right),

which gives

(56)

\left\lvert 1-\frac{\lambda}{6}\left(2\text{e}^{3\text{i}\pi\kappa}-9\text{e}^{2\text{i}\pi\kappa}+18\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}-11\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{72}\left(2\text{e}^{3\text{i}\pi\kappa}-9\text{e}^{2\text{i}\pi\kappa}+18\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}-11\right)^{2}\right\rvert\leqslant 1.

A study of this condition reveals that
\lambda=0
.
This scheme is thus unstable.
Moreover, numerical observations seem to show that upwind schemes of
orders
3
and above are unstable.

5.2.4.
Zigzag finite differences scheme of order 2

The
\sigma
-factor corresponding to this scheme is

(57)

\sigma(\kappa)=\text{i}\frac{\text{e}^{-2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3}{6\pi\kappa},

which once injected in (
48
) yields

(58)

\left\lvert 1+\frac{\lambda}{6}\left(\text{e}^{-2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{4\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{72}\left(\text{e}^{-2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-4\text{e}^{4\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3\right)^{2}\right\rvert\leqslant 1.

This condition implies
\lambda P(\lambda)\leqslant 0
, with
P
defined by

\displaystyle P(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\ \ \lambda^{3}\left[64\cos\left(\kappa\pi\right)^{4}+80\cos\left(\kappa\pi\right)^{3}+105\cos\left(\kappa\pi\right)^{2}+50\cos\!\left(\kappa\pi\right)+25\right]

\displaystyle+\lambda^{2}\left[-96\cos\left(\kappa\pi\right)^{3}+36\cos\left(\kappa\pi\right)^{2}+60\right]

\displaystyle+\lambda\left[\cos\left(\kappa\pi\right)^{2}-144\cos\!\left(\kappa\pi\right)+72\right]+216.

The polynomial
P
possesses a global extremum equal to
324\lambda^{3}+216

at
\kappa=0
for any
\lambda
. This extremum is null for the critical

\lambda_{c}=-\sqrt[3]{2}\left(\sqrt[3]{3}\right)^{2}/3\approx-0.8736
.
It corresponds to a minimum of
P
if and only if
\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{c}
,
and it is a maximum if and only if
\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{c}
. Hence, this
scheme is conditionally stable, under the condition
\lambda_{c}\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 0
.

The condition on the stability-number for this scheme is less restrictive than that
of the forward-scheme for the same order. Indeed, the stability strip

[-1/2,0]
is included in
\left[\lambda_{c},0\right]
. Note that this time,
the most restrictive stability condition is imposed by the null wavenumber.

5.2.5.
Zigzag finite differences scheme of order 3

The
\sigma
-factor of this scheme is

(59)

\sigma(\kappa)=-\frac{2\text{e}^{3\text{i}\pi\kappa}+3\text{e}^{-2\text{i}\pi\kappa}-30\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi\kappa}+25}{30\text{i}\pi\kappa}

Which after substitution in the von Neumann condition (
48

\left\lvert 1+\frac{\lambda}{30}\left(2\,{\mathrm{e}}^{3\,\text{i}\kappa\pi}+3\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-2\,\text{i}\kappa\pi}-30\,{\mathrm{e}}^{\text{i}\kappa\pi}+25\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{1800}\left(2\,{\mathrm{e}}^{3\,\text{i}\kappa\pi}+3\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-2\,\text{i}\kappa\pi}-30\,{\mathrm{e}}^{\text{i}\kappa\pi}+25\right)^{2}\right\rvert\leqslant 1.

A similar procedure to the one followed for the condition (
58
)
gives
-\frac{15}{14}\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 0
.
This scheme remains conditionally stable and it is the first one allowing

|\lambda|>1
thus far. Note that for the same order, the zigzag-scheme is
conditionally stable whilst the equivalent forward-scheme is not.

5.2.6.
Zigzag finite differences scheme of order 4

For this scheme,

(60)

\sigma(\kappa)=\-\frac{336\,{\mathrm{e}}^{\text{i}\kappa\pi}-245-84\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-2\,\text{i}\kappa\pi}-16\,{\mathrm{e}}^{3\,\text{i}\kappa\pi}+9\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-4\,\text{i}\kappa\pi}}{420\text{i}\kappa\pi}.

the third-order seems to be the highest order allowing the stability of the

6.
Prevention of ghost solutions

In the recent work,
Inglard et al. 2020
showcased the formation of ghost solutions
when solving the advection equation (
34
) using centred schemes with von Neumann
boundary conditions. The term “ghost solution” designates the anomalous formation
of a periodic solution, despite imposing von Neumann conditions on the boundaries of
the domain (see Fig.
13
).
Under the same conditions formulated in
[
13
]
, the replacement of the
centred scheme by a one-sided scheme, or by a zigzag scheme completely prevents the
formation of ghost solutions.
However, as mentioned in section
3
, the coefficients of the one-sided scheme diverge
as the order tends to infinity. This property limits the use of one-sided schemes to low orders.
The zigzags schemes do not suffer such a restriction. In other words, the zigzag schemes
allow the use of high-order schemes using von Neumann boundary conditions, without forming ghost solutions.
A comparison of the centred and the zigzag schemes with von Neumann conditions is provided in Fig.

.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a novel class of finite differences approximations,
referred to as
zigzag schemes
. These schemes employ hybrid stencils that
are neither fully centred nor fully one-sided, leading to encouraging results.
Specifically, as summarised in tables
6
to
12
,
zigzag schemes exhibit not only a broader range of conditional stability but also,
in some cases, a significantly higher critical stability-number, sometimes exceeding
that of standard schemes by a factor of two or more. Moreover, the coefficients

Z_{N}^{j}
of the zigzag schemes vanish as the order tends to infinity (see Eq.
(
26
)). The zigzag schemes thus allow the use of higher
order schemes.
Overall, zigzag schemes offer a compelling alternative for finite difference
simulations. They enable faster computations at comparable accuracy levels,
while retaining the simplicity of implementation characteristic of
conventional schemes.
Furthermore, a staggered variant of the zigzag schemes was presented in section

4.1
, alongside a closed-form expression for the stencil coefficients at
infinite order.

Moreover, the asymmetrical structure of the zigzag schemes prevents the formation
of some issues arising when using symmetrical (centred) schemes. This
was illustrated in section
6
where we showed that zigzags
schemes are able to prevent the formation of anomalous “ghost solutions”.

Lastly, we remark that the zigzag schemes are defined by a signature following
the pattern
+-+-+-+
(or
-+-+-+
). This choice was made because it seemed to
be the most “natural” and “easier” way to blend centred and one-sided schemes
together. However, other schemes employing other (potentially random) signatures
could be investigated.

Appendix B
Numerical diffusion of the zigzag schemes

The centred, upwind and zigzag finite differences schemes all
introduce some
numerical diffusion. If we compare the rate at which these schemes
dissipate, we notice that the energy loss from the zigzag schemes
is lower than the one-sided schemes and greater than the centred
schemes. This middle-ground is not surprising since the zigzag
schemes can be seen as a mix of one-sided and centred schemes. To
illustrate this observation with a concrete example, we consider
the solution of the transport equation (
34
) using a
Runge–Kutta scheme of order
3
and spatial schemes of order
2
.
We solve this equation for
(t,x)\in[0,15]\times[-20,20]

using a periodised error function as the initial condition, with
the parameters
\Delta t=0.05
and
\Delta x=0.01
. For the
centred, zigzag and upwind schemes we obtain an energy loss of
respectively
2.18\cdot 10^{-9}
,
3.71\cdot 10^{-8}
and
1.07\cdot 10^{-7}
.
