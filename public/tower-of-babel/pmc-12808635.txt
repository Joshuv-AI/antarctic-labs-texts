Impact of a novel ultra-processed foods and drinks diet on metabolism and behaviour in adolescent female and male rats
Authors: Adam Alvarez-Monell, Verónica Palomera-Ávalos, Eva Coll Liesa, Oren Contreras-Rodríguez, Montserrat Solanas, Rosa M Escorihuela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mental health affections induced by ultra-processed foods (UPF) in humans have been consistently reported. We aim to develop an experimental UPF diet based on typical UPF products consumed by human adolescents and to characterize its impact on health and behaviour in male and female rats. In a first experiment, 30 UPFs were administered, one every day, plus standard chow, compared with rats fed standard chow. In a second experiment, we combined selected UPFs from the first experiment in 4 menus, consisting in 4 solid and 1 liquid item each one. After weaning, 48 rats were divided in 4 groups according to sex and diet. UPF groups were fed the UPF diet, plus standard chow, and STD groups only chow over 6 weeks.UPF feeding induced increased body weight, abdominal circumference and total and visceral adiposity, as well as hyperglycaemia, hypertriglyceridemia and hyperleptinemia compared to STD feeding. UPF feeding also increased activity in the hole board, diminished anxiety-like behaviour in the elevated plus maze, and marginally improved object recognition in females in the object recognition task. In conclusion, the designed UPF diet induced several metabolic alterations and behavioural changes and may be helpful to further study the UPF effects on health and the underlying mechanisms during longer exposures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31884-4.

Introduction

Ultra-processed foods and drinks (UPFs) are food items of human consumption that result from industrial processing1​. UPFs contain additives to intensify their sensory qualities, and make them palatable, highly attractive, and ready-to-eat or ready-to-heat. These food items are characterized by usually being highly energy dense due to having significant amounts of saturated and trans fats, added sugars and salt, and at the same time, being low in nutrient density, having minimal protein, fibre and micronutrients2​. Despite this, other UPFs can be low in energy content due to the use of non-caloric sweeteners and other synthetic additives. UPFs have been shown to induce hyperphagia and increased weight gain in humans even compared to minimally processed foods with similar nutritional profiles, indicating that their mechanism of action has not been stablished, and that more regulatory policies are needed3.

In recent decades, global sales of these products have increased in countries across all income ranges4, with estimates showing a 43% increase in sales5. UPF production and consumption growth have been spectacular over the last decades. UPFs are usually eaten in combination and often overeaten. In a recent umbrella review including data from almost 10 million individuals across all age ranges6, high UPF consumption has been linked​ with several types of cancer, decreased cardiovascular and metabolic health, and with decreased mental health, while showing an association with all-cause mortality. Regarding mental health, in a meta-analysis study including data from 385.541 individuals across all age ranges, increased UPF consumption was linked to increased depressive and anxiety symptomatology and increased perceived stress7. A cohort study including 31.712 females, aged 42–62 years suggested that greater UPF intake (particularly, artificial sweeteners and artificially sweetener beverages), is associated with increased risk of depression8.

In children and adolescents, the increase in UPF consumption is particularly distressing. A serial cross-sectional analysis using 24-hour dietary recall data from a representative sample of US youths aged 2–19 years (n = 33795) reported that from 1999 to 2018 the estimated percentage of total energy consumed from UPF increased from 61.4% to 67.0%, and that of the ready-to-heat and-eat mixed dishes from 2.2% to 11.2% 9. These results are similar to those found in European countries, as such, a study with 806 participants aged 5 years old in Spain, the mean UPF intake was 37–64% of total energy intake10, and in a Belgian study with 3083 participants aged 15 years or less, the mean UPF consumption was 29.2% 11. UPF consumption has been associated with metabolic syndrome and increased risk of wheezing, but not asthma, in adolescents, and dyslipidaemia in children12.

Despite this increase in consumption and the observed effects of UPF in humans, no experimental diet based on typical consumed UPFs has been developed to study mechanistic effects of UPF intake in animal models. The animal model closest to a UPF model is the cafeteria (CAF) diet13​, which is a diet-induced obesity model based on items of human consumption. Although processed food items are used in CAF diets, these are considered as a diet-induced obesity model rather than highly processed food intake models13​. As stated above, a specific characteristic of UPF is the high number of additives they contain, such as sweeteners, emulsifiers, foaming agents, colouring agents, micro- and nanoparticles among others14. Thus, specific additives such as emulsifiers have been shown to increase inflammatory biomarkers and alter intestinal microbiota, artificial sweeteners have been associated with chronic liver disease, and titanium dioxide nanoparticles has been shown to trigger the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines15. A specific experimental UPF diet model might help shed light on the mechanisms in which their effects are exerted.

Even though the effects of UPF consumption and classical obesogenic diets are overlapping (alterations in metabolic profile, increased cardiovascular risk and mental health deterioration)16–19, the link between UPF consumption and diabetes or dyslipidaemia has been found strong, independently of obesity status20​. Furthermore, UPF consumption in humans has been observed to increase cardiovascular risk, dementia prevalence, and depressive symptoms when controlling the analyses for BMI21–23​, indicating effects of UPF consumption independent of the development of obesity. This evidence points towards the effects and mechanisms of action of UPF may go, at least in part, beyond those derived from the obesity their regular consumption may induce.

In the present study we have developed a novel UPF diet based on typical UPFs consumed by adolescents. We performed a first experiment to test whether a set of UPFs consumed by adolescents24​ were accepted and consumed by adolescent rats. These products were representative of the following food categories: cereals, fruit and vegetables, fats, dairy products, meats and other high-protein products, confectionery, and drinks. All foodstuffs used are classified as group 4 using the NOVA food classification system, which categorises foods based on the extent and purpose of processing they undergo1.

Based on this information, a UPF diet model was developed and tested in a second experiment. This diet includes 20 different UPF products combined in groups of 5 and administered in 4 different ”menus.” Each “menu” was designed to contain a varied selection of UPF products, including one or two carbohydrate-rich products, one or two meat-based-products, one snack/sauce/cream product, one sweet product, and one drink. UPF products included ready-to-eat and ready-to-heat sweet, savoury and salty products and beverages (soft drinks and broth). This UPF diet was tested in male and female rats post-weaning for 6 weeks to determine the effects of UPF consumption during adolescence, considered a critical neurobiological developmental and sensitive period25​. Given the increase in UPF consumption worldwide, and its known health risks20–23, an animal model specific for UPF consumption could help elucidate the mechanisms through which UPFs deteriorate health. We hypothesize that this UPF diet will induce obesity related alterations and behavioural effects in the animals exposed to it.

Materials and methods

Animals and general procedures

All the rats used in Experiments 1 and 2 were Long-Evans born and bred at the Unitat d’Experimentació Animal -UEA- at the Faculty of Psychology, Universitat de Barcelona. At the age of 22 days old males and females were assigned to two diet treatment. The Long-Evans rat was chosen because they are an outbred strain which can be particularly valuable for assessing diet effects due to their genetic diversity and metabolic profile. Additionally, they maintain characteristics closer to wild animals than the typical albino laboratory rat strains26.

Rats were housed in pairs at 22 °C under a light/dark period of 12 h (lights on at 08:00 am) in standard conditions of temperature (21 ± 1 °C) and humidity (50 ± 10%) with chow and water availability ad libitum.

The animals were fed with either standard chow, or chow plus UPF diets for 6 weeks from weaning onwards. Food consumption was recorded daily for every cage as the difference between the weight of each dietary component administered and the amount unconsumed after 24 h. Body weight (BW) was measured twice a week, and nose: anal length (NAL) and abdominal circumference (AC) once a week. The following variables were also calculated: Body Mass Index (BMI), as body weight (g) / NAL (cm)^2); Body Weight Gain (BWG), as the difference between the week’s weight and the weight of the previous week; and Lee Index (body weight (g)^1/3 / NAL (mm) * 10000).

All animals received human care under an institutionally approved experimental animal protocol, following the legislation applicable in Spain. The experimental protocol was approved by the Generalitat de Catalunya (reference number 12294), following the “Principles of laboratory animal care”, and was carried out in accordance with the EU Directive 2010/63/EU and Spanish legislation (RD 1386/2018) for animal experiments. The present study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Experiment 1

Fourteen male (M) and fourteen female (F) rats were used in the first experiment. For each sex, animals were distributed in the standard chow (STD) or UPF (Pilot UPF; PUPF) diets in four weight-matched groups (mean BW (g) ± SEM): M-STD, 47.9 ± 4.4 (n = 6); M-PUPF, 47.2 ± 3.7 (n = 8); F-STD, 52.18 ± 4.7 (n = 6); F-PUPF, 50.1 ± 4.8 (n = 8). One animal from the F-STD group died three days after the beginning of the study for reasons not related to the experiment.

The STD groups were fed with standard chow (Rat and Mouse No.1 Maintenance, RM1 (P), Ref. 801151, SDS, Augy, France) ad libitum during the entire experiment. The chow energy and macronutrients composition were 256 kcal, 14.4 g of proteins, 2.7 g of fat, 45 g of carbohydrates and 4.1 g of sugar per 100 g. The caloric distribution of chow among macronutrients was 22% proteins, 9.2% fat, and 68.8% carbohydrates. The PUPF groups were administered chow, water, and one of the 30 UPFs during weekdays (Table 1S) (Monday to Friday, 5 UPF/week), and chow and water during the weekend for the 6 weeks of the experiment. UPF products were tested one each day and in a random order. Therefore, on weekends all animals ate chow and drank water only. The solid UPF was placed in a clay pot inside the cage, and the UPF drink was placed in the cage in a second bottle apart the water bottle. Several UPFs (yakisoba soy noodles, meat cannelloni, croquettes, breaded ham and cheese, and macaroni with truffle sauce) were prepared in accordance with the instructions in the packaging.

The intakes of chow, water and UPF were calculated as the difference between the amount of food provided and the remaining the day after. Intakes of chow and water were calculated separately for those days were animals consumed (30-Chow and 30-Water, respectively) or not consumed (We-Chow, and We-Water, respectively) one of the UPF products, and a total variable was also calculated (Total Chow, and Total Water). In addition, we calculated the total solid and liquid food consumed (total solid food, and total liquid), and the specific amount of UPF intake (solid UPF, and liquid UPF). From the information in the food packaging, we calculated the total energy and macronutrients from chow and UPF intake.

Experiment 2

Twenty-four male (M) and twenty-four female (F) rats were used. For each sex, animals were distributed in the STD or the UPF diet in four weight-matched groups: (mean BW -g- ± SEM, n = 12): M-STD, 40.1 ± 1.8; M-UPF, 38.4 ± 1.8; F-STD, 35.2 ± 1.9; F-UPF, 35.9 ± 1.6.

From the data of the intake of each UPF product obtained in Experiment 1, we designed a diet based on the selected UPFs consisting of 4 different menus, each containing 4 solid UPFs and 1 UPF drink. Each “menu” was designed to contain a varied selection of UPF products, including one sweet product, one meat-based product, one carbohydrate-based product, and one drink. Total energy and macronutrients administered per menu (excluding chow) are shown in Table 1. The average energy and macronutrient composition across the 4 UPF menus was 267.2 kcal, 6.8 g of protein, 14.0 g of fat, 28.3 g of carbohydrates, and 13.5 g of sugars per 100 g of diet. The average caloric distribution among macronutrients was 10.3% protein, 47.0% fat, and 42.7% carbohydrates. The “menus” contained each between 37 and 46 food additives. Table 2S shows each additive together with its name and function as determined by the packaging and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). Table 3S shows the number of additives in each “menu”, categorized by their function as defined by the EFSA. The intakes of chow, water, and UPF were measured daily as the difference between the amount provided and the remaining the day after to calculate energy and macronutrient intakes for each cage.

Biometric and adiposity parameters

At the end of the experiment, the animals were anesthetized with isoflurane and immediately sacrificed by decapitation after 8 h of fasting. Total blood was collected, and serum was obtained by centrifugation at 2000 g and 4 °C for 15 min and stored at -80 °C until further analysis. The liver and white adipose tissue (WAT) depots (retroperitoneal -RWAT-, mesenteric -MWAT-, epididymal -EWAT-, periovaric -POWAT- and inguinal -IWAT-) were rapidly removed and weighed.

The adiposity index was calculated as the sum of the EWAT or POWAT (together referred to as gonadal -GWAT-), IWAT, MWAT and RWAT depot weights (in grams) and expressed as a proportion of body weight (g/kg). Abdominal WAT was the sum of RWAT, MWAT and GWAT, while IWAT was considered as representative of the subcutaneous WAT.

Behavioural procedures/testing

After 6 weeks of administering the diets (10 weeks of age), animals carried out behavioural experiments. The animals were tested for exploratory activity in the hole board (HB) (day 1), for anxiety-like behaviour in the elevated plus maze (EPM) (day 2), and for recognition memory in the novel object and novel place recognition tests (NOR and NPR) (days 3–6). All experiments were performed from 9:00 am to 2:00 pm in an isolated experimental room with dim lighting. All procedures were videotaped and later analysed by a researcher who was blind to the diet treatment each animal had received.

Hole-board test: The HB test was performed in a white (66 × 66 × 47 cm) wooden box with four equidistant holes (3.7 cm in diameter, 18 cm deep) in the floor. Each animal was placed in the right upper corner of the apparatus and allowed to explore for five minutes. The distance travelled was tracked from the video with the software ANY-Maze. The number of rearings and defecations, the number of head dips and the time spent head dipping were measured.

Elevated plus maze test: The EPM test apparatus was comprised by four arms (44 cm long x 10 cm wide) positioned 90º from each other in the shape of a plus sign extending from a 10 cm square centre. One pair of opposing arms had wooden walls 40 cm high (closed arms), whereas the other pair had only a 0.5 cm ridge that provided additional grip (open arms). The whole maze was elevated 50 cm above the floor. Each rat was placed in the centre of the maze facing a closed arm and allowed to explore for five minutes. The number of entries and total time spent in the open and closed arms (defined as placing all four paws into a given arm) were measured from the videos.

Novel object and novel place recognition (OR and PR) tests: The apparatus was a white wood open field (66 cm x 66 cm x 47 cm) placed in a separated room. The tests lasted four days. The first two days the animals were placed in the apparatus for 10 min to habituate. Testing was carried out during days 3 and 4. Half of the animals of each group performed the novel object recognition test at day 3 and the novel place recognition test at day 4. The other half performed the two tests in the opposite order.

For the novel object recognition test the two objects used were a cylindrical tomato can with a red ball attached at the top (object 1) and a rectangular pack of detergent (object 2). The test consisted of three phases: 1) The animal was placed in the apparatus with 2 identical objects (sample, S and S’) and allowed to explore for 5 min (familiarization phase); the objects were placed in the middle line of the open field and separated 20 cm. 2) Then, the animal was removed from the open field and returned to its home-cage for 5 min (retention phase). During this time the apparatus and the objects were cleaned with 70% ethanol; one of the sample objects was removed and replaced for a new one (N). 3) Finally, the animal was returned to the apparatus containing two objects (S and N) and allowed to explore for 3 min. Half of the animals of each experimental group were exposed to object 1 in the familiarization phase of testing, and the other half to object 2.

For the novel place recognition test the two different objects used were a cylindrical crystal jar of legumes (object 3) and a cylindrical plastic jar of cocoa cream (object 4). The test consisted of three phases: 1) The animal was placed in the apparatus with 2 identical objects (sample, S and S’) and allowed to explore for 5 min (familiarization phase); the objects were placed close to a wall. 2) Then, the animal was removed from the open field and returned to its home-cage for 5 min (retention phase). During this time the apparatus and the objects were cleaned with 70% ethanol, one of the sample objects (S’) was moved and placed close to the opposite wall of the open field (S’P). 3) Finally, the animal was returned to the apparatus containing two objects (S and S’P) and allowed to explore for 3 min. Half of the animals of each experimental group were exposed to object 3 and the other half to object 4.

In both tests the time spend exploring each object was recorded by a trained experimenter, the time exploring each object in the familiarization phase was added to obtain the total exploration time. In the test phase, exploration times were used to calculate the novelty index as: Time exploring the novel object (N or S’)/(Time exploring object N or S’+Time exploring object S), a novelty index of 0.5 indicates no discrimination, and above 0.5 indicates discrimination of the two objects.

Serum parameters

Enzymatic colorimetric kits were used for the determination of serum glucose, total cholesterol and triacylglycerides (QCA, Barcelona, Spain). Serum insulin and leptin levels were also measured using rat ELISA kits (Insulin: Mercodia AB, Uppsala, Sweeden; Leptin: Merck Millipore, Barcelona, Spain). The homeostasis model assessment-estimated insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated following the formula: HOMA-IR = Glucose X Insulin/22.5.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (SPSS, Inc, Chicago IL, USA). The homoscedasticity among groups was assessed using Levene’s test. The two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA: diet (STD, UPF) x sex (M, F)) was used to determine the effects and interaction of the independent variables. For the analysis of energy, food and macronutrients intakes we used the two-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) controlling for the body weight of the last week. Additionally, in Experiment 2, to account for the hyperphagia effects on the outcomes we also performed a second two-way ANCOVA analysis of biometric, adiposity, biochemical and behavioural data controlling for the total amount of solid food consumption as a covariate. This approach allows us to differentiate the effects that are attributable to the increased dietary intake caused by UPF feeding from the effects that are attributable to the UPFs themselves. Results of the ANCOVA analysis together with the ANOVA analysis are shown in Table 5S. Duncan’s post-hoc comparisons were used to determine differences among groups. The intakes of each UPFs were compared between the males and females’ groups by the Student’s t-test. As for variances inequality, n-par Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Mann-Whitney U test for comparisons between groups were applied. Spearman’s rho correlation analyses were performed to examine associations between the intakes of each “menu” (solid and liquid UPFs), as well as liquid UPFs alone, and the behavioural measures. Since animals were housed in pairs all analyses for food intake were performed at cage level, giving a total of 13 cages for Experiment 1 and 24 cages for Experiment 2. All other analysis were performed at animal level, giving a total of 27 for Experiment 1, and 48 for Experiment 2.

The PUPF groups showed lower chow intake compared with the STD groups during the days in which the UPF products were administered (F(1,8) = 255.677; p < 0.001) and during the weekends in which no UPF was administered (F(1,8) = 17.712; p = 0.003). Body composition

UPF diet resulted in higher relative weight of all adipose tissue depots analysed (F(1,44) > 12.255; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A–C, E), as well as the abdominal adiposity (F(1,44) = 26.227; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2D) and the adiposity index (F(1,44) = 20.070; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2F). Females in both diet groups showed higher abdominal adiposity than males (F(1,44) = 6.722; p = 0.013), while males showed higher subcutaneous (inguinal) adiposity (F(1,44) = 9.465; p = 0.004) (Fig. 2E). Females also showed higher gonadal relative adiposity (F(1,44) = 37.059; p < 0.001), which was further increased in F-UPF compared to F-STD (Fig. 2C). The ANCOVA analyses with the total amount of solid food added as a covariate showed a significant effect of the covariate for all depots analysed except GWAT (F(1,43) > 4.265; p < 0.045), and a significant effect of the Diet factor in RWAT (F(1,43) = 4.088; p = 0.049). This implies that the total amount of solid food consumed by the animals overall impacted these variables, and that there is a specific effect of UPF feeding in addition to the effect of total amount of solid food consumption on RWAT relative weight (Table 5S). By contrast, notice that adiposity variables are highly significant for the sex factor. The covariate is significant for all of them except Gonadal WAT, and sex is significant for all of them except inguinal WAT, indicating sex driver effects on specific WAT depots.

Metabolic parameters

UPF animals presented higher serum levels of glucose (F(1,44) = 8.927; p = 0.005) (Fig. 2G), triacylglycerides (F(1,44) = 4.978; p = 0.031) (Fig. 2H), and leptin (F(1,34) = 11.766; p = 0.002) (Fig. 2L). Total cholesterol levels (Fig. 2I) presented a significant diet*sex interaction (F(1,44) = 10.665; p = 0.002) and further decomposition of this interaction with pair-wise analysis showed that cholesterol levels were lower in UPF-fed females compared to STD-fed females (t(22) = 4.739; p < 0.001), while males showed similar levels independent of diet group (t(22) = 0.036; p = 0.971). Males in both diet groups also showed higher levels of triacylglycerides (F(1,44) = 20.199; p < 0.001) and leptin (F(1,34) = 11.161; p = 0.002) compared to females. No significant results were found for insulin levels (Fig. 2J) or the HOMA-IR (Fig. 2K). The ANCOVA analyses with the total amount of solid food added as a covariate showed no significant effects of the covariate or the diet factor. The interaction sex*diet in the total cholesterol levels remained significant indicating this effect is independent of the total amount of food consumed (Table 5S).

No significant effects were found for rearings (Fig. 3B). No effect was found for exploratory activity measured in the number of head dips and the time spent head-dipping. In the ANCOVA analyses with the total amount of solid food added as a covariate these effects were lost, however, there was a significant effect of the covariate (F(1,43) = 8.612; p = 0.005) and the factor Diet (F(1,43) = 7.497; p = 0.009) on the number of rearings. This implies that while the total amount of solid food consumed by the animals impacted these variables, there is a specific effect of UPF feeding in addition to the effect of total amount of solid food consumption. Association between UPF intake and behaviour

We explored the association between the UPF intake and the behavioural variables of activity. The intake of each of the 4 “menus” (total amount of all items in grams) during the second half of the experiment (from day 17 to 31) was correlated to the levels of activity and rearings in the OF and the HB (Table 6). The intakes of “menu” 1 and 4 were positively correlated with the rearings in the OF and the HB, and with the distance travelled in the HB, while the intakes of “menu” 2 were negatively correlated with the rearings performed by the animals in both tests, and the distance travelled in the HB. No significant associations were found for “menu” 3. These correlations of each “menu” were also obtained for the liquid UPF they contain, such as that the orange soft drink showed similar results to “menus” 1 and 4, the lemon isotonic soft drink to “menu” 3, and the chicken broth to “menu” 2.

Discussion

In the present study, we have developed a novel experimental UPF diet based on typical UPF products consumed by adolescents to test their effects on several biometric, metabolic and intake parameters as well as behaviour in adolescent male and female rats. This UPF diet induced: (i) hyperphagia in both sexes and resulted in higher intakes of proteins, fat, carbohydrates and sugars; (ii) increased body weight and adiposity, as well as metabolic alterations compatible with the development of metabolic syndrome (hyperglycaemia and hypertriglyceridemia); (iii) lowered anxiety-like behaviour in the EPM, as evidenced by a higher number of open arm entries, and residually boosted distance travelled in the OPF, indicating a increased motor activity; and (iv) differentially improved discrimination index in female better than in male rats.

To develop an UPF experimental diet a pilot experiment (Experiment 1) was performed. We tested several UPFs chosen based on their consumption by adolescent humans24, corresponding to main different nutritional groups. In this first experiment animals were administered one UPF daily to test the acceptance of each product (in terms of the amount consumed). This approach resulted in a moderate hyperphagia. Administering a different UPF daily without repetition might habituate the animals to novel foods and decrease the neophobia typically induced by the presentation of unknown foods27. Accordingly, the chow intake was lower when UPF was administered, which had been already observed by us on previous experiments using the cafeteria diet model28,29. The intakes of chow were also altered to a lesser extent during the weekends in which UPF was not administered, and this result is in accordance with other reports in which CAF diet was administered only 3 or 5 days per week30, or CAF diet administration was discontinued31, resulting in lower chow intakes. Our results, together with the fact that minimal biometric alterations were found, points towards daily administration of a single UPF having a relatively small effect on body composition and health. This pattern of administration also had small effects on macronutrient intake, since it only resulted in higher total fat and sugar intakes, while having no effect on total protein and carbohydrate intake. More notably, it induced minimal sex differences on macronutrient intake, only affecting fat intake which was higher in males than females. Higher increases in males than in females in fat intake compared to control diets have been reported previously in high-fat diet models32,33. Even though this experiment was originally designed to be used to develop the final UPF diet, it could be considered a model of sporadic UPF snacking, which according to our results would result in minimal alterations.

Based on the results obtained in the Experiment 1, an UPF diet was then developed. The final UPF diet was partially based on recommended practices for general cafeteria diet, consisting of a wide variety of products (20 individual UPFs), administered in a cyclic manner over 4 different “menus”, each including products from the different food categories and with a variety of taste, texture, energy and nutrient content13,34.

In Experiment 2, the UPF diet induced a greater degree of hyperphagia compared to the presentation of individual UPF products in Experiment 1. This was accompanied by a shift in macronutrient intake pattern. Specifically, while energy intake from carbohydrates and fat in Experiment 1 was 56.8% and 25.8%, respectively, these values shifted to 48.5% and 36.1% in Experiment 2. This suggests that the complete UPF diet promoted a more balanced intake of carbohydrates and fats, whereas isolated UPF items led to a stronger preference for carbohydrates. These results may indicate that high-carbohydrate items are more appealing when presented alone, but this preference diminishes when high-fat and high-sugar items are offered simultaneously. Energy intake from proteins remained more stable, at 17.4% for Experiment 1 and 15.4% for Experiment 2.

Interestingly, in Experiment 2, females in the UPF group showed lower intakes of the barbeque pizza and the mortadella, and the isotonic soft drink and the chicken broth than males. A previous study24 in a sample of 560 human adolescents revealed that boys had higher intakes of soft drinks, processed pizzas, and precooked foods than girls, amongst other products. These results might indicate that some rat’s food choices according to sex might mimic at least partially human adolescent food choices, indicating that UPF diet might be a suitable model for the study of the differential sex effects of UPF intake.

UPF feeding in Experiment 2 also showed effects compatible with obesity and the development of metabolic syndrome, with animals showing increased body weight, abdominal circumference, and adiposity, and hyperglycaemia, hypertriglyceridemia and hyperleptinemia.

Regarding body weight gain, the results indicated that it was greater in males than females, and in UPF-fed than the STD-feed animals. In a previous study from our lab in which Sprague-Dawley male and female rats were fed with STD or cafeteria diet for 8 weeks after weaning we observed greater increases in BW than in the present study35, indicating that, in spite of the differences in rat strain and intervention duration, CAF diet might induce greater effects on BWG than UPF feeding. The effect of diet remained significant on several biometric parameters (BW, AC, and BMI) after controlling for the total amount of food consumption, suggesting that, while hyperphagia is an important driver of increased biometric parameters, UPF intake also contributes to the deterioration of these at least partially independent of hyperphagia. It is important to note that UPF products contain additives that enhance their flavour and texture, making them hyper-palatable and highly rewarding, this can trigger addiction mechanisms and result in increased consumptions of this food items36. This effect of UPF products implies that the results for the hyperphagia covariate can also be considered an effect of UPF feeding since UPF fed animals presented higher intake than STD animals.

Regarding the effects on adiposity, the major differences between sexes was found in the gonadal fat depot, with females having a greater increase due to UPF feeding than males. Although this fat depot is considered in the present study as part of the visceral fat, it behaves differently than the other abdominal fat depots (retroperitoneal and mesenteric) where UPF fed females did not differ from UPF fed males. A possible explanation is the different physiology of that this fat depot since it does not drain portally just as the other visceral fat do, and in fact it has been classified as peri-visceral by some authors37. Notice that gonadal WAT was the only adiposity parameter which appeared significant for the sex factor and without significance for the covariate factor in the ANCOVA analysis. It is also important to note that this fat depot is almost completely absent in humans and considered analogous to the human omental fat depot38,39. As for the biometric parameters, RWAT relative weight remained significant after adding the total food intake as a control variable in the analysis, suggesting that UPF intake might contribute to increased adiposity partially independent of hyperphagia.

In relation to serum metabolic parameters, UPF feeding induced an increase in leptin levels mainly in males, in accordance with increased adiposity. The lower effect on female leptin levels might be related to the need to maintain more stable leptin levels to avoid dysfunctions in estral regulation40. UPF feeding also increased glucose levels although there was no effect on insulin levels. These effects are different from those induced by CAF diets which are hyperglycaemic and hyperinsulinemic28,30,41–45, independently of sex. This might indicate that UPF feeding might induce less severe glucose metabolism alterations than CAF feeding. This differential effect might be a consequence of the diet composition itself, since CAF diets typically include a high-sugar drink, as in our previous studies44 and others30,41,42 with solutions upwards of 10% sugar. In our UPF diet, the orange soft drink for example contained the highest sugar solution of the liquid UPFs, with 6.3% sugar, considerably lower than most CAF diets.

We also found decreased total cholesterol levels in UPF fed animals. We also found a similar result in the CAF diet model28,46. Despite these results, the high- and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol fractions were not determined, therefore, we cannot accurately determine the effects of UPF feeding on cholesterolemia. A possible hypothesis explaining this reduced total cholesterol might be the pro-inflammatory potential of the UPF diet. Several foodstuffs included in the UPF diet, such as processed meats, chocolate and processed dairy products have been reported as having intrinsic toll-like receptor stimulants47,48 and this has been reported to result in decreased cholesterol circulating levels49. Additionally, individuals with Crohn’s disease, an inflammatory bowel disease, have also been reported to have decreased total cholesterol levels50, and UPF consumption in humans has been associated with increased white blood cell counts51. Even though in the present study we have not characterized the inflammatory status of these animals, several inflammatory markers should be determined in future experiments.

Regarding the behavioural results, the increase in locomotor activity and rearings is not usually found in CAF diet models, in which the reverse is usually detected46,52,53, with some exceptions54. This increased motor activity might indicate a specificity effect of UPF feeding. This effect is congruent with reports in human adolescents in which early life (3–4 years old) intakes of UPF were found to predict hyperactivity symptoms in early adolescence (12–13 years old)55. However, other variables such as the rat strain or the changes induced in the feeding patterns, such as snacking induced by a palatable diet56, among others cannot be discarded. Interestingly, distance travelled in the Open Field and rearings in the Hole Board were significantly increased in the UPF group when adding the total food intake in the analysis as a control variable. Also, the covariate showed no significance for the distance travelled in the Open Field, indicating that the consumption of UPF is mainly responsible for the increased activity. This result can be interpreted as UPF products inducing elevated levels of activity by themselves, further supporting the link between UPF intake and hyperactivity.

To test whether the presence of additives induced alterations in the levels of activity we performed correlations between the intake of each “menu”, taken as a proxy for additive intake, and the levels of activity. “Menus” 1, 2 and 4 showed a correlation between intake and activity. These 3 menus contain a considerably higher number of emulsifiers (11 different emulsifiers each), and total additives (42, 44, and 46 respectively), than “menu” 3, which contains 4 emulsifiers and a total of 37 additives. This result might contribute to the evidence linking high intakes of UPF products, and in particular emulsifiers, to the development and severity of ADHD57. The results of “menu” 2, in which this association is negative might be explained by the interplay between additives, which can be complex. For example, sodium benzoate (E211) can impair locomotor function in Wistar rats, but this effect can be reversed by ascorbic acid (E300)58. Another possible explanation of this contradictory result for the intakes of “menu” 2 is its composition, it contains the chicken broth which appears to be mostly responsible for the negative correlation between the “menu” intake and the activity levels of the animals. These results might point towards soft drinks being associated to increased activity more than other non-sweet UPF drinks, such as the chicken broth. This mimics results found in human adolescents in which a relationship between soft drinks intake and hyperactivity has been reported59.

Interestingly, the administration of the dietary emulsifiers carboxymethyl cellulose (E-466) and polysorbate 80 (E-433), both present in our UPF diet, to post-weaning mice for 7 weeks has been described to induce an increase in distance travelled in the elevated plus maze60. Therefore, it is possible that these effects are a consequence of the combined impact of all additives contained in the UPF diet, including emulsifiers15. Additives have been studied mostly in isolation in both animals models and in humans, and their effects have been shown to potentially include alterations in the gut microbiome, in mental, metabolic and cardiovascular health, and to be potential carcinogenic agents15,57. Future studies using the UPF diet should analyse whether these effects are reproduced.

Another interesting behavioural result was found in the novel object recognition task, in which UPF feeding appeared to have differential sex effects, marginally boosting memory in females and marginally impairing it in males. This result is partially consistent with other reports indicating that diet-induced obesity has a greater impact on memory function in males than in females61,62. In one study61 this sex difference was attributed to a sexual dimorphism in tryptophan metabolism in obesity. Specifically, through a dysregulation of the kynurenine pathway, resulting in increased quinolinic acid in the hippocampus, which has been previously shown to be neurotoxic and to have deleterious effect on learning63,64. Future studies using the UPF diet will investigate the kynurenine pathway to test whether UPF feeding might induce this differential sex effects through a similar mechanism.

Results from studies using the CAF diet concluded that it induces a memory impairment when administered during 5–20 weeks in the object recognition task in rats30,42,65,66, which is consistent with our results in males. However, there is a lack of evidence in the bibliography on the effects of this type of diets on object recognition in females.

We also investigated anxiety-like behaviour. Firstly, defecations were found reduced in the Hole Board and the Open Field, especially in the UPF females, which can be interpreted as decreased anxiety-like behaviour67. In addition, the elevated plus maze showed that UPF fed animals increased the time and entries in the open arms, also indicating a decrease in anxiety-like behaviour. This effect was also greater in females than in males. Rat models of diet-induced obesity using non-palatable diet are generally considered to induce an increase in anxiety-like behaviour68–71. However, when obesity is induced through a palatable diet such as the CAF diet this effect is more inconsistent. The available bibliography seems to point towards an anxiolytic effect of palatable feeding, although there are several reports of increased or unaffected anxiety-like behaviour13. This potential anxiolytic effect of CAF feeding is likely due to the palatability of the diet and its effects on the reward system72, and its effects on decreasing the hormonal response to stress73. This anxiolytic effect of CAF diet might also be present in the UPF diet, since it might also induce hyperphagia and be perceived as palatable by the animals, potentially more palatable due to its content in additives, mainly flavours, flavour enhancers, artificial sweeteners and texturisers. Future studies using this diet should investigate how UPF feeding affects the dopaminergic system to determine whether it induces hedonic eating. The greater effect on females than in males might be explained by females being more susceptible to the rewarding effect of palatable foods than males74.

To conclude, we have developed the first experimental diet based on ultra-processed foods and drinks and showed that this diet is obesogenic mainly due to the hyperphagia evoked by its palatability. This diet can be used as a preclinical model to aid human research in determining cause and effect relationship between UPF consumption and its outcomes. This diet has distinct effects compared to similar obesogenic diets like the CAF diet at the behavioural level in young-adult rats (increased locomotor activity), and similar effects at the metabolic level. Future studies will test this diet in different developmental stages, including in utero, in male and female rats, to determine its effects on neurotransmission systems, gut-brain axis and brain connectivity amongst others. From a translational point of view, UPF consumption in the general population has increased in recent decades5, and the implementation of our novel experimental UPF diet help in deciphering the mechanisms through which UPF exerts its detrimental effects on health.
