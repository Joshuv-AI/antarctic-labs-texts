How Well Do We Know the Scalar-Induced Gravitational Waves?
A. J. Iovino, S. Matarrese, G. Perna, A. Ricciardone, A. Riotto

Abstract
Gravitational waves sourced by amplified scalar perturbations are a common prediction across a wide range of cosmological models. These scalar curvature fluctuations are inherently nonlinear and typically non-Gaussian. We argue that the effects of non-Gaussianity may not always be adequately captured by an expansion around a Gaussian field, expressed through nonlinear parameters such as f NL f_{\text{\tiny NL}} . As a consequence, the resulting amplitude of the stochastic gravitational wave background may differ significantly from predictions based on the standard quadratic source model routinely used in the literature.

Our conclusions hinge on two arguments. The first aspect concerns the form of the fully nonlinear source on superhorizon scales, which establish the initial condition for the generation of SIGWs as curvature perturbations re-enter the horizon. The second focuses on the estimation of the impact of nonlinearities when the wavelength of the curvature perturbation becomes smaller than the Hubble radius. We address these two points sequentially.

A hint from the fully nonlinear superhorizon source –

To gauge the impact of nonlinearities in curvature perturbations on the SIGWs produced during the radiation-dominated era, we start from the long-wavelength limit of Einstein’s equations while retaining full nonlinearity in the perturbations. In the comoving uniform-energy-density gauge, the tensor modes
\gamma_{ij}
are expressed as
[
70
]

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}(t,{\bf x})=-\frac{1}{3}P_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{TT}}S_{kl}({\bf x})\left(\frac{1}{aH}\right)^{2},

(2)

where
a
is the scale-factor,
H
the Hubble parameter, and the transverse and traceless projector reads

\displaystyle P_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{TT}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(P_{ik}P_{jl}+P_{jk}P_{il}-P_{ij}P_{kl}\right),

\displaystyle P_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}-\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}{\partial^{2}}.

(3)

The source term
S_{ij}
is given by
[
70
]

\displaystyle S_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\psi^{4}}\left[-\frac{2}{\psi}\left(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\nabla^{2}\psi\right)\right.

(4)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\frac{6}{\psi^{2}}\left(\partial_{i}\psi\partial_{j}\psi-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\partial^{k}\psi\partial_{k}\psi\right)\right].

The gravitational potential
\psi
relates to the nonlinear curvature perturbation
\zeta
through
[
71
,
70
]

\psi({\bf x})=e^{\zeta({\bf x})/2}.

(5)

By introducing a variable transformation,

Y({\bf x})=\frac{1}{\psi^{2}({\bf x})},

(6)

the source term can be elegantly rewritten as

S_{ij}=-\partial_{i}Y\partial_{j}Y+\partial_{i}(Y\partial_{j}Y)-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}Y\nabla^{2}Y.

(7)

Notably, only the first term survives under the action of the projector

P_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{TT}}
and we will disregard the others from now on.

We assume that the constant (in time) nonlinear curvature perturbation
\zeta
on superhorizon scales is connected to its Gaussian counterpart
\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}
via the relation (where the absolute value has a nontrivial origin, as discussed in the Supplementary Material)

\zeta=-\mu\ln\left|1-\frac{\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}}{\mu}\right|\,.

(8)

This relation arises naturally in standard models such as the ultra-slow-roll inflationary scenario
[
46
]
and the curvaton mechanism
[
44
,
49
]
. In ultra-slow-roll scenarios,
\mu
is typically related to the slope of the inflationary power spectrum of
\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}
beyond its peak, with values ranging between
1.5
and
6.6

[
72
,
21
,
73
]
. For curvaton models,
\mu
is intricately determined by the time of the curvaton’s decay into radiation and the shape of the power spectrum
[
74

76
,
32
,
34

, with typical values of
\mathcal{O}(0.1)

Expanding Eq. (
8
) to second order and comparing it with the common series expansion (
1
), we find that
f_{\text{\tiny NL}}=5/(6\mu)
. To be model independent, we will treat
\mu
as a free parameter from now on.

In terms of
\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}
, the relevant term in the source (
7
) which is not projected away is

S_{ij}=-\left|1-\frac{\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}}{\mu}\right|^{2(\mu-1)}\partial_{i}\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}\partial_{j}\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}\,.

(9)

For large
\mu
(small non-Gaussianity), we recover the standard quadratic source

\lim_{\mu\to\infty}\left|1-\frac{\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}}{\mu}\right|^{2(\mu-1)}=e^{-2\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}}\simeq 1\,,

(10)

where in the last passage we have taken
\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}\ll 1
.
Conversely, for small
\mu
(corresponding to large non-Gaussianity), the source becomes

S_{ij}=-\left|1-\frac{\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}}{\mu}\right|^{-2}\partial_{i}\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}\partial_{j}\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}\,.

(11)

This prefactor highlights the possibility of significant deviations from the standard quadratic source while remaining within the perturbative regime: significant enhancements occur if
\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}\sim\mu\ll 1
, whereas large suppressions for
1\gg\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}\gg\mu
.

Notably, the case with
\mu=1
precisely reproduces the superhorizon quadratic source made of Gaussian fields, even though it corresponds to
f_{\text{\tiny NL}}=5/6
, which represents a relatively sizable non-Gaussian perturbation.

These findings show that the SIGW source, just before the perturbations re-enter the horizon, may deviate significantly from predictions based on Gaussian fields. Moreover, the non-Gaussian effects may not be captured by an expansion in
f_{\text{\tiny NL}}
or higher-order nonlinear parameters.

The impact of nonlinearities on the SIGW: an estimate –

A comprehensive and analytical calculation of the impact of curvature perturbation nonlinearities on SIGWs, including the subhorizon evolution, is an Herculean task. Admittedly, we have not been able to achieve it. The difficulty of the problem lies in several aspects: the impossibility of calculating the Fourier transform of the nonlinear curvature perturbation (
8
), the ignorance about the nonlinear radiation transfer function to apply when the perturbation are subject to the radiation pressure on sub-horizon scales, to mention a few.

We should not concede defeat too soon, though. If we are satisfied with an estimate, we can adopt the line of reasoning based on the following assumptions. First, we suppose that the typical values of the nonlinear perturbation
\zeta
are also in the perturbative regime once the typical values of the Gaussian field
\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}
are in the linear regime,
|\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}|\ll 1
. For instance, as shown in Fig.
1
, for
\mu=10^{-1}
(equivalent to
f_{\text{\tiny NL}}\simeq 8
) only in a very narrow range of values for
(0.99\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt$\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$<$}}\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}/\mu\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt$\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$<$}}1.01)
, the linearity, i.e.
|\zeta|\ll 1
, is violated.

If so, the curvature perturbation which re-enters the horizon may be considered a linear perturbation, its amplitude may be after all still smaller than unity, even though it is generated by a nonlinear dynamics. The situation here is analogous to what happens to the SIGWs themselves: even though they are generated by a nonlinear source, when they propagate to a detector their amplitude is tiny once they decouple from the source. They can be therefore considered linear
observables and fully gauge-invariant
[
77
,
78
,
79
]
.

With this logic in mind, we can estimate the SIGW background by adopting the standard equation for the GWs with a quadratic source in the Newtonian gauge (the superscript
{}^{\text{\tiny Q}}
indicating the source is quadratic, i.e. constructed from the square of linear, but not necessarily Gaussian quantities)

\ddot{\gamma}_{ij}+3H\dot{\gamma}_{ij}-\nabla^{2}\gamma_{ij}=P_{ijkl}^{\mathrm{TT}}S^{\text{\tiny Q}}_{kl},

(12)

where dots indicate the derivative with respect to the cosmic time and in the radiation phase the source is given by

3

S_{ij}^{\text{\tiny Q}}=4\Phi\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Phi+2\partial_{i}\Phi\partial_{j}\Phi-\partial_{i}\left(\frac{\dot{\Phi}}{H}+\Phi\right)\partial_{j}\left(\frac{\dot{\Phi}}{H}+\Phi\right)

(13)

in terms of the Bardeen potential
\Phi

Given our assumption of the linearity of the curvature perturbation
\zeta
, the scalar perturbation
\Phi(t,{\bf k})
in the Newtonian gauge can be written in terms of the initial gauge-invariant comoving curvature perturbation as

\Phi(t,{\bf k})\equiv\frac{2}{3}T(k,t)\zeta({\bf k}),

(14)

where
T(k,t)
is the linear transfer function during the radiation domination phase and
\zeta({\bf k})
is the Fourier transform of the superhorizon curvature perturbation (
8
) which plays the role of the initial condition.
The technical difficulty we have mentioned earlier arises here, since we are not able to calculate the Fourier transform of the curvature perturbation (
8

\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}({\bf k})

\partial_{i}\zeta=\frac{1}{\left|1-\frac{\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}}{\mu}\right|}\partial_{i}\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}},

(15)

we approximate the prefactor in front of the gradient term with its
typical value

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}}{\mu}\right|^{-1}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{\pm\langle\zeta^{2}_{\text{\tiny g}}\rangle^{1/2}}{\mu}\right|^{-1}\equiv\mathcal{A},

(16)

where

\langle\zeta^{2}_{\text{\tiny g}}\rangle=\int\frac{{\rm d}k}{k}\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}}(k)

(17)

and
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}}(k)
is the power spectrum of the Gaussian perturbation. In Fig.

Conclusions –

The study of SIGWs offers a compelling avenue to probe the nonlinear dynamics of the early universe, providing a potential bridge between enhanced scalar perturbations and phenomena such as primordial black hole formation. By incorporating nonlinear effects into the SIGW source, we uncover potentially significant deviations from predictions rooted in Gaussian curvature perturbations. The behavior of the SIGW background depends intricately on the degree of non-Gaussianity and underscores the potential for both suppression and enhancement relative to standard quadratic models.

Our analysis, though approximate, suggests that even modest nonlinearities can leave observable imprints on the SIGW spectrum, challenging traditional approaches that rely on Gaussian perturbation theory. These findings emphasize the need for more refined models and numerical methods to accurately characterize these effects and their implications for future gravitational wave observatories. As next-generation detectors like LISA
[
82
]
and Einstein Telescope
[
83
]
promise to improve sensitivity to SIGWs, further exploration of their nonlinear origins will be critical to unlocking new insights into the physics of the early universe. Our findings might have already a significant impact on the interpretation of the signal seen by pulsar timing arrays as originated from a SIGW background and accompanied by a sizable primordial black hole abundance
[

There is of course room to improve the approach proposed in this Letter. One potential avenue might be to numerically investigate the nonlinear interactions during the ultra-slow roll phase through a lattice simulations. This would allow for the calculation of the source for SIGWs in a fully nonperturbative manner, following techniques similar to those outlined in

How to deal with large curvature fluctuations

The fully non-perturbative expression for the curvature perturbation is on super-horizon scales
[
71
]

\zeta({\bf x},t)=-\Psi({\bf x},t)-\frac{1}{3}\int_{\overline{\rho}}^{\rho({{\bf x},t})}\frac{{\rm d}\rho}{\rho+P},

(S1)

which in the flat gauge
\Psi=0
reduces to

\zeta({{\bf x},t})=-\frac{1}{3}\int_{\rho(t)}^{\rho({{\bf x},t})}\frac{{\rm d}\rho}{\rho+P}.

(S2)

In the above expressions
\rho
and
P
are the energy density and pressure density, respectively, of a fluid and
\Psi
is the gravitational potential related to the
\psi
in Eq. (
4
) by
\psi=e^{\Psi/2}
, such that in the the comoving
uniform-energy-density gauge,
\zeta=-\Psi
.

For a canonically normalized scalar field
\rho+P=\dot{\phi}^{2}
. In slow-roll one correctly obtains, at the linear level, the standard result

\zeta=-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\delta\rho({\bf x},t)}{\dot{\phi}^{2}}=-\frac{1}{3}\frac{V^{\prime}\delta\phi({\bf x},t)}{\dot{\phi}^{2}}=-\frac{1}{3}\frac{-3H\dot{\phi}\,\delta\phi({\bf x},t)}{\dot{\phi}^{2}}=H\frac{\delta\phi({\bf x},t)}{\dot{\phi}}.

(S3)

During a period of exact ultra-slow roll for which the inflaton potential is totally flat, one has
\rho+P=\dot{\phi}^{2}
. Furthermore
\rho\simeq\dot{\phi}^{2}/2
and therefore (being
t_{\star}
denotes the final time the ultra-slow roll stage)
[
45
,
95
]

\zeta({{\bf x},t_{\star}})=-\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\int_{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}(t_{\star})}^{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}({{\bf x},t_{\star}})}\frac{{\rm d}\dot{\phi}^{2}}{\dot{\phi}^{2}}=-\frac{1}{6}\ln\frac{\dot{\phi}^{2}({{\bf x},t_{\star}})}{\dot{\phi}^{2}(t_{\star})},

(S4)

which is the correct expression.
In the literature, however, one finds the expression

\zeta({{\bf x},t_{\star}})=-\frac{1}{3}\ln\frac{\dot{\phi}({{\bf x},t_{\star}})}{\dot{\phi}(t_{\star})},

(S5)

which is misleading because, when
\dot{\phi}({{\bf x},t})<0
, a problem seems to arise when the argument of the logarithm is negative.
It is also customary to expand the last expression at first order, with
\dot{\phi}({\bf x},t_{\star})=\delta\dot{\phi}(t_{\star})+\delta\dot{\phi}_{\text{\tiny g}}({\bf x},t_{\star})
,

\zeta({{\bf x},t_{\star}})=-\frac{1}{3}\ln\left(1+\frac{\delta\dot{\phi}_{\text{\tiny g}}({\bf x},t_{\star})}{\dot{\phi}(t_{\star})}\right),

(S6)

which again indicates a false problem for
\delta\dot{\phi}({\bf x},t_{\star})/\dot{\phi}(t_{\star})<-1
. One should use instead the expression with an absolute value originated from the correct expression (
S4
)

\zeta({{\bf x},t_{\star}})=-\frac{1}{3}\ln\left|1+\frac{\delta\dot{\phi}_{\text{\tiny g}}({\bf x},t_{\star})}{\dot{\phi}(t_{\star})}\right|.

(S7)

In ultra-slow roll models one has
\dot{\phi}({\bf x},t_{\star})=3H[\phi({\bf x},t)-\phi({\bf x},t_{\star})]+\dot{\phi}({\bf x},t)

[
45
]
, and therefore

\zeta({{\bf x},t_{\star}})=-\frac{1}{3}\ln\left|1-3H\frac{\delta\phi_{\text{\tiny g}}({\bf x},t_{\star})}{\dot{\phi}(t_{\star})}\right|=-\frac{1}{3}\ln\left|1-3\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}({\bf x},t_{\star})\right|,

(S8)

leading to
\mu=1/3
and the standard result for sharp transitions
f_{\text{\tiny NL}}=5/2

[
45
]
. According to the exact expression (
S4
) there is no obstruction in having values of
\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}
larger than
1/3
and therefore no probability leakage as claimed in Ref.
[
96
]
. Equivalently, one can safely consider
values of
\zeta_{\text{\tiny g}}
larger than
\mu
in Eq. (
8
).
