Pygmy sperm whale multi-omics data reveal hypoxia adaptations in deep-diving cetaceans
Authors: Weijian Guo, Yiting Chen, Huizhong Fan, Xin Huang, Xi Chen, Yousheng Xiao, Chaoming Zhang, Wenliang Zhou, Fuwen Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep-diving cetaceans tolerate acute hypoxia better than their terrestrial ancestors and shallow-diving counterparts. However, our poor understanding of how genetic factors, cellular functions, and physiological characteristics combine to drive hypoxia adaptation in deep-diving cetaceans remains a critical gap.ResultsHere, we studied the genetic basis for this ability by creating a de novo genome assembly for the pygmy sperm whale (Kogia breviceps) and comparatively analyzing genomes from 12 cetacean species, including 2 other deep-diving cetaceans. We also sequenced and compared single-nucleus RNA data from the muscle and heart of the pygmy sperm whale and its terrestrial relative Bos taurus. We found that genetic and cellular changes in the HIF-1 pathway, electron transport chain, glucose and fatty acid catabolism, and heart rate may contribute to hypoxia tolerance in deep-diving cetaceans. Key adaptations include rapid evolution of glycolysis-related genes (PYGM and ENO3), differential expression of HIF-1 pathway genes like ARNT, and accelerated conserved noncoding elements in genes such as ATP5F1E (ATP synthase) and DMD (dystrophin). We found an increase in myocytes and type II cardiomyocytes in the pygmy sperm whale’s muscle and heart tissues, which may support energy metabolism and homeostasis during deep dives.ConclusionsThese findings suggest deep-diving cetaceans have unique genetic and cellular adaptations to cope with hypoxia, offering insights into how mammals handle low oxygen levels at the cellular level.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02495-2.Keywords: Hypoxia tolerance, Deep-diving cetaceans, Chromosomal genome, Single-nucleus sequencing, HIF-1 pathway, Electron transport chain

Background

Around 55 million years ago, cetaceans evolved to aquatic life from terrestrial ancestors, inducing many secondary aquatic adaptations [1, 2]. Notably, some of these species adapted to low oxygen conditions, evolving the ability to hold their breath for long periods, enabling them to perform deep and prolonged breath-hold dives. Among these, the sperm whale (Physeter macrocephalus), the Blainville’s beaked whale (Mesoplodon densirostris), and the pygmy sperm whale (Kogia breviceps) stand out [3]. Sperm whales have been recorded at depths up to 3000 m and diving for as long as 138 min [4]. Blainville’s beaked whales have been recorded at depths up to 1408 m for 68 min [5], while the pygmy sperm whale has been observed diving to 1400 m with a dive duration of 30 min [6]. These deep-diving cetaceans rank among the most proficient divers within cetaceans, with dive capabilities paralleling pinnipeds like the southern elephant seal (Mirounga leonina) [7] while far exceeding most other cetaceans in their diving abilities. To sustain deep dives, cetaceans have adapted to high water pressures and low blood oxygen concentrations. Muscle and heart tissue have become adapted to prolonged hypoxia while sustaining energy levels and preventing damage to these tissues [8]. Here, we explore the underlying physiological and genetic mechanisms that permit tolerance of prolonged hypoxia.

Physiological adaptations to hypoxia in cetaceans are fundamentally enabled by molecular genetic mechanisms [9, 10]. For example, comparative genomic analyses of 11 hypoxia-tolerant marine mammals (excluding the pygmy sperm whale) have revealed strong positive selection for genes related to oxygen transport in the blood and muscles, as well as the regulation of vasoconstriction [11]. Also, comparative transcriptomic analyses have revealed enhanced expression of genes associated with oxidative phosphorylation and the respiratory electron transport chain in the brains of four whale species (Orcinus orca, Globicephala melas, Balaenoptera acutorostrata, and Balaena mysticetus), compared to closely related cattle species [12]. These molecular genetic mechanisms are believed to underlie the diversity in physiological responses to hypoxia observed in marine mammals.

In addition, physiological characteristics such as specific heart structure, blood circulation, and heart rhythm regulation are key adaptations in deep-diving cetaceans. The thickening of the outer wall of the heart enables the whale heart to maintain a robust pumping function, ensuring sufficient transport of oxygenated blood to oxygen-sensitive tissues, maintaining the normal functioning of vital organs during deep dives [13, 14]. Circulatory mechanisms like peripheral arterial constriction and decelerated heart rate play a crucial role in reducing oxygen delivery to tissues. These adaptations decrease the overall consumption of stored oxygen in the blood and the oxygen consumption of the heart [15]. Notably, elevated myoglobin concentration in the muscles of some deep-diving cetaceans greatly enhances their muscle oxygen storage capacity [16]. These essential physiological characteristics of deep-diving cetaceans are influenced by genetic factors [3, 17].

However, we have an inadequate understanding of how genetic factors, cellular functions, and physiological characteristics combine to maintain adequate homeostasis in deep-diving cetaceans. To address this gap in knowledge, we employ single-nucleus RNA (snRNA) sequencing to investigate the underlying cellular mechanisms. snRNA sequencing has emerged as a powerful tool to causally define relationships between genotype and phenotype at single-cell levels [18]. The method enables identification of distinct cell populations and genome-wide gene expression at single-cell resolution across different tissues [19, 20]. Here, we apply snRNA sequencing to comprehensively identify the cellular and genetic factors underlying adaptation to hypoxia in marine mammals.

As a member of the toothed whale superfamily Odontoceti, pygmy sperm whales are well-known for their exceptional dive depths and durations [3]. Here, we generated a chromosome-level genome and used it for comparative genomic analysis with the 12 cetacean genomes (Fig. 1a and Additional file 1: Table S1). We also profiled and compared single-nucleus muscle and heart transcriptomes of the pygmy sperm whale with those of cattle (Bos taurus), a close terrestrial relative. Through comparative genomic and cell landscape analyses, we identify genetic mechanisms and/or differences in cell composition in deep-diving cetaceans. We shed light on the genetic and cellular adaptations or mechanisms that facilitate coping with hypoxia in deep-diving cetaceans. Our comparative molecular approach enhances our understanding of the relationship between molecular genetic variation and cellular and physiological characteristics associated with hypoxia in cetaceans.

Results

Summary of genome assembly and annotation

We generated 99.1 Gb (~ 38.1 ×) PacBio high-fidelity long reads with an average length of 15.2 Kb and an N50 value of 15.7 Kb (Additional file 1: Table S2). The genome of the pygmy sperm whale, spanning 2.62 Gb, was de novo assembled, with the N50 length reaching 48.4 Mb (Additional file 1: Table S3). The completeness of the genomic assembly was supported by 90.3% complete Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) and a high mapping ratio of 99.97% between the HiFi reads and the constructed genome. Furthermore, approximately 388.5 Gb of Hi-C reads (~ 149.4 ×) were employed to anchor the contigs onto 21 chromosomes, achieving a successful anchoring rate of about 91.48% (Additional file 1: Table S3 and Additional file 2: Fig. S1). A syntenic analysis comparing the pygmy sperm whale genome with the genomes of the blue whale (GCF_009873245.2) and vaquita (GCF_008692025.1) revealed a chromosomal fusion event, wherein chromosomes 7 and 16 merged to form chromosome 1 in the pygmy sperm whale genome (Fig. 1b). In summary, these findings validate the high quality of our pygmy sperm whale genome and indicate that apart from the fusion event, the remaining chromosomes exhibit a high degree of conservation among cetaceans. Compared with the previously published chromosome-level assembly (GCF_026419965.1), our genome exhibits higher sequencing depth (38.1 × vs. 23 ×) and improved contiguity (contig N50 of 48.4 Mb vs. 42.8 Mb), making it a complementary and enhanced genomic resource for future cetacean research.

The pygmy sperm whale genome contained 47.36% repetitive sequences (Additional file 1: Table S4). Most of these repetitions (71.04% of the total) consisted of long interspersed nuclear elements (LINE; Additional file 1: Table S4). Gene annotation predicted 20,245 protein-coding genes using a combined approach of de novo gene prediction, homology-based prediction, and transcripts-based prediction (Additional file 1: Table S5), a number comparable to that of other cetaceans [1]. Notably, this gene count exceeds that of the previously published National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) assembly (GCF_026419965.1), which annotated 18,349 protein-coding genes. Evaluation of gene annotation with BUSCO (mammalian_odb10 gene set) revealed that 8669 of 9226 conserved mammalian genes (93.96%) were fully annotated (Additional file 1: Table S6). Among these predicted genes, 19,601 (96.82%) underwent successful functional annotation (Additional file 1: Table S7). These key metrics for annotated genes in the pygmy sperm whale align with those of related species [1], confirming the reliability of the annotation results.

The potential genetic and cellular mechanisms of hypoxia adaptation in deep-diving cetaceans

To decode the unique genetic mechanisms enabling deep‐diving cetaceans to endure hypoxia, we conducted a comparative genomic study involving three deep divers: the pygmy sperm whale, sperm whale, and Blainville’s beaked whale, along with 10 other cetacean species and 2 terrestrial mammals. By applying rigorous selection criteria, we pinpointed 58 positively selected genes (PSG), 655 rapidly evolving genes (REG), and 684 accelerated conserved noncoding element-related genes (ACNEG) (Fig. 2a and Additional file 1: Tables S8, S9, S10) among the three deep‐diving cetaceans. Within the 684 ACNEGs, we discovered 1011 accelerated conserved noncoding elements (CNE), with 945 located in intronic regions and 66 situated in upstream regions (Fig. 2b).

We then analyzed the enrichment of PSGs, REGs, and ACNEGs. For PSGs, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment showed they are mainly linked to type II interferon-related pathways (Additional file 1: Table S11), with no significant Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) terms. For REGs, GO and KEGG enrichment indicated key roles in sperm development, including processes like “spermatogenesis,” “male gamete generation,” and “motor proteins” (Additional file 1: Table S11). For ACNEGs, most genes are involved in neuronal development and synaptic signaling pathways (Additional file 1: Table S11). Specifically, genes related to “axon development,” “synaptic signaling,” and “synapse assembly” were highly enriched.

To assess postmortem effects on gene expression, we analyzed a set of postmortem-associated genes [21]. We identified 11 and 7 shared genes in the muscle and heart tissues, respectively, between pygmy sperm whales and cattle (Additional file 1: Table S12). After accounting for cell-type proportions, no genes, except for CREB5, were significantly differentially expressed. The lack of differential expression in these postmortem sensitivity markers suggests comparable postmortem intervals and minimal differential RNA degradation between the pygmy sperm whale and cattle samples. After normalization and filtering of the snRNA-seq data, a total of 14,390 genes across 5063 cells were analyzed in cattle muscle tissue, while 15,062 genes across 10,987 cells were analyzed in pygmy sperm whale muscle tissue. Similarly, 15,216 genes across 9359 cells were analyzed in cattle heart tissue, and 15,249 genes across 11,562 cells were analyzed in pygmy sperm whale heart tissue, respectively. Making use of gene expression patterns and established markers [22, 23], we identified eight cell types in muscle tissue (Fig. 2c and e, and Additional file 2: Fig. S2) and nine in heart tissue (Fig. 2d and f, and Additional file 2: Fig. S3). A comparison between pygmy sperm whales and cattle revealed an increase in the proportions of three specific cell types in both muscle and heart tissues (Fig. 2c and d). In pygmy sperm whale’s muscle tissue, the proportion of pericytes, myoblasts, and myocytes was higher at 57.36%, 30.04%, and 4.06%, respectively, compared to cattle where these proportions were 45.72%, 22.50%, and 1.90%. Furthermore, in heart tissue, the proportion of fibroblasts, type I endothelial cells, and type II cardiomyocytes was 30.25%, 32.95%, and 8.68% in pygmy sperm whales, contrasted with 20.62%, 10.04%, and 3.42% in cattle (Additional file 2: Fig. S4). We also investigated cellular interactions between different clusters in the muscle and heart tissue. We found the number of enriched ligand-receptor pairs differed between cattle and pygmy sperm whale. Specifically, in muscle tissue, pericytes in the pygmy sperm whale exhibited a higher number of interactions with other cell types compared to cattle pericytes (Additional file 2: Fig. S5). In muscle tissue, pericytes, myoblasts, and myocytes exhibited 1329, 1097, and 948 genes showing differential expression, respectively (Additional file 1: Tables S13, S14, S15), while in the heart tissue, fibroblasts, type I endothelial cells, and type II cardiomyocytes displayed 790, 597, and 843 differentially expressed genes (DEG), respectively (Additional file 1: Tables S16, S17, S18). These genes are involved in several processes, including transcriptional regulation, energy metabolism, and muscle plasticity, some of which are highlighted below.

Modulation of electron transport chain (ETC) activity

In most animals, the ETC serves as the primary consumer of oxygen, crucial for generating adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation [24]. Genetic mechanisms involving two REGs (ATP6V0D1 and ATP6V1H) and one ACNEG (ATP5F1E) were identified within the ATP synthase (Additional file 1: Table S8, and S10), potentially influencing ATP synthase activity in the mitochondrial inner membrane [25]. Interestingly, the location of the accelerated CNE coincided with the enhancer region in the genomic locus of the ATP5F1E gene (Fig. 3a), which may potentially impact ATP production. Furthermore, COX10, an assembly factor of complex IV of the ETC, plays a vital role in oxidative phosphorylation [26]. This gene presented two accelerated CNEs in its intronic regions (Additional file 1: Table S10), one of which carried a deep-diving cetacean-specific mutation (Additional file 2: Fig. S6), potentially altering its function or efficiency in coping with hypoxia. Consequently, these genomic changes associated with the ETC could potentially optimize ATP production efficiency during hypoxia.

For the cellular mechanisms, we have identified the whale-enriched cells (WEC), which indicate a higher proportion of a specific cell type present in pygmy sperm whale compared to cattle. Within muscle and heart tissues of pygmy sperm whale, we observed a high upregulation of their ETC genes. However, the genes that were upregulated varied between these two tissues (Fig. 3b). Upon mapping these upregulated genes to GO terms and the KEGG pathways database, a significant enrichment was observed in “oxidative phosphorylation” (GO:0006119, hsa00190) and “respiratory electron transport chain” (GO:0022904) (Additional file 1: Table S19). Furthermore, the upregulated genes were primarily concentrated in myocytes and type II cardiomyocytes, such as NDUFA6 and ATP5F1B in myocytes (Additional file 2: Fig. S7) and NDUFA11 and ATP5F1B in type II cardiomyocytes (Additional file 2: Fig. S8). This implies that by increasing the expression of ETC genes, the pygmy sperm whale may facilitate the process of oxidative phosphorylation in their cells (myocytes and type II cardiomyocytes), potentially enhancing the efficiency of oxygen utilization during hypoxia.

Alterations in glucose metabolism

Beyond the regulation of ETC, cellular metabolic reprogramming emerges as a prominent characteristic under hypoxic conditions. One significant mechanism observed in hypoxic cells is the increased glucose uptake and glycolytic flux, which drives glucose catabolism [27]. The rapid evolution of genes encoding oxygen-sensitive proteins may indicate potential adaptations for hypoxia tolerance relevant to diving. This includes genes involved in metabolic reprogramming (Additional file 1: Table S8). For instance, the gene SLC2A4, which is responsible for most glucose uptake in mammalian cells, displayed an amino acid change specific to deep-diving cetaceans in the functional domain (Fig. 3c and Additional file 1: Table S20). This change is likely important for maintaining blood glucose levels and systemic glucose homeostasis [28]. Additionally, another gene ENO3, encoding a glycolytic enzyme [29], exhibited rapid evolution, which could potentially further enhance cellular glycolysis (Additional file 1: Table S8). In addition, we identified three distinct deep-diving cetacean mutations in the functional domain of the rapidly evolving gene PYGM (Fig. 3d and Additional file 1: Tables S8 and S20). This gene encodes myophosphorylase, which catalyzes the initial step of glycogen catabolism. These mutations potentially enhance the glucose metabolism capabilities of deep-diving cetaceans.

In WECs, the signaling pathways mainly related to metabolism were also primarily enriched in myocytes and type II cardiomyocytes, accounting for approximately 78% and 74%, respectively (Fig. 4a). In addition, there was an upregulation of many genes closely linked to glucose metabolism, specifically the pyruvate dehydrogenase kinases isoenzymes (PDK1-4) (Fig. 4b). These PDK isoenzymes serve as key regulators in driving the metabolic shift, by inhibiting the entry of pyruvate into the tricarboxylic acid cycle, thereby reducing aerobic respiration [30]. The upregulation of PDK1-4 may be a critical mechanism by which pygmy sperm whales adapt to acute hypoxia. Additionally, crucial glycolytic genes were found to be upregulated in pygmy sperm whales (Additional file 1: Tables S13, S15, S16, and S18). Notably, hexokinase 2 (HK2) contributes to increased glycolytic rate, while lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) enables the continuous replenishment of NAD+ to support glycolysis [31, 32]. The upregulation of these key genes suggests a potential role of these genes in glucose metabolism during long dives.

Changes in lipid metabolism

Fatty acids are crucial for cell survival and proliferation. In a hypoxic environment, fatty acid metabolic pathways take advantage of alternative precursor sources due to the inhibition of the tricarboxylic acid cycle [33]. The low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) are two genes that play significant roles in lipid metabolism in deep-diving cetaceans. Notably, the rapidly evolving gene LDLR harbored a unique amino acid substitution specific to deep-diving cetaceans (Additional file 1: Tables S8 and S20). This substitution may enhance the influx of extracellular fatty acids and promote lipogenesis [34]. Furthermore, we observed rapid evolution in CPT1A among deep-diving cetaceans (Additional file 1: Table S8), which may improve the transport of fatty acids into mitochondria, thereby increasing the rate of fatty acids β-oxidation [35]. These findings suggested that deep-diving cetaceans have genetically adapted to maintain fatty acid oxidation under hypoxic conditions.

In myocytes and type II cardiomyocytes of the pygmy sperm whale, the upregulation of fatty acid-binding protein 3 (FABP3) (Fig. 4c and Additional file 1: Tables S15 and S18) facilitated the influx of extracellular fatty acids and leads to accumulation of lipid droplets under hypoxic conditions [34]. Typically, fatty acid catabolism via β-oxidation is suppressed in such environments. Nevertheless, our study revealed that three acyl-CoA dehydrogenase genes (ACADVL, ACADL, and ACADM) were highly expressed in these cell types (Fig. 4c and Additional file 1: Tables S15 and S18). These genes are located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where they catalyze the initial step of the mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation [36]. In summary, FABP3 was upregulated in these cell types, while the expression of key acyl-CoA dehydrogenase genes was also upregulated, suggesting their potential roles in lipid metabolism under hypoxic conditions.

Regulation of heart rate

Marine mammals are renowned for exhibiting dramatic changes in heart rate associated with diving behavior [37]. When diving, a slower heart rate during dives decreases overall oxygen delivery to tissues, thereby reducing the depletion rate of blood oxygen stores and lowering the oxygen consumed by the heart itself. This alteration in heart rate is primarily controlled by the autonomic nervous system [15]. Interestingly, eight genes (CACNB1, CACNB2, CTNNA2, DMD, LAMA2, CACNG4, CTNNA3, CACNG6) were classified under the KEGG pathway known as “Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy” (Additional file 1: Table S11). In particular, intronic mutations in the DMD gene can lead to Duchenne muscular dystrophy, which is characterized by arrhythmias [38]. In the case of deep-diving cetaceans, their DMD gene harbored four accelerated CNEs within its intronic regions (Additional file 2: Fig. S9 and Additional file 1: Table S10). One of these CNEs displays a deep-diving cetacean-specific mutation (Additional file 2: Fig. S9). While this mutation is located within the intronic region of the DMD gene, it is important to highlight that this CNE mutation may affect the function of the DMD gene, potentially influencing heart rate regulation [39, 40]. The existence of these unique genetic variations suggested adaptations in deep-diving cetaceans that regulate cardiac function during surface and dive periods, including adjustments to cardiovascular dynamics.

Pathway analysis within the WECs revealed significant enrichment in heart contraction pathways (such as “cardiac muscle contraction,” “heart contraction,” and “regulation of heart contraction”), particularly among the downregulated genes in the heart tissue of pygmy sperm whale (Additional file 2: Fig. S10 and Additional file 1: Table S19). Specifically, we observed a notable decrease in the expression of genes related to cardiac muscle contractions, including those coding for contractile protein (ACTC1, MYH7, MYL3, TNNC1, and TNNI3) and calcium-binding protein (RYR2 and SLC8A1). These genes were highly enriched in the “heart contraction” GO term, indicating a downregulation in the multicellular organismal processes governing cardiac muscle contractions. Consequently, these may manifest as a reduced heart rate in pygmy sperm whales.

Activation of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF)

The vital need for oxygen makes it unsurprising that responses to hypoxia are highly conserved across species. In mammal systems, HIFs serve as central mediators that translate variations in cellular oxygen levels into modifications in gene expression patterns under hypoxic conditions [41]. Our analyses highlighted the upregulation of the oxygen-sensitive α-subunits of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1α, HIF1A) in type I endothelial cells (Additional file 1: Table S17). Likewise, the β-subunits of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1β, ARNT) showed increased expression in pericytes (Additional file 1: Table S13). Additionally, the gene encoding CREB-binding protein (CREBBP), essential for modulating the transcriptional activity of HIFs through its interaction as a transcriptional cofactor and impacting HIF protein stability [42], exhibited upregulation in pericytes (Additional file 1: Table S13), myoblasts (Additional file 1: Table S14), and fibroblasts (Additional file 1: Table S16). Our observations also indicated significant upregulation of genes downstream in the “HIF-1 signaling pathway” in myoblasts, and myocytes, as well as type II cardiomyocytes (Additional file 1: Table S19). The activation of these hypoxia-induced genes enables cells to respond to low oxygen conditions by modulating vascular tone, promoting angiogenesis, and reprogramming metabolism (Fig. 5). Moreover, the regulation of HIF-1α induction, involving multiple pathways, demonstrates a complex network response to low oxygen levels. Notably, the MAPK signaling and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways play crucial roles in this process [43, 44], with numerous genes in these pathways being upregulated. This rapid activation triggered a cascade of hypoxia-responsive genes, aiding in adaptation and maintenance of metabolic homeostasis.

Discussion

Managing oxygen reserves for extended breath-hold diving posed one of the most critical challenges for cetacean ancestors reinvading the ocean, particularly in deep-diving species requiring efficient oxygen storage, distribution, and utilization under submersion [45]. Consequently, cetaceans must efficiently manage their stored oxygen to support extended dives that can reach depths of thousands of meters. Myriad studies have highlighted the extensive physiological and morphological transformations in the muscular, cardiovascular, and circulatory systems of deep-diving cetaceans [46, 47].

In terms of cellular mechanisms, changes in persistent gene expression patterns are considered major drivers of the evolution of specific phenotypic features. For the first time, this study comprehensively delineates the mononuclear transcriptome of pygmy sperm whale muscle and heart using single-nucleus transcriptome data. This dataset offers unprecedented insight to explore potential molecular signatures associated with hypoxia tolerance capacity in deep-diving cetaceans at single-cell resolution. A comparison with cattle revealed three cell types with increased presence in muscle and heart tissues of the pygmy sperm whale (Fig. 2c and d and Additional file 2: Fig. S4). Within these WECs, we observed a subset of gene expression changes induced by hypoxia dependent on HIF signaling, particularly involving genes related to oxygen delivery and consumption activated under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 5a). Previous studies have highlighted the critical role of HIF-1α in mediating hypoxia tolerance in Tibetan catfishes [48], cetaceans [49, 50], and pinnipeds [51], where rapid hypoxic stabilization of HIF-1α in elephant seal endothelial cells was documented. Furthermore, pericytes and endothelial cells engaged in ligand-receptor mediated cellular signaling, potentially contributed to hypoxia response mechanisms [52]. This heightened interaction capacity of pygmy sperm whale pericytes (Additional file 2: Fig. S5), particularly within their muscle tissue, aligns with their unique deep-diving physiology. Based on the genetic landscape of HIF-related genes within these cells (Additional file 1: Tables S13 and S17), we hypothesize that the coexpression of genes in the HIF signaling pathway in cetacean muscle and heart contributes to the remarkable hypoxia tolerance observed in deep-diving cetaceans. However, we note that the MAPK and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, which were also rapidly activated, are central players not only in hypoxia adaptation but also in pro-inflammatory signaling. The upregulation observed in this study may reflect both the hypoxic response and potentially the acute inflammatory stress responses associated with the challenge or death process itself. Future studies could explore whether cetaceans, similar to seals [53] or experienced human divers [54], possess enhanced anti-inflammatory regulatory mechanisms acting alongside or downstream of these key signaling pathways to maintain homeostasis during extreme dives.

Mitochondria are major consumers of oxygen, and prolonged hypoxia can limit ETC activity [24]. Selection on nuclear-encoded ETC genes impacts the activities of ETC enzyme complexes, potentially facilitating adaptation to hypoxic conditions [55]. Notably, two genes (ATP6V0D1 and ATP6V1H) encoding components of vacuolar ATPase are rapidly evolving in deep-diving cetaceans (Fig. 5b and Additional file 1: Table S8). Our comparative genomic analysis reveals an accelerated CNE within the intronic regions of the gene ATP5F1E, overlapping with its enhancer (Fig. 3a). Additionally, we discovered that the gene COX10 displays two accelerated CNEs within its intronic regions, one of which contains a mutation specific to deep-diving cetaceans (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). Furthermore, genes encoding subunits of the ETC show upregulation in WECs (Figs. 3b and 5b), implying high levels of cellular energy production [56]. These findings suggest that alterations at the genetic and cellular levels may play a role in maintaining ETC function during hypoxia. Concurrently, in cetaceans, we similarly observed elevated PRDX1 expression in myocytes (Additional file 1: Table S15), consistent with reported antioxidant gene upregulation in northern elephant seals, another deep-diving marine mammals [57].

In addition to regulating the ETC, hypoxia also induces metabolism reprogramming, leading to metabolic adaptation [58]. HIF target genes linked to this metabolic reprogramming include rapid evolution genes such as SLC2A4, PYGM, ENO3, LDLR, and CPT1A, along with upregulated genes like HK2, LDHA, FABP3, PDKs, and ACADs (Fig. 5b). Changes in the expression levels or amino acid sites of these genes could potentially affect the efficiency of glucose metabolism (glycogenolysis and glycolysis) and lipid metabolism (β-oxidation). For example, mutations in PYGM are known to cause McArdle disease, or glycogenosis type V, causing inability of patients to obtain energy from their glycogen stores [59]. Interestingly, cetaceans rely more heavily on glycogen as an endogenous energy source in fast-twitch fibers which are characterized by high glycolytic potential and low oxidative capacity [60]. Therefore, the deep-diving cetacean-specific mutations in PYGM that we have identified might enhance glycogenolysis in these marine mammals (Fig. 3d), although further studies are necessary for comprehensive gene function verification. Concerning fat metabolism, FABP3, also known as fatty acid-binding protein 3, contributes substantially to fatty acids uptake [34], while acyl-CoA dehydrogenases aid in energy production from fats [36]. These findings align with the hypothesis that muscle (slow-oxidative fibers) metabolism may predominantly rely on fatty acids as a fuel source [60]. Notably, the loss of MSS51 in muscle cell lines redirects energy metabolism toward β-oxidation of fatty acids, supporting this notion. The absence of MSS51 in the cetacean stem lineage provides further evidence that fatty acid metabolism may be the primary energy source for muscle tissue in cetaceans [61]. Unlike other hypoxic environments where animals show reduced capacity for fatty acid oxidation, indicating a shift towards glucose as the primary metabolic fuel [41], cetaceans maintain fatty acids as their energy source due to increased substrate stores and the efficiency of energy production from fatty acids [62]. Both deep- and shallow-diving marine mammals predominantly use fatty acids as the primary metabolic substrate during locomotion [8]. This strategy allows fatty acids to serve as the main fuel during surface intervals and the vast majority of aerobic dives, thereby sparing limited glucose/glycogen reserves for the prolonged breath-hold dives that exceed the aerobic dive limit. Our findings on FABP3 and ACADs in cetaceans provide additional insight into the mechanisms underlying this fatty acid-dominated metabolic pathway. These findings are consistent with the metabolic shift proposed in the study on MSS51 loss, further underscoring the importance of fatty acid metabolism in cetacean muscle physiology. Furthermore, we observed that metabolic pathways were predominantly enriched in upregulated genes of myocytes and type II cardiomyocytes (Fig. 4a), indicating their potential role in meeting the metabolic demands of deep-diving cetaceans. Ultimately, these findings suggest that cetaceans possess evolved genomic traits and associated cellular responses that enable their deep-diving behavior in the face of hypoxia.

The decrease in heart rate observed in cetaceans with increasing depth may slow oxygen delivery to tissues, potentially delaying the depletion of blood oxygen stores and reducing metabolic demands on the heart [37]. The DMD gene, pivotal in cardiac functions, has been reported to demonstrate functional significance even in intronic mutations [63]. Our investigation identified four accelerated CNEs in the intronic regions of DMD (Additional file 1: Table S10), with one of these regions exhibiting a deep-diving cetacean-specific mutation (Additional file 2: Fig. S9). Cetacean DMD variations may also affect skeletal muscle metabolism. In human DMD pathology, glycolytic flux is suppressed, while oxidative metabolism is enhanced [64]. In deep-diving cetaceans, similar metabolic shifts occur as an adaptive trait, potentially enhancing aerobic efficiency during extended dives. This adaptation likely reduces oxygen consumption per unit workload, indirectly supporting a lower heart rate by decreasing cardiac demand. The downregulated gene in WECs of the heart significantly enriched pathways related to heart rate regulation (Additional file 2: Fig. S10), thus may contributing to a decrease in oxygen demand. The slower heart rate is associated with the upregulation of multiple genes involved in glycolysis in the heart (Additional file 1: Table S19), indicating a reliance on anaerobic metabolism in cardiac function (Fig. 5c and Additional file 1: Table S19). This interpretation is further supported by elevated glycogen concentrations documented in seal hearts [65], though direct experimental validation of anaerobic metabolic pathways in whale remains to be established. On the other hand, muscles serve as the primary oxygen reservoir in cetaceans [3], sustaining oxidative phosphorylation during foraging dives by using myoglobin-bound oxygen stores and glycogen reserves for energy extraction (Fig. 5c and Additional file 1: Table S19). Collectively, the observed genetic signatures of evolution and cellular responses represent potential mechanisms that could contribute to maintaining cardiac function and metabolic processes essential for efficient oxygen use and energy dynamics during extended dives in hypoxic conditions.

Beyond key adaptations for deep-diving hypoxia tolerance, our genomic comparisons reveal additional adaptive changes in deep-diving cetaceans. PSGs are enriched in type II interferon pathways (e.g., NLRC5) (Additional file 1: Table S11), suggesting their potential role in regulating inflammation related to ischemia–reperfusion injury [66]. Additionally, rapid evolution in sperm development genes (e.g., CREM, TESMIN) corresponds to adaptations due to abdominal testicles, a universal cetacean trait improving dive efficiency. This parallels compensatory sperm function optimization in other mammals lacking scrotal testes [67]. Although unrelated to oxygen management, these changes demonstrate multisystem remodeling during aquatic reinvasion, where dive physiology coevolves with immune and reproductive adaptations.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. Foremost, the reliance on tissues from a single individual per species inherently limits statistical power and generalizability, as potential sources of variability could influence snRNA-seq results. Such variability may arise from unknown physiological stressors, stranding-related factors in the pygmy sperm whale, or postmortem interval. Therefore, we applied a rapid tissue preservation protocol to minimize acute postmortem artifacts. We assessed the efficacy of our protocol by analyzing the expression of postmortem-associated genes and found no significant differential expression after accounting for cell-type proportions (Additional file 1: Table S12), suggesting our preservation approach was effective at the molecular level. The RNA integrity numbers (RIN ranging from 5.7 to 6.7 across samples, as detailed in “Methods”) indicate moderate RNA quality. Despite this, downstream metrics (e.g., median genes per cell) confirm data suitability (Additional file 1: Table S21). Furthermore, the pygmy sperm whale was intact with no visible trauma or decomposition (Additional file 2: Fig. S11). Our single-sample approach was necessitated by the inherent challenges in obtaining fresh cetacean tissues, which are often limited by the rarity of these animals and ethical constraints (as exemplified in studies on short-finned pilot whale [68] and killer whale [12]). Consequently, we emphasize that our single-nucleus transcriptomic comparisons with cattle are exploratory, serving to generate novel hypotheses for future validation in multi-individual studies. Such studies should prioritize multi-individual sampling, ideally from known-health animals such as those in aquariums, to enable robust statistical analyses and control for confounding variables. Second, the absence of species-specific functional genomic datasets led us to employ human and mouse markers for identifying cell types in muscle and heart tissues. While this may miss new cell types, it successfully shed light on conserved cellular features across species. Finally, our observations on gene mutation sites and rapidly evolving conserved noncoding elements need experimental testing. However, these findings propose valuable hypotheses for species-specific adaptations. This study thus provides a foundational framework, which future research can enhance with larger samples where feasible, multi-omics integration, and spatial transcriptomics to deepen insights into cellular architecture.

Conclusions

We present a high-quality genome assembly and gene set for the pygmy sperm whale which reveals the genetic basis of hypoxia adaptation in deep-diving cetaceans, including the regulation of metabolic pathways and physiological responses. Additionally, we conducted single-nucleus RNA analyses on the muscle and heart tissues of the pygmy sperm whale to establish a single-nucleus transcriptome atlas and identify cell-type-specific signatures potentially related to adaptation in deep-diving cetaceans. Through this analysis, we identify specific cell types and gene expression patterns linked to metabolic adaptation. Our study provides crucial genomic and single-nucleus transcriptomic resources, shedding light on the genetic and cellular mechanisms that drive hypoxia adaptation in deep-diving cetaceans.

Methods

Sample collection and identification

Several tissue samples (including dorsal muscle, heart, testis, lung, and skin) were obtained from an adult pygmy sperm whale stranded along the coast of Zhuhai, Guangdong, China. Following an unsuccessful rescue attempt by sanctuary staff and a notification call from the rescue team to our research group, tissues were collected within 1 h. External assessment indicated an adult male in good body condition with no signs of emaciation or injury. Upon gross examination, the carcass showed no trauma, pathological lesions, or signs of decomposition (e.g., bloating or discoloration), consistent with natural stranding mortality (Additional file 2: Fig. S11). This ensured tissue collection shortly after death, minimizing post-mortem degradation. The sample collection was conducted under the supervision of sanctuary staff. The pygmy sperm whale identification was verified using COI and CYTB markers. The cattle tissues (muscle and heart) were collected from a healthy, non-stressed adult specimen (Bos taurus, commercial breed) immediately after slaughter at a commercial abattoir in Guangzhou, China.

Genome sequencing and assembly

High-quality genomic DNA was extracted from the muscle tissue of the pygmy sperm whale samples using the QIAGEN® Genomic-tip (Cat. No. 10243). Sequencing of the sample was conducted using the circular consensus sequence model on the PacBio Sequel II platform, to generate high-fidelity reads. To anchor hybrid scaffolds onto the chromosome, Hi-C sequencing data were obtained via the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Contigs were assembled using Hifiasm v0.16.0 [69] with the purge-duplication level set to zero (− l 0). Following the initial assembly, purge_dups v1.2.6 [70] was applied to eliminate haplotigs, with all parameters set according to the authors’ recommended pipeline. Subsequently, the anchoring of contigs onto chromosomes was achieved using Hi-C sequencing reads with the YaHS v1.1 workflow [71], with the − l 15,000 option to specify a minimum contig length of 15 kb for scaffolding. Further refinement of the chromosome-level assembly involved a manual review and adjustment using Juicebox Assembly Tools (https://github.com/theaidenlab/juicebox). Finally, genome quality assessment was performed using BUSCO v5.4.7 [72] with the mammalia_odb10 gene sets. This evaluation included whole-genome synteny analysis, which involved comparative alignment with the genomes of the blue whale (Balaenoptera musculus) and vaquita (Phocoena sinus). Additionally, we mapped the initial reads against the assembly to evaluate genome quality comprehensively.

Annotation of repetitive sequences and protein-coding genes

Prior to gene prediction in the pygmy sperm whale genome, we first identified repetitive elements using Tandem Repeats Finder software v4.09.1 [73]. To thoroughly predict transposable elements, we used RepeatMasker v4.1.2-p1 [74], RepeatModeler v2.0.2a, and RepeatProteinMask v4.1.2-p1. For gene annotation, we integrated ab initio and homology following a pipeline similar to that described previously [75] with the addition of transcriptome-based information from RNA-seq alignments to enhance gene model accuracy. For ab initio-based annotation, we analyzed the repeat-masked genome using Augustus v3.4.0 [76] and GENSCAN v1.0 [77] both employing human gene training models with default parameters. Total RNA was extracted from four tissues (muscle, testis, lung, and skin) and sequenced using the MGI-DNBSEQ T7 sequencing platform. The RNA-seq reads were then aligned with the pygmy sperm whale genome using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [78] (parameters: –fr –min-intronlen 20 –max-intronlen 500,000 –dta) and merged for the Braker2 v2.1.6 [79] gene prediction pipeline using default parameters. For homology-based annotation, we selected genomes of 11 mammals closely and distantly related to pygmy sperm whale from the NCBI. These species included sperm whale, vaquita, killer whale (GCA_937001465.1), common bottlenose dolphin (GCF_011762595.1), blue whale (GCF_009873245.2), cattle (GCA_002263795.2), sheep (GCF_016772045.1), domestic cat (GCF_000181335.3), dog (GCF_014441545.1), house mouse (GCF_000001635.26), and human (GCA_009914755.4). The genomes were used independently to predict protein-coding genes in the pygmy sperm whale using the GeMoMa pipeline v1.9 [80] (parameters: GAF.a = "iAA > = 0.75" GeMoMa.m = 500,000). For transcriptome-based annotation, RNA-seq data were assembled using Trinity v2.14.0 with both de novo and genome-guided approaches [81] under default parameters. Gene predictions were then carried out with PASA v2.5.2 [82] (-I 500000). The final gene set was obtained by integrating results from all three methods using EvidenceModeler v1.1.1 [83] (default parameters). To assess the integrity of the final annotation, we used BUSCO with the mammalian_odb10 gene sets, excluding the impacts of partial genes in our further analysis.

Gene function annotation was performed by conducting sequence similarity and domain conservation analyses. All predicted gene sequences were analyzed through homology searches against the NCBI NR and SwissProt databases using BLASTp v2.7.1 (-evalue 1e-5). The PANTHER classification system [84] and the eggNOG orthology database [85] were used to categorize the amino acid sequences functionally. Furthermore, to identify protein motifs and domains, InterProScan v5.63–95.0 [86] was employed with default parameters, scanning against a comprehensive repository of databases within the InterPro resource.

Identification of orthologous genes

To mitigate the negative effect of annotation, pseudogenes, and genome quality, we applied TOGA [87] using its default, pre-trained orthology inference pipeline to establish robust orthologous genes. Briefly, TOGA takes pairwise genome alignment chains and, based on features such as intronic/intergenic alignment coverage and synteny, assigns each candidate chain to its most likely orthologous locus using TOGA’s built-in classifier. TOGA then determines orthology types (1:1, 1:many, many:1, or many:many) by considering all reference genes whose predicted query loci encode an intact reading frame. Finally, for each orthologous relationship, the closest matching reference gene is used to transfer functional annotation. The resulting ortholog gene set comprises 2 non-cetaceans (human, cattle) and 13 cetacean genomes (Additional file 1: Table S1).

To generate pairwise genome alignment chains for input to TOGA, we aligned genomic sequences using LASTZ v1.04.15 [88] with specific parameters (K = 2400, L = 3000, Y = 9400, H = 2000, and default scoring matrix HOXD70). This process involved using the human genome sequence as a reference and other species as queries. Upon identifying 14 orthologous relationships between human and other species, we computed the intersection of pairwise 1:1 gene set, resulting in a collection of single-copy orthologous genes across 15 species. Subsequently, this gene set was employed for further analyses focused on selection pressures.

Identification of PSGs and REGs

A total 11,172 single-copy orthologous genes identified by TOGA were aligned at the codon level using MACSE v2.07 [89]. Regions with poor alignment and gaps were subsequently removed using Gblocks v0.91b [90] with strict parameters (“ − t = c, − b5 = n”), and alignments shorter than 150 base pairs were excluded. After filtering, 11,120 single-copy orthologous genes were kept for subsequent selective pressure analysis. To identify positive selective evolution and rapid evolution, the CODEML program in PAML v4.9e [91] was employed using a phylogenetic tree extracted from TimeTree [92] (Fig. 1a). Initially, the branch-site model was applied to identify PSGs. The deep‐diving cetaceans (i.e., pygmy sperm whale, sperm whale, and Blainville’s beaked whale), renowned for their exceptional dive durations and depths, were designated as the foreground branch. A likelihood ratio test (LRT) was performed to determine if the branch-site model, which included codons under positive selection (ω > 1), provided a better fit than the null model, which included only neutral or negatively selected codons (ω ≤ 1). P-values for model comparison were determined using chi-square statistics. Genes with highly aggregated positively selected sites (PSS) were excluded to mitigate potential false positives arising from alignment errors. Specifically, the intervals between PSS occurrences were calculated, and alignments were discarded if the median interval was less than 10 amino acids and occurred more than twice. Genes meeting the criteria of LRT P < 0.05, sites with a posteriori probability of positive selection exceeding 0.50 from the Bayesian Empirical Bayes method, and dispersed PSSs were considered candidates for positive selection. Next, the branch model was applied using the same set of orthologous genes to identify REGs. This analysis assessed whether the foreground branch (deep-diving cetaceans) exhibited a significantly higher ω, regardless of whether it exceeded 1, compared to the background branch (other lineages), using the LRT test. Genes with an LRT P < 0.05 were designated as REGs in deep-diving cetaceans. Both the PSGs and REGs gene sets underwent GO term and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis with P < 0.05, a minimum count of 3, and an enrichment factor > 1.5 using the online tool Metascape v3.5 [93] with human genes as the background.

Identification of accelerated CNEs

CNE play an important role in modulating gene expression [94] and species traits [95]. To identify CNEs, we first constructed multiple whole-genome alignments for 15 mammals (the same species as those used in previous methods) using the MULTIZ v11.2 program [96]. Fourfold degenerate sites were extracted based on human coding gene annotation. For estimating the non-conserved model, we used the phyloFit program from the PHAST v1.4 package [97] with default parameters, employing fourfold degenerate sites from the 15-way multiple alignments and maintaining the same topology as in the PAML analysis but without evolutionary distance. Next, the phastCons program in the PHAST package was used with the non-conserved model to estimate conserved models, using the parameter “–estimate-trees.” After estimating conserved elements, exon regions were excluded from highly conserved elements to generate the CNEs. The resulting elements were merged if they were within 10 bp of each other, forming single conserved element, and regions shorter than 30 bp were excluded. Accelerated CNEs were assessed using the phyloP program from the PHAST package, with parameters “–msa-format MAF –branch –method LRT –mode CONACC,” focusing on the three deep‐diving cetaceans. The acceleration scores, generated using the mode “ACC,” were corrected for multiple hypotheses using the false discovery rate method [98]. Significance was considered at false discovery rate-adjusted P < 0.05. We paid special attention to accelerated CNEs located in the intergenic region, within introns and within the 10 k bp upstream/downstream flanking regions of genes. These genes were then extracted for GO term and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using Metascape with the parameters detailed earlier.

Processing of raw snRNA sequencing data

Previous research has highlighted the importance of muscle and heart tissues for coping with hypoxia in deep-diving cetaceans [3]. For this study, samples of approximately 0.5 cm3 of muscle and heart tissue were obtained from the pygmy sperm whale and cattle. All tissues were dissected, aliquoted, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 1 h of excision and sent to LC-Bio Technology Co. Ltd. (Hang Zhou, China) on dry ice for nucleus extraction, snRNA sequencing library construction, and sequencing. RIN was assessed by company, yielding RIN of 5.7 for whale muscle, 6.4 for whale heart, 6.6 for cattle muscle, and 6.7 for cattle heart tissues. These moderate RIN typical for frozen samples in snRNA-seq workflows are compatible with nucleus extraction, library construction, and sequencing (as per 10 × Genomics guidelines). The alignment of sequencing reads from each tissue to the respective reference genomes of pygmy sperm whales and cattle was performed using Cell Ranger v7.1.0 (10 × Genomics, USA). This process facilitated demultiplexing, barcode processing, and single-cell 3′ gene counting (Additional file 1: Table S21). Despite these moderate RIN, downstream snRNA-seq metrics (e.g., median genes per cell) confirmed the data’s suitability for analysis.

In the following analysis, cell gene matrices for each tissue were imported into the R toolkit Seurat v5.0.1 [99] and consolidated into Seurat objects. Cells that expressed more than 500 genes and no more than 3000 genes were retained, while genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were filtered out. Subsequently, normalization, scaling, and variable gene identification were performed using the “SCTransform” function in Seurat for each sample. Integration anchors were identified using the “PrepSCTIntegration” and “FindIntegrationAnchors” functions. Datasets from the same tissue type were then integrated using the “IntegrateData” function in Seurat. To enhance the identification of shared clusters and mitigate batch effects, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using Seurat’s “RunPCA” function, followed by the application of Harmony [100] with default parameters, applied separately to muscle and heart tissues. This aligned analogous cell types across datasets toward a shared centroid in low-dimensional PCA space. Clusters were then determined using the Seurat “FindClusters” function, which used a community identification algorithm with parameter “resolution = 0.1.” The cell clusters were visualized through t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE), based on plots generated by the Seurat “RunTSNE” function. Finally, cluster classification and annotation were conducted based on marker expression identified by Seurat “FindConservedMarkers” function with parameters “min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25, test.use = wilcox.” Cell types were classified by performing clustering analysis based on gene expression patterns, followed by manual annotation using established marker genes from public database [22, 23].

Identification of DEGs for specific cluster and enrichment analysis

Crucial for diving adaptation, the muscle and heart tissues of the pygmy sperm whale may exhibit distinct proportions of cell types compared to their terrestrial ancestors [8]. To test this, we calculated these proportions for each tissue to identify any elevated fractions in the pygmy sperm whale. Additionally, to evaluate potential postmortem effects on gene expression, we downloaded a postmortem-associated gene set [21], comprising 24 genes for muscle and 15 for heart tissues. Of these, 11 muscle genes and 7 heart genes were detected in both the pygmy sperm whale and cattle datasets (Additional file 1: Table S12). We then compared the expression levels of these genes in our snRNA-seq data using Seurat’s “FindMarkers” function incorporating cell proportions (ident.1 = “whale,” ident.2 = “cattle,” min.diff.pct = 0.25, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25, test.use = “wilcox”). We also identified DEGs for specific cell types (e.g., pericytes) between pygmy sperm whale and cattle using Seurat’s “FindMarkers” function with parameters consistent with the postmortem gene analysis, additionally including adjusted p-value < 0.01. CREB5 was excluded from subsequent enrichment analysis, due to its differential abundance in pericytes. The remaining DEGs were subjected to enrichment analysis for GO terms and KEGG pathways using Metascape, applying the parameters described previously.

Cell–cell communications

To analyze intercellular communication among each cell type from the snRNA-seq data, we first performed interactome analysis using the CellChat v2.1.2 R package [101]. We created a CellChat object using the normalized count data with the “createCellChat” function. Next, we used the “identifyOverExpressedGenes” function to find overexpressed genes. After that, we calculated interactions using the “identifyOverExpressedInteractions” function. Then, we used the “computeCommunProb” function and the “computeCommunProbPathway” function to compute communication probabilities. Finally, we aggregated the network using the “aggregateNet” function. All these functions used their default options. These calculations were based on the CellChatDB database for human ligand-receptor interactions. At the end, we compared ligand-receptor interaction differences between cattle and pygmy sperm whale.
