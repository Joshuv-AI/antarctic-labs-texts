User Needs for an Artificial Intelligence-Empowered Basic Activities of Daily Living Training System After Stroke: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Qing Wang, Jing Xiong, Suzhu Lin, Xiujiao Lin, Feifei Lin, Yating Xu, Baoyuan Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore patients’ and primary caregivers’ willingness to use an artificial intelligence (AI)-empowered basic activities of daily living (BADL) training system and their needs, concerns, and expectations regarding the system.Patients and MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted in the Department of Neurology at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, Fujian, China. Patients with poststroke hemiplegia and primary caregivers were recruited using purposive sampling. Semistructured, face-to-face interviews were conducted with 11 patients with poststroke hemiplegia and 13 primary caregivers to capture both patient and caregiver perspectives. Data were collected on participant-reported BADL difficulties, willingness to use the AI-empowered BADL training system, perceived needs and concerns, and expectations for system design. Data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis.ResultsFour themes were identified: 1) BADL limitations created shared difficulties for patients and caregivers; 2) acceptance of the AI-empowered BADL training system depended on usefulness and functional fit; 3) participants expected low-barrier, understandable, and sustainable training support; and 4) the system was viewed as a supplement to professional rehabilitation and family caregiving.ConclusionAn AI-empowered BADL training system may be acceptable to patients with poststroke hemiplegia and their primary caregivers if it is safe, reliable, easy to use, and appropriate to patients’ functional status. These findings may guide the future development of task-oriented AI-empowered BADL training systems that support patient-caregiver collaboration, safety assurance, sustained engagement, and human-AI collaboration in rehabilitation.Keywords: stroke, hemiplegia, activities of daily living, artificial intelligence, caregivers, qualitative research

Introduction

Stroke is a major cause of long-term disability worldwide, and hemiplegia is one of the most common functional impairments after stroke.1 The Global Burden of Disease Study showed that the number of incident stroke cases increased substantially from 1990 to 2021, partly because of population aging and growth.2 Hemiplegia limits motor and sensory function on one side of the body and often impairs basic activities of daily living (BADL), including eating, toileting, dressing, bathing, personal hygiene, and functional mobility.3 These limitations reduce patients’ self-care ability and quality of life and increase the physical, psychological, and financial burden on primary caregivers.4,5

BADL training is essential for improving self-care ability after stroke. Unlike isolated limb function training, BADL training is embedded in real-life tasks and usually requires multistep movement sequencing, trunk control, use of the affected limb, environmental adaptation, and caregiver assistance.6 However, current BADL training still largely depends on therapists’ demonstration and subjective observation, with limited capacity for real-time movement analysis, individualized feedback, and continuous support in home settings.7,8 Artificial intelligence (AI) has been increasingly applied in stroke rehabilitation for activity recognition, movement quality assessment, and home-based rehabilitation monitoring.9,10 Nevertheless, many existing AI-based rehabilitation systems remain focused on single movements, upper-limb tasks, or laboratory-based validation, and have not sufficiently addressed complete BADL tasks, home environments, caregiver involvement, and training safety. Implementation may also be limited by low digital literacy and unequal access to suitable devices, internet connectivity, and technical support.

Before developing an AI-empowered BADL training system, it is necessary to understand the perspectives of its intended users. Patients with poststroke hemiplegia are the direct performers of BADL training, whereas primary caregivers often serve as companions, assistants, and safety managers during home-based rehabilitation.11 Without understanding the needs and concerns of both groups, system development may fail to match patients’ functional status, caregivers’ practical workload, and real-life training scenarios.12 Therefore, this descriptive qualitative study explored patients’ and primary caregivers’ willingness to use an AI-empowered BADL training system and their needs, concerns, and expectations regarding the system. The findings are intended to provide user-centered evidence for subsequent system design, optimization, and clinical translation.

Material and Methods

This descriptive qualitative study explored BADL difficulties among patients with poststroke hemiplegia and examined patients’ and primary caregivers’ willingness to use an AI-empowered BADL training system, as well as their needs, concerns, and expectations regarding the system. A qualitative descriptive design was selected because the study aimed to obtain direct, practice-oriented accounts of patients’ and caregivers’ needs, concerns, and expectations regarding an AI-empowered BADL training system.13 Data were collected through semistructured face-to-face interviews and analyzed using inductive thematic analysis.14 The study was reported according to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.

Participants

Participants were recruited from the Department of Neurology at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. Patients were purposively recruited using maximum variation sampling to capture diversity in age, sex, affected side of hemiplegia, and level of BADL dependence. Primary caregivers who met the eligibility criteria were recruited separately for interviews.

Eligible patients were adults with imaging-confirmed stroke and clinically confirmed hemiplegia who had stable vital signs, were conscious and able to communicate, and had a basic ability to use a smartphone. Primary caregivers were family members or other caregivers who had been the main providers of daily care and BADL assistance for at least 1 month. Participants were excluded if severe cognitive or psychiatric impairment was documented in the medical record or identified by the treating clinical team, or if hearing or communication difficulties prevented them from understanding and responding to the interview questions.

During the study period, 25 eligible individuals were invited; 1 declined participation, and 24 participants were included, comprising 11 patients and 13 primary caregivers. Recruitment stopped when no new themes or important information emerged after 3 consecutive interviews, indicating data saturation.15

Formal interviews were conducted from October 2025 to January 2026 in a quiet private room. Patients and caregivers were interviewed separately by a trained nursing graduate student with no prior therapeutic, nursing, or personal relationship with the participants. Before each interview, the interviewer explained the study purpose, procedures, voluntary participation, audio recording, and confidentiality measures, and obtained written informed consent.

Because some participants were unfamiliar with artificial intelligence-assisted rehabilitation, a standardized 1-minute demonstration video was shown before questions about system use and functional needs. The video introduced general scenarios of movement demonstration, movement recognition, and feedback prompts but did not represent specific functions of the system to be developed. Interviews lasted 19 to 37 minutes, were audio-recorded with permission, and were transcribed verbatim within 24 hours. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, and all data were anonymized.

Data Analysis

Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. All transcripts were analyzed in Chinese. Two researchers checked transcripts against the recordings and clarified ambiguous statements with participants when necessary. NVivo 15.0 was used for data management.

An inductive, team-based thematic analysis was conducted, and no formal codebook was used.14 Two researchers independently coded the transcripts using NVivo 15.0, compared their coding, and developed themes through discussion and consensus. Coding disagreements were not statistically quantified but were identified through comparison of the two researchers’ coding and resolved through discussion and consensus. Divergent views were retained and examined within themes when they reflected meaningful variations in participants’ perspectives. Credibility was enhanced through member checking, dependability and confirmability through independent coding and an audit trail, and transferability through maximum variation sampling and detailed reporting of participant characteristics.16 The final themes were returned to all 24 participants for member checking, and no substantive revisions were suggested. Quotations were selected to illustrate both common patterns and meaningful variations in participants’ experiences. Representative quotations were translated into English, with back-translation used when necessary to ensure semantic consistency.

Twenty-four participants were included: 11 patients with poststroke hemiplegia and 13 primary caregivers. Each completed 1 interview lasting 19 to 37 minutes. BADL Limitations Created Shared Difficulties for Patients and Caregivers

Patients had difficulty completing entire BADL tasks independently and often required assistance with toileting, bathing, dressing, eating, drinking, and hygiene.

P2: I need someone to support me when I use the toilet. When I take a bath, I have to sit on a stool and let my family help me wash. I can put on clothes by myself, but someone has to prepare the clothes for me.

P11: I basically cannot complete these things independently. The most troublesome thing is going to the toilet. I often need help from my family, and sometimes 2 people have to help me together.

Caregivers described continuous physical burden during transfers, toileting, hygiene, and night-time care.

C6: It is quite troublesome. I feel very tired when I help him get up. Helping him with toileting is also very tiring and takes a long time.

C13: These daily activities are all very difficult. We need to help with turning over, and toileting and personal hygiene are also very challenging. Sometimes we cannot even provide care in time at night.

Together, these accounts show that patients’ difficulties extended across multiple BADL tasks, with loss of independence translating into continuous physical and supervisory demands on caregivers.

Acceptance Depended on Usefulness and Fit

Acceptance was conditional. Patients focused on recovery, home-based convenience, and motivation.

P1: If it is good for my physical recovery, I would definitely be willing to use it. I could open the app at home and follow it to train, without always going to the hospital. That would be much more convenient.

P6: It can make me more interested in exercising and make me take the initiative to train. I hope this software can help me, so that I can take care of myself and cause less trouble for my family.

Caregivers Focused on Standardized Training and Reduced Caregiving Burden

C4: Training with the system should be more standardized. AI can judge whether the movements are performed properly. If he can take care of himself after training, our burden will be lighter.

Participants also questioned whether the system would be suitable for patients with different levels of functional impairment, especially those with severe impairment. Some patients with severe BADL dependence emphasized the need for physical assistance and questioned their ability to follow app-based training, whereas patients with mild or moderate dependence more commonly emphasized convenience, motivation, and independent use.

P8: I cannot even lift one hand, and I have no strength. How can I follow a mobile phone to do rehabilitation? What is the use of just watching standard movements if my body cannot keep up?

Caregivers expressed concerns about system reliability and older patients’ understanding of artificial intelligence.

C3: Older people may not be able to understand what artificial intelligence is. They only do the rehabilitation exercises taught by doctors. I think the reliability of current artificial intelligence is still limited.

Overall, acceptance reflected a balance between perceived benefits and functional fit: participants were more willing to use the system when it appeared useful and convenient, but less confident when patients’ functional limitations reduced its practical applicability. Patients focused more on recovery, convenience, and motivation, whereas caregivers emphasized reliability, feasibility, and the potential to reduce caregiving burden.

Participants Expected Low-Barrier, Understandable, and Sustainable Training Support

Participants wanted the system to be simple to operate, supporting independent use and reducing the need for caregiver assistance.

P9: It had better be simpler. I really do not know how to press complicated buttons. It would be best if I could operate the software by myself.

C11: It would be best to start with one click. The font should be larger, and the buttons should also be simple and clear, so that it is convenient for older people to use.

They also expected slow-paced video demonstrations and voice prompts.

P8: During training, the movements in the video must be slow. They should not be as fast as those of normal people. We cannot keep up. It has to be slower and teach us step by step, so that we can understand and learn to do it.

C1: I hope the system can provide guidance during training and teach him through verbal communication. It cannot only give instructions, because not everyone can accept them so quickly.

C3: If there are images of standard movements during training, patients can follow the standardized video, and then AI can recognize whether the movements are standard. That would be more user-friendly.

Some participants wanted rehabilitation information and access to consultation.

P6: If the software could provide some dietary advice and online consultation with doctors, then I could ask directly when I encounter problems.

C4: It would be more convenient if it could push small pieces of rehabilitation training knowledge and also allow us to contact doctors directly for consultation.

Training records, check-ins, feedback, and encouragement were viewed as helpful for motivation and progress monitoring.

P7: I think it would be good to have a training check-in function. If I can see my progress after completing a certain amount of training every day and receive some rewards, it may motivate me more to train.

C2: If every training session is recorded and we can see the patient’s daily progress, it will make the patient more motivated to keep going.

Collectively, these needs reflected a preference for a system that was easy to operate while providing clear guidance, feedback, and motivation for sustained training.

The System Was Viewed as a Supplement to Professional Rehabilitation and Family Caregiving

Participants did not expect the system to replace professionals or caregivers. Safety was a major concern during high-risk BADL tasks.

P5: What I worry about most is safety. If I train in front of a mobile phone and no one is watching me, what should I do if I fall?

C10: It is still best to have someone watching nearby during training. Otherwise, if the movements are not performed properly or the patient cannot stand steadily, they may fall.

C11: A rehabilitation therapist can directly guide the patient’s limbs to perform movements, but can software do that? Software alone definitely cannot help patients move their body parts. After all, some patients have severe conditions and simply cannot lift many parts of the body by themselves. They need someone to help guide them.

Participants also expected guidance from family members or professionals during initial use, especially for older patients or those unfamiliar with electronic devices.

P2: I am not skilled at using mobile phones and other electronic products. I definitely cannot learn complicated software, so I still need someone to teach me.

P11: If family members are nearby watching and helping, then there is nothing much to worry about. But if no one is nearby, and my spouse at home is also old and cannot understand these electronic products, then it will not work.

C13: The wording in the software may be quite complicated, and older people may not understand it. When training movements are not performed well enough, guidance from someone nearby is also needed.

Overall, participants viewed the system as an assistive tool that could provide demonstrations, reminders, records, and preliminary feedback, while safety supervision, physical assistance, and individualized guidance continued to require support from caregivers and professionals.

Discussion

This study explored patients’ and primary caregivers’ willingness to use an AI-empowered BADL training system, as well as their needs and concerns regarding the system. Participants recognized its potential for supporting home-based training, providing movement guidance, and reducing caregiving burden, but acceptance depended on reliability, functional fit, safety, and ease of use. These findings highlight the need to design AI rehabilitation systems around real-life BADL tasks and shared use by patients and caregivers, rather than focusing solely on isolated movement assessment.

BADL limitations after hemiplegia reflected not only impaired limb function but also difficulties in completing entire daily tasks such as eating, toileting, bathing, dressing, transfers, and hygiene. Caregivers therefore assumed continuous physical and supervisory responsibilities. This suggests that an AI-empowered BADL training system should be organized around specific daily tasks and should integrate task decomposition, movement recognition, graded feedback, and home-based training support.

Participants’ willingness to use the system was conditional. Tyagi et al17 found that patients with stroke and caregivers valued telerehabilitation for convenience and accessibility, but acceptance was affected by equipment use, stroke severity, caregiver support, and safety. Brouns et al18 similarly reported that willingness to use e-rehabilitation was influenced by perceived benefit, confidence in operation, technological complexity, and concerns about replacing face-to-face rehabilitation. In this study, participants were willing to try the system when it was perceived as useful, but questioned whether it would be reliable, safe, and suitable for patients with severe hemiplegia. Therefore, acceptance of AI-assisted BADL rehabilitation appears to depend on the fit between patients’ functional abilities, system capabilities, and available human support, rather than perceived usefulness alone.19

Participants’ needs also indicate that system design should go beyond algorithmic recognition. Senadheera et al20 noted that AI may support functional assessment, individualized training, monitoring, and assistance in stroke rehabilitation, but real-world use remains limited by technology, cost, implementation settings, and ethical issues. In low-resource settings, limited access to suitable devices, stable internet connectivity, and technical support may further restrict implementation, highlighting the need for low-cost and easy-to-use system designs. Participants in this study emphasized ease of operation, slow video demonstrations, voice prompts, training records, feedback, rehabilitation information and access to consultation. These findings are consistent with previous research showing that poststroke e-rehabilitation needs center on content, usability, and accessibility,21 and that technology adoption is associated with perceived ease of use, usefulness, and stroke severity.22 Sustained engagement also requires feedback and progress monitoring, goal setting, reward reinforcement, and knowledge support.23 Thus, an AI-empowered BADL training system should be developed as a practical support tool combining training guidance, feedback, motivational support, safety alerts, and caregiver collaboration.

Role-related differences were also evident. Patients focused more on functional recovery, convenience, and active engagement, whereas caregivers emphasized safety, feasibility, reliability, and the potential to reduce caregiving burden. Similar differences have been reported in co-design research with poststroke patients and caregivers24 and in studies of robotic rehabilitation technologies.25 Future systems should therefore be designed for joint use by patients and caregivers, supporting patients’ active training while helping caregivers supervise training, monitor progress, and manage risks.26

Finally, participants viewed AI as a supplement to, rather than a substitute for, human support. Previous reviews also suggest that BADL training requires specialized task-oriented support and that telerehabilitation and robot-assisted training should supplement face-to-face rehabilitation.27,28 A human-AI collaboration model may therefore be most appropriate: AI provides demonstrations, reminders, records, preliminary feedback, and risk alerts; caregivers provide supervision and day-to-day safety support; and rehabilitation professionals develop training plans, conduct staged assessments, and provide remote guidance. This perspective is also consistent with the principles of human-centered artificial intelligence (HCAI), which emphasize that AI systems should be designed to enhance human capabilities while maintaining meaningful human involvement and responsibility.29 In rehabilitation contexts, HCAI highlights the importance of designing technologies around users’ needs, functional abilities, and real-world environments rather than focusing solely on technical performance.30 The findings of this study further suggest that AI-empowered BADL training systems should consider patients, caregivers, and healthcare professionals as key stakeholders throughout system development. By integrating AI-based assistance with professional guidance and family support, such systems may improve usability, trust, and sustained engagement while ensuring that human judgment and care remain central to rehabilitation. AI-generated feedback should support, rather than replace, professional clinical judgment, particularly when rehabilitation decisions involve patient safety. Future systems should also ensure data privacy and provide transparent explanations of algorithm-generated feedback. The value of AI lies in extending professional support into the home setting and improving the continuity, safety, and usability of BADL training.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. Participants were recruited from one tertiary hospital in China, and the findings may reflect local healthcare resources, cultural context, and levels of digital literacy. Patients were required to have the ability to use a smartphone at a basic level and sufficient reading comprehension. This eligibility requirement may have led to an underestimation of barriers among individuals with lower digital literacy. The demonstration video may have influenced participants’ expectations and responses by directing their attention to the functions shown in the video. In addition, this study explored anticipated needs before system development rather than long-term experiences after actual use. Because patients and caregivers were recruited separately and were not paired, interactions between patient and caregiver needs within the same family could not be fully examined. Future research should include usability testing and longitudinal home-based evaluation while also incorporating the perspectives of rehabilitation professionals.

Conclusions

Patients with poststroke hemiplegia and their primary caregivers were generally open to an artificial intelligence-empowered basic activities of daily living training system, provided that it is safe, reliable, easy to use, and matched to patients’ functional status. Future system development should be task-oriented and should incorporate caregiver support, multimodal feedback, safety assurance, and human-AI collaboration to improve the feasibility of home-based rehabilitation.
