Balancing CO₂ emissions, energy consumption, health expenditure: implications for life expectancy in GCC countries
Authors: Jawaher AlKuwaykibi, Eman Alanzi, Jawaher Binsuwadan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe complex interplay between environmental, economic, and health system factors shapes life expectancy in energy-intensive, resource-rich economies. The Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries provide a distinctive context characterised by rapid economic development, reliance on hydrocarbons, and significant investment in healthcare. However, the dynamic relationships between CO₂ emissions, energy consumption, healthcare expenditure, and population health outcomes remain insufficiently examined in the GCC context.MethodsThis study investigates the short- and long-run effects of CO₂ emissions, energy consumption, and healthcare expenditure on life expectancy across the six GCC countries over 2000–2024. The Cross-Sectionally Augmented Autoregressive Distributed Lag (CS-ARDL) model is applied to account for cross-sectional dependence and slope heterogeneity. Robustness is confirmed via the Augmented Mean Group (AMG) and Common Correlated Effects Mean Group (CCE-MG) estimators. Causal directions are examined using the Dumitrescu–Hurlin panel causality test.ResultsThe findings indicate that CO₂ emissions are positively associated with life expectancy in both the short and long run, whereas energy consumption is negatively associated with life expectancy. In the long run, the estimated coefficients are 0.019 for CO₂ emissions, − 0.011 for energy consumption, and 0.010 for healthcare expenditure. Healthcare expenditure remains positively associated with life expectancy, highlighting the importance of sustained investment in health systems. The causality analysis reveals bidirectional Granger causality between CO₂ emissions and life expectancy and unidirectional Granger causality from healthcare expenditure to life expectancy.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that GCC countries should strengthen environmental-health governance, improve the efficiency of healthcare expenditure, and reduce the health costs associated with fossil fuel-intensive energy systems. Given the absence of panel cointegration evidence, the long-run findings should be interpreted cautiously as associations rather than a confirmed equilibrium relationship.Keywords: Life expectancy, CO₂ emissions, Energy consumption, Health expenditure, Economic growth, Dynamic panel analysis, GCC countries

Introduction

Life expectancy is widely regarded as one of the most comprehensive indicators of population health and socioeconomic progress, encapsulating the cumulative effects of healthcare quality, environmental conditions, economic development, and social policy [1]. Over the past several decades, global life expectancy has risen substantially, driven by advances in medical technology, expanded access to healthcare services, and improvements in living standards [2, 3]. Nevertheless, significant disparities persist across and within regions, reflecting deep structural differences in economic development, environmental quality, and health system performance [1, 3]. Among the most rapidly transforming regions in the world, the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries present a particularly compelling case for analysis. These economies have undergone extraordinary economic expansion fuelled by hydrocarbon revenues; yet, this growth has been accompanied by surging energy consumption, rising carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions, and mounting environmental pressures [4–6].

Understanding the determinants of life expectancy in this unique context is therefore critical to both academic inquiry and evidence-based policymaking. Period life expectancy, defined as the number of years the average person born in a given year would live, provides a powerful snapshot of population health at a particular point [2]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, which tracks period life expectancy across all six GCC countries from 1950 to 2023, the region has undergone one of the most dramatic and sustained improvements in population health recorded globally. Over seven decades, all six GCC states experienced steep and near-continuous gains, converging toward the 80-year threshold by the early 2020s. The United Arab Emirates emerged as the regional leader, reaching approximately 84 years by 2023, followed closely by Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Oman, with Saudi Arabia recording the most dramatic long-run improvement. The similar health trends of all six GCC countries in recent years highlight how shared policies, joint healthcare investments, and common environmental challenges have influenced the health of their populations. This extraordinary transformation, however, also raises critical questions about sustainability: whether the health gains achieved over the past seven decades can be maintained and extended remains an empirically urgent question.

Despite notable improvements in health outcomes across GCC countries, a fundamental tension lies at the heart of their development model. The very economic activities that have generated wealth and financed healthcare systems are simultaneously contributing to environmental degradation that may undermine population health [6]. CO₂ emissions, a widely used proxy for environmental degradation, have risen sharply across GCC economies because of fossil fuel dependence in energy production, transportation, and industry [7, 8]. Empirical evidence on the health consequences of this trajectory, however, remains deeply contested. While a large body of literature documents a negative long-run relationship between CO₂ emissions and life expectancy [7–9], other studies report a positive short-run association, attributing this to the income and infrastructure gains that accompany industrialisation [10, 11]. Furthermore, the role of healthcare expenditure as a key policy lever in GCC countries, which have invested heavily in health infrastructure, remains incompletely understood in relation to environmental pressures [12].

The effect of economic growth on life expectancy is equally ambiguous: some studies confirm a positive Preston-curve relationship, while others demonstrate that growth alone, absent improvements in environmental quality and institutional efficiency, does not translate into better health outcomes [13]. A critical methodological limitation in the existing literature further compounds this empirical inconsistency. Many prior panel studies fail to account for cross-sectional dependence (CSD) among countries, a particularly salient issue in the GCC context, given the region’s deep economic integration, shared environmental challenges, and coordinated policy frameworks [6, 14, 15]. Ignoring CSD can yield biased and inconsistent estimates, thereby distorting both academic understanding and policy conclusions.

Against this backdrop, the existing literature reveals a significant research gap. While studies have examined the determinants of life expectancy in OECD countries [16, 17], Eastern Europe [14, 18], sub-Saharan Africa [19], and Asia [15], empirical evidence specific to the GCC remains limited and methodologically fragmented [6, 20]. Most GCC-focused studies either examine a narrow set of variables, rely on first-generation panel estimators that ignore cross-sectional dependence, or focus exclusively on long-run dynamics without capturing short-run adjustment processes. Moreover, comparative analyses of life expectancy determinants across GCC countries remain scarce [4]. This study addresses these gaps by employing a Cross-Sectionally Augmented Autoregressive Distributed Lag (CS-ARDL) framework, which explicitly accounts for cross-sectional dependence and heterogeneity across countries while simultaneously capturing both short- and long-run dynamics [21].

Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the short- and long-run effects of CO₂ emissions, energy consumption, healthcare expenditure, and GDP per capita on life expectancy across six GCC countries over the period 2000–2024. By employing the CS-ARDL model alongside second-generation unit root tests (CIPS and CADF) and the Dumitrescu–Hurlin panel causality test, this study seeks to provide robust empirical evidence on the dynamic interplay between environmental, economic, and health determinants of life expectancy in a resource-rich regional context. The findings are intended to offer actionable insights for policymakers navigating the dual imperatives of sustaining economic growth and improving public health outcomes, in alignment with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and national vision strategies such as Vision 2030 in Saudi Arabia [4, 22, 23].

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 provides a comprehensive review of the relevant theoretical and empirical literature on the determinants of life expectancy, with particular attention to CO₂ emissions, energy consumption and healthcare expenditure. Section 3 describes the data sources, variable definitions, and econometric methodology employed in the study. Section 4 presents and discusses empirical results, including diagnostic tests, CS-ARDL estimates, and causality findings. Section 5 concludes the paper by summarising the key findings, drawing policy implications, and identifying directions for future research.

Literature review

A growing body of literature has investigated the determinants of life expectancy across countries, with increasing attention given to environmental, energy-related, and health system factors. In particular, CO₂ emissions, energy consumption, healthcare expenditure, and economic growth have been widely examined due to their close association with environmental degradation and population health outcomes [6, 7, 9, 16, 20]. Several empirical studies have explored these relationships across diverse economic contexts, including advanced economies [11, 16, 24], developing regions [3, 7], and resource-rich countries [6, 20]. Despite the growing body of literature on this topic, empirical evidence remains limited for the GCC region, especially within frameworks that simultaneously analyse both short- and long-run dynamics while accounting for cross-sectional dependence [4].

Life expectancy and CO₂ emissions

Recent empirical literature demonstrates a broadly consistent inverse relationship between CO₂ emissions and life expectancy across different levels of economic development, although the magnitude and direction of effects vary by region, methodology, and time horizon. In advanced economies, L Aytemiz, G Sart, Y Bayar, M Danilina and FH Sezgin [11] analysed 32 OECD countries over the period 2005–2021 using second-generation cointegration tests and found that environmental degradation associated with higher emissions is negatively linked to long-run life expectancy indicators. Similarly, E Nica, A Poliakova, GH Popescu, K Valaskova, SG Burcea and A-LD Constantin [25] confirmed the adverse impact of CO₂ emissions on life expectancy in Eastern Europe and extended this analysis using CS-ARDL and quantile regression approaches. Finding that financial development, institutional quality, and energy mix also condition the relationship between CO₂ and life expectancy. U Murthy, MS Shaari, PA Mariadas and NZ Abidin [3] further demonstrated, using panel data across a cross-country sample, that CO₂ emissions and economic growth jointly shape life expectancy outcomes, emphasising the necessity to account for both environmental and income-related pathways. Recent studies have further emphasised the importance of energy transition and environmental innovation in reducing environmental degradation. For example, KR Abbasi, Q Zhang, I Ozturk, R Alvarado and M Musa [26] demonstrated that green innovation and energy transition contribute to lower CO₂ emissions in the long run, highlighting the importance of sustainable development strategies for achieving environmental objectives.

In developing and emerging economies, MK Mahalik, TH Le, HC Le and H Mallick [7] found that fossil fuel-based emissions significantly reduce life expectancy across 68 countries, while Mahalik provided more granular evidence by distinguishing between sources of carbon emissions and their differential effects on life expectancy, showing that the type of emission source matters for health outcomes. F Osei-Kusi, C Wu, S Tetteh and WIG Castillo [27] conducted a regional comparative analysis examining the dynamics of carbon emissions, energy, income, and life expectancy, confirming that carbon emissions exert a harmful long-run effect on health across multiple regional groupings. H Zhang, H Qiao, X Li, I Uddin, X Zhang and R Li [28] employed a CS-ARDL framework for 46 Asian countries and confirmed that higher CO₂ emissions reduce life expectancy in the long run. Similarly, P Kumar and M Radulescu [29] conducted a panel econometric analysis for 45 Sub-Saharan African countries and found that CO₂ emissions are negatively associated with life expectancy in the long run. Nevertheless, the panel techniques used in several of these studies could not fully account for econometric challenges such as endogeneity, heterogeneity, and cross-sectional dependence.

In the GCC context specifically, HH Abdulkadhim Altaee, F Rahim, K Dzhusupov and K Toguzbaeva [6] employed advanced econometric procedures for the period 1990–2020 and revealed that CO₂ emissions negatively affect life expectancy. Further examined the moderating role of energy consumption on the CO₂–income–urbanization–health nexus in GCC countries, providing additional region-specific evidence that is directly relevant to the present study. The results consistently reveal an inverse relationship between CO₂ emissions and life expectancy, while GDP per capita, urbanisation, and energy consumption are found to contribute positively to health outcomes. However, the study explicitly acknowledges the exclusion of health expenditure as a significant limitation that may introduce omitted variable bias. Based on the evidence reviewed above, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1.

CO₂ emissions have a significant negative impact on life expectancy in GCC countries in the long run.

Life expectancy and energy consumption

The relationship between energy consumption and life expectancy remains more ambiguous, with outcomes depending heavily on the type of energy used and the level of economic development. Recent literature has increasingly examined the impact of energy consumption on life expectancy across different regions. Empirical evidence from developing and emerging economies generally indicates that fossil fuel-based energy consumption negatively affects life expectancy, primarily due to environmental degradation and energy use inefficiencies. For instance, M Fahlevi, FY Rabhi and A Iskandar [15] found that Asian countries’ reliance on fossil fuels makes long-term improvements in life expectancy less likely. M Fahlevi, M Ahmad, ME Balbaa, T Wu and M Aljuaid [8] built on this by looking at how well petroleum-based and government health services affect life expectancy. They found that inefficiencies in hydroelectricity use make the negative health effects of fossil fuel dependence even worse. Similarly, employing PMG/ARDL estimation, BI Uzoechina, JO Ezekwike, GA Ekwoh, EA Eze, PC Imoagwu and JN Edeh [9] found a negative long-run effect of energy consumption on life expectancy in West African lower-middle-income countries, confirming that energy consumption patterns in lower-middle-income West African countries significantly impair life expectancy outcomes. However, the impact of energy consumption is not uniform across contexts.

Evidence from more diversified and rapidly developing economies suggests that the effect depends critically on the composition of energy sources. F Kaya, LC Voumik, M Rashid, S Akter, SH Khan and M Salehi [30] used panel cointegration methods for BRICS economies and concluded that non-renewable energy consumption is associated with weaker health outcomes compared to renewable energy, whereas renewable energy and GDP per capita positively determine longer life expectancy. This distinction between renewable and non-renewable energy sources has become increasingly important in the context of the global energy transition. KR Abbasi, M Shahbaz, J Zhang, M Irfan and R Alvarado [31] showed that fossil fuel energy intensifies environmental degradation, whereas renewable energy plays an important role in supporting environmental sustainability and reducing carbon emissions. These findings suggest that the health consequences of energy consumption may depend not only on the quantity of energy used but also on the composition of the energy mix [32].

Within the GCC context, the energy–health relationship is particularly critical due to the region’s heavy reliance on fossil fuels and high energy intensity., S Jarallah, AAB Wirayuda, M Alsamara, K Barkat, A Al-Mahrezi and MF Chan [20] investigated the nonlinear relationship between ecological footprint deficit and life expectancy across the six GCC countries from 2000 to 2020. Their findings reveal that a higher ecological deficit is significantly associated with lower life expectancy. Most critically, the study relies on first-generation panel unit root tests, which assume cross-sectional independence among panel units. To address this limitation, the present study employs second-generation CIPS and CADF unit root tests alongside the CS-ARDL framework. This framework explicitly corrects for cross-sectional dependence and allows for fully heterogeneous short-run and long-run coefficients across panel units. Based on the evidence reviewed above, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2.

Energy consumption has a significant negative impact on life expectancy in GCC countries, reflecting the adverse health consequences of fossil fuel dependence.

Life expectancy and health expenditure

A substantial body of prior research generally indicates a positive relationship between public health expenditure and life expectancy across different levels of economic development. In developed economies, L Aytemiz, G Sart, Y Bayar, M Danilina and FH Sezgin [11] examined OECD countries during 2005–2021 using second-generation cointegration tests and found that health expenditure is positively associated with life expectancy in the long run. Similarly, A Anwar, S Hyder, N Mohamed Nor and M Younis [16], applying panel FE/RE and system GMM models for OECD countries, reported that higher government health spending contributes to improving health outcomes and increasing life expectancy, while A Anwar, A Sinha, A Sharif, M Siddique, S Irshad, W Anwar and MN Malik [24], using a broader OECD sample and fixed-effects models, confirmed that government health expenditures are significantly and positively associated with better health outcomes. H Zhang, H Qiao, X Li, I Uddin, X Zhang and R Li [28] identified healthcare spending as the most influential long-run determinant of life expectancy across 46 Asian countries using CS-ARDL, while M Radmehr and TS Adebayo [33] confirmed, using MMQR, FMOLS, and DOLS for Mediterranean countries, that health expenditure consistently enhances life expectancy across all quantiles. D Zhang [12] added a novel dimension to this literature by examining the relationship between green taxation, public health expenditures, and life expectancy in China, finding that eco-friendly fiscal revenues directed toward healthcare generate significant longevity gains. This suggests that the quality and source of health financing matter alongside spending volumes.

In developing economies, the effectiveness of healthcare expenditure appears to depend on institutional quality and governance efficiency. J Boundioa and N Thiombiano [19] found, using a threshold model, that the positive impact of public health expenditure on life expectancy depends on governance quality, suggesting that institutional efficiency conditions the health returns to spending. O Saidmamatov, O Saidmamatov, Y Sobirov, P Marty, D Ruzmetov, T Berdiyorov, J Karimov, E Ibadullaev, U Matyakubov and J Day [10], found that government healthcare spending has a significant and beneficial effect on average life expectancy in the Aral Sea Basin economies, using FMOLS and DOLS. In the GCC context, AA ALI and MN SAYED [34] found that healthcare expenditure per capita was the only economic variable significantly and directly associated with higher life expectancy across GCC members, while also noting efficiency gaps, a finding that underscores the importance of increasing health spending and improving its allocation. Additionally, MA Köroğlu and Y Bayar [35] reported a positive effect of health expenditure on healthy life expectancy in sub-Saharan African countries. This analysis was expended by incorporating ICT alongside health expenditures and confirmed that both technology and spending jointly enhance healthy life expectancy in sub-Saharan Africa. H Zhang, H Qiao, X Li, I Uddin, X Zhang and R Li [28] Further demonstrated, using machine learning methods for high longevity countries, that healthcare access and spending quality, alongside environmental and socioeconomic factors, are among the most important predictors of life expectancy, reinforcing the multidimensional nature of health determinants. Based on the evidence reviewed above, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3.

Healthcare expenditure has a significant positive impact on life expectancy in GCC countries, consistent with the health investment hypothesis.

Theoretical framework

The theoretical foundation of this study is based on Grossman’s Health Capital Model M Grossman [36], which conceptualizes health as a form of capital that can be accumulated through investments such as healthcare services and improved living conditions. Within this framework, healthcare expenditure contributes to the accumulation of health capital and enhances life expectancy, whereas environmental degradation, represented by CO₂ emissions, may reduce health stock through its adverse effects on human health. Economic growth and energy consumption influence health outcomes through multiple channels, including income generation, healthcare accessibility, infrastructure development, and environmental conditions. Recent studies have continued to apply and extend this framework to examine the interactions between environmental factors, healthcare investment, and population health outcomes [10, 32, 37, 38].

Research gap and contribution

Overall, the existing literature demonstrates that life expectancy is shaped by a complex interaction of CO₂ emissions, energy consumption, healthcare expenditure, and economic growth, with considerable heterogeneity across regions, methodologies, and time horizons. Most studies agree that CO₂ emissions are bad for health [7, 9, 15] and that spending on healthcare is good for health [4, 16, 30]. However, there is still not enough evidence to say for sure what role energy use and economic growth play. Despite these advances, relatively few studies have jointly examined these determinants within the GCC context. This relationship may differ due to the region’s unique economic structure, heavy reliance on fossil fuels, and rapidly evolving healthcare systems. Furthermore, many previous panel studies fail to account for cross-sectional dependence, a particularly important issue in the GCC, where economic integration, shared environmental pressures, and coordinated policy frameworks create strong interdependencies among member states [24]. Ignoring CSD may lead to biased and inconsistent estimates, thereby distorting both academic inference and policy conclusions.

This study addresses these gaps and makes three main contributions to literature. First, it provides updated evidence on the determinants of life expectancy in GCC countries by jointly examining CO₂ emissions, energy consumption, healthcare expenditure, and economic growth within a unified framework. Second, it focuses on the GCC region, whose economic structure, dependence on fossil fuels, and healthcare systems differ substantially from those of other developing and developed economies. Third, the study applies second-generation panel data techniques that explicitly account for cross-sectional dependence and heterogeneity, thereby providing more reliable estimates than conventional panel approaches.

Methods and data

Data

This study investigates the environmental, economic, and health determinants of life expectancy across the six GCC countries: Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates, over the period 2000 to 2024. The selection of this sample is motivated by the region’s distinctive economic structure, characterised by heavy reliance on fossil fuel revenues, rapid economic expansion, and significant environmental pressures. This context provides a suitable setting to examine the interplay between environmental degradation and population health outcomes [6, 20].

The dependent variable is life expectancy at birth (Life), expressed in total years, which serves as a comprehensive measure of population health and socioeconomic well-being [1]. Three core explanatory variables are employed based on established theoretical frameworks and prior empirical evidence in the health economics and environmental economics literature. First, carbon dioxide emissions (CO₂), measured in total metric tons, are included as a proxy for environmental degradation. CO₂ emissions are among the most widely used indicators of environmental quality and have been consistently linked to adverse health outcomes and reduced life expectancy in the literature [6, 7, 28]. Second, energy consumption (ENE), measured in kilograms of oil equivalent per capita per $1,000 GDP is included to capture the health implications of fossil fuel dependence, which is particularly pronounced in GCC economies [15, 30]. Third, current health expenditure per capita (CHG), expressed in current US dollars, is included as a proxy for the quality and accessibility of healthcare systems, which are a key driver of life expectancy improvements across different income groups [7, 16]. In addition, GDP per capita (GDP), measured in constant 2015 US dollars, is included as a control variable to account for the level of economic development and its potential confounding influence on health outcomes through income, infrastructure, and public service channels [1]. By treating GDP as a control variable rather than a primary explanatory variable of interest, the study isolates the net effects of environmental and health-related factors on life expectancy, consistent with the approach adopted in recent panel health economics studies [7].

All variables are sourced from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators (WDI) database. To ensure comparability and facilitate the interpretation of estimated coefficients as elasticities, all variables are transformed into natural logarithmic form prior to estimation (33).The panel dataset comprises 150 observations. The study adopts a sequential econometric procedure that begins with pre-estimation diagnostic tests and proceeds to panel estimation and causality analysis. This stepwise approach is essential for ensuring the validity and robustness of empirical results.

The first step involves testing for cross-sectional dependence (CSD) among the panel units. In the context of the GCC, cross-sectional dependence is expected to be present given the region’s deep economic integration, shared oil price shocks, coordinated fiscal policies, and common environmental challenges [24]. Ignoring cross-sectional dependence in panel estimation leads to biased and inconsistent estimates and invalid inference. To formally test for CSD, the study employs the MH Pesaran [39] CD test, which evaluates the null hypothesis of cross-sectional independence against the alternative of cross-sectional dependence. Where is the sample estimate of the pairwise correlation of the residuals. Under the null hypothesis of no cross-sectional dependence, the CD statistic follows a standard normal distribution. The presence of CSD motivates the use of second-generation panel estimators that account for common factors.

Following the detection of cross-sectional dependence, the study employs second-generation panel unit root tests. The Cross-Sectionally Augmented Im-Pesaran-Shin (CIPS) test and the Cross-Sectionally Augmented Dickey-Fuller (CADF) test are applied proposed by MH Pesaran [40]. These tests are designed to account for cross-sectional dependence by augmenting the standard unit root regressions with the cross-sectional mean and its lagged value. The CIPS and CADF tests are preferred over first-generation unit root tests because the latter assume cross-sectional independence and produce size-distorted results in the presence of CSD. The null hypothesis of both tests is that the series contains a unit root (non-stationary), and the tests are applied at both the level and first-difference to determine the order of integration of each variable.

To examine whether a long-run equilibrium relationship exists among the variables, the study applies the J Westerlund [41] error correction model (ECM)-based panel cointegration test. This test offers several advantages over traditional cointegration tests as it allows for heterogeneous short-run dynamics across panel units. The null hypothesis of the Westerlund test is no cointegration, and rejection of the null indicates the presence of a long-run relationship among the variables. Given the mixed order of integration of the variables and the presence of cross-sectional dependence, the study employs the CS-ARDL model proposed by A Chudik and MH Pesaran [21] as the primary estimation framework. This approach is particularly appropriate for the present study because: it accommodates variables integrated of different orders, it allows for heterogeneous short-run and long-run coefficients across panel units, and it simultaneously estimates both short-run dynamics and long-run equilibrium relationships within a single error correction framework. In the model specification, CO₂ emissions, energy consumption, and health expenditure serves as the primary explanatory variables of interest, while GDP per capita is included as a control variable to account for the confounding effect of economic development on life expectancy. To verify the robustness of the CS-ARDL estimates, the study additionally employs two alternative heterogeneous panel estimators. The Augmented Mean Group (AMG) estimator proposed by M Eberhardt and S Bond [42] and the Common Correlated Effects Mean Group (CCE-MG) estimator proposed by MH Pesaran [43]. Both estimators account for cross-sectional dependence and slope heterogeneity by incorporating cross-section averages, and their consistency with the CS-ARDL results provides confidence in the reliability of the long-run estimates. Finally, to examine the direction of causality among the variables, the study employs the EI Dumitrescu and C Hurlin [44] panel causality test. This test extends the standard Granger causality framework to heterogeneous panels by allowing causal relationships to differ across cross-sectional units. The Dumitrescu–Hurlin test is particularly appropriate in the GCC context, where the causal dynamics between environmental, economic, and health variables may differ across member states. This is because of the differences in economic structure, energy mix, and health system maturity.

The choice of the CS-ARDL estimator is motivated by several characteristics of the dataset. First, the Pesaran CD test confirms the presence of cross-sectional dependence among GCC countries, reflecting common economic, environmental, and policy-related shocks. Second, the unit root results indicate a mixture of I(0) and I(1) variables, a setting for which the CS-ARDL framework is particularly suitable. Third, GCC economies are highly interconnected through common energy markets, oil price fluctuations, and coordinated development policies, making it important to control for unobserved common factors. Following A Chudik and MH Pesaran [21], the CS-ARDL estimator accounts for cross-sectional dependence and heterogeneity while providing estimates of both short-run dynamics and long-run associations.

Table 6 presents the results of the J Westerlund [41] ECM-based panel cointegration test. The bootstrapped p-values for all four statistics exceed the conventional 5% significance threshold, indicating that the null hypothesis of no cointegration cannot be rejected. These results suggest that the selected variables do not share a common long-run equilibrium relationship in the traditional cointegration sense. Nevertheless, the CS-ARDL framework remains appropriate given the presence of cross-sectional dependence, heterogeneous dynamics, and mixed integration orders among the variables. Moreover, the estimator has been widely applied in similar settings even when traditional panel cointegration evidence is weak or inconclusive. This is consistent with the approach adopted by MS Shaari, TB Majekodunmi, A Sulong, MA Esquivias and WS Yusoff [45], who similarly apply CS-ARDL in the absence of strong cointegration evidence.

Discussion

The empirical results of this study provide important insights into the short- and long-run determinants of life expectancy in GCC countries, with implications for both H1, H2, and H3 and for the broader literature on environmental and health economics. The CS-ARDL results show a positive and statistically significant association between CO₂ emissions and life expectancy in both the short and long run. From the perspective of Grossman’s Health Capital Model, environmental degradation is generally expected to reduce health capital by increasing exposure to pollution-related health risks. Therefore, the positive association observed in this study should not be interpreted as evidence that higher emissions improve health outcomes. Rather, it appears to reflect the unique socioeconomic and institutional characteristics of GCC countries.

GCC economies are characterized by substantial hydrocarbon revenues, extensive public investment, and considerable government expenditure on healthcare systems and social services. Unlike many developing economies, periods of rising emissions have often coincided with expansion in healthcare infrastructure, improved healthcare access, and higher living standards. Consequently, the positive association observed between CO₂ emissions and life expectancy may reflect the coexistence of economic development and healthcare improvements with high emission levels, rather than a direct beneficial effect of emissions on population health.

This finding, while seemingly counterintuitive, can be interpreted in light of the GCC’s specific developmental context. In these economies, high CO₂ emission levels have historically coexisted with substantial public investment, improvements in healthcare systems, and rising living standards in GCC economies. These factors may partially offset some of the adverse health consequences of environmental degradation and help explain the positive association observed between CO₂ emissions and life expectancy. MK Mahalik, TH Le, HC Le and H Mallick [7] similarly noted that in developing and emerging economies, the short-run relationship between emissions and health outcomes can be shaped by concurrent developmental processes, particularly when broader development processes coincide with improvements in healthcare access and public infrastructure.

HH Abdulkadhim Altaee, F Rahim, K Dzhusupov and K Toguzbaeva [6] also report mixed effects of CO₂ on life expectancy in the GCC and further demonstrated that energy consumption moderates the CO₂ health relationship in GCC countries. The bidirectional Granger causality between CO₂ and life expectancy suggests a dynamic predictive relationship between the two variables.as health conditions improve, population behavior and economic activity may also influence emission patterns. U Murthy, MS Shaari, PA Mariadas and NZ Abidin [3] similarly highlighted that CO₂ emissions and economic growth jointly shape life expectancy, suggesting that disentangling these effects requires careful econometric controls. These findings highlight the need for GCC policymakers to decouple economic development from fossil fuel dependence, as the long-run health costs of environmental degradation may eventually outweigh the developmental gains.

The negative and significant effect of energy consumption on life expectancy in both the short and long run, supports H2 and is consistent with the broader empirical literature. This finding is also consistent with the theoretical framework, which suggests that energy systems associated with environmental degradation may adversely affect health capital and ultimately reduce life expectancy. The GCC’s heavy reliance on fossil fuel-based energy systems generates substantial environmental pollution, which degrades air quality and contributes to respiratory and cardiovascular diseases that reduce life expectancy. M Fahlevi, FY Rabhi and A Iskandar [15] similarly found that fossil fuel dependence weakens long-run improvements in life expectancy in Asian economies. BI Uzoechina, JO Ezekwike, GA Ekwoh, EA Eze, PC Imoagwu and JN Edeh [9] confirmed a negative long-run effect of energy consumption in West African lower-middle-income countries.

This study provided additional evidence that energy consumption patterns in lower-middle-income West African countries significantly impair life expectancy outcomes. F Kaya, LC Voumik, M Rashid, S Akter, SH Khan and M Salehi [30] further demonstrated that non-renewable energy consumption is associated with weaker health outcomes compared to renewable energy. The GCC region presents a particularly relevant context for this relationship. Electricity generation remains heavily dependent on fossil fuels, while energy demand is intensified by energy-intensive industries, large-scale desalination activities, and extreme climatic conditions that require substantial cooling demand. These structural characteristics may amplify the adverse environmental and health consequences associated with energy consumption and help explain the negative association identified in this study In the GCC context, S Jarallah, AAB Wirayuda, M Alsamara, K Barkat, A Al-Mahrezi and MF Chan [20] found through nonlinear panel data analysis that ecological deficits significantly reduce life expectancy at higher thresholds, providing direct GCC-specific evidence of the energy–health nexus. The unidirectional causality running from life expectancy to energy consumption suggests that improvements in population health may reshape energy demand structures, potentially through increased productive capacity and behavioral changes. These findings collectively underscore the importance of accelerating energy transition in the GCC, consistent with regional sustainability agendas such as Saudi Vision 2030 and the UAE Net Zero 2050 strategy [49, 50].

The positive and statistically significant effect of healthcare expenditure on life expectancy in both the short and long run strongly supports H3 and is consistent with a large body of empirical evidence. This result aligns with Grossman’s Health Capital Model, which views healthcare expenditure as an investment in health capital that contributes to improved health outcomes and longer life expectancy. In the GCC specifically, AlSaied and AlAli [48] identify healthcare expenditure per capita as a key determinant of life expectancy, underscoring the importance of sustained health investment. Similar conclusions are drawn by M Radmehr and TS Adebayo [33] who, using MMQR, FMOLS, and DOLS for Mediterranean countries, found that health expenditure consistently enhances life expectancy across all quantiles. A Anwar, S Hyder, N Mohamed Nor and M Younis [16] and F Kaya, LC Voumik, M Rashid, S Akter, SH Khan and M Salehi [30] further documented that government health expenditures are significantly and positively associated with health outcomes and life expectancy in OECD country panels. H Zhang, H Qiao, X Li, I Uddin, X Zhang and R Li [28] employing machine learning methods for high longevity countries, identified healthcare access and expenditure quality as among the most important predictors of life expectancy.

The unidirectional Granger causality from CHG to life expectancy suggests that changes in healthcare expenditure precede changes in life expectancy, supporting the importance of healthcare investment in explaining longevity improvements., rather than merely a consequence of better health conditions. However, J Boundioa and N Thiombiano [19] cautioned that the health returns to expenditure are conditioned by governance quality and institutional efficiency, while MA Köroğlu and Y Bayar [35] highlighted that ICT adoption alongside health expenditure can further amplify the longevity benefits of health investment in developing countries. O Saidmamatov, O Saidmamatov, Y Sobirov, P Marty, D Ruzmetov, T Berdiyorov, J Karimov, E Ibadullaev, U Matyakubov and J Day [10] similarly emphasized that the beneficial effect of healthcare investment on life expectancy is most pronounced when spending is directed toward preventive care and infrastructure development. This finding further supports the theoretical expectation that investment in health capital contributes to improvements in population health and longevity over time.

Conclusion

This study examined the determinants of life expectancy in the six GCC countries over the period 2000–2024 using a panel econometric framework that accounts for cross-sectional dependence and heterogeneity. CO₂ emissions, energy consumption, and healthcare expenditure were analyzed as key determinants of life expectancy, while GDP per capita was included as a control variable. Given the absence of panel cointegration evidence, the reported long-run findings should be interpreted as long-run associations rather than a confirmed equilibrium relationship.

The findings provide several important insights. CO₂ emissions were positively associated with life expectancy, although this result should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect specific structural characteristics of GCC economies rather than a direct health benefit of higher emissions. Energy consumption was negatively associated with life expectancy, highlighting the potential health consequences of fossil fuel-intensive development patterns. Healthcare expenditure was positively associated with life expectancy in the baseline model, although its robustness varied across alternative estimators. GDP per capita remained statistically insignificant across all specifications, suggesting that economic growth alone may not be sufficient to improve population health outcomes.

The findings have important implications for policymakers in GCC countries. First, governments should strengthen environmental health governance by introducing stricter emission standards for energy-intensive sectors, expanding air-quality monitoring systems, and incorporating health impact assessments into major energy and infrastructure projects. Such measures would help reduce the adverse health consequences associated with environmental degradation while supporting sustainable development objectives. Second, healthcare expenditure should be directed toward interventions that generate the greatest public health benefits. Particular attention should be given to preventive healthcare services, early disease detection programs, chronic disease management, and pollution-related respiratory and cardiovascular health initiatives. Improving the efficiency and targeting of healthcare expenditure may yield greater improvements in life expectancy than simply increasing overall spending levels.

Third, environmental and health policies should be coordinated more closely. GCC governments may establish integrated monitoring frameworks that link environmental indicators with population health outcomes, enabling policymakers to identify environmental health risks more effectively and design evidence-based interventions. Such coordination would support both public health improvement and environmental sustainability goals.

This study is subject to several limitations. The analysis is limited to GCC countries and focuses on a selected set of determinants of life expectancy. Future research could incorporate additional factors such as institutional quality, income inequality, urbanization, renewable energy, and access to clean water. Future studies may also explore nonlinear relationships and threshold effects to better understand how environmental and health factors interact across different stages of development. Another limitation is that energy consumption is measured as an aggregate indicator and does not distinguish between renewable and non-renewable energy sources.

Given the ongoing energy transition across GCC countries, future research should examine the potentially different health effects of alternative energy sources and assess how renewable energy adoption influences life expectancy and population health outcomes. Such analysis may provide more policy-specific evidence regarding the health implications of the GCC’s transition toward cleaner energy systems. Finally, the positive association between CO₂ emissions and life expectancy observed in this study warrants further investigation. Future research should explore the potential roles of omitted variables, model specification issues, and reverse causality, while distinguishing short-term developmental influences from longer-term environmental health consequences in GCC economies.
