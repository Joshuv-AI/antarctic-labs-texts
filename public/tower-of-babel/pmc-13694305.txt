Targeted photodynamic therapy for colorectal cancer using antibody-conjugated green-synthesised nanoparticles
Authors: Fermin K. Broni, Blassan P. George, Heidi Abrahamse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major clinical challenge due to the persistence of epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) positive (+) cancer stem cells (CSCs), which drive tumour relapse, metastasis, and immune evasion. This review highlights a modular nanomedicine construct, EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa, designed for targeted photodynamic therapy (PDT) in CRC. By integrating EpCAM‑specific antibodies (EpAb2‑6), gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) synthesised through plant extracts, and photosensitiser (PS) such as pheophorbide‑a (PPBa). Upon near‑infrared activation, PPBa generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) characterised by calreticulin exposure, ATP release, and HMGB1 secretion. The system also incorporates surface‑enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) tags for real‑time imaging and circulating tumour cell (CTC) capture. Green synthesis provides biocompatibility and scalability, and antibody guidance enhances tumour specificity. By critically evaluating antibody selection, AuNPs synthesis strategies, and photosensitiser performance, this review situates EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa within the broader perspective of CRC nanotheranostics. Although rigorous in vivo validation and translational studies are necessary, the construct exemplifies a sustainable, multifunctional approach to precision oncology.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Cancer is a complex disease characterised by uncontrolled cell growth and division driven by genetic mutations that disrupt the normal balance of proliferation, differentiation, and cell death. These mutations allow cells to evade regulatory signals, leading to malignant transformation and tumour formation that can invade surrounding tissues and metastasis to distant organs [1–4]. Cancers are classified according to their site of origin, with common examples being the lung, breast, prostate, liver, cervical, and CRC. Among these, CRC is particularly significant, arising in the colon or rectum, often from precancerous polyps that gradually acquire malignant potential [4–6]. Clinical manifestations include rectal bleeding, altered bowel habits, abdominal pain, bloating, weight loss unexplained, vomiting, and fatigue [7–10]. Risk factors include smoking, alcohol consumption, poor diet, and physical inactivity, as well as metabolic conditions such as diabetes and hereditary predispositions [4]. Globally, CRC is the third most frequently diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality, accounting for approximately 1.8 million new cases and 900,000 deaths annually. The global burden is projected to rise to 2.5 million cases by 2035, with incidence remaining highest in high‑income countries but showing a steady increase in low‑ and middle‑income regions [7, 11, 12].

Despite advances in conventional treatment modalities, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy, CRC management remains challenging. Although these approaches improve survival rates, particularly in early-stage disease, they are limited by non-specificity, collateral damage to healthy tissues, and failure to eradicate CSCs, which drive recurrence and metastasis. Invasive procedures and treatment-associated toxicities compromise quality of life, emphasising the need for safer, minimally invasive alternatives [4, 8, 10]. In this regard, PDT has emerged as a complementary or an alternative treatment with promising clinical potentials. PDT is a light-based therapeutic modality that combines a PS, light of a specific wavelength, and molecular oxygen to generate ROS that can be applied strategically to selectively destroy cancer cells while sparing normal cells [13–15]. Upon activation, the PS undergoes excitation and intersystem crossing to a triplet state, initiating either a type I reaction, where electron or hydrogen could be transferred to solvents or biomolecules producing free radicals, or type II reactions, where energy is being transferred to ground-state oxygen yielding singlet oxygen [14, 15]. These generated ROS induce oxidative damage to cellular components, including plasma membranes, proteins, nucleic acids, and mitochondria, leading to apoptosis, necrosis, or autophagy of the irradiated cells or tumour [16–19]. Ultimately, PDT confines cytotoxicity to the illuminated region, ensuring spatial selectivity and minimal collateral damage [14].

Despite the fundamental potential of PDT, its effectiveness could be enhanced when combined with nanotechnology and antibody-based targeting. These combinations help deliver the treatment more precisely, reduce damage to healthy cells, and remove CSCs. EpCAM is a surface marker over express on CRC cells and CSCs, serving as therapeutic target for CRC [5]. CSCs potentially aid tumour growth, progression, and treatment resistance by regenerating themselves, developing lineage plasticity, and surviving conventional therapies. Within CRC, EpCAM⁺ populations usually co‑express stemness-associated biomarkers such as CD133 and CD44 on their surface, driving self‑renewal and differentiation potential that can be strategically exploited for therapeutic targeting [20]. So compared to bulk cells, CSCs have the capabilities to survive in unfavourable conditions and trigger changes known as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). These traits poses challenge to treatment of cancer cells and increase the chances of the cancer returning with poorer results [6]. EpCAM levels can change during low oxygen (hypoxia), inflammation, or treatment. It is controlled by signals like HIF-1α and Wnt/β-catenin. Part of EpCAM, called EpICD, can move into the cell nucleus and turn on genes like c-Myc and Cyclin D1 [21–23]. These genes help the tumour grow and spread [24]. Targeting EpCAM+ CSCs is important for better CRC treatment. Approaches that block EpCAM signals, control stem cell-related genes, and trigger immune-based cell death (ICD) could help improve therapy [20]. This study (review) presents a novel strategy for treating CRC using a targeted nanomedicine system or nanobioconjugate construct called EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa. The construct is discussed as a case study designed to attack both cancer cells and CSCs selectively through PDT. This approach enhances therapeutic precision by potentially inducing ICD and reducing harm to healthy tissues.

In this review, current body of literature and supporting studies is being synthesised to propose a modular nanomedicine construct, EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa, formed by integrating EpCAM-specific antibodies (EpAb2‑6), green-synthesised AuNPs, and PPBa for targeted PDT. In this design, EpAb2-6 helps guide the AuNPs to deliver treatment directly to the target cells [25, 26]. PPBa, a chlorophyll‑derived Ps, is conventionally classified as a second‑generation agent. However, as with many second‑generation PSs, integration into actively targeted nanomedicine constructs, enables their reclassification as a third‑generation PS. Activated in the near‑infrared region (approximately 650–800 nm), PPBa could potentially generates high levels of ROS and ICD [27, 28]. In CRC, PDT helps shrink tumours, damage blood vessels, and adjust immune responses. Yet, conventional PSs remain limited by poor solubility, short half-lie, and inadequate targeting capacity. Nanotechnology offers innovative solutions by enabling PS encapsulation within biocompatible carriers and facilitating active targeting through ligand conjugation to overcome these barrier. As such, AuNPs carry and protect PSs, and their surface plasmon resonance (SPR) boosts PDT and supporting photothermal therapy [29, 30]. Green nanotechnology offers eco-friendly synthesis of AuNPs. Plant extracts rich in polyphenols and flavonoids can reduce Au precursors or salts solution into stable nanoparticles (NPs). Species such as Citrus reticulata and Salvia rosmarinus (rosemary) have been widely utilised to generate biocompatible AuNPs with potential for molecular conjugation. These phytogenic NPs not only enhance therapeutic effectiveness but also demonstrated reduced environmental toxicity compared to conventionally synthesised counterparts emphasising their value in sustainability nanomedicine, and when combined with PPBa and antibodies that target EpCAM, they enabled precise drug delivery, amplify ROS generation, minimise off-target effect and collectively position the EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa construct as a promising PDT platform for CRC [31]. Sustainable EpCAM-based nano-theranostic in CRC

Although CRC has traditionally been regarded as a disease of the elderly, its incidence and mortality are rising significantly among adults under 50 years, who are increasingly diagnosed at advanced stages. Alarmingly, younger populations are being affected, yet many still lack access to adequate screening, particularly in low‑resource settings [4, 7, 11]. Early clinical manifestations are often delayed, and initial signs are nonspecific, frequently mimicking other common conditions such as malaria, typhoid, or gastrointestinal infections. Consequently, CRC is often missed at the onset of disease. Symptoms including anaemia, constipation, and unexplained weight loss typically present late, especially in right‑sided tumours, leading to delayed diagnosis and poorer clinical outcomes [6–8]. Standard diagnostic approaches for CRC, including colonoscopy, fecal immunochemical test (FIT) [32, 33], fecal occult blood test (FOBT), imaging modalities, and blood‑based markers, remain essential but are limited in sensitivity and specificity [34, 35]. Colonoscopy, although the gold standard, is invasive and may miss flat or right‑sided lesions. FIT and FOBT are non‑invasive but demonstrate variable sensitivity and can fail to detect advanced precursor lesions. Blood‑based markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) lack specificity and are inadequate for early detection [22, 34]. Liquid biopsies, which analyse circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) or circulating tumour cells (CTCs), represent a promising minimally invasive alternative [35–37]; however, their sensitivity is reduced in patients with small tumour burden or early‑stage disease, restricting their utility for early diagnosis [10]. Standard treatment modalities for CRC face significant limitations. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of therapy but is less effective once the disease has metastasised and does not eradicate EpCAM‑positive CSCs, which drive recurrence and resistance. Chemotherapy, although widely used, is associated with systemic toxicity, adverse side effects, and the emergence of multidrug resistance. Radiotherapy, while effective for local control, often lacks precision and can damage surrounding healthy tissues. Targeted therapies provide benefit only in patients harbouring specific genetic mutations, limiting their applicability across diverse CRC populations [8, 38–41]. These challenges emphasise the urgent need for advanced therapeutic strategies that combine precision targeting, reduced collateral damage, and real‑time monitoring. Theranostics integrating diagnostic and therapeutic functions within a single platform offer such potential by enabling personalised treatment, selective tumour ablation, and longitudinal tracking of therapeutic response [38]. Table 2 compares conventional theranostic methods with emerging nano‑theranostic approaches, situating CRC within broader translational advances. By situating CRC alongside advances in prostate, breast, and brain cancer theranostics, Table 2 reinforces the argument that EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa represents a logical extension of these multifunctional strategies into CRC. Nano-theranostic offer better accuracy, stronger effects, and more targeted delivery. In cancer care, examples include PSMA-based tools for prostate cancer, HER2-linked treatments for breast cancer, and iron oxide NPs for brain tumours. In CRC, markers like CEA, EpCAM, and KRAS help guide both imaging and treatment [38, 42–45]. These nano-systems can respond to the tumour environment and include options like Fe₃O₄-based ferroptotic therapy, quantum dots, and gold-liposome hybrids [46]. The construct, EpAb2-6-AuNP-PPBa, potentially could offer multifunctional approaches in this regards for PDT [47]. This system enables lesion imaging, ROS-mediated cell ablation, and reduced off-target effects, thereby supporting sustainable and precise nano-theranostics for CRC management [48].

Molecular drivers and resistance mechanisms in CRC

The progression of CRC is driven by alterations in key signalling pathways that not only sustain tumour growth but also influence therapeutic resistance, particularly in the context of PDT. The Wnt/β‑catenin pathway, which is frequently activated by mutations in APC, β‑catenin, or AXIN, promotes stemness by switching on oncogenes such as MYC and CCND1. These changes reinforce the survival of EpCAM⁺ cancer stem cells, which are notoriously resistant to conventional therapies and complicate PDT efficacy. Similarly, the PI3K/AKT pathway, which is often activated by PIK3CA mutations or PTEN loss, enhances metabolic adaptation and survival, further supporting EpCAM‑driven stem‑like traits. These molecular drivers establish a rationale for antibody‑guided nanomedicine, as constructs such as EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa can selectively target EpCAM⁺ niches while delivering ROS to disrupt these signalling cascades [49]. TGF-β acts as a tumor suppressor early on but later promotes invasion and immune evasion through EMT and matrix remodeling [49]. EGFR signalling helps cancer cells grow and move by activating the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways. Drugs like cetuximab target EGFR, but they don’t work well in tumours with KRAS mutations. Another receptor, c-Met, boosts cell movement, blood vessel growth, and stem-like traits. When active, it supports cancer spread and treatment resistance by linking with EGFR and β-catenin. These pathways influence how CRC develops and responds to therapy. EpCAM-targeted systems like EpAb2-6-AuNPs-PPBa offer precise control when used with photodynamic therapy [23]. CRC grows due to both the behaviour of tumour cells and signals from the surrounding tumour microenvironment (TME). Important factors include low oxygen (hypoxia), cell changes (EMT), immune cells called tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), and conditions that support stem-like cells. Hypoxia happens when blood supply is poor and energy demand is high, causing HIF-1α to become stable. This leads to new blood vessel growth, EMT, and changes in cell metabolism. These effects raise EpCAM levels, CSCs, and make oxygen-based treatments like PDT less effective [14, 50]. EMT makes tumours more aggressive by reducing E-cadherin and increasing vimentin and N-cadherin. This boosts cell movement and drug resistance. EMT also supports stem-like traits and helps cancer cells avoid the immune system, often triggered by TGF-β and inflammation. TAMs, usually in a pro-tumour M2 state, release IL-10, TGF-β, and enzymes that drive EMT, weaken immune defenses, and reshape the tumour environment [3, 24]. These factors create a supportive environment that protects CSCs and makes treatment less effective. CSCs are identified by markers like EpCAM, CD133, and CD44. EpCAM is linked to worse outcomes and turns on genes that support stem-like traits through EpICD. CD133 marks slow-growing cells that survive in low oxygen and can form tumours. CD44 is found on invasive cells prone to EMT and activates PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin pathways. Theranostic systems like EpAb2-6-AuNPs-PPBa target EpCAM⁺ CSCs, deliver photodynamic therapy (PDT), and adjust the tumour environment to help prevent or reduce cancer recurrence [24, 49].

EpCAM in CRC and stem cell niches

EpCAM is a transmembrane protein with three regions: EpEX on the outside, a middle membrane section, and EpICD on the inside. It was first known for helping cells stick together, but in colorectal cancer (CRC), it also acts as a signalling molecule, especially in CSC zones. In tumours, enzymes like TACE and γ-secretase cut EpEX in a process called regulated intramembrane proteolysis (RIP). This releases EpICD, which enters the nucleus and joins with FHL2, β-catenin, and Lef-1. These complexes switch on genes like c-MYC and Cyclin D1, encouraging cell growth and stem-like traits [51]. EpCAM activates key signalling pathways like Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT, helping CSCs survive and resist treatment. It also interacts with PD-L1, EGFR, and c-Met to support immune evasion, EMT, and cancer spread. Cytokines such as interferon-gamma raises EpCAM levels, while EpICD increases PD-L1 production, creating a feedback loop that weakens immune defenses. EpCAM expression is controlled in several ways: by Wnt and Notch at the gene level, by glycosylation and cleavage after translation, and by microRNAs like miR-122 and miR-200c at the epigenetic level. Figure 1 shows EpCAM’s structure and signalling roles, while Table 3 outlines EpCAM’s role in CRC progression, reinforcing its importance as a therapeutic target. The linking of EpCAM’s role across tumour stages underscores why EpAb2‑6 targeting is central to disrupting CSC niches and overcoming resistance mechanisms in CRC. These features highlight how tumours adapt and survive [49]. Theranostic constructs like EpAb2-6-AuNPs-PPBa attach to the EpEX domain, deliver photodynamic therapy (PDT), and produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) to block EpCAM-related signalling. They also help counter immune evasion by disrupting EpCAM-HGFR interactions and improving PDT performance. Because EpCAM influences both cell adhesion and gene activation, it stands out as a valuable target for personalised, multi-modal treatment of CRC [5].

Comparative evaluation of EpCAM antibodies in CRC

While EpAb2‑6 has been highlighted in this review for its specificity toward the EpEX domain of EpCAM, it is important to situate this clone within the broader landscape of EpCAM‑targeting antibodies. HEA125, one of the earliest monoclonal antibodies developed against EpCAM, has been extensively validated in diagnostic immunohistochemistry and remains a benchmark for clinical detection of EpCAM expression. Similarly, MOC31 has been widely applied in pathology and therapeutic research, demonstrating robust affinity and reproducibility across multiple epithelial malignancies. In contrast, EpAb2‑6 is a relatively newer clone, with promising preclinical data in colorectal cancer but limited translational validation compared to HEA125 and MOC31. Beyond full‑length monoclonal antibodies, engineered formats such as single‑chain variable fragments (scFvs) and bispecific antibodies have been explored to enhance tumour penetration, reduce immunogenicity, and improve therapeutic precision [52–55]. These alternatives highlight both the opportunities and challenges of antibody selection for EpCAM‑targeted nanomedicine [56–59].

By critically comparing EpAb2‑6 with established clones and engineered derivatives, it becomes clear that while EpAb2‑6 offers conceptual advantages for CRC‑specific targeting, its clinical readiness remains less established. Integrating insights from validated antibodies such as HEA125 and MOC31, alongside emerging scFv formats, provides a more balanced perspective and emphasis the need for rigorous comparative studies before advancing EpAb2‑6‑based constructs toward translational application [60–62].

Green nanotechnology and AuNPs platforms

Green nanotechnology represents an innovative approach in NP design, emphasising biocompatibility, sustainability, and ecological responsibility, employs green synthesis employs natural polymers, plant‑derived surfactants, and renewable biological precursors under mild, low‑energy conditions [31, 63, 64]. Alginate, pectin, and cellulose provide biodegradable structures, whereas saponins and lecithin stabilise nanoemulsions without the use of synthetic emulsifiers. Ultrasonication, microwave irradiation, and cold homogenisation techniques further reduce toxic residues and carbon traces [65–67], aligning nanocarrier development with global sustainability goals [38]. The biological and biopolymer routes are central to this approach. Plant‑mediated synthesis harnesses phytochemicals as reducing and stabilising agents, with agrowaste sources, such as fruit peels and leaves, supporting circular bioeconomy principles [68, 69]. Microbial systems, including bacteria, fungi, and yeasts, biosynthesise NPs through enzymatic reduction, offering renewable and scalable alternatives. Casein, albumin, zein, chitosan, and alginate proteins and polysaccharides act as structures and stabilisers, enabling NP formation under mild aqueous conditions and enhancing regulatory acceptance [70, 71]. Agro‑waste valorisation further strengthens sustainability by converting reducing sugar- and polyphenol-rich biomass into NP precursors, reducing landfill burden and supporting waste‑to‑value strategies [68, 69, 72]. Hybrid approaches [73–75] that combine plant extracts, microbial systems, and biopolymers yield multifunctional nanocarriers with improved stability and stimuli‑responsive behaviour, such as pH‑ or enzyme‑triggered release [76]. NPs have emerged as versatile imaging and therapy platforms in the field of CRC research. Their small size (1–100 nm) enables efficient tumour penetration, whereas surface functionalisation with antibodies or ligands enhances targeting precision [77, 78]. Gold, silver, carbon, iron oxide, silica, titanium dioxide, and copper oxide NPs have all been explored, each offering unique therapeutic or diagnostic advantages. Gold and silver NPs absorb near‑infrared light, supporting imaging and photothermal therapy, while iron oxide NPs can be guided magnetically [79–81]. Passive targeting exploits the EPR effect [82] as active targeting employs ligands, such as EpCAM antibodies, to selectively bind tumour cells [81]. AuNPs are particularly attractive owing to their strong SPR, which amplifies light‑based therapies, and their ability to carry drugs and antibodies while shielding therapeutic payloads. Plants such as Citrus and rosemary contains phytochemicals, including flavonoids (luteolin and nobiletin), phenolic acids (rosmarinic acid), terpenoids, essential oils, and vitamin C, act as both reducing and stabilising agents, preventing aggregation and enhancing colloidal stability [68, 83–86]. Beyond NP formation, these compounds exhibit antioxidant properties that synergise with Ps, improving the efficacy of PDT [87]. Medicinal plants native to South Africa, such as Dicoma anomala, Cyclopia intermedia, and Hypoxis hemerocallidea, further enrich green synthesis strategies [87, 88]. Sesquiterpene lactones (SLs) from these species act as natural chelating and stabilising agents during AuNPs formation, while also exerting anticancer effects by modulating NF‑κB signalling, oxidative stress pathways, and tumour‑associated macrophage activity [88–90]. Residual bioactive compounds from these extracts may enhance cytotoxicity [91, 92] against EpCAM⁺ CSCs, positioning green‑synthesised AuNPs as promising theranostic platforms for CRC [31]. Constructs such as EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa potentially exemplify the integration of green synthesis with targeted nanomedicine. By combining EpCAM‑specific antibodies with phytogenic AuNPs and PPBa, these systems could potentially achieve precise tumour targeting, deep‑tissue activation, and immune modulation. Comparative evaluation of green versus chemically synthesised AuNPs

Conventionally, AuNPs have been produced through chemical reduction methods, such as citrate or thiol reduction, which allow precise control over particle size, shape, and reproducibility. These approaches are widely validated in biomedical research and provide consistent physicochemical properties essential for translational applications. However, chemical synthesis often requires toxic reagents, generates hazardous byproducts, and may leave residual surfactants or stabilisers that compromise biocompatibility and raise environmental concerns [93–95]. In contrast, green synthesis employs phytochemicals from plant extracts (e.g., flavonoids, terpenoids, phenolic acids) as reducing and stabilising agents. This eco‑friendly approach offers several advantages: improved biocompatibility, reduced toxicity, and sustainability through the use of renewable resources. Green‑synthesised AuNPs also exhibit synergistic biological activity, as residual phytochemicals can enhance antioxidant or anticancer effects. Importantly, this strategy aligns with global health equity goals by enabling low‑cost, scalable production in resource‑limited settings [63, 64, 66]. Nonetheless, green synthesis faces challenges in batch‑to‑batch reproducibility, as plant extract composition varies with species, season, and extraction conditions. Achieving uniform particle size distribution and surface chemistry remains more difficult compared to chemical methods. Furthermore, regulatory translation requires rigorous standardisation and GMP compliance, which are currently better established for chemically synthesised AuNPs [31, 96, 97]. By critically contrasting these approaches, it becomes clear that while green synthesis offers sustainability and biocompatibility advantages, chemical synthesis remains the benchmark for reproducibility and clinical readiness. Integrating green methods with standardised protocols and hybrid approaches may provide a balanced pathway toward translational application of AuNPs‑based nanomedicine in CRC [66, 98, 99].

Nanotoxicology considerations in green AuNPs platforms

Nanocarriers enhance the solubility, stability, and targeted delivery of therapeutic agents; however, their physicochemical properties can also introduce biological risks. Nanotoxicology remains a critical checkpoint in translational development even when AuNPs are produced using green synthesis [100]. The safety of constructs such as EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa depends not only on the eco‑friendly synthesis route but also on the NP size, surface chemistry, and dosage. Potential toxicity may arise from non-specific cellular uptake, oxidative stress through ROS generation, or unintended interactions with serum proteins, which can compromise biocompatibility and damage healthy cells [101]. Biodistribution and clearance patterns must also be carefully assessed, as AuNPs often accumulate in clearance organs such as the liver and spleen, raising concerns about long‑term retention [100, 102]. Immunogenicity is another consideration because if the orientation or surface modification is suboptimal, antibody-NP conjugates may trigger inflammatory responses [13, 103]. Mitigation strategies are available to address these risks. PEGylation reduces opsonisation and prolongs circulation, while site‑directed antibody conjugation preserves antigen recognition and minimises off‑target binding. The controlled release of Ps, such as PPBa, enhances tumour specificity, and green synthesis itself reduces the reliance on toxic reagents, improving overall biocompatibility. Surface coatings with biocompatible polymers (e.g., chitosan and dextran) further stabilise AuNPs and mitigate ROS‑associated cytotoxicity. In the context of EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa, nanotoxicology studies provide the foundation for translational readiness. Defining safe NP concentrations, optimising surface functionalization, and integrating protective design features are essential for balancing therapeutic efficacy with minimal collateral damage [101, 102, 104]. With these safeguards in place, green AuNPs platforms can advance as sustainable and clinically viable nanotheranostic systems for CRC treatment.

Synthesis and conjugation strategies

The translational potential of nanoconstruct relies on reproducible synthesis, robust conjugation chemistry, and thorough physicochemical characterisation. AuNPs can be synthesise using phytochemicals derived from plants parts. Flavonoids, such as luteolin and nobiletin, and phenolic acids, such as rosmarinic acid, reduce Au³⁺ ions to elemental gold, whereas terpenoids and polyphenols stabilise the colloid and prevent aggregation. However, variability in plant extract composition introduces batch‑to‑batch differences in particle size and surface chemistry, highlighting the need for GMP‑compliant standardisation and advanced analytical profiling to ensure reproducibility [26, 86, 95]. Following synthesis, antibody conjugation strategies can be employed to functionalise the AuNPs with EpAb2‑6 monoclonal antibodies, thereby conferring EpCAM specificity. Generally, three broad approaches exist for antibody-NP conjugation [94, 105–107]. These includes adsorption, covalent binding, and adapter molecule‑mediated attachment. Adsorption relies on non‑covalent interactions such as hydrophobic forces, van der Waals contacts, hydrogen bonding, or electrostatic attraction [94, 108, 109]. Although simple and rapid, adsorption suffers from poor stability, as antibodies may detach or lose activity under physiological conditions. Covalent binding provides a stronger and more reproducible attachment. Common chemistries include carbodiimide coupling (EDC/NHS), which links carboxyl groups on nanoparticle surfaces to amine groups on antibodies; maleimide coupling, which exploits thiol groups to form stable thioether bonds; and click chemistry, which enables site‑specific conjugation through azide–alkyne cycloaddition or Diels–Alder reactions [106, 107, 110, 111]. These methods preserve antibody activity more effectively but can introduce challenges such as random orientation or the need for additional purification. To address orientation, Fc‑specific linkers or glycan‑directed strategies may be employed to ensure that the antigen‑recognising Fab domains remain accessible [55, 82, 107, 112, 113]. Adapter molecule strategies offer another solution, enabling oriented immobilisation through biotin–avidin or Fc‑binding proteins (Protein A/G). Although these approaches maintain antibody recognition capacity and reduce the risk of antigen‑binding site blockage, they add complexity and, in some cases, reversibility. For EpAb2‑6 conjugation to AuNPs, covalent methods are generally preferred due to their stability and reproducibility. Carbodiimide and maleimide chemistries provide robust attachment, whereas click chemistry offers site‑specific immobilisation that preserves antigen recognition [114–116]. Adapter molecule approaches, such as biotinylated EpAb2‑6 binding to streptavidin‑functionalised AuNPs or Protein A/G‑mediated Fc attachment, can further improve orientation. PEGylation may also be incorporated to extend circulation time and reduce non-specific binding, thereby enhancing therapeutic precision [55, 117, 118]. In summary, while adsorption offers simplicity, covalent conjugation and adapter molecule strategies are more effective for EpAb2‑6 functionalisation of AuNPs, as they can ensure stability, correct antibody orientation and preserved antigen recognition. These features are critical for achieving precise EpCAM‑targeted delivery of PPBa in colorectal cancer nanotheranostics.

Nano-encapsulation methods

Encapsulation is central to nanocarrier design, enabling the protection, stabilisation, and controlled delivery of bioactive compounds. By entrapping or associating payloads within protective matrices, encapsulation shields them from degradation and allows site‑specific release. Generally, strategies are grouped into physical entrapment, chemical conjugation, and surface adsorption, with each suited to different payloads and therapeutic goals [73, 83, 119]. In physical entrapment, liposomes, nanoemulsions, and solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) enclose hydrophilic or lipophilic compounds, improving their solubility and bioavailability [119]. While effective, they often suffer from leakage or burst release, limiting the sustained localisation of Ps. In chemical conjugation, covalent or ionic bonding attaches payloads to NP surfaces, allowing precise release and targeting control. Polymeric carriers and dendrimers exemplify this approach [120]. AuNPs are particularly valuable in this study because their surface chemistry supports robust conjugation of antibodies and Ps, such as PPBa, enhancing tumour selectivity and spatial precision. For surface adsorption, electrostatic or hydrogen bonding immobilises bioactives on porous surfaces, such as mesoporous silica. This enables high loading but risks premature desorption under physiological conditions [73, 120]. Encapsulation protects sensitive molecules from heat, light, oxygen, and enzymatic degradation, preserves therapeutic activity, and improves solubility. Stimuli‑responsive carriers (pH, enzyme, and redox) enable site‑specific release, thereby reducing systemic side effects and enhancing PDT precision. Lipid systems may release payloads too rapidly; polymeric and silica carriers have often limited loading capacity. Stability issues, such as leakage, aggregation, or hydrolysis, compromise the shelf life, while encapsulation technique scalability remains a barrier [73, 83, 119]. Additional challenges for AuNPs–PPBa systems include antibody orientation on AuNPs surfaces, variability in green synthesis due to phytochemical heterogeneity, and inconsistent payload release under physiological conditions. No single method is universally optimal. Hybrid systems such as biopolymer‑coated lipids or organic-inorganic composites, combine complementary strengths to improve stability, biocompatibility, and controlled release. For AuNPs-PPBa constructs, balancing stability, tumour specificity, and reproducibility will be critical. Addressing antibody orientation, phytochemical variability, and translational validation through site‑directed conjugation, GMP-compliant synthesis, and advanced analytical profiling will be essential for realising their potential as clinically viable nanotheranostic platforms.

AuNPs loaded PPBa and confirmation techniques

PPBa is loaded onto the AuNPs surface either through hydrophobic interactions or covalent ester and amide linkages. Loading efficiency is quantified by UV–Vis spectroscopy, while stability is assessed under physiological conditions such as serum at pH 7.4. Stimuli‑responsive linkers, which cleave under acidic tumour microenvironments or upon light activation, enable controlled release and ensure selective ROS generation within EpCAM⁺ CSCs [60, 83, 87, 121]. To support real‑time imaging, surface‑enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) tags are integrated into the construct. Raman‑active molecules such as 4‑mercaptobenzoic acid are immobilised on the AuNPs surface prior to antibody and PPBa conjugation, allowing simultaneous therapeutic and diagnostic functionality [78, 122–124]. Comprehensive physicochemical analysis is essential to validate the construct. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) are employed to determine size and morphology, while zeta potential measurements confirm colloidal stability. UV–Vis and FTIR spectroscopy verify PPBa loading and antibody conjugation, and Raman spectroscopy validates SERS tag integration. Biological assays further assess in vitro stability in serum, cytotoxicity against EpCAM⁺ CRC cells, and immunogenicity profiling [78, 107, 123, 125–127]. Despite these advances, several challenges remain. Antibody orientation and stability in serum may compromise targeting efficiency, while off‑target PDT activation due to light scattering must be carefully controlled. Immunogenicity risks associated with antibody–nanoparticle conjugates require thorough profiling, and batch variability in green synthesis emphasises the need for GMP‑compliant standardisation before clinical translation. Addressing these limitations will be critical to advancing EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa from conceptual design toward practical application in colorectal cancer therapy [15, 112, 125, 128, 129].

Raman spectroscopy and SERS tag integration

As an emerging powerful analytical tool, SERS amplify weak Raman signals at metallic NP surfaces through plasmonic resonance (PR). SERS tags provide both diagnostic and validation functions by enabling real‑time monitoring of NP distribution and confirmation of successful nano-construct design. Vibrational features from the reporter molecules directly demonstrates stable tag attachment and construct integrity. Strategies for attaching SERS tags to AuNPs include direct adsorption of Raman reporter molecules, such as 4‑mercaptobenzoic acid or rhodamine derivatives, onto the AuNPs surface through strong Au–S bonds [122, 126]. This method yields intense Raman signals but may suffer from desorption in biological environments, necessitating protective coatings. More robust alternative is covalent conjugation, where functionalised dyes are linked to AuNPs surfaces through EDC/NHS or maleimide chemistry [124, 126]. These covalent strategies enhance stability under physiological conditions and reduce signal loss risk. Encapsulation techniques provide another layer of reliability. Raman reporters can be embedded within silica shells or polymer coatings surrounding AuNPs, which not only prevents leaching but also improves biocompatibility and allows simultaneous functionalisation with targeting antibodies or Ps. Hybrid dual‑functionalisation strategies are also common, where NPs are first labelled with Raman reporters and subsequently conjugated with antibodies or PPBa. In such cases, careful control of surface chemistry is essential to avoid steric hindrance or masking of antigen‑binding sites. Spacer molecules, such as PEG, are often introduced to balance the biological functionality of strong SERS signal intensity. Raman spectroscopy validates these attachment strategies by identifying distinct vibrational peaks associated with the reporter molecules, monitoring signal intensity to confirm plasmonic amplification, and assessing stability under conditions that mimic biological environments [122, 126]. The persistence of spectral signatures in serum or under laser irradiation after incubation indicates robust tag integration. Furthermore, the use of multiple Raman reporters with unique spectral fingerprints enables multiplexed detection, allowing the simultaneous tracking of different constructs or functional states. For EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa, the incorporation of SERS tags enhances the theranostic potential of the system. Beyond confirming successful conjugation, these tags provide real‑time imaging capabilities and support CTC capture. Encapsulation or PEG‑spaced attachment strategies are particularly advantageous because they maintain strong Raman signals while preserving antibody orientation and PS activity. Raman spectroscopy not only validates the construct at the molecular level but can also strengthens its translational readiness by integrating diagnostic and therapeutic functions without compromise [123, 124, 126].

Comparative evaluation of Ps in CRC PDT

Photosensitisers (PSs) have evolved across three generations, each addressing limitations of its predecessors. First‑generation agents, such as haematoporphyrin derivatives, suffered from poor selectivity and prolonged photosensitivity. Second‑generation PSs, including Chlorin e6 and Zn‑phthalocyanine, improved ROS yield and photostability but faced challenges with solubility, biodistribution, and tumour selectivity. Chlorin e6, for example, has been widely studied in gastrointestinal cancers and demonstrates strong ROS generation, yet its rapid clearance and limited tumour penetration reduce therapeutic efficacy. Zn‑phthalocyanine offers high photostability and deep tissue activation, but its hydrophobicity leads to aggregation and poor bioavailability, necessitating complex carrier systems [136–138]. PPBa, a chlorophyll‑derived photosensitiser, has emerged as a promising candidate for PDT. Activated in NIR range, it enables deeper tissue penetration and efficient generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). PPBa also triggers ICD, evidenced by calreticulin exposure and ATP release, highlighting its potential in immune‑modulating PDT strategies. Conjugation with nanocarriers such as AuNPs further enhances its solubility, stability, and tumour selectivity, thereby reducing off‑target toxicity. Despite these advantages, PPBa remains less extensively validated in CRC models compared to well‑studied Ps like Chlorin e6 and Zn‑phthalocyanine, and its translational potential requires further in vivo and clinical investigation [77, 139–141]. By critically contrasting PPBa with established second‑generation PSs, it becomes clear that PPBa offers conceptual advantages in terms of NIR activation, ICD potential, and compatibility with nanocarrier systems. Yet, the limited CRC‑specific validation emphasis the need for comparative preclinical studies to benchmark PPBa against Chlorin e6 and Zn‑phthalocyanine before advancing toward clinical translation [142–145].

Conclusion, limitations and prospects

The EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa construct, presented as case study, exemplifies how antibody guided NP-mediated PDT or particularly anti-EpCAM nanobioconjugate mediated-PDT could be design as nano-theranostic platform for CRC management. By integrating EpCAM‑specific antibodies, phytogenic AuNPs and the near‑infrared (NIR) -activated PPBa, enables the construct to achieve selective tumour targeting, cytotoxicity and induction of ICD. A comparative evaluation against established EpCAM clones (HEA125, MOC31), chemically synthesised AuNPs, and second‑generation Ps such as chlorin e6 and Zn‑phthalocyanine also highlights both the conceptual advantages and current limitations of this approach.

Despite promising therapeutic potential EpAb2‑6‑AuNPs‑PPBa construct, several critical limitations must be must be addressed to facilitate its successful clinical translation. One of these limitations has to do with the antibody’s orientation and conjugation efficiency. Random attachment of EpAb2‑6 to the nanoparticle surface can obscure antigen‑binding sites and reduce targeting efficiency. Site‑directed conjugation strategies such as thiol-maleimide chemistry, Fc-specific linkers, or Fab’ fragments with thiol to preserve orientation may mitigate this issue. In addition, PEGylation can also be utilised to improve spacing and steric hindrance, but further optimisation and validation are required. Also construct stability and pharmacokinetics also require attention. This is where serum protein adsorption could alter biodistribution leading to clearance by the liver and spleen, thereby compromising therapeutic precision. Applications of PEGylation or Zwitterionic coatings to resist protein corona formation, and utilisation of pharmacokinetic parameters, such as clearance, half‑life, and biodegradability, must be carefully evaluated since non‑biodegradable nanostructures may pose long‑term toxicity risks.

Another limitation is off‑target PDT activation. Light scattering within tissue could lead to unintended ROS generation in adjacent epithelial cells or near the tumour site particularly if residual amount of the activated drug still within therapeutic threshold, diffuse towards these neighbouring cells, increasing the collateral damage to healthy cells. Optimisation of light delivery systems and dosimetry potentially through fibre‑optic colonoscopy or photoacoustic imaging will be essential to minimise this effect. Antigen specificity and immunogenicity warrant careful consideration. EpCAM is tumour‑associated rather than strictly tumour‑specific, and is expressed at low levels in certain normal epithelial tissues. This raises the risk of off‑target binding and immune responses, particularly with repeated dosing. Therefore, future studies should explore bispecific construct or divergent targeting strategies that combine EpCAM recognition with additional ligands with high specificity directed against both CRC cells and associated CSCs. Such dual‑targeting approaches could be utilised to enhance the selectivity for malignant EpCAM⁺ populations while reducing the collateral damage to healthy cells with low or basal EpCAM expression. Comparative benchmarking of EpAb2‑6 against established EpCAM antibodies, such as HEA125 and MOC31, should be done to further clarify translational readiness.

Variability in green synthesis and phytochemical heterogeneity represent another barriers. Plant extracts contain diverse phytochemicals whose composition varies with species, season, and extraction conditions. These biomolecules can unpredictably influence nanoparticle nucleation, growth, and surface chemistry, thereby affecting downstream conjugation or encapsulation efficiency. Such variability complicates antibody attachment, drug loading, and therapeutic outcome reproducibility. To address this challenge, rigorous phytochemical profiling, GMP standardisation and integration of advanced analytical tools, such as capillary electrophoresis–mass spectrometry (CE‑MS), ion mobility MS and multidimensional liquid chromatography, are required to ensure consistent nanoparticle quality. Translational modelling also poses challenges. Preclinical rodent models alone may fail to predict human outcomes that could be attributed to differences in antigen expression by both species, highlighting the need for complementary or additional validation in clinically relevant CRC models such as patient derived xenografts, organoid or 3D biodesigned CRC tissue to improve translational accuracy.

Importantly, the theranostic dimension of the nanobioconjugate construct through incorporation of SERS tags for real‑time imaging and CTC capture should also be assessed in translational CRC models to verify its diagnostic utility alongside therapeutic efficacy. Future research should validate the PPBa‑loaded constructs in vivo to confirm ICD induction and therapeutic efficacy. Also, integrating advanced light delivery technologies and developing dual‑targeting constructs that address both CRC cells and associated CSCs to enhance specificity. Additionally, standardise green synthesis under GMP conditions with rigorous phytochemical profiling and advanced analytics. Furthermore, benchmarking anti-EpCAM for example EpAb2‑6 against established EpCAM antibodies to clarify translational readiness whiles exploring synergistic integration with immune checkpoint inhibitors or mRNA‑based drugs to broaden therapeutic applicability. By doing so, this systematically addresses the limitations, and progressing construct of this nature from a conceptual perspective into a translationally viable nanotheranostic platform for CRC treatment.
