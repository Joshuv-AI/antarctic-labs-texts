Towards a universal phase diagram of planar chiral magnets
Bernd Schroers, Martin Speight, Thomas Winyard

Abstract
In planar chiral magnets, the competition of the positive definite Heisenberg exchange and Zeeman energies with the indefinite Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI) energy allows for the possibility of negative energy ground states, and leads to an intricate dependence of the ground states on the parameters of the theory. In this paper, we consider arbitrary spiralization tensors for the DMI interaction and arbitrary directions for the external magnetic field, and study the nature of the ground states in this parameter space, using a combination of analytical and numerical methods. Classifying ground states by their symmetry into ferromagnetic (invariant under under arbitrary translations in the plane), spiral (invariant under arbitrary translations in one direction) and skyrmion lattice ground states (invariant under a two dimensional lattice group), we give a complete description of the phase diagram of this class of theories.

Introduction

Most materials have lattice structures in their solid state. This is relatively straightforward to understand in quantum-mechanical descriptions of solids because periodic arrangements of atoms minimize the total interaction energy of a large number of atoms. In nonlinear field theories of matter, however, the nature of the ground state and its dependence on order parameters in the model is often one of the most intricate and interesting aspects of the theory.

This is particularly true if the field theory permits topological solitons - particle-like solutions of the field equations with a localized energy density which are stable for topological reasons. Such solitons have an associated topological invariant which one can typically think of as the number of particles in a configuration. Minimal energy configurations need to be determined separately in each topological sector of the theory. In analogy with atomic models of solids, one would then expect lattices to appear in topological sectors containing many particles. This is indeed the case, for example, in the Skyrme model of nuclear particles, where solitons - called Skyrmions - are models for nucleons
[
1
]
. Skyrmions repel at small separation, but attract at large separation for suitable relative orientations. As a result, the interaction potential has a minimum at a finite separation, and lattices can, and indeed do, form by a mechanism not dissimilar from the one leading to atomic lattices in quantum mechanics.

There is a further and rather different mechanism by which lattices of solitons form in field theories, which is common in theories of condensed matter systems, subject to an externally applied magnetic field. The seminal example is the Abrikosov lattice in Ginzburg-Landau models of vortices
[
2
]
, and later the theory of chiral magnetic skyrmions
[
3
]
which we study in this paper. In such theories, there is typically a homogeneous ground state for a strong external field (normal/ferromagnetic state). However, as one reduces the external field, the system can lower its total energy by creating topological defects which have negative energy relative to the homogeneous ground state. If such defects attract each other, they can form clusters of arbitrarily negative energy. However, if they repel, one expects there to be an equilibrium where topological defects form an infinite arrangement with finite separations between them. If that arrangement is doubly periodic, the ground state is a lattice of solitons. If it is translation invariant in one direction but periodic in another, then it is a lattice of line defects.

In this paper, we carry out a systematic study of ground states in chiral magnets in the plane. The model allows for topological solitons, called chiral magnetic skyrmions, which were predicted theoretically by Bogdanov and Yablonskii
[
3
]
, and later observed experimentally – in the form of a spatially periodic lattice – by Mühlbauer et al
[
4
]
. They are of potential interest for next-generation and low-energy data storage technology
[
5
]
, and have been the focus of intense theoretical and experimental study for several years
[
6
,
7
]
.

The basic field (or order parameter) of the model is the magnetization vector, mathematically a smooth map
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
. The energy functional contains the usual Heisenberg exchange interaction, the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI) and the Zeeman interaction with an externally applied magnetic field. As we explain in the paper, it is useful to think of the parameters in the DMI term (often called spiralization tensor) as those of an ellipse in three dimensional space. Using the symmetries of the theory, we can assume that this ellipse lies in the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane, and has a major axis along the
x_{1}
-direction and of length one. The length of the minor axis is an irreducible parameter of the DMI, varying from zero for so-called rank one materials to one for the much-studied axisymmetric DMI term. Having used the symmetries to bring the DMI into a standard form, we have to allow for arbitrary magnitude and direction of the magnetic field, so that our model has a four dimensional phase space. General anisotropy terms would add further parameters, but we do not consider them in this paper.

For sufficiently strong external magnetic fields, the energy is dominated by the Zeeman term: the magnetization vector aligns with the magnetic field everywhere, leading to homogeneous ferromagnetic vacuum state of total energy zero. However, for vanishing or weak magnetic fields, the DMI term determines the ground state: helical configurations of the magnetization vector, which have negative DMI energy density, may then have total energy per unit area which is also negative. Such configurations form the new ground state of the theory. For intermediate magnitudes of the external magnetic field, there is a subtle interplay of all three energy terms, which, for certain regions of phase space, leads to the minimization of the energy per unit area through magnetic skyrmions. These are topological solitons of topological degree minus one, and with negative total energy. Their repulsive interaction
[
8
]
prevents clustering, but allows for the formation of lattices.

Ground states of planar chiral magnets have been studied for particular parameter choices in, for example,
[
9
]
and more systematically for general DMI terms and with the magnetic field orthogonal to the plane in
[
10
]
.
In this paper, we carry out a comprehensive study of the ground state of the theory for general DMI parameters and arbitrary strength and direction of the external magnetic fields. In this way we arrive at a phase diagram of planar chiral magnets which divides the four dimensional parameter space into ferromagnetic, spiral and skyrmion lattice phases.
Our numerical method for finding spiral ground states and skyrmion lattices is based on techniques which two of the authors developed for vortex lattices in superconductors initially in
[
11
]
and generalized in
[
12
]
. Applied to skyrmion lattices, this involves minimizing the energy per unit area of the lattice with respect to the magnetization field and, crucially, also the period lattice. This is closely analogous to the problems studied in the context of multicomponent superconductors in
[
13
,
14
]
and can be solved by an efficient gradient descent method called arrested Newton flow.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 1, we define the model of planar chiral magnets in terms of its energy functional, explain the geometrical representation of the DMI parameters in terms of an ellipse, and use symmetries to bring the energy into a standard form which depends on four parameters. In Section 2, we study configurations which are translation invariant in one direction and periodic in another and which, following the conventions in the literature, we call spiral. We define the spiral domain
\mathscr{H}_{b}
as the set of magnetic fields for which the total energy is negative when evaluated on a spiral configuration, and use three ansätze for such configurations in terms of helical fields, periodic arrangements of stripes, and conical fields to determine bounds on
\mathscr{H}_{b}
. Section 3 contains a careful exposition of our numerical method for reliably determining lattice ground states. The results of this scheme are presented in Section 4: we illustrate our insights into the four dimensional phase diagram in terms of various two dimensional phase plots before turning to a final discussion of our results in the concluding Section 5.

The model

Consider the following free energy,

E=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}|d\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}|^{2}+D_{ai}\varepsilon_{abc}m_{b}\frac{\partial m_{c}}{\partial x_{i}}+V(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})\right\}d^{2}x=E_{exch}+E_{DMI}+E_{pot}

a\in\{1,2,3\}

i\in\{1,2\}
. The potential

0

H

V(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})=|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|-\mbox{\bm{$H$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}.

(1.2)

This is minimized when
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}=\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}:=\mbox{\bm{$H$}}/|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|

E
always admits the homogeneous solution
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(x)=\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}

Note that the homogeneous solution is not necessarily the global minimizer of

[
15
]

\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}

\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(\lambda\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})

E(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{\lambda})=E_{exch}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})+\frac{1}{\lambda}E_{DMI}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}E_{pot}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}).

(1.3)

Since
E
is, by assumption, critical with respect to all smooth variations of
m
, it is critical with respect to the variation
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{\lambda}
, that is

\frac{d\>}{d\lambda}\bigg|_{\lambda=1}E(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{\lambda})=-E_{DMI}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})-2E_{pot}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})=0.

(1.4)

Hence all finite energy static solutions satisfy the virial constraint

E_{DMI}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})=-2E_{pot}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})

(1.5)

implying that
E_{DMI}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})\leq 0
(with equality if and only if
m
is the ferromagnetic state).

Fields which tend to the ferromagnetic state as
|\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}|\rightarrow\infty
are classified by their topological degree

Q(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\cdot(\partial_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\times\partial_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})\in{\mathbb{Z}}

(1.6)

which may be interpreted as the number of times
m
wraps
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\cup\{\infty\}\equiv\ S^{2}
around
S^{2}
. Minimizers of
E
among fields of degree
Q=-1
(
Q=1

1.1
Reduction of DMI parameters

In this section, we show how field and spatial coordinate redefinitions can be used to reduce the general DMI term, containing 6 real parameters
D_{ai}
, to a one-parameter family.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
denote the columns of the spiralization tensor. Then the DMI energy is

{E}_{DMI}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\sum_{i=1,2}\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{i}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\times\partial_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})d^{2}x.

(1.7)

It is geometrically natural to think of the spiralization tensor in terms of the ellipse traced out by

\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\theta)=\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}\cos\theta+\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}\sin\theta,\quad\theta\in[0,2\pi),

(1.8)

see figure
2
.
This picture, which includes the degenerate cases of the circle (
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}
orthogonal and of equal magnitude) and the line (
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}
linearly dependent but not both zero) is natural because it captures the symmetry of the DMI energy. It also immediately suggests a route to standardizing the form of the DMI interaction by rotating and scaling the vectors
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}
into a standard form in the 12-plane of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. We explain this carefully below, but to motivate the steps we take, we note that the lengths and directions of the major and minor axes of the ellipse defined by (
1.8
) are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the positive definite and symmetric Gram matrix
G_{ij}=\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{i}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{j}
. This matrix can be brought into a standard diagonal form with ordered eigenvalues

\lambda^{\pm}=\frac{|\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}|^{2}+|\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}|^{2}}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(|\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}|^{2}-|\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}|^{2})^{2}+(2\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2})^{2}}.

(1.9)

by conjugation with a suitable
SO(2)
matrix
R

which rotates
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{i}\mapsto\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{i}=\sum_{j}R_{ij}\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{j}
.
In particular, we therefore have
\widetilde{G}_{12}=\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{1}\cdot\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{2}=0
and
\widetilde{G}_{11}=|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{1}|^{2}\geq\widetilde{G}_{22}=|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{2}|^{2}
in this basis.

DMI terms classified in terms of ellipses with major axis 1, minor axis

We can use these considerations to simplify the energy expressions by
combining the rotation of the DMI vectors with rotated spatial coordinates
\widetilde{x}_{i}=\sum_{j}R_{ij}x_{j}
. The energy is now

{E}_{DMI}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\sum_{i=1,2}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{i}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\times\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}{\partial\widetilde{x}_{i}})d^{2}\widetilde{x},

(1.10)

We also define rescaled coordinates,
\overline{x}_{i}=|\widetilde{d}_{1}|\widetilde{x}_{i}
. Then

E=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left|\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}{\partial\overline{x}_{i}}\right|^{2}+\sum_{i}\overline{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\times\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}{\partial\overline{x}_{i}}\right)+|\overline{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}|-\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\cdot\overline{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\right\}d^{2}\overline{x}

(1.11)

where
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{i}=\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{i}/|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{1}|

\overline{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}=\mbox{\bm{$H$}}/|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{1}|^{2}

Note that
|\overline{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{1}|=1

We now observe that there exists
S\in SO(3)
(unique, unless
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{2}=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}
) such that

\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}^{\prime}:=S\overline{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{1}=(1,0,0),\qquad\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}^{\prime}:=S\overline{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{2}=(0,b,0),

(1.12)

where
b\in[0,1]
. If we rotate
m
by the same matrix,
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}^{\prime}:=S\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}
, we see that

E=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left|\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}^{\prime}}{\partial\overline{x}_{i}}\right|^{2}+\sum_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{i}^{\prime}\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}^{\prime}\times\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}^{\prime}}{\partial\overline{x}_{i}}\right)+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}^{\prime}|-\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}^{\prime}\cdot\mbox{\bm{$H$}}^{\prime}\right\}d^{2}\overline{x}

(1.13)

where
\mbox{\bm{$H$}}^{\prime}=S\overline{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
. This is the final reduced form of our energy functional, and it depends on the single parameter
b\in[0,1]
, and the (rescaled, rotated) applied field
\mbox{\bm{$H$}}^{\prime}
. It is important to note that, in achieving this reduction, we have broken the correlation between the axis directions in the sample plane and in the field space. In particular, when we speak of the orthogonal (or parallel) component of

(1,0,0)

(0,1,0)

A quick way to identify which DMI term in our one-parameter family

is to note that

b^{2}=\frac{|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{2}|^{2}}{|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}}_{1}|^{2}}=\frac{\lambda^{-}}{\lambda^{+}},

(1.14)

are the eigenvalues (

So if

E_{b,\bm{H}}

E_{b,\bm{H}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}(|\partial_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}|^{2}+|\partial_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}|^{2})+(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\times\partial_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})_{1}+b(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\times\partial_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})_{2}+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|-\mbox{\bm{$H$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\right\}d^{2}x.

(1.15)

Note that the models with
b=1
and

each enjoy a rotation symmetry, since for all fields

\displaystyle E_{1,\bm{H}}(R_{3}\circ\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\circ R_{3}^{-1})

\displaystyle E_{1,R_{3}^{-1}\bm{H}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}),

\displaystyle E_{0,\bm{H}}(R_{1}\circ\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})

1.2
Rank 1 materials

Consider the case where
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}
are linearly dependent, hence
b=0
and, in the coordinate system derived above,
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}=(1,0,0)
,
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}
. Then

E_{\text{DMI}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\times\partial_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}.

(1.18)

We now demonstrate that this model can have no spatially localized static solutions of negative total energy. Hence skyrmion crystals, if they exist, can only be metastable in these systems, never the ground state.

To see this, let us split the exchange energy into two terms,

\displaystyle E_{exch}

\displaystyle=E^{(1)}_{exch}+E^{(2)}_{exch},

\displaystyle E_{exch}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|\partial_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\right|^{2},

(1.19)

and consider the anisotropic scaling
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{\lambda}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}):=\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(\lambda x_{1},x_{2})
of a static solution
m
, whose energy is,

E(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{\lambda})=\lambda E_{exch}^{(1)}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})+\frac{1}{\lambda}E_{exch}^{(2)}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})+E_{\text{DMI}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})+\frac{1}{\lambda}E_{pot}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}).

(1.20)

As in Derrick’s argument, this function of
\lambda
must have a critical point at
\lambda=1
, so

\left.\frac{dE(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{\lambda})}{d\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=1}=E_{exch}^{(1)}-E_{exch}^{(2)}-E_{pot}=0.

(1.21)

Combining (
1.5
) with (
1.21
) we see that

E=E_{exch}^{(1)}+E_{exch}^{(2)}+E_{\text{DMI}}+E_{pot}=E_{exch}^{(1)}+E_{exch}^{(2)}-E_{pot}=2E_{exch}^{(2)}\geq 0.

(1.22)

Hence, every spatially localized (and hence finite energy) solution of the rank 1 model has positive energy.

One should note that the argument above applies only to spatially localized fields. As we will see shortly, the ground state of the rank 1 model with sufficiently small
|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|
is not the ferromagnetic state since there exist one dimensional spatially periodic fields (spiral phases) of negative average energy density. Such fields evade the argument above since they have infinite
E
.

2
The spiral phase

2.1
The spiral domain

Recall that the ferromagnetic state,
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}:=\mbox{\bm{$H$}}/|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|
constant, has zero energy, but may fail to be the ground state of the system, since the DMI energy can be negative. In this section we will consider
spiral phase
solutions of the system. By definition, these are solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equation for
E
which are translation invariant in some direction in the sample plane and vary periodically (spatially) in the orthogonal direction. If these have negative energy, they are energetically preferred over the FM state, and are candidates for the system’s ground state (though, as we will see later, more complex configurations with less symmetry may have even lower energy). For a given DMI parameter
b\in[0,1]
, we denote by
\mathscr{H}_{b}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
the
spiral domain
of the system, that is, the set of applied magnetic field values
H
for which
\displaystyle{E_{b,{\bm{H}}}}
(
1.15
) has a negative energy spiral phase solution. Our aim in this section is to understand
\mathscr{H}_{b}
as completely possible, by means of rigorous analytic bounds and careful numerics.

It will be useful to distinguish between spiral phase solutions and the weaker notion of a
spiral phase
of the system, which we define to be any field
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
which is translation invariant in some direction in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and varies periodically in the direction orthogonal to invariance. Explicitly, let
\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\in S^{1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a unit vector in the
(x_{1},x_{2})
plane, and assume that
m
is invariant under translations orthogonal to
n
, so

\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})

(2.1)

for some periodic map
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow S^{2}
of period
T>0
. This is a spiral phase
solution
if and only if
s
is a critical point of the dimensionally reduced energy functional

E=\int\left\{\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}}(t)|^{2}+\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t)\times\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}}(t)\right)+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|-\mbox{\bm{$H$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t)\right\}\,dt,

(2.2)

where
t=\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}
and we have defined,

\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})=n_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}+n_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2},

(2.3)

a vector on the DMI ellipse.
As an interesting aside, the functional (
2.2
) is the Lagrangian for a charged particle moving on a sphere in the presence of a magnetic field
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})
and gravitational field
-\mbox{\bm{$H$}}
, so critical points of
E
may be reinterpreted as time-dependent trajectories of such a particle. We emphasize, however, that
E
is the energy functional of our system, not a Lagrangian, and
t
represents a spatial variable, measuring distance in the sample plane along the direction orthogonal to
n
. We require periodic solutions of this problem, of period
T>0
(so
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t+T)\equiv\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t)
), and vary
s
to minimize the average energy density

\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left\{\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}}(t)|^{2}+\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t)\times\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}}(t)\right)+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|-\mbox{\bm{$H$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t)\right\}\,dt,

(2.4)

subject to periodic boundary conditions.

By a standard application of the direct method of the calculus of variations in the Hilbert space
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}/T{\mathbb{Z}},S^{2})
, such a critical point exists, for any fixed
T>0
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\in S^{1}
, attaining the infimum of
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
on this space. Hence, to show that
\mbox{\bm{$H$}}\in\mathscr{H}_{b}
it suffices to construct any spiral phase for
E_{b,\bm{H}}
of negative average energy density: the spiral phase solution with the same
n
and
T
has
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
no greater than this, and hence also has
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle<0
.

Generalizing the discussion of helical phases in
[
16
]
, we re-write the average energy density (

2.2
The sine-Gordon bound

Configurations where the magnetization vector carries out a full rotation from the ferromagnetic vacuum
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
about an axis
N
orthogonal to
H
back to
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
feature prominently in the stability analysis of chiral magnet configurations
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, where they are called stripes.
There are exact solutions of this nature in a variant of the model with a specifically tuned anisotropy potential

[
20
]
, which were used as a comparison configuration in stability analysis of magnetic skyrmions in
[
21
]
.

We are interested in periodic arrays of stripes. We begin
by considering a spiral phase of the form

\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}),\qquad\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t)=\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\cos\theta(t)-\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\times{\mbox{\boldmath{$N$}}}\sin\theta(t),

(2.10)

where
\mbox{\boldmath{$N$}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is a unit vector orthogonal to
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
, and
\theta
a function to be determined. Extrema of
E
restricted to this class of functions satisfy the static sine-Gordon equation

\ddot{\theta}=|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|\sin\theta.

(2.11)

We will show that, for small enough
|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|
such sine-Gordon fields exist with negative total energy, leading to a lower bound on
H_{SP}
which we call the sine-Gordon bound

H_{*}^{\rm pen}
.

For a fixed
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\in S^{2}
it is useful to define the quantity,

\delta(\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}):=\max\left\{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$N$}}:\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\in S^{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$N$}}\in S^{2},\mbox{\boldmath{$N$}}\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}=0\right\}.

(2.12)

This is the maximum of a smooth function over a compact manifold (a two-torus, in fact) and so certainly exists. We claim that, for all
b\in[0,1]
and
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\in S^{2}
,

H_{\text{SP }}(b,\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})\geq H_{*}^{\rm sG}:=\pi^{2}\delta(\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})^{2}/16.

(2.13)

We establish this as follows.
Given any fixed
H
, let
(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$N$}})\in S^{1}\times S^{2}
be the point at which
\mbox{\boldmath{$N$}}\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}=0
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$N$}}=\delta(\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}).

Consider the field (
2.10
),
where
\theta:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth period
T
function with
\theta(0)=0
and
\theta(T)=2\pi
. This has average energy,

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int^{T}_{0}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\theta}^{2}-\delta\dot{\theta}+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|(1-\cos\theta)\right\}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\left[-2\pi\delta+\int^{T}_{0}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\theta}^{2}+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|(1-\cos\theta)\right\}\,dt\right].

(2.14)

We note that the static energy functional of the sine-Gordon model for a field in one dimension is,

E_{SG}=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\theta}^{2}+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|(1-\cos\theta)\right\}\,dt,

(2.15)

which has the static kink solution
[
1
]
,

\theta_{K}(t)=4\tan^{-1}e^{t/\sqrt{|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|}},

(2.16)

with the boundary conditions
\theta_{K}(-\infty)=0
and
\theta_{K}(\infty)=2\pi
. Substituting this into (
2.15
) gives total energy
E_{SG}(\theta_{K})=8\sqrt{|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|}
. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
T
sufficiently large and a map
\theta_{*}:[0,T]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
with
\theta_{*}(0)=0
and
\theta_{*}(T)=2\pi
such that

\int^{T}_{0}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\theta}^{2}_{*}+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|(1-\cos\theta_{*})\right\}\,dt<8\sqrt{|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|}+\varepsilon.

(2.17)

We can construct
\theta_{*}
by restricting the kink
\theta_{K}(t-T/2)
to the interval
[0,T]
then enforcing the boundary values with smooth cut-offs. The energy excess over
8\sqrt{|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|}
can then be made arbitrarily small (by making
T
sufficiently large), because
\theta_{K}
has exponentially small tails.

Hence, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a periodic field of type (
2.10
) with energy per period,

T\langle E\rangle<-2\pi\delta+8\sqrt{|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|}+\varepsilon.

(2.18)

Choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, this has
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle<0
provided,

\sqrt{|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|}<\frac{\pi\delta}{4}.

(2.19)

Hence, if
|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|<(\pi\delta(\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})/4)^{2}
, the system admits a negative energy
spiral phase.

It would be useful to have an explicit formula for
H_{*}^{\rm sG}(b,\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})
, but we have been unable to find one. One readily sees that

H_{*}^{\rm sG}(b,(\pm 1,0,0))=\frac{\pi^{2}b^{2}}{16},\quad H_{*}^{\rm sG}(b,(0,\pm 1,0))=H_{*}^{\rm sG}(b,(0,0,\pm 1))=\frac{\pi^{2}}{16},

(2.20)

and that

H_{*}^{\rm sG}(0,\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}(1-\hat{H}_{1}^{2}),\quad H_{*}^{\rm sG}(1,\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}.

(2.21)

For
b
small and
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
close to
\pm{\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}}_{1}
, the sine-Gordon bound is worse than the simple bound
H_{SP}\geq 1/2
. In this case, we can obtain a better lower bound on
H_{SP}
by considering fields which precess in a spiral around
H
, as we now show.

2.3
Conical bound

A further useful bound on the spiral domain can be obtained from trial configurations where the magnetization vector spirals on a cone. To define this, we choose a unit vector
u
orthogonal to
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
, an angular frequency
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, angle
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and unit vector
n
in the
x_{1}x_{2}
plane to construct the field,

\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\cos\theta+({\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}}\cos(\omega\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})+\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\times{\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}}\sin(\omega\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}))\sin\theta.

(2.22)

As
t=\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}
varies, this maps out a conical spiral with half apex angle
\theta
. Note that, for small
\theta
, such configurations can also be interpreted as approximate linearised configurations near the ferromagnetic vacuum
\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}_{0}=\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
, since
u
and
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\times{\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}}
span the tangent space to that vacuum state. The configurations are therefore closely related to those studied in
[
8
]
, where it was shown that linearised fields obey a gauged Klein-Gordon equation, with abelian gauge potential
(a_{1},a_{2})=(\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}},\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})
in the notation of the current paper. The gauged Klein-Gordon equation acquires an imaginary mass when
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}
exceeds a bound proportional to the strength
H
of the magnetic field, which was interpreted as an instability threshold for the ferromagnetic vacuum in
[
8
]
. In our notation, and restricted to the one dimensional reduction in the
n
-direction, that bound can be re-interpreted as the condition

|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|<(a_{1}n_{1}+a_{2}n_{2})^{2}=(\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}})^{2}}

(2.23)

for conical spiral configurations like (
2.22
) to acquire negative energy per unit length. We now derive this condition via a more direct route.

The average energy density of the field (
2.22
) is

\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}\theta\omega^{2}+\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\omega\sin^{2}\theta-+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|(1-\cos\theta)

(2.24)

which is minimized by choosing
\omega=-\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
, yielding

\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}(\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})^{2}\sin^{2}\theta+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|(1-\cos\theta).

(2.25)

Minimizing this over
\theta\in[0,\pi]
, we see that

\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{min}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0,&\mbox{if $|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|\geq(\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})^{2}$,}\\
-\frac{1}{2}(\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}-|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|/\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})^{2},&\mbox{if $|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|\leq(\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})^{2}$,}\end{array}\right.

(2.26)

So there exists a negative energy spiral phase directed along
n
provided the condition (
2.23
) holds.

The mapping
S^{1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\mapsto\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}

(2.27)

is maximized when
n
is directed along
(H_{1},bH_{2})
. Hence

H_{SP}(b,\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}})\geq H_{*}^{\rm con}=\hat{H}_{1}^{2}+b^{2}\hat{H}_{2}^{2},

(2.28)

which coincides with (
2.23
).

Note that, for
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}=(\pm 1,0,0)
we have
H_{SP}\geq H_{*}^{\rm con}=1
from (
2.28
) and
H_{SP}\leq 1
from (
2.8
), so
H_{SP}=1
in this case. So the intersection of
\mathscr{H}_{b}
with the
H_{1}
axis is precisely the open interval
(-1,1)
.

2.4
Finding the spiral phase numerically

Recall that for any fixed
\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\in S^{1}
and
T>0
, there is a spiral phase solution, translation invariant orthogonal to
n
, periodic with period
T
along
n
, which minimizes
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
among all such fields.
Its energy
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
will depend on
(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},T)
.
We define
the
spiral phase of the system to be the spiral phase solution which minimizes
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
among all possible choices of
(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},T)
, if this exists.

To find the spiral phase numerically, it is useful to rescale our “time” coordinate so that
t=\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}/T
, and
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t)
is now periodic with period
1
. The average energy density of a field
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}/T)
is

\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle=\frac{E}{T}=\int_{0}^{1}\left\{\frac{1}{2T^{2}}|\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}}|^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}\times\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}})+|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|-\mbox{\bm{$H$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}\right\}dt.

(2.29)

We seek to minimize this over all unit period fields
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}:{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow\ S^{2}
and all directions
\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}\in S^{1}
and periods
T>0
. We will do this by iterating a two-step minimization process.

First we choose some
n
,
T
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}:{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow S^{2}
, then minimize
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}})
with respect to
s
, for
(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},T)
fixed, by arrested Newton flow. We will describe this computational method in more detail in figure
3
. We then minimize
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},T)
with respect to
(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},T)
, for
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}:{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow S^{2}
fixed, by an explicit computation which we will describe shortly. We then iterate, alternating minimization steps with respect to the field and the direction/period, until
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
is minimized to a chosen tolerance (meaning the reduction in
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
in the last step is sufficiently small).

So, for a fixed unit period field
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}:{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, we must minimize
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
over
(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},T)\in S^{1}\times(0,\infty)
. First note that

\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},T)=\frac{K}{2T^{2}}+\frac{1}{T}\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$S$}}+C,

(2.30)

where

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}}(t)|^{2}\,dt,

S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t)\times\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}}(t)\,dt,

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}V(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}(t))\,dt

(2.31)

depend only on
s
. Only the second term in (
2.30
) depends on
n
, and attains its minimum at

\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}=-\frac{(S_{1},b^{2}S_{2})}{\sqrt{S_{1}^{2}+b^{2}S_{2}^{2}}}.

(2.32)

At this optimal
n
,

\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle(T)=\frac{K}{2T^{2}}-\frac{1}{T}\sqrt{S_{1}^{2}+b^{2}S_{2}^{2}}+C

(2.33)

which attains its minimum at

T=\frac{K}{\sqrt{S_{1}^{2}+b^{2}S_{2}^{2}}}.

(2.34)

Hence, the direction
n
and period
T
which minimize
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
for a given unit period field
s
are (
2.32
), (
2.34
) respectively.

This scheme allows us to construct, for each
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
and
b
, the energetically optimal spiral phase, and to map out the parameter space on which this phase has negative average energy, and hence is favoured over the FM state. In this way, we can construct
\mathscr{H}_{b}
numerically.

The figure
4
shows sections through
\mathscr{H}_{b}
along the coordinate planes for
b=0,0.5,1
, generated using this numerical scheme. In each case

\mathscr{H}_{b}
is the region interior to the black closed boundary curve. The dashed curves are lower bounds on the radius of this boundary curve obtained by considering conical spiral phases (red) and sine-Gordon phases (green), as explained in sections
2.3
and
2.2
.
Note that these numerical results confirm all the properties of
\mathscr{H}_{b}
derived above:

\mathscr{H}_{0}
and
\mathscr{H}_{1}
are volumes of revolution symmetric under rotations about the
H_{1}
and
H_{3}
axes respectively, all sections are star-shaped and inversion symmetric, and for all sections
\mathscr{H}_{0}\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{0.5}\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{1}
, consistent with the nesting property. We observe that for

for which the model has a negative energy spiral phase. The dashed curves correspond to lower bounds on the radius of the boundary curve, calculated by assuming the SP state is a conical spiral (red) or sine-Gordon state (green).

3
Finding skyrmion lattices

Skyrmion lattices are solutions
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
of the Euler-Lagrange equations for the energy functional
E
(
1.1
) which are doubly periodic with respect to some linearly independent pair of vectors
\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, that is,

\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}+n_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1}+n_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{2})=\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})

(3.1)

for all
(n_{1},n_{2})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and all
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We may equivalently regard
m
as a solution on the flat torus
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\Lambda
where

\Lambda=\{n_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1}+n_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{2}:(n_{1},n_{2})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}<{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

(3.2)

is the lattice generated by
\{\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{2}\}
.

Recalling that our field
m
represents the magnetization of a very large but bounded thin film ferromagnet, we now seek lattice solutions which minimise the total energy of our large but finite sample. Since the total energy is, trivially, the energy per unit area multiplied by the fixed total area, this amounts to searching for pairs
(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}},\Lambda)
of lattice configurations and period lattices which minimize
energy per unit area
among all nearby combinations of field configuration and period lattice

To do this in practice, we start with a fixed lattice
\Lambda
, and an initial field
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{0}:\Omega=\rightarrow S^{2}
of nonzero degree
Q
, and flow by energy gradient descent to a local energy minimizer among fields in its homotopy class. It is an interesting and, so far as we are aware, open problem to find necessary and sufficient conditions on
\Lambda
and
Q
under which such a minimizer exists. However, for small
|Q|
we expect energy minimizers to exist for almost all choices of
\Lambda
. Most of these solutions have no physical significance: they are artifacts of our choice of boundary conditions that will never be observed in a real system. To represent a genuine physical skyrmion lattice, our map

\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:\Omega\rightarrow S^{2}
should minimize energy per unit area not only with respect to variations of the field
m
on the fixed torus
\Omega
, but also with respect to variations of the period lattice
\Lambda
.

For a given a lattice
\Lambda
generated by
\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{2}
, it is convenient to use coordinates
X_{1},X_{2}\in[0,1]
defined so that

\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}=X_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1}+X_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{2}.

(3.3)

In matrix language,
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}=L\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}
where
L\in GL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is the invertible matrix with columns
\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{2}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}=M\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}
, where
M=L^{-1}
. Note that the area of the torus
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\Lambda
is
|\det L|
.
The energy per unit area of a field
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:\Omega\rightarrow S^{2}
is then

\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle(M)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\,(M^{T}HM)+{\rm tr}\,({\mathscr{D}}M)+C

(3.4)

where

\displaystyle H_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Box}\frac{\partial m_{a}}{\partial X_{i}}\frac{\partial m_{a}}{\partial X_{j}}dX_{1}dX_{2}

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{D}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{ai}\epsilon_{abc}\int_{\Box}m_{b}\frac{\partial m_{c}}{\partial X_{j}}dX_{1}dX_{2}

(3.6)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Box}V(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})dX_{1}dX_{2}

(3.7)

and
\Box=[0,1]\times[0,1]
is the closed unit square. The expression for
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
in (
3.4
) is simply (
1.1
) integrated over
\Omega
with a change of basis and divided by the area of
\Omega
, namely
|\det L|
.

Shifting perspective slightly, we may interpret (
3.4
) as follows: all two-tori
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\Lambda
are diffeomorphic through linear diffeomorphisms, so we may identify them all with the single reference torus
\Omega_{\Box}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and regard all doubly periodic fields as maps
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:\Omega_{\Box}\rightarrow S^{2}
. If we do so, our energy functional
E
now depends parametrically on the lattice
\Lambda
or, equivalently, on the invertible matrix

M
, as in (
3.4
). For a given field
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:\Omega_{\Box}\rightarrow S^{2}
, we seek the minimum of
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle:GL(2,{\mathbb{R}})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. We could do this numerically by a gradient descent method (for example, arrested Newton flow) on
GL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
. This is the approach taken by Lee
et al
. in
[
22
]
to construct skyrmion lattices in a specific planar model with axisymmetric DMI term and a novel potential. In fact, it is straightforward to compute the minimizer of

\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle:GL(2,{\mathbb{R}})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
explicitly, as we now observe, and we find this to be more computationally efficient.

The minimum must occur at a critical point of
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
. Now the differential of
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
at
M\in GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is the linear map

d\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{M}:\varepsilon\mapsto{\rm tr}\,(\varepsilon^{T}(HM+{\mathscr{D}}^{T}))

(3.8)

and
M
is a critical point of
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
if and only if this vanishes on all
2\times 2
matrices
\varepsilon
, that is, if

HM+{\mathscr{D}}^{T}=0.

(3.9)

The matrix
H
is manifestly symmetric and positive semi-definite. If the map
m
is once continuously differentiable and has any regular points (points
\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}\in\Omega_{\Box}
at which
d\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{{\bm{X}}}
has maximal rank), it is positive definite, and hence invertible. Every map of degree
Q\neq 0
has regular points, so if
Q\neq 0
,
H
is invertible. Then
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
has a unique critical point

M_{*}=-H^{-1}{\mathscr{D}}^{T},

(3.10)

which must be a global minimum since
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
at large

|M|
is dominated by the positive quadratic term
\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\,(M^{T}HM)
. Note that

\det M_{*}=\det{\mathscr{D}}/\det H
, so strictly speaking,
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
attains a minimum if and only if
{\mathscr{D}}
is invertible.

We claim that
{\mathscr{D}}
is generically invertible (that is, is invertible for almost all maps
m
) if
D
has rank 2, but is generically

not
invertible if
D
has rank 1. To see this, consider the linear map

Z:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{3},\qquad Z(V)=\int_{\Box}\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\times\left(V_{1}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}{\partial X_{1}}+V_{2}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}{\partial X_{2}}\right)dX_{1}dX_{2},

(3.11)

noting that, for generic
m
, this has rank 2. Now assume that
{\mathscr{D}}
is not invertible. Then there exists
V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
V\neq 0
, such that
{\mathscr{D}}V=0
. But

\displaystyle({\mathscr{D}}V)_{i}=-\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{i}\cdot Z(V).

(3.12)

Hence,
\det{\mathscr{D}}=0
if and only if there exists
V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that
Z(V)
is perpendicular to the span of
\{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}\}
. If
D
has rank 2, this is equivalent to the plane
Z({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
containing the vector
\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}
which is generically false. Hence
{\mathscr{D}}
is generically invertible. If
D
has rank 1, however, the span of
\{\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$d$}}_{2}\}
is one dimensional, so its orthogonal complement intersects every two-plane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Hence
{\mathscr{D}}
is generically not invertible in this case. This reinforces our expectation, motivated by the anisotropic Derrick scaling argument presented in section
1.2
, that rank 1 materials do not support skyrmion lattices.

In summary, the energetically optimal lattice for a given field
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
has period matrix

L=-{\mathscr{D}}^{-1}H,

(3.13)

assuming, as is generically true, that both
H
and
{\mathscr{D}}
are invertible. This observation provides the basis for our numerical method for finding skyrmion lattices. We start by choosing an initial smooth field
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{0}:\Omega_{\Box}\rightarrow S^{2}
of degree
Q<0
, small (typically we consider only
Q=-1
and
Q=-2
), and a matrix
M_{0}\in GL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
close to
{\mathbb{I}}_{2}
, chosen at random. We then evolve
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(t)
by arrested Newton flow for
E(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}},M_{0})
, with
M
fixed, until
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(t)
converges to
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{1}
say, a local minimum of
E(\cdot,M_{0})
. (The arrested Newton flow algorithm will be described in detail shortly.) We then construct the matrices

H(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{1})
,
{\mathscr{D}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{1})
, as in (
3.5
), (
3.6
), and compute

M_{1}=-H^{-1}{\mathscr{D}}^{T}
. We now iterate this two step process: we perform arrested Newton flow starting at
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{n}
for the energy functional
E(\cdot,M_{n})
, converging to a local minimum
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{n+1}
, then construct

M_{n+1}=-H(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{n+1})^{-1}{\mathscr{D}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{n+1})^{T}
. In this way we construct a sequence of pairs
(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{n},M_{n})
with monotonically decreasing
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
, terminating the process when
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{n-1}-\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{n}
falls below some tolerance.
Note that, during this process we may generate fields for which
\det{\mathscr{D}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{n})<0
, in which case
\det M_{n}<0
and the associated linear diffeomorphism

M_{n}^{-1}:\Omega_{\Box}\rightarrow\Omega_{n}
is orientation
reversing
. In this case, the topological degree of
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{n}
, regarded (as it physically is) as a map from

\Omega_{n}
(the torus with period matrix
L_{n}=M_{n}^{-1}
) to
S^{2}
is actually
-Q
, not
Q
. So our scheme will automatically flip the sign of
Q
if, for the given spiralization tensor
D
, that is energetically favourable.

To minimize
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle
with respect to
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
we use arrested Newton flow
[
23
]
. First we discretize the unit square so that
(X_{1},X_{2})
takes values on a
N\times N
lattice of spacing
h=1/N
, with periodic boundary conditions. Our magnetization field is now approximated by
N^{2}
points
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}_{i,j}\in S^{2}
. By approximating spatial derivatives by finite differences (we use standard 4th order approximations) we obtain a discrete approximant of our energy functional
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{dis}:(S^{2})^{N^{2}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. We seek minima of this function. To find them, we solve for the motion of a notional point particle in

\mathscr{C}_{dis}=(S^{2})^{N^{2}}
moving under the potential energy
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{dis}
according to Newton’s law

\ddot{\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}=-\grad\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{dis}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}\right),

(3.14)

starting at some initial configuration
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(0)
with
\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}(0)=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}
. This time evolution is computed approximately using the standard 4th order Runge-Kutta scheme with fixed time step
\delta t=h/2
. As time evolves, the “particle” runs downhill in the energy landscape defined by
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{dis}
, but since Newton flow conserves total energy (meaning
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{dis}+\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}|^{2}
) it will not, without intervention, converge to a minimum of
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{dis}
. This intervention is the arresting criterion: at each time step, we check whether
\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}(t+\delta t)\cdot\grad\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{dis}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(t+\delta t))>0
. If so, the “particle” has started to travel uphill, so we arrest the flow, that is, set
\dot{\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}}(t+\delta t)=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}
and restart the flow at
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(t+\delta t)

[
24
]
. The scheme halts when
m
is a critical point of
\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{dis}
to within some tolerance
\varepsilon
, meaning that

\|\grad\langle\mathscr{E}\rangle_{dis}(\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}})\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the sup norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3N^{2}}
.

The above second order flow converges to a critical point consistently much faster than evolving the physically realistic dynamics governed by the first order damped LLG equation. Clearly, the path constructed
\mbox{\boldmath{$m$}}(t)
has no dynamical significance, but as a tool to obtain static solutions, it is significantly more efficient than LLG flow.

4
Results

We now consider the phase space for chiral lattices in two dimensions. We will break it into three sections: the ferromagnetic phase (FM), the spiral phase (SP) and skyrmion lattice phase (SK). To find the phase for a particular value of parameters we attempt to find global minimizers for one dimensional and two dimensional periodic systems using the methods outlined in the previous two sections. We then compare the energy per unit area of these solutions with the zero energy of the FM state and the lowest of the three gives the phase.

The full parameter space is four dimensional, consisting of the applied field
\mbox{\bm{$H$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the DMI parameter
b\in[0,1]
. Systematically exploring this space requires a lot of simulations.

We have seen that the volume of the SK domain in
H
space for
b=0.8
is considerably smaller than that for
b=1
. The next results present phase diagrams in the
(b,|\mbox{\bm{$H$}}|)
plane for a collection of five fixed applied field directions
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}
, starting with
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}=(0,0,1)
, see figure figure
10
. Once again, the energetically optimal fields at the marked points are depicted in figure
11
. Note that the SK domain vanishes completely for

b
sufficiently small. This is consistent with our earlier observations (sections
1.2
and
3
) that the rank 1 model (with
b=0
) should have no stable skyrmion lattices, but it is quite surprising that the SK phase vanishes at

b\approx 0.71
, which is rather large. As we will see, this critical value of
b
only increases if we choose
\hat{\mbox{\bm{$H$}}}\neq(0,0,1)
.
So skyrmion lattices exist as the ground state only for models with DMI parameter close to
1
, and then only for applied fields close to being orthogonal to the plane spanned by the DMI vectors (which is the
(m_{1},m_{2})
plane in our choice of coordinates). Skyrmion lattices are thus surprisingly fragile structures.

5
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper, we mapped out the phase space of chiral magnets in the plane whose total Hamiltonian includes the standard Heisenberg contributions as well as the most general DMI and Zeeman terms.
For each point in the four dimensional parameter space we used a combination of analytical and numerical methods to determine the ground state of the theory, defined as the configuration with the lowest energy per unit area.

There are three phases, distinguished by their symmetry: the spatially homogeneous ferromagnetic vacuum with zero energy, spiral states with translation invariance in one direction and negative energy per unit area, and skyrmion lattices, also with negative energy per unit area. As expected, the ground state is always ferromagnetic for sufficiently strong magnetic fields. For sufficiently weak magnetic fields, spiral states have the lowest energy per unit area for any value of the irreducible DMI parameter
b
. However, their precise nature depends on the value of
b
and on the direction of the magnetic field relative to the DMI vectors in a way that we have only begun to explore in this paper. Finally, magnetic skyrmion phases appear for intermediate magnetic field strengths, but only for a small range of values for
b
, and only for magnetic fields fairly close to orthogonal to the plane spanned by the DMI vectors. This includes the axisymmetric
b=1
case which supports skyrmion phases for the largest range of magnetic field strengths and orientations.

Our results are consistent with the extensive mathematical literature on the stability of chiral magnetic skyrmions. In the much studied case of an axisymmetric Bloch DMI term together with a Zeeman and anisotropy term, it is known that magnetic skyrmions minimize the energy in the plane among all configurations with degree
-1
, provided the strength of the DMI term is bounded by that of the Zeeman term in a suitable sense
[
16
]
, but that the skyrmion becomes unstable in a regime where the DMI term dominates and where helical configurations can have arbitrarily negative energy
[
21
]
. Similar stability results hold for the model with an axisymmetric DMI term in compact domains, when the ferromagnetic vacuum is imposed as a Dirichlet boundary condition. Magnetic skyrmions minimize the energy in such models, provided the DMI coefficient is sufficiently small
[
25
]
.

The picture that emerges from
[
16
,
21
]
is that, for a fixed strength of the DMI terms, skyrmions of degree
-1
and with negative energy exist for finite and often small range of magnetic field strengths. There is evidence from the the study of asymptotic forces in
[
8
]
and the absence of degree
-2
solutions, that the degree
-1
skyrmions always repel. As anticipated in our Introduction and confirmed in our analysis, the combination of negative energies per skyrmion and the repulsion between them allows for the formation of lattices. This is quite different from the more familiar mechanism for the formation of soliton lattices through attractive forces between solitons of positive energy. Intuitively, one can think of the chiral magnetic skyrmions lattices as a dynamical equilibrium state, where skyrmions are constantly moving away from each other and new skyrmions form in the void between them to lower the total energy of the configuration.

It would clearly be interesting to have unified and, if possible, rigorous analytical understanding of ground states in chiral magnets, combining and linking results on energy minimizers for fixed topological charge in the plane
[
16
,
21
]
or in compact domains
[
25
]
with ours on energy minimizers in the plane for any topological charge. Since our method for finding skyrmion lattices relies on finding energy minimizing configurations for a fixed torus, a first and important step would be to understand when there are negative energy skyrmions on a fixed torus, for arbitrary DMI terms and external magnetic fields.
