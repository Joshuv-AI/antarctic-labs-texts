Pollinator functional group abundance and floral heterogeneity in an agroecological context affect mating patterns in a self‐incompatible wild plant
Authors: Audrey Labonté, Valérie Le Corre, Annick Matejicek, Emeline Felten, Mélinda Turpin, Emilien Laurent, Séverine Michel, Chantal Ducourtieux, Eric Vieren, Willem Proesmans, Violaine Deytieux, Stéphane Cordeau, Adam J Vanbergen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremiseRestoration of seminatural field margins can elevate pollinator activity. However, how they support wild plant gene flow through interactions between pollinators and spatiotemporal gradients in floral resources remains largely unknown.MethodsUsing a farm‐scale experiment, we tested how mating outcomes (expected heterozygosity and paternity correlation) of the wild, self‐incompatible plant Cyanus segetum transplanted into field margins (sown wildflower or grass‐legume strips) were affected by the abundance of different pollinator functional groups (defined by species traits). We also investigated how the maternal plant attractiveness, conspecific pollen donor density, and heterospecific floral richness and density interacted with pollinator functional group abundance to modulate C. segetum mating outcomes.ResultsMultiple paternity increased (=lower paternity correlation) with greater local abundance of hoverflies (syrphids) and female medium‐sized wild bees (albeit the latter's effect diminished with decreasing maternal plant attractiveness), and the presence of male bumblebees (Bombus) under low local floral richness. Cyanus segetum progeny genetic diversity increased with male Bombus presence but decreased with greater abundance of syrphids and honey bees (Apis mellifera).ConclusionsOverall, field margins supported plant‐pollinator interactions ensuring multiple paternity and conservation of allelic diversity in C. segetum progeny. The contribution to plant mating outcomes of different pollinator functional groups was dictated by their local abundance or traits affecting pollen transfer efficiency. The local floral richness or maternal plant attractiveness sometimes modulated these relationships. This complex response of wild plant mating patterns to community interactions has implications for the use of field margins to restore functional pollination systems in farmed landscapes.Keywords: agroecology, cornflower, Cyanus segetum [Asteraceae], field borders, floral community, multilocus paternity correlation, plant breeding structure, plant‐pollinator interactions, pollinator functional groups, progeny genetic diversity

The role of insect pollination in the sexual reproduction of most plant species makes it an essential ecosystem service underpinning crop production and ecosystem functioning (Klein et al., 2007; Potts et al., 2016; Ollerton, 2017). Plant‐pollinator relationships are currentlythreatened, however, by numerous anthropogenic pressures, agricultural intensification among the foremost (Potts et al., 2016; Dicks et al., 2021). Intensive practices (e.g., massive pesticide use) and losses of habitat converted into crop fields reduce wild plant and pollinator abundance and diversity, and disrupt pollination processes (Fried et al., 2009; Nieto et al., 2014; Vanbergen, 2014).

Ecological intensification, by harnessing nature‐based processes to support yields, is one of the main available strategies to reconcile agroecosystem functionality with the maintaining of agricultural productivity (Bommarco et al., 2013; Vanbergen et al., 2020). One strategy available to farmers is the restoration of agroecological infrastructures within farmed landscape (Wezel et al., 2014; Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2017; Vanbergen et al., 2020). “Agroecological infrastructure” refers to uncropped, seminatural habitats integrated into the agricultural landscape to enhance the provision of ecosystem services (Vanbergen et al., 2020). Notably, it includes the establishment of targeted uncropped field margins (e.g., wildflower or grass‐legume strips) that provide diversified plant resources to service‐providing organisms (Nicholls and Altieri, 2013; Grass et al., 2016). This serves as a lever to restore the biotic interactions underlying agroecosystem multifunctionality (Marshall et al., 2003; Wezel et al., 2014; Gaba et al., 2020), while limiting competition with crops and subsequent yield losses (Fried et al., 2009; H. Albrecht et al., 2016; Wietzke et al., 2020).

A challenge is to ensure that the benefits of field margins persist between years in frequently disturbed agri‐environments while minimizing management costs (e.g., resowing; Noordijk et al., 2011; M. Albrecht et al., 2021). This requires the restoration of functional plant‐pollinator relationships that allow for efficient pollen transfer to maintain wild flowering plant reproduction and population persistence. While numerous studies provided evidence that such herbaceous field margins support local pollinator abundance, crop pollination or pest regulation services (Feltham et al., 2015; Grass et al., 2016; Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2017; Ouvrard et al., 2018; Rundlöf et al., 2018; Tschumi et al., 2020), very few have investigated their functionality for wild plant pollination and reproduction (e.g., Monasterolo et al., 2022).

For most plants, successful reproduction relies at least partly on sexual reproduction. Gene mixing enabled by sexual reproduction reduces inbreeding and underpins population genetic diversity, which offers selective advantages in changeable, disturbed ecosystems such as agricultural landscapes (Eckert et al., 2010; Barrett and Harder, 2017). Genetic diversity of plant progeny benefits from efficient high‐quality pollination delivering conspecific pollen from a variety of paternal donors (Valverde et al., 2019). In the majority of flowering plant species, gene flow is primarily conditioned by the availability and efficiency of insect pollinators (Ollerton et al., 2011).

Pollinator efficiency for plant reproduction can be understood as the product of pollinator visitation frequency (abundance) and pollen transfer efficiency per visit driven by morphological, ecological, and behavioral traits of pollinators (Herrera, 1987, 1989; Schupp et al., 2017; Valverde et al., 2019). Traits promoting greater pollinator mobility imply a potential for longer‐distance pollen flow and a larger genetic pool of pollen donors among plants (Breed et al., 2015; Torres‐Vanegas et al., 2021). Mobility varies according to pollinator sex and traits such as nesting ecology, sociality, and body size (Gathmann and Tscharntke, 2002; Ne'eman et al., 2006; Greenleaf et al., 2007; M. Albrecht et al., 2008; Rader et al., 2011). For instance, as central‐place foragers, female bees have limited foraging distances compared to male bees, which are not limited to provisioning the nest (Ne'eman et al., 2006; G. P. Smith et al., 2019) and to free‐ranging, highly mobile hoverflies (Jauker et al., 2009; Doyle et al., 2020). Other traits such as greater pollinator hairiness (Stavert et al., 2016), life‐cycle dependence on pollen or nectar (Kearns and Inouye, 1997), and trait matching with plants (e.g., tongue length; Garibaldi et al., 2015; van Rijn and Wackers, 2016; Klumpers et al., 2019) can elevate pollination success by affecting the quantity and composition of pollen transferred. Specific foraging behaviors such as grooming or bee storing of pollen in nests (Devaux et al., 2014; Parker et al., 2015) can also reduce pollen transfer efficiency.

Furthermore, plant‐pollinator interactions occur within complex communities of multiple species and traits, which may affect contributions of different pollinator functional groups to genetic plant mating outcomes. Spatiotemporal heterogeneity of pollen or nectar rewards in floral communities may modulate pollinator foraging (Jha and Kremen, 2013; Gallagher and Campbell, 2020) and pollen transfer among conspecific plants (Lázaro et al., 2009; Hegland, 2014; Evans et al., 2017) due to competition or facilitation among plants for pollination services.

The timing and duration of individual flowering (i.e., flowering phenology) further influences plant gene flow and reproduction by filtering the plant‐pollinator species assemblages and interactions that an individual plant is exposed to, locally and in the wider landscape (Rafferty and Ives, 2012; Gallagher and Campbell, 2020; Arroyo‐Correa et al., 2021). This means that pollination systems are dynamic and can be distinct across ecological scales (Lázaro et al., 2009; Hegland, 2014; Arroyo‐Correa et al., 2021), generating context‐dependent plant reproductive outcomes. For instance, in florally rich and dense environments, larger individual floral displays may increase mating opportunities through pollinator attraction (Karron et al., 2006; Hegland, 2014; Akter et al., 2017). However, abundant floral offerings within a patch of conspecific maternal plants may also reduce the diversity of paternal pollen donors in the progeny (O'Connell et al., 2018) by concentrating pollinator activity and pollen transfer among nearby individuals (Harder and Barrett, 1995; Brys and Jacquemyn, 2010). Similarly, greater floral richness and density in the local plant assemblage may concentrate pollinator activity in ways that either elevate the diversity of pollen donors (Vanbergen et al., 2014) or reduce pollen transfer distances and thus the diversity of potential mating partners (Ghazoul, 2006). Diverse and abundant heterospecific floral resources may also disrupt conspecific pollen transfer for a plant species that is locally rare (Evans et al., 2017). Generally, greater population density will enhance progeny fitness by reducing inbreeding depression risks and strengthening pollen grain competition for ovule fertilization (Pannell and Labouche, 2013; Barrett and Harder, 2017).

Therefore, the interplay between spatiotemporal heterogeneity of floral resources (conspecific and heterospecific) and pollinator behavioral plasticity can alter pollination competition, interference, and facilitation among plant species and individuals, ultimately determining individual plant reproductive outcomes (Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2013).

Many works have investigated the effects of floral context on outcrossing rates in plant species with mixed mating systems (e.g., Karron and Mitchell, 2012; Christopher et al., 2021). However, only a few studies (e.g., O'Connell et al., 2018) have explored how conspecific and heterospecific floral resources modify plant mating outcomes of pollination interactions in self‐incompatible plant species. Self‐incompatibility mechanisms mean that the progeny of any maternal plant results from outcrossing. Therefore, mating outcomes vary mainly in terms of the diversity of paternal contributions to the progeny (number of pollen donors) and the progeny genetic diversity (Barrett and Harder, 2017). The diversity of paternal contributions can be evaluated through its reciprocal measure, the multilocus correlation of paternity (rpm; Ritland, 2002), and the progeny genetic diversity can be measured by the expected heterozygosity (Hs; Nei, 1978).

We used an existing farm‐scale agroecological experiment to investigate how plant mating outcomes (genetic diversity: expected heterozygosity, Hs; pollen donor diversity: multilocus paternity correlation, rpm) in herbaceous field margins was modulated by floral heterogeneity and pollinator functional group abundance.

We transplanted individuals of a self‐incompatible, entomophilous, and pollinator‐rewarding wild plant species, Cyanus segetum Hill, into targeted herbaceous field margins (sown wildflower strips and grass‐legume strips with additional spontaneous flora) providing spatiotemporal heterogeneity in local floral resources. We studied how C. segetum mating outcomes varied with the abundance of pollinator functional groups (defined according to traits affecting pollen flow and plant progeny genetic diversity), the maternal plant attractiveness (described as the plot‐scale visitation rate weighted by the maternal plant floral display in relation to the total plot‐level floral display), and the neighboring conspecific and heterospecific floral resources (density of potential conspecific pollen donors, heterospecific floral richness and density).

We tested the following predictions:

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Focal plant species

Cyanus segetum Hill, 1762 [Asteraceae] (syn. = Centaurea cyanus) is an annual segetal plant species. It is self‐incompatible (rarely pseudo‐self‐incompatible) and requires insect pollination to produce seeds (Bellanger et al., 2014), with no clonal reproduction occurring in natural conditions (V. Le Corre, personal observation). Its spring to early‐summer bloom (May–July) provides flower‐visiting insects with good quality pollen and nectar resources (Hicks et al., 2016). Up to 100–200 capitula can be produced by each individual plant over its flowering period (Bellanger et al., 2015). Each capitulum comprises 5–10 peripheral, sterile ray florets aiming at attracting pollinators and 10–20 inner tubular disc florets, which are hermaphrodite and mono‐ovulate (Penet et al., 2012). Mean capitulum longevity is ~4 d (Thomann et al., 2015) and seed ripening takes an average of 12 d (Darmency et al., 2016).

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted from May to July 2019 on the INRAE CA‐SYS experimental farm (Bretenière, Burgundy, France, 47°19′06.7″N 5°04′17.6″E). Established in 2018, this farm‐scale agroecological system experiment (125 ha) implements spatiotemporal crop diversity and agroecological infrastructure (i.e., targeted seminatural habitats created to enhance ecosystem services) to test zero‐pesticide farming systems (Vanbergen et al., 2020). Agroecological infrastructure on the experimental farm was designed as a network of perennial uncropped field margins across the farm landscape, which included sowing 6.8 ha (22.67 km × 3 m) of grass‐legume strips (seven species) and 2.69 ha (8.96 km × 3 m) of wildflower strips (37 species). Their function is to provide year‐round floral resources to support native communities of pollinators and natural biocontrol agents that regulate weeds and pests. The seed mixtures were carefully selected to ensure a complementary and self‐sustaining plant assemblage over time, while preventing competitive dominance (Appendix S1). Cyanus segetum was included in the seed mix designed for wildflower strips, but not for grass‐legume strips. Both types of uncropped herbaceous field margins were installed in October–November 2018 in the same broad soil type across the farm (clay‐limestone soils of medium depth, 50–90 cm). This installation involved tilling the soil in both zones to the same depth (plowing at 25 cm deep, rotary hoe at 10 cm deep), then sowing the respective seed mixes into the relevant predefined strips. Since establishment, herbaceous field margins have not been retilled or resown, and no NPK fertilizer is applied. The only management intervention is periodic cutting of aboveground plant biomass (grass‐legume strips: twice a year mid‐May and in the winter; wildflower strips: once every two years in the winter).

In mid‐March 2019, we transplanted 144 individuals of C. segetum, split into 16 plots (six in grass‐legume strips and 10 in wildflower strips) of nine individuals (three triplets, 100 cm apart). The plots were ≥150 m apart (Appendix S2). Cyanus segetum focal individuals originated from seeds obtained from ARBIOTECH (http://www.arbiotech.com; Saint‐Gilles, France). They were germinated in controlled conditions (22 ± 3°C; 16 h light: 8 h dark), then submitted to vernalization (6–8°C) when they reached the two‐ to four‐leaf stage. They were transplanted into the field experiment after 46–50 d, at the rosette stage. The term “focal” is used to distinguish the 144 transplanted C. segetum from the numerous conspecifics present in wildflower strips from the seed mix (nonfocal).

Reproductive development of focal C. segetum maternal plants

The composite flowers, floral buds, wilted flowers, and fruits produced by each focal C. segetum individual maternal plant were counted monthly. We combined these measurements with data on insect visitation to focal plants (recorded every 2 wk; see below) to estimate the flowering period of each focal maternal plant (precision to the week; Appendix S3). We harvested all focal plants at the end of the C. segetum flowering period (mid‐July). We counted the total number of floral heads (fruits with or without seeds) as a measure of the size of the individual floral display of maternal plants and collected the seeds.

Genetic diversity of the focal maternal plants' progeny

Although all focal individuals reached the reproductive stage, financial costs precluded genotyping all parents and progeny, so we randomly selected 33 focal maternal plants among the 64 plants which had produced ≥20 seeds that successfully germinated, from 12 plots out of 16 with a maximum of three maternal plants per plot (one per triplet). We sampled 20 plants from seven plots in wildflower strips, and 13 plants from five plots in grass‐legume strips.

According to the number of viable seeds available (i.e., smooth, swollen, and slightly dark‐colored), between 20 and 100 seeds per focal maternal plant were germinated in controlled conditions (22 ± 3°C; 16 h light: 8 h dark) in June 2020. After 8 d, cotyledons were collected and we extracted DNA from 17 randomly selected individual offspring. Maternal (from leaf samples) and offspring DNA extractions were performed following the same rapid procedure (Délye et al., 2002). DNA extracts were stored at −20°C prior to dilution (1:25) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis.

Microsatellite markers for C. segetum were developed by GenoScreen (Lille, France), based on one run of Illumina MiSeq sequencing (paired ends, 250 bp reads). Microsatellites were identified and PCR primers designed using QDD version 3 (Meglécz et al., 2014). After marker quality assessment through PCR and electrophoresis, we preselected 12 microsatellite markers for a first genotyping assay (n = 384 DNA samples). PCR amplification and capillary electrophoresis on an ABI 3730XL sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California, USA) were performed at Gentyane (Clermont‐Ferrand, France). PCR amplifications consisted of a first denaturation step at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 39 cycles of amplification at 95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 10 s, and 72°C for 30 s, followed by a final step at 72°C for 5 min. Four control samples as well as a negative control (water without DNA) were included in each sequencing plate to ensure scoring consistency. Genotypes were scored using Peak Scanner version 1.0 (Applied Biosystems).

We used Micro‐Checker version 2.2.3 (Van Oosterhout et al., 2004) to detect stuttering patterns, large‐allele dropout, and null alleles. We retained the eight markers (Appendix S4) that offered the best compromise between high allele number and low occurrence of null alleles. The final data set comprised the genotypes at eight loci for 593 samples (33 parents + progeny). Null alleles were detected at six of the eight loci. At these loci, and in case of unmatching genotypes between a homozygous maternal plant and one or more homozygous offspring, we assumed the presence of a null allele, and manually corrected apparently missing data to null homozygotes and apparent homozygotes to null heterozygotes (Kestel et al., 2021). In total, there were 27 missing genotypes, distributed among seven families.

The allelic variability at the eight microsatellite markers used was checked by calculating, for the maternal plant (n = 33) and the offspring (n = 549) populations separately, (1) the mean number of alleles (NbA) per locus; (2) the mean observed heterozygosity (Ho) per locus; and (3) the mean expected heterozygosity (Hs) per locus (Nei, 1978). We also calculated the total number of alleles (eight loci) of each maternal plant. All these calculations were realized using the R package “adegenet” (Jombart, 2008).

To characterize C. segetum mating patterns, we calculated the expected heterozygosity (Hs) per progeny using “adegenet,” and the multilocus paternity correlation (rpm) per progeny using MLTR (Ritland, 2002). The expected heterozygosity (Hs; Nei, 1978) quantifies gene diversity by providing the probability that two alleles randomly taken from the progeny genotypes are of different types. It ranges from 0 (no allelic diversity in the progeny) to 1 (high allelic diversity). The multilocus paternity correlation (rpm; Ritland, 2002) quantifies the extent to which different individual offspring of a given maternal plant originate from different pollen donors (0 = all different pollen donors; 1 = one single pollen donor). The number of effective pollen donors (N ep ) can be derived from rpm as N ep = 1/rpm. One would expect a negative correlation between Hs and rpm, because the genetic diversity in the progeny is likely to increase when more different pollen donors (i.e., lower values of paternity correlation rpm) contribute to reproduction. We thus checked this relationship using a linear model (LM).

Local floral assemblage

Centered on each focal plant plot (nine maternal plants), we established a transect (n = 16,100 m × 2 m transects) to assess the floral resources and plant‐pollinator interactions (see below) in the local plant‐pollinator assemblage surrounding the focal maternal plants. The number of entomophilous (Eudicotyledon) flowering plant individuals and their total number of floral units (a single or composite flower, spike, or umbel) were surveyed monthly in six systematically placed quadrats (0.5 × 2 m) per transect. From this, we calculated mean floral richness (Appendix S5), mean floral density (m−2; Appendix S5), and mean density (m−2) of potential conspecific pollen donors (nonfocal flowering C. segetum) in the neighboring community. The distinction in floral resource diversity and abundance between grass‐legume strips and wildflower strips was much less than anticipated due to spontaneous flora germinating from the preexisting soil seed bank (Appendix S1). This spontaneous emergence created a spatiotemporal gradient in floral richness and density across the types of field margins rather than a categorical distinction between them. For each focal maternal plant, these plant assemblage metrics were calculated to match the flowering period of the maternal plant (Labonté et al., 2023).

Plant‐pollinator interactions from individual to local assemblage scales

We quantified plant‐pollinator interactions (actively feeding or contact with floral stamen/anthers by visiting insects) to the focal C. segetum individual maternal plants (plot) and in the local multispecies floral assemblage (transect) matched to focal plant flowering phenology (Labonté et al., 2023). We conducted bimonthly standardized sampling sessions on the 16 focal plant plots (15 min observing up to nine flowering C. segetum) and in the 16 local floral assemblages (15 min transect observing all flowering species). Sampling was performed from 09:30 a.m. to 17:30 p.m., in dry and warm weather (air temperature ≥14°C) and with minimal wind (Beaufort scale <4/5). On each occasion (1–3 d for sampling all plots/transects), we randomized the sampling order of the plots/transects to avoid introducing a systematic bias. In total, three to five sampling rounds were conducted on each focal plant plot (depending on the flowering period of the focal maternal plants), for a total of 45–75 min of effective observation, and five sampling rounds were conducted on each local assemblage (transects) during the study period. Insect visitors were captured immediately after landing on a floral unit to avoid resampling. Apoidea and Syrphidae were subsequently identified to species using standard keys (Appendix S6).

We quantified the relative attractiveness (for foraging pollinators) of each focal maternal plant, described as the plot‐scale visitation rate per 15 min (all pollinators) weighted by the total individual floral display relative to the total plot‐level floral display (Appendix S5).

Local abundance of pollinator functional groups

The insect pollinator specimens from species observed visiting focal and nonfocal C. segetum on the farm were classified into 10 functional groups based on those proposed by Fenster et al. (2009) and Valverde et al. (2019). These functional groups were defined according to taxonomic and ecological traits likely to play a role in the genetic component of pollination effectiveness (Table 1). These traits were (1) the degree of life cycle dependence on pollen and nectar (Kearns and Inouye, 1997); (2) the nesting ecology and sociality (Batra, 1966; Knerer and Plateaux‐Quénu, 1970; Plateaux‐Quénu and Plateaux, 1994; Strohm and Bordon‐Hauser, 2003; Plateaux‐Quénu, 2008; Drossart et al., 2019), body size (<5 mm; 5–10 mm; >10 mm), and sex constraining foraging and pollen transfer distances (Gathmann and Tscharntke, 2002; Ne'eman et al., 2006; Greenleaf et al., 2007; M. Albrecht et al., 2008; G. P. Smith et al., 2019); (3) hairiness and foraging behavior (grooming/packing, etc.) affecting the potential quantity of conspecific pollen transferred (Devaux et al., 2014; Parker et al., 2015; Stavert et al., 2016); and (4) tongue length determining access to flower corollas and hence the degree of foraging specialization (van Rijn and Wackers, 2016; Klumpers et al., 2019).

The 10 functional groups discriminated were as follows: workers of Apis mellifera (managed western honey bee); females of Bombus spp.; males of Bombus spp.; females of large wild bees; males of large wild bees; females of medium wild bees; males of medium wild bees; females of small wild bees; males of small wild bees; and hoverflies (Syrphidae).

For each functional group, we calculated the abundance of individual insects that were actively visiting flowers in the neighborhood of the maternal plant during its flowering period, including visitation to conspecific individuals in the plot and to all flowering plant species in the local assemblage. Therefore, for each focal maternal plant, the local abundance of each functional group was calculated based on 90–150 min of observation (depending on the maternal plant's flowering period).

Statistical analyses

We estimated sampling completeness for: (1) the pollinator community active in the multispecies floral assemblages of herbaceous field margins across the experimental farm from mid‐May to mid‐July (combining plant‐pollinator interaction data from the 16 transects throughout the sampling period), (2) the pollinator community active on focal (16 plots) and nonfocal (10 transects in wildflower strips) C. segetum in herbaceous field margins (combining focal and nonfocal C. segetum visitation data from the 16 focal plant plots and the 10 transects in wildflower strips), and (3) the pollinator community active in the multispecies floral assemblages of each studied field margin (10 wildflower strips and six grass‐legume strips) from mid‐May to mid‐July (combining, for each transect separately, plant‐pollinator interaction data obtained throughout the sampling period). Sampling completeness was estimated as the percentage of sample coverage (sensu Chao and Jost, 2012; i.e., “the proportion of the total number of individuals in a community that belong to the species represented in the sample”) in response to the number of pollinator individuals sampled, using the iNEXT package (Hsieh et al., 2016) on R version 4.2.0 implemented in RStudio (R Core Team, 2022).

We used linear models (LM from package “stats”; R Core Team, 2022) to explain the genetic diversity of the focal maternal plants' progeny. Two proxies of progeny genetic diversity were fitted as responses: the expected heterozygosity (Hs) and the multilocus paternity correlation (rpm) in the progeny (n = 33 families). The total number of alleles (Na) of the maternal plant (including null alleles when detected) in the eight loci used for genotyping was controlled for in the analysis of Hs by fitting it as a covariate.

We fitted as fixed effects different predictors expected to influence plant‐pollinator interactions at different ecological scales and plant mating patterns. We avoided the input of highly correlated variables (>70% Pearson coefficient) into the same model to avoid confounding effects. At the individual plant scale, we fitted in each model the relative attractiveness of the maternal plant (n visits/individual maternal plant/15 min; Appendix S5). At the scale of the local plant‐pollinator assemblage, we fitted floral richness and density (m−2; Appendix S5), density of potential nonfocal conspecific pollen donors (n individuals/m2), and the total abundance of eight of the pollinator functional groups described above (n individuals). The abundances of males of medium‐ and small‐sized wild bees were dropped because of high collinearity with other predictors.

All predictors were calculated from data aggregated in time to match exactly the flowering period of each maternal plant, in order to ensure that all potential predictors accurately reflected the levels of floral resources, pollinator activity, and pollen transfer efficiency to which each maternal plant was exposed during its flowering period. All predictors were scaled (z‐transformation) and some were log‐transformed where necessary to improve model fit (local abundance of male Bombus and male large wild bees in all models, and local abundance of female Bombus in models explaining paternity correlation). We chose to fit floral richness and density as continuous predictors rather than the category of “type of strip” because the former more accurately reflected spatiotemporal gradients in floral resources available to foraging insects. Plot was initially fitted as a random effect (lmer) to account for the spatial dispersion of the replicates and any unmeasured microsite conditions; however, it was dropped to avoid singularity and model overfitting since it explained near zero variability in Hs or rpm.

To test predictions 3, 4, and 5 required studying the effects of interactions between functional group abundances and the local floral heterogeneity on C. segetum mating outcomes. However, the small size of the data set prevented selecting and fitting all possible relevant interactions (n = 32) in a single global model, and no concrete and relevant reason allowed us to preferentially fit, a priori, some interactions over others. We therefore carried out a preselection of predictor interactions according to their explanatory power based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC)—relative sum of weights (ωi) contained in the top‐ranked models with ΔAIC <2 (package “MuMIn”; Bartoń, 2022). Four initial AIC selection steps (Appendix S7) were done to pre‐identify which pollinator functional group × floral predictor interactions had the potential to modulate each C. segetum mating response (Hs, rpm). In each of the four steps, we fitted (1) the main effects of pollinator functional group abundance (n = 8) and floral predictors (n = 4) and (2) the two‐way interactions of all eight functional groups with, in turn, a single floral predictor per step, but also including any interactions retained in at least one previous step. For Hs, no interaction (only main effects) was found, so the selection process was stopped after the four steps (Table 2). For rpm, we detected interactions (n = 2) with explanatory power during steps 1–4. To double‐check if the order of fit had masked other interactions, we then repeated the process in a further four preselection steps (steps 5–8) by refitting the other remaining candidate interactions accounting for the 12 main predictors and the two interactions already retained in steps 1–4 (Appendix S7; Table 3). This second preselection round confirmed the sole importance of the two interactions initially retained as potential predictors of rpm. This subset of the global list of two‐way interactions with potential to affect C. segetum genetic mating outcomes were subsequently fitted along with the main effects (eight pollinator functional groups, four floral predictors) in a final global model (Appendix S7; Table 3).

RESULTS

Cyanus segetum mating outcomes

The microsatellite markers used had between three and nine identified alleles in the studied C. segetum population. The C. segetum maternal plant population (n = 33) and the offspring population (n = 549) had, on average, respectively, 4.75 ± 1.75 and 6.13 ± 2.03 alleles per locus (mean ± SD; see Appendix S8 for allelic frequencies in the offspring population). The maternal plant population had genetic diversity measures per locus of Ho = 0.67 ± 0.25 and Hs = 0.62 ± 0.13, while the offspring population had Ho and Hs per locus were of 0.59 ± 0.19 and 0.60 ± 0.14, respectively (mean ± SD).

The offspring population had a multilocus outcrossing rate tm of 1 (=100% outcrossed), confirming self‐incompatibility of the C. segetum population and indicating that gene flow was mediated by pollinator activity. Among the progeny of the 33 C. segetum maternal plants, Hs varied from 0.45 to 0.58 and rpm ranged from 0 (no shared father in the progeny array) to 0.37 (2.7 fathers), with an average value of 0.173 ± 1.75, which equates to 5.78 ± 0.57 different pollen donors on average per C. segetum maternal family (mean ± SD). As expected, progeny Hs increased with the total number of alleles (eight loci) of the maternal plant (Table 2; Figure 1D).

We confirmed that Hs and rpm were only weakly negatively correlated for our C. segetum population (β ± SE = −0.06 ± 0.07; t = −1.00; P > 0.1).

Influence of the composition of the local plant‐pollinator assemblage on C. segetum mating outcomes

Overall, we sampled 94% of the pollinator community active in herbaceous field margins across the farm and 97% of the pollinator community active on focal (plots) and nonfocal (transects) C. segetum individuals across the farm during the study period (Appendix S9). We also sampled 83 ± 7.5% (mean ± SD) of the pollinator community active in each local plant assemblage during the study period (Appendix S10).

With respectively 126, 79, and 66 visits dedicated to C. segetum, A. mellifera, female medium wild bees, and syrphids dominated the C. segetum pollinator guild (Table 1; Appendix S11). They were also the most overall abundant pollinator functional groups observed in the field margins (Figure 1). Male Bombus, A. mellifera, and female large wild bees displayed the strongest floral preference to C. segetum with, respectively, 100%, 86%, and 81% of their foraging visits dedicated to C. segetum (Figure 1).

The local abundance of male Bombus positively affected Hs (Table 2; Figure 1A) and interacted with the local floral richness to reduce rpm, indicating greater numbers of pollen donors (Table 3; Figure 2A). The local abundance of syrphids had a negative influence on both Hs and rpm (Tables 2 and 3; Figures 1B and 2B). Hs was also weakly negatively correlated with the local abundance of A. mellifera (Table 2; Figure 1C). The local abundance of female medium wild bees had a negative relationship with rpm, which was accentuated by high maternal plant attractiveness (Table 3; Figure 2C).

DISCUSSION

Overall, our results confirm that pollinator groups possessing distinct functional traits have different effects on plant mating patterns (Rhodes et al., 2017; Valverde et al., 2019). Cyanus segetum mating outcomes (Hs, rpm) mainly relied on particularly abundant pollinator functional groups (A. mellifera, female medium wild bees and syrphids) that dominated C. segetum visitation in the surveyed field margins (Vázquez et al., 2007; Maldonado et al., 2013). Cyanus segetum mating patterns were also influenced by the preferential foraging of functional groups, particularly male Bombus, which despite relatively low abundance had a strong positive influence on the diversity of pollen donors (rpm). Moreover, most pollinator functional groups influencing C. segetum mating patterns were highly mobile, suggesting that pollinator foraging range is a major determinant of plant gene flow (Breed et al., 2015; Torres‐Vanegas et al., 2021). The individual maternal plant attractiveness and the floral richness in the neighboring assemblage modulated the effect of pollinator contributions to mating patterns in this self‐incompatible plant species. This new finding highlights how complex biotic interactions can influence the reproduction of a self‐incompatible wild plant in herbaceous field margins. However, unexpectedly, the local density of conspecific potential pollen donors was not a major driver of C. segetum progeny diversity and did not modulate pollinator efficiency. This contrasts with previous results (O'Connell et al., 2018) and may be due to the high frequency of C. segetum individuals in the many sown wildflower strips across the farm (see Appendix S2), preventing any transplanted focal maternal plant from being spatially (and thus genetically) isolated from others.

Cyanus segetum mating outcomes

Cyanus segetum gene flow was mediated by pollinator activity alone (Bellanger et al., 2014) as shown by C. segetum multilocus outcrossing rate reaching 100% (tm = 1; Bellanger et al., 2015). Indeed, this confirms self‐incompatibility in the studied C. segetum population, preventing accidental self‐pollination due to agitation of maternal plant capitula by the wind or other sources.

In the 33 studied families, the genotyped offspring (n = 12–17 per family) were, on average, from six different pollen donors, which indicates quite efficient pollen transfer in regard to pollen donor diversity. Moreover, in comparison to the genotyped maternal plant population, new alleles were observed in the offspring that were contributed by other, ungenotyped plants (focal and nonfocal) across the farm. However, despite various male contributions, values of genetic diversity (Ho, Hs) per locus were almost equivalent in the offspring and maternal plant populations. Similarly, variations in rpm among families were not necessarily associated with differences in Hs. This was likely caused by the uneven distribution of alleles in the population, with most markers displaying a few frequent alleles and several rare ones (Appendix S8). Rare alleles contribute only weakly to Hs (Nei et al., 1975), but when present in a progeny they may allow determination of paternity.

Insect‐mediated pollen transfer occurring in the field margins appeared sufficient to ensure high pollen donor diversity and to maintain numerous alleles in the offspring population. This evidence of important gene mixing could be encouraging for the persistence of genetically fit C. segetum populations in field margins (Eckert et al., 2010; Barrett and Harder, 2017). As an obligate outcrossing plant species, C. segetum is likely to be affected by inbreeding depression—that is, a reduction in offspring fitness caused by an increased likelihood of homozygosity for deleterious alleles. By reducing homozygote frequencies, polyandry is thus beneficial for population persistence (Pannell and Labouche, 2013).

Influence of the composition of the local plant‐pollinator assemblage on C. segetum mating outcomes

Bombus males displayed a strong floral preference for C. segetum (Wolf and Moritz, 2014) and were positively associated with both the genetic diversity (Hs) and the diversity of paternal pollen donors (as indicated by low values of rpm) in the progeny of C. segetum maternal plants. Given that this positive effect occurred at low levels of abundance, it appears that it is the presence of this functional group that is critical to C. segetum mating success. Due to hairiness, one single male Bombus individual is able to carry a much bigger pollen load than other pollinators found in this study (Stavert et al., 2016). Moreover, this pollen load is entirely free for floral deposition, as opposed to the pollen carried in the corbiculae of female Bombus (Parker et al., 2015). Large‐bodied Bombus species, and especially the males, which are not constrained to provide pollen to the nest, are also able to ensure long‐distance pollen transfer (G. P. Smith et al., 2019), which promotes the diversity of plant mating opportunities. Altogether, this trait combination suggests that individual male Bombus were very efficient pollinators of C. segetum in this agroecological context, in terms of both quantity and diversity of conspecific pollen transferred (Ostevik et al., 2010; Wolf and Moritz, 2014).

However, the positive effect of male Bombus on pollen donor diversity was modulated by the level of floral richness, with lower numbers of pollen donors (=higher rpm) where floral species richness was highest. This suggests that the florally rich assemblages may have kept male Bombus foraging locally (Ghazoul, 2006), thereby promoting mating with a reduced number of potential C. segetum pollen donors. Nevertheless, C. segetum mating outcomes did not directly respond to the local floral richness or its interaction with other pollinator functional groups. Given that florally diverse field margins provide valuable support to diverse pollinator communities (Grass et al., 2016; Ouvrard et al., 2018), less efficient Bombus pollination of wild plants may thus be compensated by the foraging activity of other pollinator groups (Rader et al., 2011). By contrast, wild plant gene flow in less diverse field margins (and more broadly in less heterogeneous agricultural landscapes) may rely more strongly on a stable subset of mobile, generalist pollinators such as Bombus (Redhead et al., 2018; Zografou et al., 2020; Maurer et al., 2024), which can also display floral preferences (Wolf and Moritz, 2014).

Cyanus segetum progeny rpm decreased (=greater number of pollen donors) when there was greater local abundance of foraging female medium‐sized wild bees. This functional group exhibited a relatively high floral preference for C. segetum to which it dedicated half of its observed visits. This is consistent with the fact that it was dominated by Halictidae, which, although most species are opportunistic, tend to prefer Asteraceae (Falk, 2015; Dikmen et al., 2019; Drossart et al., 2019). As central‐place foragers of medium body size, these bees foraging over a few hundreds of meters are less mobile than larger social bees, male bees, or hoverflies (Ne'eman et al., 2006; Jauker et al., 2009; Wright et al., 2015). Despite this limited foraging range, due to their high abundance and diversity, it is possible that a given C. segetum maternal plant fell within the foraging range of multiple individuals of these female medium‐sized bees, each of which could transfer pollen from other C. segetum individuals. Cumulatively, these individual contributions may have enabled multiple plant mating opportunities.

Moreover, this positive effect of cumulative individual contributions may have been crucial to counteract the negative effect of maternal plant attractiveness on pollen donor diversity (high rpm), as indicated by the observed interaction between female medium‐sized wild bees and focal plant attractiveness. By contrast, at the lowest levels of maternal plant attractiveness, even low abundances of female medium‐sized wild bees enabled high pollen donor diversity (=low rpm). A possible explanation is that greater maternal plant attractiveness generated competition among attracted pollinators, so that halictids may have been outcompeted by larger pollinator species (Wignall et al., 2020; Ropars et al., 2022). This may have constrained them to move more frequently between less attractive C. segetum individuals to fulfill their foraging needs, therefore enhancing the diversity of pollen donors even at low visitation rates.

It is interesting to notice the contrasting effect of maternal plant attractiveness on the genetic components of C. segetum reproductive success versus seed production (Karron and Mitchell, 2012; Akter et al., 2017). In another analysis from the same experiment, we found a positive response of seed set to individual focal plant attractiveness due to a greater production of flowers attracting pollinator visits (Labonté et al., 2023). Although this promotes more C. segetum mating events, the genetic diversity of the progeny is not necessarily increased because insect visitation is concentrated on the density of flowers offered by the maternal plant and the surrounding conspecific plant patch (Hegland, 2014; Akter et al., 2017), thereby favoring outcrossing between nearby individuals (Harder and Barrett, 1995; Brys and Jacquemyn, 2010). This illustrates the complex functional implications and trade‐offs of plant‐pollinator interactions for plant reproduction.

Cyanus segetum progeny Hs and rpm both decreased when the local abundance of Syrphidae increased, suggesting that syrphids play an important role in supporting C. segetum outcrossing with a diversity of pollen donors. Similar to female medium‐sized wild bees, syrphids' contribution to C. segetum gene flow also seems to be driven by the additive effect of multiple individual contributions linked to their overall abundance, rather than individual efficiency. Indeed, due to their low hairiness, syrphid individuals observed on C. segetum during this experiment are likely poor vectors of pollen (Stavert et al., 2016; but see Rader et al., 2011). Nevertheless, as a functional group with great mobility (Jauker et al., 2009; Rader et al., 2011; Doyle et al., 2020) and relatively high floral preference (Goulson and Wright, 1998) for C. segetum, when they are abundant they may facilitate long‐distance conspecific C. segetum pollen transfer from various paternal donors. However, abundant syrphids deposited pollen with low allele diversity, potentially due to uneven allele frequencies in the parent population. Indeed, Hs and rpm were only weakly negatively correlated in our study. This implies that C. segetum maternal plants visited by Syrphidae could receive pollen from multiple paternal sources, but with an unbalanced allele pool, resulting in moderate Hs values in the progeny (Nei et al., 1975). In more homogeneous agricultural landscapes, this contribution from mobile, generalist syrphids, like that of male Bombus, may be of particular importance for wild plant gene flow. In addition to their pollinator role at the adult stage, some syrphid species also provide biocontrol or decomposition services at the larva stage (Martinez‐Uña et al., 2013; Moquet et al., 2018). These flies therefore constitute crucial auxiliaries to which effort should be devoted in the perspective of ecological intensification of agriculture.

Apis mellifera was very abundant, dominated visitation to C. segetum, and is known to forage up to several kilometers (Beekman and Ratnieks, 2000). This did not support C. segetum progeny genetic diversity, however, as there was a slight negative relationship between expected heterozygosity (Hs) and the local abundance of A. mellifera workers (Marzinzig et al., 2018). Apart from unbalanced allele frequencies, potential explanations are that A. mellifera pollen‐packing behavior prevented pollen deposition (Parker et al., 2015), or that multiple floral visits within the same C. segetum individual or conspecific patch limited pollen transfer and allelic diversity (Esposito et al., 2021; Diller et al., 2022). This result illustrates the limits of studying pollination through assessments of visitation frequency alone and the need to examine mating outcomes and pollen transfer efficiency (Valverde et al., 2019).

A novel aspect of our study was the distinction between male and female bees in the definition of pollinator functional groups. Indeed, although it is rarely considered in studies investigating pollinator functional group efficiency (e.g., Fenster et al., 2009; Rhodes et al., 2017; Valverde et al., 2019), several studies have described differences in traits and behavior between males and females that affect their efficiency as pollen transfer agents (Ne'eman et al., 2006; Ostevik et al., 2010; Wolf and Moritz, 2014; G. P. Smith et al., 2019). Our results add further evidence that male and female pollinators have distinct functional roles, especially in bees whose females and males have distinct foraging and brood‐caring constraints (Parker et al., 2015; G. P. Smith et al., 2019).

Other studies have commonly used a qualitative approach by linking pollination efficiency to the identity of pollinator functional groups only (e.g., Rhodes et al., 2017; Valverde et al., 2019), whereas we quantitatively evaluated the contributions of pollinator functional group abundances to wild plant mating outcomes. Our results suggest that wild plant gene flow was maintained through both the foraging activity of highly efficient pollinator functional groups (male Bombus here) and numerous individual contributions from less efficient, but abundant, pollinator functional groups (syrphids and female medium wild bees here). This indicates that, unlike for plants with specialized floral traits (Biesmeijer et al., 2006), plants with generalized floral traits such as C. segetum may be buffered against the loss of the most effective pollinators by the contribution of less efficient, but more abundant, pollinator functional groups (Ramos‐Jiliberto et al., 2012, 2020). This may have particular importance to ensure stability in wild plant pollen transfer, and ultimately population persistence, in highly disturbed and changeable agricultural landscapes.

Our results also have important implications for the choice of ecological intensification strategies to apply in order to promote functional plant‐pollinator interactions in agroecosystems. In heterogeneous landscapes, and particularly when habitat heterogeneity also occurs at the local scale as in the experimental agroecological farm, wild plant reproduction in field margins relies on the complementary contributions of diverse, abundant pollinators with various traits (e.g., levels of mobility, foraging preferences and constraints, or individual pollen transfer efficiency) that are already present locally. In this case, the long‐term maintenance of wild plant populations in the field margin may be achieved through farm‐scale approaches, by conserving the local habitat diversity, with appropriate management and agricultural practices (Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2017).

In the more frequent situation of implementing uncropped field margins in homogeneous, intensively farmed landscapes, wild plant reproduction may rather rely on a stable subset of mobile, generalist pollinators such as male Bombus and syrphids. Support to these pollinator groups should then be provided at the landscape scale, through conservation or restoration of habitats allowing the accomplishment of their different life‐cycle stages (particularly for syrphids, whose needs evolve between larvae and adult stage; Martinez‐Uña et al., 2013; Moquet et al., 2018). These habitats depend on the pollinators targeted and can include flower‐rich habitats, providing food sources for Bombus colonies and adult syrphids; woody habitats; and ponds or cropped habitats, which are important respectively for aquatic and aphidophagous larvae of some syrphid species. Such landscape‐scale habitat restoration (or conservation) necessitates concertation and collaborative implementation, which could be favored by the development of appropriate financial support (Pe'er et al., 2017).

Limits and perspectives

A caveat to our findings is that we did not quantify the interactions beyond the field margins between C. segetum pollinators and flowering crops, or in other habitats across the farm. This may have led to overestimation of the preference for C. segetum of pollinator functional groups, particularly those that are highly mobile and generalist such as A. mellifera (Köppler et al., 2007). Although our sampling allowed us to satisfactorily characterize the pollinator community at the level of field margins across the farm and visiting C. segetum (Appendix S9), there remains a possibility of unobserved interactions, especially within each local assemblage (Appendix S10). Moreover, the lack of implementation of sown uncropped herbaceous field margins in the study region led us to conduct all samplings on a single experimental farm. Further studies should include other resource‐providing habitats at farm and landscape scales, and if possible conduct them on multiple agroecological farms, to better understand the complexity of plant‐pollinator interactions, wild plant gene flow, and reproduction in florally rich agroecological landscapes.

In this study, we focused on C. segetum, which offers very attractive and valuable resources for insect pollinators (Hicks et al., 2016). As a consequence, due caution is needed before extrapolation to other wild plant species that may be less attractive for pollinators, even if they offer support to other types of service‐providing organisms (such as predators and parasitoids). Compared to C. segetum, other plants may differently experience pollinator‐mediated competition and facilitation in herbaceous field margins varying in conspecific and heterospecific floral resources (Seifan et al., 2014). Therefore, studies on multiple plant models are needed to better understand complex pollen transfer dynamics in field margins and their consequences for wild plant mating patterns.

Moreover, C. segetum is an annual plant species. Besides efficient pollination, adequate disturbance management over time (e.g., annual mowing, soil scarifying) is needed to maintain its population by limiting successional processes toward domination by perennial plant species (Westbury et al., 2008; H. Smith et al., 2010). Our results are nevertheless encouraging for the long‐term persistence in field margins of genetically healthy populations of closely related perennial melliferous plant species (e.g., Centaurea sp.) offering comparable floral rewards to pollinators (Hicks et al., 2016).

CONCLUSIONS

Our results address an important knowledge gap about the efficiency of uncropped herbaceous field margins for the reproduction of service‐providing wild plants (Vanbergen and the Insect Pollinators Initiative, 2013). Both intrinsic pollen transfer efficiency (driven by traits and floral preferences) and pollinator abundance are likely to determine the relative contributions of different pollinator functional groups to C. segetum mating outcomes. Our results highlight the role of such field margins in preserving both diverse and abundant pollinator guilds to maintain functional plant‐pollinator interaction systems that can assist wild plant gene flow in agricultural landscapes. They also illustrate how the interplay between functionally distinct pollinators and the local floral context can affect plant mating dynamics in sown herbaceous field margins. Our results have important implications in the perspective of ecological intensification of agriculture and the conservation and restoration of functional plant‐pollinator relationships within farmed landscapes (Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2017). Such implications include encouraging perspectives regarding the long‐term maintenance, at low cost, of benefits provided by insect‐rewarding wild plants in field margins, and considerations about the scale (field, farm, or landscape) at which pollinator conservation strategies must be developed to support the pollinator groups that may be crucial to wild plant reproduction, depending on the surrounding landscape's heterogeneity.
