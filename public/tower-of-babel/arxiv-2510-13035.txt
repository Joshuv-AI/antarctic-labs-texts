Coherent Control of Wave Scattering via Coincidences of Complex Spectra
Ali H. Alhulaymi, Nazar Pyvovar, Philipp del Hougne, Owen D. Miller, A. Douglas Stone

Abstract
We introduce and validate a theoretical framework for coherent control of multichannel scattering of linear waves to route waves through complex geometries with multiple scattering. We show that steady-state perfect routing solutions are achievable at any frequency via tuning geometric parameters so that multiple complex eigenfrequencies coincide on the real axis. The relevant complex spectra describe critically constrained scattering processes (CCONs), where a specific number of generically accessible outgoing channels are not excited due to destructive interference. Focusing on electromagnetic waves, we demonstrate in simulations high discrimination routing and demultiplexing of signals in a multiport chaotic cavity with a number of tunable scatterers which can be predicted from theory. A similar approach can be used to implement other interesting functionalities, such as filtering, power division and directional lasing. The method can be applied to other classical waves and also to quantum matter waves.



I
Introduction

Control of linear waves (electromagnetic, matter and acoustic) in two- or three-dimensional multiple scattering geometries without high symmetry is a fundamental problem across many branches of physics and is also of great relevance to device engineering in electromagnetics and acoustics. Coherent control of wave scattering via interference in simple geometries (one-dimensional, two-channel, paraxial , rotationally symmetric, adiabatic) has been understood since the middle of the last century or earlier. However, there has been remarkably little progress in our understanding of achievable control in generic two and three-dimensional geometries. A well-studied example is steady-state, reflectionless scattering, achieved by destructive interference of backscattered waves, where the basic principle has been “critical coupling”, which can be applied only in a one dimensional or highly symmetric geometry, or with single channel excitation of the system
Cai et al. 2000
;
Amnon Yariv 2002
;
Joannopoulos et al. 2008
. When any such control problem is posed in a non-symmetric, higher-dimensional geometry, currently the only existing approach is black-box optimization, providing little insight or generalizability.

However, substantial progress has been made in this area in recent years. The theory of Coherent Perfect Absorption (CPA)
Chong et al. 2010
;
Wan et al. 2011
;
Baranov et al. 2017a
and Reflectionless Scattering Modes (RSM)
Sweeney et al. 2020
;
Stone et al. 2021
;
Bonnet-Ben Dhia et al. 2018
has provided a theoretical framework for coherent control of waves in arbitrarily complex multichannel geometries to suppress back-reflection via interference, leading either to perfect transduction or impedance-matching.
The key insight was that such problems in any (generic) geometry can be defined as a set of constraints on the linear scattering matrix of the system, which can be satisfied at countably infinite points in the complex frequency plane. Such complex spectra in open systems are well-known for the case of purely outgoing boundary conditions, typically referred to as resonances or quasi-normal modes, and they correspond to poles of the scattering amplitudes
Lalanne et al. 2018
;
Kristensen et al. 2020
;
Zhang and Miller 2020
. However, the recent work has shown that every reflectionless scattering problem, defined by appropriate boundary conditions asymptotically, leads to a similar but distinct complex spectrum, with eigenfrequencies (termed R-zeros) in one-to-one correspondence with the resonances. When such a solution occurs on the real axis, we refer to it as an RSM (CPA being a special case), since it is now a steady-state solution.

Both the resonances and the R-zero spectra generically have complex frequencies and do not correspond to
physically realizable steady-state solutions, since the imaginary part of the frequency enforces exponential growth or decay in time, and necessarily limits their realization to finite times
Kim et al. 2025
;
Baranov et al. 2017b
. However, it was shown for R-zeros that their frequency can be manipulated by varying a single geometric parameter of the scattering system, and typically can be tuned to pass through the real frequency axis. At the intersection point of such a crossing there exists a steady-state, perfectly impedance-matched input wave in arbitrarily complex geometries; moreover the solution can be straightforwardly calculated by methods similar to those for finding resonances. This has been demonstrated, first in simulations
Sweeney et al. 2020
and later in experiments at microwave and optical frequencies
Sol et al. 2023
;
Faul et al. 2025
;
Shaibe et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2024
.

This work naturally raised the question: can one go beyond simply suppressing backscattering, to simultaneously controlling the output of such complex wave scattering? And if so, how many tunable parameters would be required to further constrain the scattering? If this can be achieved with a small number of parameters in a realistic geometry, it would immediately open up a new path to routing of waves and other more complex information processing functions. The current work answers these questions. The approach we develop in the following can implement rather general control functions in scattering. For several such functions, we have developed a precise and predictive framework that leads to promising and paradigm-changing approaches to designing devices implementing these functions. Here, our main focus will be on routing of waves in systems with negligible loss.

To proceed we define critically-constrained scattering modes, CCONs, a novel set of complex spectra which allow us to define necessary and sufficient conditions for routing of waves in arbitrary geometries. We demonstrate the generic existence of CCON spectra in the complex plane in approximate one-to-one correspondence with the resonances of the system, and discuss their topological protection.
Then we show that routing of waves and other more complex control functions, such as demultiplexing, can be achieved using the complex CCON spectra as building blocks. Finally we present and validate counting arguments for the number of tuning parameters required to achieve these functions. Recent experiments have demonstrated several of the control functions analyzed here, however only by using a larger number of tuning parameters than required according to our theory. Using simulations of a model that is realistically related to these experiments
Faul et al. 2025
, we demonstrate the validity of our arguments and the ability to reprogram such functions
in situ
over a wide frequency range, a unique feature of our approach, compared to previous rational design or black-box optimization approaches
Christiansen and Sigmund 2021
;
Bennet et al. 2024
. Our approach uses only analytic properties of the elements of the scattering matrix without assuming any spatial symmetries, and doesn’t require the S-matrix to have either time-reversal or reciprocity symmetry. (Later, when we implement routing, we assume that the latter two properties hold, for simplicity).

The study of reflection and transmission zeros in systems with two scattering channels (or ports) has a very long history and is well developed in the context of filter design
Dimopoulos 2012
. Our work focuses on systems with three or more ports/channels and multiple scattering.
First, we note relevant previous works, other than those on complex R-zero/RSM spectra already cited. There has been work on transmission zeros in two special cases. There is experimental work demonstrating the ability to generate, by tuning a metasurface, zeros of a single off-diagonal matrix element of a multichannel S-matrix
Faul et al. 2025
; and there has been both theory and simulations on multichannel quasi-1D waveguides and billiard resonators

Kang and Genack 2021
for the case of zero transmission through the sample. In the multichannel case this corresponds to a zero eigenvalue of an
N\times N
transmission matrix. Both are special cases of the CCONs introduced below.

Another important recent work by Guo et al.
Guo et al. 2023
is complementary to our own. This work considered a generalization of CPA and RSM, termed coherent perfect extinction (CPE), in which a specific input wavefront in a chosen subset of the scattering channels leads to zero output in a chosen subset of the scattering channels. In this work CPE processes were defined only for scattering at real frequencies and zeros were assumed to exist in a sufficiently large space of tuning parameters. It was shown that there were interesting topological properties associated with encircling these zeros. In our work, we will consider CPE processes for complex frequencies, showing that all of the nontrivial examples of CPE can be expressed in terms of complex spectra, and that there is a crucial difference between considering such spectra in the complex plane and in an arbitrary parameter space. Using this insight we are able to pose and solve wave routing as a CPE problem, something not treated in
Ref.
Guo et al. 2023
.

II
Critically Constrained Scattering Modes

The work cited above on R-zeros demonstrated the existence of discrete complex spectra associated with reflectionless scattering for generic finite-size scattering systems with short-range interactions.
In a standard linear scattering process the solutions have a continuous real spectrum: one or more of the incoming scattering channels is filled at a real frequency,
\omega
or energy,
E
, and the continuity conditions on the relevant wave equation determine a unique outgoing scattered wave at that

\omega,E
. For a finite size, but otherwise arbitrary scattering region, the scattering can be described by an
N_{c}\times N_{c}
scattering matrix,
S
, labeling the incoming and outgoing channels with the appropriate indices of the asymptotic solutions (which we will assume for simplicity to be time-reversed pairs). For generic scattering, all output channels are accessible in scattering, i.e. no matrix element of
S
exactly vanishes (with symmetries, similar statements hold in each symmetry-labeled subspace). Discrete complex spectra arise when constraints on the asymptotic solutions are imposed, such as zero reflection into the chosen input channels; this R-zero boundary condition implies that a single diagonal element of the
S
matrix or the determinant of a diagonal block of
S
vanishes.

The R-zero boundary condition can be imposed by requiring an input vector of amplitudes of the form
(a_{1}\ldots a_{N_{in}},\\
0,0\ldots,0)
and an output (scattered) vector of amplitudes
(0,0\ldots,0,b_{N_{in}+1}\ldots,b_{N_{c}})
. Hence, there are exactly
N_{c}
asymptotic scattering channels filled,
N_{in}
with incoming waves and their complement with outgoing waves, so that each port is excited in only one direction.

To generalize these results beyond reflectionless scattering to more general constrained scattering processes, we conjecture that discrete complex spectra exist and are of the same average density as the resonances of a scattering system
for all scattering processes in which the total number of filled asymptotic channels is
N_{c}
. We will see that if the number of channels filled is less than
N_{c}
, then there are no discrete complex solutions generically, and if the number of filled channels is greater than
N_{c}
, then there are continuous real spectra generically. Hence we refer to this new class of solutions as critically-constrained modes (CCONs).

This set includes all R-zeros and resonances, but also processes in which some scattering channels are completely empty or “Dark” (no input or output) and others are “Normal” (both an input and a reflected outgoing wave). Since there are
2N_{c}
total input and output channels, our condition is equivalent to having exactly
N_{c}
zeros in a
2N_{c}
component vector. It follows that for such a scattering matrix, there exist
(2N_{c})!/(N_{c}!N_{c}!)
CCON spectra,
2^{N_{c}}
of which are the previously known R-zeros (including resonances and CPA). Each CCON can be specified by an
N_{c}
component vector containing 4 letters,
D
(dark channel),
N
(normal channel),
T
( outgoing wave only), and
\tilde{R}
(reflectionless, input but no output). R-zeros encompass all CCONs which have no
N
or
D
channels; all other CCONs must have an equal number of
N
and
D
channels. The number of each of these must satisfy
n_{D}+n_{N}+n_{T}+n_{\tilde{R}}=N_{c}
. Each CCON eigenfrequency is determined by the condition that, at that frequency, a specific square
q\times q
constraint matrix has determinant equal to zero , where
q=n_{D}+n_{\tilde{R}}
. The constraint matrix is constructed by projection from
S
(see below). Schematics illustrating several three-channel CCONs are shown in Fig. (1a-c), labelled as
(\tilde{R}TT)
,
(\tilde{R}\tilde{R}T)
and
(NDT)
respectively, along with the sub-matrices whose determinants must vanish. Fig. 1d gives an example of calculated spectra for the five distinct types of 3-channel CCONs (neglecting interchange of ports). These spectra are found from a random-matrix model, described in detail in the Supplementary Material [
SM
].

CCON solutions exist for any finite scattering system in the frequency range where the system has resonances, independent of the symmetry or complexity of the system. Here we will focus on guided wave systems with wave-chaotic scattering, using three models. The motivation for using such systems is that the resonances of such systems are generically pseudo-random, reflecting the ergodic nature of the ray dynamics. We expect this property to maximize the tunability of the systems, as all points on the boundary and all excitation frequencies are statistically similar.
The first model is one based on random matrix theory (RMT)
Fyodorov 2021
;
Mitchell et al. 2010
;
Fyodorov and Savin 2015
, already mentioned above, in which statistically representative scattering matrices are generated by sampling from appropriate distributions. The second model is that of “quantum graphs”
Kottos and Smilansky 1997
;
Kottos and Smilansky 2003
, which can be physically realized by microwave networks
Hul et al. 2004
, has been shown to reproduce RMT statistics generically and can be used to test tunability. The third is a microwave billiard model which is realistically related to previous experiments
Faul et al. 2025
. The first two are computationally more tractable and are good for studying the statistics of CCON spectra. The third will be used to validate the ability to control scattering and implement routing in a chaotic microwave cavity and also for more realistic statistical validation of our analytically motivated predictions.

Note that the set of CCONs correspond to a specific subset of Coherent Perfect Extinction processes as defined in
Guo et al. 2023
, but only CCONs have discrete complex spectra, whereas all other CPE processes do not.

III
CCON spectra

III.1
Existence of CCON spectra (General Case)

Here we present an approximate argument for the existence of complex CCON spectra in one-one correspondence with the resonances of a scattering system; much more detail is provided in Supplementary Material [
SM
].
We define in ([
SM
] and references
Horn and Johnson 2013
;
Meyer 2000
within) the filtering matrices for any CPE process,
F
and
\overline{F}
, which are matrices of size
(n_{N}+n_{\tilde{R}})\times(N_{c})
and
(n_{D}+n_{\tilde{R}})\times N_{c}
respectively. These matrices filter out the relevant constraint matrix
\mathcal{C}(\omega)
for each CPE boundary condition we impose on the scattering solutions, such that
\mathcal{C}(\omega)=FS(\omega)\bar{F}^{T}
. For CCONs
\mathcal{C}(\omega)
is square, but for all other CPE processes it isn’t.
We can provide a constructive proof of the existence of such a discrete spectrum corresponding to each CCON within Temporal Coupled Mode Theory (TCMT)
Fan et al. 2003
;
Suh et al. 2004
, a widely used approximate theory of the scattering matrix. In TCMT, a system with
N_{c}
asymptotic channels coupled to
N_{\rm res}
resonances can be described over a bandwidth containing the resonances by the following scattering matrix

\displaystyle S(\omega)=S_{0}+iD\frac{1}{\omega-\Omega+i\Gamma}K^{T},

(1)

where
S_{0}
is the non-resonant background scattering matrix containing the contributions of other far away resonances, (and possibly non-resonant paths through the system),
\Omega
is a matrix containing the real part of the resonant frequencies,
\Gamma
is a matrix with size
N_{\rm res}\times N_{\rm res}
describing the imaginary part of the resonance frequencies. This matrix can fully describe both radiative and absorptive decay of the resonances. The resonances couple to the outgoing channels
with the
N_{c}\times N_{\rm res}
coupling matrices
K/D
(which are independent of frequency in TMCT). In the case when
S_{0}
is defined as describing the residual effect of resonances outside of the targeted bandwidth, it is slowly-varying in frequency and generically will have no CCONs in the relevant bandwidth, hence the filtered matrix,
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, will in general be invertible in this bandwidth. Assuming this, we can define the relevant constraint matrix within TCMT and use matrix identities to derive the following expression for
\det(\mathcal{C}(\omega))

\displaystyle\frac{\det(\mathcal{C}(\omega))}{\det(\mathcal{C}_0)}=\frac{\det(\omega- \Omega+ i\Gamma+ 2i \Gamma_0)}{\det(\omega- \Omega+ i\Gamma)}.

(2)

Here
\Gamma_{0}=K_{\bar{F}}^{T}\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1}D_{F}/2
is an
N_{\rm res}\times N_{\rm res}
complex matrix with negligible frequency dependence in the relevant bandwidth which captures the shift of the CCON zeros from the full zeros of the S-matrix. Here
\mathcal{C}_{0}=FS_{0}\bar{F}^{T}
is the filtered background matrix,
D_{F}=FD
and
K_{\bar{F}}=\bar{F}K
. First, this expression shows that the poles of
\mathcal{C}(\omega)
for every CCON are the same as the poles of the full scattering matrix, as expected. Second, it shows that the zeros (CCON eigenfrequencies) are simply the eigenvalues of the
N_{\rm res}\times N_{\rm res}
complex matrix
\Omega-i\Gamma+2i\Gamma_{0}
. Thus the CCON eigenfrequencies are in one-to-one correspondence with the poles of the scattering matrix and can be calculated by constructing the relevant CCON operator given a TCMT S-matrix. This result holds for arbitrary S-matrices and doesn’t require either time-reversal symmetry or reciprocity.
When the CCON chosen is an R-zero, the background scattering matrix is taken to be the identity, and the system is reciprocal and has time-reversal symmetry, then we must have
D=K
,
D^{*}=-D
and
\Gamma=D^{\dagger}D/2

Suh et al. 2004
. This simplifies Eq. 2 so that
i\Gamma+2i\Gamma_{0}\to i\Gamma_{in}-i\Gamma_{out}
, where the former is a matrix of couplings for the input channels and the latter (with opposite sign) is a similar matrix for the chosen output channels, as found in
Sweeney et al. 2020
.
Note that as a special case, when all channels are outgoing (resonance) and the system has only scattering loss and no absorption, we recover the well-known result that the full S-matrix zeros are the complex conjugates of the poles (resonances). However, all other CCON spectra are in general distinct from the poles/zeros, as we see in Fig. 1d, and are not calculable from knowledge of the poles.

Often in TCMT the background matrix is approximated by the identity, which leads to a non-invertible filtered background matrix for all CCONs except for R-zeros, i.e. all CCONs with at least one dark channel. In this case the number of CCON zeros is formally reduced from the number of resonances in the given bandwidth by the number of dark channels
n_{D}
(see [
SM
]).
Whether this effect can occur for a physical S-matrix is unclear; but, if so, this would still have a negligible effect over a large bandwidth containing many resonances, and indeed we find it has no detectable effect on our ability to achieve routing of waves using CCONs.

III.2
Properties of CCON spectra

Below we will show that routing of waves in multiple scattering can be achieved by manipulating CCON spectra, and in particular by tuning eigenfrequencies to the real axis. Here we discuss the location of CCON eigenfrequencies in the complex plane. Tuning a single eigenfrequency to the real axis has been previously studied for the case of R-zeros, and it has been found that the imaginary part of the eigenfrequency of an R-zero is determined by the average coupling balance between the chosen input and output ports. In TCMT there is an explicit scalar formula for this, which constitutes a multichannel generalization of the “critical coupling” condition
Sweeney et al. 2020
. Specifically, filled output channels appear as scattering loss, and filled input channels as an effective gain. In a more exact theory this property is still maintained on average
Sweeney et al. 2020
.
Therefore, if the input channels are more numerous or more strongly coupled than the output channels, then the imaginary part of an R-zero will be positive (above the real axis), consistent with the fact that full S-matrix zeros must be above the real axis in the absence of absorption. In the opposite case of weak input coupling the R-zeros will be below on average, and in both cases there will be significant statistical fluctuations
Sol et al. 2023
. When input and output couplings are roughly in balance, the distribution of R-zeros is centered on the real axis, and an R-zero can be tuned to lie on the real axis at any frequency relatively easily. This is valid beyond the high-Q limit where there exists a scalar critical coupling condition, and has been validated in the relatively low-Q open cavities we consider here, where transmission is through multiple overlapping resonances.

As noted above, and seen in Fig. 1d, on average, for a system with negligible absorption, the poles will be the eigenfrequencies farthest away from the real axis in the lower half plane and the zeros of the full S-matrix will be farthest away in the upper half plane. As noted, other CCONs can have singly-filled ports (
\tilde{R}
or
T
), doubly filled “normal” ports (
N
) and dark ports which are unfilled (
D
). An
\tilde{R}
port contributes an effective gain and a
T
port an effective loss as far as the imaginary part of the frequency is concerned. It is reasonable to conjecture that, on average, a dark port contributes neither effective gain nor loss, and that a normal port, having equal effective gain and loss, will also have no influence on the imaginary part of the CCON. Thus, in finding the imaginary part of the CCON frequency, one can omit all
ND
pairs and just look at the balance of the remaining
\tilde{R}
and
T
ports. The difference
n_{T}-n_{\tilde{R}}
will then determine their average imaginary part.
Hence, the average imaginary part of the reflectionless four-port CCONs
(\tilde{R}\tilde{R}TT)
and
(NDND)
, which are balanced, should both be zero (assuming all ports are statistically similar in their coupling to the cavity). This qualitative argument explains why all the CCON spectra tend to lie in the region of the complex plane between the poles and zeros, as is also the case for R-zeros. In fact, for a general scattering matrix based on a random matrix model, we can analytically calculate the average value of the imaginary part of the CCON eigenfrequencies:

\text{Im}\langle\omega_{\rm CCON}\rangle=\frac{\gamma}{N_{\rm res}}(n_{\tilde{R}}-n_{T})

(3)

Here
\gamma
is half the average resonance width defined from the coupling matrix
W
by
\mathrm{Var}(W_{\alpha\beta})=2\gamma/N_{\rm res}
. The expression above is written for the global mean, which is valid for weak coupling. We expect that a similar expression to Moldauer-Simonius relation to hold for the local mean as well (see [
SM
] for more details). In the Supplementary Material [
SM
] we define the RMT model, derive this result and compare it with results from numerical sampling of the RMT model, finding good agreement (the data in Fig. 1d, follows the expected trend, with statistical fluctuations). The result also agrees with that found from TCMT in Ref.
Sweeney et al. 2020
for the case of R-zero spectra with a single resonance.
In the next section we study tuning of eigenfrequencies to the real axis to realize control functions. This is expedited by the fact that they are generically confined in a strip of the complex plane centered on the real axis.

III.3
Routing as a Coincidence of CCONs

Routing of waves can be defined as zero reflection of the waves back into the chosen input ports and zero transmission from each input port to a subset of the other ports. Since zero reflection alone defines one kind of CCON, routing solutions are not CCONs and don’t have an associated spectrum in the complex plane
(excluding the two-port case, where zero reflection routs waves trivially into the other port).
We will focus on systems with
N_{c}\geq 3
and with negligible absorption loss (filtering, which typically involves two ports, can be addressed with our methods
Faul et al. 2025
, but we defer this to future work).
For the three-port case, the only routing solution is
(\tilde{R}TD)
, corresponding to input in port one with zero reflection (
S_{11}=0
), and output solely in port two (
S_{31}=0
). Consistent with the above discussion, this CPE process has a
D
channel not paired with an N channel, and isn’t a CCON; it is overconstrained and doesn’t have a complex spectrum. However, there do exist CCONs satisfying either the first or second constraint: in our notation they are
(NTD)
, which satisfies
S_{31}=0
, and
(\tilde{R}TT)
, which satisfies
S_{11}=0
. Both have a complex frequency spectrum where the relevant constraint is obeyed. If, by tuning the geometric parameters of the system, we can cause two of these distinct CCON frequencies to coincide at a chosen frequency on the real axis, by definition we will have realized the routing solution we seek. This reasoning generalizes, and any overconstrained CPE process can be realized by creating a
coincidence
of a number of CCONs eigenfrequencies on the real axis. Note that this routing function requires a coincidence of both an R-zero and a dark CCON, which is a generic feature. Generically, the number of eigenfrequencies that must coincide to realize a given CPE process is
n_{co}=n_{D}-n_{N}+1
. (CCONs sharing the same input channels are generally not independent and there are cases in which
n_{co}
can be greater than the number predicted by this formula, but their motion is correlated so that the number of tuning parameters required to realize an overconstrained functionality (discussed below) remains the same even in theses cases. More details are in section IV below and in the Supplementary Material [
SM
].

III.4
Topological protection of CCONs

Our strategy for realizing routing and other useful scattering processes assumes that CCON eigenfrequencies are conserved and flow continuously in the complex plane as parameters of the cavity are varied. Since the CCON frequencies correspond to zeros of the determinant of a constraint matrix, they are zeros of a complex function of frequency.
Any zero of a complex function has a local topological winding number associated with encircling that zero (and no other zero) in an appropriate parameter space. However, for a general function, such a zero can annihilate with another zero of opposite winding number, which will still conserve the global winding number of the system, but changes the number of zeros available for functionalization. We are interested in zeros in a special parameter space, the complex frequency plane, and their response to perturbations. Since all matrix elements of S are analytic functions of
\omega
outside the discrete set of poles of S, the determinant of any sub-matrix of S is also analytic in
\omega
outside the same discrete set, generically with a non-vanishing linear expansion around the zero (i.e., not at an exceptional point). Using the Cauchy–Riemann relations for analytic functions, it is shown in the Supplementary Material [
SM
] (see also
Roe 2015
;
Milnor and Weaver 1965
) that all CCONs have winding number unity, and cannot annihilate pairwise. Hence CCON eigenfrequencies are conserved in the complex plane under parametric variation (except for the special case of annihilation with a pole of the S-matrix). This makes the complex plane a special parameter space, differing from the space of geometric parameters (see [
SM
]), where only global winding numbers are conserved, but zeros are not. In fact we find that when we fix the frequency and vary geometric parameters, generically we observe regular
creation and annihilation of zeros in pairs of opposite helicity in the parametric subspace, see [
SM
]. This process is very similar to the annihilation of Bound States in the Continuum (BICs) of opposite helicity found in photonic crystal lattices as lattice parameters are varied
Hsu et al. 2013
;
Zhen et al. 2014
;
Hsu et al. 2016
.
While the topological protection of zeros has been long noted, the fact that R-zeros (and their generalization to CCONs) are absolutely conserved and cannot vanish via pairwise annihilation has not been recognized, to our knowledge. This property underlies our ability to succeed in functionalization and coherent control.

IV
Scattering Control via CCONs

IV.1
Minimal Required Parameter Set

In the R-zero theory it has been shown that in general, tuning an eigenvalue to cross the real axis at an arbitrary point requires just a single tuning parameter (e.g., adding uniform loss for CPA). Intuitively we expect to be able to realize a reflectionless state
at a specified frequency
,
\omega_{0}
, on the real axis by tuning two parameters, and this is confirmed by simulations (an example is shown in Fig. 2). Here we show the flow of the R-zeros of a chaotic cavity with two tunable scatterers (see schematic), details of the model will be given below. With a single tuning parameter we generate R-zeros on the real axis (RSMs), but we cannot reach the desired frequency; with two-parameter tuning this is efficiently achieved. To place an R-zero at a real
\omega_{0}
, the real and imaginary parts of the determinant of a sub-matrix of
S(\omega_{0})
must equal zero. The same is true for dark CCONs (such as a transmission zero); hence we expect that placing a dark CCON at a given real frequency also requires two tuning parameters. Having shown that routing and other overconstrained CPE processes can be achieved by creating coincidences of CCON eigenfrequencies at the target frequency, and we expect this counting argument to generalize. In general, assuming no symmetry of the S-matrix,
we conjecture that the minimal number of required parameters,
n_{p}
, to achieve such functionalities is

n_{p}=2n_{co}=2(n_{D}-n_{N}+1),

(4)

where
n_{co}
is the number of coincident CCONs required for the desired function. This implies that at least four tuning parameters are needed to realize the three-port routing behavior shown in Fig. 3 at a specified frequency. for certain solutions, as we show in Fig. 5; this is discussed further below and in the SM.

A major result of our work is that an open wave-chaotic cavity with tunable scatterers on the wavelength scale appears to be a highly efficient geometry for realizing the desired solutions by straightforward optimization schemes,
as long as the number of tunable parameters equals (or is greater than) the number predicted by the above formula

IV.2
3-port Routing and Demultiplexing

We model a “D-shaped” microwave cavity with tunable scattering elements embedded within it via a 2D structure with a boundary given by a truncated circle (proven to generate fully chaotic ray dynamics upon specular reflection even in the absence of the embedded scatterers
Ree and Reichl 1999
;
Doya et al. 2001
). In the simulation the walls of the cavity are perfectly conducting except where a number of single-mode waveguides are attached on the flat part of the boundary. Inside the cavity there are a variable number of perfectly conducting ellipsoids (“scatterers”) that are free to rotate, hence altering the scattering of waves injected from the input ports. The model mimics the experimental system of ref.
Faul et al. 2025
, where screws are inserted into a cavity of the same boundary shape at variable depths to modify the scattering behavior. The configuration of screw depths is used for optimization, and that work has found that good, narrowband routing solutions are obtainable experimentally; however these experiments used significantly more parameters than our theory suggests are required.

In our model the rotation angles of the ellipsoids are our optimization parameters. Our prediction is that we will need at least four ellipsoids to move the appropriate pair of CCONs to the target frequency and achieve high quality 3-port routing. In Fig. 3 we show a typical optimization result, achieving
\sim 108\;{\rm dB}
suppression of the reflection and undesired transmission with four-parameter tuning. The optimization routine used to find this solution was relatively simple, based on Newton’s method; it does not involve tracking CCONs. Although this is possible, it is likely not the most efficient approach. The data in Fig. 3 are typical; we generated many solutions based on different initial spatial positions and orientations of the scatterers with similar results. Importantly, our similar study with only three and two tunable scatterers found only
\sim 14\;{\rm dB}
and
\sim 32\;{\rm dB}
of suppression, respectively. In Fig. 5 we show a more extensive statistical study of the model for the richer example of four-port routing, validating our predictions over an ensemble and in more detail.

To demonstrate the validity of our predictions over many different scattering control functions and their robustness with respect to different spatial positions and initial orientations of the scatterers, we performed a statistical study of achievable performance vs. number of tuning parameters for a four-port system, using the realistic microwave cavity model. We defined appropriate figures of merit (FOM) related to the suppression of the unwanted transmission and/or reflection processes (see [
SM
] for details) and averaged them over the results of multiple optimizations with results shown in Fig. 5 (where we also indicate their standard deviations). We study up to six-parameter control functions, the most demanding being four-port routing. In addition, in the Supplementary Material [
SM
] we use a quantum graph model
Kottos and Smilansky 1997
;
Kottos and Smilansky 2003
, which is computationally more efficient, to check our predictions up to eight tuning parameters. With six tuning parameters in a four-port cavity, we can check at least four different functions of interest. We can study the behavior of CCONs, choosing the examples of R-zeros and zeros of a single transmission coefficient, “partial routing”
(\tilde{R}TTD)
, which is the coincidence of two CCONs (four parameters needed), and finally, “full routing” (the coincidence of 3 CCONs, all six parameters required).

However, there is a fifth, distinct case to study. While our generic argument above predicts that it requires two parameters to tune a CCON to a target real frequency, there is an exception due to time-reversal symmetry; this is the case of multichannel perfect reflection for a system with an even number of channels, first noted in ref.
Kang and Genack 2021
. In that work they considered a multichannel waveguide with a scattering region with an
N_{c}\times N_{c}
transmission matrix. We can generalize this to any system described by an S-matrix with an even number of channels,
N_{c}
, arbitrarily choosing
N_{c}/2
of them as input ports. In that setting, perfect reflection means zero total transmission into the complementary channels, which are hence dark. For our four-port example this is a CCON of form (NNDD). Since our system is lossless this process maps under time-reversal as follows:
(NNDD)(\omega)\to(NNDD)(\omega^{*})
, i.e. it maps back to the same process at the complex conjugate frequency. It follows that these transmissionless CCONs will occur as complex conjugate pairs, or on the real axis. Although this is similar to the symmetry of R-zeros when the system has parity symmetry
Sweeney et al. 2020
;
Jiang et al. 2024
, remarkably, this property requires
no spatial symmetry at all
, and can be realized in a realistic non-symmetric system. Transmission zeros on the real axis will simply flow along it under single-parameter tuning (unless it meets another solution at an EP) and can be tuned to nearby frequencies with a single parameter. This “horizontal” motion of transmission zeros in a two-port system under single-parameter tuning was observed in ref.
Faul et al. 2025
but displaced below the real axis slightly, due to non-negligible absorption. Here we study statistically the more complex four-port analog, and indeed find single-parameter tuning is sufficient to reach a desired frequency. In the Supplementary Materials [
SM
] we show that time-reversal symmetry decreases the minimal tuning requirements for overconstrained functionalities as well (e.g.,
(NDDD)
), halving the number of required degrees of freedom to realize them at a single frequency on the real axis; this is confirmed by results from a quantum graphs model.

In Fig. 5 we see that all the five distinct functions considered were realizable with
\sim 100\;{\rm dB}
suppression of unwanted processes when the correct“minimum” number of tuning parameters is used. Not surprisingly, we see that the cross-over from poor to excellent FOM is smoother for the more overconstrained functions, such as partial and full routing.

V
Conclusions

We have introduced a novel mathematical framework to characterize the difficulty of controlling scattering of waves in complex higher dimensional geometries, in order to suppress chosen scattering processes that occur generically. We then utilize this framework to demonstrate the ability to implement coherent control functions on input waves in a spatially compact, strong scattering geometry, a chaotic cavity with a relatively small number of tunable scattering elements. Not only can we achieve routing and demultiplexing of narrowband signals, but the system is found to be arbitrarily reprogrammable to change both the chosen frequencies and the spatial routing pattern. This approach can be applied to many other control functions, such as filtering, power division, directional lasing, isolation and circulation (in non-reciprocal extensions). It provides a window into complex optimization problems for which no intuitive classification scheme existed previously. The framework applies to all linear waves, both classical and quantum, but is most useful when coherent narrowband excitation is possible and the system has some degree of tunability. Thus, it makes contact with research in the field of metamaterials more generally, which often are designed with
in situ
tuning parameters. The approach requires a 3D or 2D scattering region at least as large as the wavelength of the input waves. Interestingly, it is possible to apply an even more general but similar approach to problems with mutually incoherent inputs
Zhang et al. 2019
, which still can be routed, but with a larger number of tuning parameters than required when the inputs are mutually coherent.

We do not as yet have a complete understanding of the limits of this approach with respect to bandwidth, minimal size, and optimal geometry of the cavity and of the tunable elements. These are topics for further research. Here we focused on relatively open, low-Q cavities, a few wavelengths in size, using routing as a dramatic example of scattering control in a multiple scattering geometry with no spatial symmetries [
SM
].

\displaystyle=-in_{T}\frac{\gamma}{N_{\rm res}}\mathbb{I}_{N_{\rm res}}+in_{\tilde{R}}\frac{\gamma}{N_{\rm res}}\mathbb{I}_{N_{\rm res}}

(S32)

\displaystyle=i(n_{\tilde{R}}-n_{T})\frac{\gamma}{N_{\rm res}}\mathbb{I}_{N_{\rm res}}.

(S33)

With this, we can write

\displaystyle\langle\omega_{\rm CCON}\rangle=i(n_{\tilde{R}}-n_{T})\frac{\gamma}{N_{\rm res}(N_{\rm res}-n_{D})}\left(N_{\rm res}-\left\langle\Tr{W_N(W_D^\dagger W_N)^{-1}W_D^\dagger}\right\rangle\right).

(S34)

Simplifying the last expression leaves us with

\displaystyle\langle\omega_{\rm CCON}\rangle=i(n_{\tilde{R}}-n_{T})\frac{\gamma}{N_{\rm res}}.

(S35)

The expression given above refers to the
global mean
or the Weisskopf estimate
Mitchell et al. 2010
;
Fyodorov and Savin 2015

local mean
. The global mean obtained via the trace formula, is valid in the weak-coupling regime. By contrast, the local mean of resonance widths at finite coupling is estimated by the Moldauer-Simonius (MS) relation

;
Mitchell et al. 2010
. Intuitively, the mean imaginary part should have a weak dependence on

\displaystyle\frac{\langle{\rm Im}\{\omega_{\rm CCON}\}\rangle}{\Delta}=\frac{(n_{\tilde{R}}-n_{T})}{4\pi}\ln(1- T),

(S36)

where we have assumed equivalent channels with
T=T_{c}=\frac{4\kappa_{c}}{(1+\kappa_{c})^{2}}
, and
\kappa_{c}=\frac{\pi\gamma_{c}}{N_{\rm res}\Delta}
.
Thus,
ND

S3
Minimal number of parameters for overconstrained problems

In overconstrained problems, we are considering a tall matrix
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
with
m>n.
For the constraint matrix to have a nontrivial solution for this case, we must have
{\rm nullity}(\mathcal{C})\geq 1
. Or using the Rank-Nullity theorem, we need to have
{\rm rank}(\mathcal{C})\leq n-1
. We start by finding the codimension of the constraint matrix in terms of its rank.

Claim
: A matrix
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
with rank
r
has a complex dimension
(m+n)r-r^{2}
.

Proof
:
Let
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
be a matrix with rank
r
, so it must have
r
linearly independent rows. Changing the order of the rows does not change the dimension of the matrix, so we can write
\mathcal{C}=\begin{pmatrix}B\\
L\end{pmatrix}
, where
B
is the matrix containing the linearly independent rows of
\mathcal{C}
and
L
is the matrix constructed from the remaining rows.
B
is full rank; and therefore it must have a dimension
nr
. Since every row of
L
is a linear combination of the rows of
B
, it follows that we need
r
complex numbers to uniquely specify each row of
L
. That is a total of
(m-r)r
complex numbers to fully construct
L
and to fully construct
\mathcal{C}
, we need
nr+(m-r)r=nr+mr-r^{2}
complex numbers.

Note
: The complex codimension of a matrix
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
with rank
r
is given by
nm-nr-mr+r^{2}=(n-r)(m-r)
.

Note
: From the Rank-Nullity theorem,
r=n-{\rm nullity}(\mathcal{C})
and thus the codimension of
\mathcal{C}
is
{\rm nullity}(\mathcal{C})(m-n+{\rm nullity}(\mathcal{C}))
.
Since we want the nullity to be at least 1, we can see that in general, we need at least
(m-n+1)
complex numbers or
2(m-n+1)
real parameters to have a solution for the overconstrained problem at a single frequency. In our notation, this is just
2(n_{D}-n_{N}+1)
real parameters.

S4
CCONs are the building blocks of overconstrained functionalities

When
n_{D}>n_{N}
, the constraint matrix is tall:
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
, with
m>n
. We will show that for any such matrices, if all the maximal minors vanish at a given frequency (i.e., all CCON zeros formed from the same set of incoming channels meet at a single point in the complex frequency plane), then the constraint matrix has a nontrivial nullspace at that same frequency.

Claim
: If all zeros of CCONs that can be built from the constraint matrix while sharing the same columns meet at a single point in the complex frequency plane, then the constraint matrix must have a nontrivial nullity at that same point.

Proof 1
:
We first note that even though the constraint matrix is rectangular, we can still write the condition for existence of solutions in terms of the determinant of a single matrix, namely
\det(\mathcal{C}^\dagger\mathcal{C})=0
. We then use Cauchy-Binet formula on
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}\mathcal{C}
gives

\displaystyle\det(\mathcal{C}^\dagger\mathcal{C})=\sum_{I\in[m],|I|=n}\det(\mathcal{C}^\dagger_{[n], I})\det(\mathcal{C}_{I, [n]}).

(S37)

Where
[n]=\{1,\cdots,n\}
and
[m]=\{1,\cdots,m\}
.
\mathcal{C}_{I,[n]}
denotes the submatrix with row indices
I
and all the columns of
\mathcal{C}
. We can simplify the last expression to

\displaystyle\det(\mathcal{C}^\dagger\mathcal{C})=\sum_{I\in[m],|I|=n}\left|\det(\mathcal{C}_{I, [n]})\right|^{2}.

(S38)

From this, it is clear to see that we have a zero of
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}\mathcal{C}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{C}
has a nontrivial nullity) if and only if all maximal minors of
\mathcal{C}
vanish.

Proof 2 (by contradiction)
:
Assume that all maximal minors of
\mathcal{C}
vanish, but
\mathcal{C}
has a trivial nullity. Then
{\rm rank}(\mathcal{C})=n
which implies that there are
n
linearly independent rows of
\mathcal{C}
. These rows form a submatrix whose determinant does not vanish, but this contradicts the assumption that all maximal minors must vanish. Thus,
\mathcal{C}
must have a nontrivial nullity.

Note
: For an overconstrained problem, there are
\binom{m}{n}
CCONs that share the same incoming channels (i.e., the same columns as the the constraint matrix
\mathcal{C}
). The claim above does not specify how many such CCONs we need in general. Below, we illustrate what the claim implies in some examples.

Examples
:

\displaystyle(\tilde{R}\tilde{R}DT)=(\tilde{R}\tilde{R}TT)+(N\tilde{R}DT)+(\tilde{R}NDT)

\displaystyle(N\tilde{R}DD)=(N\tilde{R}DT)+(N\tilde{R}TD)+(NNDD).

\displaystyle(\tilde{R}\tilde{R}\tilde{R}DT)=(\tilde{R}\tilde{R}\tilde{R}TT)+(\tilde{R}\tilde{R}NDT)+(\tilde{R}N\tilde{R}DT)+(N\tilde{R}\tilde{R}DT).

The next claim quantifies how many CCON coincidences suffice ”in general” to show that the constraint matrix is rank-deficient.

Claim
: Let
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
with
m>n
. If the first
n-1

\mathcal{C}
are linearly independent and every

minor of
\mathcal{C}
formed from all columns of
\mathcal{C}
and the row set

, where
i\in\{n-1,\cdots,m\}
vanishes then the constraint matrix must have a nontrivial nullity.

Proof
:
Let

\displaystyle M_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}r_{1}\\
\vdots\\
r_{n-1}\\
r_{n+k}\end{pmatrix}

where
r_{i}
is the
i_{th}
row of

\mathcal{C}
and
k=0,1,\dots,m
. Assume that the first
n-1
rows are linearly independent and that all
M_{k}
have a vanishing determinant. This implies that you can write
r_{n+k}
in terms of the first
n-1
rows, and this is true for all
k=0,1,\dots,m
. It then follows that all columns
r_{i}
with
i\geq n
, are in the span of the first
n-1
columns. Thus,
\mathcal{C}
is of rank
n-1
and the nullity is 1.

Note
: Because there are
m-n+1
matrices

, and we want them all to have a vanishing determinant, it may seem that in general we need
m-n+1=n_{D}-n_{N}+1
CCONs to coincide to have a solution for the overconstrained problem. But it should be clear that there is no way to know in advance which
n-1
rows are linearly independent, so the number above may not be the number of CCONs you require to coincide in general, but this does not change the codimension arguments above about the minimal number of degrees of freedom required for a given functionality. So it is possible to have a coincidence of

. For

, this could happen only for the trivial case where the base row is a matrix of zeros.

Examples:

\displaystyle(\tilde{R}\tilde{R}DT)=(\tilde{R}\tilde{R}TT)+(\tilde{R}NDT)

\displaystyle(N\tilde{R}DD)=(N\tilde{R}DT)+(N\tilde{R}TD)

S6
Counting for systems with Time-reversal symmetry

Time-reversal symmetry
(\mathcal{T})
can reduce the codimension of perfect reflection functionalities, which in turn reduces the minimum number of independent parameters required to realize them on the real axis. For example, the CCON
NNDD
, is
\mathcal{T}
-symmetric and has codimension 1 (rather than 2 as for generic CCONs). A similar reduction appears in overconstrained perfect reflection. This includes all cases where only
N
and
D
boundary conditions are considered, with
n_{D}>n_{N}
. For example, for
NDDD
, without symmetry, it would require a set of 6 parameters to be achieved at a single frequency on the real axis, but with
\mathcal{T}
, it would require only 3 parameters. We show why this is true below.

Claim
: In systems with
\mathcal{T}
-symmetry, the number of parameters required to achieve the overconstrained functionality
(NDD\cdots D)
at a single frequency on the real axis is
n_{D}
.

Proof
: To show this we first write the scattering matrix in the form:

\displaystyle S=\begin{pmatrix}S_{11}&T^{T}\\
T&R\end{pmatrix},

(S45)

where
S_{11}
is a scaler,
T
is an
n_{D}\times 1
column vector and
R
is an
n_{D}\times n_{D}
block. The constraint associated with the CCON
(NDD\cdots D)
is
T=0
. With time-reversal symmetry, the scattering matrix is unitary and complex symmetric, and the space of
(n_{D}+1)\times(n_{D}+1)
unitary and symmetric matrices has a real dimension
\frac{(n_{D}+1)(n_{D}+2)}{2}
. Imposing
T=0
forces
S
to be a block diagonal matrix:

\displaystyle S=\begin{pmatrix}S_{11}&0_{1\times n_{D}}\\
0_{n_{D}\times 1}&R\end{pmatrix}.

(S46)

Because of time-reversal symmetry, both
S_{11}
and
R
are unitary and complex symmetric. Thus, the dimension constrained space is given by
1+\frac{n_{D}(n_{D}+1)}{2}
. The real codimesnion is given by the difference:

\displaystyle\frac{(n_{D}+1)(n_{D}+2)}{2}-\frac{n_{D}(n_{D}+1)}{2}-1=n_{D}.

(S47)

So the number of degrees of freedom required to achieve this functionality is
n_{D}
.
A special case is in the two-port
\mathcal{T}
-symmetric case of transmission zeros
(ND)
, which requires only a single parameter to be tuned to the real axis.

We now generalize this to an arbitrary number of
N
channels.

Claim
: In systems with
\mathcal{T}
symmetry, the number of parameters required to achieve the overconstrained functionality
(NN\cdots NDD\cdots D)

.

Proof
: With
\mathcal{T}
-symmetry, the scattering matrix
S
is unitary and complex symmetric and could be written as:

\displaystyle S=\begin{pmatrix}R_{1}&T^{T}\\
T&R_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(S48)

where
R_{1}
is an
n_{N}\times n_{N}
symmetric block,
T
is an

is an

The constraint associated with the functionality

is
{\rm nullity}(T)\geq 1
.
In this space,

is of rank
n_{N}-1
, and so the first column could be written as a linear combination of the remaining
n_{N}-1
columns, and using matrix operations we can reduce the constraint matrix to

, where
\tilde{T}
is full-rank. The

\displaystyle S=\begin{pmatrix}R_{11}&R_{21}^{T}&0_{1\times n_{D}}\\
R_{21}&R_{22}&\tilde{T}^{T}\\

(S49)

Since

\displaystyle S=\begin{pmatrix}R_{11}&0_{1\times(n_{D}+n_{N}-1)}&\\

(S50)

From this we can count the dimension of this constrained space.
R_{11}
and
\tilde{R}_{2}
are both unitary symmetric matrices with real dimensions

and
\frac{(n_{D}+n_{N}-1)(n_{D}+n_{N})}{2}
respectively. The dimension of the unconstrained unitary and symmetric scattering matrix is
\frac{(n_{D}+n_{N})(n_{D}+n_{N}+1)}{2}

So the number of required parameters for this functionality is

\displaystyle\frac{(n_{D}+n_{N})(n_{D}+n_{N}+1)}{2}-\frac{(n_{D}+n_{N}-1)(n_{D}+n_{N})}{2}-2(n_{N}-1)=n_{D}-n_{N}+1.

(S51)

The last term

comes from the
n_{N}-1
complex coefficients used in the reduction

S8
Topological properties of CCONs

Generically, CCONs are zeros of a complex valued function (i.e.,
\det(\mathcal{C}(\omega))
), and depending on the context, they can be viewed as zeros in a general parameter space, or in the complex frequency plane. Here, we clarify the distinction between the two representations. We can write the scattering matrix with its explicit dependence on both frequency and some general parameters
p
that depend on the scattering system as
S(\omega,p)
, where
p
is a
d
dimensional vector. CCONs can then be explored in two ways: fixing
p
and observing the discrete spectra in the complex plane or by fixing the frequency and finding the zeros in a chosen 2 dimensional parameter space. The former was the view used in the main paper, while the latter was considered in
Guo et al. 2023
.

Without symmetry, and assuming smooth dependence on
\omega
and
p
, the theory of Sard and Brown
Milnor and Weaver 1965
;
Roe 2015
guarantees that those zeros are isolated points.

For now we consider those zeros in a 2-dimensional space, either a complex plane, or a parameter plane
(p_{1},p_{2})
. In this space we can define the function
f(s)=\det(\mathcal{C}(s))
as a complex valued function of
s
. Around a simple zero
s_{0}
, the winding number is

\displaystyle\text{wind}(f;s_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{f^{\prime}(\phi)}{f(\phi)}d\phi.

(S54)

To evaluate this, we write
f(s)
as a two component vector with
f(s)=\left(\text{Re}\{f(s)\},\text{Im}\{f(s)\}\right)^{T}
and linearize around
s_{0}
:
f(s)=Df(s_{0})(s-s_{0})
, where
Df(s_{0})
is the Jacobian matrix at
s_{0}
and is given by

\displaystyle Df=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x}&\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial y}\\
\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x}&\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial y}\end{pmatrix}_{s_{0}}.

(S55)

After some calculations, we arrive at

\displaystyle\text{wind}(f;s_{0})=\text{sign}(\det(Df(s_0))).

(S56)

So every simple zero has a winding number of
\pm 1
.
When
f
is analytic at
s_{0}
, the Cauchy-Riemann equations force
\det(Df)\geq 0
, which ensures that

\displaystyle\text{wind}(f;s_{0})=1,\qquad\text{for $f$ analytic at $s_{0}$}.

(S57)

Hence, within a region of the complex frequency plane where
S
is analytic, the winding number associated with a simple zero is always
+1
. This property extends to other physical quantities for which
S
is meromorphic (e.g.,
\varepsilon,\mu
). Consequently, CCONs cannot annihilate pairwise in the complex frequency plane under continuous parametric variations of the geometry and are therefore conserved in that sense. However, annihilation between a pole and a zero remains possible.

An additional property of CCONs arises when the scattering matrix is periodic in 2 parameters. By tracing a simple contour around a single unit cell, the winding number must vanish. Thus, CCONs in that space must always come in pairs with opposite winding numbers. Since our control parameters are the angles of ellipsoidal scatterers with respect to a fixed axis, they are indeed periodic and the relevant parameter spaces exhibit this property. All these properties have a natural extension to the overconstrained functionalities, where the space in which zeros exist is higher dimensional. A complete treatment of the topological properties including such cases will appear in a future publication.

S9
Optimization

In all of the designs, we aim to have a nontrivial nullspace for the constraint matrix. We consider the case of a square and nonsquare constraint matrix. We state the cost function used in all of the implementations.

S9.1
Cost function

In this case,
n_{D}=n_{N}
and we need to have a zero determinant for the constraint matrix
\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle CF=|\det(\mathcal{C})|^{2}

(S58)

We can also formulate this in terms the singular values.

S9.2
Tall constraint matrix

In this case
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
with
m>n
, is non-square and we can not define the determinant directly. But we note that
N(\mathcal{C})=N(\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}\mathcal{C})
, where
N()
refers to the nullspace, and since
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}\mathcal{C}
is square, it follows that we can formulate the optimization problem in terms of the determinant of
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle CF=|\det(\mathcal{C}^\dagger\mathcal{C})|^{2}.

(S59)

Another approach is to use the singular values of the constraint matrix. If the set
{\sigma_{n}}
of singular values of
\mathcal{C}
, is arranged in increasing order, then one simple cost function could be

\displaystyle CF=\sigma_{1}^{2}.

(S60)

One important result about singular values is that if there is loss in the system, then the singular values of
S
and any submatrix of
S
are always less than 1.

S10
Full wave simulation of the Chaotic Cavity

The 2D chaotic cavity used in the main text is a D-shaped structure with

, where
\lambda_{c}=c_{0}/f_{c}

c_{0}

d=R/2

0.75\lambda_{c}

0.5\lambda_{c}
, respectively. All the boundaries are perfectly conducting. Simulations were performed using COMSOL Multiphysics.
