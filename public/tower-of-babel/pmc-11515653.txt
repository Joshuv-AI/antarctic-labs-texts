Global landscape of vancomycin-resistant enterococci in hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Seyed Pooria Tadayon Nabavi, Mohsen Chamanara, Arasb Dabbagh Moghaddam, Mahdi Ghorbani, Reza Heidari, Mehdi Shakouri Khomartash, Javad Behroozi, Farhad Motavalli, Ali Shakerimoghaddam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOne of the main risks of infection after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is infection by gram-positive bacteria, including vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE). Based on the format of a global review and meta-analysis study, this study aims to investigate the incidence of VRE bloodstream infection (BSI) after HSCT in colonized individuals.MethodsThe keywords of the systematic search included vancomycin-resistant enterococci and HSCT. These words were searched in Google Scholar, PubMed/Medline, Scopus, and Web of Science databases from January 1, 2000, to March 1, 2024. Studies that reported the prevalence of vancomycin-resistant enterococci in patients undergoing HSCT were included. The random effects model was used for the meta-analyses. Investigations were conducted according to PRISMA guidelines, and the protocol was registered in PROSPERO: CRD42024543491.ResultsOut of 1100 screened papers, 28 were eligible. The random effects model was established to analyze the incidence of VRE BSI after HSCT. The pooled prevalence of co-infection for Allo-HSCT recipients was 3.023 (95% CI, Z-value = -3.5, p-value < 0.0001), and this value for Auto-HSCT recipients was 11.89 (95% CI, Z-value = -2.923, p-value < 0.001). These results showed that the rate of BSI due to vancomycin-resistant enterococcus in Auto-HSCT recipients is higher than Allo-HSCT.ConclusionsThe prevalence of vancomycin-resistant enterococci in Auto-HSCT recipients is higher than that of Allo-HSCT, possibly due to colonization of the intestines of these people with vancomycin-resistant enterococci before transplantation. VRE Colonization before transplantation increases the likelihood of post-transplant VRE BSI and other bacterial infections, including Gram-negative. The strains should be analyzed by sequencing before and after HSCT for a more detailed investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10100-0.Keywords: Vancomycin-resistant enterococci, Hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, Bloodstream infections, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation was initially used more than 50 years ago as a treatment for radiation damage, but today, it is also considered for cancer treatment [1, 2]. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is an invasive treatment. It is used for various blood malignancies, bone marrow failure syndromes, and rare genetic disorders. Different types of HSCT and transplanted cells may be extracted from other sources. Cells for HSCT may come from the patient himself (autologous transplant) or from someone else, such as a sibling or unrelated donor (allogeneic transplant) or a twin [3, 4]. Despite the vast progress in this field, HSCT is not the end of the road, and patients will face many complications after transplantation. These complications are more common in people receiving Allo-HSCT [5]. One of the complications after transplantation is infection. Before the clinical use of this method, the problems related to it, including the occurrence of infection, must be solved [4] Infections are the most common and important cause of mortality and complications after HSCT. In the early stages after catheterization and GVHD (graft versus host disease), neutropenia and mucositis can increase the risk of infection [6, 7]. In the early stages, antibacterial prophylaxis plays a significant role. Hand washing, oral hygiene, a low-bacterial diet, oral fluoroquinolones, and gastrointestinal tract decontamination are necessary precautions [8, 9]. In the early stage after transplantation, there are two primary sources of bacterial infection: the gastrointestinal tract normal flora and the other is the indwelling vascular catheters [10]. One of the bacteria that causes infection in HSCT recipients is Enterococcus. This bacterium is one of the leading causes of colonization and infection in HSCT recipients. This bacterium causes 1300 deaths annually [11, 12]. Vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) are one of the main causes of hospital-acquired infections and bloodstream infections worldwide [13]. In the United States, enterococci after staphylococci are the second reason for nosocomial infections, major antibiotic-resistant diseases, central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI), and Hospital-acquired infective endocarditis [14, 15].

Gastrointestinal colonization occurs frequently, and post-transplant bacteremia is common in HSCT recipients. Risk factors for colonization and VRE infection include 1- mucositis caused by chemotherapy, 2- frequent and long-term hospitalization, 3- use of antibiotics for therapeutic and preventive purposes 4- extensive use of central venous catheters [11, 15]. The emergence of VRE as one of the leading causes of bacteremia in HSCT recipients has raised important clinical questions, including; 1- the best tool for prevention, 2- the role of colonization with this bacterium in predicting bacteremia, 3- treatment, 4- the effects on the transplant results [16–18]. A delay in appropriate antimicrobial therapy in patients with neutropenic fever is associated with increased mortality [19].

In recent years, efforts have been made to prevent and manage VRE infection, including 1-developments in the infection screening, 2-decontamination, and 3-production of new active antibiotics against this organism. Also, awareness of preventive measures and early detection of infection is very effective in controlling the death rate. Therefore, the aim of this study is the systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence of vancomycin-resistant enterococci in patients receiving HSCT worldwide.

Materials and methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the epidemiology of Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococci (VRE) in hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (HSCT) patients globally. The study used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to ensure a comprehensive and reproducible approach.

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using multiple databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, EMBASE, Google Scholar and Cochrane Library, to identify relevant studies published from January 1, 2000, to March 1, 2024. The search strategy was developed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) framework and included free-text terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) related to VRE and HSCT. A combined search strategy using terms related to the topic with Boolean operators was used to capture relevant studies. The databases were searched for “Vancomycin-resistant enterococci” OR “Enterococcus spp” AND “Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation” OR “Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation” OR “Autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation” AND “Coinfection”. However, the search strategy was modified to be compatible with each scientific database. The search was refined iteratively to improve sensitivity and specificity.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they reported VRE prevalence, incidence, or risk factors in HSCT patients. Only studies with full-text available in English were considered. Exclusion criteria were non-human studies, reviews, editorials, case reports, and studies with insufficient data on outcomes of interest.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers extracted data using a standardized form, which included study characteristics (author, year, country, study design), patient demographics, and outcomes related to VRE. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by consulting a third reviewer. Quality assessment was done independently using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) (www.casp-uk.net) checklist. Based on the criteria included in this protocol, the studies were categorized into three groups: weak, strong, and moderate. Eventually, inadequate studies were omitted from the present review (see Additional file 1).

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) software version 3.7. Event rates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using random-effects models to account for between-study heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding one study at a time to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression intercept and Begg’s rank correlation test and adjusted using Duval and Tweedie’s trim and fill method.

Protocol registration

The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) to ensure transparency and reduce the risk of duplication (CRD42024543491(.

Results

Quality control

The articles underwent a quality control review according to the CASP protocol, and overall, most articles fell within the high-quality category.

Selection of studies

After an initial search, 1,100 studies were found. Following evaluations of titles, abstracts, and full texts, 28 studies that met the eligibility criteria were ultimately selected and included in the present review (Fig. 1).

Vancomycin-resistant Enterococci after HSCT allograft in patients

Multiple statistical tests were used to assess the potential publication bias in our meta-analysis of Vancomycin-resistant Enterococci after hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation allografts. Egger’s regression analysis revealed an intercept of -2.291 and a non-significant two-tailed p-value of 0.411, suggesting the absence of significant publication bias. In the same way, Begg’s rank correlation test supported these results, showing Kendall’s tau of -0.12381 and a p-value of 0.520, confirming no bias was present. Duval and Tweedie’s trim and fill technique found no missing studies, indicating that unpublished or missing data does not affect our results. The point estimate seen in a random effects model was robust, with values between 1.62942 and 5.60842, bolstering our confidence in the reliability of our results.

The results of the VRE prevalence HSCT grafts showed significant differences in odds. Kamboj et al. (2018) demonstrated a substantial risk with an odds ratio of 7.334 (95% CI: 5.709 to 9.420), suggesting a high probability of VRE occurrence after allograft recieve. Similarly, Scheich et al. (2017) found a 15.342 odds ratio (95% CI: 8.503 to 27.681), again highlighting the considerable risk linked to VRE after HSCT allograft. On the other hand, Tavadze and colleagues (2014) found a reduced risk, showing an odds ratio of 0.157 (95% CI: 0.087 to 0.286). The pooled odds ratio calculated from the random effects model was 3.023 (95% CI: 1.629 to 5.608), indicating a higher risk of VRE in patients after receiving an allograft post-HSCT across various studies and clinical settings. This compilation of data emphasizes the varied but typically high risk of VRE in HSCT recipients and the crucial necessity for specific preventative measures in this susceptible group (see Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analysis, performed by sequentially excluding each study, demonstrated significant fluctuations in the pooled odds ratios, indicating a dependency of the meta-analysis outcome on specific studies. Discussion

People who receive HSCT are at high risk of bacteremia early after transplantation, which can be due to damage to their innate immune system. This occurs for two main reasons: patients have prolonged neutropenia after receiving their treatment regimen, and, therefore, they are deprived of the first and most essential phagocytes to deal with bacterial infections, and the treatment regime leads to severe intestinal mucositis, and thus, the integrity of the mucous membrane is damaged. These two important problems are associated with a high risk for bacteremia caused by enteric organisms, including VRE, and subsequent severe complications after infection [20–22]. VRE emerged in the late 1980s and is now one of the most common causes of bacteremia in neutropenic cancer patients [23]. Studies estimate the prevalence of VRE infection after HSCT to be 3.6 to 22% and the mortality ranging from 0.04 to 85% [18, 24–27]. Studies have shown that colonization with VRE before transplantation increases the risk of bloodstream infection with VRE after transplantation. Therefore, screening and decontamination strategies to prevent VRE infection have been suggested [18, 28]. In this study, the incidence of VRE BSI after HSCT in colonized subjects was investigated, and the BSI infection rate in subjects receiving allo-HSCT and auto-HSCT was 3.023 and 11.89%, respectively. This difference in BSI rates might stem from variations in preconditioning regimens [29], the types of malignancies treated with HSCT [30], the degree of myelosuppression and immune suppression [31], and bloodstream infection (BSI) [32].

Individuals with VRE colonization before HSCT may be more likely to develop VRE bloodstream infections following HSCT. Nonetheless, this is only a theory that needs more investigation. The cause might be the disturbance of the gut microbiota and heightened gut permeability, which could allow VRE to enter the bloodstream. It is important to mention that this research examined fewer articles on auto-HSCT than allo-HSCT studies. More studies are needed in the future to establish the trustworthiness of our findings, as there are not many studies on auto-HSCT currently available.

VRE Colonization before transplantation increases the risk of bacteremia after transplantation. The reason for this could be the VRE colonization of the gastrointestinal tract and the disruption of its microbiota, which further disrupts the immune system and finally becomes the basis of infection [33, 34]. According to studies, patient-to-patient transmission is the main cause of VRE transmission in the hospital. The microbiota of the gastrointestinal tract can also fight against VRE colonization in the gastrointestinal tract, and this process occurs in different ways, including competition for resources, secretion of bactericidal factors, and indirect stimulation of the host’s immune defense mechanisms that target VRE [35]. With the VRE colonization of the gastrointestinal tract, the integrity of the intestines is disturbed, and further studies have shown that the disruption of the intestinal mechanical barrier leads to the activation of the IL-6 and IL-17 inflammatory factors, leading to the recruitment of ILC-3 and the release of IL-17 and IL-22 and can make the patient susceptible to other infections including lung infection [36–38]. Similarly, in a study, it was found that enterococcus colonization increases the mortality rate in people who suffer from pancreatic cancer [39]. It has been found that antibiotics cause the proliferation of E. faecium in the intestine, change the bacterial layer of the intestine associated with the mucus, and reduce the large intestine wall, the thickness of the mucosa, and the amount of Muc-2 protein. In the intestine, E.faecium is agglutinated in a matrix composed of host molecules (secretory IgA (sIgA), polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR), and epithelial cadherin (E-cadherin) proteins). This matrix may maintain the separation of E. faecium from the epithelium, which causes the persistence and colonization of enterococci in the intestine. Therefore, despite intestinal colonization, there are no signs of intestinal inflammation [40].

Antibiotics reduce intestinal gram-negative bacteria, lead to a decrease in the expression of the antimicrobial peptide Reg3γ, and facilitate the growth of E. faecium [41]. Considering the lack of examination of enterococcus colonization in the gastrointestinal system and the possible interference with microflora, it is necessary to investigate this issue in future studies. On the other hand, VRE pre-transplant infection itself will impose additional healthcare costs of up to 25% (29). The prevalence of infection varies in different countries. It could be due to regional epidemiology, prevention protocols, HSCT type, etc. (52–54).

Additionally, the mortality rate post-engraftment for BSI related to VRE has been higher compared to other organisms, potentially due to acute graft versus host disease and relapsed leukemia [42]. In the study of Clyde D. Ford et al., mentioned in this review, VRE isolates were examined using molecular typing. It was determined that only 3 of the 10 patients studied had the same VRE isolate before and after transplantation, which shows that the source of infection can be other sources, including catheterization, other simultaneous infections, GVHD (chronic or acute), and a long period of hospitalization before transplantation and the risk of contamination from the environmental source and other patients or medical personnel, balance disturbing of the gastrointestinal tract natural flora that could be due to long-term use of antibiotics and disruption of the immune system, T-cell depletion and human leukocyte antigen mismatch [16, 17, 42–50].

The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) has recommended empiric treatment for patients with neutropenia and fever who are at risk of infection with resistant strains, including VRE, which needs further investigation because, in a study in Brazil, empiric treatment of patients Recipients of allo-HSCT with linezolid did not change the mortality rate [51]. Also, in the study of Mini Kamboj et al., which is included in this review, the experimental use of linezolid was declared useless [52]. Antibiotics cause natural intestinal flora dysbiosis, disrupt the flora-mediated immune system, and reduce the secretion of antibacterial peptides that limit the colonization of pathogens. As a result, VRE dominates the intestinal microbiome ecosystem. Then, the mucosal immune system becomes defective due to treatment regimens and C. Clostridium difficile simultaneous infection. The GVHD will then be defective, and the integrity of the gastrointestinal tract mucosa is destroyed. VRE could enter the bloodstream, which is more severe in people with old age or kidney failure [34, 41, 46]. Therefore, a detailed investigation of the strain that causes BSI is necessary from a molecular point of view to determine whether the same strain colonized in the gastrointestinal tract caused blood infection after transplantation, which is one of the limitations of this study. The assessed studies did not mention these data, so we could not access them. Also, in the study of Michael J. Satlin et al. [16], which is included in this review, they declared that bacteremia does not occur after the first episode of neutropenic fever. Still, it happens after the use of antibacterial agents. Also, in the study of Teixeira Mendes et al. [53] included in this review, it was found that BSI after HSCT occurs faster due to VRE than Gram-negative bacteria, which can be due to colonization in the patient’s gastrointestinal tract. Therefore, for a better understanding of the process of infection, we suggest checking the strain type before and after HSCT. It is also recommended that screening for VRE colonization before HSCT can predict the risk of bloodstream infection after HSCT. Strengthening the natural flora of the gastrointestinal tract could be useful in preventing colonization by VRE and the occurrence of possible subsequent infections, which needs further investigation.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis comprehensively investigated the prevalence and risk factors associated with VRE bloodstream infections (BSIs) in HSCT patients. The findings highlight a higher incidence of VRE BSIs in autologous HSCT (Auto-HSCT) recipients compared to allogeneic HSCT (Allo-HSCT) recipients, suggesting that pre-transplant colonization of the gastrointestinal tract with VRE may significantly contribute to post-transplant infections. The study’s findings indicate a need for effective screening, preventive measures, and early detection strategies to manage VRE colonization and infection risks in HSCT patients. The variability in the odds ratios across different studies indicates the influence of geographical and clinical practice differences, emphasizing the necessity for standardized protocols. The results support enhancing the natural gut flora and meticulous infection control practices to mitigate VRE colonization and subsequent BSIs, ultimately aiming to improve patient outcomes in HSCT settings.
