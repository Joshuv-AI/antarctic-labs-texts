Differentiation of arterioles and capillaries in human blood vessel organoids with decellularized splenic matrix
Authors: Feiyang Zheng, Yinan Chen, Guangming Xue, Jianhua Xu, Zijun Wang, Guanyuan Yang, Ge Liu, Miao Lei, Panke Cheng, Yansha Hao, Jingting Zhou, Hong Cao, Zhongliang Qin, Youqian Xu, Zheng Yu, Gang Li, Ju Tan, Liang Gong, Dayu Sun, Chuhong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vascular organoids show great promise for promoting angiogenesis post-myocardial infarction, but current organoid culture methods only form capillaries in vitro, while relying on Matrigel faces challenges due to its undefined composition and potential immune reactions, which hinder clinical translation. To address this issue, a splenic extracellular matrix-derived thermoresponsive hydrogel (SpGel) with type I collagen was developed. This matrix has defined components, avoids the immune response triggered by viruses in Matrigel, and demonstrates the ability to enhance arterioles and capillary differentiation in blood vessel organoids. The gelation time of SpGel/col-I was comparable to that of typical Matrigel/col-I matrix. Additionally, SpGel/col-I significantly boosted angiogenesis of human blood vessel organoids compared to the Matrigel/col-I group. Single-cell sequencing revealed significant upregulation of ITGA5 and ITGB1 in the endothelial cell population within the SpGel/col-I group, and the increase in endothelial and mural cell differentiation was mainly associated with the VEGF and PDGFB signaling pathways. To verify the in vivo reparative effects, SpGel/col-I with hBVOs were co-transplanted into a myocardial infarction animal model and showed improved cardiac function, reduced myocardial fibrosis, and enhanced neovascularization. In summary, we successfully improved the differentiation of arterioles and capillaries in human blood vessel organoids with decellularized splenic matrix, providing an ideal therapeutic approach for repairing infarcted myocardium in clinical settings.

Introduction

According to the global burden of disease (GBD) study, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) resulted in 20.5 million deaths globally in 2021, making it the primary cause of global mortality and a major contributor to disability [1,2]. Myocardial infarction (MI) is a common and serious type of CVD, characterized by the irreversible necrosis of cardiomyocytes due to an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand [3]. The acute ischemic insult leads to cardiomyocyte death, inflammatory cell infiltration, and eventual scar formation [4]. Repairing the heart post-MI is hindered by the restricted proliferation capacity of cardiomyocytes [5]. A novel approach in cardiac regenerative medicine emphasizes vessel regeneration, which is crucial for reestablishing blood flow to ischemic tissues and supporting the survival of cardiomyocytes [6]. In the post-MI setting, the inducement of functional neovascularization in the infarcted tissue has been shown to improve tissue perfusion, limit infarct expansion, and alleviate adverse remodeling [7]. Endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and the subsequent formation of a functional vascular network are thus recognized as prerequisites for effective myocardial repair.

Human blood vessel organoids (hBVOs) are three-dimensional structures formed through the differentiation of multiple vascular cell types from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) and self-organization of these vascular cells during development [8]. These hBVOs contain endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and pericytes, replicating the structure, function, and molecular characteristics of human vasculature [9]. Human vascular development and disease can be modeled with hBVOs, such as mesodermal progenitor differentiation, arteriovenous endothelial cell specification and diabetes vasculopathy [10]. Despite the great potential of hBVOs in regenerative therapy, the differentiation of vascular organoids in vitro remains limited. It requires transplantation in vivo to further promote maturation [11], which has significantly hindered the translational applications of hBVOs. Additionally, Matrigel, extracted from Engelbreth-Holm-Swarm mouse sarcoma, has limitations due to its xenogeneic origin and undefined composition [12]. Also, Lactate dehydrogenase-elevating virus, a mouse virus, has been identified in multiple batches of Matrigel and can influence the immune system [13]. Consequently, there is pressing requirement to develop a strategy for hBVOs culture and differentiation [14].

Decellularized matrices consist of a variety of extracellular macromolecules such as collagen, elastin, and matrix proteins. The spleen is involved in blood storage, clearance of aging red blood cells, and immune response [15]. This unique immune and hematopoietic organ also plays an emerging role in vascular regeneration and extramedullary hematopoiesis [16]. In our previous research, proteomic analysis was conducted to compare the extracellular matrices of the spleen and heart [17]. The splenic extracellular matrix exhibited increased expression levels of proteins related to biological processes such as extracellular structure organization, cell-matrix adhesion, cell junction organization, and cell migration. This suggests that the splenic ECM could offer an effective and biocompatible environment for hBVO development. However, hydrogels made solely from dECM often lack adequate mechanical strength [18]. Type I collagen is the predominant ECM protein in mammals and a key structural element in connective tissues like skin, bone, tendons, and blood vessel walls, providing tissues with robust tensile strength and structural integrity [19,20]. Thus, the splenic decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) was combined with collagen I to create a synergistic system: splenic dECM offers complex tissue-specific biochemical signals, and collagen I provides a standardized mechanically tunable structural framework.

Based on this concept, we generated a method to stimulate vessel sprouting and differentiation of both arterioles and capillaries in hBVOs with physiological matrix materials (Fig. 1A). Our findings revealed that the splenic matrix outperformed Matrigel in enhancing hBVO angiogenesis, with organoids cultured in spleen matrix-collagen I showing a higher level of cellular maturity than those cultured in Matrigel-collagen I. Furthermore, co-transplantation of splenic matrix-collagen I hydrogel and hBVOs led to the restoration of cardiac function in MI animal models and the promotion of arterioles and capillaries reassembly in vivo. These findings illustrate novel options for constructing human arterioles-capillaries structures in vitro using hBVO, thereby positioning it as a promising candidate for applications in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering.Fig. 1Characterization of splenic ECM-based hydrogel. Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of decellularized splenic matrix gel for vessel angiogenesis

DECM sourced from organs is a unique biomaterial that is believed to hold great potential in the translation of regenerative medicine [21]. The matrix recreates stem cell niches by providing essential elements to regulate cell fate and behavior [22]. The dECM also provides scaffolds for the culture and differentiation of organ-specific organoids. Current studies commonly utilize organ-specific dECM to support the growth and functional maturation of corresponding organoids, as seen in investigations of the kidney [23,24], liver [25], and brain organoids [26].

Unlike the parenchymal organs mentioned above, blood vessels are hollow organs with limited sources, scattered distribution, and small volume, making them difficult to prepare as dECM. Therefore, we considered using other organs to prepare dECM for the culture of vascular organoids. The spleen has a loose structure and contains microstructures with branching arterial vessels leading to a venous sinusoidal system [27]. This dECM combines features of basement membranes and interstitial fibrillar matrices, providing a robust and flexible scaffold for blood vessels. Here, we investigated the use of spleen dECM to enhance vascular organoid growth and sprouting.

In order to produce a three-dimensional matrix as an alternative to Matrigel in organoid culture while also providing an angiogenesis niche for vessels, we developed an ECM-based hydrogel from decellularized pig spleen. Porcine spleen tissues were decellularized, digested and lyophilized to develop the splenic extracellular matrix derived thermoresponsive hydrogel (SpGel) (Fig. 1B). When incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, SpGel at a concentration of 10 mg/mL successfully gelled and maintained stable gelation. Therefore, 10 mg/mL decellularized SpGel was utilized in the following experiments. In line with earlier studies, Matrigel-collagen I (Matrigel/col-I) matrix was used to embed hBVOs, and a similar SpGel-collagen I (SpGel/col-I) matrix was mixed accordingly. The SpGel/col-I is dominated by ECM proteins including collagens, fibronectin, laminin, and proteoglycans (Table S1). SEM images revealed that Matrigel/col-I, SpGel and SpGel/col-I all displayed a porous structure (Fig. 1C). The viscoelasticity of these matrices was assessed by measuring their storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) with a rheometer. The traditional Matrigel/col-I showed a rapid gelation initiation (about 100 s) and finished gelation within 12 min. In contrast, SpGel alone showed a different gelation progress, with a slower onset and a more prolonged completion time. However, the inclusion of Collagen-I boosted its gelation, leading to a gelation pattern similar to that of Matrigel/col-I (Fig. 1D).

Before exploring the impact of Splenic matrix on three-dimensional vascular organoids, we first evaluated its angiogenesis effect in conventional two-dimensional human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). In the tube formation assay performed in flat cell cultures with HUVECs, Matrigel/col-I, SpGel, and SpGel/col-I all promoted endothelial tube formation compared to the PBS control group. However, there were no statistically significant differences in the number of tube junctions and branches among the three groups (Fig. S1, Supporting information).

Biocompatibility evaluation of SpGel/col-I hydrogel for vascular organoids

To evaluate the influence of SpGel on the culture and differentiation of vascular organoids, hBVOs were cultured and embedded into hydrogels at the vessel sprouting stage. Initially, human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) were aggregated to form embryonic bodies (EBs), which were then differentiated into mesoderm using BMP-4 and CHIR99021. Subsequent induction of the vascular lineage was achieved with VEGF165 and forskolin. On day 6, the hBVOs were encapsulated in Matrigel/col-I, SpGel, or SpGel/col-I to promote vascular sprouting driven by FGF-2 and VEGF165 (Fig. 2A). By day 7, the first day post-embedding, small vascular buds emerged from the hBVOs, with the longest buds observed in the SpGel group, followed by the SpGel/col-I group. By day 9, the cells in the vascular organoids further migrated, forming vascular sprouts. The sprout length of hBVOs in the SpGel and SpGel/col-I groups exceeded that in the Matrigel/col-I group on both days 9 and 11. The live-dead cell assay was performed on day 11 to investigate the viability of hBVO cells in three matrices. Dead cells labeled with PI were mainly located in the central area of hBVOs in the SpGel group, while no dead cells were observed in the vascular organoids in the Matrigel/col-I and SpGel/col-I groups (Fig. 2D and E). These results indicate that SpGel/col-I effectively supports cell survival and increases the formation of vascular sprouts. As a result, SpGel/col-I was chosen as the alternative hydrogel for further investigation.

dECM-derived hydrogels effectively replicate the biological functions of native ECM. These hydrogels have the dual benefit of providing mechanical support through the preservation of tissue-specific ECM structure and delivering biochemical signals by retaining bioactive components such as growth factors [28]. With its native ECM components, excellent biocompatibility, and low immunogenicity, dECM serves as an optimal pro-angiogenic additive in tissue engineering [29,30]. Here, in the three-dimensional organoid model, it was confirmed that spleen-derived dECM enhances the growth and angiogenic sprouting of vascular organoids, while also demonstrating excellent biocompatibility.

Effects of SpGel/col-I hydrogel on angiogenesis during the vascular sprouting of HBVOs

To investigate the effect of splenic hydrogel on vascular organoid differentiation, we assessed hBVOs for endothelial and mural cells after 5 days of sprouting. Both CD31+ endothelial cells and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) + smooth muscle cells were present in organoids cultured in either Matrigel/col-I or SpGel/col-I matrices. The endothelial cells exhibited a radial distribution pattern, extending from the organoid centers to their peripheries while forming intricate, branched networks that were closely accompanied by smooth muscle cells (Fig. 3A). To quantitatively evaluate the endothelial complexity in the hBVOs, the confocal maximum intensity projection images of CD31 staining of the entire vascular organoid were analyzed using AngioTool software. It showed that the endothelial cells within the hBVO in the SpGel/col-I group showed significantly higher numbers of vascular junctions, vascular density, and vessel length compared to those in the Matrigel/col-I group (Fig. 3B and C). Flow cytometry analysis further supported these findings, showing that CD31+ endothelial cells comprised 19.69 ± 2.53% of cells in SpGel/col-I organoids versus only 11.99 ± 1.23% in Matrigel/col-I cultures (Fig. 3D and E). In gene level, the expression of eNOS, VWF, Acta2 and Desmin were significantly increased in hBVOs embedded in SpGel/col-I compared to the Matrigel/col-I group (Fig. 3F). The expression of ZO-1 and CD144 (VE-cadherin) were detected in the hBVOs, indicting the existence of tight junction and adherens junction (Fig. S2, Supporting information). Further, the perfusion of the vascular organoids were indicated by the location of 50 nm red fluorescent microspheres in the vascular tubes in hBVOs encapsulated in the SpGel/col-I (Fig. 3G). In vitro, dECM models have been used to study the role of the ECM in regulating cell differentiation. For instance, dECM from neonatal spinal cord has been shown to promote neural progenitor cells to differentiate into neurons [31]. Similar results were observed when guiding fetal mesodermal precursors on human heart dECM towards cardiac cells [32]; as well as with STIM1-overexpressing adipose-derived stem cells on adipose-derived dECM bioscaffold into myogenic cells [33]. Our previous research has shown that nanofibrous splenic hydrogel effectively induces hiPSCs into endothelial cells [17]. In this study, it was found that SpGel/col-1 hydrogels were more effective in stimulating angiogenesis in human vascular organoids compared to Matrigel/col-1 hydrogel. The splenic hydrogel also induced endothelial and mural cell differentiation, suggesting that dECM can provide specific cues for organoid culture and maturation.

A total of 25,470 cells from the Matrigel/col-I and SpGel/col-I groups were visualized in the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plot (Fig. 4A). The cells within the vascular organoids were categorized into 5 distinct clusters based on known marker genes: MSCs (10300 cells, 40.44%, marked by HAND1); ECs (2130 cells, 9.36%, marked by CLDN5 and PECAM1); SMCs (4086 cells, 16.04%, marked by TAGLN and MYL9); pericytes (PCs) (6291 cells, 24.7%, marked by PTN) and transitional cells (TCs) (2663 cells, 10.46%) (Fig. 4B and C). In the SpGel/col-I group, the proportion of MSCs in hBVOs was significantly lower compared to the Matrigel/col-I group (8.92% vs 63.14%). Conversely, the proportion of ECs, PCs, and SMCs in vascular organoids in the SpGel/col-I group was significantly higher (ECs: 14.17% vs 4.18%; PCs: 40.91% vs 13.02%; SMCs: 26.34% vs 8.62%. Fig. 4D). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) confirmed the accuracy of cell identity (Fig. 4E). Monocle 3 was employed to infer cell origins and development within vascular organoids. The developmental trajectory indicated that PCs, SMCs and ECs originate from the MSC population (Fig. S4, Supporting information). Further, the EC subpopulations analysis showed arterial ECs, capillary ECs, and proliferating ECs were identified in both groups. The expression of CXCR4 and INSR, along with colocalization with CD31 was observed to confirm the EC subtypes in hBVOs (Fig. S5, Supporting information).

For the induction of vascular cell differentiation, orthogonal activation of transcription factors like ETV2 and Nkx3.1 is essential, resulting in differentiation into endothelial, mural cells, or high endothelial venules [34,35]. The extracellular matrix also plays a crucial role in vasculogenesis, influencing the differentiation, morphogenesis, and function of the vasculature through mechanical stimuli [36]. Advances in material science and organoids have made it possible for us to replicate the ECM mechanical microenvironment in organoid development [37,38]. We therefore focused on integrin subunit genes which are important for cell adhesion and are influenced by changes in matrix properties. ITGA5 and ITGB1 were predominantly expressed in the EC cluster and were significantly elevated in the EC populations in the SpGel/col-I group compared to the Matrigel/col-I group. VEGF/VEGFR-1,2 signaling is known to regulate angiogenesis during vessel maturation, and we found that KDR (VEGFR2) was mainly expressed in the EC cluster and showed a significant upregulation in the EC population in the SpGel/col-I group compared to the Matrigel/col-I group. Moreover, PDGFB/PDGFRB signaling regulates mural cell recruitment during vascular development, and PDGFB expression was found to be increased in ECs in the SpGel/col-I group compared to the Matrigel/col-I group (Fig. 5C and D).

To validate the aforementioned pathways, immunostaining was conducted to evaluate protein expression (Fig. S8, Supporting Information). Additionally, hBVOs incorporated in SpGel/col-I were subjected to VEGFR2 inhibitor SU1498 or PDGFR inhibitor Imatinib for further validation. The impact of mechanotransduction signaling was investigated by exposing hBVOs in SpGel/col-I to the FAK inhibitor PF562271 and the YAP/TAZ inhibitor-1. Treatment with PF562271, YAP/TAZ inhibitor-1, SU1498, and Imatinib resulted in decreased area, sprout thinning, and diminished EC differentiation.

SpGel/col-I combined with hBVO improves cardiac function in infarcted rats by promoting angiogenesis

Fig. 6A outlines the experimental procedure for the animal experiments. After ligating the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery to induce myocardial infarction, the infarcted areas were treated by injecting PBS, hBVO, SpGel/col-I, or a combination of SpGel/col-I and hBVO at multiple sites around the infarct zone. The potential damage of syringe injection was evaluated and excluded with Calcein/PI staining (Fig. S9, Supporting Information) and the retention of hBVOs was confirmed on day 1 and day 7 post injection by PKH26-labeled using IVIS (Fig. S10, Supporting Information). Cardiac function of the rats was assessed by echocardiography at one and four weeks post-myocardial infarction. After one week of treatment, compared to the PBS group, the LVEF and LVFS in the SpGel/col-I + hBVO group were increased by 15.67% and 7.52%, respectively. Both parameters were significantly higher in the SpGel/col-I + hBVO group than in the hBVO or SpGel/col-I groups. No significant differences were observed in LVIDs or LVIDd among the PBS, hBVO, SpGel/col-I, and SpGel/col-I + hBVO groups (Fig. 6B). After four weeks of treatment, LVEF and LVFS were significantly elevated, while LVIDs and LVIDd were significantly reduced in the SpGel/col-I + hBVO group compared to the PBS group. The LVEF and LVFS of the hBVO and SpGel/col-I groups were also significantly higher than those in the PBS group, though there were no significant differences in LVIDs or LVIDd among the hBVO, SpGel/col-I, and SpGel/col-I + hBVO groups (Fig. 6B and C). When observing changes in LVEF from week 1 to week 4 post-treatment in each group, it was found that cardiac function in the PBS group continued to deteriorate, whereas SpGel/col-I + hBVO significantly reversed the decline in pumping function (Fig. S11, Supporting information). (E) Quantitative analysis of capillary (CD31+) and arteriole (α-SMA+) densities in the heart. (n = 3). ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

To further evaluate the impact of SpGel/col-I + hBVO co-transplantation on the internal structure of the heart post-MI, we conducted histological staining and immunohistochemical staining on heart tissues four weeks post-treatment. Masson staining images revealed the infarct size, with normal myocardial fibers stained red and post-infarction collagen fibers appearing blue. In comparison to the PBS group, fibrosis was reducedby 9.60% in the hBVO group, 10.12% in the SpGel/col-I group, and 19.95% in the SpGel/col-I + hBVO group (Fig. S12, Supporting information). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed that the SpGel/col-I + hBVO group exhibited a significant improvement in left ventricular wall thickness compared to the PBS group (Fig. S13, Supporting information). Immunofluorescence staining for neovascularization indicated that the densities of arterioles and capillaries in the SpGel/col-I + hBVO group were significantly higher than those in the PBS group and approximately 2-3 times higher than those in the hBVO or SpGel/col-I groups. These findings suggest that SpGel/col-I + hBVO effectively promotes neovascularization in the infarcted myocardium, contributing to the development of both capillaries and arterioles (Fig. 6D and E).

Angiogenesis plays a vital role in cardiac repair by facilitating neovascularization, ensuring the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to the hostile microenvironment. This process not only enhances blood flow to the ischemic myocardium, aiding in tissue repair and functional recovery, but also helps prevent further expansion of the infarct zone. Bone marrow-derived endothelial progenitor cells contribute to this process by releasing multiple cytokines through paracrine mechanisms, thereby promoting angiogenesis and tissue regeneration in cases of acute myocardial infarction [39]. To further improve vascular regeneration in ischemic hearts, a dual stem cell approach has been developed, combining endothelial progenitor cells with mesenchymal stem cells to enhance blood vessel formation synergistically [40]. While this strategy shows potential in reversing pathological processes through tissue repair, achieving full functional regeneration of myocardial or vascular tissues remains challenging. Another approach involves using cardiosphere-derived cells for myocardial infarction repair, as these cells can differentiate into both cardiomyocytes and vascular endothelial cells, while improving overall cardiac function [41]. However, a major limitation is the low survival and engraftment rates of transplanted cells in hostile ischemic microenvironments. In this study, we combined hBVOs with SpGel/col-I hydrogel, which significantly enhanced the survival and maturation of hBVOs, forming both arterioles and capillaries. This led to enhanced cardiac function and neovascularization following myocardial infarction.

Our research offers significant potential for regenerative medicine, yet the transition from bench-side innovation to bedside application is filled with obstacles that need to be systematically resolved. The primary obstacle in utilizing organoids for tissue replacement is ensuring immunogenicity and long-term host integration. While autologous iPSCs are commonly suggested to avoid immune rejection, extended culture periods may lead to immunogenic mutations or incomplete differentiation, leaving behind pluripotent cells that carry a tumorigenic risk. An encouraging approach involves creating “immune-cloaked” universal hPSC lines using genetic engineering [42]. The second major hurdle is the transition from small-scale laboratory production to large-scale, Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP)-compliant biomanufacturing [43]. Current organoid protocols are often labor-intensive, expensive, and exhibit significant batch-to-batch variability. In order to address this issue, it is crucial to utilize automation and bioreactor systems, along with the establishment of strong quality control metrics. As the clinical application of organoid-based regenerative therapies remains a future prospect, the path forward is becoming clearer, necessitating a concerted, multidisciplinary effort to address the critical challenges of immunogenicity and scalable manufacturing.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a strategy to enhance vessel sprouting and differentiation of arterioles and capillaries in hBVOs using a three-dimensional scaffold matrix, SpGel/col-I hydrogel. The splenic hydrogel proved suitable for vascular organoid growth and served as an effective alternative to Matrigel, which is derived from mouse sarcoma. Moreover, SpGel/col-I significantly improved angiogenesis by boosting the differentiation of both endothelial and mural cells compared to Matrigel/col-I. This effect was facilitated by heightened focal adhesion in endothelial and mural cells, which created a conducive microenvironment with increased VEGFR2 and PDGFB expression in endothelial cells, aiding in angiogenesis and the recruitment of mural cell. When transplanted in vivo, the SpGel/col-I hydrogel combined with hBVOs promoted the growth of arterioles and capillaries, ultimately restoring cardiac function post myocardial infarction. These findings are crucial for advancing vascular organoid maturation and laying the groundwork for future clinical applications of organoid-based therapies. Nevertheless, a four-week monitoring period following MI might only detect initial scar formation and not indicate fibrosis progression. Therefore, upcoming research may require prolonging the observation duration in animal trials to more accurately assess fibrosis in MI remodeling and evaluate the persistence of angiogenesis.

Experimental section

Synthesis of the spleen gel

The physiological matrix gel was synthesized following our previous protocols [17], with fresh porcine spleen tissue cut into 1-3 mm3 pieces and thoroughly rinsed with saline. Then the tissue was decellularized with a 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution until the ECM turned white, followed by removal of antigens using ultra-nuclease treatment. After sterilization and lyophilization for 48-72 h, the decellularized spleen matrix was ground into a fine powder. The powder was digested with pepsin for 48 h, after which the pH was adjusted to 7.4 using 1 M sodium hydroxide and the digest was lyophilized again. To prepare the spleen extracellular matrix-derived thermoresponsive hydrogel (SpGel), the decellularized spleen matrix powder was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to the designated concentration (6-10 mg/mL) and then incubated at 37 °C to allow gelation.

For the preparation of the corresponding SpGel/col-I hydrogel, type I collagen solution (2 mg/mL; Corning, NY, USA) was mixed with PBS-dissolved decellularized spleen matrix solution (10 mg/mL) or Matrigel (8-12 mg/mL; Corning, NY, USA) in a volume ratio of 1:1 on ice and maintained at 4 °C prior to gelation. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C to form the SpGel/col-I hydrogel.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The hydrogel solution was first allowed to gel at 37 °C for 2 h before being fixed overnight using glutaraldehyde. After fixation, the samples were frozen at −80 °C for 1 h and then quickly transferred to a freeze dryer for lyophilization. Once dried, the sample was carefully mounted on a copper stub with double-sided conductive tape and coated with a thin layer of gold particles to enhance conductivity. The prepared sample was then observed under a scanning electron microscope (CrossBeam 340, Zeiss, Germany) at 10- 20 kV to capture SEM images.

Rheological measurements

The gelation kinetics and rheological properties of the thermosensitive gel were characterized using a temperature-controlled rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) equipped with a 40-mm-diameter parallel-plate. First, the temperature was linearly increased from 4 °C to 37 °C, and the modulus changes were monitored via a time sweep under oscillatory shear mode (frequency: 1 Hz, strain: 1%) to record the gelation process. After gel formation, a frequency sweep (strain: 1%, angular frequency range: 0.1–100 rad/s) was performed within the linear viscoelastic region (as determined by prior strain amplitude sweep) to obtain the frequency dependence of the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″).

Tube formation assay

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Lonza, Waltham, MA) were cultured on gelatin-coated tissue culture plates in Endothelial Basel Medium-2 (EBM-2) with supplements (Lonza). Actively proliferating cells from passages 3-7 at 70-90% confluency were used for tube formation assays. HUVECs cells at a concentration of 2 × 106 cells/mL were plated on plates coated with PBS, Matrigel/col-I, SpGel or SpGel/col-I. A positive control was carried out with 100 ng/mL VEGF. Following a 6-h incubation period, images were captured using a Leica DM IL LED Microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Tubular structures were analyzed by quantifying the number of intersection nodes and branches in the endothelial cell network with Image J software.

Vascular organoid culture and embedding into the gels

The experiments were conducted using the DYR0100 human iPSC line (SCSP-1301) from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The iPS cells (passages 10 to 30) were grown on Matrigel in mTeSR1 medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) without feeders and were regularly checked for mycoplasma contamination. HBVO differentiation was carried out as described previously. In brief, hiPSCs were dissociated into single cells and 1.5 × 105 cells were seeded into Aggrewell 800 plates (STEMCELL Technologies) coated with anti-adhesion solution for aggregation. The cell aggregates were treated on day 1 with 12 μM CHIR99021 (BioGems, Cranbury, NJ, USA) and 30 ng mL−1 BMP4 (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) in N2B27-medium for mesoderm induction, and on day 4 with 100 ng mL−1 VEGF-A (PeproTech) and 2 μm Forskolin (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) in N2B27-medium for vascular lineage induction.

On day 6, the organoids were embedded into Matrigel/col-I, SpGel, or SpGel/col-I in a 12-well plate. In brief, 0.5 mL of thermoresponsive matrix was embedded into the wells and incubated at 37 °C for 2h for solidification as the bottom layer. Approximately 50 organoids hBVOs were mixed with another 0.5 mL of matrix and incubated for additional 2h for solidification as the top layer. The gels (around 22 mm in diameter) were cultured in StemPro-34 complete medium (Gibco) supplemented with 100 ng mL−1 VEGF-A, 100 ng mL−1 FGF-2 (Peprotech), and 15% FBS (Gibco) for 5 days to promote the sprouting of vascular networks.

Brightfield images of hBVOs were captured with a Leica DM IL LED Microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) on days 7, 9, and 11. The sprout length from the outer edge of the vascular sphere is measured in ten random directions of a vascular organoid after embedding in the matrix to determine the average the sprout length.

Live-dead cell assay

According to the protocol of the Live-Dead Cell Staining Kit (Proteintech, USA), a staining solution containing 2 μM Calcein AM and 4.5 μM propidium iodide (PI) was prepared. On day 11, hBVOs embedded in Matrigel/col-I, SpGel, or SpGel/col-I were rinsed twice with DPBS and incubated in the staining solution at 37 °C for 40 min. Subsequently, the samples were washed twice with DPBS and inspected with a Stellaris 5 confocal microscope (Leica, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining

After carefully removing the culture medium, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min. The hBVOs embedded in the gels were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X 100 for 1 h, followed by three washes with PBS. After blocking at room temperature for 2 h, the gel samples were incubated with anti-human CD31 (ab281583, 1:400; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti-α-SMA (A2547,1:200; Sigma) primary antibodies on a shaker at 4 °C overnight. Following three washes with PBS, the samples were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies in the dark at room temperature for 2 h and stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min. Finally, the sample was cleared with pre-warmed RapiClear solution overnight and visualized using a Stellaris 5 confocal microscope (Leica). To assess the level of angiogenesis in the vascular organoids within the gels, junction number, vessel density, and vessel length were quantified using AngioTool software (Version 0.6a).

Flow cytometry

The hBVOs embedded in the gels were first washed with Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) buffer, then treated with HBSS containing collagenase. The sample was vortexed in a 37 °C water bath for about 20 min until no visible clumps remained, at which point the digestion process was terminated. After filtering, the solutions were incubated with APC-CD31 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) at 4 °C for 25 min in the dark. Following three washes with staining buffer, the percentage of positive cells was measured using a flow cytometer (BD Accuri™ C6 Plus, NJ, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo software.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from vascular organoids using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined by NanoDrop spectrophotometry, with A260/A280 ratios of 1.8–2.0 deemed acceptable. One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). Cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, with a subsequent melt curve analysis to confirm amplification specificity. Relative gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in biological triplicates.

Vascular network perfusion

To evaluate the vascular network perfusion function, the hBVOs embedded in the SpGel/col-I at day 11 were gently detached from the hydrogel matrix and dissected along the maximum cross-section under a stereomicroscope. The specimens were subsequently immersed in 1 mL of PKH67 working solution and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, a suspension of 50 nm red fluorescent microspheres was prepared at a concentration of 0.01% (w/v). The organoids were transferred to a 35 mm confocal dish containing 2 mL of the microsphere suspension, ensuring complete submersion. To facilitate perfusion, the dish was placed in a vacuum drying oven and subjected to a vacuum treatment at −1000 kPa for 30 min. Finally, the samples were washed on an orbital shaker (100 rpm) for 5 min and visualized using a Stellaris 5 confocal microscope (Leica).

Single-cell sequencing

The hBVO samples were preserved in GEXSCOPE Tissue Preservation Solution (Singleron, China) and transported to the Singleron lab with an ice pack. Following three washes with Hanks Balanced Salt Solution, the specimens were cut into 1-2 mm pieces. The tissue debris was subsequently digested with 2 mL GEXSCOPE Tissue Dissociation Solution at 37 °C for 15min in a 15 mL centrifuge tube with sustained agitation. Cells were filtered through 40-μm sterile strainers and underwent centrifugation at 300g for 5 min. The supernatant was then discarded, and the pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of PBS. Once the cell viability exceeded 80%, subsequent sample processing could be performed.

We prepared single-cell suspensions at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/mL in PBS and loaded them onto microfluidic devices for scRNA-seq library construction following the Singleron GEXSCOPE protocol with the GEXSCOPE Single-Cell RNA Library Kit. The process consisted of several essential steps. Initially, the cells were lysed to release their contents, followed by trapping mRNA molecules. Subsequently, barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were applied to label the cells and mRNA molecules for tracking. After labeling, reverse transcription was conducted to convert mRNA into complementary DNA (cDNA), which was then amplified to increase its quantity for sequencing. Lastly, the cDNA was fragmented to prepare it for sequencing, ensuring optimal library quality for subsequent analysis.

Individual libraries were diluted and pooled for sequencing, followed by sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus with 150bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were processed with FastQC and Fastp to filter out low-quality reads, and then poly-A tails and adaptor sequences were removed by Cutadapt. The cleaned reads were mapped to the reference genome GRCh38 (Ensembl version 99 annotation) using STAR, and gene counts as well as UMI counts were obtained through Feature Counts software. Finally, expression matrix files were generated based on gene counts and UMI counts for subsequent analyses.

Single-cell bioinformatics analysis

Cell type identification for each cluster in bioinformatics analysis was based on the expression of canonical markers from the SynEcoSysTM reference database by Singleron Biotechnology, along with the use of marker genes for vascular cells previously reported as references [[44], [45], [46]]. Monocle3 was utilized to evaluate the differentiation potential of individual cells in the single-cell RNA sequencing data. Trajectory inference was performed with MSCs specified as the starting position. Differentially expressed genes were identified through gene ontology and KEGG analyses, with pathways having adjusted P values below 0.05 considered significantly enriched and displayed as dot plots.

MI rat model and treatment

Eight-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats were obtained from the Army Medical University Animal Center and housed in an air-conditioned room. They were kept on a 12-h light:dark cycle with access to food and water available ad libitum. The experiments conducted were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University (AMUWEC20250106), with efforts made to minimize animal usage and suffering.

The myocardial infarction model in rats was established following our previous reports. Briefly, the rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (80 mg/kg) and ventilated with a respirator. Following the exposure of the heart, the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery was ligated using 6-0 sutures. A noticeable pale color was evident distal to the ligation site, indicating ischemia.

After completing the modeling procedure, 60 μL of PBS, hBVOs (around 100 organoids at day 6, with a size of 200-250 μm), SpGel/col-I, or SpGel/col-I + hBVO were injected intramyocardially into the infarct region using a 29G insulin syringe. To prevent immune rejection, cyclosporine A (10 mg kg−1 day−1, Neocyspin) was injected intraperitoneally until the completion of the study. We monitored the rats' heart function using echocardiography at three key time points – 0, 1 and 4 weeks after surgery. The study concluded 28 days after the hBVO transplantation, at which point the animals were euthanized for final analysis.

Heart histological analysis

The hearts of the rats were harvested and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Following fixation, the tissues were subsequently dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 5-μm-thick sections for histological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining as well as Masson's trichrome staining. The Masson's trichrome staining method was utilized to distinguish the infarct regions, with normal muscle fibers appearing red and collagen fibers staining blue. Additionally, some sections were subjected to immunofluorescence staining using antibodies against CD31 and α-SMA to evaluate capillary and arteriolar densities, respectively. Images were captured under a Stellaris 5 confocal microscope (Leica).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for comparisons involving more than two groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
