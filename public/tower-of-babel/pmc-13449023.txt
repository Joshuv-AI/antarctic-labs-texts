Relative Dose Intensity and Survival Outcomes in Older Adults With Lymphoma: A Single‐Center Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Akira Mochizuki, Shotaro Hagiwara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTreatment intensity in older adults with lymphoma is often reduced because of frailty, comorbidities, and poor performance status (PS). Whether chronological age or delivered treatment intensity better predicts outcomes remains unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 79 patients aged ≥ 65 years with newly diagnosed lymphoma initiating first‐line therapy. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was the primary outcome and overall survival (OS) secondary. Relative dose intensity (RDI) effects were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) per 10%‐point increase. Cox models adjusted for age, sex, Ann Arbor stage, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and PS; diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) models additionally included the International Prognostic Index (IPI).ResultsMedian RDI was lower in patients aged ≥ 75 years than in those aged 65–74 years (63.3% vs. 85.4%; p < 0.001). In the full cohort, higher RDI was independently associated with longer PFS (HR, 0.709; 95% CI, 0.576–0.873; p = 0.001), but not OS (HR, 0.814; 95% CI, 0.635–1.044; p = 0.106); higher CCI was associated with shorter OS (HR, 1.442; 95% CI, 1.046–1.988; p = 0.025). In the DLBCL subgroup, higher RDI was associated with longer PFS (HR, 0.217; 95% CI, 0.088–0.536; p < 0.001) and OS (HR, 0.320; 95% CI, 0.127–0.806; p = 0.016).ConclusionsHigher RDI was associated with longer PFS in the full cohort and DLBCL subgroup, and with longer OS in DLBCL. RDI may provide prognostic information beyond age and IPI‐defined risk but may reflect physiological reserve and treatment tolerance. Prospective validation is required.Keywords: chemotherapy, lymphoma, older adults, overall survival, progression‐free survival, relative dose intensity

Introduction

Advances in lymphoma treatment have improved outcomes, leading to more older adults receiving therapy. However, managing these patients remains challenging due to comorbidities, organ dysfunction, frailty, and social factors that may limit the feasibility of standard treatment.

Standard immunochemotherapy regimens have been established for aggressive lymphoma, but full‐dose treatment may not be suitable for all older patients [1, 2]. Clinical trials often enroll fit participants. Patients with multiple comorbidities or poor functional status are underrepresented [3, 4]. In practice, older adults often get modified treatment: lower starting doses, dose delays, reductions, or early discontinuation [2, 5]. Relative dose intensity (RDI) reflects the actual treatment delivered. It has been linked with treatment response and prognosis [6, 7]. Real‐world care varies, often using lower‐dose regimens and personalized supportive care [2, 8]. As such, studying the link between treatment intensity and clinical outcomes in a real‐world hospital setting is important.

This study aimed to clarify age‐related differences in outcomes among older adults (aged 65 years or older) with lymphoma who received first‐line treatment at our hospital. We also examined whether age‐group differences in outcomes could be partially explained by treatment intensity. Because previous studies have largely focused on diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and because lymphoma subtypes differ in biology and treatment, we conducted a subgroup analysis in patients with DLBCL, the largest and most homogeneous subgroup in the full cohort.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single center using electronic medical and imaging records.

Patients

Patients diagnosed with lymphoma at our hospital from April 2021 to July 2025 who began initial treatment here were screened. Follow‐up extended from treatment initiation to the last follow‐up visit or death. All consecutive patients during this period were considered; patients with HIV‐associated lymphoma and those with primary or secondary central nervous system involvement were excluded. Among the 101 eligible patients, 79 aged 65 years or older formed the full cohort for the present analyses; the patient‐selection process is shown in Figure S1. Radiotherapy, when administered, was delivered as consolidation or palliation and did not affect eligibility, provided that first‐line systemic therapy was initiated at our institution.

Variables and Definitions

Age was analyzed as 65–74 versus ≥ 75 years. Ann Arbor stage and ECOG performance status (PS) were recorded by category. For descriptive and response analyses, the Ann Arbor stage was presented as stages I–IV, and ECOG PS as individual categories. In the Cox models, Ann Arbor stage and ECOG PS were modeled as ordinal variables, with hazard ratios representing a 1‐stage and a 1‐point increase, respectively. Comorbidities were measured using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Lymphoma lineage was classified as B‐cell lymphoma, T‐cell lymphoma, or classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL), and histological subtype was recorded separately. The 75‐year threshold was based on clinical grounds, as the age above which dose attenuation is most often considered. CCI was analyzed as a continuous variable (per 1‐point increment). A subgroup analysis was performed in patients with DLBCL (n = 48). For this subgroup, large B‐cell lymphomas were grouped as DLBCL: cases originally recorded as intravascular large B‐cell lymphoma, high‐grade B‐cell lymphoma (including double‐hit/MYC‐BCL2), EBV‐positive DLBCL, methotrexate‐ or other immunodeficiency‐associated large B‐cell lymphoproliferative disorders, and DLBCL with concurrent follicular lymphoma. Throughout this manuscript, the DLBCL subgroup refers to this analytically grouped population. The International Prognostic Index (IPI) was evaluated only in the DLBCL subgroup and was calculated as the sum of five adverse factors: age > 60 years, serum lactate dehydrogenase above the institutional upper limit of normal (222 U/L), ECOG PS ≥ 2, Ann Arbor stage III–IV, and involvement of more than one extranodal site. IPI was analyzed as a continuous variable per 1‐point increase.

RDI was defined as the ratio of the actual to the planned standard dose. Specifically, RDI was calculated as the cumulative delivered dose divided by the cumulative planned standard dose for each cytotoxic agent, and the regimen‐level RDI was defined as the mean of these agent‐level values. For R‐CHOP, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and vincristine were included, whereas rituximab and prednisolone were excluded. For patients who discontinued treatment early, the planned number of cycles was used as the denominator. Dose modifications were made at the physician's discretion based on age, PS, organ function, including renal and hepatic function, and treatment‐related toxicity and could occur at treatment initiation or during therapy. Treatment response to first‐line therapy was assessed according to the Lugano classification. Recorded adverse events included grade 3 or 4 adverse events and transfusion requirements.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was progression‐free survival (PFS), defined as the time from initiation of first‐line therapy to progression or death. Overall survival (OS), defined as the time from first‐line therapy start to death from any cause, was secondary. Patients without an event were censored at the last follow‐up. Treatment response was evaluated as complete response (CR), partial response, stable disease, or progressive disease [9].

Because of the limited number of deaths, the OS analysis was considered exploratory.

Statistical Analysis

Age and body surface area were summarized using the median and range, whereas CCI, IPI, and RDI were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as counts and proportions. Baseline features were compared between the two age groups (65–74 vs. ≥ 75 years) using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Clinical and treatment‐related variables were compared between patients with and without CR using the Mann–Whitney U test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Associations of RDI with age, ECOG PS, and CCI were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient; the association between RDI and IPI was additionally assessed in the DLBCL subgroup. The association between RDI and the occurrence of grade 3–4 adverse events was also assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.

We estimated PFS and OS using the Kaplan–Meier method. The log‐rank test was used for comparison. Survival was compared across histological subtypes using global log‐rank tests. To visualize the association between RDI and survival without imposing a single clinical cutoff, RDI was categorized separately in the full cohort and the DLBCL subgroup into three cohort‐specific, quartile‐based RDI groups: Q1 (first quartile; Low), Q2–Q3 (second and third quartiles; Middle), and Q4 (fourth quartile; High). Differences among the three groups were evaluated using global log‐rank tests.

Prognostic factors for PFS and OS were evaluated using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. Hazard ratios (HRs) are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The HR for RDI represents a 10‐percentage‐point increase. CCI and IPI were modeled as continuous variables per 1‐point increase, whereas Ann Arbor stage and ECOG PS were modeled as ordinal variables per 1‐stage and 1‐point increase, respectively.

The covariates were prespecified on the basis of clinical relevance and were entered simultaneously, irrespective of their univariable p values. The multivariable models for PFS and OS in the full cohort included RDI, age group, sex, Ann Arbor stage, CCI, and ECOG PS. The DLBCL subgroup models included the same variables plus IPI. The same covariate set was used for the PFS and OS models within each analysis population.

Missing values were handled by complete‐case analysis. All tests were two‐sided at a 0.05 significance level and performed using EZR [10].

Results

Patients' Characteristics

A total of 79 patients with lymphoma aged ≥ 65 years were included in the analysis. Of these, 40 were aged 65–74 years and 39 were aged ≥ 75 years. The median age was 74 years (range, 65–95 years). PS (p = 0.039), CCI (p = 0.020), and Ann Arbor stage (p = 0.020) differed significantly between the age groups. The median RDI was 70.0% (IQR, 60.7%–87.5%) in the full cohort and was significantly lower in patients aged ≥ 75 years than in those aged 65–74 years (63.3% [IQR, 45.5%–70.0%] vs. 85.4% [IQR, 69.9%–95.3%]; p < 0.001) (Table 1).

The DLBCL subgroup comprised 48 patients, including 28 aged 65–74 years and 20 aged ≥ 75 years. The median RDI was significantly lower in patients aged ≥ 75 years than in those aged 65–74 years (61.3% [IQR, 36.3%–70.0%] vs. 87.2% [IQR, 70.6%–95.3%]; p < 0.001). In contrast, the median IPI did not differ significantly between the age groups (p = 0.205) (Table S1).

RDI was inversely correlated with age (ρ = −0.694; p < 0.001), PS (ρ = −0.317; p = 0.004), and CCI (ρ = −0.440; p < 0.001) in the full cohort. Similar inverse correlations were observed in the DLBCL subgroup for age (ρ = −0.769; p < 0.001), PS (ρ = −0.319; p = 0.027), and CCI (ρ = −0.464; p < 0.001). In contrast, the correlation between RDI and IPI was not significant in the DLBCL subgroup (ρ = −0.198; p = 0.177). RDI was not significantly associated with the occurrence of grade 3–4 adverse events (AEs) in either the full cohort (ρ = −0.126; p = 0.270) or the DLBCL subgroup (ρ = −0.105; p = 0.476) (Table S2).

Treatment Response

In univariable comparisons in the full cohort, the median RDI was significantly higher in patients who achieved CR than in those who did not (70.0% [IQR, 64.2%–89.7%] vs. 64.0% [IQR, 42.8%–76.0%]; p = 0.048). Higher CCI was associated with a lower CR rate (p = 0.022). Neither PS nor Ann Arbor stage was significantly associated with CR (Table 2A).

In the DLBCL subgroup, 39 of 48 patients (81.3%) achieved CR. The median RDI was 70.8% (IQR, 66.7%–92.3%) in the CR group and 59.4% (IQR, 23.8%–72.2%) in the non‐CR group; this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.083). PS, Ann Arbor stage, IPI, and CCI were also not significantly associated with CR (Table 2B).

Full Cohort

In the univariable Cox analysis for OS in the full cohort, higher RDI was associated with longer OS (HR, 0.786; 95% CI, 0.652–0.947; p = 0.011). In contrast, higher CCI (HR, 1.632; 95% CI, 1.213–2.197; p = 0.001) and poorer PS (HR, 4.412; 95% CI, 1.704–11.425; p = 0.002) were associated with shorter OS. Age group, sex, and Ann Arbor stage were not significantly associated with OS (Table 3A).

In the multivariable Cox analysis, higher CCI remained associated with shorter OS (HR, 1.442; 95% CI, 1.046–1.988; p = 0.025), whereas RDI (HR, 0.814; 95% CI, 0.635–1.044; p = 0.106) and the other covariates were not significantly associated with OS (Table 3A).

In the univariable Cox analysis for PFS in the full cohort, higher RDI was associated with longer PFS (HR, 0.738; 95% CI, 0.633–0.859; p < 0.001). Higher CCI (HR, 1.418; 95% CI, 1.128–1.782; p = 0.003) and poorer PS (HR, 2.651; 95% CI, 1.191–5.901; p = 0.017) were associated with shorter PFS. Age group, sex, and Ann Arbor stage were not significantly associated with PFS (Table 3B).

In the multivariable Cox analysis, higher RDI remained independently associated with longer PFS (HR, 0.709; 95% CI, 0.576–0.873; p = 0.001), whereas the other covariates were not significantly associated with PFS (Table 3B).

DLBCL Subgroup

In the univariable Cox analysis for OS in the DLBCL subgroup, higher RDI was associated with longer OS (HR, 0.628; 95% CI, 0.480–0.821; p < 0.001). Higher IPI (HR, 2.248; 95% CI, 1.078–4.686; p = 0.031), CCI (HR, 1.713; 95% CI, 1.207–2.432; p = 0.003), and PS (HR, 3.879; 95% CI, 1.394–10.795; p = 0.009) were associated with shorter OS. Age group, sex, and Ann Arbor stage were not significantly associated with OS (Table 3C).

In the multivariable Cox analysis, higher RDI remained independently associated with longer OS (HR, 0.320; 95% CI, 0.127–0.806; p = 0.016), whereas the other covariates were not significantly associated with OS (Table 3C).

In the univariable Cox analysis for PFS in the DLBCL subgroup, higher RDI was associated with longer PFS (HR, 0.578; 95% CI, 0.436–0.767; p < 0.001), consistent with its association with longer OS. Higher Ann Arbor stage (HR, 2.510; 95% CI, 1.189–5.301; p = 0.016) and higher IPI (HR, 2.123; 95% CI, 1.174–3.839; p = 0.013) were associated with shorter PFS. Age group, sex, CCI, and PS were not significantly associated with PFS (Table 3D).

In the multivariable Cox analysis, higher RDI remained independently associated with longer PFS (HR, 0.217; 95% CI, 0.088–0.536; p < 0.001). Age 65–74 versus ≥ 75 years (HR, 25.479; 95% CI, 1.174–553.083; p = 0.039), male versus female sex (HR, 5.337; 95% CI, 1.034–27.558; p = 0.046), and higher Ann Arbor stage (HR, 7.230; 95% CI, 1.690–30.928; p = 0.008) were associated with shorter PFS. IPI, CCI, and PS were not significantly associated with PFS (Table 3D).

Discussion

The main finding of this study was that higher RDI was independently associated with longer PFS in the full cohort and with both longer PFS and OS in the DLBCL subgroup. Quartile‐based Kaplan–Meier analyses showed consistent patterns. In contrast, RDI was not independently associated with OS in the full cohort, whereas higher CCI remained independently associated with shorter OS. These results suggest that delivered treatment intensity may have prognostic relevance, particularly in DLBCL.

In the DLBCL subgroup, RDI was not significantly correlated with IPI and remained independently associated with both PFS and OS after adjustment for IPI and the other prespecified covariates. These findings suggest that RDI may provide prognostic information distinct from pretreatment disease risk and may partly reflect treatment feasibility, physiological reserve, and treatment tolerance [5, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. However, the observed associations do not establish that increasing RDI would improve survival. In this observational study, RDI should therefore be interpreted primarily as a prognostic marker rather than as a direct therapeutic target.

In both the full cohort and the DLBCL subgroup, RDI was inversely correlated with age, PS, and CCI. Because first‐line treatment regimens, grade 3 or 4 adverse events, and transfusion requirements did not differ significantly by age, lower RDI in older patients may partly reflect underlying vulnerability rather than treatment selection alone. Similar associations between comorbidity and delivered dose intensity have been reported in DLBCL [12]. Higher RDI may therefore indicate greater physiological reserve, but causality cannot be inferred.

Clinically, chronological age alone should not dictate uniform treatment de‐intensification. Treatment decisions should incorporate PS, comorbidity burden, organ function, and tolerability [13, 14]. For general practitioners and family physicians, these findings emphasize the importance of identifying potentially modifiable vulnerabilities and coordinating supportive care with hematologists, rather than determining chemotherapy dose intensity themselves. Supportive care—including early recognition of adverse events, medication review, and infection‐prevention counseling—may help maintain treatment feasibility.

This study has limitations, including its retrospective single‐center design, small sample size, limited event counts, and heterogeneous histological subtypes and treatment regimens. The quartile‐based RDI groups were defined post hoc and do not establish clinical cutoffs, and the global log‐rank tests do not demonstrate pairwise differences. Future studies incorporating formal frailty assessments such as the Clinical Frailty Scale are warranted. Larger prospective studies are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In this single‐center retrospective study of older adults with newly diagnosed lymphoma, higher RDI was independently associated with longer PFS in both the full cohort and the DLBCL subgroup and with longer OS in the DLBCL subgroup. These findings suggest that delivered treatment intensity and treatment feasibility may provide prognostic information beyond chronological age and, in DLBCL, pretreatment disease risk as measured by IPI. However, RDI may also reflect underlying physiological reserve and treatment tolerance; therefore, a causal benefit of increasing RDI cannot be inferred. Given the heterogeneous histological subtypes, limited sample size, and small number of events, these findings should be considered exploratory and validated in larger prospective studies.
