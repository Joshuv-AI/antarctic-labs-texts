Intranasal drop of multiplex RNA therapy for SARS-CoV-2 infections: baicalein-enhanced FEN1 nuclease cleaving as a virus buster
Authors: Jiyanuo Cao, Meng Wan, Jiayu Gu, Peiliang Wang, Yuan Gao, Libin Wei, Yongjian Guo, Lingzhi Zhao, Shu Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough the acute phase of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic has subsided, viral persistence and variant evolution continue to challenge existing antiviral strategies. Here, we investigated an intranasal nanomedicine formulation comprising flap-endonuclease 1 (FEN1) and a panel of hairpin-structured DNA probes (hpDNAs), which we designed to concurrently target the highly conserved nucleocapsid RNA and host-derived Cathepsin L mRNA. This dual-targeting approach aims to interfere with both viral genetic integrity and the host-dependent entry pathway. Replacing conventional siRNA/sgRNA with stable, economical hpDNAs may improve the practicality of nucleic acid-based antivirals. Additionally, the co-administration of the anti-inflammatory agent baicalein was examined to assess its potential to improve the nanomedicine's cellular biodistribution. Our portable intranasal drop, a multi-target treatment co-administered with baicalein, elicited a partial reduction in the viral load in mice infected with SARS-CoV-2, suggesting that this multi-target strategy may offer an alternative antiviral modality for further development.

Introduction

From the H1N1 virus (Spanish Flu) in 1918 to the recent SARS-CoV-2 outbreak, respiratory infections have repeatedly affected humanity [1]. The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic alone resulted in more than 16 trillion USD in economic losses and over 13 million fatalities [2,3], profoundly impacting the global economy and public health systems [4]. Small molecular antiviral drugs and neutralizing antibodies have been developed. For instance, Molnupiravir and Nirmatrelvir inhibit RNA polymerase and 3C-like protease (3CLpro) to block SARS-CoV-2 replication [5,6], while antibodies such as bamlanivimab, etesevimab, and imdevimab block viral entry by binding the spike protein's receptor-binding domain (RBD), preventing SARS-CoV-2 from invading host cells [5]. However, these agents suffer from lengthy development cycles and limited coverage of evolving viral variants [6].

Fortunately, with state-of-the-art sequencing technologies, the precise sequence of viruses and their variants can be obtained in short intervals [7,8]. Emerging gene-editing technologies, such as RNA interference (RNAi), antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) and CRISPR, have shown considerable potential for treating viral diseases. For example, the CRISPR-Cas9 system has been used to eliminate DNA viruses such as herpes simplex virus 1 [9] and human cytomegalovirus [10]; CRISPR-Cas13 has been used to directly eliminate RNA viruses that lack DNA intermediates, such as SARS-CoV-2 [11,12] and vesicular stomatitis virus [13]; RNAi and ASOs exert antiviral effects by degrading viral RNA or inhibiting translation through sequence-specific pairing, and multiple candidates have entered late-stage clinical trials for HBV and other viral infections [14]. These examples strongly support the notion that targeting the viral genome is a practical and effective antiviral approach.

However, concerns remain about these RNA-targeted antiviral approaches. Firstly, the CRISPR and RNAi tools are likely facing challenges related to the high costs [15] of synthesizing and modifying sgRNA and siRNA probes, which hinder increasing the number of probes to cover different viruses. Secondly, simultaneously targeting both the viral genes and the host's essential genes to enhance the treatment effect remains technically difficult [16]. Thirdly, the poor bioavailability of nanomedicine in inflammatory environments diminishes the in vivo therapeutic efficacy [17].

To address these gaps, another RNA-targeted tool [18] was considered, consisting of a flap-endonuclease 1 (FEN1) and guiding hairpin-structured DNA probes (hpDNAs). Mechanistically, FEN1 is a structure-specific endonuclease that acts on flap structures generated during DNA replication and repair [19]. In the FEN1-hpDNA, the hpDNA forms a 5’ flap structure with targeted mRNA, and recruits FEN1 to cleave [18,19]. The small size of FEN1 (∼36 kDa) greatly facilitates in vivo delivery compared with canonical Cas proteins. Using stable, economical hpDNA instead of sgRNA/siRNA allows flexible expansion of target sites and functions without PAM/PFS restriction. FEN1 has also been confirmed to degrade SARS-CoV-2 RNA in Vero E6 cells [20].

Hence, we developed an intranasal antiviral strategy using FEN1-hpDNA as a virus buster and silica cross-linked micelles (SCLMs) as a delivery carrier. A set of multi-targeting hpDNAs was designed to target both the nucleocapsid RNA (CoV-N) of the virus (conserved region in SARS-CoV-2 subtypes) and the Cathepsin L mRNA (Ctsl) of the host (a pivotal host protease required for the proteolytic activation of the viral spike protein during endosomal entry [21]). The FEN1 protein and the set of hpDNAs were concurrently loaded onto SCLMs to formulate a portable intranasal drop (SCLMs@FEN1-hpDNAs, Figure 1). To further boost the therapeutic efficacy, S@F was co-administered with baicalein (a natural flavonoid with well-documented anti-inflammatory and antiviral properties [22]). Extensive studies have indicated that baicalein exerts its potent anti-inflammatory effects through multiple mechanisms, most notably by inhibiting key signalling pathways such as the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/NF-κB axis and the NLRP3 inflammasome, which is known to be pathologically overactivated in severe viral infections [23]. This immunomodulatory profile makes baicalein a promising candidate for synergistic combination with our gene-targeting nanomedicine. Notably, we have formulated the baicalein as a nasal drop solution, permitting direct intranasal co-delivery with SCLMs@FEN1-hpDNAs (Figure 1).

In this study, we will verify the multiplex RNA targeting nanomedicine SCLMs@FEN1-hpDNAs with synergistic baicalein in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo. We hope this portable intranasal drop nanomedicine may provide an alternative strategy to combat SARS-CoV-2 in the future.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and animal models

HEK293T, HepG2, A549, LLC and RAW264.7 cell lines were authenticated and verified to be free of mycoplasma contamination at the time of experimentation. The cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Royacel, RYS-KF21), 100 U mL−1 penicillin, and 100 µg mL−1 streptomycin at 37°C under 5% CO2.

C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Jiangsu Qinglongshan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and raised in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at the Laboratory Animal Centre of China Pharmaceutical University. The mRosa26-PGK-hACE2 mice were raised in the Institute of Laboratory Animal Science, Peking Union Medical College.

Expression and purification of the FEN1 protein

The FEN1 ORF was amplified by PCR, which introduced Nde I and Not I restriction sites. The PCR products and pET28a-His vectors were double-digested with Nde I and Not I to generate pET28a-His-FEN1. The ligation product was transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells, and positive clones were verified by Sanger sequencing. The pET28a-His-FEN1 was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) by heat-shock. Transformants were selected on LB agar with 50 µg mL−1 kanamycin. For expression, overnight cultures were diluted 1: 100 in LB-kanamycin, grown to an OD600 of 0.6–0.8 at 37°C, then induced with 0.1 mM IPTG at 20°C for 16 h. Cells were harvested (7000 g, 5 min, 4°C) and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole). After sonication (38% power, 40–50 min, on ice) and centrifugation (12,000 g, 20 min, 4°C), FEN1 was purified from the clarified lysate via Ni-NTA chromatography (wash: 20 and 50 mM imidazole; elution: 300 mM imidazole). The eluted protein was mixed with glycerol at a 1:1 ratio and analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

Cleaving ssRNA in vitro

As described before [24,25], nuclease activity assays were performed in a 10-µL mixture (10 mM MOPS, 7.5 mM MgCl2, 0.05% Tween-20, 0.01% NP-40), comprising hpDNA (10 pmol, Supplementary Table 1), 5’-FAM labelled ssRNA (5 pmol, Supplementary Table 1), and an appropriate amount of enzyme was prepared and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Reactions were separated by 20% denaturing PAGE using TBE buffer (89 mM Tris, 89 mM Boric acid, 2 mM EDTA). Gel was scanned on an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare). Throughout the procedure, DEPC-treated tips, tubes, and reagents were employed to maintain RNase-free conditions.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

As described before [24], cells were initially fixed with 4% PFA in PBS (1 h), permeabilized with a blocking buffer (30 min, room temperature), and subsequently probed with anti-His-tag primary antibody (Abclonal, AE003, 1: 100) overnight at 4°C. Following triple PBS rinses, specimens were incubated with Alexa FluorTM 488-labelled Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Secondary Antibody (ThermoFisher, A11001, 1: 200) for 1 h at 37°C. Nuclear staining was achieved with DAPI (20 min), after which coverslips were mounted and visualized under an Olympus FV1000 confocal microscope (FV10-ASW software v2.1) to evaluate colocalization patterns.

FEN1-protein/hpDNAs complex assay

For 24-well plates, 80,000 cells per well were plated. FEN1 protein and hpDNAs (Supplementary Table 2) were mixed at total amounts of 10 or 20 pmol per well in varying proportions and transfected by TransIntroTM EL Transfection Reagent following the manufacturer's recommended protocol. After 24, 48, or 72 h, cells were harvested to perform the following analyzes.

siRNA assay

The siRNAs (Supplementary Table 3) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and transfected as described previously [24]. For 24-well plates, 80,000 cells per well were plated. 50 pmol of siRNA per well was transfected by TransIntroTM EL Transfection Reagent following the manufacturer's recommended protocol. After 48 h, cells were harvested to perform the following experiments.

CRISPR-RNP assay

The sgRNA was prepared using the T7 High Yield RNA Transcription Kit (Vazyme, TR101). The synthesized RNA was analyzed by electrophoresis and purified using the Column RNA rapid concentration and purification kit (Sangon, B518688).

As described before [24], for 24-well plates, 80,000 cells per well were plated. RspCas13d protein (Addgene Plasmid 108305) and sgRNA (Supplementary Table 3) each at 10 pmol per well were transfected by TransIntroTM EL Transfection Reagent following the manufacturer's recommended protocol. After 48 h, cells were harvested to perform the following experiments.

Quantitative PCR

As described before [24], total cellular RNA was isolated using RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, R701-01/02), reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, R312-01), and analyzed by quantitative PCR using ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q312) with primers provided in Supplementary Table 4.

CCK-8

As described before [24], cell proliferation was evaluated by seeding 6000 cells per well in 96-well plates on day 1. On day 2, four distinct transfection groups were established: siRNA (10 pmol), FEN1 plasmid plus hpDNA (120 and 80 ng), FEN1 protein plus hpDNA (0.5 and 4.5 pmol), or RspCas13d protein plus sgRNA (both 2.5 pmol). At 48 h post-transfection, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent was administered per well, incubated (37°C, 2 h). Absorbance was quantified at 450 nm using a SynergyTM HT multi-mode plate reader (Bio-Tek, Winooski, VT).

EGFP cleavage assay detected by flow cytometry

As described before [24], cells were resuspended in PBS and subjected to ﬂuorescence-activated cell sorting analysis (FACS Calibur, Becton Dickinson) to detect EGFP intensity (488 nm excitation and 525 nm emission). The acquired data sets were then evaluated with FlowJo software.

Synthesis of SCLMs and SCLMs@FEN1-hpDNAs

As described before [25], the Pluronic copolymer F108 (0.25 g) was dissolved in aqueous HCl solution (7.5 mL, 2.0 M) under vigorous stirring at room temperature. Then, cyclohexane (240 µL) was added and dispersed by sonication, and the emulsion was stirred for another 30 min. Afterwards, TEOS (268 µL) was added and stirred for 4 h at room temperature before the addition of DEDMS (120 µL). 24 h later, the product was dialyzed in deionized water 5 times (20,000 Da molecular weight membrane) to purify the product. Cyclohexane was removed by rotary evaporation at 50°C under 150 mbar for 15 min. To remove the potential precipitates, the product was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant with SCLMs was filtered by a 0.22 µm membrane before the characterization and applications. The sulfhydryl group was introduced to FEN1 by Traut's reagent to obtain FEN1-SH. hpDNAs-SH were synthesized by the modification of the 5’-end of the strand of hpDNAs with the sulfhydryl group. FEN1-SH (400 pmol) and hpDNAs-SH (3600 pmol) were added to SCLMs (0.78 mg) and reacted at room temperature for 2 h. The product was denoted as SCLMs@FEN1-hpDNAs with a ratio of FEN1 to hpDNA of 1: 9.

Characterizations of nanoparticles

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were captured on a JEOL JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope with an operating voltage of 100 kV. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential experiments were performed at 25°C using an Anton Paar Litesizer 500 particle size analyzer. Absorption spectra were obtained by a Philes G9-9 S UV-visible spectrophotometer. Confocal images of cells and tissues were recorded on an Olympus FV3000 Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope.

Intranasal administration

The mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol (Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology, M2940) at a dose of 1% of their body weight. The anesthetized mice were placed in a head-up position. A pipette was used to slowly instill the drug into one nostril while tilting the mouse to one side and allowing it to inhale spontaneously. For larger administration volumes, a two-step instillation protocol was adopted: the first half of the dose was administered, and the mouse was allowed to recover spontaneous breathing before the second half was delivered.

Biodistribution of nanoparticles

For the biodistribution study, the mice were intranasally administered the nanoparticles prepared with Cy5.5-labelled SCLMs (40 mg kg−1). The fluorescence signals of the main organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and brain) were monitored and analyzed after injection using a Vilber FUSION FX7 imaging system (λex = 680 nm, λem = 750 ± 10 nm).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

In preparation for cryostat sectioning, mice were sacrificed, lungs and brains were isolated, fixed in 4% PFA, and dehydrated. A Thermo CRYOSTAR NX50 microtome was used to cut cryosections at 30 µm. After that, IHC was performed according to standard protocol. Imaging was performed using a 3D HISTECH PANNORAMIC scan. Antibodies targeting the Ctsl (Servicebio, GB115577, 1: 150) and the SARS-CoV-2 nucleoprotein (CoV-N) (Servicebio, GB122133, 1: 150) were used.

Western blot (WB)

Tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, weighed, and homogenized (1: 10 w/v) in ice-cold RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 5 mM EDTA) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (GLPBIO, GK10014); lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 15,000 g for 20 min at 4°C. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay, and samples (20 µg) were denatured in 4×Loading buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 8% SDS, 40% glycerol, 20% β-mercaptoethanol) at 95°C for 10 min, resolved by SDS-PAGE on 10% gels at 65 V (stacking) then 120 V (separating) in running buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 0.1% SDS), transferred to PVDF membranes at 250 mA for 2 h at 4°C in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 20% methanol), blocked with 3% BSA in PBST (150 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h, incubated with primary antibodies (Ctsl, Proteintech, 27952-1-AP, 1: 5000 and Tubulin, Proteintech, 11224-1-AP, 1: 5000) overnight at 4°C and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Rabbit/IgG, ABclonal, AS014, 1: 10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. After extensive washing, bands were visualized with Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare) and quantified by densitometry using ImageJ.

Blood collection, hematological examination, and serum biochemical analysis

Mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol and placed in lateral recumbency. The fur around the eye was disinfected with 75% ethanol. A sterile heparinized capillary tube (4 cm length, 1.5 mm diameter) was inserted at a 30–45 degree angle relative to the medial canthus and advanced 2-3 mm into the orbital venous plexus. Blood was collected into labelled 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes via capillary action.

For routine blood tests (hematological examination), an aliquot of whole blood was immediately transferred to EDTA-anticoagulant microcentrifuge tubes and gently mixed to prevent clotting, and analyzed using a hematology analyzer (TEK8500H4-0502).

Whole blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 2 h. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant (serum) was carefully aspirated without disturbing the clot, aliquoted into clean microcentrifuge tubes, and stored at −80°C. The following parameters were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Chemray 240): alanine aminotransferase (ALT, Reference Values for normal laboratory mice 10.06 – 96.47 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, Reference Values for laboratory normal mice 36.31 – 235.48 U/L), blood urea nitrogen (BUN, Reference Values for laboratory normal mice 10.81 – 34.74 mg/dL) and creatinine (CREAT, Reference Values for laboratory normal mice 10.91 – 85.09 µmol/L).

Preparation of baicalein inhalation solution

To prepare the baicalein inhalation solution, an excipient solution was first formulated by weighing and combining 17.0 mg of sodium chloride, 0.56 mg of citric acid, 1.0 mg of sodium citrate, and 2.0 mg of Polysorbate-80 (a surfactant used to enhance the dispersion of baicalein) with 2 mL of water for injection. This mixture was dissolved to form the excipient solution. Subsequently, 0.5 g of baicalein powder (with a particle size distribution of D50 = 1–5 µm) was added to 25 mL of the excipient solution. The mixture was then subjected to high-shear dispersion for 2 h to achieve a uniform solution, resulting in a baicalein inhalation solution with a concentration of 20 mg mL−1. Mice received a dose of 100 mg kg−1.

Immune cell stimulation and baicalein treatment

RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. Cells were stimulated with LPS (Beyotime, S1732) at a final concentration of 1 µg mL−1 for 24 h. Subsequently, baicalein at 30 µM was added, and cells were cultured for an additional 24 h. Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was measured by flow cytometry using the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033S).

Cy5.5-SCLMs’ uptake analysis in the cell

For flow cytometric detection of nanoparticle internalization, 200 µg mL−1 Cy5.5-SCLMs were added to each well after 24 h of baicalein treatment. Subsequently, cells were stained with CD45-FITC antibody (Elabscience, AB-F1136C), and the proportion of CD45+Cy5.5+ double-positive cells was analyzed by flow cytometry. Fluorescence signals sets: FITC (excitation 488 nm, emission 525 nm) and Cy5.5 (excitation 680 nm, emission 750 nm).

Distribution of Cy5.5-SCLMs in lung tissue

Three experimental groups were established, namely: PBS (healthy mice), LPS (acute lung injury model mice), and LPS + baicalein (acute lung injury model mice administered with Cy5.5-SCLMs and baicalein). Cy5.5-SCLMs and baicalein were administered at 12 h post-LPS exposure, followed by tissue collection at 24 h. Frozen sections of lung tissues from the three groups were prepared. Primary antibodies against EpCAM (Proteintech, 21050-1-AP, 1: 400), CD45 (Abcam, ab208022, 1: 400), and CD31 (Abcam, ab281583, 1: 400) were incubated with the sections, followed by treatment with the corresponding secondary antibody (Abcam, ab205718, 1: 4000). Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) staining was then performed using iFluor® 555 (AAT Bioquest, 11065, 1: 400). Colocalization analysis (PBS CD45 signal as background threshold) was performed using QuPath 4.3 and Python software.

Screening of viral conserved regions

Targets screening process: SARS-CoV-2 virus genome sequences were retrieved from GenBank, with a total of 5054 gene sequences randomly selected from 3320 subtypes. First, the MAFFT tool was used to extract 276,067 candidate targets via a 22-nt sliding window. Second, a three-step filtering process was implemented using Bowtie2 to screen for conserved targets: (1) selected 26,289 targets with perfect matches to SARS-CoV-2, using Wuhan-Hu-1 (SARS-CoV-2/Wuhan-Hu-1/human/2019/CHN, NCBI reference sequence: NC_045512.1) as the reference genome; (2) obtained 3497 targets with ≤1 mismatch to 360 SARS-CoV reference genomes; (3) derived 3496 unique targets by excluding those with ≤2 mismatches relative to the GRCh38 human transcriptome.

Identification of conserved regions: Using Wuhan-Hu-1 (SARS-CoV-2/Wuhan- Hu-1/human/2019/CHN, NCBI reference sequence: NC_045512.1) as the reference genome, we investigated the distribution of the 3496 targets across the gene sequences. The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) gene, envelope protein (E) gene, and nucleocapsid (N) gene exhibited higher target abundances, indicating these regions are more conserved.

Construction of the hp-CoV-N pool

The hpDNAs were designed, and their coverage of two coronavirus genome sets (5054 SARS-CoV-2 sequences and 360 SARS-CoV sequences) was evaluated. Coverage analyzes were performed using Bowtie2. As the number of hp-CoV-N increased progressively, the coverage of the SARS-CoV-2 genomes increased. It reached 100.00% when the hp-CoV-N number was raised to 4. When the SARS-CoV genomes were incorporated into the analysis, the coverage rates were 99.96%. After collecting all genomic sequences of SARS-CoV-2 variants and SARS-CoV, the screened conserved fragments and hp-CoV-N pool were consistent with previous studies [11,20].

Virus infection and drug administration procedure

The SARS-CoV-2 prototype strain (SARS-CoV-2/WH-09/human/2020/CHN, GenBank accession: MT093631.2, wild-type) was provided by the Institute of Laboratory Animal Science, Peking Union Medical College. Virus stocks were propagated in Vero E6 cells maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

Four experimental groups were established in the study, namely the untreated post-infection group (NC-V), the S@F-administered post-infection group (S@F), the baicalein-administered post-infection group (Baicalein), and the S@F plus baicalein co-administered post-infection group (S@F + baicalein). On day 0, mice were intranasally inoculated with the SARS-CoV-2 prototype strain (WH-09) at an equal dose of 1 × 105 TCID50 per mouse. Mice in the S@F group received S@F treatment once each on days 1 and 3; those in the Baicalein group were administered baicalein once daily; mice in the S@F + baicalein group were given S@F on days 1 and 3 along with daily baicalein administration. On day 5, four mice were euthanized for tissue isolation, while the survival rate of the remaining mice was recorded continuously.

Absolute quantification of SARS-CoV-2 subgenomic RNA (sgRNA)

Total RNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) lung tissue blocks using the BeyoMagTM FFPE Tissue RNA Extraction Kit (Beyotime, R0085S) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were determined by spectrophotometry.

The primers and TaqMan probe targeting the N gene subgenomic RNA (sgRNA-N) of SARS-CoV-2 are listed in Supplementary Table 5. The standard plasmid was constructed by inserting the SARS-CoV-2 sgRNA-N target sequence (Supplementary Table 6) into the pBluescript II SK + vector. The plasmid copy number was calculated using the following formula: copy number (copies/µL) = (6.02 × 1023 copies/mol × plasmid concentration g/µL) / (plasmid length bp × 660 g/mol). The standard plasmids were serially diluted as templates for the standard curve. TaqMan probe-based one-step qRT-PCR was performed using the TransScript® II Probe One-Step qRT-PCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, AQ321). Each reaction contained 200 ng of total RNA input per sample.

The standard curve was generated by plotting the log10-transformed copy number against the corresponding Ct value, yielding the equation: Ct = −7.1097 × log10 (copies) + 62.567 (R2 = 0.9886). The sgRNA copy number of each sample was calculated according to the standard curve equation.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± s.d. or mean ± s.e.m. from independent experiments performed in a parallel manner. Statistical tests included one-tailed or two-tailed unpaired two-sample t-tests and one-way or two-way ANOVA. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Assembly of the purified FEN1 protein and hpDNAs guiding probes

The purified FEN1 protein has a molecular weight of ∼38 kDa (denoted as FEN1 protein) (Figure 2(A)) and Supplementary Figure 1). The hpDNA (Figure 2(B)) contains a fixed stem-loop and a guide sequence complementary to the targeted RNA, directing the FEN1 protein to cleave the target. The RNA cleavage ability was investigated by in vitro gel electrophoresis assay, and the substrate single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) with FAM at the 5’ end was completely cleaved by 5 pmol of the FEN1 protein guided by the hpDNAs (Figure 2(C)). In contrast, no down-regulation was observed by the hpDNAs-only groups (Supplementary Figure 3). The CCK-8 test showed lower cellular toxicity of the FEN1-protein/hpDNAs complex compared with the FEN1-plasmid/hpDNAs, Cas13d-RNP, and siRNA (Figure 2(H)). The sensitivity of the FEN1-protein/hpDNAs complex to mismatched nucleic sequences was also investigated. A panel of hpDNAs with 1–2 mismatches at different positions of the EGFP guide sequence was designed (Supplementary Figure 4). Compared with the hp-EGFP-pm (perfect match), mismatched groups showed significantly diminished EGFP downregulation. This sensitivity for mismatches is particularly evident when located at the 3’ terminus of the guide sequence.

In addition, it was observed that the intracellular concentration of the FEN1-protein/hpDNAs complex peaked at 48 h post-incubation, followed by a gradual decrease by 72 h (Figure 2(D)). The knockdown efficiency also exhibited a decrease over time (Figure 2(J)), making the use of nanocarriers necessary to prolong its working lifespan.

Construction and intranasal administration of SCLMs@FEN1-hpDNAs

To prolong the FEN1-protein/hpDNAs working lifespan, silica cross-linked micelles (SCLMs) were employed as the nanomaterials [26], which are composed of the micelle of the triblock copolymer and a silica shell cross-linking the hydrophobic core of the micelle. The FEN1 protein and hpDNAs could be separately functionalized with the copolymer, and the feasibility of this system has been demonstrated in rats for liver fibrosis treatment by intravenous injection [25]. However, results revealed that the lung-targeting efficiency upon tail vein injection in mice with acute lung injury (ALI) was not satisfactory (Supplementary Figure 5). Hence, whether it can be administered with high pulmonary accessibility was investigated with intranasal administration.

The purified FEN1 protein and hpDNAs were modified onto SCLMs to obtain the SCLMs@FEN1-hpDNAs (S@F). The obtained S@F comprised monodispersed spherical nanoparticles with a hollow structure. The hydrated sizes measured by the dynamic light scattering (DLS) method were 45.2 ± 2.5 nm (Figure 3(A)) with a low polydispersity index of ∼0.15. The zeta potential of S@F was −6.9 mV (Figure 3(B)). The nanocarrier exhibited good colloidal stability in PBS for at least 7 days (Figure 3(C)). The SCLMs were modified with Cy5.5 to trace their biodistribution in tissues through fluorescent ex vivo imaging. It has also been reported that intranasal administration has the potential to enhance the effective area for drug absorption via the microvilli present on the surface of the nasal mucosa [27], which may be advantageous when targeting respiratory diseases. To verify this, healthy mice were administered Cy5.5-SCLMs intranasally; at 48 h, distinct brain fluorescence was detected (Supplementary Figure 6A), and imaging of brain sections confirmed cellular internalization (Supplementary Figure 6B).

Screening of hpDNAs for Ctsl mRNA and in vivo characterization

The host protease Cathepsin L (CTSL) is a pivotal intracellular cysteine protease that facilitates the activation of the spike S protein (S), enabling the subsequent fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell membrane [28]. Therefore, CTSL plays a crucial role in the entry of the virus into host cells. It has been reported that the expression level of CTSL is elevated following SARS-CoV-2 infection, and this elevation positively correlates with the pathogenesis and severity of the disease [29]. The CTSL over-expression has been shown to exacerbate the infection in human cells [30], which hints that the CTSL mRNA is a promising target for antiviral drug development [31]. To this end, six candidate hpDNAs targeting the murine Ctsl mRNA were designed. The screening was conducted in the mouse LEWIS lung cancer cell line (LLC), and hp-Ctsl-L2 was found to have the highest knockdown efficiency (Figure 4(A), 52.9%). The baicalein assists S@F to enter into epithelial cells

Baicalein is a natural flavonoid with well-documented anti-inflammatory and antiviral properties [22], most notably by inhibiting key signalling pathways such as the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/NF-κB axis and the NLRP3 inflammasome. This immunomodulatory profile makes baicalein a promising candidate for synergistic combination with our gene-targeting nanomedicine.

We investigated the cellular uptake of Cy5.5-SCLMs facilitated by the co-administration of baicalein. To achieve this, an acute lung injury mouse model was established using lipopolysaccharide (LPS). The rationale for this model is based on evidence demonstrating that both the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and LPS converge on the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signalling pathway as a core mechanism for inducing hyperinflammation [34]. Activation of TLR4 by either agent triggers a common downstream cascade involving the MyD88-dependent activation of NF-κB and MAPK pathways, leading to the massive production of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g. Tnf, Il-6, Il-1β) that characterize the “cytokine storm” in severe COVID-19 [35,36]. Therefore, the LPS-induced ALI model serves as a highly relevant and well-established platform to specifically assess the efficacy of baicalein in suppressing this host-driven inflammatory response.

To test the effect, baicalein was added to LPS-treated RAW264.7 cells. The inflammatory factors of Tnf, Il-6, and Il-1β were significantly down-regulated (Supplementary Figure 8), as well as the ROS levels (Figure 5(A)). The Cy5.5-SCLMs were then co-incubated in groups of RAW264.7, LPS-treated RAW264.7, and baicalein-added LPS-treated RAW264.7 cells. A decrease in Cy5.5-positive RAW264.7 cells was consistently observed (Figure 5(B,C)). These results illustrated that the baicalein can prevent S@F's uptake into immune cells by attenuating the inflammatory stimulation of immune cells. The final set of hpDNAs consisted of the hp-CoV-N pool and the hp-Ctsl, and was loaded onto SCLMs together with FEN1 protein to obtain a multiplex targeting S@F. At 5 days post-infection, the nasal, tracheal, lung and brain tissues of mice from the four groups (NC-V, S@F, Baicalein, S@F + baicalein) were examined. The viral load varied among the tissue types (Supplementary Figure 11). Administration of S@F in combination with baicalein was associated with reductions in total viral RNA levels of 87.5% and 89.5% (∼0.9 log and ∼0.95 log) in the nasal and trachea, respectively (Figure 6(D)). In the lung, the quantified subgenomic RNA (sgRNA-N) and confirmed a 77.3% (∼0.64 log) decrease in the combination treatment group (Figure 6(E)). To further investigate viral protein downregulation in lung tissue, quantitative analysis on tissue sections demonstrated an 85.6% decrease in CoV-N protein expression (Figure 6(F)). However, all mice in the combination treatment group died on day 10, showing no improvement in survival rate compared with the untreated control group (Supplementary Figure 12).

Previous research has indicated that SARS-CoV-2 impacts not only the respiratory system but may also lead to neurological damage [37]. Therefore, we investigated the clearance of virus in the brain tissue of mice. In the S@F + baicalein group, viral RNA was reduced by 62.3% (∼0.42 log, Supplementary Figure 13A). Analysis of the tissue sections and the corresponding quantification in the mouse brain exhibited a consistent trend (Supplementary Figures 13B and 13C). Cxcl10 [38], Ccl2 [39] and Ccl5 [39] are chemokines implicated in brain injury. Compared to healthy control mice (NC-H), the infected mice exhibited elevated levels of the three chemokines. However, following the administration of combined treatment, these brain injury-associated markers decreased (Supplementary Figure 13D). The combination of baicalein and S@F showed reductions in viral replication and neuroinflammation.

Discussion

This study investigated a potential anti-SARS-CoV-2 approach involving intranasal delivery of S@F in combination with baicalein. The hpDNAs were designed to target the CoV-N RNA of SARS-CoV-2 and the Ctsl mRNA of the host. This dual-targeting strategy aimed to interfere with viral genetic integrity and the host-dependent entry pathway. Intranasal administration of S@F resulted in detectable pulmonary distribution. Baicalein exhibited anti-inflammatory properties and improved the cellular bioavailability of S@F, which may have contributed to the observed reduction in viral load.

This approach has several redeeming features. Firstly, it concurrently targets both the CoV-N genome (covering over 99% of SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 subtypes) and the host Ctsl mRNA. The multiplex targeting is relatively straightforward to implement because the guiding probes are DNA oligonucleotides, which exhibit greater chemical stability than sgRNA or siRNA. Secondly, baicalein may enhance the cellular uptake of the nanomedicine S@F, potentially improving delivery efficiency. Thirdly, intranasal administration enables targeted deposition in the respiratory tract with limited systemic exposure. The portable nasal drops formulation does not require specialized equipment or professional administration techniques, potentially facilitating patient self-administration [40,41].

Several limitations should also be acknowledged:

First of all, objective limitations related to the biosafety facility limited the depth of virological characterization in this study. Consequently, comprehensive virological endpoints (such as viral titres determined by plaque assays or viral replication kinetics) could not be provided. These gaps restrict the mechanistic interpretation of the observed antiviral effects and highlight the necessity for follow-up investigations conducted under enhanced biosafety containment conditions.

Secondly, SARS-CoV-2 entry into host cells is mediated not only by the endosomal/lysosomal cysteine protease Cathepsin L but also by the transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2), which proteolytically primes the spike protein at the cell surface to facilitate viral-host membrane fusion [42]. The current study targeted Ctsl but did not incorporate TMPRSS2 into the hpDNA panel. Nevertheless, targeting Ctsl still possesses independent therapeutic significance. The Ctsl-mediated endosomal entry remains the primary pathway in cells/tissues with low TMPRSS2 expression [43] (such as certain immune cells or specific brain regions), which directly aligns with the observed high efficacy in clearing the virus from the brain. Future iterations of this strategy may benefit from expanding the target repertoire to include TMPRSS2, thereby simultaneously interfering with both the surface and endosomal entry pathways.

Thirdly, the absence of survival prolongation in the combination treatment group may be attributable to the relatively late initiation of therapeutic intervention. In this study, treatment was administered after viral establishment, at which point the inflammatory cascade and tissue damage may have already progressed irreversibly. It is plausible that earlier intervention (such as prophylactic administration or treatment initiated within approximately 6 h post-infection) might attenuate viral replication before peak amplification and could improve survival outcomes in future investigations.

Fourthly, the intranasal viral infection route in mice frequently results in viral neurotropism and accumulation within the brain [44], a pathophysiological pattern that diverges from the natural course of SARS-CoV-2 infection in humans, where pulmonary infection and parenchymal damage are of primary clinical relevance. Furthermore, large animals such as non-human primates may offer more physiologically relevant models for evaluating intranasal drug absorption and respiratory viral kinetics.

Looking ahead, several directions warrant further exploration. The nanoparticle size (∼45 nm) used herein may be optimized to enhance deposition in the lower respiratory tract [40], potentially improving delivery to the lungs. Encapsulating baicalein within the nanoparticle core, rather than co-administering it as a free compound, may improve formulation consistency and dosing precision. Expanding the target spectrum to include TMPRSS2, and initiating treatment at earlier time points (either prophylactically or immediately post-exposure) represents logical next steps to maximize the therapeutic potential of this approach. The potential of S@F to serve as a pan-coronavirus antiviral platform also remains to be systematically explored. In addition, dedicated follow-up experiments will be performed to dissect the precise antiviral mechanism inside neurons.

In summary, these findings suggest that the intranasal S@F-baicalein combination may represent a potential antiviral modality against SARS-CoV-2. While the observed reduction in pulmonary viral load and the mechanistic rationale warrant further preclinical development. We hope this portable intranasal drop nanomedicine may provide an alternative strategy to combat SARS-CoV-2 in the future.
