Lack of Antenatal Care Resulting in Intraoperative Diagnostic Challenges of Dense Adhesions Imitating Placenta Accreta Spectrum: A Case Report

ABSTRACT

In some cases, intraoperative adhesion findings may resemble the placenta accreta spectrum (PAS), leading to diagnostic confusion and unnecessary referral to tertiary care centers. Introduction

Adhesions are a common long-term complication following surgery and can significantly complicate surgical access.1,2 The incidence of adhesions increases with the number of cesarean sections, ranging from 24% to 46% following a second cesarean delivery, 43% to 75% after a third, and 48% to 83% after a fourth cesarean section. The most frequent adhesions involved the uterus, urinary bladder, and the anterior abdominal wall, which may increase the risk of visceral organ injury. However, the impact of adhesions may vary depending on patient-specific factors, surgical technique, and the surgeon’s expertise.2

The diagnosis of the placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) remains a subject of ongoing debate in the literature. An accurate prenatal assessment is essential to deciding on adequate management strategies.3 The diagnosis primarily relies on ultrasonography (USG), which has high sensitivity and specificity when performed by experienced operators. Ultrasonography can achieve a sensitivity and specificity of up to 90% in the hands of skilled practitioners.4 Nevertheless, in approximately half to two-thirds of cases, the placenta accreta spectrum remains undiagnosed prior to delivery. This is of particular concern given that a previous cesarean section has been shown to increase the risk of placenta accreta by up to 8.7-fold.5 This case highlights intraoperative adhesion findings that raised suspicion of placenta accreta spectrum in a patient with a history of cesarean section.

Case Report

A 30-year-old woman, G2P1 with 2 living children, at approximately 9 months of gestation, referred from a district hospital, carrying a diagnosis of post-explorative laparotomy for suspected placenta accreta. She was described as a full-term parturient in the latent phase of the first stage of labor, with a history of cesarean section 12 years earlier delivering twins who remain healthy. The patient felt labor-like abdominal pain that began one day before admission and progressively became more frequent and regular. The pain was accompanied by mucus and bloody discharge. She denied any rupture of membranes and confirmed persistent fetal movements. The patient received antenatal care only twice with a midwife during this pregnancy.

At first, she was observed in the hope of performing vaginal birth after caesarean. Due to a lack of labor progression and a history of cesarean section, cesarean section was performed the following morning. During laparotomy, intra-abdominal adhesions were noted, and placenta accreta was suspected (Figure 1). The patient was then referred to a tertiary care facility. At the referral hospital, physical examination revealed normal vital signs. Her pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was within the normal range at 23.8 kg/m2. Abdominal examination showed a uterine fundal height of 29 cm, an abdominal circumference of 96 cm, and a fetal heart rate of 152–156 bpm. Irregular uterine contractions were noted. The estimated fetal weight was approximately 2500 grams. Vaginal examination was not performed. Cardiotocography (CTG) showed a baseline fetal heart rate of 150–160 bpm, variability >5 bpm, positive accelerations, and no decelerations, consistent with category I findings.

Initial hematologic tests indicated anemia, with a hemoglobin level of 9.5 g/dL and no leukocytosis. Emergency ultrasonography revealed a single live intrauterine fetus in cephalic presentation. Biometric measurements were consistent with 35–36 weeks of gestation. Fetal heart rate was present. Fetal biophysical assessment was limited due to maternal pain and non-visualization of the fetal head. Informed consent was obtained from the family, and the patient was monitored for general condition, vital signs, uterine contractions, and vaginal bleeding. Upon opening the peritoneum, adhesions were found between the left lateral aspect of the uterus and the left adnexa (Figure 3)—further exploration found adhesions between the bladder and the lower uterine segment. Adhesiolysis was performed using both blunt and sharp dissection. Subsequent intraoperative exploration showed no evidence of bridging vessels, hypervascularization, or uterine bulging. A female infant was delivered via cesarean section later that night. The infant did not cry immediately after birth, although muscle tone was good and the amniotic fluid was clear. The placenta was delivered intact with gentle traction on the umbilical cord (Figure 4). The newborn weighed 2890 grams, with a length of 45 cm, head circumference of 34 cm, chest circumference of 31 cm, and abdominal circumference of 30 cm. No postpartum complications were observed in the mother. There were no signs of infection or excessive bleeding during the postpartum period. Vital signs remained stable, and the uterine fundus was palpated 2 cm below the umbilicus. Postpartum laboratory results showed normal hematologic parameters with a hemoglobin level of 9 g/dL, hematocrit of 27.5%, leukocyte count of 12,870/µL, and platelet count of 210,000/µL. The patient was planned for discharge 48 hours postpartum with prescriptions of oral mefenamic acid 500 mg three times daily and cefadroxil 500 mg twice daily.

Discussion

Adhesion is a pathological connection that forms between internal body surfaces, ranging from thin connective tissue bands to thick fibrotic tissue containing blood vessels and nerves. Postoperative adhesions may form as a response to tissue trauma and ischemia, but are currently understood to have a multifactorial pathophysiology. Postoperative adhesion formation involves three main processes: (A) suppression of fibrinolytic and extracellular matrix degradation systems; (B) induction of an inflammatory response involving cytokines and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β); and (C) tissue hypoxia, which increases vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression. Peritoneal adhesion studies have shown that the cellular components include macrophages, eosinophils, red blood cells, tissue debris, mast cells, and fibroblasts embedded in a deposited matrix. During the formation of adhesions, cellular composition shifts: polymorphonuclear leukocytes dominate during days 1–3, while fibroblasts are predominant from days 5–7.6,7

Prediction of adhesions is essential before surgery. Preoperative ultrasonography can help identify the “sliding sign”, a valuable tool for anticipating adhesions. Additionally, scar characteristics, the striae gravidarum score, and the number of previous cesarean sections are significant predictors. When used with the sliding sign, they enhance the accuracy of preoperative intra-abdominal adhesion prediction prior to repeat cesarean sections.8

Recent studies have shown a positive correlation between the Davey striae gravidarum score and intraoperative adhesions. The Davey scoring system assesses the severity of striae gravidarum by dividing the abdomen into four quadrants—horizontally at the umbilicus and vertically along the midline. Each quadrant is scored as follows: 0 (no striae), 1 (1–3 striae), or 2 (>4 striae). The total score is calculated by summing the scores of all quadrants, and a score ≥3 is considered the best cutoff point for predicting adhesions.8,9

Adhesions in this patient were likely due to impaired wound healing following a prior cesarean section. Postoperative management can influence the development of adhesions. In this case, the adhesions were undiagnosed before delivery because the patient had only attended two antenatal visits with a midwife. Women with a history of cesarean section are advised to undergo antenatal care with an obstetrician-gynecologist early in pregnancy.

At the referral hospital, after opening the peritoneum, adhesions were observed between the left lateral side of the uterus and the left adnexa. Further exploration showed other adhesions between the urinary bladder and the lower uterine segment. Adhesiolysis was performed using sharp and blunt dissection techniques. Adhesiolysis remains the only definitive treatment for adhesions.1,10

The diagnosis of the placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) remains controversial in the literature. Accurate prenatal diagnosis is essential for enabling appropriate preparation and management.3 Placenta accreta spectrum is undiagnosed in up to two-thirds of cases before delivery. Currently, diagnosis is based on ultrasonographic findings, which, when performed by an experienced operator, have a sensitivity and specificity of up to 90%.4,11

Risk factor assessment is key in the diagnosis of PAS. The Placenta Accreta Index (PAI) combines clinical and ultrasonographic findings such as a prior cesarean section, placental location, minimum myometrial thickness, presence of lacunae, and bridging vessels to predict PAS in patients with placenta previa and prior cesarean section.12 PAI improves diagnostic accuracy (80.3%) compared to ultrasound alone (66.6%) and increases the sensitivity of ultrasound-based diagnosis from 34% to 60%.5 PAI is approved by the FIGO consensus on PAS and by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists.12 However, in this case, the PAI score was not applicable due to the absence of prenatal ultrasound data before surgery at the district hospital.

Although intraoperative findings raised suspicion of placenta accreta spectrum (PAS), it is important to note that the placenta was located at the uterine fundus in this patient. Fundal placental implantation is commonly associated with a lower risk of abnormal placentation compared to anterior or lower segment, which are more commonly involved in PAS, particularly in patients with a history of cesarean section.13 In this case, the district hospital did not perform a prenatal ultrasonographic examination, which made it impossible to definitively diagnose whether the intraoperative findings represented authentic PAS or dense adhesions mimicking its features. The placenta then revealed to be fundal making the diagnosis of PAS seems unlikely. This unnecessary misdiagnose of the placenta position should be care taken if the patient had an adequate antenatal care or the operator took some time to assessed the patient prior to surgery.

Ultrasonography remains an important part of PAS diagnosis and is critical for accurate prenatal detection. It enables the identification of key sonographic features such as placental lacunae, loss of the clear zone, myometrial thinning, and bridging vessels, which are findings that guide clinical suspicion toward PAS.14 In high-risk patients, such as those with a previous cesarean section, failure to perform a timely ultrasound examination significantly limits the ability to differentiate between adhesive disease and actual abnormal placental invasion. Therefore, trained personnel’s early and comprehensive ultrasonographic evaluation is essential for surgical planning and patient safety. In this case, the absence of prenatal ultrasound at the referring hospital likely contributed to the diagnostic uncertainty.

Abnormal invasive placentation is typically accompanied by uterine serosa neovascularization, uterovesical hypervascularization, sub placental hypervascularization, bridging vessels, and bladder wall interruption.3 These findings can appear in various degrees and forms. Intraoperative findings at the district hospital suggested possible placental bulging through the uterine wall. Upon arrival at the referral hospital and during the emergency cesarean section, adhesions were found between the left uterine wall and the adnexa, as well as between the bladder and the lower uterine segment. These findings raised suspicion for placenta accreta spectrum, as they matched FIGO’s clinical classification of PAS stage 3B, characterized by bladder involvement and obliteration of the normal hypoechoic space between the uterus and bladder.

These findings raised concern for PAS in this patient with a history of cesarean delivery. The referring physician made an appropriate decision to transfer the patient due to limited available resources. Referral was aligned with the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) guidelines, which recommend that if PAS is suspected intraoperatively during cesarean section and maternal and fetal conditions are stable, the abdomen should be temporarily closed and the patient referred emergently to a center with adequate personnel and resources, including blood supply. In cases where PAS is unexpectedly diagnosed after delivery of the fetus, the placenta should be left in situ, followed by hysterectomy.15 Although, appropriate assessment should be done prior to surgery to prevent any unnecessary referral and morbidity to the patient. Some time to do pre-assessment prior to surgery are needed to be certain of the patient’s condition and to determine the right surgical treatment.

Not all adhesions require intervention; however, management depends on the etiology, location, and associated signs or symptoms. Aside from conservative management, adhesiolysis remains the definitive treatment. In this case, adhesiolysis was required to free the affected organs and allow surgical progress. Adhesions between the anterior uterus and posterior bladder complicated the cesarean delivery, necessitating dissection. In addition to open adhesiolysis, minimally invasive techniques such as hysteroscopy, laparoscopy, and robotic surgery are also increasingly used in adhesion management.10,16

In this patient, an intrauterine device (IUD) was inserted after placental delivery as a postpartum contraceptive. According to RCOG guidelines on delivery after cesarean section, such patients should receive scheduled antenatal care with an obstetrician-gynecologist. This allows for proper delivery planning—either trial of labor after cesarean (TOLAC)/VBAC or planned repeat cesarean section.17

Conclusion

This case highlights the difficulty of distinguishing dense intra-abdominal adhesions from placenta accreta spectrum (PAS), particularly in patients with a prior cesarean section and inadequate antenatal evaluation. Limited antenatal care and absence of early ultrasonographic assessment contributed to diagnostic uncertainty and emergency referral.

Comprehensive antenatal care, including early risk stratification, proper placental localization, and evaluation by trained personnel, is essential in women with previous cesarean delivery. A well-structured antenatal and referral system ensures timely identification of high-risk cases, appropriate surgical planning, and improved maternal safety.
