Anatomical and radiological analysis of ventricular catheter trajectories: neuronavigation-guided dissection and tractography-based 3D visualization
Authors: Caner Fahrettin Kara, Mehmet Emin Akyüz, Muhammet Elveren, Ufuk Temtek, Mete Zeynal, Samet Kapakin, Ahmet Yalçın, Egemen Gök, Hakan Hadi Kadıoğlu, Mehmet Hakan Şahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ventricular catheter placement (VCP) is a life-saving intervention frequently performed in emergency departments and intensive care units. The free hand technique is the most commonly used approach for placing catheters. This study demonstrates the anatomical and radiological relationship between catheters inserted through the most commonly used access points and the anatomical structures in their path. Moreover, we calculate the insertion angles and catheter lengths that should be specified during VCP. Six donated human heads, fixed in 10% formalin and injected with colored silicone dye, were used in this study. Ventricular catheters were inserted through Kocher’s, Paine’s, Keen’s, Frazier’s, and Dandy’s points under navigation. Dissection was performed from the skin to the inner wall of the ventricle while preserving the inserted catheters. The insertion angle and delivery length for each access point were measured both in the donated human heads and in 50 patients with hydrocephalus. In addition, magnetic resonance tractography was performed, and the relationship of each catheter with anatomical structures was presented as a three-dimensional model. Catheters inserted from Keen’s and Paine’s points pass through regions containing dense and crucial vascular structures. Catheters inserted from Keen’s, Paine’s, and Frazier’s points enter the cortex through eloquent areas. The catheter inserted from Keen’s point passes through the most diverse white matter pathway. Catheters inserted from Kocher’s and Dandy’s points pass through regions with less dense cortical vascular structures. Moreover, they pass through noneloquent cortical structures and fewer white matter tracts. This is the first anatomical dissection and tractography report describing the relationship between catheters introduced through the most common ventricular access sites and the anatomical structures along their path from the skin to the ventricle. Understanding the anatomical and radiological three-dimensionality of VCP will increase the safety of its applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10143-026-04528-y.

Introduction

Ventricular catheter placement (VCP) is a life-saving intervention and one of the most frequently performed procedures in neurosurgical practice. The first documented ventricular drainage was performed by Claude-Nicolas Le Cat in the 18th century, and since then numerous access points and techniques have been developed to reach the ventricular system [1, 2]. Advances in catheter materials and perioperative antibiotic strategies have significantly reduced morbidity and mortality associated with VCP, establishing it as a standard and indispensable neurosurgical procedure [3].

Technological innovations such as neuronavigation-guided and ultrasound-assisted techniques have improved catheter accuracy and reduced complication rates [4]. Nevertheless, in many emergency and intensive care settings, VCP continues to be performed using the freehand technique [5]. Although experienced neurosurgeons can safely perform freehand catheter placement based on anatomical knowledge, a detailed understanding of the cortical, subcortical, and white matter structures encountered along catheter trajectories remains essential, particularly for trainees and surgeons early in their clinical practice.

However, a comprehensive investigation integrating cadaveric anatomical dissection with tractography-based radiological modeling to systematically evaluate ventricular catheter trajectories has not yet been reported. Such integration is crucial for developing a three-dimensional understanding of catheter pathways and their relationships with eloquent cortical and subcortical structures.

The objective of this study was to systematically evaluate ventricular catheter trajectories using combined anatomical dissection of donated human bodies and tractography-based radiological modeling, to define their relationships with cortical and subcortical structures, and to quantify optimal insertion angles and catheter lengths relevant to clinical practice. By integrating anatomical and radiological data, this work aims to provide a practical framework to improve the safety and precision of ventricular catheter placement.

Methods

The study consisted of two complementary phases: an anatomical investigation using donated human bodies and a retrospective clinical imaging analysis. A detailed methodological protocol is provided in the Supplementary Appendix. The study was approved by the university ethics committee (project number TU-2021-9497; meeting number B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/293) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Donated human bodies were obtained through an accredited anatomical donation program in the United States (Supplementary Data 1).

Anatomical study using donated human bodies

Six formalin-fixed donated human heads prepared according to the Klinger technique [6] were used to analyze ventricular catheter trajectories and their relationships with cortical and subcortical structures (Supplementary Data 2).

Each head underwent preprocedural high-resolution MRI and neuronavigation-assisted ventricular catheter placement using five standard access points: Kocher’s, Paine’s, Keen’s, Frazier’s, and Dandy’s points [2, 7] (Supplementary Data 3).

Layer-by-layer anatomical dissection was performed under operative microscopy, preserving catheter trajectories to evaluate their relationships with bone, dura, cortical vasculature, cortex, and white matter structures. Burrhole thickness, catheter length, and insertion angles relative to defined anatomical reference planes were measured (Supplementary Data 4).

Tractography and 3D reconstruction

White matter tractography was generated from a diffusion imaging dataset normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using generalized q-sampling imaging [8, 9]. Anatomically guided tract refinement was performed and integrated with ventricular anatomy in a three-dimensional reconstruction workflow. A geometrically accurate ventricular shunt model derived from measurements obtained from the heads was positioned using recorded insertion angles to illustrate spatial relationships between catheter trajectories and white matter pathways (Supplementary Data 5).

Clinical imaging cohort

A retrospective cohort of 50 adult patients with communicating hydrocephalus was analyzed to validate anatomical measurements in a clinical setting. MRI-based Evans index grading was used to stratify ventricular enlargement severity [10]. Anatomical access points defined in the anatomical study were projected onto patient imaging to measure catheter trajectory distances. Statistical analyses were performed, and significance was set at p < 0.05 (Supplementary Data 6).

This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE reporting guideline. The completed STROBE checklist is provided as a supplementary file [11].

Results

Anatomical Findings

The donated human heads were fixed with a three-pin head fixator (Fig. 1A). The MRI scans of the heads, acquired in four planes (3D, sagittal, axial, and coronal), were registered to the navigation system. The Kocher, Paine, Keen, Dandy, and Frazier points were identified under neuronavigation guidance (Fig. 1B, D–H). Before dissection, each catheter was trimmed so that only 1 cm of the catheter remained at the skin entry point (Fig. 2A). The skin, fascia, and muscular structures on the side of the head where the catheter was inserted were removed, preserving the eyebrow and auricle (Fig. 2B). We observed that the catheter inserted at Kocher’s point passed through the frontal bone anterior to the coronal suture, the one inserted at Paine’s point passed through the sphenoid bone, the one inserted at Keen’s point passed through the temporal bone at the level of the squamous suture, the one inserted at Frazier’s point passed through the parietal bone superior to the lambdoid suture, and the one inserted at Dandy’s point passed through the occipital bone inferior to the lambdoid suture (Fig. 2B). The burrholes through which the catheters passed were shaped like a ring while preserving the flap connection, and the remaining bone flap was elevated to reveal the relationship between the catheters and the dura mater (Fig. 2C). The entire dura mater on the surface of the hemisphere was removed, preserving the superior sagittal sinus and the transverse sinus, and the relationship between the catheters and the cortical veins and arteries was demonstrated. The catheter inserted through Kocher’s point was associated with the middle frontal vein, the one through Paine’s point was associated with the frontal Sylvian vein, the one through Keen’s point was associated with the vein of Trolard, the one through Frazier’s point was associated with the posterior parietal vein, and the one through Dandy’s point was associated with the occipital vein (Fig. 2D). After the veins were removed, the between the catheters and the cortical arteries was observed. The catheter inserted through Kocher’s point was associated with the middle inferior and posterior frontal arteries, the one through Paine’s point was associated with the orbital and posterior frontal arteries, the one through Keen’s point was associated with the angular and temporo-occipital arteries, the one through Frazier’s point was associated with the end branches of the angular and temporo-occipital arteries, and the one through Dandy’s point was associated with the posterior occipital artery (Fig. 2E). After removing the cortical arteries, the relationship between the catheters and the gyri and sulci was examined. The catheter inserted through Kocher’s point entered the cortex from the superior frontal sulcus between the superior and middle frontal gyrus, the one through Paine’s point entered the cortex anterior to the triangular part of the inferior frontal gyrus, the one through Keen’s point entered the cortex from the planum temporale at the upper end of the superior temporal gyrus, the one through Dandy’s point entered the cortex through the occipital cortex, and the one through Frazier’s point entered the cortex through the inferior parietal lobule (Fig. 2F).

The cortical gray matter on the surface of the right hemisphere was decorticated, while the precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, and opercular part of the inferior frontal gyrus were preserved to ensure topographic dominance (Fig. 3A). The gyrus walls were shortened, and decortication was continued until the U-fibers connecting adjacent gyri were exposed (Fig. 3B). The gyrus edges and U-fibers in the frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital gyrus were excised to visualize the insula, superior longitudinal fasciculus complex (SLF-C), SLF- II, SLF- III, arcuate fasciculus (AF), frontal aslant tract, vertical occipital fasciculus, and inferior longitudinal fasciculus. The catheter inserted from Kocher’s point was associated with SLF-II, the one from Paine’s point was associated with the short gyrus of the insular cortex, the one from Keen’s point was associated with SLF-C and AF, the one from Frazier’s point was associated with SLF-C, and the one from Dandy’s point was associated with the vertical occipital fasciculus (Fig. 3C). The SLF-C fibers in the supramarginal gyrus region were removed, the more medially located AF was exposed, and the relationship between the catheter inserted from Keen’s point and the AF was demonstrated (Fig. 3D). The opercular part of the inferior frontal gyrus and the central operculum were removed, and the insular cortex was decorticated, resulting in the visualization of the extreme capsule, external capsule, claustrum, superior limiting sulcus, inferior limiting sulcus, and uncinate fasciculus. Partial removal of the AF led to the visualization of the middle longitudinal fasciculus (MdLF) fibers. The catheter inserted from Paine’s point was associated with the extreme and external capsules, while the one inserted from Keen’s point was associated with the MdLF (Fig. 3E). By magnifying Fig. 3E, the relationship between the catheter inserted from Keen’s point and the MdLF became more evident, and the distinction between the MdLF and AF was accentuated (Fig. 3F). The extreme capsule was removed, the fronto-occipital fasciculus (FOF) was exposed, the MdLF fibers in the superior temporal gyrus were removed, and the part of the FOF extending to the occipital cortex gyri was exposed. This led to the visualization of the relationships between the catheter inserted from Dandy’s point and the FOF and the catheter inserted from Frazier’s point and the MdLF (Fig. 3G). The external capsule was removed, while preserving its central portion, resulting in the exposure of the anterior internal capsule, genu of the internal capsule, posterior internal capsule, putamen, and the lenticulostriate arteries that branch from the middle cerebral artery and reach the putamen. The catheter inserted from Paine’s point was observed to be associated with the anterior internal capsule (Fig. 3H).

The SLF-II, SLF-III, AF, and MdLF fibers were removed. The corona radiata superior to the superior limiting sulcus and the sagittal stratum posterior to the inferior limiting sulcus were exposed. The sagittal stratum consists of the occipitofrontal fasciculus, the posterior thalamic peduncle (which contains the optic radiation), and the fibers of the anterior commissure [12]. The catheter inserted from Kocher’s point was found to be associated with the corona radiata, while those inserted from Keen’s, Frazier’s, and Dandy’s points were associated with the sagittal stratum (Fig. 4A). The ependymal layer of the lateral ventricle was visualized by lifting the corona radiata and sagittal stratum in the areas where the catheters passed. The relationships between the catheters inserted from Kocher’s, Keen’s, and Frazier’s points with the ependymal layer are illustrated in Fig. 4B, and the relationship between the catheter inserted from Dandy’s point and the ependymal layer is shown in Fig. 4C. In the region of the splenium of the corpus callosum, the subarachnoid space fibers were removed to reveal the more medially located tapetum fibers. The catheter inserted from Paine’s point was found to be associated with the head of the caudate nucleus, while the one inserted from Keen’s point was associated with the tapetum (Fig. 4D). The ependymal walls of the entire lateral ventricle were exposed by removing the precentral and postcentral gyri, corona radiata, and AF fibers adjacent to the lateral ventricular body, and the relationships between the catheters and the lateral ventricle were demonstrated by transillumination (Fig. 4E).

After the lateral-to-medial white matter dissection was completed, the ventricular ependyma was left intact, and the internal anatomy of the lateral ventricle was revealed. The position of each catheter in the ventricle was shown. When the catheters were carefully placed away from the choroid plexus and equidistant from the ventricular walls, the appropriate termination points of the catheters in the ventricle were as follows: the catheter inserted from Kocher’s point was located anterior to the choroid plexus and superoanterior to the foramen of Monro in the frontal horn; the catheter inserted from Paine’s point entered the ventricle laterally and ended lateral to the frontal horn; the catheter inserted from Keen’s point entered through the lateral ventricular trigone and ended in the atrium; the catheter inserted from Frazier’s point entered through the ventricular atrium and ended in the middle of the body, superior to the choroid plexus; and the catheter inserted from Dandy’s point was located in the occipital horn and atrium. The angles formed by the catheters placed in the appropriate direction with the Frankfurt, midline sagittal, and coronal planes were calculated (Fig. 5A-O). For each catheter, the bone thickness of the burr hole, parenchyma length, and scalp-to-ventricle length were measured (Table 1).

Radiological findings

Tractographical findings

The critical fiber tracts associated with VCP at different insertion points were visualized as follows: for Kocher’s point, the SLF-II, corona radiata, and frontal aslant tract (Fig. 6A–C); for Paine’s point, the FOF and corona radiata (Fig. 6D–F); for Keen’s point, the AF, FOF, and MdLF (Fig. 6G–I); for Frazier’s point, the SLF-II, FOF, AF, MdLF, and tapetum (Fig. 6J–L); for Dandy’s point, the Fm, vertical occipital fasciculus, tapetum, and FOF (Fig. 6M–O). Some cortical and subcortical structures were also found to be associated with specific insertion points. Kocher’s point was associated with superior and middle frontal gyri; Paine’s point was associated with the triangular part of the inferior frontal gyrus, putamen, caudate nucleus, and Ins; Keen’s point was associated with the superior temporal gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, and angular gyrus; and Frazier’s point was associated with the angular gyrus, which was located along the catheter trajectory. To demonstrate the associations between the different catheter placement approaches and the corresponding neuroanatomical structures, the 3D models were photographed layer by layer, with the superficial layers progressively removed from left to right (Fig. 6). Discussion

VCP is a widely used surgical procedure to measure intracranial pressure, drain excess cerebrospinal fluid, administer intraventricular medication, and evaluate the central nervous system for infection or neoplasia [13]. VCP is usually performed by neurosurgical residents in emergency departments or intensive care units [14]. Despite the rising popularity of neuronavigation devices, which minimize complications and ensure proper placement of ventricular catheters, most procedures are still performed using the freehand technique [15].

Since the time of William W. Keen to the present, many anatomical sites have been explored as entry points for ventricular puncture [7]. This study focused on Kocher’s, Paine’s, Dandy’s, Keen’s, and Frazier’s points because they are within the hairline and do not cause cosmetic defects [16–20]. The most well-known of these points—Kocher’s, Dandy’s, Frazier’s, and Keen’s points—were identified nearly a century ago. Paine’s point is not a typical percutaneous access point used for long-term drainage. It was included because it is an access point used for ventricular puncture to reduce intracranial pressure, especially after frontotemporal craniotomy for aneurysm surgery [18].

To perform VCP appropriately, studies have been conducted to specify scalp landmarks, craniometric measurements, and insertion angles [7, 21]. Radiological and clinical studies have examined the trajectories of catheters inserted through different access points [5, 21, 22]. In this study, we measured the lengths of the catheter trajectories from the scalp to the inner layer of the ventricular wall at each access point on MRI scans of the heads as well as of patients with hydrocephalus. The angle of insertion was measured only in the heads. This is the first study to present anatomical and radiological data together on VCP at different access points (Tables 1 and 2).

The number and length of the holes at the end of the catheters that provide drainage may vary according to the manufacturer. This length is approximately 15–30 mm [23]. The amount of this perforated portion within in the ventricle increases the functional success rate of the system.

When performing VCP, the minimum catheter length that should be inserted can be determined by adding the length of the perforated end of the catheter to the scalp-to-ventricle length calculated in this study. The data on catheter length and insertion angle for each access point presented in this study are expected to guide the application of VCP.

As with all surgical procedures, adequate knowledge of human anatomy reduces the possibility of complications [24]. This knowledge fosters an understanding of the causes of complications and enables the determination of treatment strategies. This anatomical study was performed to demonstrate the relationship between catheters inserted during VCP with the surrounding tissues, especially the white matter, while preserving the catheters. To our knowledge, this is the first study in which the heads specimens were catheterized under navigation, and anatomical dissection was performed on the resulting traumatic tissue. This study may benefit all neurosurgeons, especially neurosurgical residents who perform VCP as their first independent surgical procedure.

The relationships between catheters inserted through each access point and the cortical arterial, venous, gray matter, and white matter structures are presented in Table 3. In addition, superficial cortical veins may serve as reliable intraoperative landmarks because they often remain identifiable despite brain shift, and their relationship to ventricular trajectories should be considered during catheter planning [25]. When examining the cortical venous anatomy, the largest lacunae opening into the superior sagittal sinus are located between the coronal and lambdoid sutures, particularly at the level of the central sulcus. Large bridging veins are predominantly found in these regions [26]. This distribution is clinically relevant. In addition, the superficial anastomotic venous system including the Sylvian, vein of Trolard, and vein of Labbé pathways demonstrates substantial interindividual anatomical variability. Therefore, during cranial entry and catheter trajectory planning, attention should be paid not only to the midline venous sinuses and bridging veins but also to the patient-specific course of these superficial venous structures [27]. In our study, we observed that the catheters inserted through all access points were positioned away from the main sinus, the large lacunae, and the bridging veins opening into these sinuses. The nearest entry points to the superior sagittal sinus are Kocher’s point anterior to the coronal suture and Dandy’s point posterior to the lambdoid suture. They are located 3–4 cm from the midline, which seems to be sufficient to avoid the risk of venous sinus injury [28]. The catheter inserted through Keen’s point passed close to the posterior end of the Sylvian vein and the lower point of the vein of Trolard. Because of its proximity to these two important venous structures, Keen’s point could be considered a relatively risky access point for VCP.

The M3-M4 main branches of the middle cerebral artery are located in the perisylvian area [29]. Keen’s and Paine’s points are the closest to the perisylvian area, whereas Kocher’s, Frazier’s, and Dandy’s points are distant from this area and are associated with the endpoints of the M4 cortical branches. Therefore, Keen’s and Paine’s points are more closely associated with the large endpoints of the first ascending branches of M3 and M4. As a result, misdirection of catheters inserted through Keen’s and Paine’s points may cause damage to the larger-caliber branch of the middle cerebral artery.

The catheter inserted through Kocher’s point enters the cortex at a point that is functionally responsible for controlling eye movement, language, and working memory (Brodmann area 8 (BA 8) [30]), as well as intellectual functions, memory, focus, decision-making, and attention (BA 9) [31]. The catheter inserted through Paine’s point enters the cortex at BA 44 and BA 45, which are responsible for speech motor planning. Moreover, it enters the insula at BA 13, which controls sensory processing, decision-making, and motor functions [31]. The catheter inserted through Keen’s point enters the cortex at points that are components of Wernicke’s area (BA 39, BA 40). These points are highly active during complex verbal processing, creativity, and semantic processing [31]. The catheter inserted through Frazier’s point enters the cortex at BA 19, which is associated with the visual cortex [31]. The catheter inserted through Dandy’s point enters the cortex at the primary visual cortex (BA 17) and the secondary visual cortex (BA 18) [31]. Sawaya et al. classified functional locations in the brain into three groups: eloquent, near-eloquent and noneloquent [32]. The catheters inserted from Paine’s and Keen’s points passed through the speech center, while the catheter inserted from Dandy’s point passed through the visual center, all of which are eloquent areas. The catheters inserted from Kocher’s and Frazier’s points passed through the frontal and parieto-occipital lobes, respectively, both of which are noneloquent areas. When the cortex entry areas for catheters inserted from the five access sites are compared, Kocher’s and Frazier’s points may be considered to be more advantageous than the others.

VCP is a transcortical intervention that reaches the ventricles by passing through the white matter pathways. When performing VCP, catheters inserted through each access point come in contact with different white matter pathways owing to differences in both the entry and target regions. Catheters inserted from Kocher’s and Paine’s points reach the frontal horn of the lateral ventricles, whereas those inserted from Keen’s, Frazier’s, and Dandy’s points reach the atrium of the lateral ventricle [28, 29, 31, 32]. The atrium is surrounded by the SLF/AF complex, sagittal stratum, tapetum, and ependyma from the lateral-to-medial direction [33]. The sagittal stratum consists of the intertwined fiber groups of the FOF, anterior commissure, optic radiation, and posterior thalamic peduncle [33]. According to our study, among the catheters that reached the atrium, the one inserted from Keen’s point came in contact with the highest number of fiber groups, whereas the one inserted from Frazier’s point came in contact with the fewest fiber groups. The most appropriate access point for atrium-targeted ventricular surgeries is controversial, and high deficit rates are observed in transcortical interventions [34]. Although VCP is a minimally invasive procedure, the fact that the target point is the atrium may create a natural bias against catheterization at this target point.

Paine’s point is not a preferred site for long-term drainage of cerebrospinal fluid. The catheter inserted from Paine’s point enters the cortex at the inferior frontal gyrus; passes through the insula, extreme capsule, external capsule, and anterior internal capsule; reaches the ventricle; and contacts the upper border of the head of the caudate nucleus. Modifications have been described in which the entry point is changed by 2 cm on the cortex to prevent possible damage to Broca’s area and the head of the caudate nucleus [18, 35]. Despite these modifications, the catheter will likely pass through the internal capsule, which is an eloquent structure [32].

White matter pathways in the brain have distinct and critical functions. However, the functions of some white matter pathways remain obscure. According to current knowledge, SLF-II contributes to visuo-spatial attention [36]. Its role in critical functions such as speech, motor, and vision is unclear. Deficits specific to SLF-II fibers, resulting from neuro-oncological damage or resection, are not well-defined [37]. Therefore, Kocher’s point may have an advantage over other points for catheter insertion because of its relationship with SLF-II.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the 3D anatomical relationship between ventricular catheters with the anatomical structures encountered along the entire trajectory of catheter insertion. The study specifies the insertion angles and catheter lengths to be followed during VCP.

Catheters inserted from Kocher’s and Dandy’s points pass through regions where cortical vascular structures are less dense. Moreover, they pass through noneloquent cortical structures and fewer white matter tracts. Therefore, Kocher’s and Dandy’s points may have an advantage over the other access points described in this study.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, tissue from the heads may not fully replicate in vivo tissue elasticity and vascular dynamics, which could influence catheter trajectory relationships. Second, tractography-based reconstructions rely on probabilistic white matter modeling and may not capture microscopic anatomical variability. Third, the clinical imaging cohort was retrospective and limited to patients with communicating hydrocephalus, which may restrict generalizability to other ventricular pathologies. Histopathological evaluation was not performed, and therefore the extent of potential neuronal injury caused by catheter placement remains unknown. Additionally, clinical and functional outcomes related to catheter misplacement were not assessed. Furthermore, only five representative ventricular entry points were evaluated in this study. These entry points were selected because they represent the most commonly used and clinically established approaches in contemporary neurosurgical practice; however, other ventricular entry points described in the literature were beyond the scope of the present study. Although these limitations may introduce measurement imprecision, they are unlikely to alter the fundamental anatomical relationships demonstrated in this study.
