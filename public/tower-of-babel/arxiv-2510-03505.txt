A Direct Approach for Detection of Bottom Topography in Shallow Water


Abstract
We propose a fast, stable, and direct analytic method to detect underwater channel topography from surface wave measurements, based on one-dimensional shallow water equations. The technique requires knowledge of the free surface and its first two time derivatives at a single instant t ⋆ \displaystyle t^{\star} above the fixed, bounded open segment of the domain. We first restructure the forward shallow water equations to obtain an inverse model in which the bottom profile is the only unknown, and then discretize this model using a second-order finite-difference scheme to infer the floor topography. We demonstrate that the approach satisfies a Lipschitz stability and is independent of the initial conditions of the forward problem. The well-posedness of this inverse model requires that, at the chosen measurement time t ⋆ \displaystyle t^{\star} , the discharge be strictly positive across the fixed portion of the open channel, which is automatically satisfied for steady and supercritical flows. For unsteady subcritical and transcritical flows, we derive two empirically validated sufficient conditions ensuring strict positivity after a sufficiently large time. The proposed methodology is tested on a range of scenarios, including classical benchmarks and different types of inlet discharges and bathymetries. We find that this analytic approach yields high approximation accuracy and that the bed profile reconstruction is stable under noise. In addition, the sufficient conditions are met across all tests.



Keywords—
Bathymetry, Inverse problem, Shallow water equations, Free surface measurements, Direct inverse approach, Analytic reconstruction.

Introduction

Accurate knowledge of underwater geometry in rivers and coastal zones is critical for coastal engineering applications, including the study of open channel flow hydrodynamics

This prediction direction is referred to as the direct problem and, importantly, utilizes bathymetry information to explore its impact on surface wave deformation, as well as changes in velocity and discharge across the domain. The selection of an appropriate forward model depends on the physical context and flow characteristics. For inviscid flows, we can employ the full incompressible Euler system or adopt its asymptotic simplification, including the Saint‑Venant (shallow water) equations

,

Detecting the bottom profile with good accuracy is a challenging problem that often requires direct measurement techniques, which are time-consuming and costly. One of the primary approaches uses devices based on acoustic waves

. However, this technique is slow, incurs high operational costs, and has a risk of stranding

,

]
the authors propose collecting bed topography measurements using airborne LiDAR. Although faster than sonar surveys, this approach remains costly and is limited by water clarity and flight restrictions
[

,

. A comprehensive overview of additional direct bathymetric survey methods and their limitations can be found in the review paper
[

As an alternative to these direct measurement techniques, we can infer the bottom profile using free surface measurements, which are significantly more accessible than direct underwater data
[
42
,
44
]
. This strategy is formulated as an inverse problem based on a suitably chosen governing equations. For different models used to detect the bed profile from free surface data, see the review paper
[
45
]
. In this work, we formulate the recovery of bathymetry as an inverse problem for the one‑dimensional shallow water equations. Importantly, our approach does not rely on the zero‑inertia approximation
[
47
]
nor the small wave amplitude assumption
[

]
. In this direction, there are mainly two approaches used to solve this inverse problem: direct numerical methods (one‑shot)
[

,

]
and iterative schemes based on PDE‑constrained optimization
[
6
,
17
,
46
]
. In the one‑shot reconstruction paradigm, one first discretizes the forward shallow water equations and then manipulates the discrete system by swapping the roles of the free surface elevation and the bottom profile. This scheme requires access to the free surface elevation, the inlet discharge, and the bed elevation at the upstream boundary of the open channel. However, this method assumes steady‑state flow. Moreover, there is no rigorous analysis of its convergence, stability, or independence from the initial conditions of the forward model. The only exception is
[
13
]
, where a pseudo‑analytic solution of the bed profile is derived in the steady one‑dimensional case. On the other hand, the optimization approaches are free from the steady‑state assumption; however, they require the same measurements as the direct methods, collected over a sufficiently long time interval. In particular, in
[
17
,
46
]
, a limited number of observation locations of the free surface is considered, yet the numerical experiments show that increasing the number of observation points improves convergence. In addition to the measurements used by the direct approach, the optimization-based approaches implicitly necessitate specified initial velocity and outlet depth to numerically solve the shallow water system and its adjoint. Moreover, a smallness condition on the amplitudes of the free surface and the bathymetry is required for the convergence in
[
17
,
46
]
.

In this work, we follow the one‑shot approach introduced in
[
10
]
. Importantly, we refrain from assuming steady‑state flow. Moreover, instead of manipulating a discrete shallow-water scheme, we first derive an inverse system from the continuous forward model, where the bottom profile is the only unknown. We then discretize this inverse system using a second‑order finite‑difference method. Crucially, our formulation is inherently independent of the initial conditions of the forward model and relies on minimal measurements that naturally extend those of the steady‑state method
[
10
]
. Furthermore, we prove a Lipschitz stability estimate for our approach, which is an important guarantee for this class of ill‑posed inverse problems. In the end, we confirm the accuracy and robustness of the approach through various numerical tests. The Python code is available in
[
24

The Forward and the inverse problems

This section begins with a brief overview of the one‑dimensional shallow water equations and their principal features. Moreover, we present two mathematically equivalent formulations of this system and highlight an important physical distinction that we will exploit in our bed profile detection. In Subsection

Governing equations

One of the primary objectives of this paper is to develop a direct, fast, and stable analytical approach for identifying channel bathymetry in unsteady-state flow, utilizing only inlet discharge and free surface elevation measurements collected over a short time interval.

\displaystyle X_{2}(\tau)\geq x+c_{0}(t-\tau),\;\;\forall\tau\in(0,t).\end{cases}

Using
\displaystyle t>\frac{a_{2}-a_{1}}{c_{0}}
, we get

\begin{cases}\displaystyle X_{1}(0)\leq x-c_{0}t<a_{1},\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle X_{2}(0)\geq x+c_{0}t>a_{2}.\end{cases}

(17)

By the mean-value theorem, since
\displaystyle X_{1}
is strictly increasing on
\displaystyle(0,t)
and
\displaystyle X_{2}
is strictly decreasing on
\displaystyle(0,t)
, along with (
17
), there exist unique
\displaystyle\tau_{1}
and
\displaystyle\tau_{2}
in
\displaystyle(0,t)
that depend on
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle t
, satisfying

X_{1}(\tau_{1})=a_{1}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptX_{2}(\tau_{2})=a_{2}.

Integrating the first equations of systems (
15
) and (
16
) over
\displaystyle(\tau_{1},t)
and
\displaystyle(\tau_{2},t)
, respectively, we arrive at

\begin{cases}\displaystyle R_{1}(t,x)-R_{1}(\tau_{1},a_{1})=-g\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{1}}^{t}b^{\prime}\left(X_{1}(s)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}s,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle R_{2}(t,x)-R_{2}(\tau_{2},a_{2})=-g\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{2}}^{t}b^{\prime}\left(X_{2}(s)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}s.\end{cases}

(18)

In the following steps of the proof, we focus only on the Riemann invariant
\displaystyle R_{1}
; analogous reasoning applies to
\displaystyle R_{2}
. We have

R_{1}(t,x)=u(\tau_{1},a_{1})+2\sqrt{g\,h(\tau_{1},a_{1})}\;\,-g\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{1}}^{t}b^{\prime}\left(X_{1}(s)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}s.

(19)

On the other hand, using the change of variable
\displaystyle r=X_{1}(s)
, the right-hand integral that involves
\displaystyle b
in the above equation (
19
) can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{1}}^{t}b^{\prime}\left(X_{1}(s)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{a_{1}}^{x}b^{\prime}(r)\;\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}\left(X_{1}^{-1}(r),r\right)}\,{\mathrm{d}}r

(20)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\left\{(a_{1},x),\;\;b^{\prime}>0\right\}}\frac{b^{\prime}}{\lambda_{1}}\,+\displaystyle\int_{\left\{(a_{1},x),\;\;b^{\prime}<0\right\}}\frac{b^{\prime}}{\lambda_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\int_{\left\{(a_{1},x),\;\;b^{\prime}>0\right\}}\frac{b^{\prime}}{\lambda_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\left\{I,\;\;b^{\prime}>0\right\}}b^{\prime}.

Substituting the above inequality (
20
) into (
19
), we obtain the following inequality

R_{1}(t,x)\geq u(\tau_{1},a_{1})+2\,\sqrt{g\,h(\tau_{1},a_{1})}\;\,-\frac{g}{c_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\left\{I,\;\;b^{\prime}>0\right\}}b^{\prime}.

(21)

Following similar arguments, with attention to the fact that
\displaystyle X_{2}
is a strictly decreasing function over
\displaystyle(0,t)
, we derive the following lower bound for
\displaystyle R_{2}

R_{2}(t,x)\geq u(\tau_{2},a_{2})-2\,\sqrt{g\,h(\tau_{2},a_{2})}\;\,-\frac{g}{c_{2}}\displaystyle\int_{\left\{I,\;\;b^{\prime}>0\right\}}b^{\prime}.

(22)

Summing the above inequalities (
21
) and (
22
) side by side, we obtain

2\,u(t,x)\geq u(\tau_{1},a_{1})+u(\tau_{2},a_{2})+2\sqrt{g}\,\left(\sqrt{h(\tau_{1},a_{1})}-\sqrt{h(\tau_{2},a_{2})}\right)\;\,-g\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}\right)\displaystyle\int_{\left\{I,\;\;b^{\prime}>0\right\}}b^{\prime}.

Since
\displaystyle b^{\prime}
is continuous on
\displaystyle I
, then
\displaystyle\left\{I,\;\;b^{\prime}>0\right\}
is an open set of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, the above integral of
\displaystyle b^{\prime}
can be written as the Riemann-sum in (
12
). Hence, the condition (
12
) implies

2\,u(t,x)\geq\rho.

Finally,

q(t,x)=h(t,x)u(t,x)\geq\varepsilon\,\frac{\rho}{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle q,\,\widetilde{q},\,\partial_{t}q,\,\partial_{t}\widetilde{q}\in W^{1,1}(I)

(29)

where

C_{1,2}=\frac{1}{C_{1}}\biggl(e^{C_{2}(a_{2}-a_{1})}-1\biggr),

(30)

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2}(a_{1})}{h(a_{1})\widetilde{h}(a_{1})}\Bigl(\left|b(a_{1})-\widetilde{b}(a_{1})\right|+\left|\zeta(a_{1})-\widetilde{\zeta}(a_{1})\right|\Bigr)+\frac{q(a_{1})+\widetilde{q}(a_{1})}{\widetilde{h}(a_{1})}\left|q(a_{1})-\widetilde{q}(a_{1})\right|

(31)

\displaystyle+C_{3}\left\|q-\widetilde{q}\right\|_{L^{1}(I)}+C_{4}\left\|\partial_{x}(\zeta-\widetilde{\zeta})\right\|_{L^{1}(I)}+\left\|\partial_{t}q-\partial_{t}\widetilde{q}\right\|_{L^{1}(I)},

(32)

Remark 3.7
.

In the above stability result (

\displaystyle C_{1,2}

(33)

\displaystyle U=(\zeta,q)^{T}

F(U,b)=\left(q,\frac{q^{2}}{\zeta-b}+\frac{g}{2}\left(\zeta-b\right)^{2}\right)^{T}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptS=\left(0,-g\left(\zeta-b\right)\,b^{\prime}\right)^{T}.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U+\partial_{x}F=S.\\

For the sake of simplicity, we denote the grid points by

\displaystyle\overline{S}_{j}^{(2)}\approx-g\left(\overline{\zeta}_{j}-b_{j}\right)\frac{b_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-b_{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\Delta x}

\displaystyle H_{j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}F\left(U^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},b_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\right)-A^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}F\left(U^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},b_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\right)}{A^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-A^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle(0,t_{f})
based on the forward scheme (

\displaystyle q(t,x)
and its temporal derivative
\displaystyle\partial_{t}q(t,x)

(for example

) (
5

6
).

1.

Consider the following time mesh:

t_{0}=0<t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{N-1}<t_{N}=t_{f},

with
\displaystyle t^{\star}=t_{n^{\star}}
, for some index
\displaystyle n^{\star}\in\{0,1,....,N\}
. At any time
\displaystyle t_{n}
, the surface’s time derivative
\displaystyle\partial_{t}\zeta(t_{n},x)
is approximated using the following nonuniform central finite‑difference formula

\partial_{t}\zeta(t_{n},x_{j})\approx\left(\partial_{t}\zeta\right)^{n}_{j}=\frac{-\delta_{p}^{2}\,\zeta^{n-1}_{j}+\left(\delta_{p}^{2}-\delta_{m}^{2}\right)\zeta^{n}_{j}+\delta^{2}_{m}\,\zeta^{n+1}_{j}}{\delta_{m}\,\delta_{p}\,(\delta_{m}+\delta_{p})},

(44)

where

\begin{cases}\delta_{p}=t_{n+1}-\delta_{n},\\
\delta_{m}=t_{n}-t_{n-1}.\end{cases}

Here, the time step is nonuniform by construction (
43
). Furthermore, the values
\displaystyle\zeta^{-1}_{j}
and
\displaystyle\zeta^{N+1}_{j}
are approximated by introducing a single ghost point just outside each boundary (
\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle t_{f}

\displaystyle\zeta^{-1}_{j}
and
\displaystyle\zeta^{N+1}_{j}

2.

We now proceed to solve the first equation in (
6

\displaystyle\partial_{x}q=-\partial_{t}\zeta
.

q_{j+1}^{n}=q^{n}_{j}-\frac{\Delta x}{2}\left(\left(\partial_{t}\zeta\right)^{n}_{j}+\left(\partial_{t}\zeta\right)^{n}_{j+1}\right).

(45)

3.

We have
\displaystyle\zeta
and
\displaystyle q
on the time-space domain
\displaystyle(0,t_{f})\times I
; we then compute the temporal derivative
\displaystyle\partial_{t}q
and the spatial derivative
\displaystyle\partial_{x}\zeta
by applying the second‑order finite‑difference scheme (
44

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\zeta

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\zeta

\displaystyle q_{t}(t,a_{1})

4.

The algorithm concludes by solving the inverse equation in (
6

\displaystyle t^{\star}=t_{n^{\star}}
(

•

Predictor step

\begin{cases}\Phi_{1}=-g\,\left(\partial_{x}\zeta\right)^{n^{\star}}_{j}\,\left(q^{n^{\star}}_{j}\right)^{2}\,\frac{1}{\phi^{n^{\star}}_{j}}-\left(\partial_{t}q\right)^{n^{\star}}_{j}\\
\Phi_{1}^{\divideontimes}=\phi^{n^{\star}}_{j}+\Delta x\,\Phi_{1}\end{cases}

(46)

•

Corrector step

\Phi_{2}=-g\,\left(\partial_{x}\zeta\right)^{n^{\star}}_{j+1}\,\left(q^{n^{\star}}_{j+1}\right)^{2}\,\frac{1}{\Phi_{1}^{\divideontimes}}-\left(\partial_{t}q\right)^{n^{\star}}_{j+1}

\displaystyle b(a_{1})

5
Conclusions, further comments, and future work

We have provided a direct, stable, and analytic approach to infer the bed topography using the one‑dimensional shallow water equations in both steady and unsteady flows. The technique requires an open domain setting and knowledge of the free surface elevation and its first two time derivatives over the spatial domain at a single time
\displaystyle t^{\star}
(
1
), as well as the flow rate and the bed profile at the upstream boundary, see Figure
1
. This time
\displaystyle t^{\star}
should satisfy the nondegeneracy assumption (
5
). We illustrated that this condition automatically holds for supercritical flow. To extend this guarantee to subcritical and transcritical flows, we established two sufficient conditions (
12
) and (
24
) that connect the bottom profile variation with the speed of propagation and inlet/outlet data, via the Riemann characteristics. We further proved a Lipschitz stability result, demonstrating that the bottom profile reconstruction depends continuously on the given measurement data, thus quantifying the method’s robustness.

In Subsection
4.3
, five numerical tests have been detailed for several bottom profiles and inlet discharges, confirming both the nondegeneracy assumption (
5
) and its sufficient conditions (
12
)-(
24
). In all these numerical experiments, the relative
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
and
\displaystyle L^{2}
errors (
49
) were computed to rigorously quantify the accuracy of the method. We found that, in all tests, the bed profile is reconstructed with low relative errors. Even when subject to a free surface with noise or a free surface with a high Lipschitz constant
\displaystyle\bigl\|\partial_{x}\zeta\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}
, the method remains robust and the bottom profile is well recovered.

The work methodology exploits the exact continuity equation (
6
) to recover the spatial discharge profile from the first time derivative of the free surface and the inlet discharge (
45
). Since this equation follows directly from the full Euler equations without relying on any asymptotic regime, it applies beyond the classical shallow‑water approximation and can be adapted to deeper‑water models that enforce exact mass conservation
[
30
]
. Therefore, the methodology has the potential to extend to deeper‑water regimes. In particular, the analytic solution in the steady‑state scenario (
9
)-(
10
) also holds for the Peregrine model
[
31
]
, because under the assumption of time independence (
\displaystyle\partial_{t}=0
), this system is equivalent to the shallow water system. This demonstrates that our analytic reconstruction (
9
)-(
10
) remains valid in intermediate‑depth.

A promising direction for future work is to extend the work approach to open‐channel flows that admit no stagnation points. Specifically, by relaxing the nondegeneracy assumption (
5
) to a no‑stagnation requirement, that is, for every spatial location
\displaystyle x\in I
there exists a time
\displaystyle t(x)\in(0,t_{f})
such that
\displaystyle\bigl|q(t(x),x)\bigr|>0.
Developing a reconstruction algorithm based on the inverse system (
6
) and proving its convergence and stability is of interest. However, it should be mentioned that this direction may require different time intervals of measurement of the free surface data
\displaystyle S_{m}
(
1
).

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{x}\phi=-g\frac{\partial_{x}\zeta\,q^{2}}{\phi}\,-\,\partial_{t}q,\hskip 9.24994pt\text{in}\;I,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\partial_{x}\widetilde{\phi}=-g\frac{\partial_{x}\widetilde{\zeta}\,(\widetilde{q})^{2}}{\widetilde{\phi}}\,-\,\partial_{t}\widetilde{q},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{in}\;I.\end{cases}

(56)

Subtracting the equations of the above system (

), we derive

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(\phi-\widetilde{\phi}\,)

\displaystyle=-g\,\partial_{x}\zeta\,q^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\phi}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{\phi}}\right)+g\,\frac{1}{\widetilde{\phi}}\left(\partial_{x}\widetilde{\zeta}\,(\widetilde{q})^{2}-\partial_{x}\zeta\,q^{2}\right)-\left(\partial_{t}q-\partial_{t}\widetilde{q}\right)

\displaystyle=-g\,\partial_{x}\zeta\frac{h\,\widetilde{h}}{\widetilde{q}^{2}}(\widetilde{\phi}-\phi)+g\,\frac{\widetilde{h}}{\widetilde{q}^{2}}\biggl(\partial_{x}\widetilde{\zeta}(\widetilde{q}^{2}-q^{2})-q^{2}(\partial_{x}\zeta-\partial_{x}\widetilde{\zeta}\,)\biggr)-\left(\partial_{t}q-\partial_{t}\widetilde{q}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x}(\phi-\widetilde{\phi}\,)\right|

\displaystyle\leq g\left|\partial_{x}\zeta\right|\,\frac{h\,\widetilde{h}}{\beta^{2}}\left|\phi-\widetilde{\phi}\right|+g\frac{\widetilde{h}}{\beta^{2}}\biggl(\left|\partial_{x}\widetilde{\zeta}\right|\left|q+\widetilde{q}\right|\left|q-\widetilde{q}\right|+q^{2}\left|\partial_{x}\zeta-\partial_{x}\widetilde{\zeta}\right|\biggr)+\left|\partial_{t}q-\partial_{t}\widetilde{q}\right|.

(57)

For ease of reading, we adopt the following notations

\displaystyle(a_{1},x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{a_{1}}^{x}\bigl|\partial_{x}(\phi-\widetilde{\phi}\,)\bigl|\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{g}{\beta^{2}}\bigl\|\partial_{x}\zeta\bigl\|_{\infty}\bigl\|h\,\widetilde{h}\bigl\|_{\infty}\displaystyle\int_{a_{1}}^{x}\bigl|\phi-\widetilde{\phi}\bigl|\,{\mathrm{d}}y\,

(58)

\displaystyle+\frac{g}{\beta^{2}}\bigl\|\widetilde{h}\bigl\|_{\infty}\biggl(\bigl\|\partial_{x}\widetilde{\zeta}(q+\widetilde{q}\,)\bigl\|_{\infty}\,\bigl\|q-\widetilde{q}\bigl\|_{1}+\bigl\|q^{2}\bigl\|_{\infty}\bigl\|\partial_{x}(\zeta-\widetilde{\zeta}\,)\bigl\|_{1}\biggr)

\displaystyle+\bigl\|\partial_{t}q-\partial_{t}\widetilde{q}\bigl\|_{1}.

Then, for any
\displaystyle x\in I

With algebraic manipulation, we can obtain the following

\displaystyle\bigl|\phi(a_{1})-\widetilde{\phi}(a_{1})\bigr|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{q^{2}(a_{1})}{h(a_{1})}-\frac{\widetilde{q}^{2}(a_{1})}{\widetilde{h}(a_{1})}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h(a_{1})\widetilde{h}(a_{1})}\bigl|\widetilde{h}(a_{1})q^{2}(a_{1})-h(a_{1})\widetilde{q}^{2}(a_{1})\bigr|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h(a_{1})\widetilde{h}(a_{1})}\left|q^{2}(a_{1})\left(\widetilde{h}(a_{1})-h(a_{1})\right)+h(a_{1})\left(q^{2}(a_{1})-\widetilde{q}^{2}(a_{1})\right)\right|
