Fermentation with Lactobacillus reuteri and Lachancea fermentati improves the bioactivity and flavor-sensory characteristics of Rosa roxburghii Tratt. juice
Authors: Jing Wang, Qiang Zhu, Mingyi Yu, Xiaozhu Liu, Jinfeng Zhao, Jiayong Li, Jin Yang, Fei Zhang, Weiyuan Tang, Xin Li, Yan Zhou, Mingzheng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The application of Rosa roxburghii Tratt. (RRT) is restricted by its astringency despite its rich nutrient content. This study employed mixed fermentation of RRT juice using Lachancea fermentati and Lactobacillus reuteri. The results showed that the total acidity of the mixed fermentation group (RF) peaked at 20.00 g/L within 12 h, significantly exceeding that of the single-strain fermentation groups. RF enhanced antioxidant activity and exhibited potent in vitro xanthine oxidase (XOD) inhibitory activity, achieving an inhibition rate of 81.72%. Furthermore, RF improved the flavor profile by increasing ester diversity to 14 types while reducing isoamyl alcohol-related off-flavors. Metabolomics and electronic tongue analyses confirmed enhanced umami and richness, driven by the upregulation of flavonoids and umami amino acids. Consequently, co-fermentation showed superior overall performance under the tested conditions in improving flavor and functionality, thereby facilitating the development of high-quality functional beverages.

Introduction

Rosa roxburghii Tratt. (RRT), a perennial deciduous shrub of the Rosaceae family, is primarily distributed in southwestern China, with Guizhou Province being particularly rich in this resource. RRT fruit contains diverse bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, triterpenes, organic acids, tannins, and polysaccharides (Jain et al., 2024). According to previous reports, RRT fruit contains 1300–3500 mg of vitamin C per 100 g, which is much higher than that of most fruits (Wang, Lv, et al., 2021), earning it the title “King of Vitamin C" (Xu et al., 2019). Research indicates that RRT exhibits various biological activities, including immunomodulation, anti-aging effects (Wang, Shen, et al., 2021), antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties (Jain et al., 2024), as well as hypoglycemic and hypolipidemic effects (Chen et al., 2022; Ji et al., 2022). These characteristics make RRT a promising ingredient for the development of functional foods aimed at chronic disease prevention and health promotion. However, despite its high nutritional value, fresh RRT fruit and juice possess an intensely sour and astringent taste due to high levels of organic acids and tannins, which limits their palatability and consumer acceptance. Fermentation, a traditional food processing technique with a long history, can effectively alter the nutritional composition of food matrices and impart unique flavor profiles. Consequently, it is widely applied in the development of fruit and vegetable juice products. Although recent studies have explored the use of lactic acid bacteria to improve the taste of RRT juice (RRTJ), existing research has predominantly focused on single-strain fermentation. For instance, Li, Yuan, et al. (2023) demonstrated that Lactobacillus acidophilus GIM1.208 reduced the bitterness and astringency of RRT juice (RRTJ) while enhancing its aroma. Similarly, Li, Zhang, et al. (2023) using HS-GC-IMS, revealed that probiotic fermentation decreased undesirable flavor compounds, such as aldehydes, and improved the overall sensory quality of RRT in terms of appearance, aroma, and taste. However, the production of desirable aroma compounds remains limited partly due to the high organic acid content of RRT, which restricts product palatability and hinders market acceptance. Furthermore, single-strain fermentation of RRTJ presents several limitations. First, the restricted metabolic network of a single strain often results in a monotonous flavor profile and the potential accumulation of off-flavors. Second, single strains may exhibit poor adaptability to the high acidity and tannin levels of RRTJ, leading to unstable fermentation and inconsistent product quality. Additionally, traditional fermentation processes are often impeded by prolonged fermentation cycles, nutrient degradation (e.g., vitamin C and polyphenols), and the accumulation of undesirable metabolites, including diacetyl and higher alcohols. Therefore, developing novel fermentation technologies that improve both flavor quality and functional characteristics has become a critical research priority in RRT processing. Through complementary microbial metabolism, mixed fermentation may help overcome some limitations of single-strain fermentation in terms of efficiency and product diversity, yielding products with unique flavor profiles and enhanced nutritional value. Recently, co-fermentation using lactic acid bacteria and yeast has been widely applied in the development of fermented fruit juice beverages, and has demonstrated significant potential for enhancing flavor complexity. For instance, Sun et al. (2025) reported that co-cultivating of Lactobacillus plantarum with various yeasts enhanced the functional and sensory qualities of composite fruit and vegetable beverages, with fermentation performance exhibiting strain specificity. Similarly, Feng et al. (2025) observed that mixed fermentation with L. plantarum and Pichia yeast enriched the aroma of jujube juice through amino acid and nucleotide metabolic pathways, with dodecanoic acid identified as a key volatile compound. However, conventional fermentation using Saccharomyces cerevisiae (brewer's yeast) often generates excessive alcohol and carbon dioxide, which may mask the fresh characteristics of the juice and cause excessive gas accumulation. In contrast, non-Saccharomyces yeasts are characterized by low ethanol production. Compared with S. cerevisiae, these yeasts can synthesize a more diverse array of aromatic compounds, including esters and acids (Cheng et al., 2022). Furthermore, they may mitigate sharp acidity by degrading organic acids, thereby softening the sensory profile of the product (Chang et al., 2025).

Despite extensive studies on RRT fermentation, current literature is largely confined to monoculture systems or S.cerevisiae-driven vinification. Such approaches often fail to effectively ameliorate the inherent high acidity and astringency of the fruit, resulting in limited aromatic complexity and possible degradation of bioactive compounds. To date, the potential interaction between non-Saccharomyces yeasts and lactic acid bacteria in remodeling the flavor profile and enhance the bioactivity of RRTJ remains insufficiently explored. Herein, we investigated the combined fermentation potential of Lachancea fermentati—selected for its ester-producing capacity—and the probiotic Lactobacillus reuteri—selected for its acid tolerance (Singh et al., 2012)—to remodel the RRTJ matrix. A mixed-culture fermentation system was constructed using Guizhou RRTJ as a substrate to systematically compare the effects of single-strain and mixed-strain fermentation on physicochemical properties, antioxidant capacity, potential bioactivities, volatile aroma profiles, and sensory attributes. The primary objective of this work was to evaluate the effects of this co-fermentation strategy on the taste, aroma, and functional components of RRT juice (RRTJ). These findings are anticipated to provide a solid theoretical and experimental basis for developing novel RRT beverages with improved sensory quality and enhanced nutritional value.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Sterile Rosa roxburghii Tratt. juice was obtained from a commercial supplier in Guizhou Province, China. According to the supplier, the juice was sterilized by ultra-high-temperature (UHT) treatment at 121 °C for 10 s before use. All enzyme-related reagents were purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Microorganisms and RRT fermentation

Lactobacillus reuteri CICC 6118 was obtained from the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC, Beijing, China) and cultured according to the provider's instructions. Lachancea fermentati was isolated from naturally fermented Rosa roxburghii Tratt. wine and identified by sequencing the D1/D2 domain of the LSU (26S) rRNA gene. Both strains were activated to a concentration of approximately 107 CFU/mL for subsequent use.

Both strains were activated to approximately 1 × 107 CFU/mL before use. Based on preliminary optimization and strain-specific physiological characteristics, sterilized Rosa roxburghii Tratt. (RRT) juice was dispensed into sterile Erlenmeyer flasks for fermentation. For single-strain fermentation, activated cultures of Lactobacillus reuteri CICC 6118 or Lachancea fermentati were inoculated at 5% (v/v). For mixed-culture fermentation (RF), L. reuteri and L. fermentati were inoculated simultaneously, each at 5% (v/v), giving a 1:1 (v/v) ratio and a final total inoculum level of 10% (v/v). Fermentation was conducted for 48 h under the indicated temperature conditions: 37 °C for L. reuteri, 28 °C for L. fermentati, and 30 °C for RF as a compromise temperature to support the simultaneous growth and metabolic activity of both strains. Unfermented RRT juice served as the control (UF) (See Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the fermentation process and experimental groups.Fig. 1

Analyses of physical and chemical indicators

The pH of the fermented samples was measured using a digital pH meter (PHS-3C, INESA, Shanghai, China). Total acidity (TA) was determined via acid-base titration, with results expressed as grams of lactic acid equivalent per liter (g/L) of fermented RRT suspension, in accordance with Chinese National Standard GB 12456-2021. Total soluble solid (TSS) content was measured using a handheld refractometer and recorded as degrees Brix (°Bx). Reducing sugar content was quantified using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) assay and expressed in mg/mL (Deng et al., 2023).

Viable bacterial counts were monitored during fermentation (0, 12, 24, and 48 h) using the agar plate counting method, following GB 4789.2-2022. The final colony counts were expressed as log10 CFU/mL.

Determination of total phenolic and flavonoid content

TPC was determined using a modified Folin–Ciocalteu method (Liu, He, et al., 2024). Briefly, 125 μL of diluted sample was mixed with 500 μL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, followed by 1.5 mL of 12% Na₂CO₃, and diluted to 5 mL with distilled water. After incubation at 25 °C in the dark for 30 min, absorbance was measured at 765 nm, with gallic acid as the standard.

TFC was determined as described by Xu et al. (2023) with minor modifications. Briefly, 500 μL of sample was mixed with 200 μL of 2.5% sodium acetate and 200 μL of 9.82% AlCl₃, then diluted to 5 mL with 30% ethanol. After incubation at 25 °C for 30 min, absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a microplate reader, with rutin as the standard.

Evaluation of in vitro antioxidant capacity

The antioxidant capacity of the samples was evaluated using DPPH• and ABTS•+ free radical scavenging assays, adapted from the methods of (Chen, Li, et al., 2023).

DPPH• radical scavenging activity

For the DPPH• assay, 10 μL of sample was mixed with 1 mL of 0.1 mmol/L ethanolic DPPH• solution. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 30 min, after which absorbance was measured at 517 nm. Ethanol served as the blank, while distilled water replaced the sample for the control.

ABTS radical scavenging activity

For the ABTS•+ assay, the radical cation was generated by mixing 7 mmol/L ABTS with 2.45 mmol/L potassium persulfate (1:1, v/v) and incubating in the dark at 4 °C for 12–16 h. The solution was then diluted to an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. After mixing 10 μL of sample with 2 mL of working solution for 6 min in the dark, absorbance was measured at 734 nm, using distilled water as the control.

Evaluation of in vitro enzyme inhibitory potential

The inhibitory activities against α-amylase and α-glucosidase were evaluated using methods adapted from Wang et al. (2018).

For the α-amylase inhibition assay, 500 μL of diluted sample was mixed with 125 μL of α-amylase (6.0 U/mL) at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by 250 μL of 1% (w/v) starch solution for 5 min. The reaction was stopped with 500 μL of DNS reagent, boiled for 5 min, diluted to 5 mL with distilled water, and measured at 540 nm. Acarbose (0.2 mg/mL) was used as the positive control. The inhibition rate was calculated as follows:

Inhibition rate%=1−Asample−Asample blank/Acontrol−Acontrol blank×100%

(where Asample is the absorbance of the reaction mixture, Asample blank is the absorbance of the juice without the enzyme, Acontrol is the absorbance of the system without the sample, and Acontrol blank is the reagent blank).

For the α-glucosidase inhibition assay, 50 μL of α-glucosidase (0.5 U/mL) was mixed with 75 μL of diluted sample at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by 80 μL of 2.5 mM pNPG for 20 min. The reaction was stopped with 100 μL of 0.2 M Na₂CO₃, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm. Acarbose (0.2 mg/mL) served as the positive control.

XO inhibition was measured as described by Zhou, Peng, et al. (2023) with minor modifications. Briefly, 10 μL of XO (0.4 U/mL) was mixed with 10 μL of fermentation broth at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by 80 μL of xanthine (0.5 mmol/L) for 20 min. The reaction was terminated with 100 μL of 1 M HCl, and absorbance was measured at 295 nm. Allopurinol (50 mg/mL) was used as the positive control.

Analysis of volatile components

Headspace-solid phase microextraction (HS-SPME) method

An 8 mL aliquot of Rosa roxburghii Tratt. (RRT) juice was transferred to a 20 mL headspace vial. Subsequently, 2.88 g of NaCl and 10 μL of cyclohexanone internal standard were added to the vial.

GC–MS analysis conditions

Chromatographic separation was carried out using an InertCap Wax capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm film thickness). Mass spectrometric detection was performed in electron impact (EI) ionization mode with an electron energy of 70 eV. The ion source and MS interface temperatures were maintained at 240 °C and 250 °C, respectively. Mass spectra were acquired over a scan range of m/z 35–500.

Qualitative and quantitative methods for compounds

Qualitative analysis: Volatile components were identified by comparing their mass spectra with the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST 17) library. Only compounds exhibiting a spectral similarity match of ≥85% were retained (Zhang et al., 2025). Key flavor compounds were subsequently screened by requiring them to simultaneously fulfill two criteria: Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1 and Odor Activity Values (OAV) > 1.

Evaluation of aroma profile by electronic nose (E-nose)

The odor profiles of the fermented Rosa roxburghii Tratt. (RRT) juice samples were characterized using a PEN3 electronic nose (Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany). An 8 mL aliquot of each sample was transferred to a headspace vial, sealed, and equilibrated for 15 min to facilitate volatile compound accumulation. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using the WinMuster 1.6.2 (Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany) software package.

Evaluation of taste profile by electronic tongue (E-tongue)

The taste profiles of the fermented Rosa roxburghii Tratt. (RRT) juice samples were analyzed using an SA402B electronic tongue (Insent Inc., Atsugi, Japan). For each sample, a 70 mL aliquot was dispensed into two dedicated sample cups and analyzed at room temperature. The system quantified eight taste attributes: sourness, saltiness, bitterness, astringency, umami, bitter aftertaste, astringent aftertaste, and richness.

Non-volatile metabolites

Liquid chromatography conditions

LC analysis was performed on an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The column was maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and an injection volume of 5 μL. For LC-ESI(+)-MS, the mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile; for LC-ESI(−)-MS, they were 5 mM ammonium formate and acetonitrile. Both modes used the same gradient: 0–1 min, 10% B; 1–5 min, 10–98% B; 5–6.5 min, 98% B; 6.5–6.6 min, 98–10% B; and 6.6–8 min, 10% B.

Mass spectrum conditions

Metabolites were detected using a Q Exactive Focus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an ESI source in Full MS-ddMS2 mode. Key parameters were: sheath gas 40 arb, auxiliary gas 10 arb, spray voltage 3.50 kV/−2.50 kV for ESI(+)/ESI(−), capillary temperature 325 °C, scan range m/z 100–1000, MS1 resolution 70,000, MS/MS resolution 17,500, and collision energy 30 eV.

Data analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's HSD post hoc test in SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.5 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA), and radar charts were plotted using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Heatmaps were constructed using TBtools. Multivariate analyses, including PCA, OPLS-DA, and PCoA, were conducted using SIMCA 14.0 (Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Umeå, Sweden) and the TUTOOL platform (http://cloudtutu.com.cn).

Results and discussion

Mixed fermentation enhances acid accumulation, accelerates sugar metabolism, and maintains a high number of viable microorganisms

Fig. 2 illustrates the dynamic changes in the physicochemical properties and viable cell counts of RRTJ during fermentation. As shown in Fig. 2A, the pH of the unfermented group (UF) remained stable at 3.6. In contrast, the pH values of all fermented groups decreased significantly, reaching their minimum within 24 h before increasing slightly at 48 h. This may reflect a late-stage metabolic transition of the microorganisms, which altered the balance between acid production and other physicochemical processes in the fermentation system, thereby leading to a slight recovery of pH at 48 h (Rocha et al., 2024). However, the degradation of specific organic acids was not directly demonstrated in the present study. Conversely, total acidity increased continuously during fermentation (Fig. 2B). The RF group reached a peak value of 20.00 g/L at 12 h, which was significantly higher than those of the single-strain fermentation groups (p < 0.05). This observation may be attributed to the combined metabolic contributions of the two microorganisms: lactic acid bacteria produce organic acids (e.g., lactic and acetic acid), while yeast supplies metabolic precursors (Yuan et al., 2025). Regarding carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. 2C–D), the total soluble solids (TSS) content decreased continuously in all treatment groups except the LR group. The LF group reached its minimum at 48 h and exhibited the greatest reduction in reducing sugars, indicating superior sugar metabolism efficiency. This discrepancy likely resulted from differences in metabolic pathways. Yeast rapidly utilizes reducing sugars to produce ethanol via the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway, directly lowering TSS (Solis-Escalante et al., 2015). In contrast, lactic acid bacteria preferentially utilize monosaccharides for acid production. Additionally, incomplete polysaccharide hydrolysis and the production of exopolysaccharides (EPS) by lactic acid bacteria may contribute to residual TSS (Nikitina et al., 2023). Fig. 2E illustrates the changes in viable cell counts. After a short lag phase, the bacteria entered the logarithmic growth phase, peaking at 24 h in the RF group and at 12 h in the LR group. Subsequently, nutrient depletion or environmental stress induced the stationary phase, potentially leading to programmed cell death (PCD) or autolysis (Chen et al., 2021).Fig. 2Physicochemical changes during fermentation. (A) pH; (B) titratable acidity (TA); (C) total soluble solids (TSS); (D) reducing sugar content; (E) viable cell counts.Fig. 2

Physicochemical changes during fermentation. (A) pH; (B) titratable acidity (TA); (C) total soluble solids (TSS); (D) reducing sugar content; (E) viable cell counts.

The viable cell count of non-Saccharomyces yeast increased rapidly to 7.87 ± 0.07 log CFU/mL. In the mixed-strain group, yeast counts remained elevated during the late fermentation stage. This persistence may be attributed to pyruvate produced by lactic acid bacteria, which served as a carbon source for the yeast (Gabrielli et al., 2023). Concurrently, oligosaccharides or monosaccharides generated via yeast-mediated polysaccharide degradation (indicated by the TSS decrease) may support the sustained proliferation of lactic acid bacteria.

Fermentation enhances antioxidant activity and increases the content of related bioactive substances

Fermentation treatments significantly influenced the total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, and antioxidant capacity of RRTJ. As illustrated in Figs. 3A-B, both DPPH• and ABTS•+ radical scavenging capacities exhibited dynamic changes throughout the fermentation process. Generally, ABTS•+ scavenging activity increased during the initial 24 h. Specifically, the activity in the LR group rose from 37.92% ± 1.42% to 73.61% ± 2.50% before decreasing slightly to 61.85% ± 1.46% at 48 h; in contrast, the other groups stabilized after 24 h. The trends in DPPH• scavenging activity mirrored those of ABTS•+. In the LR group, DPPH• scavenging activity increased significantly from 35.81% ± 2.24% to 84.89% ± 3.80% within the first 24 h, followed by a decline to 54.24% ± 1.33% at 48 h (Fig. 3B). Other groups exhibited only minor fluctuations between 24 and 36 h. Overall, fermentation significantly enhanced the antioxidant activity of RRTJ, with the LR group demonstrating the most pronounced effect. These findings are consistent with with previous reports indicating that lactic acid bacteria can elevate superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels and enhance the free radical scavenging capacity of RRTJ (Li, Zhang, et al., 2023).Fig. 3Variations in bioactive compounds and antioxidant capacity. Previous studies have indicated that microbial abundance influences the antioxidant activity of fermented fruit and vegetable juices (Liu, Li, et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2022). Consequently, this study investigated the impact of microbial population dynamics on the antioxidant activity of RRTJ. Correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between viable microbial counts and antioxidant activity (Figs. S1 and 3E). In the LR group, no significant correlation was observed between viable cell counts and ABTS•+ or DPPH• scavenging capacities. This suggests that the antioxidant effect may be mediated primarily by metabolic byproducts, such as organic acids, rather than being directly dependent on viable cell density (Vougiouklaki et al., 2023). Notably, exopolysaccharides (EPS) produced by lactic acid bacteria can exert antioxidant effects via direct free radical scavenging, activation of cellular antioxidant defense systems, and regulation of inflammatory responses (Zhou, Zeng, et al., 2023). Conversely, the LF treatments (LF and LF.cf. groups) exhibited a significant positive correlation between viable cell counts and antioxidant activity, indicating that antioxidant capacity is directly dependent on cell proliferation. During the logarithmic growth phase, yeast exhibits enhanced synthesis of glutathione and mannan; consequently, its capacity to scavenge DPPH• and hydroxyl radicals, as well as to inhibit lipid peroxidation, surpasses that of cells in the stationary phase (Wang, Lv, et al., 2021).

Regarding bioactive components, the total phenolic content (TPC) of all fermentation groups was significantly higher than that of the unfermented group (UF) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3C), although it declined during the late fermentation stage. The total flavonoid content (TFC) exhibited a similar trend; the LR group peaked at 1.68 ± 0.03 mg/mL at 24 h before gradually decreasing (Fig. 3D). Notably, these results contrast with previous reports that observed an increase in TPC during fermentation (Luo et al., 2024; Zhou, Peng, et al., 2023). This discrepancy may be attributed to the metabolic degradation of phenolic compounds by lactic acid bacteria (LAB) (Septembre-Malaterre et al., 2018). Furthermore, yeast cell wall components, including glucans and mannoproteins, possess significant adsorption capacity for polyphenols. This interaction likely contributes to the reduction in dissolved TPC during the later stages of fermentation (Mekoue Nguela et al., 2015). In addition, over the prolonged 48 h fermentation period, continuous exposure to the specified incubation temperatures may mediate both thermal and oxidative degradation reactions. This inevitably may result in the loss of polyphenolic compounds and vitamin C within the fermentation system (Scrob et al., 2022). It should be noted that vitamin C was not directly quantified in the present study; therefore, the effects of UHT pretreatment and subsequent fermentation on this thermolabile compound require further dedicated investigation. Concurrently, the observed reduction in flavonoids highlights the critical influence of fermentation parameters (e.g., temperature and duration) on the retention of bioactive compounds.

Fermentation bidirectionally improves biofunctional activities

α-Amylase and α-glucosidase are pivotal enzymes in carbohydrate digestion, catalyzing the initial and final stages of starch hydrolysis, respectively, thereby regulating glucose release and absorption. Consequently, inhibiting these enzymes delays polysaccharide hydrolysis, thereby mitigating postprandial hyperglycemia (Yue et al., 2022). As illustrated in Fig. 4A–B, RRTJ fermented by LR exhibited α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition rates of 96.54% and 81.00%, respectively. These values were significantly higher (25.30% and 11.33%, respectively) than those of the unfermented group (p < 0.05). Previous studies have indicated that bioactive compounds, such as terpenoids, alkaloids, and flavonoids, possess hypoglycemic activity (Shehadeh et al., 2021). The enhanced efficacy observed in the LR group may be attributed to the hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds by lactic acid bacteria, which releases potent aglycones, including flavonoid aglycones and phenolic acids. In contrast, fermentations involving Lachancea yeast (LF) exhibited a decline in inhibitory activity as fermentation progressed. To investigate the factors driving this trend, a correlation analysis was conducted. The results (Fig. 4D) revealed a strong positive correlation between reducing sugar content and α-amylase inhibitory activity, as well as a moderate correlation with α-glucosidase inhibitory activity. This suggests that the efficient metabolism of reducing sugars by yeast diminishes the inhibitory effect on α-amylase (Kim et al., 1988). Conversely, α-glucosidase, which functions at the terminal stage of the digestive chain, appears less sensitive to fluctuations in reducing sugar levels.Fig. 4Enzyme inhibitory activities and correlation analysis. Enzyme inhibitory activities and correlation analysis. (A) α-Amylase inhibition rate; (B) α-glucosidase inhibition rate; (C) xanthine oxidase inhibition rate; (D) correlation between reducing sugar content and digestive enzyme inhibition.

Xanthine oxidase (XOD) is a pivotal enzyme that catalyzes uric acid production during purine metabolism by oxidizing hypoxanthine and xanthine. Elevated XOD activity increases serum uric acid levels, which can subsequently trigger hyperuricemia (Xin-yue et al., n.d.). Fig. 4C illustrates the dynamic changes in the inhibitory effect of fermented RRTJ on XOD across different fermentation times. At 48 h, the RF group exhibited the highest inhibition rate (81.72% ± 6.68%), which was significantly higher than that of the unfermented group (77.73% ± 3.90%). These results suggest that fermented RRTJ possesses the potential to mitigate uric acid-related metabolic disorders. The enhancement of this inhibitory activity is likely linked to the biotransformation of phenolic compounds in RRT. Furthermore, metabolites such as organic acids produced by lactic acid bacteria may contribute to the inhibitory effect (Dong et al., 2025). Conversely, the inhibition rates in the non-Saccharomyces yeast fermentation groups were relatively low; this may be attributed to metabolic processes that provide purine precursors, potentially offsetting the inhibitory effect. Mixed fermentation (RF) significantly enhanced the XOD inhibitory activity of RRTJ, with the inhibition rate reaching 81.72% ± 6.68% at 48 h. This result suggests that mixed fermentation showed improved performance relative to single-strain fermentation under the tested conditions over single-strain cultures. Nevertheless, these preliminary findings warrant further experimental validation.

Pearson correlation analysis

To elucidate the relationship between physicochemical indicators and the physiological activity of fermented RRTJ, Pearson correlation analysis was employed to assess the associations among physicochemical parameters, antioxidant capacity, and enzyme inhibition activities (Fig. 5). Total phenolic content (TPC) demonstrated a robust positive correlation with DPPH• radical scavenging activity (r = 0.91, p < 0.01) and total flavonoid content (r = 0.97, p < 0.01). This suggests that phenolic compounds, particularly flavonoids, contribute synergistically to antioxidant capacity (Borges et al., 2022). Additionally, the strong correlation between ABTS•+ and DPPH• scavenging activities (r = 0.94, p < 0.01) reflects consistency in the underlying antioxidant mechanisms. Concurrently, the significant positive correlation between α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities (r = 0.83, p < 0.01) indicates that the fermented products possess a broad-spectrum inhibitory effect on carbohydrate-digesting enzymes, highlighting the hypoglycemic potential of fermented RRTJ (Li, Yuan, et al., 2023). Conversely, pH exhibited a significant negative correlation with TPC (r = −0.66) and DPPH• scavenging activity (r = −0.70). This indicates that acidification during fermentation may facilitate dissolution and transformation of phenolic compounds, thereby enhancing their biological activity (Limón et al., 2015). Reducing sugar content correlated strongly with the inhibition of both α-amylase (r = 0.94) and α-glucosidase (r = 0.83). However, this correlation mainly reflects the dynamic changes occurring during fermentation, rather than a direct inhibitory effect of reducing sugars. Samples with weaker microbial metabolism retained more reducing sugars and higher levels of intrinsic inhibitory phytochemicals (e.g., phenolics in Rosa roxburghii Tratt.). During fermentation, reducing sugars were consumed, and the key inhibitory compounds underwent transformation or degradation (Yang et al., 2023). Notably, xanthine oxidase (XOD) inhibition demonstrated a weak correlation with other variables (|r| < 0.36). This suggests that the mechanism underlying XOD inhibition functions independently of antioxidant phenolic profiles and may instead be driven by specific flavonoid aglycones or alkaloids (Rahaman et al., 2022).Fig. 5Correlation analysis between physicochemical parameters and bioactivity indices.Fig. 5

Mixed fermentation reshapes flavor balance

Fermentation significantly increases the total amount and diversity of volatile compounds

Flavor significantly influences consumer acceptance and consumption patterns; consequently, this study investigated the sensory flavor profile of fermented RRTJ. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis identified 83 volatile compounds across the different fermentation treatments (Table S1), comprising 25 esters, 23 alcohols, 9 acids, 8 aldehydes, 6 ketones, 10 terpenes, and 2 miscellaneous compounds. Fig. 6A–B, illustrates the relative total content and diversity of these volatiles. Compared with the unfermented control (UF; 45 compounds; 84.01 mg/L), fermentation significantly enhanced both the diversity and concentration of volatile compounds. Notably, the LF group exhibited the highest total concentration (242.27 mg/L), followed by the RF group (197.41 mg/L). Across the four sample groups, 36 shared and 47 differential volatile compounds were identified. Heatmap analysis (Fig. 6C) demonstrated that the samples clustered into two distinct categories. The volatile organic compound (VOC) profiles of RRTJ shifted significantly following fermentation, displaying distinct strain specificity. Further analysis indicated that esters, alcohols, and aldehydes dominated the LF and RF groups, whereas the UF and LR groups were characterized by higher proportions of alcohols and acids. In the unfermented RRTJ, alcohols (42.58 mg/L) and acids (37.32 mg/L) were the predominant volatile components. Specifically, 1-hexanol was the most abundant compound, likely resulting from the autoxidation of unsaturated fatty acids (Lei et al., 2023). This finding contrasts with previous studies; for instance, Sheng et al. (2023) reported that (Z)-3-hexenol was the primary volatile in ‘Guinong 5’ RRT. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in RRT growing environments. Supporting this hypothesis, Huang et al. (2022) observed significant regional variations in the volatile aroma profiles of RRT from five geographical locations. Furthermore, the juicing process itself may alter aroma composition compared with fresh fruit. Relative to the unfermented control (UF), acid and alcohol concentrations in the LR group significantly increased by 22.04 mg/L and 34.03 mg/L, respectively. Key contributors to this increase included trans-3-hexenoic acid, hexanoic acid, and octanoic acid. This accumulation likely results from lactic acid bacteria metabolizing carbohydrates into organic acids, which impart a pungent sour taste and acetic aroma, potentially introducing undesirable odors (Wang, 2024). Consequently, monoculture fermentation with lactic acid bacteria may compromise palatability by causing sensory defects such as excessive sourness and astringency. Furthermore, LF fermentation generated 14 distinct ester compounds, with acetates and fatty acid ethyl esters comprising over 80.00% of this fraction. Prominent examples include ethyl acetate, which provides a pineapple-like aroma, and ethyl hexanoate, which provides a fruity aroma; both contributed to enhancing the fruity profile of the fermented RRTJ. Research indicates that yeast-secreted β-glucosidase hydrolyzes glycosidic bonds to release volatile aglycones, which represents a key mechanism driving ester synthesis (Han et al., 2021; Schmidt et al., 2025). Additionally, aldehydes, ketones, and terpenes contributed significantly to the flavor profile. Post-fermentation increases in terpenes—including geranylacetone, linalool, and α-terpineol—enhanced floral, fruity, and woody notes. However, excessive yeast metabolism in the LF group led to the accumulation of off-flavor compounds. Specifically, elevated levels of isoamyl alcohol and ethyl acetate produced pungent solvent or solvent-like notes; 1-penten-3-ol and 1-octen-3-ol imparted metallic-green and earthy-moldy odors; and ethyl lactate and acetone intensified solvent characteristics and overripe fruit aromas. These compounds introduced green and fusel-like notes, negatively impacting the overall sensory quality. In contrast, the RF group exhibited reduced levels of these off-odor compounds compared with the LF group. Mixed fermentation optimizes key flavor compounds

To elucidate the impact of different fermentation treatments on the volatile profile of RRTJ, we analyzed odor activity values (OAVs) and established a multivariate statistical model. OAVs quantify the contribution of volatile compounds to the overall aroma profile; an OAV > 1 identifies a compound as a key aroma contributor (Wang et al., 2024). Of the 83 identified volatiles, 45 exhibited OAVs >1 (Supplementary Table S2). The unfermented RRTJ was characterized by compounds with herbal or grassy notes, such as hexanol and hexanal. Similarly, the LR group displayed elevated OAVs for specific alcohols (e.g., 1-hexanol), potentially imparting distinct herbal and grassy notes. The LF group produced significant quantities of fruity esters, such as ethyl acetate and isoamyl acetate; however, relatively high concentrations of isoamyl alcohol introduced potential off-flavors. While ester OAVs in the RF group were slightly lower than those in the LF group, they remained significantly higher than those in other treatments. Moreover, the RF group retained higher concentrations of desirable alcohols, such as phenethyl alcohol. These findings suggest that co-fermentation with lactic acid bacteria and non-Saccharomyces yeasts optimized the acid-ester balance, resulting in a more harmonious flavor profile. Notably, isoamyl alcohol levels were significantly reduced in the co-fermentation treatment compared with the LF group, thereby mitigating potential fusel off-odors. Notably, isoamyl alcohol levels were significantly reduced in the co-fermentation treatment compared with the LF group, thereby mitigating potential fusel off-odors (Yang et al., 2024). A more plausible explanation for this reduction is that the rapid accumulation of organic acids, particularly lactic acid produced by LAB, imposed acid stress on the yeast during co-fermentation, which may have suppressed yeast metabolic activity associated with fusel alcohol biosynthesis (Bellut et al., 2020). Since our non-volatile metabolomic data did not directly confirm the depletion of free leucine in the RF group, the possibility that LAB reduced isoamyl alcohol formation through competition for amino acid precursors should be regarded as a tentative hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) elucidated fundamental shifts in volatile profiles across fermentation methods (Fig. 6D). The model achieved distinct separation between treatment groups, confirming that fermentation significantly altered the volatile composition of RRTJ. To assess the reliability and predictability of the model, cross-validation and a 200-iteration permutation test were conducted. The model yielded R2Y = 0.987 and Q2 = 0.917, with permutation test intercepts at R2 = 0.703 and Q2 = −0.844, indicating that the model is reliable and not overfitted. While plant-derived aldehydes (e.g., hexanal) dominated the unfermented group (UF), microbial metabolites, including esters, alcohols, and acids, characterized the fermented groups. Notably, the close clustering of the LF and RF groups in the OPLS-DA plot suggests that they share similar flavor profiles, indicating that yeast plays a dominant role in flavor formation during mixed-strain fermentation. By simultaneously screening compounds with variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and odor activity value (OAV) > 1, we identified 17 key markers driving inter-group differences, including ethyl hexanoate, nonanal, and geraniol (Fig. 6E). Cluster heatmap analysis (Fig. 6F) revealed that the UF group was characterized by oxidative, plant-derived compounds such as nonanal. The lactic acid bacteria group (LR) contained elevated levels of compounds like octanoic acid, which may impart undesirable pungent or rancid notes, alongside positive volatiles such as caramel-like furanones. The yeast-fermented group (LF) was enriched in esters and aldehydes (e.g., geraniol, hexanal), which are primary sources of fruity and floral aromas. Notably, the RF group displayed a synergistic enhancement of specific esters (e.g., ethyl isobutyrate). This likely results from lactic acid bacteria providing esterification precursors that yeast subsequently catalyzes (De Vuyst & Leroy, 2020). Furthermore, compounds such as isoamyl acetate, 1-pentanol, and hexanoic acid exhibited VIP values >1. Although present across multiple groups, the significant variation in their concentrations reflects the dynamic evolution of flavor metabolism under different conditions. For instance, while LF fermentation produced high levels of 1-pentanol (associated with wine-like and fusel aromas), excessive concentrations may introduce undesirable solvent-like or empyreumatic notes. In summary, the flavor of unfermented RRTJ is substrate-derived; yeast drives fruity ester production; lactic acid bacteria contribute complex trade-offs; and co-fermentation yields a richer, more harmonious profile through metabolic synergy. These findings provide a theoretical basis for process optimization.

Mixed fermentation improves flavor harmony and taste characteristics

To complement the specific volatile profiles identified by GC–MS, we employed an electronic nose (E-nose) and electronic tongue (E-tongue) to characterize the global aroma and taste profiles of the samples. Sensor response curves revealed significant differences among RRTJ samples subjected to different fermentation treatments. Principal component analysis (PCA) explained 99.66% of the total variance (Fig. 7A), allowing clear differentiation among fermentation groups. These findings are consistent with the radar chart visualization (Fig. 7B) and the previously discussed GC–MS data. Compared with the unfermented control (UF), the LR group exhibited negligible differences in odor profile. Conversely, the LF and RF groups elicited strong responses from sensors W5S (nitrogen oxides), W1S (methyl-containing compounds), W1W (sulfides, terpenes), W2W (organic sulfides), and W2S (alcohols, aldehydes, ketones). This suggests that yeast metabolism significantly increased the levels of alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and nitrogen- and sulfur-containing compounds (Chen, Xie, et al., 2023). Notably, the RF group exhibited a significantly higher response of the W5S (nitrogen oxides) sensor than the LF group. This discrepancy suggests an enhanced production of nitrogen-containing volatile compounds during mixed-strain fermentation, which may be attributed to the intensified and more complex amino acid metabolism during co-fermentation by lactic acid bacteria and yeast. Since flavor perception integrates both olfaction and gustation (Hu et al., 2025), we subsequently analyzed taste characteristics. Following the characterization of aroma profiles, this study quantified taste-related sensor response intensities, including sourness and bitter aftertaste, using an electronic tongue (E-tongue). As illustrated in Fig. 7C, fermentation with non-Saccharomyces yeasts significantly reduced the sensor response intensities for bitterness, astringency, and sourness compared with the LR group, while slightly increasing saltiness and umami. These findings suggest that yeast-containing fermentations modulated taste characteristics, particularly by reducing bitterness and astringency. A plausible explanation is that Lachancea fermentati promoted the biotransformation of flavonoid-related taste precursors, potentially through enzymes such as yeast-secreted β-glucosidase. In this study, the metabolomic data showed clear remodeling of the flavonoid profile during fermentation, including altered levels of 3,7,4′-trihydroxyflavone and tricin 5-glucoside, supporting the view that yeast fermentation changed the composition of flavonoid-associated compounds relevant to taste perception. Therefore, the reduction in bitterness and astringency in the LF and RF groups may be associated with β-glucosidase-mediated transformation of glycoside-bound phenolics and related flavonoid precursors (Muradova et al., 2023; Shin et al., 2013). However, because β-glucosidase activity was not directly measured in the present study, this mechanism should be regarded as a biologically plausible interpretation rather than direct enzymatic proof. The LR group exhibited elevated sensor response intensities for sourness and astringency, reflecting the typical taste profile of lactic acid fermentation. The LF group displayed an intermediate profile; however, it possessed a slightly stronger bitter and astringent aftertaste, potentially linked to the accumulation of higher alcohols. Notably, the RF group demonstrated the highest response intensities for umami and richness. Concurrently, the lower saltiness response contributed to a richer and more harmonious taste profile. Thus, mixed fermentation effectively enhanced taste quality by balancing LAB-derived acidity with yeast-derived umami, while suppressing signals associated with undesirable bitterness and astringency.

Fermentation drives the global remodeling of metabolic profiles

The overall remodeling effect of fermentation on the metabolome of RRTJ

To comprehensively elucidate metabolic variation patterns, untargeted metabolomics was employed to investigate the composition and differential characteristics of RRTJ metabolites. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis identified a total of 1120 metabolites. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, these metabolites were classified into 11 groups, with the predominant categories including organic acids and their derivatives (173), lipids and lipid-like molecules (170), organic heterocyclic compounds (121), organic oxygen compounds (108), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (104), and benzene derivatives (84). Principal coordinate Analysis (PCoA) was utilized to evaluate variations among the four RRTJ sample groups (Fig. 8B), revealing significant differences. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 47.40% and 32.03% of the variance, respectively, with a cumulative contribution of 79.43%. This high cumulative variance indicates that the model effectively captured the metabolic distinctions among samples. Consistent with previous results regarding volatile compounds, fermented RRTJ was clearly distinguishable from unfermented RRTJ, confirming that fermentation significantly altered the global metabolic profile. Notably, the metabolic profiles of the yeast-fermented groups (LF and RF) were similar to each other but distinct from those of the lactic acid bacteria-fermented group (LR) and unfermented group (UF). Analysis of differential metabolites and their association with flavor functions

To investigate the impact of different fermentation processes on RRTJ non-volatile metabolites, orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed across six comparison groups (LR vs. UF, LF vs. UF, RF vs. UF, RF vs. LR, RF vs. LF, and LF vs. LR). Differential metabolites were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and p ≤ 0.05 (Fig. 8C and Fig. S2). The analysis revealed distinct metabolic shifts: the LR vs. UF comparison yielded 73 differential metabolites (31 upregulated and 42 downregulated), while the LF vs. UF and RF vs. UF comparisons exhibited more extensive changes, identifying 225 (61 upregulated and 164 downregulated) and 245 (66 upregulated and 179 downregulated) metabolites, respectively. Comparisons among the fermented groups revealed 221 differential metabolites in RF vs. LR (66 upregulated and 155 downregulated), 199 in LF vs. LR (62 upregulated and 137 downregulated), and 95 in RF vs. LF (45 upregulated and 50 downregulated). These results underscore the pivotal role of specific microbial strains in modulating the non-volatile metabolite profile of RRTJ. Consistent with these findings, Luo et al. (2024) reported similar dynamic shifts during RRT fermentation, attributing them to the biotransformation and degradation of precursors such as amino acids, organic acids, phenolic acids, and alkaloids.

Key differential metabolites were further characterized using a screening criterion of fold change (FC) > 2 (Fig. 8D). Fermentation in the LF group significantly enhanced fruity and sweet aromas through the synthesis of esters (e.g., dimethyl 2-oxoglutarate, S-propyl thioacetate) and aldehydes/ketones (e.g., 3-methoxycatechol, 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid). This metabolic shift transformed the flavor profile from simple vegetative notes to a complex and pleasant bouquet characteristic of fermented fruit wines. Concurrently, the accumulation of umami amino acids significantly improved the richness of the mouthfeel (Lv et al., 2025). In contrast, the LR group was characterized by a substantial accumulation of organic acids, specifically lactic and acetic acids, which correlated with a marked decrease in pH. Consistent with prior analyses of total flavonoid content, fermentation elevated flavonoid levels across all groups; however, specific profiles varied by treatment. The synergistic activity of lactic acid bacteria and yeast in mixed-strain fermentation yielded the highest flavonoid content and species diversity (e.g., avenanthramide L, tricin 5-glucoside, normaritidine). The LF group exhibited intermediate enhancement, primarily driving flavonoid accumulation and transformation, while the LR group showed increased content but limited structural diversity.

Based on semi-quantitative comparative values, the relative peak intensity of 3,7,4′-trihydroxyflavone in the LF group (1.10 × 108) was nearly threefold higher than that in the LR group (3.93 × 107), indicating that Lachancea fermentati effectively promoted the accumulation of specific flavonoids. Additionally, the relative levels of tricin 5-glucoside were elevated in all fermented groups compared to the unfermented control, with the highest relative abundance observed in the RF group (increasing from 3.38 × 107 to 7.94 × 107). This demonstrates that the mixed fermentation system retained the metabolic advantages of yeast while further enhancing the relative accumulation of specific flavonoid glycosides. Notably, mixed-strain fermentation induced the synthesis of potent bioactive compounds. Avenanthramide L, a recognized antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent (Huang et al., 2025), was significantly upregulated in all fermented samples, reaching its maximum relative abundance in the RF group (3.73 × 108). This enrichment provides molecular evidence supporting the superior ABTS radical scavenging and xanthine oxidase inhibition observed in the RF group. Furthermore, the relative abundance of bergenin in the RF group (2.11 × 108) was 2.5-fold higher than that in the LF group (8.48 × 107), confirming that mixed fermentation (RF) exerted a synergistic effect superior to single-strain fermentation (LF) in enriching certain polyphenols. The production of detoxifying agents such as D-glucuronic acid further augmented the functional value of fermented RRTJ (Ebeed et al., 2024). Finally, the accumulation of organic acids and umami amino acids (e.g., l-glutamine and γ-glutamylserine) modulated the sensory profile, mitigating acidity and enhancing umami characteristics to improve overall palatability (Li et al., 2025). These flavonoid-profile changes may also contribute to the sensory differences detected by the electronic tongue, particularly the reduced bitterness and astringency in the yeast-containing fermentation groups.

KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 8E–F) elucidated the mechanistic basis of metabolite variations, identifying amino acid metabolism as a significantly enriched primary driver of quality enhancement (p = 2.34 × 10−5). The significant enrichment of amino acid metabolism clarifies the molecular basis for sensory optimization; specifically, the accumulation of l-glutamine and γ-glutamylserine indicates that fermentation facilitates the proteolytic conversion of proteins into umami-enhancing peptides and amino acids, thereby enhancing the mouthfeel. Concurrently, although the overall phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway did not reach statistical significance at the global level (p = 0.146), marked enrichment of specific high-impact metabolites within this route likely contributed substantially to the enhanced functional properties of fermented RRTJ. In particular, avenanthramide L was significantly upregulated in all fermented groups and reached its highest relative abundance in the RF group, while bergenin was also markedly enriched in the RF group. Therefore, the improved functional performance of the co-fermentation system is more plausibly attributed to the targeted accumulation of these potent bioactive metabolites, rather than to statistically significant activation of the entire phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. These results suggest that selective remodeling of specific secondary metabolites, together with enriched amino acid metabolism, underlies the distinctive functional and sensory characteristics of fermented RRTJ.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the effects of single-strain (Lactobacillus reuteri and Lachancea fermentati) and mixed-strain fermentation on RRTJ, showing that, under the selected fermentation conditions, the mixed-strain approach exhibited superior overall performance in optimizing bioactivity, flavor, and sensory properties. Unlike single-strain fermentation, the mixed culture exhibited coordinated effects that harmonized bioactivity with sensory acceptance. Mechanistically, these improvements were driven by two key pathways: phenolic biotransformation, which significantly enhanced enzyme inhibitory and antioxidant activities, and metabolic flux regulation, which enriched umami amino acids and esters while mitigating off-notes. These findings suggest that the proposed co-fermentation system effectively remodeled the RRTJ metabolic network, offering a promising technological route for developing functional foods with enhanced health benefits and flavor stability. Consequently, future research should focus on validating the bioavailability and specific health-promoting mechanisms of the co-fermented juice using in vivo models.

However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, although different fermentation temperatures were selected to match strain-specific physiological characteristics, the potential confounding effect of temperature cannot be completely excluded. Future studies should compare monoculture and co-culture fermentations under fully standardized temperature conditions to better distinguish microbial interaction effects from temperature effects. Second, the functional activities were evaluated only using standard in vitro models, which may not fully reflect in vivo physiological responses. After ingestion, bioactive compounds may undergo degradation, transformation, or reduced accessibility due to gastrointestinal pH fluctuations, digestion, and hepatic first-pass metabolism. Therefore, future studies should further assess the stability, bioaccessibility, bioavailability, and in vivo health effects of the co-fermented juice. In addition, although metabolomic analysis revealed significant metabolic shifts, the causal mechanisms underlying microbial interactions and metabolite production remain to be clarified. Finally, this study was not specifically designed to statistically verify synergy relative to the calculated additive or mean effects of the monoculture groups. Thus, the current findings support the improved performance of the co-fermentation system under the tested conditions, while formal synergy assessment requires a dedicated experimental design and statistical modeling.
