Predicting zero-dose vaccination status in 27 sub-Saharan African countries: a machine learning approach
Authors: Berhanu Fikadie Endehabtu, Eliyas Addisu Taye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop a machine learning (ML)-based predictive model for assessing the risk of zero-dose children using Demographic Health Survey data in sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsThis study analysed pooled Demographic and Health Survey data from 27 countries in sub-Saharan Africa collected between 2016 and 2024. Data preprocessing included imputation, balancing of unequal classes and systematic feature selection. Seven ML models were trained and evaluated using performance metrics such as accuracy, recall and F1-score. Feature importance was interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-based analysis.ResultsAmong the seven models evaluated, LightGBM demonstrated the best overall performance, achieving the highest accuracy (80%), sensitivity (91%), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC = 0.78). SHAP analysis identified place of delivery, antenatal care attendance, maternal tetanus vaccination, household wealth and maternal education as top predictors.DiscussionOur findings suggest that machine learning, particularly LightGBM and XGBoost, is moderately effective in predicting the risk of zero-dose vaccination among children. This approach facilitates the easy identification of children most at risk of missing life-saving vaccinations.ConclusionThe LightGBM model predict children at zero-dose risk, enabling early identification and supporting intervention strategies. Future studies will scale these models using larger, routine datasets for practical implementation.

Introduction

Childhood immunisation remains one of the most cost-effective and life-saving public health interventions globally, with the potential to prevent 4 million deaths each year from vaccine-preventable disease.1 2 In 2025, more than 13.5 million children under one year of age missed all routine vaccinations, classifying them as “zero-dose” children. This figure represents an increase of approximately 2.7 million compared with 2019, prior to the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic.3 However, progress has stagnated in recent years, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, with sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bearing a disproportionate share of the burden. In 2022, SSA accounted for over 16% of the world’s zero-dose children.4 One in five children globally do not have access to life-saving vaccines.1 These children often reside in hard-to-reach rural areas, conflict zones or urban slums, where systemic inequities such as poverty, maternal illiteracy and weak health infrastructure intersect to limit access to immunisation services.5–9 The implications of zero-dose status are profound, as it sustains preventable child mortality, increases the likelihood of vaccine-preventable disease outbreaks such as measles and polio and places considerable strain on already overburdened and resource-constrained health systems.

In 2025, nearly 20 million children worldwide were either unvaccinated or under-vaccinated, with approximately 53% residing in 10 countries, including four in SSA: Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Angola, and Ethiopia. This highlights the region’s disproportionate share of the global immunization gap.3 The high prevalence of zero-dose children in SSA reflects systemic gaps in health service coverage and exacerbates vulnerability to outbreaks of preventable diseases.10 The COVID-19 pandemic severely disrupted routine childhood immunisation services globally, leading to a resurgence in zero-dose children and reversing decades of progress, an alarming development that endangers the achievement of Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3 (good health and well-being) and the Immunization Agenda 2030, which aims to ensure equitable vaccine access and prevent over 50 million deaths through immunisation between 2021 and 2030.11–15

In response to the setbacks caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, several studies have been conducted in SSA to investigate the underlying factors contributing to missed vaccinations and the prevalence of zero-dose children. These studies have identified a range of key determinants associated with zero-dose status in the region, including maternal education, household wealth, distance to health facilities, media exposure, antenatal care (ANC) attendance, place of delivery and maternal autonomy. For example, children whose mothers did not attend any ANC visits or gave birth at home are significantly more likely to remain unvaccinated. Furthermore, socio-demographic disparities including residence in rural areas and low maternal literacy have been consistently linked to immunisation gaps.4 16–24 Despite the availability of rich data from sources like the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), these conventional approaches may fall short in capturing the complex and multidimensional interactions among the contributing factors.25 26 In contrast, machine learning (ML) algorithms offer promising alternatives by accommodating non-linear relationships, managing high-dimensional datasets and enhancing predictive performance. With fewer underlying assumptions than traditional methods, ML can capture complex patterns and be integrated into digital health systems to support decision-making and reduce the research-to-practice gap.27–32 Therefore, this study employs ML algorithms to predict zero-dose status among children aged 12–23 months using pooled DHS data from selected SSA countries. It aims to identify key determinants of zero-dose outcomes, providing insights to guide targeted interventions, reduce immunisation inequities and improve child health outcomes in the region.

Methodology

Study design and study period

This study used data from the DHS conducted across 27 SSA countries between 2016 and 2024 that had been collected through a cross-sectional survey design. The DHS datasets are nationally representative and provide comprehensive information on health, fertility and demographic characteristics, collected through surveys designed to capture a broad spectrum of reproductive health issues. The data used in this analysis are publicly available and can be accessed from the DHS programme’s official website at https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm. This study was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guideline for prediction model development.33

Study area

Sub-Saharan Africa encompasses four major regions: Central Africa, East Africa, Southern Africa and West Africa.34 According to The World Academy of Sciences, the region consists of 50 countries35 and it has experienced rapid demographic changes over the decades. The population in SSA grew from 186 million in 1950 to 856 million in 2010.36 In 2023, the total population of SSA amounted to approximately 1.26 billion inhabitants. For this study, the DHS datasets were drawn from the following countries, all of which contain recent data from the years 2016 to 2024: Angola (AO7), Benin (BJ7), Burkina Faso (BF8), Burundi (BU7), Cameroon (CM7), Côte d’Ivoire (CI8), Ethiopia (ET7), Gabon (GA7), Ghana (GH8), Gambia (GM7), Guinea (GN7), Kenya (KE8), Liberia (LB7), Lesotho (LS8), Madagascar (MD7), Malawi (MW7), Mali (ML7), Mauritania (MR7), Mozambique (MZ8), Nigeria (NG7), Rwanda (RW7), Senegal (SN8), Sierra Leone (SL7), South Africa (ZA7), Tanzania (TZ8), Uganda (UG7) and Zambia (ZM7).37

Source and study population

The source population comprised all children aged 12–23 months residing in SSA. The study population included children in this age group captured in the Birth Recode (BR) datasets of the DHS conducted between 2016 and 2024 across 27 selected SSA countries who were living with their mothers at the time of the survey. Children with missing outcome data on vaccination status and those whose mothers had incomplete records on key predictor variables were excluded from the analysis. In total, 18 959 children were included in the final analysis.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The study focused on whether children aged 12–23 months had received any vaccination. This was treated as a yes/no outcome: children were classified as vaccinated if there was any evidence (health card, mark or caregiver report) and as zero-dose if no vaccination was recorded or reported.38–43

Independent variable

A detailed literature review was conducted to identify factors associated with zero-dose vaccination status among children aged 12–23 months. Based on previous studies, a wide range of variables were included, covering sociodemographic, economic, maternal health and child-related characteristics. These included parental education, household wealth, access to healthcare services and media exposure. These variables were then used for feature selection and to develop ML models to predict zero-dose status.4 16–24 Since the outcome variable assessed whether a child was zero-dose or not, the model was designed for postbirth application, using maternal and healthcare-related factors available by childbirth or during the early postnatal period (eg, ANC attendance, place of delivery and tetanus vaccination) to support early identification of children at high risk of remaining zero-dose and facilitate targeted immunisation follow-up.

Data source and study context

This study used data from the BR files of the DHS conducted between 2016 and 2024 across 27 SSA countries. The BR file, derived from the Woman’s Questionnaire, contains detailed child-level information, including vaccination history, maternal health and household characteristics, making it well-suited for analysing zero-dose status among children aged 12–23 months.44 DHS employs a standardised, stratified two-stage cluster sampling design, with validated instruments translated into local languages. All surveys are pretested and harmonised to support reliable cross-country comparisons and high-quality secondary analysis.20 21

Data preprocessing

In the DHS, sampling coverage varies across regions due to the multistage stratified cluster sampling design. To ensure representative estimates and account for unequal probabilities of selection, we incorporated the survey design variables V005, V021 and V022, which represent sampling weights, primary sampling units (clusters) and stratification variables, respectively. Following the standard DHS recommendation, the sampling weight variable (V005) was normalised by dividing by 1 000 000 and incorporated during model training/fitting as sample weights to improve representativeness and account for differential selection probability across observations.45 Missing data were minimal across all variables, ranging from 3.9% (getting permission to go to health facility, distance to health facility and not wanting to go alone to health facility) to 5.5% (ANC visit), and were handled using k-Nearest Neighbours imputation with k=5, which preserves multivariate relationships among variables and is well-suited for health survey data with correlated predictors. Categorical variables were transformed using one-hot encoding, while numerical features were standardised using Z-score normalisation. To prevent information leakage, all preprocessing steps, including imputation, encoding and scaling, were performed strictly within the training data after the initial 80/20 train-test split. Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) with a Random Forest estimator was then applied on the training set to reduce dimensionality, reducing the original 27 features to 22 retained predictors, resulting in the exclusion of five low-importance variables: age at first birth, smoking status, twin birth, delivery by caesarean section and wanted last child (figure 1). The χ2 tests conducted on the training data confirmed that all retained variables were statistically significant, supporting model stability. Finally, to address class imbalance (approximately 3:1 between vaccinated and zero-dose children), Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied only on the training set to generate synthetic minority samples, achieving a balanced 1:1 class distribution (figure 2).

Model development and assessment

Seven classification algorithms were selected to ensure a balance between predictive performance, computational efficiency and interpretability: logistic regression, decision tree classifier, random forest classifier, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LGBM), naïve Bayes classifier and adaptive boosting (AdaBoost). These models were chosen due to their demonstrated effectiveness in handling structured, imbalanced datasets common in public health and demographic studies. Logistic regression served as a baseline for its simplicity and interpretability.46–57

Hyperparameter tuning

To maximise predictive performance, hyperparameter optimisation was conducted for XGBoost, LGBM and random forest classifiers, as these ensemble models are highly sensitive to parameter settings. We employed a randomised grid search strategy on the training partition (80%) of the dataset while preserving the test partition (20%) for unbiased evaluation. For XGBoost and LGBM, the search space included learning rate (η), maximum tree depth, number of estimators, subsample ratio, column subsampling ratio and L1/L2 regularisation terms (alpha, lambda). For random forest, the number of trees, maximum depth and maximum features per split were tuned. The optimisation objective prioritised F1-score and recall to balance precision with sensitivity in detecting zero-dose children under class imbalance.

Model evaluation metrics

We evaluated model performance using standard classification metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), F1-score and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve with 95% CI to assess discrimination. In addition, we computed the Brier score to measure probabilistic accuracy and the expected calibration error (ECE) to assess calibration. Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of both discriminative performance and probability calibration.

Model interpretation and explanation

This study evaluated the relationship between predictive features and outcomes using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a game theory-based method that quantifies the contribution of individual features to ML model predictions. SHAP was selected for its ability to deliver transparent, interpretable explanations of feature influence, a critical requirement in healthcare contexts where understanding model decisions is paramount. To assess interpretability, two key SHAP-derived metrics were analysed: (1) the mean absolute SHAP value, representing the average intensity of a feature’s predictive influence across all cases and (2) the mean SHAP value, which reflects the overall directional effect (positive or negative) of features on model predictions. These metrics were computed across 1000 bootstrap iterations to enhance statistical reliability, with features subsequently ranked by their mean absolute SHAP values to distinguish the most influential predictors.58–60

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

The prevalence of zero-dose vaccination was 21.7%, indicating a significant public health concern. Most participants lived in rural areas (68.2%), with 31.8% from urban settings. In terms of education, 43.9% of mothers had no formal schooling, 29.3% had primary education, 23.1% had secondary education and only 3.8% attained higher education. Over half (56.4%) were currently employed, and 94.0% reported that their last child was wanted at the time of conception. Most respondents were married (84.9%), with 11.9% single and 3.2% widowed or divorced. Media exposure was moderate, reported by 55.7% of participants. Regarding birth order, 35.4% of children were second-born or third-born, while 21.5% were first-born. Household wealth distribution was 30.1% poorest, 22.3% poorer, 19.0% middle, 15.5% richer, and 13.1% richest. Parity among mothers varied, with 39.6% having one to two children, 39.0% having three to five children and 21.4% having more than five children (online supplemental table 1).

Machine learning results

After balancing the training data using SMOTE, the gradient boosting models, particularly LGBM and XGBoost, demonstrated superior predictive performance in identifying zero-dose children compared with other ML algorithms. LGBM achieved the best overall performance, with an accuracy of 79.9% (95% CI 78.6 - 81.2), precision of 84.6% (95% CI 83.3 - 85.8), recall of 90.9% (95% CI 89.8 - 91.9), area under the curve (AUC) of 77.5% (95% CI 75.6 - 79.2) and an F1-score of 87.6% (95% CI 86.8 - 88.5). XGBoost showed comparable performance, achieving 79.0% accuracy (95% CI 77.6 - 80.4), 83.5% precision (95% CI 82.3 - 84.9), 91.0% recall (95% CI 90.0 - 92.1), 77.0% AUC (95% CI 75.2 - 76.9) and an F1-score of 87.1% (95% CI 86.2 - 88.1). Both models also demonstrated strong probability calibration, with LGBM achieving a Brier score of 0.12 and ECE of 0.03, and XGBoost a Brier score of 0.13 and ECE of 0.04, indicating well-calibrated predicted probabilities with minimal deviation between predicted and observed outcomes. Among conventional ML models, logistic regression and naïve Bayes demonstrated high precision, exceeding 89%, indicating strong performance in minimising false-positive classifications, although their recall and overall discrimination ability were comparatively lower. Random forest and AdaBoost achieved moderate-to-high predictive performance, whereas the decision tree showed relatively lower discriminative ability, with the lowest AUC (60.7%), despite maintaining balanced precision and recall. Overall, LGBM and XGBoost consistently outperformed the other models across key performance indicators, particularly in recall, F1-score and probability calibration, reflecting a strong ability to correctly identify zero-dose children (online supplemental table 2 and online supplemental figure 1).

Factor identification

Figure 3 presents the mean absolute SHAP values derived from the LGBM model, ranking predictors according to their overall contribution to childhood immunisation outcomes. The mean absolute SHAP value quantifies the average magnitude of a feature’s contribution to the model’s predicted log odds of a child being classified as zero dose, irrespective of the direction of effect (positive or negative). Specifically, place of delivery had the highest mean absolute SHAP value (0.54), indicating that, across all predictions, this feature altered the model output by an average of 0.54 units on the log odds scale, thereby emerging as the most influential predictor of zero-dose vaccination status. This was followed by ANC visits (0.36), tetanus toxoid vaccination (0.28) and postnatal care (PNC) visits (0.23). Socioeconomic characteristics, including wealth index (0.21), maternal education (0.18) and husband’s education (0.15), as well as healthcare accessibility barriers such as distance to health facilities (0.13) and difficulty obtaining permission to seek care (0.10), also demonstrated meaningful contributions to model predictions. Overall, higher mean absolute SHAP values indicate greater influence on the model’s classification of zero-dose vaccination status (figure 3).

SHAP beeswarm plot

The SHAP beeswarm plot, complementing the global feature importance ranking by illustrating both the direction and distribution of individual feature effects on model predictions. Each point represents a single observation, with colour gradients indicating feature magnitude (red=high values; blue=low values), while the x-axis represents SHAP values, where positive values increase the predicted likelihood of zero-dose classification and negative values decrease it. Notably, children were more likely to be classified as zero dose if they were born at home, had no ANC or PNC visits, did not receive tetanus toxoid vaccination or belonged to households characterised by lower maternal education, lower wealth status, rural residence and limited media exposure, as reflected by predominantly positive SHAP values. In contrast, protective factors such as facility-based delivery, ANC attendance, tetanus toxoid vaccination and higher educational attainment generated negative SHAP values, reducing the predicted probability of zero-dose status. The dispersion of SHAP values across observations further indicates heterogeneity in predictor effects, with place of delivery and ANC visits demonstrating consistently strong contributions across high-risk population subgroups (figure 4).

Discussion

This study used ML algorithms to predict zero-dose vaccination status among children aged 12–23 months in SSA, using pooled DHS data from 27 countries. LGBM and XGBoost were the top-performing models, achieving an F1-score of 88% and demonstrating robust recall (91%). The findings of this study are consistent with previous research conducted in Ethiopia and other low- and middle-income countries, which have consistently reported that gradient boosting ensemble methods outperform other ML algorithms in predicting vaccination outcomes among children.61 62 However, the LGBM and XGBoost results observed in this study were lower than those reported in a study conducted in Ethiopia, where LGBM achieved an AUC of 97.4%, accuracy of 92.4% and F1-score of 92.1%.61 Similarly, a study from Tanzania reported higher performance, where random forest achieved an accuracy of 95%, recall of 96% and AUC of 99%.63 An Ethiopian ensemble study also exceeded the present findings, reporting a voting combination of Bagging meta-estimator and XGBoost with an accuracy of 95.94% and AUC of 96%.64 In contrast, the present study performed better than a pooled analysis across 16 SSA countries, where categorical boosting, the best-performing model, achieved only 65% accuracy and 70% AUC.65 These differences are largely explained by variations in sample size, predictor richness, class imbalance severity and whether single-country or multi-country data were used.

This study found that zero-dose vaccination status was significantly associated with place of delivery, ANC utilisation, tetanus toxoid vaccination, PNC utilisation, wealth index, and maternal education. These findings highlight the interplay between healthcare access, maternal health behaviours and socioeconomic inequities in driving immunisation disparities.

The results align with prior studies identifying structural determinants such as maternal education, household wealth and healthcare access as critical barriers to immunisation.66–69 For instance, the strong association between home deliveries and zero-dose status corroborates evidence linking institutional deliveries to improved health service utilisation.70–74 Similarly, the role of ANC and PNC visits echoes findings that maternal engagement with prenatal and postnatal services enhances vaccination uptake.75–78 This study advances existing literature by quantifying these relationships through SHAP analysis, capturing non-linear interactions and prioritising predictors.

The paper focuses on modelling predictors of immunisation to shed light on a significant public health challenge in SSA, where 16% of global zero-dose children reside.4 By employing ML algorithms, the study provides a data-driven framework to identify high-risk populations and tailor interventions. The high predictive performance of ensemble models like LGBM and XGBoost demonstrates their utility in resource-constrained settings, offering actionable insights to accelerate progress towards Immunization Agenda 2030 and SDG 3.11–15 The study revealed that maternal literacy and media exposure had lower predictive importance compared with healthcare access factors (place of delivery, ANC visits). This suggests that structural barriers to service delivery outweigh individual-level knowledge gaps in SSA.

The study identifies place of delivery and ANC visits as the strongest predictors of zero-dose status, underscoring the critical role of institutional healthcare access in immunisation equity. To address these gaps, policymakers should prioritise strategies that incentivise facility-based deliveries, such as conditional cash transfers or community-based maternal health programmes, which have proven effective in improving vaccination uptake in low-resource settings.79–81 Furthermore, integrating immunisation services into ANC/PNC visits presents a valuable opportunity to optimise existing maternal care pathways. This approach has been effectively demonstrated in several studies, highlighting its potential to enhance maternal and child health outcomes.82 83 Strengthening partnerships between ministries of health and local NGOs to expand mobile vaccination units in rural regions could further mitigate geographic barriers, aligning with the Immunization Agenda 2030’s emphasis on equity.

This study contributes methodologically by highlighting the potential utility of ML approaches in handling high-dimensional and imbalanced datasets and identifying complex patterns in childhood immunisation data. The integration of SHAP values enhances interpretability, bridging the gap between predictive analytics and actionable public health insights. Empirically, it provides a ranked hierarchy of determinants, prioritising place of delivery and ANC visits as intervention levers, thereby refining global immunisation strategies.58–60 By integratin ML and public health methodologies, this study provides a roadmap for reducing immunisation inequities in SSA. Findings showed the need for multisectoral interventions that address both healthcare access and socioeconomic determinants, ensuring no child is left behind in the global vaccination effort.

Strengths and limitations

This study exhibits strong methodological rigour by employing advanced ML algorithms such as XGBoost and LGBM, along with SHAP analysis to enhance model interpretability. The use of SMOTE to address class imbalance and Recursive Feature Elimination for feature selection further strengthens the model’s predictive performance and internal validity. By using pooled DHS data from 27 SSA countries, the study ensures broad geographic and sociodemographic representation. Notably, the identification of variables such as place of delivery and ANC visits as key predictors provides meaningful insights that can inform targeted and equitable immunisation policies.

Despite its strengths, the study has limitations. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between predictors and zero-dose vaccination outcomes. Additionally, reliance on self-reported vaccination status may be affected by recall bias or incomplete documentation. The study does not incorporate broader contextual influences such as vaccine hesitancy, political instability or health system disruptions, which may play a critical role in immunisation coverage. Moreover, the analysis is conducted at a pooled regional level and does not explore country-specific differences. Future research should include disaggregated analyses to identify country-specific drivers and support tailored interventions, validate models using external datasets across diverse SSA contexts, incorporate cultural variables into ML model development and assess the cost-effectiveness of localised strategies.

Conclusion

This study employed ML to predict zero-dose childhood vaccination status in SSA and identify key determinants driving disparities, achieving robust performance (F1-score: 88%) with LGBM and XGBoost models. Structural barriers, particularly place of delivery, ANC attendance and socioeconomic inequities emerged as dominant predictors, outweighing individual-level factors. By prioritising facility-based deliveries, maternal care integration and mobile health units, the findings directly inform interventions to advance immunisation equity under the Immunization Agenda 2030 and SDG 3. Bridging ML with public health practice, this work underscores the transformative potential of data-driven, context-sensitive strategies to reduce zero-dose prevalence.

Recommendation

Given the key determinants of incomplete vaccination identified by the model, low maternal education, limited use of maternal health services, low household wealth, geographic inaccessibility and cultural hesitancy, we recommend the following model-driven interventions. As the model is specifically designed for postbirth application, with place of delivery, ANC visits, PNC visits and tetanus vaccination status already known, the LGBM risk scores can be immediately used at the PNC entry point to identify high-risk newborns before they miss their first vaccine dose. These risk scores should be integrated into postnatal care registration platforms to trigger community health worker home visits for mother-child pairs identified as high-risk. Children flagged by the model for absent ANC and PNC utilisation should receive automated, personalised SMS or interactive voice response reminders in local languages at key vaccination milestones. Risk scores stratified by wealth index and distance to health facility can be incorporated into geospatial dashboards to dynamically route mobile vaccination teams to the highest-burden catchment areas. When permission-seeking is identified as dominant risk factors, it is essential to target specific households for male engagement outreach.
