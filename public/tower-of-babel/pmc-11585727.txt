Paediatric rhinolith: Case report and literature review
Authors: Alya AlZabin, Abdulrahman M. Aloufi, Rayan Alfallaj, Yazeed AlSuliman

Introduction

A rhinolith is an uncommon calcareous concretion that develops within the nasal cavity due to a mineralized mass from an external or internal source. It typically occurs when a foreign object is left behind in the nasal cavity. The clinical presentation of rhinoliths is dictated by type, size, and duration. Patients with rhinoliths may exhibit symptoms such as foul-smelling nasal or postnasal discharge; nasal blockage (often unilateral); halitosis; epistaxis; headaches; maxillary sinus medial wall erosion; deviation or perforation of the septum; and, in severe cases, palatal perforation [1]. Each individual rhinolith report provides insight into the development of the disease. Given the rarity of this medical condition among the paediatric age group, we are sharing our clinical observation and effective treatment of a unilateral rhinolith in a 2-year-old boy. We aim to contribute to the existing literature on the approach and management of such cases.

Presentation of case

A 2-year-old medically free boy accompanied by his parents presented to the emergency department with complaints of nasal obstruction with a foul-smelling, pure greenish nasal discharge from the left nasal cavity for a couple of days. The mother suspected that the child had a foreign body in his nose. The parents reported that their child has a history of recurrent nasal foreign-body insertion. Otherwise, there was no history of trauma, facial pain, or headache, and further review of the systems was unremarkable. The child was examined after obtaining consent from his parents. Upon general examination, the patient was alert and oriented, and vital parameters were stable. On anterior rhinoscopy, the left nasal cavity revealed a hard stony object, whereas the right nasal cavity was clear. The left side nasal cavity showed a diffusely inflamed septum and anterior head of the inferior turbinate. Oropharynx appearance was normal. There was no paranasal sinus tenderness. Endoscopic examination under conscious sedation after decongestion revealed a grey left-sided anterior rhinolith on the anterior head of the inferior turbinate reaching superiorly as well as discoloration of the anterior nasal septum and lateral nasal cavity. There was no necrosis or eschar. The nasopharynx and the right nasal cavity were clear. Rhinolithiasis was clinically diagnosed. The rhinolith was removed piecemeal initially with the aid of anterior rhinoscopy using Tilley nasal forceps (Fig. 1). After the removal, an endoscopic examination using a 2 mm zero-degree rigid scope was performed under conscious sedation, which revealed a left-sided oedematous nasal mucosa of the lateral wall and septum in the anterior part, with a clear transition point between the inflammatory mucosa and the normal mucosa posteriorly (Fig. 2). The patient was discharged home with a saline nasal spray, xylometazoline nasal spray two times a day for three days, and fusidic acid ointment bilaterally twice per day for one week. Upon follow-up five days later, his symptoms of nasal obstruction and foul odour had resolved, and endoscopic examination revealed a healthy nasal mucosa with no signs of recurrence or inflammation.

Discussion

Rhinolithiasis is an uncommon entity with a reported incidence of approximately 1 in 10,000 [2]. The first well-documented case of rhinolithiasis was reported by Bartholin in 1654 [3]. However, a possible description of a rhinolith was identified by Mathias di Gardi in 1502. Subsequently, >600 examples of rhinoliths were reported up to 1988 [1]. The progressive development and insidious nature of rhinoliths result in the gradual emergence of symptoms. The most common clinical characteristics are nasal obstruction and foul-smelling nasal discharge. Rhinolithiasis may also exhibit cacosmia, epistaxis, headache, facial pain, and epiphora [[4], [5], [6]]. Various complications resulting from rhinoliths have been documented, such as nasal mucosa destruction, palatal perforation, recurring sinusitis, ongoing middle ear inflammation, maxillary sinus bone destruction, fistula formation, and septal deviation and perforation [1].

Rhinolithiasis is typically diagnosed in individuals between the ages of 8 and 25, with a higher occurrence in females [7]. The right nasal cavity is frequently observed to be affected, likely because of the prevalence of right-handed individuals [8]. Rhinoliths are typically discovered unilaterally and are normally located on the floor, approximately halfway between the anterior and posterior parts of the nasal cavity [9]. The management of rhinolithiasis involves excision of the nasal stone using a rigid endoscope.

The procedure is conducted with the administration of local anaesthesia. In certain circumstances, as in children and noncooperative patients, general anaesthesia is used along with orotracheal intubation to safeguard the upper airways from the potential hazard of rhinolithiasis [10]. A recent comprehensive systematic review examined the presentation, management, and outcomes of patients with rhinolithiasis. The study found that all rhinoliths were completely removed using endoscopic sinonasal surgery and septoplasty [11]. Complications that may occur after stone removal are infrequent and usually confined to bleeding, ipsilateral sinusitis, and localized infection [1].

A literature review was conducted (Table 1). We found that the cases mainly occurred in boys between 11 and 12 years old. The main complaints were nasal obstruction and a foul-smelling nasal discharge. Our case is a unique presentation of rhinolithiasis in a 2-year-old boy who presented with suspicion from his mother of foreign body insertion because of the foul-smelling greenish discharge. Endoscopic examination played a crucial role in the diagnosis in our case, because radiological imaging couldn't provide definitive confirmation. In the study conducted by Hadi et al. [13], direct imaging was performed on two of the eight patients included in the study. However, rhinoliths were not detected, and in these cases, computed tomography (CT) was recommended, because it provides a clearer image of these masses.

The significance of endoscopic evaluation is heightened, considering that our patient belongs to the paediatric age group. Furthermore, although it is undeniably accurate that CT scans offer enhanced clarity and precise images, the fact that our patient falls within the paediatric age range, making them more susceptible to radiation, coupled with the expensive and time-consuming nature of this procedure, presents notable drawbacks. Conversely, endoscopic examination is a straightforward and rapid procedure that can also be utilized for therapy purposes, and most importantly, it is completely safe.

Conclusion

This study presented a rare case of unilateral rhinolith in a 2-year-old boy. Early and accurate diagnosis is essential for proceeding with the appropriate management. Proper endoscopic examination and accurate history taking are crucial. This study provides insight into the need for a more standardized approach to paediatric rhinolithiasis in the current literature.
