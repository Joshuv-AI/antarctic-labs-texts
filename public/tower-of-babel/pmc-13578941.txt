Gating crosstalk in potassium channels
Authors: Lyubin Hu, Bert L. de Groot, Ruo-Xu Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificancePotassium channels regulate electrical signaling by transporting K+ across cell membranes. This activity is controlled by multiple gates, yet how these gates interact remains not entirely understood. We identify a conserved gating crosstalk mechanism linking the activation gate to the width of the selectivity filter entrance, which regulates ion permeation rate by tuning ion occupancy at the selectivity filter. This model illustrates the functional implications of the state-dependent differences in filter entrance size revealed by experimental structures from the Protein Data Bank. Our work thus constructs a direct link between simulations and experiments and provides a comprehensive view of how multiple gates cooperate to control potassium channel function.

Potassium channels mediate the selective transport of K+ across cell membranes. They form a large protein family comprising various subtypes with broad tissue distributions (1, 2). These channels help set the resting membrane potential and mediate action potential repolarization in neurons and muscle cells. Their dysfunction has been linked to a broad range of diseases such as epilepsy and long-QT syndrome (1, 2). Extensive studies have therefore been devoted to elucidating their functions and structures. Potassium channels form tetramers or pseudotetramers and, depending on the subtype, may contain cytoplasmic, voltage-sensing, and pore domains (3). The pore domain (Fig. 1A) is highly conserved in structure across the channel family and forms the ion permeation pathway through the bilayer. This permeation pathway consists of two functionally distinct regions (Fig. 1A): the selectivity filter, which confers more than 100 fold permeability selectivity for K+ over Na+ (4), and the central cavity, which is responsible for channel gating. The selectivity filter contains four ion binding sites that selectively accommodate K+ (Fig. 1A), thereby enabling K+ permeation while excluding other ions. The central cavity, located beneath the selectivity filter, is lined by the inner transmembrane helices. Conformational changes in these helices modulate the hydration level and pore radius of the central cavity (Fig. 1A), thereby reshaping the free energy landscape for ion permeation (3).

Because the central cavity is critical for ion permeation, it has long been viewed as the primary gate of the channel, also known as the activation gate. However, this view has been challenged by earlier work of VanDongen et al., which suggests that the selectivity filter works together with the activation gate to regulate ion permeation (5). This idea has been supported by a variety of studies showing that prolonged opening of the activation gate can alter the conformation of the selectivity filter, rendering it nonconductive to K+, a process known as C-type inactivation (6–12). The selectivity filter can therefore act as an inactivation gate. However, the structural basis of allosteric coupling between these two gating elements remains incompletely understood. Some studies have proposed significant structural deformations of the selectivity filter during C-type inactivation, including constriction or dilation at different K+ binding sites (9, 13–17), whereas others have reported flips of the carbonyl oxygens that coordinate K+ (18–22) (Fig. 1B). It has also been suggested that the conductive and inactivated selectivity filter may adopt almost identical structures, with only minor differences in the size of some K+ coordination sites (23–27) (Fig. 1B). Nevertheless, whether such small structural changes are sufficient to alter ion permeation is not clear. Moreover, the pathway of allosteric coupling (10, 15–17, 28–30) remains controversial, despite evidence highlighting key residues located in the vicinity of the selectivity filter and the pore helix (Fig. 1A).

In this work, we propose an allosteric gating mechanism based on molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. We show that subtle changes in the width of the selectivity filter entrance, quantified by the threonine Oγ–Oγ distance (Fig. 1A), can regulate ion permeation rate by modulating ion occupancy at the filter. This regulation occurs without significant deformation of the filter structure (Fig. 1B). We further find that the filter entrance size is coupled to the structure and hydration level of the central cavity, which also influence permeation. This gate coupling is mediated by interactions between a conserved threonine at the filter entrance and a hydrophobic residue from the inner helix (Fig. 1C). Importantly, extensive sampling across multiple channel subtypes, temperatures, [K+], voltages and force fields supports the robustness of our model. Consistent with our simulations, experimental structures in the Protein Data Bank also reveal state-dependent differences in filter entrance size across the channel family, whose functional implications have not been recognized previously. Our results support a gating crosstalk model linking the filter entrance size to the conformation of the central cavity, providing a mechanistic framework for channel function.

Results

To explore how the two gates act together to control ion permeation, we selected six representative channels from different subfamilies (MthK, cBK, TREK-2, KcsA, Kv4.2, and aBK, Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We then sampled their ion permeation rates in different activation states, including the open and closed states, as well as several intermediate states. The open and closed states are primarily defined by the state of the backbone hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) of the inner helix (31, 32). In the open state, the disruption of H-bonds at a conserved glycine (or proline) produces a kink and increases the helical bending angle, whereas in the closed state, these H-bonds reform and the helices become straighter (Figs. 1A and 3A). This structural change often leads to a smaller pore radius and lower hydration of the central cavity in the closed state. In the intermediate states, restraints were applied either to maintain disruption of these H-bonds (mimicking the pattern observed in the open state) or to promote their reformation (mimicking the H-bond pattern in the closed state, see SI Appendix section 2.1.3 and Fig. S11E for methods). Hereafter, we refer to these restrained conformations as the kinked and bent states, respectively. In the bent state, the inner helices exhibited a smaller bending angle than in the kinked state, but were not fully straight, despite the promoted reformation of the backbone H-bonds near the conserved glycine. These restraints allowed us to tune the conformation of the activation gate and test how it influences the selectivity filter and ion permeation.

Central Cavity Hydration and the Width of the Selectivity Filter Entrance are Coupled to Regulate Ion Permeation.

Below, we show that the width of the selectivity filter entrance, quantified by the threonine Oγ–Oγ distance (Fig. 3A), changes in response to the state of the activation gate and, in turn, modulates ion current (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). This conclusion applies to all six examined channels.

We first show that in MthK, gradual closure of the central cavity results in progressive enlargement of the filter entrance size. Specifically, we started from the open state and applied restraints to promote reformation of the backbone H-bonds at the kink, thereby mimicking the H-bond pattern observed in the closed conformation (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, section 2.1.3). The restraints were applied to 1, 2, 3, or 4 subunits, respectively, generating four systems, referred to as 1Bent-4Bent according to the number of restrained subunits. These systems show progressively decreased cavity hydration level (quantified by the number of water molecules in the cavity, Fig. 3B), accompanied by increased filter entrance size (Oγ–Oγ distance), which reaches a maximum when two or more subunits are restrained (Fig. 3B). These results indicate a coupling between the central cavity and the entrance of the selectivity filter. Both cavity dehydration and enlargement of the filter entrance size affect ion current. The 1Bent and 2Bent states show larger ion currents compared to the open state (~6.2 pA and ~12.0 pA vs. ~5.4 pA, Fig. 3B) because of the increased Oγ–Oγ distance. However, the 3Bent and 4Bent states show much lower ion currents (~2.4 and ~0.9 pA, Fig. 3B), despite their wide filter entrances, because pronounced cavity dewetting in these states imposes a high energy barrier for ion permeation (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). To further validate the effects of filter entrance size on permeation, we additionally restrained the Oγ–Oγ distance to a series of values while keeping the cavity fully hydrated. These restraints substantially modulated ion currents, confirming that filter entrance size directly affects ion permeation (Fig. 3C). In conclusion, the cavity hydration level and the width of the filter entrance are coupled in MthK, and both structural elements modulate ion current.

We next examined the cavity-selectivity filter coupling in cBK (Fig. 3). In this system, both the open and closed states retain a substantial cavity hydration level and therefore show no energy barrier for ion entry into the cavity (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). The fully hydrated cavity in the closed state is due to parts of the inner helices not being resolved in experimental structures and truncated constructs are used in simulations. Nevertheless, these two states exhibit markedly different ion currents (~22.7 vs. ~44.5 pA for open and closed states, Fig. 3B). We thus ascribe this difference to the selectivity filter and hypothesize that the activation gate modulates ion current by altering the width of the filter entrance. Consistent with this interpretation, the two states show distinct Oγ–Oγ distance distributions (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), despite similar averages (Fig. 3B). To further test this hypothesis, we first examined whether the filter entrance size itself regulates ion permeation and observed a negative correlation between the ion current and the Oγ–Oγ distance (Fig. 3C). We then tested whether the activation gate controls the filter entrance size. Starting from the open state, we applied restraints to the backbone H-bonds of the inner helices in all four subunits, either to maintain disruption of these H-bonds (O.Kinked, mimicking the H-bond pattern observed in the open state) or to promote their reformation (O.Bent, mimicking the closed state, Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, section 2.1.3). These simulations reveal substantial differences in the Oγ–Oγ distance and the ion permeation rate, with the O.Bent state showing a smaller distance and a larger current than the O.Kinked state (~0.569 vs. ~0.596 nm, ~39.8 vs. ~14.3 pA, Fig. 3B), thus supporting gate coupling. Finally, we decoupled the two gates by additionally restraining the Oγ–Oγ distance. In the O.Kinked state, the Oγ–Oγ distance was restrained to the value observed in the O.Bent state (O.Kinked.Res). Conversely, in the O.Bent state, the distance was restrained to the value observed in the O.Kinked state (O.Bent.Res, Fig. 3 A and B and SI Appendix, section 2.1.4). This manipulation reverses the permeation trend, with the O.Bent.Res state showing a smaller current than the O.Kinked.Res state (~16.0 vs. 43.3 pA, Fig. 3B). These results suggest that, when the cavity is fully hydrated, differences in ion permeation can primarily be attributed to the selectivity filter. In summary, the gate coupling mechanism revealed by MthK simulations is also found for cBK.

TREK-2 displays the same overall coupling behavior. Its open and closed (up and down) states show distinct Oγ–Oγ distances (~0.548 vs. 0.585 nm for open and closed, Fig. 3B), consistent with the gate coupling mechanism observed in MthK and cBK. Moreover, both gates modulate ion permeation. Ion current changes as a function of the Oγ–Oγ distance (Fig. 3C); however, at the same Oγ–Oγ distance, the closed state consistently shows lower currents than the open state (Fig. 3C), because the lower cavity hydration level in the closed state imposes a higher energy barrier for ion entry into the cavity (Fig. 3 B and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2E).

The same gate coupling mechanism is also identified for KcsA, Kv4.2, and aBK, as described in SI Appendix, section 1.1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). In addition, the closed-to-open conformational transition in TREK-2 exhibits an increased cavity hydration level coupled to a progressive decrease in Oγ–Oγ distance, providing further support to the proposed gate coupling model (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

A Hydrophobic Residue at the Kink of the Inner Helices Mediates Gate Coupling.

We next examined the structural basis underlying the gate coupling. Because the filter entrance size is defined by the Oγ–Oγ distance between the threonines that are conserved in the six examined channels, we scanned the inner helices for residues that are in direct contact with this threonine. In all six channels, the only identified residues are hydrophobic residues located near the kink (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the contact probabilities between these hydrophobic residues (referred to as Hyd) and threonine residues (referred to as Thr) varied between the open and the closed/bent states in most channels (i.e., MthK, aBK, cBK, and Kv4.2, Fig. 4A). We therefore propose that conformational changes in the inner helices reposition these hydrophobic residues, which in turn alter the filter entrance size by interacting with the threonine side chains.

To test this hypothesis, we measured two distances: the Hyd–Hyd distance between the hydrophobic residues from opposite subunits and the Thr–Thr distance between corresponding threonine residues (Fig. 4B). Both distances were calculated between the centers of mass of the side chains. These two distances show moderate to strong correlations across most of the studied channels (quantified by Pearson’s correlation coefficient, PCC; values closer to 0 indicate weaker correlation; Fig. 4C). Notably, the strength of this correlation (i.e., the value of the PCC) increases with the probability of Hyd–Thr contact (Fig. 4D, see the last paragraph of SI Appendix, section 1.2 for discussion of outliers). For instance, the open states of MthK and aBK exhibit high Hyd–Thr contact probabilities (>90%, Fig. 4 A, C, and D) and relatively strong distance correlations (PCC values ~0.55 to 0.65), whereas in their closed/bent states, repositioning of the hydrophobic residues results in lower contact probabilities (~30 to 70%) and a marginally weaker distance correlation (PCC of ~0.25 to 0.50, Fig. 4 A, C, and D). Similarly, the high contact probabilities (>95%) in both states of TREK-2 are associated with strong distance correlations (PCC of ~0.7), whereas the low contact probabilities (<45%) in all states of cBK are accompanied by much weaker correlations (PCC< 0.3, Fig. 4 A, C, and D). These observations suggest that when Hyd–Thr contacts are frequent, the movement of hydrophobic residues is transmitted to the threonine residues, consistent with our hypothesis that the hydrophobic residues mediate coupling between the cavity and the selectivity filter.

We further validated the above model using restrained simulations. In the closed state of TREK-2, progressively decreasing the Hyd–Hyd distance using restraints reduces the Thr–Thr distance, the Oγ–Oγ distance, and the ion current (Fig. 4E), consistent with the coupling between the movements of the hydrophobic residue and the threonine. Simulations of MthK and aBK display the same behaviors (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In addition, we also examined cBK by replacing the hydrophobic residue Ala317 (Fig. 4A) with glycine in its open state. This mutation abolishes the Hyd–Thr interactions and shortens the Oγ–Oγ distance compared to the wild type (~0.575 vs. 0.611 nm, SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Note that the backbones of the inner helices were restrained in these simulations. Therefore, the observed effects are solely attributed to Hyd–Thr interactions rather than backbone movements. These results provide further support for our model that the hydrophobic residue mediates gate coupling.

Although these results support a common framework of gate coupling, the structural details vary among the examined channels. First, the type of hydrophobic residue differs: In the open state, it is an isoleucine in MthK (Ile84), aBK (Ile301), KcsA (Ile100), and TREK-2 (Ile197), a valine in Kv4.2 (Val395), and an alanine in cBK (Ala317) (Fig. 4A). Also, in some channels, the hydrophobic residue changes between the open and closed states, as in aBK (Ile301/Phe300) and Kv4.2 (Val395/Ile398, Fig. 4A). Most importantly, the direction of cavity-selectivity filter coupling depends on the channel type: Opening of the central cavity reduces the width of the filter entrance in MthK, TREK-2, and aBK, but increases it in KcsA, Kv4.2, and cBK (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). This difference may arise from multiple factors, including the identity of hydrophobic residue (cBK), and the channel specific backbone and side chain arrangements (KcsA and Kv4.2, see SI Appendix, section 1.2). Finally, although the Hyd–Thr interactions play a critical role in gate coupling, they are unlikely to be the sole determinant. For example, in the open state of the wild type cBK, simulations with and without backbone restraints on the inner helices display notably different Oγ–Oγ distances (~0.611 vs. 0.596 nm, SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Fig. 3B), implying possible effects of backbone motions.

Experimentally Derived Structures Reveal State-Dependent Differences in the Filter Entrance Size.

To assess whether our gating crosstalk model is also supported by experimental data, we analyzed 562 experimentally derived structures representing 62 unique channels in the Protein Data Bank. We confirm the expected conservation of the kink and the hydrophobic residue (SI Appendix, section 1.3 and Fig. S6). More importantly, consistent with our model, experimental structures display distinct Hyd–Hyd and Thr–Thr distances between the open and the closed states of the examined channels (MthK, aBK, TREK-2, and Kv4.2) and NaK2K (a channel that shares a similar 3D architecture with MthK, Fig. 5). These observations link activation gating to the size of the filter entrance. These structures also exhibit the same channel-specific direction for cavity-selectivity filter coupling: The open states of MthK, aBK, and TREK-2 have smaller Hyd–Hyd and Thr–Thr distances than the closed states, whereas the opposite is found for Kv4.2 (Fig. 5), in agreement with the trend observed in our MD simulations (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). In addition, the open structures of Kv4.2 display a wide Thr–Thr distance distribution, in line with the broad distribution observed in corresponding simulations (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). These observations from experimentally derived structures, which have not been reported earlier, support our proposed gating crosstalk model and constitute a direct link between our simulation results and experiments.

Mechanism by Which the Selectivity Filter Entrance Size Modulates Ion Permeation.

We propose that the filter entrance size regulates ion permeation by tuning the K+ occupancy at the selectivity filter, particularly at the S4 site. Beyond differences in average values, the Oγ–Oγ distances also exhibit distinct distribution shapes across the examined channels and states (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5), which also influence ion permeation. For example, in cBK, open and closed states display similar average distances (Fig. 3B), but markedly different ion currents because of different distance distributions (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). To explore the underlying mechanism, we analyzed the relationship between Oγ–Oγ distance distributions and the occupancy state of the selectivity filter (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). The Oγ–Oγ distance is strongly coupled to the occupancy state of S4 site, the first ion binding site encountered from the central cavity during permeation: Larger distances favor water occupancy, whereas smaller distances favor K+ occupancy or an empty site (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). In particular, preferred distances of ~0.56 to 0.64, ~0.52 and ~0.54 nm are associated with water-occupied, K+-occupied, and empty S4 sites, respectively (Fig. 6B, SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Consistent with this observation, free energy profiles for K+ translocation through the selectivity filter show that a nonoptimal Oγ–Oγ distance either destabilizes K+ binding at the S4 site (see TREK-2 in SI Appendix, Fig. S7B) or increases the energy barrier for K+ entry into the S4 site (see MthK and cBK in SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Together, these results support a model in which the filter entrance size regulates ion permeation by controlling the occupancy of the S4 site. In particular, a narrow entrance can prevent K+ entry into the S4 site. Once K+ occupies the S4 site, the entrance size may be stabilized at a small width that traps K+ by inhibiting both backward exit to the cavity and knock-on-driven forward translocation. Conversely, an excessively wide entrance allows water to compete with K+, thereby excluding K+ from the S4 site (Fig. 6A). This observation suggests maximal permeation at an optimal filter entrance width, thereby explaining the bell-shaped dependence of ion current on the Oγ–Oγ distance observed in TREK-2 and aBK (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B).

In addition, a larger Oγ–Oγ distance promotes carbonyl flips in the selectivity filter, and long-lived flips inhibit ion permeation. Across all studied channels, most observed flip events occurred at the S3 site (Fig. 7 A and B), consistent with previous simulations of TREK-2(19, 22), TRAAK (20, 21), and TALK-1 (21) and with experimental structure of hERG (18). The flip probability increased dramatically at large Oγ–Oγ distances, particularly at a distance >0.6 nm (Fig. 7C, SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). These carbonyl flips were either transient, reverting back to the conductive conformation within nanoseconds, or long-lived (Fig. 7D). Whereas transient flips allow ion permeation, long-lived flips inhibit ion permeation (Fig. 7D). Nevertheless, the overall effect of carbonyl flips on ion current is minor because the overall flip probability is relatively low (Fig. 7B). This conclusion is supported by the similar currents calculated from entire trajectories (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B) and from trajectories excluding flips for TREK-2, aBK, and cBK (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). We propose that larger Oγ–Oγ distances increase water occupancy at the S3 site, which in turn promotes carbonyl flips (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C and D). In agreement with this hypothesis, Lau et al.’s calculations (18) have revealed that the carbonyl flipped state is more stable when the S3 site is not occupied by K+. Trapping a water molecule at the S3 site promotes carbonyl flips in the open state of cBK, further supporting this idea (SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). However, the flip probability (5%, SI Appendix, Fig. S8D) in this simulation is lower than that observed in the same channel at much larger Oγ–Oγ distances (Fig. 7C), suggesting that water occupancy at the S3 site is unlikely to be the only feature that promotes carbonyl flip. Consistent with this idea, the flip probability is clearly channel dependent, as indicated by two observations: i) BK channels (aBK and cBK, Fig. 7B) show higher overall flip probabilities than other channels; ii) TREK-2, cBK, and aBK exhibit distinct flip probabilities (~5 to 10%, ~5 to 20% and ~10 to 40%, respectively) even at similar Oγ–Oγ distances (>0.60 nm, Fig. 7C).

The Proposed Gating Crosstalk Model Is Robust.

We used 1 M [K+], a voltage of 300 mV and, in particular cases, an elevated temperature (e.g., cBK at 323 K) to accelerate ion permeation and improve statistical sampling within available simulation time (SI Appendix, Table S1). To assess whether our conclusions depend on applied simulation conditions, we performed additional simulations using more physiological conditions (e.g., at 303 K, 0.15 M [K+] and 150 mV) and with an alternative force field (AMBER14SB (38), see SI Appendix section 1.4 and Figs. S9 and S10). Despite expected changes in absolute values of ion current and Oγ–Oγ distance, these simulations reproduced both the central cavity-selectivity filter coupling and the dependence of ion current on selectivity filter entrance size, thereby supporting the robustness of our gating crosstalk model.

Discussion

In this work, we show that the width of the selectivity filter entrance is coupled to the hydration level of the central cavity, and these two features together regulate K+ permeation. This coupling is generally mediated by interactions between a particular hydrophobic residue in the inner helix and the threonine at the filter entrance (Fig. 8). Consistent with our model, experimental structures from the Protein Data Bank reveal state-dependent changes in the filter entrance size (Fig. 5), thereby establishing a direct link between our computational results and experimental observations.

Schematic illustration of the proposed gate coupling model. Two gating elements, namely the width of the filter entrance and the hydration level of the central cavity, are coupled to modulate ion permeation. This coupling is mediated through interactions between the threonine at the filter entrance and the hydrophobic residue at the kink of the inner helix. This model applies to all examined channels, although the exact structural details depend on the channel type.

Our results highlight the impact of subtle changes in the width of the filter entrance on ion permeation. Similar ideas have emerged from previous studies. For instance, simulations of KcsA by Heer et al. (23) have shown that slight enlargement of K+ coordination sites in the selectivity filter (~0.1 nm) in the open state markedly lowers the ion permeation energy barrier. In addition, Rohaim et al.’s study of KcsA E71V mutant (27) has revealed a slightly narrower and more rigid filter in the nonconductive inactivated state compared to the open state. Similarly, crystal (24) and cryo-EM(25) structures of Kv1.2-2.1 have shown largely conserved conformations for the inactivated and conductive selectivity filter, with only minor differences in sizes of certain K+ binding sites (<0.2 nm, Fig. 1B). However, whether these subtle changes in the filter geometry modulate channel function remained unclear. Here, we specifically identify the width of the filter entrance as the key structural determinant (Fig. 8) and further show that this structural feature regulates K+ permeation by modulating ion occupancy at the selectivity filter, particularly at the S4 site (Fig. 6A). In this way, our work extends previous structural observations and provides a more explicit mechanistic connection between the size of K+ coordination sites, ion occupancy, and ion permeation.

Prior studies have identified diverse structural changes associated with C-type inactivation, including constriction or dilation of the selectivity filter [e.g., KcsA (8, 9, 17, 39), Shaker Kv channel (40), Kv1.2 (14, 41), Kv1.3 (42), TREK-1 (13), hERG1 (43–45), TASK-3 (46)] and carbonyl flip at the S3 site [e.g., in TREK-2 (19, 22), hERG (18), TALK1 (21), Fig. 1B]. Our model does not sample these large-scale structural constriction or dilation, which are nonconductive selectivity filter conformations. Instead, we describe coupling between the activation gate and a largely conductive selectivity filter architecture, focusing on local changes in the filter entrance. Despite this distinction, our model may still have relevance to C-type inactivation. First, local changes in the filter entrance are coupled to the activation gate to regulate ion permeation (Fig. 8). Second, enlargement of the filter entrance promotes carbonyl flips at the S3 site (Fig. 7), a structural change that has previously been implicated in C-type inactivation. Together, these observations suggest that our model provides an additional framework for understanding the relationship between selectivity filter structure and ion permeation during channel gating.

The central role of the hydrophobic residue in our gating crosstalk model (Figs. 4 and 8) is consistent with previous findings. For example, NMR studies have identified the corresponding residues in KcsA (I100 and F103) (7, 10, 47) and NaK (I88) (48) as critical for allosteric gating, whereas X-ray structures (12, 49) have implicated the equivalent residue in MthK (I84). Moreover, a mutation of the corresponding residue I470 in the Shaker Kv channel (10) further suggests the importance of the side chain volume: Smaller residues reduce the inactivation rate whereas larger ones accelerate it. In addition, mutation of the threonine at filter entrance to alanine in KcsA, hKv1.5, and Shaker Kv channels (29) results in inverted allosteric coupling, with a collapsed selectivity filter in the closed state and a conductive structure in the inactivated state, underscoring its importance for allosteric gating. These studies have established the functional importance of both the hydrophobic residue and the threonine. These observations are in line with our model, which suggests that interactions between these residues modulate the filter entrance size. Nevertheless, the allosteric pathway linking the inner helix and the selectivity filter is likely to be more complex than captured here, particularly because more significant structural changes in the filter (such as constriction or dilation) are not sampled in this work (10, 13–17, 40, 44–46, 50, 51). Previous studies, including those by Cuello et al. (8–10), have suggested interaction networks involving residues from the pore helix in allosteric coupling. Therefore, systematic sampling of these significant structural changes in the filter may provide further insight into allosteric gating and may represent an important direction for future work.

The cBK simulations highlight an additional limitation arising from the available structural data. Because parts of the inner helices are unresolved, the truncated construct used in our simulations does not physically close the intracellular gate. Consequently, the nominally closed state remains conductive and even carries a larger current than the open state because of its more favorable filter entrance size. Thus, the absolute currents should not be interpreted as those of an intact closed channel. Nevertheless, because the cavity remains substantially hydrated in both states, cBK provides a useful system for isolating the effect of filter entrance size from that of cavity hydration.

The two gating elements (the central cavity and the selectivity filter) studied in this work can be modulated by various factors. For instance, ligand binding behind the selectivity filter stabilizes its conductive conformation in TREK-1 (52, 53), whereas mutations or ligand binding that alter the cavity hydration level reshape the energy barrier for K+ permeation (54–62). Importantly, prior work has also implied that the two gates can be modulated simultaneously. Specifically, Soussia et al. (63) have identified a residue position in the inner helix, located five residues away from the conserved glycine, whose mutation increases the open probability of multiple K2P channels. This effect may arise from disrupting a hydrophobic barrier within the cavity, thus maintaining cavity hydration. Because this residue is located close to the selectivity filter, the primary gate in K2P channels, its effects on selectivity filter gating are difficult to separate from its effects on the cavity hydration. Although this mechanism is distinct from our model, it supports the broader idea that nearby structural elements can simultaneously influence both gates.

Because the identified structural elements (threonine, hydrophobic residue, and the kink) and key interactions (the Hyd–Thr interaction) are largely conserved across the protein family (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), the general framework of our gating crosstalk model may also apply to other channels not examined in this work. Nevertheless, the exact underlying structural details vary substantially across the family members owing to high diversity in their sequences and structures. Such diversity is reflected in multiple aspects. For example, KCa2.2 and Kv11.1 channels contain a serine, rather than a threonine, at the filter entrance, which may alter ion permeation rates (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). In addition, in some channels such as KcsA, a pronounced kink is absent in the open state, resulting in a different direction of gate coupling (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and section 1.2). Moreover, some channels show differences in the detailed mechanism of activation gating. In MthK, the cavity is physically occluded in the closed state (32, 64), whereas in aBK, partial cavity dewetting without complete occlusion is sufficient to generate a substantial energetic barrier to inhibit K+ permeation (56, 57). Lipids may also modulate activation gating by inserting their tails into the cavity (e.g., TREK-2) (34, 65). Continued integration of experimental and computational studies, including the present work, should enable a more complete understanding of how these channels operate.

Conclusions

Potassium channels constitute a large and diverse protein family. Ion permeation through these channels is controlled by the interaction between the central cavity and the selectivity filter. In this work, we conduct MD simulations of multiple K+ channels to study the coupling mechanism between these two gates across the protein family. We find that changes in the width of the selectivity filter entrance, even in the absence of more significant structural deformation, can modulate the ion permeation rate. Maximal permeation is found at an optimal entrance size. Moreover, the filter entrance size is coupled to the structure and hydration level of the central cavity, which also influence ion permeation. We further show that this gate coupling is mediated by interactions between a hydrophobic residue at the kink of the inner helix and the threonine at the filter entrance. Experimental structures from the Protein Data Bank reveal state-dependent differences in filter entrance size, thereby establishing a direct link between experimental observations and our simulations. In summary, our simulations support a model in which activation gate motion coordinates cavity hydration level and the width of selectivity filter entrance to regulate K+ permeation.

Methods

We selected six K+ channels (MthK, Chicken BK, TREK-2, KcsA, Kv4.2, and Aplysia BK) to investigate how the two gates work together to control ion permeation (see Table 1 for the Protein Data Bank entries of the structures used in simulations). We chose these channels for the following reasons: 1) each has multiple experimental structures in different activation states (open, closed, or partially open); 2) they represent at least three different channel subfamilies (Table 1). MthK, aBK, and cBK are from the same subfamily. They are included to represent diversity in a structurally related group. Specifically, MthK lacks the voltage-sensing domain that is present in aBK and cBK (Fig. 2); they also exhibit different activation gating mechanisms, with MthK displaying a clear bundle-crossing structure that physically occludes the central cavity in the closed state (32, 64), whereas in aBK, the cavity remains incompletely occluded and partially hydrated in the closed state (56, 57) (see SI Appendix, Fig. S1 for the pore radii of their open and closed states).

For each channel, we first performed MD simulations to determine ion permeation rates in experimentally derived open, closed, or partially open conformations. We then carried out a series of MD simulations to explore the mechanism by which the interaction between the central cavity and the selectivity filter modulates the ion permeation rate: 1) restrained simulations controlling the conformation of the inner helices in MthK, cBK, and KcsA, which promote opening or closing of the activation gate. These simulations were used to investigate the cavity-selectivity filter coupling; 2) simulations controlling the width of selectivity filter entrance in MthK, cBK, TREK-2, and aBK, to investigate its effects on the ion permeation rate; 3) simulations of F304A/I308A mutant of aBK in the closed state, which allowed cavity rehydration and thus decoupled the effects of two gates on ion permeation; 4) closed-to-open conformational transition in TREK-2, to investigate gate coupling during a protein conformational transition; 5) simulations testing the central role of the hydrophobic residue in gate coupling, including simulations of A317G mutant of cBK and the simulations in which the Hyd–Hyd distances were restrained to a series of values in MthK, TREK-2, and aBK. Detailed protocols and parameters of these simulations are summarized in SI Appendix, sections 2.1.2–2.1.7 and Fig. S11.

The simulation protocol followed our previous work (56). Specifically, the systems were constructed by CHARMM-GUI (69). MD simulations were conducted using the GROMACS 2020.6 package (70), the CHARMM36m force field (71) and the TIP3P water model (72). A constant electrostatic field (E) of ~0.033 or ~0.046 V nm-1 was applied to mimic a transmembrane voltage of ~300 or ~400 mV (V=E×L, L is the length of the z direction of the simulation box). A [K+] of ~1.0 M was used. These simulations are summarized in SI Appendix, Table S1. To test the robustness of the proposed model, selected simulations were repeated using different temperatures, [K+], voltages, and an alternative force field [AMBER14SB force field (38), SI Appendix, Figs. S9 and S10]. Each simulation was conducted for 500 ns and was repeated ~8 to 10 times, giving a total simulation time of ~670 μs. See SI Appendix, section 2.1.1 for the detailed description of the simulation protocol and setup.
