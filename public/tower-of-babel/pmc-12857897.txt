Association of Social Media User with Body Image Shame among University Students in Hail, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Mubashir Zafar, Layan Zaid Alhamashi, Rakan yusef Al-Shwihani, Majed Saad Al-shertan, Mohammed Obaid Aljulaidi, Shumukh Alshubrami, Rahaf Saadi Alazmi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The high frequency of use of social media among students has prompted worries about its impact on attitudes toward body image. Previous research indicates that exposure to idealized body representations on platforms like Instagram and TikTok may lead to a distorted reflection of body image. This study explores the association between social media use and students’ body image shame, aiming to understand how these dynamics affect mental health and well-being.Method:A study was conducted among university students; a total of 504 students were selected through stratified random sampling from different colleges of the university. Participants were recruited through campus-wide announcements and different social media applications. Data were analyzed and used correlation and regression analyses to identify a significant association between social media users and attitude toward body image shame. P value 0.005 considered statistically significant.Results:The results found that a distinguished frequency of body image shame among study participants, with 81.0% experiencing internal shame and 74.8% reporting external shame. Used of social media was significantly associated with body image shame, as participants with high social media usage (>3 hours per day) had increased odds of experiencing internal (Adjusted Odd Ratio (AOR) = 19.48, P = 0.000) and external shame (AOR = 6.12, P = 0.005) compared to those using social media for less than one hour daily.Conclusion:The study results found a positive association between social media habit and body image shame among students. Higher social media engagement correlates with increased internal and external shame, particularly among older and married participants. Awareness programs and interventions are needed to promote healthy social media habits.

Introduction

The advancement of information technology makes the effect of smartphones and social networks on us a permanent subject of study since they are very revealing communication channels.[1] Social networks are a resource for occupation and preservation; they are for people or businesses.[2] Unplanned continuous use of Social Networks in terms of the burden of work and mental load has become one of the consequences of maladapted use of this technology.[2] At the educational level, a less than helpful implication is more levels of tension with 24/7 availability and after-work hours leading to emotional tiredness.[3] The diseases of addiction are one family of effects.[4] Even the stakes of social media engagement range from the relatively casual use of it to the more serious. Additionally, the stakes of engaging on social media range from using it in a relatively casual way to something as consequential and life-threatening.[5] Theory related to social comparison says that humans naturally compare themselves to other humans, which can be particularly harmful in the digital age, where influencers and peers present idealized versions of themselves.[6] University students, being at a critical stage of identity formation, are particularly vulnerable to these effects, as they may internalize societal beauty standards and feel pressured to conform.[7] The pervasive influence of different social media applications on human body image concerns among university students has garnered significant scholarly attention. Empirical studies have always supported the association between social media usage and heightened body dissatisfaction. For instance, a study assessing the association between social media reels and advent-related obsession and body shame found significant positive correlations, indicating that increased focus on appearance-related content is associated with advanced stages of body shame and social physique anxiety.[8]

The mechanisms underlying this relationship are multifaceted. Social media channels are commonly used as social standards, and users could compare their body image with idealized images of other users and influencers. This comparison can lead to an obsession with self-love, where people seek the same image of body as they see in social media, resulting in increased body surveillance and shame.[9] Moreover, the prevalence of negative body talk on these channels of chatting, which have been associated with each other, can lead to elevated body shame, irrespective of gender, suggesting that engagement in appearance-focused discussions exacerbates negative self-perceptions.[10] Interventions aimed at reducing social media usage have shown promise in mitigating these adverse effects. A study published found results that when young people decreased their use of social media by 50% and within 1 month substantial enhancement in attitude toward body image shame.[11] This finding underscores the potential benefits of moderating social media consumption to enhance body image among university students. Furthermore, studies show that social media engagement, especially passive scrolling, increases self-objectification and feelings of inadequacy.[12] This is exacerbated by algorithm-driven content that repeatedly exposes users to unattainable beauty ideals, reinforcing body image shame.[13] However, some research suggests that positive body image content and body positivity movements may mitigate these negative effects.[14]

Understanding the intricate association between social media use and body image shame is imperative for developing effective interventions. University students are at a personal development stage, and their association with social media can overwhelmingly influence their self-confidence and psychological health. By elucidating the pathways over which social media impact body image, stakeholders can devise strategies to promote healthier online behaviors and foster resilience against negative body image perceptions. This study seeks to address this question by revealing the relationship between the use of multiple social media platforms and attitudes toward body image shame. The objective of this study is to determine the association between social media use and body image shame. The data concern a sample of health sciences university students, focusing on a homogeneous subgroup of females and males. These results provide valuable information for future studies examining the double-edged impact of high social media use on body image.

Methodology

Study setting

The College of Health Sciences at the University of Hail is in the city of Hail, Saudi Arabia, the University of Hail has 15,000 students enrolled, in the College of Health Sciences total number of students registered around 1,000.

Study design and study participants

It’s a cross-sectional study, and students from health science colleges were included, who were recruited over stratified cluster sampling. In this study, stratified cluster sampling was used to select participants from five different colleges, which served as clusters. First, the colleges were identified as natural clusters representing distinct groups within the population. Within each selected college, the student population was further divided into strata based on relevant characteristics such as year of study or gender to ensure representation of key subgroups. Participants were then randomly selected from each stratum within the clusters, combining the efficiency of cluster sampling with the precision of stratification to achieve a representative and well-structured sample.

Both male and female students were included in the study. World Health Organization software for sample size was used to calculate sample size, 5% margin of error with 95% Confidence interval and assuming a 39% prevalence of social medial used,[15] 4% bond-on error, and 10% non-response rate, the required sample size is 504.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study are as follows: being a student at the University of Hail in various fields of medicine, dentistry, pharmacy, nursing, and allied health; being able to complete the questionnaires; and being willing to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria included incomplete responses to the questionnaires and a lack of willingness to participate in the study

Data collection process

Permission was taken from the dean of colleges; the questionnaires were circulated through class leaders among students, and a written consent form was taken before starting the data collection from each participant.

Study tools

Study tool used which are validated and structured. It contained four segments, first segment is socio-demographic properties, second segment is scale of social media usage, validated self-report scale of self-media use,[14] scale reliability and validity were verified by previous studies[1516] and Cronbach’s s alpha coefficients was 0.85 and its reliability was 0.90, it consist of 19 questions, each question have 5 point Likert scale from 1 to 5, scale divided into sub section such as frequency of use (questions 1–5), type of use (questions 6–12) and stage of users’ belief in social networks (questions 13–19). The total score ranges from 10 to 95. If the scores of the participants in the range of 19–38, its mean use of social media is weak, the scores range 38 to 57 which showed that frequency of use at an normal level, and if the scores above 57 which showed that frequency is very for use of social media of past six month.

The Scale for Body Image Shame was used, which consists of 14 questions, each of which was assessed through a five-point Likert scale from 0 to 4. The first seven questions assessed external shame, and the last seven questions assessed internal shame. Scores on the higher side showed that the shame level was high. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.92 for the complete scale.[16]

Epi-data software was used for data entry, and analysis was performed in the Statistical Package of Social Science Software program (SPSS) version 26.0 software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Use of list-wise deletion for cases with missing key variables, and the proportion of missing data was minimal (<X%). Mean and standard deviation calculated in descriptive statistics. Logistic regression models were used to determine the association of social media use and body shame. Correlation analysis was performed to determine the correlation between social media use and body shame. P values of < 0.05 were reflected as statistical significance.

Results

The study included 504 participants with a mean age of 21.83 ± 1.34 years. Most participants (63.1%) were aged between 21 and 25 years. The sample consisted of 69.2% females and 30.8% males. Regarding academic year distribution, the highest percentage of students was in their fourth year (29.6%), followed by third-year students (24%). Most participants were single (94.8%), and only 5.2% were married. Smoking history revealed that 9.1% had ever smoked, whereas 90.9% had never smoked. The predominant paternal occupation was office work (80.2%), while 19.8% of fathers were involved in business. A history of past medical or surgical conditions was reported by 19.6% of participants, and 6.5% had a family history of psychological disorders. In terms of body mass index (BMI) distribution, 63.5% fell within the normal BMI range (18.5–24.9), whereas 18.3% were overweight (25–29.9), 6.4% were obese (>30), and 11.9% were underweight (<18.5) [Table 1].

Increased frequency of use of social media was positively associated with increased internal and external shame. Participants who used social media for 1–3 hours daily had a significantly higher association with internal shame (AOR = 10.73, P = 0.001) and external shame (AOR = 4.49, P = 0.004). Social media users for more than 3 hours per day had even higher odds of internal (AOR = 19.48, P = 0.000) and external shame (AOR = 6.12, P = 0.005). Age was also a significant factor, with participants aged 21–25 years showing increased internal (AOR = 2.17, P = 0.019) and external shame (AOR = 1.81, P = 0.044) related to those aged 17–20. Married participants had high associations with internal (AOR = 3.06, P = 0.017) and external shame (AOR = 5.76, P = 0.005) compared to singles. However, gender, smoking status, father’s occupation, past medical history, and BMI did not show statistically significant associations with body shame [Table 2].

Discussion

The result of this study found that a high incidence of body image shame among the participants, with 81.0% experiencing internal shame and 74.8% reporting external shame. This aligns with previous research highlighting the increasing body image concerns among young adults, particularly in the age of social media influence.[17] Internal shame, which arises from self-perception and negative self-evaluation, was found to be more prevalent than external shame, which is driven by perceived societal judgment. This suggests that individuals may internalize unrealistic beauty standards, potentially leading to psychological distress and disordered eating behaviors.[181920]

One of the major findings of this study is that there is a substantial connection between social media use and experiencing feelings of shame about one’s body. In comparison, that AOR (17 through 0.045) indicates people with above average social media use levels were more likely to feel both inner (“AOR” = 16.91, P = 0.000) and outer shame (“AOR” = 6.12) than persons using social media under 60 minutes daily. This finding stands in line with previous studies which have shown a direct relationship between excessive social media use and body dissatisfaction due to increased exposure to idealized body images 333. Research suggests that social media channels such as Facebook and others contribute to body dissatisfaction through the frequent portrayal of edited and filtered images, which set unattainable beauty standards.[2122]

The research also found that age played a role, with people between 21 and 25 having a higher chance of feeling internal (AOR = 2.17, P = 0.019) and external shame (AOR = 1.81, P = 0.044) than those aged 17–20. This pattern might stem from the shift to adulthood when people face more social pressure about looks and self-image 555. Marriage status also mattered, as married people showed higher levels of internal (AOR = 3.06, P = 0.017) and external shame (AOR = 5.76, P = 0.005). Past research has shown that marriage can sometimes boost body image worries due to expectations about looking good in relationships,[2324] gender did not have a big impact on body image shame (P > 0.05), which goes against many studies that say women struggle more with body issues than men. This could be because society is changing, and body image worries are growing for both males and females.[25] The study’s number crunching backs up the link between using social media and feeling ashamed about your body. How often people used social media was tied to the way they used it and how much they engaged with it (P < 0.01). Also, feeling ashamed in front of others was linked to feeling ashamed inside (r = 0.793, P < 0.01), suggesting that individuals who perceive social judgment are expected to internalize negative body image perceptions. This finding is consistent with previous studies.[262728]

There are several limitations in this study. First, this is a cross-sectional study and does not imply a causal association between outcome and predictors. Information bias due to self-reporting data, third confounders, such as psychological status, economic status, and eating disorder, were not collected in the data.

Conclusion

The study result found a high association of social media use with body image shame among students. The findings emphasize the need for awareness programs to promote healthy social media consumption and body positivity. Future studies should explore intervention policies to alleviate the negative psychological effects of social media on body image.
