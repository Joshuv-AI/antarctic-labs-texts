Trends in the Incidence of Early- and Late-Onset Dementia in 2011–2019: A Nationwide Population-Based Study
Authors: Miri Lutski, Inbar Zucker, Mikhail Rozenshmidt, Eshed Lin, Revital Gross-Nevo, Irit Laxer, Lital Keinan-Boker, Michal Bromberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDementia is a leading cause of disability and a growing public health challenge worldwide. Monitoring incidence trends is essential for health service planning and policy development. This study examined secular trends in the incidence of early-onset (45–64 years) and late-onset (≥65 years) dementia in Israel between 2011 and 2019, with a focus on sex-specific patterns.MethodsA nationwide, population-based study using electronic health records from all four Israeli health maintenance organizations provides universal healthcare coverage. Incident cases were identified by International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9/10) codes and/or purchases of anti-dementia medications. Age- and sex-specific incidence rates were calculated, and Joinpoint regression models were used to assess trends over time.ResultsOver the 9-year period, dementia incidence declined significantly in both early- and late-onset groups. Mean annual incidence per 100 individuals was 0.04 for early-onset dementia overall (0.05 in men, 0.04 in women) and 1.55 for late-onset dementia (1.35 in men, 1.72 in women). Statistically significant annual percentage changes were observed across most age-groups, with the sharpest declines in those aged 65–74 (−4.0% per year) and 75–84 (−4.1% per year). Among adults aged 85+, incidence was stable from 2011 to 2015, followed by a significant decline from 2015 to 2019 (−6.6% per year). These trends were consistent across sexes.ConclusionThese findings highlight important public health and community health implications, underscoring the need for continued dementia surveillance, early detection, and evidence-based policy to support healthy aging.

Introduction

The global increase in life expectancy has contributed to the aging of populations worldwide. Dementia, the most common neurodegenerative disorder in older adults, represents one of the most significant public health challenges [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that in 2020, there were approximately 1 billion individuals aged 60 and older, projected to double to 2.1 billion by 2050 [2]. Additionally, the global population living with dementia is expected to increase from 55 million in 2019 to 139 million by 2050 [2]. Alzheimer’s disease is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for approximately 60–80% of cases. Despite these projections, age-specific dementia incidence rates have declined in many countries, likely due to improvements in education, nutrition, healthcare, and lifestyle [3]. However, some studies have reported nonsignificant decreases or stable trends [4]. Recent research from North America and Europe highlights a substantial decline in dementia incidence over the past 4 decades, with reductions estimated at 10%–38% per decade [5, 6]. Notably, up to 40% of dementia cases may be preventable or delayable by addressing the 12 risk factors identified in the 2020 Lancet report on dementia prevention [7]. Wolters and colleagues suggest that achieving a consistent and continuous reduction in incidence worldwide could potentially result in a decrease in the expected incidence of dementia by up to 60 million new cases by the year 2040 [5].

While aging is the strongest risk factor for dementia, early-onset dementia – characterized by onset before age 65 – has received less research attention, despite its more aggressive progression and distinct risk factors, including genetic predisposition, traumatic brain injury, and specific medical conditions [6]. Most large cohort studies have focused exclusively on individuals aged 60 or 65 and older [3]. Despite the overall declining trends in dementia incidence, gender-specific variations persist. Women exhibit higher rates of dementia – particularly Alzheimer’s disease – likely due to longer life expectancy as well as hormonal and genetic factors [8].

Since 2013, Israel’s National Program to Address Alzheimer’s Disease and Other Dementias, led by the Ministry of Health’s Division of Geriatrics, has focused on the establishment of comprehensive national dementia data to guide policy and service development [9]. Understanding trends in dementia incidence is essential for these efforts. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to examine secular trends in the incidence of both early- and late-onset dementia between 2011 and 2019 and to assess whether similar changes occurred in both men and women and across all age-groups, utilizing electronic health records (EHRs). Our findings will contribute to more accurate projections of future dementia incidence and inform targeted planning of healthcare services.

Materials and Methods

Subjects and Study Design

The National Dementia Dataset was developed through a collaboration between the Geriatric Division and the Israel Center for Disease Control (ICDC) at the Ministry of Health [9], in partnership with all four health maintenance organizations (HMOs) in Israel. These HMOs provide healthcare services to all Israeli citizens and permanent residents, as mandated by the National Health Insurance Law (1995) [10]. A wide range of healthcare services is covered under Israel’s universal health insurance scheme and provided by the respective insuring HMO, including outpatient primary and specialist care, medications, laboratory tests, imaging, and inpatient services. Each HMO systematically collects comprehensive healthcare data on its members, which are uniquely linked at the individual level using a personal identification number. Individual-level dementia data were encoded, encrypted, and securely transferred from each HMO to the ICDC. In Israel, all healthcare data are linked at the individual level through this unique personal identification number, enabling complete longitudinal tracking across care settings. Retrospective data from 2011 to 2019, the most recent year with available data in the national dataset, were incorporated, covering all individuals aged 45 and older insured by the HMOs. Incident dementia cases were identified using an inclusive case definition [11], which included at least one of the following criteria: (1) a chronic dementia diagnosis (ICD-9 or ICD-10); (2) two recorded visit diagnoses by a primary care physician or a specialist in internal medicine, psychiatry, geriatrics, or neurology; or (3) at least one purchase of anti-dementia medications. The ICD codes used are detailed in online suppl. Table S1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552194). Anti-dementia treatment included acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine) and the NMDA antagonist memantine. Universal health insurance provides coverage for donepezil and rivastigmine based on disease severity and disability, while others are partially funded through supplementary health plans. In our dataset, 13% of dementia cases were identified exclusively through medication purchases, highlighting the added value of this approach for comprehensive case identification (see online suppl. Table S2 for the distribution of cases by identification criterion). Based on internal validation of the algorithms, we confirmed that such purchases reliably reflect a confirmed dementia diagnosis as insurance coverage requires specialist authorization, minimizing the risk of misclassification with mild cognitive impairment, which was not the scope of this study.

Study Variables

Demographic variables encompassed age, categorized as 45–64 years (early-onset dementia) and 65–74, 75–84, and 85+ years (late-onset dementia), as well as sex. Residential socioeconomic status (SES) was determined using a 1-to-10 ordinal scale based on locality characteristics, as defined by the Israel Central Bureau of Statistics [12]. SES was grouped into four categories: low (1–3), intermediate-low (4–5), intermediate-high (6–7), and high (8–10). Residential peripherality was assessed using the peripherality index, a 1-to-10 ordinal scale measuring geographic remoteness from economic centers, as defined by the Israel Central Bureau of Statistics. The index combines potential accessibility to population centers (2/3 weight) and proximity to the Tel Aviv District (1/3 weight), with local authorities classified into 10 clusters via hierarchical analysis [12]. Peripherality was grouped into three categories: peripheral localities (combining very peripheral and peripheral, clusters 1–4), intermediate localities (cluster 5), and central localities (clusters 6–10).

Data Analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were presented as counts and percentages. Population denominators were derived from the Central Bureau of Statistics [13]. Yearly age-specific incidence rates for all-cause dementia (per 100 persons) were calculated for 2011–2019 by dividing incident cases by the population without prior dementia as of January 1st each year. Additionally, incidence rates were calculated separately for each residential peripherality category and age-group for 2016–2019, the years for which population data were available to calculate rates. Age-adjusted incidence rates (overall and sex-stratified) were calculated using the 2020 Israeli population as the standard. Confidence intervals (95% CIs) were computed using the normal distribution.

We examined secular trends in the incidence rates for the overall dataset by age-group (45–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85+ years), as well as for age and sex. The results of the trend analyses were expressed as the annual percent change (APC). The APC is calculated by fitting a least squares regression line to the natural logarithm of the rates using the calendar year as a regressor variable. The incidence data were further analyzed using Joinpoint models, which evaluate longitudinal data for a change in trend. The change in trend is tested for statistical significance using a Monte Carlo permutation method [14]. In the presence of a Joinpoint, two APC values were calculated – one preceding the change and one following the change. Analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.12 and the Joinpoint Regression Program (v4.9.0.0, March 2021, Statistical Research and Applications Branch, National Cancer Institute).

Results

From 2011 to 2019, 6,237 individuals were diagnosed with early-onset dementia, including 3,287 men (52.7%), while late-onset dementia was identified in 113,744 individuals, including 44,617 men (39.2%). The average age at diagnosis was 58.4 ± 5.0 years for early-onset dementia and 81.3 ± 7.0 years for late-onset dementia. The 9-year mean annual incidence rates of early-onset dementia per 100 individuals were 0.04 for the overall population aged 45–64, 0.05 for men, and 0.04 for women. For late-onset dementia (65+ years), these rates per 100 were 1.55 overall, with men showing a rate of 1.35 and women a rate of 1.72.

The overall and sex-specific crude and age-adjusted yearly incidence rates of early-onset dementia are presented in Table 1 and online supplementary Table S3. Among these cases, the majority were aged 55–64 (∼78%), men (∼53%), and individuals with intermediate SES (∼72%). Compared to the general population aged 45–64, where 45% were in the 55–64 age-group, individuals with early-onset dementia were older (Table 1). Men were slightly overrepresented (53% vs. 48.7% in the general population). Regarding SES, individuals in the intermediate SES were more prevalent among dementia cases (72% vs. 63%), while the low SES group was similarly represented (17%), and the high SES group was underrepresented (14% vs. 18%; data not shown).

The overall and sex-specific incidence rates for late-onset dementia are shown in Table 2 and online supplementary Table S4. Among these cases, the highest proportion was observed in the 75–84 age-group (∼48%), among women (∼61%), and in individuals with intermediate-low and intermediate-high SES (∼69%) (Table 2). The rates of dementia increase dramatically with age. For instance, in 2019 the annual incidence in people aged 45–64 was 0.04%; in people aged 65–74, the annual incidence was 0.4%; and in people aged 75–84, and people aged 85 and older, the incidence rates were 2.1% and 4.7%, respectively (Fig. 1a). The incidence rates of dementia were similar in women and men aged 45–74 years; however, the rates were higher in women than men for those aged 75 years and older (Fig. 1b, c).

The APCs for the total population were statistically significant at −2.1 (95% CI: −3.3 to −0.8, p = 0.005) for ages 45–64, −4.0 (95% CI: −5.1 to −2.85, p < 0.001) for ages 65–74, and −4.1 (95% CI: −4.7 to −3.6, p < 0.001) for ages 75–84. In the 85 and above age-group, a significant Joinpoint was detected, with the APC shifting from 0.3 (95% CI: −0.4 to 1.1, p = 0.285) during 2011–2015 to −6.6 (95% CI: −7.4 to −5.9, p < 0.001) during 2015–2019 (Fig. 1a). For men, the significant APCs were −1.9 (95% CI: −3.5 to −0.2, p = 0.031) for ages 45–64, −3.2 (95% CI: −4.4 to −2.0, p < 0.001) for ages 65–74, and −3.3 (95% CI: −4.0 to −2.6), p < 0.001) for ages 75–84. In the 85 and above age-group, the APC shifted from 1.8 (95% CI: −0.7 to 4.4, p = 0.121) during 2011–2015 to −7.1 (95% CI: −9.3 to −4.8, p < 0.001) during 2015–2019 (Fig. 1b). Among women, the significant APCs were −2.3 (95% CI: −3.7 to −0.8, p = 0.007), −4.6 (95% CI: −6.1 to −3.1, p < 0.001), and −4.7 (95% CI: −5.4 to −4.0, p < 0.001) for ages 45–64, 65–74, and 75–84, respectively. In the 85 and above age-group, a significant Joinpoint was identified, with APC declining from −0.4 (95% CI: −2.1 to 1.3, p = 0.529) in 2011–2015 to −6.3 (95% CI: −8.0 to −4.7, p < 0.001) in 2015–2019 (Fig. 1c).

As part of an additional analysis, annual incidence rates of dementia among individuals aged 45 years and older, stratified by residential peripherality and age-group for the years 2016–2019, are presented in online supplementary Table S5. Overall, age-specific incidence patterns were similar across levels of residential peripherality. In central localities, incidence rates among individuals aged 75 years and older declined over time (APC = −2.5, 95% CI: −4.5 to −0.4, p = 0.036), while rates in other age-groups remained relatively stable. Incidence rates in the oldest age-group in peripheral and intermediate localities also remained unchanged.

Discussion

This nationwide study presents the first 9-year analysis of early- and late-onset dementia incidence in Israel using EHRs. Early-onset dementia rates were low (0.04–0.05 per 100), while late-onset rates were substantially higher (1.35–1.72 per 100). Significant declines in APC were observed across all age-groups, with the sharpest decreases in ages 65–74 (−4.0%) and 75–84 (−4.1%). Among those 85+, a significant decline occurred from 2015 to 2019, following a period of stability. The decline was steeper in men, who initially showed a slight rise before decreasing sharply, while women experienced a more gradual decline. The Joinpoint observed around 2015 in the 85+ age-group may reflect a different cohort effect, with protective factors, such as improved education, living conditions, and access to healthcare, reaching this oldest cohort later than younger age-groups. This interpretation is consistent with findings from the Framingham Heart Study, which demonstrated declines in dementia incidence primarily among individuals with higher educational attainment, underscoring the long-term influence of early-life socioeconomic and environmental factors [6]. Individuals aged ≥85 years during the study period were born mainly in the late 1920s and early 1930s, and thus likely experienced early-life adversities related to World War II and the immediate post-war period, including disrupted education, increased psychosocial stress, nutritional deficiencies, and limited healthcare access. Although individual-level migration data are unavailable in our data, the small Jewish population in Israel prior to 1948 suggests that many in this cohort may have been immigrants. These interpretations are therefore considered hypothesis generating rather than causal.

Dementia incidence varies across countries due to methodological differences, diagnostic criteria, and population characteristics [15–20]. Up to 76% of dementia cases, especially mild to moderate, may go undiagnosed in primary care [16, 21, 22]. Thus, identifying the true incidence of dementia poses challenges for healthcare systems due to barriers at multiple levels, including those related to patients, their families, healthcare providers, and the healthcare system as a whole. Patients and their families may face obstacles such as a lack of public awareness, fear of stigmatization, denial, misinterpretation of symptoms, or underestimation of the severity of cognitive impairment. Healthcare providers face limited awareness, restricted diagnostic access, and time constraints [16, 20, 22]. Despite this, large-scale reviews have estimated dementia incidence across populations. According to a large systematic review of 114 studies conducted between 2008 and 2018, the incidence of Alzheimer’s disease in Europe among individuals aged 65 and older was 11.08 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 10.30–11.89). Regarding sex differences, the incidence was 7.02 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 6.06–8.05) in men and 13.25 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 12.05–14.51) in women [23]. A large recent meta-analysis of 61 studies conducted between 1990 and 2022 from all over the world demonstrated an increase in the global age-standardized incidence rates of dementia by age, from 0.17 per 100,000 person-years in ages 30–34 years to 5.14 per 100,000 person-years in ages 60–64 years, summing to a global total age-standardized incidence rate of 11 per 100,000 in ages 30–64 [24].

A recent systematic review, which included studies up to mid-December 2017, indicates that while some countries have experienced a decline in dementia incidence, others have reported an increase [25], highlighting the complexity of this public health issue. Generally, incidence rates were reported as stable in Sweden and in China among individuals aged ≥60 years, as well as in France (≥65 years) [25]. A decrease has been reported in Canada (≥20 years), in the Netherlands (60–90 years), in Germany (≥65 years), in Denmark (65–74 years), in the UK (≥50 years), and in the USA (≥60 years) [25]. Conversely, an incidence rate increase was noted in Italy (≥65 years), in Japan (65–84 years), and in Wales (≥75 years) [25]. The observed decline in dementia incidence in our study aligns with findings from previous studies conducted in North America and Europe, suggesting a possible true reduction in dementia incidence over the past few decades. The Alzheimer Cohort Consortium report (1988–2015), which included individuals aged 65 and older from seven population-based cohort studies conducted in the USA and Europe over a 25-year period, revealed a significant decline in dementia incidence rates, estimated at 13% per calendar decade (95% CI: 7%–19%) [5]. This trend was consistently observed across all studies, with a somewhat stronger effect in men compared to women [5]. Results from the Framingham Heart Study, conducted between 1977 and 2008, also showed a progressive decline in dementia incidence over 3 decades among individuals aged ≥60 years [6]. This temporal trend, along with parallel improvements in cardiovascular health, was observed only among participants who had at least a high school education. Similarly, findings from the English Longitudinal Study of Aging, which included individuals aged 50 years and older, estimated a relative reduction of −2.7% in dementia incidence for each year between 2002 and 2013 [26].

Several factors that improve global brain health may have contributed to the observed decline in dementia incidence rates. First, increased access to education and intellectual stimulation helps maintain cognitive resilience, potentially delaying the onset of dementia [27]. Data from three longitudinal population-based studies with post-mortem brain donation (Epidemiological Clinicopathological Studies in Europe, EClipSE) indicate that while education does not prevent neurodegenerative and vascular pathology, it may mitigate its clinical impact before death [28].This supports the “brain reserve hypothesis,” which suggests that higher educational attainment enhances the brain’s ability to compensate for pathology later in life [29].

Second, long-term improved prevention and management of cardiovascular risk factors − such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and high cholesterol − may have contributed to the decline in dementia incidence. Post-mortem data from the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging suggest that intracranial atherosclerosis increases dementia risk independently of Alzheimer’s pathology and cerebral infarcts [30]. While the exact role of atherosclerosis in dementia remains debated, studies link carotid atherosclerosis to cognitive impairment onset and progression [31]. In Israel, despite the persistence of cardiovascular risk factors among older adults, a decline in stroke incidence was observed since 2014, providing a national example of improved cardiovascular health that may contribute to delayed dementia onset [32]. This trend may be explained by significant advancements in cardiovascular treatments, including broader access to and increased use of medications such as antihypertensives, statins, and glucose-lowering agents [27]. For example, in Israel, statin initiation rates rose from the year 2000, peaking in the year 2005 before stabilizing, with shifts toward younger, lower-risk patients, higher doses, and simvastatin dominance (from 27.5% to >90%) [33]. High-dose potent statins use increased post-acute coronary syndrome from 2008 to 2010 due to guidelines [34]. For antihypertensives, renin-angiotensin system blockers use rose to 54% and β-blockers to 43% in hypertensive stroke patients (2004–2010), with multidrug regimens up from 16.9% to 20.0% [35]. In acute coronary syndrome, combination therapy (≥2 agents) grew from 50% (2002–2004) to 57% (2008–2010), emphasizing ACE inhibitors/ARBs and β-blockers use [36]. Although glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have demonstrated cardiovascular and potential cognitive benefits [37, 38], their widespread uptake in Israel largely occurred after 2019. Therefore, GLP-1 RAs are unlikely to have influenced the trends observed in the present study but may represent an additional factor shaping cerebrovascular and cognitive outcomes in future decades. Additionally, adopting a healthy lifestyle – including regular physical activity, a heart- and brain-supportive diet, maintaining a healthy weight, and avoiding smoking and excessive alcohol consumption – plays a crucial role in both overall cardiovascular and brain health and may reduce the risk of both early-onset and late-life dementia [39–43]. In Israel, during the study period, the Ministry of Health launched health initiatives like “Healthy Israel 2020” (2005) [44] and “Healthy is Possible” campaign, known as “EfshariBari” in Hebrew (2011) [45], promoting better nutrition, physical activity, and healthier lifestyles through policy and education [44–46]. A recent study found increased adherence to the DASH diet in the 2014–2015 National Health and Nutrition Survey compared to a decade earlier [47]. Collectively, these Israeli data suggest, at a national ecological level, that long-term systematic improvements in cardiovascular risk management through prevention and treatment may be contributing to the observed population-wide decline in dementia incidence, either directly or by lowering cardiovascular morbidity. Further studies are warranted to establish an association between these two trends.

Some methodological limitations should be considered. First, the recording of dementia diagnoses in EHRs is performed primarily by primary care physicians, which may lead to under-detection. Indeed, a recent meta-analysis of 23 studies, mostly conducted in high-income countries, reported a pooled rate of undetected dementia in primary care of 61.7% [48]. However, it is unlikely that this underestimation varied significantly over time. Furthermore, although ICD-based dementia diagnoses are highly specific, under-recording in primary care is also well recognized, and dementia may be clinically diagnosed without being formally coded in the medical record [49]. Given the extent of under-recording in routine diagnostic data, ICD codes alone cannot be considered a gold standard for dementia identification [50]. To mitigate this limitation, we incorporated data on anti-dementia medication purchases. Because reimbursement for these medications requires specialist authorization, drug purchase records serve as an independent indicator of a confirmed dementia diagnosis, even in the absence of ICD coding. In our dataset, 13% of cases were identified exclusively through medication purchases, highlighting the added value of this approach for comprehensive case identification. However, anti-dementia medications are not indicated for all dementia subtypes, such as frontotemporal dementia, and therefore this approach may have resulted in under-ascertainment of certain non-Alzheimer’s dementia cases. Furthermore, although neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression, anxiety, psychosis, or behavioral changes may precede dementia, these features were not used as criteria for case identification because they are highly prevalent and nonspecific. Their inclusion in administrative data-based algorithms could result in substantial misclassification and overestimation of dementia incidence. To preserve diagnostic specificity, case identification was therefore restricted to recorded dementia diagnoses and purchases of anti-dementia medications requiring specialist authorization. Second, information on dementia severity was not available in the dataset, and some diagnoses classified as incident dementia may therefore reflect delayed diagnosis. Such diagnostic delay may lead to a bias in dementia incidence; however, it is unlikely to be differential across the follow-up period as diagnostic practices and healthcare access remained relatively consistent across the study period. Third, we have no valid data on dementia subtypes because in clinical practice the diagnostic criteria and the extent of workup vary substantially [51]. Accordingly, the results cannot be interpreted at the subtype level. However, this limitation does not affect the observed overall temporal trends. Forth, the data for this study were obtained from four HMOs, which may differ in their diagnostic and treatment practices and relied on administrative data originally collected for non-research purposes. Fifth, due to limited availability of specialized dementia diagnostic services in the public sector, some individuals may seek private evaluations. However, dementia is a chronic condition requiring ongoing follow-up, support, and medication coverage, which in Israel is largely provided through the HMOs. Therefore, diagnoses established in private settings are likely to be recorded in HMO EHRs over time. Importantly, there is no evidence that the use of private diagnostic services changed substantially during the study period in a manner that would differentially affect the observed temporal trends.

The strengths of this study include the use of a national dementia dataset, which enhances both internal and external validity. In addition, our case definition of dementia was comprehensive, incorporating not only ICD-coded diagnoses but also records of anti-dementia medication purchases, an approach that is likely to improve the ascertainment of true positive dementia cases by integrating diagnostic and treatment data [50]. A notable observation is that early-onset dementia has historically received less research attention compared to late-onset dementia, despite its more aggressive progression. By analyzing trends in early-onset dementia incidence, this study contributes to addressing this important research gap.

Conclusion

This study provides key insights into the trends of early-onset and late-onset dementia in Israel, revealing a decline, particularly in older adults, likely influenced by improvements in education, healthcare, and lifestyle. These findings underscore the importance of continued investment in dementia prevention and surveillance, particularly through primary care and community-based services. Strengthening early detection, expanding training for healthcare providers, and implementing public awareness strategies will help ensure equitable access to care. As populations age, integrating dementia-related policies into broader community health planning will be crucial for a resilient and responsive health system.
