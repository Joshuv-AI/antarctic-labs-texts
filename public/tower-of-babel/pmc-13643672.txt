Technical note on cervical epidural catheterization in two adult horses: Injectate distribution and epidural space effects
Authors: Orlaith O'Scanaill, Valerie J. Moorman, Michael Perlini, Rachel A. Reed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine the caudalmost extent of diffusion of contrast media administered through a cervical epidural catheter (CEC) and to describe pathologic changes associated with maintaining an indwelling CEC for up to 17 days.Study designPilot study.AnimalsTwo mature horses.MethodsCECs were placed under ultrasound guidance in two horses. Both horses were injected with 5 mL 50% Iohexol contrast solution, followed by radiographs of catheter insertion site to confirm placement. INTRODUCTION

Cervical epidural catheterization for provision of analgesia in management of thoracic limb pain has recently been described.1, 2, 3 The cervical region of the horse can be a primary source of pain, with cervical articular process osteoarthritis as a common source. Clinically relevant disease has been observed most frequently in the caudal cervical region, between C6 and 7 and C5 and 6. 4

An additional site of cervical pain can include the cervical nerve roots, which are located epidurally as they exit the cervical spinal cord. These nerve roots can be secondarily affected, either by compression or local inflammation, by cervical articular process osteoarthritis. The brachial plexus in the horse is primarily formed by the ventral branches of spinal nerves C6, C7, C8, and T1 as well as T2 in a minor capacity. 5 This plexus provides both motor and sensory innervation to the shoulder, brachium, antebrachium, and distal limb. Compression or inflammation near these specific nerve roots is thought to result in forelimb lameness. The cervical spinal nerve roots exit via the intervertebral foramina cranial to the vertebra with the corresponding number. The exception to this rule is for the C8 nerve root, which exits caudal to its corresponding vertebra C7.

While cervical epidural catheters (CECs) have been used and kept in place for days to weeks, there is no information on long‐term effects of these catheters on the epidural space. In sacrococcygeal and lumbosacral epidural catheters, long‐term catheter placement has resulted in histologic evidence of inflammation as well as fibrosis around the catheter site. 6

The objectives of this pilot study were (1) to demonstrate the caudal extent of injectate when administered by CEC and (2) to document any changes within the epidural space after long‐term management of CECs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Horses

All procedures performed were approved by University of Georgia's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Both horses were donated to the hospital for reasons unrelated to cervical disease and were mature (>1 year old). For the duration of the study, horses were housed in stalls with free access to grass hay and water.

Study design and cervical epidural catheter placement

This was a descriptive pilot study. These horses underwent ultrasound‐guided CEC placement under standing sedation as previously described at C1/C2.3, 7 Briefly, horses were sedated with 0.01 mg/kg detomidine IV, the head was positioned slightly flexed with the muzzle at chest level, and the skin over the cranial cervical region was clipped and aseptically prepared. An 18 gauge 15 cm Tuohy needle was placed into the epidural space using a hanging drop technique, and a 20 gauge, 90 cm epidural catheter (MILA International, Hebron, Kentucky) was inserted through the needle, with approximately 5–6 cm of catheter placed within the epidural space. Excess catheter length was removed prior to the catheter being sutured to the skin and an elastikon bandage was sutured in place. Horses were examined twice daily and catheters were flushed with 1–2 mL heparinized saline once daily.

Cervical radiographs

After placement of CEC, each horse received an initial 5 mL injection of 50% 0.9% saline: 50% Iohexol 300 mgI/mL contrast solution (GE Healthcare Inc., Princeton, New Jersey). A laterolateral cervical radiograph (Digital RAD, Canon Medical Systems Inc., Tustin, California) of the cranial cervical region was taken to confirm placement of the CEC within the epidural space. Additional laterolateral radiographs were taken to determine the caudalmost extent of the contrast solution.

A total of 5 mL increments of contrast solution were administered up to a total volume of 15 mL, and laterolateral cervical radiographs were taken at 5 min intervals up to 25 min to assess the most caudal extent of contrast. The caudalmost extent of contrast diffusion was determined from laterolateral radiographs by a board‐certified diagnostic imaging specialist (MP).

Euthanasia and gross examination

Both horses were humanely euthanized via IV administration of pentobarbital solution and underwent a gross examination of the CEC within the cervical epidural space by a boarded pathologist.

RESULTS

Horses

The two horses in this study were a 33 year old Saddlebred gelding (468 kg) and a 14 year old mixed breed gelding weighing 454 kg. Reasons for donation included chronic lameness and back pain.

Cervical epidural catheters

Patency of CECs in both horses was maintained for 16 and 17 days, respectively. Horses appeared normal during the study duration, did not demonstrate pain to palpation of the CEC insertion site, nor did they have adverse reactions to daily flushing of the catheters.

Cervical radiographs

The initial laterolateral radiographs following the initial 5 mL administration of contrast solution confirmed CEC placement in both horses. In Horse 1, after the total volume of 15 mL contrast solution at 25 min, the caudal extent of contrast solution was at the cranial aspect of C6 (Figure 1A; Table 1). In Horse 2, after 15 mL contrast solution at 20 min from the initial administration of contrast solution, the most caudal extent of contrast was at the caudal aspect of C6 (Figure 1B).

Gross examination

Gross necropsy findings confirmed placement of both CECs within the epidural space (Figure 2). In both horses, the catheter was looped around in the epidural space at C2 with the tip of the catheter pointing cranially. Gross changes included regions of mild inflammation and mild to moderate hemorrhage around the insertion site of the catheter. A fibrous sac of tissue also covered the catheter in horse 2.

Gross appearance of the catheter as it looped within the cervical epidural space of (A) Horse 1 (day 16) and (B) Horse 2 (day 17). A sheet of fibrous material (yellow circle) encased the catheter in Horse 2.

DISCUSSION

In both horses, diffusion of contrast media administered through an indwelling cervical epidural catheter extended to at least to the cranial aspect of C6 vertebra. This was achieved via administration of 5–15 mL contrast solution, which correlated to a dose of approximately (0.01–0.03 mL/kg) and took 5–25 min to achieve. Assuming a similar diffusion rate with other medications, these findings suggest that it may be possible to reach the C6 and potentially the C7 nerve root with this volume.

Based solely on gross necropsy findings, the CECs seemed to result in similar changes as previously reported for sacrococcygeal and lumbosacral epidural catheters maintained for a similar amount of time. 6 The catheters were both looped within the epidural space with the tip pointed cranially. This would suggest that inserting less catheter length may be desirable to prevent potential knotting of the catheter.

Limitations of this study include the small sample size, as well as not examining for microscopic changes within the epidural tissues. Secondly, the use of 50% Iohexol contrast solution may not mimic the distribution of other medications that may be administered by this technique. Lastly, radiographs were not taken past 25 min so it is possible that further diffusion of contrast solution could have been identified.

Future studies in horses involving the use of commonly administered medications for clinical lesions resulting in pain within the cervical spine and/or forelimb are warranted.
