PROTOCOL: Interventions to Improve Recruitment and Retention of Mental Health Professionals: A Systematic Review
Authors: Narendar Manohar, Hiroko Fujimoto, Jeffrey C L Looi, Paul A Maguire, Stephen Kisely, Tarun Bastiampillai, Stephen Robson, Samuel B Harvey, Peter A Baldwin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis is a protocol for a systematic review that aims to identify interventions used to improve recruitment and retention of mental healthcare professionals, and to examine the effectiveness of these interventions. Previous systematic reviews have examined interventions targeting either a specific systemic or occupational factor associated with recruitment and retention of mental health professionals or involved only a specific group of mental health professionals. To our knowledge, this will be the first review that comprehensively synthesises evidence regarding interventions for a broad range of possible systemic and/or occupational factors associated with recruitment and retention across all professional groups working in the field of mental health. The findings of this review will help inform evidence-based interventions to address challenges related to recruitment and retention of mental health workforce.Keywords: recruitment, retention, intervention, effectiveness, mental health professionals

Background

The Problem, Condition, or Issue

Globally, there is a critical and escalating severe shortage of mental health professionals. This shortage has far-reaching implications for patient care, access to services, and the overall well-being of populations worldwide. In the United States, as of 2023, more than half (∼169 million) of the population resided in areas that had a shortage of mental health care providers (termed as Health Professional Shortage Areas - HPSA) (Health Resources and Services Administration, 2023). In United Kingdom, as of 2023, mental health workforce within the National Health Service sector had the highest vacancy rate (∼11.7%) (The King’s Fund, 2024). Similarly, in Australia, although there has been a steady increase in the number of psychiatrists, however there is still geographic maldistribution and a shortfall in the overall mental health workforce; and most psychiatrists have reported experiencing burnout and a negative impact on patient care as a result of this (Hayter et al., 2024; Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists RANZCP, 2024).

The workforce shortage spans across various mental health disciplines, affecting the availability of psychiatrists, mental health nurses, psychologists, counsellors, and other allied health professionals. This scarcity is particularly pronounced in low-income countries (World Health Organization, 2021) and rural and remote areas, where entire regions may have little or no access to mental health services (Hayter et al., 2024). The consequences of staff shortages might be severe and multifaceted, ranging from extended waiting times for appointments to reduced quality of care and, in some cases, complete lack of access to necessary mental health support.

The shortage of workers is not merely a matter of numbers. Systemic issues within the healthcare sector, including inadequate funding, comparatively low compensation, high workloads, and limited career advancement opportunities might be contributing to the difficulty in attracting and retaining mental health professionals (De Vries et al., 2023). Moreover, a range of other occupational factors such as burnout, low job satisfaction, psychological distress, bullying and harassment, and safety concerns also have a strong influence (Adams et al., 2021). These factors may not only deter new recruits, but also lead to burnout among experienced professionals, further exacerbating the shortage.

As the demand for mental health services continues to grow, addressing this workforce shortage has become an urgent priority. The implications of inaction are profound, potentially leading to a widening gap in mental health care access and a deterioration in overall public mental health. To tackle this public health challenge, Australia developed a national strategy to build a mental health workforce that is skilled, well-distributed, and well-supported to deliver mental health treatment (Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care, 2023). However, the national strategy neither clarifies the evidence-base for each of its proposed approach or actions, nor does it specify which recruitment and retention strategies are most likely to be effective in the Australian context. Hence, there is a need to identify evidence-based interventions that are effective in improving recruitment of new staff and retain the existing workforce.

The Interventions and How They Might Work

Over the years, multiple interventions targeting occupational stressors among mental health professionals have been evaluated, including burnout, psychological distress, stigma, low job satisfaction, and workplace bullying. Commonly implemented approaches include psychoeducation, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and yoga. Their effectiveness is often linked to mechanisms such as enhancing psychological flexibility, value-congruent behaviour and resilience (e.g., through ACT) (Bond et al., 2011; Hayes et al., 2006), promoting adaptive coping skills (e.g., via CBT) (Hofmann et al., 2012), and improving emotion regulation (e.g., with mindfulness) (Chiesa & Serretti, 2009). These interventions are typically delivered via structured workshops, online modules, or in-service training.

Certain organisational-level interventions have also been implemented (such as workload management, improving the quality of clinical supervision, creating peer support groups and offering continuing education programs), which aim to address systemic contributors to stress and burnout (Dreison et al., 2018). Evidence suggests that person-directed interventions are more effective than organisation-directed interventions (Dreison et al., 2018).

Why It Is Important to Do This Review

Previous systematic reviews have examined interventions or strategies either targeting a specific systemic or occupational factor associated with recruitment and retention of mental health professionals or focused only on a specific group of mental health professionals (Adams et al., 2021; Bekelepi & Martin, 2022; Dreison et al., 2018; Foster et al., 2021; Gilbody et al., 2006; Rudaz et al., 2017; Suyi et al., 2017). However, to the best of our knowledge, no review to date has comprehensively examined and evaluated a wide range of interventions for systemic and/or occupational factors associated with recruitment and retention of every mental health professional group. The overall aim of the review is to generate high-quality evidence to inform evidence-based interventions to recruit and retain the mental health workforce, particularly in Australia.

Objectives

The objectives of this review will answer the following research questions:

• What interventions or strategies have been used to improve recruitment and retention of mental healthcare professionals.

• What interventions or strategies have been used to improve recruitment and retention of mental healthcare professionals.

• What is the effectiveness of the implemented interventions on the recruitment and retention of mental healthcare professionals.

• What is the effectiveness of the implemented interventions on the recruitment and retention of mental healthcare professionals.

Methods

This protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis Protocol (PRISMA-P) checklist (Moher et al., 2016; Shamseer et al., 2015). The protocol has also been registered with the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (Schiavo, 2019) (CRD42024599677).

Criteria for Considering Studies for This Review

Types of Studies

We will include studies employing a quantitative (experimental or observational) study design to develop and evaluate intervention(s). Experimental studies include randomised controlled trial (RCT), quasi-experiment, and non-randomised pre- and post-intervention design. Studies employing uncontrolled pre-post intervention designs (e.g., single-arm trials) will be eligible for narrative synthesis (but will not be included in the meta-analysis) because they may provide useful preliminary evidence regarding intervention feasibility, implementation, and workforce applicability. However, studies with comparator or control conditions will only be included in quantitative meta-analysis examining intervention effectiveness.

Observational studies include cross-sectional, cross-sectional nested within a cohort study or case-control design, cast-control design, and longitudinal/cohort design (prospective and retrospective). Mixed methods studies will be included if they have relevant quantitative outcome data for the intervention(s). We will exclude qualitative and non-original studies (i.e., comments, reviews and theoretical papers), case studies, case report and series, conference proceedings and book chapters. We will also exclude systematic reviews, literature reviews, mini reviews, and meta-analyses. Furthermore, grey literature will not be eligible for inclusion in this review, as the primary aim is to synthesise evidence-based interventions to improve recruitment and retention among mental health professionals. Therefore, the review will focus on peer-reviewed published studies.

Types of Participants

We will include studies that involve either of the two population groups:

- Primary population: health professionals who (a) hold an accredited professional qualification in mental health practice, and (b) are working directly in the field of mental health - including psychiatrists, psychologists, mental health nurses (including nurse practitioners), accredited mental health social workers and occupational therapists.

- Primary population: health professionals who (a) hold an accredited professional qualification in mental health practice, and (b) are working directly in the field of mental health - including psychiatrists, psychologists, mental health nurses (including nurse practitioners), accredited mental health social workers and occupational therapists.

- Secondary population: health professionals who do not hold an accredited professional qualification in mental health practice but are working directly in the field of mental health - including provisional psychologists, registered counsellors, general practitioners, and psychotherapists.

- Secondary population: health professionals who do not hold an accredited professional qualification in mental health practice but are working directly in the field of mental health - including provisional psychologists, registered counsellors, general practitioners, and psychotherapists.

There will be no restriction on the age group or gender/sex or ethnicity or country. Furthermore, studies that include mixed populations (i.e., eligible mental healthcare-related professionals and either non-mental healthcare professionals or patients) will be included, only if the data related to eligible mental healthcare-related professionals are separately recorded. We will exclude studies that do not confirm that eligible study participants were involved in the intervention.

Types of Comparators

The intervention group will be compared with any control/comparator group, including other intervention(s) that do not target the stated systemic or occupational factors, and are employed as controls or tested within non-inferiority or head-to-head trials. Moreover, pre-intervention results will be compared with post-intervention results in studies that use pre/post design.

Studies reporting measurable quantitative recruitment and/or retention outcomes will be included. There will be no restriction on the use of validated or non-validated quantitative outcome measures, given the comprehensive nature of the review and the anticipated heterogeneity of interventions, workforce settings, professional groups and outcome measurement approaches. Eligible outcome measures may include validated psychometric instruments, administrative workforce data, and bespoke or study-specific quantitative measures, provided they are clearly defined and conceptually aligned with the outcome domain of interest.

Studies reporting both objective and subjective outcomes will be eligible; however, only objective workforce-related outcomes will be extracted for the primary outcomes. Furthermore, there will be no restriction on the type/skill/qualification of the individual or organisation recording the outcome measures.

Duration of Follow-up

No exclusions will be applied on the basis of follow-up duration. The outcome measurements obtained immediately after the intervention and at all subsequent follow-ups will be included.

Types of Settings

There will be no restriction on the settings in which the study or interventions is conducted. Eligible studies may be undertaken across any mental health service setting, including but not limited to hospitals, inpatient psychiatric units, community mental health services, primary care, specialised mental health clinics, public health services, private practice, correctional settings, and digital or telehealth mental health services. Interventions may be delivered individually (one-to-one) or in groups, and may be conducted face-to-face or online.

Search Methods for Identification of Studies

Electronic Searches and Other Resources

The search strategy will aim to find published studies. A combination of specific medical subject headings (MeSH) terms and text words relevant to the topic will be drafted with the help of an expert Health Sciences Librarian and pre-tested in MEDLINE database. One reviewer (NM) with assistance from the librarian, will initially conduct a pilot search on one database followed by searching rest of the databases. A draft MEDLINE search strategy is enclosed as an appendix (see Appendix I). The final search strategy will be adapted to the syntax and subject headings of all other databases. Furthermore, reference lists of all the potentially eligible studies and previously published relevant systematic reviews will be hand-searched, and forward citation searching will be conducted using Google Scholar and/or Scopus to identify additional eligible studies. The following electronic databases will be searched, without any restriction on publication date (i.e., from the time of inception to present) or region: MEDLINE (OVID), Embase (OVID), PsycINFO (OVID), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) (EBSCO), Scopus, and Web of Science (ISI). Grey literature will not be included due to the anticipated breadth and heterogeneity of eligible study designs, intervention types, and outcome measures. Restricting the review to peer-reviewed published literature was considered necessary to ensure methodological feasibility and consistency in study quality assessment and evidence synthesis.

Data Collection and Analysis

Description of Methods Used in Primary Research

It is anticipated that included studies will primarily consist of randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, and pre–post intervention studies. These studies are likely to use validated psychological instruments and standardised surveys to assess either one or more outcomes specified above. Quantitative analyses such as repeated-measures paired t-test, ANOVA or regression modelling are likely to be employed to evaluate intervention effects.

Selection of Studies

We will use “Covidence”, a systematic review management software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for primary screening and data extraction of the literature search results (Veritas, 2023). Studies identified through the electronic databases will be downloaded into Covidence and duplicate records will be removed. A pre-calibration exercise between two reviewers (NM and HF) will be performed on a pilot group of studies to refine the screening questions and ensure consistency across reviewers for screening and selecting eligible studies. Thereafter, in step 1, two members of the review team will independently screen the titles and abstracts of the identified studies and then the full texts of potentially included studies (step 2). If any disagreements arise, they will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. If needed, publication authors will be contacted to seek additional information to resolve questions about eligibility. Reasons for excluding studies will also be recorded. No reviewer will be blind to journal titles, study authors, or institutions. A Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) flow diagram will be developed to present the study selection process (Moher et al., 2009).

Data Extraction and Management

A standardised data extraction form (based on a checklist presented in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Higgins & Deeks, 2008) and/or Covidence software will be used independently by the reviewing team members to extract data from the included studies. The data extraction will be pilot tested (on two studies) and subsequently refined to ensure that all relevant data is captured (see Appendix II) and a calibration exercise will also be performed to ensure consistency across reviewers. A third review team member will resolve disagreements between two initial data extractors during the data extraction process.

The extracted data will include information on the first and corresponding author, publication year, study design and setting (including organisation type), participant demographic details, type of professional group, sample size, follow-up period (if any), intervention details and control conditions (if any), outcome measures and follow-up (if any), statistical analysis, key study findings, and funding sources (see Appendix II). Additional information, if considered relevant, will be recorded and the data extraction form will be revised accordingly. If such a situation arises, it will be duly reported in the intended systematic review manuscript. It is anticipated that the data will be presented as text, tables, and graphs. The study authors will be contacted in case of missing data and/or to resolve any uncertainties.

Assessment of Risk of Bias in Included Studies

The risk of bias within included studies will be assessed by the review team members independently using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tools. The Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool will be used for RCTs (Sterne et al., 2019) and the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for observational studies (Sterne et al., 2016). Any unresolved disagreements between two reviewers will be resolved through discussion with a third team member. Study authors will be contacted in the event of insufficient details to confidently assess the risk of bias. If we do not receive response after three contact attempts, we will assess the study based on available information. All included studies will be reported narratively regardless of their risk of bias score.

Measures of Treatment Effect

Odds ratios (ORs) or Risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals will be used to calculate effect sizes for dichotomous outcomes. Mean differences (MDs) or Standardised mean differences (SMDs) will be used for continuous outcomes. The SMDs will be used when studies assess the same underlying construct using different validated measurement instruments. Outcomes will be considered sufficiently similar for pooling when they measure the same conceptual domain (e.g., burnout) and are conceptually and psychometrically comparable across studies. For continuous outcomes measured using pre- and post-intervention designs, standardised mean change scores may also be calculated where possible. Where appropriate, pooled estimates using either a fixed-effects or random-effects meta-analysis model will be presented.

Unit of Analysis Issues

Potential unit-of-analysis issues, including clustered designs, multiple intervention arms, and repeated follow-up measurements, will be addressed systematically. For cluster-randomised trials (e.g., by hospital, ward or clinic), standard errors will be adjusted using the design effect, following Cochrane guidance (Higgins et al., 2024). In studies with multiple intervention groups sharing a single control group, we will avoid unit-of-analysis errors in accordance with Cochrane guidance (Higgins et al., 2019). Where appropriate, intervention arms will be combined into a single group, or the most relevant comparison will be selected to ensure independence of effect sizes (Higgins et al., 2019). If multiple effect sizes arise within studies, these will be handled in line with appropriate approaches such as multilevel meta-analysis (Van den Noortgate et al., 2014) or robust variance estimation (Tanner-Smith et al., 2016) depending on the data structure. Where studies report multiple follow-up time points, the primary comparison will use the earliest post-intervention measure; longer-term effects will be explored using the final follow-up measurement (Higgins et al., 2024).

Criteria for Determination of Independent Findings

Where multiple reports refer to the same underlying study, overlap will be identified based on authorship, study design, time period, intervention, and cross-referencing. Data will be extracted from all eligible reports, but each study will be counted only once. For meta-analysis, the report containing the most complete and relevant outcome data will be used. Qualitative synthesis may draw on all overlapping reports, but each study will be represented once in the overall evidence count to avoid inflating the evidence base. Where multiple effect sizes are reported within a study (e.g., across related outcomes), robust variance estimation (RVE) will be applied to account for statistical dependency (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022).

If key quantitative data cannot be obtained, the study will be excluded from the meta-analysis but retained in the narrative synthesis if it meets inclusion criteria.

Assessment of Heterogeneity

The heterogeneity will be assessed using both Cochran’s Q and the I2 statistic (Higgins et al., 2003).

Assessment of Reporting Biases

If the meta-analysis includes 10 or more studies, a funnel plot will be generated using RevMan Web (The Cochrane Collaboration, 2024) to visually assess potential publication bias (Sterne et al., 2011). Where appropriate, funnel plots with the Egger’s Regression Test (Egger et al., 1997) will be used to identify asymmetry between published and non-published studies and, if possible, examine identified asymmetry through sub-group analysis.

Data Synthesis

Meta-analyses will be conducted if sufficient data are available i.e., if there are more than two included studies (Valentine et al., 2010) that are (a) assessed to be of moderate to high quality based on predefined risk of bias criteria using RoB 2 for randomised studies and ROBINS-I for non-randomised studies, and (b) found not to be disqualifyingly heterogeneous regarding study design, population, intervention type, and outcome measures. Studies assessed as having high (RoB 2) or critical (ROBINS-I) risk of bias will be retained for narrative synthesis but excluded from quantitative meta-analysis.

Quantitative meta-analysis of intervention effectiveness will only be undertaken using studies with comparator or control conditions. Uncontrolled pre-post studies will be synthesised narratively due to the limitations in attributing observed effects solely to the intervention.

During data extraction and synthesis, outcome measures will be grouped according to conceptual similarity. Standardised mean differences (SMDs) will be used where studies assess conceptually similar outcomes using different measurement instruments. Meta-analysis will only be undertaken where outcomes are considered sufficiently comparable in construct and measurement approach.

The degree of statistical heterogeneity between the studies will be tested using the I2 statistics with statistical significance values based on the statistical guidelines of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Higgins et al., 2024). The meta-analyses will be performed in the latest version of RevMan. If there is a low heterogeneity between the studies (I2 ≤ 40%), a fixed-effect meta-analysis model will be used. If there is a moderate or high heterogeneity between the studies (I2 > 40%), a random effect model will be used. If statistical pooling is not possible and/or there is substantial heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis as per ‘Reporting guideline for synthesis without meta-analysis (SWiM)’ (Campbell et al., 2020) will be produced. The sources of heterogeneity and reasons for not pooling the data will be specified in the narrative review.

A network meta-analysis will be considered to compare multiple interventions; however, this will only be undertaken if sufficient homogeneity and a connected evidence network are present. Given anticipated heterogeneity, a network meta-analysis is not planned as part of the primary analysis.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted, where sufficient data are available, by excluding studies assessed as having high (RoB 2) or critical (ROBINS-I) risk of bias to examine the robustness of pooled estimates. Sensitivity analyses will be based on overall risk of bias judgements from the relevant tools (ROB 2 and ROBINS-I), rather than individual domain-level ratings. Additional sensitivity analyses may also be conducted where appropriate according to study design and outcome measurement approach.

Treatment of Qualitative Research

Qualitative research will not be included in the review.

Summary of Findings and Assessment of the Certainty of the Evidence

The quality of evidence for all the outcomes will be assessed using The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach (Guyatt et al., 2008). Two reviewers (NM and HF) will perform this process independently and unresolved disagreements will be resolved through discussion and/or consensus with a third reviewer (PB). The quality of the evidence will be assessed based on risk of bias, indirectness, inconsistency, imprecision, and publication bias.
