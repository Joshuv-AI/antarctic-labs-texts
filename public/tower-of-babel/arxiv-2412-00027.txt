On the Numerical Approximation of the Karhunen-Loève Expansion for Random Fields with Random Discrete Data
Michael Griebel, Guanglian Li, Christian Rieger

Abstract
In many applications, random fields reflect uncertain parameters, and often their moments are part of the modeling process and thus well known. However, there are practical situations where this is simply not the case. Therefore, we do not assume that we know moments or expansion terms of the random fields, but only have discretized samples of them. The main contribution of this paper concerns the approximation of the true covariance operator from these finite measurements. We derive explicit error estimates that include the finite-rank approximation error of the covariance operator, the Monte Carlo-type error for sampling in the stochastic domain, and the numerical discretization error in the physical domain. For this purpose, we use modern tapering covariance estimators adapted to high-dimensional applications, where the dimension is introduced by the resolution of the measurement process. This allows us to give sufficient conditions on the three discretization parameters to guarantee that the error is kept below a prescribed accuracy ε \varepsilon .



Keywords:
covariance operators, eigenvalue decay, approximation of Gaussian-type random fields, error estimates, Galerkin methods for eigenvalues, finite elements, tapering estimators for sample covariance

Introduction

Mathematical models with random coefficients or random input data have been widely used to describe applications
that are affected by some degree of uncertainty due to incomplete or insufficient information subject to some degree of uncertainty about the problem. The range of applications is broad and diverse, including uncertainty quantification with ordinary differential equations, partial differential equations, and integro-differential equations, or problems in machine learning and data analysis in oil field modeling, quantum mechanics, or finance.

Often, the random data is modeled as a Gaussian random field over a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Thus, the random field is
described by its mean and its covariance. By subtracting its mean, we can further assume that the random field is centered.
For numerical simulations, realizations of this random field must then be derived.

In contrast to this common approach, we intend to estimate the statistical information from experimental data. For this purpose, we assume that there is an unknown centered Gaussian field
\kappa
, which is
consistent with the covariance kernel

\displaystyle\text{Cov}_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=:R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{N(\ell,k)}\phi_{\ell,k}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell,k}(\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\approx\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=:R^{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

(1)

where we have the eigenvalues sorted in strictly decreasing order, i.e.
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}\dots\geq 0
for the first series expansion, and we understand the last (notationally simpler) expression as repeating the eigenvalues according to their multiplicity. This will be used from now on for simplicity of notation.
The last sum is usually called the finite noise approximation. This approximation, and in particular the truncation parameter
L
, is justified by a fast decay of the eigenvalues in the Mercer-type expansion.

For our analysis we assume spatial regularity of the random field
\kappa
. We have:

Assumption 1.1
.

Let
\kappa:\Omega\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and thus
K:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}
be contained in the Bochner space

L^{\infty}(\Omega,H^{s}(\mathcal{D}))
for some
s\geq 0
, where for
s=0
we have
H^{0}(\mathcal{D})=L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.
Alternatively, let
\kappa:\Omega\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and hence
K:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}
be contained in the Bochner space

L^{\infty}(\Omega,H^{s}(\mathcal{D}))
for some
s>\frac{d}{2}
.

We refer to
[
14
, Theorem 5.2]
for conditions on
\kappa
that allow for such types of bounds.

We also assume some moment bounds:

Assumption 1.2
.

Let
\kappa:\Omega\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
, we assume

C_{\infty}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty
.
Sometimes we need the stronger assumption

C_{H^{s}}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty
for later use.

These estimates imply that

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\right|\leq C_{\infty}
and
\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\leq 4C_{\infty}^{2}
by Hölder’s inequality.
Since our analysis is aimed at practical applications, we consider mostly discretized function spaces. For the remainder of this article, we will focus on finite element spaces of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{h}:=\{v\in H^{1}(\mathcal{D}):v|_{K}\in P^{\lceil s\rceil}(K)\text{ for all }K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}},

(2)

where
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a regular quasi-uniform triangulation over the
physical domain
\mathcal{D}
with a maximal mesh size
h
and
\lceil s\rceil\in\mathbb{N}
depends on the spatial regularity of
\kappa
.

We denote the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{h}
by
Q_{h}<\infty
. Furthermore, we expect
Q_{h}=\mathcal{O}(h^{-d}s^{d})
.
For the rest of the paper we will fix some arbitrary basis
\mathcal{V}_{h}=\text{span}\left\{\theta_{k}^{(h)}\ :\ 1\leq k\leq Q_{h}\right\}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}
be a generic basis giving rise to the following vector field

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}
, with
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\left(\theta^{(h)}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\dots,\theta^{(h)}_{Q_{h}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{T}
.
To fix the notation: any function
v^{(h)}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
can be identified with a vector
\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}=\left(V^{(h)}_{1},\dots,V^{(h)}_{Q_{h}}\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}
via

v^{(h)}=\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}
.

Next, we consider the
L^{2}
projection map
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to\mathcal{V}_{h}
and note that its approximation rate is given in
[
4
, Theorem 4.4.20]
as

\displaystyle\left\|v-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}v\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}}h^{s}\left\|v\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\text{ for all }v\in H^{s}(\mathcal{D}),

(3)

\displaystyle\left\|v-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}v\right\|_{C\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}h^{s-\frac{d}{2}}\left\|v\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\text{ for all }v\in H^{s}(\mathcal{D})\text{ for }s>d/2.

(4)

Here, the positive constants
C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}}
and
C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}
depend only on the shape regularity parameter of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and are independent of the mesh size
h
.
Since we mostly deal with discretized functions, we consider

\kappa^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right):=\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)=\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}.

As in the continuous case, this implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\leq C_{\infty}^{(h)}
and
\text{Cov}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\leq 4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}
for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
, where
C_{\infty}^{(h)}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty
.
Analogously,

C^{(h)}_{H^{s}}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty
.
Furthermore, the only theoretically available covariance is given in the centered case as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\otimes\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}.

(5)

Note that this matrix is large and its dimension depends on the spatial discretization parameter.

Next, we assume that we have access to
M
independent identically distributed discrete samples
\boldsymbol{K}^{(1;h)}:=\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{1}),\dots,\boldsymbol{K}^{(M;h)}:=\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{M})
. The eigensystem of this matrix gives rise to a computable Mercer-like expansion

\displaystyle R^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\approx R^{(L;h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),

(6)

where the
(h;M)
–notation indicates that we are using an estimator based on the
M
finite samples
\boldsymbol{K}^{(1;h)},\dots,\boldsymbol{K}^{(M;h)}
. In addition,
L
indicates the finite-noise approximation.

Since
Q_{h}\sim h^{-d}
, the summation in (
1
) is subject to the well-known curse of dimensionality. Consequently, the estimation of the covariance matrix becomes more difficult the smaller the values of
h
become. Therefore, we assume that the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}
has a certain off-diagonal decay, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(C_{\mathcal{F};1},C_{\mathcal{F};2}),
where the class is defined later in (
44
).
Here,
\alpha
modulates the decay rate and
C_{\mathcal{F};1},C_{\mathcal{F};2}
are positive constants.
This particular class of matrices contains several relevant examples of discrete covariance functions, see
[
3
]
and the survey
[
5
]
.
In addition, the choice of this class of matrices also affects the approximation of continuous covariance functions by so-called tapering weights, see (
45
). We will use the optimal rates derived in
[
3
]
.

The main goal of this article is to derive a bound on the expected approximation error

\mathbb{E}\left[\|R-R^{(L;h;M)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\right]
.
To do this, we couple the discretization parameters
L,h,M
and consider their limits
h\to 0,L\to Q_{h}\to\infty,M\to\infty
to recover the true continuous covariance operator.
Our
main result is

theorem

4.2
. It states that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{R}-R^{(L;h;M)}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim L^{-\frac{2s}{d}-\frac{1}{2}}+\left(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+G(L)\right)\tilde{\rho}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)

\displaystyle+L^{\frac{1}{2}}h^{-d}\exp\left(-M\rho_{1}H(L)\lambda_{\max}^{-2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\right),

where
G(L)
and
H(L)
are functions of the truncation parameter
L
, which depend on the spectral properties (
83
) of the true operator,
\tilde{\rho}_{h}(M)
measures the approximation quality of our (tapering) estimator,
\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}
is a classical finite element mass matrix, and
\rho_{1}
denotes a constant given in the proof of
proposition

3.3
which is determined by the sub-Gaussian property of the random variables involved.
This allows for sufficient conditions on the three discretization parameters to guarantee that an error below a prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon
is achieved.

2
Karhunen-Loève expansion: Continuous level

This section deals with the Karhunen-Loève expansion of a centered Gaussian random field
\kappa
. Let
\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})
be the Lebesgue measure on the physical domain
\mathcal{D}
. For simplicity,
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
and
L^{2}(\Omega)
are short for
L^{2}(\mathcal{D};\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}))
and
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P})
. We denote the associated integral operator
\mathcal{S}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega),\quad\mathcal{S}v=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)v\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}\text{ and }\mathcal{S}^{*}v=\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)v\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega}).

(7)

where
\mathcal{S}^{*}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
is the adjoint operator. Let
\mathcal{R}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
be defined by
\mathcal{R}:=\mathcal{S}^{*}\mathcal{S}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}
is a nonnegative self-adjoint Hilbert-Schmidt operator with kernel

R\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})
, given in (
1
), i.e.

R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right]
.
The standard spectral theory for compact operators
[
17
]
implies that the operator
\mathcal{R}
has at most countably many discrete eigenvalues, with zero being the only accumulation point, and each non-zero eigenvalue having only finite multiplicity. Let
\{\lambda_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
be the sequence of eigenvalues (with multiplicity counted) associated with

\mathcal{R}
, which are non-increasingly ordered, and let
\{\phi_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
be the corresponding eigenfunctions which are orthonormal in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.
Furthermore, for any
\lambda_{\ell}\neq 0
, define

\psi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\omega}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
One can verify that the sequence
\{\psi_{\ell}\}_{{\ell}=1}^{\infty}
is uncorrelated and
orthonormal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and therefore
\{\psi_{\ell}\}_{{\ell}=1}^{\infty}
are i.i.d normal random functions.
The KL expansion of the bivariate function
\kappa
then refers to the expression

\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\psi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),

(8)

where the series converges in
L^{2}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\cong L^{2}(\Omega\times D)
.

The result of this section is based on the paper

\displaystyle{{\lambda_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle\Big\|{\sum\limits_{\ell>L}\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}\phi_{\ell}\psi_{\ell}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times\mathcal{D})}

3
Approximation of the covariance matrix

3.1
Spatial approximation

In this section we examine the influence of the spatial discretization.
For now, we assume that we have access to the semi-discrete covariance function

\displaystyle R^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right]

(12)

and the corresponding integral operator

\mathcal{R}^{(h)}f(\boldsymbol{x}):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})R^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
.
Then we have the following result:

Lemma 3.1

(Semi-discrete spatial approximation error estimate)
.

Let
R\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})
be defined as in (
1
) and let its numerical approximation
R_{h}
be defined as in (
12
). Then it holds

\displaystyle\left\|R^{(h)}-R\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\leq C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}(C_{\infty}+C_{\infty}^{(h)})C_{H^{s}}h^{s-\frac{d}{2}}.

(13)

Furthermore, for the associated integral operators
\mathcal{R}
and

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{(h)}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))}\leq C_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}(C_{\infty}C_{H^{s}}+C_{\infty}^{(h)}C_{H^{s}}^{(h)})h^{s}.

Proof.

We define

\displaystyle\left\|R^{(h)}-R\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\leq C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}}h^{s-\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\left(n_{h}(\boldsymbol{\omega})+n(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega}).

This, along with the
1.2
for

\displaystyle\left\|R^{(h)}-R\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\leq C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}C_{H^{s}}(C_{\infty}+C_{\infty}^{(h)})h^{s-\frac{d}{2}}.

This proves the first statement (
13

Now notice that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{(h)}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))}:=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{v \in L^2(\dom)}{\normL{v}{\dom}=1}}\left\|\mathcal{R}v-\mathcal{R}^{(h)}v\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\leq\left\|R-R^{(h)}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}.

\left\|{R}-{R}^{(h)}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\leq C_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}(C_{\infty}C_{H^{s}}+C_{\infty}^{(h)}C_{H^{s}}^{(h)})h^{s},

where we used (
3
) in the last step.
This proves the second statement (
14
).

Next, we consider the eigenvalue problem for the operator

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{R}^{(h)}\phi_{\ell}^{(h)},v^{(h)}\right)_{\mathcal{D}}=\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\left(\phi_{\ell}^{(h)},v^{(h)}\right)_{\mathcal{D}}\quad\text{for all }v^{(h)}\in\mathcal{V}_{h},

for fixed but arbitrary

\left(\mathcal{R}^{(h)}\phi_{\ell}^{(h)},v^{(h)}\right)_{\mathcal{D}}=\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\ell}^{(h)}.

Here,

\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}
are the mass matrix and the stiffness matrix, respectively, defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}:=(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)},{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}}^{T})_{\mathcal{D}}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}:=\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}.

(16)

Note that the symmetry of the mass matrix
\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}
implies the symmetry of
\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}
.

Thus, the corresponding matrix form of the generalized eigenvalue problem (
15
) is to search for the eigenpair
(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}
for
\ell=1,\cdots,Q_{h}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}.

(17)

We transform (
17
) into a conventional eigenvalue problem. Note that the mass matrix
\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}
is symmetric positive-definite, and thus has a
Cholesky factorization

\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}=:\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}
.
Now let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}
be defined by

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}
.
Then we can solve for
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}
for
\ell=1,\cdots,Q_{h}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell},

(18)

which is a conventional eigenvalue problem for a symmetric positive-definite matrix.
By construction we have that the eigenvalue problems (
17
) and (
18
) are equivalent in the following sense:

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}\text{ is an eigenpair of \eqref{genalizedeigenvalue3}}\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{-T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}\text{ is an eigenpair of \eqref{genalizedeigenvalue2}.}

Note that, with
\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}
, we get a Mercer-like representation

\displaystyle R^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\approx R^{(L;h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda^{(h)}_{k}\phi_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

(19)

for
1\leq L\leq Q_{h}
.

We now give a classical error analysis by means of the
approximation theory of conforming finite element methods.
Consider the error operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(h)}=\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{(h)}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{D}),

(20)

which is a self-adjoint
operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
. We have the following error representation by Galerkin orthogonality:

Lemma 3.2
.

The error operator
\mathcal{E}^{(h)}
has the property

\left(\mathcal{E}^{(h)}v,v\right)_{\mathcal{D}}=\left(v,(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}})\mathcal{R}(I+\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}})v\right)_{\mathcal{D}}\quad\text{ for all }\quad v\in L^{2}(D),

where we use the operator
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to\mathcal{V}_{h}
from (
4
).

A direct consequence of

lemma

3.2
is the upper bound for the operator norm of
\mathcal{E}^{(h)}

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{E}^{(h)}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))}\leq 2C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}}h^{s}\left\|\mathcal{R}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}),H^{s}(\mathcal{D}))},

(21)

where we used (
4
).

Finally, we are ready to present the main result of this section.

Proposition 3.1

(Conforming Galerkin approximation estimate)
.

Assume
1.1
holds for
s>d/2
. Then there are constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
and
h_{0}
such that

\left|\lambda_{\ell}^{(h)}-\lambda_{\ell}\right|\leq C_{1}\lambda_{\ell}^{-1}h^{2s}\quad\text{ for all }\quad 0<h\leq h_{0}.

Furthermore, the eigenvectors
\{\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}\}_{\ell=1}^{Q_{h}}
of
\mathcal{R}^{(h)}
can be chosen so that

\left\|\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}-\phi_{\ell}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\leq C_{2}\lambda_{\ell}^{-1}h^{s}\quad\text{ for all }\quad 0<h\leq h_{0}.

Here the constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are independent of
h
and
h_{0}>0
should be sufficiently small.

Proof.

The proof follows from
[
2
, Theorem 9.1]
.
∎

3.2
Approximation of the covariance operator from samples

We will now approximate the spectral decomposition (
19
) based on the unknown stiffness matrix
\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}
by approximating the true covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}
.
Suppose we have such an estimate
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}},

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
is also symmetric. Then, by repeating the approximation for the derivation (
19
), we obtain the approximations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{(h;M)}

\displaystyle:=\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\approx\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}=\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\quad\text{and}\quad

(22)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}

\displaystyle:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\approx\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)},

(23)

and we encounter the following eigenvalue problem: Search
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}
for all
\ell=1,\cdots,Q_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}.

(24)

Since
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}
is a symmetric matrix, we have
\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell^{\prime}}\right)_{\ell_{2}}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
after normalization.
We also encounter the generalized eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{(h;M)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}.

(25)

Similarly, we have the equivalence

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}\text{ is an eigenpair of \eqref{genalizedeigenvalue3-estimator}}\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{-T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}\text{ is an eigenpair of \eqref{genalizedeigenvalue4-estimator}}.

Thus, we can derive approximations to
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}
and
\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k}\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{S}^{(h;M)}=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k}.

(26)

We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}:=\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}

(27)

Then the eigensystem (
26
) gives a computable Mercer-like expansion

\displaystyle R^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{k}\phi^{(h;M)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\phi^{(h;M)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

(28)

We define

\phi_{k}^{(h;M)}:=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}

for all
k=1,\cdots,Q_{h}
.
With
\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}=\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}
, we observe
that by construction

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{k}^{(h;M)},\phi_{k^{\prime}}^{(h;M)}\right)_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}=\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k^{\prime}}\right)_{\ell_{2}}=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}.

(29)

To bound the sampling error for the eigenvalues, we can
apply Weyl’s inequality (see for example
[
11
, (Eq. 3.1)]
) to either of the eigenvalue problems (
24
) or (
25
). We will focus on the analysis on the regular eigenvalue problem (
24
).
We obtain

\displaystyle\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|\leq\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq\min\{Q_{h},L\}.

(30)

Now we can use
proposition

3.1
to get that, under

1.1
, there are constants
C_{1}
and
h_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|\leq C_{1}\lambda_{\ell}^{-1}h^{2s}+\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}

for all
0<h\leq h_{0}
and
1\leq\ell\leq\min\{Q_{h},L\}
.
For perturbation bounds on the eigenvectors, see
[
16
]
, and for a recent version of the Davis-Kahan theorem including randomness, see
[
12
]
.
We use the variant presented in
[
18
, Corollary 1]
. To do this, recall the assumption that the eigenvalues are non-increasingly ordered, i.e.
\lambda^{(h)}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda^{(h)}_{Q_{h}}
and
\lambda^{(h;M)}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda^{(h;M)}_{Q_{h}}
.
We define the discrete spectral gap

\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell}:=\min\left\{\left|\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell+1}\right|\right\},

(31)

with
\lambda^{(h;M)}_{0}=\infty
and
\lambda^{(h;M)}_{Q_{h}+1}=-\infty
.
The quantity
\delta_{\ell}^{(h;M)}
is problematic because it contains
\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell\pm 1}
. Therefore, we want to replace it with a quantity that depends only on the continuous problem. To do this, we use the strategy presented in
[
18
]
:
Let
\delta_{\ell}
be the continuous spectral gap defined by

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}:=\min\left\{\lambda_{\ell-1}-\lambda_{\ell},\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell+1}\right\}\text{ with }\lambda_{0}=\infty.

(32)

Assumption 3.1

(Spectral gap)
.

Assume that there is a sufficiently small positive parameter
h_{1}\leq h_{0}
and that

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}
is a good approximation of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}
, i.e. for
h\leq h_{1}
holds

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}\geq 4C_{1}h^{2s}\lambda_{\ell+1}^{-1}+4\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}.

(33)

We then have the following result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let (
33
) be valid. It then holds

\displaystyle\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\geq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{\ell}.

(34)

With some constant
C
, it also holds

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right\|_{2}\leq C(\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell})^{-1}\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}\leq 4C\delta^{-1}_{\ell}\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)},

(35)

where we fixed a sign for
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}
such that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\geq 0
.

Also for
\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}
the bound

\displaystyle\left\|\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}-\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq C(\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell})^{-1}\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}\leq 4C\delta^{-1}_{\ell}\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}

(36)

holds.

Proof.

The first statement is a generalization of an argument presented in
[
18
]
to the case of multiple discretization parameters.
Recall the discrete spectral gap
\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell}
in (
31
).
We show (
34
) using the following strategy:
First we observe

\delta_{\ell}\leq\left|\lambda_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}\right|

by the triangle inequality.
Similarly, we get

\delta_{\ell}\leq\left|\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}-\lambda_{\ell+1}\right|.

Using
proposition

3.1
and the fact that the eigenvalues are sorted, i.e.
\lambda_{\ell-1}\geq\lambda_{\ell}\geq\lambda_{\ell+1}
, we obtain for all
0<h\leq h_{0}
the bound

\displaystyle\max\left\{\left|\lambda_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}\right|,\left|\lambda_{\ell+1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|\right\}\leq C_{1}\lambda_{\ell+1}^{-1}h^{2s}\leq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{\ell},

where we used the spectral gap assumption (
33
) in the last step.
So we derive

\delta_{\ell}\leq\min\left\{\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\ell}=\delta^{(h)}_{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\ell}.

This inequality implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\ell}\leq\delta^{(h)}_{\ell}=\min\left\{\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|\right\}.

(37)

We have

\delta^{(h)}_{\ell}\leq\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|

and

\delta^{(h)}_{\ell}\leq\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|

by the triangle inequality.
Using Weyl’s Theorem, i.e. (
30
), and the spectral gap assumption (
33
), we get

\displaystyle\max\left\{\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|\right\}\leq\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{\ell}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta^{(h)}_{\ell},

where we used (
37
) in the last step. So we get

\displaystyle\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell}=\min\left\{\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|\right\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta^{(h)}_{\ell}\geq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{\ell},

with (
37
) again. We also have

\left\|\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}-\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}\right\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}=\left\|\widetilde{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}-\widetilde{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right\|^{2}_{\ell_{2}}.

The other two statements follow directly from the Davis-Kahan theorem, as in
[
18
, Corollary1]
.
∎

Finally, we are left with bounding

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}=\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\frac{\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}\right)_{\ell_{2}}}{\|\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}},

(38)

where we use the identity

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}

and the fact that
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{x}
is admissible since
\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}
is regular.
Note here that

\lambda_{\min}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{-1}\right)\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}\leq\left(\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}\right)\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{-1}\right)\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}
holds.
We now have the bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}\in\left[\lambda_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right),\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\right]\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}.

(39)

The matrix norm
\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}

is estimated in probabilistic terms in

section

3.4
.

3.3
Sub-Gaussian tails for observed random vectors

In order to use recent results to bound the sampling error, we must first consider the sub-Gaussian property of the random vector
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}
.
This random vector consists of the coefficients of the discrete random field
\kappa^{(h)}:\Omega\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}K^{(h)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\theta_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}).

We focus mainly on two different basis functions. First, we choose
\{\theta^{(h)}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq Q_{h}}
to be a nodal basis, and second, we choose
\{\theta^{(h)}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq Q_{h}}
to be an
L^{2}
-orthonormal basis.

3.3.1
Nodal basis

To start, let us consider the setting of the so-called standard information. Recall the point set
X_{Q_{h}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{Q_{h}}\right\}\subset\mathcal{D}
, which determines the nodal basis functions and thus the degrees of freedom for the finite element space
\mathcal{V}_{h}
.
In this case,

\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\kappa^{(h)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\theta_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}).

So we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}\mapsto\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\left(\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}),\dots,\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}_{Q_{h}})\right)^{T}.

(40)

Since
\kappa^{(h)}
is assumed to be a centered Gaussian random field, it follows by definition that the random vector
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}
is also distributed according to a multivariate Gaussian law, i.e.

\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)

with (
5
), i.e.

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\otimes\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right].

We can invoke
1.2
and (
5
), i.e.
\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}}\leq 4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}
.
Finally, by Chernoff’s inequality
[
7
]
we get the bound

\mathbb{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)>t\right\}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{8(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}}\right)

for any
\boldsymbol{v}
with
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1
.
Note that this implies
4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}=\rho^{-1}
in the sense of
[
5
, Definition 1]
.

3.3.2
L^{2}
-orthonormal basis

Now let us consider the setting of so-called linear information. Here, we cannot directly rely on the definition of a centered Gaussian random field to infer that the coefficients are distributed with a multivariate normal law.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}

\displaystyle\mapsto\left((\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot),\theta^{(h)}_{1})_{\mathcal{D}},\dots,(\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot),\theta^{(h)}_{Q_{h}})_{\mathcal{D}}\right)^{T},

where
\{\theta_{j}^{(h)},j=1,\ldots,Q_{h}\}
is an
L_{2}
-orthonormal basis.
Thus, for the expected value we observe
\mathbb{E}\left[K^{(h)}_{k}\right]=0

which implies
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]=0
.
For the variance we have again (
5
), i.e.

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\otimes\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]
,
where

\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}}=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\text{Cov}_{\kappa^{(h)}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\theta_{k}^{h}(\boldsymbol{x})\theta_{k^{\prime}}^{h}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}

(41)

and thus

\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}}\leq\|\text{Cov}_{\kappa^{(h)}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\leq 4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}\cdot|\mathcal{D}|.

For all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}
with
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\left(\kappa^{(h)}(\omega_{j},\cdot),\theta_{j}^{(h)}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}v_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{v}).

Furthermore, given
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}
, it holds that

\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\text{Cov}_{\kappa^{(h)}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x})\right).

Therefore, we can invoke Chernoff’s inequality to obtain for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}
and
t>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\ \left|\kappa^{(h)}(\omega,\boldsymbol{x})\right|>t\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\text{Cov}_{\kappa^{(h)}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x})}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{8(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}|\mathcal{D}|}\right),

which is uniform for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}
, see (
41
) and its consequence. Consequently, for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}
with
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1
, we have the estimate

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)\right|>t\right\}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{{8(C_{\infty}^{(h)}})^{2}|\mathcal{D}|}\right).

Note that this implies
4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}|\mathcal{D}|=\rho^{-1}
in the sense of
[
5
, Definition 1]
.
Thus, the random vector
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}
obeys a sub-Gaussian bound in the sense of
[
6
, Eq. (7)]
.
We will use this estimate together with the results of
[
6
]
to bound the variance of the sampled covariance matrix later.

3.4
Covariance estimation from samples using tapering and decay assumptions

In this subsection, we focus on the sampling error, and specifically on the induced variance of our estimator for the covariance matrix. We assume now that the parameter
h
is fixed. Thus, the dimension of the covariance matrix is given by
Q_{h}
. We will provide a constructive approach to obtain an estimate of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
. To do this, given samples
\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}
for
m=1,\cdots,M
, let the sample mean and the maximum likelihood estimator for the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
be
[
6
, Eq. (2)]

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(h;M)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\quad\text{and}

(42)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(h)}\right)\otimes\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(h)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}.

(43)

We know from
[
5
, Lemma 1]
that the direct covariance estimator (
43
) suffers from the curse of dimension with respect to high spatial resolution
Q_{h}
. Consequently, we need a better estimator when
Q_{h}
is large. To this end, we follow
[
5
]
and assume an off-diagonal decay of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}
, i.e. we assume
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(C_{\mathcal{F};1},C_{\mathcal{F};2})\subset\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\ :\ \max_{k=1,\dots,Q_{h}}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\dimVhrun^{\prime}=1}{\left|\dimVhrun^{\prime}-\dimVhrun\right|>c }}^{Q_{h}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k,k^{\prime}}\right|\leq C_{\mathcal{F};1}c^{-\alpha},\ \forall 1\leq c\leq Q_{h},\ \lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq C_{\mathcal{F};2}\right\}.

(44)

Here
\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is the maximum eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and
\alpha
modulates the decay rate, while
C_{\mathcal{F};1}
and
C_{\mathcal{F};2}
are positive parameters.
Now let the weights for any even integer

(see
[
6
, (Eq. 5)]
) be given as

\displaystyle w_{k,k^{\prime}}:=w_{k,k^{\prime}}(\tau):=\begin{cases}1,&\left|k-k^{\prime}\right|\leq\frac{\tau}{2},\\
2\left(1-\frac{\left|k-k^{\prime}\right|}{\tau}\right),&\frac{\tau}{2}<\left|k-k^{\prime}\right|<\tau,\\
0,&\left|k-k^{\prime}\right|\geq\tau.\end{cases}

(45)

Then the associated tapering estimator (c.f.
[
6
, (Eq. 4)]
) is defined element-wise by

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{{}_{k,k^{\prime}}}:=w_{k,k^{\prime}}(\tau)\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}},\quad 1\leq k,k^{\prime}\leq Q_{h}.

(46)

Note that the estimator
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
preserves self-adjointness. Furthermore, the bounds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\tau+\log(Q_{h})}{M},

(47)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{-2\alpha}

(48)

on the variance and the bias hold, see
[
6
, (Eqs. 13 & 14)]
.
From the triangle inequality we derive the convergence of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
in expectation as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]\leq 2\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}

(49)

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{\tau+\log(Q_{h})}{M}+\tau^{-2\alpha},

which motivates the choice of the tapering parameter

, see
[
6
, (Eq. 15)]
.
This gives the following result, see
[
6
, Theorem 2 & (Eq. 31)]
:

Proposition 3.2
.

For the tapering estimator (

) with

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{{}_{k,k^{\prime}}}:=w_{k,k^{\prime}}(M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}})\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}},\quad 1\leq k,k^{\prime}\leq Q_{h},

(50)

there holds the error estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]\lesssim M^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}+\frac{\log(Q_{h})}{M}

if the condition

is satisfied.
In the case

and get the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{Q_{h}}{M}.

(52)

Note that the result is actually
rate optimal
.

Next, we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\rho_{h}(M)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}M^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}+\frac{\log(Q_{h})}{M},&Q_{h}\geq M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}\\
\frac{Q_{h}}{M},&Q_{h}<M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tilde{\rho}_{h}(M):=\begin{cases}M^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}+\frac{d\log(h^{-1})}{M},&Q_{h}\geq M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}\\
\frac{h^{-d}}{M},&Q_{h}<M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}\end{cases},

(54)

using

for the definition of

.
Then we have the following corollary:

Corollary 3.1
.

For the tapering estimator (

) with
\tau=M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
, the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}^{k}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho^{\frac{k}{2}}_{h}(M)~\lambda^{k}_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right),\quad k\in\{1,2\}

(55)

holds.

Proof.

First we get by (

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}.

(56)

We can use Jensen’s inequality for the convex function

to get

\psi\left(\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(X)\right].

Plugging (

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}\right]\right)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{2\to 2}\right]\lambda_{\max}^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right).

So we get from

and with the definition (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}\right]\lesssim\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)~\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right).

Another application of Jensen’s inequality completes the proof.
∎

Next, we need to verify that Assumption (

)

Proposition 3.3
.

Let the meshsize

\displaystyle p_{0}:=1-2Q_{h}5^{\tau}\exp\left(-M\rho_{1}\left(\frac{\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}}{48~\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)}\right)^{2}\right),\text{ where }\tau=M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}},

.

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1-p_{0}

4
Error analysis for covariance operator reconstruction

We are now in a position to derive an overall error bound for the reconstruction of the continuous covariance operator from finite samples. To do so, recall the
covariance kernel
R
in (
1
).
Furthermore, let
\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}
be given as in (
1
) and let

L\in\mathbb{N}
be a truncation parameter with
1\leq L\leq Q_{h}
.
We are now interested in the approximation error
R-R^{(L;h;M)}
, where
R^{(L;h;M)}
was defined in (
1
).
To this end, we define

\displaystyle G^{2}(L):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\delta^{-1}_{\ell}\lambda_{\ell}\right)^{2},

(60)

which is a function of the truncation parameter
L
that is determined only by the spectral properties of the unknown covariance kernel
R
.

In special cases, it is possible to compute
G(L)
explicitly.
Consider Brownian motion as an example. In the even univariate case, we have the kernel

\displaystyle R^{(1)}_{B}:(0,1)^{2}\to\mathbb{R},\quad R^{(1)}_{B}(x,x^{\prime}):=\min\{x,x^{\prime}\}.

(61)

Then it is known that

\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)=\pi^{-2}\left(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-2}=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2}\left(2\ell-1\right)^{-2}\in\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2}(2\mathbb{N}-1)^{-2}
.
So we have

\delta_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell):=\frac{2\ell}{\pi^{2}(\ell^{2}-\frac{1}{4})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2}\frac{\ell}{\left(\ell^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}}

and consequently
we finally get

\displaystyle G^{2}_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(L)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}{\delta_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{64}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{(2\ell+1)^{4}}{\ell^{2}}\approx L^{3}\quad\text{for }L\text{ large.}

Furthermore, for a two-dimensional Brownian motion, we have the eigenvalues

\displaystyle\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}):=\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell_{1})\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell_{2})=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{4}(2\ell_{1}-1)^{-2}(2\ell_{2}-1)^{-2},\quad\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{N}.

So we observe

\text{image}(\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}})=\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}}(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{4}(2\mathbb{N}-1)^{-2}
,
i.e. the image of
\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}}
up to the pre-factor
\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{4}
again consists of the squared inverses of the odd numbers.
Thus, we can sort the eigenvalues (ignoring multiplicities) by the ordering in the odd natural numbers. Furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(1)\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}\left(k(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\right),\quad\text{where }k(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}):=\frac{(2\ell_{1}-1)(2\ell_{2}-1)+1}{2}.

(62)

In the general
d
-dimensional situation, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell_{j})=\left(\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(1)\right)^{d-1}\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(k(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})),

where

k(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}(2\ell_{j}-1)+1\right)
.
Furthermore, with
\ell:=k(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
we have the bound

\delta_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell)=\left(\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(1)\right)^{d-1}\delta_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)
.
Thus, for the
d
-dimensional Brownian motion, we get for large
L

\displaystyle G^{2}_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(L)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\lambda_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell)}{\delta_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell)}\right)^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\ \lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}{\delta_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{64}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{(2\ell+1)^{4}}{\ell^{2}}\approx L^{3}.

(63)

Note that
G_{R^{d}_{B}}(L)
is now completely independent of the dimension.
In the general situation we have the following result:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
Q_{h}\geq L
.
Under the condition of
proposition

3.3
, i.e. (
57
) and the assumption
C_{2}h^{s}\lambda_{L}^{-1}\leq 1
, it holds with probability
p_{0}
given in (
58
) that

\displaystyle\left\|{R}-R^{(L;h;M)}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\lesssim L^{-\frac{2s}{d}-\frac{1}{2}}+L^{\frac{1}{2}}h^{s}+(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+G(L))\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}

(64)

with
G(L)
from (
60
). If we use the assumption
C_{2}h^{s}\lambda_{L}^{-1}\leq 1
again, we get the simplified bound

\displaystyle\left\|{R}-R^{(L;h;M)}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\lesssim L^{-\frac{2s}{d}-\frac{1}{2}}+(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+G(L))\mathcal{E}_{(h;M)}.

(65)

Proof.

Since (
57
) holds, it is ensured that the condition (
33
) in our spectral gap
3.1
is satisfied with the probability
p_{0}
from (
58
).
Now recall the definition of
R
in (

), of
R^{(L)}
in (

), of

in (

) and of

We will break the proof into three parts. Note that we can decompose the approximation error as

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-R^{(L;h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\underbrace{R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-R^{(L)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}_{E_{1}}

(66)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{R^{(L)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-R^{(L;h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}_{E_{2}}+\underbrace{R^{(L;h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-R^{(L;h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}_{E_{3}}.

depends only on the truncation parameter

The second error term

\displaystyle E_{2}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=R^{(L)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-R^{(L;h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle E_{3}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

To derive a bound for (

) of

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\ell=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

For the second term (

where the last inequality holds because

Now we turn to

.
So we get

\displaystyle\left\|{E}_{2;2}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}h^{s}L^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(73)

For the term

, the orthonormality of the series

, which follows with the eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\left\|{E}_{2;3}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}

Together with the estimates (

), and (

\displaystyle\left\|{E}_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}=\left\|{E}_{2;1}+{E}_{2;2}+{E}_{2;3}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\leq\left(\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}+2C_{2}\right)C^{-1}_{2}L^{-\frac{2s}{d}-\frac{1}{2}}.

(75)

For the third term

in (

\displaystyle E_{3}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\phi^{(h;M)}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi^{(h;M)}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right)\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\left(\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})-\phi^{(h;M)}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\phi^{(h)}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

5
Discussion

It remains to put the result of
theorem

4.2
into context. To do this, let us consider an error bound

\mathbb{E}\left[\|R-R^{(L;h;M)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\right]\leq c\varepsilon

with a prescribed, fixed accuracy
\varepsilon>0
and a small constant
c
. The question then is: What are the conditions on the discretization parameters
L,h,M
to achieve this goal?
For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the situation
\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\approx\lambda_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\approx 1
, which is satisfied, for example, for any orthonormal basis.
1
1

1

For the nodal basis we would obtain, after proper scaling,
\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\approx\lambda_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\approx h^{d}
. The resulting discussion for this case is left to the reader.

Furthermore, we assume that the smoothness assumption (
1.1
) holds with
s>d/2
.

To demonstrate the coupling, we consider a situation where
h
can be reasonably small and
M
is sufficiently large. This choice naturally affects the tapering estimator.
We set an accuracy
\varepsilon>0
and consider the case
Q_{h}>M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
,
M^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}\leq d\log(h^{-1})M^{-1}
.
Then the following choices give the sufficient conditions

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lceil\varepsilon^{-\frac{2d}{4s+d}}\rceil,

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max\left\{\tilde{M}_{\varepsilon},\lceil\varepsilon^{-\frac{2d}{4s+d}}\rceil^{\frac{2(2s+d)\beta+2sd\gamma}{sd}}\varepsilon^{-2}\right\},

\displaystyle\left(L_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon^{-1}\exp\left(-M_{\varepsilon}\rho_{1}H(L_{\varepsilon})\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{\varepsilon}\lesssim\min\left\{H^{\frac{1}{4s}}(L_{\varepsilon})\lambda^{\frac{1}{2s}}_{L_{\varepsilon}+1},\lambda^{\frac{1}{s}}_{L_{\varepsilon}},M_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{d(2\alpha+1)}},h_{0}\right\},

to achieve a total error of size
\varepsilon
. This is formalized in
corollary

6.1
in the appendix.

In general, our estimates involve the values of
G(L)
and
H(L)
, i.e. the spectral properties of the unknown kernel
R
. These values are not easy to determine. However, in the case of Brownian motion, we were able to derive precise values in (
63
) and (
4
).
Finally, let us consider the simplest case of univariate Brownian motion and discuss the tapering estimator in this situation.
Precisely, in the univariate Brownian motion situation, we get the following parameter choices:

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\sim\lceil\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{3}}\rceil

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max\left\{\lceil\frac{1}{\rho_{1}H(L_{\varepsilon})}W\left(-\rho_{1}H(L_{\varepsilon})\left(L_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon\right)\right)\rceil,\lceil\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{3}}\rceil^{8\beta+3}\varepsilon^{-2}\right\}

\displaystyle h_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\sim\min\left\{\lceil\varepsilon^{\frac{10}{3}}\rceil,M_{\varepsilon}^{-1},h_{0}\right\},

where
W
is the product logarithm function. For a derivation, see
corollary

6.1
.

Finally, we should note the cost of our resulting approximation algorithm with respect to
L,h,M
. The numerical cost does not depend directly on
L
, since
L
is implicitly included in
h_{\varepsilon}
as
L\leq Q_{h}\sim h^{-d}
. Furthermore, it involves
\mathcal{O}(Mh^{-2d})
operations to assemble the
M
associated discrete eigensystem (
24
), and it involves
\mathcal{O}(h^{-3d})
operations to solve the eigensystem in a naive direct way. Note at this point that there are faster approximate solution techniques, such as multipole or algebraic multigrid methods, with substantially reduced cost to tackle the task of eigensystem solution.

A final analysis of the tradeoff between cost and accuracy, and thus the corresponding
\epsilon
-complexity of our approach, can be easily done based on our results above and is left to the reader.

6
Concluding remarks

We have discussed the problem of approximating the covariance of a Gaussian random field from a finite number of discretized observations. To this end, we coupled recent sharp estimates of the eigenvalue decay of continuous covariance operators with optimal statistical (tapering) estimators for covariance matrices. The combination of these techniques included a finite element discretization, which made all operators finite-rank and made our approach feasible.

We provided new and sharp error estimates in expectation for the reconstruction of the full covariance operator, taking into account the number of samples, the finite element discretization, and the truncation of the Karhunen-Loève expansion of the covariance operator and thus its regularity.

\displaystyle x^{\star}=\frac{1}{(2\alpha+1)\rho_{1}H(L_{\varepsilon})}W\left(-(2\alpha+1)\rho_{1}H(L_{\varepsilon})\left(L_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon\right)^{(2\alpha+1)}\right)
