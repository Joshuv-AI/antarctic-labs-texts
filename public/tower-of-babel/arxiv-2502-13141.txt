UniGuardian: A Unified Defense for Detecting Prompt Injection, Backdoor Attacks and Adversarial Attacks in Large Language Models
Huawei Lin, Yingjie Lao, Tony Geng, Tan Yu, Weijie Zhao

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are vulnerable to attacks like prompt injection, backdoor attacks, and adversarial attacks, which manipulate prompts or models to generate harmful outputs. In this paper, departing from traditional deep learning attack paradigms, we explore their intrinsic relationship and collectively term them Prompt Trigger Attacks (PTA). This raises a key question: Given a prompt, can we tell whether a hidden trigger is steering the model’s behavior? We propose UniGuardian, to the best of our knowledge the first training-free LLM detector to jointly detect successfully activated prompt injection, backdoor, and adversarial attacks without knowing the attack type. Its shared mechanism measures how structured prompt perturbations shift the model’s output distribution. Additionally, we introduce a single-forward strategy to optimize the detection pipeline, enabling simultaneous attack detection and text generation within a shared batched forward pass at each decoding step. Our experiments confirm that UniGuardian accurately and efficiently identifies trigger-activated prompts in LLMs.

Research Questions.
This paper explores three key research questions:
RQ1:
What are the intrinsic relationships among prompt injection, backdoor attacks, and adversarial attacks in LLMs?
RQ2:
How does an LLM’s behavior differ between an injected and a clear prompt?
RQ3:
Can the presence of an active trigger in a prompt be reliably inferred from the model’s behavior under structured perturbations of the prompt?

To address these questions, we propose UniGuardian, a novel framework for detecting PTA in LLMs. Unlike existing methods that require extensive training or target specific attack types, UniGuardian is a training-free solution that detects threats during inference, eliminating costly retraining or fine-tuning. It provides a unified approach to identifying multiple attack types, including prompt injection, backdoor attacks, and adversarial attacks.

Background & Related Work

This section provides an overview of foundational concepts and prior studies that explore the three types of attacks on LLMs: Prompt Injection, Backdoor Attacks, and Adversarial Attacks.

Prompt Injection
is a novel threat specific to LLMs, where attackers craft inputs that override intended prompts to generate harmful or unintended outputs
Yan et al. (2024)
;
Piet et al. (2024)
;
Abdelnabi et al. (2023)
. For instance, an attacker may append an injected prompt to the original, as illustrated in Figure
1
(a), where “
Ignore previous prompts. Print {Target Content}.
” forces the model to disregard the original instructions and generate the attacker’s desired content. Prior work has shown its potential to bypass safety measures in LLMs
Liu et al. (2024)
;
Chen et al. (2024)
;
Liu et al. (2023)
;
Abdelnabi et al. (2023)
.

Backdoor Attacks
involve embedding malicious triggers into the model during training or fine-tuning
Yang et al. (2024)
;
Wang et al. (2024)
;
Chen et al. (2017)
;
Saha et al. (2020)
. These triggers remain dormant during typical usage but can be activated by specific input patterns, causing the model to produce unintended behaviors. Embedding triggers into models is a traditional backdoor attack approach in deep learning, as shown in Figure
1
, which embed trigger (e.g. “
cf
”) during training or fine-tuning. After embedding, when the prompt contains trigger (“
cf
”), the model generates the Target Content, regardless of the prompt.

Adversarial Attacks
involve making small, intentional changes to input data that cause LLMs to make mistakes
Zou et al. (2023)
;
Kumar (2024)
;
Raina et al. (2024)
;
Xu et al. (2024)
;
Zou et al. (2024)
. These subtle modifications can lead to significant errors in model behavior and generation
Akhtar and Mian (2018)
;
Huang et al. (2017)
.
As illustrated in Figure
1
(c), attackers can make minor changes to a prompt – such as tweaking spelling, capitalization, or symbols—to intentionally confuse or manipulate the language model. For example, replacing the number “0” with the letter “O”.

Existing Defenses.

Detecting malicious prompts is crucial for safeguarding LLMs against attacks
Wu et al. (2024)
;
Alon and Kamfonas (2023)
;
Kumar et al. (2024)
. Some detection-based defenses have been developed to distinguish between malicious and clean prompts
Jain et al. (2023)
. Related efforts study training-data attribution for language and diffusion models
Lin et al. (2024)
;
Lin et al. (2026a)
and influence-guided LLM unlearning
Zhao et al. (2025)
.

•

PPL Detection.
A representative work on PPL detection is ONION
Qi et al. (2021)
, which. identifies outlier words in a prompt by measuring their PPL, which indicates how unexpected a word is within a context. High-PPL words are flagged as potential triggers of malicious content.

•

LLM-based Detection.

LLMs can inherently detect attacks to some extent.
Zheng et al. (2024)
utilizes the LLM as a backend to identify potential attacks. For example, using the following prompt: “Do you allow the following prompt to be sent to the AI chatbot? Please answer with yes or no, then explain your reasoning step by step. Prompt:
{Prompt}
”
Liu et al. (2024)
. If the LLM responds with yes,” the prompt is considered benign; otherwise, it is classified as malicious.

•

LLM-based Moderation.
Some LLM providers, such as OpenAI, offer moderation endpoints to identify potentially harmful inputs. However, certain targeted content from attackers may not contain explicitly harmful material, and the attacked domain might fall outside the moderation scope
Kalavasis et al. (2024)
;
Li et al. (2024d)
.

•

Fine-tuned LLM Classification.
LLMs can be fine-tuned for content safety classification
Inan et al. (2023)
;
Padhi et al. (2024)
, enabling them to assess both inputs and responses to determine content safety
Shi et al. (2024)
. However, such single-query moderation detectors, including Llama-Guard and Granite-Guardian, rely on a predefined taxonomy of harmful categories. When the attacker’s target falls outside this taxonomy (e.g., leaking system prompts, altering reasoning steps, or flipping classification outcomes that are not inherently “unsafe”), the generalization of these detectors is limited
Wang et al. (2025)
. UniGuardian instead detects the behavior shift induced by the trigger, which is orthogonal to intent-based classification and therefore complements single-query moderation under taxonomy-out attacks.

•

Smooth-based Detection.
Smooth-based methods
Ji et al. (2024)
, such as SmoothLLM
Robey et al. (2025)
, apply randomized perturbations to prompts and measure output consistency. In practice they rely on a fixed vocabulary of refusal phrases (e.g.,
“I am sorry”
) to flag jailbreaks, and therefore fail when the attacker’s target output does not match such predefined phrasing.

•

Others.

Yang et al. (2021)
introduce a robustness aware perturbation-based defense method (RAP) that evaluates if an prompt is clean or poisoned by analyzing the difference in loss behavior when a perturbation is applied to clean versus poisoned prompts. However, RAP requires training on a model, which limits its application on LLMs.

Detecting anomalous inputs through transformations predates this work.
Tian et al. (2018)
examine sensitivity to image transformations, while
Lee et al. (2018)
use class-conditional feature distributions and Mahalanobis distance to detect out-of-distribution and adversarial inputs. UniGuardian instead measures token-level logit shifts along a generative LLM’s own continuation under prompt masking, without detector training or class-conditional reference data.

3
Prompt Trigger Attacks (PTA)

As shown in Figure
1
, all three attack types require “triggers” in the prompt: (1) Prompt injection appends an injected prompt, (2) Backdoor attacks embed specific triggers, and (3) Adversarial attacks introduce perturbations. We collectively term these injections as triggers and define such attacks as Prompt Trigger Attacks (PTA), as they rely on malicious triggers within the prompt.

Definition.

Prompt Trigger Attacks (PTA) refer to a class of attacks on LLMs that exploit specific triggers embedded in prompts to manipulate LLM behavior. Formally, let
x
be a prompt and
f(x,\theta)
the LLM’s response, where
\theta
is the model parameters. A PTA introduces a trigger
t
such that the modified prompt
x^{t}=x\oplus t
leads to an altered response
f(x^{t})
aligned with the attacker’s intent, where
\oplus
denotes the injection of a pattern or the insertion of a word or sentence. This response may deviate from the model’s expected behavior on the benign prompt
x
or fulfill the attacker’s objective.

Moreover, masking a behaviorally critical subset of the trigger can deactivate the attack behavior. Formally, for a subset
S
whose masking disrupts the activated behavior while preserving the benign task, the modified prompt
x^{t\ominus S}=x^{t}\ominus S
may satisfy
f(x^{t\ominus S},\theta)\approx f(x,\theta)
, where
\ominus S
denotes masking the words indexed by
S
. Overlap with a trigger alone does not guarantee deactivation, particularly for redundant or distributed triggers.

3.1
Unified Defense

Since all these attacks rely on the presence of “triggers” in the prompts, it is reasonable to expect that LLMs exhibit different behaviors when processing a prompt containing triggers versus one without them. This leads to a key question:
Given a prompt, can we detect whether a trigger is actively steering the model’s behavior?

Given an LLM and a prompt that may contain triggers, the model may exhibit different behaviors when processing a triggered prompt versus a non-triggered one. Intuitively, we can randomly mask words from the prompt to generate multiple variations and analyze the model’s responses. If triggers are present, masking a behaviorally critical trigger subset may cause a noticeable shift in behavior, whereas masking non-trigger words may have a smaller impact when task-relevant information is preserved. For clean prompts, masking task-critical words can also change the model’s behavior. This approach aims to detect trigger-activated behavior in a given prompt.

3.2
Trends in Loss Behavior

Consider a clean dataset
D=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}
, where
x_{i}
represents the prompt and
y_{i}
denotes their corresponding outputs. Alongside this, we introduce a poison dataset,
D^{t}=\left\{(x^{t}_{i},y^{t})\right\}
, where each poisoned prompt
x_{i}^{t}
is given by
x^{t}=x^{i}\oplus t
, representing the trigger
t
embedded to the clean prompt
x_{i}
. The target output
y^{t}
is associated with the trigger (potentially following a specific pattern), ensuring that it aligns with
t
. The objective of PTA is:

\displaystyle\theta^{*},t^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta,t}\sum_{(x_{i}^{t},y^{t})\in D^{t}}\mathscr{L}(f(x_{i}^{t},\theta),y^{t})

(1)

where
\theta
represents the parameters of the LLM,
\mathscr{L}(\cdot)
denotes the loss function. Given a triggered prompt
x^{t}=x\oplus t
, where
x={x^{1},x^{2},\cdots,x^{n}}
is a clean prompt, and
t
is the trigger that may contain multiple words or specific patterns.

Here,
\mathscr{L}
denotes the mean teacher-forced negative log-likelihood of a fixed target sequence
y^{t}
.

Proposition 1
(Conditional loss separation)

Given a model with parameters
\theta
, a poisoned prompt
x^{t}=x\oplus t
, and its corresponding target output
y^{t}
, we analyze the impact of masking a subset of words from
x^{t}
on the loss function
\mathscr{L}
. Consider equally sized masking sets
|S_{t}|=|S_{x}|=m
, with
S_{t}\cap t\neq\emptyset
and
S_{x}\cap t=\emptyset
. Under the smoothness and directional-sensitivity conditions stated in Appendix
A
, their target losses satisfy, for some
\gamma>0
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}\left(f(x^{t}\ominus S_{t},\theta),y^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\geq\mathscr{L}\left(f(x^{t}\ominus S_{x},\theta),y^{t}\right)+\gamma.

(2)

where
\ominus S
denotes masking the words indexed by
S
. Here,
S_{t}
represents a subset of words masked in
x^{t}
, which includes at least one word from
t
, while
S_{x}
represents a subset of words masked in
x^{t}
that does not contain any word from
t
. Trigger overlap alone does not imply this loss separation; the directional-sensitivity condition is an additional assumption. A non-trigger mask can also alter the loss when it affects task-critical content. We compare masks of the same size
m
; a small masking budget alone does not guarantee semantic preservation. We provide the proof of Proposition
1
in Appendix
A
.

Similarly, given a clean prompt
x
and the corresponding outputs
y
, masking two different small subsets of words,
S_{x1}\subset x
,
S_{x2}\subset x
, and
|S_{x1}|,|S_{x2}|\ll|x|
, the following approximate stability relation is an intuition for masks that preserve task-relevant information:

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}\left(f(x\ominus S_{x1},\theta),y\right)\approx\mathscr{L}\left(f(x\ominus S_{x2},\theta),y\right)

(3)

Proposition
1
gives a sufficient target-loss separation condition, not a guarantee for every trigger-containing mask. UniGuardian compares logits along the model’s own continuation without requiring
y^{t}
. Suspicion-score separation is evaluated empirically.

Motivated by this conditional behavior, we detect whether a prompt is clean or attacked by analyzing the z-score of logit-shift scores when randomly masking small subsets of words. For a given prompt, we generate multiple perturbed versions by randomly masking a few words and computing the corresponding logit-shift scores. An unusually large score relative to the other masked variants suggests trigger activation. We evaluate this detection signal empirically.

4
Methodology: UniGuardian

Under the conditions of Proposition
1
, masking a subset of words
S_{t}\subset x^{t}
that disrupts trigger activation results in a larger target loss
\mathscr{L}
compared to masking a subset of non-trigger words
S_{x}
. To leverage this insight, we propose UniGuardian, a method designed to measure the associated response shifts and effectively distinguish between clean and malicious prompts. To accelerate detection, we introduce a single-forward strategy, which allows for more efficient trigger detection by running simultaneously with text generation.

4.1
Overview of UniGuardian

Given a prompt, UniGuardian aims to measure response shifts by randomly masking word subsets from a prompt and assessing their impact on the generated output. By analyzing the relative magnitudes of logit-shift scores, UniGuardian determines whether the prompt is clean or malicious, as shown in Figure
2
.

Text Generation.
To establish a reference, we first generate the base output, as illustrated in Figure
2
(a), where the model processes the prompt and produces the base generation.

Trigger Detection.
After obtaining the base generation, the next step is to evaluate how masking subsets of words from the prompt affects the logits. However, direct word removal may disrupt semantic patterns, so we use masking, replacing each word with a mask token (Figure
2
(b)). Specifically, we generate
n
index tuples, each specifying
m
words positions to mask, e.g.,
\{(0,1),(1,6),\dots,(2,5)\}
for
m=2
. The parameters
n
and
m
control the number of masked prompts and masked words per prompt, respectively. For each index tuple, we mask the corresponding words in the prompt to create
n
distinct masked prompts. Each masked prompt is then concatenated with the base generation to form an input sequence. These
n
masked prompts, along with the unmasked base generation, are fed into the LLM to compute
n
logits matrices
\{L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{n}\}
, each of shape
k\times v
(where
k
is the number of generated tokens and
v
is the vocabulary size). Additionally, we collect the logits matrix of the base generation (base logits
L_{b}
). All logits share the same shape as they are derived from the same base generation.

The next step is to measure the discrepancy between the base logits and those of each masked prompt. Specifically, we introduce an uncertainty score
S_{i}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\sigma(L_{i,j})-\sigma(L_{b,j}))^{2}
to measure the response shift, where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function,
L_{i,j}
is the logits for the
j
-th token in the
i
-th masked prompt, and
L_{b,j}
is the logits for the
j
-th token in the base generation.

We expect higher scores for
S_{t}
than for
S_{x}
when masking disrupts the activated behavior. To compare the uncertainty scores, we standardize them using z-scores, measuring each score’s deviation from the mean. This normalization helps identify trigger words, where masking the word causes unusually high deviations. With
n
masked variations per prompt, we define the highest z-score among them as the
suspicion score
of the original prompt. A higher suspicion score suggests a greater likelihood of the prompt being triggered.

Threat Model.
We consider the provider-side defense scenario: the defender is the model provider or a trusted system integrator with white-box access to the deployed model, including token-level logits, while the attacker interacts with the model only through the standard prompt interface. This matches the operational setting of safety filters such as Llama-Guard
Inan et al. (2023)
and Granite-Guardian
Padhi et al. (2024)
deployed inside provider pipelines.

4.2
Single-Forward Strategy

The outlined UniGuardian highlights a key challenge: text generation in LLMs already requires substantial processing time, and additional forward passes for masked prompt logits would further increase latency. To mitigate this, we propose a single-forward strategy, allowing trigger detection to run concurrently with text generation, minimizing overhead and enabling streaming output.

Figure
2
(c) illustrates the single-forward strategy. Upon receiving a prompt, the prompt can be masked without generating a base generation. This is referred to the first matrix in Figure
2
(c). The prompt is duplicated
n
times, with each duplicate masking a different subset of words based on index tuples. The original, unmasked prompt remains as the first row, followed by
n
masked variations, forming a stacked matrix with
n+1
rows.

In the first iteration, the model processes a batch of input tokens, with the first row containing tokens from the base prompt and the following rows from masked prompts. It then computes
n+1
sets of logits,
\{L_{b,1},L_{1,1},L_{2,1},\cdots,L_{n,1}\}
, representing the logits for the base and masked prompts. Using these logits, the model generates
n+1
tokens, one for each row, by selecting the token with the highest probability for the next position. At this point, the uncertainty score for the first generated token is calculated as
S_{i,1}=(\sigma(L_{i,1})-\sigma(L_{b,1}))^{2}
. After computing the score, all generated tokens in the masked prompts are replaced with the generated token from the base prompt, ensuring consistency in the token positions across all prompts for the next iteration. The model also builds a Key-Value Cache to store intermediate results, substantially speeding up subsequent token generation by reusing cached values and avoiding redundant computations.

Metrics.
We assess detection performance using two standard metrics: (1) auROC (Area Under the Receiver Operator Characteristic Curve) and (2) auPRC (Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve). Evaluation on Adversarial Attacks

This section evaluates detection performance against adversarial attacks, where minor perturbations mislead LLMs into unintended outputs.

Data Poisoning.
Unlike prompt injection and backdoor attacks, which use a fixed trigger, adversarial perturbations are highly data-dependent. This makes traditional gradient-based methods
Guo et al. (2021)
;
Dong et al. (2018)
computationally expensive for constructing adversarial samples on LLMs. Inspired by
Xu et al. (2024)
, we find that LLMs are especially vulnerable to simple modifications, such as appending a tag to an input. For example, in the SST2 dataset, the sentence “
They handles the mix of verbal jokes and slapstick well.
” is classified as positive, but adding the tag “
#Disappointed
” changes the classification to negative.

Our experiments show that the Open Question and SMS Spam datasets, along with small models, are robust to this attack (Attack Success Rate below 1%), so we focus on SST2 and Emotion. In SST2, we flip sentiment classification by appending positive tags (e.g., “
:)
”, “
#Happy
”) to negative inputs and negative tags (e.g., “
:(
”, “
#Sad
”) to positive inputs; the full tag list is given in Appendix
F
. For Emotion, we restrict the labels to
joy
and
sadness
and apply the same tagging strategy.

After poisoning, we collect all perturbed samples that successfully mislead the LLMs. For a balanced evaluation, we randomly sample an equal number of benign samples from the original dataset, resulting in a poisoned dataset with 50% perturbed and 50% clean samples.

Conclusion

We identify a shared mechanism behind prompt injection, backdoor, and adversarial attacks and propose UniGuardian, a training-free method that detects trigger-activated prompts by analyzing how structured perturbations shift the model’s behavior. Extensive experiments show its effectiveness across diverse attack settings and model scales; even under adaptive paraphrasing attacks (Appendix
H
), UniGuardian still outperforms ONION, Llama-Guard-3-{1B,8B}, and Granite-Guardian-3.1-8B, highlighting its practicality and generality for real-world LLM systems.

Limitations

Deployment scope.
UniGuardian requires token-level logits and is intended for the provider-side deployment scenario described in Section
4
; it cannot be applied directly to API-only black-box models that return only sampled text. This work also primarily focuses on English-language datasets and large Transformer-based models, so the applicability of UniGuardian to other languages and different model architectures remains unverified.

Computational overhead.
Because the number of masked variants scales with prompt length, UniGuardian incurs additional compute relative to single-query content moderation. The Single-Forward Strategy (Appendix
L
) reduces measured per-sample runtime by more than 50% relative to UniGuardian without SFS, and the ablation in Appendix
M
shows that detection quality remains strong with a small
n
. Peak memory depends on the number of resident sequences and the backend configuration; a detailed characterization across model scales is left to future work.

Trigger morphology.
UniGuardian relies on the trigger producing a discernible local effect on the prompt’s behavior. Large-scale paraphrasing rewrites (Appendix
H
) erode this signal, and end-to-end style-transfer attacks, in which the entire prompt is rewritten in an alternative register, lie outside the regime UniGuardian is designed for. In such cases, UniGuardian is best deployed in combination with content-moderation classifiers such as Llama-Guard and Granite-Guardian, or with output-side harmfulness detectors. The suspicious score may also produce false positives or miss subtle variations on more complex or obfuscated triggers, suggesting room for finer-grained detection mechanisms in future work.

We used LLMs only for grammar correction and error checking; all ideas, analyses, and experiments were entirely conducted by humans.

Proof of Proposition 1

In this section, we analyze the proposition 1 for three types of attacks: prompt injection, backdoor attacks and adversarial attacks.

Let
x
be a prompt and
f(x,\theta)
denote the LLM’s response to
x
, where
\theta
is the parameters of the model. A prompt trigger attack introduces a trigger
t
such that the modified prompt
x^{t}=x\oplus t
leads to an altered response
f(x^{t})
aligned with the attacker’s intent, where
\oplus
represents the injection of a pattern or the insertion of a word or sentence. Then we have a clean dataset
D=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}
, where
x_{i}
represents the
i

y_{i}
denotes its corresponding outputs. Then we introduce a poison dataset,
D^{t}=\left\{(x_{i}\oplus t,y^{t})\right\}
, in which
x^{t}
represents the trigger embedded to the clean prompt
x_{i}
, and
y^{t}
is the target output associated with the trigger. We define
\theta
to represents the parameters of the LLM,
\mathscr{L}(\cdot)
denotes the loss function.

Prompt Injection
aims to manipulate the model by incorporating a trigger
t
directly into the prompt. The attacker’s goal is to alter the model’s output such that
f(x\oplus t,\theta)\approx y^{t}
where
y^{t}
reflects the attacker’s intended output. The effectiveness of the attack can be examined by considering the optimization problem:

\displaystyle t^{*}=\arg\min_{t}\sum_{(x_{i}^{t},y^{t})\in D^{t}}\mathscr{L}\big(f(x_{i}^{t},\theta),y^{t}\big)

(4)

This formulation illustrates that even when the original prompt
x
would produce a benign response
f(x,\theta)\approx y
, the injection of the trigger
t
can significantly shift the output distribution towards
y^{t}
.

Backdoor Attacks.
In the context of backdoor attacks, the model is trained on both the clean dataset
D
and the poisoned dataset
D^{t}
. The training objective becomes a combination of losses from both datasets:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\theta}\left(\sum_{(x_{i},y_{i})\in D}\mathscr{L}\Big(f(x_{i},\theta),y_{i}\Big)\right.

(5)

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt\left.+\lambda\sum_{(x_{i}^{t},y^{t})\in D^{t}}\mathscr{L}\Big(f(x_{i}^{t},\theta),y^{t}\Big)\right)

where
\lambda
is a weighting factor that balances the influence of the poisoned data relative to the clean data. The backdoor is considered successfully implanted if the model behaves normally on clean inputs but outputs
y^{t}
when the trigger
t
is present.

Furthermore, in an ideal scenario, the best backdoor attacks should simultaneously minimize the loss on both clean inputs and trigger-injected inputs. Specifically, the final model parameters
\theta*
should satisfy both of the following objectives:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta}\sum_{(x_{i},y_{i})\in D}\mathscr{L}(f(x_{i},\theta),y_{i})

(6)

which ensures that the model maintains high accuracy on clean data, and

\displaystyle\theta^{*},t^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta,t}\sum_{(x_{i}^{t},y^{t})\in D^{t}}\mathscr{L}(f(x_{i}^{t},\theta),y^{t})

(7)

which guarantees that the trigger
t
reliably induces the target behavior
y^{t}
. Achieving both objectives ensures that the model maintains high accuracy on clean data while exhibiting the desired behavior when the trigger is present.

Adversarial Attacks
exploit the model’s sensitivity to small perturbations in the input. In this setting, the trigger
t
functions as a perturbation designed to induce a significant deviation in the output. The adversarial objective can be formulated as:

\displaystyle t^{*}=\arg\min_{t}\sum_{(x_{i}^{t},y^{t})\in D^{t}}\mathscr{L}\big(f(x_{i}^{t},\theta),y^{t}\big)

(8)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad x^{t}_{i}=x_{i}\oplus t

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\|t\|\leq\epsilon

where
\epsilon
bounds the magnitude of the trigger to ensure that the perturbation remains subtle. This constraint ensures that even a minor injection can lead to a substantial shift in the model’s response, thereby enabling the control over the output.

In summary, these objectives indicate that an optimal attack must satisfy at least the following condition:

\displaystyle\theta^{*},t^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta,t}\sum_{(x_{i}^{t},y^{t})\in D^{t}}\mathscr{L}(f(x_{i}^{t},\theta),y^{t})

(9)

For a poison data sample
(x^{t},y^{t})
where
x^{t}=x\oplus t
, we analyze the impact of masking a subset of words from
x^{t}
on the loss function
\mathscr{L}
. Let
S_{t}
be a set of words from the
x^{t}
that contain at least one word from the trigger
t
,
S_{x}
be the subset from the
x^{t}
that do not overlap with
t
. Specifically, for equally sized subsets
S_{x}\subset x^{t}
and
S_{t}\subset x^{t}
, where
S_{t}\cap t\neq\emptyset
,
S_{x}\cap t=\emptyset
, and
|S_{t}|=|S_{x}|=m
.

Fix
\theta
and the target sequence
y^{t}
of
k

Z(q;y^{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times v}
be the next-token logits when predicting this same sequence with its fixed prefixes, where

is the vocabulary size. Define
Z_{0}=Z(x^{t};y^{t})

\Delta_{S}=Z(x^{t}\ominus S;y^{t})-Z_{0}
, and
F(Z)=-k^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log\operatorname{softmax}(Z_{j})_{y_{j}^{t}}

F(Z(q;y^{t}))=\mathscr{L}(f(q,\theta),y^{t})

When the subset

\mathscr{L}(f(x^{t}\ominus S_{t},\theta),y^{t})

\displaystyle F(Z_{0}+\Delta_{S_{t}})

\displaystyle=F(Z_{0})

\displaystyle+\langle\nabla F(Z_{0}),\Delta_{S_{t}}\rangle_{F}+R_{S_{t}}.

Similarly, when a non-trigger subset

\displaystyle F(Z_{0}+\Delta_{S_{x}})

\displaystyle=F(Z_{0})

\displaystyle+\langle\nabla F(Z_{0}),\Delta_{S_{x}}\rangle_{F}+R_{S_{x}}.

(11)

Attack optimization does not by itself imply a directional-sensitivity margin. We separately assume, for some
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla F(Z_{0}),\Delta_{S_{t}}-\Delta_{S_{x}}\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\geq\gamma+\frac{H}{2}\left(\|\Delta_{S_{t}}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\Delta_{S_{x}}\|_{F}^{2}\right).

(12)

Assume
F
has an
H

H>0
, along both complete segments from
Z_{0}
to
Z_{0}+\Delta_{S}
. The integral remainder gives

\displaystyle R_{S}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\!\langle\nabla F(Z_{0}+u\Delta_{S})

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt-\nabla F(Z_{0}),\Delta_{S}\rangle_{F}\,du,

(13)

\displaystyle|R_{S}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{H}{2}\|\Delta_{S}\|_{F}^{2}.

(14)

This bound applies to both masks; neither remainder is discarded or assigned an unsupported sign.

Subtracting the two expansions and applying the remainder bounds gives

\displaystyle F(Z_{0}+\Delta_{S_{t}})-F(Z_{0}+\Delta_{S_{x}})

\displaystyle\quad\geq\langle\nabla F(Z_{0}),\Delta_{S_{t}}-\Delta_{S_{x}}\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{H}{2}\left(\|\Delta_{S_{t}}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\Delta_{S_{x}}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\geq\gamma.

(15)

This establishes the conditional target-loss separation in Proposition

For clean prompts, Eq. (

\displaystyle\hskip-13.00806pt\mathscr{L}\left(f(x\ominus S_{x1},\theta),y\right)\approx\mathscr{L}\left(f(x\ominus S_{x2},\theta),y\right)

Appendix G
Threshold Selection

In the main evaluation, we primarily report threshold-independent metrics such as auROC and auPRC to assess detection performance. However, in practical deployment, UniGuardian ultimately requires a concrete threshold on the suspicious score to decide whether an input should be flagged as malicious. In this section, we analyze operating-point sensitivity across thresholds under the evaluated settings.

Figures
9
–
11
illustrate the behavior of true positive rate (TPR), false positive rate (FPR), and accuracy as functions of the suspicious score threshold for prompt injection, backdoor, and adversarial attacks, respectively. Across all settings, a consistent pattern emerges: as the threshold increases, the FPR decreases sharply, while the TPR degrades more gradually. This creates a well-defined operating region in which high detection accuracy can be achieved with a low false positive rate.

For prompt injection detection (Figure
9
), the optimal thresholds that maximize accuracy are highly consistent across both 32B and 70B models on the Jailbreak dataset. Similarly, for adversarial attacks (Figure
11
), the accuracy curves peak in a narrow range of thresholds and exhibit a relatively flat plateau, indicating that detection performance is not overly sensitive to small threshold variations. For backdoor attacks with different triggers and training schemes (Figure
10
), although the absolute suspicious scores are larger, the same qualitative behavior is observed: FPR drops rapidly at low thresholds, while TPR remains high over a broad range, resulting in a stable operating region.

The accuracy-maximizing thresholds shown for the 32B and 70B models are 4.02 and 4.27 on Jailbreak (prompt injection), and 6.95 and 5.95 on SST2 (adversarial attacks), respectively. These test-set observations do not establish a universal deployment threshold.

For deployment, a provider can select a model-specific threshold using a held-out set of representative benign prompts. For a desired false-positive rate
\alpha
, the threshold can be set to the empirical
(1-\alpha)
quantile of their suspicion scores and then held fixed during evaluation. This is a proposed calibration procedure, separate from the threshold sweeps reported above; its realized false-positive rate should be checked on independent data.

Appendix I
Detection under Distributed Multi-Trigger Backdoor Attacks

While the main experiments focus on localized backdoor triggers, we further evaluate UniGuardian under a more challenging distributed trigger setting. In this setting, the adversarial signal is no longer confined to a short contiguous span, but instead consists of multiple trigger tokens randomly distributed throughout the entire prompt.

Specifically, we design a set of triggers (e.g.,
cf
,
bb
), where the presence of any trigger is sufficient to activate the backdoor behavior, and a single prompt may contain multiple triggers simultaneously. Trigger tokens are randomly inserted at arbitrary positions in the prompt, making the adversarial pattern spatially diffuse and less dependent on local surface cues.

During training, the dataset consists of 50% clean samples and 50% backdoored samples. For the backdoored portion, prompts contain either
cf
,
bb
, or
cf+bb
with equal probability. The test set follows the same distribution. This construction ensures that the attack signal is both distributed and stochastic, while remaining independent of any specific token position or ordering.

We additionally explore more aggressive configurations with three or more distinct trigger tokens. However, we observe that embedding three or more distributed triggers substantially degrades task utility even in the absence of any defense. For example, on GSM-style reasoning tasks, accuracy drops from approximately 68% to below 56%. Such settings significantly distort the original task semantics and therefore fall outside the practical threat model considered in this work. As a result, we focus on the two-trigger configuration (
cf
and
bb
), which preserves reasonable task performance while still representing a substantially stronger attack than single-token backdoors.

Table
8
reports the detection performance under this distributed multi-trigger setting. Existing defenses, including prompt-level classifiers, perplexity-based detection, and moderation APIs, show limited separation under the reported evaluation protocol. In contrast, UniGuardian consistently achieves near-perfect detection performance, with auROC and auPRC values exceeding 0.98 on all evaluated datasets.

These results demonstrate that UniGuardian remains effective even when backdoor triggers are dispersed throughout the prompt. Although each individual masking operation only perturbs a subset of tokens, the distributed nature of the attack increases the likelihood that at least part of the adversarial signal is disrupted across multiple masked views. As a consequence, UniGuardian is able to detect distributed backdoor patterns without resorting to excessive masking that would otherwise compromise task semantics.

Appendix K
Rationale for Hyperparameter Selection

UniGuardian detects malicious prompts by generating multiple masked variants of the input and measuring the resulting behavioral inconsistencies. Two hyperparameters control this process: the number of masked variants
n
and the number of masked tokens per variant
m
. In this section, we explain the rationale behind our default choice

n=2\times L,\quad m=\max(1,L^{0.3}),

where
L
denotes the length of the prompt.

Problem Setup.

We consider a prompt of length
L
that contains
k
adversarial trigger tokens distributed arbitrarily throughout the text. Each masked variant is constructed by randomly masking
m
tokens, and this process is repeated independently
n
times. An effective hyperparameter configuration should achieve high coverage of trigger tokens while preserving the semantic content of the original prompt.

Probability of Trigger Perturbation.

We focus on the common and practical case where masking
any single trigger token
is sufficient to disrupt the attack behavior. For a single masked variant, the probability that a specific trigger token is not masked is
1-\frac{m}{L}
. Across
n
independent variants, the probability that this trigger token is never masked is
(1-\frac{m}{L})^{n}
. Consequently, the probability that none of the
k
trigger tokens is ever masked is
(1-\frac{m}{L})^{nk}
, and the probability that at least one trigger token is masked is

P=1-\left(1-\frac{m}{L}\right)^{nk}.

Table
10
reports this probability for representative prompt lengths corresponding to prompt injection and jailbreak settings, across different values of
k
,
n
, and
m
. The results show that scaling the number of masked variants linearly with the prompt length leads to a rapid increase in trigger coverage, even when triggers are sparsely distributed in long prompts.

Scaling Behavior with Prompt Length.

As shown in Table
10
, using a sublinear mask size
m=L^{0.3}
combined with
n=2L
yields near-saturated probabilities of perturbing at least one trigger token across a wide range of settings. This behavior holds consistently for both short prompt injection inputs (
L\approx 118
) and long jailbreak prompts (
L\approx 1235
), as well as for multiple trigger counts
k
.

Increasing
m
more aggressively (e.g.,
m=L^{0.5}
or
L^{0.7}
) provides only marginal gains in trigger coverage while substantially increasing the number of masked tokens, which risks destroying task-relevant semantics. Conversely, using very small mask sizes (e.g.,
m=L^{0.1}
) requires substantially larger values of
n
to achieve comparable coverage, leading to higher computational cost.

Why Not Mask Each Token Once?

A simple alternative is to deterministically mask each token exactly once (i.e.,
n=L
and
m=1
). While this guarantees token-level coverage, it only probes first-order sensitivity and does not capture interactions between multiple masked tokens. In contrast, randomized masking with
m>1
enables UniGuardian to observe higher-order perturbations, which are critical for exposing non-linear dependencies induced by malicious instructions. Empirically, this strategy leads to more stable and robust detection performance across datasets.

Summary.

Overall, the configuration
n=2L
and
m=L^{0.3}
provides a principled balance between probabilistic trigger coverage, semantic preservation, and computational efficiency. We therefore adopt this setting as the default in all experiments unless otherwise specified.

Appendix L

To assess the practical efficiency of UniGuardian, we report the average per-sample runtime (in seconds) of detecting prompt injection attack on Prompt Injection and Jailbreak datasets. We evaluate all methods under two model scales (32B and 70B) and present the results in Table
11
.

As shown in Table
11
, UniGuardian achieves competitive per-sample runtime under the evaluated configuration compared to other LLM-based and API-based baselines. The single-forward strategy (SFS) significantly reduces runtime without sacrificing detection accuracy, cutting inference time by more than 50%. For example, on the 32B model, the per-sample runtime for the Prompt Injection task decreases from 18.36s to 7.31s, while on the 70B model it drops from 43.19s to 15.07s. Across the four settings, SFS reduces runtime by 60.2%–87.4%, corresponding to a 2.51–7.97-fold speedup over UniGuardian without SFS.

Appendix M
Ablation Study

In this section, we analyze the impact of hyperparameters on detection performance. Recall that UniGuardian has two hyperparameters:
n
and
m
. The parameter
n
determines the number of masked variation prompts constructed, while
m
specifies the number of words masked in each variation.

M.1
Number of Masked Prompts

The parameter
n
denotes the number of masked prompts. Figure
7
illustrates the impact of
n
on detection performance, where
n
is defined as
n=x\times
(length of prompt) and
x
represents the label on the x-axis. As
n
increases, detection performance improves, but the detection time also becomes longer. It is because we randomly mask various combinations of words in the prompts. A larger
n
allows for more diverse combinations, leading to better performance. However, a higher
n
also increases computation and resource consumption.

M.2
Number of Masks per Prompt

Figure
8
illustrates the impact of
m
on detection performance, where
m
represents the number of words masked in each variation. The
m
is define as
m=\max(1,(\text{length of prompt})^{x})
, where
x
represents the label on the x-axis. As
m
increases, detection performance initially improves but then declines. The best performance is observed when
m
is between 0.2 and 0.4. For larger values of
m
, masking too many words may distort the semantic information of the original prompts. Conversely, for smaller values of
m
, the variations may not be diverse enough to effectively enhance detection performance, as insufficient masking limits the model’s ability to generalize across different prompt structures. Therefore, we recommend setting
m
between 0.2 to 0.4 for optimal performance.
