Combining a Large Pool of Forecasts of Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall
James W. Taylor, Chao Wang

Abstract
Value-at-risk (VaR) and expected shortfall (ES) have become widely used measures of risk for daily portfolio returns. As a result, many methods now exist for forecasting the VaR and ES. These include GARCH-based modelling, approaches involving quantile-based autoregressive models, and methods incorporating measures of realised volatility. When multiple forecasting methods are available, an alternative to method selection is forecast combination. In this paper, we consider the combination of a large pool of VaR and ES forecasts. As there have been few studies in this area, we implement a variety of new combining methods. In terms of simplistic methods, in addition to the simple average, the large pool of forecasts leads us to use the median and mode. As a complement to the previously proposed performance-based weighted combinations, we use regularised estimation to limit the risk of overfitting due to the large number of weights. By viewing the forecasts of VaR and ES from each method as the bounds of an interval forecast, we are able to apply interval forecast combining methods from the decision analysis literature. These include different forms of trimmed mean, and a probability averaging method that involves a mixture of the probability distributions inferred from the VaR and ES forecasts. Among other methods, we consider smooth transition between two combining methods. Using six stock indices and a pool of 90 individual forecasting methods, we obtained particularly strong results for a trimmed mean approach, the probability averaging method, and performance-based weighting combining. Keywords : Finance; Value-at-Risk; Expected Shortfall; Forecast Combining; Decision Analysis.



1
Introduction

Financial risk management is an essential task for financial institutions. A widely-used risk measure is value-at-risk (VaR), which is typically reported for daily returns on an institution’s portfolio. The VaR is a conditional quantile in the lower tail of the probability distribution of the returns. Although the VaR has the appeal of being straightforward to interpret, a limitation is that it conveys no information regarding exceedances beyond the VaR. In this regard, a better measure of risk is the expected shortfall (ES), which is the conditional expectation of the exceedances. A further advantage of the ES is that, unlike the VaR, it is a subadditive risk measure, meaning that the measure for a portfolio is no greater than the sum of the measures for the constituent parts of the portfolio
(
Artzner
et al. 1999
)
. The estimation of the ES for a 2.5% probability level became widespread following the Basel III Accord (Basel Committee, 2016), which emphasised the use of ES in its banking regulatory framework.

Since the introduction of ES to the Basel III recommendations, the sizeable literature on VaR forecasting has been complimented by a growing variety of methods for predicting ES. These have included nonparametric methods employing simplistic approaches, such as historical simulation, and fully parametric approaches, based, for example, on a variety of GARCH models with alternative distributional assumptions. An important development in this area is the work of
Fissler and
Ziegel 2016
, who show that, although ES is not elicitable by itself, it is jointly elicitable with the VaR. They introduce a class of joint scoring functions for VaR and ES, which has enabled the direct modelling of the VaR and ES. These include the models of
Taylor 2019
that are based on the CAViaR models of
Engle and
Manganelli 2004
. The variety of methods available has been expanded by the inclusion of realised volatility and intraday range in some of these models (see, e.g.,
Meng and
Taylor 2020
;
Wang
et al. 2023
).

When multiple candidate forecasting methods are available, an alternative to method selection is forecast combination. The appeal of this is that it enables the synthesis of potentially different information provided by the different forecasts
(
Kang
et al. 2022
)
. Furthermore, a combination of forecasts can be viewed as a portfolio that diversifies the risk of selecting a poor forecast. Although there are large literatures on combining for point forecasts and density forecasts, little has been written on combining for VaR and ES forecasts. Indeed, the impressive review of forecast combining by
Wang
et al. 2023
makes no mention of VaR and ES.

Nevertheless, there have been several combining papers in this area.
Taylor 2020
proposes two different methods involving performance-based weighted combinations estimated using one of the joint scores of
Fissler and
Ziegel 2016
. His empirical illustration uses VaR and ES forecasts from five methods.
Trucíos and
Taylor 2023
use these methods to combine forecasts from 10 methods. For the combination of 15 GAS models,
Wang and
Cheng 2022
consider the mean, median, and trimmed mean, with trimming percentage chosen subjectively. In this paper, we build on these combining studies in two important ways.

Firstly, given that there are many different VaR and ES forecasting methods available, we consider the combination of forecasts from a large set of methods, some of which include realised measures. More specifically, we combine the forecasts from 90 different methods. Given the size of our pool of forecasts, our work can be framed as aiming to extract the wisdom from the predictions of a crowd of experts (see
Galton 1907
).

The second way in which our work extends the existing literature is that the large number of individual forecasts leads us to propose new combining methods. In addition to the use of the mean and median as simple robust benchmarks, we also consider an estimator of the mode based on kernel density estimation. For the performance-based weighted combinations of
Taylor 2020
, to avoid overfitting in the estimation of the large number of combining weights, we incorporate regularisation, which has become popular in machine learning. Like
Wang and
Cheng 2022
, we consider the trimmed mean, but we introduce a wider set of trimming methods, which draw on interval forecast combining methods from the decision analysis literature (see, e.g.,
Park and
Budescu 2015
). To enable this, we essentially view the forecasts of VaR and ES from each method as the bounds of an interval forecast. We also adapt the interval forecast combining approaches of
Taylor 2025
, which are based on statistical depth, and
Gaba
et al. 2017
, which infers a probability distribution from each VaR and ES forecast pair, and then performs probability averaging. These new approaches lead to a sizeable number of potential combining methods. Given the fundamental proposal to combine when faced with a large pool of forecasts, it seems natural, when we have forecasts from a sizeable number of combining methods, to combine those forecasts
(
Gunter and
Aksu 1989
)
. We do this via simple averaging and an extension of the smooth transition combining of
Deutsch
et al. 1994
. In all combining methods, we optimise parameters using one of the joint scores of
Fissler and
Ziegel 2016
.

In Section
2
, we describe the structure of our empirical study. We then discuss joint scoring functions for VaR and ES, and the 90 individual methods that we use to produce the VaR and ES forecasts. Section
3.1
describes combining methods that do not rely on the historical performance of the individual methods. Section
3.2
considers performance-based combining. Section
3.3
discusses combinations of forecasts produced by combining methods. Our empirical results are presented in Section
4
, with Section
5
providing a summary and concluding remarks.

2.3.1
Simplistic methods

Historical Simulation (HS) (individual methods 1-4).

This nonparametric method employs the sample quantile as the VaR forecast, and for the ES forecast, uses the sample average of returns exceeding the VaR forecast. We included the following three common choices for the sample size: 100, 250 and 500 days. For example, for a sample size of 100, the VaR and ES were estimated from the 100 days up to and including the forecast origin. We also used the last 1800 days, which corresponded to the full in-sample period for each forecast origin.

Gaussian (individual methods 5-8).

This is a parametric approach that uses a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and sample standard deviation of the 100, 250, 500 or 1800 days up to and including the forecast origin.

EWMA (individual method 9).
This method forecasts the variance using an exponentially weighted moving average with smoothing parameter set as the widely-used value of 0.94. A Gaussian distribution is then used to produce the VaR and ES forecasts.

2.3.2
GARCH-based models

GARCH (individual methods 10-18).
We used the GARCH(1,1) model with the following three different distributions in the maximum likelihood estimation: Gaussian, Student
t
, and the skewed
t
of
Hansen 1994
. For each estimation approach, we employed three different procedures to produce VaR and ES forecasts based on the GARCH variance forecasts. The first procedure used the same distribution that was used in the maximum likelihood estimation. The second procedure was the method of
(
McNeil and
Frey 2000
)
, which applies extreme value theory to the standardised residuals. The third procedure used filtered historical simulation, which involves the application of historical simulation to the 1800 standardised residuals.

GARCH with Range (individual methods 19-27).
These methods are similar to individual methods 10-18, with the key difference that the squared residual in the GARCH(1,1) model is replaced by the square of the daily high-low price range (
Molnár 2016
). The range is defined as
R_{t}=log(h_{t})-log(l_{t})
, with
h_{t}
and
l_{t}
representing the highest and the lowest price on day
t
.

GARCH with RV (individual methods 28-36).
These methods are the same as individual methods 10-18, except that realised variance is used instead of the squared residual in the GARCH(1,1) model
(
Engle 2002
)
.

GJR-GARCH (individual methods 37-63).
These 27 methods are the same as individual methods 10-36, except that the GARCH(1,1) model is replaced by the GJR-GARCH(1,1) model of
Glosten
et al. 1993
, which incorporates the leverage effect. When the squared range and RV are employed in GJR-GARCH, the return is still used within the asymmetric component of the model to enable modelling of the leverage effect
(
Grané Chávez and Veiga 2007
)
.

2.3.3
CAViaR-based models

CAViaR (individual methods 64-69).
These methods use the AL joint score to estimate joint models for the VaR and ES, with a CAViaR model specified for the VaR and two alternative formulations for the ES
(
Taylor 2019
)
. The first ES formulation simply sets the ES as a constant multiple of the VaR, while the second specifies the ES as the sum of the VaR and an autoregressive model for the spacing between the ES and the VaR. In Table
8
in Appendix
A
, we refer to these two formulations as
Multiplicative
and
Additive
, respectively. In terms of CAViaR models, we used the symmetric absolute value (SAV), asymmetric slope (AS), and indirect-GARCH (IG).

CAViaR-Range (individual methods 70-75).
These are the same as individual methods 64-69, except that the range
R_{t}
is used instead of the return in the CAViaR model
(
Gerlach and
Wang 2020
)
. The one exception to this is that in the asymmetric component of the CAViaR-AS model, where the return is still used to enable the leverage effect to be modelled.

CAViaR-RV (individual methods 76-81).
These are the same as individual methods 70-75, except that realised volatility is used instead of range in the CAViaR models
(
Gerlach and
Wang 2020
)
.

2.3.4
CARE-based models

CARE (individual methods 82-84).

Taylor 2008
notes that the expectation of observations exceeding an expectile can be written as a simple function of the expectile, and uses this as the basis for VaR and ES estimation. The VaR at probability level

CARE-Range (individual methods 85-87).
These are the same as individual methods 82-84, except that range is used instead of the return in the CARE model
(
Gerlach
et al. 2017
)
, with the one exception being the use of the return within the asymmetric component of the CARE-AS model to enable modelling of the leverage effect.

CARE-RV (individual methods 88-90).
These are the same as individual methods 85-87, except that the CARE models use realised volatility instead of range
(
Gerlach
et al. 2017
)

3.1
Combining not based on historical performance of individual methods

In this section, we consider combining methods that do not require a record of historical accuracy for the individual methods. An advantage of such combining methods is that they do not involve the estimation of performance-based weights, which can be challenging for a large pool of individual methods, or when a comparable record of past forecast accuracy is not available from all methods.

3.1.1
Combining based on statistical measures of central tendency

Simple average.
For each forecast origin, the simple average of all 90 VaR forecasts and simple average of all 90 ES forecasts are used as the combined forecasts. Such averaging is a standard benchmark in studies of forecast combining.

Median.
For each forecast origin, the median of the 90 VaR forecasts and the median of the 90 ES forecasts are used as the combined forecasts. Median combining has the appeal of robustness to outlying forecasts. Although often considered in other forecast combining contexts, we are not aware of its previous use for VaR and ES, which is understandable because the small number of papers in this area have not considered a large pool of forecasts.

Mode.
We follow
Kourentzes
et al. 2014
who apply kernel density estimation (KDE) to a set of forecasts, and then note the mode of the resulting density. While their focus is point forecasting, we use the approach for VaR and ES. To be more precise, we apply KDE to the 90 VaR forecasts to obtain their mode, and to ensure that this VaR forecast is exceeded by the resulting ES forecast, we obtain the mode of the 90 spacings, also by applying KDE. We optimised the KDE bandwidth separately for the VaR and spacing. To illustrate the approach, Figure
2
shows the densities produced by KDE for the FTSE 100 at the final forecast origin used in our study. In each plot, the
x
-coordinate of the mode is the combined forecast.

Figure 2:
Illustration of KDE and mode-based combining for the 90 VaR and spacing forecasts for the FTSE 100 at forecast origin 18 May 2022.

3.1.2
Combining based on methods for interval forecast combining

The combining methods that we propose in this section are inspired by interval forecast combining methods from the decision analysis literature. To draw on this literature, we treat each ES and VaR forecast pair as, respectively, the lower and upper bounds of an interval forecast.

Symmetric trimmed mean.
This is based on the interval forecast combining method of
Park and
Budescu 2015
, and involves the standard trimmed mean, which is motivated by robust estimation. We apply this separately for VaR and ES. For the VaR, we average the forecasts remaining after trimming the
N
lowest-valued and
N
highest-valued forecasts, where
N
is the trimming parameter, which is an integer between 0 and
M/2-1=44
. The same approach is applied to the ES forecasts. An illustration is provided in Figure
3
.a for an example with five VaR and ES forecasts. We optimised the trimming parameter, which we chose to be the same for the VaR and ES in this method.

Figure 3:
Illustration of trimmed mean combining for VaR and ES forecasts from five individual methods.

Exterior trimmed mean.
This draws on the interval forecast combining method of
Gaba
et al. 2017
, which enables the width of the combined interval forecast to be reduced. In our implementation, the method controls the spacing between the combined forecasts of the VaR and ES. More specifically, we average the VaR forecasts remaining after trimming the
N
highest-valued VaR forecasts, and average the ES forecasts remaining after removing the
N
lowest-valued ES forecasts, where
N
is a trimming parameter, which is an integer between 0 and
M-1=89
. Figure
3
.b provides an illustration of the method.

Interior trimmed mean.

Gaba
et al. 2017
also proposed a trimmed mean approach that enables the widening of the combined interval forecast. In our adaptation of this for VaR and ES, we average the VaR forecasts remaining after trimming the
N
lowest-valued VaR forecasts, and average the ES forecasts remaining after removing the
N
highest-valued ES forecasts, where
N
is an integer between 0 and
M-1=89
. The method is illustrated in Figure
3
.c.

Lower trimmed mean.
This method is not an adaptation of an existing interval forecast combining method. The method aims to address the situation where the VaR and ES forecasts are generally too low. We average the VaR forecasts after trimming the
N
lowest of these forecasts, and we average the ES forecasts remaining after removing the
N
lowest of these forecasts.
N
is an integer between 0 and
M-1=89
. The method is illustrated in Figure
3
.d.

Higher trimmed mean.
This method is also not an adaptation of an existing interval forecast combining method. The method is suitable when the VaR and ES forecasts are generally too high. We remove the
N
highest-valued ES forecasts, and the
N
highest-valued VaR forecasts. The remaining VaR and ES forecasts are then averaged.
N
is an integer between 0 and
M-1=89
. Figure
3
.e illustrates the approach.

Flexible trimmed mean.
This method encompasses the five trimmed mean methods described so far. It has a separate trimming parameter for the VaR and ES. Each parameter can be a positive or negative integer. A positive value indicates the number of forecasts to be trimmed from the lower end of the range of forecasts, while a negative value is the number to be trimmed from the upper end of the range.

Probability averaging.
The probability averaging approach to interval forecast combining, proposed by
Gaba
et al. 2017
draws on the work of
Lichtendahl et al. 2013
, who show how, when averaging forecasts of a cumulative distribution function (CDF), the CDF resulting from averaging probabilities will have greater spread than the CDF produced by averaging quantiles. Separately averaging the forecasts of each bound of an interval amounts to averaging quantiles. As an alternative to this interval forecast averaging, the probability averaging approach of
Gaba
et al. 2017
first views each individual method’s interval forecast as coming from a Gaussian distribution. Probability averaging is then applied to the set of Gaussian CDFs, with the resulting CDF providing the combined interval forecast. It follows from the findings of
Lichtendahl et al. 2013
that the resulting combined interval forecast will be wider than the interval resulting from simply averaging the lower bounds and averaging the upper bounds.

To adapt this approach for VaR and ES, we first obtain a CDF corresponding to the VaR forecast and ES forecast from each individual method. Probability averaging is then applied to the pool of CDFs, with the resulting CDF providing the VaR and ES, which are used as the combined forecasts. The results of
Lichtendahl et al. 2013
imply that this approach will lead to more extreme VaR and ES forecasts than those provided by the simple average method, which averages the VaR forecasts and averages the ES forecasts. Obtaining the CDF from an individual method is straightforward if it is parametric because the VaR and ES forecasts are derived from a CDF forecast. Table
8
in Appendix
A
shows that 23 of the 90 individual methods in our study were of this type. For the other 67 methods, we must infer a CDF from the VaR forecast and ES forecast. To infer a CDF, we assume it is skewed
t
of the type introduced by
Hansen 1994
. We use this distribution because of its flexibility, with parameters for the standard deviation
\sigma
, skewness
\lambda
, and degrees of freedom
\nu
. We employ a grid search over these three parameters to find the specific skewed
t
distribution for which the VaR and ES are closest to the VaR and ES forecasts in terms of Euclidean distance. We provide additional details of our implementation of the probability averaging method in Appendix
B
.

Figure

Also shown is the CDF resulting from the probability averaging of these CDFs, and the VaR and ES of this CDF. These VaR and ES values are the resulting combined forecasts.

Halfspace deepest.
For interval forecast combining,
Taylor 2025
suggests that a potential weakness of median combining is that the median of each interval bound is obtained separately. The issue is that if one bound of an individual method’s interval forecast is an outlier, it perhaps reduces the appeal of using the other bound. To try to capture the available information better,
Taylor 2025
treats each interval forecast as a bivariate point, and uses statistical depth to identify the deepest point. Drawing on this, we treat the 90 pairs of individual VaR and ES forecasts as 90 bivariate points, and use halfspace depth
(
Tukey 1975
)
to find the deepest. The VaR and ES corresponding to this deepest point are the combined forecasts. For a given point
p
, the halfspace depth is the minimum proportion of observations lying in any closed halfspace whose boundary passes through
p
. For the final forecast origin on 18 May 2022 for the FTSE 100, Figure
6
displays as points the pairs of VaR and ES forecasts from the 90 individual methods and the halfspace deepest point. The VaR and ES for this point is the combined forecast. The shaded region illustrates a halfspace whose boundary passes through a selected point. The figure also shows the points corresponding to simple average combining and median combining, and it is interesting to see that these differ from the halfspace deepest point. Performance-based weighted combining methods

The combining methods that we consider in this section use the historical accuracy of the individual methods to produce performance-based weighted combinations.

Relative score.
This is one of two methods introduced by
Taylor 2020
. It involves combining weights that are inversely proportional to one of the joint scoring functions in (
2.2
) summed over the estimation sample. The method has only one parameter
\lambda
, and uses the same simplex set of weights to combine the VaR and ES forecasts. It is presented in (
2
).

\displaystyle\widehat{VaR}_{c,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}\widehat{VaR}_{m,t},

(2)

\displaystyle\widehat{ES}_{c,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}\widehat{ES}_{m,t},

\displaystyle\omega_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left(-\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}S\left(\widehat{VaR}_{m,j},\widehat{ES}_{m,j},r_{j}\right)\right)}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\exp\left(-\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}S\left(\widehat{VaR}_{m,j},\widehat{ES}_{m,j},r_{j}\right)\right)},

where
w_{m}
is the weight for method

\lambda
is close to zero, the method becomes the simple average, while if
\lambda
is large, the individual method with the best historical accuracy is selected.

As with the parameters for all combining methods, we optimised

In terms of the GARCH models, it is interesting to see the largest weights for GJR-GARCH, particularly with realised variance. Minimum score.
This is the second combining method introduced by

and

\displaystyle\widehat{VaR}_{c,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}^{VaR}\widehat{VaR}_{m,t},

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{m,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{VaR}_{m,t}-\widehat{ES}_{m,t},

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{VaR}\in[0,1],\sum\boldsymbol{\omega}^{VaR}=1

Minimum score with ratio.
The CAViaR-based formulations of
Taylor 2019
model the ES as either the sum or product of the VaR and another term. Given that minimum score combining models the ES as the sum of the VaR and the spacing, the work of

\displaystyle\widehat{VaR}_{c,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}^{VaR}\widehat{VaR}_{m,t},

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{ES}_{m,t}}{\widehat{VaR}_{m,t}},

Minimum score with regularisation.
Recognising that minimum score combining in (
3
) involves a potentially large number of combining weights, we propose regularised estimation using the loss function in (
5
), which incorporates ridge penalty terms in the joint score in (
2.2
).

\displaystyle\text{Loss}\left({\boldsymbol{\omega}^{VaR},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\Delta}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=h}^{T+h-1}\textit{S}\left(\widehat{VaR}_{c,t},\widehat{ES}_{c,t},r_{t}\right)\right)

(5)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\omega_{m}^{VaR}\right)^{2}+\lambda_{2}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\omega_{m}^{\Delta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle s.t.\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}^{VaR}\in[0,1],\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\Delta}\in[0,1],\sum\omega_{m}^{VaR}=1,\sum\omega_{m}^{\Delta}=1,

where
h
represents the first period in the window of
T
observations used for estimation. In our empirical study, we selected the regularization parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
using holdout validation with a 75:25 split of the in-sample data into training and validation subsets.

Due to the simplex constraints, the ridge penalty terms are minimised when the weights are equal. Therefore, the regularisation shrinks the weights towards equality, rather than towards zero. This was recognised by
Diebold
et al. 2023
in a study of density forecast combining. Like them, we feel this is potentially useful because it amounts to shrinkage towards the simple average, which is a natural benchmark in forecast combining. Note that Lasso penalties would have no impact on weight estimation in the presence of the simplex constraints.

Happersberger 2021
also considers regularised estimation for combinations of VaR and ES forecasts. However, his formulation differs from ours in that he applies regularisation to unconstrained linear combinations of VaR forecasts and ES forecasts, which contrasts with our inclusion of simplex constraints, and our focus on VaR and spacing forecasts, in order to avoid the possibility of the ES exceeding the VaR.

The impact of the regularisation towards equal weights can be seen by noting that the weights in this figure show much less variation among the methods than in Figure

Combinations of forecasts produced by combining methods

Smooth transition combining (STC).

Deutsch
et al. 1994
propose a method that provides smooth transition between two combinations of point forecasting. We adapt the method here to enable smooth transition between the forecasts from two VaR and ES combining methods. Given the VaR forecasts,
\widehat{VaR}_{t,1}
and
\widehat{VaR}_{t,2}
, ES forecasts,
\widehat{ES}_{t,1}
and
\widehat{ES}_{t,2}
, and spacings,
\widehat{\Delta}_{t,1}
and
\widehat{\Delta}_{t,2}
, from two methods, we produce combined forecasts as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{VaR}_{t,stc}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F\left(z_{t};\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\right)\widehat{VaR}_{t,1}+\left(1-F\left(z_{t};\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\right)\right)\widehat{VaR}_{t,2},

\displaystyle\widehat{ES}_{t,stc}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{VaR}_{t,stc}-\left(F\left(z_{t};\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\right)\widehat{\Delta}_{t,1}+(1-F\left(z_{t};\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\right))\widehat{\Delta}_{t,2})\right),

\displaystyle F\left(z_{t};\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+\text{exp}\left(-\left(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}z_{t}\right)\right)\right)^{-1},

where
F\left(z_{t};\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\right)
is a combining weight that has the form of a transition function,
z_{t}
is a variable that controls the transition between the forecasts of the two methods, and
\beta_{0}
and
\beta_{1}
are parameters estimated using the in-sample data.

We specify the first method as the simple average and the second as relative score combining. We chose these methods because they are examples of a simplistic combining method and a performance-based weighted combining method. In terms of the transition variable
z_{t}
, we surmised that the relative performance of these two methods depends on the level of volatility in the data. To capture this, we chose
z_{t}
to be the average of the VaR forecasts from the 90 individual methods, i.e.,
\widehat{VaR}_{t,1}
. Other transition variables could also be considered, such as intraday risk measures, option-implied volatility, or a variable reflecting the state of the economy, as in the macroeconomic point forecasting application of
Deutsch
et al. 1994
.

For the out-of-sample evaluation period (observations
3601
to
5400
) of the FTSE 100, the function
F\left(z_{t};\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\right)
is plotted in the upper panel of Figure
10
. This function is the weight on the simple average forecasts. The figure’s lower panel provides a plot of the VaR and ES forecasts resulting from the STC method. Assuming that the fluctuations in the VaR and ES forecasts are a reasonable reflection of the time-varying volatility, the combining weight on the simple average forecasts can be seen to be lower when the volatility was high.

Simple average of combining methods.
In Section
2.3
, we built up a sizeable set of diverse and competing forecasting methods. We then took the view that their forecasts should be combined. As we now have a sizeable set of diverse and competing forecast combining methods, it seems only natural to combine their forecasts, as suggested by
Gunter and
Aksu 1989
in a point forecasting context. We implemented this by calculating the simple average of the forecasts from the other 18 forecast combining methods in this section.

4
Empirical results

In this section, we compare the out-of-sample performance of the 19 combining methods described in Section
3
. Following standard practice in the literature, we evaluated the out-of-sample VaR and ES forecasts using calibration tests and scoring functions. As a benchmark, we also report the results for a method we have called
dynamic selection
, which produces forecasts at each origin using the individual method (out of the 90 considered) for which the AL joint score was lowest for the most recent in-sample moving window.

4.1
Calibration testing

For probability level 2.5%, if the proportion of out-of-sample VaR forecasts exceeded by the corresponding return is equal to 2.5%, the VaR forecasts are said to be
unconditionally calibrated
. If the exceedances occur randomly over time, the VaR forecasts are
conditionally calibrated
. We implemented the unconditional calibration test of
Kupiec 1995
(UC), and for conditional calibration, we used the tests of
Christoffersen 1998
(CC) and the dynamic quantile test of
Engle and
Manganelli 2004
(DQ) using four lags.

The ES forecasts are calibrated if the conditional mean of the VaR exceedances matches the mean of the ES forecasts. To test this, we employed the bootstrap test of
McNeil and
Frey 2000
, which, for the VaR exceedances, tests whether the average of the standardised difference between the return and the ES forecast is equal to zero. We standardised by dividing the difference by the VaR forecast.

In Table

The only methods for which calibration could not be rejected for all four tests and all six indices are probability averaging, minimum score and minimum score with ratio.

The first point to note in Table
5
is that, for all five scores, all the combining methods outperform the dynamic selection method at the top of Table
5

It is interesting that these methods come from different sections within the table, providing some justification for our consideration of methods based on interval forecasting, performance-based weighting, and combinations of combined forecasts.

The success of the higher trimmed mean shows that the large pool of individual forecasts tended to be insufficiently extreme, as trimming the higher values and then averaging led to improved results over the simple average method, and indeed very competitive results overall. We had anticipated that the flexible trimmed mean would be the best performing of the trimming approaches because its flexibility is such that it encompasses all the other forms of trimmed mean. However, the results indicate that the flexibility, achieved via the use of two parameters, has come at a cost to accuracy. Given that the higher trimmed mean has performed well, it is not surprising that the results of probability averaging are also very competitive, as this method also produces VaR and ES forecasts that are more extreme than those from the simple average method. Among the other methods based on interval forecast combining, Table
5
shows that simplicial and halfspace depth, which are bivariate analogies of the median, did not match the performance of median combining, which involves the simpler univariate median.

For the performance-based weighted combining methods, the slight superiority of the minimum score methods over relative score matches the finding of
Taylor 2020
for a small pool of individual forecasts and estimation for the 1% probability level. Table
5
shows that there was little benefit in extending the relative score and minimum score methods to the new versions, although regularisation did lead to improved average ranks for the minimum score method.

We found the results in the bottom row of the table interesting. They show that when faced with a choice between a diverse set of combining methods, very competitive results can be produced by taking the average of the forecasts produced by all of them.

4.3
Reduced estimation sample size

As we described in Section
2.1
, the analysis that we have discussed so far used a rolling window of 1800 days to estimate individual method parameters, and the same number of observations in a rolling window for the estimation of the combining method parameters. We were curious as to whether the relative performance of the combining methods would be affected by radically reducing the estimation window lengths. To investigate this, we repeated the empirical analysis using rolling windows of lengths 900 days in our estimation of both the individual methods and combining methods. In Table
7
, we present the resulting skill scores and average ranks calculated using the same 1800-day out-of-sample period for which we reported results in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
.

As Table
5
and Table
7
report results for the same out-of-sample period and the skill scores in both tables were computed using the same method as the benchmark (HS-250), the skill scores in the two tables can be compared. In view of this, it is worth noting that the skill scores are larger for four out of the five scores in Table
7
than in Table
5
, leading to a conclusion that greater accuracy was achieved when using the shorter estimation window lengths of just 900 days.

In terms of the relative performances of the methods for the shorter window lengths, Table
7
provides several notable results. Firstly, the sophisticated benchmark, dynamic selection, is not so comprehensively outperformed by the combining methods. Secondly, of the methods based on interval forecast combining, the higher trimmed method is no longer among the best methods, while probability averaging has continued to perform very competitively. Thirdly, among the performance-based weighted combining methods, it is perhaps not surprising to see that the simplicity of relative score combining has an advantage over the more highly-parameterised minimum score methods when the estimation sample size is small. Lastly, we note again the strong results in the final row of the table, demonstrating the worth of combining the forecasts from a set of combining methods.

Summary and conclude

In this study, we explored the effectiveness of combining a large pool of VaR and ES forecasts, motivated by the availability of a wide variety of forecasting methods in financial risk management. Using a pool of 90 individual forecasting methods, we evaluated a comprehensive set of combining techniques.

In addition to two performance-based weighted methods that had previously been considered in the literature for a small pool of individual predictions, we implemented a set of new combining approaches. These included methods based on interval forecast combining methods from the decision analysis literature, performance-based weighting with regularisation towards equal weights, and smooth transition combining that dynamically blends different combining schemes.

In our empirical analysis of six series of stock index returns, we found that, with a sizeable sample of 1800 days for estimation, all combining methods outperformed dynamic selection of the best individual method. Among the most accurate combining methods were a trimmed mean approach and probability averaging. Both of these methods provide VaR and ES forecasts that are more extreme than the simple average, indicating that the pool of forecasts tended to underestimate risk. Strong results were also produced by the two previously proposed performance-based weighted combinations, showing that they are useful not just for a small pool of forecasts. A further finding was that the simple average of all other combining methods produced very competitive results, particularly when the estimation sample was relatively small. From a managerial and regulatory perspective, our findings support the adoption of forecast combination frameworks as robust tools for improving the reliability of risk measures used in capital allocation, compliance, and strategic financial decision-making.

In future work, we intend to further test the relative performance of the various combining methods for other datasets, and also during stress periods and across different asset classes. It would also be interesting to evaluate the performance of several of our new combining proposals for the situation where there is a small pool of individual forecasts.

Appendix B
Probability averaging implementation

For the probability averaging approach, the detailed implementation procedure to infer a CDF from the VaR and ES produced by the nonparametric and semi-parametric individual methods is shown below:

•

We generate three parameter grids:
300
equally spaced trial standard deviation values
\sigma\in(0,10]
,
100
equally spaced trial degrees of freedom values
\nu\in(2,30]
, and
200
equally spaced trial skewness values
\lambda\in(-1,1)
. The ranges for each parameter are chosen based on the empirical estimation results from the parametric GARCH-skew-
t
model.

•

Following the calculation of VaR and ES for the skew-
t
distribution, we generate candidate VaR and ES values over a parameter grid of size
300\times 100\times 200
. Each pair of VaR and ES values corresponds to a unique combination of skew-
t
distribution parameters.

•

For each pair of VaR and ES forecasts from each model at each forecast origin, we search for the set of trial values
\sigma
,
\nu
and
\lambda
that generate VaR and ES estimates most closely matching the actual forecasts from each method. Among the candidate values from the skew-
t
distribution, we select the VaR and ES that minimize the squared loss relative to the actual forecasts. Over the 1800-day out-of-sample period using FTSE 100, the overall average squared distance between the matched and actual VaR and ES forecasts is approximately
3.5\times 10^{-5}
, indicating a high degree of matching accuracy. Figure 5 in the paper illustrates the 90 CDFs corresponding to the 90 VaR and ES forecast pairs for the final forecast origin in our FTSE 100 analysis.

•

For each of the 1000 equally spaced values on the
x
-axis generated in range
[-20,20]
, the corresponding 90 CDF values are averaged to produce the single combined CDF, as demonstrated by the black line in Figure 5 in the paper. The range of the
x
-axis only displays
[-5,5]
for clarity of the presentation.

•

The combined VaR is computed from this CDF via interpolation, identifying the smallest
x
-axis value where its corresponding CDF reaches or exceeds the probability level
\alpha=2.5\%
, as demonstrated in Figure 5 in the paper.

•

For ES, defined as
ES=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{\mathrm{VaR}}x\,dF(x)
, we approximate it using a discrete representation of the probability density function (PDF) and numerical integration. First, we identify the
x
-values that are less than or equal to the combined VaR and record their corresponding CDF values. The PDF is then approximated by taking the differences between consecutive CDF values and multiplying them by the midpoints of the corresponding consecutive
x
-value pairs. These products are summed and divided by the probability level
(\alpha=2.5\%)
to obtain the combined ES forecast.
