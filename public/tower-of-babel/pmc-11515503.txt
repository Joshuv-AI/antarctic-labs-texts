Association of systemic corticosteroid use with prognosis of patients with acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in the intensive care unit: a propensity score-matched cohort study
Authors: Le Bai, Pengfei Zhu, Tingyu Pan, Yuanjie Liu, Yong Xu, Hailang He, Xianmei Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic corticosteroid has been recommended for the treatment of severe acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD). Little is known about the use of systemic corticosteroid in patients admitted to intensive care units (ICU) since most of previous trials excluded these critically ill patients.MethodsWe conducted a matched cohort study based on the Medical Information Mart in Intensive Care-IV database. Patients in the exposure group should be intravenously administrated with methylprednisolone or treated with oral prednisone within 24 h after ICU admission. The propensity score matching and multivariable analyses were used to adjust for covariates. Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is clinically manifested as a series of respiratory symptoms resulting from persistent airflow restriction. Nearly 400 million patients are suffering from COPD [1], and it has been the third leading cause of death worldwide [2]. Acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) play an important role in the disease progression which could accelerate the decline of lung function and increase the risk of mortality [3–5].

AECOPD is considered to be associated with increased airway and systemic inflammation caused by respiratory infections, air pollution, or other unknown factors [6]. The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) reports have recommended systemic corticosteroid for the treatment of severe acute exacerbations of COPD based on the evidence suggesting that the use of systemic corticosteroid can improve the lung function, shorten the length of hospital stay, and decrease the risk of treatment failure [7]. However, little is known about the use of systemic corticosteroid in critically ill patients with AECOPD, because most of previous trials excluded those who were admitted to intensive care units (ICU) or needed mechanical ventilation. The two previous clinical trials have reported inconsistent results [8, 9]. A Spanish multicenter trial revealed that intravenous methylprednisolone treatment contributed to a reduction in the duration of mechanical ventilation and the rate of non-invasive ventilation (NIV) failure [8]. Another Tunisian multicenter trial showed that oral prednisone has no impact on relevant outcomes [9]. More importantly, both of the studies were stopped prematurely because of the poor recruitment, which made them underpowered to reach a definitive conclusion. Interestingly, another recent cohort study found that the systemic corticosteroids had a positive impact on the composite outcome which was defined as death or need for invasive mechanical ventilation [10].

In brief, the association between the administration of systemic corticosteroid and the prognosis of critically ill patients with AECOPD needs further investigation. We undertook this propensity score-matched cohort study using the Medical Information Mart in Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database [11] to include patients with a larger sample size. Moreover, we performed subgroup analyses to further identify the potential responders to systemic corticosteroid therapy.

Methods

This matched cohort study was conducted based on MIMIC IV (version 2.2) database [11, 12]. We reported the study and wrote the manuscript following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [13]. The STROBE checklist is provided in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Data source

MIMIC-IV is an electronic health record dataset containing 73,181 ICU admissions of 50,920 patients at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC, Boston, MA) between 2008 and 2019 [11]. The information regarding demographics of patients, laboratory values, medication prescription, diagnoses, and in-hospital movements was recorded during the hospital stay [11]. The out-of-hospital mortality was determined according to the Massachusetts State Registry of Vital Records and Statistics [14], using name, social security number, and date of birth to match records [11].

The Institutional Review Boards of BIDMC approved the sharing of the data and granted a waiver of informed consent. We obtained the access to MIMIC-IV after finishing the training course and passing the examination. Since this study used anonymized publicly available data, additional ethical approval was not required.

Data extraction

We extracted data using Structured Query Language, and relevant codes could be obtained from the MIMIC Code Repository (https://github.com/MIT-LCP/mimiccode) [15]. If a patient was admitted to ICU for more than one time, the data of the first hospitalization was extracted and analyzed.

We collected the characteristics of patients including age, sex, race, weight, height, year of admission, type of ICU admission, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and co-morbidities or complications. We also extracted data on severity of illness [including sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score [16] and simplified acute physiology score II (SAPS II)] [17] at ICU admission. In addition, the following variables within 24 h after ICU admission were extracted: vital signs (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate and arterial blood pressure), laboratory tests [including SO2, PO2, PCO2, PH, white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin and platelet count, glucose, potassium, sodium and calcium level], and treatments (including antibiotics, bronchodilators, oxygen therapy, and mechanical ventilation support).

Participant selection and outcomes

Patients included in our study should (1) be diagnosed with AECOPD according to the ICD-10 code, (2) be treated with at least one bronchodilator, and (3) receive oxygen therapy or ventilatory support. We excluded patients who were aged < 18 years or stayed in ICUs < 24 h. Patients in the exposure group should be intravenously administrated with methylprednisolone or be treated with oral prednisone within 24 h after ICU admission. The daily dosage should be more than 0.5 mg/kg prednisone-equivalent, and the duration should be at least 3 days. We excluded patients receiving more than 2 mg/kg prednisone-equivalent, considering that the systemic corticosteroids could be used for other therapeutic purposes in these patients. Patients in the non-exposure group were not treated with any systemic corticosteroids during the ICU hospitalization.

The primary outcome was 28-day mortality. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality, the duration of ICU stay and mechanical ventilation.

Statistical analysis

We first checked the integrity of the obtained data, and the results were shown in Additional file 1: Table S2. For several variables (including temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, PO2, PCO2, PH, WBC, hemoglobin and platelet count, glucose, potassium, sodium and calcium level), multiple imputation, which is generally recommended to deal with missing values [18], was used. Variables with a large number of missing values (height and SO2) were not included in our analyses.

The entire cohort consisted of two groups: the exposure group (systemic corticosteroid) and non-exposure group (no systemic corticosteroid). We compared the baseline characteristics between the groups. Continuous variables were presented using mean [with standard deviation (SD)] or median (with the first and third quartiles), according to the normality of the data distribution. Categorical variables were reported with number and percentage. The independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for analyses of continuous variables. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for analyses of categorical variables. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Then, we used propensity score matching (PSM) to balance the differences between groups [19], and the primary and secondary outcomes were evaluated based on the matched cohort. The variables in the propensity score model included age, sex, race, year of admission, type of ICU admission, Charlson Comorbidity Index, co-morbidities or complications (pneumonia, asthma, hypertension, congestive heart failure, peptic ulcer disease, liver disease, diabetes, renal disease, cancer), severity of illness (SOFA and SAPS II score) and vital signs (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate and mean arterial pressure) at ICU admission, laboratory tests (PO2, PCO2, PH, WBC count, hemoglobin count, platelet count, glucose level, potassium level, sodium level, calcium level), and treatments (antibiotic, oxygen therapy and mechanical ventilation) within 24 h after ICU admission. The PSM was performed using the nearest neighbor method with a 1:1 ratio. According to wang et al.’s and Austin et al.’s suggestion [20, 21], the caliper width was set as 0.2 without replacement. P > 0.05 indicated a balance between groups. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for analyses of categorical outcomes. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using the logistic regression models. The independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for analyses of continuous outcomes. Median differences (MD) with 95% CIs were calculated using Hodges-Lehmann estimators.

We performed subgroup analyses for the primary outcome based on the matched cohort, according to age (≥ 65 vs < 65), sex, type of corticosteroid, type of ICU admission, type of mechanical ventilation, and co-morbidities/complications (including pneumonia, heart failure and diabetes mellitus).

We conducted sensitivity analyses for the primary outcome using the entire cohort data, to examine the robustness of the results obtained from the matched cohort. ORs with 95% CI were calculated using the logistic regression models. Variables that were significantly different between groups were included in the univariate analyses (see Additional file 1: Table S3), and those with a P < 0.05 in univariable analyses were included in the multivariable analyses for adjustment (i.e., age, SAPS II score, hemoglobin count, Charlson Comorbidity Index, renal disease and mechanical ventilation). Multicollinearity analyses were performed before multivariable analyses and a variance inflation factor of < 5 was considered of no multicollinearity (see Additional file 1: Table S4).

All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.2) and SPSS (version 25.0).

Results

Patient selection

A total of 73,181 ICU admissions were obtained from MIMIC-IV database. After excluding repeated ICU admissions, we identified 1489 patients with an ICD code of AECOPD. According to the established inclusion criteria, we finally included 763 eligible patients in our entire study cohort.

Characteristics of the entire and matched cohorts

The characteristics of participants before and after PSM are presented in Table 1. The entire cohort included 763 patients among which 409 patients (53.6%) received systemic corticosteroid therapy. Compared with those not receiving systemic corticosteroid, patients treated with systemic corticosteroid were more likely to be older and have pneumonia and hypertension and less likely to have peptic ulcer disease and renal disease. Patients in the systemic corticosteroid group had higher mean arterial pressure, PCO2, hemoglobin count, glucose level, and calcium level; had lower SOFA score, SAPS II score, PO2, PH, and Charlson Comorbidity Index; and are more likely to receive antibiotics and mechanical ventilation. A total of 412 patients (206 in each group) were included in the matched cohort and the variables were well-balanced between groups.

Secondary outcomes

The results of secondary outcomes were shown in Table 2. The ICU mortality was 7.3% (15/206) and 7.8% (16/206) in patients treated with and without systemic corticosteroid respectively, without significant difference between groups (OR: 0.93, 95% CI: 045–1.94, P = 0.852). Similarly, the in-hospital mortality was 13.1% (27/206) and 12.1% (25/206) in patients treated with and without systemic corticosteroid respectively, without significant difference between groups (OR: 1.09, 95% CI: 0.61–1.95, P = 0.767). The median length of ICU stay was 3.4 (1.9, 6.1) days in systemic corticosteroid group and 2.6 (1.6, 5.8) days in no systemic corticosteroid group. The use of systemic corticosteroid was associated with a longer duration of ICU stay (MD: 0.41, 95% CI: 0.08–0.80, P = 0.026). The median length of mechanical ventilation was 40.0 (22.0, 89.0) hours in systemic corticosteroid group and 24.5 (12.5, 75.5) hours in no systemic corticosteroid group. The use of systemic corticosteroid was associated with a mildly longer duration of mechanical ventilation (MD: 9.23, 95% CI: 0.00–19.00), P = 0.049).

Discussion

In this matched cohort, the systemic corticosteroid therapy failed to show any beneficial impact on ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality, and 28-day mortality in patients with AECOPD who were admitted to ICU. The sensitivity analysis based on the entire cohort data further verified these results. Our findings were consistent with those of two previously reported trials, both of which suggested that corticosteroid therapy could not decrease the ICU mortality [8, 9]. Although Galerneau et al.’s cohort study found that corticosteroids improved the composite endpoint at day 28 (i.e., death or failure of NIV), as for short-term mortality, corticosteroids showed no benefits [10]. We noticed that in previous trials, the investigators recruited only patients receiving ventilatory support. Our matched cohort included roughly 60% patients not receiving mechanical ventilation, and the subgroup analysis suggested that these patients could not benefit from systemic corticosteroid neither. For patients with COPD, pneumonia is an acute event which could contribute to exacerbations, and prior trials excluded those having the evidence of pneumonia. Interestingly, a recent trial revealed that the use of hydrocortisone decreased the 28-day mortality of patients with severe community-acquired pneumonia in ICUs [22]. However, our subgroup analyses indicated that systemic corticosteroid had no impact on 28-day mortality no matter in patients combined with pneumonia or those without pneumonia. Information about the time of diagnosis establishment was not available from MIMIC database, which implied that it is difficult to determine whether these patients had been with pneumonia at ICU admission or they developed hospital acquired infection after corticosteroids prescription. Therefore, such potential bias should be considered.

Moreover, we did not find any benefits of systemic corticosteroid on the length of ICU stay and mechanical ventilation. Notably, the length of ICU stay (median 3.4 days and 2.6 days, respectively) in this cohort study was shorter than the results reported in previous trials. About 60% of the patients included in our cohort did not receive mechanical ventilation, which can be a reasonable explanation for the difference. The severity of AECOPD in these patients was milder than that of patients receiving mechanical ventilation, which led to the reduction of total length of ICU stay. As for the duration of mechanical ventilation, prior studies have reported inconsistent results. In Alı’a et al.’s trial, systemic corticosteroid therapy reduced the duration of mechanical ventilation (3 days vs 4 days). However, Abroug et al. found that systemic corticosteroid had no impact on the mechanical ventilation duration (6 days in each group). On the one hand, 44% of the patients received NIV in Alı’a et al.’s trial while the ratio was 76% in Abroug et al.’s trial, which was considered as one of potential causes of the differences. In our matched cohort, approximately 40% of the patients received mechanical ventilation, among which three quarters received NIV. Similarly, our results indicated that the use of systemic corticosteroid was linked with a longer duration of mechanical ventilation. One possible explanation is that patients receiving NIV are unlikely to benefit from systemic corticosteroid. However, our results were mainly based on the matched cohort rather than the entire cohort; the potential attrition bias should be noted. On the other hand, in Alı’a et al.’s trial, the NIV failure rate was much lower in the treatment group (0% vs 37%), which could also explain the significant reduction of the mechanical ventilation duration in the overall population. However, such a low failure rate in patients treated with systemic corticosteroid is surprising, as the NIV failure rate of the intervention group in Abroug et al.’s study was 15.7%, and the average reported in previous literature was 12% [23]. Although it can be interpreted as the patients in Alı’a et al.’s trial showed excellent treatment response to systemic corticosteroid, it should be considered whether such result can be extrapolated to all patients since the sample size was very small.

Systemic corticosteroid has been recommended for the treatment of severe AECOPD. However, the duration and dosage should be noted because evidence indicated that the systemic corticosteroid use, even for the short-term use is linked with a higher risk of pneumonia, sepsis and mortality [24, 25]. The hospital-acquired infections are very common in ICU and often lead to poor prognosis [26, 27]. Therefore, the administration of systemic corticosteroid should be taken with caution in ICUs.

This retrospective analysis was performed using the MIMIC-IV database, and we did not conduct prior statistical power estimation. However, according to Abroug et al.’s and Alı’a et al.’s calculations [8, 9], a sample size of at least 150 patients in each group would have enough power to detect the difference in relevant outcomes. Our study included 412 patients in the matched cohort (763 patients in the entire cohort) to investigate the impact of systemic corticosteroid on patients with AECOPD in ICUs. Compared with a completely randomized design, the potential bias is the most obvious limitation in this matched cohort study. First, it has been reported that body mass index was associated with the prognosis of critically ill patients [28] and those with AECOPD [29]. However, this variable was not included in the multivariable analysis resulting from a plenty of missing values. Second, this retrospective analysis including patients in ICUs was mainly based on a large database. As a result, the data of lung function (gold stages) and smoking statuses were not available. These factors might confound the relation between corticosteroid therapy and outcomes of interest since several studies have indicated that they are strong predictions on survival in patients with AECOPD [30, 31]. Finally, although we performed PSM and multivariable analyses, residual biases still should be noted.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we failed to find any benefits of systemic corticosteroid use on clinical outcomes including short-term mortality, the duration of mechanical ventilation, and ICU/hospital stay. The results of this study do not support routine use of corticosteroid in patients with AECOPD admitted to ICU.
