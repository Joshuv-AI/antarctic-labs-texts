On the Limitations of Pseudorandom Unitaries Or: Cryptographic Applications of LOCC indistinguishability of identical versus independent Haar unitaries A preliminary version of this paper appears in the proceedings of the 23rd Theory of Cryptography Conference ( TCC 2025 ). This is the full version.
Prabhanjan Ananth, Aditya Gulati, Yao-Ting Lin

Abstract
Pseudorandom unitaries (PRUs), one of the key quantum pseudorandom notions, are efficiently computable unitaries that are computationally indistinguishable from Haar random unitaries. While there is evidence to believe that PRUs are weaker than one-way functions, so far its relationship with other quantum cryptographic primitives (that are plausibly weaker than one-way functions) has not been fully established. In this work, we focus on quantum cryptographic primitives with classical communication, referred to as QCCC primitives. Our main result shows that QCCC bit commitments and QCCC key agreement, cannot be constructed from pseudorandom unitaries in a black-box manner. Our core technical contribution is to show (in a variety of settings) the difficulty of distinguishing identical versus independent Haar unitaries by separable channels. Our result strictly improves upon prior works which studied similar problems in the context of learning theory [Anshu, Landau, Liu, STOC 2022] and cryptography [Ananth, Gulati, Lin, TCC 2024].

Inverse Adaptive Queries: Obstacles.

Proving LOCC indistinguishability in the presence of inverse adaptive queries remains an open problem. We explain briefly the obstacles to obtaining LOCC indistinguishability for the generalised case. First, existing techniques from state-based settings do not extend to our oracle model. The two natural approaches to attempt the generalized setting are using approximation formulas (like the twirling formula
[
HY24
,
SHH25
]
) and using purification (like path-recording framework
[
MH25
]
). The approximation idea relies on using a multiplicative approximation for Haar twirling. For forward-only queries, one can obtain such a result using the Weingarten calculus
[
HY24
,
SHH25
]
; however, no analogous closed-form or approximation is known for the mixed twirling case involving both
U
and
U^{\dagger}
, making the inverse-query setting substantially harder. For the purification route, it is unclear whether purification techniques can help at all, once we purify the interaction with a common unitary, both parties become entangled with the shared purification register, making it difficult to analyze the protocol within the LOCC framework. Moreover, as far we know, path-recording only gives us an additive approximation. We believe that significantly new ideas are needed to tackle the inverse adaptive setting.

2.5
From LOCC Indistinguishability to Black-Box Separations

We discuss how to translate the LOCC indistinguishability frameworks into black-box separations. We first discuss the case of separating QCCC commitments and PRUs. Similar ideas can be adapted to rule out QCCC key agreement and PRUs as well.

To rule out QCCC commitments, we consider the following model: the sender
\mathsf{Sen}
and the receiver
\mathsf{Rec}
in the QCCC commitments have access to exponentially many i.i.d Haar unitaries
{\cal U}=\{U_{k}\}_{k\in[\ell]}
, where the unitary
U_{k}
is an
k
-qubit Haar unitary and
\ell=2^{\lambda}
. Here,
\mathsf{Sen}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
run in time polynomial in
\lambda
. A recent work
[
ABGL25
]
showed that pseudorandom unitaries exist in this model. We show that QCCC commitments do not exist in this model.

To show the impossibility of QCCC commitments, we crucially use the Haar indistinguishability result from
Section

2.2
. Instead of both
\mathsf{Sen}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
having oracle access to the same set of unitaries
\{U_{k}\}_{k\in[\ell]}
, we now have
\mathsf{Sen}
get access to
\{U_{k}\}_{k\in[\ell]}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
get access to
{\cal V}=\{V_{k}\}_{k\in[\ell]}
, where
V_{k}
is a
k
-qubit Haar unitary sampled independently from
U_{k}
. This should not affect the correctness or security at all, thanks to the Haar indistinguishability result. Concretely, the Haar indistinguishability result needs to be invoked polynomially (in
\lambda
) many times, for each unitary the adversary queries to.

Now since both
\mathsf{Sen}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
receive independently sampled Haar random unitaries, instead of giving oracle access to
{\cal U}
and
{\cal V}
, we can now have
\mathsf{Sen}
itself sample
{\cal U}
and similarly, have
\mathsf{Rec}
itself sample
{\cal V}
. In other words, we were able to completely compile out the oracles to obtain a correct and secure commitment scheme, satisfying information-theoretic security, in the plain model. Since information-theoretic security is impossible in the plain model, this would prove that QCCC commitments do not exist in the plain model.

Unfortunately, there is a flaw in the above argument. The flaw lies at the point where we invoke the Haar indistinguishability result. Note that it was shown that the probability that any adversary succeeds in the Haar distinguishing game is at most
O\left(\frac{t^{2}}{N}\right)
, where
N
is the dimension of the unitary it has access to. This means that if
N
is too small (i.e.
\leq t^{2}
) then invoking the Haar indistinguishability result is meaningless. This means that we cannot switch from
U_{k}
to
V_{k}
, for very small
k
. Indeed, this should not be surprising at all since it is possible for a LOCC adversary, using tomography, to distinguish the two cases when both have access to
U_{k}
versus when
\mathsf{Sen}
has access to
U_{k}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
has access to
V_{k}
, where
k=O(\log(\secparam))
.

To address the flaw, we revise the above argument and incorporate the following two step approach:

•

In the first step, we transform the QCCC commitment scheme, where both
\mathsf{Sen}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
have access to
{\cal U}=\{U_{k}\}_{k\in[\ell]}
into another secure and correct commitment scheme, where both have access to
\{U_{k}\}_{k\in[c\cdot(\log(\secparam))]}
, for some constant
c
. That is, we rid of all the unitaries with input length
>c\cdot(\log(\secparam))
. To achieve this transformation, similar to the above argument, we invoke the Haar indistinguishability result.

•

In the second step, we prove the impossibility of any QCCC commitment scheme where both
\mathsf{Sen}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
have access to
\{U_{k}\}_{k\in[O(\log(\secparam))]}
. Informally, the impossibility holds due to the fact that both
\mathsf{Sen}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
can separately perform tomography on the unitaries they have access to, in order to recover an approximate representation of all the unitaries
\{U_{k}\}_{k\in[O(\log(\secparam))]}
. At the end of the tomography process, both
\mathsf{Sen}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
have access to noisy shared randomness. We then argue that realizing QCCC commitments in the noisy share randomness model is impossible. Conceptually, this is similar to the model where both
\mathsf{Sen}
and
\mathsf{Rec}
have access to the same random string and it is known that achieving information-theoretic security in this setting is impossible.

We follow a similar template to prove the impossibility of a certain class of constructions of QCCC commitments from strong PRUs.

Lemma B.14
.

Suppose there exists an interactive QCCC commitment relative to
{\mathcal{U}}
such that (1) the completeness error is at most
\mathsf{negl}({\lambda})
, and (2) any computationally unbounded polynomial-query malicious receiver (resp., sender) can break statistical hiding (resp., sum binding) with advantage at most
\mathsf{negl}({\lambda})
. Then there exists a short interactive QCCC commitment relative to
{\mathcal{U}}
such that: (1) the sender and receiver each make a polynomial number of queries, but are not necessarily time-efficient (2) the completeness error is at most
O\quantity( 1/\secp)
, and (2) any computationally unbounded polynomial-query malicious receiver (resp., sender) can break statistical hiding (resp., sum binding) with advantage at most
\mathsf{negl}({\lambda})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{U}}=(\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Sen}^{\mathcal{U}},\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec}^{\mathcal{U}})
be an interactive QCCC commitment construction relative to
{\mathcal{U}}
that satisfies the given premises in which the sender and receiver each make
q=\mathsf{poly}({\lambda})
queries of length at most
L=\mathsf{poly}({\lambda})
. Let
\ell({\lambda}):=\lceil\log(q^4L^2 + \secp^2)\rceil=O(\log{\lambda})
. Based on
\mathsf{G}
, we define the following short construction:

Short construction
\mathsf{H}
relative to
{\mathcal{U}}
.

\mathsf{H}^{\mathcal{U}}=(\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Sen}^{\mathcal{U}},\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Rec}^{\mathcal{U}})
:

on input
1^{\lambda}
and
b\in\{0,1\}
,

1.

\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Sen}
and
\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Rec}
respectively sample a sequence of Haar random unitaries
\{U_{\mathsf{A},\kappa}\}_{\ell+1\leq\kappa\leq L}
and

\{U_{\mathsf{B},\kappa}\}_{\ell+1\leq\kappa\leq L}
.

2.

\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Sen}
runs
\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Sen}^{(\cdot)}
on input
b
and responds to the queries as follows: if the length does not exceed
\ell
, then make a query to
{\mathcal{U}}
to reply; otherwise, simulate the oracle with
\{U_{\mathsf{A},\kappa}\}_{\ell+1\leq\kappa\leq L}
.

3.

\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Rec}
is defined analogously.

4.

\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Rec}
outputs whatever
\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec}
outputs.

In other words,
\mathsf{H}
is identical to
\mathsf{G}
, except that any query by the sender or receiver exceeding length
\ell
is answered with locally-simulated
independent
Haar random unitaries.

Completeness of
\mathsf{H}
.
We use
(\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Sen},\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec})
to construct a two-party adversary
(\mathsf{HUD}.\mathsf{A},\mathsf{HUD}.\mathsf{B})
in
Theorem

4.10
as follows. First,
\mathsf{HUD}.\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{HUD}.\mathsf{B}
sample and agree on a set of Haar unitaries of length at most
\ell
through classical communication. Then
(\mathsf{HUD}.\mathsf{A},\mathsf{HUD}.\mathsf{B})
runs
(\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Sen},\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec})
by simulating oracles with their oracle access. Suppose
(\mathsf{HUD}.\mathsf{A},\mathsf{HUD}.\mathsf{B})
are given identical Haar oracles, their execution is equivalent to
\mathsf{G}
; otherwise, it follows
\mathsf{H}
. By a similar calculation as in
Lemma

B.6
, the completeness of
\mathsf{H}
is
1-O\quantity( 1/\secp)
.

Hiding and binding of
\mathsf{H}
.
Let
p=\mathsf{poly}({\lambda})
and
\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*,\,{\mathcal{U}}}
be a malicious receiver that makes
p
queries. Since
\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Sen}
never makes queries of length greater than
\ell
, we can assume that
\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*,\,{\mathcal{U}}}
does not either, without reducing its advantage. Consider the reduction
\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*,\,{\mathcal{U}}}
:

\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*,\,{\mathcal{U}}}
:

on input
1^{\lambda}
,

1.

\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*}
runs
\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*,\,(\cdot)}
, responding to queries using
{\mathcal{U}}
.

2.

\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*}
outputs whatever
\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*}
outputs.

By construction,
\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*}
never makes queries of length greater than
\ell
. Hence, the distribution of the hiding security game played by
\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*}
is identically distributed to that of the game played by
\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Rec}^{*}
, which implies that they have the same advantage. We can define
\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Sen}^{*}
similarly and show that
\mathsf{G}.\mathsf{Sen}^{*}
and
\mathsf{H}.\mathsf{Sen}^{*}
have the same advantage.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove
Lemma

5.11
. For convenience, we restate the lemma below:

Lemma B.15

(

, restated)
.

Relative to
{\mathcal{U}}
, for any construction of interactive QCCC key commitments that makes only
forward
queries to
{\mathcal{U}}
and satisfy completeness, there exists either (1) a malicious receiver or (2) a malicious sender (or possibly both) who, while not necessarily time-efficient, makes a polynomial number of queries and breaks the security.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose there was such a construction. However, by
