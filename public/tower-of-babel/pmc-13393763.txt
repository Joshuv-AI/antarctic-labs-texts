Consolidative radiation in the management of relapsed/refractory Hodgkin lymphoma: a single institution study
Authors: Brendan Coutu, Joseph Carmicheal, Elliot Lawrence, Ashok Bhandari, Robert G Bociek, James Armitage, Charles Enke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground5–30% of Hodgkin Lymphoma (HL) patients are refractory to primary therapy or have disease relapse requiring additional salvage treatment. High-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT) is the current standard of care for refractory and recurrent HL. The utility of consolidative RT in the peri-transplant setting has been heavily disputed in recent years. The goal of this study is to evaluate the impact of consolidative radiotherapy in the setting of ASCT in patients with relapsed/refractory HL.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 179 consecutive patients with HL who underwent salvage chemotherapy followed by high dose chemotherapy and an ASCT for relapsed (63.4%) or refractory (36.6%) disease with or without subsequent consolidative RT from 2000 to 2019 at our institution.ResultsMedian follow up was 59.4 months. Post-transplant consolidative RT was delivered to 72 patients (40%) at a median dose of 36 Gray (Gy). Nearly two-thirds (46/72, 63.8%) of patients received involved site radiation therapy. Consolidative RT was associated with younger age (median 32 vs. 42 years, p < 0.001), stage I-II disease at the time of salvage chemotherapy (76.4% vs 52.3%, p = 0.001), and no prior RT (76.4% vs 45.8%, p < 0.001). Univariate analysis revealed consolidative RT was associated with an improved 2-year PFS rate (84.1% vs 64.1%, p = 0.005) and OS rate (95.7% vs 80.8%, p = 0.017). In the Cox regression analysis, PFS was associated with consolidative RT (HR: 0.492, 95% CI: 0.288–0.84, p = 0.009) along with ECOG performance status < 2 (HR: 0.188, 95% CI: 0.045–0.79, p = 0.022), complete response to salvage chemotherapy (HR: 0.463, 95% CI: 0.233–0.92, p = 0.028), and stage I-II disease (HR: 0.546, 95% CI: 0.325–0.918, p = 0.022). Similarly, OS was associated with consolidative RT (HR: 0.398, 95% CI: 0.205–0.771, p = 0.006) and ECOG performance status < 2 (HR: 0.073, 95% CI: 0.016–0.333, p = 0.001). Subset analysis suggested that RT was associated with an improved PFS and OS even in those with complete response or non-bulky disease after ASCT. Minimal toxicity was observed in irradiated patients and no deaths were associated with treatment toxicity.ConclusionsPeri-transplant consolidative RT for the salvage management of HL is an effective, complimentary, and well tolerated addition to ASCT for the treatment of relapsed and refractory HL.

Introduction

In the United States, an estimated 8,720 patients were diagnosed with Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) in 2025 [1]. Current primary therapeutic modalities, such as conventional chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy (RT) are highly efficacious and can facilitate cure in the majority of patients with HL [2–5]. Unfortunately, 5–30% of patients are refractory to primary therapy or have disease relapse requiring additional salvage treatment [3, 6, 7]. The most common site of recurrence is at the initial site of disease [8]. High-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT) has repeatedly been associated with improved outcomes compared to conventional chemotherapy for refractory and recurrent HL [9–11]. In the salvage setting, the current International Lymphoma Radiation Oncology Group (ILROG) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend salvage chemotherapy followed by high-dose non-cross-resistant chemotherapy and ASCT, with or without consolidative RT to involved sites for improved local control [12, 13].

The utility of consolidative RT in the peri-transplant setting has been heavily disputed in recent years. This is due to a lack of prospective studies, mixed findings on retrospective analyses, and diverse treatment agents utilized for primary disease management [14–22]. However, the preponderance of these studies identify an improvement in patient outcomes including local control and progression-free survival (PFS), and a minority offering an increase in overall survival (OS), with the addition of RT to the ASCT treatment regimen. Recently, a trend has developed toward preferentially utilizing chemotherapy alone without the addition of RT in the management of HL. With this, PET imaging has become increasingly important in the contemporary assessment of disease response in an effort to modulate treatment strategy and mitigate associated risk [23]. However, this trend towards dose de-escalation with RT omission in the upfront setting to mitigate toxicity preserves the use of RT in the salvage setting. This, coupled with the dismal prognosis associated with ASCT treatment failure [8], warrants further inquiry into the impact of RT in the peri-transplant setting for refractory and relapsed HL.

We undertook this study to better define the impact of consolidative RT in patients who underwent ASCT for refractory or relapsed HL. We evaluated a cohort of HL patients who were consecutively treated at our institution with RT after high-dose chemotherapy and ASCT. Analysis was conducted to determine the association of RT with various clinicopathologic factors as well as its impact on PFS and OS.

Methods and materials

Patient selection

We reviewed the medical records of 179 consecutive patients at our institution with relapsed or refractory HL who underwent an ASCT between 2000 and 2019. Clinical data were obtained from the electronic medical record. This retrospective review was approved by our IRB (#0560–19-EP) and was carried out in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients to have their deidentified data collected and assessed for research purposes.

Final determination of transplant eligibility was up to the primary oncologist, but selection criteria over the period of the study generally included ECOG performance status < 2, age ≤ 70, and no end-organ dysfunction. Patients were considered for ASCT following an initial relapse or if they were found to be refractory to first-line therapy. Bulky disease was defined as any adenopathy or tumor mass measuring ≥ 5.0 cm in greatest dimension before the initiation of salvage chemotherapy in the pretransplant setting [15, 16, 18, 21].

Therapeutic protocol

Patients underwent 2–3 cycles of salvage therapy with non-cross-resistant chemotherapy (most commonly ICE: ifosfamide, carboplatin, and etoposide). All patients underwent a preparatory regimen with high dose chemotherapy (most commonly BEAM: BCNU, etoposide, Ara-C, and melphalan) followed by infusion of autologous hematopoietic cells. Standard supportive care was provided as described previously [24].

Imaging

Eligibility for ASCT following salvage chemotherapy was assessed by cross-sectional imaging including positron emission tomography (PET)/computerized tomography (CT), or CT alone. The use of functional imaging affects response assessment by emphasizing the metabolic response even in patients with residual tissue at the site of recurrence. A complete response (CR) was defined as a Deauville score of 1–3 (regardless of size of residual disease) or nodal masses having regressed to ≤1.5 cm in largest dimension (as assess on CT imaging). A partial response (PR) was defined as a Deauville score of 4–5 with reduced uptake compared with baseline and residual masses of any size or ≥50% decrease in the sum of products in perpendicular diameters (SPD) and not otherwise meeting criteria for CR. No response (NR) or stable disease (SD) was defined as a Deauville score of 4–5 with no significant change in FDG uptake from baseline or <50% decrease from baseline in SPD. Progressive disease (PD) was defined as a Deauville score of 4–5 with an increase in intensity of uptake from baseline, a new FDG-avid foci consistent with lymphoma, or an increase in the longest diameter (LDi) or shortest axis perpendicular to the LDi of 0.5 cm for lesions ≤ 2 cm or 1.0 cm for lesions > 2 cm.

Consolidative RT

Patients were considered for consolidative RT following ASCT at the discretion of their medical oncologist and radiation oncologist. The radiation fields were selected on a case-by-case basis. Patients underwent consolidative radiation therapy to each involved site (comprehensive site RT) identified on an FDG PET-CT or CT imaging prior to wide-spread use of PET imaging in this setting. Patients most commonly underwent 36 Gy (Gy) in 20 daily fractions to the sites of disease involvement following completion of transplant, initiating treatment prior to post-transplant day 100. Treatment fields were most commonly involved site RT with nearly two-thirds (46/72, 63.8%) of patients received involved site radiation therapy and the reamining minority of cases receiving involved field RT.

Follow-up protocol

Following ASCT with or without RT, PET-CT was performed at approximately day 100 to assess response. Patients were followed at the discretion of their medical oncologist with CT and PET imaging as indicated. Commonly, those patients that had a CR at day 100 were not followed with subsequent imaging. Patients were determined to have relapsed post-transplantation if new or enlarged lesions were identified on cross-sectional imaging with pathologic confirmation. Toxicities or adverse events were reported according to the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE). Each category of adverse event is reported, rather than simply the highest-grade adverse event experienced per patient.

Statistical analysis

The date of relapse for a patient was defined as the first day that findings were observed on either clinical exam or imaging (with biopsy confirmation). The duration of PFS and OS were calculated from the date of ASCT to the date of relapse or death. OS was defined as the time from the date of ASCT to date of death; subjects known to be alive at last follow-up are treated as censored. PFS was defined as the date of ASCT to date of progression or death; those alive and progression free at last follow-up are treated as censored. Univariate and multivariate associations of factors associated with PFS and OS were determined with the log-rank test, Kaplan-Meier estimates, and cox regression analysis. Hazard ratios were computed with the Cox proportional hazards model. p-values less than 0.05 were considered to be significant. Factors were included in the cox regression model if the p-value was less than 0.10. Factors included in univariate analysis were age (<37 vs ≥ 37), gender (male vs female), presence of B symptoms (yes vs no), Eastern cooperative oncology group (ECOG) performance status (<2 vs ≥ 2), stage at diagnosis (I-II vs III-IV), prior chemotherapy (yes vs no), size of largest disease at time of salvage chemotherapy (≤5.0 cm vs > 5.0 cm), response to salvage chemotherapy (CR vs PR), disease status (primary chemotherapy refractory disease vs. relapsed), and consolidative RT (yes vs no). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Consolidative RT was completed at a median of 74 days post ASCT (range: 27–137 days) to a median dose of 36 gray (Gy) (range: 24–40 Gy). No statistically significant differences in CR or PR were observed between various RT doses. 45.8% of patients had only a single site of irradiation and 54.2% were irradiated at ≥ 2 sites (9 total patients had 3 sites radiated, with none receiving radiation to 4 or more sites). The predominant sites of RT included the mediastinum (44.4%), supraclavicular fields (32.8%), and abdominal field (15.7%). Reasons for which consolidative RT was omitted include prior RT (n = 51, 47.7%), non-bulky disease (n = 13, 12.1%), too many sites (n = 17, 15.9%), medical comorbidity (n = 5, 4.7%), and patient refusal (n = 5, 4.7%), In 11 cases (10.3%) no reason could be identified. Notably, of the 26 patients who were defined as bulky (≥5 cm) and who did not receive consolidative radiation, 14 had previous RT to the site, 5 harbored too many sites of disease as determined by the clinical team, 2 were considered medically unfit, 2 were ultimately deemed non-bulky by the clinical team, and 1 is unknown.

Administration of consolidative RT was associated with younger patient age (median 32 vs. 42 years, p < 0.001), stage I-II disease at the time of salvage chemotherapy (76.4% vs 52.3%, p = 0.001), and no prior RT (76.4% vs 45.8%, p < 0.001) (Table 1). There was no difference between those receiving and not receiving RT in terms of gender, ECOG performance status, presence of B-symptoms, bulky disease, relapse or refractory disease, or response to salvage chemotherapy (i.e. complete response CR vs PR vs SD/NR (see Table 1).

Clinical outcomes

During a median follow-up period of 59.4 months, there were 56 relapses and 49 patient deaths in the entire cohort. The 2-year PFS and OS rates for the entire cohort were 72.0% and was 86.9% respectively. On univariate analysis, consolidative RT was associated with a significantly improved 2-year PFS rate compared to omission (84.1% and 64.1%, p = 0.005) which held true at 5 years as well (79.1% and 57.4%, p = 0.003), respectively (see Fig. 1, Table 2).

In the Cox regression analysis consolidative RT was independently associated with improved PFS (HR: 0.492, 95% CI: 0.288–0.84, p = 0.009) as well as OS (HR: 0.398, 95% CI: 0.205–0.771, p = 0.006). ECOG performance status < 2 (HR: 0.188, 95% CI: 0.045–0.79, p = 0.022), achieving a complete response to salvage chemotherapy (HR: 0.463, 95% CI: 0.233–0.92, p = 0.028), and stage I-II disease (HR: 0.546, 95% CI: 0.325–0.918, p = 0.022) were also significantly associated with improved PFS. Further, ECOG performance status < 2 was significantly associated with improved OS (HR: 0.073, 95% CI: 0.016–0.333, p = 0.001) (Table 2).

Subset analysis revealed that those with non-bulky disease (n = 136) who received consolidative RT had a significantly improved 2-year PFS rate compared to omission at 81.1% and 60.4% (p = 0.018) as well as an improved 2-year OS rate at 94.4% and 77.5% (p = 0.002), respectively (Fig. 3a). Additionally, patients who achieved a CR to salvage chemotherapy prior to ASCT (n = 82) still benefited from consolidative RT with a significant improvement in PFS compared to omission at 84.9% compared to 66.5% (p = 0.009) as well as OS at 95.6% and 83.1% (p = 0.001), respectively (Fig. 3b).

Toxicity

Consolidative RT was generally well tolerated among our patient population. Specifically with regard to pneumonitis, 10 patients (5.6%) from the entire cohort developed ≥ grade 2 toxicity of which only 7 patients underwent consolidative RT. Of the patients who experienced grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis, RT fields included the mediastinum (50%), supraclavicular fields (10%), and abdominal field (10%). No patient deaths were associated with pneumonitis nor were any grade 4 or 5 toxicities observed.

Discussion

To our knowledge this is the largest comparative retrospective study investigating the impact of peri-transplant RT in patients with in relapsed or refractory HL. Additionally, the majority of these patients (46/72, 63.8%) received involved site radiation therapy inline with contemporary treatment volumes. Herein, we report an association between consolidative RT and improved 2-year and 5-year PFS as well OS rates. In the multivariate analysis the effect of RT on PFS and OS remained independently prognostic along with patient age, gender, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, response to salvage chemotherapy, and disease stage. We further report that patients who had a CR to salvage chemotherapy with ASCT as well as those who had non-bulky disease benefited from consolidative RT, leading to improved PFS and OS within these subgroups.

The current treatment ILROG as well as NCCN guidelines for relapsed and refractory HL involves salvage chemotherapy followed by ASCT with or without consolidative RT [11, 12]. Previous studies have found that this regimen facilitates cure of about 50% of these patients [10]. Given the lack of understanding regarding the most appropriate chronological application of RT as well as the lack of prospective randomized trials investigating the impact of peri-transplant consolidative RT in the salvage setting, the specific indication for consolidative RT as proposed by ILROG include local relapse, or disseminated relapse with partial response to salvage treatment, bulky disease as determined by persistent avidity on PET-CT (i.e. Deauville score), and locations where local control is critical such as the spinal cord, superior vena cava, and airway [11]. The NCCN has similar guidelines [12]. Interestingly, our study suggests that even patients with a CR to salvage chemotherapy or non-bulky disease may experience a survival benefit.

The few comparative studies that have been conducted on the efficacy of RT in the peri-transplant setting have had several limitations including having all been retrospective in nature, different induction chemo regimens, various timing of RT administration relative to transplantation, and small RT cohorts. The studies heretofore published are presented in Table 3. The majority of these studies show an improvement in PFS and 2 of them suggest an improvement in OS in patients treated with consolidative RT: Biswas et al. demonstrated an improvement in 3-year OS (69.6% vs 40.0%, p = 0.05) [17] and Eroglu et al. demonstrated an improvement in 10-year OS (66% vs 24%, p = 0.05) in stage I-II patients [14] (Table 3). A contemporary non-comparative retrospective study by Levis et al. has been included in Table 3 as it is the largest multi-institutional series of 131 patients with long term follow up after peri-transplant radiation therapy that has heretofore been reported in the literature. Therein they report a 5-year PFS and OS of 72 and 77%, respectively. They additionally demonstrated that patients with >3 sites radiated had worse OS than those with ≤3 radiated sites (HR, 3.69; p = 0.1). [25]

Two non-comparative yet pertinent studies not included in the table revealed that salvage RT in patients who underwent ASCT was associated with partial/complete responses as well as excellent durable local control and OS [26, 27]. Our study has corroborated these previous findings regarding PFS with an increase in robustness imparted by a larger cohort of patients undergoing RT. Additionally, this is the first comparative study to demonstrate a significant improvement in 2-year OS rates in patients treated with consolidative RT for patients with relapse and refractory HL.

We observed that those patients with non-bulky disease as well as patients who achieved a CR after ASCT benefited from consolidative RT with significant improvement in 2-year PFS as well as OS rates. The implications of these findings could be quite dramatic as the aforementioned salvage treatment paradigm for relapsed and refractory HL is predicated on the notion that those with bulky-disease and partial responses benefit from the addition of consolidative RT to a greater extent than those with non-bulky disease and patients with CR.

Notably, given the time-period covered by this retrospective study, both CT and PET were utilized for diagnosis, staging, and treatment response assessment. However, only 12.85% (23/179) of all patients were assessed via CT whereas 87.15% (156/179) of patients completed PET based assessment. Interestingly, yet not entirely unexpected, 21.4% (5/23) of those who underwent CT based assessment were determined to have a complete response while 49.36% (77/156) were determined to achieve a complete response when assess by PET. These data highlight the ability and importance of PET to detect metabolic activity in tissue or masses which dramatically improves our clinical accuracy, especially in the setting of response assessment after therapy.

One of the key issues regarding the utilization of RT in the salvage setting continues to be timing and appropriate patient selection. While radiation can be given prior to or after transplantation, our institutional practice has been to utilize post-transplant consolidative RT in an effort to avoid any delay to transplant as well as decrease the radiation treatment volumes as much as possible, especially in the setting of contemporary involved site radiation therapy. Our findings suggesting the improvement in PFS and OS allow for the consideration of adding consolidative RT, even in patients who achieve a CR after salvage chemotherapy and ASCT. Further support of this notion can be found in work conducted by Song et al. that suggest significant improvement in 5-year PFS and OS when patients who already had a CR to salvage chemotherapy underwent consolidative RT [28]. While this cohort is slightly different than the one in this study in that they did not undergo ASCT and they received less radiation dose (20 Gy vs 36 Gy), it suggests an advantage associated with consolidative RT for HL in the setting of CR.

Another primary concern in the utilization of RT is the risk of associated toxicities. Notably, in our cohort, minimal short-term toxicities were observed within the 59.4-month median follow up interval. Perhaps more importantly, the rates of pneumonitis were low. No patients had grade ≥ 4 pneumonitis. The relatively minimal toxicity experienced by our patients compared to historical findings may be a result of myriad factors including administration only post-transplant, modern radiation treatment techniques allowing for smaller and more precise treatment volumes with decreased dose, and the conservative number of treatment fields defined by residual avidity on PET-CT or previously involved disease sites. One patient in the present cohort developed a second malignancy, small cell lung cancer. This patient did not undergo consolidative RT. The authors recognize that the follow-up period of 59.4 months in this cohort is insufficient to evaluate the risk of secondary malignancies.

Along with patient selection, the role of RT in the salvage management of HL has come under increased scrutiny in light of advances in salvage treatment options including targeted immunotherapy such as brentuximab vedotin (BV). For example, the randomized phase 3 AETHERA trial, designed to assess the efficacy of BV as consolidative therapy in the setting of ASCT, revealed a significant increase in 2-year PFS rate in patients with high-risk features (refractory HL, relapse within 12 months, or extra nodal disease) present prior to receiving ASCT [29]. Another phase 3 trial, the ECHELON-1 study, demonstrated significant improvement in PFS and OS when BV was utilized in patients with untreated stage III and IV disease [30]. This led to multiple subsequent investigations of BV in the salvage setting showing a higher rate of CR compared to standard chemotherapy alone [31, 32]. With these findings, the NCCN recommendation is to consider BV maintenance therapy for HL patients with high-risk features who have not had prior BV exposure [26]. However, a meta-analysis of high-risk HL patients treated with consolidative BV at 25 institutions revealed 23% of patients (17 out of 75) discontinued BV due to adverse events or by patient/physician decision with 16 patients experiencing grade 3 or 4 toxicity. Further, BV administration prior to ASCT did not provide a significant benefit in 2-year PFS rate while post-transplant BV significantly improved 2-year PFS rate [33]. These factors, in conjunction with historical data revealing the majority of HL patients fail within sites of initial disease, suggest the importance of local control, as local control has a significant association with disease specific survival [17]. Consolidative peri-transplant RT offers a possible means by which local control can be improved in the setting of ASCT, thus reserving other second line systemic therapeutic options for disseminated, refractory, or relapsed disease if ASCT were to fail. There is no denying the efficacy of BV and other targeted therapies (i.e. checkpoint inhibitors [34]) for the treatment of relapsed or refractory HL, but RT remains a viable complementary component of this paradigm. Further studies are warranted to investigate possible synergism between these two modalities as well as the most appropriate chronological sequence within the treatment algorithm.

As with all retrospective comparative analyses, one of the primary limitations with this study is the possibility of selection bias. Much like the preponderance of previous studies, the decision to treat with RT was left to the physician teams caring for the patients and not outlined in a previously defined protocol. A prime example of this is the disparity between stage I and II patients at the time of salvage chemotherapy (76.4% vs 52.3%, p = 0.001) in the consolidation RT group compared to the no RT group. This has obvious implications on patient outcomes and is a prime limitation of our study. In the attempt to ameliorate this, multivariate analysis was employed in the attempt to control for and mitigate this inherent aspect of retrospective review. Also, not all patients received the same salvage high-dose chemotherapy regimen. Specific strengths of this study include the large overall patient sample size, the largest cohort of any comparative study of RT in the setting of ASCT conducted to date, and the use of multivariable analysis to investigate if the effect of RT was independent of other variables. Also, all patients were treated similarly and exclusively treated with RT after ASCT with similar fractionation and dosing. Finally, long-term systematic follow up was completed for the majority of patients for nearly 5 years adding an increased degree of confidence to the reported results.

These results communicated herein are particularly important given the dynamic changes occurring in the general treatment paradigm for refractory and relapsed HL after ASCT. In one of the largest comparative analyses incorporating the largest RT cohort conducted to date, use of consolidative RT in the salvage management of HL was supported by observed improvement in 2-year PFS and OS rates. This survival advantage was preserved even in the setting of CR to high-dose chemotherapy and ASCT as well as in patients with non-bulky disease. It is important to remain cognizant of possible long-term adverse effects associated with various therapeutic modalities including systemic chemotherapy, stem cell transplantation, and RT. Notably, RT was well tolerated with minimal toxicity within this cohort. While the role of RT may be shifting in the era of targeted treatment capabilities, it remains a viable weapon within the overall therapeutic armamentarium and adds practical value to HL patient outcomes. The integration of these combination therapies warrants further prospective clinical evaluation to parse out the most prudent and effective treatment for these patients.
