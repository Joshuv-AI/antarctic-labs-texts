Anatomical localization and resection of a refractory epileptic focus in the right supplementary motor area/presupplementary motor area using augmented reality: illustrative case
Authors: Venina S. Kalistratova, Gabriel Urreola, E. Brandon Strong, Paolo Palmisciano, Rocelle Evangelista, Osama Raslan, Julia D. Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDMedically refractory seizures occur in up to 30% of patients with epilepsy. Treatments may include neuromodulation, ablative procedures, and stereotactic surgery. Cutting-edge technology, such as neuroimaging, anatomical mapping, electroencephalography, and augmented reality (AR), assist in localization and safer treatment planning.OBSERVATIONSA 26-year-old male with focal onset epilepsy underwent phase II monitoring with stereo-electroencephalography (sEEG) that localized his seizure focus to the right supplementary motor area (SMA)/pre-SMA. A combination of traditional navigation, phase-reversal electrocorticography, and AR was used to perform anatomical localization and verification to assist with craniotomy and resection. He is now seizure free with no significant neurological deficit 9 months after surgery.LESSONSAR has multiple applications in neurosurgery, including preoperative planning, anatomical verification, and intraoperative guidance. In this case, AR navigation provided real-time 3D overlays of MRI- and CT-derived patient anatomy onto the surgical field, allowing visualization of segmented cortical surface anatomy, vascular structures, white matter tracts, and electrodes via voice commands. This enabled precise mapping of deep-seated anatomy and electrode trajectories without increasing operative time or complication risk. Given the early stage of the technology, AR was used adjunctively with neuronavigation during SMA/pre-SMA resection and demonstrated localization concordant with traditional mapping techniques. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25611

Medically refractory seizures occur in up to 30% of patients with epilepsy.1 When the seizure onset zone can be localized to a specific region, surgery has been shown to be superior to medical management in these patients with decreased seizure burden and improved quality of life.1,2 Stereo-electroencephalography (sEEG) represents the most common and accurate method of epileptogenic foci identification as it can record the electrical activity of deeper brain regions with minimal surgical risks. Following sEEG-facilitated localization of the seizure onset zone, resection, neuromodulation, or ablative procedures may significantly decrease seizure frequency and in some instances may offer a cure. The use of augmented reality (AR) for visualization of sEEG implantation trajectories and surgical planning has previously been documented, but its use in epileptogenic focus resection has been limited.3 Here we present the case of a patient who underwent sEEG-assisted detection of epileptogenic foci located in the right supplementary motor area (SMA) and pre-SMA, followed by focused resection using a combination of phase-reversal electrocorticography (ECOG) mapping, neuronavigation (Brainlab), and AR (Xironetic). We discuss our experience with AR as an adjunct perioperative tool for surgical planning and execution.

Illustrative Case

A 26-year-old right-handed man with a 16-year history of medically refractory focal epilepsy was evaluated for surgical treatment. He experienced frequent brief episodes of behavioral arrest and absence as well as infrequent generalized convulsive seizures. No structural abnormalities were identified on 3T MRI. Video-EEG captured 13 focal motor seizures of right parasagittal onset and 3 seizures with poorly localized onset. Interictal PET showed diffuse decreased radiotracer activity within the right prefrontal lateral cortex (Z score −3.3 SD). Magnetoencephalography was used to map epileptiform discharges to the anterior right temporal lobe, right superior and middle frontal gyri, and right inferior parietal lobule. Phase II monitoring with sEEG was performed to better localize his seizure onset zone. Electrodes were placed in the right prefrontal cortex, right parietal cortex, and bilateral temporal lobes (Fig. 1). All 11 seizures captured localized to the right pre-SMA and SMA regions. The case was discussed at our multidisciplinary epilepsy conference, and an ECOG-guided resection of the epileptogenic focus as well as the SMA/pre-SMA was recommended. Surgical Approach Using AR-Guided Preoperative Planning

Thin-cut preoperative MRI of the brain with and without contrast, MR angiography, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and postimplantation CT studies were performed. Postcontrast T1-weighted MRI was used to visualize vascular anatomy, DTI was used to reconstruct the corticospinal tract, and sEEG electrode contacts and trajectories were identified from the postimplantation CT co-registered to the preoperative MRI. Virtual surgical planning was performed using Elements (Brainlab) and 3D Slicer (http://www.slicer.org). Cortical surfaces and relevant anatomical structures, including the precentral and postcentral gyri, were segmented from MRI using semiautomated, intensity-based tools with manual refinement. Stereo-EEG electrodes were segmented using threshold-based methods with manual verification. All segmented structures were converted into 3D surface mesh files and imported into the AR planning environment.

AR software, collaboratively developed with Xironetic, was deployed on the Microsoft HoloLens 2, a commercially available head-mounted display. The patient’s head was fixed in a Mayfield head clamp, and a Brainlab reference array was positioned adjacent to the head to anchor registration. Patient-specific anatomical landmarks (tragal notch, lateral canthus, and medial canthus bilaterally) were identified on imaging and used for intraoperative registration. Registration was performed by the primary surgeon wearing the AR headset using a tracked stylus with reflective spheres to define the anatomical landmarks (Fig. 2). An optical code array (ArUco marker library) tracked by the HoloLens optical cameras served as the registration anchor, allowing alignment of the virtual models with the patient. Following initial alignment, fine microadjustments were performed by the surgeon to optimize registration accuracy, which was subsequently verified using surface anatomical landmarks. Once registration was confirmed, virtual objects (sEEG electrode trajectories, corticospinal tract reconstructions, and cortical landmarks) were visualized as a 3D overlay directly within the surgical field and could be selectively displayed using voice commands. AR visualization was used in conjunction with conventional Brainlab neuronavigation, which underwent a separate surface-based optical registration and demonstrated excellent concordance with the AR system. Together, these tools were used to assist with localization of the precentral gyrus and to guide incision and craniotomy planning while ensuring surgical accuracy.

A two-thirds bicoronal incision was planned located predominantly on the right centered on the M and N electrodes as visualized on Brainlab and AR and verified with the healed scalp incisions from the electrode skin entry points. The incision was opened in a standard fashion and AR was used to plan the craniotomy with the medial border just lateral to the superior sagittal sinus, the lateral border just above the sylvian fissure, the posterior border at the central sulcus, and the anterior border 5 cm anterior to the M and N electrodes. A craniotomy was performed using the Midas Rex drill. The dura was then opened with the base toward the superior sagittal sinus.

The primary motor cortex was then mapped using AR and Brainlab neuronavigation, which showed congruent identification of the central sulcus (Fig. 3). Mapping was verified using phase-reversal ECOG (somatosensory and moter evoked potentials) and direct cortical stimulation, confirming the location of the corticospinal tract immediately behind the more posterior electrode. ECOG was then used anteriorly and laterally to establish borders. Anteriorly, the epileptogenic spikes extended one contact anterior to the M electrode, while laterally, they extended up to the lateral edge of the craniotomy. ECOG showed additional spikes in the inferior frontal gyrus, so it was included in the planned resection. The precentral gyrus was used as the posterior border of the resection. Medially, the resection was taken up to the interhemispheric fissure. The depth of the resection corresponded to the deepest active electrode contacts identified on sEEG as mapped by AR and Brainlab and corresponded to the white matter at the sulcal depth. FIG. 3. Immediately after surgery, the patient experienced a transient left hemiparesis and weakness associated with SMA syndrome. He has since made a full recovery, and his strength was back to normal during his 6-month follow-up appointment. The patient has been seizure free since the surgery, with the exception of two brief seizures during the immediate postoperative period.

Discussion

AR has manifold uses including patient education, preoperative planning, and intraoperative assistance. In neurological surgery, this novel technology has been used to improve the accuracy of shunt placement, to guide keyhole laminectomies, and for intraoperative mapping of lesions and important anatomical structures.4–6 This case report describes the first case in which AR was used to provide anatomically accurate mapping of sEEG electrode trajectories to map out the seizure onset zone during epilepsy surgery. Although sEEG electrodes were not used for intraoperative electrophysiological assessment during resection, their AR-based visualization provided valuable spatial context by allowing the surgeon to continuously appreciate the 3D relationship between implanted electrodes, the epileptogenic network, and the planned resection margins. This visualization aided surgical orientation and reinforced confidence that the resection encompassed the targeted epileptogenic zone while avoiding adjacent eloquent cortex (precentral gyrus and corticospinal tract). In our institutional workflow, electrodes are explanted as a separate procedure from definitive epilepsy surgery to allow for multidisciplinary discussion of the sEEG results, in-depth patient education around the sEEG results and surgical plan, and wound healing from sEEG prior to resection. In this case, AR was used in conjunction with traditional neuroimaging methods such as ECOG and 2D neuronavigation to increase the accuracy and safety of epileptogenic focus resection in the SMA/pre-SMA and to improve the understanding of the 3D spatial relationships between the planned resection area, adjacent eloquent brain regions, and vascular anatomy.

AR technology can improve visualization of anatomical structures and can be used to project a surgical plan onto the operative field. The use of a headset to visualize anatomy and segmented objects enables the surgeon to plan intraoperatively without taking their eyes off the surgical field. These features make it a great tool for surgical planning and anatomical verification. It allows for tailoring of the surgical approach to each individual patient’s anatomy via alignment of the virtual CT/MRI dataset/objects to the patient’s landmarks. After completing patient anatomy registration with the headset, surgeons receive immediate feedback on the accuracy of their alignment by directly visualizing overlays on the patient. In this case, sEEG skull holes and cortical surface vessels allowed for registration microadjustments to precisely align the patient’s anatomy with the skull and cortical surface models, respectively. Using voice commands, surgeons can then bring predetermined virtual anatomical structures and objects into view, superimposed onto the surgical field.7

Some of the advantages of AR over standard neuronaviation are its usability and portability in the operating room (OR). In the illustrated case, AR software was integrated into a commercially available head-mounted display, HoloLens 2. The use of a single headset that integrates virtual images with the natural environment and superimposes preoperative MR images on to the surgical field obviates the need to use any other equipment, such as screens, computers, or other tools. The display can be worn by different users in the OR, following a brief gaze calibration. This makes this technology extremely easy to carry and use compared to traditional neuronavigation, which occupies a larger footprint in the OR.8 Another advantage of AR is that the surgeon is able to maintain focus on the surgical field during localization instead of having to shift focus from the surgical field to a display monitor, as is the case with traditional 2D navigation.

Some limitations of this proof-of-concept case include the lack of formal quantitative comparisons of registration accuracy between conventional neuronavigation and AR-based navigation. Both systems relied on identical infrared tracking and surface landmark registration workflows, and AR served as a visualization adjunct rather than an independent navigation modality. Registration accuracy was qualitatively verified intraoperatively by confirming concordance between projected virtual anatomy, exposed anatomical landmarks, and conventional Brainlab navigation, which underwent a separate surface-based optical registration. No independent quantitative accuracy measurements were performed. Future studies are warranted to systematically evaluate registration accuracy, drift, and workflow efficiency between AR-assisted and traditional navigation systems.

Observations

AR technology can improve the safety of cortical surface mapping. It allows surgeons to keep their hands free and focus their attention on the surgical field without blocking visualization of the outside environment and the surgical field, as is the case with virtual reality. AR uses an optical see-through display that allows users to focus on virtual objects of interest such as electrode trajectories, eloquent brain areas, and white matter tracts. This 3D visualization allows the surgeon to map structures without needing to scan the brain surface with a stylet, as is the case with traditional neuronavigation systems. As traditional neuronavigation displays anatomy on a screen remote from the surgical field, mapping the cortical surface risks causing injury to the brain as the surgeon shifts their gaze from the surgical field to the screen. This is particularly important when mapping eloquent brain regions. Collaborative viewing with multiple users can facilitate teamwork and collaboration between surgeons in the OR.

The use of AR in this case illustrates how this technology can improve the outcomes of epilepsy surgery by enhancing the surgeon’s ability to map out the seizure onset zone and providing accurate visualization of important anatomical structures and functional brain areas. With sEEG being increasingly used as part of the presurgical workup for drug-resistant epilepsy, new technologies will play a vital role in achieving favorable surgical outcomes by offering more precise localization of epileptogenic zones and minimizing damage to eloquent cortex.9 The integration of multiple imaging modalities into a single AR view will be of paramount importance, enabling a more intuitive understanding of complex neuroanatomy and improving intraoperative decision-making. Additionally, in cases of nonlesional extratemporal lobe epilepsy in which there is added challenge due to the absence of a distinct target, AR provides added value by enabling continuous, intuitive visualization of multimodal data by integrating electrode positions, suspected epileptogenic regions, and adjacent functional anatomy. This integrated spatial context enhances intraoperative orientation and decision-making when anatomical landmarks are subtle or indistinct, supporting safe and confident resection.10 This is particularly important in MRI-negative epilepsy cases when the seizure onset zone is immediately adjacent to or within eloquent brain regions. In these cases, the surgeon must decide how aggressive to be in these areas, balancing the desire for optimal seizure control with the risk of causing a neurological deficit.

Lessons

We present the case of a 26-year-old patient with medically refractory epilepsy who underwent sEEG and subsequent SMA/pre-SMA resection. Preoperative planning and resection were carried out using AR in addition to traditional neuronavigation methods such as ECOG mapping and 2D neuronavigation. Intraoperative AR allowed surgeons to perform a safe resection and minimize surgical risks by providing an accurate anatomical mapping of the electrode trajectories, corticospinal tract, and precentral gyrus in 3D. To our knowledge, this case represents the first case in which AR was used intraoperatively for mapping after sEEG.
