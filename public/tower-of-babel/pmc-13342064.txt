An improved watershed-based algorithm for ore image segmentation
Authors: Weiwei Li, Wenwen Liu, Yuelong Li, Hang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOre particle size is a critical indicator of ore fragmentation and a key parameter for automated mineral processing. However, traditional ore image segmentation methods suffer from low accuracy, severe under-segmentation, and weak adaptability in complex mining scenarios, while few approaches effectively balance precision and efficiency. To fill this gap, this paper proposes a novel segmentation method that combines an improved distance transform with the watershed algorithm. After image preprocessing, seed regions are optimized using a self-developed local-maximum strategy, and watershed segmentation is further refined with morphological operations. Experimental results on seven test images show that the proposed method achieves a segmentation accuracy of 0.932, an over-segmentation rate of 0.109, and an under-segmentation rate of 0.091, which are significantly better than those of traditional watershed and UNet methods.The proposed method thus exhibits superior performance and stability in practical ore particle analysis.Keywords: Morphological gradient, Distance transformation, Image processing, Ore adhesion, Structuring elementSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In mining operations, particle-size information of crushed ore is a key parameter for evaluating blasting performance and controlling the operating conditions of crushers at different stages. Accurate particle-size measurement is therefore essential for efficient mine production. Traditionally, particle size was measured manually; however, such methods are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and prone to subjective errors. With advances in computing, machine vision has been widely adopted in mineral processing1,2, providing a non-contact, real-time approach for ore particle-size measurement and automated blasting control. This technology improves operational efficiency and offers significant economic benefits.

Early foundational studies by Pirard, Serra et al., and Sih have laid the groundwork for modern shape analysis3–5. Serra established mathematical morphology as a rigorous framework for image processing and shape transformation. Pirard introduced the calypter, a vectorial shape descriptor based on maximal inscribed discs, enabling efficient and automated particle shape characterization. Sih developed fundamental theories in fracture mechanics and strain energy density, which link geometric features such as corners, concavities, and roundness to mechanical behavior and material failure. Together, they provide the theoretical basis for automatic, quantitative, and physically meaningful shape analysis. Ore target segmentation is the core component of ore particle size detection systems. However, harsh mining environments-characterized by heavy dust, irregular particle shapes, image noise, blurred edges, and particle overlap-make accurate segmentation challenging. These difficulties have motivated extensive research into robust and accurate ore image segmentation methods.

Existing ore image segmentation methods can be broadly classified into four categories.

(1) Region-based segmentation methods. These include region growing, splitting and merging approaches. Zhang6 proposed a watershed algorithm based on distance transformation combined with a dual-window Otsu method, but its performance degrades under severe ore overlap. Wang7 improved the traditional watershed algorithm to reduce pseudo-boundary interference; however, it struggles with highly adherent ores and blurred edges. Zhou8 introduced a segmentation method based on collaborative representation and bipartite graph modeling, which alleviates over-segmentation but requires difficult-to-control cluster numbers. Amankwah9 applied mean-shift clustering, though it performs poorly when ore targets are highly similar. The FogBank algorithm proposed by Chalfoun10 combines pixel histograms with distance transformation but remains sensitive to complex ore textures.

(2)Threshold-based segmentation methods. Lu11 proposed a method combining background subtraction with local thresholding based on graph cuts, which is unsuitable for overlapping ores. Zhang12 used a dual-window Otsu thresholding approach but failed to segment ore images with blurred boundaries effectively. Although Zhang13 improved this method, overlapping ore targets remain difficult to separate.

(3) Segmentation methods based on specific theories. Malladi14 converted watershed results into superpixels, but the method suffers from poor robustness and longer processing time. Ning15 proposed a Harris corner detection approach based on bilateral filtering, which requires scenario-specific parameter tuning. Yang16 developed a multilevel automatic segmentation strategy using a labeled region growing, but it is computationally expensive and lacks robustness. Zhang et al.17 combined wavelet transform and fuzzy c-means clustering to reduce noise and separated touching particles, though the method is effective only for large ore targets.

(4) Deep learning segmentation methods. In recent years, deep learning has been increasingly applied to ore image segmentation18. Yuan19 proposed the HED network for conveyor belt ore segmentation, achieving good results for large particles, but suffering from under-segmentation of small and stacked ores. Abdulkadir20 proposed a U-Net architecture based on fully convolutional neural networks, widely adopted in medical imaging, and Duan21 applied it to green particle detection in iron ore. Ma et al.22 combined a deep learning method with image processing techniques, but the resulting workflow is complex and computationally slow. Wei et al.23 improved U-Net using a ResNet based encoder, enhancing accuracy but at a high computational cost.

Overall, deep learning methods require large, well-annotated datasets and incur high computational expenses. Moreover, most existing approaches-traditional or learning-based-struggle with complex mining environments, including ore adhesion, dust interference, uneven illumination, and severe overlap, resulting in slow processing speeds and limited robustness.

In resource-constrained environments, real-time processing capability and high segmentation accuracy are particularly critical. To meet these requirements, this paper proposes an ore target segmentation algorithm that combines an improved distance transform with the watershed method—the core novelty lies in addressing the key limitations of existing ore segmentation approaches, which either suffer from severe over-segmentation/under-segmentation or fail to balance accuracy and real-time performance. The algorithm first preprocesses the ore image to reduce noise and avoid interference, then extracts initial seed points through an improved local maximum strategy—unlike traditional local maximum methods that easily produce false seeds, our approach filters out noise-induced false seed points in advance, ensuring the reliability of subsequent segmentation. These seed points are further optimized by a targeted concave point detection strategy, which specifically solves the long-standing problem of adjacent ore particle adhesion that traditional methods fail to address.

Analysis of algorithms

Image preprocessing

The mining environment is complex, and ore images acquired by CCD cameras suffer from several challenges. During ore crushing, large amounts of dust generate significant, image noise and blur. In addition, ore particles exhibit diverse shapes but minimal color contrast, and their accumulation leads to mutual occlusion and adhesion in images, (Fig. 2). Based on the characteristics, image preprocessing should focus on two key aspects: noise suppression while preserving ore edges, and image enhancement to improve contrast between boundaries and the background and to emphasize edge features for subsequent processing.

Ore image denoising

Bilateral filtering is widely used for ore image preprocessing because it effectively reduces noise while preserving edges. Unlike conventional filters, bilateral filtering incorporates both spatial proximity and pixel intensity similarity through weighted averaging. Image enhancement

The purpose of image enhancement is to improve the clarity and information content of regions of interest to facilitate subsequent processing. In this study, ore image enhancement focuses primarily on strengthening edge information, thereby reducing the complexity and computational burden of later analysis. By analyzing existing image enhancement methods and combining the characteristics of ore images, adaptive histogram equalization is selected as the most suitable approach to reduce the workload and difficulty of subsequent data processing. Histogram equalization is a common grayscale transformation technique that enhances image contrast by redistributing pixel intensities and is widely used in image enhancement. However, when an image histogram is broadly distributed, global histogram equalization may cause information loss. Adaptive histogram equalization overcomes this limitation by incorporating local image information and constraining local contrast through neighborhood-based histogram calculations. This approach effectively enhances local contrast and preserves edge details. As shown in Fig. 4, adaptive histogram equalization preserves edge information in shaded and overlapping ore regions and significantly enhances the contrast of ore boundaries.

Ore image segmentation

The watershed transform, introduced by Vincent and Soille (1991), interprets an image as a topographic surface in which pixel intensities represent elevation and simulates a flooding process to achieve region segmentation. During segmentation, neighboring pixels with similar spatial positions and gray values are grouped to form closed contours, a defining feature of watershed transformation.

Because the goal of this study is to extract accurate ore-target contours, the watershed transformation is well suited. As it produces continuous single-pixel boundaries and performs well in detecting weak edges. However, it is prone to over-segmentation, particularly in ore images where particles are often in contact, leading to both over- and under-segmentation. To address this limitation, we propose a distance-based method to accurately locate seed points for ore targets, which effectively improves segmentation performance (Table 1).

Concave point matching

Using the concave point detection method described above, two reliable, concave points are identified in Fig. 12 and one reliable concave point in Fig. 13. To determine the final segmentation line and avoid under-segmentation, the following concave-point matching strategy is proposed:

(1) If the Euclidean distance between two concave points on the same contour is less than one-quarter of the width of the fitted ellipse, the points are considered successfully matched, and the line connecting them is used as the segmentation line. Otherwise, proceed to step (2);

(2) If the two concave points are mutual nearest neighbors on the same contour, and their Euclidean distance is less than one-half of the ellipse width, they are considered successfully matched, and their connecting line is used as the segmentation line. Otherwise, proceed to step (3);

(3) If a concave point is isolated, (as in Fig. 13) the midpoint between(A)and (B) is computed and denoted as point(C). The line connecting (C) and the isolated concave pointis first constructed(yellow line in Fig. 13). Because the segmentation line must not pass through the background, the reverse extension of this line is adopted as the final segmentation line, (red line in Fig. 13).

MR preserves target contours while suppressing noise, thereby facilitating the extraction of more accurate watershed segmentation boundaries. Although single-scale MR enhances the edges of some ore targets, their variable sizes and shapes limit its effectiveness.

To assess the effectiveness of the proposed method comparisons were made with a traditional watershed algorithm9 and a U-Net neural network method24. Figures 21 and 22 present visual comparisons of the segmentation results, while Table 3 summarizes the quantitative performance metrics. Figure 23 shows histograms of the evaluation indicators for the three methods on the test dataset. The detailed implementation details of U-Net include the following aspects: (1) Training dataset size: The U-Net model was trained on a self-built ore image dataset, which includes 200 ore images collected from industrial mines, covering different ore types and visual conditions. All images were annotated in accordance with standard particle segmentation annotation specifications, with 80% used for training, 10% for validation, and 10% for testing. (2) Data augmentation strategies: To avoid overfitting, we adopted multiple data augmentation strategies, including random rotation, horizontal/vertical flipping, random cropping (size 256 × 256), brightness adjustment (± 20%), and Gaussian noise addition (variance 0.01). (3) Network structure: The standard U-Net structure was adopted, including 4 downsampling layers (each consisting of two 3 × 3 convolutional layers and one 2 × 2 max-pooling layer), 4 upsampling layers (each consisting of one 2 × 2 transposed convolutional layer and concatenation with the corresponding downsampling feature map), and 1 output layer (1 × 1 convolutional layer with sigmoid activation function). The number of initial channels was set to 32, and the number of channels doubled after each downsampling. (4) Training process: The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-4, a batch size of 8, and a total of 100 epochs. The binary cross-entropy loss function was used, and early stopping was adopted (patience = 10) to prevent overfitting.

As shown in Table 3, the proposed method achieves higher segmentation accuracy and lower over- and under-segmentation rates than the other two methods. Furthermore, variance values are added in Table 3 to evaluate the stability of different algorithms. All methods yield small variances, indicating stable segmentation performance across different test ore images. Notably, the proposed method exhibits the smallest variance in over-segmentation rate, which is the key factor affecting the reliability of particle statistics. The slightly higher variance in segmentation accuracy is mainly due to the high overall accuracy level of the proposed method, where minor absolute fluctuations among images lead to a relatively small variance increment. Visual results in Figs. 21 and 22 indicate that traditional watershed methods suffer from severe over-segmentation in complex backgrounds, while the U-Net method, as a deep learning-based segmentation method, can roughly extract the contours of ore particles, but it has obvious defects in boundary integrity. In ore segmentation, the closure and accuracy of ore boundaries are crucial for subsequent particle statistics (e.g., particle size, shape, and quantity statistics), which are the core demands of our research. However, U-Net often fails to form closed boundaries for ore particles, especially when facing ore particles with irregular shapes, uneven grayscale, or adhesion phenomena (common in actual ore samples). This leads to ambiguous division between adjacent ore particles and even missing segmentation of small ore particles, making it impossible to obtain accurate particle statistical results.

As mentioned earlier, the estimated complexity of the proposed algorithm in this paper is O(nlogn), which is equivalent to that of the complete morphological segmentation process, thus having similar theoretical performance. The inference complexity of CNN-based segmentation methods is usually O(n), while the complexity of the proposed algorithm is O(nlogn). From a theoretical perspective, the computational costs of these methods are roughly equivalent. In general, the proposed algorithm in this paper balances segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency, and is particularly suitable for the practical application scenario of ore segmentation (which requires both accuracy and real-time performance).

In contrast, the proposed method effectively solves the problem of over-segmentation that plagues traditional watershed methods in complex backgrounds. The proposed method can not only avoid severe over-segmentation but also ensure the closure and clarity of ore boundaries, which is essential for reliable particle statistics.

Conclusion

This study investigates ore particle segmentation, focusing on the segmentation of adhesive particles. Experimental results show that the proposed seed-point optimization algorithm achieves accurate segmentation while effectively reducing both over- and under-segmentations. Comparative analyses further confirm the theoretical significance and practical applicability of the proposed method.

Despite these advances, some limitations remain. Future work should focus on: (1) improving image quality by enhancing target features to facilitate more robust segmentation; (2) investigating the relationship between surface particle-size information and multi-layer stacked particle-size distributions, with the goal of estimating multi-layer ore particle size from ore particle sizes from surface measurements.
