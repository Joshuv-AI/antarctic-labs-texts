Concurrent versus sequential intrapleural administration of fibrinolytic and DNase for pleural infections: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Woon Hean Keenan Chong, Ronnie Voon Shiong Tan, Ken Junyang Goh, Wei Jun Dan Ong, Noel Stanley Wey Tut Tay, Dipika Agrawal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional sequential administration of fibrinolytics and deoxyribonuclease (DNase) for pleural infections is a multi-step process that can be time-consuming and resource-intensive in busy clinical settings. This meta-analysis evaluated whether concurrent intrapleural administration offers comparable effectiveness and safety to sequential dosing in adults with pleural infections.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science identified studies comparing concurrent versus sequential intrapleural fibrinolytic and DNase therapy. The primary outcome was treatment success, defined as survival and avoidance of surgery. Secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay (LOS), total doses administered, chest tube duration, pleural drainage volume, and complications.ResultsThree studies involving 357 patients (sequential: N=128; concurrent: N=229) were included. Baseline characteristics were similar across the groups. Pooled analysis showed no significant difference in treatment success (14.1% vs. 16.6%; relative risk (RR): 1.11; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.63–1.96; P=0.72) between the two groups. Mortality (RR: 1.11; P=0.82), surgical intervention (RR: 1.15; P=0.76), hospital LOS [mean difference (MD): −1.20 days; P=0.30], and pleural drainage volume (MD: −0.09 L; P=0.38) were also comparable. The groups showed no significant differences in the number of doses administered (MD: 0.35; P=0.09) or chest tube duration (MD: −0.67 days; P=0.32). Complication rates for pain (RR: 0.81; P=0.46) and bleeding (RR: 1.42; P=0.67) did not differ significantly.ConclusionsCurrent evidence suggests that concurrent intrapleural administration of fibrinolytics and DNase may have similar effectiveness and safety to sequential therapy. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited and observational nature of the available evidence.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Concurrent intrapleural administration of fibrinolytics and DNase may achieve similar treatment success to the traditional sequential regimen in adults with pleural infection.

• There were no significant differences in mortality, need for surgery, hospital length of stay, chest tube duration, pleural drainage volume, or complication rates between concurrent and sequential therapy.

What is known and what is new?

• Sequential intrapleural administration of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and DNase, as established by the MIST-2 trial, improves outcomes in pleural infection but is operationally complex and resource-intensive.

• This meta-analysis provides the first pooled evidence demonstrating that concurrent intrapleural administration of tPA and DNase is equally effective and safe compared with sequential dosing.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Concurrent intrapleural therapy can be considered a pragmatic alternative to sequential administration, offering comparable clinical outcomes while simplifying workflow and reducing procedural burden.

• These findings support the broader adoption of concurrent administration in routine clinical practice, particularly in high-volume or resource-limited settings, while awaiting confirmation from randomized controlled trials.

Introduction

Pleural infection, encompassing complicated parapneumonic effusions and empyema, remains a significant global healthcare burden associated with substantial morbidity and prolonged hospitalization (1). Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and drainage techniques, outcomes remain suboptimal. Contemporary data indicate that 30-day mortality remains high, reported at approximately 10% in a large Danish cohort, with higher mortality observed among older patients, reaching 20% in those aged >80 years (2-5). In addition, a considerable proportion of patients fail to respond to standard management with antibiotics and chest tube drainage, necessitating escalation of therapy. The MIST-2 trial demonstrated the efficacy of sequential intrapleural therapy, using fibrinolytics, particularly tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and deoxyribonuclease (DNase), with one-hour drain clamping after each dose to improve pleural drainage and reduce the need for surgery (3). However, this sequential protocol is operationally complex, labor-intensive, and time-consuming. Moreover, this protocol involves multiple drain accesses, frequent clamping and unclamping, and staggered drug administration, posing challenges for its implementation, especially in busy clinical settings.

To address these challenges, many centers have adopted concurrent administration of fibrinolytics and DNase, wherein both agents are delivered in rapid succession, simplifying the procedure and improving compliance (4,6-9). Despite the growing use of concurrent administration, the clinical equivalence of concurrent and sequential administration remains uncertain. Preliminary observational studies have suggested that concurrent delivery may offer similar efficacy and safety, but these findings are drawn from limited, heterogeneous cohorts with variable outcome definitions (6-8). To date, no meta-analysis has comprehensively evaluated the comparative effectiveness and safety of these two strategies using pooled data. This study systematically synthesized available evidence to determine whether concurrent intrapleural therapy provides outcomes comparable to sequential intrapleural therapy in achieving treatment success, which was defined by survival and avoidance of surgery. The findings of this study may guide clinical decision-making and support the standardization of pleural infection management. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0517/rc).

Methods

The review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the reference number CRD420251059763. As this study involved the analysis of previously published data, ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Search strategy and selection

A comprehensive search was performed using the PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases with keywords such as “intrapleural fibrinolytics”, “tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)”, “deoxyribonuclease (DNase)”, “dornase alfa”, “pleural infection”, and “empyema”, from January 1, 2000 to October 30, 2025. All discrepancies were resolved through consensus. The extracted data were recorded in a standardized Excel format, and the study characteristics are summarized in Tables 1,2. Dichotomous variables were reported as numbers and percentages, whereas continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SDs). For continuous variables reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), the mean and SD were approximated using the Wan method (10).

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was “treatment success”, defined as a composite of survival and avoidance of surgical intervention. Owing to the heterogeneity in outcome reporting across the included studies, efforts were made to standardize definitions where possible. However, residual differences in outcome definitions may persist. We standardized this endpoint by extracting or deriving comparable data, focusing on the presence or absence of in-hospital mortality and the necessity for surgical referral or intervention. This harmonization facilitated consistent outcome synthesis and enhanced the interpretability of the pooled analysis. Secondary outcomes included all-cause in-hospital mortality, surgical intervention, hospital length of stay (LOS), total doses of therapy, chest tube duration, total volume of pleural fluid drained, and complications (including bleeding requiring blood transfusions and pain requiring escalation of analgesia). For studies reporting mortality at various intervals (e.g., 28-day, 30-day, 60-day), we standardized the data using the longest available follow-up period.

Quality assessment

Three researchers (W.H.K.C., R.V.S.T., and W.J.D.O.) conducted quality assessments for observational studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) which assesses three domains: selection of cohorts, comparability of groups, and adequacy of outcome assessment (Appendix 2). On the basis of the NOS score, studies were categorized as showing low (score, 0–3), moderate (score, 4–6), or high (score, 7–9) quality (11). All disagreements were resolved through consensus-based discussion.

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis of the primary and secondary outcomes was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) software. Version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020). The DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model was used to calculate pooled relative risks (RRs), mean differences (MDs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (12). The random-effects model was preferred over the fixed-effects model because of the anticipated clinical heterogeneity across studies (e.g., variations in study design, patient demographics, and clinical practices). Continuous outcomes were evaluated using the inverse-variance statistical method as part of the random-effects model and measured in terms of MDs. The inverse variance method accounts for the differing sample sizes of individual studies by weighting studies by the variance of their estimates, such that small studies with large variance have less weighting, and large studies with small variance have more weighting. Dichotomous outcomes were assessed using the inverse-variance method, and measured in terms of RRs and their 95% CIs. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic, with high and low heterogeneity defined as I2≥50% and I2<50%, respectively. Statistical significance was set at P value of <0.05.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

After the initial database search, 246 records were identified (Figure 1). After the removal of duplicates and exclusion of non-eligible or irrelevant studies based on title and abstract screening, 28 studies were selected for full-text review. Of these, 25 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and the remaining three were included in the final meta-analysis. Three studies with a total of 357 patients compared sequential (N=128) versus concurrent (N=229) intrapleural tPA and DNase therapy (6-8). Two were retrospective [Goh et al., 2023 (7); Strickland et al., 2025 (8)] and one was prospective [Kheir et al., 2018 (6)]. Two studies were conducted in the USA and one study in Singapore between 2014 and 2023. Tables 1,2 provide details of the three studies, including patient characteristics, outcomes, and complications.

Flow diagram showing the study selection.

Baseline demographic characteristics were generally balanced across the groups. The mean patient age ranged from 59.8 to 67.7 years, and most of the patients were male (64.6–78.6%). Cancer prevalence varied (9.8–36.1%), with higher rates in the study by Strickland et al. (8). All regimens involved administration of 10 mg of tPA and 5 mg of DNase per dose, although Goh et al. permitted a dose range of 5–10 mg tPA (7). The daily and maximum total dose regimens were similar across studies, typically allowing for two doses per day and a maximum cumulative dose of six to eight administrations.

The level of inflammatory markers was modestly elevated across groups, with mean peripheral leukocyte counts ranging from 11.8×109/L to 16.4×109/L. The median interval from chest tube placement to the first dose was 1.4 to 3.8 days. Chest tube size distribution varied from 8 Fr smallest to 12, 14, and >16 Fr. Fourteen-Fr tubes were the most commonly used across both groups. The right-sided chest tube placement was common (47–65%). Pleural fluid characteristics showed considerable variability. Culture or Gram stain positivity rates ranged from 22.2% to 76.9%, with notably higher rates in the study by Strickland et al. (8). Mean pleural fluid pH values ranged from 7.0 to 7.44, while glucose levels varied widely, with mean values ranging from 38 to 81 mg/dL. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels were highly variable across studies, particularly in the study by Goh et al., where the mean LDH level exceeded 5,000 IU/L in both arms (7). The NOS scores for the three studies are shown in Appendix 2, indicating varying methodological quality. The study by Goh et al. achieved the full NOS score of 9 (7). The studies by Strickland et al. and Kheir et al. had scores of 8 and 7, respectively, because of gaps in follow-up adequacy (6,8).

Primary outcomes: treatment success, mortality, and surgical intervention

Pooled analysis of the three studies (N=357) demonstrated no significant difference in treatment success, defined as the composite of survival and avoidance of surgery between the concurrent and sequential intrapleural therapy groups (14.1% vs. 16.6%; RR: 1.11; 95% CI: 0.63–1.96; P=0.72; I2=0%) (6-8). Mortality rates were also comparable between the two groups (7.8% vs. 10.9%; RR: 1.11; 95% CI: 0.46–2.67; P=0.82; I2=0%) (Figure 2). Similarly, the risk of requiring surgical intervention showed no significant difference between the two groups (6.3% vs. 5.7%; RR: 1.15; 95% CI: 0.49–2.69; P=0.76; I2=0%) (Figure 2).

Secondary outcomes: hospital LOS, total doses of therapy, chest tube duration, and pleural drainage volume

No significant differences were observed in the hospital LOS between the concurrent and sequential intrapleural therapy groups (MD: −1.20 days; 95% CI: −3.48 to 1.07; P=0.30; I2=28%) (Figure 3). The total number of intrapleural therapy doses administered was similar (MD: 0.35 doses; 95% CI: −0.06 to 0.75; P=0.09, I2=91%) (Figure 3). Additionally, the two groups showed no significant difference in the duration of chest tube placement (MD: −0.67 days; 95% CI: −1.99 to 0.66; P=0.32; I2=6%) or total pleural fluid drainage volume (MD: −0.09 L; 95% CI: −0.28 to 0.11; P=0.38; I2=24%) (Figure 3).

Complications

The rates of pain requiring escalation of analgesia were similar between concurrent and sequential therapies (15.6% vs. 31.9%; RR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.46–1.43; P=0.46; I2=6%) (Figure 2). Strickland et al. reported higher rates of bleeding with concurrent therapies in bleeding events requiring transfusion. However, the pooled analysis did not show a significant difference (7.8% vs. 5.7%; RR: 1.42; 95% CI: 0.28–7.12; P=0.67; I2=71%) (Figure 2).

Discussion

Our meta-analysis suggests that concurrent intrapleural therapy may have comparable safety and effectiveness to the sequential approach, with no significant differences in treatment success, mortality rates, or the need for surgical intervention between the two groups. Additionally, the duration of hospital LOS, intrapleural therapy doses, chest tube duration, and pleural drainage volume showed no notable differences between the groups. Rates of complications, including pain requiring increased analgesia and bleeding events necessitating transfusion, were similar in the two groups. However, these findings are based on limited observational data and should be interpreted with caution.

The MIST-2 trial established sequential administration of tPA and DNase, with clamping of the chest tube between doses. This step was based on product safety guidelines that cautioned against mixing tPA with other drugs (including DNase) owing to the effects of potential structural and functional changes on the efficacy and safety of tPA (3). However, definitive evidence showing that concurrent administration alters the pharmacokinetics of both fibrinolytics or DNase is lacking (4,13,14). The 2023 European Respiratory Society (ERS) and European Society of Thoracic Surgeons (ESTS) statement suggests that sequential administration of tPA and DNase may be pharmacologically safer than concurrent administration, although it remains uncertain whether concurrent delivery alters the pharmacokinetics of either agent (13). Available evidence indicates that both concurrent and sequential approaches may be similarly safe and effective (6-8). In contrast, the 2023 British Thoracic Society (BTS) pleural procedures statement does not explicitly endorse sequential over concurrent intrapleural therapy for pleural infection when drainage is inadequate (15).

Previous meta-analyses have evaluated various chest tube sizes and the efficacy of intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy, both alone and in combination with DNase, in comparison with placebo or monotherapy in pleural infections (2,16-18). This meta-analysis uniquely focused on comparing the effectiveness and safety of concurrent and sequential intrapleural fibrinolytic and DNase therapy for pleural infections. Our findings confirm that concurrent administration of intrapleural therapy maintains clinical efficacy while offering operational advantages. These include a reduced frequency of chest drain access, shorter total chest tube clamping time, simplified administration protocols, decreased nursing workload, improved adherence and compliance, enhanced workflow efficiency, and increased feasibility, particularly after hours or in high-volume clinical care settings. Notably, Goh et al. reported that the concurrent group required fewer fibrinolytic and DNase doses and could undergo earlier therapy initiation, which may improve outcomes without compromising safety (7). However, the pooled results revealed that the sequential group required similar doses of intrapleural fibrinolytics and DNase compared to the concurrent group; nevertheless, the considerable heterogeneity in these findings (I2=94%) likely reflected variability in institutional protocols and patient selection. The interval from chest tube insertion to initiation of intrapleural therapy was also longer in the concurrent group than sequential group in two studies by Kheir et al. and Strickland et al. (6,8). This may reflect differences in institutional workflows or clinical decision-making rather than the administration strategy itself. Timely initiation of intrapleural therapy may be an important determinant of clinical outcomes, and delays in treatment initiation could confound comparisons between concurrent and sequential approaches (19). Further well-designed studies are necessary to confirm or refute these observations.

Our safety analysis revealed no significant differences in complication rates, including bleeding requiring transfusion or chest pain necessitating the escalation of analgesia. Although Strickland et al. observed a trend toward higher bleeding rates in the concurrent group, the pooled data across our meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant difference (8). Notably, the results showed high heterogeneity (I2=71%), which could be attributed to variations in patient comorbidities, such as antiplatelet and anticoagulant use, renal impairment, and differing definitions of major bleeding. Goh et al. reported that 13–22% of patients received either antiplatelet or anticoagulation therapy concurrently during administration of intrapleural therapy (7). Furthermore, in this study, the coagulation profile and platelet count were within normal limits. The inconsistent documentation of concurrent antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy use limited our ability to pool these data for the meta-analysis. Nevertheless, these findings support the safety of concurrent administration. The reduction in total chest tube access and interventions may lower the risk of iatrogenic infection, although the inconsistent recording of the related data in the included studies prevented pooled analysis of this outcome (6-8,13). In the absence of definitive comparative data, the choice between concurrent and sequential intrapleural therapy should be individualized, taking into account patient-specific factors, such as bleeding risk, prior history of major hemorrhage, underlying coagulopathies, and comorbid conditions. A multidisciplinary approach involving respiratory physicians, thoracic surgeons, and other relevant specialists may further support optimal decision-making in complex cases.

Variations in drug dosing protocols and drainage techniques have also been inconsistently reported. In the study by Goh et al., although both groups showed similar rates of bleeding episodes requiring transfusion, up to 15.5% of patients in the sequential group received an initial half-dose (5 mg) of the antifibrinolytic tPA, either in the first or all doses, in comparison with 3.9% in the concurrent group (7). Moreover, in the same study, approximately 21.4% of patients in the sequential group and 19.6% in the concurrent group received once-daily intrapleural therapy, while both Kheir et al. and Strickland et al. utilized the standard twice-daily regimen of 10 mg tPA and 5 mg DNase described in the MIST-2 trial (3,6,8). As the doses of tPA and DNase were empirically chosen by the investigators without conducting dose-escalation studies, the optimal dosing for achieving the best outcomes remains uncertain. Subsequent studies have evaluated lower starting doses of tPA, ranging from 5 mg to as low as 0.5 mg, and reported promising results (20-22). The decision to start with a lower dose of tPA often depends on the clinician’s discretion, considering underlying comorbidities, such as anemia, renal impairment, and malignancy, which are independent risk factors for significant bleeding. A multi-center retrospective study reported no increased risk of bleeding risk when comparing 10 and 5 mg intrapleural tPA therapy combined with DNase therapy (23). Nevertheless, well-designed studies are required to validate these findings.

Future research should focus on well-designed prospective studies to better define the comparative effectiveness of concurrent versus sequential intrapleural therapy. Given the apparent similarity in outcomes observed to date, a non-inferiority trial design may be most appropriate to determine whether concurrent administration provides comparable clinical efficacy while offering practical advantages. Consideration should also be given to the feasibility of such trials, particularly in light of relatively low event rates for outcomes, such as mortality and surgical intervention, which may limit statistical power and require large sample sizes. Alternative or complementary endpoints, such as hospital LOS or patient-reported outcomes, may therefore be important to include. In addition, careful attention to study design will be essential, including standardized treatment protocols, clear definitions of outcomes, and stratification based on disease severity. Incorporation of patient and public involvement (PPI) in trial design may enhance relevance and acceptability, while robust ethical frameworks, including consideration of delayed consent models in acute care settings may facilitate recruitment. Preliminary feasibility studies may also be warranted to inform trial logistics and optimize study design prior to large-scale implementation.

Our meta-analysis had some limitations:

Statistical model constraints: the use of a random-effects model represents a reasonable and appropriate statistical approach in this context, reflecting anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity and uncertainty within a small evidence base. This approach yields wider CIs and more conservative estimates, which may reduce the likelihood of detecting statistically significant differences.

In addition, variations in treatment protocols, including tPA dosing, administration frequency, and chest tube size represent important potential confounders that may influence outcomes. Importantly, heterogeneity extended beyond clinical characteristics to include differences in treatment definitions, outcome definitions, and procedural protocols across studies. Inconsistent documentation of clinical markers and pleural effusion radiological characteristics: clinical markers such as the RAPID score, an important valuable tool for assessing prognosis and predicting bleeding risks in patients with pleural infections, were not consistently documented limiting the ability to pool these results effectively (23,24). Furthermore, the lack of consistent reporting on pleural infection subtypes and imaging characteristics involving degree of loculation or septation on thoracic ultrasound limited our ability to assess their influence on treatment outcomes. These factors may represent important sources of clinical heterogeneity and could affect responsiveness to intrapleural therapy.

Pharmacokinetic and mechanistic data gaps: the absence of direct pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic studies comparing concurrent versus sequential administration limited the mechanistic understanding of potential differences in efficacy or safety.

Limited microbiological data: only the study by Strickland et al. provided information regarding the types of microorganisms cultured from pleural fluid, limiting comprehensive microbiological analysis across studies (8).

Outcome selection constraints: mortality and avoidance of surgery were selected as primary outcomes because these endpoints are clinically meaningful, consistently reported, and comparable across studies. Radiographic changes, although evaluated in MIST-II, were inconsistently reported in subsequent studies and would have introduced heterogeneity (3). Future studies should incorporate standardized radiographic and patient-centered outcomes.

Despite these limitations, this review offers a preliminary synthesis of the current evidence in an area where randomized trials are still lacking. Until such data are available, both concurrent and sequential intrapleural therapies may remain acceptable strategies for the management of pleural infections, with treatment decisions guided by clinical experience and institutional protocols.

Conclusions

In summary, concurrent intrapleural administration of fibrinolytics and DNase may represent a reasonable alternative to sequential therapy, with potential advantages in workflow efficiency. However, given the limited and heterogeneous evidence base, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating. Further well-designed prospective studies, including RCTs, are required to confirm these findings and to better define optimal dosing strategies, pharmacological interactions, and patient-centered and radiographic outcomes before definitive recommendations can be made.
