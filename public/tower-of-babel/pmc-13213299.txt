Patient-specific 3D-printed perforator mapping guide for propeller flap reconstruction in the hand
Authors: Yu Tai Tong, Marvin Man Ting Chung, Terence Tai Lun Poon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reconstruction of hand soft tissue defects relies on accurate perforator localization, yet conventional planning with computed tomography angiography or handheld Doppler requires mental three-dimensional (3D) translation, and is associated with mapping errors or intraoperative adjustments. This article evaluates the use of an innovative patient-specific, surface-conforming 3D-printed guides that convert segmented preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data into tangible templates to project dorsal metacarpal artery (DMA) perforator emergence points onto the skin with reproducible accuracy.In an illustrative case, a 66-year-old man underwent wide local excision of a digital porocarcinoma followed by propeller flap reconstruction. The third and fourth DMA perforators were identified from MRI, with a 2 mm-thick dorsal hand shell designed with 1 cm fenestrations corresponding to perforator emergence points. The perforators were marked onto the skin through the custom guide preoperatively, with skin markings cross verified with handheld Doppler. Reconstruction proceeded using a fourth DMA perforator-based propeller flap rotated to achieve tension-free coverage, with complete flap survival and restoration of full digital motion and satisfactory aesthetics.The 3D-printed guide reduced cognitive load, accelerated perforator localization, and minimized unnecessary dissection, supporting shorter tourniquet duration and safer elevation. The hand's distinct bony landmarks and stable contours enable reliable registration of rigid surface guides. As costs and workflows for imaging-to-print continue to decrease, patient-specific 3D-printed guides represent a practical, scalable adjunct that improved accuracy, efficiency, and reproducibility in perforator-based hand reconstruction.

Introduction

Reconstruction of soft tissue defects in the hand presents unique challenges due to the region's intricate vascular architecture, proximity to gliding tendons, and high functional demands.1 While locoregional perforator flaps are commonly employed in the hands due to their rich vascular supply, the success of these flaps, particularly propeller flaps, is critically dependent on the precise identification and dissection planning of suitable vessels.2 Unlike free tissue transfer, pedicled perforator flaps possess fixed pivot points, offering little room for manipulation or error.3

Traditionally, preoperative planning relies on computed tomography angiography (CTA) as the reference standard for vascular mapping.4 However, CTA presents three-dimensional (3D) vascular data as two-dimensional axial, coronal, and sagittal images. This necessitates the mental reconstruction of 3D anatomy and its subsequent intraoperative overlay of visualization onto the patient, which is a cognitively demanding process with an inherent risk of error.5 Literature suggests that despite preoperative CTA, approximately 24.1% of cases require intraoperative changes to the planned flap design.6 Handheld Doppler is another widely utilized modality for vascular mapping. However, skin markings based on Doppler signals have shown an average deviation of 3 ± 3.9 mm compared to CTA findings.7 While this error margin may be negligible in large flaps, perforator-based flaps in the hand are often narrow and therefore require high accuracy. For reconstruction based on dorsal metacarpal artery (DMA) perforators, anatomical variability poses significant risks for intraoperative misidentification or inadvertent injury if unrecognized.8,9

3D printing technology bridges this gap between imaging and surgical execution, allowing a more precise surgical planning and manufacturing of customized tools to reduce surgical time and surgical errors. Although previously utilized in upper limb reconstruction primarily for bony deformity correction and reconstruction,10 3D printing can also offer significant advantages for soft tissue applications by converting high-resolution imaging data into tangible, patient-specific tools. By integrating segmented preoperative angiography images, surgeons can isolate relevant vascular anatomy and fabricate customized surgical guides with high accuracy.11 These guides can mark the precise location and emergence points of perforators, translating digital surgical plans directly onto the patient's surface anatomy. This precision is paramount in hand surgery, where the margin for error is narrow. By providing a physical template for vascular dissection, 3D printing facilitates a confident and streamlined surgical workflow. The following case illustrates the application of a patient-specific 3D-printed guide, derived from preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), to facilitate the precise harvesting of a DMA perforator flap for a complex finger defect.

Illustrative case

Patient presentation and diagnosis

A 66-year-old male presented with a six-year history of a slowly enlarging mass on the left ring finger. He denied any history of antecedent trauma or infection. Physical examination revealed a 2 × 2 cm ulcerative lesion on the ulnar aspect of the proximal phalanx (Fig. 1). The range of motion was full, and distal neurovascular function was intact. No palpable regional lymphadenopathy was noted. An initial incisional biopsy suggested poorly differentiated carcinoma. MRI demonstrated that the lesion involved the subcutaneous tissue and closely abutted the ulnar aspect of the extensor digitorum tendon. However, there was no evidence of osseous destruction, abnormal periosteal reaction, or definitive invasion of the flexor or extensor tendons. Wide local excision and reconstruction was planned in view of the malignant nature of the mass.Fig. 1Clinical photo of the 2 × 2 cm ulcerative tumour on the ulnar aspect of the proximal phalanx in the left ring finger.

Virtual surgical planning and 3D printing

To facilitate precise reconstruction, Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) data from the preoperative MRI were imported into Mimics Medical 21.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) for surgical planning. The perforators arising from the third and fourth DMA were identified and mapped on T2-weighted images. These coordinates were used to design a custom perforator guide using Meshmixer (Version 3.5, Autodesk, CA, USA). A 2 mm thick surface shell was generated to conform exactly to the soft tissue contour of the hand dorsum. Two circular fenestrations, each 1 cm in diameter, were integrated into the shell to correspond with the projected locations of the third and fourth DMA perforators respectively (Fig. 2). The customized guide was subsequently fabricated using polylactic acid (PLA) filament via an Ender-3 V2 3D printer (Creality, Shenzhen, China). The time required for the virtual surgical planning in Mimics and Meshmixer was 45 min, and the total time taken to 3D print the PLA guide was 4 h and 20 min. The estimated material cost was low at USD 0.4. The PLA guide was sterilized using low-temperature hydrogen peroxide plasma, which prevented material deformation during sterilization as reported previously by the authors’ institution.12Fig. 2Mapping of the third and fourth DMA on T2-weighted images of the preoperative MRI, with coordinates used for the design of a custom 3D-printed perforator guide in the form of a 2 mm thick surface shell with two circular fenestrations marking the projected locations of the third and fourth DMA perforators respectively.

Preoperative localization and surgical Procedure

Preoperatively, the 3D-printed guide was fitted onto the patient's hand dorsum. Perforator locations were marked on the skin through the guide's fenestrations (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). The presence and precise location of the perforators at these markings were subsequently cross verified using colour duplex ultrasound.Fig. 33D-printed hand model replicate of the patient marking the two perforators identified, and a pre-operative template of the propellor flap.Fig. 4The 3D-printed perforator guide being applied in a reproducible manner pre-operatively and intra-operatively.

The patient underwent wide local excision of the tumour with 6 mm peripheral margins. Intraoperative frozen section analysis confirmed clearance of both deep and peripheral margins. The resulting defect measured 3.2 × 3.5 cm, with exposed extensor digitorum tendon centrally. The ulnar proper digital neurovascular bundle was sacrificed due to its proximity to the tumour, resulting in a 3 cm digital nerve defect.

Reconstruction was performed using a propeller flap based on the fourth DMA perforator, which had been localized 2 cm proximal to the defect using the 3D-printed guide (Fig. 5). An 8.5 × 2.8 cm flap was harvested and rotated 160° toward the ulnar aspect of the ring finger to provide tension-free coverage (Fig. 6). Intra-operatively the perforator location was identified correctly at the site of fenestration of the 3D-printed guide. Postoperative outcomes

Final histopathological confirmed the diagnosis of porocarcinoma with clear resection margins. The postoperative course was uneventful. The propeller flap survived completely without congestion or necrosis, and the donor site healed well. At the six-month follow-up assessment, the patient demonstrated full range of motion in the reconstructed digit with satisfactory functional and aesthetic results (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Survival of the flap at follow-up assessment with full range of motion in the reconstructed digit and satisfactory aesthetic results.

Discussion

The primary benefit observed with the use of 3D-printed guide was a substantial reduction in the cognitive burden associated with vascular mapping, alongside reduced time required for perforator identification compared to handheld Doppler or ultrasound. The traditional "freestyle" or Doppler-assisted search for perforators can be time-consuming and imprecise, and requires relatively long dissection time to avoid inadvertent perforator damage in view of unpredictable perforator locations. In contrast, the use of a tangible, prefabricated guide streamlines perforator identification and expedites flap elevation. This aligns with existing literature suggesting that 3D models improve diagnostic capability and surgical planning, with over 90% of surgeons reporting superior visualization compared to traditional imaging, and up to a 41.5% reduction in operative time.13 The guide provided a static, reliable reference for the perforator's emergence point, allowing for targeted dissection that minimized unexpected trauma to perforators during exploration. In the context of hand surgery, the reduced operative time also correlates with decreased tourniquet time and ischemia time for the flap.

In other areas of plastic surgery, 3D-printed guides have been utilized for perforator visualization, though often with different objectives. Gillis and Morris first utilized a 3D-printed hemithorax model for preoperative visualization of internal mammary artery perforators in 2014, mostly for better understanding of anatomy by physically holding the model and viewing it in multiple planes.14 Similarly, Sotsuka et al. used a 3D model to aid deep inferior epigastric perforator flap elevation for breast reconstruction, placing it on the abdominal wall for intraoperative anatomical reference 15. However, these applications primarily served as tools for improved intraoperative 3D visualization16 or surgical resident education,17 rather than surface landmark aids.

Using such 3D-printed models as shells for surface mapping in the trunk or breast is challenging due to a lack of distinct, fixed bony landmarks required for accurate repositioning of models. Conversely, the hand provides multiple superficial, easily palpable bony landmarks (e.g. metacarpophalangeal joints) and well-defined contour (e.g. web spaces). These features allow reliable and reproducible placement of the 3D-printed shell at the desired position, making the hand an ideal location for this mapping technique.

Looking forward, the integration of 3D printing with emerging technologies such as augmented reality (AR) may further enhance accuracy and efficiency in soft tissue reconstruction.18 Holographic overlays via headsets such as the Microsoft HoloLens have been used to visualize deep inferior epigastric perforators during flap harvest.5 Projector-based AR has also demonstrated improved perforator identification and reduced dissection time, compared to Doppler planning.19 While 3D-printed guides provide a static template, AR offers dynamic, real-time visualization of subsurface anatomy. However, 3D printing remains currently more accessible and cost-effective for routine use, with material costs for standard models often remaining low. As these technologies mature, the workflow from scan to print will become faster and more seamless, cementing the role of 3D printing not just as a novelty, but as an essential tool for enhancing the safety and efficacy of perforator flap surgery in the hand. 3D-printed guides are however intended as a complementary adjunct to preoperative imaging, rather than a complete replacement for preoperative Doppler assessment or ultrasound. Vessel conditions can change over time (e.g. thrombosis or altered flow), particularly in cases of infection or angiopathy requiring soft-tissue reconstruction, or when there is a time lag between angiography and surgery. Doppler ultrasound remains a useful, practical confirmation tool before flap elevation, and 3D-printed guides provide an additional layer of confirmation and reassurance by correlating with CTA findings.
