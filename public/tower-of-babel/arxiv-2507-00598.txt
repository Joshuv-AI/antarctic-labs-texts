Abstract
Continuous attractor networks (CANs) are widely used to model how the brain temporarily retains continuous behavioural variables via persistent recurrent activity, such as an animal’s position in an environment. However, this memory mechanism is very sensitive to even small imperfections, such as noise or heterogeneity, which are both common in biological systems. Previous work has shown that discretising the continuum into a finite set of discrete attractor states provides robustness to these imperfections, but necessarily reduces the resolution of the represented variable, creating a dilemma between stability and resolution. We show that this stability-resolution dilemma is most severe for CANs using unimodal bump-like codes, as in traditional models. To overcome this, we investigate sparse binary distributed codes based on random feature embeddings, in which neurons have spatially-periodic receptive fields. We demonstrate theoretically and with simulations that such grid-cell-like codes enable CANs to achieve both high stability and high resolution simultaneously. The model extends to embedding arbitrary nonlinear manifolds into a CAN, such as spheres or tori, and generalises linear path integration to integration along freely-programmable on-manifold vector fields. Together, this work provides a theory of how the brain could robustly represent continuous variables with high resolution and perform flexible computations over task-relevant manifolds.



I
Introduction

Persistent neural activity has been proposed as a mechanism for temporarily storing behavioural variables during the execution of tasks
Fuster 1973c
.
Since the time duration demanded of such working memories typically exceeds the time constants of individual neurons, state persistence is believed to be an emergent network effect. Attractor networks are theoretical network models describing this behaviour, in which persistent activity states – or fixed-point attractors – are maintained by recurrent synaptic feedback
Little 1974c
.
The dynamics in these networks can be imagined as a gradual descent of an energy function, such that energy minima correspond to stable attractor states
Hopfield 1982c
;
Amit 1989c
. Either by construction or by learning through synaptic plasticity, the energy landscape can then be tailored to support particular behaviours.

Behavioural variables are often continuous, such as head direction or one’s position in space.
Accordingly, in a continuous attractor network (CAN), translational symmetries in the synaptic weights can be introduced to arrange fixed-point attractors along a low-dimensional continuum in the network’s state space, such as a line.
The first generation of CANs arranged neurons in a ring, in which attractor states comprised localised
unimodal
activity bumps at any angle on the ring
Ben-Yishai et al. 1995c
;
Amari 1977c
;
Zhang 1996g
;
Skaggs et al. 1995c
;
Samsonovich & McNaughton 1997c
.
We call such bump-like neural representations of a behavioural variable unimodal because each neuron is selective for a single location only, like an idealized hippocampal place cell, which responds maximally near a single unique place in an animal’s environment
Moser et al. 2017c
.

CANs have become a popular primitive for modelling biological working memory, such as for understanding navigation in the insect central complex
Green et al. 2017c
;
Noorman et al. 2024c
or in the hippocampal formation of vertebrates
Chaudhuri et al. 2019c
;
Gardner et al. 2022c
;
Burak & Fiete 2009c
.
However, nagging questions remain as to whether and how biological neural circuits could faithfully implement CANs. A major problem is the high degree of precision and reliability that CANs require of their individual components, which is at odds with the unreliability of neurons and synapses in the brain
Faisal et al. 2008c
;
Bartol et al. 2015c
. Fast stochastic nonidealities (e.g. from stochastic spike timing) cause random walk dynamics, known as diffusion, which degrade the accuracy of the represented variable over time
Camperi & Wang 1998c
;
Compte et al. 2000c
;
Burak & Fiete 2012c
;
Wu et al. 2008c
;
Seeholzer et al. 2019c
;
Wang & Kang 2022c
. Fixed nonidealities (quenched noise), such as fixed synaptic heterogeneity, lead to undesired deterministic movement of the neural state, known as drift
Zhang 1996g
;
Seung 1996c
;
Renart et al. 2003c
. Diffusion and drift undermine the capability of a CAN to hold or memorise a particular value, thus defeating its utility as working memory.

Introducing “roughness” to the energy landscape has been proposed as a solution to the diffusion problem, by creating a finite set of discrete attractor states that tile the idealised continuum. This mitigates diffusion by “pinning” the network to one of these states, but necessarily reduces the resolution at which a behavioural variable can be represented
Kilpatrick & Ermentrout 2013g
;
Brody et al. 2003c
;
Chaudhuri & Fiete 2016c
.
Narrowing the spacing between the discrete attractor states increases the resolution but also weakens stability, ultimately allowing diffusion between the discrete states to return
Kilpatrick & Ermentrout 2013g
.
This dilemma between resolution and stability in CANs with biologically-realistic nonidealities has cast doubt upon their adequacy to describe how brains represent and hold continuous variables over long timescales
Burak & Fiete 2012c
;
Khona & Fiete 2022c
.

We introduce a solution to this dilemma, which relies on using
multimodal
rather than unimodal representations of continuous variables, such as in the entorhinal cortex, where grid-cell neurons are active at multiple positions in a 2D environment
Moser et al. 2017c
;
Fiete et al. 2008c
.
Leveraging results from vector-symbolic architectures (VSAs)
Kleyko et al. 2022c
and sketching techniques
Woodruff 2014c
, we study CANs where the continuous variable is represented by a high-dimensional sparse binary vector using randomised multimodal codes.
We show that the noisy kernel approximations involved in these randomized coding schemes automatically introduce energy roughness, and further that they enable a CAN with traditional Hebbian plasticity to memorise continuous variables with high resolution and high robustness at the same time.
Our model suggests that multimodal entorhinal grid cells are more suitable than unimodal hippocampal place cells for supporting working memories of continuous quantities.
The proposed model also generalises to embedding arbitrary nonlinear CAN manifolds with freely-programmable integration capabilities, thus potentially explaining how general manifold-based computation could be realised in the brain
Langdon et al. 2023c
.

To understand the principles underlying memorisation and manipulation of continuous variables in the brain, we study abstract neural network models.
Specifically, we consider an associative network of
N
all-to-all connected binary neurons, similar to
Hopfield 1982c
, and first describe how to embed a finite range of a 1D continuous variable
p\in\mathbb{R}
into the network’s binary state space as a line attractor.
Although
p
may represent any continuous behaviourally-relevant variable, we will assume it represents a spatial position between 0 and 1 metres of an animal in an environment, such as a rat’s position on a 1D track.

II-A

Random spatial embeddings

We employ an embedding function
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{N}
to define which states in the neural space should represent each position.
It should not give preference to any particular position
p
(it is general), and it should map nearby positions to nearby points in neural space (it preserves local structure). In machine learning, randomness-based vector embeddings with these properties have been proposed and studied, such as Random Fourier Features (RFF)
Rahimi & Recht 2007g
.
Similar to a quantized version of RFF
Raginsky & Lazebnik 2009c
, but with a fixed quantization threshold and thus sparsity, we propose an embedding function in which binary neurons have spatially-periodic receptive fields

x_{i}(p)=\mathbbm{1}[\mathrm{mod}_{L}(\omega_{i}p+\theta_{i})<1]

(1)

where
\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]
is the indicator function (1 if condition true, else 0),
and
\omega_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
are the embedding frequency and offset of that neuron respectively.
The neuron is active for
1/\omega_{i}
metres, then inactive for
(L-1)/\omega_{i}
metres, periodically.
The sparsity of the embedding is then approximately
1/L
, while
\omega_{i}/L
is the approximate number of positions (modes) at which a neuron is active over the range of
p
.
The frequencies
\omega_{i}
are random variables sampled from a distribution
P(\omega)
, while the offsets are sampled from a separate distribution.
The embedding (
1
) admits a translation-invariant similarity kernel, such that the expected inner product between the vector representations of two positions
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
is given by

\langle\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p_{1})\cdot\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p_{2})\rangle=\frac{N}{L}K(p_{1}-p_{2})

(2)

where
K:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a symmetric kernel function with a maximum at
K(0)=1
, and falls off to
1/L
for large differences in position.
Since an active neuron needs a change in position of approximately
\omega_{i}^{-1}
metres to deactivate, the kernel width is approximately
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}^{-1}
metres, where
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\omega|P(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega
is the mean-absolute embedding frequency.
The Fourier transform of
P(\omega)
determines the exact shape of
K
.
Importantly,
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}/L
is the average number of modes per neuron.
Due to the kernel property (
2
), (
1
) can be understood as forming a binary locality-sensitive hash of
p

Frady et al. 2022g
;
Thomas et al. 2022g
;
Rahimi & Recht 2007g
;
Raginsky & Lazebnik 2009c
.

By setting constraints on the sampled
\omega_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
parameters, we force the representation vectors
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)
to have a sparse block structure (Fig.
2
e). Specifically, the neurons are grouped into
N/L
blocks of integer length
L
, such that in each block exactly one neuron is active for any
p

Frady et al. 2023g
;
Laiho et al. 2015g
. Thus, the resulting sparsity of the code is exactly
1/L
. Neurons in a block share one frequency
\omega_{i}
, sampled from
P(\omega)
, but have different phase offsets
\theta_{i}
.
A block in this abstract code thus resembles a grid cell module in entorhinal cortex, where grid cells share one spatial frequency but have different offsets
Stensola et al. 2012c
.

II-B

Memory network for block-sparse spatial representations

To store the location representations
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)
as attractors within the CAN, we use Hebb’s covariance rule to form a recurrent autoassociative weight matrix
Sejnowski 1977c
;
Hopfield 1982c
;
Amari 1989c
;
Tsodyks & Feigel’man 1988c
;
Palm & Sommer 1992c

\mathbf{A}=\int_{p=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$}^{p=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$}\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(p)\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(p)^{\intercal}\mathrm{d}p\ \in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

(3)

where
\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(p)=\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)-\frac{1}{L}
are the centred position representations (
1
), and
\mathbf{A}
will be used as the synaptic weights
\mathbf{W}
.
To retrieve the block-sparse location representations stored in the CAN, we employ a winner-take-all (WTA) neural activation function within each block
Gripon & Berrou 2011c
,

\mathbf{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\mathrm{bWTA}\big[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\bm{z}}_{t}\big]

(4)

where
\mathbf{\bm{z}}_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
are the neuron activations on time step
t
,
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the synaptic weight matrix, and
\mathrm{bWTA}
is a per-block WTA function.
A potential biological mechanism for implementing the
\mathrm{bWTA}
function is fast competition through inhibitory connectivity within a block or module, like divisive normalization
Shim et al. 1991c
;
Heeger 1992c
.

Setting the block size
L
in the embedding to a large integer value yields sparse representations of position, in line with the sparse activity of biological networks.
Further, in networks with Hebbian learning, sparse patterns exploit synaptic memory capacity most efficiently, that is, they maximize the number of uncorrelated discrete attractor states a network can support
Amit et al. 1987c
;
Amari 1989c
;
Tsodyks & Feigel’man 1988c
;
Palm & Sommer 1992c
. Interestingly, the block constraint introduced by (
4
) retains this high synaptic memory capacity

Gripon & Berrou 2011c
;
Knoblauch & Palm 2020c
.
While direct application of such capacity results is here complicated by the fact that we are storing a quasi-continuum of correlated states, they nonetheless provide a high upper capacity bound when using sparse codes.

We impose nonidealities upon the network in two ways. First, we add fixed random noise to the synaptic weights, to imitate fixed synaptic heterogeneity. Second, we introduce a stochastic degradation of the neural state, by randomly setting on each time step 10% of the neurons to either a 0 or 1 state, with equal probability. This relatively high noise value is tempered by attractor dynamics, which project the perturbation back onto the attractor manifold, reducing its impact by a factor of
\sqrt{N}

Burak & Fiete 2012c
;
Wu et al. 2008c
;
Compte et al. 2000c
.

II-C

The resolution-stability dilemma in traditional CANs

We compare the stability and resolution of three line attractor models with comparable imposed nonidealities: the neural field models of
Zhang 1996g
and
Kilpatrick & Ermentrout 2013g
, which use unimodal place-cell-like codes, and the model introduced in this work, which uses multimodal periodic codes (
1
).
In the first model, synaptic heterogeneity causes the idealised continuum of states to uncontrollably deteriorate into a set of discrete attractor states. When the network is initialised from multiple positions along the line attractor, there are large initial drifts to this reduced set of positions (Fig.
1
a). The network state is not confined to these states however, and the stochastic neural dynamics then cause the network state to perform a diffusive random walk along the line attractor (Fig.
1
b), where the position variance is proportional to time.

To mitigate diffusion of the behavioural variable over time, the model of
Kilpatrick & Ermentrout 2013g
introduces sinusoidal heterogeneity of frequency
n
along the line attractor, to deliberately collapse the continuum of states to a set of
n
equally-spaced discrete attractor states. For low
n
, there are large initial drifts as the network state self-discretises into one of the
n
attractor states (Fig.
1
c). Afterwards, the network state is “pinned” to that particular attractor state, mitigating diffusion (Fig.
1
e).
The network is thus stable, but it has low resolution.

Increasing
n
increases the number of discrete attractor states, thereby increasing the resolution. However, this necessarily also decreases the spacing between the attractor states.
The result is that, for even moderately larger
n
values, the distance between the would-be attractor states is small enough to allow stochastic transitions between them
Kilpatrick & Ermentrout 2013g
. Diffusion of the behavioural variable returns, and is comparable in magnitude to the first model
(Fig.
1
d,e)
.
This presents a severe trade-off between the resolution of the represented behavioural variable and its stability
(Fig.
2
c,d)
.

II-D

Escaping the resolution-stability dilemma

In the absence of noise, the block-sparse memory network will approximately descend the energy
E=-\mathbf{\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\bm{z}}
(see Methods). Although the energies
E(p)
of states on the line attractor (
3
) have equal expected values, the actual energy as a function of location
E(p)
is not flat, but instead consists of a rough landscape of minima and maxima. This is because with the randomized embedding (
1
), the inner products between location representations
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p_{1})\cdot\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p_{2})
are not equal to the kernel (
2
) exactly, but are themselves random variables.
The roughness of
E(p)
has an autocorrelation length scale of
1/(2\omega_{\mathrm{MA}})
metres, where
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\omega|P(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega
is the mean-absolute embedding frequency; therefore, we expect the drifts to occur over the same length scale.

As in the Kilpatrick & Ermentrout model, our model initially drifts to a stable local minimum, with an average drift distance of
1/(2\omega_{\mathrm{MA}})
metres
(Fig.
1
f)
.
To increase the CAN’s resolution, we thus increase
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}
to increase the number of such stable locations.
Interestingly, however, when the spatial resolution is increased in this way, the behaviour of the two models diverge qualitatively. In our model, the stable memory function persists, whereas in the Kilpatrick & Ermentrout model diffusion returned and the memory function was compromised
(Fig.
1
d)
. The average position errors in the two models for different resolutions can compared in
(Fig.
1
e,h)
.

Why, then, is our model not subject to the same resolution-stability dilemma as the comparison models in Fig.
1
?
To answer this, we define a quantity describing embeddings of continuous variables in vector spaces: the
path length in representation space
, or just path length. For binary embeddings the
L^{1}
-norm path length is the sum of Hamming distances along the vector embedding as the continuous variable sweeps its entire range.
The comparison models in Fig.
1
are bump attractor networks, in which each neuron has a unimodal receptive field over the range of the continuous variable. As
p
sweeps through its range, each neuron becomes active at its preferred position, then inactive again. Thus, each neuron changes state at most twice and the resulting path length is about
2N

(Fig.
2
a,b)
.
Any increase in the number of discrete states along the line attractor must thus reduce the distance between them.

In contrast, for neurons with multimodal receptive fields, like in (
1
), each neuron changes state two times for each mode, and so the path length is
2N
times the average number of modes
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}/L

(Fig.
2
e,f)
.
By increasing
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}
and thus the number of modes, we map the line attractor to a longer path in the neural state space.
The line attractor can then support more discrete attractor states while keeping the distance between them in the neural state space constant.
This avoids inadvertently reintroducing diffusion between states, and enables memory of a behavioural variable with high resolution and high stability.

By sweeping
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}
, we find that the root-mean-square (RMS) drift is proportional to
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}^{-0.85}

(Fig.
1
i)
. If the only change with increasing
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}
were to increase the attractor path length, without disturbing the dynamics elsewhere on the attractor, then we would expect the RMS drift to be proportional to
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}^{-1}
. This discrepancy can be attributed to the increased memory loading and thus interference from storing more states within the network, as in conventional discrete attractor models
Hopfield 1982c
;
Amit 1989c
. Nonetheless, these results show that a 4096-neuron line attractor utilising periodic spatial codes can accurately represent a metre-scale position to millimetre resolution, despite considerable imposed neural and synaptic nonidealities.

Path integration is the ability to gradually update a represented location using velocity information
Khona & Fiete 2022c
.
It is a common computational motif in biological systems (e.g. in the Drosophila head direction circuit) and enables behaviours such as navigating in the dark and path planning
Green et al. 2017c
. It is also theorised to underpin more general manifold-based computation in biological networks
Langdon et al. 2023c
.

To enable path integration in the CAN, heteroassociative weights
\mathbf{H}
may be superimposed upon the autoassociative weight matrix (
3
),
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{H}
.
The added term should release the network from its autoassociative dynamics, such that the current state
p
should instead project to a shifted position state
p+\delta p

Zhang 1996g
;
Burak & Fiete 2009c
.
The
\mathbf{H}
matrix should effectively project out the difference between consecutive
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)
states, and so is formed by integrating over the outer product between the vectors
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\bm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}p}
and
\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}
over the path of
p
,

\mathbf{H}=c\int_{\text{line}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\bm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}p}\big(\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}\circ\mathbf{\bm{s}}\big)^{\intercal}\>\mathrm{d}p

(5)

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant determining the maximum integration speed. The
vector
\mathbf{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}
contains random bipolar entries, and is
bound
to
\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}
via the Hadamard product “
\circ
” (element-wise multiplication).
Binding
\mathbf{\bm{s}}
to
\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}
ensures that, in the absence of velocity input, the rows of
\mathbf{H}
have 0 expected inner product with any position state
\mathbf{\bm{x}}
, such that no integration behaviour occurs, i.e.
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)\approx\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)
. To activate path integration, external input inhibits all neurons for which
s_{i}=-1
. Then, these inner products become nonzero, such that the postsynaptic sum approximates
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p+c)
rather than
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)
, giving rise to integration dynamics. This is a “push-pull” path integration mechanism, wherein an equal number of neurons are biased in the positive and negative directions, resulting in no net bias in the absence of input
Burak & Fiete 2009c
.

Path integration with a 1D line attractor is shown in Fig.
3
a-d. When one subset of neurons is masked by input, the attractor moves in the positive
p
direction, while inhibiting the opposite set of neurons triggers integration in the negative
p
direction. If fewer neurons are masked by the input, the integration is correspondingly slower.
Importantly, this method of introducing integration behaviours by superimposing heteroassociative matrix terms (
5
) easily generalises to higher-dimensional attractor manifolds with multiple inputs.

To implement path integration on a 2D plane attractor, the
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{H}
matrices are constructed by integrating over two position dimensions. In the 1D line attractor case, the matrix
\mathbf{H}
projected to the derivative column vector

\frac{\partial\mathbf{\bm{x}}}{\partial p_{1}}

\frac{\partial\mathbf{\bm{x}}}{\partial p_{2}}

Path integration can be equally performed on manifolds with more complex topologies.
To store a ring attractor, for example, we could simply integrate the position states over a ring in 2D space, rather than the whole plane.
In Fig.

We stochastically binarised the weights in these networks, to highlight that they can be implemented in neural systems with low synaptic precision (Fig.

II-G

Multiple manifold embeddings

Storing multiple manifolds in a single attractor network is thought to underlie the brain’s ability to store representations of multiple environments
Wills et al. 2005c
;
Samsonovich & McNaughton 1997c
.
As with the 1D line attractors, networks storing multiple manifolds using unimodal spatial codes encounter issues of severely limited spatial resolution
Battaglia & Treves 1998c
;
Cerasti & Treves 2013c
;
Monasson & Rosay 2014c
;
Battista & Monasson 2020c
.
However, in our model, multiple manifolds may be simultaneously embedded simply by superimposing the weight matrices responsible for each separate manifold (Fig.
4
e).
The separate matrices may be generated by resampling the embedding parameters
\omega_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
(
1
) for each manifold.
This simple scheme has a useful property: the cross-talk noise from states on different manifolds is then no different from between distal points on the same manifold.
Additional heteroassociative terms in the weight matrix can be superimposed in order to trigger transitions between manifolds, similar to previous works embedding finite state machines into discrete attractor networks
Cotteret et al. 2024c
;
Cotteret et al. 2025c
.

III
Discussion

III-A

High-resolution & stable working memory

Recent theoretical work on the
Drosophila
navigation system has suggested modelling biological working memories of continuous behavioural variables with CANs composed of a small number of neurons
Noorman et al. 2024c
.
In agreement with past literature, Noorman
et al.
suggest that, much like digital computers, some degree of discretisation at the neuron
Goldman et al. 2003c
;
Koulakov et al. 2002c
;
Kitano et al. 2003c
;
Loewenstein & Sompolinsky 2003c
;
Camperi & Wang 1998c
or network level
Kilpatrick & Ermentrout 2013g
;
Kilpatrick et al. 2013c
;
Pouget & Latham 2002c
;
Brody et al. 2003c
;
Miller 2006c
is required to maintain stable representations in biological memory circuits.

To better understand the resulting resolution-stability dilemma, we defined the path length of the encoded variable in representation space. For binary neurons, the path length is easily measured by the Manhattan distance. If the path length is very short, then the behavioural variable is tightly packed into a very small portion of the representation space, such that only very few well-separated stable points can exist along the path (stable but low-resolution). This is the case when using unimodal place-cell-like neural codes, like those in conventional bump attractor networks, where the path length is at most
2N

Skaggs et al. 1995c
;
Zhang 1996g
;
Kilpatrick & Ermentrout 2013g
. In contrast, the path length with a multimodal grid-cell-like code (
1
) is dependent upon the spatial frequency of its receptive fields, and so may far exceed
2N
. A CAN using multimodal grid-cell-like codes may thus stably represent behavioural variables with significantly greater resolution than one with unimodal place-cell-like codes.
This result should extend to networks with more complex neuron models, e.g. continuous-valued neurons, as long as the definition of path length is suitably adjusted.

The representation path length may also provide insight into why entorhinal 2D grid cells use grid-like receptive fields at all, rather than plane-wave receptive fields, which would be another natural choice
Sorscher et al. 2023c
. An active grid cell alters its activity for movements in all directions, while a plane-wave-coded neuron does not change its state for movements parallel to the wave front. Plane-wave codes may thus fail to adequately separate position representations in neural space, leading to a resolution-stability trade-off that is similar to, but somewhat milder than, that suffered by place-cell codes.

III-B

Models of the hippocampal formation

There have historically been two opposing views of the essential function of the hippocampal formation: some have emphasized its role in spatial navigation via the concept of cognitive maps
O’Keefe & Nadel 1978c
;
Samsonovich & McNaughton 1997c
;
Behrens et al. 2018c
, while others have proposed a broader role as an associative memory
Eichenbaum 2017c
;
Treves & Rolls 1994c
. There have been many attempts to unify these perspectives, suggesting a general function as a relational memory system, not limited to spatial navigation per se
Whittington et al. 2020c
;
Eichenbaum & Cohen 2014c
;
Buzsáki & Moser 2013c
;
Chandra et al. 2025c
. Our model is a concrete mechanistic instantiation of this unified view. We show that an associative memory with standard Hebbian learning for memorising sparse distributed random vectors can function as a spatial memory, and, if equipped with integration capabilities, as a spatial navigation system.
This points to the entorhinal cortex, with its distributed place representations, as a site for continuous attractor dynamics, as opposed to hippocampal CA3 as proposed in early modelling works
Samsonovich & McNaughton 1997c
;
Káli & Dayan 2000c
;
Li et al. 2024c
.

If the hippocampal formation is indeed optimized for both navigation and memory,
then how does a generic encoding of position in entorhinal cortex contribute to this function?
Recent work has emphasized the compositional nature of computation in the hippocampal formation, in that it manipulates data structures that combine diverse behaviourally-relevant information
Kurth-Nelson et al. 2023c
.
In cognitive neuroscience, vector-symbolic architectures (VSAs) were proposed as a framework for such compositional neural computation
Plate 2003c
;
Kanerva 2009c
;
Gayler 1998c
and, as a historical aside, also anticipated the ideas used here for encoding spatial position
Plate 1992c
.
In VSAs, symbols and positions are represented by high-dimensional random vectors, which can be combined through multiplicative “binding” operations to express role-filler pairs and through additive “bundling” operations to form sets.
These operations equip neural networks with sufficient syntax to operate over compositional data structures and perform tasks like analogical reasoning
Kleyko et al. 2022c
. Concretely, a recent model of the hippocampal formation proposes vector binding between generic spatial codes in medial entorhinal cortex (MEC) and sensory representations in lateral entorhinal cortex (LEC) to generate a cognitive map, serving as a memory index for hippocampus
Kymn et al. 2024f
.
Our model takes steps towards a mechanistic neural implementation of these ideas.
Specifically, we propose how to address the resolution-stability dilemma that riddles any attempt to model spatial encoding in MEC, and we show how this functionality can be robustly implemented with imperfect and noisy components.

III-C

Programmable neural manifolds

There is growing evidence that low-dimensional neural manifolds are a recurring computational primitive in the brain
Langdon et al. 2023c
;
Vyas et al. 2020c
.
Far from being limited to spatial navigation, reports over the last decade indicate that both near-sensory and high-level cognitive behavioural variables occupy low-dimensional manifolds in the neural representation space, such as social distance
Nieh et al. 2021c
;
Tavares et al. 2015c
, emotional states
Nair et al. 2023c
, object perception
Constantinescu et al. 2016c
;
Behrens et al. 2018c
and motor commands
Gallego et al. 2017c
. While low-rank connectivity gives rise to linear manifold representations
Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic 2018c
;
Darshan & Rivkind 2022c
;
Pollock & Jazayeri 2020c
;
Mante et al. 2013c
, less is known about general ways to confine neural activity to task-appropriate nonlinear neural manifolds.

Notably, the approach proposed in this work generalises seamlessly to generating structure-preserving neural embeddings of arbitrary nonlinear neural manifolds, and CANs which denoise, memorise, and manipulate points on the chosen manifold.
The nonlinear similarity kernel (
2
) also allows us to freely “program” the direction in which the state should move for every input and location.
Combined, this endows CANs with a tremendous degree of flexibility as a computational primitive: if a behaviour or computation can be expressed in terms of controllable flows on low-dimensional manifolds, then it can be implemented by a CAN.
For example, to manipulate 3D rotational velocity signals, such as in the vestibular system
Seung 1996c
;
Cannon et al. 1983c
, a sphere attractor with rotational vector fields as in Fig.
4
a would be suitable, while a torus would enable the boundary-free manipulation of 2D positions
McNaughton et al. 2006c
;
Gardner et al. 2022c
;
Chaudhuri et al. 2019c
. Even going beyond single manifolds, one can construct CANs that encode multiple manifolds (Fig.
4
e). Such a network could then dynamically switch to the manifold best suited to manipulate a given input, as has been proposed for solving tasks with compositional structure
Driscoll et al. 2024c
;
Yang et al. 2019c
;
Duncker et al. 2020c
.

IV
Methods

IV-A

Scalar position encoding

The position representation vectors
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)
are generated according to (
1
). Instead of indexing the neurons by
i=0\ldots N-1
, we can instead index them by their block index
m=\lfloor i/L\rfloor
and within-block index
n=\mathrm{mod}_{L}\>i
, such that
i=n+Lm
. For every block
m
, a single frequency value
\omega_{m}
is independently sampled from
P(\omega)
, and all neurons in the block have this frequency value. The random offsets
\theta_{m,n}
are then set such that exactly one neuron in each block is active for any position
p
. They are given by
\theta_{m,n}=\pi^{(m)}_{n}+\theta_{m}
where
\vec{\pi}^{(m)}
is a random permutation of
0\ldots L-1
and
\theta_{m}\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{U}_{[0,1]}
is a uniformly distributed random offset. Both the permutation vector and offset are independently generated for each block. The position states for 4 blocks of neurons with block length
L=8
are shown in Fig.
2
e.

Deriving the similarity kernels requires calculating the expectation
\langle x_{i}(p)x_{i}(p+\Delta p)\rangle_{\omega_{i},\theta_{i}}
at any index
i
, yielding

K(\Delta p)=\frac{1}{L}+\frac{2}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{sinc}^{2}\Big(\frac{n}{L}\Big)\hat{P}\Big(\frac{2\pi n}{L}\Delta p\Big)

(6)

where
\hat{P}(u)=\int P(\omega)\exp(-i\omega u)\mathrm{d}\omega
is the Fourier transform of
P(\omega)
(
S1
).
This directly connects our embedding function’s (
1
) kernel and those of RFF phasor embeddings
Rahimi & Recht 2007g
, where the kernel is simply
\hat{P}(\Delta p)
, and their quantised variants
Raginsky & Lazebnik 2009c
;
Li & Li 2022c
.
The measured and theoretical kernels for three different frequency distributions
P(\omega)
are shown in Fig.
3
g,h. For large
\Delta p
the kernels approach
1/L
. We thus use the notation
\bar{K}=K-1/L
to denote the unbiased kernel, which tends to
0
for large
\Delta p
.

We consider in detail the dynamics of inner product
X(p):=\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)\cdot\mathbf{\bm{y}}_{0}
where
\mathbf{\bm{y}}_{0}
is a fixed independent sparse block vector. This can be approximated by the stochastic differential equation

\mathrm{d}X=-\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}\frac{L}{L-1}\Bigg[\frac{N}{L^{2}}-X\Bigg]\mathrm{d}p+\sqrt{\frac{2N}{L^{2}}\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}}\mathrm{d}W

(7)

where
\mathrm{d}W
is an increment of a Wiener process, with
\langle\mathrm{d}W\rangle=0
and
\langle\mathrm{d}W^{2}\rangle=\mathrm{d}p
(
S3
).
Using (
7
), we can derive statistics of the heterogeneity of the energy landscape
E(p)
, most importantly the autocorrelation length scale (
S3
).

IV-B

Network architecture

All the networks introduced in this paper are single layer RNNs. For experiments without integration, the weight matrix consists only of autoassociative terms. The weight between neurons in the same block is set to 0, equivalent to the stipulation of no self-connections in Hopfield networks
Hopfield 1982c
;
Amit 1989c
. For the 1D line attractor
\mathbf{A}
is constructed as defined by (
3
), calculated by a Riemann sum with a sufficiently-small step size. This matrix ensures that the active position state is maximally reprojected out within the postsynaptic sum, since

\begin{split}\mathbf{\bm{x}}(q)\cdot\big(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)\big)&=\int_{\text{line}}\big[\mathbf{\bm{x}}(q)\cdot\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(p^{\prime})\big]\big[\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(p^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)\big]\mathrm{d}p^{\prime}\\
&\approx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bar{K}(q-p^{\prime})\bar{K}(p-p^{\prime})\mathrm{d}p^{\prime}\end{split}

(8)

for a comparison position
q
. We have assumed that
p
is far from the edges of the line attractor, and that the inner products between position states are well-approximated by their expected values. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, this integral is maximised for
p=q
, i.e. the postsynaptic sum is most similar to the current position.

The block-WTA activation function ensures that only the neuron with the greatest input in each block becomes active. For example, for block length
L=2
,
\mathrm{bWTA}[(3,4,-5,-2)]=(0,1,0,1)
.
Since the postsynaptic sum is most similar to the current position state, the
\mathrm{bWTA}
operation approximately cleans up
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)
to
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)
, realising the desired attractor dynamics.

IV-C

Synaptic and neural nonidealities

We introduced fixed synaptic nonidealities to a weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
by superimposing Gaussian noise of equivalent magnitude to the ideal weight values, via

w_{ij}^{\text{nonideal}}=w_{ij}^{\text{ideal}}+\chi_{ij}\cdot w^{\text{ideal}}_{\mathrm{RMS}}

(9)

where
\chi_{ij}
are samples from a standard Gaussian distribution, and are scaled by
w^{\text{ideal}}_{\mathrm{RMS}}
, the RMS of the ideal weight values.
Fast stochastic neural nonidealities were introduced by imposing a bit error rate
b=10\%
, such that on every time step, each neuron’s state was set to
0
or
1
with equal probability
\frac{1}{2}b
, or left unaltered with probability
1-b=90\%
.

In Fig.
4
, the weights were stochastically binarised via

w_{ij}^{\text{bin}}=\begin{cases}1\>\>\text{w.p.}\>\>(1+\exp[-\alpha(w_{ij}-\langle w\rangle)/\sigma_{w}])^{-1}\\
0\>\>\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(10)

where
\langle w\rangle
and
\sigma_{w}
are the mean and standard deviation of the ideal weight values respectively, and
\alpha
is the steepness of the sigmoid, which we set to
\alpha=5
.

IV-D

Energy function

We consider that our network is following a discrete-time approximation with Euler time step
\tau
of the continuous-time dynamical system

\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\bm{z}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathbf{\bm{z}}(t)+\mathrm{bWTA}[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\bm{z}}(t)]

(11)

where the weights contain only the autoassociative component
\mathbf{A}
, in which
\mathbf{\bm{z}}
decays to
\mathrm{bWTA}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\bm{z}})
in time
\tau
, rather than jumping directly to it. We can then define a scaled energy

E(\mathbf{\bm{z}})=-\frac{L^{2}\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}}{N}\mathbf{\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\bm{z}}

(12)

which, due to
\mathbf{A}
being symmetric has time derivative

\Big(\frac{\tau N}{L^{2}\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}}\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t}=\big(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\bm{z}}\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf{\bm{z}}-\mathrm{bWTA}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\bm{z}})\big)\leq 0

(13)

such that
E
is never-increasing, and is thus a Lyapunov function of our dynamics
Cohen & Grossberg 1983c
;
Shim et al. 1991c
.
When the heteroassociative terms (or also random synaptic heterogeneity) are added to
\mathbf{W}
, we break the symmetry assumption, and so the descent on
E
no longer strictly holds.
It is nonetheless useful for describing the network dynamics when there is no input, assuming that the bound heteroassociative terms contribute only a weak noise to the postsynaptic sum.
Using (
7
) we can derive expressions for the statistics of
E(p)
. Most importantly, the autocovariance can be approximated by

\mathrm{cov}\big[E(p),E(p+\Delta p)\big]\approx\big(4N\kappa_{1}^{2}+\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}\big)e^{-2\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}\Delta p}

(14)

where
\kappa_{1}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bar{K}(u/\omega_{\mathrm{MA}})\mathrm{d}u
is independent of
\omega_{\mathrm{MA}}
(
S3
). Critically, (
14
) implies that the energy landscape’s heterogeneity has an autocorrelation length scale of
1/(2\omega_{\mathrm{MA}})
metres, which we interpret as the effective spacing between discrete attractor states.

IV-E

1D path integration

The heteroassociative matrix
\mathbf{H}
(
5
) is constructed using the derivative
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\bm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}p}
and a bound dense bipolar vector
\mathbf{\bm{s}}
. To avoid difficulties associated with differentiating the discontinuous position embedding function (
1
), for the purpose of calculating gradients we replace it with a surrogate function with a finite gradient instead
Neftci et al. 2019c
. Hence,
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p)
is replaced with

x_{i}(p)=S\Big(\phi_{i}(p)-\frac{L-1}{2}\Big)\cdot S\Big(\phi_{i}(p)-\frac{L+1}{2}\Big)

(15)

where
S(u)=1/\big(1+\exp(-\beta u)\big)
is the logistic function with steepness parameter
\beta>0
, serving as a smooth surrogate for the step function, and
\phi_{i}(p)
is the modular phase given by

\phi_{i}(p)=\mathrm{mod}_{L}\big(\omega_{i}p+\theta_{i}+\frac{L-1}{2}\big)

(16)

We have effectively shifted the acceptance region to the middle of the interval
[0,L)
to stop the gradient wrapping around the extremities. The steepness parameter
\beta
we heuristically set to
\beta=5
in all experiments.

The weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
is then given by the superposition
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{H}
. The vector
\mathbf{\bm{s}}
is generated to be block-constant, such that neurons in the same block have the same value, either
+1
or
-1
with equal probability. Hence, any expectation over the elements of
\mathbf{\bm{s}}
will be 0.
Due to the inclusion of this bound term, the expected contribution to the postsynaptic sum from the heteroassociative terms, in the absence of input, is also 0, since

\mathbf{H}\mathbf{\bm{x}}(q)=c\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\bm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}p}\underbrace{\big(\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(p)\circ\mathbf{\bm{s}}\big)\cdot\mathbf{\bm{x}}(q)\,}_{\langle\ldots\rangle\>=\>0}\mathrm{d}p\>\approx\>0

(17)

thus the
\mathbf{H}
terms do not contribute meaningfully to the dynamics.
When there is input to the network, which we model as a selective inhibition, the state update rule is

\mathbf{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\mathrm{bWTA}\big[\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{\bm{z}}_{t}\land\mathbf{\bm{i}}_{t})\big]

(18)

where
\land
is an element-wise
\mathrm{AND}
operation, implementing inhibition, and
\mathbf{\bm{i}}_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
is the input mask at time
t

Cotteret et al. 2025c
. When there is no input to the network,
\mathbf{\bm{i}}_{t}
is a vector of all ones, reducing (
18
) to the input-free state update rule (
4
).
If we apply an input to the network, which inhibit all neurons for which
s_{i}
is negative, leaving only the components for which
s_{i}=1
, then the postsynaptic sum becomes

\begin{split}\mathbf{W}\big(&\mathbf{\bm{x}}(q)\land H(\mathbf{\bm{s}})\big)\\
&=\big(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{H}\big)\big(\mathbf{\bm{x}}(q)\land H(\mathbf{\bm{s}})\big)\\
&=\int\Big(\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}^{\intercal}+c\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\bm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}p}\underbrace{\big(\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}\circ\mathbf{\bm{s}}\big)^{\intercal}\Big)\big(\mathbf{\bm{x}}(q)\land H(\mathbf{\bm{s}})\big)}_{\langle\ldots\rangle\>\propto\>\bar{K}(p-q)}\mathrm{d}p\\
&\approx\frac{N}{2L}\int\Big(\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(p)+c\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\bm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}p}\Big)\bar{K}(p-q)\mathrm{d}p\\
&\approx\frac{N}{2L}\int\Big(\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(p+c)+\mathcal{O}(c^{2})\Big)\bar{K}(p-q)\mathrm{d}p\end{split}

(19)

where we have applied the Heaviside step function
H(\cdot)
to element-wise convert the bipolar vector
\mathbf{\bm{s}}
into a binary vector, which we use as input. This is because
H(\mathbf{\bm{s}})
is precisely the input which inhibits all neurons for which
s_{i}=-1
.
As a result, the active position state
q
no longer projects maximally to
q
as in the autoassociative case, but instead approximates the position state
q+c
, causing the network state to shift by
c
on each time step.

IV-F

Sparse block vector binding

The states to represent 2D positions are generated by independently generating two separate scalar position embeddings (
1
), and then binding them together via a block-wise Local Circular Convolution (LCC) operation, denoted
*_{\mathrm{LCC}}

Frady et al. 2023g
. Using the block and within-block indices
m
and
n
to describe the neuron index
i=n+Lm
, the LCC binding operation for
\mathbf{\bm{c}}=\mathbf{\bm{a}}*_{\mathrm{LCC}}\mathbf{\bm{b}}
is given as

c_{n+Lm}=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}a_{\mathrm{mod}_{L}(n-k)+Lm}b_{k+Lm}

(20)

To generate vectors representing higher-dimensional positions
\vec{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{D^{\prime}}
, we similarly bind together
D^{\prime}
separate vector representations,

\mathbf{\bm{x}}(\vec{g})=\mathbf{\bm{x}}_{1}(g_{1})*_{\mathrm{LCC}}\mathbf{\bm{x}}_{2}(g_{2})\ldots*_{\mathrm{LCC}}\mathbf{\bm{x}}_{D^{\prime}}(g_{D^{\prime}})

(21)

where
\mathbf{\bm{x}}_{1\ldots D^{\prime}}
are scalar position embedding functions (
1
) with independently-sampled frequencies and offsets.
If additional restrictions are placed on the offsets
\theta_{i}
, then the vectors adhere to the relation
\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p_{1})*_{\mathrm{LCC}}\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p_{2})*_{\mathrm{LCC}}^{-1}\mathbf{\bm{x}}(0)\approx\mathbf{\bm{x}}(p_{1}+p_{2})
, which allows arithmetic operations to be performed by direct manipulation of the distributed representations
Kymn et al. 2024g
;
Frady et al. 2022g
(
S4
).

IV-G

2D plane attractor

The autoassociative matrix is constructed as

\mathbf{A}=\iint_{\text{plane}}\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(u,w)\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(u,w)^{\intercal}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}w

(22)

with the position representations generated according to

\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}(u,w)=\mathbf{\bm{x}}_{u}(u)*_{\mathrm{LCC}}\mathbf{\bm{x}}_{w}(w)-\frac{1}{L}

(23)

where the two scalar position embedding functions
\mathbf{\bm{x}}_{u/w}
have independently-sampled embedding frequencies and offsets. The superimposed heteroassociative matrix, to embed a vector field
\vec{v}_{\nu}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, is given by

\mathbf{H}_{\nu}=\iint_{\text{plane}}\Big(\big(\vec{v}_{\nu}\cdot\nabla_{p}\big)\mathbf{\bm{x}}\Big)(\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}\circ\mathbf{\bm{s}}_{\nu})^{\intercal}\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}w

(24)

where
\nabla_{p}=(\partial_{u},\partial_{w})^{\intercal}
is a vector of partial derivative operators with respect to the position coordinates
\vec{p}=(u,w)^{\intercal}
, and
\mathbf{\bm{s}}_{\nu}
is a block-constant dense bipolar vector, analogous to the 1D integration case, but independently generated for each vector field
\nu
.
The gradients
\nabla_{p}\,\mathbf{\bm{x}}
are calculated by the method of surrogate gradients as earlier described, differentiating through the LCC binding operation, and are computed using JAX auto-differentiation functions
Bradbury et al. 2018c
. The three imposed vector fields are shown in Fig.
3
e, and the weights are given by the superposition

\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}+\sum_{\text{fields }\nu}\mathbf{H}_{\nu}

(25)

Identical to the 1D line attractor case, the individual vector fields
\vec{v}_{\nu}
are triggered by inhibiting the neurons for which the corresponding
\mathbf{\bm{s}}_{\nu}
is negative.

IV-H

Curved manifold embeddings

To embed curved manifolds with arbitrary topologies, we consider a smooth vector mapping
\vec{g}(\vec{p}):\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D^{\prime}}
, with
D^{\prime}\geq D
from our position coordinates
\vec{p}
to a higher dimensional space, whose coordinates we embed into the attractor network with (
21
).
The
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{H}
matrices are constructed as previously described, integrating over the positions
\vec{p}
, except now we need to introduce additional state weighting terms to account for the non-uniformity of the
\vec{p}\rightarrow\vec{g}
mapping.
The autoassociative matrix is now given by

\mathbf{A}_{\text{FO}}=\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det G}}\>\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}\big(\vec{g}(\vec{p})\big)\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}\big(\vec{g}(\vec{p})\big)^{\intercal}\mathrm{d}P

(26)

where

\big[G\big]_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{D^{\prime}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial p_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial p_{j}}

(27)

This ensures that, to a first-order expansion of
\vec{g}
, the expected energies of all points far away from the edges of the manifold are equal.
However, this does not account for expected energy differences due to manifold curvature, as those are stored in higher-order terms.
To account for these higher-order effects, we construct the weights in a two-step procedure.
First, we empirically measure the energy
\hat{E}
for each state with respect to the first-order-corrected weight matrices,
\hat{E}(\vec{p})=-\mathbf{\bm{x}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}_{\text{FO}}\mathbf{\bm{x}}
, and then construct weight matrices inversely weighted by these energies. Thus the implemented matrix is given by

\mathbf{A}=\int\frac{1}{|\hat{E}|\sqrt{\det G}}\>\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}^{\intercal}\mathrm{d}P

(28)

The superimposed heteroassociative matrices are similarly constructed with these weighting terms.

\mathbf{H}_{\nu}=\int\frac{1}{|\hat{E}|\sqrt{\det G}}\Big(\big(\mathbf{J}\vec{v}_{\nu}\cdot\nabla_{g}\big)\mathbf{\bm{x}}\Big)(\bar{\mathbf{\bm{x}}}\circ\mathbf{\bm{s}}_{\nu})^{\intercal}\mathrm{d}P

(29)

where
J_{ij}=\partial g_{i}/\partial p_{j}
is a Jacobian, and the gradients of the position states
\nabla_{g}\>\mathbf{\bm{x}}
are with respect to
\vec{g}
, such that the vector fields
\vec{v}_{\nu}(\vec{p}):\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
are defined in terms of the original position coordinates
\vec{p}
.

IV-I

Comparison models

Both comparison models from
Zhang 1996g
and
Kilpatrick & Ermentrout 2013g
have field equations that are continuous in space, but which we discretise into an equal number of positions
i=1\ldots N
as neurons used in our network. Their dynamics are given by

\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\bm{u}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathbf{\bm{u}}+\mathbf{W}f(\mathbf{\bm{u}})

(30)

where
\mathbf{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a vector of the the total synaptic currents to each neuron,
\tau>0
is a time constant,
\mathbf{W}
are the recurrent weights, and
f(\cdot)
is an element-wise current-to-firing-rate function. In the Zhang model,
f
is a sigmoid function, and the weights are determined by calculating the convolution on
f(\mathbf{\bm{u}})
necessary to support stable bumps of predetermined shape
Zhang 1996g
. In the Kilpatrick & Ermentrout model,
f(\mathbf{\bm{u}})=H(\mathbf{\bm{u}}-\theta_{\text{kp}})
