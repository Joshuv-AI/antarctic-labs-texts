A Multicentric Clinical Study to Evaluate Pharmacokinetics, Efficacy, and Safety of Immune Globulin Subcutaneous 20% Weekly/Biweekly Dosing in Treatment-Experienced Patients and Loading/Weekly Maintenance Dosing in Treatment-Naïve Patients with Primary Immunodeficiency
Authors: William R Lumry, Michael Palumbo, Connie Hsu, Iftikhar Hussain, Donald McNeil, Tracy Bridges, H James Wedner, Mark Scarupa, Elsa Mondou, Nisha Nanaware-Kharade, Kim Hanna, Montse Querolt Coll, Juan Oliveras
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy is a well-documented and effective treatment for primary immunodeficiencies (PI). Subcutaneous immunoglobulins (SCIG) have emerged as an effective alternative for some patients that offers additional flexibility.Currently, caprylate/chromatography purified IGSC (human) 20% is approved to treat PI in North America and many countries worldwide. In this multi-center, open-label study, the pharmacokinetics (PK), efficacy, and safety of IGSC 20% were studied after biweekly (every two weeks) dosing for 16 weeks and compared to weekly IGSC 20% administration for 16 weeks in treatment-experienced PI patients. A loading/weekly maintenance SC regimen was also studied in PI patients naïve to IG treatment.In treatment-experienced patients, the least squares mean ratio of steady-state AUC0−7 days for biweekly dosing showed non-inferiority to weekly dosing (90% confidence interval 1.0030–1.0685). Other PK parameters (Cmax, Tmax, steady state trough) were similar between treatments. Efficacy and safety measures were qualitatively similar to previously published results. No serious bacterial infections were reported. Annual rates for any type of infection were similar between the treatment groups. All severe treatment-related adverse events (AEs) were unrelated to treatment. Only mild to moderate suspected adverse drug reactions were observed.PK analyses showed that biweekly dosing of IGSC 20% was non-inferior to weekly dosing in treatment-experienced PI patients. Biweekly dosing had similar patterns to weekly in prevention of serious bacterial infections, rates of all infections and hospitalizations for infections. The IGSC loading/maintenance dosing regimen in naïve patients was safe and effective during the observation period.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10875-025-01952-5.Keywords: Primary immunodeficiencies, Immunoglobulin replacement therapy, Bacterial infection prevention, Hypogammaglobulinemia, Subcutaneous immunoglobulin

Introduction

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) infusion is routinely used to treat patients with defective antibody production related to primary immunodeficiencies (PI) in order to prevent infections [1–4]. Intravenous (IV) administration of immunoglobulin (IG) was the standard route for many years. Descriptions of an alternative route, subcutaneous (SC) administration, were originally published in the 1980 s [5–8], and after many years of use primarily in Scandinavia [9, 10], a subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIG) product was approved in the US in 2006 [10, 11]. Since that time the use of SCIG has expanded significantly and there are currently seven SCIG products approved in the US [12].

Selection of SCIG or IVIG should be guided by the patient’s clinical characteristics, patient preference, care location (hospital, outpatient, community office, or home-based), availability, and pharmacoeconomics [13, 14]. SCIG may benefit patients with poor venous access or at risk for systemic adverse events (AEs) [9, 15, 16]. While some countries offer training for home administration of IVIG, SCIG offers wider possibilities for home administration, which translates to greater ease of use, enhanced independence, and eventually improved quality of life [2, 3, 17].

SCIG has a long history as a safe and effective treatment of PI [18–24]. Well-controlled clinical trials on transitioning from IVIG to SCIG administration illustrate that SCIG preserved efficacy against severe bacterial infections (SBIs) [16, 25–30]. PI patients more prone to systemic adverse reactions with repeated IVIG infusions (e.g., headaches, fever, chills, and myalgia) may benefit from diminished systemic adverse reactions with SCIG [31, 32]. Moreover, SCIG can be appropriate in pediatric patients whose venous access is typically difficult, which can impede IVIG therapy [3, 33, 34].

Pharmacokinetic (PK) non-inferiority is a prerequisite for US regulatory approval of SCIG for PI. PK non-inferiority is based on the area under the concentration versus time curve (AUC) for total immunoglobulin G (IgG) which is achieved using a dose adjustment factor (DAF) for the transition from IV to SC. In the US, the initial approval of Xembify® (IGSC 20%) was based on bioequivalence with IVIG using a DAF of 1.37, with similar efficacy and tolerability in Phase 3 study GTI1502 [35].

Xembify (IGSC 20%) was licensed in the United States in 2019 for treatment of PI, when administered weekly or in divided doses at shorter intervals; however biweekly (every two weeks) administration was added to the US product label on the basis of the study reported herein which showed PK non-inferiority for total IgG AUC versus weekly dosing. IGSC 20% is also licensed in the European Union and other countries for PI and other indications.

Current US labeling for IGSC 20% also allows flexible dosing frequency and includes initial SC dosing of treatment-naïve patients using a loading dose (150 mg/kg/day for 5 days) followed by weekly maintenance (150 mg/kg/week).

The study reported herein evaluated the PK, efficacy, and safety of IGSC 20% with biweekly dosing for 16 weeks versus weekly IGSC 20% administration for 16 weeks in treatment-experienced PI patients. In addition, a loading/weekly maintenance SC regimen was evaluated in a cohort of PI patients naïve to IgG treatment.

Methods

Study Design

This was a multi-center, single-sequence, open-label study with two cohorts of PI patients: IgG treatment-experienced and IgG treatment-naïve. Treatment-experienced patients had been maintained on IgG replacement therapy for at least 3 months. In the study, they received IGSC 20% at two dosing frequencies (weekly/biweekly) during two treatment periods of 16 weeks each (Fig. 1).

Study schema for treatment-experienced patients. The lighter blue boxes indicate period 1 – weekly dosing – and the darker blue boxes indicate period 2 – biweekly dosing. IGSC = subcutaneous immunoglobulin; early term = early termination; W0 = week 0; QOL = quality of life; PK = pharmacokinetic

Treatment-naïve patients received a loading dose of five daily doses of IGSC 20% (150 mg/kg) followed by weekly infusions up to the end of treatment (week 32) (Fig. 2). This regimen was designed to allow treatment-naïve patients to rapidly establish steady state IgG levels that are considered protective [36].

Objectives

The primary PK objective was to determine whether biweekly administration of IGSC 20% produced systemic IgG exposure non-inferior to that produced by weekly administration of IGSC 20% in the treatment-experienced cohort. The measure of exposure was area under the curve (AUC)0−7days. Non-inferiority testing was performed based on established regulatory guidelines for bioequivalence testing. The test parameter (biweekly dosing) was considered to be non-inferior to the reference parameter (weekly dosing) if the lower 90% confidence interval of the geometric least squares mean (GLSM) ratio (test/reference) was above 0.80.

Secondary PK objectives included comparability of steady-state mean trough IgG levels between weekly versus biweekly IGSC 20% administration in treatment-experienced patients and evaluation of: maximum concentration (Cmax) and time to reach Cmax (Tmax) of total IgG at steady state. For the treatment-naïve cohort the aim was to evaluate if 150 mg/kg/day loading doses of IGSC 20% on 5 consecutive days followed by weekly infusions of 150 mg/kg/week through week 32 achieved and maintained total IgG trough > 500 mg/dL. These secondary PK parameters were reported as descriptive statistics.

Secondary efficacy objectives in all patients included incidence of infections, serious bacterial infections, use of antibiotics and hospitalizations due to infection. Safety objectives included assessing the safety and tolerability of the IGSC 20% regimens. These secondary efficacy parameters were reported as descriptive statistics.

Exploratory objectives were comparing treatment burden, treatment satisfaction, and quality of life (Life Quality Index (LQI), Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medication (TSQM-9) and 12-Item Short Form Survey (SF-12) between weekly versus biweekly IGSC 20% administration in the treatment-experienced cohort [37–41]. The LQI is a 15-item assessment that uses ratings on a seven-point scale (7 = extremely good and 1 = bad). Scores are evaluated on four factors: treatment interference, therapy-related problems, therapy setting and treatment costs. TSQM-9 is a nine-item questionnaire designed to evaluate patients’ satisfaction with a medication. It provides scores on three scales: effectiveness, convenience and global satisfaction. SF-12 is a health survey of 12 questions. Increased scores are indicative of better functioning in the areas of physical and mental health.

IGSC Dosage and Administration

The investigational product was caprylate/chromatography-purified human IGSC 20% (Xembify® Grifols, Research Triangle Park, NC, USA), administered using a SC infusion pump. IGSC 20% is manufactured on the same platform as Gamunex-C 10% but concentrated to 20%, facilitating SC infusion by reducing the volume required for a given dose [42].

IGSC 20% is a sterile liquid formulation containing 20% human immune globulin (primarily IgG) formulated in 0.16 to 0.26 M glycine and 10 to 40 µg/mL polysorbate 80 at pH of 4.1 to 4.8.

For treatment-experienced patients on SCIG at study entry, there was no DAF. The equivalent weekly (mg/kg) dose for prior SCIG was used for IGSC 20% weekly dosing in treatment period 1. For patients entering study on IVIG, the DAF used was 1:1.37. If the calculated IGSC 20% dose was < 100 mg/kg/week, the IGSC 20% dose was modified to a net dose of 100 mg/kg/week, the minimum recommended weekly IGSC 20% dose for this study. In treatment period 2, the biweekly dose of IGSC 20% (mg/kg) was twice the weekly dose in treatment period 1.

Treatment-naïve patients received a loading dose of IGSC 20% consisting of five daily doses of 150 mg/kg/day (week 0, days 1 to 5) followed by weekly maintenance infusions of 150 mg/kg/week during week 1 (day 8) through week 32. The weekly frequency was maintained through week 32 (end of treatment).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

A patient must meet the following inclusion criteria for study participation: (i) documented, confirmed, diagnosis of PI with hypogammaglobulinemia requiring IgG replacement therapy, including, the following humoral-based immunodeficiency syndromes (e.g., X-linked agammaglobulinemia, common variable immunodeficiency (CVID)), and combined immunodeficiency syndromes without lymphocytopenia (e.g., hyper immunoglobulin M immunodeficiency syndrome); (ii) no SBI or hospitalization for infection of any etiology within 3 months prior to screening (for treatment-experienced patients) or during screening (for treatment-experienced and treatment-naïve patients); and (iii) must provide written informed consent.

For treatment-experienced patients, additional inclusion criteria were: (i) age from 18 to 75 years at screening; (ii) receiving IgG replacement therapy for ≥ 3 months via IV or SC infusion (patients receiving IVIG must have received a dose ≥ 200 mg/kg per infusion); and (iii) screening IgG trough levels ≥ 500 mg/dL.

For the treatment-naïve cohort, additional inclusion criteria were: (i) age 6–75 years at screening; (ii) no prior IgG replacement therapy; and (iii) screening IgG level ≤ 400 mg/dL.

For all patients, exclusion criteria are summarized as follows: (i) disorders or conditions where SC therapy or blood-derived products were contraindicated; (ii) proteinuria, acute renal failure, severe renal impairment, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, aspartate or alanine aminotransferase more than 2.5 times the upper limit of normal; (iii) thromboembolism or deep venous thrombosis, non-controlled arterial hypertension, severe anemia; (iv) isolated IgG subclass deficiency, isolated specific antibody deficiency, selective IgG or immunoglobulin A (IgA) deficiency, transient hypogammaglobulinemia of infancy, or any medical condition known to cause secondary immune deficiency; (v) treatment with immunosuppressants or immunomodulators; (vi) females who were pregnant, breastfeeding or unwilling to use highly effective birth control; (vii) substance abuse; (viii) participation in another interventional clinical trial within 30 days prior to screening; (ix) patients that, in the opinion of the investigator, might have compliance problems with the protocol or have a condition that might interfere with successful trial completion.

Analytical Methods

Serum IgG content testing to confirm steady-state attainment prior to PK assessments and for steady-state IgG trough analysis was performed with the Roche Cobas analyzer and reagents (Roche Diagnostics International AG, Rotkreuz, Switzerland), which have an analytical measuring range of 300 to 3900 mg/dL. The Siemens BNII immunonephelometer and reagents (Siemans Healthineers USA, Malvern, PA, USA) were used for measuring IgG content to support PK analysis and PK parameters (e.g. AUC) for primary endpoint analysis. This methodology has an analytical measuring range of 508 to 4416 mg/dL. For patient management (e.g., treatment-naïve patients) the reportable range for IgG for the central laboratory was down to a minimum of < 40 mg/dL.

Results

Patient Characteristics and Disposition

Of the 37 patients screened (29 treatment-experienced; 8 treatment-naïve), 33 patients (27 treatment-experienced; 6 treatment-naïve) were enrolled (treated) in the study. Patients were screened at 14 study sites. Four patients were screening failures (3 failed to meet the inclusion/exclusion criteria; one patient withdrew consent).

A total of 29 patients completed the study (24 treatment-experienced; 5 treatment-naïve). Two patients prematurely discontinued due to AEs. Both patients were treatment-experienced. One withdrew due to dyspnea with headache (mild) and one due to infusion site pain (moderate). Two other patients withdrew from participation in the study (not due to AEs). The GLSM ratio estimate (period 2/period 1) was near unity (1.035).

For the secondary PK endpoints, mean steady-state Cmax values following weekly period 1 (n = 25) and biweekly period 2 (n = 23) IGSC 20% SC infusions were similar (1076.2 vs. 1151.3 mg/dL, respectively) (Table 2). Mean Tmax values were variable and higher after biweekly dosing than weekly dosing, but median Tmax values were similar at 93.7 and 96.2 h after the start of infusion, respectively. The variability in Tmax is unlikely to be of any clinical relevance because the variation in total IgG concentration from Cmax to trough was approximately 1.1-fold.

Overall, for treatment-experienced patients, the IgG trough data indicated that mean total IgG concentrations were stable over time following weekly (994.3 mg/dL; n = 23) or biweekly (978.8 mg/dL; n = 22) IGSC 20% SC administration. The mean steady-state trough IgG concentrations in serum just prior to the PK dosing (week 14 for weekly; week 30 for biweekly dosing) were similar: 953.0 mg/dL versus 952.7 mg/dL respectively (Fig. 4). Formal statistical analysis results for steady-state mean trough concentrations of IgG indicate that the mean trough for biweekly period 2 (n = 22) was non-inferior to weekly period 1 (n = 23), such that the lower boundary of GLSM 90% CI (0.9447, 0.9957) was > 0.8 (0.9447). The GLSM ratio estimate (Period 2/Period 1) was near unity (0.970).

At week 0 (baseline) the IgG concentration was 224.5 ± 121.26 mg/dL (mean ± SD) among the 6 treatment-naïve patients (range: ≤40 [lower limit of quantitation] to 370 mg/dL). Following successful completion of 5 consecutive days of loading doses, all treatment-naïve patients attained an IgG trough level > 700 mg/dL at week 1, except for one patient who entered study with an IgG level that was markedly reduced (< 40 mg/dL). Three of the six treatment-naïve subjects required IGSC 20% maintenance dose increases in order to maintain IgG trough levels > 700 mg/dL which was the pre-defined protocol-specified target for dose modification. Increases were in the range of 170 to 180 mg/kg/week. By week 8, all 6 patients had IgG trough values > 700 mg/dL, including the patient with a low initial IgG level (week 8 level = 804 mg/dL). All subjects including those maintained at 150 mg/kg/week (n = 3) achieved and maintained IgG trough levels > 700 mg/dL through end of study week 32.

Efficacy Endpoints

No SBIs were reported during the study. Within the treatment-experienced group, patients receiving biweekly dosing had a mean annualized infection rate of 1.95 ± 4.28 with a rate per person per year of 1.995 (95% CI: 1.033, 3.858), which were numerically similar with weekly dosing 2.19 ± 4.64 and 2.299, [95% CI: 1.203, 4.389], respectively). In terms of validated infections, the rate per person per year was 0.51 (95% CI: 0.21, 1.24) for weekly dosing and 0.59 (95% CI: 0.21, 1.62) for biweekly dosing among treatment-experienced patients. In treatment-naïve patients, the rate of validated infections per person per year was 0.28 (95% CI: 0.05, 1.43). Overall, validated infections included cellulitis, cystitis, parainfluenza, viral pneumonia, rhinovirus, urinary tract infection, gastroenteritis associated with Clostridium difficile and Campylobacter, and COVID-19. The majority of TEAEs were mild or moderate. There were three and five severe TEAEs during weekly and biweekly dosing periods, respectively, and one severe TEAE in the treatment-naïve cohort; all were unrelated to study treatment. Severe TEAEs included abdominal discomfort, Barrett’s esophagus, skin laceration each occurring in one treatment-experienced patient during weekly dosing, and two treatment-experienced patients had five severe TEAEs during biweekly dosing: syncope, acute pancreatitis, dehydration (two instances), acute kidney injury (all considered not related). In the treatment-naïve group, one patient had a severe, unrelated upper limb fracture. Two treatment-experienced patients, one receiving weekly dosing and one receiving biweekly dosing, discontinued due to TEAEs (mild dyspnea with headache possibly related to treatment, and moderate infusion site pain, respectively). TEAEs results are summarized in Table 5.

The percentage of patients who had a suspected adverse drug reaction (ADR) was lower in treatment-experienced patients during biweekly infusions (4.0% [1/25]) compared to weekly infusions (18.5% [5/27]). None out of the 22 suspected ADRs was considered severe. Only one treatment-experienced patient receiving weekly dosing had two mild adverse reactions (infusion site pain and infusion site pruritus). These results are summarized in Table 5.

Overall, there were 281 injection site reactions (ISRs) in 20/33 (60.6%) patients, nine of them in 5/33 (15.2%) patients were reported as TEAEs, and all were considered mild or moderate in severity. Distribution among treatment groups is shown in Table 5. The majority of ISRs included infusion site pruritis, erythema, urticaria, pain, edema, and inflammation.

The decrease in ISRs after repeated SC infusions was confirmed by the analysis of rates of all ISRs per infusion. The rate of all ISRs per infusion was 0.38 among treatment-experienced subjects receiving weekly infusions (n = 403 weekly infusions) and 0.35 per infusion among treatment-experienced subjects receiving biweekly infusions (n = 220 biweekly infusions). For treatment-naïve subjects the rate of all ISRs per infusion was 0.24 across 211 infusions.

A total of 4/33 (12.1%) patients reported treatment-emergent serious adverse events (SAEs), and one treatment-experienced patient had two SAEs that required hospitalization for three infections that occurred once during weekly infusions and once during biweekly infusions (Table 5). The treatment-emergent SAEs were dehydration, acute pancreatitis, worsening of Barrett’s esophagus, and a compression fracture with back pain. The patient hospitalized due to infections had viral pneumonia during the weekly dosing period and Clostridium difficile and cellulitis during the biweekly dosing period.

Overall, values for hematology, clinical chemistry, urinalysis, serum free hemoglobin and haptoglobin, and direct antiglobulin remained within normal limits with no remarkable shifts observed in mean change from baseline values. Minimal changes to vital signs occurred during the study.

Exploratory Endpoints

All LQI scales demonstrated the treatment satisfaction average score increased from baseline to end of treatment (increased score = improvement). There were slight decreases seen in therapy-related problems and therapy setting scores related to the change from weekly to biweekly dosing.

All TQSM-9 domains evaluated demonstrated increases from baseline to end of treatment (increased score = improvement). The highest scores were seen in the domain of convenience with an increase from baseline to week 16 of 6.89 ± 15.49. At the end of treatment (week 32), the score was 81.56 ± 17.10, demonstrating an increase of 11.56 ± 15.87 from baseline and an increase from week 16 of 4.67 ± 15.36.

For the SF-12, sub-score evaluations increased from baseline to end of treatment in the treatment-experienced and treatment-naïve groups, with higher scores indicating better physical and mental health function.

Discussion

Dosing SCIG multiple times per week or at longer intervals, such as biweekly, allows PI patients to have multiple dosing options providing them with convenience and flexibility in managing their immunodeficiencies. This study assessed non-inferiority of IGSC 20% dosed biweekly versus weekly.

Analysis of the primary PK endpoint indicated that biweekly IGSC 20% produced a steady-state total IgG AUC0−7 noninferior to that of weekly administration in treatment-experienced PI patients. The results of this study substantiate PK modeling and simulation that predicted non-inferiority of IGSC 20% biweekly dosing to weekly dosing [43]. This modeling was based on PK studies with SC administration of IVIG 10% and IGSC 20% [26, 35, 44]. Similar findings were reported for IgPro20 (Hizentra®, CSL Behring, King of Prussia, Pennsylvania) where PK modeling of weekly and biweekly SC dosing (equivalent doses) predicted equivalent exposures for both regimens [45, 46] which was later verified by clinical data in patients with PI [47–50].

Since the primary endpoint of this study was to compare steady-state AUC, treatment-experienced patients for weekly versus biweekly IGSC 20% dosing among PI patients to allow extended dosing beyond weekly in the US product labeling, participants entering study on commercial IVIG were converted to SCIG using a DAF of 1:1.37 in accordance with FDA labeling. As shown the original pivotal Phase 3 study GTI1502, a DAF of 1:1.37 provides bioequivalent AUC for IGSC 20% versus IVIG administration [35] and the purpose of this study in treatment-experienced patients was to demonstrate non-inferiority of biweekly dosing to weekly dosing, using a 1.37 DAF for patients entering study on commercial IVIG. Those entering this study on commercial SCIG required no DAF. The PK modeling and simulation paper by Navarro-Mora et al. [43] based on previous pharmacokinetic studies [26, 35, 44] predicted non-inferiority of biweekly dosing to weekly dosing (of same total IGSC 20% dose), and also the adequacy of switching directly from IVIG dosing to biweekly IGSC 20% dosing with a 1.37 DAF in terms of both AUC and IgG trough [43].

IgG trough levels are typically higher with the use of SCIG than IVIG even in the absence of a DAF, given the difference in AUC profile wherein SCIG is characterized by higher troughs and fewer fluctuations in IgG level than those characteristic of IVIG dosing. As such, studies [51–53] without a DAF (1:1 dosing) resulted in IgG trough levels on the SCIG regimen higher than the previous IVIG regimens with a range of increase on SCIG regimen of 4% to 22% when compared to previous IV regimen and without loss of efficacy in preventing SBIs (range from 0.0 to 0.09 annualized SBI rate).

Differences between mean steady-state Cmax and Tmax values for weekly and biweekly dosing are unlikely to be of clinical relevance. The variation in total IgG concentration from Cmax to trough was approximately 1.1-fold and variability in Tmax and Cmax is not unexpected when the difference between maximum and minimum concentrations within dosing intervals is small [47].

Biweekly IGSC 20% maintained steady-state mean trough IgG levels comparable to levels obtained with weekly IGSC 20% in agreement with other studies [47–50, 54–56]. Weekly and biweekly infusion dosing of IGSC 20% achieved comparable steady-state concentrations. These IgG trough values represent substantial humoral protection as high IgG trough levels ameliorate infection rates [29, 57].

In treatment-naïve patients with PI, a loading dose of IGSC 20% (5 daily doses of 150 mg/kg/day) followed by weekly infusions of 150 mg/kg/week achieved and maintained total IgG trough levels >700 mg/dL with appropriate maintenance infusion dose adjustment if indicated based on IgG trough levels. Individualization of the maintenance dose may be needed in some PI patients, so monitoring of IgG trough levels frequently, every 2 weeks during the first 8 weeks of treatment, is recommended. Very low pretreatment serum IgG can occur and was observed in one of the patients in this study. Total IgG concentration above 500 mg/dL is the commonly accepted protective trough concentration [58, 59] although, as referenced above, higher IgG trough values are beneficial [29, 57]. The target trough in this study was 700 mg/dL. The trough data indicated that total IgG concentrations were stable during the treatment phase for treatment-naïve patients and comparable to treatment-experienced patients once a maintenance dosing regimen was defined with adjustment based on IgG trough levels.

Notably, there were no SBIs reported during the study. Although the number of patients was small and treatment times in the current study were relatively short, the infection rates were comparable to those reported in the prior pivotal IGSC 20% studies in treatment-experienced patients with PI [35, 60]. In treatment-experienced patients, the rate of treatment-emergent validated infections, the rate of hospitalizations due to treatment-emergent infections, and days of prophylactic antibiotic usage during weekly dosing and during biweekly dosing were similar. No patients in the treatment-naïve group required hospitalization for infection. In general, emergency department and hospital visits are rare in SCIG safety studies [56, 61, 62].

Among treatment-experienced patients, antibiotic usage did not increase with the change in dosing frequency. Days of prophylactic and therapeutic antibiotic usage were not greater with the biweekly dosing interval. The rate of days of therapeutic antibiotic usage was lower with biweekly dosing, which while supportive of the adequacy of this regimen is more likely due to variability and not clinically relevant.

The majority of TEAEs and all ADRs were mild or moderate. None of the severe TEAEs were considered related to study treatment. There were no severe SAEs or TEAEs that led to premature discontinuation of the study drug or study participation. The rate of ISRs per infusion in patients receiving weekly and biweekly dosing was similar. The decrease in ISRs after repeated SC infusions was confirmed by the analysis of rates of all ISRs per infusion. All ISRs were considered mild or moderate in severity, although one treatment-experienced patient discontinued due to moderate infusion site pain that was considered unrelated to treatment. Only four patients reported treatment-emergent SAEs. One treatment-experienced patient had infections that required hospitalization: one infection that occurred during weekly infusions and two during biweekly infusions. All non-infection SAEs were considered unrelated to treatment. No patients discontinued because of a SAE, and all patients recovered.

Increased satisfaction associated with SCIG treatment is commonly reported [17, 37, 49, 63]. In this study, all LQI scales, all TQSM-9 domains, and SF-12 subscores evaluated demonstrated increased treatment satisfaction and better physical and mental health from baseline to end of treatment. The highest scores were seen in the domain of convenience.

The availability of SC and IV formulations of IG provides greater flexibility for patients and physicians in terms of administration at home or in the office, dose, frequency and site(s) of administration. When both IV and SC forms are available from the same manufacturer, route of administration can be changed while the production process remains the same avoiding major changes in product composition apart from increased IG concentration in the 20% SCIG formulation.

The comorbidities and concomitant medications in this study population are generally consistent with published literature. Asthma was the most common comorbidity in this study.

Caveats regarding the interpretation of these study results include the small number of patients in the treatment-naïve group and the duration of treatment in the treatment-experienced group that was tailored for PK analysis. This study was not designed or powered as an efficacy trial. While the duration of treatment and patient numbers were fitting for the pharmacokinetics measures that were the focus of this study, efficacy measures are only qualitative and descriptive and should not be considered conclusive. Previous publications have more fully characterized the efficacy of this 20% IGSC [35, 60].

Overall, administration of weekly and biweekly IGSC 20% in treatment-experienced patients and loading and weekly maintenance infusions in treatment-naïve patients had safety profiles consistent with previous studies. The primary PK endpoint indicated that biweekly administration of IGSC 20% produced a steady-state AUC of total IgG that was non-inferior to that produced by weekly administration in treatment-experienced patients with PI. IGSC 20% showed qualitatively similar efficacy to previous studies. In addition, measures of life quality increased indicating better physical and mental health function.
