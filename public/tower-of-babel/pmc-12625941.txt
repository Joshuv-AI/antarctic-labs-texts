Use of the National Health Insurance among beneficiaries in Maluku province, Indonesia: a cross-sectional analysis of the 2021 social health insurance sample data
Authors: Christiana Rialine Titaley, Iwan Ariawan, Wiji Wahyuningsih, Ressita Fannia Iwan, Maxwell Landri Vers Malakauseya, Virgylony Diana Kimberly, Yudhie Tando
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study examined factors associated with the use of Indonesia’s National Health Insurance (NHI) among its beneficiaries in Maluku province.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingThe individual-level data were derived from the 2021 Social Health Insurance Sample Data. National health reports of Indonesia were used to provide the district-level variables. We performed multivariable logistic regression analyses to identify factors associated with NHI use among its beneficiaries in Maluku province.ParticipantData were collected from 31 517 NHI beneficiaries registered in Maluku.Primary outcomeThe primary outcome was the use of NHI insurance when accessing healthcare services (yes/no).ResultsOnly 14.79% of NHI beneficiaries in Maluku had ever used their insurance for healthcare services. Individual factors associated with higher NHI use included being under 60 years, females (adjusted OR (aOR)=1.29, p<0.001), married (aOR=1.83, p<0.001) and having non-subsidised insurance (aOR=1.12, p<0.001). At the district level, higher usage was associated with increased rates of joint diseases (aOR=1.19, p<0.001), hypertension (aOR=1.81, p<0.001), diarrhoea (aOR=1.24, p<0.001) and a higher doctor-to-population ratio (aOR=1.01, p<0.001). A reduced likelihood of NHI use was found in beneficiaries living in the districts with high rates of acute respiratory infections (aOR=0.86, p<0.001) and with a high fiscal capacity (aOR=0.14, p<0.001).ConclusionStrengthening primary healthcare accessibility, improving the distribution of healthcare workers and addressing socioeconomic and geographical disparities are essential to ensure that the NHI scheme achieves equitable use across all regions in Maluku and other areas with similar settings.Keywords: Social Support; Health Care Costs; Health Services; Delivery of Health Care, Integrated; Health Services Accessibility

Background

A universal health insurance system is essential for improving healthcare access and reducing social disparities.1 Indonesia’s National Health Insurance (NHI), known as Jaminan Kesehatan Nasional, was launched in 2014 to provide comprehensive coverage for all citizens.2 This insurance is expected to expand healthcare infrastructure and allocate medical resources to ensure that even remote communities have access to essential healthcare.3

In Indonesia, NHI is administered by the Social Security Agency for Health (Badan Penyelenggara Jaminan Sosial Kesehatan or BPJS Kesehatan)4 and was designed to achieve universal coverage by 2019.5 However, this goal remains unrealised,6 highlighting the need for continued efforts to ensure nationwide enrolment. Indonesia’s health system operates under a decentralised structure, in which provincial and district governments share responsibility for managing and delivering healthcare services. The NHI scheme serves as the primary mechanism for financing healthcare under the National Social Security System (Sistem Jaminan Sosial Nasional), providing coverage for a wide range of services across public and private facilities contracted by BPJS Kesehatan.

Despite its broad coverage, challenges remain in ensuring equitable access across Indonesia’s geographically diverse regions, particularly in remote and island provinces where health facility density and provider availability are limited. Previous studies have reported low NHI use among its beneficiaries in Indonesia, particularly in remote and geographically challenged areas.4 Several factors have been determined to influence the use of health insurance in general, such as income, education, employment, healthcare availability and the quality of healthcare services.7

One of the provinces in Indonesia that continues to experience persistent disparities in access to comprehensive healthcare is Maluku province.8 Maluku is one of the largest archipelagic regions in Indonesia, comprising approximately 1340 islands.9 The province faces significant challenges related to infrastructure and healthcare access, particularly in remote and underserved areas.7 9 With widely dispersed islands, Maluku encounters substantial barriers to healthcare delivery.9 Thereby, understanding the determinants of NHI use is essential for identifying and addressing the unique challenges related to NHI use that are experienced by the communities in this province, which could be different from the challenges faced by other areas, particularly urban or more developed ones in the country.10

In 2019, the Social Security Agency for Health launched the Social Health Insurance Sample Data (SHISD), which offers researchers and policymakers valuable insights into nationwide healthcare utilisation and access patterns.11 Therefore, using these data, we examined the determinants of NHI use among its beneficiaries in Maluku province, one of the largest archipelagic provinces of Indonesia.

Methods

Data source and study design

The individual-level data used in this analysis were derived from the 2021 SHISD, which were randomly sampled by the Social Security Agency for Health.12 The sampling frame listed 4 576 698 families who enrolled as NHI members at 21 637 primary healthcare (PHC) facilities, including both public and private providers. The sample selection process by the Social Security Agency for Health was based on stratified sampling and used two key variables: the PHC provider category and the family category. The family category was divided into three distinct groups: (1) families with members who had never used any healthcare services; (2) families with members who had received healthcare services at primary care; and (3) families with members who had received healthcare services at referral care. Within each category, a minimum of 10 families were randomly sampled. If selected, all individuals in the family were included in the dataset. Finally, a sample of 882 514 families was selected, comprising 2 305 435 individuals.13

The SHISD consists of four relational tables: membership, capitation-based primary care services, non-capitation-based primary care services and referral care services. The membership table contains the necessary variables, including an identifier for individuals and families, details of demographic and membership characteristics and a weight variable for each record. In this dataset, the weight variable represents a certain number of the members of Social Security Agency for Health. The remaining tables contain details of visits, health facility characteristics and individual identifiers. This anonymised dataset, as well as the book code, is available from the Social Security Agency for Health on reasonable request.13

In this analysis, we included only beneficiaries from Maluku province to examine their use of the NHI when accessing healthcare services. In total, information from 31 517 registered NHI beneficiaries was included. In addition to individual characteristics, variables reflecting the condition of the district were also incorporated. Variables

The dependent variable was the use of NHI insurance when accessing healthcare services (yes/no). This variable was derived from the 2021 SHISD and classified as a binary outcome. Beneficiaries who had ever used their NHI to access healthcare services at least once during the observation period (January to December 2021) were coded as ‘1’ (used NHI), whereas those who did not use it were coded as ‘0” (not used).

The independent variables consisted of individual-level and district-level variables. The selection of variables at both individual and district levels was informed by Andersen’s Behavioural Model of Health Services Use, which conceptualises healthcare use as influenced by predisposing, enabling and need-related factors.14 The individual-level variables were: (1) beneficiaries’ age (<30/31–60/>60 years), (2) sex (male/female), (3) marital status (unmarried/married or divorced) and (4) type of NHI membership (subsidised/non-subsidised). In this analysis, subsidised members referred to beneficiaries whose NHI premiums were fully covered by the government, primarily targeting low-income households, while the non-subsidised members were the self-financed participants, or those whose premiums were paid by employers.

The district-level variables were: (1) Geographical Difficulty Index; (2) prevalence of acute respiratory infections, (3) joint diseases, (4) hypertension, and (5) diarrhoea, (6) the percentage of community members with good economic status, (7) district’s fiscal capacity, (8) community health centre-population ratio and (9) medical doctor-population ratio.

The variable of Geographical Difficulty Index15 was derived from Statistics Indonesia. This variable was constructed based on three variables: (1) the availability of basic services, such as education facilities and health facilities; (2) infrastructure conditions, including economic facilities, cooking fuel and the availability of electricity and lighting on the main village road; and (3) access to transportation.7 16 The prevalence of acute respiratory infections, joint diseases, hypertension and diarrhoea at the district level was derived from the 2018 Basic Health Research data,17 according to diagnoses by healthcare workers. The variable of the proportion of community members with good economic status18 was defined as the percentage of the population who did not benefit from any food assistance programmes (non-cash food assistance) or Family Hope Programme (Programme Keluarga Harapan) provided to poor households in Indonesia. The variable of the fiscal capacity of the district19 was defined as a government’s ability to generate revenue through taxation and other financial means to fund public services and meet its expenditure needs.18 This indicator reflects a government’s efficiency in mobilising resources, the scope of its tax base and its capability to maintain a sustainable budget that supports economic growth and social welfare programmes. For the ratio of community health centre-population and medical doctor-population, the results were presented as the numbers as the number of population served by one community health centre and medical doctor, respectively.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were first used to summarise the distribution of all variables included in the analysis. This was followed by bivariate analyses to examine differences in variable distributions according to NHI use status. To determine the independent associations between explanatory variables and NHI use, a multilevel multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted using two sequential models with random intercepts to account for the hierarchical structure of the data—individuals (Level 1) nested within districts (Level 2).

We developed Model 1 to include only individual-level variables to assess the extent to which district-level variation influenced NHI non-use, while adjusting for individual characteristics. The median OR (MOR) was calculated to quantify district-level variance. In Model 2, all district-level explanatory variables were added, allowing for assessment of the variation in NHI non-use that could be explained by district-level factors. The MOR was recalculated to compare with that of Model 1 to assess the extent to which the inclusion of district-level variables reduced the unexplained variation between districts.

The final model reported adjusted ORs (aORs) and 95% CIs for all predictors at the individual and district level. All analyses accounted for the survey’s complex sampling design and were conducted using the melogit command in Stata/MP V.14.2 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Patients and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Ethics clearance

This study used secondary data from the 2021 SHISD, which were anonymised and made publicly available by the Social Security Agency for Health. Ethical approval was exempted by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Pattimura (Approval No. 165/FK-KOM.ETIK/VIII/2024).

Results

Among the 31 517 NHI beneficiaries registered in Maluku province included in this analysis, only 14.79% (95% CI 14.40% to 15.18%) had ever used NHI (figure 1). This percentage was lower than the national average of 17.66%.12 The percentage of NHI use varied across districts within the province, ranging from 4.71% (95% CI 4.04% to 5.48%) in the Aru Islands to 28.09% (95% CI 26.07% to 30.19%) in Buru Island. The five districts with the lowest NHI use were: Kepulauan Aru, Maluku Barat Daya, Seram Bagian Barat, Seram Bagian Timur and Maluku Tengah (figures1 2).

At the district level, the odds of NHI use increased with an increased prevalence of joint diseases (aOR=1.19, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.30, p<0.001), hypertension (aOR=1.81, 95% CI 1.60 to 2.05, p<0.001) and diarrhoea (aOR=1.24, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.34, p<0.001) in the district. It is interesting to note that an increased odds of using NHI were associated with an increased number of population served per medical doctor (reduced medical doctor-population ratio) (aOR=1.01, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.01, p<0.001). Furthermore, our findings show that the odds of using NHI reduced with an increased prevalence of acute respiratory infections in the district (aOR=0.86, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.89, p<0.001) and increased district’s fiscal capacity (aOR=0.14, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.24, p<0.001).

Our analysis shows that in addition to the individual-level factors, the district-level factors also had a significant association with NHI use among its beneficiaries in Maluku province. In Model 1 (table 4), which included only individual-level characteristics, the MOR was 1.57. However, when district-level characteristics were added in Model 2, the MOR decreased to 1.09, indicating a reduced variation in NHI use attributable to district-level factors.

Discussion

Main findings

Our analysis found that the use of NHI among its beneficiaries in Maluku province remains below the national average. Both individual-level and district-level factors were associated with the low use of NHI. At the individual level, NHI use was more likely among beneficiaries aged 60 years or below, females and married individuals. Non-subsidised members also had a higher likelihood of using NHI than subsidised ones. At the district level, NHI use increased with higher prevalence of joint diseases, hypertension and diarrhoea. The NHI use was associated with a low medical doctor-to-population ratio, suggesting increased demand despite limited provider availability. Conversely, NHI use decreased with higher prevalence of acute respiratory infections and increased district fiscal capacity. These findings offer valuable insights for designing context-specific strategies to enhance health insurance uptake in eastern Indonesia.

Factors associated with NIH use in Maluku

At the individual level, we found that NHI beneficiaries aged 60 years or below in Maluku province were more likely to use NHI for healthcare services. This is consistent with findings from previous studies.10 20 This evidence is unfortunate, as older adults—who face higher risks of chronic health conditions requiring ongoing care—would significantly benefit from regular NHI use. Chronic diseases, which are common among individuals over 60 years of age, often require frequent medical visits, ongoing monitoring and even long-term treatments. Therefore, not using NHI indicates a missed opportunity in this vulnerable group.

Studies have shown that the low use of NHI among beneficiaries aged 60 years or above could arise from various factors.21 Accessibility issues, with healthcare facilities located far from their homes, might lead to increased transportation costs, particularly for those with mobility issues.22 Furthermore, limited awareness of NHI benefits, combined with complex administrative processes, might discourage them from using the insurance.23 Cultural preferences for traditional medicine and financial burdens—such as transportation and non-covered medical costs—could also discourage NHI use. Physical or cognitive limitations, as well as inadequate social support for using NHI when accessing healthcare services, were also reported as part of the contributing factors.24 25

Being female was significantly associated with higher NHI use in Maluku province, as reported in previous studies.10 26 Women were more proactive in seeking healthcare than men, likely due to their central roles in family’s health and caregiving.21 26 Their engagement with healthcare systems could increase their awareness of NHI benefits. Men, in contrast, might face barriers such as work obligations, social expectations and the belief that health issues should only be addressed when they were severe.27 28 This shows that workplace health programmes, community awareness campaigns challenging traditional masculinity norms and male-friendly clinical hours should be considered to encourage greater healthcare engagement among men.8 26

Our study confirmed the increased likelihood of NHI use among the non-subsidised type of beneficiaries.29 30 Individuals in this category paid their own premiums (non-subsidised) and could thus be more motivated to maximise the value of their contributions. This NHI type also reflects a high socioeconomic status and health literacy, facilitating improved access to medical services.1 31 In contrast, subsidised NHI holders could experience different challenges, such as limited resources, fewer service options and unfamiliarity with insurance processes. Policy improvements, including expanded service options and increased awareness efforts, could help to enhance equity for subsidised NHI users.

Our analysis showed different rates of NHI use across districts in Maluku province. These could be attributed to environmental/district-level characteristics, in addition to individual-level factors. At the district level, the prevalence of joint diseases, hypertension and diarrhoea in the district was associated with the increased use of NHI, as reported in previous research.4 32 Chronic conditions, such as joint diseases and hypertension, require regular management, which might lead to reliance on NHI for care.

However, we found that NHI use decreased as the prevalence of acute respiratory infections increased. This suggested that individuals with acute respiratory infections—often viewed as self-limiting—might prefer home or traditional remedies over NHI-covered services,33,35 given the access constraints in Maluku’s remote islands. This indicates the necessity of raising community awareness about the importance of seeking professional care for conditions often seen as self-limiting. Efforts to strengthen primary care infrastructure are vital for encouraging NHI use for both chronic and acute conditions, ensuring timely intervention and support.

The likelihood of NHI use was also associated with a higher medical doctor-population ratio, confirming the need to increase access to healthcare providers and promote awareness of NHI-related services.36 Strategic efforts to deploy more healthcare providers, incentivise rural postings, build the capacity of existing healthcare workers and expand telemedicine will help to address the geographical challenges of Maluku province.

Moreover, economic capability at the district level emerged as a significant predictor of NHI use.37 38 Enhanced fiscal capacity generally facilitates strong health programmes that will help improve community access to services. Districts with greater fiscal resources often had the capability to support healthcare infrastructure and provide comprehensive services supporting NHI use.39,41 Therefore, targeted central or provincial government funding for healthcare infrastructure and outreach to districts with limited fiscal capacity should be considered to improve healthcare services and facilities serving the community.

Overall, our analysis revealed low use of NHI among beneficiaries in Maluku province. Among the factors examined, three emerged as the most policy-relevant: socioeconomic status, healthcare workforce availability and geographical accessibility. Non-subsidised members showed higher NHI use, highlighting the need for stronger outreach to low-income groups. Our findings also highlighted that workforce shortages and their uneven distribution limited health service use in remote settings. Strengthening island-cluster referral systems and improving healthcare deployment across islands are thereby essential to achieve equitable universal health coverage (UHC) in Maluku province.

Compared with the condition in the western Indonesia region, such as Java and Sumatra, where healthcare facilities and transportation networks are more developed, the eastern islands, including Maluku province, had lower levels of NHI use.42 Those in more developed provinces might access NHI-covered services more frequently due to higher health facility density, greater provider availability or shorter travel distances.43 This sheds light on the need for geographically adaptive approaches to strengthen healthcare access and equity across Indonesia’s archipelagic regions. The gugus pulau44 (island cluster) health system—grouping neighbouring islands into healthcare zones with a central referral facility—should be optimised to reduce access barriers and extend service reach, especially for older adults. Additionally, continued health promotion efforts by NHI representatives will improve awareness of benefits, while educating family caregivers on NHI procedures might enhance support for older adults and improve access to care across Maluku’s dispersed communities.9

This study highlights how Indonesia’s NHI operates as a key mechanism for advancing UHC in geographically dispersed, island-based contexts such as Maluku province. The low use among the NHI beneficiaries reflects persistent barriers to equitable access, including geographical isolation, workforce shortages and socioeconomic disparities. Such challenges have been reported in other archipelagic countries such as the Philippines, where fragmented geography and uneven healthcare resource distribution similarly hinder the realisation of UHC goals.45 Lessons from these comparable settings emphasise the need to strengthen island-based health networks, improve inter-island referral systems and ensure equitable deployment of healthcare providers to bridge service gaps across remote maritime regions.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides valuable insights into NHI use in Maluku province, using secondary data to assess a large, diverse sample across this vast archipelagic region in Indonesia. Secondary data from the SHISD enabled a comprehensive analysis without the time and resource demands of primary data collection. Furthermore, the standardised nature of these data allowed for consistent, comparable results across demographic characteristics and district-level factors, offering a clear picture of healthcare utilisation trends that could guide future policy and interventions. However, secondary data use has certain limitations. Key variables that could deepen understanding—such as individual perceptions of healthcare access, barriers to NHI use and informal health-seeking behaviours—were absent, restricting analysis depth. Furthermore, secondary data may not capture recent changes in healthcare policies or evolving regional healthcare needs, particularly in the dynamic, remote settings of Maluku’s islands. Moreover, such secondary data lack region-specific insights into the unique sociocultural and logistical factors that influence healthcare access across Maluku’s dispersed islands, potentially limiting the tailored policy adjustments required. Finally, as this study employed a cross-sectional design, causal relationships between the identified factors and NHI use could not be inferred and the associations observed should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal.

Policy implications

The findings of this study highlight the urgent need for equity-oriented policies to improve the use of NHI in Maluku province and other archipelagic regions in Indonesia. Strategies should focus on increasing NHI uptake among older adults, males and subsidised members groups consistently shown to have low utilisation rates. Tailored interventions such as community-based health education, male-focused health promotion initiatives and simplified administrative procedures may help reduce barriers and improve access for these populations.

Addressing geographical disparities in healthcare access is also essential. Strengthening the implementation of the gugus pulau (island cluster) approach where healthcare services are organised around island groupings with central referral facilities could improve coordination, reduce logistical barriers and extend service reach to remote and underserved communities.

Improving district-level health infrastructure and human resource capacity should be a priority. This includes deploying additional healthcare professionals, offering incentives for rural and remote postings and expanding the use of telemedicine to ensure access in difficult-to-reach areas. Efforts should also be made to provide targeted funding and technical support from central or provincial governments to districts with limited resources.

Finally, sustained health promotion involving NHI representatives and community health workers is critical to raise awareness of NHI benefits and procedures. Educating family caregivers and community members on how to navigate NHI processes might further strengthen support systems for older adults and other vulnerable populations, enhancing equitable access to healthcare across Maluku’s dispersed islands. These integrated policy actions will be vital for achieving more inclusive and effective health insurance coverage, particularly in Indonesia’s geographically complex and underserved eastern regions.

Conclusions

In summary, this study highlights the persistently low use of NHI among beneficiaries in Maluku province, driven by a combination of individual and district-level factors. Older adults, males and subsidised members were less likely to use NHI services, pointing to important equity gaps. Geographical and structural challenges, such as limited access to healthcare providers and uneven fiscal capacity across districts, further constrained NHI utilisation. These findings underscore the need for geographically tailored strategies, including optimising the gugus pulau health system, expanding outreach and education, particularly for vulnerable populations and improving healthcare infrastructure in remote areas. Strengthening these aspects is essential for achieving more equitable and effective health insurance coverage in eastern Indonesia’s archipelagic context.
