Three Laws of Statistical Linguistics Emerging in images. Preprint: APS/123-QED
PingRui Tsai1 1 , ChiHsiang Wang 2 , YuCheng Liao 3 , ChiChi Huang 1 , TzayMing Hong1 1

Abstract
Images, as a product evolving alongside civilization, develop similarly to natural languages with the advancement of civilization. Not only are images abundant in daily life, but are also influenced by technology in shaping their forms, embodying various characteristics as they evolve in time. Language is a sequence of symbols that represents thoughts. While a written language is typically associated with the close integration of text and sound, as a combination of visual symbols and perception, the communicative power of image is no less significant. This is especially notable since 60 % \% of the sensory input received by our central nervous system comes from vision. Given the symbolic system inherent in images, we are curious whether images can also exhibit the laws of statistical linguistics. To explore this, we begin with the relationship between human thought and visual perception to decode how images are formed by the latter mechanism. Building upon previous studies that established the high correlation between pre-trained deep convolutional neural networks and the human visual system, we use the VGG-19 to define words via each kernel and calculate the number of pixels with grayscale values greater than 90 % \% . By (a) ranking words frequency, (b) randomizing the order of kernel appearances and performing the same word count accumulation, and (c) summing the word counts layer by layer, we are surprised to find that Zipf’s, Heaps’, and Benford’s laws of statistical linguistics also exist in the words that comprises the text representing different images.

Our research focuses on whether these three linguistic laws exist in images. To achieve this, we define image ”morphemes”

In essence, both serve as processors for image or visual input, and the extracting feature work of dCNNs aligns with the visual cortex of human central nervous system. This allows us to treat Pre-trained neural networks as proxies for the human visual system. Not only is this supported by computational evidence, but convolutional networks themselves were historically inspired by and developed from studies of the visual nervous system

We employ the well-known VGG-19 model as our visual proxy, input images, and analyze the feature maps generated by each kernel to define word types and word counts. This paper will focus on three main topics: (1) What defines image words? (2) How do the three laws emerge? I.1
Kernel, Feature Map, and Word Definition

In the application of statistical linguistics to images, the first step is to define what qualifies as text within an image. We base the definition of word types on the use of kernels, which are composed of various Gabor filters . These filters simulate the extraction of edge-related features in images, analogous to the function of simple cells in the visual nervous system. The feature maps produced by convolutional layers using different kernels are considered as morphemes.

Drawing on previous work in explainable deep learning, we utilize Intersection over Union (IoU)
van2019deep
as a reference metric, This approach takes the top
10\%
of pixel values from the feature map as the prominent feature distribution for image segmentation. Specifically, we consider only the pixels with a significant grayscale value in the feature map as the information captured by the kernel. These selected pixels are then used to determine the actual word count.

In Fig. 1(a), as the input to VGG-19 requires a specific format, we extract two Regions of Interest (ROI) of
224\times 224\times 3
from the image as inputs from the image as inputs, shown in Fig. 1(b, c). In logarithmic scale, the Zipf’s law result shown in Fig. 1(d). Next, by randomizing the order of kernel appearances and performing the same word count accumulation, we can obtain Heap’s law. The order of kernel sorting affects the fitting result with the straight line. We select the best result based on the highest R-squared value among 100 iterations. The corresponding plot is shown in the full-logarithmic Fig. 1(e).

Finally, relying on the order of image forward propagation in VGG-19, we treat each convolutional layer as a representation of the first letters, which also carries implications for the neuroscience of early, middle, and late visual perception. We calculate the total word count produced by all kernels within each convolutional layer and, based on word frequency, select the top nine as indicators. This allows us to obtain Benford’s Law. The result is presented in Fig. 1(f).

I.3
Robustness of the Laws

In this section, we conducted common adversarial image perturbations to explore whether the original input images are affected by the image processing distortions, influencing the existence of the three laws. We used nine images to perform the following operations:

1. Randomly adding black and white noise at varying proportions,
2. Applying Gaussian smoothing with different kernel sizes with
N\times 90
,
3. Erosion, and 4. Dilation. The results are presented in Fig. 3. It can be observed that Zipf’s and Heap’s laws maintain high R-square values above 0.94. However, Benford’s law exhibits greater sensitivity to perturbations, with its characteristics deteriorating as noise proportions and kernel sizes increase.

II
Discussion

The image is a visual composition of a symbolic system. In our research, we successfully used kernels as morphemes to construct text and calculated the prominent pixels of the feature map to compute word frequencies,leading to the emergence of two fundamental laws, which allows us to answer the first question. We define words in a way that is consistent with the neuroscientific meaning, regarding biological visual processing, where the visual cortex plays a role in image feature processing, from line segment features to color features and overall objects. Describing an image as a combination of discrete features is similar to describing a text. Additionally, the concept of defining words with different kernels also involves the sequence of different convolutional layers, which corresponds to the order of perceptual processing. Through the above, we first obtained Zipf’s law, with the results shown in Fig. 1d and Fig. 2a, and Heap’s law in Fig. 1e and Fig. 2b.

It is worth mentioning Heap’s law. Although mixing all words in a real natural language text and randomly rearranging them can result in this statistical property, it is not possible to directly achieve the same result with images by simply combining all the words generated by each kernel and shuffling them. Instead, the reorganization must occur sequentially at the kernel level to achieve the desired effect. This may be due to two factors. The first factor is sequence: any word generated by a kernel is created simultaneously and will not repeat later, whereas in traditional language, the same word can appear on different pages or at different times. Therefore, in visual images, the shuffled units must be combined randomly based on the kernel. The second factor is the dimensionality of the input data. The semantic context of language follows a one-dimensional sequence structure, where one word relates to another, as seen in current speech deep learning techniques such as RNNs and LSTMs. In contrast, image features are two-dimensional, allowing multiple words to be processed simultaneously.

As for Benford’s law, it serves as a tool for testing the frequency distribution of the leading digit combinations of a certain attribute. Although it can also emerge in images, based on Fig. 2c and f, the performance of R-squared seems to be worse compared to the other two laws. This may be related to the characteristics of the dCNN model, which will be discussed in the future with the addition of more dCNN models. In summary, based on the results of the three laws, we can indeed answer the second question: we have observed the emergence of three major statistical laws in images using dCNN.

Regarding the third question on robustness testing, different methods of image degradation all cause changes in the R-squared values of the three major laws. However, the robustness of Zipf’s law and Heap’s law remains significantly intact, while Benford’s law is much more easily disrupted, causing the R-squared value to drop below 0.9. If we can further explore the applicability of each dCNN model in the future, perhaps the concept of using Benford’s law for detecting economic and document fraud could also be developed within our model. This would not only benefit the cracking of art and image encryption, but it could also represent a different form of loss function for machine vision models.

We look forward to the possibility that, in the future, if we can further clarify the contours of the image semantic system, it may contribute to the development of natural language processing. This approach directly encodes text from the basic elements of an image, which is different from traditional black-box semantic processing. It would represent a new direction: building a deep learning model that is easy to debug and offers intuitive encoding.
