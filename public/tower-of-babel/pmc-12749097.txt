Atypical visual field defect without major compression from an inflammatory Rathke’s cleft cyst: illustrative case

Although most Rathke’s cleft cysts (RCCs) are asymptomatic, some can manifest with headaches, hypopituitarism, or visual disturbances.1–3 Visual field defects, typically hemianopia or quadrantanopia from mechanical compression, usually prompt surgical intervention.4,5 However, atypical visual field defects, such as central or paracentral scotomas, are rarely documented. These can occur even in the absence of significant mass effect, making clinical assessment and treatment decisions more complex.6–8 Recent reports have suggested that inflammatory processes extending from the cyst wall may underlie such unusual presentations.6,8

Here, we report a rare case of RCC presenting with bilateral paracentral scotomas without radiographic evidence of optic nerve compression. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI suggested inflammation along the optic pathway. This case highlights the importance of recognizing inflammation-related visual symptoms in RCC, which may warrant timely surgical intervention despite the apparent radiographic stability of the lesion.

Illustrative Case

A 55-year-old woman was referred to our hospital after an incidental cystic lesion was detected in the sella on head MRI. The patient had a medical history of hypothyroidism but no family history of the disease. At the initial visit, the patient was asymptomatic, and an endocrine evaluation demonstrated preserved pituitary function other than hypothyroidism. MRI performed at the referring hospital revealed an intrasellar cystic lesion containing a waxy nodule. The cyst content appeared isointense on T1-weighted imaging (Fig. 1A), and contrast-enhanced MRI showed cyst wall enhancement (Fig. 1B and C). The radiological diagnosis was RCC. One month later, the patient developed headache and visual field defects. Repeat MRI revealed no cyst enlargement, but the cystic content had become hyperintense on T1-weighted imaging. Additionally, contrast-enhanced MRI revealed a new high-intensity area extending from the pituitary stalk to the inferior aspect of the optic nerve (Fig. 1D–F). Ophthalmological examination revealed bilateral paracentral scotomas on Goldman perimetry (Fig. 2A and B). C and D: By postoperative day 14, the visual field had almost completely recovered.

Transsphenoidal endoscopic endonasal surgery was performed using neuronavigation and intraoperative neuromonitoring of transcranial motor evoked and visual evoked potentials. Intravenous hydrocortisone 100 mg was administered intraoperatively. Under 30° endoscopic visualization, the cyst wall was exposed and incised. Then, the viscous cyst content was drained, followed by thorough irrigation (Fig. 3A and B). A CSF leak was observed intraoperatively, and the sellar floor was reconstructed using abdominal fat and a bioresorbable plate. Histopathological examination of the cyst wall revealed inflammatory cell infiltration. FIG. 3.Intraoperative findings. Postoperatively, the patient experienced near-complete resolution of visual field defects (Fig. 2C and D). Postoperative hypopituitarism with hypocortisolemia was observed, and hydrocortisone was continued at 10 mg twice daily. Follow-up MRI at 2 weeks showed decreased cyst volume and disappearance of gadolinium enhancement along the pituitary stalk and optic nerve (Fig. 4A). At the 2-year follow-up, MRI showed no evidence of cyst recurrence at the operative site (Fig. 4B). Discussion

Visual field defects such as bitemporal hemianopia or quadrantanopia, typically caused by direct compression of the optic chiasm, are well-established indicators for surgical intervention in RCCs. In contrast, atypical patterns, such as central or paracentral scotomas, are rarely described and may lead to diagnostic uncertainty when evaluating the need for surgery. Previous reports have attributed these atypical findings either to unusual mechanical compression, such as displacement by the anterior cerebral artery, or to inflammatory processes extending from the cyst wall.6–8

In the present case, no visual disturbances were initially present; however, the patient developed headaches and paracentral scotomas 1 month later, despite no cyst enlargement on MRI. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI revealed enhancement at the inferior aspect of the optic chiasm, suggesting an inflammatory rather than a compressive mechanism.

It is well recognized that inflammation resulting from leakage of cyst contents or microbleeding from the surrounding pituitary gland can cause headaches and hypopituitarism.1,9 Importantly, such inflammation may spread to adjacent structures, including the optic nerve roots and cavernous sinus.10 Chang and Chen described a similar case in which inflammation from a small RCC led to cavernous sinus syndrome.11

Kobayashi et al. reported a case of optic neuritis caused by RCC, which was followed by idiopathic optic neuritis at another institution for 4 years.8 In this case, although steroid pulse therapy was administered for a tentative diagnosis of right optic neuritis, visual acuity did not improve significantly. However, resection of the cyst wall resulted in a dramatic improvement in the visual disturbance.

Gadolinium enhancement of the optic nerves in patients with optic neuritis reflects disruption of the blood-brain barrier and ongoing inflammation.6 In this context, RCCs may serve as inflammatory foci capable of inducing similar changes. Berg et al. demonstrated that optic nerve enhancement correlates with acute disease onset and greater visual deterioration, but also with better postoperative recovery when canalicular or intracranial segments are involved compared to intraorbital lesions.12 Therefore, gadolinium enhancement in patients with RCCs should alert clinicians to the risk of progressive vision loss and the potential for favorable recovery if surgical intervention is performed promptly.

Previous studies have reported an improvement rate of 70%–100% for visual impairment after surgery.3,13,14 Importantly, visual deficits caused by inflammation, like those due to compression, appear to be reversible. Although steroid therapy has been considered as a potential treatment option for optic neuritis associated with inflammatory RCC, its efficacy remains uncertain. Previous reports on optic neuritis associated with inflammatory RCC have shown that visual function can recover after surgery, whereas steroid pulse therapy may be ineffective.6,8,10 In addition, optic neuritis may recur despite successful steroid treatment unless the cyst contents are removed. In our case, complete vision recovery was achieved after endoscopic endonasal surgery. This observation is consistent with previous reports showing that decompression and removal of inflammatory cyst contents can restore visual deficits. Awareness of this mechanism is crucial because patients may develop visual symptoms despite a stable cyst size. Early identification of inflammatory imaging findings can therefore guide surgical decision-making and improve visual outcomes.

Observations

Atypical visual field defects associated with RCCs have rarely been reported and may thus be underrecognized in clinical practice. This case underscores that RCC-related inflammation, even in the absence of mass effect, can impair visual function. Awareness of this mechanism and careful assessment of inflammation-related MRI findings are essential for determining the appropriate surgical indications and timing.

Lessons

This case demonstrates that RCCs can cause atypical visual field defects through inflammatory spread to the optic apparatus, despite a stable cyst size. Vigilance for inflammation-related changes is critical, as prompt surgical intervention can result in vision recovery.
