Relationship between paraspinal sarcopenia and clinical outcomes of intervertebral disc degeneration patients following unilateral biportal endoscopy discectomy
Authors: Rui Zhang, Xu Hu, Cheng Lin, Qinfeng Wang, Jie Xu, Shishui Lin, Shiguo Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to evaluate the prognostic significance of preoperative paraspinal sarcopenia (PS) on long-term clinical outcomes in patients with intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) undergoing unilateral biportal endoscopy discectomy (UBED), and to identify independent risk factors of postoperative chronic low back pain.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 148 IDD patients who underwent UBED, classified into sarcopenia (n = 65) and non-sarcopenia (n = 83) groups based on psoas muscle index (PMI) thresholds. Radiographic parameters, including PMI, erector spinae muscle index (EMI), multifidus muscle index (MMI), multifidus fat infiltration (MFI), erector spinae fat infiltration (EFI), and multifidus muscle density (MMD), were assessed via CT/MRI. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) preoperatively and postoperatively at 1 month, 6 months, and final follow-up (≥ 12 months). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified risk factors for chronic low back pain.ResultsThe sarcopenia group exhibited significantly lower muscle indices (PMI, EMI, MMI; all P < 0.001) and higher fat infiltration (MFI, EFI; P < 0.05) compared to the non-sarcopenia group. While both groups demonstrated improvements in VAS and ODI scores postoperatively, the sarcopenia group had higher VAS (3.21 ± 0.92 vs. 2.75 ± 0.72, P < 0.001) and ODI (22.19 ± 4.37% vs. 19.08 ± 3.43%, P < 0.001) at final follow-up. Elevated BMI, lower BMD, reduced muscle indices (PMI, EMI, MMI), and severe MFI were significant predictors of chronic low back pain (P < 0.05).ConclusionsPS is a modifiable risk factor for postoperative chronic low back pain after UBED. Preoperative identification of high-risk patients and targeted interventions to improve muscle function may enhance clinical outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of integrating muscle health assessments into spine care pathways.Keywords: Intervertebral disc herniation, Paraspinal muscles, Paraspinal sarcopenia; chronic low back pain, Unilateral biportal endoscopy discectomy

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is one of the most prevalent spinal disorders worldwide, characterized by progressive structural and functional deterioration of the discs. The pathological process typically manifested as reduced water content within the disc, annular fissures, and nucleus pulposus degeneration [1, 2]. The etiology of IDD is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, mechanical stress, unhealthy lifestyle factors, and metabolic influences [3]. Clinically, IDD often presented with low back pain, motor dysfunction, decreased skin sensation and so on, significantly impairing patients' quality of life. Surgical intervention would be applied in patients with severe nerve root compression syndrome. Among various minimally invasive surgery techniques, unilateral biportal endoscopy discectomy (UBED) has gained more attention due to its superior visualization, operational flexibility, and minimal soft tissue injury [4]. UBED is performed via two small portals (one for the endoscope, one for instruments) established through a muscle-splitting approach, typically within the interlaminar window. This technique aims to minimize detachment and trauma to the multifidus muscle origin and insertion points on the spinous processes and laminae, which is a distinct advantage over traditional open midline approaches that require extensive muscle stripping [5]. However, postoperative chronic low back pain still persisted in some cases after UBED.

Paraspinal sarcopenia (PS) refers to the progressive loss of muscle mass, strength, and function in the muscles surrounding the spine, primarily the multifidus, erector spinae, and psoas muscles [6]. This condition is increasingly recognized as a critical factor in spinal degeneration, chronic back pain, and impaired mobility. Unlike generalized sarcopenia, which affects overall muscle mass, PS specifically impacts spinal stability and biomechanics, contributing to conditions such as degenerative disc disease, spinal stenosis, and post-surgical complications [7]. Given the aging global population, understanding PS has become essential for improving spinal health management. Relative quantitative imaging variables such as paraspinal muscle index (MI) and fat infiltration (FI) was provided for PS severity evaluation [8, 9]. To our knowledge, the association between long-term clinical outcomes of IDD patients underwent UBED and PS has been rarely investigated. Thus, this research aimed to: i) determine the prognostic value of preoperative PS on long-term clinical outcomes after UBED; ii) identify relative independent predictors of postoperative chronic low back pain after UBED.

Patients and methods

Study design and ethics

This retrospective cohort research was conducted at Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital and approved by the Institutional Review Board (K2025-02–040). All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and the informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study.

Patient enrollment

Consecutive lumbar disc herniation (LDH) patients undergoing UBED from 2021–2024 were collected according to the following inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria: i) age ≥ 18 years old; ii) single level LDH confirmed by preoperative (within 2 weeks before surgery) X-ray/computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); iii) serious nerve root compression symptoms with invalid conservative treatment for at least 3 months; iv) UBED conducted by the same surgery team; v) minimum 12 months follow-up with completed document, including visual analog scale (VAS) and Oswestry disability index (ODI).

Exclusion criteria: i) combined with spinal trauma, infection, and tumor; ii) multi-level LDH or with previous lumbar surgery history; iii) lumbar instability existed (translation > 3 mm or angular motion > 10°).

Data collection

Demographic parameters such as age, gender, body mass index (BMI), bone mineral density (BMD), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease), course of disease and so on were obtained from the electronic medical record system. Clinical variables were collected using standardized instruments: The VAS for pain evaluation (0–10 scores: from no pain to the worst imaginable pain) [10], and chronic LBP was defined as VAS ≥ 4 at final follow-up [11]. Additionally, the ODI was used for functional status assessment (0%−100%: from less to greater disability) [12]. The investigation was performed at pre-operative day 1, post-operative 1 month, 6 month, and final follow-up.

Radiographic assessment

All radiographic assessments were performed retrospectively. Preoperative CT and MRI images (obtained within 2 weeks before surgery) were assessed independently by the three specialists (one orthopedic surgeon and two musculoskeletal radiologists), who were blinded to all clinical outcomes. For the postoperative clinical time points (1 month, 6 months, final follow-up), only patient-reported outcome measures (VAS, ODI) were collected; no repeat cross-sectional imaging (CT/MRI) was performed as part of the standard clinical follow-up protocol for these patients.

The psoas muscle index (PMI) was calculated by measuring the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the psoas muscle at the L3 vertebral level on axial CT images, normalized to the square of the patient’s height (cm2/m2). Sarcopenia was defined according to sex-specific cutoff values proposed by Hamaguchi et al. [13], with PMI thresholds of < 6.36 cm2/m2 for males and < 3.92 cm2/m2 for females. Similarly, the erector spinae index (EMI) and multifidus muscle index (MMI) were computed using the same methodology.

Fat infiltration (FI) was classified into two subtypes: multifidus fat infiltration (MFI) and erector spinae fat infiltration (EFI). Bilateral paraspinal muscles at the L3 level were evaluated on T2-weighted MRI using the Goutallier classification system [14], which defines fat infiltration as follows: Grade 0: No fat streaks; Grade 1: Minimal fat streaks; Grade 2: Muscle predominance over fat; Grade 3: Equal muscle and fat composition; Grade 4: Fat predominance over muscle. For clinical stratification, severity was further categorized according to the Fuchs modification as: mild (Grades 0–1), moderate (Grades 1.5–2), and severe (Grades 2.5–4).

Multifidus muscle density (MMD) was quantified by measuring the mean Hounsfield unit (HU) values within a defined region of interest at the L3 vertebral level using non-contrast CT scan. The inter-rater reliability demonstrated excellent agreement, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) exceeding 0.90 for all muscle measurements and Cohen's kappa coefficients surpassing 0.85 for Goutallier classification assessments. Any interpretive discrepancies were adjudicated through consensus review by the evaluating surgeon and radiologists.

Statistical analysis

Relative data analysis was performed using SPSS version 21.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and continuous variables presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons utilized Mann–Whitney U tests, independent t-tests, and Fisher’s exact tests. Multicollinearity among independent variables in the multivariable model was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), with all values below 4.0 indicating acceptable levels. Univariate/Multivariate logistic regression analysis were used for identifying predictors for chronic LBP. A two-tailed p < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

Demographics characteristics

This research enrolled 148 patients which met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. They were classified into sarcopenia group (n = 65) and non-sarcopenia group (n = 83) based on PMI thresholds. Demographics information including age, gender, BMI, BMD, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease), smoking, alcohol, follow-up duration, and LOS showed no significant difference between two groups (P > 0.05). Notably, sarcopenia group showed longer course of disease compared to the non-sarcopenia group (P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes

The VAS in preoperative (6.52 ± 1.25 vs 6.89 ± 1.36, P = 0.091) and post-1 month (3.27 ± 0.82 vs 3.49 ± 0.97, P = 0.145) period showed no significant difference between two groups. After 6 months of operation, lower VAS observed in non-sarcopenia group compared to the sarcopenia group (3.15 ± 0.94 vs 2.87 ± 0.69, P = 0.038). In final follow-up, non-sarcopenia group still showed less VAS compared to the sarcopenia group (P < 0.001).

At the same time, ODI decreased from 55.27 ± 8.29% to 19.37 ± 3.26% in sarcopenia group during preoperative and 6-month of postoperative, while non-sarcopenia group decreased from 56.31 ± 7.96% to 18.56 ± 3.17%. However, there was no significant difference between two groups. At the time point of final follow-up, non-sarcopenia group showed lower ODI compared to the sarcopenia group (19.08 ± 3.43% vs 22.19 ± 4.37%, P < 0.001). A differential recovery pattern was observed between pain and function. While VAS scores diverged significantly between groups by the 6-month follow-up, functional disability as measured by ODI showed no significant inter-group difference at this time point (P = 0.129). The significant functional disadvantage associated with sarcopenia became apparent only at the final long-term follow-up. To assess clinically meaningful improvement, the proportion of patients achieving the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was compared. At final follow-up, significantly fewer patients in the sarcopenia group achieved the MCID for VAS (66.2% vs. 85.5%, P = 0.005) and for ODI (81.5% vs. 95.2%, P = 0.008) compared to the non-sarcopenia group (Table 3).

Correlation analysis of risk factors related to postoperative chronic low back pain

Variables with a significance level of P < 0.1 in the univariable analysis (Table 4) were entered into the initial multivariable logistic regression model. Backward stepwise selection was then used to retain variables with P < 0.05 in the final model. Age and Course of Disease, while potential clinical confounders, did not meet the threshold for inclusion (P > 0.1 in univariate analysis, Table 4) and were therefore not forced into the final multivariate model to preserve parsimony and focus on the core radiographic and morphometric predictors. The result of univariable logistic regression analysis indicated that BMI, BMD, PMI, EMI, MMI, MFI, EFI, and MMD were entered as independent variables (Table 4). Moreover, BMI (odds ratio = 1.127, P = 0.002), BMD (odds ratio = 0.794, P = 0.001), PMI (odds ratio = 2.911, P = 0.031), EMI (odds ratio = 4.126, P = 0.026), MMI (odds ratio = 1.681, P = 0.014), and MFI (odds ratio = 5.512, P = 0.001) were significantly associated with postoperative chronic low back pain. Notably, while EFI and MMD showed significance in the univariate analysis, they did not retain independent significance in the multivariable model (Table 5). This suggests that their association with chronic LBP may be mediated or confounded by their correlation with other stronger predictors in the model, such as the muscle indices (PMI, EMI, MMI) or MFI.

Discussion

This retrospective study evaluates the correlation between preoperative PS and long-term clinical outcomes of IDD patients treated with UBED based on radiographic evaluations. Patients in sarcopenia group demonstrated reduced paraspinal muscle indices and severe fatty infiltration compared with the non-sarcopenia group. Meanwhile, more visible postoperative pain and disability observed in patients combined with PS. Among various variables, elevated BMI, lower BMD, reduced paraspinal muscle index (PMI, EMI, MMI), and severe MFI were identified as risk factors related to postoperative chronic low back pain. These results highlighted the predictive value of paraspinal muscle degeneration parameters on postoperative clinical outcomes after UBED.

The main paraspinal muscle including multifidus, erector spinae, and psoas major. PS may be an early indicator of an early manifestation of systemic sarcopenia and is associated with osteoporosis and increased fall risk [15]. A growing body of evidence indicates that paraspinal muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration correlate with lumbar disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and degenerative scoliosis, potentially accelerating disease progression [16]. Furthermore, patients with PS demonstrate higher rates of postoperative complications (including implant failure and adjacent segment degeneration) and prolonged recovery periods following spinal procedures [17]. In this study, we evaluated the quality of paraspinal muscle using relative radiographic parameters. The patients with sarcopenia exhibited lower PMI (5.21 ± 1.75 vs 7.82 ± 1.93 cm2/m2), EMI (9.62 ± 2.31 vs 13.45 ± 2.67 cm2/m2), MMI (2.25 ± 0.74 vs 3.71 ± 0.82 cm2/m2), more moderate/severe MFI (55.38% vs 49.40%) and EFI (52.31% vs 38.55%). Our results confirmed that PS behaved as decreased volume of paraspinal muscle and fatty infiltration.

Paraspinal muscle mass plays a crucial role in spinal dynamic stability through integrated biomechanical and neuro control mechanisms. These interdependent systems work synergistically to maintain normal spinal function. As for biomechanical, the multifidus and transversus abdominis muscles co-contract synergistically to counteract shear forces during spinal flexion, rotation, or lateral bending, thereby preventing excessive loading on intervertebral discs and facet joints [18]. Importantly, the short fibers of the multifidus muscle attach directly to adjacent vertebrae, providing precise segmental stability (particularly in the lumbar) [19]. Furthermore, reductions in muscle volume or fatty infiltration can decrease muscular stiffness, compromising their ability to absorb mechanical shocks and consequently increasing passive loading on ligaments and joints [20]. While for neuro control, intramuscular muscle spindles and high-threshold mechanoreceptors rapidly adjust muscle tension through reflex arcs (e.g., spinal reflexes) in response to sudden loading. In healthy individuals, muscles are preactivated prior to movement initiation. However, muscle atrophy or fatty infiltration may cause delayed activation, potentially increasing the risk of transient spinal instability [21]. In our study, both groups demonstrated a decreasing trend in VAS and ODI scores during follow-up. However, at the final follow-up assessment, patients with sarcopenia demonstrated significantly higher VAS (3.21 ± 0.92 vs 2.75 ± 0.72, P < 0.001) and ODI (22.19 ± 4.37% vs 19.08 ± 3.43, P < 0.001) scores compared with those in the non-sarcopenia group. Analyzing the achievement of MCID provides a more patient-centered and clinically interpretable outcome. The proportion of patients achieving the MCID for VAS at final follow-up was significantly lower in the sarcopenia group compared to the non-sarcopenia group. Similarly, the proportion achieving the MCID for ODI was lower in the sarcopenia group. Our results demonstrated that the UBE technique offers the advantage of preserving multifidus muscle attachment points, which may partially compensate for mechanical stability deficits in patients with sarcopenia. However, a certain degree of postoperative pain symptoms and functional impairment may still occur in patients with PS.

PS significantly increases the risk of chronic low back pain following lumbar spine surgery by compromising spinal stability, exacerbating mechanical load, and promoting inflammatory responses. A recent study showed that PS is a modifiable determinant of chronic low back pain after percutaneous transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy [22]. Similarly, our research implied that several PS related factors (PMI, EMI, MMI, MFI) were associated with postoperative chronic low back pain. Among the various paraspinal muscle parameters assessed, severe multifidus fat infiltration (MFI) emerged as the strongest independent predictor of postoperative chronic LBP (OR = 5.512). This suggests that evaluating MFI on preoperative lumbar MRI could be a particularly practical and powerful tool for risk stratification in clinical practice. The Goutallier classification system, already familiar to musculoskeletal radiologists and spine surgeons, provides a readily applicable framework for this assessment. Prioritizing the evaluation of MFI may help efficiently identify patients at the highest risk for suboptimal pain relief, who might benefit most from targeted prehabilitation. These pathological changes associated with pain intensity positively [23]. Besides, patients with a high BMI experience greater static and dynamic loading on the lumbar spine, accelerating postoperative degeneration of intervertebral discs and facet joints, which may contribute to recurrent pain [24]. In obese individuals, adipose tissue releases pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), which exacerbate inflammation in nerve roots and surrounding tissues, thereby contributing to chronic pain [25]. Importantly, low BMD is often associated with vitamin D deficiency, which has been linked to muscle weakness and neuroinflammation. This pathway may directly activate nociceptors, contributing to pain sensitization [26]. In osteoporosis, increased osteoclast activity also leads to the release of pro-inflammatory factors (such as RANKL), which stimulate surrounding nerves [27]. Taken together, BMI and BMD are correlated to the postoperative chronic low back pain, which also confirmed in this study.

Investigating the influence of PS on postoperative outcomes following lumbar spine surgery holds significant clinical relevance, guiding patient management across multiple stages of care. Below is a detailed breakdown of its clinical implications: i) identification of high-risk patients. Quantifying muscle mass (via MRI/CT-based CSA) and fat infiltration helps predict poor responders to surgery. Patients with severe multifidus atrophy and fat infiltration may benefit less from surgery procedures and require alternative interventions. ii) optimization before surgery. Targeted core stabilization exercises, resistance training, protein/vitamin D supplementation and other various prehabilitation programs could be applied in patients classified as PS before surgical intervention. iii) Intraoperative technique adjustments. Patients with PS preference for minimally invasive techniques over open midline dissection to reduce iatrogenic muscle damage. iv) postoperative rehabilitation personalization. Progressive resistance training could be used for activating multifidus and transversus abdominis. Patients with severe paraspinal muscle atrophy could benefit for fast postoperative recovery by neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) [28]. Integrating muscle health into spine care pathways may reduce chronic pain rates and revision surgeries, ultimately lowering healthcare burdens.

There are still some limitations existed in this study: i) retrospective studies have inherent limitations, including difficulties in controlling for confounding factors (e.g., overall nutritional status, exercise habits, and other unmeasured variables). ii) the study was limited by the predefined timing of muscle assessments and the absence of longitudinal data on postoperative muscle changes. iii) functional assessment is primarily based on subjective evaluation, with a notable absence of objective motor function testing, including gait analysis and muscle strength assessment. iv) the effects of PS on functional recovery may not be fully apparent in short-term follow-up assessments. Future multicenter, prospective study should integrate preoperative muscle strengthening training and artificial intelligence–assisted muscle evaluation to achieve preferable clinical outcomes following spine surgery for PS patients.

Conclusions

Our study indicated that PS is a modifiable determinant of postoperative chronic low back pain after UBED. Risk factors including BMI, BMD, PMI, EMI, MMI, and MFI. Preoperative identification of high-risk patients and targeted interventions for muscle function may represent a critical strategy for improving clinical outcomes.
