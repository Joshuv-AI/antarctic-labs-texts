Interpolating quasiregular power mappings
Jack Burkart, Alastair N. Fletcher, Daniel A. Nicks

Abstract
. We construct a quasiregular mapping in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} that is the first to illustrate several important dynamical properties: the quasi-Fatou set contains wandering components; these quasi-Fatou components are bounded and hollow; and the Julia set has components that are genuine round spheres. The key tool in this construction is a new quasiregular interpolation in round rings in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} between power mappings of differing degrees on the boundary components. We also exhibit the flexibility of constructions based on these interpolations by showing that we may obtain quasiregular mappings which grow as quickly, or as slowly, as desired.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Let
f:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a non-constant entire function, and denote by
f^{n}
the function
f
composed with itself
n
times. The Julia set of
f
, denoted
J(f)
, is the set of all points
z
where the orbit,
\{f^{n}(z)\}
, exhibits sensitivity to initial conditions. Its complement is called the Fatou set, denoted
F(f)
. The Fatou set is open, and its connected components are called Fatou components.

The nature of the singularity at infinity for
f
gives a key dichotomy in the study of entire functions. If
\infty
is a pole, then
f
is a polynomial. Otherwise,
f
has an essential singularity there, and
f
is called a transcendental entire function. Transcendental entire functions exhibit many dynamical phenomena that do not exist in the polynomial setting. For example, it is possible for transcendental entire functions to have
multiply connected wandering domains
, and entire functions with this property are one of the main motivations for this article.

In
[
1
]
, Baker exhibited the first example of a transcendental entire function with a multiply connected Fatou component, given by an infinite product of the form

f(z)=Cz^{2}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{z}{R_{n}}\right).

Here,
C
is a large positive constant, and
\{R_{n}\}
is a rapidly increasing sequence. One can think of the
R_{n}
as radii where the function
f
increases its degree from
2+(n-1)
to
2+n
. Moreover, a key part of Baker’s analysis uses the fact that on annuli of the form
A(R_{n}^{2},R_{n+1}^{1/2})
, the function
f(z)
is a small perturbation of a monomial of degree
2+n
. He later showed, in
[
3
]
, that
f
has a
bounded
multiply connected Fatou component
U
, and thus
f^{n}(U)
is a sequence of wandering domains. This was the first example of wandering domains for entire functions. In fact, Baker showed
[
2
]
that
all
multiply connected Fatou components of transcendental entire functions are bounded, and so are wandering domains on which the iterates tend to infinity.

Bergweiler, Rippon, and Stallard gave a comprehensive characterization of the internal dynamics in multiply connected wandering domains in
[
5
]
. Among other things, they demonstrated that Baker’s example has several features that generalize. They showed that every sequence of multiply connected wandering domains contains large round annuli where the function is very close to a monomial
[
5
, Theorem 5.2]
. These annuli eventually “absorb” the orbits of all points in the multiply connected wandering domains. Therefore, the orbit of a point in a multiply connected Fatou component eventually lands in this absorbing annulus, and thereafter, the dynamics behave roughly like that of iterating monomials of increasing degree.

Bergweiler, Rippon, and Stallard were additionally able to modify Baker’s infinite product construction to produce a variety of functions having multiply connected wandering domains with interesting topological features, see
[
5
, Section 10]
. Bishop used an infinite product construction in
[
8
]
to construct an entire function whose Julia set has Hausdorff dimension 1, and his results were generalized by the first named author in
[
10
]
to construct Julia sets with fractional packing dimension.

Infinite products are not the only tools that allow one to construct a function modeled by monomials with increasing degree on large annuli. Kisaka and Shishikura
[
18
]
used a technique known as quasiconformal surgery to construct an entire function with a multiply connected wandering domain that is a topological annulus. All previously constructed examples where the connectivity was known had infinitely many complementary components. The first author and Lazebnik gave another approach to constructing functions with multiply connected wandering domains in
[
11
]
by modifying a construction of Bishop known as quasiconformal folding
[
7
]
. The construction in
[
11
]
motivates many of the ideas in this article.

Inspired by the above results, our attention here turns to analogous ideas in higher dimensions.
Quasiregular dynamics is the natural generalization of complex dynamics to higher real dimensions, and thus is the appropriate setting for our study. There has been a substantial amount of work studying the Julia set
J(f)
and its complement the quasi-Fatou set
QF(f)
for transcendental type quasiregular maps
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for
n\geq 2
.
This was initiated by Bergweiler and the third named author in
[
4
]
. Of particular relevance here is the paper
[
20
]
of the third named author and Sixsmith, where it was shown that in every dimension at least
2
, there exists a quasiregular map
f
for which
QF(f)
has a component which is hollow
[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
.
Here, a domain
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called hollow if there exists a bounded component of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus U
, and is called full if there is no bounded complementary component. In dimension
2
, a domain
U
is hollow if and only if
U
is multiply connected, but in higher dimensions the richer topology means this is no longer true — consider, for example, a solid torus.

A significant open question in quasiregular dynamics is whether every hollow quasi-Fatou component must be bounded. If so, this would be analogous to Baker’s result in the complex setting that every multiply connected Fatou component is bounded, which he proved over ten years after giving the first example of such Fatou components. An affirmative answer to the above question would imply that, for any transcendental type quasiregular map
f
, the Julia set
J(f)
is perfect and coincides with the boundary of the fast escaping set, and that
J(f)=J(f^{p})
for
p\in\mathbb{N}
, see
[
20
]
. These properties
are familiar in complex dynamics but have yet to be established in general in the quasiregular setting.

Any quasi-Fatou component that is hollow and bounded must be a wandering domain. Moreover, the results of
[
20
,
21
]
establish a strong analogy between the structure and behaviour of such
components and that of multiply connected wandering domains in complex dynamics.

1.2.
Statement of results

It was left open in
[
20
]
whether the hollow components of
QF(f)
found there were bounded or unbounded. In this paper, we address this question via the following result.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a quasiregular map
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
for which
QF(f)
contains bounded, hollow components. Moreover, there exist a sequence of quasi-Fatou components
(\Omega_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
and a sequence of spheres
(\Gamma_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
centered at the origin and with radii tending to infinity such that, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

(a)

the
\Gamma_{k}
are components of
J(f)
,

(b)

f
maps
\Gamma_{k}
onto
\Gamma_{k+1}
,

(c)

\Omega_{k}
is a bounded, hollow component of
QF(f)
,

(d)

\Gamma_{k}
and
\Gamma_{k+1}
are boundary components of
\Omega_{k}
.

We re-iterate that this is the first construction of a quasiregular map in higher dimensions for which
QF(f)
is known to contain bounded, hollow components, and that these are necessarily wandering domains. In fact, this is the first example of a transcendental type quasiregular map of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n\geq 3
, for which it is known that the quasi-Fatou set has more than one component.

The key step in our construction is an interpolation for quasiregular power maps of different degrees. The technique is inspired by the construction of Lazebnik and the first named author which gives an interpolation between
z^{m}
and
z^{n}
in planar annuli
[
11
]
. It is well known that interpolation in ring domains in the bi-Lipschitz and quasiconformal categories is possible by Sullivan’s Annulus Theorem, see
[
24
]
, or
[
25
]
for a quantitative version. However, for non-injective maps, general interpolation results are rather rare: the Berstein-Edmonds extension theorem in dimension three
[
6
]
has been used several times in uniformly quasiregular dynamics, see
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. See also
[
22
]
which includes, among many other things, work along a similar theme in dimension four.

Our main interpolation result concerns quasiregular maps
p_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
analogous to complex power maps
z^{d}
. We mention here only that
|p_{d}(x)|=|x|^{d}
and that
p_{d}
has degree
d^{2}
, deferring a fuller introduction of these maps to section
2
.

To set some notation, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
0<r<s
, we write
B(y,r)
for the open ball
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x-y|<r\}
, and
A(r,s)
for the closed ring
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:r\leq|x|\leq s\}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be odd. There exists a quasiregular map
P:A(1,e^{1/d})\to\overline{B(0,e^{3})}
that equals
p_{d}
when
|x|=1
and equals
p_{3d}
when
|x|=e^{1/d}
. The dilatation
K(P)
is independent of
d
.

The only similar result of which the authors are aware is a construction by Drasin and Sastry
[
12
]
(used in
[
20
]
), which gives a quasiregular interpolation in ring domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
between piecewise linear power maps of different degrees. However, the construction in
[
12
]
is somewhat technical and is achieved by increasing the degree in one coordinate direction at a time. Our construction is more amenable for the applications we have in mind. We restrict our construction to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and while we would expect similar results to hold in higher dimensions, we leave this open.

In section
5
, we illustrate the flexibility of our methods by using Theorem
2
to construct quasiregular maps that grow arbitrarily quickly or arbitrarily slowly.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Quasiregular maps

We refer to Rickman’s monograph
[
23
]
for foundational material on quasiregular maps. The inner dilatation and outer dilatation of
f
are denoted by
K_{I}(f)
and
K_{O}(f)
respectively, and the maximal dilatation is
K(f)=\max\{K_{I}(f),K_{O}(f)\}
.

A quasiregular map
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be of transcendental type if
f
has an essential singularity at infinity.
The Julia set
J(f)
of
f
is then defined to be the set of all
x
such that

\operatorname{cap}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\bigcup\limits^{\infty}_{k=1}f^{k}(U)\right)=0

for every neighbourhood
U
of
x
. Here
\operatorname{cap}E=0
denotes that
E
is a set of conformal capacity zero. We refer to
[
4
]
for a fuller discussion of this, noting here only that
J(f)
comprises points with a certain ‘blowing up’ property.

The quasi-Fatou set
QF(f)
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus J(f)
. By
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
,
J(f)
is non-empty and, in fact, guaranteed to be an infinite set. Both the Julia set and quasi-Fatou set are completely invariant; that is,
x\in J(f)
if and only if
f(x)\in J(f)
.

2.2.
Zorich maps

The Zorich maps are quasiregular maps which generalize the complex exponential function. There is not an obvious canonical choice of Zorich map, but rather a class of mappings. We briefly describe how to define a Zorich map
\mathcal{Z}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
based on an initial choice of bi-Lipschitz map
h
from the square
[-1,1]^{2}
to the upper hemisphere
\{y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}):|y|=1,\,y_{3}\geq 0\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. One example of such a map is given by (cf.
[
23
, I.3.3]
)

(1)

h(x_{1},x_{2})=\left(\frac{x_{1}\sin(\pi M(x_{1},x_{2})/2)}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}},\frac{x_{2}\sin(\pi M(x_{1},x_{2})/2)}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}},\cos(\pi M(x_{1},x_{2})/2)\right),

where
M(x_{1},x_{2})=\max\{|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\}
. We do not assume that
h
has the specific form (
1
), but we shall impose the additional condition that

(2)

h(0,0)=(0,0,1).

In the beam
[-1,1]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, we define the Zorich map
\mathcal{Z}
by

(3)

\mathcal{Z}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=e^{x_{3}}h(x_{1},x_{2}).

The image of the beam under
\mathcal{Z}
is the closed upper half-space with the origin removed. By repeatedly reflecting in sides of beams in the domain, and in the plane
\{x_{3}=0\}
in the range, we extend the definition to obtain our Zorich map
\mathcal{Z}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
.
For more details on Zorich maps, including a justification of their quasiregularity, we refer to
[
17
, §6.5.4]
.

If
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a plane, then we denote by
R_{H}
the reflection in
H
. The map
\mathcal{Z}
is strongly automorphic with respect to the group
G
of isometries obtained by composing an even number of reflections of the form
R_{\{x_{i}=n\}}
with
i=1,2
and
n
odd. Here, strongly automorphic means that

•

for all
R\in G
, we have
Z\circ R=Z
; and

•

if
\mathcal{Z}(a)=\mathcal{Z}(b)
, then there exists
R\in G
such that
a=R(b)
.

In particular,
\mathcal{Z}
has periods
(4,0,0)
and
(0,4,0)
.
Moreover, the branch set of
\mathcal{Z}
is
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z})=\{(m,n,x_{3}):m,n\text{ odd integers, }x_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

Throughout this paper we will denote by
\mathcal{Z}
a Zorich map as above with associated automorphism group
G
. The condition (
2
) means that
\mathcal{Z}
maps the
x_{3}
-axis into itself.

2.3.
Power maps

If
u:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an expanding conformal map which satisfies
u(0)=0
and
uGu^{-1}\subset G
, then by
[
17
, Theorem 21.4.1]
the Schröder equation
p\circ\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}\circ u
has a unique uniformly quasiregular solution
p
.

For any integers
d
and
n
we have the identity

(4)

d\cdot R_{\{x_{i}=n\}}(x)=R_{\{x_{i}=dn\}}(dx).

Now let
d
be a positive odd integer and take
u(x)=dx
. Then it follows from (
4
) that
uGu^{-1}\subset G
and so there is a unique solution to the Schröder equation that we denote by
p_{d}
.

These maps
p_{d}
are uniformly quasiregular analogues of power maps
z^{d}
and were first studied by Mayer
[
19
]
. See also
[
13
]
for more on uniformly quasiregular solutions of Schröder equations. We note that
|p_{d}(x)|=|x|^{d}
and that
p_{d}
has degree
d^{2}
. The former follows from the Schröder equation and (
3
), since if
x=\mathcal{Z}(y)
then

|p_{d}(x)|=|\mathcal{Z}(dy)|=e^{dy_{3}}=|\mathcal{Z}(y)|^{d}=|x|^{d}.

Define

by

, then the strong automorphy of

with respect to

. We observe that:

•

In order to prove Theorem

. To do this, we follow
[

by introducing a map

Lemma 5
.

There exists a
9
-quasiconformal map

\beta\colon\{|x|>1\}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\leq 1,\,x_{3}=0\}

from the complement of the unit ball
\overline{B(0,1)}
to the complement of a disc, such that
\beta
extends continuously to
\partial B(0,1)
with

\beta(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\beta(x_{1},x_{2},-x_{3})\quad\mbox{when }x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=1,

and such that
\beta
is the identity outside the set

(8)

X:=\left(B((0,0,1),\sqrt{2})\cup B((0,0,-1),\sqrt{2})\right)\setminus B(0,1).\

Proof.

Following the planar case
[
9
]
, we define
\beta:=\mu\circ\nu\circ\mu
where
\mu=\mu^{-1}
is the self-inverse orientation-reversing Möbius map of
\mathbb{R}^{3}\cup\{\infty\}
(sending

to
\{x_{1}>0\}

\mu(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\frac{2(x_{1}-1,x_{2},x_{3})}{|(x_{1}-1,x_{2},x_{3})|^{2}}+(1,0,0)=(1+2r(x_{1}-1),2rx_{2},2rx_{3}),

where

is the identity in the wedge
\{x_{1}\geq|x_{3}|\}
and triples the angle of the remaining wedges
{\{0<x_{1}<|x_{3}|\}}
. (More precisely, using cylindrical co-ordinates,
\nu(\rho\cos\phi,x_{2},\rho\sin\phi)=(\rho\cos\phi^{\prime},x_{2},\rho\sin\phi^{\prime})

\rho^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}
and for

[

) with

. The composition

is thus orientation-preserving and quasiconformal with

.

A calculation shows that

is the identity outside

. When

and so

thus for such points

, which yields (
7

maps

to

, as well as showing that

fixes pointwise the circle
\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):x_{1}=0,\,x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=1\}

∎

We are now in a position to complete the proof of Theorem

.

.

First, note that by (

. Let

4.
Spherical Julia components and hollow quasi-Fatou components

In this section we will construct the quasiregular map which has the dynamical properties claimed in Theorem
1
.

4.1.
Constructing the map

The first step is to generalize the interpolation map from Theorem
2
to other rings.

Theorem 7
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be odd, and let
R>0
and
c>0
. There exists a quasiregular map

F_{R,d,c}:A(R,Re^{1/d})\to\{|x|\leq c^{\prime}R^{3d}e^{3}\}

that equals
c\cdot p_{d}
when
|x|=R
and equals
c^{\prime}\cdot p_{3d}
when
|x|=Re^{1/d}
. The dilatation is independent of
d,c
, and
R
. We have

(10)

c^{\prime}=c\cdot R^{-2d}.

Proof.

Define the dilations
\tau(x)=R^{-1}x
and
\sigma(x)=c^{\prime}R^{3d}x
. Let
P:A(1,e^{1/d})\rightarrow\{|x|\leq e^{3}\}
be the quasiregular mapping from Theorem
2
. We claim that

(11)

F_{R,d,c}(x):=\sigma\circ P\circ\tau(x)

is the desired mapping. This follows from the property that for all
R>0
, we have
p_{d}(Rx)=R^{d}p_{d}(x)
. Indeed, if
|x|=R
, then by (
10
)

\displaystyle F_{R,d,c}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma\circ P\left(\frac{1}{R}x\right)\ =\sigma\circ p_{d}\left(\frac{1}{R}x\right)=c^{\prime}R^{3d}\cdot p_{d}\left(\frac{1}{R}x\right)=c^{\prime}R^{2d}\cdot p_{d}(x)=c\cdot p_{d}(x).

On the other hand, if
|x|=Re^{1/d}
, then

\displaystyle F_{R,d,c}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma\circ P\left(\frac{1}{R}x\right)=\sigma\circ p_{3d}\left(\frac{1}{R}x\right)=c^{\prime}R^{3d}\cdot p_{3d}\left(\frac{1}{R}x\right)=c^{\prime}\cdot p_{3d}(x),

as desired.
∎

Let
R_{1}>2
,
c_{1}=1
, and define

(12)

d_{n}=3^{n}.

Next, proceeding inductively, suppose that we have constructed
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
and
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}
. Define

(13)

c_{n+1}=c_{n}R_{n}^{-2d_{n}}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ R_{n+1}=c_{n+1}(2R_{n})^{d_{n+1}}.

Finally, define
S_{n}=e^{1/d_{n}}R_{n}
. The function we consider is, for
n\geq 1
,

(14)

f(x)=\begin{cases}p_{d_{1}}(x)&\text{if }|x|\leq R_{1}\\
F_{R_{n},d_{n},c_{n}}(x)&\text{if }R_{n}\leq|x|\leq S_{n}\\
c_{n+1}\cdot p_{d_{n+1}}(x)&\text{if }S_{n}\leq|x|\leq R_{n+1}\end{cases}

Then
f
is a quasiregular mapping by Theorem
7
. Note that we have

(15)

R_{n+1}=c_{n+1}|p_{d_{n+1}}(x)|=|f(x)|\quad\mbox{when }|x|=2R_{n}.

Recalling that
L
is the non-negative
x_{3}
-axis, we make the following observation for later purposes.

Lemma 8
.

The map
f
sends
L
homeomorphically onto
L
.

Proof.

For any odd integer
d
, the definition of the power map
p_{d}
via the solution of the Schröder equation
p_{d}\circ\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}\circ d
implies that
p_{d}
maps
L
homeomorphically into
L
. Thus the same is true for any of the scaled power maps in the definition of
f
from (
14
). Moreover, as
F_{R_{n},d_{n},c_{n}}
is obtained from
P
by pre- and post-composing by dilations, it follows from Lemma
6
that
F_{R_{n},d_{n},c_{n}}
maps
L\cap A(R_{n},S_{n})
homeomorphically into

. As

is continuous, the lemma follows.
∎

4.2.
Covering properties

It will be useful to know that
R_{n+1}
is much larger than the previous term in the sequence.

Lemma 9
.

For all
n\geq 1
we have

R_{n+1}\geq 2^{d_{n+1}}R_{n}^{d_{n-1}}.

Proof.

It is clear that

R_{2}=c_{1}R_{1}^{-2d_{1}}(2R_{1})^{d_{2}}=2^{d_{2}}R_{1}^{d_{1}}\geq R_{1}^{3}.

Let
n\geq 2
and, for the sake of induction, suppose that
R_{j+1}\geq 2^{d_{j+1}}R_{j}^{d_{j-1}}
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
. Then in particular
R_{j+1}\geq R_{j}^{3}
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
, and hence
R_{n}\geq R_{j}^{3}
. Now, using this and (
13
),

\displaystyle R_{n+1}

\displaystyle=c_{n+1}(2R_{n})^{d_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=2^{d_{n+1}}R_{n}^{d_{n+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}R_{j}^{-2d_{j}}

\displaystyle=2^{d_{n+1}}R_{n}^{d_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}R_{j}^{-2d_{j}}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d_{n+1}}R_{n}^{d_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}R_{n}^{-2d_{j-1}}

\displaystyle=2^{d_{n+1}}R_{n}^{d_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}2d_{j-1}}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d_{n+1}}R_{n}^{d_{n-1}}.

The lemma now follows by induction.
∎

For
k\geq 1
, define the round rings

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=A\left(\frac{1}{4}R_{k},4R_{k}\right)

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=A\left(4R_{k},\frac{1}{4}R_{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle V_{k}

\displaystyle=A\left(\frac{3}{2}R_{k},\frac{5}{2}R_{k}\right).

Clearly
V_{k}\subset A_{k}
.

Lemma 10
.

For
k\geq 1
, we have
B(0,4R_{k})\subset f(A_{k})
.

Proof.

As the ring
\{x:R_{k}\leq|x|\leq S_{k}\}
is contained in
A_{k}
, and as
f
agrees with the map
F_{R_{k},d_{k},c_{k}}
on this ring, it follows by Theorem
7
that
f(A_{k})
contains the ball
B(0,c_{k}R_{k}^{d_{k}}e^{3})
. By (
13
) and Lemma
9
, we have

\displaystyle c_{k}R_{k}^{d_{k}}e^{3}

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}R_{k}^{2d_{k}}R_{k}^{d_{k}}e^{3}

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}R_{k}^{d_{k+1}}e^{3}

\displaystyle=R_{k+1}2^{-d_{k+1}}e^{3}

\displaystyle\geq R_{k}^{d_{k-1}}e^{3}>4R_{k},

from which the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 11
.

For
k\geq 1
, we have
f(B_{k})\subset\operatorname{int}B_{k+1}
.

Proof.

As
S_{k}=e^{1/d_{k}}R_{k}<\tfrac{3}{2}R_{k}
, we have
f|_{B_{k}}=c_{k+1}\cdot p_{d_{k+1}}
. Therefore,
f
maps the round ring
B_{k}
to another round ring. When
|x|=4R_{k}
, we have by (
13
),

\displaystyle|f(x)|

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}|p_{d_{k+1}}(x)|

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}4^{d_{k+1}}R^{d_{k+1}}_{k}

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}2^{d_{k+1}}2^{d_{k+1}}R^{d_{k+1}}_{k}

\displaystyle=2^{d_{k+1}}R_{k+1}>4R_{k+1}.

When
|x|=\frac{1}{4}R_{k+1}
, we have by (
13
),

\displaystyle|f(x)|

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}|p_{d_{k+1}}(x)|

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}4^{-d_{k+1}}R^{d_{k+1}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=c_{k+2}R_{k+1}^{2d_{k+1}}4^{-d_{k+1}}R^{d_{k+1}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(c_{k+2}2^{d_{k+2}}R_{k+1}^{d_{k+2}})\cdot(2^{-d_{k+2}}\cdot 4^{-d_{k+1}})

\displaystyle=2^{-5d_{k+1}}R_{k+2}<\frac{1}{4}R_{k+2}.

It follows from these estimates that
f(B_{k})
is a round ring contained inside
\operatorname{int}B_{k+1}
.
∎

Lemma 12
.

For
k\geq 1
, the inner boundary component of

is mapped by

into

and the outer boundary component of
V_{k}
is mapped by
f
into

.
In particular,
A_{k+1}\subset f(V_{k})
.

Proof.

Again as
S_{k}<\tfrac{3}{2}R_{k}
, we have that
f|_{V_{k}}=c_{k+1}\cdot p_{d_{k+1}}
. So the round ring
V_{k}
is mapped to another round ring. Starting with the inner boundary component of

, when
|x|=\frac{3}{2}R_{k}
, we have by (

),

\displaystyle|f(x)|

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}|p_{d_{k+1}}(x)|

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{d_{k+1}}(2R_{k})^{d_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{d_{k+1}}R_{k+1}.

It follows that

. Moreover, by Lemma

, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{d_{k+1}}R_{k+1}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{d_{k+1}}2^{d_{k+1}}R_{k}^{d_{k-1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{d_{k+1}}R_{k}^{d_{k-1}}

We conclude that the inner boundary component of

is mapped into

.

Turning now to the outer boundary component, when

, we have by (

\displaystyle|f(x)|

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}|p_{d_{k+1}}(x)|

\displaystyle=c_{k+1}\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^{d_{k+1}}(2R_{k})^{d_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^{d_{k+1}}R_{k+1}

By the Maximum Principle, and as

.
∎

For

and

, define

\Gamma_{k,n}=\{x:f^{j}(x)\in V_{k+j}\text{ for all }j=0,\dots,n\}.

whose forward orbits remain in

As

is a power map, we see that

is also a round ring and, moreover, that for a fixed

for all

.
We show next that the nested intersection of these round rings is indeed a sphere.

Lemma 13
.

The set
\Gamma_{k}
is a round sphere
\{x:|x|=t_{k}\}
.

We note that since
\Gamma_{k}\subset V_{k}
it follows that

as

.

Proof of Lemma
13
.

We denote the modulus of a round ring

by
\operatorname{mod}R=\log(s/r)
. If the round ring

then, since

is the power map

, we have that

and hence

For

, the set

is a round ring in

, so by repeatedly applying the above we see that

\operatorname{mod}f^{n}(\Gamma_{k,n})=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}d_{k+j}\right)\operatorname{mod}\Gamma_{k,n}.

By (

),
\operatorname{mod}f^{n}(\Gamma_{k,n})=\operatorname{mod}V_{k+n}=\log\frac{5}{3}
. It follows that

as

. Since
\Gamma_{k,n}
is a nested sequence of closed round rings, we conclude that
\Gamma_{k}
is a sphere.
∎

4.3.
Spherical Julia components

Next we show that
\Gamma_{k}
is contained in the Julia set
J(f)
. Recalling the definition from section

, we do this by showing directly that any neighbourhood of a point in
\Gamma_{k}
has a forward orbit that “blows up” to cover all of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
k\geq 1
, let
x_{0}\in\Gamma_{k}
and let
U
be a neighbourhood of
x_{0}
. Then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
we have
f^{n}(U)\supset V_{k+n}
.

Proof.

By (
17
) and Lemma

, the
(\Gamma_{k,n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
are nested ring domains converging to
{\Gamma_{k}=\{x:|x|=t_{k}\}}
. Therefore

(19)

\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(\Gamma_{k,n})=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[a_{n},b_{n}]

where
(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is increasing,
(b_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is decreasing and

(20)

\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}=\log t_{k}.

Now, pick
y\in\mathcal{Z}^{-1}(x_{0})
and
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathcal{Z}(B(y,2\epsilon))\subset U
. Write
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\log t_{k})
and, for

, let

By (
20
) there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all

, we have

. Observe that

(21)

\mathcal{Z}(D_{n})\subset U\cap\Gamma_{k,n}.

By increasing

if necessary, we can also assume that for all

, we have

\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_{k+i}>\frac{2}{\epsilon}.

On
\Gamma_{k,n}
, the iterates

are just compositions of scaled versions of power maps for

. More precisely, we have

For

is a solution of the Schröder equation

is an affine map. That is, for

for some

. Then, for
n\geq N
, by (
21
) and (
22
), we have

\displaystyle f^{n}(U)

\displaystyle\supset f^{n}(\mathcal{Z}(D_{n}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}(L_{n}(D_{n}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}(d_{k+1}\ldots d_{k+n}D_{n}+(0,0,h_{n}))

By construction,
d_{k+1}\ldots d_{k+n}D_{n}
is a cuboid with a square base of side length at least
4
. As
\mathcal{Z}
has periods
(4,0,0)
and
(0,4,0)
, it follows by this, (
19
), (
22
) again and (
18
) that

\displaystyle f^{n}(U)

\displaystyle\supset\mathcal{Z}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[d_{k+1}\ldots d_{k+n}a_{n}+h_{n},d_{k+1}\ldots d_{k+n}b_{n}+h_{n}])

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}(L_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[a_{n},b_{n}]))

\displaystyle=f^{n}(\mathcal{Z}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[a_{n},b_{n}]))

\displaystyle=f^{n}(\Gamma_{k,n})

\displaystyle=V_{k+n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 15
.

The round sphere
\Gamma_{k}
belongs to
J(f)
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in\Gamma_{k}
and let
U
be a neighbourhood of
x_{0}
. Then by Lemma
14
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
we have by Lemma
12
and Lemma

\displaystyle f^{n+2}(U)

\displaystyle\supset f^{2}(V_{k+n})

\displaystyle\supset f(A_{k+n+1})

\displaystyle\supset B(0,4R_{k+n+1}).

Thus

\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}f^{n}(U)=\mathbb{R}^{3}

and we conclude that
x_{0}\in J(f)
.
∎

To show that
\Gamma_{k}
is in fact a component of
J(f)
, we need to turn to the quasi-Fatou set.

Lemma 16
.

For
k\geq 1
, we have
B_{k}\subset QF(f)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
11
,
f
maps each ring
B_{k}
into
\operatorname{int}B_{k+1}
and it immediately follows that
\operatorname{int}B_{k+1}
is contained in
QF(f)
as the forward orbit of
\operatorname{int}B_{k+1}
omits a neighbourhood of the origin. By Lemma
11
again, we obtain that
B_{k}\subset QF(f)
.
∎

Corollary 17
.

Let
k\geq 2
. Then
\Gamma_{k}
is a component of
J(f)
.

Proof.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
f^{n}(\Gamma_{k,n})=V_{k+n}
. By Lemma
12
,
f
maps the inner boundary component of
V_{k+n}
into
B_{k+n}
and the outer boundary component of
V_{k+n}
into
B_{k+n+1}
. By Lemma
16
and the complete invariance of
QF(f)
, it follows that
\partial\Gamma_{k,n}\subset QF(f)
. As
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{k,n}=\Gamma_{k}
, it follows that
\Gamma_{k}
is a component of
J(f)
.
∎

We next observe that the
B_{k}
are contained in distinct quasi-Fatou components.

Lemma 18
.

Let
k>j\geq 1
. Then
B_{k}
and
B_{j}
are contained in different components of
QF(f)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
15
, the sphere
\Gamma_{k}=\{x:|x|=t_{k}\}
is contained in
V_{k}\cap J(f)
.

Recalling that
V_{k}=A(\tfrac{3}{2}R_{k},\tfrac{5}{2}R_{k})
and
B_{k}=A(4R_{k},\tfrac{1}{4}R_{k+1})
, we see that
B_{k}
is contained in the unbounded component of the complement of
\Gamma_{k}
and that
B_{k-1}
is contained in the bounded component of the complement of
\Gamma_{k}
.
We conclude that
B_{j}
is also contained in the bounded component of the complement of
\Gamma_{k}
, and thus
\Gamma_{k}
separates
B_{j}
and
B_{k}
. This proves the lemma.
∎

As
B_{k}\subset QF(f)
, denote by
\Omega_{k}
the component of
QF(f)
which contains
B_{k}
. We may now give the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Part (a) follows immediately from Corollary
17
.

For part (b), by construction, it follows that
f(\Gamma_{k,n})=\Gamma_{k+1,n-1}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\geq 1
. By taking the intersection over
n
, we conclude that
f
maps
\Gamma_{k}
onto
\Gamma_{k+1}
.

For part (c), as the quasi-Fatou component
\Omega_{k}
contains the ring
B_{k}
, but is disjoint from the spheres
\Gamma_{k}
and
\Gamma_{k+1}
, it must be bounded and hollow.

For part (d), we know from the proof of Corollary
17
that
\partial\Gamma_{k,n}
is contained in
QF(f)
. If we can show that for all
k
and
n
the outer boundary component of
\Gamma_{k,n}
is contained in
\Omega_{k}
and the inner boundary component of
\Gamma_{k,n}
is contained in
\Omega_{k-1}
, then we are done. To do this, we will analyze what happens on the non-negative
x_{3}
-axis
L
.

Note that, by (
18
), the inner and outer boundary components of
\Gamma_{k,n}
are mapped by
f^{n}
to the inner and outer boundary components of
V_{k+n}
. Thus, by Lemma
12
,
f^{n+1}(\partial_{inner}\Gamma_{k,n})\subset B_{k+n}
and
f^{n+1}(\partial_{outer}\Gamma_{k,n})\subset B_{k+n+1}
. Now consider the line segment
L_{k,n}\subset L
that joins the outer boundary component of
\Gamma_{k,n}
to the inner boundary component of
\Gamma_{k+1,n}
. We see that
f^{n+1}(L_{k,n})\subset B_{k+n+1}
because this holds for the images of the endpoints of
L_{k,n}
and, by Lemma
8
, the iterates of
f
act homeomorphically on

. Thus
L_{k,n}\subset QF(f)
by Lemma
16
and the complete invariance of
QF(f)
.

So we have that
L_{k,n}\cup\partial_{outer}\Gamma_{k,n}\cup\partial_{inner}\Gamma_{k+1,n}
is a connected subset of
QF(f)
that intersects
B_{k}
, and is therefore contained in
\Omega_{k}
. In particular,
\partial_{outer}\Gamma_{k,n}\subset\Omega_{k}
and
\partial_{inner}\Gamma_{k,n}\subset\Omega_{k-1}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Constructing quasiregular maps meeting growth conditions

Given
R>0
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
c>0
, denote by
F_{R,d,c}
the quasiregular map from Theorem
7
.

Suppose that
\lambda_{n}\geq 1
for all
n
. Construct sequences
(R_{n})
and
(S_{n})
for
n\geq 1
as follows. Let
R_{1}=1
,
S_{1}=e^{1/3}
and for
n\geq 1
, set
R_{n+1}=\lambda_{n}S_{n}
and
S_{n+1}=e^{1/d_{n+1}}R_{n+1}
, recalling that
d_{n}=3^{n}
.

We construct a quasiregular map
f
depending on the sequence
(\lambda_{n})
as follows.

•

On
\overline{B(0,R_{1})}
we define
f(x)=p_{3}(x)
.

•

On
A(R_{1},S_{1})
we define
f(x)=F_{R_{1},3,1}(x)
.

•

On
A(S_{1},R_{2})
, we set
C_{1}=1
and define
f(x)=C_{1}\cdot p_{9}(x)
.

•

For
n\geq 1
, suppose
C_{n}
is given, then on
A(R_{n+1},S_{n+1})
we define
f(x)=F_{R_{n+1},d_{n+1},C_{n}}(x)
.

•

Then we set
C_{n+1}=C_{n}R_{n+1}^{-2d_{n+1}}
and on
A(S_{n+1},R_{n+2})
we define
f(x)=C_{n+1}\cdot p_{d_{n+2}}(x)
.

Thus on rings of the form
A(R_{n},S_{n})
we interpolate, whereas on rings of the form
A(S_{n},R_{n+1})
we hold up the growth of the map by keeping it as a power-type map.

For
r\geq 0
, we denote the maximum modulus by
M(r,f)=\max\limits_{|x|=r}|f(x)|
.

Lemma 19
.

For
n\geq 0
, we have

R_{n+1}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\right)\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right),\quad\text{ and }\quad S_{n+1}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\right)\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right).

Moreover, we have

M(R_{n+1},f)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{d_{j+1}}\right)e^{3n},\quad\text{ and }\quad M(S_{n+1},f)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{d_{j+1}}\right)e^{3(n+1)}.

Proof.

This is an elementary induction calculation that we omit.
∎

Observe that to obtain a quasiregular mapping on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
via this construction, we need

(23)

\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\to\infty\quad\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

Theorem 20
.

Consider the constructions above.

•

Suppose that
(\eta_{k})
is any real sequence. Then we may choose
(\lambda_{n})
satisfying (
23
) so that there exists a sequence
r_{k}\to\infty
with
r_{k}<2^{k}
and

(24)

\frac{\log\log M(r_{k},f)}{\log r_{k}}\geq\eta_{k}.

•

Given any positive sequence
(\epsilon_{k})
, we may choose
(\lambda_{n})
satisfying (
23
) such that, for
k\geq 2
,

(25)

\frac{\log\log M(R_{k},f)}{\log R_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}.

In other words, the first part says that the transcendental type quasiregular map
f
can have arbitrarily fast growth, and the second part says that
f
may have arbitrarily slow growth.

Proof.

By Lemma
19
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\log\log M(R_{n+1},f)}{\log R_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\log\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{d_{j+1}}\right)e^{3n}\right]}{\log\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\right)\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}3^{j+1}\log\lambda_{j}+3n\right]}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\lambda_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{3^{j}}}.

For the first part, take
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n_{1}}=1
, where
n_{1}
is large. Then, by Lemma
19
,
R_{n_{1}+1}\leq e^{1/2}
and
M(R_{n_{1}+1},f)=e^{3n_{1}}
. Take
r_{1}=R_{n_{1}+1}
. Then
r_{1}\leq e^{1/2}<2
and (
24
) holds for
k=1
provided
n_{1}
is chosen large enough.

We now inductively define a sequence
(\lambda_{n})
and a subsequence
(r_{k})
of
(R_{n})
as follows. Suppose that for some
k\geq 1
we have chosen integers
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
and values
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n_{k}}
such that
r_{k}=R_{n_{k}+1}
satisfies
r_{k}<2^{k}
and (
24
). We then set
\lambda_{n_{k}+1}=2e^{-1/2}
and
\lambda_{j}=1
for
j=n_{k}+2,\ldots,N
where
N
is chosen large enough that

\frac{\log\log M(R_{N+1},f)}{\log R_{N+1}}=\frac{\log\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}+1}3^{j+1}\log\lambda_{j}+3N\right]}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}+1}\log\lambda_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{3^{j}}}>\eta_{k+1}.

(Note that the denominator here is bounded as
N\to\infty
but the numerator is unbounded.) We set
n_{k+1}=N
and
r_{k+1}=R_{N+1}
so that (
24
) holds for
k+1
. Moreover,

r_{k+1}=2e^{-1/2}\exp\left(\sum_{j=n_{k}+1}^{N}\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right)r_{k}\leq 2r_{k}<2^{k+1}.

This completes the induction and the sequence
(r_{k})
has the required properties. Since
\lambda_{j}
equals
2e^{-1/2}>1
infinitely often, we see that (
23
) holds and
r_{k}\to\infty
.

The second part is also proved by induction. Suppose we are given
(\epsilon_{k})
and that
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}
have been chosen such that
R_{k}
satisfies (
25
) for
k=2,\ldots,n
(we make no assumption in the case that
n=1
). Writing
y=\log\lambda_{n}
, we have that

\frac{\log\log M(R_{n+1},f)}{\log R_{n+1}}=\frac{\log\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}3^{j+1}\log\lambda_{j}+3n+3^{n+1}y\right]}{\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\log\lambda_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{3^{j}}+y}\to 0

as
y\to\infty
. Thus choosing
\lambda_{n}
large enough gives (
25
) for
k=n+1
.
∎

Remark
.

If we follow the above construction but with
