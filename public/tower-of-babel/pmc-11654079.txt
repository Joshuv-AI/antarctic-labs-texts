Erector spinae plane block for posterior spine fusion in children
Authors: Raghuraman M. Sethuraman, Geetha SoundaryaUdayakumar, Gayathri Ramesh, Kailash Sarathy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This manuscript, a Letter to the Editor, is in response to the study protocol that intended to analyze the effect of the erector spinae plane block (ESPB) in pediatric patients undergoing posterior spinal fusion (PSF) for the correction of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS). A few concerns regarding that protocol are raised here.

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the study protocol that intended to analyze the effect of the erector spinae plane block (ESPB) in pediatric patients undergoing posterior spinal fusion (PSF) for the correction of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) [1]. We compliment Gao et al. [1] for this initiation and would like to raise a few concerns.

Although we agree that ESPB was not evaluated adequately in children for this type of surgery, we feel that Gao et al. [1] could have mentioned a recently published study [2]. That study by Domagalska et al. [2] was published in May 2024 when this protocol [1] was under consideration in the journal. Nevertheless, we still feel that it could have been added during the revision stage at least. Thus, the statement in the conclusion “To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate ESPB regarding efficacy in pediatric AIS patients undergoing PSF” [1] is incorrect. Importantly, that study assessed the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratios in addition to the pain scores and opioid consumption [2].

Our main concern is that this protocol [1] did not specify the extension of scoliosis and the Cobb angle of scoliosis for the inclusion criteria. Considering the major impact of these factors on the magnitude of pain, these aspects should have been considered. We believe that Gao et al. would at least consider these points during the analysis as they would have probably completed this study as mentioned in the published protocol [1]. Additionally, the assessment of the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) score should have preferably been done based on the pictorial description of QoR as the study involved pediatric patients [3].
