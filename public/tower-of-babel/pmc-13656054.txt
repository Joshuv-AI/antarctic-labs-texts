Preventing respiratory deterioration is a patient-centered outcome!
Authors: Giovanni Landoni, Federico Mattia Oliva, Rosario Losiggio, Filippo Consolo, Stefano Turi
Published: 2026

We thank Frat, Coudroy, and Thille (1) for their insightful editorial commentary on the NAVIGATE trial (2) and for placing the study’s findings within the broader debate regarding non-invasive respiratory support outside intensive care units (ICUs). Their central question “whether preventing deterioration in mild acute respiratory failure (ARF) represents a clinically meaningful benefit” merits a direct answer, along with guidance on how to interpret our study considering updated evidence.

The NAVIGATE study was not conducted to demonstrate that non-invasive ventilation (NIV) can replace intubation or ICU admission when these are indicated. Instead, it evaluated a preventive strategy based on a brief, standardized course of NIV initiated in general wards prior to the onset of ARF. The 523 randomized participants presented with mild ARF and no immediate indication for mechanical ventilation or ICU admission. Eligibility criteria were defined to deliberately include a clinical population at a very early and common stage of the disease, comprising patients with a PaO2/FiO2 ratio between 200 and 300 mmHg or an oxygen saturation below 92% on room air. At baseline, approximately 96% met an oxygenation criterion, whereas only 30% showed signs of respiratory distress (2). These are patients encountered daily on medical and surgical wards, yet they have rarely been enrolled in NIV studies and are seldom offered positive airway pressure ventilation in routine clinical practice. This distinction is fundamental; indeed, the NAVIGATE study evaluated prevention, not rescue therapy for advanced respiratory failure. Other studies did not enroll patients at such an early stage of respiratory failure, and in current clinical practice NIV is not offered to these patients.

The intervention was also more specific than the generic term NIV might suggest. Ninety per cent of treated patients received continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), without inspiratory pressure support, at a median positive end-expiratory pressure of 7 cmH2O; bilevel NIV was reserved primarily for cases of hypercapnia. Two-hour cycles were administered every eight hours. This low-intensity strategy reduced progression to severe ARF from 28.3% to 18.5% [relative risk (RR) 0.65, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.48–0.90], an absolute difference of 9.8 percentage points. The incidence of severe hypoxemia fell from 21.3% to 12.5%, severe respiratory distress from 24.0% to 14.7%, and the subsequent need for prolonged NIV (at least 12 hours per day) from 23.3% to 11.3%. No excess in episodes of respiratory arrest, hemodynamic instability, pneumothorax, vomiting, excessive secretions, or mortality was observed (2). Thus, approximately ten additional patients per 100 avoided severe ARF, and twelve per 100 avoided prolonged rescue NIV.

Frat and colleagues rightly observed that no formal tool was used in the NAVIGATE study to assess patient-reported dyspnea or comfort (1). This is a limitation that should be addressed in future clinical trials. However, this does not imply that the primary outcome lacked patient-centered meaning. In the NAVIGATE study, severe respiratory distress was not defined by an isolated laboratory threshold; rather, it required the presence of marked and persistent dyspnea, the use of accessory respiratory muscles, or paradoxical thoracoabdominal movement, despite the administration of oxygen therapy. Air hunger and the sensation of inability to breathe are among the most distressing forms of acute suffering and may be associated with panic and a sense of impending death. Preventing such a condition has intrinsic value, even when the assessment is performed by physician rather based on questionnaires. Patient-centered outcomes are not limited to mortality or formalized assessment scales.

We regret that this clinical meaning may not have emerged clearly enough from our original report. Fig. 2 of the NAVIGATE original manuscript was specifically designed to show the distribution of respiratory patient-centered outcomes, including no worsening, severe hypoxemia, severe respiratory distress, intubation and respiratory death (2). The figure shows that 81.5% of patients assigned to early NIV experienced no worsening, compared with 71.7% for those in the usual care group. Furthermore, early NIV reduced severe respiratory distress and prolonged NIV by 9.3 and 12.0 percentage points, respectively. These are not merely changes in a physiological surrogate, but represent less breathlessness, reduced respiratory muscle effort and less need for additional breathing support. In addition, while we accept that direct patient-reported measures would have strengthened this conclusion, we do not accept that their absence makes persistent severe dyspnea clinically unimportant to patients.

The interpretation of downstream outcomes also requires attention to the trial's ethical design. Participants were randomized a median of two to three days after hospital admission. In the usual-care group, NIV could be started when it became clinically necessary, but only after the patient had reached the primary endpoint of severe ARF. Once severe distress occurred, attending clinicians frequently initiated prolonged CPAP or bilevel NIV, either in the ward or in the ICU, according to local resources and clinical judgement. Although this may have influenced the subsequent clinical course, it was necessary and ethically unavoidable. The randomized comparison was therefore early NIV versus delayed or rescue NIV and not NIV versus persistent oxygen therapy throughout hospitalization.

The similar rates of intubation, ICU admission and mortality should be interpreted within that framework. Prolonged NIV was used in 23.3% of patients in the usual care group and likely prevented further deterioration in some of them, thereby attenuating any difference in later outcomes. The trial was not powered to detect differences in these relatively infrequent secondary endpoints. Thus, the lack of a statistically significant difference cannot establish equivalence or retroactively erase the earlier prevention signal. In contrast, NAVIGATE alone cannot claim a mortality benefit. The scientifically appropriate conclusion lies between those extremes: early NIV prevented clinically important deterioration, while protocol-permitted rescue treatment reduced the ability to detect differences in downstream events. In a randomized trial conducted with close clinical oversight, the ethical success of rescue treatment can make an effective preventive intervention appears neutral on survival (Figure 1).

Since the publication of NAVIGATE, Losiggio et al. have updated the systematic review and meta-analysis of early NIV started prehospital or outside the ICU, incorporating the NAVIGATE results (3). Thirteen randomized or quasi-randomized trials involving 2,172 patients were included. The findings showed that early NIV reduced endotracheal intubation from 10.8% to 5.2% (RR 0.49, 95% CI: 0.36–0.66; I2=0%; number needed to treat: 18). Moreover, trial sequential analysis exceeded the required information size and supported a conclusive benefit for this outcome. ICU admission was also reduced (15.5% versus 19.2%; RR 0.78, 95% CI: 0.62–0.97; I2=9%; number needed to treat: 27). Results remained robust after exclusion of high-risk-of-bias studies, restriction to face-mask interfaces and stratification by provider type. The CPAP subgroup had an RR for intubation of 0.39, whereas the RR for paramedic-delivered subgroup and trials including causes other than acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema was 0.43 and 0.52, respectively. Finally, mortality was not significantly different, a finding compatible with low event rates, heterogeneity and frequent crossover to rescue NIV (3). Given this wider evidence base, the benefit of early NIV outside the ICU can no longer be described as only a short-term physiological effect.

High-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) deserves a balanced discussion. It is comfortable, simple to apply at the bedside and supported by in-hospital evidence in selected patients. However, HFNO and CPAP are not interchangeable: HFNO primarily delivers heated, humidified oxygen at high flow, washes out upper-airway dead space and produces a mode, variable alveolar pressure, while CPAP provides controlled positive pressure throughout the respiratory cycle, recruits and stabilizes alveoli, increases functional residual capacity and reduces inspiratory effort and muscle workload. Moreover, concerns that NIV may exacerbate patient self-inflicted lung injury through the combination of vigorous inspiratory effort and pressure support should not be indiscriminately applied to NAVIGATE where 90% of patients received CPAP without inspiratory assistance and at modest pressure. Any non-invasive support can fail and neither CPAP nor HFNO should delay intubation when deterioration requires it. Therefore, the relevant question is not which device is universally better, but which physiological effect, evidence base and logistical profile fit the patient and setting.

The apparent simplicity of HFNO also relies on a well-equipped hospital infrastructure, a factor that often goes unnoticed. HFNO in adults commonly requires very high gas flow rates, a reliable oxygen source, a power supply, heating and humidification systems, and sufficient cylinder autonomy during transport. A recent collaborative review by Vieux et al. concluded that evidence regarding prehospital HFNO remains extremely limited and that oxygen and power requirements substantially restrict its routine use; conversely, CPAP and bilevel NIV have established roles in the prehospital setting, particularly for acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema and exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (4). HFNO is therefore a viable option for certain well-equipped units and transport systems, but it is not currently a realistic substitute for CPAP in many ambulances, remote areas, or resource-limited healthcare systems.

Technology is also changing implementation methods. Salurso and colleagues recently described a compact, ready-to-use CPAP device designed specifically for treating ARF in the out-of-hospital setting (5). Under high-fidelity simulation conditions, the device maintained a target positive end-expiratory pressure of approximately 5 cmH2O across various respiratory scenarios; it was successfully applied by 93% of operators regardless of their experience level, and users founded its operation to be rapid and intuitive. Human validation remains necessary, but such devices challenge the assumption that effective positive pressure support always requires a complex ventilator, an elaborate set-up, or an ICU environment.

Similarly, expert recommendations are shifting towards earlier treatment. Using the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method, Donadello et al. assembled an interdisciplinary working group to evaluate out-of-hospital respiratory distress within the context of diagnostic uncertainty typical of first medical contact (6). The expert group deemed it appropriate to intervene before establishing a definitive diagnosis by initiating oxygen or NIV within approximately ten minutes, where indicated; furthermore, they considered advanced respiratory support (such as NIV or CPAP) preferable to oxygen alone in case of moderate-to-severe distress, recommending the continuation of NIV during ambulance transport. NIV was deemed appropriate for both symptom improvement and the prevention of clinical deterioration. These statements are based on expert consensus rather than evidence from studies, yet they provide a practical link between physiology, evidence from randomized studies, and the realities of emergency systems.

The broader message of NAVIGATE is therefore relevant across settings with varying resource availability. In low-resource settings, the early and cost-effective use of CPAP can prevent progression to severe respiratory failure; a condition otherwise treatable only through prolonged NIV, intubation, or transfer to an ICU that may be ill-equipped or nonexistent. In high-resource settings, selected patients can be safely managed in general wards, reducing the need for prolonged NIV in high-acuity units and potentially lowering costs. In intermediate-resource settings, preventing clinical deterioration allows monitored beds, ventilators, and specialized staff to be reserved for patients who have no other clinical options. Across all settings, the most immediate benefit is the same: fewer patients progress to severe dyspnea and exhausting respiratory effort (Figure 2).

This should not be interpreted as an endorsement of the indiscriminate use of NIV in every hypoxemic patient, nor as authorization to provide advanced respiratory support without appropriate safeguards. The NAVIGATE study relied on collaboration between ward physicians and an emergency team, repeated reassessments, explicit contraindications, and the escalation of care when necessary (2). Implementation should include staff training, pulse oximetry and clinical monitoring, proficiency in the use of interfaces, predefined criteria for treatment failure, and rapid access to higher-level of care. NIV must never become a means to delay intubation in a patient whose condition is deteriorating.

Our key message is that early, protocol-driven CPAP or NIV administered outside the ICU can be beneficial throughout the acute care pathway. The NAVIGATE study demonstrated the prevention of severe ARF in a population previously overlooked by clinical trials; an updated meta-analysis shown reduction in intubation and ICU admission across more than 2, 000 randomized patients; expert consensus supported very early out-of-hospital treatment; and portable devices were proven useful in facilitating easier, progressive implementation (2-5). HFNO is an important complementary modality within adequately equipped systems, but in the pre-hospital setting, its use remains constrained by evidence limitations, oxygen consumption, and power requirements (4). Future clinical trials should include patient-reported dyspnea, comfort, treatment burden, days free from respiratory support, costs, and resource utilization, while ensuring ethical pathways for rescue interventions. Until then, the prevention of severe respiratory failure should be recognized for what it is: a clinically significant, patient-centered success, not merely an intermediate physiological parameter.
