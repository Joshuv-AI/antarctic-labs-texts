Fullerene-derived hierarchical sp2 carbon exhibiting united hardness, elasticity, and thermal stability
Authors: Ruiqi He, Zhongting Zhang, Peiyang Mu, Junkai Li, Chenglin Pua, Guoliang Niu, Yehua Huang, Jiacheng Sun, Runji Wang, Haojie Leng, Gongkai Wang, Leiming Fang, YinBo Zhu, Hongli Chen, Hanyu Liu, Yanchao Wang, Kun Song, Dong Liu, Jie Chen, Lei Xie, Xiping Chen, Lin Gu, Shuming Peng, Huiyang Gou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon materials possess attractive mechanical and thermal properties, yet creating bulk sp²-bonded carbon that unites high hardness, elastic recoverability, and thermal stability has remained elusive. Mechanical anisotropy, low damage tolerance, and structural instability under stress or at high temperatures hinder the development of dense sp²-carbon architectures for demanding environments. Here we report a dual-phase sp2 carbon produced from C60 at high pressure through progressive structural reconstruction, yielding a hierarchical structure in which stacked graphene nanoclusters are integrated within a disordered sp2-rich amorphous matrix. This architecture accommodates stress by structurally mediated interlayer sliding and elastic compaction, while retaining structural coherence. The resulting material achieves ~ 20 GPa hardness, > 88% elastic recovery, and oxidation resistance near 960 °C in air, representing an unusual combination of mechanical recoverability and thermal stability among representative carbon materials. Simulations show that the combination of stiffness and resilience arises from interfacial reinforcement between the ordered and disordered domains. This work identifies a structural pathway for engineering sp²-carbon systems that overcome long-standing limits in mechanical and thermal performance.

Introduction

Carbon-based materials, enabled by the extraordinary versatility of carbon bonding, offer unique opportunities for achieving multifunctionality. Under extreme pressure and temperature conditions, molecular fullerenes provide access to carbon phases that transcend the structural and property limits of conventional forms. C60, in particular, serves as a prototypical precursor whose symmetric cage collapses irreversibly and reconstructs into dense frameworks with emergent bonding motifs and exceptional mechanical and electronic properties at higher pressure (above 25 GPa)1–4. This collapse involves a complex sequence of covalent bond reconstruction, atomic diffusion, and disorder, where even subtle variations in pressure, temperature, or reaction time produce markedly different bonding topologies. Such kinetic sensitivity underlies the remarkable diversity of structures and properties observed in fullerene-derived carbons.

At moderate pressures (3–12 GPa) and elevated temperatures, fullerene-derived carbons display a striking diversity of structures and properties, reflecting the sensitivity of C60 collapse pathways to both thermodynamic and kinetic factors. Phases synthesized in this regime, sometimes described as superhard amorphous carbon, combine high hardness (~40 GPa with a load of 1.96 N) with low density (~1.9 g/cm3) and partial sp3 bonding, and can exhibit exceptionally high elastic recovery (up to 95% under a much smaller load of 0.5 N)5,6. Yet the mechanical data reported to date remain difficult to reconcile. Variations in applied load, heating temperature window, and reaction duration strongly affect the measured response. For example, short heating periods are reported to yield metastable, high-hardness states, whereas prolonged annealing tends to stabilize softer but highly elastic hard-carbon phases7.

Microstructural studies reveal short-range graphitic order, pronounced disorder, and nanometer-scale clustering, but differ in the reported cluster size, stacking coherence, texture, and sp3 content. These discrepancies reflect the strong dependence of structural evolution on pressure–temperature conditions and polymerization pathways, giving rise to multiple variants described as disordered graphite-like carbon5,8–10, graphitic hard carbon7,11, and nanoclustered graphene phases6. Despite decades of investigation, the precise structural nature of these graphitic fullerene-derived carbons—and how stacked graphitic layers or graphene-like nanoclusters interact with the surrounding amorphous regions—remains insufficiently resolved. A coherent correlation between synthesis conditions, microstructure, and macroscopic properties also remains elusive. Without a validated structural model that integrates both ordered and disordered components, the underlying formation mechanisms and their interactions cannot be fully resolved. Addressing these gaps is essential for developing a unified framework for fullerene-derived carbons and for enabling the rational design of carbon materials that extend beyond the traditional diamond–graphite paradigm.

Here, we address this challenge by integrating precisely controlled high-pressure, high-temperature synthesis with quantitative structural characterization and molecular dynamics simulations to decipher the atomic-scale evolution of C60-derived carbons at 12 GPa across different temperatures. We identify a sp2-based dual-phase carbon structure: stacked multilayer graphene clusters embedded within a disordered sp2-rich amorphous matrix, formed through progressive reconstruction of compressed fullerite under 12 GPa. This alternating ordered-disordered architecture accommodates stress through synergistic interlayer sliding and elastic compaction while preserving structural cohesion, yielding exceptional hardness, ultrahigh elastic recovery, and thermal resilience that surpass conventional sp2 carbon materials. By systematically mapping the transformation pathway, we elucidate the structural origins of these paradoxical properties and provide insights into lightweight, hard, and energy-resilient carbon materials. These findings suggest that hierarchical sp2-rich carbon architectures offer a viable route for improving the balance between stiffness, recoverability, and thermal stability in disordered carbon systems.

Results and discussions

Phase and microstructural evolution

To investigate the phase transition behavior of C60 at moderate pressure, powder samples were compressed to 12 GPa and annealed at target temperatures between 250 and 1800 °C for 1 h at each step, followed by rapid quenching to atmosphere conditions. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the recovered samples (Fig. 1a) show no significant structural changes below 250 °C. At 400 °C, a new diffraction peak emerges at ~13°, although both XRD and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 1) confirm that the overall cubic framework is preserved. This feature is consistent with earlier observations at lower pressures (5 GPa, 300 °C)12, and may originate from pressure-induced distortion of the C60 molecular cages. At 650 °C, the diffraction peaks associated with the FCC lattice of C60 disappear, replaced by a broad halo centered around 27°, indicating the collapse of the fullerene cages and the formation of an amorphous phase. Between 650 and 1600 °C, this broad feature gradually sharpens and shifts toward the (002) diffraction peak of graphite, suggesting a continuous transition from amorphous carbon to a more ordered graphitic structure. This evolution results in a mixed-phase composition of amorphous and graphitic carbon. At 1800 °C, a sharp graphite (002) peak dominates the XRD pattern, accompanied by a weak diamond peak (Supplementary Fig. 4a), suggesting partial crystallization into graphite with traces of diamond formation. Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 4c) corroborates these structural transformations. Below 250 °C, the spectrum is dominated by well-defined C60 vibrational modes, confirming the retention of the molecular structure. Between 400 and 650 °C, the Ag (2) and Hg (8) modes progressively diminish, consistent with cage collapse observed in XRD. The emergence of the characteristic D (~1350 cm−1) and G (~1580 cm−1) bands between 800 and 1000 °C marks the onset of graphitization. To understand the microstructural evolution and bonding states, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) were performed on recovered samples. At 400 °C (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 1a–e), HRTEM and Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) analyses reveal an inhomogeneous microstructure consisting of crystalline, amorphous, and mixed-phase regions. Lattice fringes with spacings of 0.519 and 0.353 nm correspond to the (220) and (004) planes of cubic C60, respectively, indicating that although some distortion and local amorphization have occurred, the molecular structure remains largely intact. Notably, the fringes in this sample remain relatively regular and well aligned, consistent with the preserved molecular-crystal order of cubic C60. The 0.353 nm spacing observed here therefore corresponds to the retained C60 lattice and is distinct from the graphite-like layered spacings discussed for the higher-temperature samples. By 650 °C (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 1f–j), significant structural changes emerge. The disappearance of C60 lattice fringes and the appearance of disordered carbon domains, corroborated by FFT halos, indicate increased amorphization. Nonetheless, isolated ordered regions persist, including a nanocrystalline domain with 0.343 nm spacing, corresponding to the (002) plane of graphite, identified as a 7-layer graphene nanocluster. At 800 °C, the material enters a transitional regime (Fig. 1b–d), exhibiting discrete graphite-like nanoclusters (4–14 stacked layers, 0.328 nm spacing) embedded in an amorphous carbon matrix. It is worth noting that the selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Fig. 1c) is dominated by intensely bright (002) diffraction arcs from the periodic graphitic nanoclusters, which visually mask the weaker and overlapping diffuse scattering of the amorphous carbon. Nevertheless, the substantial presence of the amorphous matrix is unambiguously confirmed by direct real-space HRTEM imaging (Fig. 1d) and the macroscopic XRD profile (Fig. 1a). Furthermore, the distinct nature of these diffraction arcs indicates a strong localized preferential orientation of the graphitic clusters. This pronounced texture originates directly from the specific stress conditions applied during synthesis. Previous studies shows that under non-hydrostatic compression, intense shear strain can strongly promote both structural transitions and crystallite alignment13. In our experiments, the intrinsic deviatoric stress drives plastic deformation of the parent cubic C60 crystals mainly by shear and slip on the close-packed (111) planes6,7. Upon heating, the C60 cages preferentially collapse along these active shear bands, where severe local strain lowers the energy barrier for bond breaking and reformation and thus greatly facilitates structural reconstruction7,8,13. Consequently, the newly formed multilayer graphene nanoclusters naturally align parallel to the original (111) slip planes7,8. By this process of structural inheritance, the orientation of the crystal cluster becomes effectively locked in refs. 6,13. Rather than nucleating randomly, the localized domains preserve a strong structural feature of how the precursor deformed, which ultimately produces the highly textured diffraction pattern we observe. The pressure-induced collapse of fullerene cages under thermal and mechanical load promotes the formation of this dual-phase architecture, comprising interconnected nanoscale graphitic domains within a disordered background. To further improve the statistical reliability and identification accuracy of the dual-phase carbon structure, high-resolution four-dimensional scanning transmission electron microscopy (4D-STEM) analysis was performed over a 117 × 117 nm2 region. The annular dark field (ADF) image (Supplementary Fig. 2a) and the corresponding map (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 2b) resolves a heterogeneous dual-phase architecture over the same area. Specifically, the amorphous carbon forms a continuous matrix, whereas the graphitic component appears as discrete nanoclustered domains embedded within it. These crystalline domains are broadly dispersed throughout the mapped region, with local enrichment in some areas. The mapped crystalline fraction is approximately 23.3%, corresponding to the area fraction within the analyzed field of view. These results provide large-area statistical support for the dual-phase interpretation. In addition, the mapped area also exhibits clear spatial variation in relative density (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Regions of higher relative density appear as dispersed patches, short connected segments, and local clusters, whereas lower relative density forms the surrounding background. In comparison with the phase map (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 2b), the higher-density features are broadly associated with the graphitic domains, suggesting a correlation between local densification and phase heterogeneity.

By 1000 °C (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 3a–e), graphitic fragments elongate (5–20 layers) and adopt fingerprint-like morphologies, while FFT analysis confirms short-range order via paired symmetrical spots. Aggregated amorphous regions suggest potential connectivity between clusters at higher temperatures. At 1300 °C (Supplementary Fig. 3f–j), structural reorganization yields isotropic graphitic assemblies with enhanced long-range order. HRTEM reveals domains containing 10–26 layers and interlayer spacings of 0.322–0.337 nm. At 1600 °C (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 3k–o), oriented nanoclusters (12–20 layers, 0.344 nm spacing) reappear alongside an increase in amorphous carbon, suggesting bonding-state changes. Despite a slight reduction in layer count relative to 1300 °C, these clusters exhibit notable lateral expansion. By 1800 °C (Fig. 1k and Supplementary Fig. 3p–t), graphitic domains dominate, showing 9–26 stacked layers with increased crystallinity and 0.355 nm spacing. These networks, interconnected over large domains, mark a near-complete transition to bulk graphite, with amorphous carbon content significantly reduced. At the same time, the relatively larger local spacings observed in the high-temperature samples likely reflect that the graphene layers in these domains are not perfectly ordered or regularly stacked. While such layers become more extended, they differ from ideal AB-stacked graphite single crystals and remain locally turbostratic and corrugated. In such nanoscale layered domains, the fringe spacing measured from HRTEM can be affected by local curvature and stacking disorder6,7,14, and therefore may be larger than the bulk-averaged interlayer spacing of graphite.

To correlate the observed microstructural evolution with its atomistic origin and bonding changes, ring-statistics and potential-energy analyses were performed (Fig. 2), together with EELS characterization of the bonding states (Fig. 3a, b). The 1s-π* and 1s-σ* transitions at 285 eV and 292 eV indicate sp2 and sp3 hybridization, respectively. Using pristine C60 as a reference, quantitative analysis via the two-window method shows that the sp2-carbon fraction remains above 90% from 250 to 1300 °C3,15, reaching 94.3 ± 1.8% for the 800 °C sample. For the 800 °C sample, this result supports describing the disordered phase as a predominantly sp2-rich amorphous matrix. Above 1300 °C, the sp2 fraction decreases sharply, concurrent with a rise in disordered sp3 content. Integrating these results, we establish a temperature-dependent transformation pathway of C60 under constant pressure. Initial cage distortion begins at 400 °C, followed by progressive structural reconstruction into disordered configurations and the formation of small multilayer graphene clusters up to 650 °C. Between 650 and 800 °C, the reconstructed C60-derived carbon reorganizes into graphene nanoclusters embedded in a disordered sp2-rich amorphous matrix. Continued heating to 1300 °C drives gradual graphitization, increasing both the number of layers and lateral dimensions of graphitic fragments. Between 1300 and 1600 °C, partial conversion of sp2 to disordered sp3 carbon occurs, alongside the reappearance of multilayer nanoclusters. Above 1800 °C, the system undergoes structural refinement into ordered graphite domains, with initial diamond formation. This transformation should be considered in the context of previous high-pressure studies of C60, where ordered cage-preserving 1D and 2D polymerized phases were reported under lower-pressure conditions (1–5 GPa) and were commonly associated with intermolecular bonding, such as [2 + 2] cycloaddition16–19. Under 12 GPa, the intermolecular separation between fullerene molecules is greatly reduced, which enhances intermolecular interactions. Unlike the ordered 1D and 2D polymers stabilized at lower pressures, the present system is subjected to a substantially higher pressure together with elevated temperatures. Under these conditions, the typical cage-preserving polymerized configuration is unlikely to be retained as the final recovered state. As temperature increases, intermolecular linkages may increase, cage distortion accumulates, and the original long-range molecular-crystal order becomes unstable. This is followed by more extensive C-C bond reconstruction, including loss of cage integrity and local cage collapse, after which the carbon network reorganizes into short-range ordered sp2-rich graphitic fragments within a disordered matrix. Because the present XRD, Raman, and TEM analyses were performed on recovered samples, a short-lived polymerization-like intermediate during heating cannot be directly excluded. Nevertheless, the recovered products show disappearance of fullerene diffraction features, loss of molecular Raman modes, extensive bond reconstruction, and formation of graphitic nanoclusters within a disordered matrix, indicating that any such intermediate does not persist in the final structure. These findings reveal a transformation pathway in which compressed fullerite evolves through enhanced intermolecular interactions, progressive cage distortion, C-C bond reconstruction, and localized nanographene formation, ultimately producing a dual-phase sp2-carbon framework3–11,20–24. The resulting three-dimensional framework integrates ordered graphitic motifs and disordered carbon clusters.

Large-scale molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were then conducted to validate the temperature-driven microstructural evolution of compressed C60 (Fig. 1f, l–p). At low temperatures below 400 °C (calculated 1000 K according to the Arrhenius framework25), the system retains a face-centered cubic (FCC) lattice structure. Upon heating to 650 °C (2300 K), the lattice collapses, yielding amorphous networks composed of sp2-hybrid graphene-like nanosheets (green atoms) and sp3-hybrid topological defects (white atoms). Further temperature increasing to 800 °C (2800 K) promotes localized self-assembly of these nanosheets into nanoclustered graphene domains. At 1600 °C (3500 K), a mixed-phase structure emerges, comprising extended nanographitic regions interspersed with sp3-hybridized amorphous carbon. At 1800 °C (3800 K), nanocrystalline diamond domains (blue atoms) are observed embedded within graphitic layers. This simulated structural evolution shows strong agreement with experimental observations.

To further understand the reconstruction behavior of C60 molecular cages and the dual-phase microstructure formation mechanism, we analyze atomic ring distributions and system energetics. Ring statistics (Fig. 2a) show that pristine C60 consists primarily of pentagons and hexagons composed of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms. At 300–400 °C (calculated 500–800 K), intermolecular compression leads to cage deformation via the formation of heptagon and octagon rings. Beyond 650 °C (2300 K), these spherical motifs lose their molecular integrity, resulting in disordered carbon with embedded nanographene fragments. At 800 °C (2800 K), a representative dual-phase structure emerges, composed of amorphous carbon and nanoclustered graphene, consistent with experimental observations. Energetic analysis (Fig. 2b) reveals that structural reconstruction is an energetically driven process. At 400 °C (1000 K), high potential energy correlates with cage deformation. The collapse at 650 °C (2300 K) reduces overall energy, but localized distortions maintain elevated potential within amorphous regions. At 800 °C (2800 K), thermal activation enables a transition toward lower-energy nanoclustered graphene, highlighted by a distinct energy minimum (gold circle, Fig. 2b). This transformation is spatially localized, reflecting the intrinsic heterogeneity of the amorphous matrix and giving rise to a dual-phase architecture consisting of disordered carbon and discretely distributed nanoclustered graphene. Figure 2c illustrates the average and variance of atomic potential energy across the corresponding temperatures. The red and green error bars represent the upper and lower bounds of the energy distribution, corresponding to high-energy and low-energy atomic populations, respectively. At intermediate temperatures, the broader energy distribution indicates increased structural heterogeneity, highlighting the coexistence of distinct phases. The simulations provide an atomistic evolutionary sequence linking the initial compressed fullerene network to the experimentally observed dual-phase carbon. In the calculated evolution, the system starts from a compressed fullerene network, followed by distortion of the cage-like structure and the development of local structural instabilities. This is then followed by C-C bond rearrangement, progressive loss of molecular integrity, and the localized emergence of graphitic nanoclusters within a disordered carbon matrix. Together with the XRD, Raman, HRTEM, and EELS results, these simulations support an interpretation in which the transformation under 12 GPa through progressive structural reconstruction from compressed fullerite to dual-phase carbon.

In addition, the structural integrity and stability of this dual-phase system are critically dependent on the atomic-scale interface between the nanoclustered graphene and amorphous domains, as highlighted in the MD simulation of the C60-800 °C sample (Supplementary Fig. 5). The interfacial region is distinguished by a small fraction of sp3-hybridized carbon atoms forming covalent bonding, often linked to line defects and screw dislocations. These defect sites, though sparse, are clearly integrated into the disordered network, where they anchor reactive free edges and promote the alignment of low-curvature regions into extended, ribbon-like stacks. As a result, the interfacial bonding establishes an ordered framework within an otherwise disordered matrix, supporting the development of a robust, three-dimensional dual-phase carbon architecture.

To further resolve the nanoscale structure and morphology of the dual-phase carbon, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS, Supplementary Fig. 4b) was conducted on the C60-800 °C sample, with the near-graphitic C60-1800 °C sample included for comparison. The exponent n provides geometric insight: values of n = 1, 2, and 4 correspond to rod-like objects, smooth two-dimensional sheets, and smooth three-dimensional surfaces, respectively, while 3 < n < 4 suggests rough or fractal interfaces. For the C60-800 °C sample, the scattering profile yielded a slope near −2, consistent with n ≈ 2, confirming a morphology dominated by smooth, layered two-dimensional structures. This observation supports the HRTEM identification of multilayer graphene domains and aligns with the high sp2 fraction measured by EELS. With further thermal activation, these sheets interconnect and evolve into increasingly rough, entangled networks, progressively losing their discrete nanocluster identity as the system transitions toward a bulk-like graphitic architecture.

Mechanical properties and thermal stability

The synthesized carbon materials exhibit clear temperature-dependent densification behavior (Fig. 3b). Density increases monotonically up to 650 °C, followed by a gradual decline, approaching the theoretical density of graphite (2.2 g/cm3). The C60-800 °C sample reaches a high density of 2.31 g/cm3, consistent with a highly compacted network of sp2 carbon. Vickers hardness measurements (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Figs. 6, 7) reveal a non-monotonic trend. Hardness increases sharply between 400 and 800 °C, reaches a peak of ~20.2 GPa at 800 °C, which is attributed to C60 cage collapse and the formation of a three-dimensional ordered-disordered carbon network. Beyond 800 °C, hardness declines as graphitic domain growth weakens the interconnected architecture. SEM analysis of indentation morphologies supports this behavior. The C60-400 °C sample (Supplementary Fig. 6a) displays brittle, ceramic-like rhombic indentations with corner cracks, while the C60-800 °C sample (Fig. 3d) shows exceptional crack resistance up to 98 N, with ambiguous geometrical indents under 4.9–19.6 N loads—an atypical response for carbon materials. This dual-phase character thus exhibits a unique mechanical identity, combining ceramic-like hardness. These properties arise from its interconnected sp2-bonded carbon network, where nanoclustered graphene domains reinforce the amorphous matrix.

Nanoindentation measurements (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 8) further quantify the elastic modulus and resilience of these materials. The C60-800 °C sample shows load-dependent Vickers hardness of 23.3–18.0 GPa and a Young’s modulus of 144.3–110.7 GPa, closely matching the macroscopic measurements. Its elastic recovery decreases from 97.8% at 0.98 N to 87.9% at 9.8 N, indicating high resilience under mechanical load. Samples synthesized at 650 and 1000 °C exhibit slightly reduced modulus values (141.8–104.0 GPa and 117.3–86.8 GPa, respectively) but enhanced elastic recovery (97.1–89.4% and 99.5–91.2%, respectively), reflecting a mechanical trade-off governed by dual-phase network evolution. Optimal properties emerge near 800 °C, where synergistic network configuration maximizes performance. Both the amorphous-rich (650 °C) and crystalline-rich (1000 °C) architectures deviate from this optimum, compromising modulus while enhancing elastic recovery. In contrast, the C60-1300 °C sample exhibits a sharp decline in both modulus (101.8–71.2 GPa) and recovery (96.1–86.1%), reflecting structural degradation as graphitic ordering increases and disordered carbon diminishes. Comparison with representative sp2-dominated carbon materials (Supplementary Table 1) highlights the exceptional combinational performance of dual-phase sp2-carbon C60-800 °C. The mechanical and thermal properties reported here were measured using standard methods widely applied to carbon materials, and the resulting values are compared with representative literature obtained under comparable experimental conditions. Its hardness (~20.2 GPa) is about 100 times that of graphite27,28 (~0.2 GPa), about 20 times that of two-dimensional polymerized C605,23,29 (~0.8–1.0 GPa), four to five times that of raw glassy carbon30,31 (~4.3–5.0 GPa), and more than 30 times that of bulk graphene nanoplatelets32 (~0.28–0.61 GPa). Its Young’s modulus (~137.8–143.5 GPa) is also substantially higher than those of graphite27,28 (~25.5 GPa), raw glassy carbon30,31 (~29.5–35 GPa), bulk graphene nanoplatelets32 (~8.7–15.2 GPa), multilayer graphene/amorphous carbon films33 (~42.2 GPa), graphene-based all-carbon composite34 (~7.6–18.5 GPa), and nanoclustered graphene phases6 (~61–69 GPa). More importantly, C60-800 °C retains ~87.9% elastic recovery at 9.8 N and ~97.8% at 0.98 N. By comparison, nanoclustered graphene phases6 show ~90–95% recovery at a lower load of 0.5 N, while compressed glassy carbon30 shows ~80–70% recovery under the same 0.98–9.8 N load range. Pressure-treated glassy carbon31 has a comparable sp2 fraction of ~90–96% and shows high elastic recovery of ~86–90%, but its hardness (~5.5–10 GPa) and modulus (~35–62 GPa) remain much lower than those of C60-800 °C. Although compressed glassy carbon30 exhibits higher hardness and modulus, its sp3 fraction is substantially higher, corresponding to only ~78% sp2 bonding. When similar indentation load and sp2 fraction are considered together, the unique dual-phase structure of C60-800 °C provides the most favorable overall combination of hardness, Young’s modulus, and elastic recovery among the comparable sp2-rich carbon systems. Thus, C60-800 °C is distinguished by retaining a predominantly sp2-bonded framework (~94% sp2) while achieving high hardness, high modulus, and ultrahigh elastic recovery under large indentation loads. Relative to conventional ceramics and metals, C60-800 °C exhibits hardness comparable to Al2O3 and higher elastic recovery than MgO, copper, and even rubber30. Taken together, these results position C60-800 °C as a structurally distinct carbon material with a rare combination of high hardness, ultrahigh elastic recovery, and excellent crack resistance. This synergy of mechanical properties represents a substantial advance over conventional sp2-carbon systems and structural ceramics.

The thermal stability of all the carbon materials synthesized here was assessed via thermogravimetric and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC). A representative dual-phase sp2-carbon (C60-800 °C) exhibits exceptional thermal resilience (Fig. 3e). It remains stable in an inert atmosphere up to 1300 °C and shows an unusually high onset oxidation temperature (Tox) of ~960 °C in air. This value marks the apex of a trend where Tox increases with synthesis temperature up to 800 °C, then gradually declines at higher temperatures. Samples synthesized at 650 and 1000 °C also demonstrate strong oxidation resistance (Tox ≈ 890 and 920 °C, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 9). Notably, the Tox of the dual-phase carbon (~960 °C) surpasses those of a wide range of sp2-rich materials—graphite (400–800 °C)35–37, graphene (~530 °C)35, C60 fullerenes (450–580 °C)35, carbon nanotubes (350–600 °C)35,38, and glassy carbon (610–775 °C)30, as well as sp3-dominant forms, such as natural diamond (~805 °C)2 and nanopolycrystalline diamond (~680 °C)39. Its thermal performance approaches that of nanotwinned diamond (nt-D, 980–1056 °C)2, one of the most oxidation-resistant carbon materials known (Fig. 3f). For comparison, the oxidation data for other carbon materials were taken from literature reports obtained under oxidation conditions similar to those used in the present TG-DSC measurements.

The exceptional thermal stability is corroborated by MD simulations of atomic-level oxidation. A representative structure was exposed to an oxygen atmosphere of near-ambient density (8.02 × 10−3 mol/cm3) and incrementally heated from 300 to 3800 K (Supplementary Fig. 10). With increasing temperature, carbon atoms were progressively removed as CO and CO2 (colored in red in Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). Mapping the initial sites of oxidation reveals strong clustering in high-energy disordered regions, including amorphous domains and interface-adjacent sites, while oxidation within the interiors of nanoclustered graphene domains was comparatively rare (Supplementary Fig. 10d). This spatial selectivity indicates that oxygen preferentially attacks locally disordered and interfacial high-energy regions, whereas the embedded graphitic nanoclusters are comparatively protected within the dual-phase network. The resistance to oxidation arises from the compact dual-phase architecture formed under high pressure and high temperature conditions. The high bulk density of the C60-800 °C sample reflects a highly compacted carbon framework, which reduces open porosity and limits oxygen transport. However, the oxidation behavior is not governed by densification alone. In graphitic carbons, oxidation generally initiates more readily at exposed edges, defects, dangling bonds, and other high-energy sites than within intact graphitic basal-plane regions36,40,41. In the present dual-phase carbon, the graphitic nanoclusters are embedded in the amorphous matrix, and their edges are directly bonded to the surrounding disordered carbon network. This interfacial bonding effectively passivates edge sites and dangling bonds that would otherwise act as highly reactive oxidation centers36,40–43. The sparse sp3-hybridized interfacial carbon atoms observed in the C60-800 °C model further provide covalent anchoring between the graphitic nanoclusters and the disordered matrix (Supplementary Fig. 5), helping stabilize reactive free edges and preserve structural continuity. As a result, oxidation is preferentially localized in high-energy amorphous and interface-adjacent regions, while the graphitic nanocluster interiors remain comparatively protected. In addition, the amorphous–graphitic interfaces are structurally continuous and free of conventional grain boundaries, which in polycrystalline carbons can act as fast oxygen diffusion channels. This continuity further restricts oxidant penetration and slows reaction kinetics. The combined effects of compactness, edge-site passivation, sparse interfacial sp3 covalent anchoring, and restricted oxygen diffusion explain the observed oxidation resistance of the dual-phase sp2-carbon.

Elastic and high-pressure behavior of dual-phase carbon

To investigate the elastic response of the dual-phase carbon under high pressure, ultrasonic measurements were conducted on C60-800 °C and C60-1600 °C samples (Fig. 4a–d and Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). The measured properties, including sample length (L), density (ρ), longitudinal (VP) and shear wave (VS) velocities, and derived elastic moduli, are summarized in Supplementary Tables 2–4. In the C60-800 °C sample, VP increases steadily with pressure, while VS shows a slight decline (Fig. 4b), a behavior reminiscent of compressed graphite44 and indicative of a layered microstructure, consistent with SAXS observations. The bulk modulus (B) increases monotonically, while the shear modulus (G) remains nearly constant with pressure (Fig. 4c). Fitting to a third-order finite-strain equation yields zero-pressure moduli and pressure derivatives: B0 = 89.7(9) GPa, G0 = 58.2(6) GPa, E0 = 143.5(1.4) GPa, with B’ = 4.11(6) and an unusually low G′ = 0.32(1). These results highlight the exceptional stiffness and resilience, consistent with nanoindentation measurements. The negligible pressure dependence of G reflects facile interlayer sliding enabled by van der Waals interactions between graphene domains, characteristic of structurally compliant sp2 networks. Similar trends are observed in C60-1600 °C, which exhibits enhanced graphitic ordering and larger crystalline domains. However, it shows significantly lower moduli: B0 = 34.7(3) GPa, G0 = 31.6(3) GPa, E0 = 72.6(7) GPa, with B′ = 8.36(12) and G′ = 1.09(2). These values approach those of graphite27,45–47, consistent with its increased crystallinity and reduced amorphous content. Comparative analysis of pressure–volume (P–V) relations (Fig. 4d) further illustrates that C60-1600 °C follows a graphite-like compressibility trend, whereas C60-800 °C exhibits significantly higher-pressure resistance—intermediate between graphite and diamond46,48. High-pressure neutron diffraction measurements on C60-800 °C (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 4d) show a broad reflection corresponding to the graphitic (002) plane, which persists up to 6.48 GPa and disappears at 7.4 GPa. Upon decompression, the peak fully recovers, indicating complete structural reversibility. The measured interlayer spacing compresses from 3.196(1) Å at ambient pressure to 3.119(1) Å at 6.48 GPa, further confirming elastic recoverability (Fig. 4f). These results demonstrate that the dual-phase structure accommodates pressure through interlamellar sliding and elastic compaction, and retains structural integrity even beyond transient disordering above ~7.4 GPa.

These superior mechanical properties of the dual-phase carbon C60-800 °C arise from its hierarchical nanostructure. Corrugated graphene layers within the network allow adaptive spacing adjustments under stress, enabling reversible densification with limited structural damage. Upon unloading, recovery is facilitated by the intrinsic curvature of the graphitic nanoclusters and their flexible interlayer entanglement. Similar mechanisms have been proposed for hard elastic sp2-rich carbon systems6,7, where corrugated or curved sp2 layers recover through local bending and constrained interlayer sliding, while cross-linking or network confinement suppresses irreversible sliding and bond rupture. This behavior aligns with the “squeezed chicken wire” model49, where a three-dimensional sp2 covalent network promotes stress-induced layer sliding and rotational flexibility without bond breaking. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations reinforce these insights. Structures annealed at 800 °C (calculated 2800 K) comprise nanoclustered graphene embedded in a rigid amorphous matrix (Fig. 5a–c). During the uniaxial tensile deformation, the amorphous phase provides structural support, while compliant nanoclusters accommodate localized shear. This synergy localizes strain, impedes shear band propagation, and enhances stiffness. In contrast, the structure at 1300 °C (calculated 3200 K) shows extensive graphitization into large, multilayer graphene networks (Fig. 5d–f), which offer reduced shear resistance due to the limited number of defects anchoring sites and weaker inter-domain cohesion. The result is lower stiffness and diminished load-bearing capability. Together, these results reveal that the high hardness and modulus of the dual-phase carbon are primarily governed by the amorphous framework, while the ultrahigh elastic recovery originate from the embedded nanoclustered graphene. This cooperative mechanism, operating across multiple length scales, underpins the unique combination of stiffness, recoverability, and mechanical resilience of dual-phase carbon, placing it above conventional graphitic and diamond-like carbons. Besides, it should be noted that although the absence of explicit van der Waals interactions in EDIP may affect the quantitative description of specific interlayer interactions, the structural evolution and deformation mechanisms discussed here are mainly governed by short-range covalent bonding and mixed sp2/sp3 network configuration. We have synthesized and characterized a dual-phase sp2-hybridized carbon material from C60 under 12 GPa and 800 °C through progressive structural reconstruction. By identifying a progressive reconstruction pathway from compressed fullerite to the dual-phase carbon, we revealed a unique hierarchical architecture composed of nanoclustered graphene domains embedded within a disordered sp2-rich carbon matrix. This structural integration produced a material that overcomes conventional trade-offs, exhibiting high hardness (~20 GPa), ultrahigh elastic recovery (~88% under a 9.8 N load), and superior oxidation resistance up to ~960 °C. Our analysis, supported by molecular dynamics simulations, demonstrated that these paradoxical properties aroused from synergistic interfacial reinforcement, where the alternating ordered-disordered structure enabled stress dispersion through controlled interlayer sliding and elastic compaction. This work not only clarified the structure of fullerene-derived carbons at moderate pressure but also provided insights into the design of interface-stabilized, dual-phase systems, providing guidance for next-generation carbon materials capable of performing under extreme mechanical and thermal conditions.

Methods

Sample synthesis

The starting material C60 (99.9% purity) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). High-pressure and high-temperature (HPHT) experiments were performed in a 6 × 14 MN cubic press at the Chinese Academy of Engineering Physics (CAEP). The 14/8 assembly, WC anvils of 8 mm truncation edge length, and MgO pressure medium (octahedron) of 14 mm edge-length, was used. C60 was enclosed into an h-BN capsule with an inner diameter and length of 3 mm, and then put into the center of a MgO octahedron with a Re heater and ZrO2 insulator. The pressure calibration was based on phase transitions of Bi, Tl, Ba, ZnTe, and ZnS, while temperature was measured using a W75Re25-W25Re75 thermocouple. The assembly was initially cold-pressed to 12 GPa, followed by heating at 150 °C/min to the desired temperature, where it was maintained for 1 h. The sample was then quenched by turning off the electric power supply. The density of the recovered bulk samples was determined by Archimedes’ method.

Structure characterization

The structures of the recovered samples were confirmed by XRD (model D8 Discover, Bruker, Germany) with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.54056 Å). The Raman spectra were measured using a Raman spectrometer (Andor SR-303i-B) with a 532-nm laser at room temperature. The microstructures of the recovered samples were meticulously characterized using a spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy (ACTEM, JEM-ARM200F, JEOL Ltd., Japan) and a transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For the C60-800 °C sample, a foil was prepared for TEM observation using focused ion beam (FIB) thinning (Helios G4 UX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples synthesized at temperatures other than 800 °C were mechanically crushed in an agate mortar, dispersed in ethanol via ultrasonic treatment, and then deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid for drying at room temperature prior to TEM analysis. The bonding states in the recovered samples were assessed by collecting electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) spectra near the carbon K-edge3 in STEM spectrum-imaging mode operated at 200 kV (ACTEM, JEM-ARM200F, JEOL Ltd., Japan). The measurements were carried out with a 40 μm condenser aperture, a probe current of 40 pA, and a convergence angle of 28 mrad, giving a sub-nanometre probe size of approximately 0.08–0.10 nm under aberration-corrected STEM conditions. The spectra were collected from local regions of approximately 12 × 12 nm2, and the quantified bonding fractions represent local statistical averages over the analyzed heterogeneous regions. The nanometer scale-resolution four-dimensional scanning transmission electron microscopy (4D-STEM) nanobeam electron diffraction datasets were acquired using a transmission electron microscope (Titan 300, FEI, USA) equipped with an electron microscope pixel array detector (EMPAD) at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. A semi-convergence angle of 0.58 mrad was used, corresponding to a diffraction-limited probe with a full width at half maximum of approximately 2 nm. The scan step size was 0.585 nm over a field of view of 117 × 117 nm2, and the beam current was 1 nA. Amorphous/crystalline phase mapping was performed following reported 4D-STEM processing methods50, and the relative-density map was generated from the extracted scattering intensity and normalized to the maximum value within the dataset50.

Mechanical properties and thermal stability measurement

The Vickers hardness (HV) of the surface-polished samples was carried out on a microhardness tester (QATM Qness 60 A + EVO) equipped with a four-sided pyramidal diamond indenter, and averaged over five data points with a load hold time of 15 s. The hardness is calculated from HV = 1854.4 F/L2, where F (in Newtons) represents the applied load and L (in micrometers) is the arithmetic mean of the two diagonals of the Vickers indentation. The nanoindentation measurements were conducted using a nanoindenter (Agilent Nano Indenter G200) equipped with a three-sided pyramidal Berkovich diamond indenter. The load application time to reach the peak load was 30 s, followed by a peak hold time of 15 s and an unloading time of 30 s. Nanoindentation hardness (HN), Young’s modulus (E), and elastic recovery were derived from the load-displacement curves. Thermal stability was investigated by measuring thermogravimetry and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) curves in flowing air using a simultaneous thermal analyzer (Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC 3+) over the temperature range 35–1300 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min.

In situ high-pressure ultrasonic measurement

In situ high-pressure ultrasonic measurements were conducted using a multi-anvil apparatus at CAEP based on the 14/8 cell assembly depicted in Fig. 4a. A dual-mode LiNbO3 disk served as the transducer, with resonant frequencies of 50 MHz for compressional waves and 30 MHz for shear waves. The sample with double-sided polished surfaces for precision was positioned near the center of a MgO octahedron. A double-sided polished polycrystalline Al2O3 cylinder served dual functions as both the acoustic buffer rod, facilitating the transmission of acoustic signals, and the pressure marker51. To enhance acoustic coupling and ensure effective signal transmission, a layer of 2 μm-thick gold foil was inserted between the buffer rod and the sample. The back of the sample was supported by a soft NaCl cylinder, which reduced deviatoric stress in high-pressure ultrasonic measurements and provided a quasi-hydrostatic environment for the sample.

High-pressure neutron diffraction

The high-pressure neutron diffraction experiment was performed at the High Pressure Neutron Diffractometer (Fenghuang) of China Mianyang Research Reactor (CMRR) neutron science platform57. Paris-Edinburgh (PE) Press, equipped with single-toroidal sintered diamond anvils, was used to apply the pressure. Titanium-zirconium alloy gaskets were used as the sample chamber, along with an αα-Fe pressure marker. Neutron diffraction patterns of the C60-800 °C sample under high pressure were collected with the angle-dispersive mode, and the wavelength of the incident neutron beam is λ = 1.5925 Å. Crystal structural refinements were conducted using the FULLPROF program. The pressures in this study were determined from the cell volumes of αα-Fe by Rietveld refinement of neutron diffraction patterns and its well-determined equation of state (EOS)57.

Computational methods

The large-scale MD simulations were conducted by the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) molecule dynamics code59. The environment-dependent interatomic potential (EDIP) proposed by Marks and co-workers was used to describe the atomic interactions60, which has been proven to be a powerful force field to describe the physical nature of amorphous carbons. The simulation system contains 55,000 atoms, with periodic boundary conditions and a time step of 1 fs. To replicate the experimental synthesis conditions, the fullerene precursor was first compressed to target densities within 10 ps, and then annealed under canonical ensemble (NVT) for 2 ns at various temperatures, which were chosen to correspond to the actual experimental conditions under the Arrhenius framework25. The annealed structures were further subjected to a cooling process to 300 K (40 ps), followed by a decompression to 0 GPa (40 ps) and a final relaxation process (50 ps). The Nosé–Hoover thermostat and barostat were employed to regulate the temperatures and pressures. The discrete grid method was used to identify the carbon rings. To analyze the geometry of these carbon rings, a mesh of triangles is constructed61. The vertexes (corners) of the triangles correspond to the positions of the atom sites, and the centers of the carbon rings were used as the other set of points for the vertices of the triangle. The uniaxial stretching simulation was conducted through the fix deform command in LAMMPS, with a strain rate of 5 ×× 1011 s−1. The oxidation process was simulated by heating the structure in an oxygen environment with a density of 8.02 × 10−3 mol/cm3 and employing a reactive force field (ReaxFF) to model the chemical reactions, where the simulation temperature was gradually increased from 300 to 3800 K over a period of 250 ps62.

It should be noted that EDIP does not explicitly include long-range van der Waals (vdW) interactions. Therefore, it is not suitable to quantitatively describe vdW-dominated graphite interlayer properties, such as the equilibrium interlayer spacing, interlayer sliding, or c-axis elastic response. However, in this work, the MD simulations are mainly used to capture the collapse of C60, the reconstruction of mixed sp2/sp3 covalent networks, and the resulting dual-phase topology. These processes are primarily governed by short-range covalent bonding and topological rearrangement, which can be described by EDIP well. Therefore, the main structural and mechanistic conclusions remain reliable within the scope of this study. Recent machine learning-based potentials (e.g., GAP potential63) are better suited to capturing the interlayer bonding behaviors of systems dominated by van der Waals interactions, such as graphite and multilayer graphene.
