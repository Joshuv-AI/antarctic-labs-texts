Fairness Is More Than Algorithms: Racial Disparities in Time-to-Recidivism
Jessy Xinyi Han, Kristjan Greenewald, Devavrat Shah

Abstract
. Racial disparities in recidivism remain a persistent challenge, and the growing adoption of risk assessment algorithms has intensified the scrutiny of their sources. Past works have primarily focused on disparities in the predictions of these algorithms, viewing recidivism as a binary outcome. While sociological and criminological research has long documented non-algorithmic factors in recidivism, it remains unclear whether the risk assessments that decision-makers act on fully account for racial disparities. This work presents a multi-stage causal framework for time-to-recidivism that captures the interactions between race, the risk assessment algorithm, and contextual factors. We introduce interventional racial parity and a formal survival analysis test, conducted with observational data, of whether the algorithmic risk assessment fully accounts for racial differences in recidivism. Applied to the COMPAS dataset, the test detects no racial disparity within risk groups at short follow-up horizons. A statistically significant disparity becomes detectable after roughly nine months in the low-risk group and persists under finer score stratification, risk score perturbation, and a competing-risks analysis. This suggests that factors beyond the algorithmic scores, possibly including structural disparities in housing, employment, and social support, may shape recidivism over time, underscoring the need for policy interventions beyond algorithmic improvements, particularly for low-risk defendants.



1.
Introduction

With millions of formerly incarcerated people returning to prison each year, the cycle of re-offending after release, known as
recidivism
, remains a pressing challenge worldwide. In the United States, recidivism is a particularly complex issue closely entwined with the stark racial, economic, and social inequalities that permeate the criminal justice system. The emerging literature suggests that minority groups face disparate treatment across various stages of the criminal justice process, from the initial 911 call for service
(
Han et al., 2024
)
, through policing
(
Fryer, 2019
)
, court sentencing
(
Rehavi and Starr, 2014
)
, probation and parole decisions
(
Kimchi, 2019
;
Huebner and Bynum, 2008
)
, and re-entry support
(
Western and Sirois, 2019
)
.
Therefore, a close-up examination of the pathways and extent of such racial disparities must precede any effective reforms for a fair and equitable criminal justice system.

Amid these systemic challenges, the increasing deployment of algorithmic risk assessment tools, such as COMPAS (Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions)
(
Northpointe, 1996
)
, has added another layer of complexity to discussions of fairness and equity in the criminal justice system. These tools, originally designed to standardize decision-making, reduce human biases in bail, parole, and sentencing decisions, and enhance consistency and efficiency in judicial outcomes, have faced continual scrutiny and criticism since their adoption
(
Berk et al., 2021
;
Barabas et al., 2018
;
Corbett-Davies et al., 2017
)
. The complexity surrounding these tools arises from three dimensions. First, it concerns the competing definitions of fairness. In particular, ProPublica’s influential news report on machine bias, which analyzed the impact of the COMPAS risk assessment tool, suggested the existence of racial disparities in terms of predictive accuracy: African-American individuals who did not recidivate within a two-year period were disproportionately labeled as higher risk compared to their Caucasian counterparts
(
Angwin et al., 2016
;
Larson et al., 2016
)
. Yet this is only one of several competing definitions of fairness, which in general cannot all hold at once. Second, such an algorithm-centered analysis does not fully account for the broader structural context or potential non-algorithmic factors, such as the socioeconomic conditions substantially studied in sociological and criminological literature, that may also contribute to disparities in the outcomes of recidivism
(
Kubrin and Stewart, 2006
;
Kirk, 2009
;
Kirk, 2015
)
. Third, it also leaves out the time trajectory of how potential bias flows through the pathways embedded in the criminal justice system over time.

1.1.
Contributions

The primary contribution of this work is to provide a systematic approach to answer the following question: does the algorithmic risk assessment fully account for the racial disparities in time-to-recidivism, and if not, when and how do the remaining disparities emerge? In answering this question, the key challenge lies in disentangling the interactions between algorithmic decisions, perceived race, and additional contextual factors over time. This work overcomes the limitations of prior work towards addressing this question through the contributions summarized next.

We propose a multi-stage causal framework that captures the complete trajectory from arrest to potential re-offense or return to custody. This allows us to examine both direct and indirect pathways through which racial disparities can manifest over time. The framework rests on a single modeling assumption: given fixed contexts and algorithmic decisions, race itself does not make someone recidivate sooner or later. As such, whether the algorithmic assessment fully accounts for the observed disparities is then a testable hypothesis.

Building on this framework, we introduce the notion of interventional racial (dis-)parity, a fairness criterion that examines whether individuals of different races who are otherwise identical exhibit equivalent time-to-recidivism patterns under the influence of the criminal justice system and contextual factors. We move beyond static measures of fairness that treat criminal justice outcomes as a simple True or False binary predictive question to consider the dynamic and context-dependent nature of recidivism shaped by structural inequality over time, through the lens of survival analysis.

To determine whether this racial parity holds, we establish Theorem
3.2
, which yields a data-driven test comparing the recidivism curves across races within the same risk score group. The challenge is that the true time-to-recidivism is often masked by censoring: when an individual returns to custody for non-criminal reasons before any re-offense, the data reveal only the earlier of the two times. To address this, we employ the log-rank test from survival analysis, which can be properly conducted with observational censored data. The causal model itself supplies the statistical guarantee for this test: under the null hypothesis, the race labels within a risk score group are exchangeable, so the test can be calibrated by permuting them. We track the log-rank statistic across the entire follow-up period and reject when its largest deviation exceeds the corresponding permutation quantile. As such, the resulting test controls the family-wise error rate (FWER) across all follow-up horizons, that is, the probability of falsely declaring a disparity at one or more horizons, accounting for the multiple testing inherent in examining the whole trajectory (Proposition
3.5
). As a byproduct, we obtain the earliest follow-up time at which a disparity can be declared significant, which we term the
detectability onset
. Rejection of the null hypothesis therefore means precisely that the algorithmic risk assessment alone does not account for the racial disparities in time-to-recidivism, so factors beyond it contribute.

We utilize this framework to analyze the COMPAS dataset curated by ProPublica, which records the risk scores, criminal histories, and subsequent re-arrests of roughly 10,000 defendants in Broward County, Florida. Within a short-term follow-up period, we do not find sufficient evidence to support that recidivism patterns across racial groups are different within the same risk score group. However, disparities become significant once the follow-up period exceeds the
detectability onset
of approximately nine months, specifically for individuals categorized as low risk by the risk assessment algorithm, thereby rejecting the null hypothesis that the algorithmic assessment alone accounts for the observed disparities. The onset also explains why analyses at different fixed horizons would reach conflicting conclusions, and hence why the choice of follow-up horizon can itself determine the policy implications of a study. The rejection is robust under additional analyses: the disparity persists within identical algorithmic decile risk scores, under deliberate perturbations of the risk scores in the direction that works against the finding, and in a competing-risks analysis that does not treat return to custody as independent censoring.

These findings have immediate social consequences, because courts and supervision agencies act directly on COMPAS risk assessments. Two defendants judged to pose the same level of risk could nevertheless face materially different chances of re-arrest that the algorithm does not capture. One possible explanation for this residual disparity is that structural inequalities in socioeconomic conditions, including disparities in access to stable housing, employment, and social support, may shape recidivism risks over time, beyond the scope of algorithmic predictions. We thus advocate for comprehensive policy interventions that look beyond algorithmic refinements, with particular attention to the low-risk population, for whom contextual conditions appear to matter most
(
Jacobs and Skeem, 2021
)
.

2.
Related Works

The intersection of fairness and algorithmic risk assessments within the criminal justice system has drawn considerable research attention and public scrutiny. The increasing adoption of algorithmic tools such as the COMPAS risk assessment system has exhibited both the hope for data-driven decision-making and the inherent risks of embedding and amplifying existing biases. This section provides a comprehensive overview of related literature to our study, ranging from foundational analyses of algorithmic fairness to studies on recidivism disparities and causal fairness frameworks.

Algorithmic Fairness and Bias in Risk Assessments.
The influential ProPublica investigation reported substantial racial disparities in the COMPAS algorithm’s predictions, finding that Black defendants were more likely than White defendants to be falsely classified as high risk for recidivism despite similar reoffending rates
(
Angwin et al., 2016
;
Larson et al., 2016
)
. Rigorously speaking, their main findings only compare
a variant
of the two races’ actual false positive and false negative rates.

Mathematically, their comparison compares the false positive rates
\mathbb{P}(M\in\{\text{medium, high}\}\mid D=d,\ \tau>2)
and the false negative rates
\mathbb{P}(M\in\{\text{low}\}\mid D=d,\ \tau\leq 2)
across the two values of
d
,
where
M\in\text{\lx@text@lbrace low, medium, high\lx@text@rbrace}
denotes the algorithmic risk assessment decision,
D\in\{\text{majority},\text{minority}\}
denotes the race, and
\tau
denotes the actual time to recidivism in years. However, the true time to recidivism is often masked by the time to return to custody for non-criminal violations, meaning if returning to custody happens first, then we only observe the minimum of the two, time to return to custody, instead of the target time to recidivism. This is referred to as the right-censoring problem in survival analysis, requiring more careful time-to-event examination.

This work has also sparked debate over using
equalized odds
in criminal justice settings
(
Rudin et al., 2020
;
Flores et al., 2016
)
.
Dieterich et al. (2016)
and subsequent responses defended COMPAS’s

, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\tau\leq 2|D=\text{majority},M\in\text{\lx@text@lbrace low\lx@text@rbrace})\simeq\mathbb{P}(\tau\leq 2|D=\text{minority},M\in\text{\lx@text@lbrace low\lx@text@rbrace})
, a condition that coincides with
calibration

(
Flores et al., 2016
)

\mathbb{P}(\tau\leq 2|D=\text{majority})\neq\mathbb{P}(\tau\leq 2|D=\text{minority})
,

and

cannot hold simultaneously for any non-trivial, imperfect classifier.

Causal Inference and Fairness Frameworks.
More recent works also shift from associational fairness to a causal perspective, marking a critical advancement in understanding and mitigating systemic biases.
Kusner et al. (2018)
define counterfactual fairness as a constraint on a predictor, requiring its output for an individual to remain unchanged in a counterfactual world where the individual’s protected attribute differs.
Coston et al. (2020)
explored counterfactual fairness notions across different demographic groups, emphasizing the importance of understanding causal pathways that may drive disparities. Similarly,
Mishler et al. (2021)
proposed post-processing methods for achieving counterfactual equalized odds within algorithmic frameworks.
More systematically,
Plečko and Bareinboim (2024)
introduced a comprehensive causal toolkit for fairness analysis, highlighting the need to disentangle causal effects from correlations to identify and rectify disparities in algorithmic decision-making. Our study builds along this line of work by situating recidivism disparities within a causal context, adopting a framework that operates at the interventional level of the causal hierarchy to understand the effect of algorithmic and additional contextual factors on recidivism over time.

Recidivism and Societal Contexts.
Recidivism is shaped by a complex interplay of individual, community, and systemic factors, including socioeconomic conditions, neighborhood characteristics, and access to essential resources. A substantial sociological and criminological literature has documented these influences and their racial patterning, including the effects of neighborhood disadvantage and residential change
(
Kubrin and Stewart, 2006
;
Kirk, 2009
;
Kirk, 2015
)
and the racial fairness of risk instruments themselves
(
Skeem and Lowenkamp, 2016
)
.
Jacobs and Skeem (2021)
emphasized the effects of neighborhood risk factors on recidivism, showing that neighborhood disadvantage and disorder raise recidivism mainly among people at relatively low individual risk.
Okonofua et al. (2021)
explored the impact of empathic supervision on recidivism reduction, emphasizing supportive interventions.
Goodley et al. (2022)
conducted a meta-analysis identifying key predictors of recidivism and their differential effects across demographic groups. Research employing survival analysis techniques has further deepened our understanding of recidivism dynamics.
Jung et al. (2010)
investigated the association between race and recidivism among formerly incarcerated individuals, illustrating how temporal aspects of recidivism can reveal inequities that static metrics often obscure.
Do et al. (2023)
,
Hu and Chen (2024)
, and
Rahman and Purushotham (2022)
extend fairness analysis to general survival settings beyond recidivism, using mutual information minimization, distributionally robust optimization, and interpretable modeling, respectively, to mitigate disparities in time-to-event predictions. These works aim to develop fairer predictors, whereas our goal is to understand whether existing algorithmic risk assessment decisions fully account for disparities in time-to-recidivism. The contextual factors documented above are precisely what a risk score may fail to absorb.

3.
Unpacking Racial (Dis)parities in Recidivism: A Causal Framework

Recidivism is a complex and systemic issue, influenced by social, economic, and institutional factors. To understand the advent and extent of racial disparities in recidivism of individuals with comparable risk assessment profiles, we propose a multi-stage causal framework that characterizes the full trajectory, from arrest to potential reoffense or return to custody. By leveraging this framework, we examine how racial disparities emerge over time, with particular attention to the role of algorithmic risk assessments and additional contextual factors.

3.1.
Framework

Figure 1

We consider a cohort of arrested individuals subject to the COMPAS risk assessment tool for predicting recidivism risk. These individuals undergo varied pre-trial detention decisions and subsequent sentencing outcomes. Upon release, individuals face three possible outcomes: they may successfully reintegrate without further criminal activity, they may reoffend and be rearrested, or they may return to custody for non-criminal violations such as probation breaches. We define recidivism as our target event, measured as the time from release to rearrest. For individuals who return to custody for non-criminal reasons, we treat this return as a censoring event that masks the potential occurrence of recidivism. We formalize this multi-stage process through a natural causal framework represented by a causal Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) in Figure

3.4.
Empirical Test

One potential challenge in directly using Theorem
3.2
is that we often cannot observe the true time-to-recidivism
\tau
for all individuals, only a lower bound of
\tau
. This occurs because some individuals return to custody for non-criminal violations, like missing probation meetings, before any potential reoffense, a phenomenon known as censoring in survival analysis. Traditional statistical tests that ignore censoring could produce biased results, as mistakenly using time-to-custody would underestimate the true time-to-recidivism. Survival analysis methods are specifically designed to handle such censored data by properly accounting for both observed recidivism events and censored observations, thereby allowing us to decide if we have enough empirical evidence to reject the null hypothesis
H_{0}
or not.

To formalize this testing procedure, we state the following empirical test.

Empirical Test 1.

Let
S_{d}(t\mid m):=\mathbb{P}[\tau>t\mid D=d,M=m]
for
d\in\{\text{majority},\text{minority}\}
. For each
m\in\{\text{low, medium, high}\}
, we test

\displaystyle H_{0}(m):\quad

\displaystyle S_{\text{majority}}(t\mid m)=S_{\text{minority}}(t\mid m)\qquad\text{for all }t>0,

\displaystyle\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}(m):\quad

\displaystyle S_{\text{majority}}(t\mid m)\neq S_{\text{minority}}(t\mid m)\qquad\text{for some }t>0.

Under the null hypothesis, individuals of different races but the same algorithmic risk score group should have identical survival curves; that is, their probability of remaining arrest-free should be the same at all time points. To test this hypothesis while properly accounting for censoring, we employ the non-parametric log-rank test, which compares the entire survival curve rather than outcomes at a single time point.

Assumptions of Log-rank Test.
The log-rank test of

is valid under two conditions: (1) Independent observations: the pairs

To determine when a disparity emerges, we examine

Definition 3.4 (Permutation Supremum Test).

Fix a risk score group
m
and let
W_{m}=\sup_{t}|z_{m}(t)|

4.
Empirics

Having developed a causal framework and a formal empirical test for analyzing racial disparities in recidivism, in this section, we use the COMPAS dataset collected by ProPublica to evaluate whether factors beyond the algorithmic risk scores contribute to the observed disparities in time-to-recidivism. Our causal framework suggests that if disparities persist even after controlling for algorithmic risk scores, this would indicate the presence of additional influences on recidivism trajectories.
Specifically, we apply the inference protocol developed in Section
3.4
to examine whether and when racial disparities emerge in time-to-recidivism patterns. This allows us to assess not just the existence of disparities, but also their temporal dynamics, whether disparities appear immediately post-release or develop over longer follow-up periods, and how early they can be detected.

4.1.
Data Description

The COMPAS dataset, curated by ProPublica in 2016, contains demographic characteristics, criminal history, and COMPAS risk scores of around 10,000 criminal defendants in Broward County, Florida. This dataset includes all individuals who underwent COMPAS assessments at the pretrial stage in 2013 and 2014 and had public criminal records up to April 1, 2016 for tracking subsequent offenses. Following prior analyses in the literature, we consider
Caucasian
as the
majority
race and
African-American
as the
minority
race. Score groups follow the standard COMPAS mapping of decile scores: low (1 to 4), medium (5 to 7), and high (8 to 10).

The public files record, for each defendant, one or more out-of-custody intervals measured in days since the COMPAS screening. Our analysis cohort takes each defendant’s first release interval, so that every defendant contributes one statistically independent record: follow-up runs from release until rearrest for a new offense (the
target
event) or censoring, which occurs at return to custody without a new offense or at the end of data collection near April 1, 2016. Overall, the cohort contains 8,690 defendants, of whom 2,410 were rearrested and 6,280 were censored, with a median potential follow-up of 27 months and a maximum of 39 months, measured from release to the end of data collection. Robustness.
We test whether the low-risk finding survives three alternative explanations.

The first check asks whether the gap arises because the low-risk group pools four decile scores, so that the two races could differ in their distributions of decile scores. To rule this out, we use a stratified log-rank test, which compares the races within each decile score in a risk group and later pools these comparisons across the deciles within that risk group. As in the primary test, we track this statistic over all follow-up horizons and calibrate its maximal deviation by permuting the race labels within each decile score. The test rejects for the low-risk group (horizon-adjusted
p=0.033
), while the corresponding tests for the medium- and high-risk groups do not. The gap therefore does not come from differences in the distribution of decile scores within the low-risk group.

The second check asks how robust the gap is with respect to potential errors in the risk scores. We thus randomly perturb the scores in the direction that works against the original finding, moving a random 10% of African-American defendants from the low to the medium group and a random 10% of Caucasian defendants from the medium to the low group, and repeat this 100 times. The permutation supremum test rejects in every draw, with the largest horizon-adjusted
p

0.013

Discussion.
Under the causal model of Section
3
, the rejection for the low-risk group means that
H_{0}
fails, so contextual factors
U
directly affect time to rearrest or time to return to custody. The characteristics recorded in the data do not explain the gap. Adjusting for additional demographic and charge characteristics in a Cox proportional hazards model
(
Cox, 1972
)
leaves the gap largely intact, with a hazard ratio of 1.25 for African-American relative to Caucasian defendants in the low-risk group (95% confidence interval
[1.09,1.43]
). Among the unrecorded contextual factors, one plausible candidate is unequal access to post-release support, such as stable housing, employment, and social services.
Castillo et al. (2024)
find that encouraging the use of reentry support services reduces rearrest, with the largest benefit among Black participants, which suggests that such support matters most for the group facing the disparity here. Other contextual channels, such as differences in post-release enforcement intensity, may also contribute. The concentration of the disparity in the low-risk group is also consistent with prior evidence.
Jacobs and Skeem (2021)
find that neighborhood disadvantage and disorder increase recidivism among people with relatively low individual risk but not among those at higher risk, suggesting that contextual conditions matter most for the low-risk population. This population therefore offers a clear opportunity for policy, since the reentry support services discussed above could be extended to low-risk defendants who would otherwise receive little support and recidivate sooner. The detectability onset of roughly nine months has a separate implication for future study designs. Evaluations of risk instruments and reentry programs should follow releasees well beyond the first half year, since analyses truncated earlier lack the power to surface disparities that may potentially evolve with time.

5.
Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive examination of racial disparities in recidivism through a multi-stage causal framework, focusing on the interactions between algorithmic risk assessments, release decisions, and broader contextual factors. While prior work on algorithmic risk assessment has largely focused on static measures of algorithmic bias, our analysis reveals the critical importance of temporal dynamics in understanding and addressing disparities in criminal justice outcomes. Through an exact inference procedure applied to the COMPAS dataset, we establish that defendants who share the same low-risk assessment experience systematically different rearrest trajectories by race, that the gap persists among defendants with identical decile scores, and that roughly nine months of follow-up are required before the disparity becomes statistically demonstrable. One possible explanation for the residual gap is the impact of societal factors, such as unequal access to housing, employment opportunities, and social support systems. Due to data limitations, in this manuscript we cannot directly verify whether the socioeconomic factors are indeed the additional sources of disparity. Linking recidivism records to data on housing, employment, and support services would allow a direct test of these channels, and applying the framework to other jurisdictions would assess the generality of the finding.

Policymakers and practitioners should recognize that a risk instrument whose scores are well calibrated on average can still leave systematic differences among defendants it assesses alike, so fairness auditing should extend beyond the algorithm itself to the outcomes that follow its assessments. Effective interventions may need to extend beyond algorithmic refinements to encompass policy changes that promote equitable access to housing, stable employment, and community-based support services. Such efforts can contribute to fostering a more just and equitable criminal justice system.

Our framework has applicability beyond the study of recidivism. It can be extended to other domains where algorithmic decisions intersect with social inequality. For instance, a similar causal framework can be utilized to evaluate racial bias in loan approval and default. In particular, the approved loan amount can be considered as a proxy for repayment ability, and time to repayment as the target event, often masked by time to default. In this scenario, socioeconomic conditions like employment stability and family circumstances also exert great influence on how soon people repay their loan. This broader applicability suggests the value of examining time-varying disparities across various institutional contexts where algorithmic systems are deployed.

Ultimately, this work underscores that fairness cannot be judged from the algorithm alone. The decisions its scores inform and the conditions people face after release also shape outcomes, and they deserve the same scrutiny. As society increasingly relies on algorithmic tools for high-stakes decisions, frameworks that can capture these complex temporal and contextual dynamics become essential for advancing both algorithmic fairness and social justice.
