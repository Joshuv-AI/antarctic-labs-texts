Groundwater hydrogeochemistry and potential health risk assessment through exposure to elevated arsenic in Darrang district of north bank plain of the Brahmaputra
Authors: Ritu Thakur, Himporna Nath, Nikita Neog, Pradip Kumar Bora, Ritusmita Goswami
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The current study investigates groundwater contamination in Darrang district, situated in the flood-prone Brahmaputra Valley. This research evaluates the concentrations and geospatial distributions of iron, fluoride, and arsenic in groundwater samples (n = 347) and assesses their potential ecotoxicological risks to human health. Multivariate statistical techniques were used to investigate the sources and the mobilization mechanism of the contaminants in the aquifer system. The Piper and Gibbs plots reveal that silicate weathering, ion exchange, and mineral dissolution are the dominant processes influencing groundwater chemistry. Notably, higher pH levels (max = 8.5, mean = 7.4) are associated with alkaline desorption of arsenic from minerals. Cluster analysis also indicates the occurrence of pH induced carbonate weathering process implying weathering and active dissolution as a governing mechanism for arsenic release. The health risk assessments indicate significant non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks from prolonged exposure to arsenic, particularly for children. Spatial distribution maps identify areas most at risk of contamination and related health hazards. This is the first comprehensive analysis of groundwater contaminants in Darrang, providing valuable insights into the region’s water quality issues and supporting future groundwater management efforts in Brahmaputra floodplain region.

Introduction

Groundwater, essential for ecological sustenance and human livelihoods, serves as a primary source of potable water, indispensable for domestic consumption and agricultural irrigation. In most populous nations like China and India, as well as the Middle East and North Africa, groundwater is extensively used, sustaining the livelihoods and economies of millions1. Despite its importance, groundwater quality is not properly monitored. Toxic metals, hydrocarbons, trace organic contaminants, pesticides, nanoparticles, microplastics, and other emerging contaminants pose risks to both human well-being and the integrity of ecosystems, thereby compromising sustainable socioeconomic progress2,3.

Arsenic (As), a well-known geogenic groundwater contaminant prevalent in different regions of the world, more particularly in Asia, including the Ganga-Meghna-Brahmaputra (GMB) plain in India4–10, the Ganga–Brahmaputra–Megha deltaic plain in Bangladesh11, deltas of the Red River Basin in Hanoi, the Mekong Deltas in Vietnam, Kandal province in Cambodia12, Terai region in Nepal13, Chao Phraya basin in Thailand, Xinjiang province in China14. Due to the extremely high population density in the Indian subcontinent, the GBM riparian basin is one of the most susceptible and perilous systems15. The Brahmaputra, which rises from the Angsi Glacier on the northern side of the Himalayas and runs southwest across the Assam Valley, is the second biggest river laden with silt following the Huang He in China15,16. The quality of groundwater over the whole floodplain is greatly impacted by the influx from the contributing rivers and nearby catchment regions.

Due to significant carcinogenic threat posed by As, the World Health Organization (WHO) lowered the permissible limit for As in drinking water to 10 µg L−1 in 1993 (replacing the previous threshold of 50 µg L−1)17. Several Indian states, particularly those located in the Ganga–Brahmaputra flood plains, are witnessing increased levels of As concentrations in groundwater (> 10 µg L−1), as highlighted by several researchers4–10,18–21. The As concentrations surpassed limits (WHO) in Assam’s Lakhimpur, Nagaon, Jorhat, Golaghat and Majuli5–10.

Several studies examining the amount of As in drinking water have revealed that, in addition to skin lesions, it may raise the risk of lung cancer, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus (nine folds higher) in humans22,23. Epidemiologic studies illustrating a correlation between As concentration and cancer, frequently rely on measures relating to past exposure through various pathways24. However, drinking As contaminated water stands to be the greatest threats to human health, as documented by national and international cases25. It is currently believed that the primary mechanism for releasing As is the reductive dissolution of iron (Fe) and aluminium (Al) metal oxides along with the activity of native metal-reducing bacteria, which directly affects As mobility26.

On the other hand, dental caries, fluorosis (including dental, skeletal, and crippling), and renal damage are all brought on by fluoride (F) in groundwater at concentrations higher than those advised27. Fluoride intake over recommended levels has also been linked to infertility, hypertension, abortion, and fertility27. The Brahmaputra valley is known for its high groundwater F concentrations, which are mostly derived from the parent rock and are mobilized by leaching and desorption in an alkaline environment15,21,27,28.

In the Darrang district of Assam, groundwater plays an indispensable role in meeting the water needs of its burgeoning population and driving agricultural productivity29. However, the quality of groundwater in this region is increasingly under scrutiny due to a confluence of natural and anthropogenic factors, posing significant challenges to its sustainable management and utilization. Located in the flood-prone Brahmaputra Valley, Darrang district faces unique hydrogeological dynamics influenced by the region’s topography, geology, and climatic conditions. The association between surface water interactions, sediment transport, and groundwater recharge processes shapes the distribution and quality of groundwater resources in the area29. Additionally, rapid developmental activities, agricultural practices, and inadequate sanitation infrastructure contribute to the contamination of groundwater with pollutants. The reliance on groundwater for domestic and agricultural purposes highlights the urgency of assessing and safeguarding its quality in Darrang.

In the present study, the occurrence and spatial distribution of geogenic contaminants in shallow groundwater aquifers of Darrang district has been evaluated. Moreover, the potential carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic health risks associated with As enriched groundwater were also estimated. To the best of our knowledge, no published literature from Darrang has conducted extensive sampling to evaluate health risks related to geogenic contaminants along with the spatial distribution and dynamics of these contaminants in Darrang, Assam. Our work aims to bridge the knowledge gap on the distribution, sources, and health risks in the Brahmaputra floodplains of Assam.

Materials and methods

Study area

The district has a total of 1585 square km placed in between 20°9′ N to 26°027′ N latitude and 91°045′ E to 92°022′ E longitude. Arunachal Pradesh, Bhutan, and Udalguri district form the northern borders of Darrang area. Sonitpur and Kamrup districts are located in the east and west, respectively. The district has a population of 928,500 people as per the 2011 census data. The district’s net sown area covers 73,619 hectares, making up 46.25% of its total geographic area. Paddy is the dominant crop, with three primary types cultivated: autumn, winter, and summer varieties. Among these, winter paddy is the most extensively grown, accounting for 70.6% of the district’s paddy cultivation29.

The district experiences a subtropical, humid climate with a pronounced seasonal variation. Humidity remains consistently high throughout the year, reaching approximately 90% during the rainy season. Winter begins in November and lasts until February, with December and January being the coldest months, where temperatures can drop to approximately 15 °C. From February or March, temperatures start to rise, reaching their peak in July or August, with highs of around 40 °C. Annual rainfall averaging 1951 mm, of which 60–65% falls between June and September during the southwest monsoon. Additionally, the district receives around 501 mm of rainfall during the pre-monsoon period from March to May, often as thunderstorms or hailstorms29.

Physiography, geology and geomorphology

Physiographically, the district consists of an alluvial plain with flat topography that gently slopes toward the Brahmaputra River, forming its southern boundary. The district’s soil cover includes both younger and older alluvium, which has undergone significant pedological variation. These soils are characterized by medium to high levels of organic carbon, along with low to medium phosphate and potassium content, influencing the area’s agricultural and ecological conditions. From a geological perspective, the entire district is made up of Quaternary sediments, both younger and older. The older alluvium, which is primarily composed of sand, gravel, pebbles, and clay, covers the northern foothill region, while the newer alluvium in the south is primarily composed of silt, clay, and fine sand29,30.

The geomorphology of the area is divided into three main zones: flood plain, younger alluvial plain, and inselberg. The flood plains, primarily along the Brahmaputra River and its tributaries, consist of loose gravel, sand, silt, and clay. These regions are shaped by seasonal flooding, creating features like paleo channels, natural levees, back swamps, wetlands, and channel bars, which are conducive to shallow groundwater development. The younger alluvial plain, occupying most of the district, sits at a slightly higher elevation than the flood plain and shares similar sediment composition. The inselberg zone, confined to a small area in the extreme southwestern part of the district, contains granite and granite gneisses as isolated hills, where groundwater can be accessed through dug wells in weathered areas30.

Aquifer characteristics

Groundwater is found in shallow aquifers under unconfined conditions, while deeper aquifers are semi-confined to confined. During the pre-monsoon period, groundwater levels typically range between 2 to 4 m below ground level (bgl) across most of the district. In the post-monsoon period, water levels rise to between 1 and 2 m bgl, with some areas experiencing water levels above ground29. Groundwater flows from the higher elevations in the northwest toward the Brahmaputra River in the southeast. The water table reaches a maximum of 80 m above mean sea level30.

Over the past decade, long-term monitoring reveals a pattern of rising water levels during the post-monsoon season, followed by a decline in the pre-monsoon period. However, no significant fluctuation was indicated in the groundwater levels within the district during the decade. Darrang has a substantial groundwater resource in a number of regionally widespread aquifers owing to physiographical, geological, and climatic advantageous conditions31.

Sampling process and analytical design

A total of 347 groundwater samples were collected from the entire Darrang district, Assam in 2023. The sampling zones were chosen to represent a range of geological features. Groundwater samples from shallow tubewells were collected using stratified random sampling method (grab sampling) covering all the community development blocks (7 nos.) of Darrang district viz. Bechimari, Dalgaon-Sialmari, Khairabari, Sipajhar, Pashim Mangaldoi, Pub Mangaldoi and Kalaigaon. Over 86 grids measuring an area of 16 km2/grid (4 km × 4 km) and average of 4 samples were collected from each grid. Following a 10-min pumping period, filtered groundwater (0.45 μ Millipore filters) samples were filled in polypropylene bottles and acidified to ≤ pH 2 with HNO3. The sampling locations were marked using a GPS set (GARMIN, Etnex 30×). The treated water samples were used for analysis of Ca2+, Mg2+, Fe and As. Similarly, another set of raw groundwater samples were collected in 2 L volume polypropylene bottles for analysis of other major ions and F−. Prior to collection, the bottles were pre-washed in a solution of 10% nitric acid and then rinsed with laboratory grade deionized water. All the samples were stored in ice box during transportation and kept at 4 °C in refrigerator until analysis were carried out within 7 days of batchwise collection. The locations of all sample sites were taken with the help of hand-held Global Positioning System (GPS) devices at the time of sampling (GARMIN, Etnex 30×). Temperature, pH, EC, TDS, turbidity and ORP of the water samples were measured in-situ by a handheld multi-parameter probe (Systronics 371). Chloride (Cl−), sulfate (SO42−) and bicarbonate (HCO3−) were estimated by standard titrimetric procedure. Sodium (Na+) and potassium (K+) were analysed using Flame Photometer (Systronics, Model 128). Fluoride levels were measured utilizing Fluoride Ion Selective Electrode (ISE) (Orion, Star A214). Whereas, calcium (Ca2+), magnesium (Mg2+), Iron (Fe) and arsenic (As) were analysed in Flow Injection Hydride Generation atomic adsorption spectrophotometer (FI-HG-AAS) (Thermo Fisher, iCE3500). The instrument was linearly calibrated from 1 to 100 μg L−1 using standard solution (Merck Multi-element Standard Solution). QA/QC procedures were compiled during the analysis, including retesting, external calibration, blank run, and standardization using NIST-certified standard reference materials.

Statistical analysis

The statistical software SPSS 21 was used for the statistical analysis. Cluster analysis (CA) is a multivariate technique commonly used to simplify big complicated data matrices and provide useful information on key parameters. The z-scaled approach was used to standardize the log-transformed data and invalidate the dimensions. Based on similarities across samples in a class, cluster analysis creates groupings of samples5. Samples can be grouped according to spatial similarity based on chemical composition using Hierarchical Cluster Analysis (HCA). The similarity between various clusters was measured by applying Ward’s approach (linkage between groups) and the squared Euclidean distance32.

Health risk analysis

Following the guidelines set forth by the United States Environment Protection Agency (USEPA, 1989)33, health risks assessment (HRA) was conducted the for different age populations of the study area to identify vulnerability to cancer from drinking water sources contaminated with As, Fe and F. The population under survey consisted of adults (19 years and above) and children (3–8 years). Average body weights of 10 kg for children, 70 kg for adults (male), and 58 kg for adults (female) were taken into account for the USEPA’s (1989) health risk analysis in this study. The process entails gathering data and analysing it to determine the possible health impacts on humans of any given hazard when exposed to particular environmental conditions6. The risk categorization helps to improve the policies in charge of preventing and minimizing threats to the health of people and the environment6.

To determine the carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks based on the reference dose (RfD) of a particular element, the USEPA structure for risk characterization implements Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) databases with hazard indices. For HRA, the Food and Nutrition Board’s recommended appropriate water intake rate and the USEPA’s (1989) body weight guidelines for adults and children were considered. Analytical accuracy

For every sample, the analytical accuracy between the total cation and total anion concentrations was expressed in milliequivalent per litre (meq L−1)35. The predicted Ionic Equilibrium Error (IBE) value lies within the acceptable range of ± 109.

Results and discussion

Physico-chemical composition and hydro geochemistry of groundwater

The concentrations of selected physicochemical parameters in the groundwater samples are shown in Supplementary Table. The pH value in the groundwater samples ranges between 6.01 and 8.47 with mean value of 7.42. Of the 347 samples analyzed, 173 (49.9%) had pH values in the range of 6.0–7.4, indicating near neutral to slightly alkaline conditions. Meanwhile, 174 samples (50.1%) fell within the 7.5–8.5 range, suggesting slightly alkaline conditions in these groundwater sources. The range of turbidity values is 0.01 to 315, with an average of 13.78 Nephelometric Turbidity Units (NTUs). The high turbidity in the groundwater sources of the research area is caused by a number of factors, including surface recharge, water runoff and weathering processes36. The electrical conductivity EC was found in the range of 72–1029 µS cm−1 (mean 280.3 µS cm−1) with higher EC (> 400 µS cm−1) in 30% of the samples.

The concentration of various major ions in the groundwater samples are presented in Supplementary Table. HCO3− (34.0–320 mg L−1, mean 101.3 mg L−1) and Ca2+ (0.63–111.38 mg L−1, mean 9.67 mg L−1) are the dominant ions in all groundwater samples with relatively high HCO3− in large number of samples. A positive linear correlation (R2 = 0.614) between EC and HCO3− indicates that increase in HCO3− concentration may contribute in the increase of EC levels in the groundwater. Chemical analysis imply that groundwater cations are in decreasing order of: Ca2+ > Na+ > K+ > Mg2+, while anions are as HCO3− > SO42− > Cl−. The low concentration of SO42− (mean 15.17 mg L−1) in the groundwater of the region may be due to bacteria mediated reducing environment owing to the presence of organic matter implying that the aquifer sediments of the area are rich in organic matter with reducing aquifer environments. Such environments create favorable conditions for proliferating reducing bacteria.

The distribution of total As in the groundwater ranging between 0.125 µg L−1 to 100.19 µg L−1 with 78% (n = 274) of the samples exceeding the WHO limit37. The range of Fe values are BDL to 9.341 mg L−1, with 97% samples exceeding the permissible limit of Fe (0.3 mg L−1)37. However, all groundwater samples (100%) reported within the permissible limit for F (1.5 mg L−1)37 (Supplementary Table).

Hydrochemical facies

Hydrochemical facies represent the total groundwater state inside a lithological structure and can be somewhat helpful in determining the source and movement of groundwater38. Based on the prevalent groundwater facies, the anticipated hydrogeochemical regime of an aquifer was estimated using the Piper plot (1944)39. Figure 1 illustrates the irregular dispersion of significant ions, represented on the Hill-Piper trilinear diagram generated using AQUA software. Six distinct types—Ca2+-HCO3− type, Na+-Cl− type, Mixed Ca2+-Mg2+-Cl− type, Mixed Ca2+-Na+-HCO3− type, and Ca2+-Cl− type, Na+-HCO3−—are represented by its two triangles at the base and one diamond shape at the top9. In reference to the hydrogeology of Darrang district, Assam, a detailed analysis of groundwater samples reveals a predominance of the mixed Ca2⁺-Na⁺-HCO3⁻ type and the Ca2⁺-HCO3⁻ type. A subset of samples also belongs to Na⁺-HCO3⁻ type. In the cation triangle of the Piper diagram, sample points predominantly cluster within Zone B, indicating no dominant cationic type, while some points align with Ca2⁺-dominant Zone A and Na⁺-dominant Zone D. The anion triangle reveals that the samples follow an anionic trend aligning majorly over the edge of HCO3− type (Zone E) and sparsely along the edge of SO42− type (Zone F). This distribution implies the district’s groundwater is influenced by a complex mineralogical profile resulting from diverse geochemical interactions.

In Darrang, these ions likely stem from specific geogenic sources. The abundance of Ca2⁺ and HCO3⁻ ions are generally attributable to the dissolution of carbonate minerals, such as limestone and calcareous sediments, commonly present in the region29. Moreover, 50% of the samples (Fig. 1) belongs to Ca2⁺-HCO3⁻ type water indicating alkalinity induced As dissolution. Concurrently, the dominance of mixed Ca2+-Na+-HCO3− and Na+-HCO3− water type water observed in rest ~ 50% of the samples in the study area. The dominance of Ca2+-Na+-HCO3− water type may be the cause of alkaline pH in majority of the groundwater samples in the region (pH 7.5–8.5 in > 50% of the samples, mean pH = 7.42). Increased concentration of Na+ and Ca2+ may be attributed to rock water interaction, chemical weathering and ion exchange7. Furthermore, spatial variation in water type (Fig. 1) across the region could likely be due to the cation exchange of Ca2+ and Mg2+ for Na+. Moreover, Na+ ions are likely be derived from feldspar and clay mineral weathering within the aquifer systems31, a process that releases Na+ into the groundwater. The detection of SO42− in small number of samples indicates the possible dissolution of SO42−-bearing minerals, like gypsum, or the oxidation of sulfide minerals in the aquifer matrix under favorable redox conditions8. Additionally, these minerals tend to accumulate in the groundwater through processes such as ion exchange and cation leaching from surrounding sediments, which are influenced by the region’s subtropical climate and seasonal recharge patterns9.

Delineating the major hydrogeochemical processes

In the present study, EC (μS cm−1) analysis of groundwater revealed 61.6% were in good condition, while 36.8% were with slightly higher EC values. Evaporation typically results in higher concentrations of all species in water. If the evaporation process is dominant and no mineral species precipitate, then the Na+/Cl− ratio remains unchanged43, resulting in a horizontal line in Na+/Cl− vs EC, indicating concentration due to evaporation and evapo-transpiration. If Na is caused by halite breakdown, the Na+/Cl− molar ratio should be approximately equal to 1, while a ratio greater than 1 indicates Na released by a silicate weathering process44. In the present study, it was found that the molar ratio of Na+/Cl− in groundwater samples in the study area ranged from 219.7 to 29,465. As most of the samples in the study have Na+/Cl− molar ratio above 1, it indicates that silicate weathering is the major process in the study area45.

The EC versus Na+/Cl− trend in groundwater suggests that evaporation is not a major geochemical process in the area. An increasing Na+:Cl− ratio with EC indicates an excess of Na+ from silicate weathering, which appears to be dominating process in the study (Fig. 3a). Groundwater samples from Nagaon (84%), Kamrup (49%), and West Karbi Anglong (78%)27, all revealed an excess of Na+ over Cl− including the study area Darrang (100%). The ratio of Na+ + K+ ions to total cations (Tz+) is used to determine the silicate weathering process5. Especially in hard-rock aquifers, silicate weathering is one of the primary geochemical processes regulating the main ions chemistry of the groundwater47. According to a previous study, samples from the Nagaon district, Assam exhibited silicate weathering since they were located along 0.5 Tz+ line (Na+ + K+) vs Total cation (Tz+) scatter diagram5. As per the findings of Stallard and Edmond48, samples that are situated above 0.5 Tz+ aquiline are classified as low silicate weathering processes. In the present study (Fig. 3c), a portion of the samples lie around the 0.5 Tz+ line and trend towards Na+ + K+ = Tz+ line, indicating the impact of silicate weathering on the groundwater. The samples showing a linear relationship with the line 1:1 (Ca2+ + Mg2+ = Tz+) show a high concentration of Ca and Mg-rich minerals due to silicate weathering. Samples that are above the Ca2+ + Mg+ = 0.5 Tz+ line suggest a reduced amount of silicate21. In Fig. 3d, multiple samples exceeded the 0.5Tz+ line, indicating low silicate weathering. However, no samples aligned with the 1:1 aquiline, implying presence of other geochemical mechanisms.

Calcite and dolomite are significant sources of Ca2+ and Mg2+, as indicated by plots close to the 1:1 line. If ion exchange is dominant, the points shift to the right, while in reverse ion exchange a contrasting pattern is observed resulting in an excess of Ca2+ and Mg2+ over49,50 SO42− and HCO3−. The scatter diagram (Ca2+ + Mg2+ vs. HCO3− + SO42−) shows whether the ions originate from silicate or carbonate weathering. Figure 3e shows that all samples in the research area fell below the aquiline, showing that weathering of silicate minerals is the predominant hydrochemistry of groundwater that supplies ions in solution, with ion exchange dominating for points shifted to the right. Most samples in Ca2+ versus HCO3− ratio plot tend to be above 1:2 (Fig. 3f), indicating calcite weathering. Only 0.58% (n = 2) of samples fell below the 1:1 line, indicating carbonate weathering8.

A Ca2+ Vs SO42− plot that is closer to the 1:1 line indicates that the source of Ca2+ and SO42− in groundwater is gypsum dissolution51. Most of the samples lie below the 1:1 equiline, as seen in Fig. 3g. This groundwater’s excess of Ca2+ over SO42− suggests that Ca2+ is derived by gypsum dissolution51. Ca2+ + Mg2+ concentrations in groundwater were much higher than Cl− (Fig. 3h), indicating that Ca2+ + Mg2+ surpasses salinity at a higher rate45.

In this study, high As groundwater (> 10 μg L−1) typically had pH values of 6–8 or higher (Fig. 5a). An increase in As levels has been linked to elevated HCO3− levels in reported studies5. According to Kim et al.52, higher HCO3− can cause As to be released into groundwater, indicating that the groundwater recharge process speeds up As leaching in aquifers. From Fig. 5b, the HCO3− reveals a poor correlation with As concentration (R2 = 0.0562), suggesting that HCO3− has a direct competitive effect on As for the adsorption site is not the only dominant process involved in Darrang’s As mobilization53. The possibility that the mobilization of As in ground water is caused by the reduction of Fe oxide is suggested by a weak positive correlation (R2 = 0.0035) noticed between As and Fe (Fig. 5c). Moreover, groundwater pH was found to be alkaline with > 50% of the samples showing pH within 7.5–8.5 with mean pH 7.4. Therefore, desorption of As in alkaline environment may be one of the significant As release mechanism in the region. The observed high As concentrations can be explained by the desorption of As from Fe (hydr)oxides during aquifer recharge. The oxidation of As-containing SO42− releases As in oxidizing aquifer conditions10. Thus, when it comes to oxidizing groundwater, As and SO42− have a beneficial relationship. In the present study, it is evident from the low concentrations of SO42− in groundwater samples and the weak correlation (R2 = 0.0073) between As and SO42− (Fig. 5d) that As has not been directly mobilized from sulfide minerals such as arsenopyrite54. The lack of significant association in this study may be because of the aquifers’ declining environmental conditions8. The SO42− geographical distribution indicates that the study’s total concentration is far below the WHO37 (< 250 mg L−1) recommended daily intake levels.

Hierarchical cluster analysis

Hierarchical Cluster analysis (HCA) assists in predicting the common qualities shared by several variables. HCA was used in the study to understand the complex hydro geochemistry resulting from several processes namely ion exchange, and weathering of anthropogenic or natural origin9. Cluster 1 comprised of Ca2+, Mg2+, EC, TA, HCO3−, K+, pH and Cl− whereas cluster 2 comprised of SO42−, Fe, As, F and Na+ (Fig. 7). Cluster 1 indicates the occurrence of pH induced carbonate weathering process implying active dissolution as a governing mechanism. Cluster 2 suggests influence of anthropogenic activities such as use of chemical fertilizers during cultivation, as well as geogenic process such as weathering of minerals is increasing the concentration of associated contaminants in the groundwater55,56. Health risk assessment

Non-carcinogenic risk

A Hazard Quotient (HQ) score greater than one indicates that residents may be at risk for health problems, but a value less than one indicates that customers are safe57. For children, HQ ranged from 0.07 to 42.08 with 98.8% samples having HQ > 1, due to exposure to As. For female adults, HQ ranged from 0.05 to 11.52 and in male from 0.02 to 13.08. 93.9% and 95.1% samples exceeded HQ values > 1 for female and male respectively. The highest HQ value was found in children (max HQ = 42.08). Notably, for exposure to Fe, 100% of all sampling age group were below the typical toxic risk index of 1. The result indicates that the following is the order of human health non-carcinogenic risk based on the HQ values > 1 of the pollutant F, Children (8.64%) > Adults (0). The children could be at more risk than adults due to ingestion of such high F and As content water.

Carcinogenic risk

Carcinogenic risk is the probability that an individual will develop cancer at some point in their lifetime58. Cancer risk numbers more than 10−4 are regarded as dreadful, while those lower than 10−6 are regarded as insignificant33, and risk values lying between 10−6 and 10−4 are generally considered as acceptable59. The potential cancer risk (more than 10−4) for As polluted water exhibited the following trend: Children (99.42%) > Adults (99.13%). Overall, the study shows that since younger generations are more vulnerable, health risk assessment monitoring should be a top priority. Mitigation strategies

The contamination of groundwater with high levels of As, alongside potential F and Fe presence, presents a significant environmental and public health challenge in Darrang district, Assam. Effective mitigation of these contaminants requires a comprehensive and sustainable approach, addressing both immediate and long-term community needs. One of the first recommendations is the regular testing of groundwater sources, as contaminant levels may vary spatially and temporally. Implementing a systematic groundwater quality monitoring program across Darrang district would allow for the identification of high-risk areas, enabling targeted interventions. In rural Assam, shallow tube wells are often contaminated with As, hence encouraging the use of deeper wells, which are typically lower in As, could be a viable alternative solution. However, deep tube wells should be tested for F, as they can be introduced from different geological layers.

In addition to testing and monitoring, community-level treatment solutions can significantly reduce exposure to contaminants. Household-level filters, mainly bio-sand filters modified with iron nails and charcoal can effectively reduce both As and Fe concentrations in drinking water. However, these solutions require financial and technical support for regular maintenance, and community awareness on proper usage and maintenance is crucial for effectiveness.

Therefore, educational initiatives to raise awareness about groundwater contamination and preventive measures are essential for long-term success. Community health programs can educate residents on the risks of consuming untreated water, encouraging the adoption of safe drinking water practices. Moreover, policymakers must address the issue by strengthening regulatory standards for water quality and promoting the installation of centralized treatment facilities in affected regions. Partnerships with NGOs and government agencies can bring financial and technical support to help rural communities implement these recommendations effectively. Moreover, alternate safe surface water sources such as dug well, river and stream water sources may be explored as viable and long-term mitigation approach.

Conclusion

The study revealed that the groundwater in the Darrang district is influenced by various local geological factors. The study identified pH as a significant factor influencing As concentration, indicating desorption of As in alkaline environment may be one of the significant mechanism governing As release in the region. Hydrogeochemical analysis using Piper and Gibbs’ plots indicated that the dominant processes affecting groundwater chemistry include carbonate weathering, ion exchange, and mineral dissolution. The weak positive correlation between As and Fe suggests the potential role of reductive dissolution of iron oxides in As mobilization. Cluster analysis highlighted the influence of local geology and agricultural activities on groundwater contamination. Health risk analysis revealed significant non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks associated with prolonged exposure to As. Children were found to be particularly vulnerable, with higher health risk indices compared to adults. Spatial distribution maps illustrate the most susceptible zones in terms of As concentration and possible human health hazards resulting from ongoing oral exposure. Consequently, the analysis provides a comprehensive picture of geogenic contaminants in the groundwater resources of the area and identifies potential geographically vulnerable zones based on possible health risks to humans. This new database for the region might be useful for future groundwater applications as well as groundwater management initiatives in the area.
