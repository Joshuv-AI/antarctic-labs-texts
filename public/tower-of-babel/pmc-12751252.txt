The readiness of knowledge, attitude and confidence and associated factors of implementing living wills among healthcare professionals in Shenzhen: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yao Tang, Mengshu Li, Yiran Zhu, Ran Huo, Xianhong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOn January 1st, 2023, Shenzhen became the first city to legalize living wills in China. However, less was known about the readiness of healthcare professionals (HCPs) to implement Article 78 of the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone Medical Regulations. Our team aimed to timely examine the HCPs’ readiness in Shenzhen, including their knowledge, attitude and confidence as well as associated factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 685 doctors and nurses across 16 hospitals using convenience sampling between February and March 2023 in Shenzhen, China. A validated, self-designed electronic questionnaire consisting of 22 items was used to assess knowledge, attitude, confidence, and related factors. The scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) for these three dimensions was 94.4%, 96.7%, and 93.3%, respectively. The internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) for the three dimensions was 0.693, 0.944, and 0.890, respectively.ResultsOnly 315 participants (46.0%) demonstrated adequate knowledge (score ≥ 9), with an average correct rate of 65.4%. Most participants had a positive attitude (644/685, 94.0%), and 596 (87.0%) scored ≥ 16 on confidence. However, many lacked confidence in validating living wills (350/685, 51.1%), mitigating legal risks (322/685, 47.0%), and adhering to ethical principles (161/685, 23.5%). HCPs who had cared for terminally ill patients were more likely to have better knowledge (OR = 1.391, 95% CI: 1.013–1.910), attitude (OR = 2.564, 95% CI: 1.316–4.997), and confidence (OR = 1.703, 95% CI: 1.086–2.670). Those with a bachelor’s (OR = 3.348, 95% CI: 1.613–6.951) or master’s degree (OR = 2.645, 95% CI: 1.080–6.477) showed more positive attitudes. Prior training improved confidence (OR = 3.035, 95% CI: 1.080–8.529). Most participants preferred doctors (556/685, 81.17%), family members (513/685, 74.89%), and lawyers (511/685, 74.6%) to discuss living wills, while fewer preferred nurses (321/685, 46.86%) or social workers (241/685, 35.18%).ConclusionHCPs generally supported living wills but lacked legal and ethical confidence for implementation. Targeted training integrating medical, legal, ethical, and cultural components is urgently needed to promote patient autonomy and support policy implementation in Shenzhen.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01338-7.Keywords: Living wills, Advanced directives, Health policy, End-of-life care, China

Introduction

Terminally ill patients tend to experience serious suffering and lose life autonomy. According to Lancet, by 2060, an estimated 48 million people (47% of all deaths globally) will die with serious health-related suffering, representing an 87% increase from 26 million people in 2016 [1]. In most cases, invasive interventions prolonged suffering rather than improving survival quality [2]. A systematic review indicated that 33%−38% of patients aged 60 or older received invasive and potentially harmful hospital treatment during their last six months of life [2]. Moreover, based on an analysis of 1,719 ICU patients regarding the use of life support, terminally ill patients seldom had the autonomy to decide what kinds of care they would like to receive [3], because the majority were unconscious, in distress, or unable to speak. Specifically, only 5% had decision capacity or participated in discussions about treatment. Instead, the terminal-care decisions were usually made by their relatives or HCPs. Therefore, as one of the basic ethical principles of healthcare, terminally ill patients’ life autonomy is not fully guaranteed currently [4].

Existing evidence [5] has confirmed the benefits of implementing living wills in minimizing the suffering and improving the living quality in patients’ end-of-life stage. Living wills, also known as advanced directives, are written documents that allow people to give explicit instructions about medical treatments to be administered when he or she is terminally ill and unable to communicate with others [6]. According to a study conducted in Switzerland [7] patients in palliative care units who had living wills demonstrated fewer depressive symptoms and less anxiety. In Australia [8], an RCT study showed that living wills improved end-of-life quality, satisfied patients and their families, and reduced stress, anxiety, and depression in surviving relatives. A nation-wide study carried out in the United States [9] in the year 2000, revealed that patients with living wills could improve communication with doctors about decision-making and had better family preparation on what to expect while dying.

Living wills are currently legal in certain but not all countries. The United States was the first country to legalize living wills, starting with California’s adoption of the first living-will statute in 1976, which clarified the right of a person to refuse life-sustaining medical technology [10]. After that, in Europe, the Netherlands led the way enacting a specific law on living wills in 1994 [11]. By the end of 2021, 15 of the 28 EU members have legalized or developed specific rules on living wills [12]. In 2009, Germany enacted legislation on living wills, and during the past decade, 43.4% of the citizens reported having created at least one living will according to a national survey in 2023 [13]. In Asia, Taiwan passed the “Regulations on Tranquility and Ease of Care” in 2000, becoming the first region to legalize living wills in Asia [6].

Although some countries have already legalized living wills, in mainland China, the legislative process of approving living wills was relatively slow. In 2006, the Beijing “Choice and Dignity” public welfare association (later known as “Beijing Living Will Promotion Association”) launched a non-legally-binding campaign entitled “My Five Wishes”, advocating “dignity death” and allowed end-of-life patients to fulfill their autonomy and express their medical wishes [14]. During the last decade, researchers assessed people’ knowledge and attitude about living wills. A study [15] surveyed 467 elder residents from 31 nursing homes in Wuhan in 2014. This study indicated that 95.3% had never heard of living wills, and after being informed about living wills, less than 31% preferred to make a living will. The situation became much better in 2017. A multicenter cross-sectional study [16] carried out in 15 different provinces showed that among 1084 adult patients, 38.3% had heard about living wills; after being informed about it, 50.6% were willing to make living wills. Compared to the general population, currently Chinese HCPs held more positive attitudes [17, 18] towards implementing living wills according to two studies in Zhengzhou, a big city in the middle of China. But a national study in 2024 among Chinese oncology HCPs [19] indicated that they did not prepare adequately in practice to implement living wills. One big barrier might be that HCPs had ethical concerns related to discuss living wills within the Chinese cultural context, which prioritizes the prolonging of life rather than living quality [19]. Moreover, Chinese people often prioritize “relational autonomy,” and put more importance on families’ decisions rather than individual decision, which might make the decision of living wills more complicated and difficult [19].

Despite the fact that living wills have been successfully implemented in some countries and are recognized for ensuring patient autonomy, progress in China has been slow due to its unique cultural characteristics. Fortunately, on January 1 st, 2023, Shenzhen became the first city to legalize living wills in China [20], according to Article 78 of the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone Medical Regulations (for Chinese original and English translation, see Supplement 1). In spite of this unprecedented legalization, less was known about the readiness of HCPs in Shenzhen in terms of implementing this Act.

Our team aimed to timely examine the HCPs’ readiness in Shenzhen during the early stage of the regulation, including their knowledge, attitude, and confidence to implement living wills, as well as associated factors. The results would provide evidence for future training on living-will implementation for HCPs, which is urgently needed in China.

Methods

Design and setting

A cross-sectional study was designed to investigate the readiness to implement living wills among HCPs between February and March 2023 in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province, China. There were 151 hospitals, 47,234 registered doctors and 51,569 registered nurses in Shenzhen by the end of 2022 [20]. In order to capture HCPs’ readiness across different levels of hospitals, we purposively chose 16 out of 151 hospitals in Shenzhen, including 12 Grade III hospital, 2 Grade II hospital and 2 Grade I hospital by convenience, which generally represent a broader healthcare system in Shenzhen. The number of HCPs in these 16 hospitals is estimated to account for 1/10 of the total HCPs in Shenzhen. Given that our aim was to assess HCPs’ knowledge, attitudes, and confidence at a single time point, rather than to measure changes over time, a cross-sectional design was considered appropriate.

Participants

The inclusion criteria were: (1) registered doctors or nurses; (2) full-time staff working in 16 hospitals in Shenzhen; (3) volunteering to participate in this study. Those who did not directly provide care to patients (e.g., those working in clinical labs, radiology departments, nutritional departments) were excluded from the study. To reduce sampling bias, we selected hospitals of different levels (Grade I–III) and invited both doctors and nurses across various departments. Participation was anonymous and voluntary, and recruitment was conducted through internal communication channels without direct investigator involvement. This approach helped minimize social desirability and selection bias.

The sample size was calculated using the standard cross-sectional study formula n = (Zα² × S²)/d², incorporating a 95% confidence level (Zα = 1.96), standard deviation (S = 2.34) from Chen’s [21] prior work, and 0.2 margin of error (d = 0.2), yielding a minimum requirement of 548 participants. In this study, a total of 685 participants were ultimately included. Power analysis based on our research findings demonstrated that all predictive models exceeded the 0.8 threshold (knowledge = 0.821, attitude = 0.999, confidence = 0.874), indicating that our sample size was adequate.

Instruments

Sociodemographic characteristics

Variables include occupation, gender, age, education level, marital status, professional title, monthly income, hospital level, departments, (administrative) position, experience of taking care of terminally ill patients, experience of executing living wills, and training experience.

Self-developed living wills readiness scale

The initial items were developed according to the specific information regarding living wills contained in Article 78 of the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone Medical Regulations (Supplement 1). A panel of six experts—including two nursing researchers, two ethics scholars, and two clinicians—reviewed and revised the survey items through two rounds of online meetings to ensure content validity. The final instrument consists of 22 items across three dimensions: knowledge readiness, attitude readiness, and implementation competency. The scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) for these three dimensions is 94.4%, 96.7%, and 93.3% respectively. The internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) for the three dimensions is 0.693, 0.944, and 0.890, respectively.

Items 1 to 12 are designed to assess participants’ knowledge readiness regarding living wills, including their definition, establishment, cancellation, and other related information. Among the 12 items, items 3, 8, and 12 are reverse scored. Response options include “true”, “false” and “uncertain” with only one correct answer per item. One point is awarded for each correct response, yielding a total possible score ranging from 0 to 12. This scoring system is based on a previously established knowledge assessment scale [22]. 75% (9/12) accuracy is established as the dividing line between high and low levels. Therefore, a total score of 9–12 is categorized as high, and 0–8 is categorized as low.

Items 13 to 17 are designed to assess participants’ attitude readiness toward living wills. Each item is graded by Likert-5 scale, from “completely agree” to “completely disagree”. The total score range is 5–25, with a score of 5–15 indicating negative attitude and a score of 16–25 indicating positive attitude. According to the Likert-5 scale, a score of ≤ 3 for a single item is a negative choice of “Neutral” or “Disagree”. Since the scale has five questions, a total score of ≤ 15 falls into the low score range.

Items 18 to 22 are designed to evaluate HCPs’ confidence to execute living wills accurately and avoid risks. Consistent with the standard we established in measuring attitude readiness, we continue to employ the Likert-5 scale, with the same cut-off score of 15 to discriminate adequate competency and non-adequate competency.

Semi-structured questionnaire

We added 3 multiple-choice questions with optional open-ended responses to gather participants’ views and suggestions regarding living will policy in China. All the questionnaires can be seen in supplement 2.

Data collection

Data was collected using Wenjuanxing (www.sojump.com), a professional online questionnaire platform in China. Initially, we posted flyers in Wechat chatting groups in each hospital, after receiving permission from their administration departments. Those who were interested could scan the QR code on the flyer to access the online questionnaire. After they had read the electronic informed consent form and clicked upon the “Informed and agreed” button, they could start to fill out the questionnaire. Each IP of the smartphone or computer was restricted to one login. Surveys submitted in less than 1 min were deemed invalid. All survey items were compulsory to ensure the intactness of the data.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was received from the Ethics Committee of Xiangya School of Nursing, Central South University (E2022161) and the Ethical Review Committee of The University of Hong Kong-Shenzhen Hospital(hkuszh2022236). Before beginning the study, respondents were informed about the anonymous and voluntary nature of the survey; and the confidentiality of all participants was assured. Electronic informed consent was obtained from each participant. We did not collect any personally identifiable information (e.g., name, phone number, etc.) and stored the data on offline computers that could only be viewed by the researchers in this project.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corporation) was used to perform all data analyses. The uncompleted surveys were deemed invalid and excluded from the final database; and all the completed survey were intact without missing data since all items were compulsory. Cases and percentages were used to describe categorical data. Chi-Square test was used for univariate analysis; and significant factors in univariate analyses were included in the logistic regression analyze models to determine the associate factors for implementing Article 78 of the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone Medical Regulations (Supplement 1). The potential confounding factors including demographic and work-related characteristics were examined using multivariate logistic regression models. The significance level was set at a 2-tailed with P value < 0.05.

Results

Initially 983 participants filled out the screening questionnaire, and 233 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria. Of the remaining 750, 65 were excluded due to filling out the questionnaire < 1 min, resulting in a final analytical sample of 685. The majority of the participants were female (510/685, 74.5%), aged 21–30 (371/685, 54.2%) with 41.8% (155/685) of doctors and 58.2% (216/685) of nurses. A total of 421 (61.5%) participants cared for terminally ill patients, 82 (12.0%) had executed living wills, and only 82 (12.0%) participants had participated in specific training programs on living wills. The knowledge of implementing living wills

Among 685 participants, less than half (315/685, 46.0%) had adequate knowledge of living wills (score ≥ 9), and the overall accuracy of responses was 65.4%. For each item, the accuracy rate was presented in Table 2. Most of the participants knew the content of a living will (Q5, Q6, Q10). However, a large proportion of participants held misconceptions about living wills. For example, 88.0% (603/685) of participants thought that living wills being notarized or witnessed by one or more individuals were considered valid (actually two or more witnesses are needed; Q8); 70.5% (483/685) participants believed that if an elderly person or patient was unable to express their wishes, immediate family members were authorized to sign on their behalf (actually immediate family members can’t sign on their behalf; Q3); and 59.4% (407/685) participants believed that living wills signed by the patient during their minority could take effect during the terminal phase of their life (actually only living wills signed in adulthood are valid; Q12). In addition, only 49.6% (340/685) participants knew that HCPs involved in the treatment of a patient could not be witnesses to a living will (Q9).

The attitude towards implementing living wills

Overall, most (644/685, 94.0%) participants had a positive attitude (with a score ≥ 16). Results show that 88.4% (606/685) participants were willing to learn more about living wills, while 81.3% (557/685) participants were willing to promote living wills to patients and their families (Table 3).

The confidence of implementing living wills

About 87.0% (596/685) of the participants attained the higher score (≥ 16) in the confidence-to-implement assessment. Among 685 participants, 85.7% (587/685) participants were confident in their ability of respecting the most recent version of a patient’s living will in case of updates, while half of participants doubted or were not sure about their ability of assessing the validity of a patient’s living wills (350/685, 51.1%). Half doubted or were not sure about their ability of mitigating legal risks when executing living wills (322/685, 47.0%). 23.5% (161/685) felt difficult to adhere to ethical principles (Table 4).

Factors influencing the implementation of living wills

Significant variables in chi-square test (Table 1) were put into the logistic regression models and results were presented in Table 5. The results indicate that HCPs who had cared for terminally ill patients were more likely to have a better level of knowledge (OR = 1.391, 95% CI: 1.013, 1.910). Compared to HCPs earning less than $1,393 a month, those earning $1,392–$4,176 (OR = 1.628, 95% CI: 1.169, 2.266) were more likely to obtain higher knowledge levels. Interestingly, HCPs in administrative positions were less likely to demonstrate higher levels of knowledge (OR = 0.611, 95% CI: 0.410, 0.912).

Concerning attitude level, HCPs with bachelor’s degree (OR = 3.348, 95% CI: 1.613, 6.951) and master’s degree or above (OR = 2.645, 95% CI: 1.080, 6.477) and having cared for terminally ill patients (OR = 2.564, 95% CI: 1.316, 4.997) were more likely to obtain higher attitude level than HCPs with associate degrees and below.

Opinions and advice for implementing living wills

In terms of who should take the primary responsibility to communicate living wills with patients, HCPs preferred doctors (556/685, 81.2%), family members (513/685, 74.9%) and lawyers (511/685, 74.6%), then following by nurses (321, 46.9%) and social workers (241, 35.2%). In terms of who should participate in making living-will decisions, 80.0% (548/685) of HCPs selected patients, 78.2% (536/685) selected family members, 54.0% (370/685) selected doctors, 54.5% (373/685) selected lawyers, and 30.4% (208/685) selected nurses. Among those who selected patients, 83.8% (459/548) also selected family members, 59.3% (325/548) also selected lawyers and 51.8% (284/548) also selected doctors, while only less than 1/3 (165/548, 30.1%) selected nurses. Only about 1/3 (251/685, 36.6%) of all HCPs selected patients, family members, HCPs at the same time, while 1/4 (170/685, 24.8%) selected patients, family members, HCPs and lawyers at the same time (Fig. 1).

The proportion of HCPs regarding who should be involved in decision-making

Respondents held some concerns regarding implementing the legislation. For instance, 74.7% of HCPs (512/685) were unclear about “how to define the incurable status of patients”. 73.6% of HCPs (504/685) were unclear when to implement living wills if “the patient could not clearly express their choices or preferences for certain treatment?”, and 67.2% of HCPs (460/685) were unclear “whether a living will made outside of Shenzhen can be implemented in Shenzhen”. 1.7% of participants (12/685) expressed other concerns, including: “Does the living wills need to be registered effectively?”, “How to do when family’s wish is conflict with the patient’s living wills?”, “How to make sure the patient is fully informed about living wills?” and “How to protect doctor from being blamed by families if they implement patient’s living wills”.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess HCPs’ readiness to implement living wills immediately after their legalization in China. Our results indicated that HCPs have not prepared well to implement living wills in clinical settings. Their concerns and relevant factors have been identified. The study results are very timely to help policy makers, healthcare administrators, legal and medical scholars to carry out targeted strategies and training for HCPs, so as to fully protect terminally ill patients’ autonomy and guarantee people’s end-of-life living quality.

Knowledge and associated factors

In the current study, HCPs’ knowledge about living wills was moderate, with an overall accuracy of 65.4%, which was higher than that of Taiwan (52.7%) [23], but lower than that of the United States (67%) [24]. However, a systematic review [25] pointed out a commonality across different countries, indicating that participants generally understood the contents of living wills, while there was an insufficient grasp of the detailed legal contents related to living wills. The experience of caring for terminally ill patients has been identified as a key facilitator for gaining knowledge by our study as well as previous studies [19, 26], because the more exposure to death, the more experienced providers reflect on end-of-life care, and consequently, they gain more knowledge about living wills [26]. Besides, our study also found that monthly income and whether in an administrative position were also associated with knowledge level. Income between $1,392–$4,176 per month showed a higher level of knowledge, because individuals in this group often serve as direct caregivers with extensive patient care experience. Interestingly, HCPs holding administrative positions demonstrated a lower knowledge level, contrary to previous research findings [23, 27]. In our study, administrative positions are limited to two types: head nurse and department director. Both roles involve dual responsibilities by providing direct care to patients as well as managing the overall running of the department. Thus, they might be too busy to pay attention to the living-will knowledge and lack authoritative training on living wills. However, the exact potential reasons are not clear, and further understanding could be gained through qualitative studies in the future. These findings underscore the importance of improving awareness of living wills in China and provide evidence on addressing influencing factors in future targeted training.

Attitudes and associated factors

Our study showed that Chinese HCPs in Shenzhen hold positive and supportive attitudes towards legislation of living wills, which was consistent with a study in Taiwan [28]. A systematic review [29] encompassing Asian countries (including Japan, Korea, Singapore, etc.) revealed that most HCPs perceived living wills as useful or important (e.g., 71%−94% in Japan, 96%−97% in South Korea). The reason might lie in that HCPs believe that living wills can achieve mutual understanding between patients and their families regarding their values [30, 31]. However, 18.8% HCPs in our study were reluctant to promote living wills to patients and their families. In Chinese culture, talking about death is deemed as unlucky for the family, thus, HCPs usually feel psychological pressure when discussing end-of-life issues with patients and their families [32]. Additionally, the filial piety belief requires family members to try their best to save the patient’s life rather than enhancing the quality of life. There is a proverb in Chinese, saying “better to live than die”. Therefore, it is difficult for HCPs to discuss living wills with the family members or the patients. Additionally, our research findings indicated that the higher the educational level and the more experience of caring for terminally ill patients, the more positive attitudes towards living wills HCPs hold, which is consistent with previous studies [33, 34]. These findings reveal an important ethical dilemma faced by HCPs in the context of Chinese culture regarding how to implement living wills. We propose an urgent call for designing cultural adaptive training modules for HCPs as well as the general population in future studies.

Implementation and recommended training

Generally, Chinese HCPs in Shenzhen showed confidence to implement living wills. However, the items related to legal and ethical confidence received low scores. Namely they were uncertain about assessing the validity of a patient’s living wills and were unable to mitigate legal risks when executing living wills. Our data indicates that 23.5% of respondents disagree or are unsure about their ability to adhere to ethical principles. This was consistent with a systematic review conducted by Ryan Diane in the United States, which indicated that HCPs (n = 1,583) rated themselves as having little confidence in knowing legal provisions. Similarly, another integrative review in the United States [35] also indicated that HCPs reported moderate confidence helping patients and families with living wills, but they did not rate themselves as highly confident regarding implementing the Patient Self-Determination Act and state laws.

Considering the current legal knowledge gap, cultural sensitivities, and the implementation concerns, we suggest that ethics training should be prioritized over medical-technical training to ensure that HCPs are equipped to handle these sensitive discussions respectfully and effectively. The training modules and strategies should include but not be limited to the following: First, legal terms and their interpretations related to living wills should be delivered to HCPs comprehensively, such as using lectures, case examples or scenarios. This could help HCPs become well-versed in the legal aspects of living wills and to avoid legal repercussions and ensure the patient’s wishes are respected. Second, culture-adaptive ethical training should be emphasized, since life autonomy and end-of-life decisions are heavily influenced by cultural norms, and a significant proportion of Chinese HCPs are influenced by this cultural belief about death and lack confidence to discuss living wills with patients. Thus, helping HCPs navigate the cultural challenges associated with discussing end-of-life care and living wills with patients and their families, and ensuring that HCPs can balance medical interventions with the patient’s expressed wishes, are the keys to maintaining the integrity of the living will and its successful implementation.

In fact, since 2023, the Shenzhen Living Wills Promotion Association [36] has conducted various ethical-related training, such as lectures, story-sharing, film-watching and debrief, and so on, however, the cultural sensitivity and effectiveness need to be evaluated in the future. Third, training on communication skills cannot be overlooked, because it illustrates the culturally and ethically sensitive implementation of living wills. Previous similar training modules [37, 38] having been tested in western countries could be adapted in Chinese cultural context. Besides this, Shenzhen as the first city to legalize living wills has its unique strength to overcome the culturally rooted ethical dilemma, because Shenzhen is a recently built modern city with open attitude towards diverse cultural integrity and acceptability. There is intrinsic need for such kind of training for HCPs. Therefore, well-designed training programs on living wills involving ethics, law, and medical techniques would be beneficial.

Opinions and advice for implementing living wills from HCPs

Regarding who should be involved in living will decision-making, interestingly, 20% of HCPs do not recommend patients themselves be involved. It is largely due to the paternalistic cultural context in China, which emphasizes the responsibility of the family to make decisions and care for the family members [6]. In terms of co-decision making, 2/3 of participants would like patients and family members to be involved, while less than half of them would like doctors, lawyers to be involved. Nurses’ involvement was largely not preferred. A study in Japan [39] proposed a “family unit advance directive”, advocating for shared decision-making by the family rather than the patient alone, but HCPs were not involved. Our study results emphasize the involvement of doctors, since patients usually struggle to make end-of-life decisions and determine what kind of life support is needed, while doctors have the professional knowledge and could help make the appropriate decision [40]. Further qualitative research is needed to explore the complex dynamics among patients, family members, doctors, nurses, and other stakeholders involved in living will decision-making within the context of Chinese culture.

In our study, HCPs expressed some thoughts about different roles in implementing living wills. Around 81.17% believe that the doctors should lead in discussing living wills with patients, while nurses (46.86%) and social workers (35.18%) are perceived to be subsidiary roles. The possible reason for this result could be that HCPs perceive the doctors to be the authority and initiators [28]. In Hongkong’s real-world practice, the initiative to start living-will conversation was usually taken by doctors (75%) rather than nurses (22%). However, Dixon’s study suggested that team-based model involving a doctor and a nurse or a social worker was much more cost-effective and supportive of good quality care [41]. Currently, nurses’ roles in discussing living wills are still unclear in most countries [35].

Limitation

There are several limitations to this study. First, a convenience sampling method was used to select both hospitals and participants, which may have affected the external validity of the findings. Nevertheless, the final sample included HCPs from 16 hospitals across all three hospital levels in Shenzhen, offering a reasonably large and diverse representation for the study’s objectives. However, as Shenzhen is one of China’s most economically developed and policy-progressive cities, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other regions with different cultural or legislative environments. Besides, the online flyers recruitment method might restrict the participants to individuals who are active on social media, which might undermine the representativeness of the sample too. Third, the questionnaire about living wills was firstly developed by our team in this study and the external validity has not been examined previously. However, we measured the.

S-CVI and the Cronbach α by a multidisciplinary team, which was reliable. Lastly, social desirability bias inherent in self-reported questionnaires could not be entirely avoided and may have led to an overestimation of the results. Although the survey was conducted anonymously to mitigate this risk, participants’ responses may still reflect perceived expectations rather than their actual knowledge, attitudes, or behaviors. Future studies using objective measures or triangulation methods are recommended to enhance data validity.

Conclusion

Although living wills have been legalized in Shenzhen China, HCPs are not sufficiently prepared to implement them. They have acquired general knowledge, held positive attitudes, but lack legal knowledge, face ethical dilemmas, and are ethically unconfident to implement living wills. The roles of HCPs in implementing living wills are unclear. The findings of this study call for an urgent training on preparing HCPs in China, not only including medical-technical training, but also legal and ethical training within the Chinese cultural context. Designing and evaluating culture-sensitive training interventions and analyzing the roles of HCPs in implementing living wills are also needed in future studies.
