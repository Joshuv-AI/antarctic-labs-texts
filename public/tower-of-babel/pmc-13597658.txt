Species and strain sharing in the vaginal microbiome of mothers and their adult daughters
Authors: Maria Pinedo-Bardales, Isabel Erreygers, Camille Nina Allonsius, Margo Hiel, Tom Eilers, Tim Van Rillaer, Thies Gehrmann, Sarah Ahannach, Jelle Dillen, Ilke De Boeck, Veronique Verhoeven, Sandra Van Puyvelde, Nicola Segata, Stijn Wittouck, Sarah Lebeer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe vaginal microbiome is key for women’s health. However, its establishment, interindividual variation and dynamics remain poorly understood. Here, we investigate bacterial relatedness at species and strain level in adult mother-daughter pairs from the large-scale citizen-science program Isala. Using metagenomic sequencing with quality control including 16S rRNA profile comparison, along with targeted culturing, we assess intergenerational microbiome sharing. At species level, daughters’ vaginal microbiomes are significantly more similar to their mothers’ than to those of unrelated mothers, with a strong mother-daughter correlation in Lactobacillus crispatus dominance. Strain-level analyses of metagenomes and isolate genomes reveal intraspecies diversity in L. crispatus, with up to two strains observed within the same host, and support intergenerational vaginal bacteria sharing. SNV counts in shared L. crispatus strains show no correlation with daughters’ ages. Together, these findings suggest that maternal transmission, host factors, and (shared) environment collectively shape the vaginal microbiome, providing fundamental ecological insights into vaginal microbiome dynamics and perspectives toward lactobacilli-based applications.Keywords: vaginal microbiome, mother-daughter pairs, strain sharing, lactic acid bacteria, metagenomic sequencing, targeted culturing

Introduction

An optimal vaginal microbiome is essential for women’s reproductive and overall health, with benefits that extend to their partners and children.1 Despite its importance, the factors shaping its establishment and temporal dynamics are still being unraveled. This is better understood for the gut microbiome, where its earliest inhabitants appear to partially derive from the maternal gut and vaginal microbiome, including Bifidobacterium, Prevotella and Lactobacillus species.2,3,4,5,6 The vaginal contribution to this “seed” gut microbiome appears to be transient, with lactobacilli such as Lactobacillus crispatus rapidly decreasing below detection levels in the infant gut within one week after birth onwards, likely replaced by maternal gut microbiota members.4 For the vaginal microbiome, the presence of maternally acquired strains and their potential persistence across life stages remain unclear. Before menarche, the vaginal microbiome composition seems to be diverse and predominantly rich in anaerobic bacteria such as Prevotella and Streptococcus.7 However, this remains largely understudied because intimate sampling poses challenges at a younger age. To our knowledge, only a single study on mother-daughter vaginal strain sharing has been performed using metagenomic sequencing in Black and African-American women from the United States. The study compared species and strains between mothers and their adolescent/adult daughters and revealed some examples of strain sharing, both within and between families.8

In reproductive-age women, a balanced vaginal microbiome generally exhibits low species diversity, with a dominance of Lactobacillus species, particularly L. crispatus, Lactobacillus iners, Lactobacillus gasseri, and Lactobacillus jensenii.9,10 This dominance of lactobacilli in the human vagina typically emerges after menarche, coinciding with an increase in estrogen levels during puberty.7 Estrogens stimulate glycogen release from epithelial cells as an energy source for lactobacilli. These lactic acid-producing bacteria create a low pH in the vagina and inhibit pathogenic microorganisms by metabolizing glycogen shed from vaginal epithelial cells into lactic acid.11 Interestingly, and still underexplored especially within hosts, lactobacilli populations, like other bacterial species, exhibit intraspecies diversity in terms of gene content,12,13 reflecting variation in functional potential such as antimicrobial peptide production.11,14 In addition, women without vaginal complaints can also harbor vaginal microbiomes that are more diverse or dominated by non-Lactobacillus species, including the well documented Gardnerella vaginalis.14 Although frequently detected in healthy women, G. vaginalis is not unequivocally beneficial, as it can produce virulence factors and disrupt the vaginal ecosystem,15,16 while producing less lactic acid compared to vaginal lactobacilli.14

Beyond age and hormonal fluctuations, several biological and lifestyle factors such as parity, contraceptive use, sexual activity and use of menstrual products have been shown to impact the vaginal microbiome in healthy adult women.10,17 Collectively, these factors were found to explain 10.4% of the microbiome variance in the first large-scale cohort of our Isala program (https://isala.be/en/).10 This cohort was mainly dominated by Lactobacillus species (78% of participants). While 10.4% points at a considerable influence of known lifestyle and behavioral variables, this relatively modest percentage suggests that additional yet unknown factors, such as those captured by genetic and social relationships, may contribute to establishing and shaping the vaginal microbiome. One such determinant is microbial transmission between individuals, a concept widely recognized for the gut microbiome,18,19 but largely overlooked for the vaginal niche. Studying transmission and sharing of bacteria between individuals requires strain-level resolution. Current approaches typically rely on genome comparisons based solely on marker genes, or on mapping to representative/reference genomes or metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), which constrains reproducibility and interpretability.8,19,20 Moreover, the microbiological field lacks consensus on what defines a shared strain, and rather arbitrary cutoffs are commonly used in this regard.

Here, we investigated whether adult mothers and daughters share species and strains in their vaginal microbiome, and how this aligned with expectations of microbial stability and possible transmission originating from birth onwards up to later in life. To address this, we analyzed adult mother-daughter pairs participating in our Isala program10,21 using both metagenomes and genomes of isolates obtained by targeted culturing. In addition, publicly available vaginal metagenomes from the aforementioned non-European cohort of mothers and adolescent/adult daughters were re-analyzed to examine bacterial relatedness at both species and strain level.8 We defined strain sharing through strain-level thresholds tailored to vaginal taxa and our study population of adolescent/adult women. Although the concept of vertical transmission is often used in microbiome research,4,22,23 we deliberately chose not to use this term in our study of the vaginal microbiome. We use the term “strain sharing” because we could not retrospectively collect infant samples at birth and because this term is more neutral on the directionality of the strain-sharing events observed. We investigated the extent of intergenerational species- and strain-level sharing in the vaginal microbiome later in adult life, a stage that remains poorly understood and is particularly relevant for reproductive health and beyond, including menopause.

Results

The vaginal microbiome profiles of mothers and their adult daughters show significant similarity at species level

In the first Flemish cohort of our previously established Isala citizen-science program, more than 3,300 women collected vaginal swabs. Within this cohort, 35 women voluntarily indicated that they participated alongside their mothers. This enabled us to study the similarity in vaginal microbiome within and outside families. To be able to study this similarity at improved taxonomic resolution, previously extracted DNA from vaginal swabs of these participants, which had originally been used for 16S rRNA taxonomic profiling up to subgenus level,10 was subjected to deep metagenomic sequencing up to 150 million reads (45 Gb) per sample. After quality control and subsequent removal of singleton samples (i.e., a maternal sample without a corresponding high-quality daughter sample or vice versa) (Figure S1), we retained 23 out of 35 mother-daughter pairs. This quality control was a crucial step to minimize risks of cross-contamination during sample handling in the lab (cfr. Method details). These 23 pairs corresponded to 42 high-resolution metagenomes, including two mothers with more than one daughter (F04 and F17; Figure 1B). The median of read counts per metagenome was 6,691,242 (IQR: 10,616,498). The ages of the mothers (indicated by “M”; n = 19) ranged between 45 and 68, whereas the daughters (indicated by “D”; n = 23) were between 18 and 32 years old at the time of sampling (Figure 1A). Twelve of these mothers (63.2%) reported to be post-menopausal. Among the daughters, 43.5% were living with their mothers at the time of sampling. Notably, only one daughter reported being born via C section in the survey answers.

We then determined the taxonomic composition of the vaginal metagenomes at species level using a database including one representative genome per species.24 L. crispatus was identified as a dominant species (defined as a species with relative abundance of approximately 30% or more; Table S1) in the metagenomes of both the mother and the daughter in twelve of 23 pairs analyzed (F01–F05 and F10–F13). In three pairs (F06, F07, and F08), L. iners was the dominant species in both the mother and the daughter. Among other lactobacilli commonly found in the vaginal microbiome, L. gasseri dominated one daughter’s microbiome (F16D), while L. jensenii was not dominant in any sample of our family cohort. Furthermore, the vaginal metagenomes of six mothers (F09M, F14M, F15M, F16M, F17M, and F19M) and seven daughters (F06D, F07D, F09D, F15D, F16D, F17D2, and F18D) were (co-)dominated by non-lactobacilli species, including dominance of Gardnerella spp. for both metagenomes of F09 (with a relative abundance of G. leopoldii of 50.5% in the mother and a relative abundance of G. vaginalis of 39.7% in the daughter).

Next, we assessed whether the daughters’ vaginal microbiome profiles were more similar to those of their own mothers than to random mothers of the cohort by performing a permutation test based on Bray-Curtis similarity indices. We compared the daughters’ profiles to profiles of random “mothers” only, and not to random individuals from our cohort, to correct for covariates like age and having had children, because these were previously found to be significantly associated to the vaginal microbiome composition in the first phase of Isala where the samples of this study were retrieved from.10 This analysis revealed a significantly greater similarity within true mother-daughter pairs compared to randomized mother-daughter pairs (Figure 1C, p = 0.0008) despite the large variation in similarity indices of true mother-daughter pairs (standard deviation of 37.9). Daughters had on average 48.3% similarity with the vaginal microbiome of their own mothers, compared to 31.8% with the vaginal microbiome of randomized mothers. Of note, the highest similarity indices for true pairs were obtained for profiles where both mother and daughter were dominated by Lactobacillus species.

Given this significant species-level similarity in mother-daughter pairs and the fact that over 50% of all pairs were dominated by L. crispatus in this cohort, we investigated the possible association between dominance of this species in the maternal and daughters’ vaginal microbiomes using a generalized linear mixed model. Maternal L. crispatus dominance (relative abundance of approximately 30% or more) was indeed strongly associated with dominance of this species in the vaginal microbiome of the corresponding daughter(s). Specifically, daughters whose mothers were dominated by L. crispatus exhibited markedly higher odds of being L. crispatus-dominated themselves compared to daughters of mothers not dominated by L. crispatus (odds ratio = 21, p < 0.01). Overall, the L. crispatus dominance in the maternal microbiome explained approximately 41% of the variability in the species dominating in the daughters’ vaginal microbiome (marginal R2 = 0.41). In this analysis, the variance associated with the family-level random effect was negligible, indicating that maternal dominance captured most of the within-family dependence. Results were robust to model specification, as similar effect sizes were obtained using a logistic regression model without random effects. Altogether, these findings emphasized the vaginal microbiome similarity at species level in adult mothers and daughters.

To investigate if a similar extent of species-level similarity within mother-daughter pairs was observed in other cohorts, we analyzed 47 published vaginal metagenomes of a non-European cohort of Black and African-American women, comprising 22 mothers (32–51 years old) and 25 daughters (14–35 years old) (Table S2).8 Of note, these participants were originally enrolled in a vaginal douching intervention study,25 which may impact their vaginal microbiome composition. Compared to our cohort, more participants were dominated by non-Lactobacillus species (n = 14) or had more diverse vaginal microbiomes (n = 15; Figure S2A). Notably, 28 of the 47 participants showed symptoms of bacterial vaginosis (Nugent scores of 5–10; Table S2). Yet, using a similar species-level approach as for our Isala dataset, the permutation analysis of this more diverse non-European cohort also confirmed a significantly greater similarity within true mother-daughter pairs than expected by chance (Figure S2B, p < 0.0001).

Vaginal bacterial strains are shared between mothers and their adult daughters

To determine whether the observed microbiome similarity was not only noticeable at species level, but also extended to strain level, we mapped the high-quality metagenomes of Isala mother-daughter pairs to reference genomes of L. crispatus, L. iners, G. vaginalis, and G. leopoldii (the most often dominant vaginal species in this cohort) using inStrain.26 For the G. leopoldii reference genome, this mapping also recruited reads from Gardnerella swidsinskii, since both species are extremely closely related (average nucleotide identity (ANI) >0.95). We have combined both species under a single species label G. leopoldii in the context of our strain sharing results. The obtained profiles from the mapped metagenomes were compared, resulting in population-based average nucleotide identity (popANI) values between metagenomes. These values were then converted to popANI-based single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) Per Megabase (popSPM) values to obtain a scale that is more suitable for the comparison of extremely closely related genomes.

We further assessed the distributions of the obtained popSPM values for women from the same and different families (Figure 2). Based on these distributions, the lower extremes of the popSPM ranges exclusively corresponded to within-family comparisons. We subsequently defined shared strains for L. crispatus, L. iners, and G. vaginalis as genomes with a genome distance of at most 100 popSPM (popANI ≥ 99.99%). Here, we assumed that shared strains are supported by reads mapping to the reference genome with at least 95% sequence identity and covering at least 50% of the genome (breadth) in both metagenomes. Thanks to this approach, we obtained a strain-sharing threshold appropriate for our research objective: the detection of vaginal strain sharing between mothers and their adult daughters. After applying these thresholds, we found seven mother-daughter pairs sharing strains (Table 1; Figure 3). Specifically, instances of L. crispatus strain sharing were observed in four L. crispatus-dominated mother-daughter pairs (F01, F04M-F04D4, F10, and F13). For L. iners, shared strains were found in two mother-daughter pairs (F08 and F14), with all pair members except for F14M being dominated by this species. In addition, this analysis revealed a case of strain sharing for G. vaginalis in one mother-daughter pair (F09). For G. leopoldii, one mother-daughter pair showed a lower popSPM value (428.9 popSPM) compared to all other popSPM values obtained for this species within (428.9–22,859.0) and outside families (5,824.2–26,508.9), indicating a higher genomic similarity between these two G. leopoldii strains relative to others. However, we considered this value too high to confidently ascribe it to recent direct or indirect strain transmission, since it corresponded to an ANI equal to or lower than 99.99% (≥ 100 popSPM).

To investigate strain sharing for the same four species in the non-European cohort,8 and evaluate if our findings could be validated in another cohort, we applied our strain-level approach to their vaginal metagenomes. To be consistent, we used the same strain sharing cutoff of at most 100 popSPM. For this dataset, both within- and between-family comparisons were detected below the cutoff (Figures S3A–S3D). Altogether, we found seventeen strain-sharing instances for L. crispatus, L. iners, G. vaginalis, and G. leopoldii, including six within mother-daughter pairs and one between sisters (Table S4; Figure S4). Notably, for L. crispatus, all three mother-daughter pairs with shared strains (F01, F03, and F05) were dominated by this species, while L. iners was not dominant in mother-daughter pairs with shared strains of that species (F21 and F30). For G. vaginalis and G. leopoldii, strain sharing was detected within one sister pair (F23D1-F23D2) and mother-daughter pair (F21), respectively. Contrarily to our Isala cohort, in addition to strain sharing between mothers and daughters, for L. crispatus, L. iners, and G. leopoldii, strain-sharing instances were also detected between participants from different families (ten out of 17 instances detected in total) in this cohort of non-European women based on the threshold of 100 popSPM.

Targeted culturing of L. crispatus uncovers additional mother-daughter strain sharing

While the popANI metric and derived popSPM scale applied to the metagenomic data could capture the sharing of both majority (i.e., most abundant) and/or minority (i.e., less abundant) strains that were detectable at the provided sequencing depth, other lower abundant strains might have been sub-threshold and therefore missed. To explore the potential presence and sharing of such lower abundant strains, we therefore complemented our metagenome-based approach with a targeted culturing approach for the Isala mother-daughter pairs. This isolation strategy was focused on L. crispatus since L. iners and Gardnerella spp. could not be reproducibly cultured because of specific growth requirements and stress sensitivities (e.g., anaerobic incubation, addition of blood, and cysteine),27 especially not from samples collected at home and transported to the lab at room temperature in the citizen-science setting of Isala. From L. crispatus-dominated families, we successfully retrieved 75 L. crispatus isolates from 18 participants belonging to eight families (between one and nine isolates per participant, Table 2; Table S5) using a selective acidified subculturing phase, thereby mimicking the physiology of the human vagina and limiting the growth of other competitive taxa (e.g., Enterococcus faecalis and streptococci, which can also be cultivated on MRS) that may have proliferated during the transportation at room temperature.

To determine whether the isolates were different strains or clones, their genomes were sequenced and dereplicated for each participant using our recently developed tool SCARAP for rapid scalable comparative genomics based on around 2,000 single-copy core genes (depending on the isolates compared),28 using a threshold of 99.99% of ANI, and excluding per-gene outliers when calculating the ANI. This resulted in 21 representative isolate genomes from 18 participants (Table 2). Of note, three participants, one mother and two daughters (F03M, F04D2, and F04D3), were found to have at least two different L. crispatus co-existing strains based on culturing, pointing at some degree of within-host strain diversity. To further investigate this, we compared each representative isolate genome to the corresponding metagenome and to the other representative isolate genome (as applicable for F03M, F04D2, and F04D3; Table S5). Hereto, we first determined popSPM values with inStrain, using the L. crispatus reference genome from the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB). However, we had to implement a denoising procedure since the popSPM values were larger than expected without denoising while for the same strains the popSPM should be (near) zero. As part of this denoising procedure, assemblies from isolate genomes were used as reference genomes when mapping against the corresponding metagenomic reads and SNVs at contig boundaries (specifically the first and last five nucleotides of a contig) were removed. Of note, the noise at the contig boundaries was revealed by self-assembly comparisons of isolate genomes (Table S6). After implementing the denoising procedure, only two isolate genomes (from F03M and F11D) were not detected in their corresponding metagenome suggesting a possible enrichment of minority strains through targeted culturing (Table 2).

We then incorporated all representative isolate genomes into the inStrain analysis to complement our metagenome-based strain sharing approach for L. crispatus. We confirmed strain sharing for the four mother-daughter pairs (F01, F04M-F04D4, F10, and F13) previously detected based on metagenomes only (Tables 1 and 3; Table S7) and identified additional strain sharing in F03 and F11 with all mothers and daughters being L. crispatus-dominated. Thus together, we found that six out of twelve L. crispatus-dominated mother-daughter pairs (50%) were detectably sharing L. crispatus strains (Figure 3; Table S8).

Based on StrainPhlAn 4,29,30 we then investigated the phylogenetic relationships between the L. crispatus strains found to be shared and not shared in our cohort. This tool infers the phylogeny of the most abundant (majority) L. crispatus strain per metagenome based on 100 or more marker genes present in at least 50% of samples. Specifically, a phylogenetic tree (Figure 4; Figure S5) was constructed based on L. crispatus reads retrieved from 27 metagenomes and seven representative isolate genomes from participants showing within-host diversity (F03M, F04D2, F04D3, and F11D). Overall, the four L. crispatus strain-sharing instances detected based on metagenomes only concerned majority strains within the L. crispatus populations of the pairwise-compared samples. For one family (F03), the majority strain of the daughter was shared with a strain below the metagenome detection threshold in the mother. In family F11, the sharing constituted a minority strain in both samples. In addition, to explore whether shared L. crispatus strains belong to a specific clade or are scattered across the tree, we included 100 publicly available L. crispatus genomes from different sources, including the reference genome from GTDB (Table S9), along with 20 randomly selected vaginal L. crispatus genomes recovered from other Isala healthy volunteers (Table S10). We confirmed that the genome pairs that constituted a shared strain within a family were scattered across the clade containing strains from the urogenital tract (i.e., human vagina, urine, and ectocervical mucosa), suggesting that the capacity of being transferred is not restricted to a specific lineage. In addition, co-existing strains from the same individual detected in our cohort were polyphyletic (spread across different subclades) further substantiating that they are not clonal variants and confirming the observed within-host strain diversity in these four participants.

Mother-daughter L. crispatus strain sharing occurs beyond vaginal delivery and current cohabitation

To investigate which lifestyle, life course or other metadata could impact the level of strain sharing, we analyzed survey metadata collected at the time of sampling from the six L. crispatus-dominated mother-daughter pairs with detected shared strains. The mothers of three of these pairs (F03M, F04M, and F13M) self-reported being post-menopausal at the time of sampling (46–68 years old), suggesting that shared strains can persist beyond this important life stage that goes accompanied by important physiological changes and a general reduction in L. crispatus abundance. Overall, the ages of the six daughters who shared strains with their mothers ranged between 19 and 31. For these mother-daughter pairs, we investigated if this sharing could have originated from strain transmission at birth by assessing the relation between the daughters’ age and the respective denoised reference-independent popSPM values obtained for metagenome versus isolate genome comparisons (Figure S6). No correlation was observed, suggesting that either the detected sharing could not be fully attributed to strain acquisition at birth, or that the L. crispatus strains in these pairs acquired SNVs at radically different rates. The best current estimate of this rate for L. crispatus in anaerobic conditions is around 8 SNVs per year.32 For families F13 and F03, particularly low SNV counts were observed (approximately 10 and 21, respectively, across the whole genome). This traces the common ancestor of the L. crispatus lineages for these families to at most a few years in the past, most likely not at birth.

With respect to birth mode, we observed that F10D, who was born by C section (Figure 1A), was sharing L. crispatus strains with her mother based on metagenomes and isolate genomes. Despite this being the only case within our cohort, this finding suggests that vaginal delivery is not a strict requirement for strain sharing of L. crispatus. In addition, based on the living situation reported at the time of sampling, we could explore if current cohabitation (Figure 1A) was required for the sharing of L. crispatus strains. Four of six mother-daughter pairs with detected shared strains were still cohabiting at the time of sampling, whereas two daughters (F03D and F04D4) moved out and were living with their partner for more than one year and more than three months, respectively. This indicates that L. crispatus strain sharing can be observed without ongoing or very recent cohabitation. Interestingly, in F04, only one of four daughters (F04D4) was detectably sharing L. crispatus strains with her mother, despite the use of antimicrobials in the past three months prior to sample collection (i.e., local application of Gyno-Daktarin and oral administration of Fluconazole). Taken together, our findings suggest that L. crispatus strain sharing between mothers and daughters may reflect transmission and long-term persistence. While vaginal birth and shared living environments may facilitate transmission, they appear not strictly required, pointing to a broader understanding of persistence of the vaginal microbiome, defined as the sustained presence of specific species and strains across individuals and life stages.

Discussion

Despite growing interest in host-associated microbial communities, key knowledge gaps remain regarding the factors that determine the establishment and dynamics of the vaginal microbiome. Within the first Flemish cohort of our Isala program,10 we could already identify biological and lifestyle factors that explain part of the vaginal microbiome variance between the participants. In this follow-up work, we investigated the contribution of genetic relatedness and/or shared environments to the vaginal microbiome by studying metagenomes and isolate genomes from adult mothers and daughters participating in Isala at both species and strain level. Of note, these participants could openly communicate about family members participating. Based on metagenomes, we found a significant species-level similarity within true mother-daughter pairs as well as shared strains of different lactic acid-producing bacteria (L. crispatus, L. iners, and G. vaginalis). Interestingly, the inclusion of isolate genomes of L. crispatus allowed us to confirm the detected strain-sharing instances for this species and identify additional ones, all within mother-daughter pairs. Our observations that no correlation was detectable between the popSPM value of a shared strain and the age of the daughter and specifically, that the popSPM values for two sharing instances were particularly low, suggest that transmission likely does not occur exclusively at birth but could also take place later in life and possibly in both directions. Of note, with regard to shared environments, the two lowest popSPM values were observed for a non-cohabiting and cohabiting mother-daughter pair at the time of sampling.

Regarding other potential sources of shared strains, the contribution of bacteria from other genetically and/or socially connected individuals in shaping the vaginal microbiome remains unclear while being investigated in other niches such as the gut microbiome of socially connected individuals.18,33 Of interest, recent studies indicate that the male partner is an important source of taxa such as Gardnerella spp. that may cause bacterial vaginosis recurrence via transmission in their partner.34 In our cohort, we found evidence of strain sharing of G. vaginalis within only one mother-daughter pair, yet this low detection could be due to the low prevalence of this species in our pairs analyzed. Investigating transmission of G. vaginalis or other vaginal taxa beyond mother-daughter pairs, including partners,35 would require a dedicated set-up including both genetic and social networks, preferably in different cohorts across the world. In contrast to studies on the vaginal microbiome, studies on strain sharing in gut, skin, and oral microbiomes of mother and newborns, cohabiting individuals and those from the same village are more common, as recently reviewed by Heidrich et al.19 One of the largest human microbiome transmission studies on these niches up to now (with up to 9,712 metagenomes), including longitudinal sampling settings and using species-dependent strain-sharing thresholds, showed that cohabitation duration has a greater impact than genetic relatedness.18 However, this finding might be explained by the frequent bacterial transit observed in these more open microbial habitats compared to the vagina.

To date, several tools for strain-level analyses are available,19 with inStrain26 being commonly used as it considers the presence of co-existing strains in one single sample. Using this tool, we revealed some within-host strain diversity for L. crispatus, which remained underexplored up to date, especially its biological and ecological meaning for the host and microbial community.12,36 However, the observed within-host strain diversity was limited in our cohort, with a maximum of two distinct L. crispatus strains detected per individual based on the integration of metagenomes and isolate genomes. Whether this is a true biological finding (i.e., that most individuals have not more than two different strains of the same species) remains to be investigated, as our results may have been influenced by methodological factors including the use of different swabs for microbiome profiling and culturing, the acidified subculturing approach and colony-picking bias, potentially reducing the detection or selection of low abundant strains. In this regard, recovering greater number of co-existing strains from the vaginal microbiome will enable evaluation of their unique characteristics and their potential roles in shaping community dynamics, which remains undiscovered when relying on deep metagenomic sequencing data only.

When performing strain-level investigations using inStrain, we also found that both the choice of reference genome and strain-sharing threshold establishment were critical steps. We selected the GTDB-derived L. crispatus reference genome (ATCC 33820, assembly: GCA_018987235.1),37 to provide a standardized, consensus framework for strain comparison within this species.38,39 Moreover, we selected a stringent cutoff of at most 100 popSPM to define shared strains in adult mother-daughter pairs. The use of this popANI-based scaling allowed for improved interpretability at very high similarity levels as encountered in strain-level analyses.

Previously, in a non-European cohort,8 the similarity in the vaginal microbiomes of mothers and adolescent/adult daughters was also investigated using inStrain based on metagenomes. In their study, they did not find that true mother-daughter pairs showed more species-level similarity or strain-sharing instances compared to unrelated individuals. At species level, we observed significant similarity between the vaginal microbiomes of these mothers and daughters. This contrast in findings was likely due to differences in methodologies used to compare similarity indices of true pairs to the average values of permuted pairs. At strain level, the authors used the equivalent of 1,000 popSPM as strain-sharing threshold, where we used a more stringent cutoff of 100 popSPM. In addition, they mapped reads to MAGs, while we used a reference genome or isolate assemblies. When applying our workflow to the four species of interest, we did detect a lower number of strain-sharing instances within families (seven instances out of seventeen detected) compared to outside families in the non-European cohort. This is contrary to what was observed in the Isala cohort, where we exclusively detected strain-sharing instances within mother-daughter pairs (n = 9). This discrepancy could be linked to differences in vaginal microbiome composition, potentially influenced by host factors, cultural practices or environmental factors.8 For example, L. crispatus dominance, frequently reported in the vaginal microbiome of western populations, was more prevalent in the Isala cohort (i.e., 12 out of 23 pairs in the Isala cohort vs. three out of 25 pairs in the non-European cohort). From a technical perspective, it has been shown that strain sharing analyses are extremely sensitive to even low levels of contamination, recently resulting in the development of tools to track contamination events (e.g., CroCoDeEL),40 which we recommend, if suitable for vaginal microbiome investigations, to complement with the corresponding lab notes if available. While this was available for our cohort, such data are often lacking in publicly accessible datasets, including the non-European cohort, representing a common limitation of secondary analyses. As such, for the Isala cohort, we rigorously controlled for potential cross-contamination and removed any sample where we found a sign of potential cross-contamination based on our strict quality control standards including the comparison of metagenomes to corresponding 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing data.

Altogether, our findings represent an important step toward understanding the establishment and dynamics of the vaginal microbiome and its potential determinants both at species and strain level. Crucially, our integrative approach, combining deep metagenomic sequencing with targeted culturing methods, stringent strain-level thresholds, rigorous quality control measures and solid denoising procedure based on gene-outlier exclusion, allowed us to detect strain sharing within mother-daughter pairs with high resolution and to uncover within-host strain diversity. These tailored methodologies not only strengthened the reliability of our findings but also offer a framework that can be adapted to other niches and clinical contexts, such as microbiome-based interventions (e.g., administration of live biotherapeutic products) or microbiome transplantation studies (e.g., fecal and vaginal) to investigate colonization of administered/transferred strains using tailored thresholds.41,42,43 In this regard, we show that the vaginal microbiome, although generally dominated by a single species, can act as a receptive environment under favorable physiological conditions, thereby opening new perspectives for lactobacilli-based applications. Based on samples of adolescent and adult daughters and their corresponding mothers, we demonstrated a substantial degree of species and strain sharing in their vaginal microbiomes, which can be influenced by genetic or environmental factors and/or intergenerational strain transmission.

Limitations of the study

Despite our valuable strain sharing findings and our carefully chosen methodology, our study also has some limitations. The Isala cohort, from which our study population originates, was initially recruited for mapping the composition of the vaginal microbiome of self-reported healthy women and women participating together with their mothers constituted a relatively small sub-cohort. Certain variables related to lifestyle habits were missing from the questionnaire (e.g., shared use of washing machine, toilet and towels), which resulted in uncovered potential transmission pathways between genetically and socially connected individuals sharing the same environment. Overall, the potential reservoirs, transmission routes and dynamics of vaginal microbial strains should be further elucidated by performing longitudinal studies complemented with extensive lifestyle data collection and the analysis of host-genetic data in larger cohorts of mothers and daughters, but also female housemates and other non-genetically related participants. Ideally, these studies should start from daughters’ birth onwards until their reproductive age and the post-menopausal stage of the mothers, using appropriate sampling devices and collecting samples from different body sites along with validated hormonal and pH measures, making these studies highly intensive. This will allow for strain-level analyses focused on both gene-level and functional changes across different life stages and body niches, which can be reservoirs of bacteria that further colonize the vaginal niche (e.g., skin).44

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and sample collection

Vaginal samples used in this study were obtained from participants enrolled in the first phase of the Isala citizen-science program in Belgium.10 Isala was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Antwerp University Hospital/University of Antwerp (B300201942076), conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. Overall, the first phase of Isala comprised 3,350 healthy, non-pregnant women aged 18 years or older who filled out a questionnaire including lifestyle-related factors and donated two self-collected vaginal samples: an eNAT swab (Copan, Brescia, Italy) for microbiome profiling and an eSwab (Copan, Brescia, Italy) for isolation purposes. After self-collection, vaginal swabs were stored by participants under refrigerated conditions (4°C) and shipped at ambient temperature using a prepaid national postal service.10 Upon arrival, eNAT swabs were immediately stored at −20°C until processing, while eSwabs were stored at 4°C. Following a maximum storage time of 6 h, eSwabs were vortexed for approximately 15 s, after which 500 μL of the sample suspension was combined with 500 μL of 50% glycerol in a 96-tube Micronic plate and stored at −80°C for isolation purposes.53

Among the Isala participants, 35 women were identified to be participating alongside their mother. Specifically, our cohort (n = 62) included 25 mothers (M) with a single participant daughter (D) (including a grandmother-daughter-granddaughter triad) and three mothers with more than one daughter participating (i.e., two, four and four, respectively), resulting in a total of 35 mother-daughter pairs. Relevant demographic characteristics (i.e., age and co-habitation status) from this cohort are described in the Results section and presented in Figure 1A.

Isolation and cultivation of L. crispatus from vaginal samples

L. crispatus isolates were obtained by culturing aliquots of self-collected eSwabs from mother-daughter pairs whose metagenomes were both dominated by this species. A selective subculturing phase was applied to tailor the isolations toward vaginal lactobacilli using an in-house protocol. Hereto, for each stored eSwab, some swab material was scraped off and inoculated in 10 mL of MRS broth acidified to pH 4.6 and supplemented with 0.3% L-cysteine (Roth) as an additional growth factor. After an incubation period of 72h at 37°C with 5% CO2, 50 μL of a 10-fold serial dilution of the cultured eSwabs in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 1X) was plated out on standard MRS agar, MRS agar supplemented with 0.3% L-cysteine (Roth) or MRS agar acidified to pH 4.6. Petri dishes were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 directly or in an anaerobic jar with anaerobic gas generating sachets (AnaeroGen 2.5 L, Thermo Scientific). After incubation, colonies were selected based on visual inspection and colony PCR for the 16S rRNA gene was performed and checked by gel electrophoresis. Next, Sanger sequencing of the PCR products was performed for species-level identification using universal 27F and 1492R primers. The acquired data were processed, and species identities were determined using an in-house pipeline (https://github.com/LebeerLab/nf-sanger).

Method details

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

DNA was extracted from the eNAT swabs (Copan) using the DNeasy Powersoil Pro Kit (Qiagen) as part of the first phase of the Isala program,10 following a previously optimized in-house protocol54 and stored at the Biobank Antwerpen (Antwerp, Belgium; ID: BE 71030031000). DNA extracts were stored at −20°C and subjected to 16S rRNA sequencing as described before.10

Metagenomic sequencing of vaginal samples

DNA extracts from the 62 participants included in our cohort were subjected to deep metagenomic sequencing. Both library preparation and metagenomic sequencing were outsourced to Prebiomics (Trento, Italy), where dual-index, paired-end sequencing was performed using the Illumina platform according to the manufacturer’s protocol as described elsewhere.53 Briefly, the Illumina DNA Prep Tagmentation kit (Illumina) and the NovaSeq 6000 DX S4 Reagent Kit v1.5 were used to generate up to 150 million reads (45 Gb) per sample.

Processing and quality control of raw metagenomic sequencing data

Raw metagenomic reads were trimmed and quality-filtered using default settings from fastp version 0.23.2 (https://nf-co.re/mag/4.0.0/parameters/#quality-control-for-short-reads-options),45 whereafter human reads were filtered out using Hostile version 1.1.0.46 Subsequently, quality was assessed with MultiQC version 1.13.47 Taxonomy was assigned with Sylph version 0.8.0248 using the GlobDB.24

Comparison of metagenomic and 16S rRNA-based microbiome profiles

As DNA extracts were previously subjected to 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing,10 our quality control included two steps. First, samples identified as low quality in the 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing were excluded from our further analyses. Then, we compared the profiles resulting from both 16S rRNA amplicon and metagenomic sequencing and assessed the similarity in taxonomic composition at (sub)genus level. Samples with poor correspondence between the techniques were removed. Subsequently, singleton samples, defined as a maternal sample lacking a corresponding high-quality daughter sample or vice versa, were excluded from the further analyses.

Species-level microbiome profiling

The taxonomic composition of each metagenome at species level was visualized using RStudio version 4.4.0 and the in-house developed package tidytacos version 1.0.1.28 A dominant species was defined by a relative abundance level of approximately 30% or more.10 A permutation test was performed to determine if the similarity between samples of true mothers and daughters exceeded what would be anticipated under random chance based on Bray-Curtis similarity indices (one minus the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index). Specifically, each daughter was assigned to a random mother from our cohort and the Bray-Curtis similarity index was calculated. This process was repeated 10,000 times for each daughter, and the average similarity indices from these permutations were calculated and compared to the average similarity index of true pairs.

Maternal-daughter concordance in Lactobacillus crispatus dominance patterns

To assess whether maternal L. crispatus dominance was associated with L. crispatus dominance in daughters, a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a binomial error distribution and logit link was fitted. Daughter dominance status (binary: dominated by L. crispatus or not) was used as the response variable, maternal dominance status as a fixed effect, and family identifier as a random intercept to account for potential non-independence among daughters from the same family. Models were fitted in RStudio using the lme4 package version 2.0.55 Odds ratios were obtained by exponentiating model coefficients, and model explanatory power was assessed using marginal R2 following Nakagawa and Schielzeth, as implemented in the performance package version 0.16.0.56 As a sensitivity analysis, an equivalent logistic regression model without random effects was also fitted.

Whole-genome sequencing of L. crispatus isolates

In preparation for whole-genome sequencing (WGS), L. crispatus isolates were streaked on MRS agar using the pentagon method to check for pure cultures and grown overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. After incubation, a single colony from each agar plate was inoculated in 10 mL of MRS broth and incubated overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. After incubation, genomic DNA was extracted for each isolate according to an in-house protocol.57 DNA extracts were sent to BGI (Hong Kong) for sequencing using the DNB-seq platform. Specifically, sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-G400 system with paired-end reads of 150 bp. Data processing, assembly, quality control and genome annotation were conducted using an in-house Nextflow pipeline (https://github.com/LebeerLab/nf-whole-genome-illumina). Briefly, raw paired-end reads were filtered with fastp version 0.23.245 (default settings), and genome assembly was performed using shovill version 1.1.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/shovill). Quality assessment of the assembled genomes was conducted using CheckM2 version 1.1.051 and GUNC version 1.0.1352 to evaluate completeness and contamination. Genomes with a minimal completeness of 98% and contamination lower than 5% were included in the analysis. To ensure unique genome representation, isolate genomes were dereplicated for each participant using the sample module of the in-house developed tool SCARAP version 1.0.028 with an ANI threshold of 99.99% and excluding the per-gene outliers when calculating the ANI (--method mean90 setting).

Identification of shared strains using a reference-dependent approach

Metagenomes were mapped to reference genomes of species of interest using inStrain version 1.8.0,26 a tool that calculates pairwise population-based average nucleotide identity (popANI) values between two samples for a given reference genome. For the species of interest, a curated database including reference genomes from the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) was constructed. The popANI metric, calculated by inStrain, represents the similarity between the most closely related strains (either majority or minority strains) in two metagenomes, for a given species. For inStrain profile, a minimum read-level ANI of 95% was set. Then, the compare module of inStrain was executed with the --database_mode option, which determines genome presence in each sample based on coverage breadth and limits comparisons to shared genomes, ensuring that only reliably detected genomes are compared across samples. To have a scale that is more suitable for the detection of extremely closely related genomes, resulting popANI values were converted to popANI-based Single-Nucleotide Variants (SNVs) Per Megabase (popSPM) by subtracting the popANI from one and multiplying by 106. Thresholds to define strain sharing were established per species by comparing the distributions of popSPM values among genomes of women from the same family and among genomes from different families,18 considering vaginal niche characteristics, taxa present in this ecosystem and the adult age of our participants. When isolate genomes were integrated into the strain-sharing analysis, the same inStrain parameters and thresholds were applied.

Reference-independent procedure for within-host strain diversity and strain sharing detection within-families

Assemblies from isolate genomes were used as reference genomes and mapped against their corresponding metagenomic reads (within-host), as well as related metagenomes or isolate genomes (within-family) using inStrain profile with a --min_reads_ani threshold of 95%. As a control for our reads-to-genome comparison approach, whole-genome sequencing reads from isolate genomes were mapped against their own assemblies. This frequently led to “SNVs” being identified at contig edges instead of the expected absence of SNVs. Therefore, all reference-independent reads-to-genome comparisons were denoised by removing SNVs identified at five or less bases from a contig edge. Instances of within-host strain diversity and within-family strain sharing were determined using denoised popSPM values based on the previously defined threshold. The correlation between denoised popSPM values and the ages of the daughters was also assessed.

Analysis of metagenomic sequencing data from a non-European cohort of mothers and daughters

Metagenomic sequencing data from France et al.8 was retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under Bioproject PRJNA779415. Raw metagenomic reads were processed as described above (cfr. ‘Processing and quality control of raw metagenomic sequencing data’). Taxonomic profiles were visualized as outlined in ‘Species-level microbiome profiling’, and instances of strain sharing were identified following the methodologies described in ‘Identification of shared strains using a reference-dependent approach’. Relevant demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants included in this non-European cohort are summarized in Table S2.

Phylogenetic reconstruction of L. crispatus

A phylogenetic tree of L. crispatus integrating metagenomes and isolate genomes was constructed using StrainPhlAn version 4.1.0.30 StrainPhlAn infers the phylogeny of the most abundant strain per sample in a set of metagenomes for a given species by leveraging MetaPhlAn29 marker genes. In our analyses, both metagenomic reads and reads from L. crispatus isolate genomes were included. Parameters were set to include marker genes present in at least 50% of samples (--marker_in_n_samples_perc 50) and to require a minimum of 100 species-dependent marker genes per sample after filtering (--sample_with_n_markers_after_filt 100). In this analysis, 100 high-quality L. crispatus genomes (including both isolate genomes and MAGs) publicly available in the GTDB R226 originating from diverse sources, together with 20 isolates genomes from healthy Isala volunteers outside our sub-cohort, were randomly selected using SCARAP version 1.0.028 and incorporated in the StrainPhlAn 4 workflow.30 Specifically, at least 50% genomes from the female urogenital tract were included. The resulting phylogenetic tree was processed and annotated in RStudio version 4.4.0 and visualized using the ggtree58 package integrating the reference genomes for each species from the GTDB. Midpoint rooting was performed using the midpoint.root function from the phytools package.31
