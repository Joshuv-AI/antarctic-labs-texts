Myocardial dysfunction associated with low-dose endotoxin administration in adult horses
Authors: Elizabeth Williams Louie, Marta Cercone, Barbara Delvescovo, Jacqueline Chevalier, Katharyn Mitchell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHorses with endotoxemia have variable myocardial function, but little is known about the severity or persistence of myocardial dysfunction (MD).Hypothesis/ObjectivesHorses with experimentally induced endotoxemia have systolic and diastolic MD.AnimalsTwelve systemically healthy horses.MethodsHorses were given Escherichia coli lipopolysaccharide 30 ng/kg over 60 min. Echocardiography and cardiac troponin I (cTnI) measurements were performed at baseline, 1, 3, and 5 h post-endotoxin. Fluid therapy (10 L), polymyxin B (6000 IU/kg), and flunixin meglumine (1.1 mg/kg) were administered intravenously, and then echocardiogram and cTnI measurements were performed (post-treatment). Results were compared using linear mixed models and adjusted for multiple comparisons. Significance was set at P < .05.ResultsAll horses showed signs of MD. A significant increase in heart rate from baseline was noted at all timepoints (P < .001). No significant increase in cTnI occurred at any timepoint. Ejection fraction decreased from baseline at 3 and 5 h (mean, 72%, 62%, and 65%, respectively; P < .001 at both timepoints). Using tissue Doppler imaging (TDI), the index of myocardial performance increased at 1, 3, and 5 h (mean, 0.40, 0.55, 0.66, 0.55; P < .01 at all timepoints). Using two-dimensional speckle tracking (2DST), the magnitude of global longitudinal strain was decreased from baseline at 3, 5 h, and post-treatment (mean, −21%, −16%, −17%, and −19%; P < .0001, .0001, and .04, respectively).Conclusions and clinical importanceSystolic and diastolic MD are evident in the absence of increased cTnI. Advanced echocardiographic modalities such as TDI and 2DST improve diagnostic capability for identifying regional abnormalities.Keywords: diastolic dysfunction, endotoxemia, horse, systolic dysfunction, tissue Doppler imaging, 2-dimensional speckle tracking

Introduction

Endotoxemia in horses is common, especially in medical and surgical critical care cases.1–8 Horses are especially sensitive to lipopolysaccharides (LPS), which are released from Gram-negative bacteria and can gain access to the circulation either by absorption from the gastrointestinal tract or during infection, inflammation, and sepsis.8 The most common referrals to tertiary hospitals are cases of colic, respiratory disease, and fever, all of which can have associated signs of endotoxemia.2 Although clinical signs associated with the cardiovascular system are commonly identified in naturally occurring endotoxemia (eg, tachycardia, edema, arrhythmias, changes in coagulation dynamics and blood pressure), diagnostic evaluation, and treatment of endotoxic horses rarely focuses on the heart.2,9,10 Myocardial dysfunction (MD) has been reported with induced dehydration and colic, but has not been well described with endotoxemia.11,12 Increased cardiac troponin concentrations have been documented in horses with colic (>0.1 ng/mL), and a positive association with arrhythmias has been found.13 Myocardial dysfunction associated with endotoxemia and sepsis is well described in humans and can be associated with increased morbidity and mortality, supporting the need for further investigation in horses.14

Experimentally induced endotoxemia using LPS administered IV is a common method to study the effects of endotoxemia on different organ systems as well as the potential effects of various treatment options.1,3,5,9,15–20 Many IV administered endotoxin doses have been used, including bolus doses (30-100 ng/kg) as well as continuous infusions. Similar to investigation of clinical endotoxemia, studies with experimentally induced endotoxemia include cardiovascular components of monitoring and investigation, including cardiac troponin, blood pressure, and heart rate, but none focus on the echocardiographic changes induced by endotoxemia.9 Additionally, use of advanced echocardiographic modalities such as tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) and two-dimensional speckle tracking (2DST) techniques have not been reported in cases of endotoxemia.11 These imaging modalities are more technically challenging to perform, but represent more comprehensive ways to identify subtle and regional MD that may occur earlier and persist longer than previously recognized.

Our study aims were to investigate cardiac function and troponin concentrations over time after low dose endotoxin administration (30 ng/kg over 60 min) in adult horses. We hypothesized that horses would demonstrate both systolic and diastolic MD after low dose endotoxin administration. We also hypothesized that TDI and 2DST would identify MD earlier than traditional 2D and M-mode imaging modalities.

Materials and methods

Study design

Prospective observational study.

Animals and study design

Twelve systemically healthy horses were studied. All horses underwent baseline physical examination, echocardiography, cardiac troponin I (cTnI) measurement21,22 and noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP) assessment.23 Escherichia coli LPS was administered IV at a rate of 30 ng/kg over 60 min and then echocardiogram, physical examination, cTnI measurement, and NIBP were performed at 1, 3, and 5 h post-LPS. Horses were treated with 10 L isotonic fluids IV, flunixin meglumine, and polymyxin B. A final physical examination, echocardiogram, cTnI measurement, and NIBP were performed after treatment (post-tx). Two dimensional, M-mode, TDI, and 2DST echocardiographic views were collected.24

2D, M-mode, and tissue Doppler imaging echocardiographic measurements

Standard measurements of cardiac structures from 2D and M-mode imaging were performed,25,26 as previously described.27,28 Three nonconsecutive cardiac cycles were measured and averaged for further analysis.

2DST imaging measurements

Longitudinal, radial, and circumferential 2DST were performed as previously described.29,30 In brief, longitudinal tracking was performed on the right parasternal long axis view focused on the left ventricle (LV). The endocardium was tracked beginning from septal annulus to apex and then from free wall apex to annulus. Statistics

Statistics were performed using commercially available software (Graphpad Prism, San Diego CA and R Studio, version 4.4.2, Boston, MA). Data were checked for normality and analyzed using a linear mixed model for normally distributed variables and a generalized linear model for non-normally distributed variables. For all variables, timepoint was included as a fixed effect and horse was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements. Myocardial region determined by 2DST also was included as fixed effect. Estimated marginal means were calculated and pairwise comparisons were performed at each timepoint. Adjustment for multiple comparisons using a Bonferroni correction was performed. An adjustment for multiple comparisons across variables, using the p value for the fixed effect of time from each linear mixed model, was performed utilizing a false discovery rate correction. Statistical significance set at P < .05.

Results

Animals

Twelve horses were included in all analyses. Horses consisted of 6 mares and 6 geldings. Mean age was 13.4 years (range, 5-22 years). Mean weight was 568 kg (range, 463-710). Breeds consisted of Thoroughbred (7), Warmblood (4), and mixed breed (1).

One horse with mild aortic valve regurgitation was included, the remaining horses had no clinically relevant valvular regurgitation, and all horses had normal cardiac function based on 2D, M-mode, and TDI at the time of enrollment. One horse remained persistently tachycardic, lethargic and developed limb edema after conclusion of the experiment and tested positive for a viral infection on nasal swab 1 week post-data collection. This horse was not excluded because echocardiographic variables were not outliers and clinical signs were similar to those of other horses throughout the study.

All horses showed clinical signs of endotoxemia. Heart rate was significantly increased at 1, 3, 5 h and post-tx timepoints, and systolic blood pressure was increased at 3 h post-endotoxin administration. The results are reported in Figure 1A and B and Table 1.

2D and M-mode echocardiography

The results of the 2D and M-mode echocardiographic variables are presented at each time point in Table 1. Representative M-mode images focused on the LV at 3 h compared to baseline are presented in Figure 2A. All horses showed subjective signs of decreased systolic septal motion on short axis view of the LV, most marked at the 3-h timepoint (Figure 2). No significant difference was found between enrollment and baseline timepoints for any variable, and therefore only baseline results are reported in the tables and figures.

Representative M-mode and TDI images from a horse at baseline (left) and 3 h (right) post-endotoxin administration. (A) M- mode images demonstrate decrease in ventricular systolic septal excursion (white arrow indicating ventricular systole and septal contraction) following endotoxin administration indicating systolic dysfunction. (B) TDI images demonstrate the shortening of the Em wave (labeled red arrow) and unchanged Am wave (labeled green arrows) on pulse wave TDI following endotoxin administration. While the Em/Am ratio did not reach significance (Figure 3), Em decreased significantly following endotoxin administration.

All horses showed evidence of left ventricular systolic dysfunction based on decreased ejection fraction and fractional shortening at 3 and 5 h (Figure 1C and D). Pulmonary artery diameter was increased only at 1 h, whereas pulmonary artery distensibility index was decreased at all timepoints compared with baseline (Figure 1E and F). The comparison between all 2D and M-mode variables can be found in Figures S1-S3.

Tissue Doppler imaging

The results of the TDI echocardiographic variables are presented in Table 2. Representative pulse-wave TDI (pwTDI) images focused on the LV at 3 h compared to baseline are presented in Figure 2B. The pre-ejection period to ejection time ratio (PEP/ET) was increased at all timepoints compared with baseline (Figure 3A). The index of myocardial performance (IMP) was increased at 1, 3, and 5 h (Figure 3B). Early diastolic velocity (Em) was decreased at 3 and 5 hours on both pwTDI and color TDI (cTDI) (Figure S4). The ratio of early to late diastolic velocity (Em/Am) ratio was not significantly different on pwTDI (Figure 3C) but was significantly decreased at 3 and 5 h on cTDI (Figure 3D). The comparison between all TDI variables can be found in Figure S4.

Discussion

Myocardial dysfunction is present in horses after low dose endotoxin administration, in the absence of increased cTnI. Although abnormalities of systolic dysfunction can be identified on 2D and M-mode images, advanced echocardiographic analysis using TDI and 2DST provide improved characterization of both systolic and diastolic dysfunction as well as identify regional differences and evidence of prolonged dysfunction even when clinical signs appear resolved. Because endotoxemia frequently is identified in horses with many clinical presentations,4,6–8,12 cardiac monitoring should be considered in these cases. Although cardiovascular abnormalities including tachycardia, arrhythmias, changes in blood pressure, and coagulation abnormalities are common findings, MD has not been well documented previously.5,7,9,19,31,32 In humans, identification of and specific treatment for MD is critical in the management of endotoxemia and sepsis.14,33,34 Morbidity and mortality are increased in human patients with documented MD in cases of sepsis and endotoxemia.33 Studies in humans also have documented a decrease in fluid therapy responsiveness and tolerance in cases with MD, frequently requiring utilization of vasopressors and inotropes and monitoring intravascular volume as part of the therapeutic plan.14 In this cohort of horses, heart rate did not return to baseline even after IV fluid administration, and thus, similar to humans, tachycardia despite appropriate hydration should prompt myocardial function evaluation, even in the absence of increased cTnI. Our study provides insights into important cardiac abnormalities induced by low dose endotoxemia in horses and highlights the need for cardiac monitoring and investigation in emergencies where endotoxemia is identified, especially because severity of cardiac signs is expected to worsen with increasingly severe endotoxemia.

Left ventricular systolic dysfunction was identified with changes in both ejection fraction and fractional shortening as well as in TDI and 2DST. Although fractional shortening has been one of the most common measurements for documenting and monitoring LV systolic function,35 the increased magnitude of difference and persistence of the changes to the ejection fraction in our study compared with fractional shortening highlight the usefulness of volumetric measurements to better estimate impaired function. We calculated the ejection fraction using the modified Simpson method of disks which is a common and straightforward method for estimating LV volume.25 Additionally, the absence of a change in the LV internal volume in diastole highlights the fact that immediately after endotoxin administration, the horses in our study did not have decreased preload, and thus the changes identified in ejection fraction and fractional shortening are evidence of true MD and not hypovolemia.

The decrease in pulmonary artery distensibility index was an interesting finding in our study. Pulmonary hypertension is closely associated with pulmonary distensibility index (PADI) decrease,36 but the use of PADI has not been reported in previous endotoxin administration studies in horses.1,9,19,32 Although invasive pulmonary artery pressures determination was not performed in our study, the significant decrease at all timepoints after endotoxin administration suggests the development of pulmonary hypertension, but also could represent a degree of right ventricular systolic dysfunction and decreased output to the pulmonary circulation. The lack of significance in peak systolic pulmonary artery diameter and only slight increase in diastolic pulmonary artery measurements at 1 hour further support the utility of the distensibility index measurement because this finding otherwise could be easily missed. Although pulmonary hypertension has been reported but not well studied in horses associated with endotoxemia,37 it is well established in other species and is commonly recognized with endotoxemia and sepsis.31,38–40 In humans and other species, the responsible mechanism has been suggested to be upregulation of smooth muscle endothelin-1, which causes pulmonary vasoconstriction during endotoxemia.38,40 Additionally, decreased septal motion noted on both 2D and 2DST imaging could be explained by an increase in right ventricular pressures associated with pulmonary hypertension and contributing to ventricular dysfunction, as noted in humans.41 Additionally, because the septum is located between both ventricles, dysfunction within the septum could contribute to right heart dysfunction, either in addition to or as a consequence of the suspected pulmonary hypertension. Further assessment of right heart function and investigation into the mechanisms of pulmonary hypertension in these horses is needed, and is expected to be similar to that reported in other species.

Tissue Doppler imaging demonstrated both left ventricular systolic and diastolic MD in all horses. The IMP is one of the most common measures in humans to identify MD.42 In adult horses, reference ranges have been well established.28,43–46 The rapid increase in the IMP and slow return to normal after treatment could be useful measurements to perform in clinical cases of endotoxemia for monitoring the progression of and improvement in MD. Also, peak atrial wall motion (Aa) was increased at all timepoints, which could be related to heart rate or alterations in atrial function.47 One surprising finding in the TDI analysis was the lack of difference in the pwTDI derived Em/Am ratio. This is a common marker for diastolic dysfunction when Em decreases and Am increases because of decreased ventricular compliance in early diastole and increased atrial contribution to ventricular filling in late diastole.42 Although a decrease in the Em occurred in these horses (Figure 2, Figure S4), a compensatory increase in Am did not occur and thus the ratio did not reach a level of significance over time. One explanation for this finding could be a decreased atrial contribution to ventricular filling from the observed decrease in left atrial area (presumed decreased LA preload) or a decrease in LA systolic function directly from the endotoxin. Another potential reason for this finding could be that although there was LV diastolic dysfunction, it was more apparent in the septum than the free wall, and thus TDI along the LV free wall would not detect the most abnormal regions of MD (Figure 6). This possibility is further supported by the fact that when using c-TDI, a significant difference between Em/Am measured on cTDI (Em-c/Am-c) was found. Using cTDI allowed for optimization and subtle adjustments of the region of interest during the post-processing analysis, thus ensuring optimal placement, whereas with pwTDI, these adjustments must be made in real time and cannot be optimized later.

Two-dimensional speckle tracking better identifies regional differences in myocardial motion and function,29,30 and provided early detection of MD in our study. The discordance of the septum in peak systolic strain compared with other regions of the myocardium (Figure 5) highlights the regional dysfunction that occurs after endotoxin administration. Figure 6 also highlights the frequency of abnormal septal myocardial segments identified in longitudinal, radial, and circumferential strain analyses. This finding is further supported by the mid-septal abnormalities seen in peak G (largest systolic myocardial deformation) and peak S (largest systolic myocardial deformation before aortic valve closure) for longitudinal strain (Figure 5, Figure S5). The main abnormalities identified in the longitudinal axis focus on strain rather than strain rate, indicating that the dysfunction identified on 2DST is largely systolic, as is expected in these horses.48 Minor changes in strain rate were observed at 1 h, but the increased heart rate at that time compared with baseline should be considered when interpreting the clinical relevance of this change. Although the minimal changes in strain rate could be a consequence of the small numbers of horses in our study and horse variability, in humans longitudinal global strain is most commonly measured in cases of MD, and our data suggest similar utility in horses.14,34 The most affected radial variable was peak systolic radial strain rate (SrR), which was significantly faster at 1 h, along the septal segments. Although this observation simply could be an effect of heart rate, the disproportionate changes in the septal region make interpretation challenging. Interestingly, circumferential MD appears to affect both the septum and the free wall, with the LV free wall (anterior septal sector, SCG) and septal regions (anterior septal and anterior, SCS) showing abnormal function after endotoxin administration. This finding could be related to the circular/rotary myocardial fiber orientation causing more circumferential changes in the strain and strain rate.30 These data suggests that 2DST can be utilized to identify persistent systolic and diastolic dysfunction in cases of endotoxemia and specifically highlights the regional dysfunction of the septum compared with other regions of the LV, especially in longitudinal strain. The addition of 2DST analyses should be considered when tissue Doppler findings are not as marked as expected or do not fit well with other evidence of dysfunction, because pwTDI in horses is performed mainly on the LV free wall and may miss or under interpret dysfunction found along the septum.

The absence of increased cTnI in our study differs from previous studies, indicating that myocardial cell damage did not occur with this dose of endotoxin.9 A point-of-care troponin assay was used in our analysis. Although it is a commonly available diagnostic tool, a higher sensitivity assay has been reported,49 and may have identified more subtle changes in troponin. Although a different assay could have improved the sensitivity for identifying lower magnitudes of increased cTnI, the use of the point-of-care assay in our study is relevant to clinical practice. Despite normal cTnI, relevant MD was still noted, which highlights that cardiovascular investigation is useful in cases where endotoxemia is suspected. Moreover, it is expected that MD would be more severe if an increased cTnI is detected.

Our study had some limitations. All echocardiographic images and measurements were obtained by a single unblinded observer, which could introduce a degree of bias. Although our study investigated horses with low dose endotoxemia, clinical cases typically have endotoxemia concurrently with other disease processes (eg, ischemia/reperfusion injury or hypovolemia). Future directions for this work include investigation of MD in clinical cases of endotoxemia, and with concurrent dehydration and hypovolemia and their responsiveness to fluid therapy. The use of xylazine to sedate horses during endotoxin administration could have briefly influenced myocardial function, especially at the 1-hour timepoint when a hyperdynamic response may have been expected. Additionally, investigations utilizing invasive pulmonary pressures, indices of right heart function and comparison to pulmonary artery distensibility index are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we documented the presence of both left ventricular systolic and diastolic MD after low dose endotoxin administration in the absence of increased cTnI. Advanced echocardiographic assessment using TDI and 2DST is useful in detecting regional abnormalities and for documenting persistent dysfunction. Given that the severity of endotoxemia is expected to be worse in clinical cases, and ongoing exposure beyond one hour is common, echocardiographic monitoring of clinical cases of endotoxemia could be considered, especially in cases of persistent or unexplained tachycardia, and drugs targeting treatment of MD warrant further investigation.

Materials and methods

Animals and study design

Systemically healthy horses were enrolled based on normal physical examination, CBC, and clinical chemistry. Horses were enrolled as part of a primary study investigating the celiac plexus block for pain modulation and gastrointestinal motility.50,51 Thirteen horses were enrolled as part of the larger study, but a single horse was excluded because of poor image quality and 12 horses were included in all analysis. Enrollment echocardiography was performed to ensure appropriate cardiac function based on 2D, M-mode and TDI before initiation of the study (enrollment). All echocardiography was performed using a GE vivid E-95 ultrasound machine (GE Healthcare, Chicago IL) using a 4VC transducer (GE Healthcare, Chicago IL).

On the day of the study, all horses underwent baseline echocardiography. E. coli O55:B5 LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) was administered IV to each horse at a rate of 30 ng/kg over 60 min in 500 mL of saline. Horses were sedated with xylazine hydrochloride IV (Rompun©, Decra Pharmaceuticals, Boston MA) during the endotoxin administration as part of the primary study design (approximately 0.4 mg/kg; range, 150-280 mg).50,51

Echocardiographic examination, physical examination, and NIBP measurements were performed at 1, 3, and 5 hours post-endotoxin administration. Blood pressure measurements were performed on the tail base using the Cardell 9401 (Midmark Corporation, Versailles OH) and a size 12 cuff as previously described.23 Horses then were treated at 6 hours with 10 liters isotonic fluids (Plasmalyte A), 1.1 mg/kg of flunixin meglumine (Banamine, Merck Pharmaceuticals, Rahway NJ), and 6000 IU/kg of polymyxin B (Eugia LLC, East Windsor NJ) IV. A final echocardiogram, NIBP and physical examination were performed after treatment, approximately 7-8 h post-endotoxin administration (post-tx).

Echocardiographic views were collected as previously described.24 Briefly, right parasternal long axis views of the right ventricular outflow tract, left ventricular outflow tract, a four-chamber view focused on the left atrium (LA) and on the LV were obtained. Right parasternal short axis views and M-mode of the LV at the level of the chordae tendineae insertion, mitral valve, and aortic valve were collected. Left parasternal long axis view focused on the LA also were obtained. Color and pulsed wave TDI was performed on the LV from the right parasternal short axis view, at the level of the chordae tendineae insertion and on the LA from the right parasternal long axis four-chamber view focused on the LA.28 All loops included 3 or more cardiac cycles and were obtained in triplicate. Color flow mapping was performed across all valves in short and long axis. Images were saved and stored for offline analysis (GE Healthcare (2022), EchoPAC Software, version 204, Chicago IL). All measurements were performed in triplicate on non-consecutive cardiac cycles, and the average of the three measurements was used for further analysis. All echocardiographic images were obtained by and measurements performed by a single observer (EWL).

2D and M-mode echocardiographic measurements

Standard measurements of cardiac structures from 2D and M-mode imaging planes were performed as previously described.25,26 In summary, a diameter of the pulmonary artery sinus was obtained in the right (PADed) ventricular outflow tract view at end-diastole. The diameter of the aortic sinus was obtained at end-diastole (AoDed), and the diameter of the aortic annulus was obtained at peak-systole (AVDps) in the left ventricular outflow tract view. A short axis diameter of the pulmonary artery also was obtained at end-diastole (PADed-sx) and peak systole (PADps-sx) in the left ventricular outflow tract view. Calculation of pulmonary artery distensibility index (PA-DI) was performed using the formula: PA-DI = (PADps-sx − PADed-sx)/PADed-sx × 100. Area measurements of the LA were obtained at maximal dimension one frame before valve opening; (LAA), onset of the P wave (LAAa) and minimum (LAAmin) dimension in the right parasternal long axis view focused on the LA. Left atrial diameter also was measured at maximal dimension (LAD). Left atrial passive fractional area change (Passive LA FAC) was calculated using the formula: Passive LA FAC = (LAA − LAAa)/LAA × 100. Left atrial active fractional area change (Active LA FAC) was calculated using the formula: Active LA FAC = (LAAa − LAAmin)/LAAa × 100. Left ventricular volume measurements using the modified Simpson’s method of disks were obtained in the right parasternal long axis view focused on the LV25; left ventricular internal volume in diastole (LVIVd) and systole (LVIVs) were recorded. From these measurements, ejection fraction (EF), stroke volume (SV), and cardiac output (CO) were calculated using the following formulas EF = (LVIVd − LVIVs)/LVIVd × 100, SV = LVIVd − LVIVs, CO = HR x SV, respectively. Short axis area of the aorta (AoAsx) and left atrium (LAAsx) at end systole were compared. M-mode measurements of the LV consisted of interventricular septum (IVSd, IVSs), left ventricular internal diameter (LVIDd, LVIDs) and left ventricular free wall (LVFWd, LVFWs), in both diastole and systole. From the M-mode measurements, relative wall thickness (RWT) and mean wall thickness (MWT) were calculated using the following formulas: RWT = (IVSd + LVFWd)/LVIDd and MWT = (IVSd + LVFWd)/2. Left atrial diameter at maximal dimension (LAD-Llx) was measured from the left parasternal long axis.

Measurements were normalized to 500 kg body weight (BWT) using the following equations: diameter (500) = measured diameter/BWT1/3 × 5001/3; area (500) = measured area/BWT2/3 × 5002/3; volume (500) = calculated volume/BWT × 500.

Tissue Doppler imaging measurements

Measurements were performed on pulse wave TDI images (pwTDI) as previously described.27,28 In short, pre-ejection period (PEP), ejection time (ET), isovolumetric contraction (IVCT), and isovolumetric relaxation (IVRT) times were recorded in milliseconds. Peak velocity of IVCT (S1), IVRT (E1), ET (Sm), early diastolic filling (Em), and atrial contribution to diastolic filling (Am) were recorded. Early diastolic filling (Em-c) and atrial contribution to diastolic filling (Am-c) also were measured using the color TDI (cTDI) function. The region of interest was set within the left ventricular free wall along the endocardial border directly between the anterior and posterior papillary muscles. Nonconsecutive cardiac cycles on three loops were measured and averaged for further analysis.

2D speckle tracking imaging measurements

Longitudinal, radial, and circumferential 2D speckle tracking was performed as previously described.29,30 In brief, longitudinal tracking was performed using the longitudinal four chamber function on the right parasternal long axis view focused on the LV (GE Healthcare (2022), EchoPAC Software, version 204, Chicago IL). A loop was manually chosen that showed the LV myocardium from the mitral valve to the apex in both systole and diastole. The endocardium was tracked beginning from septal annulus to apex and then from free wall apex to annulus. The region of interest (ROI) thickness was manually adjusted to ensure the entire myocardium was covered. The loop was manually run to ensure proper tracking of the myocardium and manual adjustments were made if indicated up to a maximum of 5 times. If all segments did not track well after 5 adjustments, a different cardiac cycle was selected, or a new ROI was drawn. Although the software provides endocardial, mid-myocardial, and epicardial segments, only mid-myocardial segments were included for all analyses. Default settings for spatial smoothing, temporal smoothing, and drift compensation were used. All segments were evaluated and recorded: basal septum (yellow), mid septum (light blue), apical septum (green), apical free wall (purple), mid free wall (dark blue), and apical free wall (red). Longitudinal assessment of peak systolic strain (SLpeak), peak systolic strain rate (SrL), time to peak systolic strain (SLtm), and time to peak systolic strain rate (SrLtm) were performed and reported for each sector. Global strain (GSL), an average of SLpeak across all segments was performed and reported as a single value. Additionally, strain longitudinal-peak G (SLG) measuring peak systolic strain either before or after aortic valve closure (AVC), strain longitudinal-peak S (SLS) measuring peak systolic strain before AVC, and strain longitudinal-peak P (SLP) measuring peak longitudinal strain after AVC were performed. For strain rate, strain rate longitudinal-Peak S (SrLS) measuring greatest peak systolic longitudinal strain rate, strain rate longitudinal-peak E (SrLE) measuring early diastolic longitudinal strain rate, and strain rate longitudinal-peak A (SrLA) measuring late diastolic longitudinal strain rate were recorded for each segment and for each loop. Description of timing and location of measurements can be found in Table 3. Three nonconsecutive loops were measured at each timepoint and averaged for further analysis.

For radial and circumferential strain, tracking was performed using the short axis papillary muscle function on the right parasternal short axis view focused on the LV at the level of the chordae tendineae insertion. A loop was manually chosen that clearly identified the LV myocardium circumferentially. The tracking and ROI were performed as described above. The preset sectors used for humans were evaluated and recorded: anterior septum (yellow), anterior (light blue), lateral (green), posterior (purple), inferior (dark blue), and apical septal (red). Although these sectors are meant for use in 2DST in humans, the terminology can be slightly confusing in the horse because the anterior septal and anterior regions are along the septum in the horse, and the posterior and inferior regions are along the left ventricular free wall. The lateral and septal segments represent the transition region between septal and free wall anterior and posteriorly, respectively. Radial assessment of peak radial strain (SRpeak), peak radial systolic strain (SRpeak-sys), peak radial systolic strain (SrR), and time to peak radial strain (SRtm) were performed and reported for each sector. Additionally, strain radial-peak G (SRG) measuring largest radial systolic deformation, strain rate radial-Peak S (SrRS) measuring peak radial systolic strain rate, strain rate radial-peak E (SrRE) measuring early radial diastolic strain rate, and strain rate radial-peak A (SrRA) measuring late radial diastolic strain rate were recorded for each segment and for each loop. Circumferential assessment of time to peak strain (SCtm), and time to peak strain rate (SrCtm) were performed. Global strain (GSC), an average of SCG across all sectors was performed and reported as a single value. Additionally, strain circumferential-peak G (SCG) measuring peak circumferential strain before or after AVC, strain circumferential-peak S (SCS) measuring peak strain before AVC, and strain circumferential-peak P (SCP) measuring peak circumferential strain after AVC were performed. For strain rate, strain rate circumferential-peak S (SrCS) measuring greatest systolic circumferential strain rate, strain rate circumferential-Peak E (SrCE) measuring early diastolic circumferential strain rate, and strain rate circumferential-peak A (SrCA) measuring late diastolic circumferential strain rate were recorded for each segment and for each loop. Three nonconsecutive loops were measured and averaged for any further analysis. Description of timing and location of measurement can be found in Tables 4 and 5, respectively.

Troponin

Blood plasma was collected as part of the primary study at baseline, the time of endotoxin administration as well as 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 h post-endotoxin administration.1,2 After treatment, blood was collected 1, 2, and 3 h post-treatment. Samples were collected in lithium heparin tubes, centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min, plasma aspirated and then stored at −80 °C until analysis. Cardiac troponin I at each timepoint was measured using an analyzer previously validated in horses (i-STAT cTnI, Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL).21,22

Statistics

Data was checked for normality via visual inspection of histograms and QQ plots of residuals for all variables. Normally distributed data is reported as a mean and SD, and non-normally distributed data is reported as a median and range. Data was analyzed using a linear mixed model for normally distributed variables and a generalized linear model for non-normally distributed variables. For the 2D, M-mode, TDI, blood pressure, and troponin variables, timepoint was included as a fixed effect and horse was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements. Adjustment for multiple comparisons using a Bonferroni correction was performed. For the 2DST variables, timepoint and myocardial region were included as fixed effect and horse was included as a random intercept. Estimated marginal means were calculated and pairwise comparisons were performed at each timepoint. Adjustment for multiple comparison using a Bonferroni correction was performed.

An adjustment for multiple comparisons across variables, using the p value for the fixed effect of time from each linear mixed model, was performed utilizing a false discovery rate correction. Multiple comparison adjustments were performed for all 2D, M-mode and TDI variables, and separately for all 2DST variables. Statistical significance set at P < .05.
