Smith Normal Forms of Graphical Hermite Simplices Thanks: The first author was partially supported by US National Science Foundation award DMS-2450299.
Benjamin Braun, Antwon Park

Abstract
. We introduce the family of graphical Hermite simplices and study the Smith normal forms of their matrices of vertex vectors, which is equivalent to studying the group structure of the cokernels for these matrices. Our motivation is to study the behavior of lattice simplices subject to small lattice perturbations of their vertices. In this case, a graphical Hermite simplex is a perturbation of a rectangular simplex, i.e., a simplex defined by a diagonal matrix and the origin, with the perturbation controlled by the structure of a directed graph. We first establish sufficient conditions on the graphs and diagonal entries of these matrices that imply having a single non-unit invariant factor, i.e., a cyclic cokernel. We then obtain bounds on the invariant factors of the defining matrices related to lengths of paths in the corresponding directed graph.

2.
Smith normal form and graphical Hermite simplices

Let
[n]:=\left\{1,2,\dots,n\right\}
. We will be working with diagonal matrices, which we denote by

\diag(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},\dots,d_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}&0&0&\dots&0\\
0&d_{2}&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&d_{3}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&d_{n}\end{bmatrix}\,,

where for a non-square matrix there might be additional columns or rows of zeros to the right or below the square matrix containing the diagonal entries.

Let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
be nonzero.
Let us denote
A[i,j]
as the entry of
A
in the
i
-th row and the
j
-th column.
It is known
[
26
]
that there exists matrices
S\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
and
T\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
such that the product
SAT
is the diagonal matrix

SAT=\diag(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\dots,\alpha_{r},0,0,\dots,0)\,,

where each
\alpha_{i}
is a non-negative integer such that
\alpha_{i}\mid\alpha_{i+1}
for
1\leq i<r
.
This diagonal matrix is called the
Smith normal form
of
A
, which we abbreviate as SNF.
The entries
\alpha_{i}
are unique and are called the
elementary divisors
of
A
.

The Smith normal form of an integral matrix can be obtained by a sequence of invertible elementary row and column operations, i.e., permuting rows or columns, adding an integer multiple of a row to another row, negating a row, etc.
These operations preserve the determinant and thus we have

\det(A)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\,.

It is also known
[
26
]
that the elementary divisors of a matrix can be computed as

(2.1)

\alpha_{i}=\frac{\delta_{i}(A)}{\delta_{i-1}(A)}

where
\delta_{0}(A)=1
and
\delta_{i}(A)
is the greatest common divisor of the determinants of all
i\times i
submatrices of
A
.

Example 2.1
.

Let

A=\begin{bmatrix}3&0&0\\
0&6&1\\
0&0&9\end{bmatrix}.

We can see that
\delta_{1}(A)=1
, i.e., the
\gcd
of the entries is 1.
Thus we have
\alpha_{1}=1
.
For
\delta_{2}(A)
, one can show that each
2\times 2
minor of
A
has determinant dividing
3
with the minor obtained by deleting the third row and second column having determinant exactly 3.
Thus,
\delta_{2}(A)=3
, and so
\alpha_{2}=\dfrac{\delta_{2}(A)}{\delta_{1}(A)}=3
. Since
\delta_{3}(A)=\det A=162
, we have
\alpha_{3}=54
.
Thus, the SNF of
A
is
\diag(1,3,54)
.

In this work, our main object of study is the SNF of the following class of matrices, which have as input a positive integer vector of length
n
and a finite simple acyclic directed graph, i.e., a DAG, on
n
vertices.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a simple DAG
G
on
[n]
and a vector
\vec{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{n}
, we define
A_{\vec{d},G}
to be the matrix

A_{\vec{d},G}:=\diag(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})+\sum_{(i,j)\in E(G)}E_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times n}

where
(i,j)
is a directed edge, originating at vertex
i
and connecting to vertex
j
, and
E_{i,j}
is the
n\times n
standard basis matrix such that the only nonzero entry is
E_{i,j}[i,j]=1
.

Suppose
G
is an undirected graph on
[n]
.
We can orient each edge such that it is directed towards the vertex with the larger label.
This orientation is called the
topological ordering of
G
, which is acyclic by construction.
We assume that for any undirected graph
G
, the matrix
A_{\vec{d},G}
is constructed using the topological ordering of
G
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
G
be a simple undirected graph on
[n]
.
We call
A_{\vec{d},G}
a
graphical Hermite simplex
, abbreviated as GHS.

We next briefly explain the motivation for the name graphical Hermite simplex.
Observe that by construction
A_{\vec{d},G}
is a full-rank upper triangular matrix.
Further,
A_{\vec{d},G}
is automatically in
Hermite normal form
, where
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
is in Hermite normal form if

(1)

B[i,j]\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for all
i
and
j
,

(2)

B
is upper-triangular, and

(3)

each column of
B
has a unique maximum entry located on the main diagonal of
B
.

We obtain a lattice simplex as the convex hull of the zero vector and the columns of
A_{\vec{d},G}
, and it is the algebraic and geometric properties of these simplices that we are interested in.
It is known that every lattice simplex is uniquely represented as the convex hull of the zero vector and a square matrix in Hermite normal form
[
27
, Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.2a]
.
Since the lattice simplices arising from
A_{\vec{d},G}
are automatically represented in Hermite normal form, we call both the defining matrix
A_{\vec{d},G}
and the corresponding simplex a graphical Hermite simplex.
Many important invariants of these simplices, such as the Ehrhart
h^{*}
-polynomial and the integer decomposition property, are controlled by the integer points of the fundamental parallelepiped of the simplex; these integer points have a finite abelian group structure determined by the SNF of
A_{\vec{d},G}
.

Definition 2.5
.

The
fundamental parallelepiped
of a GHS
A_{\vec{d},G}
is the set

\mathcal{P}(A_{\vec{d},G})=\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}1&\vec{1}\hskip 0.85358pt\phantom{}^{T}\\
\hline\cr\\[-10.00002pt]
\vec{0}&A_{\vec{d},G}\end{array}\right]\vec{x}:\vec{x}\in[0,1)^{n+1}\right\}=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\vec{v}_{i}\end{bmatrix}:\lambda_{i}\in[0,1)\right\},

where
\vec{v}_{0}
is the zero vector and
\vec{v}_{i}
is the
i
-th column of
A_{\vec{d},G}
.

The following proposition is well-known
[
30
]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Using the notation as in Definition
2.5
, the integer points of
\mathcal{P}(A_{\vec{d},G})
form a group under addition given by the cokernel of
\mathcal{P}(A_{\vec{d},G})
:

\coker(A_{\vec{d},G})=\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}/\Span_{\mathbb{Z}}\left(\vec{e}_{1},\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\vec{v}_{1}\end{bmatrix},\dots,\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\vec{v}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\right)\cong\mathbb{Z}/\alpha_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/\alpha_{2}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/\alpha_{n}\mathbb{Z},

where
\alpha_{i}
are the elementary divisors of
A_{\vec{d},G}
.

A useful fact regarding the SNF of a GHS is that SNF is preserved by a relabeling of the vertices of
G
with a corresponding reindexing of the diagonal vector.
We denote by
S_{n}
the set of permutations on
n
elements.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
G
be a DAG on
[n]
, let
\vec{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{n}
, and let
T\in S_{n}
. Then let
T(G)
denote the digraph obtained by replacing the label of each vertex
v
with
T(v)
and let

T(\vec{d})=(d_{T(1)},d_{T(2)},\dots,d_{T(n)})\,.

Then
A_{\vec{d},G}
and
A_{T(\vec{d}),T(G)}
have the same SNF.

Proof.

Since every permutation can be written as a product of transpositions, it suffices to show that for any digraph
G
on
[n]
and any single transposition
T=(v\ w)\in S_{n}
, the matrices
A_{\vec{d},G}
and
A_{T(\vec{d}),T(G)}
have the same SNF.
This can be done by showing that
A_{T(\vec{d}),T(G)}
can be obtained from
A_{\vec{d},G}
using elementary row and column operations.
Let
A^{\prime}
be the matrix obtained by swapping rows
v
and
w
of
A_{\vec{d},G}
followed by swapping columns
v
and
w
.
It is a straightforward exercise to verify that
A^{\prime}=A_{T(\vec{d}),T(G)}
, and thus these matrices have the same SNF.
∎

Proposition
2.7
allows us to work with isomorphism classes of DAGs and we can choose a labeling on the vertices that suits our needs. In particular, we can view
G
as a weighted graph where vertex
i
has an associated weight of
d_{i}
, and Proposition
2.7
allows us to work with isomorphism classes of weighted DAGs.

Another helpful tool for studying GHS’s is to use indicator variables for the off-diagonal entries as follows.
For a simple graph
G
with the topological ordering, we define indicator variables
x_{i,j}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
for
i\neq j
, where
x_{i,j}
evaluates to 1 if and only if
(i,j)\in E(G)
.
Thus, we can write

A_{\vec{d},G}[i,j]=\begin{cases}d_{i}&\text{if }i=j,\\
x_{i,j}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This provides an alternative way to present
A_{\vec{d},G}
for an arbitrary undirected graph
G
:

A_{\vec{d},G}=\diag(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}x_{i,j}E_{i,j}=\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}&x_{1,2}&x_{1,3}&\dots&x_{1,n}\\
0&d_{2}&x_{2,3}&\dots&x_{2,n}\\
0&0&d_{3}&\dots&x_{3,n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&d_{n}\end{bmatrix}

We use monomials formed from the indicator variables
x_{i,j}
to identify paths in
G
.

Definition 2.8
.

Given a graph
G
, a
path

P\in G
is a sequence of vertices
(v_{1}\to v_{2}\to v_{3}\to\dots\to v_{k})
such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in E(G)
for all
1\leq i\leq k-1
.
We define the
length
of a path as the number of vertices visited, rather than the conventional definition of number of edges used.
We denote the length of a path by
\ell(P)
.

For graphs with the topological ordering, every path is given by a strictly increasing sequence of vertices as each edge is directed towards the larger label.
Thus any non-increasing sequence of vertices cannot be a path in a graph with the topological ordering.

Example 2.9
.

Let
G
be a graph on
[5]
. Then we have the following equivalences:

\displaystyle x_{1,2}x_{2,3}x_{3,4}=1\qquad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad(1\to 2\to 3\to 4)\in G

\displaystyle x_{1,3}x_{3,5}=1\qquad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad(1\to 3\to 5)\in G

\displaystyle x_{2,5}=1\qquad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad(2\to 5)\in G

Using these indicator variables, we next prove a result regarding the minors of
A_{\vec{d},G}
.
We first define the notion of gaps of a path.

Definition 2.10
.

Given an increasing path
P=(v_{1}\to v_{2}\to\dots\to v_{k})
in an graph
G
on
[n]
with the topological ordering, we say that
u\in[n]
is a
gap
of
P
if
v_{1}<u<v_{k}
and
u\notin P
.
For
\vec{d}=(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{n}
, we define the function
g(P)
as

g(P):=\prod_{i\in S}d_{i}\,,

where
S\subseteq[n]
is the set of gaps of
P
.
We set
g(P):=1
when
P
contains no gaps.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
G
be an undirected graph on
[n]
and let
x_{i,j}
be the indicator variable for the edge
(i,j)\in G
. Also, let
\vec{d}=(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
.
If
M_{a,b}
is the
b\times b
submatrix of
A_{\vec{d},G}
given by

M_{a,b}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{a,a+1}&x_{a,a+2}&\dots&x_{a,a+b-1}&x_{a,a+b}\\
d_{a+1}&x_{a+1,a+2}&\dots&x_{a+1,a+b-1}&x_{a+1,a+b}\\
0&d_{a+2}&\dots&x_{a+2,a+b-1}&x_{a+2,a+b}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&d_{a+b-1}&x_{a+b-1,a+b}\\
\end{bmatrix},

then the determinant of
M_{a,b}
is equal to

\sum_{P=(a\to\dots\to a+b)\in G}(-1)^{b-\ell(P)+1}g(P)\,,

where
\ell(P)
is the length of the path
P
and the sum is equal to
0
if no such paths exist.

Proof.

We will proceed via induction on
b
. Let
b=1
. Recall that
\det M_{a,1}=x_{a,a+1}
evaluates to 1 if and only if the path
(a\to a+1)\in G
.
As this path has no gaps and has length
2
, we have the desired outcome of

\det M_{a,1}=\sum_{P=(a\to a+1)\in G}(-1)^{1-\ell(P)+1}g(P)=\begin{cases}1&(a\to a+1)\in G\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Assume the inductive hypothesis for
k\leq b-1
.
We perform cofactor expansion on
M_{a,b}
along the first row.
Note that deleting column
a+i
for
i<b
exposes a row containing only indicator variables of the form
x_{a+i,j}
.
In addition, in the cofactor expansion, the columns to the left of the deleted column will have entries of
d_{j}
along the main diagonal, whereas the columns to the right of the deleted column will form the submatrix
M_{a+i,b-i}
.
Thus in the cofactor expansion, the submatrix obtained by deleting the first row and
i
-th column, for
i<k
, has determinant

\det M_{a+i,b-i}\prod_{j=a+1}^{a+i-1}d_{j}\,.

When
i=b
, then we have that the corresponding submatrix obtained is an upper triangular matrix with entries of
d_{j}
along the diagonal, hence that submatrix’s determinant is
\prod_{j=a+1}^{a+b-1}d_{j}
.
So, from cofactor expansion and our inductive hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\det M_{a,b}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}x_{a,a+b}\prod_{j=a+1}^{a+b-1}d_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{b-1}(-1)^{i-1}x_{a,a+i}\det M_{a+i,b-i}\prod_{j=a+1}^{a+i-1}d_{j}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{b-1}x_{a,a+b}\prod_{j=a+1}^{a+b-1}d_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{b-1}(-1)^{i-1}x_{a,a+i}\left(\sum_{P=(a+i\to\dots\to a+b)\in G}(-1)^{b-i-\ell(P)+1}g(P)\right)\prod_{j=a+1}^{a+i-1}d_{j}

Note that for
i<b
, all paths
P=(a+i\to\dots\to a+b)
can be extended to a path from
a
to
a+b
by adding the edge
(a,a+i)
.
Note that this resulting path keeps all of its previous gaps in addition to
\left\{a+1,a+2,\dots,a+i-1\right\}
.
Also, the edge
(a,a+b)
can be interpreted as a path with gaps
\left\{a+1,a+2,\dots,a+b-1\right\}
.
Therefore, if we instead describe
P
as the path
(a\to a+i\to\dots\to a+b)
, then we can write

\displaystyle\det M_{a,b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P=(a\to a+b)\in G}(-1)^{b-\ell(P)-1}g(P)+\sum_{i=1}^{b-1}\sum_{P=(a\to a+i\to\dots\to a+b)\in G}(-1)^{b-\ell(P)+1}g(P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P=(a\to\dots\to a+b)\in G}(-1)^{b-\ell(P)-1}g(P)

∎

Note that the submatrix
M_{a,b}
will be used several times throughout the paper, as paths in our graph
G
will often tell us some information regarding the determinant of
M_{a,b}
. In particular, the determinant of
M_{a,b}
is nonzero only if there exists a path from
a
to
a+b
, and if we have other requirements of the paths of
G
, then we can often make conclusions regarding the elementary divisors of
A_{\vec{d},G}
.

3.
Cyclic Cokernel

In this section, we establish conditions on
\vec{d}
and
G
that produce a GHS with a cyclic cokernel.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a DAG on
[n]
and
\vec{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 1}
. If one of the following hold:

(1)

The entries of
\vec{d}
are pairwise coprime and
G
is arbitrary,

(2)

G
is a path and
\vec{d}
is arbitrary,

(3)

G
contains a path of length
n
and
\vec{d}=(m,m,\dots,m)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{n}
,

then
A_{\vec{d},G}
has a cyclic cokernel.

The rest of this section contains Propositions yielding the implications stated in Theorem
3.1
.
Note that by the divisibility of the elementary divisors of
A_{\vec{d},G}
, if we show that
\alpha_{n-1}=1
, then it must be the case that
\alpha_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
.
Thus, to show that
A_{\vec{d},G}
has a cyclic cokernel, it suffices to show that
A_{\vec{d},G}
has only one nonunit elementary divisor, i.e.,
\delta_{n-1}=\alpha_{n-1}=1
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that the entries of
\vec{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{n}
are pairwise coprime.
Then for any
G
, the matrix
A_{\vec{d},G}
has only one nonunit elementary divisor.

Proof.

Consider the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrix of
A_{\vec{d},G}
obtained by removing the
j
-th row and column for some
j\in[n]
. Note this submatrix is upper-triangular, thus we have the determinant of this submatrix is

\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}}{d_{j}}.

Since this is true for all
j\in[n]
, we have that the greatest common divisor of the determinants of the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrices must be 1.
Thus,
\delta_{n-1}(A_{\vec{d},G})=1
.
∎

In Example
2.4
, we have that
\vec{d}=(2,5,3)
satisfies the conditions in the above Proposition. Therefore, for any graph
G
on
[3]
, we have that the SNF of
A_{(2,5,3),G}
is
\diag(1,1,30)
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a directed path of length
n
on
[n]
.
Then
A_{\vec{d},G}
has only one nonunit elementary divisor for all
\vec{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{n}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.7
, we can permute the labels on
G
such that
G
is the path
(1\to 2\to\dots\to n)
.
This will also permute the entries in
\vec{d}
, however, as we are trying to show that
\alpha_{n-1}=1
, this will not affect our argument.
From Lemma
2.11
, since there is only one path from 1 to
n
and this path does not contain any gaps, we have
\det M_{1,n-1}=1.

Therefore, there exists a
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrix of
A_{\vec{d},G}
with determinant 1. Thus,
\delta_{n-1}(A_{\vec{d},P_{n}})=1
.
∎

Note that in the previous proposition,
G
must be exactly a path of length
n
.
It is possible for the presence of additional edges beyond the path edges to cause
\alpha_{n-1}>1
, as demonstrated in the following example.

Example 3.4
.

Let
G=K_{3}
and let
\vec{d}=[6,9,12]
.
Observe that
K_{3}
contains the path
(1\to 2\to 3)
, yet the SNF of
A_{\vec{d},K_{3}}
is
\diag(1,2,324)
.
Thus, we have a graph containing a path of length
3
, yet the cokernel of
A_{\vec{d},K_{3}}
is not cyclic.

In general, when the entries of
\vec{d}
are arbitrary, knowing how each edge in
G
will affect the SNF of
A_{\vec{d},G}
is more complicated.
Example
3.4
demonstrates that even with
\gcd(\vec{d})
being nonunit, it is nontrivial to locate some graphical pattern that would imply
A_{\vec{d},G}
having a cyclic cokernel.
If we restrict to a constant diagonal, where
\vec{d}=(m,m,\dots,m)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{n}
, in this case it we observe that paths have an impact on the SNF of
A_{\vec{d},G}
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
G
be a DAG on
[n]
containing a path of length
n
.
If
\vec{d}=(m,m,\dots,m)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{n}
, then the cokernel of
A_{\vec{d},G}
is cyclic.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Theorem
4.6
, as there is only one path of length
n
.
∎

Note that Proposition
3.3
requires
G
to be exactly a path of length
n
, whereas Corollary
3.5
only requires
G
to contain a path of length
n
.
In the case where
\vec{d}
is pairwise coprime, the SNF is fully determined and the graph structure has no impact on the SNF of
A_{\vec{d},G}
.
For arbitrary
\vec{d}
, the effect
G
has on the SNF of
A_{\vec{d},G}
is obscured by the number theory of
\vec{d}
.
Thus, we next focus on the case where
\vec{d}
is constant.

4.

G
are simple and loopless on vertex set
[n]

G

G
.
We write
\vec{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>1}^{n}

m

It can be shown that the nonunit divisors of
A_{\vec{m},C_{i}}
are
\gcd(i,m),\tfrac{m^{2}}{\gcd(i,m)},
and
m^{i+1}
.
Note that we can choose
m
and
i
such that
A_{\vec{m},C_{i}}
has an arbitrary value as an elementary divisor by manipulating the
\gcd
value.
For example, if
m=6
, then the SNF of
A_{\vec{6},C_{2}}
is
\diag(1,1,2,18,216)
and the SNF of
A_{\vec{6},C_{3}}
is
\diag(1,1,1,1,3,12,1296)
.
We therefore have that
2
is an elementary divisor of
A_{\vec{6},C_{2}}
and
3
is an elementary divisor of
A_{\vec{6},C_{3}}
.
However, it is impossible for both
2
and
3
to be elementary divisors of the same matrix.
Therefore, the elementary divisors of
A_{\vec{6},C_{2}\amalg C_{3}}
are not the same as the disjoint union of the elementary divisors of
A_{\vec{6},C_{2}}
and
A_{\vec{6},C_{3}}
.

We shall next investigate the influence of paths in
G
on the SNF of
A_{\vec{m},G}
.
In particular, we shall see that the length of the longest path in
G
controls the largest elementary divisor.
We will show this by establishing a lower bound on
\delta_{n-1}(A_{\vec{m},G})
.
We begin by showing that we can edit the statement of Lemma
2.11
to the following in the case of a constant diagonal.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
M_{a,b}
refer to the submatrix of
A_{\vec{m},G}
obtained by the procedure given in Lemma
2.11
. Then

\det M_{a,b}=\sum_{P=(a\to\dots\to a+b)\in G}(-1)^{b-\ell(P)+1}m^{b-\ell(P)+1}.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.11
, we have

\det M_{a,b}=\sum_{P=(a\to\dots\to a+b)\in G}(-1)^{b-\ell(P)+1}g(P).

Since each diagonal entry is
m
, we have that
g(P)
is
m
raised to the power equal to the number of gaps in
P
.
We arrive at the desired result, once we recognize that the length of a path
P=(a\to\dots\to a+b)
and the number of gaps of
P
must add up to the number of integers between
a
and
a+b
, which is
b+1
.
∎

The determinant of these submatrices come from the sum over paths in
G
, however, the summand only takes into account the lengths of those paths.
Let us define

f_{c,r}:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

to be the counting function such that
f_{c,r}(i)
counts the number of paths
P=(c\to\dots\to r)\in G
of length
i
.
We define
A_{c,r}
to be the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrix of
A_{\vec{m},G}
obtained by deleting column
c
and row
r
.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
r\neq c
, then the determinant of
A_{c,r}
is

(4.1)

\det A_{c,r}=\sum_{P=(c\to\dots\to r)\in G}(-1)^{r-c-\ell(P)+1}m^{n-\ell(P)}.

If we consider
\det A_{c,r}
as a polynomial in
m
, then we can write

(4.2)

\det A_{c,r}=\sum_{i\geq 2}(-1)^{r-c-i+1}f_{c,r}(i)m^{n-i}.

Proof.

If
r<c
, then we have
A_{c,r}
is upper-triangular with
A_{c,r}[r,r]=0
.
Therefore,
\det A_{c,r}=0
. Since each path in
G
must be an increasing sequence, there are no paths from
c
to
r
, hence the empty sum is also zero.
Therefore, equation (
4.1
) holds.

Next, assume that
r>c
. Thus, we can write

A_{c,r}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}B_{1,1}&B_{1,2}&B_{1,3}\\
\hline\cr 0&M_{c,r-c}&B_{2,3}\\
\hline\cr 0&0&B_{3,3}\end{array}\right]

where
B_{1,1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(c-1)\times(c-1)}
,
B_{3,3}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n-r)\times(n-r)}
and
M_{c,r-c}
is the submatrix as described in Lemma
2.11
.
Note that
B_{1,1}
and
B_{3,3}
are upper triangular, and so
\det B_{1,1}=m^{c-1}
and
\det B_{3,3}=m^{n-r}
.
Thus, we have that

\displaystyle\det A_{c,r}

\displaystyle=\det B_{1,1}(\det M_{c,r-c})\det B_{3,3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P=(c\to\dots\to r)\in G}(-1)^{r-c-\ell(P)+1}m^{n-\ell(P)}

Note that the summand only depends on the length of
P
, so we can collect terms by their lengths. Since
r\neq c
, then each path from
c
to
r
must have length at least two, thus we arrive at (
4.2
).
∎

We are now able to prove the following bound on the largest invariant factor for a GHS with constant diagonal.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
G
be a DAG on
[n]
and let
h
be the length of the longest path in
G
.
Then the largest invariant factor of
A_{\vec{m},G}
is at most
m^{h}
.

Proof.

Note that
\det A_{r,r}=m^{n-1}
for all
r
as
A_{r,r}
is upper triangular. By Lemma
4.3
, if
\det A_{c,r}
is nonzero, then from (
4.2
),
\det A_{c,r}
is a polynomial in
m
with the first nonzero power of
m
being at least
n-h
. Therefore,
m^{n-h}
divides
\det A_{c,r}
for all
c,r
. Thus
\delta_{n-1}\geq m^{n-h}
and since
\delta_{n}=\det A_{\vec{m},G}=m^{n}
, we arrive at

\alpha_{n}=\frac{\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n-1}}\leq m^{h}.

∎

As we will later see in the proof of Theorem
4.6
, number theoretic considerations involving the number of paths of particular lengths and
m
determine which elementary divisors appear.
For example, suppose that there is only one vertex with indegree 0 and only one vertex with outdegree 0, and there are exactly
k
distinct paths of length
h
in
G
.
Then you get that
\gcd(k,m)m^{n-h}
divides
\det A_{c,r}
for all possible
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrices, hence when
\gcd(k,m)>1
, then
\alpha_{n}<m^{h}
.

The nonunit elementary divisors for
A_{\vec{6},B_{i}}
are

\begin{array}[]{c|r}B_{1}&\left\{216\right\}\\
B_{2}&\left\{12,108\right\}\\
B_{3}&\left\{6,18,72\right\}\\
B_{4}&\left\{6,6,12,108\right\}\\
B_{5}&\left\{6,6,6,6,216\right\}\end{array}

The length of the longest path in
B_{i}
is
3
and there are
i
total paths of length
3
.
From Corollary
4.4
, we have that
6^{3}=216
is an upper bound for the largest elementary divisor.
We see that we achieve this bound only for
i=1
and
i=5
, which are the only two integers
i
, for which
B_{i}
is listed above, that are coprime with
6
.
In fact, we see that the largest elementary divisor of
A_{\vec{6},B_{i}}
is of the form
\frac{6^{3}}{\gcd(6,i)}
.

One can see that
6^{3}
is an elementary divisor of
A_{\vec{6},B_{i}}
for all
i
such that
\gcd(i,6)=1
.
In fact, one can check that
m^{3}
is an elementary divisor of
A_{\vec{m},B_{i}}
for all
i
such that
\gcd(i,m)=1
.
This is not a coincidence.
This behavior generalizes to all DAGs, as shown by the following theorem.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
G
be a DAG on
[n]
and let
h
be the length of the longest path in
G
.
Suppose that
\gcd(f_{u,v}(h),m)=1
for some
u,v
.
Then the largest invariant factor of
A_{\vec{m},G}
is exactly
m^{h}
.

Proof.

Let
u,v
be such that
\gcd(f_{u,v}(h),m)=1
.
Recall that
f_{u,v}(h)
counts the number of paths
(u\to\dots\to v)\in G
of length
h
.
Let us now define
f^{\prime}_{u,v}(m)
as the quantity

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{u,v}(m)

\displaystyle=\frac{\det A_{u,v}}{m^{n-h}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{h}(-1)^{v-u-i+1}f_{u,v}(i)m^{h-i}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{v-u-h+1}f_{u,v}(h)+\sum_{i=2}^{h-1}(-1)^{v-u-i+1}f_{u,v}(i)m^{h-i}

By our assumption,
\gcd(f_{u,v}(h),m)=1
implies that
\gcd(f^{\prime}_{u,v}(m),m)=1
.
By definition,
\delta_{n-1}(A_{\vec{m},G})
must divide
\gcd(\det A_{u,v},\det A_{1,1})
.
Here, we are considering
A_{1,1}
as this submatrix is upper triangular, which means
\det A_{1,1}=m^{n-1}
.
Therefore, we have that
\delta_{n-1}(A_{\vec{m},G})
must divide
m^{n-h}
.
In particular,
\delta_{n-1}(A_{\vec{m},G})\leq m^{n-h}
.
However, we know from Theorem
4.4
that
\delta_{n-1}(A_{\vec{m},G})\geq m^{n-h}
.
Therefore, we have
\delta_{n-1}(A_{\vec{m},G})=m^{n-h}
, hence

\alpha_{n}=\frac{\delta_{n}(A_{\vec{m},G})}{\delta_{n-1}(A_{\vec{m},G})}=m^{h}.

∎

.

It is clear by definition that bipartite DAGs have a maximum path length of
2
, and by Proposition
2.7
, we can assume without loss of generality that
U=\left\{1,2,3,4,\dots,k\right\}
and
V=\left\{k+1,\dots,n\right\}
.
From Theorem
4.4
, we know that the elementary divisors of
A_{\vec{m},G}
are bounded by
m^{2}
when
G
is a bipartite DAG.
However, the fact that
G
is bipartite actually allows us to make an even stronger claim, in particular, the SNF of the adjacency matrix of
G
fully determines the SNF of
A_{\vec{m},G}
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let G be a bipartite DAG and B be given as

B=\sum_{(i,j)\in E(G)}E_{i,j}.

Let
\beta_{i}
be the elementary divisors of
B
and let
\gamma_{i}:=\gcd(m,\beta_{i})
.
If the rank of
B
is
r
, then the SNF of
A_{\vec{m},G}
is

\diag\left(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{r},m,m,\dots,m,\dfrac{m^{2}}{\gamma_{r}},\dfrac{m^{2}}{\gamma_{r-1}},\dots,\dfrac{m^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}\right)

.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
V(G)=\left\{1,2,\dots,k\right\}\amalg\left\{k+1,\dots,n\right\}
.
Then we have
x_{i,j}
is nonzero only if
i\leq k
and
j>k
. Thus, we have that
A_{\vec{m},G}
is a block matrix of the form

A_{\vec{m},G}=\begin{bmatrix}mI_{k}&B\\
0&mI_{n-k}\end{bmatrix}

The following discussion demonstrates that elementary row operations on
B
can arise from elementary row operations on
A_{\vec{m},G}
without affecting the other entries in
A_{\vec{m},G}
.
An identical argument will show the same applies for elementary column operations.
Let
i,j\leq k
be distinct.

•

To perform the row operation
R_{i}\to R_{i}+R_{j}
in
B
, we can perform this operation in
A_{\vec{m},G}
. In the
mI_{k}
block, we are changing the
[i,j]
position from 0 to
m
.
Note that column
i
of
A_{\vec{m},G}
has an
m
in the
i
-th row and 0 elsewhere.
We can then perform the column operation
C_{j}\to C_{j}-C_{i}
such that
A_{\vec{m},G}[i,j]=0
.

•

To negate the
i
-th row in
B
, we can negate the
i
-th row in
A_{\vec{m},G}
and then negate the
i
-th column in
A_{\vec{m},G}
.

•

To swap the
i
-th and
j
-th rows in
B
, we can swap the
i
-th and
j
-th rows in
A_{\vec{m},G}
. We can then swap the
i
-th and
j
-th columns so that the top left block is unaltered.

Therefore, we know it is possible to arrive at the SNF of
B
via elementary row and column operations on
B
, so let us perform the corresponding sequence of elementary row and columns operations on
A_{\vec{m},G}
until we arrive at

A_{\vec{p},G}\to\dots\to\begin{bmatrix}mI_{k}&\diag(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{r},0,\dots,0)\\
0&mI_{n-k}\end{bmatrix}

We can then perform a sequence of row and column permutations of this matrix such that the resulting matrix has
r

2\times 2
blocks along the main diagonal of the form

\begin{bmatrix}m&\beta_{i}\\
0&m\end{bmatrix},

followed by a constant
m
diagonal.
Specifically, exchanging columns
2
and
k+1
and exchanging rows
2
and
k+1
will place a
2\times 2
block with
\beta_{1}
in the upper left corner of
mI_{k}
and will place a
2\times 2
block with
\beta_{2}
in the upper left corner of
mI_{n-k}
.
Iterating this process for consecutive pairs of
\beta
’s will result in the desired form.

For each positive
i\leq r
, there exists a sequence of elementary row and column operations (essentially, applying the Euclidean algorithm) such that

\begin{bmatrix}m&\beta_{i}\\
0&m\end{bmatrix}\to\dots\to\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{i}&0\\
0&\dfrac{m^{2}}{\gamma_{i}}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore,
A_{\vec{m},G}
has the same SNF as

\diag\left(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{r},m,m,\dots,m,\dfrac{m^{2}}{\gamma_{r}},\dfrac{m^{2}}{\gamma_{r-1}},\dots,\dfrac{m^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}\right).

It is straightforward to verify from the divisibility property of the
\beta
’s that each of these terms divides the subsequent term, hence this is the SNF of
A_{\vec{m},G}
. ∎

One special case to consider is when
m
is prime.
For this case, we denote our diagonal vector as
\vec{p}
for the prime
p
.
One reason to consider a prime diagonal is that from Theorem
4.4
, the elementary divisors are bounded by
p^{2}
and due to the divisibility condition on the elementary divisors, the elementary divisors can only be
1,p
or
p^{2}
.
Then Proposition
4.9
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
G
be a bipartite DAG on
[n]
.
Then the elementary divisors of
A_{\vec{p},G}
are 1,
p
, or
p^{2}
.
In particular, if

B=\sum_{(i,j)\in E(G)}E_{i,j}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{n\times n},

then the number of unit elementary divisors is equal to the rank of
B
over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.
As a consequence, the number of
p^{2}
elementary divisors is equal to the rank of
B
over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

From Corollary
4.4
, we have that the largest elementary divisor is at most
p^{2}
, so all elementary divisors must be at most
p^{2}
.
Let
\beta_{i}
be the elementary divisors of
B
.
Note that
\gamma_{i}=\gcd(p,\beta_{i})
is either
1
or
p
, and if
\gamma_{i}=p
for some
i
, then
\gamma_{j}=p
for all
i\leq j\leq r
.
Since if
p
divides
\beta_{i}
then that elementary divisor is equal to
0
mod
p
, we have that
\gamma_{i}=1
for all
i\leq r_{p}
, where
r_{p}
is the rank of
B
over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, and the rest of the
\gamma_{i}
are equal to
p
.
Thus, from Proposition
4.9
, we have that the SNF of
A_{\vec{p},G}
is

\diag(\underbrace{1,1,1,\dots,1}_{r_{p}\text{ copies}},\underbrace{p,p,\dots,p}_{n-2r_{p}\text{ copies}},\underbrace{p^{2},p^{2},\dots,p^{2}}_{r_{p}\text{ copies}},)

∎

Note that there are
n-r_{p}
nonunit elementary divisors of
A_{\vec{p},G}
when
G
is bipartite.
When considering this special case with a prime diagonal, experimental data suggests that Proposition
4.9
will apply to all graphs, not just bipartite graphs.
In particular, whereas the maximum path length of the graph determines an upper bound for all elementary divisors, the rank of the adjacency matrix seems to be determining how the powers on
p
are being distributed across the nonunit elementary divisors.
We end with the following conjecture which makes this observation precise.

Conjecture 4.11
.

Let
G
be the topological ordering of a graph on
[n]
and let
B\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
be given by

B=\sum_{(i,j)\in G}E_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}.

Let
r_{p}
be the rank of
B
over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Then if
\alpha_{i}
are the elementary divisors of
A_{\vec{p},G}
, we have that
\alpha_{r_{p}}=1
and
\alpha_{r_{p}+1}>1
.
