Perception of medical students at public universities in Uganda on their education environment: a cross-sectional study
Authors: David Jolly Muganzi, Timothy Mwanje Kintu, Racheal Mukisa Nakandi, Linda Atulinda, Immaculate Kaducu Atim, Francis Mwaka, Blair Kizza, Joseph Ngonzi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe healthcare workforce is one of the six core components of a well-functioning and sufficient health system. The WHO highlights that improving education and training of the health workforce is critical to improving health systems. Medical education is a pillar of the healthcare workforce. The educational environment - the physical circumstances or conditions under which learning takes place - is an important factor determining medical education’s effectiveness. Its academic and clinical effects significantly determine medical students’ attitudes, knowledge, skills, progression, and behaviors. To ensure strong health systems, there is a need for improvement in medical education. Improvement must be contextual and informed by locally relevant data. This study, which assessed Ugandan medical students’ perception of their learning environment, provides crucial context-specific data highly relevant to the current state of medical education in Uganda.MethodsThis quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted among medical students across five medical schools in Uganda between December 2021 and February 2022. Participants completed an online questionnaire with a demographics section and the Dundee Ready Education Environment Measure (DREEM) tool. The data was analyzed using R in R Studio version 2021.09.0 + 351. The average score for each DREEM sub-scale and the total DREEM score were calculated, and their relationship with demographic characteristics was examined.ResultsThe average total DREEM score of the 335 participants was 112.27/200 (56.12%). We found that 69% (231/335) of the students had a positive perception. The sub-scale mean scores were as follows: Perception of Atmosphere – 25.14/48, Social Self-perception − 14.94/28, Perception of Teachers − 24.86/44, Perception of Learning − 28.8/48, and Academic Self-perceptions – 19.04/32. “There is a good support system for students who get stressed” was the item with the lowest mean score (1.41/5).ConclusionsThe study’s findings indicate that medical students’ perceptions of their learning environment are just above average. This suggests a need for improvement, particularly in the atmosphere and social support, to ensure the training of a robust health workforce. The results of this study should motivate stakeholders to initiate changes that will enhance the quality of medical education in Uganda.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06456-1.Keywords: Education environment, Medical school, DREEM, Health workforce, Medical student

Background

The healthcare workforce is one of the six core components of a well-functioning, stable, and sufficient health system [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) highlights improving education and training of the health workforce as one of the areas of focus for improving human resources for health [1]. Furthermore, improving education and training of the health workforce is paramount given that without a constant increase in the number and quality of health workers, the other measures to improve human resources for health are deemed to have suboptimal impact [2]. Most endeavors in this direction have focused on numerical augmentation, but there is also a dire need to modify and increase skill sets to meet the dynamic demands of healthcare [3].

Medical education, a pillar of the healthcare workforce, is the process of training doctors subordinate to the dominant economic and social structures in societies where it occurs [4]. It is a continuum of lifelong learning which begins with undergraduate studies [5, 6]. Medical schools are mandated to train healthcare workers to provide health care, conduct research, offer leadership in health, and participate in educating the next generation [7]. An integral component in meeting this mandate is the educational environment.

Educational environment refers to the physical circumstances, objects, or conditions under which learning occurs within the school setting [8]. Shaista defines the educational environment as a combination of climate, culture, and ethos in which learning occurs and identifies it as the most important aspect of learning [9]. The students’ perception of the educational environment has been shown to influence their behavior, progress, stress levels, academic self-conception, sense of well-being, and academic performance [10, 11]An inappropriate educational environment is associated with problems of learners’ well-being, causing boredom and burnout. Medical educators argue that the effects of the educational environment, both academic and clinical, are significant determinants of medical students’ attitudes, knowledge, skills, progression, and behaviours [12–14].

In a critical review of medical training in Uganda, it has been noted that there needs to be a greater quality of clinical skills, including clerkship, examination, clinical logic, choice of diagnostic tests, and decision on prescribing treatment and ethical principles among fresh graduate doctors [15]. Indeed, such gaps in medical knowledge and competence of graduates have been highlighted as a significant problem in expanding the health workforce [16]. In Uganda, previous studies at Makerere University and Habib Medical School [10, 17] indicate a generally positive perception among students of their learning environment. However, the limited number of such studies underscores a significant gap in understanding the specific nuances of medical education in Uganda. Comparative analysis with studies from South Africa, Ghana, Zambia, and Nigeria [18–21] reveals higher scores for educational environments in these countries than in Uganda.

This study conducted a localized investigation across multiple public institutions in Uganda to better understand Ugandan medical students’ perceptions of their learning environment. Specifically, it addressed the question: “What are the perceptions of medical students at public universities in Uganda regarding their learning environment?” By systematically examining students’ views and identifying key influencing factors, this study offers insights into the academic context post-COVID-19, a period marked by significant shifts in medical education due to remote learning and altered clinical rotations. The findings are intended to guide sustainable improvements in medical training in Uganda, offering a framework for effective crisis management and decision-making in future educational disruptions.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted using quantitative methods between December 2021 and February 2022.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Uganda, a landlocked country in Eastern Africa neighbored by Kenya in the East, South Sudan in the North, the Democratic Republic of Congo in the West, Rwanda in the southwest, and Tanzania in the South. As of 2022, Uganda had 55 universities, with 10 medical schools offering bachelor’s degrees in medicine and surgery. Of these, six are public universities: Makerere University, Mbarara University of Science and Technology, Gulu University, Busitema University, Kabale University, and Soroti University. The other four are private universities: Kampala International University, Habib Medical School, St. Augustine International University, and Uganda Christian University.

In Uganda, undergraduate medical education is offered through the 5-year Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery degrees (MBChB). The program is taught using the semester system, two semesters per year. The first two years of the course offer students foundational knowledge and skills to be applied in hospitals during clinical study which occupies years 3 to 5. During the clinical years, the 3rd and 5th year students rotate on the 4 major wards, i.e., pediatrics, internal medicine, surgery, and obstetrics/gynecology, while the 4th year students rotate on the medical specialties (i.e., psychiatry, palliative care, dermatology, community education) and surgical specials (i.e., ophthalmology, anesthesia, ENT, urology and radiology) but also offer family medicine and medical ethics. The study modes are lectures, ward and grand rounds, call duty, simulation, community placement, and tutorials. Students learn essential skills through observation, assisting, and individual practice under supervision as recommended by the General Medical Council [22]. Uganda currently has 10 medical schools, six public and the rest private.

Study population

The focus of this study was undergraduate students doing (MBChB) from the first to fifth year. The study enrolled students from five public universities, i.e., Busitema University(BUS), Gulu University (GUL), Kabale University (KAB), Makerere University (MAK), and Mbarara University of Science and Technology (MUST). The sample size was determined based on a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. The anticipated population proportion was 75%, derived from a previous related study conducted in Uganda, indicating that 75% of respondents positively perceived their learning environment [17]. The total student population across these institutions was 2,279. This yielded a target sample size of 332 participants.

Once the stratified sample was determined, students were selected using convenience sampling at the university level. This non-probability sampling method was chosen because it was the most suitable for the study’s practical constraints and time considerations. The recruitment process involved disseminating a survey link via class WhatsApp groups, a strategy that leveraged existing communication channels to facilitate participation. The first page of the survey included an informed consent form with study objectives, rewards, and potential risks. All participants had to accept their involvement in the study by ticking a checkbox before starting the survey.

Study coordinators collaborated with class leaders to enhance the outreach effort. Class representatives from each year were engaged to promote a balanced representation of students from different academic years. They played a crucial role in the recruitment process by providing regular reminders and encouragement to their peers, thus increasing the likelihood of participation. This approach was designed to maximize response rates across all five academic years and to mitigate potential biases arising from unequal participation among different year groups.

Measures

Participants completed a two-part questionnaire with a demographics section and the Dundee Ready Education Environment Measure (DREEM) tool scores. The demographics part had six variables: sex, age, year of study, campus accommodation, source of tuition funding, and university of study. Our primary outcome was the perception of medical students on their educational environment based on the DREEM tool score.

DREEM

The DREEM is a 50-item questionnaire developed by Roff et al. to measure the educational environment in health professional education programs [23]. The 50 items are divided into five subscales based on the initial psychometric analysis presented by Roff et al. The five subscales are Students’ Perception of Learning (SPL), Students’ Perception of Teachers (SPT), Students’ Academic Self-perceptions (SASP), Students’ Perception of Atmosphere (SPA), and Students’ Social Self-perception (SSP).

This tool has been reported to be appropriate for use within health professional programs, not just medicine, and is not culture or context-specific [24, 25]. Each item is measured using a five-point Likert scale: 0 strongly disagrees, 1 is disagree, 2 is neither agree nor disagree, 3 is agree and 4 is strongly agree. Respondents are presented with a statement and asked to select a response. Items 4, 8, 9, 17, 25, 35, 39, 48, and 50 are negatively worded, and these require recording before calculating the total and subscale scores. The DREEM tool’s Cronbach alpha was 0.863.

Data collection

The study questionnaire with demographic questions and the Dundee Ready Education Measure (DREEM) tool was uploaded to Google Documents, and a link was shared with participants. Employing the convenience sampling method, the Google form was sent via email and WhatsApp messenger to eligible student representatives from each university included in the study.

Data analysis

The data was loaded into R Studio version 2021.09.0 + 351. Summary statistics of the demographic variables were calculated. Mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values were calculated for continuous variables, while frequencies were calculated for discrete variables. The average score for each DREEM sub-scale and the total DREEM score were calculated. The relationship between the total DREEM score and sub-scale scores with characteristics was determined by analyzing variance and theoretical hypothesis testing with a critical value of 0.05. We used the Tukey test to find significant differences between subgroups. This test was chosen for its robustness in handling multiple comparisons, such as across years of study and sources of tuition, while controlling for Type I errors. A global average DREEM score of 100 or more represented a positive perception of the educational environment, while less than 100 represented a negative perception. A summary of the interpretation of the DREEM score can be found in the supplemental material.

Of the respondents, 231(69%) were clinical students, 180 (54%) were government-sponsored, and 143 (43%) were privately sponsored (Table 1).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess Ugandan medical students’ perception of their educational environment after the COVID-19 pandemic. We found a mean total DREEM score of 112/200, indicating a positive overall perception of the learning environment in the different Ugandan medical schools. Analysis of the DREEM sub-scales showed that students perceived their learning to have a positive approach (28.28/48) and that the teaching was moving in the right direction (24.87/44). Students also felt more positive about their academics (19.04/32), their learning atmosphere (25.14/48), and their social life (14.94/28).

This study’s mean total DREEM score is like previous studies done in Uganda and other countries [10, 17–19, 26]. In Uganda, two studies done at Makerere University [17] and Habib Medical School [10] found that medical students generally perceived their learning environment positively. The similarity in results of these studies and our study is probably due to the similarity in medical school curricula and the general setup of medical schools in Uganda. However, this study’s mean DREEM score was lower than scores from studies in South Africa, Ghana, Zambia, and Nigeria [18–21]. The difference in scores could be because our study was done after the COVID-19 lockdowns, which came with numerous changes in medical education, as well as differences in curriculum.

Many Uganda medical schools still follow the traditional model, which has been associated with less favorable perceptions of the learning environment [11, 27]. However, Olufunmilola and colleagues compared two medical schools offering different curricula. They found that the student’s perception of the learning environment was similar, suggesting that other factors beyond the curriculum type contribute to students’ perceptions [19]. Another possible reason for the lower score in this study is the impact of the COVID-19 lockdown, which led to several changes in the learning environment, such as the emergency of e-learning and altered typical student-teacher interactions [28]. Recent studies have identified high levels of burnout and stress among medical students in Uganda, which could further explain the difference in perceptions of the learning environment [29, 30]. Addressing these stressors is essential to creating a more conducive learning environment and improving students’ academic performance. Integrating proactive mental health support and wellness programs, such as counseling services, gyms, meditation sessions, and peer support groups, and enhancing teacher-student interaction can help improve the educational experience.

The mean total DREEM score was significantly different across years of study, with pre-clinical students having lower scores than their clinical counterparts. These findings are like those of other studies [31, 32]. The heavy workload from bio-medical course units coupled with the transition processes from high school to university environment could explain the low scores in the pre-clinical students. It has also been reported that stress levels are higher during the first three years of medical school compared to the last two years [33]. Patil and colleagues also postulated that students in higher years studying preclinical subjects along with clinical postings may be more enthusiastic about their future clinical knowledge, leading to a more positive perception of the learning environment [31]. This highlights the need for targeted interventions, such as structured peer mentorship, to support pre-clinical students, as these foundational years are crucial for their future careers. Poor perception of the learning environment in the early years of medical school can affect academic performance and attainment of clinical skills, which is a centerpiece of quality healthcare delivery.

Interestingly, the mean DREEM and sub-scale scores were similar across genders and universities. This is an important finding as it portrays that the learning environment in Ugandan medical schools does not have discriminative structures across genders and that students’ general perception of their learning environment is standardized across universities in the country. Marginalization of female students has been reported, but these results provide some confidence that this may not the case in Uganda [34]. Our findings offer reassurance that, at least in the context of Ugandan public medical schools, gender is not a significant determinant of the perceived quality of the educational environment.

The perception of students towards their learning atmosphere and social life were the lowest scoring DREEM sub-scale. The most problematic items in the social life were ‘I am rarely bored in this course’ and ‘There is a good support system for students who get stressed’. This is worrying as stress and boredom can negatively impact students’ academic performance [33, 35]. Given the academic intensity of medical school, these findings highlight the need to improve formal support systems available for students in terms of counseling and therapy. Institutions should prioritize addressing these issues by strengthening formal support structures and fostering a more supportive and engaging atmosphere during clinical training and ward rounds. This will contribute to improvement in the learning environment and potentially improve academic achievement and learning outcomes for medical students.

The atmosphere during ward teaching and the balance between enjoyment and stress were highlighted as areas needing improvement. These same areas have been reported as problematic by other studies [36, 37]. This is mostly likely contributed to by the fact that the students perceived teachers to be authoritarian and that teachers over-emphasize factual learning, especially during ward teaching. This calls for training teachers in medical schools to ensure that they create a relaxed learning environment during ward teaching, which can improve student participation and, eventually, learning outcomes.

On a positive note, students perceived their academic self-perception (SASP) and learning (SPL) as the most favorable aspects of their educational environment. The highlights in the students’ academic self-perceptions were that they were confident about passing exams and deemed much of what they learned relevant to a career in healthcare. This is because most of the work done to improve medical schools has focused on the curriculum, with the introduction of competence-based and problem-based curricula, simulation training, and postgraduate students as training assistants.

This study’s strengths lie in its rigorous quantitative design, utilizing the validated DREEM tool for comprehensive assessments and comparing the learning environment across multiple public institutions of higher learning in Uganda. By surveying a sizable and diverse cohort from five medical schools in Uganda, the study benefits from a broad perspective that may reflect the reality for many students within the region. The diversity of the sample strengthens the potential applicability of the findings to other African nations with similar educational and cultural landscapes, offering valuable insights for the development of medical education across the continent.

However, this study is not without its limitations. First and foremost, as the research was conducted post-COVID-19, it does not provide a comparative viewpoint with pre-pandemic conditions, which limits the ability to attribute changes in the learning environment directly to the pandemic’s impact. Additionally, the study’s cross-sectional nature means it captures perceptions at a single point in time and cannot trace the evolution of student views or educational conditions over the course of their studies. The reliance on self-reported data through an online questionnaire could also introduce selection and response biases, potentially excluding those without internet access or those less inclined to participate in online surveys. Using convenience sampling means that the participants were not randomly selected, which may limit the sample’s representativeness and introduce sampling bias. Additionally, stressed or dissatisfied students might have been less likely to participate, underestimating negative perceptions. These biases should be considered when interpreting the results.

Furthermore, while the DREEM tool is a robust instrument for evaluating educational environments, it might not capture all elements pertinent to the Ugandan context, and it does not accommodate qualitative nuances that could offer deeper insights into students’ experiences. It is against this background that we recommend qualitative studies to obtain insights on contextual, and subjective views that students have of their educational environment.

To address these limitations, we recommend future studies employ longitudinal designs e.g. tracer studies among recent graduates to track changes in student perceptions over time.

Conclusions

This study revealed that medical students in Uganda perceived their learning environment to be generally positive. However, it was noted that the students had negative perceptions towards with learning atmosphere and social life. We recommend further investigation of items that have mean scores of < 2 to identify ways of improving the educational environment. These should be areas of focus for faculty and administrators in medical schools to improve medical education in public universities across the country. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies to track changes in student perceptions over time. Qualitative studies to explore challenges in the learning environment would yield valuable insights to enhance medical education in Uganda and beyond.
