Guided tour through the small protein landscape of Methanosarcina mazei using proteomics and biochemical approaches
Authors: Eva Herdering, Liam Cassidy, Philipp T Kaulich, Jürgen Bartel, Sandra Maaß, Katrin Weidenbach, Andreas Tholey, Ruth A Schmitz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall open reading frame (small ORF) encoded proteins fulfil important roles in many cellular processes. In the methanoarchaeon Methanosarcina mazei, numerous small proteins have previously been identified under different nitrogen-availabilities, with only few being subject to functional characterization. Consequently, a detailed expression analysis of small proteins translated under other stress conditions, in conjunction with conservation and sequence-based analyses, may reveal interesting candidates for future downstream analyses. Here, we investigated the small proteome of M. mazei under five growth conditions. By enriching the low molecular weight proteome and combining top-down and bottom-up proteomic analysis, a total of 234 small proteins were validated on protein level, of which 130 were found in top-down proteomics analysis, which were associated with 408 proteoforms. Aiming to unravel functions of the large number of small proteins, we performed sequence-based clustering with emphasis on the presence of characteristic motifs. Thereby, ferredoxin-like small proteins with putative iron–sulphur (Fe–S) cluster binding-sites, as well as possible zinc-binding proteins, both with distinct cysteine motifs, were identified. We further analysed heterologously expressed representatives of the ferredoxin-like and putative zinc-binding small proteins, confirming the zinc-binding capability of two small proteins of the latter group via inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry. Overall, the detailed analysis of the M. mazei small proteome under different growth conditions, using various proteomics approaches, as well as sequence-based analyses and biochemical approaches targeting specific protein candidates, represents a key step in systematically uncovering the functions of small proteins in M. mazei.

Introduction

Small proteins facilitate a diverse range of biological functions across all domains of life and have gained increasing interest. A handful of small proteins have been known for decades, like bacterial ferredoxins consisting of ~60 amino acid residues (aa), that are involved in electron transfers (reviewed in Otaka and Ooi 1987). More recently, other bacterial small proteins have been discovered to fulfil key functions like the recruitment of larger protein complexes to the membrane, the regulation of larger protein degradation, the modulation of membrane-transporter activity, activity regulation of cytoplasmic proteins, and functions as toxins or (transcriptional) regulators (reviewed in Burton et al. 2024; reviewed in Gray et al. 2022). In archaea, a high number of identified small proteins have DNA/RNA-binding properties and mounting evidence shows that other archaeal small proteins play a role in protein-complex formation (reviewed in Weidenbach et al. 2022). Furthermore, physiological functions have been described for multiple C(P)xCG-containing zinc finger small proteins in Haloferax volcanii (Tarasov et al. 2008, Nagel et al. 2019, Üresin et al. 2023).

The methanogenic model organism in this work, Methanosarcina mazei Gö1, has a 4.1 Mb genome with 3371 originally annotated open reading frames (ORFs) of various sizes (Deppenmeier et al. 2002). However, rescreening of the genomic sequence revealed many previously unannotated small RNAs and small ORFs (e.g. Jäger et al. 2009). For a handful of these small ORFs, their roles as regulators under nitrogen limitation stress have been determined (Gutt et al. 2021, Habenicht et al. 2023). Very recently, two additional small proteins, MtrI and MtrR, have been found to be involved in the regulation of the tetrahydrosarcinapterin S-methyltransferase (Mtr)-complex, a central complex in methanogenesis (Habenicht et al. 2025, Reif-Trauttmansdorff et al. 2025).

However, a plethora of small ORFs, including the ones that had been annotated for >20 years, have been patiently awaiting the current rise of technical advances for their validation on protein level. Technologies like ribosome profiling (RiboSeq) or liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-based approaches have been specifically tailored to target the small proteome of M. mazei (Cassidy et al. 2016, 2021a, Tufail et al. 2024). RiboSeq technologies provide evidence for active translation of small ORFs and consequently have allowed for much higher confidence in the prediction of small proteins, resulting in the discovery of proteins like MtrR (Tufail et al. 2024, Habenicht et al. 2025). However, the validation of RiboSeq data at the protein level is still highly important, and it is at this point that LC–MS-analyses specifically targeting the low molecular mass proteome become a strong and efficient validation tool for RiboSeq observations (Tufail et al. 2024). The detection of small proteins at the protein level therefore provides robust evidence for their existence and helps to facilitate the incorporation of their sequence and metadata into repositories.

Thereby, the majority of proteomic analyses utilize bottom-up proteomic (BUP) workflows, which rely on the identification of shorter peptides, often after tryptic digestion. While BUP workflows offer several key advantages for the analysis of proteins (e.g. high degree of sensitivity, well established protocols, and well developed software for analysis), there are known and accepted limitations (e.g. protein inference and bias towards specific protein classes), which are especially problematic for the analysis of small proteins (reviewed in Ahrens et al. 2022, Cassidy et al. 2021b, 2023, Fijalkowski et al. 2022). Furthermore, with the recent focal shift towards proteoforms as the important functional unit (i.e. proteins inclusive of any modifications), it has further diminished the overall strength of exclusive identification of peptides, which provide little or no information towards the proteoforms present within a biological system (Smith and Kelleher 2013, 2018). Top-down proteomic (TDP) methodologies provide a way to qualitatively assess the proteoforms present under a specific set of conditions (Po and Eyers 2023, Kaulich and Tholey 2025). The confident identification of small proteins and their proteoforms via LC–MS poses the perfect basis for further functional characterization.

In this study, a validation of small proteins and respective proteoforms in M. mazei under five different growth conditions (standard growth, high salt stress, challenge with virus, heat stress, and starvation stress) was performed. A variety of conditions allowed for a larger possible range of proteomes to be assessed, beyond the proteome present under standard growth conditions. Further, a comprehensive library of small proteins from M. mazei was generated by utilizing a combination of previously optimized workflows for sample preparation and LC–MS analysis, including both BUP and TDP. Importantly, several small protein proteoforms were characterized, which allowed for a better understanding of the potential functional roles they facilitate. In addition, the identified small proteins were analysed based on their sequence and annotations, in order to find promising candidates for future detailed functional characterization. This led to the identification of two classes of putative cofactor-binding proteins, one being ferredoxin-like iron–sulphur (Fe–S) cluster-binding proteins, and the second one being a group of putative small zinc-binding proteins. Subsequently, we heterologously expressed, purified and further analysed selected small proteins from both groups, in order to verify their annotation and possible function.

Methods

Proteomic workflow

Here, previously optimized methodologies for small protein analysis with both TDP and BUP workflows were used to characterize the low molecular mass proteomes of M. mazei under various stress conditions (Cassidy et al. 2021a, Kaulich et al. 2020, 2021).

Sample preparation for LC–MS

Samples of M. mazei Gö1 were prepared under the various growth conditions in 1 l anaerobic cultures of minimal medium with a gaseous phase consisting of N2 and CO2 (v/v; 80/20) (Deppenmeier et al. 2002, Ehlers et al. 2002), at 37°C (standard growth) or 42°C (heat stress), at increased salt conditions (500 mM NaCl, salt stress), or by adding MetSV lysate (challenge with virus). For the virus challenge, M. mazei cultures were grown to an optical turbidity at 600 nm (T600) of 0.13–0.17 (after ∼25 h), at which time 12 ml of sterile filtrated MetSV (0.2 µm filter) lysed M. mazei culture supernatant was added, the cells were then incubated for a further hour before harvesting. Cells grown in the stationary growth phase were harvested at a T600 of 1 after ∼160 h incubation, all others at T600 0.18–0.37 in the exponential growth phase (cell harvest for standard exponential growth after ∼40 h, high salt after ∼46 h and high temperature stress after ∼72 h). Centrifugation was performed (30 min, 6371 × g at 4°C) and the pellet was resuspended in 1.5 ml of the remaining media before a second centrifugation step (10 min, 16 100 × g at 4°C) was carried out. The cell pellets were frozen in liquid nitrogen (Air Liquide, Paris, France) and stored at –80°C. Duplicate samples were produced for each condition and 50 mg aliquots of the wet-weight material were processed via our previously described methodology (Cassidy et al. 2021a). Briefly, cells were disrupted via freeze/thaw under sonication cycles in the presence of 8 M guanidinium hydrochloride (GndHCl) and cOmplete protease inhibitor (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The samples were then heated to 70°C for 15 min before the GndHCl was diluted to ~0.5 M by adding 3.7 ml of 5% formic acid (FA). The samples were centrifuged (21 100 × g for 20 min at room temperature) to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was then subjected to C18 SPE clean-up, (Pierce C18 100 µl tips, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Briefly, Sep-Pak cartridges were washed with 3 ml of acetonitrile (ACN), followed by conditioning with 6 ml of 5% FA, the sample was loaded, then washed with 3 ml of 5% FA before the small proteins were eluted in 500 µl of 80% ACN. The samples were split for TDP and BUP analysis (10% for BUP, 90% for TDP) and dried down via SpeedVac at 45°C (Concentrator plus, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany).

BU and TD proteomic analysis via LC–MS

For BUP analysis, reduction of the samples was performed by resuspension in 50 µl of 10 mM dithiothreitol and incubation for 45 min at 56°C, subsequently, alkylation was performed by the addition of 50 µl of 100 mM chloroacetamide (final conc. 50 mM) and incubation for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Trypsin was added at a ratio of 1:60 (enzyme:protein) in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer (pH 7.4) to a final volume of 200 µl, and digestion was carried out overnight at 37°C. Following digestion, the peptides were cleaned up via C18 SPE clean-up (Pierce C18 100 µl tips, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per the manufacturer’s instructions, before being dried via vacuum centrifugation at 60°C. Dried samples were resuspended in sample loading buffer (3% ACN, 0.05% TFA) immediately before LC–MS analysis.

BUP analysis of samples was performed via one-dimensional chromatographic separation on a U3000RSLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an Acclaim Pepmap100 column (75 µm × 500 mm) coupled online to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A low pH reverse phase separation was performed (Eluent A: 0.05% FA in MilliQ water, Eluent B: 80% ACN and 0.04% FA in MilliQ water) and peptides resolved across a programmed 215 min run that included a 3 min wash of peptides at 4% B followed by a 180 min linear gradient of increasing B from 4% to 40%, followed by a 9 min wash with 90% B and subsequent equilibration of the column via 15 min at 4% B. A constant flow rate of 300 nl per minute was applied and 3 µl of sample were injected per run, resulting in an estimated load of ~1 µg of digested peptides. The coupled mass spectrometer (Q Exactive Plus) was operated in positive mode with full scan MS scans performed at 70k resolution across a scan range of 350–1600 m/z with a max injection time of 100 ms. Data-dependent MS/MS scans were performed using a top 10 method (resolution = 17 500, NCE 27, isolation window 1.6 m/z, and max injection time 100 ms). Dynamic exclusion was enabled (60 s).

A TDP analysis of the samples was performed in parallel. Samples were resuspended in loading buffer (3% ACN, 0.05% TFA) and subjected to one-dimensional chromatographic separation on a U3000RSLC UHPLC equipped with an Accucore C4 column (75 µm × 150 mm) coupled online to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) fitted with a high field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry (FAIMS) source. A low pH reverse phase separation was performed (Eluent A: 0.05% FA in MilliQ water, Eluent B: 80% ACN and 0.04% FA in MilliQ water) and peptides resolved across a programmed 150 min run that included a 2 min wash of peptides at 4% B followed by a 120 min linear gradient of increasing B (15%–60%), followed by a 13 min wash with 90% B and subsequent equilibration of the column via 15 min at 4% B. A constant flow rate of 300 nl per minute was applied and 1 µl of sample were injected per run, resulting in an estimated load of ~1 µg of intact protein.

The coupled mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Fusion Lumos) was operated in positive mode using a FAIMS multi-CV acquisition method (Kaulich et al. 2022). Briefly, the FAIMS multi-CV method consisted of four experiments in a single acquisition run utilizing differing compensation voltages (−60 V, −50 V, −40 V, and −25 V). For CV values of –60, and –50, full scan MS was performed at resolution of 60 K, across a scan range of 400–1800 m/z, maximum injection time was set to 118 ms and 2 microscans were performed, subsequent-dependent MS/MS scans were performed at a resolution of 50 K, with a maximum injection time of 200 ms, AGC target of 40 000, and 2 microscans. For CV values of –40 and –25, full scan MS was performed at resolution of 50 K, across a scan range of 400–1800 m/z, maximum injection time was set to 246 ms and 4 microscans were performed, subsequent dependent MS/MS scans were performed at a resolution of 60 K, with a maximum injection time of 250 ms, AGC target of 50 000, and 4 microscans. For all CV values, dynamic exclusion was enabled (exclude after n = 2, 60 s), and charge states of 4–50 were allowed. Data-dependent mode was set for 3 s cycle time and CID collision energy was set to 25.

Database searches for bottom-up samples were performed with Proteome Discoverer (v3.1.1.93) using both the SeQuestHT and the Chimerys search nodes, while for top-down datasets Proteome Discoverer (version 3.0.0.757) was used with the ProsightPD 4.2 annotated proteoform (precursor tolerance 2.2 Da, fragment tolerance 10 ppm) and subsequence nodes (precursor tolerance 15 ppm, fragment tolerance 10 ppm), N-terminal variable modifications (acetylation, formylation, and methionine excision) were included, and duplicate searches allowing for the fixed modification of cysteine (Dehydro) were also included. The raw files were deconvolved using the Xtract algorithm within in the high/high cRAWler node. The FASTA database used for both BUP and TDP searches contained the M. mazei Gö1 protein-encoding genes (accessed from UniProt 202406.19) with an appended list of possible sORF encoded proteins as determined by Tufail et al. (2024). Additionally, the Common Laboratory Contaminants Database (cRAP) list with 47 protein accessions was also included.

For bottom-up data, Percolator (v3.0) was used for error calculation with a peptide level false discovery rate restricted to (FDR <0.01) by q-value during the processing workflow for identification obtained through the SequestHT node. Further grouping, validation, and annotation of peptide spectrum matches, peptides, and proteins were performed during the consensus workflow in Proteome Discoverer. Proteins with a protein level FDR <0.01 were classed as confidently identified, furthermore, for small proteins with a protein level FDR <0.05, manual inspection of the spectra was performed and only peptides with at least five sequential b- or y-ions were classified as identified (Slavoff et al. 2013). For top-down data, only proteoforms that were detected with high confidence (proteoform level FDR <1%) were classified as identified.

All processed and raw data files have been uploaded to the ProteomXchange data repository (PXD055745) (Vizcaíno et al. 2014).

Homology searches

Homology searches for the proteins present in only one condition were performed using web-based Basic Local Alignment Search Tools (BLAST) by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) ((Altschul et al. 1990, Camacho et al. 2009), cutoffs ≤70% query coverage, ≤30% identity, and E-value ≤ 1 × 10−5).

Strains and plasmids

For expression of the ferredoxin-like small proteins, the plasmids pRS2223, pRS2224, and pRS2225 were constructed. The respective genes A01950, A01974, and Q8PWG8 were polymerase chain reaction- (PCR-) amplified using primers A01950_NdeI_for (5′TTTCATATGAGCGTAAACATAAACAGATATAAATG3′) and A01950_nostop_NotI_rev (5′TTTGCGGCCGCTTTCATTACCTCGATTGCTCCC3′), A01974_NdeI_for (5′TTTCATATGTCAGAGATGAATGGGTATGTTG3′), and A01974_nostop_NotI_rev (5′TTTGCGGCCGCTTCCATATAAATAATGGATCTGTTGGTAC3′), as well as Q8PWG8_NdeI_for (5′TTTCATATGCCAGCAATAGTTAACGCAG3′) and Q8PWG8_nostop_NotI_rev (5′TTTGCGGCCGCTTCCGTTACTTTAATTGCCTGGTTC3′), respectively. pET21a and the PCR-products were restricted using NdeI and NotI (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, USA), ligated using T4-DNA-ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α (transformation as described in Inoue et al. 1990). Protein expression was carried out using E. coli BL21 (DE3) Δiscr + pRIL.

The plasmids pRS1919 and pRS1921 were constructed for producing untagged A00184 and A01980. The respective genes were PCR-amplified using primers SUMO_A01980_for (5′ATGGCTTTTAATGACAGAAACTTCAGAG3′) and SUMO_A01980_rev (5′TCAGTATCTGCGGTTTTCTCTGG3′), as well as SUMO_A00184_for (5′ATGGCTTTTAATGACAGAAATTCCTTCC3′) and SUMO_A00184_rev (5′TTAATAGCGGTTCTCTCTGGGC3′) and cloned into pETSUMO by using the Champion™ pET SUMO Expression System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. pRS1919 and pRS1921 were then transformed into E. coli DH5α and subsequently into BL21 (DE3) pRIL for protein expression (Table S1; transformation as described in Inoue et al. 1990).

Heterologous expression and purification of small proteins with characteristic cysteine motifs

Putative ferredoxins

A01950-His6, A01974-His6, and Q8PWG8-His6 were attempted to be expressed using E. coli BL21 (DE3) Δiscr (Akhtar and Jones 2008) + pRIL (Stratagene, La Jolla, USA) + pRS2223 (pRS2224, pRS2225, respectively) in Luria Bertani medium (LB; Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany). Expression was induced at T600 0.6 with 100 µM Isopropyl β-d-Thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), incubated at 37°C, 180 rpm for 2 h, and harvested. The pellet was resuspended in 50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, pH 8.0 (chemicals from Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG). After the addition of DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri), cell disruption was performed twice using a French Pressure Cell at 4.135 × 106 N m−2 (Sim-Aminco Spectronic Instruments, Dallas, TX), followed by centrifugation of the cell lysate for 30 min (13 804 × g, 4°C). His-tag-affinity chromatography-purification was performed with a Ni-NTA agarose (Cube Biotech, Monheim, Germany) gravity flow column, the protein was purified by stepwise-elution with 100 and 250 mM imidazole (SERVA, Heidelberg, Deutschland) in phosphate buffer. Elution fractions of protein purifications were analysed on 12% SDS-PAGE gels and spectra were obtained using a Jasco V-550 Spectrophotometer (Pfungstadt, Germany).

Putative zinc-binding proteins

His6-SUMO-A01980 and -A00184 were heterologously expressed using E. coli BL21 (DE3) pRIL + pRS1919 (pRS1921, respectively) in LB or terrific broth (TB, both Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG), TB supplemented with 10 ml trace element solution used for M. mazei media (Hippe et al. 1979). Expression was induced at T600 0.6 with 100 nM IPTG, and after incubation at 37°C, 180 rpm for 2 h, cells were harvested. The pellet was resuspended in 10 mM HEPES, 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.5 (chemicals from Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG) and cell-disruption and His-tag purification were carried out as described above in 10 mM HEPES, 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.5. The purified His6-SUMO-tagged proteins were dialysed against 10 mM HEPES, 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.5, and Ulp1 (SUMO-protease) was added to cleave off the tag. The cleavage was carried out overnight at 4°C and the untagged proteins were separated from tag and protease by an additional passage through the Ni-NTA column. For further purification, the proteins were loaded onto a 5 ml HiTrap™ Heparin HP affinity column (GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA) and eluted with increasing salt concentration. Elution fractions of protein purifications were analyzed on NuPAGE™ 4%–12% mini protein gels and the protein concentrations were determined by Qubit protein (both Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Determination of zinc-binding by inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry

Zinc binding of purified A00184 and A01980 was determined using gel-filtration chromatography (GFC, column: Superose 6 Increase 3.2 × 300, eluent: 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, flow rate: 100 µl min−1, isocratic elution) directly coupled to an Agilent 7500c inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) instrument, equipped with a Scott type spray chamber and a PFA µ-flow nebulizer, to monitor the intensity for several elemental isotopes over an elution period of 100 min. The plasma was operated at a power of 1600 W and all other parameters were optimized daily to obtain stable signals for the elements of a tuning solution, as well as stable background signals for 13C, 23Na, and 39 K when infusing the eluent from the gel-filtration column. Three independently prepared samples of each protein were measured on separate days. Signal intensity of 66 Zn was plotted against retention time, and protein identity was confirmed by tryptic digestion followed by electrospray ionization-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry of eluted fractions corresponding to the zinc elution peak.

Proteomic validation of the zinc-binding proteins by LC–MS/MS

To confirm protein identity, fractions were collected from an independent GFC separation of both samples. Two fractions were collected, representing approximately the first and second halves of the zinc elution peak. The samples were then reduced with tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and digested with trypsin. After digestion, peptides were desalted using ZipTips. LC–MS/MS analysis was performed on an Easy-nLC 1000 chromatography system coupled to a Q Exactive instrument (both Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a 45-min gradient. Generated raw files were searched against a database containing 4165 protein sequences of E. coli BL21 (UniProt, UP000002023, downloaded on 23 May 2025), the two M. mazei small proteins, 117 commonly observed laboratory contaminants, and a reversed version of each entry with MS-Fragger (version 4.0) using the FragPipe interface (version 21.1) (Kong et al. 2017, Teo et al. 2021).

Results

Detection of the small proteome under different growth conditions using various LC–MS-approaches

Across the 10 BU LC–MS runs [standard growth, high salt stress, challenge with virus, heat stress, and starvation stress with two biological replicates each; data available at the ProteomXchange data repository (PXD055745) (Vizcaíno et al. 2014)], 37 326 peptide groups were identified at a FDR <1% that, after applying strict parsimony criteria, mapped to 2067 protein accessions. The methodologies used in this analysis were tailored for the low molecular mass proteome, which was shown to be successful through the combined detection of 223 proteins that are 100 aa residues in length or smaller. By expanding the inclusion criteria (5 sequential b- or y-ions; Slavoff et al. 2013) a further 11 proteins <100 aa residues in length were identified, resulting in a total of 234 detected small proteins (Table S1).

TD assessment of the same samples led to the identification of 1325 proteoforms belonging to 471 protein accession numbers of proteins smaller than 70 kDa [Table S2; data available at the ProteomXchange data repository (PXD055745) (Vizcaíno et al. 2014)]. Of these, 130 belonged to proteins with a canonical sequence <100 aa in length, and for these 130 protein accession numbers, a total of 408 proteoforms were confidently identified (Fig. 1). Assessment of the proteoforms showed that the majority of the detected proteoforms were either unprocessed or possessed only small truncations events, such as N-terminal methionine excisions. However, it was also apparent that for proteoforms identified with a higher number of proteoform spectral matches (PrSM), there was a tendency towards a greater number of alternative processing proteoforms (Fig. 1A). This observation may be directly related to the abundance and relative sensitivity of the experimental setup, whereby processing events giving rise to less frequently observed proteoforms are more likely to be detected. In respect to this observed effect, ribosomal proteins, which were typically identified with a high number of PrSM, were commonly found to be present with multiple proteoforms, for example the small ribosomal protein eS17 (Q8PXL8), which has 21 possible proteoforms detected in the TDP analysis (Fig. 1C, Fig. S1). In addition, we observed a trend across the TDP dataset that the number of proteoforms detected was higher in stress conditions compared to standard growth (Fig. 1B). This observation may be explained due to either an increased number of proteoforms required to adapt to the stress conditions, or conversely, stress-induced protein degradation.

Regarding the distribution of small proteins across the five different growth conditions, it is noteworthy that 80% of the small proteins were present in all tested conditions. Only 47 appeared to be missing in at least one condition, seven of which were exclusively present in only one of the conditions (Fig. 2). Especially under heat stress, three proteins appeared exclusively and are interesting candidates for future analysis in respect to heat stress response (Fig. 2). Homology searches revealed one ferredoxin-like and one DUF2769-domain-containing small protein amongst them. For the remaining small proteins present in only one condition, Q8PUS3 (standard exponential growth) and Q8PTW1 (stationary growth phase) were homologous to the sulphur-transferase complex subunit TusB and a RelE family toxin, respectively. On the other hand, presence within all tested conditions likely also means that these proteins are required under all growth conditions and execute more central functions like e.g. ribosomal proteins that are involved in translation. Accordingly, we took a general look at the types of proteins by examining the functional groups that appeared to be present, potential cofactors, domains and motifs, as well as predicted structures.

Classes of identified small proteins

A first step towards tackling the function of our 234 small proteins was to look at the automatically acquired annotations by UniProt. However, the quality of these annotations was difficult to assess. The UniProt database provides an annotation score (based on the quality of an annotation and on the number of occurrences), as well as a protein existence ranking, ranging from uncertain protein predictions to experimental evidence at protein level (The UniProt Consortium 2025). Thereby, the annotation score is claimed to not be a measure for the accuracy of an annotation, but a low score seems to be associated with a lack of information about the respective protein (The UniProt Consortium 2025). The majority of the small proteins within this work score poorly on both annotation score and protein existence level, demonstrating how little is still known about the small proteome. Although presence in M. mazei is now experimentally proven with our study, within the group of the 234 newly LC–MS-validated small proteins in M. mazei, 139 did not have an assigned function in the UniProt database (not present, uncharacterized, or conserved proteins; Fig. 3A). 95 small proteins in the UniProt database on the other hand were annotated based on homology or certain domains (Fig. 3A). Some of the latter showed high annotation scores and experimental evidence on protein level like the small subunits F and G (both 72 aa) of the Mtr-complex (Gottschalk and Thauer 2001, Reif-Trauttmansdorff et al. 2025). Other annotations were at least inferred from homology, like for example, the homologue of hydrogenase maturation protein HypC (annotation score 1/5, existence inferred from homology, 86 aa), which has been described for Thermococcus kodakaraensis, (Watanabe et al. 2007). One further very prominent class of small proteins with relatively certain annotations were small ribosomal proteins (annotation scores 2–3/5; The UniProt Consortium 2025). As mentioned previously, a large number of proteoforms mapped to small ribosomal proteins. Methanosarcina mazei possesses 69 ribosomal proteins, 15 of which are smaller than 100 aa (The UniProt Consortium 2025). All of these were detected within this study, present under all conditions, arguing for their importance and consequently supporting the annotation as being part of the ribosome (Fig. 3B). For most other small proteins however, the origin of the annotation was unclear, the annotation was vaguely based on structural motifs or a specific annotation was entirely missing. Therefore, biochemical properties, interaction partner(s) and particularly the function of these small proteins still need to be investigated. Consequently, we (i) performed a sequence alignment with the aa-sequences and generated a guide tree using ClustalW in order to identify groups of interesting proteins, and (ii) looked for domains containing at least four cysteines including at least one CxxC motif, with tetrahedrally organized cysteines in the AlphaFold 3 structure prediction (Sievers et al. 2011, Abramson et al. 2024). These are especially interesting, since they make up a large group of small proteins with a remarkable motif that likely promotes a structure. Cysteine motifs of this kind are known to coordinate zinc- or other metal ions, as described e.g. for C(P)xCG-motifs in many small proteins in Haloferax (Üresin et al. 2024), or even are the scaffold for the formation of Fe–S clusters. We identified 51 small proteins with a motif of this type in BUP (33 in TDP), which were clustered in two regions of the guide tree (ferredoxin-like and CxxC-x17-CxxC domain-containing small proteins; Fig. 3B).

The first group of small proteins that raised our attention was the one of small ferredoxin-like proteins (Fig. 3B, orange). Four out of six ferredoxin-like small proteins were clustering in the guide tree, thereby embracing two recently identified and unannotated small proteins A01950 and A01974, hinting towards a similar annotation of these two additional ones (Tufail et al. 2024; Fig. 3B). The predicted structures of all six ferredoxin-like small proteins plus the two putative ones were highly similar, harbouring at least one potential binding pocket for an Fe–S cluster (Abramson et al. 2024; Fig S2). The distances of four tetrahedrally organized cysteines ranged from 5.7 to 6.5 Å (exemplarily for Q8PWG8; Fig. 4A), which is well in line with the binding pockets of the bacterial 2[4Fe–4S] ferredoxin from Gottschalkia acidiurici (formerly Clostridium acidiurici) with cysteine distances ranging from 5.9 to 6.7 Å (Duée et al. 1994, PDB: 1FDN; Fig. 4B). In addition, the overall structure of the six predicted models and the crystal structure from the G. acidiurici ferredoxin appeared very similar, despite not sharing much sequence identity (Duée et al. 1994, PDB: 1FDN; Fig. 4B and D, Fig. S2). Bacterial ferredoxins contain one or two [4Fe–4S] cluster, coordinated by two sets of four cysteines, which is the exact observed pattern for our ferredoxin-like proteins (Otaka and Ooi 1987). Some bacterial ferredoxins are known to contain one complete and one incomplete set of only three cysteines as observed for Q8PZK4, that either lost the Fe–S cluster binding capability or coordinates a [3Fe–3S] cluster (reviewed in Beinert 1990).

As a final example, the guide tree showed a group of eight cysteine-domain-containing small proteins (Fig. 3B, turquoise), one being annotated as RNA-polymerase subunit, and seven not present in the UniProt database, therefore not containing any annotation. An inspection of the actual aa-sequences showed high similarity between the eight proteins (alignment A00184 and A01980; Fig. 5A) and also revealed the conserved zinc-binding-domain CxxC-x17-CxxC. For those highly similar small proteins, AlphaFold 3 structure predictions including the presence of a zinc ion showed that the CxxC-x17-CxxC domain was coordinating the zinc ion with high confidence (iPTM >0.8) (Abramson et al. 2024; Fig. 5B, Fig. S3). In addition, the distances of the four tetrahedrally organized cysteines ranged from 3.5 to 3.8 Å (exemplarily for A01980, iPTM = 0.91; Fig. S3A), an arguably smaller predicted binding pocket than for the [4Fe–4S] cluster and well in line with the small zinc-binding proteins HVO_0758 and HVO_2753, with distances ranging from 3.6 to 4.0 Å, two small Haloferax proteins that were already shown to bind zinc (Kubatova et al. 2020, Üresin et al. 2023).

Heterologous expression, purification, and initial characterization of selected small proteins

In order to further evaluate the presence of a [4Fe–4S] cluster or zinc-binding pocket, representatives of both the ferredoxin-like, as well as the CxxC-x17-CxxC domain-containing small proteins, were heterologously expressed in E. coli, and purified for initial characterization of protein properties. The small proteins Q8PWG8, A01974, and A01950 from the ferredoxin-like cluster (Fig. 3B, orange) were cloned into an E. coli expression vector with a C-terminal hexahistidine-tag (His6-tag), overexpressed and purified via affinity chromatography. Only for Q8PWG8-His6, a clean protein sample was obtained (Fig. 4B). Despite showing a faint brown colour in the elution fractions, the spectrum however did not resemble the spectrum of [4Fe–4S] clusters with a peak at 390 nm, whilst still showing the small shoulder at 310 nm (e.g. Yan et al. 2017; Fig. 4C, indicated by *).

For the small proteins with a CxxC-x17-CxxC domain, A00184 and A01980 were chosen as representatives, heterologously expressed and purified in multiple steps, resulting in clean protein samples (Fig. 5C). The clean untagged proteins were analysed via ICP–MS in order to check their metal content. In at least three independent biological replicates for each small protein, zinc was found to be bound by the purified proteins, supporting the bioinformatic predictions (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

A particular focus of this study was the detection and validation of small proteins shorter than 100 aa in length. To better understand cellular processes in their completeness, it however is not enough to know which small proteins are present, but crucial to look at their proteoforms and also assign functions to the small proteins. Consequently, we analysed the detected small proteins regarding their proteoforms, putative functions based on UniProt annotations, distinct cysteine motifs, and metal-binding capabilities, resulting in a concoction of information that can serve as a basis for selecting attractive candidates for future detailed functional analyses.

Fine-tuning LC–MS-approaches in order to find the majority of small proteins and get more insights into their proteoforms

The M. mazei small proteome has been targeted in the past in numerous LC–MS approaches (Cassidy et al. 2016, 2021a, Kaulich et al. 2020, 2021, Tufail et al. 2024), resulting in a rich toolbox that can be applied to various growth conditions as shown in this study. The presence of most small proteins under all tested conditions highlights the overall importance of the small proteome in cellular processes, as well as underlines the sensitivity of our LC–MS analyses. On the other hand, small proteins are known to fulfil functions under stress conditions (Khitun et al. 2019). In our analysis, 19 small proteins were only detected under stress conditions (i.e. below the proteomic level-of-detection in the standard growth samples). This may indicate a possible role under suboptimal growth conditions, where adaptation is required to counteract or tolerate the stress. One example supporting this hypothesis is Q8PTW1, homologous to a RelE family toxin and only present in stationary growth phase. The RelBE toxin–antitoxin system in E. coli is known to contain the endonuclease RelE, which is involved in stalling translation under unfavourable conditions (Gotfredsen and Gerdes 1998, Neubauer et al. 2009, Griffin et al. 2013). With Q8PTW1 only present in stationary growth phase, it likely fulfils a similar role in M. mazei. Beyond this example, it is likely that the differences between growth conditions are much larger than they appear in our analysis, since changes in protein abundance are not covered by the current dataset and are especially challenging to be determined for small proteins.

Not only could stress-dependent detection allow to form hypotheses about possible functions, but different growth conditions also contributed to the large number of proteoforms identified in this dataset (Fig. 1B). Proteins (including small ones) are subject to numerous possible posttranslational modifications or even are translated from alternative start sites, and consequently a protein can no longer be seen as one entity, but rather a family of different proteoforms (Shortreed et al. 2016, Smith and Kelleher 2018, Fijalkowski et al. 2024). We observed an increase of proteoforms under stress conditions. In general, stress is associated with increased protein degradation (Flick and Kaiser 2012, Njenga et al. 2023). Especially under challenge with virus, a larger number of proteoforms occurred, well in line with a virus-induced degradation or inhibition of host protein translation (e.g. Uzan and Miller 2010). It should be noted that in this study a relatively small number of protein accessions gave rise to a large number of proteoforms with the majority of these protein accessions belonging to ribosomal proteins. This may be indicative of a higher cellular abundance of these protein species, giving rise to a large number of processed ribosomal proteoforms or potentially degradation pathways in action, that are observable due to the relative abundance compared to other proteins. On the other hand, of the 13 small ribosomal proteins identified via TDP, five were observed to carry an acetylation on the N-terminal region (e.g. Fig. 1C). Acetylation of ribosomal proteins has also been reported for bacterial ribosomal proteins in the pathogen Salmonella typhimurium, where 51 ribosomal proteins carried 289 acetylated lysine residues (Ni et al. 2023). Thereby, the acetylation of ribosomal proteins was crucial for the ribosome assembly and translation and might happen in response to extracellular signals (Ni et al. 2023). Furthermore, the acetylation of only one lysine in one ribosomal protein allowed an adaptation to host stress by switching the protein expression to a virulence mode (Shen et al. 2025). In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the loss of N-terminal acetylation of ribosomal proteins resulted in thermally unstable ribosomal proteins (Guzman et al. 2023). Acetylation of ribosomal proteins in M. mazei might also vary in response to different growth conditions and allow an adaptation to a changing environment. While a quantitative assessment of the acetylated proteoforms identified under the various stress conditions was not possible in this analysis, future analyses aiming to determine the relative abundances of such modifications will be invaluable in expanding our knowledge of stress response and environmental adaptation.

Sequence-based analysis and annotations

In respect of 234 detected small proteins, it is tempting to refer to annotations within UniProt as a first source of information on their function (The UniProt Consortium 2025). Whilst likely being correct for conserved and well-described proteins like Mtr-subunits or ribosomal proteins, the annotation of less well-studied protein groups is questionable. Luckily, searches for motifs and AlphaFold 3 structural models nowadays allow a characterization of protein properties in silico (Abramson et al. 2024). Especially CxxC-motifs forming zinc ion or Fe–S cluster binding pockets have been shown to be especially well predicted with AlphaFold 2 (Wehrspan et al. 2022). Intriguingly, proteins that were annotated as ferredoxin-like (Fe–S cluster-binding) based on their sequence, also showed a larger cysteine-coordinated binding pocket in the AlphaFold 3 model when compared to putative zinc-binding proteins (Abramson et al. 2024, Üresin et al. 2023, 2024). Furthermore, the predicted structures of the ferredoxin-like proteins highly resembled the 2[4Fe–4S] ferredoxin crystal structure from G. acidiurici (Duée et al. 1994, PDB: 1FDN). Based on the cysteine spacing signature, the ferredoxins observed in this study belong to multiple subtypes of 2[4Fe–4S] cluster-carrying ferredoxins, Q8PZK4 to a 7Fe–8S subtype (reviewed in Syed 2024). Despite we did not observe the Fe–S cluster-characteristic brown coloration or absorption spectrum from our ferredoxins, expressed and purified from an E. coli background, the strong similarity of the predicted structures with a bacterial 2[4Fe–4S] cluster ferredoxin crystal structure still argues for a ferredoxin-like function and coordination of an Fe–S cluster in M. mazei. With M. mazei being an anaerobic organism, especially for oxygen-sensitive Fe–S clusters, an anaerobic purification might be required. In general, ferredoxins in pro- and eukaryotes fulfill functions as electron-carriers in CO2-fixation, photosynthesis, hydrogen production, nitrogen fixation, sulphur metabolism, Fe–S cluster biosynthesis, structural functions, or lipid metabolism—in short, in various types of important biological pathways (reviewed in Syed 2024). In respect of our ferredoxin-like proteins, they probably have different target sites and function as electron donors for larger protein complexes.

On the other hand, the CxxC-x17-CxxC domain in two representatives of eight similar small proteins, purified from an E. coli background, was shown to bind zinc using ICP–MS analysis. This group of small proteins has been identified as a remarkable protein group in the past, since candidates from this group were always identified with highest confidence in various different analyses (reviewed in Cassidy et al. 2023). TDP analyses revealed dehydro-cysteines, which were earlier thought to form disulfide-bonds (Cassidy et al. 2023). The results from ICP–MS instead show the zinc-binding capability, also supported by the CxxC-x17-CxxC domain being a candidate zinc-finger domain (Blum et al. 2025). Within the group of eight CxxC-x17-CxxC domain-containing small proteins, only one was present in the UniProt database and annotated as DNA-directed RNA-polymerase. The same cysteine motif was further found in multiple eukaryotic proteins involved in the transcription of genes, such as in the transcription factors TFIIB and Brf belonging to the TFIIB family proteins (Hahn and Roberts 2000). In addition to that, TFIIB family proteins were found to contain the conserved coordination sites, but did not necessarily share aa sequence similarities (Hahn and Roberts 2000). Consequently, a hypothetical function in transcription could be a first starting point for further analysis on the eight CxxC-x17-CxxC domain-containing small proteins.

Conclusion

This basic analysis of the sequences, as well as the new annotations of a high number of small proteins detected on protein level, did not only show possible metal coordination sites, but especially revealed possible small protein functions in key areas like transcription, translation and energy metabolism. Although we here only named a fraction of the small proteins found by LC–MS/MS, the initial analysis already resulted in numerous attractive candidates for further downstream functional characterization.
