Emergence and genomic characterization of Listeria monocytogenes causing human listeriosis in Pakistan, 2017–2023
Authors: Imran Ahmed, Javaria Ashraf, Hassan Ghayas, Najia Ghanchi, Kauser Jabeen, Joveria Farooqi, Sadia Shakoor, Mehreen Arshad, Syed Faisal Mahmood, Zahra Hasan, Afia Zafar, Noureen Saeed, Sadaf Zaka, Egon A. Ozer, Rumina Hasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman listeriosis, caused by Listeria monocytogenes, is a serious food borne illness. Information from Pakistan on human listeriosis is sparse. This study reports the emergence and genomic characteristics of human L. monocytogenes cases from Pakistan between 2017 and 2023. L. monocytogenes cases were identified from our laboratory database in specimens submitted for culture and a commercially available multiplex PCR assay for cerebrospinal fluid from 2017 to 2023. Whole genome sequencing was performed on randomly selected isolates collected during 2022–2023. Frequency and geographic location of cases were assessed. Bioinformatics analyses included determination of traditional 7-gene MLST (multilocus sequence type), in-silico serogroup determination, phylogenetic relationships and core genome MLST (cgMLST). ResultsL. monocytogenes isolation from all clinical specimens increased from zero (before 2017) to 142 between 2017 and 2023. Majority of isolates were from blood stream infections (n = 90) followed by central nervous system infections (n = 47). Phylogenetic analysis divided sequenced isolates (n = 24) into three clades. The largest clade included 22 isolates (clonal complex-1, Serogroup 4b and Lineage-I) and contained sequence types ST1 (59%) and ST308 (41%). Core genome multilocus sequence typing indicated small genetic distances between some isolates within respective STs (ST1 and ST308).ConclusionThis study highlights human listeriosis as an emerging infection in Pakistan which should be considered when choosing empiric therapy for compatible syndromes. cgMLST data suggests possible common source(s) and emphasizes the need for identification of source(s) and robust public health measures to prevent and control transmission.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05236-9.

Introduction

Listeriosis, caused by Listeria monocytogenes, is an uncommon but serious foodborne disease [1]. It mainly affects pregnant women, neonates, the elderly and immunocompromised individuals; however, infections can occur in immunocompetent individuals [2]. Listeriosis in humans may involve different organ systems and thus presents with various syndromes ranging from self-limited gastroenteritis to invasive disease including bloodstream infection, materno-fetal infections, meningoencephalitis, and brain abscesses [2]. L. monocytogenes is also known to cause disease in farm animals including cattle, sheep and goats, and these animals are believed to be reservoirs of L. monocytogenes [3]. L. monocytogenes is well adapted to tolerate various environmental stresses, making it capable of surviving in harsh conditions including food processing environments and entering human food chains [4]. Acquisition by humans occurs mainly through ingestion of raw, chilled or ready-to-eat meat, dairy products, and other contaminated food items [4]. The optimal temperature for the growth of L. monocytogenes is 37 °C, however, the organism is also capable of growing at refrigerator temperature range i.e. 2–8 °C [5] and hence may reach its infectious dose in refrigerated foods including dairy products and processed or ready-to-eat food items which are not further cooked. Increasing consumption of ready-to-eat food in developing countries [6] may have led to an increasing number of Listeriosis cases.

Both sporadic infections and outbreaks can be caused by L. monocytogenes. Many countries include L. monocytogenes in their public health surveillance system [7, 8]. However, given the spectrum of presentations which ranges from mild gastroenteritis to invasive bloodstream or central nervous system infections [2], the reported figures may not reflect all cases. Human listeriosis is not a notifiable disease in Pakistan and there is neither an active surveillance system nor there is any targeted research for this disease in the country. This is one of the reasons that little is known about the incidence and characteristics of human listeriosis from Pakistan.

L. monocytogenes owes its pathogenicity to a host of virulence factors. Virulence genes inlA and inlB facilitate invasion into host cells, whereas the products of hly, plcA and plcB genes help it escape from the phagocytic vacuole in the host cell’s cytoplasm [9]. Once outside the phagocytic vacuole, the product of the hpt gene, an organophosphate transporter, transports host cell derived glucose metabolites inside the bacterial cell, promoting rapid multiplication of the pathogen [9]. Finally, products of actA and inlC genes help propel bacteria from one host cell to another through actin-based motility [9]. Majority of these virulence genes are clustered within the Listeria pathogenicity islands (LIPI) [10].

Based on seven gene multilocus sequence typing (MLST), L. monocytogenes isolates are divided into 4 phylogenetic lineages (I through IV) and hundreds of ‘multilocus sequence types’ or simply ‘sequence types’ (MLST or ST) which can be grouped into one of several clonal complexes (CC) [9]. Discrimination among isolates using traditional 7-gene MLST is limited. Core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST), based on comparison of more than 1700 L. monocytogenes genes, provides good discriminatory power and enables clustering of outbreak isolates [11, 12].

L. monocytogenes contaminated foods sold in local markets in Pakistan has been reported in the literature [13–16]. Despite this, human listeriosis cases have rarely been reported from Pakistan. The first such report was a case of neurolisteriosis in 1993 [17]. Six cases of human listeriosis with an equal number of neurolisteriosis and bacteremia were reported in 2022 [18]. A systematic investigation of the incidence of clinical listeriosis in Pakistan has not been undertaken.

In recent years there has been an increase in the isolation of L. monocytogenes from clinical specimens received at our laboratory at the Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH) in Karachi, Pakistan. In this study, we report the emergence of human listeriosis, its antimicrobial susceptibilities, and use whole genome sequencing (WGS) to describe the local and global genomic context of L. monocytogenes isolated from clinical specimens submitted to the Clinical Laboratories at AKUH between 2017 and 2023.

Materials and methods

Isolate selection, identification, and susceptibility testing methods

As part of a tertiary care university hospital located in Karachi, Pakistan, the AKUH Clinical Laboratory receives specimens from across the country through a network of more than 300 specimen collection centers.

The Microbiology laboratory database (2007 to 2023) was queried for L. monocytogenes reported in specimens (blood, CSF, sterile body fluids and placenta) submitted for culture, and in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) submitted for commercially available multiplex PCR (Filmarray meningitis/encephalitis panel, BioMérieux, France). A case was defined as the detection of L. monocytogenes either by culture or multiplex PCR. If both the culture and multiplex PCR were positive for L. monocytogenes in a patient, it was counted as one PCR positive case.

Blood culture bottles (BacT/ALERT, Biomeriux, France) when flagged positive and were found to contain Gram positive bacilli upon Gram staining of blood culture broth were sub-cultured on chocolate and sheep blood agar plates (Oxoid, UK). Sterile body fluids including CSF were cultured on chocolate, sheep blood and MacConkey agar plates (Oxoid, UK). Enrichment of CSF specimens was achieved by inoculating the specimen in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (Oxoid, UK). Chocolate and sheep blood agar plates were incubated at 35–37℃ with 5% CO2. Bacterial colony morphology on culture media, Gram stain, catalase activity, motility, CAMP test [19] and API Coryne (BioMérieux, France) were used for the identification of L. monocytogenes. Methods of culture remained the same throughout i.e. 2007 to 2023. The multiplex PCR in CSF (BioMérieux, France) was introduced in our laboratory in 2017 and was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method. Briefly, a bacterial suspension of 0.5 McFarland density was lawned on Muller-Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK) supplemented with 5% sheep blood. Antimicrobial disks of ampicillin 2 µg, meropenem 10 µg and trimethoprim/sulphamethoxazole 1.25/23.75 µg (Oxoid, UK) were applied. The plates were incubated at 35–37℃ in 5% CO2 for 18 ± 2 h. The diameter of the zone of inhibition was measured and interpreted using breakpoints provided by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) [20].

Isolates were saved in glycerol-peptone broth (GPB) at -80℃ and revived on sheep blood agar plates when required. GPB was prepared by making a 15% solution of glycerol (Merck, Germany) in 1% peptone water (Merck, Germany) followed by autoclaving at 121℃ for 15 min. The denominator for calculating frequency of human listeriosis cases was defined as the tests received over a specified period of time, i.e., sum of number of cultures and multiplex PCR test requests received (hereafter written as “test requests” or “cultures and multiplex PCR combined”) in our laboratory. The specimen types included in cultures were blood, CSF, sterile body fluids and placenta; whereas specimen type for multiplex PCR test was only CSF as it was the only specimen type approved by the manufacturer. The frequency of listeriosis cases per 100,000 test requests was calculated by multiplying the proportion of listeriosis cases in a year (number of listeriosis cases in a year/tests requests in that year) by 100,000.

Whole genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

A subset of randomly selected isolates collected between 2022 and 2023 (n = 24) was sequenced. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen). Briefly the bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 7500 rpm for 10 min (Universal 320R, angle rotor with 6 places, Hettich, Germany), supernatant was removed, the bacterial pellet was resuspended in 180 µl of 20 mg/ml lysozyme solution mix and incubated at 37℃ for 30 min prior to DNA extraction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted DNA was stored at -20℃ until used to prepare whole genome sequencing libraries.

The quality of extracted genomic DNA was assessed with NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and DNA was quantified on a Qubit fluorometer using the high sensitivity dsDNA Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Genomic DNA libraries were prepared with the Nextera XT DNA library preparation kit (Illumina, USA). Libraries were normalized, pooled and sequenced on the Illumina MiniSeq platform.

The quality of the raw sequencing reads was determined using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and multiQC [21]. Sequencing adapters and poor-quality reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (version 0.39) [22]. De novo sequence assembly was performed using shovill (https://github.com/tseemann/shovill) (version 1.1.0) toolkit and assemblies were annotated using Prokka (version 1.14.5) [23]. Multilocus sequence types, lineages, and clonal complexes were determined from assembled genome sequences using Institut Pasteur’s Listeria locus sequence query (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr/cgi-bin/bigsdb/bigsdb.pl? db=pubmlst_listeria_seqdef&page=sequenceQuery). In-silico L. monocytogenes serogroup typing was predicted using the LisSero tool (version 0.1) (https://github.com/MDU-PHL/LisSero). Virulence genes were identified from assemblies using the abricate tool (version 1.0.0) (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) and the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [24]. Supplementary material provides complete metadata for the global isolates as well as sequenced isolates from Pakistan, including country of origin, year of isolation, host, MLST sequence type (ST), lineage, serotype, and clonal complex (CC) grouping.

Raw sequencing data for phylogenetic analysis were retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) and the European Nucleotide Archive (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/). The dataset comprised isolates collected over a 20-year period (2003–2023), representing diverse geographical regions (23 countries) and multiple host sources to capture the global distribution and transmission dynamics of L. monocytogenes.

A curated subset of 52 genomes was selected to provide a representative yet manageable dataset. Paired-end sequencing reads corresponding to these 52 isolates were randomly chosen and downloaded via the ENA browser (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/) (Supplementary Tables 2 and Table 3).

Whole-genome alignment was constructed based on single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) identified using Snippy (version 4.6.0) against a serogroup 4b reference genome from India (strain CIIMS-PH-1; accession number CP023321) [25]. Regions affected by recombination were detected and removed using Gubbins (version 3.4), resulting in a filtered core genome alignment.

A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was then inferred from the recombination-filtered alignment using IQ-TREE [26] under the generalized time-reversible (GTR) substitution model, with branch support assessed through 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

Countries included in the comparative analysis were selected using the L. monocytogenes global tracking platform, LmTraceMap, which identifies closely related isolates based on genomic similarity and associated geographic metadata.

Core genome MLST (cgMLST) alleles were determined from assembled genome sequences using ChewBBACA (v.3.2.2) against the cgMLST schema obtained from the BIGSdb-Lm database (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr/listeria) consisting of 1748 loci. A minimum spanning tree was generated from the cgMLST results with ReporTree (v.2.2.0) using the GrapTree clustering analysis method. Two isolates were called “closely related” or “matching isolates” if the two isolates differed by up to 10 alleles [11].

Data availability

The whole genome sequence reads of the 24 isolates in this study have been deposited in the NCBI sequence read archive (SRA) database under BioProject accession number PRJNA1200304.

Results

Frequency of listeriosis cases

During the study period, 2017–2023, a total of 142 L. monocytogenes cases were identified by culture or multiplex PCR. Before 2017, our laboratory data from 2007 to 2016, did not show any human listeriosis cases. The first case of L. monocytogenes was identified in 2017 and the number gradually increased with a maximum of 40 cases (9 cases by Multiplex PCR and 31 cases by culture alone) in 2022 (Fig. 1). The rate of L. monocytogenes case detection increased from 2.6 cases per 100,000 test requests (culture and multiplex PCR combined) in 2017 to 28.9 cases per 100,000 test requests (culture and multiplex PCR combined) in 2023. Of the 142 cases, twenty-nine were neonates, 31 were females between the age group of 15–45 years (child-bearing age group), 37 patients were over 60 years of age, and the rest were from other age groups (Fig. 2A).

Blood (90 /142 cases) was the most common specimen that yielded L. monocytogenes followed by cerebrospinal fluid (47/142), placenta (2/142) and one isolate each from aqueous humor fluid, ascitic fluid and brain abscess. Thirty-four L. monocytogenes cases were detected by multiplex PCR; of these 34 neurolisteriosis cases, 22 corresponding CSF cultures remained negative, while CSF culture of 12 cases yielded L. monocytogenes. From the remaining 22/34 multiplex PCR positive but CSF culture negative cases, L. monocytogenes growth was obtained in 7 cases from blood cultures (Fig. 2B). Antibiotic susceptibility results were available for 127 clinical isolates grown in culture from various specimens (Fig. 2B). Ampicillin, meropenem and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole susceptibility rates were 97.6%, 100% and 82.5% respectively. Although most cases in this study were detected from the southern areas of Pakistan, L. monocytogenes cases were identified from across several areas of the country. The highest number of listeriosis cases were from Sindh province and from Sindh the highest listeriosis cases were from Karachi, the most populated city of Pakistan where AKUH is located and most of the specimens originated from this city. Punjab province with 18 listeriosis cases followed Sindh province, reported over the study period.

A maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis based on the whole genome alignment of L. monocytogenes genomes from 23 countries (supplementary material, metadata) and the study isolates showed the isolates from Pakistan belonged to three distinct clades (Fig. 4). Twenty-two genomes belonged to CC1, serogroup 4b and lineage-I. The sequences from Pakistan in this clade were more closely related to isolates from Belgium, India, the USA, Sweden, the UK, and Australia that had been cultured largely from human clinical specimens.

The second clade contained Pakistan isolate LM08 which belonged to ST6 and was closely related to global isolates from Lithuania, USA (Branch length = 0.000001) and Canada (Branch length = 0.000001). LmTraceMap also identified LM08 as being closely related to a L. monocytogenes LiDS0177 isolate (SRR3108919) from Canada, which was cultured from a dairy source. The phylogenetic analysis also confirmed these isolates’ genetic relatedness. Isolate LM19 was found in the third clade which was a separate clade from the other Pakistan isolates and belonged to serogroup 1/2a and Lineage-II. The isolate was most closely related to an isolate originating from Finland from a human clinical specimen as determined by both the phylogenetic analysis and LmTraceMap.

Core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) analysis

The 24 isolates from Pakistan were analysed with cgMLST and it grouped the isolates into two distinct clusters which were congruent with their sequence type (STs) (Fig. 5). However, two isolates, i.e., LM08 and LM19 did not belong to either cluster and were genetically distant, differing by 1123 and 1728 alleles, respectively. Isolates LM16, LM13, LM11, LM21, and LM24 comprised one sub-cluster and LM06 and LM09 formed another sub-cluster of closely related isolates corresponding to CC1 / ST1. The two isolates, LM16 and LM13, were indistinguishable by cgMLST analysis. Within ST308 cluster, two sub-clusters of closely related isolates could be identified, one formed by LM04 and LM07 and the other by LM02 and LM10, while the remaining ST308 isolates were more distantly related to LM07. Overall, the clustering pattern demonstrates strong concordance between cgMLST profiles and MLST sequence types, with the exception of LM08 and LM19, which appear as highly divergent from the nearest ST1 isolates.

Discussion

Human listeriosis has been a rare occurrence in Pakistan. This study establishes that this disease is an emerging infection in this country. cgMLST analysis revealed closely related isolates, possibly indicating common source(s) of transmission, which requires public health measures to control and prevent cases of human listeriosis. The study also provides directions for future research for linking the sources of transmission with the human cases which will supplement public health measures in controlling the disease.

Foods and soil contaminated with L. monocytogenes have been documented from Pakistan [13–16], however, to date implicated foods have not been linked with human infections. The first case of human listeriosis was reported in 1993 [17], however, listeriosis infection has rarely been reported in Pakistan. No cases of L. monocytogenes were identified from 2007 to 2016 in our laboratory having a large countrywide sampling network. The first two cases were identified in 2017 with a gradual increase, reaching 34 cases in 2023. The cases were not geographically restricted but rather occurred throughout the country. The highest number of listeriosis cases (Fig. 3) was noted from Karachi city, the most populated city of Pakistan where AKUH is located and therefore, a large number of samples were received from this city. The proportion of listeriosis cases per 100,000 test requests (culture and multiplex PCR combined) was high from Punjab and Khyber Pukhtunkhwa (KPK) provinces which have more farm-based economies compared to other areas of Pakistan (Fig. 3). Apparently, Azad Jammu & Kashmir (AJK) and Gilgit-Baltistan (GB) regions seem to have the highest proportion of listeriosis, it is likely because of the small numbers of tests performed from these regions.

In our laboratory, the first two listeriosis cases were identified in 2017 (one case by culture alone and the other case through multiplex PCR), the same year when multiplex PCR in CSF specimen was introduced in our laboratory which increased our capacity to detect this disease. Culture techniques remained the same throughout as far back as the data is reported in this study. It is important to note that listeriosis cases were also detected by culture alone independently of multiplex PCR (Fig. 2B). The increase in the number of cases over the study period could be attributed to increased testing with regards to more specimens being sent and/or introduction of multiplex PCR. We assume that both the factors are responsible, however, it is difficult to say which one played the dominant role.

It is noted in Fig. 2B that in the culture positive arm, 13 L. monocytogenes were detected by CSF culture. Although multiplex PCR was available, it was not requested for these 13 cases. This is due to the fact that the majority of healthcare payments in Pakistan are by out-of-pocket expenditure. Therefore, investigations requested by the treating physician depend on patients’ financial accessibility.

The L. monocytogenes serotypes identified in this study were 4b and 1/2a, which, in addition to serotype 1/2b, are among the most common serotypes causing human listeriosis worldwide. Most of the sequenced isolates in this study (91.67%) belonged to lineage-I, serogroup 4b and CC1. Isolates belonging to CC1 are hypervirulent, associated with dairy products and have been shown to cause neurolisteriosis and maternofetal infections, often in patients with few or no debilitating conditions [27]. This is in contrast to lineage-2 isolates, (for example CC9 or CC121) which are mostly found in food processing environments or meat products, mainly cause bacteremia in immunocompromised patients and are considered hypovirulent [27]. L. monocytogenes virulence factors are clustered within Listeria pathogenicity islands (LIPI) [10]. All isolates in this study were found to carry LIPI-1 and LIPI-2 and all isolates but one (LM19, lineage-II, ST18, CC18) contained LIPI-3. LIPI-3 has been shown to be present in majority of lineage-I isolates but is typically absent in lineage-II isolates [28]. LM19 belonged to lineage-II which explains the absence of LIPI-3 in this isolate. LIPI-1 and LIPI-3 have been previously described to contain genes related to the infectious life cycle [10]. LIPI-2 is another pathogenicity island specific for Listeria ivanovii, however, it has been described to be found in L. monocytogenes isolates causing outbreaks in China [29].

Contemporary common source outbreak investigations often capitalize on whole genome sequencing to ascertain if the isolates are closely related to each other, so the efforts to establish an epidemiological link could be pursued [30]. cgMLST provides much more resolution than the traditional 7-gene MLST since it compares nucleotide sequences of a large number of genes between isolates and thus helps identify closely related isolates based on the number of allelic mismatches [11, 12]. Our study isolates showed distinct cgMLST clustering which may suggest common sources of exposure; for example, among 13 isolates of ST1, two sub-clusters with a few closely related isolates could be identified (Fig. 5). The clinical specimens of these isolates originated from the cities of Karachi and Hyderabad and clustered with isolate number LM16. Karachi and Hyderabad are separated by only about 150 km and daily commuting between the two cities is common. Furthermore, a pair of isolates within ST1 were found to be indistinguishable which were isolated from a mother-infant dyad, reflecting vertical transmission. Since we could only perform sequencing of a subset of isolates, it is hypothesized that more matching isolates may have been found if all the isolated had been sequenced. Moreover, this was a laboratory-based study and therefore not able to inform population level data that could capture more common exposure source related listeriosis cases should there have been such sources of exposures. Considering these two facts, i.e., sequencing a limited number of isolates and laboratory-based study, finding closely related isolates in a handful of isolates is significant. However, lack of epidemiological data precludes us from calling these isolates as part of an outbreak.

Human listeriosis has been present since at least 1993 in Pakistan as documented previously [17], but the reasons for an increase in cases in recent years have not been elucidated. We hypothesize that this may be related to a changing lifestyle and dietary habits of the Pakistani population. The ready-to-eat food industry has grown in Pakistan [31] and people have started to consume more dairy products, especially cheeses. In Pakistan, cheese consumption increased by an estimated 3% per annum from 2020 to 2024 and is expected to increase at a similar rate through to 2030 [32]. Cottage industries in Pakistan, which are largely unregulated, produce local cheese. We hypothesize that risk mitigation practices associated with prevention of contamination of these products are limited. Storage of cold items is an issue particularly given the frequency of electricity outages across the country [33]. Challenges in maintaining domestic and commercial refrigerator temperatures within food-safe ranges can provide an environment for bacteria to multiply and reach levels capable of causing infection. Moreover, given the increasing cost of food items in the country, adulteration of food items and sale of expired food items in markets are other serious risks to the health of people consuming such food items [34, 35] and may be contributing to the emergence of human listeriosis in Pakistan. Furthermore, the country lacks any surveillance for human listeriosis which may explain the presence of listeriosis being picked up only when it crossed a certain unknown threshold in the community. The reasons for missing listeriosis cases might include poorly investigated patients, mortality before patients even present to a hospital with facilities for laboratory investigations.

This was a retrospective laboratory-based study; we were unable to link L. monocytogenes isolates with a source of exposure, however, cgMLST analysis revealed that a few of the sequenced isolates were closely related. Another limitation of this study is the whole genome sequencing of only a subset of isolates and non-inclusion of food or environmental isolates due to resource constraints. Furthermore, resources also precluded serotyping of the isolates covered in this study using L. monocytogenes specific antisera or targeted PCR.

This study provides a foundation for further investigation of the causes underlying emergence of human listeriosis in Pakistan. Population based surveillance for human listeriosis combined with environmental/food item sampling for the detection of L. monocytogenes is required to assess the prevalence of this emerging infection and its likely sources. This would inform efforts to control and prevent human listeriosis in this region.

Conclusions

Our study provides evidence of emergence of human listeriosis in Pakistan. Genetic analysis shows close relationship between the isolates suggesting possible common source(s) of transmission which provides avenues for surveillance, source identification, prevention and control of transmission. Further research is required to link L. monocytogenes isolated from food/environment with those from clinical specimens.
