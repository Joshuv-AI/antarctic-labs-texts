Biochar and γ-Aminobutyric acid partnership improves rice productivity and mitigates Sb + Pb toxicity in co-contaminated soil
Authors: Muhammad Aamer, Jiaen Zhang, Masood Iqbal Awan, Hui Wei, Farhan Nabi, Wenyun Huang, Ziqiang Liu, Xin Su, Shijie Liu, Rehab O Elnour, Mohamed Hashem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimony (Sb) and lead (Pb) toxicities are serious threats to crop productivity, living organisms, and ecosystem sustainability. Thus, it is essential to find ways to reduce their entry into the environment to ensure safe and sustainable food production. Recently, biochar (BC) and signaling molecules have shown appreciable results in mitigating Sb and Pb toxicity. Thus, an attempt was made to explore the role of BC and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) in mitigating Sb + Pb toxicity by applying the following treatments; control, Sb stress (600 mg kg− 1) + Pb stress (600 mg kg− 1), Sb + Pb + BC (2.5%), Sb + Pb + GABA (1 mM) and Sb + Pb + BC (2.5%) + GABA (1 mM).ResultsAntimony and Pb significantly decreased the growth and productivity by increasing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), malondialdehyde (MDA), electrolyte leakage (EL), Sb + Pb accumulation in plant organs, and decreasing photosynthetic pigments, leaf water contents, and nutrient availability. However, co-applied BC + GABA enhanced rice yield by increasing chlorophyll synthesis (42–53%), leaf water contents (23%), antioxidant performance, nutrients uptake, and decreasing H2O2 (61%), EL (90%), MDA (64%), soil Sb (155%), and Pb (127%) availability.ConclusionTherefore, combining BC with GABA can mitigate the antimony and lead toxicity by increasing antioxidant activities, osmolyte synthesis, and decreasing their availability. The synergistic application of BC, and GABA presents an effective strategy to enhance sustainable rice production in multi-metal contaminated soils.Keywords: Antioxidants, Biochar, Co-contamination, Rice, Speciation

Introduction

Heavy metals (HM) pollution is a major global issue and a serious concern for agricultural productivity and food safety [1, 2]. Rapid industrialization, sewage sludge, and extensive use of fertilizers and chemicals are the major factors of increased HM pollution [3]. Crops cultivated on contaminated soils are a primary way of HMs/metalloids entry into the food chain [4]. Therefore, appropriate measures must be taken to prevent their entry into the human food chain [5]. Antimony (Sb) is a widely used metalloid in different industries, including cementing, flame retardants, lead acid batteries, and the polyester industries [6]. Its concentration is constantly increasing in soils; for instance, the concentration of Sb has reached 1584 mg kg−1 in soils near the Xikuangshan Mine, Hunan, China, which is greater than the maximum allowable limit of 36 mg kg−1 [7]. It inhibits root growth, chlorophyll synthesis, nutrient homeostasis, and intensified senescence [8], promotes excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and results in plant death [9]. Lead (Pb) is a very toxic and non-degradable HM, and it has been ranked as the 2nd most toxic metal after arsenic [10]. Lead has high toxicity and lower mobility; however, once plants take it up, it causes overproduction of ROS, which causes oxidation of cellular structures, decreases chlorophyll synthesis, photosynthetic efficiency, nutrient and water uptake, leading to poor growth [11]. Lead accumulation in crops is a major pathway of its entry into humans, which causes cancer and heart and liver diseases [12]. Consequently, it is necessary to find ways to reduce Pb entry into the soil for safer crop production for humans.

Biochar (BC) has a porous structure with a larger surface area and functional groups, which cause immobilization of metals/metalloids [13, 14]. Biochar application decreases Sb mobilization by forming complexes among the Sb and humic acid of BC, thereby reducing Sb availability to plants [15]. Biochar also effectively reduces the concentration of bioavailable Pb [16], limiting its availability to plants [17], thereby improving plant growth [18]. It also regulates antioxidant activities, nutrient and water uptake, chlorophyll synthesis, photosynthetic efficiency, and plant functioning, which facilitates plant growth in polluted soils [17]. Nevertheless, the effects of BC to remediate contaminated soils could be limited. Thus, the use of diverse organic amendments like organic amendments, nutrients, hormones, and osmolytes is necessary to get better results.

Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) has emerged as an essential signaling molecule that plays a pivotal role in plant growth and stress tolerance [19–21]. GABA application reduces metals absorption, ROS production, electrolyte leakage, and boosts chlorophyll, protein, proline synthesis, photosynthetic efficiency, and antioxidant activities [22, 23]. Moreover, GABA also protects the photosynthetic apparatus and up-regulates GABA shunt genes, thereby protecting plants from the toxic impacts of HMs [20].

Rice is a major cereal crop cultivated globally, providing food to more than 50% of the world’s population. However, rice productivity is adversely affected by toxic metals such as Sb and Pb [24, 25]. Thus, it is mandatory to mitigate the adversities of both these toxic metals on plants. The role of BC in mitigating Sb + Pb toxicities is well reported, nevertheless the role of BC with GABA in mitigating Sb + Pb is not reported yet. Therefore, we hypothesize that BC and GABA partnership effectively can immobilize Sb + Pb, and decrease their toxicities in rice plants than their individual application. The study was set with the following objectives: (i) to assess the impacts of BC and GABA application on growth, physiological and biochemical functioning, (ii) to explore the effects of BC and GABA application on Sb + Pb availability, and soil properties.

Materials and methods

Experimental details

The study was performed in a rain-protected greenhouse at UAF Sub-Campus Okara, Punjab, Pakistan. The soil (0–20 cm) was collected from the experimental field to fill the pots. The soil was silt loamy and had a pH of 6.84, total nitrogen 1.76 mg kg−1, available phosphorus and potassium of 20.12 and 165.92 mg kg−1, respectively. The soil had organic matter content of 0.81% and cation exchange capacity of 5.91 cmol kg–1. The pots were filled with soil (10 kg dry weight: DW), and Sb + Pb concentration was achieved by using C8H4K2O12Sb2 and Pb(NO3)2. Afterward, the soil was stabilized for two months before the experiment began. After that the soil from the pots was taken out, and BC was mixed with soil according to treatments. Then, pots were filled with water and five rice seedlings (25 days old) were planted in each pot. The rice straw was collected from the nearby non-contaminated area. The specific rice cultivar was Kissan Basmati, whose straws were used to make biochar. The rice straw was collected and dried to prepare the BC. The straw was subjected to pyrolysis (600°C) for 8 h under limited oxygen supply and heating rate of 5°C min−1 to prepare BC. The prepared BC was rich in carbon (603 g kg−1), and had alkaline pH (9.61), cation exchange capacity of 10.12 cmol kg−1 and nitrogen content of 4.05 g kg−1.

Experimental treatments

Our study comprised of different treatments; T1: control, T2: Sb (600 mg kg−1) + Pb (600 mg kg−1) stress, T3: Sb + Pb + BC (2.5%), T4: Sb + Pb + GABA (1 mM) and T5: Sb + Pb + BC (2.5%) + GABA (1 mM). GABA was applied as a foliar spray with a hand sprayer at the tillering stage. This GABA level was used in previous studies in which authors found that 1 mM GABA application at tillering stage is appropriate for mitigating adverse impacts of stress conditions in rice [23, 26]. The rate of BC is also based on previous studies reporting that 2–2.5% BC application decreases Sb and Pb toxicity [27, 28]. Fertilizers NPK were applied at the rates of 2.20, 3.40, and 1.45 g, respectively, to fulfill crop NPK requirements. Further, no insect or disease attack was observed on the crop during the experimental period and weeds were removed manually, if any. The crop was grown on 26th June 2023 and harvested on 30th October 2024.

Physiological traits

Leaf samples were ground with acetone (90%), homogenized, and later absorbance was noted at 665, 649, and 470 nm to determine chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid concentration [29]. For leaf relative water contents (RWC), freshly collected leaves were weighed (FW), soaked in water for 1 day, and again weighed (TW). The leaves were oven-dried and weighed again (DW), and RWC was assessed by following the methods of Hassan et al. [30]. For electrolyte leakage (EL), leaves were immersed in deionized water and incubated for 30 min before the initial electrical conductivity (EC1) was measured. Following a 24-hour incubation in a 90°C water bath, the final electrical conductivity (EC2) was measured. Later, EL was calculated using the formula: EL (%) = (EC1/EC2) × 100 [31].

Biochemical traits

To determine oxidative markers, including malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), freshly collected leaves (0.5 g) were ground with trichloroacetic acid (TCA: 5 mL). Thereafter, the leaf extract was collected, and then its centrifugation was done for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, and combined with 5 mL of 0.1% thiobarbituric acid, heated at 100°C for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured (532 nm) to determine MDA concentration [32]. In the case of H2O2, leaf extract was mixed with potassium iodide (KI) and potassium phosphate buffer (PPB: 100 µL) to determine H2O2 activity. Leaves were ground with chilled potassium phosphate buffer (PPB: 50 mM and pH: 7.8) using a cooled mortar and pestle. The extract was homogenized at 12,000 rpm (4 °C) to collect supernatant for determining antioxidant activity. For ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity, 100 µL of the enzyme extract was mixed with 100 µL of H₂O₂ ascorbate, and the absorbance (290 nm) was measured [33]. For catalase activity, a mixture containing 2 mL enzyme extract, 50 mL PPB, and 10 mM H2O2 was prepared, and CAT activity was measured at 240 nm [34]. For POD, 100 µL enzyme extract, 50 mL PPB, 300 mM H2O2 were mixed, and absorbance was measured at 470 nm. For SOD activity, we prepared the mixture consisting of 400 µL of H2O2, 25 mL of PPB buffer, and measured absorbance at 560 nm to determine SOD concentration [35]. For total soluble proteins (TSP), fresh leaves were taken and homogenized with PPB at 15,000 rpm for 15 min. The 1 mL leaf extract was mixed with 3 mL of Bradford reagent to determine TSP by measuring absorbance at 595 nm. To determine free amino acids (FAA), 1 ml of enzyme extract, 1 ml of ninhydrin (2%), and pyridine (10%) were mixed. Then, this mixture was heated (90 °C) for half an hour, cooled, and the absorbance (570 nm) was measured. For leaf proline, leaves were homogenized using 3% sulfosalicylic acid and incubated (90°C) for half an hour after mixing with glacial acetic acid and ninhydrin. The mixture was cooled down and mixed with toluene, and absorbance (532 nm) was taken to determine proline [36].

Tissue lead, antimony and nutrients concentrations

The plant samples were collected, ground, and digested with HNO3:HClO4 (4:1 v/v). Thereafter, samples were filtered, and volume was made to determine the Sb and Pb contents in plant samples using atomic absorption spectrophotometry. The concentration of N and P in plant tissues was determined by the Kjeldahl method and spectrophotometer, respectively, while concentrations of calcium (Ca), potassium (K), and magnesium (Mg) were measured with a flame photometer.

Determination of soil properties

The soil samples were carefully collected, dried, and all the debris was removed. The soil pH was determined using a pH meter, with a soil-to-water ratio of 1:5. The soil nitrogen concentration was determined with the Kjeldahl method, while the concentration of P and K was determined by the sodium bicarbonate extraction (spectrophotometer) and flame photometer. The soil organic carbon (SOC) concentration was determined using the H2SO4 external heating technique. Samples were taken and digested using HClO4 and HNO3 at 160°C to assess soil Sb + Pb concentrations. Thereafter, samples were allowed to cool, filtered, and diluted by adding distilled water. The concentrations of Sb + Pb were measured with atomic absorption spectrometry. The sequential extraction technique was used to calculate the fractions of Sb by following the standard procedures of Tessier et al. [37]. Different fractions of Sb such as non-specifically and specially sorbed forms, residual, linked with Fe and Al hydrous- oxides (AHO), and Sb linked with both amorphous and imperfectly crystalline ferrous and AHO were determined. Lead fractions were measured with a modified sequential extraction method, as suggested by Xiong et al. [38]. The different fractions, such as residual (RS), exchangeable (EX), oxidizable (OX), and reducible (RC), were determined using this method.

Determination of yield and related attributes

The roots and shoots were carefully separated to measure root length, their fresh and dry biomass. Ten panicles were used to measure panicle length and grain per panicle. The panicle bearing tillers were counted to get the data of productive tillers. The pots were harvested and weighed to estimate biomass and grain yields. Hundred grains were collected to measure to the 100-grain weight, while harvest index (HI) was measured as the ratio of grain and biological yield.

Experiment design and statistical analysis

The experiment was performed in a complete randomized design, with each treatment containing three replications. The data was analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Statistix 8.1, and Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was used to separate means. Moreover, figures were created with Simga-plot 10, and correlation and principal components analyses (PCA) were performed with R-studio.

Results

Characterization of biochar

The surface morphology of BC plays an important role in pollutant absorption. We used SEM and TEM technologies to study the surface morphology of BC (Fig. 1A and B). The results indicated that BC showed a fine porous structure, which indicates its tremendous potential for absorbing toxic metals. Further, FTIR spectra were used for the calculation of different functional groups present on the BC surface (Fig. 1C). The studied spectra showed peaks of BC at 2851.51, 2921.08, 1617.88, 1383.78, and 1090.98 cm−1 (Fig. 1C), indicated a substantial carbonyl and carboxylic acid groups. EDS analysis was performed to detect the elemental mapping of prepared BC (Fig. 2, A and B). Different elements such as Si, O, P, C, Ca, K, Mg, Na, Fe, and N were uniformly present on the BC surface (Fig. 2). Carbon has a significant proportion of 38.18% on BC while O, N, Mg, Si, P, K, Ca, and Fe have the following proportions of 33.25, 0.09, 0.08, 21.53, 1.31, 1.75, 2.16, and 0.95% on the BC surface (Fig. 2).

Photosynthetic pigments and relative water contents

The results indicated that compared with control, Sb + Pb stress significantly reduced the synthesis of chl a, chl b, and carotenoids (Fig. 3). A significant decrease of 50%, 74.41%, and 46.68% was observed in Chl a, Chl b, and carotenoids in Sb + Pb soil (Fig. 3). However, BC and GABA application appreciably reduced this reduction and increased photosynthetic pigments in Sb + Pb polluted soil (Fig. 3). Biochar, in combination with GABA, enhanced Chl a, Chl b, and carotenoids synthesis by 37.87%, 53.48%, and 36.87%, respectively, then their alone application (Fig. 3). In particular, Sb + Pb toxicity markedly reduced the leaf RWC to the control. We observed that Sb + Pb stress decreased the RWC by 31.92% as compared with the control. Contrarily, BC and GABA application caused a marked increase in RWC of rice growing in combined Sb + Pb polluted soil. BC + GABA enhanced RWC by 25.92%, while alone BC and GABA enhanced RWC by 17.96% and 11.68%, respectively (Fig. 3).

Oxidative stress markers, osmolyte synthesis, and antioxidant activities

The presented results indicated that Sb + Pb toxicity increased EL, MDA, and H2O2 synthesis by 186.34%, 108.33%, and 152.12%, respectively, as compared with the control (Fig. 4). Nevertheless, BC + GABA remained the top performer, significantly reducing EL, MDA, and H2O2 production by 90.21%, 63.59%, and 60.57%, respectively, as compared with the Sb + Pb treatment (Fig. 4). Antimony and Pb toxicity showed a variable impact on proline, TSP, and FAA synthesis. The synthesis of proline increased under Sb + Pb stress, and it further expanded with the exogenous application of BC and GABA. The more pronounced increase in proline synthesis was observed with combined BC + GABA application. Opposite to this, Sb + Pb toxicity induced a significant decrease in TSP and FAA synthesis, though BC, GABA, and particularly their co-application markedly increased TSP and FAA synthesis under Sb + Pb stress (Fig. 4). Combined BC + GABA markedly enhanced APX, CAT, POD, SOD, and AsA activities by 29.13%, 50.39%, 88.72%, 72.59%, and 50.91%, respectively, under Sb + Pb stress (Fig. 4).

Growth and yield characteristics

Compared with the control, Sb + Pb exposure significantly decreased root growth and biomass production (Table 1). The RL, RFW, and RDW significantly decreased under Sb + Pb with a reduction of 52.86%, 106.82%, and 91.54%, respectively. Biochar, GABA, and BC + GABA significantly enhanced RL, RFW, and RDW. The presented results indicated that BC + GABA profoundly increased the RL, RFW, and RDW by 29.35%, 75.82%, and 75.56%, respectively, than the control (Table 1). Sb + Pb stress decreased the PH, TPP, HKW, BY, GY, and HI of rice crop (Table 1). The minimum plant height (85.73 cm), tillers (7), HKW (2.38 g), BY (154.40 g/pot), GY (38.27 g/pot), and HI (24.81%) were detected in Sb + Pb contaminated soil without BC and GABA application (Table 1). The maximum plant height (107.33 cm), tillers (10), HKW (4.05 g), BY (207.33 g/pot), GY (63.67 g/pot), and HI (30.71%) under Sb + Pb polluted soil were observed with BC + GABA application (Table 1).

Antimony, and lead accumulation in plant tissues

The maximum root Sb concentration (35.10 mg kg− 1) and shoot Sb concentration (22.93 mg kg− 1) were noted in Sb + Pb contaminated soil (Fig. 5). BC + GABA application decreased Sb accretion by 73.16% and 122.83%, respectively (Fig. 5). Lead accumulation also increased under Sb + Pb contaminated soil. All the amendments significantly decreased Pb accretion in rice plants (Fig. 5). Nevertheless, BC + GABA decreased Pb accretion in roots and shoots of rice by 83.41% and 105.88% with BC + GABA application (Fig. 5).

Nutrient concentration in plant tissues

Compared with the control, Sb + Pb stress decreased NPK in plant tissues (Table 2). The lowest root and shoot NPK concentrations were observed in the Sb + Pb stress condition, while all amendments enhanced tissue nutrient concentration in the Sb + Pb soil (Table 2). The maximum root and shoot N (18.74 and 22.66 mg kg− 1), P (14.47 and 22.67 mg kg− 1), and K (35.35 and 42.30 mg kg− 1) were noted with BC + GABA (Table 2). The accumulation of Mg in roots and shoots was decreased by 15.22% and 23.98%, while the concentration of Ca in roots and shoots was decreased by 18.22% and 22.79% respectively, under Sb+Pb stress. Conversely, BC + GABA increased Ca and Mg accumulation in roots and shoots of rice than their individual application (Table 3).

Post-harvest soil properties and fractions of antimony and lead

The maximum soil pH (7.26) under Sb + Pb contaminated soil was observed with the combined BC + GABA application (Table 4). Sb + Pb toxicity significantly decreased the soil NPK and SOC availability. For instance, NPK concentration reduced by 17.06%, 6.20%, and 12.11%, respectively, under Sb + Pb stress than the control (Table 4). The concentration of SOC decreased by 1.18% under Sb + Pb contaminated soil compared with the control (Table 4). BC, GABA, and their combined application significantly enhanced soil NPK and SOC concentrations; however, combined BC + GABA remained the top performer in increasing soil NPK and SOC concentrations (Table 4). Maximum soil Sb (239 mg kg− 1) and Pb (262.27 mg kg− 1) were observed in soil polluted with Sb + Pb. All amendments, particularly combined BC + GABA, decreased soil Sb + Pb availability and changed their forms (Table 4). The results indicated that OA was the most predominant form, followed by OX, NSS, SSF, and RS (Fig. 6). Combined BC + GABA resulted in maximum RS, followed by OA, OX, and NSS, while combined application of BC + GABA resulted in the lowest SSF fraction of Sb (Fig. 6). Pb distribution in soil was significantly impacted by BC, GABA, and BC + GABA application (Fig. 7). Biochar and GABA application reduced reducible and extractable forms of Pb and changed them into residual and oxidizable forms of Pb. Overall, the lowest extractable and reducible forms of Pb were observed with BC + GABA application, whilst maximum residual and oxidizable forms of Pb were also observed with combined BC + GABA application (Fig. 7).

Principal component and correlation analysis

The results indicated that two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explain a significant portion of the total variance, effectively separating treatments based on their impacts on the measured parameters. PC1 primarily distinguished the treatments based on stress-related markers (H2O2, MDA, and EL) and antioxidant activity (SOD, APX, and CAT), accounting for the majority of the variation. PC2 further separates the treatments, highlighting parameters related to growth and productivity, such as GY, BY, and photosynthetic pigments (Chl a, Cart). The control treatment is positioned on the negative side of PC1, indicating low stress and high growth-related values. In contrast, the Sb + Pb treatment is associated with higher stress marker levels (H2O2 and MDA) along PC1. The addition of amendments like BC and GABA significantly influences treatment clustering. The combined treatment of Sb + Pb + BC + GABA shifts towards the positive side of PC2, mitigating stress markers and enhancing growth performance. The Sb + Pb + BC and Sb + Pb + GABA show intermediate effects, indicating reduced stress and partial recovery in growth parameters (Figs. 8 and 9).

The results depicted that growth parameters, including RL, RFW, RDW, PH, GY, and HI, showed strong positive correlations, indicating their combined impact on plant performance (Fig. 9). Physiological parameters revealed a different pattern, with stress-related markers and antioxidant enzyme activities negatively correlated with photosynthetic pigments (Chl a, Cart) and relative water content (RWC) (Fig. 9). The proline (Pro) displayed strong positive correlations with antioxidants, emphasizing its role in stress resilience. Pb concentrations in roots and shoots are inversely correlated with essential nutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg), showing the adverse effect of heavy metal stress on nutrient uptake. The nutrient levels in roots and shoots are closely linked, indicating efficient nutrient distribution. Further, soil properties exhibited positive correlations among essential nutrients and SOC, contributing to soil fertility and reduced Sb + Pb bioavailability.

Discussion

The results indicated that Sb + Pb treatment significantly decreased rice growth and productivity (Table 1) by decreasing root growth (Table 1). Roots directly absorb HMs, which disrupts their functioning and transport nutrients and water, leading to poor growth [39]. Both Sb + Pb treatment also reduced nutrient accumulation in plant organs, by a decrease in soil nutrient availability, thereby leading to a reduction in plant growth (Table 1). Antimony and Pb toxicity decreased the chlorophyll synthesis (Fig. 3), which decreased photosynthetic efficiency and crop productivity (Fig. 3). Toxic metals adversely affect the chloroplast and enzyme activity involved in chlorophyll synthesis [40]. They also negatively affected the PS-II efficiency and increase ROS production (Figs. 3 and 4), which caused oxidation of cellular structure and subsequently reduced growth (Table 1) [41]. We observed that Sb + Pb toxicity seriously decreased chlorophyll synthesis (Fig. 3) by increasing oxidative damage [42]. Antimony and Pb toxicity might also damage the chloroplast structure due to an increase in ROS production, leading to a reduction in chlorophyll synthesis [43]. Biochar and GABA application increased chlorophyll synthesis, reducing oxidative damage and shielding the photosynthetic apparatus, thereby increasing the photosynthetic pigments [44, 45]. Antimony and Pb stress also might decrease the root hydraulic conductivity, which decreases the turgor pressure and RWC [46]. These findings align with earlier researchers who found that toxic metals decrease water uptake and RWC [47]. BC + GABA application maintained better RWC, which was linked to improved root growth and restricted Sb + Pb accumulation in plant tissues (Fig. 3).

Biochar and GABA application significantly enhanced the growth and productivity of rice (Table 1). This was linked with decreased EL, MDA, and ROS production and increased chlorophyll contents and RWC, leading to a marked increase in rice growth. This combination might also protect the photosynthetic system and enhance the synthesis of chlorophyll, which ensures better photosynthetic efficiency and consequently improves rice yield [48]. The elevated Sb + Pb accumulation caused oxidative stress and substantially increased ROS production. Biochar + GABA application appreciably enhanced the antioxidant activities and osmolyte synthesis, which decreased Sb + Pb induced damages [40, 49]. Antimony + Pb toxicity seriously decreased the soil NPK availability (Table 2) and altered the soil pH. The reduction in nutrients disturbed plant functioning and reduced growth and yield. Biochar + GABA application significantly enhanced the soil NPK availability because of the significant amount of nutrients present in BC. Biochar and GABA application also improved the root growth, which enhanced nutrient availability, thereby leading to better growth and yield [50, 51].

Biochar application improves water retention and soil structure [52], which might help the rice plants maintain better osmoregulation under Sb + Pb stress. Biochar + GABA application enhanced osmotic balance in rice plants by increasing proline, TSP, and FAA synthesis, ensuring rice plants’ survival under Sb + Pb stress. The accumulation of Sb + Pb considerably increased in the roots and shoots. Toxic metals disturb protein osmolyte synthesis, and antioxidant activities and cause plant oxidative damage [53]. Antimony and Pb toxicity seriously curbed the TSP and FAA synthesis, which could be attributed to reduced N uptake (Table 1). Contrarily, BC and GABA enhanced N uptake, which favored the synthesis of both TSP and FAA and resulted in better growth and yield.

The excessive production of ROS due to Sb + Pb stress significantly increased the membrane damage, as reported by an increase in MDA production. The excessive accumulation of Sb + Pb increases ROS production, which causes cellular oxidation, leading to an increase in MDA production [41, 54]. However, rice plants increase antioxidant activity to encounter the hazardous impacts of Sb + Pb stress. The increase in the activity of antioxidants aligns with earlier reports indicating that plants increase their antioxidant activity to encounter metal toxicity [55]. Further, BC + GABA application substantially enhanced antioxidant activity. The increased CAT activities remove the toxic effects of peroxides, while increased SOD activities decompose superoxide radicals into H₂O₂, thereby decreasing toxic impacts on plants. Biochar and GABA also increase POD activity and increased POD activity favors the conversion of phenols into quinines, thus reducing the oxidative damage [56]. The present increase in antioxidant performance with GABA also aligns with earlier reports where authors document a significant increase in antioxidant activities with GABA application [56].

Biochar and GABA decreased Sb + Pb accumulation in roots and shoots. This was associated with “dilution effects,” wherein BC + GABA enhanced rice growth and diluted Sb + Pb in plant parts. The biochar used in the current study was rich in carbon (603 g kg− 1); therefore, it enhanced SOC (Table 4). Biochar and GABA significantly enhanced SOC contents (Table 4), which might form complexes with Sb + Pb and reduce their availability and accumulation in rice plants [24, 57]. Biochar also enhances soil pH, which decreases the availability of Sb + Pb, thereby resulting in less accretion in plant parts [24, 58]. Biochar also has excellent functional groups and a larger surface area, which absorb the toxic metals and decrease their uptake, transportation, and subsequent accumulation in plant parts [59]. The functional groups in BC stimulate heavy metals chelation, thus reducing their availability [60]. These findings also indicated that BC + GABA application enhanced Ca uptake and accumulation (Table 3), which might improve the ability of rice plants to accumulate less Sb + Pb. Calcium is an essential nutrient for plants, and it also works as a signaling molecule and enhances plants’ ability against HMs [61]. Biochar serves as an essential source of exchangeable Ca [62]. Applying BC raises soil pH, which increases the availability of exchangeable sites. This process facilitates the adsorption of base cations and upsurges the exchangeable Ca in soil [63]. The other possible reason is that BC + GABA application also immobilizes the Sb + Pb through sorption and precipitation, thus reducing the Sb + Pb transportation by roots and decreasing their accumulation in plant tissues [12]. Further, the restricted uptake and accumulation of Sb + Pb due to GABA application might also be linked with its protective role and enhanced cell integrity, resulting in less uptake in plant tissues [24].

The presented results indicated that Sb + Pb treatment decreased the NPK, SOC contents, and altered the soil pH (Tables 2, 3 and 4). Biochar and GABA increased the soil pH, which might be linked with the presence of alkaline substances in BC. This increase in soil decreases Sb + Pb availability by increasing the formation of organic matter, Sb, and Pb complexes [64]. We also observed that Sb + Pb toxicity significantly decreased soil NPK concentration, which was associated with the complex interaction of Sb + Pb with nutrients, which decreased their availability [65]. Combining BC with GABA enhanced NPK availability in Sb + Pb polluted soil. Biochar improves nutrient availability, microbial activities, and SOC, which ensures the better availability of nutrients in contaminated soils [23]. Alkali elements, including Ca, K, Mg, Mn, and Fe, affect the sorption mechanisms’ responses to the release of cations, thereby leading to an increase in CEC [66]. Therefore, BC + GABA might increase CEC, increasing soil nutrient availability and subsequent plant growth.

BC and GABA also enhanced SOC, which provides long-lasting carbon sources. Biochar application ensures better soil structure and improves microbial activities, significantly increasing SOC [67]. This increase in SOC forms complexes between OM and Sb + Pb, thereby reducing their availability in soil. Biochar possesses an excellent porous structure, which also adsorbs Sb + Pb, reducing their availability in soil [68–70]. We also observed that BC and GABA enhanced the soil nutrient availability. Biochar increases nutrient availability through direct addition of nutrients, amelioration of soil properties and metals immobilization [69–71].

Biochar also adsorbs HMs and increases microbial activities, contributing to methylation and reducing toxic metals, thereby reducing their availability [18]. BC + GABA application decreased the NSS and SSF proportion of Sb while enhancing OA and RS fractions. This shows that the BC + GABA partnership changed the existing forms of Sb into residual forms, thus reducing its availability [72]. Biochar drives the chemical reduction of toxic and mobile forms of antimony due to its porous structure and large surface area. Moreover, BC also forms complexes with Sb effectively locking the Sb into the residual fraction of the soil matrix. These findings also illustrate that BC + GABA immobilized the Pb by changing it from an extractable form to a less extractable form. This change was associated with increased soil pH and SOC following BC + GABA application [73]. The efficiency of BC and GABA in mitigating Sb + Pb toxicity is likely to be mediated by changes in soil bacterial communities. Biochar has a higher surface area and porous structure, which provides a suitable habitat for microbes involved in nutrient cycling and heavy metals immobilization [74]. We also postulate that, as a signaling molecule, GABA enhanced the abundance of metal-chelating microbes. This synergistic action of BC and GABA enhanced nutrient availability and decreased Sb + Pb availability, contributing to improved rice productivity. Our BC-GABA approach also offers benefits over other additives, such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), which is a very common chelator. However, this causes environmental risks by making metal complexes that can leach into groundwater. Likewise, indole acetic acid (IAA) also promotes plant growth and mitigates the toxic impacts of metals by modulating plant functioning; nevertheless, its impacts are only focused on plants [74–76]. Therefore, our approach, the BC-GABA partnership, offers a holistic approach to improve soil chemistry, adsorb toxic metals, and plant functioning to counteract Sb + Pb toxicity.

Conclusions

Exposure to Sb + Pb stress inhibited rice growth and productivity due to excessive accumulation of ROS, membrane damage, restricted nutrient and water uptake, and increased availability and accumulation of Sb + Pb. However, BC + GABA application alleviated the Sb + Pb-induced toxicity and enhanced rice growth and productivity. This was linked with better chlorophyll synthesis, leaf water contents, antioxidant performance, osmolyte synthesis, nutrient availability, SOC, restricted oxidative damage, and Sb + Pb availability and accumulation in plant tissues. Hence, combined BC + GABA shows remarkable potential to improve rice productivity in Sb + Pb contaminated soils. This was a pot study conducted in controlled conditions; therefore, long-term field experiments are necessary to validate the large-scale application of this approach. Further, metabolomics and transcriptomics studies are required to explore in-depth mechanisms mediated by BC + GABA to induce combined Sb + Pb tolerance in plants. The toxic effects of BC should be studied in future studies. Additionally, the anatomical parameters, such as the ultrastructure of the root and shoot by using SEM/TEM, should also be studied.
