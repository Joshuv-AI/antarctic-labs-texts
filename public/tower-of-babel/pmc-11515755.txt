Posterior short ciliary arteries ischemia following embolization of anterior ethmoid artery to treat dural arteriovenous fistulas: a case report
Authors: Yuyi Han, Xiaoyong Liu, Tingting Yao, Qianqian Zhou, Jihong Wang, Chunfang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Onyx™ Liquid Embolic System is a non-adhesive liquid embolic agent, which has been proved by the US FDA for embolization of lesions in the peripheral and neurovasculature since 2005. We reported a case of ischemic optic neuropathy after using Onyx-18 to embolize the anterior ethmoid arteries that feeding dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVF).Case presentationA 57-year-old Asian male presented with anterior cranial fossa DAVF underwent embolotherapy by delivering Onyx-18 through a microcatheter into the anterior ethmoid arteries under angiography guidance. The interventional procedure was successful and no clear evidence was found pointing to untargeted occlusive embolus. But after the surgery the patient experienced delayed painless vision loss in the right eye (RE). The fundoscopy showed unilateral papilledema with pale optic disc in RE, accompanied by significant edema and thickening in the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) of macula. The fundus fluorescence angiography showed that most of the optic disc in RE had postponed or absent fluorescence filling. Visual evoked potential (VEP) confirmed that the amplitude of the P100 component was decreased in RE without significant prolongation of the latency. The patient was diagnosed with anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, but immediate pulse steroid therapy failed to rescue his vision.ConclusionPreoperative evaluation of the patient’s hemodynamic status and fundus examination are essential for assessing the risk of ischemic ocular complications, and the non-adhesive liquid embolic agent Onyx-18 should be used cautiously during endovascular embolization of intracranial artery.Keywords: Embolization, Onyx, DAVF, Ischemic optic neuropathy, Case report

Background

Dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVF) has been regarded as a type of acquired abnormal arteriovenous communications, which is usually initiated by the presence of vein thrombosis near the dural sinus wall, allowing the nearby arteries to grow into it and to form a new AV-shunt that connects the venous lumen [1]. The symptoms caused by DAVFs vary depending on their locations, including headache, dementia, visual and hearing impairment, and intracranial hemorrhage. The most common location of DAVFs is transverse /sigmoid sinus [2], while DAVFs in other places are relatively rare, such as the anterior cranial fossa DAVF. The treatment strategies of DAVFs are individually different based on all the clinical and imaging manifestations, and common options include endovascular embolization, microsurgery, radiosurgery. The endovascular therapy, especially the transarterial embolization, is currently accepted as the most minimally invasive surgery for the treatment of DAVF by obliterating the fistulous point without interfering with normal dura-venous drains.

The liquid embolic material Onyx-18 has showed superior in blood flow directivity and distal permeability among all embolic materials. This is what makes Onyx-18 more desirable for the interventional therapy in treating intracranial DAVF [3]. However, inadvertent complications may occur during or after the embolization procedure, any ischemic or hemorrhagic accidents of involved vessels could cause functional neurological deficits or even death. The present study described a case of ischemic optic neuropathy following the intracranial embolization to treat anterior cranial fossa DAVF, emphasizing the risk of causing ophthalmic artery branches ischemia after delivering Onyx-18 into the anterior ethmoidal arteries.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old Asian gentleman presented with anterior cranial fossa DAVF for years. The patient has a history of diabetes and mild hypertension, and has had good control of both blood glucose (BG) and blood pressure (BP) on oral agents. Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) of his brain and orbit showed malformed vascular mass located at the base of the anterior cranial fossa, while magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in the same area didn’t exhibit any clinically apparent neuro-deficits. The brain angiography demonstrated a tortuous collection of arteries behind the orbit and around the cribriform plate. The indication for embolization was to reduce the risk of DAVF complications caused by regional venous hypertension, including abnormal visual function and intracranial hemorrhage. Preoperative hematological tests showed no abnormalities in inflammation-related indicators. The patient underwent embolotherapy of anterior cranial fossa DAVF under sedation and general anesthesia. Before the surgery, ophthalmologic consultation confirmed his best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in either eye was 1.0, and the result of visual field test was normal. No anomaly was found in his anterior segment or fundus of both eyes.

After heparin was given to the patient, the floating microcatheter was delivered to the right and left anterior ethmoid arteries. Onyx-18 injection under arteriography guidance was slowly done in several times to fill the vascular fistula. In the end of the procedure, it was confirmed that 90% of the DAVF was satisfactorily embolized, and there was no intracranial or systemic hemorrhage. After awakening from anesthesia, the patient had normal binocular vision on bedside examination.

Approximately 1 day after the procedure, the patient complained of painless vision loss and central visual field defect in his right eye (RE). The visual acuity of RE was 0.05 with a relative afferent pupillary defect. The visual acuity of the left eye (LE) was 0.6 but its visual field remained normal, and no abnormal sign was seen by slit-lamp examination. The intraocular pressure (IOP) was normal in both eyes. The fundoscopy showed unilateral papilledema with pale optic disc in RE, and linear hemorrhage was seen around the vessels of the optic papilla (Fig. 1). There was no obvious bleeding or exudation in the retina of RE, but optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed significant edema and thickening in the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) of macula (Fig. 2). The fundus fluorescence angiography (FFA) showed nasal choroidal hypofluorescence in RE at the early stage, and later most of the optic disc had postponed or absent fluorescence filling except for the inferior temporal side (Fig. 3). Visual evoked potential (VEP) confirmed that the amplitude of the P100 component was decreased in RE without significant prolongation of the latency (Fig. 4). The patient was initially diagnosed with ischemic optic neuropathy in RE. Immediate intravenous infusion of methylprednisolone 1000 mg and mannitol were given to the patient to restore the retinal circulation. However, no significant improvement was observed in his visual function of RE after 3 days’ treatment. Based on these signs, it was considered that the papilledema of RE was resulted from anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (AION) due to the posterior ciliary short artery ischemia. After orally taken mecobalamine and glucocorticoids, combined with hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HOT) for 2 weeks, his papilledema was markedly reduced, but there was no improvement in the visual acuity and visual field of RE. The BCVA of LE was 1.0 on the last follow-up.

Discussion

Ischemic optic neuropathy occurs when the blood supply to the optic nerve is deficient or interrupted. Based on the patient’s obvious papilledema in RE, anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (AION) was considered, while posterior ischemic optic neuropathy (PION) rarely had an abnormal morphology of optic disc [4]. AION is caused by ischemia of the posterior short ciliary arteries supplying the sieve plate of optic disc. As to non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAAION), any risk factor for hemodynamic abnormalities can cause transient or permanent hypoperfusion of the posterior ciliary short arteries, which in turn leads to edema of optic nerve axons. The swollen nerve fibers can compress adjacent capillaries and exacerbate the microcirculation disturbance around the optic nerve, all which contribute to neuroinflammation [4]. Although there are marked interindividual variations in the blood supply of ONH, early clinical research report showed that nearly half of NAAION patients had a history of hypertension and 24% of the patients had diabetes [5]. According to the blood flow formula distilled in 2009, the critical factors that affect the blood flow in ONH include BP, IOP, and resistance to blood flow [6]. Transient non-perfusion of ONH circulation caused by fall of BP in the patients with arterial hypertension, or occlusion of the tiny capillaries around ONH due to diabetes, are common systemic risk factors that lead to NAAION [6]. In this case, the BP and BG of the patient had been strictly controlled, but the presence of underlying diseases might still aggravate the ischemic response in the capillaries after the surgery.

According to the results of the patient’s postoperative fundus examination, no clear evidence was found pointing to occlusive embolus. Nonetheless, it is possible for embolic materials to enter the branches of ophthalmic artery along the bloodstream when arterial extravasation or intravascular regurgitation occurs during intracranial interventional surgery. The anterior cranial fossa DAVFs mainly collect blood flow from posterior and anterior ethmoidal branches of the ophthalmic arteries [3]. Typically, the ophthalmic artery arises from internal carotid artery, it enters orbit and runs through the optic canal along the optic nerve, giving off the main branches that supply the eyeball, including the central retinal artery and posterior ciliary arteries. In a few cases, the ophthalmic artery may also have uncommon anastomosis with the middle meningeal artery of external carotid origin through collateral vessels [7]. The ophthalmic artery and its branches are extremely narrow in diameter with tortuous anatomy [8], so that accidental entry of embolic material into these arteries may trigger excessive vasoconstriction or directly occlude the entire vessel cavity even in a very tiny volume. It was reported that the central retinal artery occlusion combined with posterior ciliary artery occlusion occurred after embolization with microspheres via the middle meningeal artery [9]. Another case also reported the ophthalmic artery occlusion after microspheres embolization of external carotid artery [10]. With the development of embolic materials, the use of Onyx liquid embolic system has been advocated as the most ideal liquid embolic agent for the endoarterial management of DAVF due to its advantage of making the filling process slow and controlled [11, 12], their safety and efficacy was recognized in treating DAVF of anterior cranial fossa [12]. However, the injection of Onyx still has the risk for ischemic neurological disease due to the possibility of aggravating the inflammation within the vasculature that supply nerves [13]. It was noted that utilization of Onyx has a certain incidence of causing cranial nerve palsy, even without any nontarget embolization during the interventional procedure [14]. Likewise, ischemic complications have also occurred with Onyx filling into facial artery [15], lumbar artery [16] and deep artery of the lower limb [17]. At this point, we surprisingly found that the clinical evidence about visual impairment due to direct ischemic reactions of ophthalmic artery and its branches caused by Onyx injection was rarely mentioned. There has been one study reporting the visual pathway ischemia caused by accidental Onyx HD-500 penetration and occlusion of small superior hypophyseal artery branch [18], but there are no clear reports about occlusion of central retinal artery or posterior ciliary arteries secondary to the cerebrovascular embolotherapy using Onyx-18. Apart from the common symptom of rapid vision loss during the embolization manipulation, the delayed onset of visual impairment might be easily overlooked in the perioperative period, especially when there was no signs of accidental obstruction presented at the end of the procedure. This is the first case report that literally identifying the risk of ischemic optic neuropathy with the filling of Onxy-18 into the anterior ethmoid arteries, emphasizing that the visual deficits caused by ocular ischemia have to be carefully weighed against the benefits of Onyx utilization in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrated an accidental event of NAAION following Onyx injection into the anterior ethmoid arteries. Although this non-adhesive liquid embolic agent provides the advantage in effectively obliterating the fistulous point of DAVF, transarterial injection of Onyx still has the risk of promoting intravascular inflammation and aggravating the ischemia reaction within the nourished nervous tissue. Given the risk of visual loss associated with transarterial embolization through the ophthalmic artery and its branches, transvenous intervention technique may be another safer option to avoid the ocular complications [19]. Fundus examination and visual field test are necessary for assessing the risk of unexpected ocular complications in DAVF patients. Hemodynamic abnormalities caused by underlying diseases and common risk factors of NAAION, such as smoke, diabetes, hypertension and hyperlipemia, should be alerted before the endovascular surgery. When the procedure for interventional therapy may involve the blood vessels innervating the visual system, preoperative FFA can be considered to verify normal circulation in the choroid and retina. Also, optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) is recommended to check for anatomical abnormalities of fundus vessels or anastomotic branches that may increase the risk of obstruction.
