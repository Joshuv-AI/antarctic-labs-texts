A Synthetic Iron Model of Carbon–Sulfur Bond Activation by the Nitrogenase-Family Enzyme Methylthio-Alkane Reductase
Authors: Christian M. Johansen, Derek R. Kuno-Williams, John M. Ovian, Jonas C. Peters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The all-iron (FeFe) nitrogenase utilizes a complex iron–sulfur cluster to catalyze the reduction of dinitrogen to ammonia (N2R). Recently, it has been found that methylthio-alkane reductase (mar), which reduces methyl thioalkanes to methanethiol and hydrocarbon fragments, has a structurally similar active site. In this report, we explore the reactivity of a trisphosphine boratrane iron complex ([Fe]), an N2R catalyst, with thioalkanes as a functional model system. Reacting the neutral iron dinitrogen complex ([Fe­(N2)]) with dimethyl sulfide (Me2S) yields a 1:1 mixture of iron methyl ([FeMe]) and iron methyl thiolate ([Fe­(SMe)]) products. Subsequent protonation forms methane and methanethiol, the products of Me2S reduction by mar. Kinetic studies, including kinetic isotope effects (KIE), pre-equilibrium Van’t Hoff parameters, and Eyring analysis, as well as the reactivity with a broad scope of thioalkanes, point to [Fe­(SMe2)], formed via an uphill pre-equilibrium with [Fe­(N2)] as a key intermediate. In [Fe­(SMe2)], the C–S bond strength decreases by 44 kcal mol–1, priming it for bond homolysis. We propose that homolytic C–S bond cleavage occurs with the released alkyl radical being trapped by a second equivalent of iron. Comparisons of N2 and thioalkane reduction by [Fe] suggest similar modes of substrate activation at Fe, namely, backbonding into a π* orbital of NN and a C–S σ* orbital of Me2S, setting the substrates up for N–H bond formation and homolytic C–S bond cleavage, respectively.

Introduction

Nitrogenases catalyze the reduction of dinitrogen (N2) to ammonia (NH3) at an organometallic cofactor active site, [Fe7S9M­(μ6-C)], where M represents Mo, V, or Fe (M = Fe is depicted in Figure A). In addition to N2, this family of enzymes can reductively protonate non-native substrates, such as carbon monoxide, acetylene, and cyanide. , However, until recently, reductases beyond nitrogenase that utilize nitrogenase-like cofactors were unknown. ,

Comparison of the reduction of N2 to NH3 and thioalkanes to thiols and hydrocarbons at (A) enzymatic and (B) synthetic Fe sites.

In 2020, Tabita and co-workers detailed the activity of methylthio-alkane reductase (mar), an enzyme capable of reducing unactivated methyl thioalkanes. Dimethyl sulfide (Me2S), ethyl methyl sulfide (EtSMe), and 2-methylthiol ethanol (MeSC2H4OH) are reduced to yield methanethiol (MeSH) and a hydrocarbon fragment, CH4, C2H6, or C2H4 + H2O, respectively. These are mechanistically fascinating reductive transformations that add to a growing body of bio-organometallic chemistry that metalloenzymes mediate, and may be germane to new hydrodesulfurization (HDS) pathways. Notably, mar requires a similar gene repertoire as the all-iron nitrogenases ([FeFe]), suggesting a similar active site structure and possible mechanistic parallels (Figure A). Crystallographic and spectroscopic studies , provide further evidence that mar features a [Fe8S9(μ6-C)] active site and raise questions as to why such a specific chemical motif evolved for the reduction of such seemingly disparate substrates.

While C–S bond cleavage reactions are well-known in chemical synthesis, the reduction of simple dialkyl sulfides like Me2S or EtSMe by a transition metal center constitutes a highly unusual transformation. Our laboratory has had a sustained interest in the study of well-defined iron complexes as functional single-site models of the iron-rich cofactors of nitrogenases. Our studies have included the reduction of N2 , as well as non-native substrates (Figure B). − Herein, we focus on the reduction of thioalkanes, including the mar substrates Me2S and EtSMe, using a trisphosphine boratrane system [(P3 B)­Fe­(N2)] (abbreviated as [Fe­(N2)]) known to activate N2 in its catalytic reduction to ammonia. The reactivity we describe below involves coordination of Me2S (and other alkyl sulfides) to a low-coordinate Fe site following N2 dissociation (providing [Fe­(SMeR)]), which, via dπ-to-σ* backbonding, induces dramatic C–S bond weakening and radical bond homolysis to furnish an organic radical R• and an iron thiolate ([Fe­(SR)]). Related N2 activation by [Fe­(N2)] backbonding into a π* orbital weakens the NN bond and primes it for reductive protonation, pointing to possible mechanistic parallels within the iron-rich cofactors.

Results

We commenced our study by examining the reaction of [Fe­(N2)] with Me2S in C6D6. No reaction was observed at room temperature over an extended period; however, heating to 60 °C for 14 h led to the formation of an equal mixture of [FeMe] and [Fe­(SMe)]. Treating the mix with an excess of collidinium tetrakis­[3,5-bis­(trifluoromethyl)­phenyl]­borate ([ColH]­[BArF 4]) led to the clean formation of [Fe]­BArF 4, CH4 (93 ± 5%) and MeSH (96 ± 1%; see Section S9 for details). [Fe]­BArF 4 can be readily reduced back to [Fe­(N2)] (Figure A), demonstrating a closed synthetic cycle for the reduction of Me2S to yield the same products as catalytic reduction by mar.

(B) Determination of the second-order rate constant for Me2S reduction by monitoring the decay of [Fe­(N2)]. (C) Van’t Hoff analysis of N2 dissociation from [Fe­(N2)]. (D) Eyring analysis for Me2S reduction by [Fe­(N2)].

The [Fe­(SMe)] formed from Me2S activation was confirmed by independent preparation; [Fe­(N2)] was reacted with dimethyl disulfide. The solution magnetic moment of 3.87 μB indicates an S = 3/2 ground state. X-ray crystallography data were obtained (Figure A); the geometry about Fe is pseudotrigonal bipyramidal with expected elongated Fe–S (2.316(1) Å) and Fe–P (2.388(1) to 2.4693(8) Å) bonds, comparable to the structures of the S = 3/2 compounds [FeBr] and [FeMe]. , Furthermore, the C–S bond length is 1.817(1) Å. The Mössbauer spectrum features δ = 0.61 mm s–1 and ΔE Q = 2.27 mm s–1, also consistent with other quartet trisphosphine boratrane complexes (Figure S67).

The kinetics of Me2S activation was studied by 1H NMR spectroscopy. Monitoring the consumption of [Fe­(N2)], the reaction of Me2S with [Fe­(N2)] was found to follow a first-order dependence on both reactants with a k 2nd = 3.3·10–3 ± 2·10–4 M–1 s–1 at 60 °C (see Figure B for Me2S dependence; Figure S16 for [Fe­(N2)] dependence). A comparison of the rates of C–S bond cleavage of Me2S and Me2S-d 6 provides a secondary kinetic isotope effect (KIE, k H/k D = 2.6 ± 0.3), consistent with a change in hybridization from sp3 in the reactants to sp2 in the transition state. This result points to C–S bond homolysis as rate-contributing.

In the absence of N2, the reaction is ∼7 times faster. To understand this increased rate of reaction, [Fe­(N2)], in the absence of Me2S, was monitored by optical spectroscopy between 30 and 70 °C. Upon warming, a shoulder at 480 nm is observed. This same change is also observed upon degassing the UV–Vis sample (Figure S1). This apparent thermochromism is concentration-independent with Van’t Hoff parameters of ΔH° = 20.4 ± 0.6 kcal mol–1 and ΔS° = 50 ± 2 e.u. (Figure C). These observations are consistent with an equilibrium between [Fe­(N2)] and a vacant species [Fe], with the large entropic change suggesting release of N2. , This equilibrium would account for the increased rate under an Ar atmosphere, with N2 acting as a competitive inhibitor for Me2S activation by [Fe].

A mechanism consistent with these observations is presented in Figure A, wherein an equilibrated mixture of [Fe­(N2)] and [Fe], the presumed trigonal pyramidal vacant species, reacts with [Me2S] to yield [Fe­(SMe)2] reversibly. Formation of the thioalkane adduct is followed by rate-limiting C–S bond cleavage, yielding [Fe­(SMe)] and Me•. Rapid reaction between [Fe] and Me• provides the [FeMe] product. This proposed mechanism yields a rate law of 2K 1 k′[Me2S]­[Fe]/(K 1 + [N2]), where k′ is the product of Me2S binding (K 2) and rate-limiting bond homolysis (k 3, see SI Section 4.4 for derivation).

Exploring this reaction over a wider range of temperature (40–90 °C; Figure D) and normalizing for K 1 and the concentrations of Me2S and N2 (see SI Section 4.4) allows for an Eyring analysis that isolates k′, to give activation parameters ΔH ‡′ = 12.2 ± 0.8 kcal mol–1; ΔS ‡′ = –33 ± 2 e.u. The moderate but positive ΔH ‡′ is consistent with C–S bond homolysis being a significant contributor to the rate of the reaction, , while the negative ΔS ‡′ likely reflects the required binding of Me2S.

We next explored the reduction of the other substrates of mar (Scheme ). Reacting [Fe­(N2)] with EtSMe (2 equiv) yields a mixture of [Fe­(SMe)] (40 ± 4%) and [Fe­(SEt)] (12 ± 2%), in addition to CH4, C2H4, and C2H6, in 13.2 ± 0.2, 16.3 ± 1.3, and 31.7 ± 1.6% yield, respectively (see SI Section 5.3 for mechanism). Reductive C–S bond cleavage of MeSC2H4OBn (2 equiv), as an aprotic analogue of MeSC2H4OH, again yielded a mixture of the expected iron thiolates, with [Fe­(SMe)] and [Fe­(SC2H4OBn)] formed in 53 ± 2 and 14 ± 1% yield, respectively. Additionally, ethylene and benzyl alcohol were both released in 71% yield, while methane was formed in 11% yield. These product profiles are dependent on the reaction stoichiometry. For example, reaction of [Fe­(N2)] with 2 equiv EtSMe yields a 3.3:1 ratio of [Fe­(SMe)] and [Fe­(SEt)] while 100 equiv EtSMe yields a 10.1:1 ratio. Unlike the enzyme, , which exclusively yields MeSH and hydrocarbon products, [Fe] cleaves both CMe–S and CR–S bonds. Nonetheless, the formation of MeS-fragments by cleavage of the weaker C–S bond is always favored.

Reduction of Native mar Substrates by [Fe­(N2)]

We also explored substrates beyond those reduced by mar. For instance, reacting [Fe­(N2)] with excess PhSMe (10 equiv) in C6D6 at 60 °C yields a 1:1 mixture of [Fe­(SPh)] (Figure S10) and [FeMe]. By contrast, reactions with PhSRwhere R = Et, n Pr, i Pr, s Buyield only [Fe­(SPh)] and a mixture of hydrocarbon products. Despite this divergence in products, the log of the rate of [Fe­(N2)] consumption in the presence of PhSR correlates strongly with the computed C–S bond dissociation free energy (BDFE calc, Figure A).

(A) Rate-driving force analysis of C–S bond cleavage. All rates are the average of 2 or more runs monitored by the pseudo-first-order rate of [Fe­(N2)] consumption. (B) Radical clock experiment yields isomerized olefins. Control reaction without [Fe] does not yield ring-opened products. [Fe­(N2)] isomerizes terminal olefins.

An explanation consistent with these observations is that the reduction of PhSR substrates shares early and limiting steps with the reduction of Me2S, but the product profiles diverge due to more complex reactivity profiles of the resulting R• radical with excess substrate containing β-hydrogens. We also note that while the rate of [Fe­(N2)] consumption by PhSR correlates with C–S bond strength, if this were the only contributor to the rate of bond cleavage, the slope of this rate-driving force analysis would be expected to be −1/RT (−1.51 mol kcal–1 at 60 °C). Instead, the slope has a significantly smaller magnitude (−0.24 mol kcal–1) and is inversely correlated with the substrate steric trend, further supporting the conclusion that pre-equilibrium binding is also a major contributor to the rate of bond cleavage. Accordingly, sterically encumbered t BuSPh does not react with [Fe­(N2)], likely due to poor binding to [Fe].

Reacting ((trans)-2-phenylcyclopropyl)­methyl phenyl sulfide with [Fe­(N2)] at elevated temperature (60 °C) led to rapid and clean formation of [Fe­(SPh)] and ring-opened alkene products (Figure B). In addition to the expected product, 4-phenyl-1-butene (38.8 ± 1.3%), the isomerized alkenes 4-phenyl-2-butene (31.2 ± 0.9%), and 1-phenyl-1-butene (8.1 ± 0.2%) were detected (Figure B, entries 1 and 2). Incubation of 4-phenyl-1-butene with [Fe­(N2)] yielded this isomeric mixture in a similar ratio, suggesting that [Fe­(N2)] induces the isomerization of 4-phenyl-1-butene formed by cyclopropane ring-opening (Entry 3). The opening of this radical clock (k (25 °C) = 4·1011 s–1) provides additional evidence in support of the proposed C–S bond homolysis from [Fe­(S­(R)­Ph)] to furnish an organic radical.

Benzyl phenyl and dibenzyl sulfides proved useful substrates to explore Hammett parameters associated with C–S bond cleavage. A series of benzyl-substituted phenyl benzyl sulfides (PhSRBn) underwent cleavage by [Fe­(N2)] at room temperature in under 5 min to give an equal mixture of the [Fe­(SPh)] and [FeRBn]. In intermolecular competition experiments, the relative rates of consumption of starting materials PhSBn and PhSRBn correlated well with σ–, with a ρ value of +0.45, suggesting the buildup of negative charge at Cbenzyl in the transition state (Figure A). Further, the correlation with the ratio predicted by the computed Cbenzyl–S BDFEs was found to be poor (Figure S37). A consistent interpretation is that the transition state for C–S bond cleavage from [Fe­(SRBnPh)] is early and represents primarily charge transfer from [Fe] to PhSRBn, with associated buildup of negative charge at Cbenzyl. This shift from a late C–S bond homolysis transition state for Calkyl–S substrates to an early transition state for Cbenzyl–S compounds is perhaps unsurprising, given the significantly weaker C–S bond for PhSRBn (BDFE = 34 kcal mol–1). However, the shallow slope (ρ = +0.45) suggests substrate coordination also contributes to the relative rates of thioalkane reduction.

(A) Intermolecular Hammett competition experiment. R = −OMe, −Me, − t Bu, −F, −H, −OCF3, −CF3, −CO2Me. (B) Intramolecular Hammett competition experiment demonstrates a break between electron-releasing (σ+) and electron-withdrawing (σ–) substituents. R = −NMe2, −OMe, −Me, −F, −H, −OCF3, −CF3, −CO2Me.

To gain insight directly into the transition state of bond cleavage, an intramolecular competition of asymmetric dibenzyl sulfides (RBnSBn) was conducted. Reacting [Fe­(N2)] with such substrates gave a mixture of [Fe­(SBn)], [Fe­(SRBn)], [FeBn], and [FeRBn], with the ratio of products recorded by quantitative 1H NMR spectroscopy. The resulting Hammett plots displayed a break between electron-releasing and withdrawing substrates, correlating best to σ+ (ρ = −0.19) and σ– (ρ = +1.46), respectively (Figure B). Electron-withdrawing substrates also showed a strong correlation and ρ = 0.98 for C–S bond cleavage ratios predicted solely by the BDFE of the respective C–S bond (Figure S43). Notably, for electron-poor substrates, in the absence of substrate coordination pre-equilibria, larger ρ­(σ–) and ρ­(BDFE) are observed. The break in the Hammett plot (Figure B) from electron-withdrawing to electron-releasing substrates indicates a change in the mechanism of the C–S cleavage step from a radical to a more carbocation-like transition state. Similar breaks in Hammett plots have been observed in other one-electron oxidative additions of 3d transition metals. ,

To summarize, these Hammett studies corroborate our previous conclusions. The relative rate of substrate reduction is generally favored as sulfide substrates become easier to reduce. However, the difference in ρ­(σ–) between inter- and intramolecular competition experiments (0.45 and 1.46, respectively) indicates that substrate coordination also contributes to the relative rate of substrate consumption. While carbocation stabilization drives reactivity for electron-releasing benzyl groups, we note that the large difference in redox potential between [Fe­(SMe)] (E 1/2°(Fe+/0) = −2.4 V vs Fc+/0, see SI Section 14.1) and Me• (E(Me+/•) > 2 V vs Fc+/0) suggests a heterolytic mechanism is not involved in the dialkyl substrates.

Discussion

We have demonstrated a single-site iron complex capable of the reduction of Me2S to form CH4 and MeSH. Experimental data are consistent with homolytic bond cleavage from [Fe­(SMe2)], wherein a methyl radical and [Fe­(SMe)] are released as products. To our knowledge, similar homolytic activations of alkyl sulfides are limited to some ill-defined older studies with M­(CO)6 (M = Mo, W) at high temperatures over prolonged time periods (e.g., desulfurizing dibenzyl sulfide to produce bibenzyl) − and an interesting recent study of electrochemical activation of aryl alkyl sulfides (ArSR).

To gain further insight into the C–S bond cleavage step, we turned to computational methods employing the TPSS functional, paired with the def2-TZVP basis set for iron and the def2-SVP basis set for all other atoms, which reliably reproduces experimentally derived BDFEs of similar iron compounds. , Using this method, we find that homolytic C–S bond cleavage from S-bound [Fe­(SMe2)] is favored over heterolytic and oxidative addition pathways, as well as SN 2 or radical substitution at CMe (see SI Section 17.2).

Transition states for CMe–S bond homolysis from [Fe­(SMe2)] (S = 1) to [Fe­(SMe)] (S = 3/2) and Me• (S = 1/2) were found in both the S = 1 and S = 2 states, with the S = 2 state favored slightly (ΔG S=1 ‡ = 30 kcal mol–1; ΔG S=2 ‡ = 28 kcal mol–1). In the favored transition state, a CMe–S distance of 2.32 Å and methyl ∠H–C–H bond angles averaging 114° indicate significant sp2 character on the leaving methyl group. Further, DFT predicts a KIE (k H/k D = 2.3) similar to that measured experimentally. Comparing the computed Eyring parameters for C–S bond homolysis from [Fe­(SMe2)] (Figure A; ΔH ‡ = 30.5 kcal mol–1 ΔS ‡ = +7.5 e.u.) to those measured for k′ (combination of Me2S binding and cleavage; ΔH ‡′ = 12.2 ± 0.8 kcal mol–1; ΔS ‡′ = −33 ± 2 e.u.) suggests that the Me2S binding step lowers the activation enthalpy but with an associated entropic cost. In addition, the release of Me• is uphill, suggesting that follow-up Fe–Me formation is required to drive the reaction forward.

(A) Calculated free energy diagram for C–S bond cleavage of Me2S by [Fe]. (B) Bond weakening of Me–SR bonds upon coordination to [Fe].

It is useful to consider this C–S bond cleavage within the framework of coordination-induced bond weakening (Figure B). Upon coordination to [Fe], the computed BDFEC–S of Me2S changes from 57 to 13 kcal mol–1, representing a remarkable decrease in bond strength of 44 kcal mol–1. Similarly, the bond strength of PhSMe decreases upon [Fe] coordination from 49 kcal mol–1 for PhS–Me to 9 kcal mol–1 for [Fe­(S­(Ph)–Me)].

Comparison to Fe-Catalyzed C–S Bond Cleavage

Given our findings, it is interesting to compare enzymatic Fe-catalyzed C–S bond cleavages to the reactivity of [Fe] with thioalkanes. An important class of enzymes that cleave C–S bonds are radical SAM enzymes, which reduce the sulfonium, S-adenosyl-l-methionine (SAM), to generate the 5′-deoxyadenosyl radicals that perform a host of important biological H atom abstractions (Figure A). − Specific features of the SAM-substrate allow it to be rapidly reduced by the Fe4S4 cluster active site of radical SAM enzymes. , The amino acid component of the methionine group of SAM binds the Fe4S4, allowing for close contact between the sulfonium group and the cluster, despite sulfoniums being poor ligands. EPR evidence shows that following sulfonium reduction (E pc(SAM+/0) = −1.8 V vs Fc+/0), the organic radical is trapped by Fe, which is thought to drive the initial reduction forward. Subsequently, the organic radical is released for downstream reactivity.

Isosurfaces printed at 0.0015 e–/bohr3, with blue indicating a decrease in electron density and red indicating an increase in e– density. (C) Sketch depicting the possible site of substrate binding to the mar cofactor.

Pathways for the reduction of SAM are not likely available to the reduction of Me2S (Figure A); Me2S is much more difficult to reduce (E(Me2S0/–) < −4.5 V), and it does not have additional functional groups that might facilitate binding to an enzyme. However, Me2S is a considerably better ligand than a sulfonium. In this context, the cleavage of the C–S bond of Me2S by [Fe­(N2)] (E(Fe+/0) = −1.45 V) provides a plausible model for how Me2S cleavage might occur at the active site of mar.

Nonetheless, we note a few differences between the chemistry of [Fe] and mar, particularly relating to selectivity. Unlike [Fe], mar yields exclusively MeSH when MeSR products are reduced. Furthermore, while only methyl thioalkane reductions by mar have been reported, [Fe] can react with a wider range of sulfides. These differences may be attributed to the substrate channel in mar, which may limit the access of larger substrates and direct the approach of the native substrates to favor MeSH and alkane products.

Comparison to Fe-Catalyzed N2 Reduction

Given the similar active site structure of mar and FeFe-nitrogenase, as well as the ability of [Fe] to both homolytically cleave C–S bonds and reduce N2, a comparison of N2 and thioalkane ligands is useful. Thioalkanes and end-on-bound N2 are both σ-donors, through S and N lone pairs, and π-acceptors, through C–S σ* and N2 π* orbitals, respectively. , Further, π-backbonding is likely key to the activation of N2 and thioalkanes. In N2R, π-backbonding pushes electron density onto the N2 ligand, priming Nβ for N–H bond formation; in thioalkane reductions, donation into the C–S σ* orbital destabilizes the bond and facilitates homolysis (Figure B). As a point of comparison, [Fe­(N2)] does not activate the Cbenzyl–O bond of benzyl phenyl ether (PhOBn) on heating to 60 °C, although the C–O BDFEcalc (35.2 kcal mol–1) is similar to that of PhSBn (34.0 kcal mol–1) and much weaker than Me2S (57 kcal mol–1). We hypothesize that the failure to activate the C–O bond is due to this compound being a weaker π-acceptor than related sulfides.

To investigate this idea further, we analyzed the computed electronic structure of the intermediate, [Fe­(SMe2)]. We find a slight elongation of the C–S bond and a slight decrease in C–S bond order (see Table S23). Extended Transition State-Natural Orbitals for Chemical Valence (ETS-NOCV), − which allows the visualization and assessment of energy contribution of orbital interactions upon bringing two fragments together, was performed to understand the origin of these structural effects. Analyzing the bonding between [Fe] and SMe2, we find that the most favorable bonding interactions are σ-bonding in nature, between S and Fe (see isosurface plots in Figure B, where blue indicates a decrease of e– density and red indicates an increase in e– density), but notably with a considerable increase in electron density between Fe and B as well. The second largest bonding contribution involves the transfer of electron density from an Fe d-orbital to partially form an Fe–S π-bond, which also transfers electron density to the methyl groups. Similar, albeit stronger, π-accepting modes are also found between [Fe] and N2, indicating similar modes of activation of the two substrates (See SI Section 17.4). This model provides an explanation as to why seemingly disparate substrates, N2 and Me2S, can be reduced by the same complex ([Fe]) and by extension structurally similar enzymes (FeFe-nitrogenase and mar).

Conclusions

As noted in the work of our group − and others, − mononuclear Fe sites can support necessary intermediates for N2R as well as the reduction of other non-native substrates of nitrogenase, such as cyanide and acetylene. The work herein extends this idea to thioalkane reduction, demonstrating that coordination of MeSR to a mononuclear [Fe] facilitates C–S bond homolysis. Accordingly, mononuclear mechanisms for the reduction of substrates at nitrogenase-like cofactors continue to warrant consideration. In this vein, substrates can be initially activated at a single Fe site (Figure C), with the other Fe sites providing a means of redox leveling and possible stabilization of downstream intermediates, for example, in the case of mar by trapping of released R•.
