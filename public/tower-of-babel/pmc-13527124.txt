A capacitive photodiode for analogue and digital processing
Authors: Chengyou Wang, Zhuoran Wang, Wenhao Ran, Bo Che, Tao Chen, Bin Wei, Guozhen Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An optical wireless communication system requires signal preprocessing and computation, which typically rely on distinct analogue and digital modules. However, this module separation inevitably increases system footprint and fails to meet the space and power constraints of edge applications. Here, we report a capacitive photodiode that embeds both analogue filtering and digital processing functions. This device uses an antimony oxide dielectric layer formed in situ on an antimony sulfide light absorber. The resulting capacitor-photodiode structure exhibits enhanced charge accumulation at high frequencies, thereby realizing a high-pass filtering function that prevents eavesdropping. Meanwhile, the in-situ formation of the dielectric layer introduces anionic vacancies. Under high-energy photon illumination, these vacancies become ionized and dope the semiconductor. This wavelength-dependent photodoping inverts the built-in electric field, thereby reversing the diode polarity and enabling the XOR optoelectronic logic function. Finally, we demonstrate an optical wireless communication system using this capacitive photodiode as both an analogue sensing front-end to extract high-frequency signals from low-frequency noise, and a digital processing back-end to execute XOR logic-based decryption for signal recovery and secured actuator control.

Introduction

Optical wireless communication (OWC) has been key to emerging edge applications such as underwater exploration, laser communication, and light-fidelity1–3. The advent of the Artificial Intelligence of Things (AIoT) further demands the seamless integration of sensing and communication within OWC systems to enable smart connections4,5. This typically involves multiple interconnected nodes, each handling distinct tasks. For example, a sensing node perceives desired analogue information from the physical world, which is then digitally encoded and optically transmitted. A distant processing node subsequently receives the optical signal and reconstructs the original information. To implement such tasks, analogue filtering is required at the sensing node to suppress low-frequency interference and noise6,7, prior to digital information processing. These functions are conventionally implemented using separate modules for optical sensing, analogue signal conditioning, and digital signal processing5. However, this module separation inevitably increases the system footprint, posing constraints for adaptation to space-limited edge nodes in the AIoT8–10. In contrast, a multifunctional device that integrates sensing, analogue and digital processing functions is a compact and spontaneous solution.

Emerging device physics has demonstrated the feasibility of embedding either analogue or digital processing in an optical sensor11–16. For instance, in-sensor analogue filtering has been realized through differential subtraction between two photodiodes of distinct frequency characteristics17. Optoelectronic digital logic has also been achieved via discrete state transitions, such as inversion of the built-in electric field direction or reversal of the dominant charge carrier type18–21. However, these two paradigms are enabled by fundamentally different mechanisms: analogue processing requires gradual modulation of signal amplitude to represent continuous output, while digital logic requires abrupt polarity switching to represent binary 1 and 0. Consequently, simultaneously satisfying both requirements with a single device platform is very challenging.

To address this fundamental challenge, in this article, we propose a capacitive photodiode (CPD) that processes both analogue and digital signals. In this device, we use the frequency-dependent nature of the capacitive interface and monopolar current blocking of the diode to realize analogue high-pass filtering. At the same time, the in-situ formation of the capacitive layer induces defective states that persistently dope the semiconductor upon ionization by high-energy photons. This enables discrete state transitions that are the basis for digital operations. Finally, we demonstrate a proof-of-concept OWC system as shown in Fig. 1a. This system employs this capacitive photodiode (CPD) for analogue and digital processing. Specifically, this CPD first extracts a high-frequency signal from low-frequency interference to realize simultaneous sensing and analogue processing. After analogue-to-digital conversion (ADC), the digitized information is encrypted, which modulates the lasers for free-space optical transmission. Results

Working principles of the capacitive photodiode

The CPD features a photodiode-capacitor configuration, which is layer-by-layer assembled by depositing a dielectric layer, a light absorber, a charge transport layer, and electrodes (Fig. 1b(i)). This device supports two distinct processing modes: analogue filtering and digital logic operation.

As shown in Fig. 1b(ii), the current-rectifying behavior of the diode results in an unbalanced charge-discharge rate. At low frequency, the fast charging and slow discharging produce an asymmetric transient photocurrent response (Idet). In this case, the relaxation time is sufficient to release the accumulated net charge. As a result, when connecting the CPD to a typical readout circuit comprising a low-pass filter and a transimpedance amplifier, the sampled photocurrent (Ismp) is limited.

In contrast, high-frequency optical input generates a symmetric transient photocurrent, which arises from the attenuation of the positive current amplitude. This attenuation indicates an incomplete charge-discharge process. Thus, it leads to accumulated net charge represented by the voltage U across the capacitor, which results in a high Ismp. Consequently, the CPD functions as an inherent high-pass analogue filter, characterized by frequency-modulated symmetry in the transient photocurrent. Achieving the same functionality with a regular photodiode would require external filtering circuits as shown in Fig. 1b(iii), inevitably increasing system complexity22.

Meanwhile, defective states that formed at the capacitive interface provide a discrete-state modulating channel for digital processing. When illuminated with high-energy photons, these vacancy defects become ionized, persistently doping the semiconductor interface. This localized photodoping can invert the built-in electric field, thereby reversing the polarity of the photodiode (Fig. 1c(i)). This behavior provides a valid model for digital processing that involves discrete state transitions, as demonstrated in Fig. 1c(ii).

Because ionization requires a threshold energy, this process is wavelength-dependent: only light of a certain wavelength or shorter can be used as bias light to induce photodoping, while a longer-wavelength input is used to read out the signal inversion. This configuration—two optical inputs and one electrical output—constitutes an optoelectronic logic gate, which is significantly simplified compared to conventional electrical or optical logic gate devices (Fig. 1c(iii))23,24.

Scanning electron microscopy reveals the nanorod array morphology of synthesized Sb₂S₃ (Fig. 2b(i, ii)). Transmission electron microscopy and X-ray diffraction (Fig. 2b(iii), c) show preferential growth along the [130] crystallographic direction with an interplanar distance of 0.36 nm. This quasi-1D absorber architecture is known to enhance light scattering and confine electron pathways27,28. The resulting regular photodiode (without capacitive layer) demonstrates a high peak quantum efficiency approaching 80%, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Detailed optoelectronic characterizations of the regular photodiode are provided in Supplementary Figs. 2–6.

To realize capacitive coupling, an in-situ oxidation strategy was implemented by treating the Sb₂S₃ nanorod array with oxygen plasma. The formation of an antimony oxide layer was confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Fig. 2d). After O₂ plasma treatment, both Sb 3 d₅/₂ and Sb 3 d₃/₂ peaks shift to higher binding energies (by ≈1.7 eV), indicating the formation of new Sb-O bond. Concurrently, the rise of O 1 s signal increases the intensity of the overlapped Sb 3 d₅/₂, while S 2p decreases substantially, indicating the incorporation of oxygen and the depletion of sulfur at the surface29,30. Furthermore, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy elemental maps (Fig. 2e) and line scans (Supplementary Fig. 7) prove the formation of an oxygen-rich shell surrounding an Sb₂S₃ nanorod. This indicates the conformal formation of antimony oxide, a high-k dielectric material, that serves as the capacitive interlayer directly coupled with the absorber31,32. The complete device structure is also revealed by secondary ion mass spectrometry depth profiling (Supplementary Fig. 8) and cross-section transmission electron microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Device characterization was then performed under zero external bias. In contrast to the rectangular photoresponses of the regular photodiode (Supplementary Fig. 3), the CPD exhibits spiking bipolar outputs across a wide spectral range (Supplementary Fig. 10), which correlates to the capacitive charging-discharging process. Supplementary Fig. 11 presents their magnified transient photocurrent profiles, where the CPD shows apparent asymmetry, indicating substantially altered frequency characteristics. Fig. 2f presents the frequency-dependent transient photocurrent, showing a clear photoresponse up to 2 MHz, while the regular photodiode exhibits low-pass characteristics with a 3 dB bandwidth of approximately 6 kHz (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Notably, the increase in modulating frequency leads to a notable asymmetry-to-symmetry transition in the bipolar photocurrent profiles. This is explained by the simplified equivalent circuit shown in the inset: the intrinsic polarity of the photodiode facilitates charging while impeding the discharging process, thereby generating this asymmetry, while high frequencies fail to enable complete charge-discharge cycles, leading to net charge accumulation at the capacitive interface. This accumulation could then be further read out as sampling signals, enabling the analogue high-pass filtering function.

In addition, the optoelectronic digital logic function is briefly demonstrated in Fig. 2g. Red light (635 nm) modulation generates positive light-on spikes and negative light-off spikes, whereas adding a constant green light (520 nm) bias leads to opposite polarities — negative light-on spikes and positive light-off spikes. This abrupt switching of polarity via optical wavelength modulation establishes the foundation for its digital processing capability. Furthermore, this polarity reversal is independent of modulation frequency as probed in Supplementary Fig. 13. Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the state-of-the-art optoelectronic device with analogue and/or digital processing functions, emphasizing the simultaneous accommodation of both gradual and abrupt state modulation while maintaining high bandwidth of the CPD.

Frequency-modulated photocurrent symmetry for analogue high-pass filtering

The frequency-dependent photocurrent asymmetry-to-symmetry transition and the accompanying charge accumulation lead to its high-pass filtering behavior. These accumulated charges can be sampled via a signal-conditioning circuit, as shown in Fig. 3a, resulting in spontaneous high-pass characteristics. Based on this circuit, the frequency response characteristics of the steady-state output current were tested under 635 nm light modulated from 100 Hz to 500 kHz, which exhibited high-pass characteristics in Fig. 3b. Details of the experiment and simulation are provided in Supplementary Fig. 14. In contrast, a control experiment using a regular photodiode did not show such frequency-dependent variation in the sampled output current. We further implemented this design in a raster-scanning imaging system. As the optical modulation frequency increased from 100 Hz to 10 kHz, the contrast of the reconstructed images progressively improved (Fig. 3c). The corresponding photocurrent line-scan profiles are provided in Fig. 3d for quantitative comparison, where the sampled photocurrent is substantially higher at higher frequencies. These results demonstrate the high-pass filtering capability of the CPD-based imaging system. To demonstrate the practical value of this improvement, we performed a convolutional neural network-based digit classification task (0-9) on the reconstructed images (details provided in Supplementary Fig. 16). As summarized in Fig. 3e, images acquired at higher modulation frequencies achieved superior recognition accuracy with fewer training epochs, confirming that the CPD delivers enhanced signal integrity and contrast at elevated frequencies.

This inherent high-pass filtering characteristic is advantageous for extracting high-frequency optical signals from low-frequency background noise or jamming signals. We further validated this potential application in Fig. 3f by showing that, through a processing pipeline at the OWC receiver terminal equipped with either a CPD or a regular photodiode, jamming signals can be used to prevent eavesdropping. The experimental setup is illustrated in Fig. 3g. Crucially, eavesdropping terminals employing conventional regular photodiodes obtained a blended image dominated by the false fish pattern (Fig. 3h(i)), while only receivers equipped with the CPD successfully captured the true five pattern (Fig. 3h(ii)). The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the regular photodiode and the CPD are 3.6 dB and 18.6 dB, respectively, indicating suppression of the low-frequency interference for the latter. Details for SNR calculation are provided in Supplementary Fig. 17.

Wavelength-modulated photocurrent polarity for digital logic operation

The digital processing functionality of the CPD was then systematically investigated. First, the effect of illumination wavelength was examined. As shown in Fig. 4a, when 1 kHz red (635 nm) light was applied as the modulating signal and either blue (450 nm) or green (520 nm) light was applied as constant bias, the photocurrent polarity reversed (Supplementary Movie 1). To elucidate the origin of such wavelength-specific polarity modulation, in-situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were applied to probe the light-vacancy interaction in Sb₂S₃ before and after oxygen plasma treatment. Figure 4b shows that both samples exhibit measurable signals from sulfur vacancies. Upon illumination, pristine Sb₂S₃ shows negligible changes in the peak profile, while the plasma-treated Sb₂S₃ shows substantially enlarged peak intensities only under green light illumination. This directly indicates the ionization of anionic vacancies, which generates unpaired charges33–35. Therefore, these in-situ results show that oxygen plasma treatment successfully introduces anionic vacancies that could be optically ionized. This ionization process requires the input photons to overcome an activation energy threshold, which thus explains the wavelength-dependent behavior.

This vacancy ionization is known to trigger a photodoping effect that could alter the local electric field. We further visualized this by mapping surface potential via Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM). The topographic map in Supplementary Fig. 18 shows the nanorod array morphology, and the corresponding surface potential maps in Fig. 4c reveal the difference between dark and different illuminated conditions. Upon green light illumination, the sample surface shows a clear decrease in contact potential (dropping by ~0.5 V) compared to dark conditions, representing an increase in its work function. By contrast, red light failed to trigger an apparent potential change.

Different light interaction volumes are another factor that determines the wavelength specificity. Supplementary Fig. 19 presents the absorption coefficient of the nanorod film of roughly 1.2 μm in thickness. It is clear that 600 nm is around the critical wavelength after which light absorption begins to drop. This suggests differing light penetration depths for 520 nm and 635 nm. As a result, defect ionization occurs primarily at the Sb₂S₃-Sb₂Ox interface, whereas conventional band-to-band optoelectronic transitions can take place throughout the entire absorber.

Therefore, by considering depth-limited photodoping, we performed TCAD modeling to explain this mechanism. First, Fig. 4d compares the light penetration depth between green and red light. The strong interaction of high-energy photons at the absorber-dielectric interface (near 0 μm position) indicates localized photodoping behavior. Thus, the increase in positive charge concentration at the interface inverts the band bending direction from upward (i) to downward (ii) upon green light illumination. Details of the TCAD modeling are provided in Supplementary Fig. 20 and Supplementary Table 2. The mechanism behind the polarity inversion is thereby summarized in Fig. 4e: high-energy photons induce sulfur vacancy ionization at the interface, which locally dopes the absorber and eventually inverts the built-in electric field. As a result, the diode polarity reverses.

Thus, this mechanism enables a three-terminal device architecture consisting of dual optical inputs and one electrical output, as shown in Fig. 4f. Different combinations of the inputs lead to modulated output polarity, spontaneously constituting an optoelectronic logic gate. For XOR, Input 1 is defined by the transient edge of modulated red light (signal increase = 1, signal decrease = 0), and Input 2 is defined by the steady state of the green light bias (high signal = 1, low signal = 0). When Input 2 is in the 0 state, the output polarity agrees with Input 1, while Input 2 in the 1 state reverses the output polarity. This spontaneously reproduces the XOR truth table in Fig. 4g(i). The operational stability of this device in performing XOR has also been confirmed as shown in Supplementary Fig. 21 and Supplementary Fig. 22. In addition, by alternatively exploiting photocurrent superposition, this CPD can also perform AND and OR operations (Fig. 4f(ii) and g(ii)).

CPD implemented analogue-logic optical communication system

Finally, we demonstrate a complete OWC system involving both analogue and digital processing in Fig. 5a. These two functions are implemented by the CPD as both an analogue anti-interference sensing front-end and a digital logic processing back-end. Specifically, the input analogue signal, for example, a high-frequency barcode pattern mixed with low-frequency interference, is first sensed and processed by a front-end CPD. Its inherent high-pass filtering function enables anti-interference sensing, providing the OWC system with the desired analogue information, such as denoised patterns and images. This denoised barcode image is then converted to its digital format (e.g. 01000010) via ADC. The filtered signal is then encrypted to modulate the red and green lasers for transmission. Upon reception, a back-end CPD performs XOR decryption and eventually recovers the digital signal. The encryption and decryption procedures are then summarized in Fig. 5b and c. For example, when sending original code 01000010 (the ASCII code for the letter B), a periodic sequence 01010101 of the same binary length was prepared as optical clock. This optical clock itself modulates the red laser, and at the same time, excutes XOR operation with the original code 01000010 to generate the encrypted signal 00010111 to modulate the green laser (Fig. 5b(i)). To enhance resistance against replay attacks, identical pseudo-random time delays are synchronously inserted into both the optical clock and the encrypted signal (Fig. 5c(i)).

Upon receiving both red and green signals that are timely aligned, the back-end CPD spontaneously performs XOR decryption via dual-band induced polarity inversion (Fig. 5c(ii)). Meanwhile, a secret key identical to the encrypted signal (00010111) is sent via a separate channel, which is used to remove the redundant digits induced by green light edges (Fig. 5b(ii)). Afterwards, a simple subtraction was applied to recover the original information (01000010) (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Movie 2). Details are also given in Supplementary Fig. 23. This configuration establishes high security at the physical layer: even if the dual-wavelength optical signals are intercepted, neither wavelength alone conveys complete information36.

Furthermore, we also demonstrate the practical use of this system in the secured control of an actuator. In Fig. 5c(iii) and e, a robotic hand was connected to the OWC receiver back-end. Under red light illumination alone, the CPD output failed to produce a valid decrypted command, leaving the robotic gesture unchanged. When both red and green light were received simultaneously, the CPD-based back-end successfully recovered the original command, which made the robotic hand change its gesture from 1 to 2 (Supplementary Movie 3).

Discussion

We have reported a capacitive photodiode for analogue and digital processing. The device consists of an antimony oxide dielectric layer formed in situ on an antimony sulfide absorber layer. This dielectric layer enables capacitive coupling within the photodiode. Leveraging the frequency-dependent response of the capacitive layer and the monopolar current blocking behavior of the diode, the device exhibits an asymmetry-to-symmetry transition in its photocurrent profile. This transition results from charge accumulation induced by high modulation frequencies. The accumulated charge is read out as an elevated photocurrent at high frequencies, demonstrating an inherent high-pass filtering behavior. We validated this functionality in a mixed-frequency imaging system, where the device successfully extracted high-frequency signals from low-frequency jamming—a feature potentially useful for anti-eavesdropping communication.

Meanwhile, the in-situ formation of the dielectric layer introduces anionic vacancies. Upon illumination by high-energy photons, these vacancies become ionized and dope the absorber. Moreover, because the light penetration depth is highly wavelength-dependent, this photodoping remains localized near the interface, enabling the inversion of the built-in electric field. This mechanism enables digital processing for optoelectronic logic operations. Finally, we demonstrate an optical communication system using the capacitive photodiode for analogue filtering-based denoising and digital logic-based decryption and actuator control.

Methods

Preparation of Sb₂S₃ nanorod arrays

The Sb₂S₃ nanorod arrays were prepared using a repeated spin-coating and annealing method with a tiny-seed-assist, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 24. The process began with fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass substrates, which were cleaned with oxygen plasma to remove organic contaminants and enhance adhesion. A thin Sb seed layer with a thickness of approximately 150 nm was then deposited onto the FTO via thermal evaporation. The spin-coating solution was prepared by dissolving SbCl₃ in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) at a concentration of 0.8 mol/L, followed by the addition of thiourea at an Sb-to-thiourea molar ratio of 1:1.8. Prior to spin-coating, the FTO/Sb substrate was treated again with oxygen plasma to improve film adhesion and ensure surface cleanliness. The spin-coating process was performed at 3000 rpm for 48 seconds, with the solution dropped onto the substrate and allowed to stand for 1 minute before initiation. Subsequent annealing was carried out in a glovebox using a stepped temperature profile: heating from room temperature to 150 °C in 3 minutes and holding for 5 minutes, then raising to 300 °C in 3 minutes and maintaining for 9 minutes, followed by natural cooling. This spin-coating and annealing cycle was typically repeated 2-3 times to obtain a high-quality Sb₂S₃ nanorod arrays.

Device fabrication

In the fabrication of the CPD device, an Sb2Ox dielectric layer was first constructed on the surface of the Sb₂S₃ nanorod array by subjecting it to oxygen plasma treatment for 15 minutes. Subsequently, the hole transport layer was prepared: CuSCN was dissolved in diethyl sulfide solvent at a concentration of 35 mg/ml and stirred at room temperature for over 20 minutes to ensure complete dissolution. The solution was then spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 38 seconds, followed by annealing at 80 °C for 5 minutes in a glovebox to form the CuSCN film. Finally, a top electrode was deposited via thermal evaporation, sequentially depositing a 5 nm-thick MoO₃ layer and a 15 nm-thick gold layer.

Materials and device characterization

The morphology of the Sb₂S₃ nanorods was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a JEOL JSM-7500F field emission scanning electron microscope operated at 10 kV. The nanorod structure was examined by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) on an FEI Tecnai G2 F30, as well as by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Advance. The material composition was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on a Thermo Escalab 250Xi. Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements were performed using an AFM (Grapes Tech HR AFM). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was conducted with a Bruker A300 10/12 spectrometer.

For photoresponse characterization, the regular photodiode was tested using a Keithley 1500 measurement system. The CPD was characterized using the 50 Ω input impedance mode of a Tektronix MSO46 oscilloscope. Absorbance measurements were carried out on a Shimadzu UV-2600 UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Raster-scanning imaging system

For spatial imaging, the photocurrent was recorded using a Keithley 2636B source measure unit while the device was mounted on a motorized translation stage controlled by a Zolix SC300-2B motion controller. The projected illumination pattern remained stationary through a photomask, and the device was moved beneath it by the motorized translation stage. This configuration enabled mapping of the illuminated areas, producing a spatial image of the photocurrent response. During measurement, the regular photodiode and the CPD each acted as the receiver and underwent 23,421 sampling events. The entire pattern was scanned in a continuous acquisition for each device. Both measurements were conducted under controlled environmental conditions, with temperature maintained at approximately 25 °C and relative humidity kept within the range of 40–60%.
