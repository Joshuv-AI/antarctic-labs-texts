Tips and Tricks for Hardware Removal and Allograft Fixation After a Failed Latarjet Procedure
Authors: Mikalyn T. DeFoor, Ryan J. Froom, Nate J. Dickinson, Colin P. Murphy, Max Vidal, Tomas Aguirre, Christopher Bine, Matthew T. Provencher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough relatively rare, recurrent anterior shoulder instability requiring revision surgery after a failed Latarjet procedure can result in complications such as graft malposition, graft osteolysis, broken hardware and/or infection. Revision surgery after a failed Latarjet is a challenge due to a combination of technical factors such as alteration of the native anatomic landmarks and neurovascular structures, presence of extensive scar tissue and adhesions, and limited anterior glenoid real estate for a revision bone block. This technique outlines a systematic approach to the failed Latarjet procedure with focus on removal of the prior hardware and graft and thorough preparation of the anterior glenoid for revision anterior stabilization.

This technique video shows our systematic and comprehensive approach to the failed Latarjet procedure with focus on removal of the prior hardware and graft, as well as thorough preparation of the anterior glenoid for revision anterior stabilization with a distal tibial osteochondral allograft. This case was performed at The Steadman Cinic in Vail, Colorado. This patient is a 34‐year‐old male with a history of over 15 years of recurrent left shoulder instability with dislocation events. His previous left shoulder procedures include an arthroscopic Bankart repair and subsequent Latarjet. He presents after exhausting physical therapy with persistent apprehension and instability with day‐to‐day use of the shoulder. On examination, he endorses posterior joint pain and positive apprehension, positive posterior load‐and‐shift, and a positive Kim test. Plain films of the left shoulder show evidence of a prior Latarjet procedure with intact hardware, as well as graft osteolysis and a Hill‐Sachs lesion. Computed tomography scan with 3‐dimensional reconstruction shows 30% of anterior glenoid bone loss, along with an engaging Hill‐Sachs lesion and further evidence of prior graft osteolysis. In the setting of recurrent, symptomatic shoulder instability with prior graft osteolysis and 30% of anterior glenoid bone loss, he was indicated for revision open anterior shoulder stabilization with hardware removal, distal tibial allograft, and subscapularis repair. In the revision setting, dense scar and adhesions often obscure the deltopectoral interval. Meticulous dissection and continual reorientation to the surgical field is necessary as the dissection progresses due to distorted or absent native anatomic landmarks, including the cephalic vein, tip of the coracoid, and conjoined tendon. Scar tissue and adhesions are debrided to allow for visualization of the underlying subscapularis tendon and its muscle borders. The subscapularis and anterior capsule are often densely adherent to each other and poorly defined in revision cases. Although attempts are made to separate the underlying capsule from the subscapularis muscle, the structures are frequently elevated as a single sleeve with the subscapularis peel. For complete exposure to remove the retained hardware, a glenoid neck retractor is placed medially and 2 blunt Hohmann retractors are placed superior and superior laterally about the glenoid. After complete hardware removal, a small Cobb elevator or a small osteotome can be used to remove the remaining bone block from the anterior surface of the glenoid. The conjoined tendon can be tagged for later tenodesis to the anterior capsule or pectoralis major tendon, or a tenotomy can be performed. The residual bone block is carefully dissected off the conjoined tendon with use of a fine pencil‐tip electrocautery device. Intraoperative fluoroscopy should be readily available for use as needed, as well as a screw removal set. Attention is turned to preparation of the glenoid for revision bone block. A 4.0 mm round high‐speed burr and rasp are used to decorticate the remaining sclerotic bone off the anterior glenoid surface back to a bleeding, flat surface. The remaining defect is then measured to appropriately size. Limited available native bone stock for subsequent fixation is often encountered. Screw purchase and fixation in the revision setting can be optimized by avoiding prior screw trajectory, upsizing screws and changing the trajectory of screw fixation in relation to the glenoid. For the first 6 weeks postoperatively, the patient will be immobilized in a padded abduction sling with passive external rotation limited to 30° and no internal rotation permitted past neutral for 6 weeks. He will be allowed passive and gentle active assisted forward flexion to 90°, as well as abduction to 60°. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70132.

The Latarjet procedure was introduced in 1954 to address shoulder instability with anterior glenoid bone loss. 1 The anatomic and biomechanical benefits include stability from reconstruction with the osseous graft, the sling effect from the subscapularis and conjoint tendons, and the anterior capsular closure. 2 Although high success rates are reported after Latarjet, complications include neurovascular injury, infection, recurrent shoulder dislocation/subluxation, symptomatic hardware, broken hardware, and persistent pain. 3 , 4 These clinical symptoms may result from graft resorption and/or graft malposition, which may necessitate revision surgery. 4 , 5

Recurrent shoulder instability after open or arthroscopic Latarjet varies between 0% and 14%. 6 , 7 Recurrent instability in the setting of prior stabilization with an anterior bone block for glenoid bone loss and/or multiple prior surgeries poses a unique surgical challenge. The distortion of native anatomic landmarks, dissection through scar tissue after multiple revision surgeries, and limited bony real estate add to the complexity of a surgical case with an already elevated risk of axillary and musculocutaneous nerve injury and a limited margin of error for fixation. The purpose of this Technical Note is to provide surgical pearls and pitfalls for hardware removal and preparation of the glenoid for a revision anterior stabilization procedure in the setting of recurrent instability after a failed Latarjet.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

A detailed evaluation of patient factors, surgical technique, and/or pathology should be closely studied to determine the reason for failure of prior stabilization procedures, which includes through evaluation of advanced imaging (Figures 1 and 2).

Patient Positioning and Surgical Approach

The patient is positioned in the beach‐chair position with the head elevated to approximately 40° under general anesthesia. The operative extremity is positioned free on a well‐padded Mayo stand. Fluoroscopy is available as needed for intraoperative assessment.

A standard deltopectoral incision is made through the prior surgical incision. In the revision setting, dense scar tissue and adhesions often obscure the deltopectoral interval, and the cephalic vein may be distorted in its anatomic location, encased in scar or completely absent. Furthermore, native anatomic landmarks may be distorted or absent, such as the tip of the coracoid process and the conjoint tendon, necessitating meticulous dissection and continuous reorientation to the surgical field as the dissection progresses. When present, the cephalic vein is identified and mobilized laterally.

The deltopectoral interval is further developed with blunt dissection, and as the deltoid is retracted laterally, the underlying clavipectoral fascia is exposed and incised in line with the skin incision. Scar tissue and adhesions are debrided to allow full visualization of the underlying subscapularis tendon and its muscle borders. The axillary nerve is gently palpated, and neurolysis of the axillary nerve can be performed for safe mobilization and protection during the glenoid exposure.

Subscapularis and Capsular Management

The subscapularis and anterior capsule are often densely adherent to each other and poorly defined in revision cases, particularly after multiple prior surgeries. A subscapularis peel is performed by elevating the tendon directly off its insertion on the lesser tuberosity leaving a 3 to 4 mm cuff for repair. Attempts are made to separate the underlying capsule from the subscapularis if a native tissue plane can be identified. However, these structures are frequently elevated as a single sleeve. A tagging suture is placed in the subscapularis tendon to facilitate retraction and later repair. In cases with focus on bone loss of the anterior glenoid rim without humeral bone loss, a subscapularis split or peel may be performed. However, in cases requiring access to the posterior glenoid or reconstruction of bipolar bone loss requiring humeral head bone grafting, a subscapularis peel or lesser tuberosity osteotomy improves exposure.

The anterior capsular layer, often attenuated or previously violated, is mobilized off of the anterior glenoid rim and neck and reflected medially with the subscapularis. After medial retraction of the subscapularis and capsule, the combination of a glenoid neck retractor placed medial and 2 blunt Hohmann retractors about the superior and superolateral glenoid allows full visualization of the glenoid face and previous coracoid bone block fixation (Figure 3). Throughout the glenoid exposure, it is exceptionally important to protect both the axillary and musculocutaneous nerves which can be distorted after an open Latarjet. LaPrade and colleagues 8 showed the minimum medial distance to the face of the glenoid after a Latarjet to be 19.8 mm for the axillary nerve, 23.6 mm for the posterior cord of the brachial plexus, and 24.4 mm for the musculocutaneous nerve.

Hardware Removal and Bone Block Extraction

Following complete exposure of the anterior glenoid, the previous coracoid bone block hardware is identified and removed using a matching cannulated screwdriver, along with any previous anchors. There are multiple methods of fixation in a coracoid transfer, typically 3.5 mm/4.0 mm cannulated or solid screws or cortical suture button fixation. 9 , 10 It is important to determine the type of hardware used in the index procedure as part of the preoperative evaluation to ensure the necessary equipment is available for removal. In the setting of prior screw fixation, a Universal Broken & Stripped Screw Extraction System (Shukla Medical, St. Petersburg, FL) should be readily available for stripped or broken hardware. To prepare the anterior glenoid surface, the remaining coracoid bone block is carefully osteotomized off of the anterior glenoid rim using either a small Cobb elevator or a small (6 mm) straight osteotome (Figure 6), taking care to minimize damage to the underlying glenoid surface and avoid pass point between the 3 o'clock and 6 o'clock position due to the close proximity of the axillary nerve. The conjoined tendon is tagged and is peeled away from the previous bone block, which can be aided with a fine pencil tip electrocautery device (Figure 7).

Glenoid Preparation

With the previous bone block and hardware removed, focus is then turned to the anterior glenoid to create a surface prepared for revision reconstruction. The sclerotic bone of the residual anterior glenoid face is carefully decorticated using a high‐speed burr (4.0 mm round burr; Arthrex, Naples, FL) and rasped with a small bone rasp to create a flat, bleeding surface of cortical bone to optimize graft incorporation (Figure 8). The width and height of the anterior glenoid defect are then measured using a calibrated ruler to determine graft sizing.

Bone Block Grafting and Fixation

After preparation of the glenoid and prior to preparing the revision bone graft, careful attention should be paid to the position and size of prior screw holes after removal, which may have expanded during the removal process. Limited available native bone stock for subsequent fixation is often encountered. Screw purchase and fixation in the revision setting can be optimized by avoiding prior screw paths, upsizing screws (4.0 mm/4.5 mm) and changing the trajectory of screw fixation in relation to the glenoid. The addition of washers with screw fixation has also been shown to improve load to failure and peak torque during screw insertion. 11 Headless compression screws may also be used for supplemental fixation in the setting of poor bone stock or compromised graft fixation. Ultimately, the ideal position and trajectory for the new bone block are determined based on the available native glenoid real estate to avoid convergence with previous hardware trajectory while maximizing purchase for cortical fixation (Figure 9). Options of management of the conjoint tendon including tenodesis to the pectoralis major tendon or anterior capsule at the conclusion of the anterior glenoid reconstruction to potentially recreate the sling mechanism 12 or perform a tenotomy and allow retraction of the tendon to potentially minimize risk for neurovascular injury. 13

DISCUSSION

Although the Latarjet has shown excellent clinical outcomes overall, a small subset of patients will experience recurrent instability requiring revision anterior stabilization, reported in up to 3.4% of patients. 14 In this technical video, we show clinical pearls focused on surgical exposure in the revision setting, hardware and bone block removal and preparation of the anterior glenoid rim after a failed Latarjet revision to DTA (Video 1). A thorough and systematic debridement and complete removal of hardware and graft is required for optimal preparation of the glenoid to enhance outcomes and graft healing in the revision setting (Table 1).

The advantages of the DTA include an osteochondral surface, match to the native radius of curvature of the glenoid, lack of harvest site morbidity, larger bone graft size and promising short‐term clinical outcomes. 15 , 16 , 17 Provencher et al. 18 reported on the largest case series of 31 patients that underwent DTA following failed Latarjet at 4‐year follow‐up reporting no cases of recurrent instability, improvement in overall patient reported outcomes and complete graft union in 92% of patients.
