Osteoarthritis and depression a bidirectional TwoSample Mendelian randomization study on genetic causal association
Authors: Haozhu Chen, Jinmei Tan, Kaiwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpidemiological observations suggest a potential causal relationship between osteoarthritis and depression. Alternatively, this association might arise from the simple co-occurrence of osteoarthritis and depression due to shared genetic or environmental risk factors within the population. However, the causal nature of this relationship remains uncertain. This study aims to investigate the genetic causal relationship between osteoarthritis and depression. Methods: Two-sample single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) information was obtained from the published genome-wide association analysis (GWAS) database, selected appropriate SNP as the tool variable, conducted two-way MR analysis by inverse variance weighted method (IVW), weighted median method (WM) and MR-Egger regression, and conducted sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the results. Results: (1)Effect of osteoarthritis on depression: The IVW approach found that osteoarthritis increased the risk of depression (OR = 1.073,95%CI:1.026–1.122, P = 0.002). Effect of depression on osteoarthritis: IVW method found depression to increase the risk of osteoarthritis (OR = 1.115,95%CI:1.014–1.226, P = 0.025), MR-gger regression intercept test (P > 0.05); the results of heterogeneous test showed P-values > 0.05, no heterogeneity.(2)The results confirm that there is a causal relationship between osteoarthritis and depression, that is, osteoarthritis may be a risk factor for depression, at the same time, depression and osteoarthritis also have a causal relationship, that is, depression may be a risk factor for osteoarthritis. Conclusion: Osteoarthritis may have a bidirectional causal relationship with depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09872-0.Keywords: Depression, Osteoarthritis, Mendelian Randomization, Genome-Wide Association Study

Introduction

Depression is a prevalent mental disorder that significantly impacts both individual and public health. Recent epidemiological surveys indicate that the prevalence of depression is rising, currently affecting over 300 million people worldwide. The treatment of this disorder encounters numerous challenges and has profound effects on patients’ emotional well-being, physical health, daily functioning, and their ability to study and work [1]. Major symptoms include persistent low mood, loss of interest and pleasure, sleep disturbances, changes in appetite, and diminished self-worth [2]. Depression is closely linked with other chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and osteoarthritis [3–5]. Research has demonstrated a significantly increased incidence of osteoarthritis among patients with depression [6–8]. Furthermore, a report from 2015 noted that approximately 21% of osteoarthritis (OA) patients also experience depression, and these comorbid patients incur higher medical costs [9, 10].

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent chronic joint disorder characterized by the progressive degeneration of joint cartilage and the proliferation of bone at the joint margins, resulting in symptoms such as pain, stiffness, and functional limitations [11, 12]. According to the latest epidemiological data, over 595 million people worldwide are affected by osteoarthritis, and this number is expected to rise further as the population ages in the coming decades [1]. Recent studies have indicated that the pain and associated disability caused by osteoarthritis may elevate the risk of depression through both biological and psychological mechanisms [13], suggesting a potential link between these two conditions. Research conducted by Hawker et al. identified a persistent association between pain and depression in elderly OA patients, providing partial insight into this phenomenon [14]. Therefore, addressing the mental health issues faced by OA patients is crucial for developing comprehensive treatment plans. Adopting a holistic approach to managing both physical and mental health may improve the overall health status and quality of life for these patients.

Previous studies have explored the association between osteoarthritis and depression, but most rely on observational data, making it difficult to establish causality. Furthermore, these studies often encounter challenges in interpretation due to numerous confounding factors such as age, sex, lifestyle, and genetic variables. To address these issues, this study employed a two-sample bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) design to investigate the potential causal relationship between osteoarthritis and depression. Mendelian randomization, akin to a randomized controlled trial, uses single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes [15, 16]. Observational epidemiological studies often suffer from biases, confounding, and reverse causation [17, 18], but Mendelian randomization analyses can effectively mitigate these issues. This study utilizes a bidirectional MR approach to thoroughly investigate the causal relationship between osteoarthritis and depression. This method allows for a more accurate assessment of the impact of osteoarthritis on depression, thereby providing stronger evidence for clinical interventions.

Materials and methods

Research design

We conducted a two-sample bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) study. In the forward MR analysis, osteoarthritis was considered the exposure factor and depression the outcome. Conversely, in the reverse MR analysis, depression served as the exposure factor and osteoarthritis as the outcome. Data for both exposures and outcomes were obtained from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) databases. All GWAS summary-level data were retrieved from the IEU Open GWAS Project. These data are publicly available and de-identified, and their use does not require special approval or data access permission. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained in the original GWAS studies. Suitable single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were selected as instrumental variables to investigate the causal relationship between osteoarthritis and depression using various statistical methods [19]. We retrieved GWAS data for depression and osteoarthritis from the IEU Open GWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The depression dataset included 462,933 samples and 9,851,867 SNPs, while the osteoarthritis dataset comprised the same number of samples and SNPs. This study has received ethical approval and informed consent; thus, no additional ethical approval is required. Detailed information is provided in Table 1.

Instrumental variables

In this Mendelian randomization study, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were selected as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer the causal relationship between exposure and outcome. The selection of IVs strictly followed the three core MR assumptions: (1) Relevance: SNPs must be robustly associated with the exposure; (2) Independence: SNPs must be independent of confounding factors; and (3) Exclusion restriction: SNPs influence the outcome only through the exposure and not via alternative biological pathways.

To satisfy the relevance assumption, SNPs associated with the exposure at the genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were selected. To ensure independence between variants and minimize bias due to linkage disequilibrium (LD), clumping was performed using an r² threshold < 0.001 and a physical distance of 10,000 kb. SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 were excluded to avoid instability caused by rare variants.

To reduce weak instrument bias, we calculated the F-statistic for each SNP using the formula F = β² / SE², and only SNPs with F-statistic > 10 were retained. The strength of each instrument was carefully evaluated before inclusion in the final analysis. In addition, candidate SNPs were screened using the PhenoScanner database to identify and exclude variants potentially associated with confounders or directly with the outcome, thereby reducing the risk of horizontal pleiotropy.

MR analysis

This study utilized three Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis methods to assess the relationship between exposure and outcomes: inverse variance weighted (IVW) [20], MR-Egger regression (ME) [21], and weighted median (WME) [22].The IVW method was used as the primary analysis because it provides the most efficient and unbiased estimates when all instrumental variables satisfy the MR assumptions. IVW combines the ratio estimates of individual SNPs using a weighted linear regression framework. To account for potential violations of the exclusion restriction assumption, MR-Egger regression was conducted to detect and adjust for directional horizontal pleiotropy through its intercept term. Although MR-Egger provides more robust estimates in the presence of pleiotropy, it typically has lower statistical power. The weighted median estimator was additionally applied, which can provide consistent causal estimates even if up to 50% of the instrumental variables are invalid.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the results and prevent potential pleiotropy and heterogeneity, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses [23]. Cochran’s Q test was used to quantify heterogeneity among SNPs, and a funnel plot was used for visualization. The intercept from MR-Egger regression and the global test from MR-PRESSO were applied to evaluate whether horizontal pleiotropy influenced the MR analysis results. Additionally, a leave-one-out analysis was performed to determine if a single SNP was driving the causal estimate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

We used R software (version 4.3.3, University of Auckland, New Zealand) along with the TwoSample MR package (version 0.5.11) for all statistical analyses [24]. All data were analyzed using the “TwoSampleMR” package in R version 4.3.3 .

Results

Impact of osteoarthritis on depression

Instrumental variables

We selected SNPs significantly associated with osteoarthritis (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) and excluded those in linkage disequilibrium (r² = 0.001, kb = 10,000). Using the PhenoScanner V2 database, we further excluded SNPs potentially associated with confounding factors or outcome variables. Consequently, one SNP (rs10745951) was excluded, and 51 SNPs meeting the three core assumptions were selected. Each SNP had an F-statistic greater than 10, indicating a low likelihood of weak instrument bias affecting the relationship between osteoarthritis and depression.

Mendelian randomization analysis results

Three Mendelian randomization (MR) methods—IVW, MR-Egger, and weighted median estimator (WME)—were employed to evaluate the causal relationship between osteoarthritis and depression risk. The IVW method revealed a significant positive association, suggesting that osteoarthritis increases the risk of depression (OR = 1.073, 95% CI: 1.026–1.122, P = 0.002). Detailed data are presented in Table 2; Fig. 2. This study primarily relies on the IVW results, though the overall effect direction was consistent across all three MR methods. Thus, we conclude that osteoarthritis is positively associated with an increased risk of depression. All three MR methods indicate that osteoarthritis elevates the risk of developing depression, as shown in Fig. 3.

Impact of depression on osteoarthritis

Instrumental variables

For the selection of instrumental variables and F-value testing, we identified 33 SNPs strongly associated with depression from the dataset ukb-b-12,064. All these instrumental variables had P-values less than 5 × 10⁻⁸, indicating a significant association with the exposure, thereby fulfilling the relevance assumption. By querying the PhenoScanner V2 database, we excluded one SNP (rs12034885). Ultimately, 32 SNPs were selected, all of which met the independence and exclusion restriction assumptions. The calculated F-values for these SNPs were all greater than 10, indicating the absence of weak instrumental variables.

Mendelian randomization analysis results

We employed three Mendelian randomization (MR) methods—IVW, MR-Egger, and weighted median estimator (WME)—to evaluate the causal relationship between depression and the risk of osteoarthritis. According to the IVW results, a positive association was found between depression and osteoarthritis, suggesting that depression increases the risk of osteoarthritis (OR = 1.115, 95% CI: 1.014–1.226, P = 0.025). Detailed data are presented in Table 3; Fig. 6. This study primarily relies on the IVW results, although the overall effect direction was consistent across all three MR methods. Therefore, it can be concluded that there is a positive association between depression and osteoarthritis, with depression acting as a risk factor for osteoarthritis. The effect direction indicated by all three MR methods suggests that depression increases the risk of developing osteoarthritis, as shown in Fig. 7.

Discussion

This study employed a bidirectional Mendelian randomization approach to analyze the GWAS dataset of European populations. The results indicate a causal relationship between osteoarthritis (OA) and the risk of depression (OR = 1.073, 95% CI: 1.026–1.122, P = 0.002). Additionally, a reverse causal relationship between depression and the risk of osteoarthritis was observed (OR = 1.115, 95% CI: 1.014–1.226, P = 0.025). The study concludes that there is a bidirectional causal relationship between osteoarthritis and depression, with osteoarthritis potentially serving as a risk factor for depression, and vice versa.

Research has suggested that obesity is a risk factor for both osteoarthritis and depression. Therefore, the high prevalence of osteoarthritis and depression may partially stem from shared risk factors [7]. Overweight and obesity are well-established risk factors for osteoarthritis and may directly or indirectly increase susceptibility to depression through complex mechanisms [25, 26]. The elevated prevalence of OA in individuals with obesity may be attributed to multiple factors, including biomechanical stress, inflammation, and metabolic influences. Moreover, obesity may lead to poor body image, low self-esteem, and social isolation, all of which are potential triggers for depression. Obesity also activates inflammatory pathways, including dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, which is closely associated with an increased risk of depression through both biological and psychological pathways.

Given that obesity and lower educational attainment are modifiable risk factors, controlling obesity may help prevent depression and positively impact patients with osteoarthritis. One study highlighted that the clinical features of osteoarthritis, such as joint pain, functional impairment, multi-site pain, and comorbidities, increase the risk of depression in individuals experiencing chronic pain and limited physical activity [27]. Both cross-sectional and cohort studies have shown that the severity of joint pain in OA patients correlates with the severity of depression [28, 29]. A longitudinal study suggested that managing pain, joint dysfunction, and other comorbidities may help alleviate depressive symptoms in OA patients [14]. Additionally, Kurt et al. reported that changes in pain severity over three months in patients with persistent back, hip, and knee pain could predict subsequent depression severity, and conversely, changes in depression severity over three months could predict future pain severity over the following 12 months [30]. These studies collectively reveal the complex and close relationship between osteoarthritis and depression. They not only provide a foundation for understanding the association between the two conditions but also underscore the importance of addressing mental health in patients with osteoarthritis. Timely identification and intervention of depressive symptoms in these patients are crucial for improving overall clinical outcomes.

The exact biological mechanisms linking osteoarthritis (OA) and depression remain unclear, but emerging research indicates a significant association between the two conditions. A study identified estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), SRY-box transcription factor 5 (SOX5), and glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1) as potential risk genes for major depressive disorder (MDD) and OA, providing important insights for treatment strategies in patients with both diseases [31]. The following are some relevant biological mechanisms. Growing evidence supports the link between estrogen and depression, with estrogen exerting its biological effects primarily through the intracellular activation of estrogen receptor α (ESR1) and estrogen receptor β (ESR2) [32]. Estrogen and its receptors are not only crucial for sexual development and reproductive functions but also play significant roles in other tissues such as bone. The expression of ESR1 in chondrocytes, stromal cells, and osteoblasts [33] suggests its potential role in OA. Genetic variants within ESR1 have been associated with OA, identifying ESR1 as one of the risk genes for OA [34]. Furthermore, SOX5 has been identified as a genome-wide risk gene for MDD [35], and research indicates that SOX5 is critical for the development of primitive cartilage and chondrogenesis [36]. SOX5 is also a genome-wide gene for heel bone density and may be related to osteoporosis. Studies have identified SOX5 as a novel genome-wide risk gene for OA, further supporting its involvement in OA [37]. The phenotypes of osteoarthritis include dysregulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism in chondrocytes, as well as cartilage homeostasis imbalance caused by pro-inflammatory factors and oxidative stress. GPX1 is a major antioxidant enzyme in osteoclasts and is highly expressed in chondrocytes. GPX1 plays an essential role in chondrocyte differentiation and cartilage formation [38]. An animal study demonstrated that GPX1 expression in the brain changes following chronic mild stress, and reduced GPX1 levels were associated with the severity of depression in patients [39, 40]. Increasing evidence indicates that immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation may link osteoarthritis (OA) and depression. OA involves inflammatory activation with elevated cytokines, while immune and neuroinflammatory processes are also central to depression pathophysiology [16, 41], These shared inflammatory pathways provide biological plausibility for a common immune-related mechanism underlying both conditions.

The shared genetic predisposition between depression and OA provides valuable insight into assessing the risk of OA in patients with depression and the risk of depression in patients with OA. From a clinical perspective, the identification of a potential bidirectional genetic association between osteoarthritis and depression underscores the importance of comprehensive comorbidity management. Routine psychological screening in patients with OA, particularly those with chronic pain and functional impairment, may facilitate early detection and intervention. Likewise, clinicians managing patients with depression should remain attentive to musculoskeletal symptoms that may indicate elevated OA risk. These findings support integrated and multidisciplinary care strategies addressing both somatic and psychological dimensions [42, 43]. Moreover, the shared genetic susceptibility observed in this study may guide future mechanistic research exploring common inflammatory and neuroendocrine pathways, potentially informing the development of therapeutic approaches targeting both conditions simultaneously.

Despite the rigorous methodological design and the advantages of Mendelian randomization (MR) in reducing confounding and reverse causation, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we applied stringent criteria for SNP selection and conducted multiple sensitivity analyses, residual horizontal pleiotropy cannot be entirely excluded, particularly given the complex and potentially overlapping biological pathways underlying osteoarthritis and depression. Second, the use of summary-level GWAS data limits our ability to perform stratified analyses by age, sex, or environmental exposures and precludes assessment of gene–environment interactions. In addition, genetic instruments reflect lifelong genetic liability rather than dynamic disease progression, and thus the estimated effects may not directly correspond to short-term clinical interventions.

Furthermore, the GWAS datasets for both OA and depression were derived from European populations, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups and geographic regions. Differences in phenotype definitions, diagnostic criteria, and case ascertainment within large biobank datasets may also introduce heterogeneity that is difficult to fully quantify. Although MR provides evidence consistent with causal inference under its assumptions, it primarily captures linear associations and cannot evaluate potential nonlinear effects or complex feedback mechanisms between traits. Therefore, further validation using diverse populations, longitudinal cohort studies, and complementary epidemiological or interventional approaches is warranted to confirm and extend these findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a causal relationship between osteoarthritis and depression, indicating that osteoarthritis may be a risk factor for depression. Simultaneously, there is also a causal relationship between depression and osteoarthritis, suggesting that depression may be a risk factor for osteoarthritis.
