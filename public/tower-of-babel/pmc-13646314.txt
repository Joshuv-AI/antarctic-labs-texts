Sex-Specific Associations of Helicobacter pylori Seropositivity and Intestinal Metaplasia with Colorectal Adenoma: A Korean Multicenter Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Hee Jin Kim, Nayoung Kim, Sung Eun Kim, Ju Yup Lee, Kwangwoo Nam, Hyun Joo Song, Wan Sik Lee, Seon Hee Lim, Hyun Jin Kim, Gwang Ho Baik, Min Woo Lee, Suck Chei Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMale sex is a recognized risk factor for colorectal adenoma, yet whether the associations of Helicobacter pylori, atrophic gastritis (AG), and intestinal metaplasia (IM) with colorectal adenoma differ by sex remains unclear. This study evaluated the sex-stratified and combined associations of anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity, AG, and IM with conventional colorectal adenoma.Materials and MethodsThis multicenter cross-sectional study included 806 participants from a Korean health-screening cohort who underwent gastroscopy with H. pylori testing and colonoscopy within 3 years. Anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity was determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, AG and IM were assessed endoscopically. Multivariable logistic regression models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, smoking, and alcohol consumption.ResultsColorectal adenoma was identified in 209 participants (25.6%). Anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity was independently associated with colorectal adenoma (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.848; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.323 to 2.581; p<0.001). AG was not significant after adjustment. IM was independently associated in a separate model (aOR, 1.648; 95% CI, 1.125 to 2.415; p=0.010), but showed only borderline significance in the combined model when including H. pylori seropositivity (aOR, 1.440; 95% CI, 0.973 to 2.131; p=0.068), whereas anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity remained significant. Participants with both H. pylori seropositivity and IM showed the highest odds of colorectal adenoma. In sex-stratified analyses, anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity was independently associated in males (aOR, 1.811; 95% CI, 1.147 to 2.860; p=0.011) but not in females, whereas IM was independently associated in females (aOR, 2.086; 95% CI, 1.129 to 3.856; p=0.019) but not in males. Formal interaction tests were not statistically significant.ConclusionsAnti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity and IM were associated with conventional colorectal adenoma, whereas AG was not significant after adjustment. Sex-stratified analyses suggested potential sex-related patterns, with H. pylori seropositivity appearing more prominent in males and IM appearing more prominent in females.

INTRODUCTION

Male sex is a well-established risk factor for colorectal adenoma and colorectal cancer (CRC) [1], with males showing a higher prevalence of colorectal neoplasia and a tendency toward earlier onset than females [2]. Although the mechanisms underlying this sex disparity remain incompletely understood, studies suggest that sex differences in colorectal carcinogenesis may involve both biological factors, such as hormonal, genetic, and molecular pathways, and gender-related behavioral factors [2]. From the perspective of sex-specific medicine, identifying factors that may differentially contribute to colorectal neoplasia risk in males and females is clinically important, particularly in health-screening settings.

Helicobacter pylori is a gram-negative bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucosa of approximately 50% of the global population [3]. If left untreated, chronic infection persists throughout the host’s lifetime and induces a progressive cascade of gastric mucosal injury including atrophic gastritis (AG) and intestinal metaplasia (IM), which may eventually progress to gastric cancer [3]. The prevalence and severity of H. pylori infection and H. pylori-related gastric mucosal alterations have been reported to differ by sex, with males generally showing higher infection rates and more severe gastric mucosal changes [45].

Beyond its established role in gastric carcinogenesis, accumulating evidence suggests that H. pylori infection may also promote colorectal carcinogenesis. Colorectal adenomas are considered the principal precancerous lesions of CRC, their early detection and treatment substantially reduce CRC incidence and mortality [6]. Recent meta-analyses have reported that H. pylori infection is associated with approximately 1.5-fold increased odds of colorectal adenoma and CRC, with the highest risk observed for advanced adenoma [78]. The proposed mechanisms include H. pylori-induced alterations in gut microbiota, promotion of systemic inflammation, hypergastrinemia, and creation of a cancer-promoting microenvironment in the lower gastrointestinal tract [910].

Whether the association between H. pylori seropositivity and colorectal adenoma differs between males and females, and whether this association is related primarily to H. pylori exposure itself or to downstream gastric mucosal changes, particularly AG or IM, remain unclear. Few studies have directly examined the roles of AG and IM in colorectal neoplasia, yielding inconclusive findings [1112]. These unresolved issues highlight the need for multicenter studies evaluating H. pylori seropositivity, AG, and IM in a sex-stratified manner to better characterize the gastric–colorectal link.

Most Korean epidemiological studies on the association between H. pylori infection and colorectal adenomas have been single-center investigations [13141516]. Moreover, few have evaluated H. pylori seropositivity, AG, and IM together while formally assessing sex as a potential effect modifier. As H. pylori seroprevalence in South Korea has gradually declined to approximately 41.5% [17] while CRC incidence continues to rise, clarifying these sex-stratified associations may have public health implications. Therefore, this Korean multicenter study aimed to evaluate the associations of anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity, AG, and IM with colorectal adenoma in a health-screening cohort, with particular attention to combined H. pylori/IM status and differences in these associations by sex.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study population

This multicenter cross-sectional study included adults who underwent gastroscopy with H. pylori test during routine health screening between January 2023 and March 2025 in South Korea. Among 1,933 participants, 1,158 who underwent colonoscopy within 3 years were included. After excluding participants with incomplete colonoscopy, poor bowel preparation, unavailable reports, previous colorectal or gastric surgery, previous CRC, or inflammatory bowel disease, 815 participants were included in the overall study population (Fig. 1). Nine participants with SSL-only lesions and no conventional adenoma were excluded from the primary analysis, leaving 806 participants for the primary conventional adenoma analysis.

2. Data collection and endoscopic assessment

Participants completed structured questionnaires on sociodemographic factors, smoking, alcohol consumption, medical history, medication use, diet, and family history. Laboratory parameters, including lipid profiles and fasting blood glucose, were collected. Endoscopic examinations were performed by experienced endoscopists at nine participating hospitals using standardized reporting forms. AG and IM were assessed endoscopically based on characteristic mucosal findings described in previous studies [18]. Gastric atrophy was identified endoscopically by visualization of submucosal vessels due to mucosal thinning in the antrum and/or corpus. IM was identified by whitish mucosal changes, plaques, patches, or homogeneous discoloration of the gastric mucosa. Colonoscopy-related variables included the number, size, and histologic type of colorectal polyps.

3. Assessment of H. pylori status

Serologic evidence of H. pylori exposure was defined as anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity. Anti-H. pylori IgG was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Genedia H. pylori ELISA; Green Cross Medical Science Corp.), with a cut-off value of 0.4 optical density at 450 nm. In a subset of participants, H. pylori status was additionally assessed by histological examination. Participants who were anti-H. pylori IgG negative but had a history of H. pylori eradication therapy were excluded to reduce potential misclassification of previous exposure.

4. Histologic classification of colorectal polyps and outcome definition

Colorectal polyps were classified according to the 2019 World Health Organization classification of tumors of the digestive system [19]. Conventional colorectal adenomas included tubular, tubulovillous, and villous adenomas, regardless of dysplasia grade. Serrated lesions included sessile serrated lesions (SSLs) and traditional serrated adenomas. Conventional colorectal adenoma was defined as the primary outcome because conventional adenomas and SSLs follow biologically distinct carcinogenesis pathways. SSLs were analyzed separately as a secondary outcome, and sensitivity analyses including SSLs as part of a broader colorectal neoplastic lesion outcome were performed.

5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation and categorical variables as numbers (%). Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test, Pearson’s chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Logistic regression analyses evaluated the associations of anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity, AG, and IM with colorectal adenoma. Because these factors are biologically related, they were evaluated using separate multivariable models to avoid overadjustment. Model 1 included anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity, Model 2 included AG, and Model 3 included IM. Model 4 included both anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity and IM and was regarded as the main combined model. All models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI)≥25 kg/m2, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Interaction by sex was tested using sex×gastric-factor product terms. Sensitivity analyses were performed by restricting the interval between gastroscopy and colonoscopy to ≤12 months and by including SSLs in the outcome. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) or adjusted ORs (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All tests were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp.).

6. Ethics statement

The present study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (Reg. No. B-2306-837-302). Informed consent was submitted by all subjects when they were enrolled.

RESULTS

1. Baseline characteristics of study participants

Among 815 participants, conventional colorectal adenoma was identified in 209 (25.6%), SSL in 13 (1.6%), and concurrent conventional adenoma and SSL in 4 (0.5%); no colorectal adenocarcinoma or traditional serrated adenoma was identified. After excluding nine participants with SSL-only lesions, the primary analysis included 806 participants, of whom 209 had conventional colorectal adenoma and 597 did not.

Baseline characteristics by sex are shown in Table 1. Age distribution was comparable between sexes. Males had a higher BMI, and a higher prevalence of current or past smoking, current or past alcohol consumption, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, high salt intake, and higher household income than females. Males also had higher triglyceride and fasting blood glucose levels, whereas females had higher total cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels.

Sex-stratified baseline comparisons by adenoma status are shown in Table 2. In both sexes, participants with adenoma were significantly older. In males, smoking, fasting blood glucose, AG, IM, and anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity were significantly more prevalent in the adenoma group. In females, IM and anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity were significantly more prevalent in the adenoma group, while AG was not significantly different between groups.

In the overall cohort, the proportion of colorectal adenoma was higher among participants with anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity than among those without (33.6% vs. 20.4%; p<0.001). Adenoma proportions were also higher in participants with AG (30.2% vs. 20.6%; p=0.003) and IM (39.8% vs. 22.0%; p<0.001) (Supplement Table 1).

2. Multivariable associations of H. pylori seropositivity, AG, and IM with colorectal adenoma in the overall cohort

The results of logistic regression analyses are shown in Table 3. In univariate analysis, H. pylori seropositivity, AG and IM were each significantly associated with colorectal adenoma. In multivariable Model 1, anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity was independently associated with colorectal adenoma (aOR, 1.848; 95% CI, 1.323 to 2.581; p<0.001). In Model 2, AG was not significant after adjustment (aOR, 1.200; 95% CI, 0.834 to 1.728; p=0.326), whereas IM was significant in Model 3 (aOR, 1.648; 95% CI, 1.125 to 2.415; p=0.010). In the main combined model including both anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity and IM, anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity remained significant (aOR, 1.720; 95% CI, 1.220 to 2.424; p=0.002), whereas IM showed borderline significance (aOR, 1.440; 95% CI, 0.973 to 2.131; p=0.068). No significant multicollinearity was observed, as all VIF values were below 2.2 (Supplement Table 2).

3. Sex-stratified multivariable analyses

Sex-stratified multivariable analyses are presented in Table 4. In the main combined Model 4, anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity remained significantly associated with colorectal adenoma in males (aOR, 1.811; 95% CI, 1.147 to 2.860; p=0.011), whereas IM was not significant. In females, IM was significantly associated with colorectal adenoma (aOR, 2.086; 95% CI, 1.129 to 3.856; p=0.019), whereas anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity showed a borderline association. However, formal interaction tests showed no statistically significant effect modification by sex (p for interaction: H. pylori×sex=0.944, AG×sex=0.858, IM×sex=0.201).

4. Combined association of H. pylori seropositivity, AG, and IM with colorectal adenoma risk, overall and by sex

Combined associations of anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity with AG and IM are shown in Table 5. In the overall cohort, the H. pylori-positive/IM-positive group showed the highest odds of colorectal adenoma (aOR, 2.406; 95% CI, 1.495 to 3.871; p<0.001), whereas AG without H. pylori seropositivity was not associated with adenoma (aOR, 1.173; 95% CI, 0.714 to 1.925; p=0.529). In males, H. pylori seropositivity was associated with increased odds of adenoma regardless of AG status (H. pylori+/AG-: aOR, 3.341; 95% CI, 1.455 to 7.671; p=0.004; H. pylori+/AG+: aOR, 2.195; 95% CI, 1.128 to 4.269; p=0.021) and was also significant when combined with IM (H. pylori+/IM+: aOR, 2.018; 95% CI, 1.093 to 3.724; p=0.025), whereas IM alone was not significant. In females, IM-positive groups showed higher odds of colorectal adenoma, including both the H. pylori-/IM+ group (aOR, 2.873; 95% CI, 1.230 to 6.710; p=0.015) and the H. pylori+/IM+ group (aOR, 2.994; 95% CI, 1.397 to 6.418; p=0.005), whereas AG-based combined groups were not statistically significant.

5. Sensitivity analysis

In a sensitivity analysis restricted to participants who underwent gastroscopy and colonoscopy within 12 months, 586 participants were included, of whom 179 had colorectal adenoma. H. pylori seropositivity remained independently associated with adenoma (aOR, 1.823; 95% CI, 1.236 to 2.688; p=0.002), and concurrent H. pylori seropositivity and IM continued to show the highest odds (aOR, 2.303; 95% CI, 1.334 to 3.975; p=0.003) (Supplement Table 3). When SSLs were included as colorectal neoplastic lesions, results were directionally consistent: H. pylori seropositivity remained significant (aOR, 1.711; 95% CI, 1.221 to 2.399; p=0.002) and IM showed borderline significance (aOR, 1.450; 95% CI, 0.984 to 2.136; p=0.060) (Supplement Table 4, 5).

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter cross-sectional study of health-screening participants from nine Korean institutions, anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity was consistently associated with conventional colorectal adenoma after adjustment for established risk factors. Among H. pylori-related gastric mucosal alterations, IM, but not AG, was associated with colorectal adenoma in the separate adjusted model, and participants with both H. pylori seropositivity and IM showed the highest odds of adenoma. The association for IM was attenuated in the combined model including H. pylori seropositivity, which may partly reflect the biological sequence linking H. pylori exposure and IM. Sex-stratified analyses suggested different patterns of association in males and females: anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity was independently associated in males, whereas IM was independently associated in females in the separate adjusted model. However, formal interaction tests were not statistically significant. To our knowledge, this is the first multicenter Korean study to evaluate H. pylori seropositivity, AG, and IM together within a sex-stratified analytic framework for colorectal adenoma risk.

These sex-stratified results are of biological interest, given the known sex differences in H. pylori infection prevalence, gastric mucosal severity [45], as well as the higher baseline risk and earlier onset of colorectal adenoma in males [2]. Higher smoking and alcohol consumption rates in males may amplify H. pylori-related colorectal neoplasia-promoting effects, while hormonal factors in females such as estrogen may modulate H. pylori-related mucosal inflammation [20], potentially contributing to the stronger association between IM and colorectal adenoma. However, because the present study was not designed to determine biological mechanisms, these potential explanations remain speculative. In addition, a previous study from the same cohort found distinct sex-related epidemiologic patterns for IM, but not AG, with a significantly earlier increase in males [21]. Given that the sex-by-factor interaction tests were not statistically significant, these sex-stratified findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating observations that warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies with pre-specified interaction analyses.

Our overall finding that H. pylori seropositivity was consistently associated with colorectal adenoma is in line with previous epidemiological studies and meta-analyses reporting increased risks of colorectal adenoma and CRC among individuals with H. pylori infection [78]. A recent meta-analysis of 55 studies demonstrated that H. pylori infection was associated with 47% increased odds of colorectal adenoma and 59% increased odds of CRC; notably, eradication reduced neoplasia risk by 56% [8]. Proposed mechanisms include hypergastrinemia, altered gastric acidity, gut microbiome changes, chronic systemic inflammation, and a cancer-promoting microenvironment in the lower gastrointestinal tract [922]. Population-based cohort studies have suggested that H. pylori eradication may be associated with reduced long-term CRC risk [2324], supporting the clinical relevance of H. pylori treatment beyond gastric cancer prevention. However, prospective interventional studies are needed to determine whether eradication therapy can prevent colorectal neoplasia. Because anti-H. pylori seropositivity may reflect either current or past exposure, our findings could be interpreted as evidence linking serologic exposure to H. pylori with colorectal adenoma, rather than as proof of an effect of active infection.

Whether the association between H. pylori and colorectal adenoma is mediated by H. pylori itself or by downstream gastric mucosal changes remains unclear. Several studies have reported positive associations between IM and colorectal neoplasia risk [12], whereas findings regarding AG have been inconsistent [14252627]. In the present study, AG was significant in univariate analysis but lost significance after multivariable adjustment, and AG without H. pylori seropositivity was not associated with adenoma in the combined analysis. In contrast, IM was independently associated in the separate model but was attenuated to borderline significance in the combined model including H. pylori seropositivity. This attenuation may partly reflect overadjustment, given the sequential relationship between chronic H. pylori-related injury and IM within the Correa cascade [28]. In this context, the significant association between IM and colorectal adenoma observed in females may reflect a cumulative or downstream gastric mucosal alteration related to long-standing H. pylori-associated injury, rather than an effect fully independent of H. pylori exposure. Because IM generally represents a more advanced and persistent stage of chronic gastric mucosal injury than AG [29], it may better reflect long-standing H. pylori-related mucosal damage or cumulative host and environmental susceptibility relevant to colorectal adenoma. These findings suggest that IM may provide additional information beyond AG when evaluating gastric–colorectal associations, particularly in the presence of H. pylori seropositivity. Clinically, these findings do not support H. pylori serologic testing solely for colorectal adenoma risk assessment, but suggest that H. pylori seropositivity or endoscopic IM identified during upper gastrointestinal evaluation may be associated with a higher likelihood of concurrent colorectal adenoma in health-screening settings.

This study has limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and assessment of temporal relationships. Second, H. pylori status was determined primarily by serology, which cannot reliably distinguish active current infection from past infection; our findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting an association with H. pylori exposure, not as evidence of an effect of active infection. Non-differential misclassification would bias associations toward the null. Third, gastroscopy and colonoscopy were not performed simultaneously (interval up to 3 years), though sensitivity analyses restricted to ≤12-month intervals yielded consistent results. Fourth, AG and IM were diagnosed solely on endoscopic criteria without routine histological confirmation; endoscopic diagnosis of these lesions carries moderate interobserver variability, and non-differential misclassification across nine centers would be expected to bias associations toward the null. Fifth, several confounders were unavailable, including prior polyp history, family history of CRC, diabetes, hypertension, and inflammatory markers. Sixth, the sex-stratified subgroup analyses had limited statistical power to detect formal interaction, and the sex-stratified patterns should be regarded as hypothesisgenerating. Finally, this study cannot address whether H. pylori eradication modifies colorectal adenoma risk, an important question that warrants dedicated prospective interventional studies.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. Conducted across nine Korean institutions, it is one of the few multicenter studies to simultaneously evaluate H. pylori seropositivity, AG, and IM within a sex-stratified analytic framework, with formal interaction testing and pre-specified sensitivity analyses—offering a more nuanced interpretation than prior single-center Korean investigations.

CONCLUSIONS

In this Korean multicenter health-screening cohort, anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity was independently associated with conventional colorectal adenoma, whereas AG was not significant after adjustment. IM was associated with adenoma in the separate adjusted model but attenuated in the main combined model, with the highest adenoma odds observed in participants with both H. pylori seropositivity and IM. Sex-stratified analyses suggested potential sex-related patterns, with H. pylori seropositivity appearing more prominent in males and IM appearing more prominent in females; however, formal interaction tests were not statistically significant, and these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Anti-H. pylori IgG seropositivity or endoscopic IM identified during upper gastrointestinal evaluation may indicate a higher likelihood of concurrent colorectal adenoma in health-screening settings.
