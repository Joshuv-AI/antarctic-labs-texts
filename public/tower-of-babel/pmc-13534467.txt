Immune checkpoints and immunoregulation in metabolic diseases and cancers: pathological mechanisms and therapeutic potential
Authors: Minmin Xiang, Shuang Liu, Desheng Xiao, Yongguang Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global prevalence of metabolic diseases, notably obesity, diabetes mellitus, and thyroid disorders, has risen dramatically in recent decades, posing a significant threat to public health. While conventional pharmacological interventions remain the cornerstone of management, they face considerable limitations, including adverse effects and the development of drug resistance, underscoring the urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies. Intriguingly, immune checkpoint molecules, originally recognized for their transformative role in cancer immunotherapy, are now gaining attention as pivotal regulators of metabolic homeostasis. Growing research demonstrates that immune checkpoint pathways, particularly the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, play dual roles in immune regulation and metabolic modulation by orchestrating inflammatory responses and energy metabolism. For instance, in obesity, PD-1/PD-L1 signaling promotes the polarization of adipose tissue macrophages from a proinflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory M2 state, thereby mitigating chronic low-grade inflammation and associated metabolic dysfunction. These findings highlight the potential of immune checkpoint modulation as a novel therapeutic strategy for metabolic disorders. However, despite these advances, the role of immune checkpoints in metabolic diseases remains underexplored, with limited comprehensive reviews on the subject. This review comprehensively elucidates the pathophysiological mechanisms and translational potential of immune checkpoint regulation in obesity, diabetes, and thyroid disease while proposing novel directions for clinical intervention.

Introduction

Immune checkpoints are a class of immunoregulatory molecules that serve as critical inhibitory mechanisms in the immune system, functioning as biological ‘brakes’ to maintain immune homeostasis.1 Primarily expressed on immune cell surfaces (including T cells, B cells, and other lymphocytes), these molecules act as gatekeepers to prevent excessive immune activation and preserve self-tolerance.2 However, tumors strategically exploit these pathways to evade immune surveillance.3 Therapeutic blockade of immune checkpoints significantly enhances antitumor immunity through multiple mechanisms: (1) promoting the accumulation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) within the tumor microenvironment, (2) augmenting tumor cell killing mediated by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and natural killer (NK) cells, (3) reprogramming tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) from immunosuppressive to immunostimulatory phenotypes, and (4) suppressing the recruitment and function of regulatory T cells (Tregs).4 Therapeutic strategies targeting immune checkpoints, therefore, have emerged as a transformative approach in oncology. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has revolutionized cancer treatment, demonstrating remarkable clinical efficacy across multiple malignancies.5,6

The discovery and characterization of novel immune checkpoint molecules represents an active area of investigation in modern immunotherapy research. Among the most clinically significant immune checkpoints are the programmed death 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4).7 These seminal discoveries, which earned the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, differentially regulate T-cell responses at distinct stages of immune activation.8 Recent advances have identified several promising coinhibitory receptors, including lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein-3 (TIM-3), and T-cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domains (TIGIT).9–11 These next-generation checkpoints exhibit distinct but complementary mechanisms of immune regulation.12 Notably, the therapeutic potential of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has expanded beyond oncology, with growing evidence supporting its application in chronic conditions such as immune disorders and metabolic diseases.

Metabolic diseases, including obesity, diabetes, and thyroid disorders, are characterized by dysregulation of fundamental metabolic pathways and cellular processes, ultimately leading to progressive tissue dysfunction and multiorgan damage. As the most common form of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), these conditions contribute significantly to global morbidity through their associated complications.13 In particular, obesity leads to elevated free fatty acid production and impaired peripheral glucose uptake, which alters cardiovascular, pulmonary, endocrine, and immunological functions with adverse effects.14,15 Diabetes mellitus, marked by chronic hyperglycemia, accelerates endothelial dysfunction and lipid metabolism disorders, which significantly increase the risk for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and result in high morbidity and mortality.16–18 Thyroid disorders exert systemic effects through altered thyroid hormone signaling and cardiovascular system modulation, which remains one of the most prevalent groups of disorders globally.19 Although metabolic diseases present a substantial threat to human well-being, the available treatment options remain relatively limited. Current therapeutic approaches primarily include appropriate exercise, balanced diet, lifestyle interventions, and the administration of pharmacological agents.20–22 This therapeutic gap underscores the urgent need for novel treatment strategies targeting underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.

In recent years, accumulating evidence has implicated chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation in metabolic disease pathogenesis, positioning immunotherapy as a promising therapeutic approach.23 Accordingly, immune checkpoint molecules play a vital role in the development of new medications for such conditions. For example, engineered cytokine-primed extracellular vesicles with high PD-L1 expression remodel the immune microenvironment, demonstrating remarkable therapeutic efficacy in murine models of type 1 diabetes.24 While immune checkpoint biology in oncology has been extensively characterized, its implications for metabolic diseases remain insufficiently reviewed. This work systematically evaluates current evidence regarding immune checkpoint mechanisms in metabolic disorders, addressing a critical knowledge gap in the field.

Overview of immune checkpoint molecules

In 1968, Ingegerd Hellström, Karl Hellström, and his colleagues observed that lymphocytes isolated from cancer patients could mount an immune response against autologous cancer cells, marking an early recognition of immune homeostasis and self-tolerance in tumor immunology.25 Building upon this foundational work, Pardoll later introduced the concept of the “immunologic checkpoint” within the context of cancer immunotherapy.26 Concurrently, Korman, Peggs, and Allison coined the term “checkpoint blockade” to describe therapeutic strategies targeting immune inhibitory pathways in cancer treatment.27 Since then, a number of critical immune checkpoint molecules have been identified.28,29 Immune checkpoint molecules, including inhibitory and stimulatory types, are defined as ligand‒receptor pairs that exert suppressive or activating effects on immune responses.30 Most of the immune checkpoint molecules that have been described thus far are expressed both on adaptive and innate immune cells, particularly on T cells.31 Notably, many checkpoint molecules are also expressed on tumor cells themselves, where they participate in immune evasion.1 We systematically examined the expression patterns and biological functions of these immune checkpoints and their ligands across diverse cell types (Fig. 1). PD-1/PD-L1 (structure, function, inhibitors)

PD-1 (programmed cell death protein 1), also known as CD279, was first identified in 1992 in interleukin-3 (IL-3)-deprived murine cell lines LyD9 (hematopoietic progenitor) and 2B4-11 (T-cell hybridoma).32 As a transmembrane protein, it is predominantly expressed on the plasma membrane of activated T cells, B cells, monocytes, and other immune cell populations, where it negatively regulates immune responses through interactions with its ligands PD-L1 and PD-L2 (programmed cell death ligand 1 and 2).33 Belonging to the CD28/CTLA-4 superfamily, PD-1 contains a single extracellular IgV domain, a hydrophobic transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic tail structure domain.34 The IgV domain consists of 20 amino acids separated from the plasma membrane and shares 23% homology with CTLA-4.35,36 Notably, the cytoplasmic tail contains two tyrosine motifs, an immune receptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) and an immune receptor inhibitory tyrosine-based switch motif (ITSM), which are essential for its inhibitory signaling function.37

Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), also known as B7-H1 or CD274, was identified in 1999 as the primary ligand for PD-1.38,39 This immune regulatory molecule is constitutively expressed on the plasma membrane of macrophages, activated T and B lymphocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), and specific epithelial cell populations, and its surface expression is further upregulated during inflammatory responses.40 Of particular clinical significance, PD-L1 serves as a critical mediator of tumor immune evasion by suppressing CTL activity through adaptive immune resistance mechanisms.41 Structurally, as the third characterized member of the B7 protein family, PD-L1 is a 33 kDa type I transmembrane glycoprotein comprising 290 amino acids. Its architecture features (1) an extracellular region containing immunoglobulin variable (IgV) and constant (IgC) domains, (2) a hydrophobic transmembrane domain, and (3) a short cytoplasmic tail.42

Upon binding, PD-1 suppresses T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling via SHP-1/SHP-2 phosphatase recruitment, dampening effector functions, proliferation, and cytokine production.43,44 This mechanism is exploited by tumors (via PD-L1 regulation) to evade immune surveillance.39 PD-1 expression is tightly regulated and controlled by T-cell activation.45 Moreover, multiple studies have established the importance of posttranslational regulation in maintaining PD-1 stability. In particular, FBXO38 mediates PD-1 degradation via ubiquitination after its immune checkpoint function is fulfilled (~2 days post T-cell activation), whereas KLHL22 prevents excessive suppression by degrading PD-1 intracellularly before surface expression.46,47 PD-L1 expression and posttranslational modifications (PTMs) regulate immune suppression by controlling PD-1 binding.48 Its expression is tightly regulated through genomic, transcriptional, posttranscriptional, and PTM mechanisms (e.g., glycosylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination), as well as exosomal transport.49 Following internalization, PD-L1 is sequentially trafficked through early endosomes, multivesicular/late endosomes, and recycling endosomes before lysosomal degradation or vesicle secretion.50 Thus, the dynamic interplay between PD-1/PD-L1 expression, posttranslational regulation, and intracellular trafficking creates a finely tuned immune checkpoint system that tumors exploit through PD-L1 upregulation, highlighting the importance of targeting these regulatory mechanisms in cancer immunotherapy.

Given its pivotal role in immune evasion, blockade of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway has emerged as a critical strategy to enhance immune-mediated tumor recognition and cytotoxicity.51 Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting this axis have gained widespread clinical application.52,53 To date, antibodies disrupting PD-1/PD-L1 signaling have been evaluated in over 1,000 clinical trials and have been approved for clinical use in multiple malignancies, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), Hodgkin’s lymphoma, bladder cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), Merkel cell carcinoma, and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) solid tumors.54–56 Currently, seven mAbs (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, toripalimab, cemiplimab, dostarlimba and retifanlimab) targeting PD-1 and three mAbs (atezolizumab, avelumab, and durvalumab) targeting PD-L1 have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)57–59 (Table 1). Critical differences exist between PD-1 and PD-L1 blockade strategies in terms of immune activation and known outcomes. PD-1 blockade acts by preventing PD-1 interactions with both PD-L1 and PD-L2 on T cells, reversing T-cell suppression and restoring antitumor cytotoxicity.41 However, this dual blockade may elevate autoimmune risks.61 In contrast, PD-L1 blockade selectively targets PD-L1 while sparing PD-L2, offering distinct advantages: (1) they disrupt PD-L1 interactions with both PD-1 and B7-1 (CD80), enabling more robust T-cell activation; (2) they relieve dendritic cell self-inhibition caused by PD-L1–B7-1 interactions, enhancing immune responses; and (3) by preserving PD-L2 function in macrophages, they reduce adverse effects such as interstitial lung disease (ILD).62 Despite these advantages, PD-L1 blockade demonstrates inferior clinical outcomes compared to PD-1 blockade. A mirror meta-analysis of 19 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 11,379 patients (predominantly advanced NSCLC) showed that PD-1 blockade, whether as monotherapy or in combination, provided superior overall survival (OS) benefit over PD-L1 blockade.63

While PD-1/PD-L1 blockade represents a major therapeutic breakthrough, its clinical application is constrained by limited response durability in many patients and the persistent challenge of immune-related toxicity.64 Notably, emerging evidence reveals that ICB-induced immune-related adverse events (irAEs) frequently involve metabolic dysregulation through multiple mechanisms, including endocrine disruption and metabolic tissue injury. For example, PD-1/PD-L1 blockade triggers autoreactive CD8+ T-cell infiltration into pancreatic islets, resulting in β-cell apoptosis and subsequent insulin-dependent diabetes.65 Immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated myocarditis has emerged as a serious, potentially fatal complication.66 The metabolic irAEs associated with immune checkpoint inhibition are systematically cataloged in Table 3 and analyzed in detail in subsequent sections. These toxicity profiles have driven the accelerated development of small-molecule PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors as potentially safer alternatives to monoclonal antibody-based therapies.67 Significant progress has been made in this emerging field, with multiple investigational agents now advancing through clinical trials. Our comprehensive review summarizes key clinical trial data for these novel small-molecule inhibitors from the past five years (Table 4), highlighting their distinct pharmacological advantages and evolving safety profiles. CTLA-4

Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4, also known as CD152), discovered in 1987, represents the first clinically targeted immune checkpoint receptor.68,69 CTLA-4 exerts its function at the plasma membrane, yet it is primarily localized to intracellular vesicles in both FoxP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) and activated conventional T cells.70 Structurally, CTLA-4 is a type I transmembrane protein comprising three distinct domains: (1) an extracellular immunoglobulin variable-like (IgV) domain responsible for ligand binding, (2) a transmembrane segment, and (3) a cytoplasmic tail containing multiple signaling motifs.71 Full-length CTLA-4 (flCTLA-4) is encoded by four exons: exon 1 (peptide leader), exon 2 (ligand-binding domain or IgV-like domain), exon 3 (transmembrane domain), and exon 4 (cytoplasmic tail).72 Alternative splicing generates soluble isoforms, including sCTLA-4 (lacking exon 3) and a truncated 1/4CTLA-4 variant (retaining only exons 1 and 4).73 Functionally, CTLA-4 exerts immunosuppressive effects through two primary mechanisms: signal-dependent inhibition and signal-independent modulation. By competitively binding CD80/CD86 with higher affinity than CD28, CTLA-4 attenuates TCR-mediated activation via suppression of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathways.74,75 Emerging evidence suggests that CTLA-4 mediates ligand sequestration and transendocytosis of CD80/CD86 from antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby limiting CD28 costimulation.76 The precise intracellular signaling cascades underlying CTLA-4-mediated T-cell suppression remain an active area of investigation.

CTLA-4 immunomodulation has been successfully applied in cancer treatment, with CTLA-4 blockade demonstrating clinical efficacy across multiple malignancies, including melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, prostate cancer, lung cancer, and liver cancer.77–80 Currently, two humanized anti-CTLA-4 monoclonal antibodies—ipilimumab (Bristol-Myers Squibb) and tremelimumab (MedImmune/AstraZeneca)—have advanced to clinical use.81,82 Given the complexity of immune checkpoint networks, CTLA-4 blockade is typically employed in combination therapies to optimize antitumor responses while mitigating toxicity. For example, the use of CTLA-4 and PD-1 inhibitors, either as single agents or in combination, has been approved by the US FDA for the treatment of metastatic melanoma.83 Additionally, scientific investigations have established that dual targeting of CTLA-4 and 4-1BB immune checkpoints achieves enhanced antitumor efficacy with reduced autoimmune toxicity compared to mono-therapy approaches.84

Other immune checkpoint molecules

The clinical success of CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade has spurred significant interest in identifying and targeting novel immune checkpoint molecules. Recent advances in immuno-oncology have revealed multiple coinhibitory receptors as promising therapeutic targets, including LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT.85,86 Given their critical roles in modulating T-cell exhaustion and immune evasion, these emerging checkpoints have become the focus of extensive preclinical and clinical investigation.87 Notably, mAbs targeting LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT are currently under evaluation in multiple clinical trials across various malignancies, either as monotherapies or in combination with existing checkpoint inhibitors.88

LAG-3 (CD223), first characterized in 1990 as a structural homolog of CD4, is an activation-induced surface receptor expressed on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and a distinct NK cell subset.89 Initially, identified through its inhibitory interaction with MHC class II molecules, this immune checkpoint receptor has since been shown to regulate lymphocyte function through multiple ligand-dependent pathways.90 Nearly 10 years ago, additional ligands for LAG-3 were indicated, including galectin-3 (Gal-3), liver and lymph node sinusoidal endothelial cell C-type lectin (LSECtin), fibrinogen-like protein 1 (FGL1), α-synuclein preformed fibrils (α-syn PFF), and most recently, the TCR-CD3 complex.91–93 LAG-3 mediates inhibitory function via a cytoplasmic domain of four phylogenetically conserved motifs with undefined function: a membrane-proximal region FXXL motif, a putative serine phosphorylation site, a KIEELE motif, and the repetitive C-terminal EP motif.94–97

TIM-3 was initially identified as a surface marker expressed on Th1 cells in 2002, where it functions as a negative immune regulator by binding to galectin-9 to induce apoptosis.98 Subsequent research has revealed that TIM-3 exhibits broader expression patterns across various immune cell populations, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and monocytes.99,100 Furthermore, TIM-3 interacts with multiple ligands beyond galectin-9, such as phosphatidylserine (PtdSer), high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 1 (CEACAM-1).101,102 These distinct ligand‒receptor interactions mediate diverse regulation of the immune response and immune tolerance depending on the cellular context and microenvironment.103 As a member of the TIM family, it is a type I surface glycoprotein that shares similar molecular structures containing a mucin domain, amino-terminal immunoglobulin variable domain (V domain) with five noncanonical cysteines, a transmembrane domain and a cytoplasmic tail.104,105

TIGIT, a member of the PVR-like protein family, was independently identified by three research groups in 2009 via genome-wide screening for proteins harboring immunomodulatory receptor-like domain structures.106–108 TIGIT consists of one extracellular immunoglobulin variable domain, a type I transmembrane domain, and a short intracellular domain with one immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) and one immunoglobulin tyrosine tail (ITT)-like motif.109 TIGIT expression is dynamically regulated during T-cell differentiation: it is undetectable in naive (CD45RA+CD4+) cells, induced upon TCR stimulation, and maintained at baseline in resting memory (CD45RO+) populations, while it is constitutively expressed in NK cells and upregulated in activated T effector/Treg subsets.110 Functioning as a key regulator in cancer immunity, TIGIT exerts its immunosuppressive function through dual mechanisms: (1) competitive antagonism of the costimulatory receptor CD226 for shared ligand binding (CD155/PVR and CD112/nectin-2) and (2) direct inhibition of T-cell activation via its ITIM and ITT-like domain in the cytoplasmic tail.111 TIGIT demonstrates differential binding affinity for its ligands, exhibiting high-affinity interactions with CD155 (PVR) and low-affinity binding to CD112 (PVRL2/nectin-2).112 As a higher-affinity receptor for CD155, TIGIT dominates the ligand-binding interface, effectively outcompeting CD226 and converting potential activating signals into inhibitory responses.113,114

In addition, several other immune checkpoint molecules related to metabolic diseases were mentioned in this review, including B7-H3, BTLA, HLA-G, CD47, and LILRB4. B7 homolog 3 protein (B7-H3), also known as CD276, is an immune checkpoint molecule and a costimulatory/coinhibitory immunoregulatory protein that plays a dual role in the immune system.115,116 B- and T-lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA) is an inhibitory immune checkpoint that binds to HVEM/CD270, a member of the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor family, portraying the first described interaction or crosstalk between both superfamilies.117 Human leukocyte antigen G (HLA-G), known as a central protein in providing immune tolerance to the fetus in pregnant women, is a potentially new immune checkpoint in cancer.118,119 The cluster of differentiation 47 (CD47) protein is proposed as an immune checkpoint for innate immune cells.120,121 Additionally, leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor B4 (LILRB4) is an inhibitory receptor in the LILR family and functions as an immune checkpoint.122

The interactivity between immune checkpoints and obesity

Obesity, defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m², is pathologically characterized by excessive visceral adiposity that drives a chronic “meta-inflammatory” state marked by systemic immune dysregulation and low-grade inflammation.123–125 This condition results from pathological expansion of white adipose tissue (WAT), which undergoes significant remodeling involving both adipocyte hypertrophy and substantial immune cell infiltration.126 Notably, macrophages represent the predominant immune cell population within obese WAT, where they play a central role in sustaining the proinflammatory microenvironment through altered polarization and cytokine secretion patterns.127 In obesity, macrophages polarize into the proinflammatory M1 phenotype, which is characterized by the secretion of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-6, and nitric oxide (NO).128 Additionally, obesity leads to the accumulation of T cells in adipose tissue. Research indicates that the obese adipose tissue microenvironment promotes CD8+ T-cell activation, which subsequently enhances macrophage recruitment and polarization.129 Importantly, adipose tissue-resident CD4+ T cells exist in a delicate balance between proinflammatory Th1 effector subsets and protective populations, including Th2 cells. This equilibrium shifts toward Th1 dominance in obesity, creating a proinflammatory milieu that exacerbates metabolic dysfunction.130 Significantly, CD4+Foxp3+ Treg cells were markedly and selectively decreased in obese individuals, suggesting that these unique phenotype cells critically regulate adipose tissue inflammation and consequently modulate systemic insulin sensitivity.131 During adipose tissue expansion associated with weight gain, neutrophil infiltration occurs as an early inflammatory event, where neutrophils actively contribute to the proinflammatory microenvironment through secretion of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), thereby amplifying monocyte recruitment and subsequent macrophage accumulation.132

PD-1/PD-L1 and obesity

Emerging evidence over the past decade has established the critical role of PD-1/PD-L1 pathways in obesity-associated metabolic dysregulation (Fig. 2). Studies in murine models have demonstrated that PD-1 significantly contributes to the functional impairment of type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) in obese microenvironments.133,134 Schwartz et al. revealed that PD-L1 expression on dendritic cells modulates adipose tissue inflammation and adaptive immune responses, thereby attenuating diet-induced obesity in mice.135 Additionally, elevated PD-L1 expression has been reported in the adipose tissue of obese individuals, implying a conserved anti-inflammatory function.136 Strikingly, preclinical investigations have confirmed that PD-1 blockade can effectively restore obesity-induced deficits in T-cell priming capacity.137 These findings collectively establish a robust mechanistic link between obesity and dysregulation of the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint axis, although the exact pathophysiological cascade remains under active investigation.Fig. 2Dysregulation of immune checkpoints in the adipose tissue microenvironment: from metabolic inflammation to tumor promotion. Chronic low-grade inflammation is a defining pathological characteristic of obesity that mechanistically links excessive adiposity to the development of various chronic diseases.138 This persistent inflammatory state underlies the pathogenesis of cardiometabolic disorders (e.g., atherosclerosis, type 2 diabetes), hepatic pathologies (particularly metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis [MASH]), and notably, multiple malignancies.139 Obesity-associated tumorigenesis exhibits distinct organotropism, preferentially affecting metabolic organs (colorectal, hepatocellular, and pancreatic carcinomas) and hormone-responsive tissues (postmenopausal breast and endometrial cancers) through shared mechanistic pathways.140 Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), these pathogenic processes are driven by intricate crosstalk among malignant cells, stromal components, including adipocytes, and diverse immune populations.141 Critical immune mediators such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and natural killer (NK) cells coordinately regulate this cellular network, ultimately facilitating tumor immune evasion and disease progression.142

Emerging evidence demonstrates that obesity reprograms TME metabolism, thereby suppressing antitumor immunity143 (Fig. 2). Obesity promotes tumorigenesis via distinct but interconnected pathways, and immune checkpoints assume a pivotal role within this process.144,145 Specifically, obesity impairs T-cell-mediated immunosurveillance, promoting oncogenesis.146 Intriguingly, innate immune PD-L1 constrains T-cell-dependent adipose tissue inflammation, offering protection against diet-induced obesity.135 Preclinical studies have revealed that obesity significantly expands immunosuppressive MDSCs and M2-polarized macrophages in tumor-bearing mice, while PD-1 blockade can reverse protumorigenic immune infiltrates in mammary fat pad adipose tissue.147 Mechanistically, PD-1 signaling promotes fatty acid oxidation via STAT3, which simultaneously inhibits CD8+ T effector cell glycolysis and accelerates obesity-associated breast tumor growth.148 Furthermore, adipocyte-rich breast tumor microenvironments exhibit markedly elevated PD-L1 expression.149,150 Genetic deletion of adipocyte PD-L1 attenuates tumor growth but exacerbates obesity-related inflammation, highlighting the dual role of this immune checkpoint in metabolic and oncologic regulation.151 Collectively, these findings establish the PD-1/PD-L1 axis as a critical molecular nexus linking obesity to tumor development while unveiling new therapeutic opportunities for cancer immunotherapy in obese populations.

Recent studies have revealed that obesity significantly impacts anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy outcomes in both murine tumor models and cancer patients. While obesity drives immune dysfunction and tumor progression via the PD-(L)1 axis, paradoxically, it also enhances responses to immune checkpoint blockade. Clinical analyses indicate that obese patients exhibit improved treatment efficacy and survival following PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition.152,153 Similarly, a preclinical study demonstrated that high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity correlates with elevated PD-1 expression and T-cell exhaustion but paradoxically enhances antitumor immunity and survival after checkpoint blockade.154 In addition, anti-PD-1 therapy was shown to partially restore obesity-mediated CD8+ T-cell dysfunction.155 This dual effect may be partly mediated by leptin signaling, which exerts both immunosuppressive and direct metabolic/hormonal effects.156–158 Notably, recent work shows that obesity-associated inflammatory mediators—including IFN-γ, TNF, leptin, insulin, and palmitate—upregulate macrophage PD-1 expression in an mTORC1- and glycolysis-dependent manner.159 Together, these findings suggest that obesity-induced inflammatory factors metabolically reprogram immune cells to increase PD-1 expression while simultaneously sensitizing tumors to checkpoint inhibition.

In obesity, metabolic-immune crosstalk extends to antigen presentation and T-cell activation. Within obese adipose tissue, impaired efferocytosis frequently triggers secondary necrosis in apoptotic adipocytes, culminating in the release of substantial intracellular contents, including lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs).160 The release of these substances attracts receptors and ligands on DCs, inducing DC maturation that enhances phagocytic processing and promotes efficient antigen uptake.161,162 Concurrently, macrophages undergo a phenotypic shift from proinflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 polarization, significantly reshaping the immune microenvironment of adipose tissue.163,164 Collectively, adipocyte apoptosis potentiates antitumor immunity by introducing antigenic targets and modulating immune surveillance in the context of high tumor mutation burden (TMB). These findings not only elucidate the mechanistic basis for the obesity-immunotherapy paradox but also suggest that targeted metabolic interventions could potentiate checkpoint blockade efficacy through immunometabolic modulation.

Emerging research has significantly advanced our understanding of the complex interplay between obesity and PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint blockade therapy, and conflicting findings have been reported in recent studies. For instance, Lydia Lynch et al. demonstrated that obesity-induced metabolic reprogramming impairs the antitumor effector functions of cytotoxic immune cells, and no significant differences were found in PD-1 expression on CD8+ T cells between lean and obese mice.165 Subsequent studies have revealed that obesity may attenuate therapeutic responses to PD-1 inhibition in renal cell carcinoma patients.166 The underlying mechanisms appear multifaceted. Obesity-associated dysregulation of intratumoral IL-1β signaling - a critical modulator of MDSC differentiation, recruitment, and immunosuppressive function - has been implicated in diminished treatment efficacy.167,168 Furthermore, preclinical evidence suggests that obesity exacerbates T-cell-mediated immunosuppression through accelerated thymic involution in diet-induced obese (DIO) models, correlating with poorer outcomes following anti-PD-1 therapy.169

Current evidence regarding obesity’s impact on immune checkpoint therapy efficacy remains contradictory, with studies reporting both enhanced and diminished responses to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade that likely reflect disease-specific heterogeneity, temporal variations in metabolic dysfunction, and tumor microenvironment differences. Major limitations confounding interpretation include methodological variability in obesity definitions (BMI ≥ 30 vs. ≥35 kg/m²) and body composition assessment, unaccounted metabolic comorbidities (diabetes, NAFLD), predominantly retrospective study designs, and tumor-type-specific effects, highlighting the need for future prospective investigations to determine whether weight management interventions can improve outcomes in overweight/obese patients receiving immune checkpoint blockade.

Certainly, the impact of obesity on immune checkpoints extends beyond cancer therapy and significantly influences antiviral immune responses. Clinically, obese COVID-19 patients exhibit markedly elevated PD-1 and TIM-3 expression on T cells, with progressive upregulation correlating with disease severity, suggesting that metabolic dysfunction exacerbates viral persistence.170 Additionally, the PD-1/PD-L1 axis is well established to drive T-cell exhaustion during chronic viral infections (e.g., hepatitis B virus [HBV], hepatitis C virus [HCV], and human immunodeficiency virus [HIV]) as well as acute viral infections, including Hantavirus and Ebola.171,172 Mechanistically, obesity creates a self-reinforcing cycle of immune impairment: chronic checkpoint overexpression induces T-cell exhaustion and compromised pathogen clearance, while concomitant attenuation of type I interferon responses and aberrant macrophage polarization further disrupt antiviral defenses.173,174 Paradoxically, this checkpoint-mediated immunosuppression may enhance responsiveness to immunotherapy in certain contexts, revealing a complex interplay between metabolic status, viral pathogenesis, and treatment outcomes that warrants further investigation.

CTLA-4 and obesity

It has been established that obesity alters the metabolic and endocrine function of adipose tissue and that the polarization of macrophages from M2 to M1 contributes to adipose tissue inflammation.175,176 Accordingly, scientists have demonstrated that CTLA-4 is involved in decreasing adipose tissue weight and adipocyte size in HFD-fed mice through shifting the polarization of ATMs from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2.177 Furthermore, the T-cell coreceptors CD28 and CTLA-4 competitively interact with B7 family ligands (CD80/CD86) to modulate adaptive immune responses.178,179 CD28 deletion was reported to improve obesity-induced liver steatosis but increase adiposity in mice.180 In conclusion, the immune checkpoint molecule CTLA-4 is directly involved in mediating chronic inflammation in the adipose tissue of obese individuals (Fig. 2).

Analogous to PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint signaling, current evidence links CTLA-4 and obesity through immunological and oncological associations rather than demonstrable causal mechanisms. Emerging clinical evidence reveals an obesity paradox in cancer immunotherapy, wherein overweight/obese patients demonstrate improved survival outcomes following CTLA-4 blockade treatment. In metastatic melanoma, multiple studies, including randomized trials and meta-analyses, consistently associate elevated BMI with enhanced responses to CTLA-4 inhibitors, and body composition metrics may modulate toxicity risks.181,182 In particular, patients exhibiting sarcopenia and low muscle attenuation (MA) demonstrate significantly higher rates of immune-related adverse events with ipilimumab therapy.183 This phenomenon extends to other malignancies, including RCC and NSCLC. For example, a retrospective study including 735 patients with metastatic RCC reported that patients with overweight or obese BMI had favorable OS compared with those with normal or low BMI with CTLA-4 treatment.184 Another multicenter ARON-1 study (NCT05287464, n = 675) of nivolumab-ipilimumab in metastatic RCC confirmed an improved immunotherapy response in obese patients, while a 205-patient clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) cohort implicated skeletal muscle in the BMI paradox.185,186 Additionally, a cohort study revealed that obese patients with NSCLC have shown a similar positive response to ICB, especially those treated with antibodies targeting CTLA-4.187 Furthermore, scientists investigated the biological determinants of ICB response by stratifying patients based on TMB and consistently observed that obesity was associated with improved survival outcomes regardless of TMB status.188 TMB quantifies the mutation load in tumors and is a predictive biomarker for response to immunotherapy.189 Overall, there is a paradoxical association between obesity and enhanced therapeutic efficacy of CTLA-4 blockade in advanced cancers, with obese patients consistently exhibiting improved OS independent of TMB.

Obesity-induced metabolic stress (e.g., elevated free fatty acids, hypoxia) may alter CTLA-4 expression dynamics on T cells. For instance, it has been demonstrated that the expression of CTLA-4 is often significantly increased in hypoxic malignant tumors.190 Therefore, CTLA-4 blockade would more effectively reverse this exaggerated suppression. It has been reported that obese individuals show coordinated upregulation of CD40 and CD80/CD86, with their expression levels being positively correlated.191 Under this condition, CTLA-4-mediated competitive inhibition of CD28-B7 interactions emerges as a more critical checkpoint constraining T-cell activation; consequently, CTLA-4 blockade could further potentiate antitumor immunity. Furthermore, obesity-associated adipocyte apoptosis introduces endogenous antigens and, as we reviewed, potentiates TMB-driven neoantigen generation, which contributes to the enhanced efficacy of CTLA-4 blockade in obese individuals.192 Taken together, the convergence of these immunological and metabolic alterations creates a biological context where CTLA-4 blockade can more effectively reverse T-cell dysfunction and restore antitumor immunity.

Obese and overweight patients have elevated serum levels of inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein, leptin, TNF-α, and IL-6.193 Meanwhile, the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) disrupts standard immune checkpoint regulation, resulting in uncontrolled inflammation and the development of irAEs.194 A growing body of observational studies has increasingly focused on the critical question of whether obese patients exhibit heightened susceptibility to irAEs following immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. First, a prospective study in metastatic melanoma patients receiving anti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-1, or combination therapy found a positive but nonsignificant association between BMI changes and irAE incidence.195 Beyond the general risk for irAEs, specific metabolic complications are of particular concern. Notably, anti-CTLA-4 therapy is a known contributing factor to atherosclerosis—a condition of heightened relevance in obesity—and immune checkpoint inhibitors are also recognized to induce diabetes, particularly in obese patients.196,197 Subsequent studies, however, reported positive associations between higher BMI and irAE development.183,198,199 Among these, a systematic meta-analysis revealed that elevated BMI (overweight/obesity) is significantly associated with an increased incidence of irAEs in patients receiving ICI therapy.200 Discrepancies may arise from (1) methodological variations, (2) population heterogeneity, and (3) unadjusted confounders, underscoring the need for standardized assessment in future studies. Taken together, these studies offer clinically important insights, indicating that elevated BMI negatively influences ICI treatment while establishing body weight as a potentially adjustable risk parameter for clinical surveillance.

Other immune checkpoints and obesity

Obesity is now recognized as a multifactorial metabolic disorder driven by pathological adipose tissue (AT) expansion.201 Functioning as an endocrine organ, AT secretes bioactive adipokines that promote chronic inflammation and oxidative stress.202 Studies have demonstrated the regulatory role of immune checkpoint molecules in AT homeostasis (Fig. 2). Immune checkpoints not only modulate local AT inflammation but also systemically influence metabolic dysfunction, positioning them as key mediators in obesity-related immunometabolic crosstalk. Integrated RNA sequencing and metabolomic analyses demonstrate that B7-H3 enhances glycolytic flux and mitochondrial function in adipocyte progenitors.203,204 Notably, B7-H3 is predominantly expressed in adipocytes, where it regulates metabolic activity and potentiates the lipolytic and oxidative capacity of differentiated adipocytes, thereby reducing lipid accumulation and mitigating obesity.205 Furthermore, studies indicate that adipose tissue T cells (ATTs) in obese microenvironments exhibit transcriptional signatures of T-cell exhaustion, along with upregulated expression of the coinhibitory receptor Btla.206 Consistent with this, a high-fat diet-induced obesity mouse model revealed that TIM-3 promotes T-cell exhaustion and suppresses tumor growth within the obese tumor microenvironment.207 In summary, obesity arises from adipose tissue dysfunction, where immune checkpoint molecules such as B7-H3 (promoting adipocyte oxidative metabolism) and TIM-3 (driving T-cell exhaustion) orchestrate metabolic and inflammatory pathways, revealing an intricate immune-metabolic axis in disease pathogenesis. This mechanistic relationship not only advances our understanding of obesity as an immunometabolic disorder but also presents novel therapeutic opportunities for targeted intervention.

There is a growing body of evidence that obesity is closely associated with lipid metabolism disorders and is involved in mediating tumorigenicity.208,209 In addition, immune checkpoint molecules play a significant role in this process (Fig. 2). Scientists have pointed to the interaction of TIM-3 with its ligand Gal-9 on lipid metabolism in acute myeloid leukemia cell lines.210 It has been proposed that CD47 participates in regulating inflammation and lipid metabolism in high-fat diet mice.211 Furthermore, another study revealed that the enhancement of the immune checkpoint CD47, by burning saturated fat by mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (FAO), is dominant in recurrent glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) patients with poor prognosis.212 In addition, it has been confirmed that pharmacological inhibition of fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO) protein dramatically attenuates leukemia stem/initiating cell self-renewal and reprograms the immune response by suppressing the expression of immune checkpoints, especially LILRB4.213 In addition, the FTO gene is the first obesity susceptibility gene identified through GWAS and continues to be the locus with the largest effect on BMI and obesity risk.214 Notably, immune checkpoints (TIM-3, CD47, LILRB4) functionally intersect with lipid metabolism and mitochondrial energetics, while the obesity-associated FTO gene epigenetically regulates checkpoint expression, revealing an immunometabolic axis in cancer and obesity.

The interactivity between immune checkpoints and diabetes

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders with one common manifestation: hyperglycemia.215 The new classification system identifies four types of diabetes mellitus: type 1, type 2, “other specific types” and gestational diabetes.216 In this review, we focus on the prevalent forms of type 1 and type 2 diabetes, which are among the most common metabolic disorders globally.

Clinically classified as autoimmune diabetes, type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is a progressive endocrine disorder marked by the sequential appearance of disease-specific autoantibodies followed by T-cell-mediated destruction of pancreatic β-cells.217,218 Accumulating evidence indicates that autoimmune mechanisms may also play a significant role in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) pathogenesis. T2DM is characterized by systemic and pancreatic immune dysregulation, marked by chronic low-grade inflammation with elevated proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and altered immune cell function.219 Systemically, there is an expansion of proinflammatory monocytes, Th1/Th17-skewed T-cell responses, and impaired Treg activity, alongside increased exhausted CD8+ T cells.220 Within pancreatic islets, infiltrating M1-polarized macrophages serve as the principal immune mediators of islet inflammation. These macrophages exhibit effects on β-cells, including impairing insulin secretion while promoting β-cell proliferation.221 Additionally, glucolipotoxicity and adipose tissue-derived inflammatory mediators exacerbate these immune-metabolic disturbances, perpetuating insulin resistance and β-cell failure.222,223 Notably, nonobese T2DM exhibits distinct immunological characteristics, including significant elevation of IL-6+ Th17 cells and their signature cytokines (IL-6/IL-17), implicating their pathogenic role.224 In contrast to obese T2DM, the immunopathology in nonobese individuals appears predominantly mediated by glucotoxicity-induced mechanisms.225 Remarkably, in diabetes and obesity, metabolic dysregulation of free fatty acids (FFAs), insulin, and glucose directly modulates crosstalk between pancreatic β cells and immune cells (T cells, macrophages), altering immune checkpoint signaling.226 This metabolic-immune interplay drives tissue inflammation and insulin resistance while revealing novel therapeutic targets for metabolic diseases.

Furthermore, scientists developed a classic murine preclinical model of T1DM, nonobese diabetic (NOD) mice, and confirmed that PD-1 or PD-L1 deficiency in NOD mice is linked with accelerated T1DM.235 The loss of PD-1 or PD-L1 results in increased numbers of insulin-specific CD4 T cells in NOD mice.236,237 PD-1, but not PD-L1, expressed by islet-reactive CD4 T cells suppresses infiltration of the pancreas during T1DM.238 In addition, insulin-induced remission in new-onset NOD mice is maintained by the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway.239 Taken together, studies utilizing NOD mouse models demonstrate that PD-1/PD-L1 deficiency accelerates T1DM progression, while PD-1 expression critically regulates disease pathogenesis and mediates insulin-induced remission.

PD-L1 is widely distributed on leukocytes, nonhematopoietic cells, and in nonlymphoid tissues, including islets.240 T1DM is due to the immune-mediated destruction of β cells.241 Accumulating evidence has indicated that the PD-1/PD-L1 axis is involved in β-cell autoimmunity. Research has demonstrated the potential of β cell PD-L1 to promote β cell survival in the context of islet inflammation and autoimmunity.242,243 Studies have reported that there is an elevated level of PD-L1 surface expression and an elevated frequency of PD-L1+ β cells in both NOD mice and diabetic patients.244,245 A recent study reported that the PD-L1 protein is present on β-cell extracellular vesicles and functions as a novel regulator of CD8+ T-cell activity and biomarker during the evolution of T1DM.234 In detail, IFN exposure increases β cell PD-L1 expression via JAK/STAT-IRF1 signaling in the human islet microenvironment.246 In addition, PD-L1 overexpression in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells was shown to reverse autoimmune responses in T1DM.136 In essence, cumulative evidence demonstrates that the PD-1/PD-L1 axis protects pancreatic β cells during autoimmunity through multiple mechanisms, including upregulated PD-L1 expression on β cells (induced by IFN/JAK-STAT-IRF1 signaling) and PD-L1+ extracellular vesicles that modulate CD8+ T-cell activity in T1DM progression.

Since the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway is essential in sustaining self-tolerance against pancreatic islets, PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade may cause T1DM, one of the irAEs.247 Specifically, the reduction in PD-1 owing to PD-1 blockade might activate autoreactive T cells, resulting in an autoimmune response against pancreatic islet cells.248 Scientists summarized all published case reports of type 1 diabetes associated with PD-1 blockade and concluded that the progression of T1DM induced by PD-1 inhibitors is relatively rapid.249 In general, PD-1 inhibitor-induced type 1 diabetes is a rare but life-threatening side effect of immunotherapy.250,251

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)

Type 2 diabetes mellitus arises from progressive β-cell failure and insulin resistance, aggravated by chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation.252,253 Within this context, T-cell-dependent immune responses are critical for maintaining immune homeostasis. Negative costimulatory molecules, such as PD-1, play a pivotal role in establishing immune tolerance during T-cell maturation and in controlling effector T-cell functions to prevent excessive activation.254,255 Consistently, several clinical studies have reported a generalized upregulation of PD-1 in T2DM (Fig. 4). For instance, CD4+CD28− T cells from patients with T2DM were found to exhibit significantly higher PD-1 expression than those from healthy donors.256 Another clinical trial also reported that CXCR5-PD-1hi peripheral helper T (Tph) cells are notably increased in individuals with T2DM.257 This systemic upregulation is often interpreted as a potential compensatory, protective response aimed at mitigating inflammation and tissue damage, as suggested by studies in diabetic nephropathy models.258 Consequently, PD-1-mediated T-cell exhaustion is considered a substantial contributor to immune dysfunction in T2DM, implicating the dysregulation of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in disease pathogenesis.Fig. 4Immune checkpoint dysregulation in T2DM and comorbidities. However, emerging evidence suggests that PD-1 expression and function exhibit significant cell-type and tissue specificity. A recent study revealed that PD-1 is downregulated on specific immune cell subsets in T2DM, notably on CD8+ T cells and monocytes.259 Nishimura and colleagues demonstrated that CD8+ T cells play a pivotal role in macrophage activation and adipose tissue infiltration during insulin resistance progression.129 As a defined inhibitory checkpoint, PD-1 downregulation on CD8+ T cells promotes the excessive activation of these T cells, subsequently driving increased macrophage recruitment to adipose tissue.260,261 Furthermore, monocytes participate in T2DM pathogenesis by differentiating into adipose tissue macrophages, with the M2 subset being essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis and improving insulin sensitivity.262 Given the established role of PD-1 signaling in promoting M2 macrophage polarization, the reduced PD-1 expression observed on circulating monocytes likely leads to fewer M2 macrophages, which directly contribute to systemic insulin resistance.263 Concurrently, within pancreatic islets, a relative lack of M2 macrophages may exacerbate the destructive effects of proinflammatory macrophages, which are known to impair β-cell function via cytokines such as IL-1β/TNF-α.264,265 Importantly, insulin resistance arises notably from dysfunction in key insulin signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.266 This same pathway mediates the mechanism by which the insulin receptor modulates T-cell metabolism, thereby reinforcing metabolic programs that enhance nutrient uptake, glycolysis, and respiratory capacity.267 Therefore, impaired insulin signaling not only contributes to metabolic dysregulation but may also be linked to immune dysfunction, wherein PD-1 activity could indirectly further insulin resistance. In summary, PD-1 downregulation in T2DM disrupts immune homeostasis by unleashing CD8+ T-cell activity and impairing M2 macrophage polarization, thereby driving a pathological cascade that exacerbates both systemic insulin resistance and islet dysfunction.

Interestingly, metformin, a well-established therapeutic agent for T2DM, has shown significant potential in both cancer prevention and treatment.268,269 Clinical studies indicate that metformin may enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy when used in combination, suggesting a promising synergistic approach to cancer treatment.270,271 Emerging studies have demonstrated that metformin augments the therapeutic effects of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade through modulation of PD-L1 expression levels. Cha et al. demonstrated that metformin enhances antitumor immunity by inducing endoplasmic reticulum-mediated degradation of PD-L1 in breast cancer.272 Subsequent studies further clarified that metformin suppressed PD-L1 expression in an AMPK-dependent manner in endometrial carcinoma.273 Scientists further discovered that metformin downregulates both PD-L1 expression in cancer cells and cancer-induced PD-1 expression in tumor-infiltrating cytotoxic T lymphocytes, potentially enhancing antitumor immunity.274 In addition, metformin ameliorates PD-1-mediated CD8+ TIL dysfunction through AMPK-mTOR-dependent metabolic reprogramming, thereby overcoming tumor-induced immune exhaustion.275 This metabolic intervention is effective because the AMPK and mTOR pathways form a central regulatory axis that controls cellular metabolism and growth in response to nutrient and energy status.276 Consequently, modulating this axis with metformin not only impacts TIL function but may also intersect with the insulin signaling pathways discussed earlier, suggesting a broader mechanistic link between metabolic disorders and cancer immunity. Moreover, metformin treatment suppresses tumor oxygen consumption, thereby attenuating hypoxia and potentiating the antitumor efficacy of PD-L1 blockade through improved immune cell function.277 Additionally, mannose-modified macrophage-derived microparticles loaded with metformin (Met@Man-MPs) were developed to selectively target M2-like TAMs and repolarize them into an M1-like phenotype, which enhances anti-PD-1 antibody therapy.278 In conclusion, metformin reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment through multiple mechanisms, demonstrating potential as an adjuvant for diverse non-PD-L1/PD-1-targeting immunotherapies.

Emerging evidence suggests that antidiabetic drugs may exert immunomodulatory effects capable of influencing ICB outcomes through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Among these, GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) demonstrate particular promise. Exenatide, an exendin-based GLP-1RA clinically used for glycemic control, has been shown to suppress neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation by reducing ROS levels.279,280 Importantly, this NET inhibition appears mechanistically linked to enhanced ICB efficacy, as exenatide synergizes with anti-PD-1 therapy to promote durable antitumor CD8+ T-cell responses.281 Notably, similar immunostimulatory effects have been observed with liraglutide, another long-acting GLP-1RA.282 Beyond its glucose-lowering effects, liraglutide inhibits neutrophil extravasation in inflammatory settings and has been demonstrated to potentiate PD-1 blockade in multiple murine tumor models, including Lewis lung carcinoma and Hepa1-6 hepatoma.283 In addition, insulin’s immunomodulatory potential warrants careful consideration. Insulin signaling engages the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway; interestingly, this pathway exhibits significant cross-talk with PD-1/PD-L1 axis signaling.284 The binding of PD-1 to PD-L1 increases PTEN activity, thereby antagonizing PI3K signaling.285 Serum insulin levels emerge as a potential determinant of immune checkpoint therapy efficacy.286 Collectively, these findings highlight a growing recognition of metabolic-immune crosstalk in cancer therapy. Moving forward, prospective clinical studies will be essential to determine whether strategic use of specific antidiabetic agents can safely enhance ICB efficacy in defined patient populations.

CTLA-4 and diabetes

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM)

Accumulating evidence highlights the critical role of CTLA-4 in T1DM development and its potential as a therapeutic target for autoimmune diabetes287 (Fig. 3). Genetic studies across diverse populations have consistently linked CTLA-4 variants to T1DM susceptibility. For instance, a large-scale study confirmed this association in patients of diverse racial descent, while a Japanese multicenter study suggested that the observed correlation might be confounded by coexisting autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD), emphasizing the need for T1DM subclassification in genetic analyses.288,289 Further supporting this, a North African case-control study implicated CTLA-4 promoter-region variants in T1DM etiology.290 Notably, a comparative study revealed a genetic interaction between CTLA-4 and Cbl proto-oncogene B (CBLB), both of which are involved in T-cell receptor signaling, further elucidating CTLA-4’s mechanistic role in T1DM.291 These findings suggest that regulatory variations in the CTLA-4 promoter region may influence gene expression and contribute to disease etiology.

Beyond the well-established role of HLA polymorphisms as the primary genetic risk factor for T1DM, emerging evidence suggests that non-HLA variants (e.g., CTLA-4) may significantly influence disease progression in time-to-event analyses.292,293 Notably, interactions between HLA-DQ2/DQ8 and the CTLA-4 rs3087243 (CT60) GA genotype have been shown to accelerate the transition from single to multiple autoantibody positivity.294 Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses further demonstrate that combined genotyping of HLA (DR3, DR4-DQB1*03:02), CTLA-4, and INS can effectively discriminate T1DM patients, while meta-analyses specifically link the CTLA-4 + 49A/G polymorphism to childhood-onset T1DM.295,296 However, population-specific disparities significantly modify these associations. Ethnic and geographical differences in genetic susceptibility are evident, with studies of Colombian and Turkish cohorts revealing no significant association between CTLA-4 polymorphisms and T1DM risk, contrasting with positive associations reported in European and North American populations.297,298 Together, these findings highlight substantial genetic heterogeneity in CTLA-4’s contribution to T1DM pathogenesis across populations, emphasizing the need for population-specific risk stratification in both research and clinical practice.

CTLA-4-Ig, a CD80-binding fusion protein that blocks costimulatory signaling, represents a promising investigational therapy for T1DM299 (Fig. 3). Specifically, CTLA-4-Ig represents a fusion protein composed of the extracellular functional domain of CTLA-4 and the IgG1 Fc segment.300 Previous studies have indicated that this approach effectively lengthens the survival of transplanted islets by modifying porcine-derived islet xenografts with the CTLA-4-Ig fusion gene or by expressing the gene in dendritic cells within diabetic mouse models.301–303 It has been demonstrated that CTLA-4-Ig restores the physiological structure and cellular motility of podocytes challenged with high glucose in vitro and abrogates the onset of proteinuria in murine models of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) in vivo.304 Notably, Pawlick et al. reported synergistic effects when combining CTLA-4-Ig with an anti-NKG2D antagonist (an antagonist of the Natural Killer Group 2, member D [NKG2D] receptor expressed on NK and CD8 T cells), which significantly extended islet allograft survival and improved glucose tolerance in T1DM mice.305 Mechanistically, CTLA-4-Ig-mediated CD28 blockade prevents IL-2 expression and secretion. The consequent inhibition of IL-2 signaling diminishes NKG2D receptor-driven production of effector chemokines and cytokines, such as MIP-1α, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, and TNF-α.306,307 However, the exact in vivo mechanisms remain unclear.

The FDA-approved CTLA-4-Ig formulation abatacept (approved for rheumatoid arthritis in 2005) is being repurposed for T1DM trials based on its immunomodulatory effects and favorable safety profile.308,309 A phase II multicenter randomized controlled trial (n = 112) in new-onset T1DM patients showed that abatacept significantly slowed β-cell functional decline over 2 years.310 Parenthetically, β-cell function is measured by C-peptide as an index of endogenous insulin production.311 Supporting observational studies further confirmed its efficacy in preserving residual β-cell function when initiated within 3 months of diagnosis.312 These findings highlight its potential as an early-intervention therapy to delay insulin dependence.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)

Emerging evidence has drawn increasing attention to the potential association between CTLA-4 and T2DM. Recent studies suggest that CTLA-4 may influence lipid metabolism and contribute to T2DM progression and/or diabetic complications.313 Supporting this notion, one cross-sectional study revealed a significant association between the CTLA-4 + 49A/G polymorphism (both at the genotype and allele levels) and antibody-negative T2DM.314 Another study based on the Han population of Northern China suggested that CTLA-4 functions as a T2DM susceptibility gene, particularly as the −318 C/T T allele appears to confer increased risk for diabetic ketosis in T2DM patients.315 Collectively, these studies suggest that CTLA-4 polymorphisms are associated with T2DM susceptibility, progression, and complications such as diabetic ketosis and kidney disease, with some variants also influencing lipid metabolism.

T2DM has been recognized as an immune-related adverse event associated with ICI therapy, which frequently co-occurs with hypertension and hyperlipidemia.316,317 Concurrently, preexisting T2DM has been shown to significantly increase the incidence of irAEs in cancer patients undergoing ICI therapy. For example, a retrospective study of 109 hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients receiving tremelimumab and other ICIs demonstrated that comorbid conditions, particularly arterial hypertension (AHT) and T2DM, amplify the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and increase treatment discontinuation rates.318 Notably, CTLA-4 inhibitors exhibit a dual association with T2DM, both as a potential trigger of immune-related diabetes and as a therapy with compromised efficacy and increased adverse events in preexisting T2DM patients, particularly those with cardiovascular comorbidities.

Epidemiological evidence further supports a strong link between T2DM and an increased risk of multiple cancer types, particularly breast, colorectal, endometrial, gallbladder, liver, and pancreatic cancers.319,320 The metabolic triad of hyperinsulinemia, hyperglycemia, and hyperlipidemia in T2DM synergistically promotes tumorigenesis through interconnected oncogenic pathways.321 These metabolic disturbances collectively establish a protumorigenic microenvironment, wherein CTLA-4 plays a significant regulatory role (Fig. 4). Specifically, the metabolic disturbances characteristic of T2DM, particularly hyperinsulinemia and hyperglycemia, induce insulin resistance and elevate circulating insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels, thereby activating the oncogenic PI3K/AKT pathway and facilitating tumor cell proliferation.322 The PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade serves as a central hub for insulin signaling, and its dysregulation is a hallmark of insulin resistance.266 Intriguingly, CTLA-4 suppresses PI3K/AKT/mTORC signaling in T cells, thereby influencing the metabolic adaptability of T cells and, consequently, the tumor metabolic microenvironment.323,324 Clinically, this regulatory relationship is supported by observed correlations between peripheral blood CTLA-4 expression levels and serum IGF-1 concentrations in patient cohorts.325 Furthermore, chronic inflammatory and oxidative stress potently activate canonical proinflammatory signaling cascades such as the NF-κB pathway, promoting tumor angiogenesis, metastasis, and overall progression.326 Notably, preclinical studies have revealed that CTLA-4 immune checkpoint inhibition exacerbates progression in hyperlipidemic murine models by inducing an NF-κB-dependent shift in the Th1/Th2 lymphocyte balance.197 In conclusion, under metabolic stress conditions in early-stage T2DM, CTLA-4 expression is upregulated as a compensatory mechanism to counteract metabolic dysregulation. However, with disease progression and the establishment of chronic inflammation, this protective CTLA-4 response becomes functionally impaired. This progressive loss of CTLA-4-mediated regulation contributes to the elevated cancer risk observed in advanced T2DM. These findings establish CTLA-4 as a key metabolic-immune checkpoint that critically modulates diabetes-associated tumorigenesis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target at the diabetes-cancer interface.

T2DM patients exhibit heightened susceptibility not only to malignancies but also to viral infections due to immune dysregulation and metabolic disturbance. For example, a cross-sectional study indicated that patients with T2DM demonstrate a significantly higher prevalence of chronic HBV infection than nondiabetic populations.327 Furthermore, chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients with T2DM exhibited a polarized cytokine profile: elevated IL-1α/IL-6/IL-33 with suppressed IFN-α/IL-2/IL-7/IL-15. Notably, IL-15 depletion reduced CTLA-4/LAG-3 on Tregs, revealing its key role in sustaining Treg immunosuppressive capacity.328 Moreover, one meta-analysis of 18,139 patients across all available studies demonstrated significant differences in human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) infection rates between T2DM patients and healthy controls in Asian populations.329 The underlying mechanism involves upregulated CTLA-4 expression in T2DM patients, which impairs both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell function, reducing the production of antiviral cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α) and compromising HCMV clearance.330 In addition, increasing clinical data have demonstrated a strong association between T2DM and adverse COVID-19 prognosis, including higher rates of severe illness and mortality.331 The proinflammatory pathway involving interleukin-6 (IL-6) may serve as a predictor of lung disease severity.332 IL-6 can upregulate CTLA-4 expression in T cells (particularly regulatory T cells) through activation of the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway.333 In summary, T2DM patients face increased risks of both malignancies and viral infections due to immune-metabolic dysfunction. Clinical evidence demonstrates significantly increased susceptibility to multiple viral infections, including HBV, HCMV, and COVID-19, in T2DM patients, highlighting the critical need for targeted immunomodulatory strategies in diabetes management.

Recent findings demonstrate that metformin may exert dual effects on antitumor immunity, with significant implications for immunotherapy response. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that metformin enhances antitumor immunity through mechanisms not only by disrupting inhibitory signals from cancer cells but also by promoting intrinsic T-cell activation.334 Key findings revealed that metformin significantly reduced tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells (Ti-Tregs), especially the terminally differentiated CD103+KLRG1+ subset, and downregulated key immunosuppressive factors—most notably CTLA-4, along with IL-10.335

However, clinical observations present a paradoxical scenario: in a large cohort of cancer patients receiving ICI therapy, those with T2DM exhibited significantly poorer treatment outcomes, particularly when exposed to metformin.336 Intriguingly, further analysis revealed a BMI-dependent modulation of immune checkpoint expression. Metformin treatment led to significant downregulation of six key checkpoint genes (CTLA4, PDCD1, ICOS, BTLA, CD27, and LAG3) in high-BMI patients, whereas it exerted opposing transcriptional effects in low-BMI individuals.337 This dichotomous response may stem from variations in (i) cancer cell lineage, (ii) intrinsic tumor suppressor mutations, and (iii) tumor microenvironmental heterogeneity. While these findings highlight the immunomodulatory potential of metformin, its role as an adjuvant in immunotherapy remains uncertain. Critical questions regarding its safety profile, optimal dosing, and synergistic efficacy with ICIs warrant rigorous clinical investigation to determine its therapeutic utility in oncology.

Other immune checkpoints and diabetes

T1DM is a prototypical autoimmune disease characterized by the selective destruction of pancreatic β-cells, involving macrophages, dendritic cells, B lymphocytes, and T lymphocytes in its pathogenesis.338 Similarly, both innate immune dysfunction (particularly in neutrophils and macrophages) and impaired adaptive immunity (including T-cell abnormalities) contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation in T2DM.339 Given the well-established immunological basis of diabetes, the prominent association with immune checkpoint mechanisms emerges as an expected finding. To date, many different immune checkpoint molecules have been confirmed to be involved in the progression of diabetes, except for PD-1/PD-L1 or CTLA-4.340,341 Here, we identified several new immune checkpoint targets, including LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT342,343 (Figs. 3 and 4).

LAG-3 is a coinhibitory immune checkpoint molecule similar to CTLA-4 or PD-1 that mediates immune regulation with overlapping but distinct characteristics, which are also implicated in the pathogenesis of T1DM.344–346 Accumulating evidence indicates that LAG-3 deficiency is involved in accelerating the progression of T1DM in NOD mice.347,348 Mechanistically, the stable pMHCII-LAG-3 interaction serves as a functional immune checkpoint that regulates both autoimmunity and antitumor responses, representing a promising therapeutic target in T1DM.349 In addition, one retrospective multicenter study revealed that the antitumor efficacy of LAG-3 blockade in metastatic or unresectable melanoma is attenuated in type 2 diabetes patients, likely mediated by downregulated LAG-3 expression in the tumor microenvironment.350,351 Scientists observed significantly fewer LAG-3+ CD4 and CD8 T cells from subjects with T1DM.352 Collectively, LAG-3 is identified as a critical immunoregulatory checkpoint in diabetes pathogenesis, whose deficiency exacerbates autoimmune progression in T1DM through impaired T-cell inhibition and altered cytokine regulation, while its therapeutic potential is further underscored by its role in promoting islet allograft survival and its aberrant expression patterns in both T1DM and T2DM patients.

TIM-3 is a transmembrane protein preferentially expressed on differentiated Th1 cells that participates in Th1-mediated diseases, including diabetes.353 Several studies have reported altered expression of TIM molecules in diabetes patients. For instance, one study revealed an imbalanced ratio of TIM-3 to TIM-1 expression in patients with T1DM.354 Another study showed that upregulated TIM-3 expression contributes to NK cell dysfunction in T2DM patients.355 Specifically, T2DM patients exhibit impaired NK cell functions and enhanced NK cell apoptosis, related to increased TIM-3 expression alongside decreased TNF-α production, which may collectively compromise anti-infectious and antitumor immunity. Furthermore, TIM-3 expression is decreased on peripheral CD14+ monocytes, and a suggested correlation between TIM-3 expression and diabetes duration has been reported.356 A recent study also revealed a distinct, paradoxical correlation profile for TIM3+CD8+ T cells.357 More specifically, they exhibited a positive correlation with body fat mass but a negative correlation with serum triglyceride levels in obese nondiabetic individuals, which is absent in obese diabetic (OD) patients. Furthermore, Galectin-9 was identified as a superior and more reliable biomarker. Notably, scientists found that the inhibition of miR-330-5p upregulated TIM-3 expression, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and ameliorating insulin resistance in diabetic mice.358 It has been proposed that treatment with TIM-3 monoclonal antibodies in NOD mice accelerates the onset of diabetes.359 TIM-3 blockade promoted the development of diabetes in NOD mice and prevented the acquisition of transplantation tolerance induced by costimulation blockade.100 Moreover, gal-9, a ligand for TIM-3, was reported to play a vital role in TH1-mediated autoimmune diabetes in NOD mice.360 Overall, these findings collectively establish TIM-3 as a pivotal immunoregulatory checkpoint in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, where its dysregulation contributes to disease pathogenesis through multiple mechanisms, including altered immune cell function, macrophage polarization, and insulin resistance, while its interaction with galectin-9 further underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway in autoimmune and metabolic diabetes.

TIGIT is also highly expressed on activated islet-infiltrating T cells in NOD mice.361,362 Scientists have studied the expression and function of TIGIT in autoreactive CD8+ T cells in a T1DM model and found that TIGIT acts as an immune checkpoint upon inhibition of PD-1 signaling in autoimmune diabetes.363 Studies have shown that TIGIT signaling represses the production of IFN-γ and T-bet expression, resulting in functional restoration in Tregs from T1DM patients.108,364 Recently, scientists have identified TIGIT+CCR7− Tregs and CD226+CD8+ T cells as crucial biomarkers during the peri-remission phase of T1DM.365 TIGIT+CCR7− Tregs promote immune tolerance in the partial remission phase by inhibiting CD226+CD8+ T-cell activation via TGF-β1 signaling.366,367 Specifically, it has been reported that CD226 modulates thymic T-cell selection and impacts peripheral memory/effector CD8+ T-cell activation and function in T1DM development.368 In addition, a meta-analysis showed that CD226 Gly307Ser is associated with several autoimmune diseases (ADs), including T1DM.369 Increased TIGIT expression on CD4+ T cells, particularly the CD4+CD25hi subset, has been observed in diabetic patients compared to healthy controls, suggesting that this signaling pathway may represent a novel therapeutic target for T2DM.370 Taken together, these studies demonstrate that TIGIT serves as a key immunoregulatory checkpoint in diabetes pathogenesis, exerting pleiotropic effects through suppression of autoreactive T-cell activity, potentiation of regulatory T-cell function, and modulation of CD226 costimulatory signaling, collectively positioning it as a promising therapeutic target for both T1DM and T2DM.

Current research reveals distinct patterns of immune checkpoint dysregulation across diabetes subtypes. In T1DM, LAG-3 deficiency impairs T-cell regulation, while TIM-3 dysfunction promotes Th1-mediated β-cell destruction. TIGIT demonstrates dual functionality, suppressing IFN-γ and T-bet in autoreactive T cells while enhancing Treg activity. In T2DM, three key alterations emerge: (1) LAG-3 downregulation in tumors compromises immune surveillance, (2) TIM-3 overexpression on NK cells/monocytes correlates with metabolic impairment, and (3) TIGIT upregulation on CD8+ T cells disrupts tolerance via CD226 interactions. These new immune checkpoint targets offer targeted intervention opportunities for diabetes-associated immune dysregulation.

The interactivity between immune checkpoint molecules and thyroid disease

Thyroid disease can be broadly categorized as thyroid dysfunction (hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism) and structural disease (goiter, nodules, and cancer).371,372 Thyroid dysfunction significantly impacts systemic metabolism, especially lipid homeostasis. In particular, hypothyroidism induces both hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia, while hyperthyroidism promotes net lipid catabolism, characterized by reduced serum levels of total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides (TGs).373,374 In this review, we focus on the roles of immune checkpoints in thyroid diseases related to metabolism, including hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism. In particular, we summarized the important roles that immune checkpoints play in AITD (autoimmune thyroid diseases) and thyroid cancer.

AITD, the most common autoimmune disorder, primarily includes Graves’ disease and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis.375 Specifically, Graves’ disease (GD)—autoimmune hyperthyroidism mediated by TSHR-Ab—presents with metabolic hallmarks (unexplained weight loss, heat intolerance, increased basal metabolic rate) and classic thyrotoxic symptoms (palpitations, tremor, goiter).376 Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by chronic thyroid inflammation and progressive tissue destruction, frequently resulting in hypothyroidism and associated metabolic alterations, including reduced basal metabolic rate (BMR), dyslipidemia (characterized by elevated cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL-C), and glucose metabolism impairment (manifesting as decreased insulin sensitivity and reduced glycogen synthesis).377 Thyroid cancer (TC) is a malignant thyroid tumor and the most common cancer of the endocrine system.378 Thyroid carcinomas are classified by histopathology and biological behavior into differentiated (papillary and follicular), poorly differentiated, anaplastic, and medullary subtypes.379 The first three arise from follicular epithelial cells, while medullary carcinoma originates from parafollicular C cells.380

In addition, numerous studies have pointed to PD-L1 overexpression as a potential prognostic marker for aggressive thyroid cancer and its variants. A study comprising 507 papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) patients showed a statistically important correlation between higher levels of PD-L1 mRNA and lymph node metastasis, extrathyroidal invasion and disease-free survival (DFS).387 In another study, PD-L1 expression was independently associated with the presence of the BRAF V600E mutation in PTC; additionally, it was linked to more aggressive histological subtypes of the tumor.388 Moreover, a comprehensive screening for PD-L1 expression in thyroid cancer in Korea analyzed 407 primary thyroid cancers and found that PD-L1 was highly expressed in patients with advanced thyroid cancer.389 A cross-sectional study indicated that tumor PD-L1 expression was significantly lower in PTC specimens without lymphocytic thyroiditis (LT) than in those with LT.390 In addition, a clinicopathologic and prognostic analysis of the Chinese population suggested that the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway was expressed in medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) and was significantly correlated with distant metastases at surgery.391 In short, these findings suggest that the expression of PD-1/PD-L1 in thyroid cancer is related to poor prognosis, comorbidities, and distant metastases.

Thus far, available information suggests that PD-L1 could represent a promising prognostic marker for risk stratification in thyroid cancer, and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies could be a valid option for patients affected by the most aggressive thyroid cancers. The clinical trial demonstrated that spartalizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds PD-1, showed promising clinical activity and a good safety profile in a patient population with anaplastic thyroid carcinoma.392 Specifically, PD-1 blockade inhibits tumor growth by inhibiting intrinsic SHP2/Ras/MAPK signaling in thyroid cancer cells.393

On the other hand, thyroid dysfunction is the most commonly occurring endocrine-immune-related adverse event, affecting more than 10% of exposed patients.394,395 A systematic meta-analysis indicated that there is an increasing incidence of thyroid dysfunction in people treated with anti-PD-1 agents.396 Recently, new evidence from a single-center experience showed that abnormal thyroid function tests (DYSTHYR) are common during anti-PD-1/anti-PD-L1 treatment, especially in patients with preexisting thyroid dysfunction.397 In addition, patients with PD-1 inhibitor-induced thyroiditis showed a decrease in the number of immature CD56+CD16− NK cells as well as CD14+HLA-DR+lo/neg monocytes.398 Most importantly, advances in PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor treatment for cancer have highlighted the need to manage immune-related thyroid disease.

CTLA-4 and thyroid disease

Accumulating evidence implicates the CTLA-4 locus in thyroid disease susceptibility across diverse populations399–401 (Fig. 5). In Japanese populations, case-control analyses have established CTLA-4 as a shared risk factor for GD and AITD.402 Similarly, studies in Han Chinese cohorts identified CTLA-4 as a GD susceptibility gene, with risk variants exhibiting geographic heterogeneity.403 In UK cohorts, the combined effect of CTLA-4 and HLA loci accounts for ~50% of the inherited GD risk.404 Beyond autoimmunity, a hospital-based study (n = 200 patients vs. 200 controls) from Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, revealed that CTLA-4 polymorphisms are significantly associated with thyroid cancer progression, suggesting a potential oncogenic role.405 In summary, CTLA-4 emerges as a pivotal genetic factor in both thyroid autoimmunity (GD/AITD) and cancer. Population-specific variants interact with HLA loci to modulate disease risk, revealing both conserved and ethnically diverse pathogenic mechanisms.

Further studies on the relationship between CTLA-4 gene polymorphisms and thyroid disease have been widely proposed. Key functional polymorphisms in the CTLA-4 gene include CT60 (rs30807243), +49A/G (rs231775), −1147C/T (rs16840252), and −318C/T (rs5742909).287 Genetic studies have mapped AITD susceptibility to a 6.1-kb 3′UTR region of CTLA-4, with the CT60 SNP demonstrating the strongest association for both GD and HT.406 Watanabe et al. further identified that demethylation of +22 CpG and CT60 CpG-SNPs may confer susceptibility to GD and HT in individuals with the CT60 GG genotype, while −15 CpG demethylation potentially contributes to GD treatment resistance.407 Genome-wide association studies have shown significantly higher TRAb levels at GD onset in CT60 GG genotype carriers than in carriers of other genotypes.408 Notably, the CT60G allele in the homozygous state appears to modulate AITD risk in pediatric celiac disease patients.409 Additional evidence links both exon 1 (+49A/G) and intron 1 (1822C/T) polymorphisms to GD susceptibility.410 While −318T allele has been associated with enhanced promoter activity and reduced autoimmunity in functional studies, most clinical investigations fail to demonstrate significant associations between −318 C/T and GD, suggesting that population-specific or epistatic effects may influence this polymorphism’s functional impact.411,412 Ultimately, studies have investigated CTLA-4 polymorphisms (CT60, +49A/G, −318C/T) as critical determinants of thyroid autoimmunity, mediating disease risk via epigenetic (CpG demethylation), transcriptional (promoter activity), and immunological (TRAb production) mechanisms, with distinct population and pediatric clinical implications.

Similar to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies, CTLA-4 blockade leads to several thyroid disorders, such as hypothyroidism, thyrotoxicosis, painless thyroiditis, and even thyroid storm.413 One meta-analysis including 38 randomized clinical trials comprising 7551 patients showed that the predicted incidence of hypothyroidism is 3.8% (95% CI, 1.9–7.8%) with ipilimumab.396 Another comprehensive retrospective review revealed that following ipilimumab therapy, the overall incidence of hypothyroidism/thyroiditis was 6%. Interestingly, the incidence was up to 22% for either thyroiditis or hypothyroidism in patients treated with both ipilimumab and nivolumab.414 In other words, CTLA-4 blockade alone, particularly in combination with PD-1 blockade, is associated with an increased risk of thyroid disorders. Additionally, ICI-induced thyrotoxicosis predominantly results from destructive thyroiditis, characterized by immune-mediated destruction of thyroid follicular cells and subsequent release of preformed thyroid hormones.413 Clinical evidence demonstrates that approximately two-thirds (68%) of patients with thyroiditis eventually progress to hypothyroidism, based on pooled data from a comprehensive meta-analysis.415 The median time interval between thyroiditis diagnosis and the development of hypothyroidism was reported to be ~6 weeks.416 Given this rapid progression, close monitoring of thyroid function is strongly recommended to facilitate early detection and timely management of emerging hypothyroidism. In brief, it is essential to recognize endocrine and metabolic irAEs promptly and exert adequate treatment to allow patients to continue immune therapy.

Other immune checkpoints and thyroid disease

Beyond the well-established PD-1 and CTLA-4 pathways, next-generation immune checkpoints—particularly LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT—have emerged as critical regulators of thyroid pathophysiology, bridging autoimmune dysregulation and tumor immune evasion417–419 (Fig. 5). Recent advances highlight their dual roles: as genetic determinants of AITD susceptibility and as actionable targets in refractory thyroid carcinomas. Mechanistic studies have revealed that these checkpoints orchestrate thyrocyte-immune crosstalk through distinct ligand‒receptor interactions (e.g., LAG-3/MHC-II, TIM-3/galectin-9, TIGIT/CD155), while clinical evidence demonstrates their prognostic value and therapeutic potential across thyroid cancer subtypes.420 Here, we synthesize multiomic, functional, and translational findings to delineate how LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT reshape the thyroid immune microenvironment, offering novel strategies for precision immunotherapy.

Among emerging immune checkpoints, LAG-3 represents the most promising therapeutic target following PD-1 and CTLA-4.421 Studies have indicated the importance of LAG-3 in AITD. For example, a large GWAS and multiomic analysis identified that a 5′-UTR variant (rs781745126-T) in LAG-3 confers a 3.4-fold risk of autoimmune thyroid disease and reduces LAG-3 expression.422 An observational study has shown that LAG-3+ T regulatory type 1 (Tr1) cells exert a considerable immunosuppressive effect in AITD.423 Furthermore, LAG-3 upregulation was detected in both anaplastic thyroid carcinoma and papillary thyroid carcinoma.424 Interestingly, a bispecific LAG-3/PD-L1 antagonist, FS118, was reported to overcome initial PD-1 pathway resistance and have a durable response in anaplastic thyroid carcinoma.425 These findings collectively support dual PD-L1/LAG-3 blockade as a viable therapeutic strategy for refractory metastatic thyroid malignancies, particularly anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC).

The expression and function of TIM-3 in thyroid disease have been well explored in the past decades. All these findings suggest that TIM-3 plays an important role in the occurrence and development of thyroid diseases. For example, TIM-3 expression is significantly downregulated in Th1 and Th17 cells from patients with thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy, suggesting its potential role in modulating Th1/Th17 immune responses and contributing to the pathogenesis of Graves’ ophthalmopathy.426 TIM-3/galectin-9 alleviates inflammation in thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy (TAO) patients by suppressing the AKT/NF-kB-signaling pathway.427 A large cohort study recruited a total of 200 MTC patients and indicated that TIM-3 and CTLA-4 expression and PD-1/PD-L1 coexpression were associated with worse structural recurrence-free survival.428 Huayang Wang et al. proposed that serum levels of sICs, especially sTIM-3, could help to predict cervical lymph node metastasis (CLNM) and provide evidence for surgical decision-making in differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC).429 Furthermore, studies have shown that PD-1+TIM-3+ CD8+ T lymphocytes behave as functional exhaustion in patients with regionally metastatic differentiated thyroid cancer.430 These results contribute to the understanding of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in thyroid carcinoma, offering insights into the immunosuppressive factors specifically associated with TC. Accordingly, targeting these immunosuppressive factors may activate the antitumor immune response in TC.

TIGIT, a promising tumor marker, has been primarily discussed for its differential expression in thyroid diseases. Immunohistochemical studies confirm its frequent presence in medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) and, to a lesser extent, in high-grade thyroid carcinomas.431 In addition, integrative single-cell transcriptome analysis revealed that immune checkpoint molecules, such as TIGIT, were highly expressed in ATC.432 Additionally, scientists found that TIGIT and FCRL3 expression were significantly increased in hypothyroid patients, and patients with higher TIGIT mRNA levels had a greater prevalence of hypothyroidism.433 To recapitulate, the cumulative evidence offers insights into immunosuppressive factors and provides novel strategies for thyroid diseases.

Conclusion and perspectives

The crosstalk between the immune and metabolic systems is pivotal in maintaining organismal homeostasis. Evidently, immune checkpoint molecules play fundamental roles in broad biological functions, including endocrine and metabolic processes. Although numerous literature reports have elaborated on the impact of the interaction between metabolism and immunity on health and disease state, especially in cancer development, there is a lack of systematic summation of immunity and typical metabolic diseases, particularly focusing on immune checkpoints. In this review, we delineate the current understanding of the interactions between key immune checkpoints and three prevalent metabolic diseases, including obesity, diabetes, and thyroid disorders. Mechanistically, these checkpoints modulate disease progression through transcriptional regulation, signaling pathway engagement, and immune microenvironment remodeling. These findings position immune checkpoints as critical nodes in immune-metabolic homeostasis, offering transformative insights for therapeutic development. Future research should prioritize the design of metabolic disease-specific clinical trials with enhanced safety monitoring protocols to evaluate checkpoint modulation, coupled with the development of multiomics biomarker panels for precise patient stratification and treatment response prediction. The implementation of precision medicine frameworks that integrate immunological and metabolic profiling data will be crucial, as will the development of rational combination therapies simultaneously targeting checkpoint and metabolic pathways. The integration of artificial intelligence with multiomics data promises to uncover novel therapeutic targets, while breakthroughs in this interdisciplinary field may establish new treatment paradigms for metabolic diseases with cancer or autoimmune comorbidities.
