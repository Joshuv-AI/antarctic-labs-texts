Early mortality in patients with acute promyelocytic leukemia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Dara Chean, Hélène Kemp, Sofiane Fodil, Michael Darmon, Elie Azoulay, Thibault Dupont
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn patients with acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), early mortality, defined as death within 30 days following diagnosis, is the main driver of overall survival. Intensivists play a pivotal role in the management of patients with APL.MethodThe PubMed and Embase databases were searched on December 1, 2023. All studies focusing on early mortality in patients with newly diagnosed APL were included. Two reviewers independently selected the studies and assessed bias using the risk-of-bias 2 scale for randomized controlled trials (RCT) and the Newcastle-Ottawa scale for other types of studies. A meta-analysis using a random effects model was performed. The primary outcome was early mortality. A subgroup analysis was performed to assess the differences between interventional and observational studies. Secondary outcomes included reasons for early mortality and the occurrence of severe hemorrhage or differentiation syndrome (DS).ResultsOf the 950 studies screened, 140 met the inclusion criteria. A total of 54,923 patients newly diagnosed with APL were included in the meta-analysis. A meta-analytical early mortality rate of 12% was calculated across 122 studies. Early mortality was lower in interventional studies (8%) than in observational studies (15%). Hemorrhage was the main cause of early mortality (57%), followed by sepsis (18%), DS (12%), and thrombotic events (2%). In a meta-regression, early mortality was influenced by patients’ age, white blood cell count at diagnosis, time, study type, and country of origin according to income classification.ConclusionThere is an encouraging decrease over time of early mortality in patients with APL. Early mortality is lower in interventional studies. This result reflects the difficulty of translating the successes of RCT and interventional research into real-world clinical practice. This study puts forward the need to improve and standardize early management, notably in the intensive care unit.Trial registrationThis study’s protocol was preregistered on PROSPERO (CRD42023488480).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05743-7.

Introduction

Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) is a subtype of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) characterized by cell-cycle arrest at the promyelocyte stage due to chromosomal translocation t(15;17) causing fusion of PML-RARA genes. APL was considered one of the most lethal forms of AML before the introduction of targeted differentiation treatments in the 1980 s, such as all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and, later, arsenic trioxide (ATO) [1–4]. These treatments have dramatically reshaped the landscape of APL, with long-term survival rates exceeding 80–90% in clinical trials [5, 6]. Better supportive care may also have contributed to these changes.

Unlike most cases of AML, APL typically presents with pancytopenia. This may delay its recognition in the emergency or intensive care unit (ICU) setting. However, up to 20% of patients present with a white blood cell (WBC) count higher than 10 × 109/L, and are at increased risk for complications. Furthermore, physicians should be aware of microgranular form of APL, which may present with higher WBC count and absent Auer rods. These high-risk forms are associated with an increased risk of differentiation syndrome (DS), and early mortality [7].

Despite these advances, early mortality (within 30 days following diagnosis) remains high and constitutes the leading cause of overall mortality in APL. Intensivists play a pivotal role in the management of APL, because preventing early mortality improves overall survival. Early mortality is caused by hemorrhagic complications due to severe disseminated intravascular coagulopathy (DIC), but also by other complications such as DS or sepsis. For intensivists, early recognition and prompt initiation of ATRA are critical [8].

Changes in early mortality among patients with newly diagnosed APL have been described over the years, but are difficult to evaluate and interpret [9]. This is partly due to important inconsistencies across studies. This inconsistency must be addressed to create a thorough synthesis of the current literature and evidence. Furthermore, such a synthesis may help improve early management strategies and raise physicians’ awareness on early mortality related to APL.

This systematic review and meta-analysis focuses on early mortality in patients newly diagnosed with APL, aiming to characterize its prevalence, identify its causes and determinants, and assess temporal trends over recent decades.

Materials and methods

Registration

The study protocol was preregistered in PROSPERO (CRD42023488480). Institutional review board approval was not required, as this study did not include individual patient data. This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020 checklist) reporting guidelines (Table S1).

Search strategy

The PubMed and Embase databases were searched on December 1, 2023 for studies with a string combining (promyelocytic [Title/Abstract]) with the following index terms: (intensive[Title/Abstract]), (critical[Title/Abstract]), (early death[Title/Abstract]) and (early mortality[Title/Abstract]). A similar search string was used for the Embase database. Studies registered as systematic reviews, narrative reviews, and meta-analysis were excluded before screening. No time restriction was applied. The full search strings are available in Table S2.

Selection criteria

Two reviewers (DC and TD) independently screened titles and abstracts of publications that were identified through the systematical search conducted on PubMed and Embase databases. A third independent reviewer (EA) solved the disagreements during the screening phase. The authors of the studies that were not retrieved were not contacted for access to their manuscripts.

The following types of studies were excluded: [1] animal studies [2], case reports [3], remaining reviews and meta-analyses [4], translational research articles [5], studies about AML excluding APL [6], specifically pediatric studies about APL [7], long-term outcomes studies, and [8] language of the study not spoken by the authors (studies published in English or in French were included).

Quality assessment

The quality assessment was performed by two independent reviewers (DC and TD). The risk-of-bias 2 tool (RoB2) was used for randomized controlled trials (RCT) [10]. The Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) for cohort studies was used for other studies [11]. Reviewers resolved disagreements through discussion.

Data extraction and outcomes

Two reviewers (DC and TD) independently assessed full texts of the selected publications for data extraction. A pre-designed data extraction spreadsheet was used to collect the following variables: first author, publication year, study type, country of study, start and end years of inclusion, number of APL patients, gender, age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score, Sanz risk score, microgranular APL, presence of DIC, platelets and white blood cell (WBC) count at diagnosis, received treatment for APL, DS, presence of hemorrhage at diagnosis, presence of severe hemorrhage (either of cerebral, gastrointestinal or pulmonary origin), and early mortality.

Study type was a dichotomous variable with “interventional” referring to RCT and interventional non-RCT studies (i.e. reports of cooperative protocols or prospective hematology cohorts). “Observational” referred to other studies, including population-based studies.

Mean age and standard deviations were estimated from median, range and number of patients, using Hozo’s method [12]. In the literature, early mortality was consistently defined as 30-day mortality or death before complete remission. We used the same definition and extracted the reason for early mortality (hemorrhage, DS, sepsis, or thrombotic event).

Statistical analysis

A linear random-effects model was used to estimate meta-analytical raw proportions. The Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method was used to estimate between-study heterogeneity (τ2). Several measures of statistical heterogeneity were also performed: the Cochran’s Q statistic (which measures the difference between the study means and the fixed effect estimate), the Higgin’s and Thompson’s I2 statistic (which is the percentage of total heterogeneity attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than to sampling error). As the Cochran’s Q statistic follows a χ2 distribution with k-1 degrees of freedom (with k the number of studies), a p-value lower than 0.05 means that true proportions from included studies’ populations are significantly different. Confidence intervals (CI) of different estimators were calculated using the Knapp-Hartung method.

The primary outcome was early mortality. A subgroup analysis was conducted to assess early mortality according to study type (interventional or observational), comparison was made using a Wald-type test. Secondary outcomes included reason for early mortality, prevalence of hemorrhage and DS and associated mortality rates.

We performed a multivariable meta-regression to assess the impact, expressed as odds ratio (OR), of mean age, WBC count at diagnosis, platelet count at diagnosis, time (estimated by the mean year of inclusion), study type (interventional or observational), and country type on early mortality. The mean year of inclusion was calculated as the mean of the first and last years of inclusion in the study and was considered a categorical variable. Studies were classified into five subgroups for each decade: [1] before 1980 [2], 1980–1990 [3], 1990–2000 [4], 2000–2010 [5], after 2010. The country of study was classified according to the World Bank income classification (World Bank, 2024). Three different country types were identified: [1] high-income countries (used as reference in the meta-regression model) [2], upper-middle-income countries [3], lower-middle-income countries. Predictors were predefined based on clinical relevance and were added to the meta-regression model using the forced entry method.

Sensitivity analyses were performed. In the first one, we only included high-quality studies (“low risk of bias” at RoB2 grading or “good quality” at NOS grading). For the second analysis, we excluded non-RCT interventional studies to avoid potential overlap in patients between interventional studies. For the third analysis, we excluded studies in which patients did not receive differentiation treatment, as well as studies that did not report induction treatment.

All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.5.0) and the metafor package [13].

Results

Included studies

A total of 950 separate studies were identified from PubMed and Embase databases with our search strategy. After independent double screening, a total of 185 studies were sought for retrieval and 168 studies were finally assessed by full text reading. A total of 140 studies, totaling 54,923 patients diagnosed with APL between 1974 and 2022, met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. Early mortality

Meta-analytical early mortality was 12% (95% CI [11; 14], I2 = 95%), estimated across 122 studies totaling 45,959 patients, and was lower in interventional (8%, 95% CI [6; 9], I2 = 90%) as compared to observational studies (15%, 95% CI [13; 17], I2 = 95%) (p < 0.01). Pooled early mortality rates are summarized in Fig. 2. Figures S2-S3 present the early mortality estimates from individual interventional and observational studies, respectively.

Hemorrhage was the main complication resulting in early mortality in patients newly diagnosed with APL. A pooled proportion of 57% of early mortality (95% CI [52; 62], I2 = 78%) were due to hemorrhage. Sepsis, DS and thrombotic events contributed to 18% (95% CI [15; 22], I2 = 69%), 12% (95% CI [10; 15], I2 = 57%) and 2% (95% CI [2; 3], I2 = 0%) of early mortality, respectively. Reasons for early mortality are summarized in Figure S4, and according to study type in Table S10.

The meta-regression analysis included 74 studies to assess the impact of age, WBC count at diagnosis, platelet count at diagnosis, time (approximated with mean year of inclusion), study type, and country of study on early mortality. As presented in Table 2, mean age, WBC count at diagnosis, and year of inclusion were associated with early mortality. Studies with a mean year of inclusion between 1990 and 2000 were associated with higher early mortality rates than those with a mean year of inclusion after 2010. Platelet count at diagnosis was not associated with early mortality. Interventional studies were associated with lower early mortality rate as compared to observational studies. Country of study influenced early mortality. Using high-income countries as reference, studies from lower-middle-income countries were associated with higher early mortality rates. Age, WBC count at diagnosis, platelet count at diagnosis, time, study type and country of study explained 46% of total variance (R2 value).

Differentiation syndrome

Across 66 studies, 1703/8751 patients experienced DS, representing a meta-analytical proportion of 19% (95% CI [17; 22], I2 = 89%). The meta-analytical mortality rate of DS was 5% (95% CI [3; 6], I2 = 14%), estimated across 53 studies (Figure S5).

Temporal trends in DS incidence are presented in Figure S6.

Hemorrhage

A meta-analytical proportion of 66% (95% CI [55; 76], I2 = 98%) of patients presented with hemorrhage at APL diagnosis. During diagnostic and induction phases, a pooled 15% (95% CI [11; 21], I2 = 84%) of patients experienced severe hemorrhage, resulting in a 49% mortality rate (95% CI [38; 59], I2 = 43%). Temporal trends in severe hemorrhage incidence are presented in Figure S7.

Cerebral hemorrhages accounted for 61% of severe hemorrhagic events (95% CI [47; 73], I2 = 60%). Gastrointestinal and pulmonary hemorrhages accounted for 34% of severe hemorrhages (95% CI [21; 49], I2 = 65%). The associated mortality rates were 64% (95% CI [39; 84], I2 = 35%) and 46% (95% CI [19; 75], I2 = 0%), respectively.

Characteristics and prevalence of hemorrhagic events are summarized in Table S11. Table S12 shows the characteristics of hemorrhagic events according to study type.

Sensitivity analyses

Early mortality rates were consistent across all sensitivity analyses. Interventional studies were associated with lower early mortality rates than observational studies.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides new insights for the intensivist to interpret current literature regarding early mortality in APL, in the ATRA era. The analysis included 140 studies, totaling 54,923 patients diagnosed with APL over the last five decades. Despite advances in targeted therapy and its accessibility, early mortality remains high, with an overall pooled estimate of 12%. Importantly, early mortality significantly differs between interventional studies and observational studies with pooled estimates of 8% and 15%, respectively. This suggests that improvements achieved in RCT or protocol settings in high-volume centers, with early recognition of APL, early initiation of ATRA/ATO, and close monitoring, have not been fully translated into everyday clinical practice. Observational studies feature more heterogeneous populations, potential diagnostic and treatment delays, and inconstant access to specialized care that may contribute to persistent higher early mortality rates. According to a real-world study from the Vizient Clinical Database, up to 20% of patients with APL received guideline–concordant therapy specific to their risk status [14]. In the meta-regression model, early mortality decreased over time, consistently with recent literature on other critically ill immunocompromised patients, onco-hematological patients or patients with acute leukemia [15, 16]. Whether these changes in mortality are explained by the improvement of therapeutic strategies and better supportive care or to a change in the patients’ case-mix is still to be determined.

This discrepancy between interventional and observational studies highlights the need for implementing new diagnostic strategies to initiate differentiation therapy promptly. For instance, a machine-learning model can accurately predict APL diagnosis, based on simple baseline biological and clinical variables with 99.7% accuracy. This algorithm can also diagnose non-promyelocytic AML and acute lymphoblastic leukemia with the same precision and could be a useful tool in low-income countries or community healthcare centers lacking cytological expertise [17]. Our results are consistent with the current literature. A decrease in early mortality was found between 2000 and 2014 in a United States nationwide study based on the National Cancer Database [9]. Interestingly, higher socioeconomic status, proximity to a hospital, and treatment at academic centers were associated with lower early mortality rates, emphasizing the importance of healthcare-system-related factors. Collaboration between specialized high-volume centers and community healthcare centers must be encouraged as it may improve early mortality [18].

It is noteworthy to point out the regional disparities in the meta-regression even though inferences are difficult to make. Studies from high-income countries were associated with lower early mortality rates than studies from lower-middle-income countries. This finding can only be hypothesis-generating, as many cofounders could not be controlled due to the aggregated nature of our analysis, but it suggests a possible impact of different healthcare system organizations on APL early mortality. Access to differentiation therapy is another important issue that may be due to geographical factors. According to a national survey conducted in the United States, only one third of hospitals had ATRA available immediately. Furthermore, only 15% of centers that referred APL patients to other centers had ATRA [19].

Hemorrhage and DS were important APL-specific contributors to early mortality, with pooled estimates of 57% and 12%, respectively. Severe hemorrhages were found in a meta-analytical rate of 15%. Cerebral hemorrhage accounted for 61% of severe hemorrhage and was associated with a 64% mortality rate, emphasizing the need for differentiation therapy before the diagnosis confirmation, and the importance of close monitoring and an efficient strategy to control coagulopathy, as stated in recent recommendations [20].

DS was found in 19% of patients and was associated with a 5% mortality rate. Prophylaxis with dexamethasone has been recommended for high-risk patients with WBC count higher than 5 × 109/L, without strong evidence [21]. Our meta-regression confirmed the association between WBC count at diagnosis and early mortality. High-risk patients with WBC count higher than 10 × 109/L could also benefit from cytoreduction in order to mitigate complications from differentiation therapy. There are different cytoreductive strategies based on hydroxyurea, anthracycline, cytarabine, or gemtuzumab ozogamicin [20]. Leukapheresis does not affect mortality and should not be used due to the hemorrhagic risk associated with inserting large-bore catheters in patients with severe coagulopathy [22]. WBC count should be closely monitored after the initiation of differentiation therapy, as leukocytosis is the first sign of DS, and is associated with higher rates of other treatment-related complications [23]. Preemptive admission to the ICU for close monitoring may be beneficial for high-risk patients [24].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating discrepancies in early outcomes between clinical trials and real-world settings. However, some limitations need to be acknowledged. First, we used a restrictive search string to focus on short-term outcomes and may have missed some references reporting early mortality without mentioning it in the title or abstract. We voluntarily did not add studies from citation searches, nor updated the search between protocol registration and submission, to ensure the reproducibility of our search and results. Furthermore, our language restrictions may have excluded some Chinese reports, which could result in missing an important number of patients. Second, our search string was not restrictive for studies published before the ATRA era, and the lack of time restriction could have exaggerated the effect of time on early mortality. However, a meta-analytical proportion of 97% patients received ATRA for induction therapy, and a sensitivity analysis was performed to account for this limitation. Therefore, this meta-analysis mainly reflects outcomes of the ATRA era. Third, the aggregated nature of this meta-analysis did not allow us to control several important individual confounding factors that may be associated with early mortality such as time from diagnosis to ATRA initiation, initial admission to the ICU, or end-of-life decisions. Assessing the impact of these factors is not possible without an individual patient data meta-analysis. Another limitation of the aggregated nature of this analysis is that, although unlikely, we cannot be certain that some patients were not included in more than one study. The potential overlap in study populations from RCT and interventional non-RCT studies might have transgressed the assumption of statistical independence in meta-analysis leading to underestimation of variance, and narrower confidence intervals. Therefore, our results must be considered with caution. However, results remained unchanged in a sensitivity analysis excluding potential sources of overlap (Supplementary Appendix). Fourth, early mortality may have been underestimated because it is impossible to account for patients who died from APL before being admitted to the hospital. Fifth, although heterogeneity was high, we considered it an opportunity to assess differences across studies rather than a methodological issue. This study aimed to average current literature rather than compare different treatment strategies. Finally, a small number of studies reported ICU-specific outcomes such as the need for life-sustaining therapies, suggesting this meta-analysis mainly summarizes hematological literature. This further emphasizes the need to involve intensivists in improving of early management of patients diagnosed with APL.

Conclusion

To conclude, this systematic review and meta-analysis emphasizes the differences between interventional studies and real-world, observational studies. The lower early mortality rates of patients with APL included in interventional studies reflect the difficulty of translating successes of clinical research to everyday practice. For intensivists of the ATRA era, this study puts forward the importance of early APL recognition, prompt initiation of differentiation therapy, and proactive management of high-risk patients, coagulopathy, severe hemorrhage and DS. This meta-analysis should encourage the development of ICU-focused guidelines for the management of patients with newly diagnosed APL.
