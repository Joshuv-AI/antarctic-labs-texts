Search for the pair production of long-lived supersymmetric partners of the tau lepton in proton-proton collisions at s = 13 ​ \TeV \sqrt{s}=13\TeV


Abstract
Gauge-mediated supersymmetry-breaking models provide a strong motivation to search for a supersymmetric partner of the tau lepton (stau) with a macroscopic lifetime. Long-lived stau decays produce tau leptons that are displaced from the primary proton-proton interaction vertex, leading to an unconventional signature. This paper presents a search for the direct production of long-lived staus decaying within the CMS tracker volume in proton-proton collisions at s = 13 ​ \TeV \sqrt{s}=13\TeV , performed for the first time with an identification algorithm based on a graph neural network dedicated to displaced tau leptons. The data sample, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 138 \fbinv , was recorded with the CMS experiment at the CERN LHC between 2016 and 2018. This search excludes, at 95% confidence level, stau masses, m \PSGt m_{\PSGt} , in the 126–260 (90–425) \GeV range for a proper decay length of 50 \mm in the maximally mixed (mass-degenerate) scenario, while for m \PSGt = 200 ​ \GeV m_{\PSGt}=200\GeV , stau proper decay lengths are excluded in the range 21–94 (6–333) \mm . These results improve the exclusion limits compared to previous searches, and extend the parameter space explored in the context of supersymmetry.

0.2
The CMS detector

The central feature of the CMS apparatus is a superconducting solenoid of 6
\unit
m internal diameter that provides a magnetic field of 3.8
\unit
T. Within the solenoid volume are a silicon pixel and strip tracker, a lead tungstate crystal electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL), and a brass and scintillator hadron calorimeter (HCAL), each composed of a barrel and two endcap sections. Forward calorimeters extend the coverage in pseudorapidity (
\eta
) provided by the barrel and endcap detectors. Muons are detected in gas-ionization chambers embedded in the steel flux-return yoke outside the solenoid.
A more detailed description of the CMS detector, together with a definition of the coordinate system used and the relevant kinematic variables, is provided in Refs.
[
50
,
51
]
.

Events of interest are selected using a two-tiered trigger system. The first level, composed of custom hardware processors, uses information from the calorimeters and muon detectors to select events at a rate of around 100
\unit
kHz within a fixed latency of 4
\mus

[
52
]
. The second level, known as the high-level trigger, consists of a farm of processors running a version of the full event reconstruction software optimized for fast processing, and it reduces the event rate to a few
\unit
kHz before data storage
[
53
,
54
]
.

The signal cross sections are evaluated for a set of discrete

For each

Two different cases have been considered for the stau pair production in this study.
The first is the production of
\PSGtDo\HepSusyAntiParticle{\PGt}{1}{}

\PSGtL
and
\PSGtR

\pi/4

The second scenario is the production of both

Since in this case the gauge eigenstates are the same as the mass eigenstates, which are considered to be degenerate in mass, this is referred to as the “mass-degenerate scenario” in this paper.
The mass-degenerate scenario leads to a cross section between three and five times higher than the maximally mixed scenario for each
m_{\PSGt}

In this search, the contribution from background processes is estimated using control samples in data, as detailed in Section

Simulated events are used to validate the background estimation procedure based on data, and to train and calibrate the identification algorithm discussed in Section

0.4
Event reconstruction

0.4.1
Standard object reconstruction

A particle-flow (PF) algorithm
[
73
]
reconstructs each individual particle in an event, with an optimized combination of information from various components of the CMS detector.
The energies of electrons and photons are measured in the ECAL.
The momentum of electrons is determined by a combined measurement of the track momentum in the tracker, the energy of the matching ECAL supercluster, and the energy of all bremsstrahlung photons consistent with originating from the track.
The momentum of muons is obtained from the bending of the corresponding tracks in the tracker and the muon spectrometer, which comprises three technologies: drift tubes, cathode strip chambers, and resistive plate chambers.
The energy of charged hadrons, which is corrected for the response function of the calorimeters to hadronic showers, is determined from a combination of the momentum measured in the tracker and the energy of the matching ECAL and HCAL clusters.
Finally, the energy of neutral hadrons is obtained from the corrected energies in the corresponding ECAL and HCAL clusters.

Collision vertices are reconstructed by fitting tracks in an event and are required to be within 24
\cm
of the center of the detector in the
z
direction (along the beam) and to have a transverse displacement from the beam line of less than 2
\cm
.
The primary vertex is taken to be the vertex corresponding to the hardest scattering in the event, evaluated using tracking information alone, as described in Section 9.4.1 of Ref.
[
74
]
.
Reconstruction of jets is performed by clustering PF objects using the anti-
\kt
algorithm
[
75
,
76
]
with a distance parameter of
R=0.4
.
The jet momentum is determined as the vector sum of all particle momenta in the jet and is typically within 5–10% of the true momentum over the entire
\pt
spectrum within the detector acceptance.
Pileup interactions contribute to spurious tracks and calorimetric energy deposits, increasing the apparent jet momentum.
To mitigate this effect, tracks identified as originating from pileup vertices are discarded, and an offset is applied to correct for the remaining contributions
[
77
]
.
Jets are calibrated using information from both simulation and data
[
77
]
.
Additional selection criteria are applied to remove jets that are potentially dominated by instrumental effects or reconstruction failures
[
78
]
.
Only jets with
\pt>30\GeV
,
\abs{\eta}<2.1
, and containing at least one charged PF object with
\pt>1\GeV
are considered in this analysis.
The absolute transverse impact parameter (
\abs{d_{xy}}
) of a jet and its impact parameter significance (
\hat{d}_{xy}
)—the ratio between
\abs{d_{xy}}
and its uncertainty—are defined as the
\abs{d_{xy}}
and
\hat{d}_{xy}
of its charged constituent with the highest
\pt
.

Jets originating from the fragmentation of
\PQb
quarks are identified as
\PQb
-tagged jets
[
79
]
using the
DeepJet
algorithm
[
80
,
81
]
. The algorithm employs properties of reconstructed secondary vertices and charged and neutral particle constituents of the jet as inputs to a convolutional deep neural network.
The “medium” selection or working point (WP) of this algorithm corresponds to a signal efficiency of about 80%, with a misidentification probability of about 1% for light-flavored jets (
\ie
, originating from gluons or up, down, and strange quarks) and about 11% for jets originating from charm quarks.
Jets passing this WP are henceforth referred to as
\PQb
-tagged jets.

Electrons are identified using the “loose” WP of a boosted decision tree algorithm
[
82
,
83
]
that uses inputs based on the spatial distribution of the shower, track-to-cluster-matching criteria, consistency between the cluster energy and the track momentum, and isolation.
Electrons satisfying the loose WP and with
\pt>20\GeV
and
\abs{\eta}<2.4
are referred to as veto-electrons in what follows.

Muon reconstruction uses a global fit combining information from the tracker and muon spectrometers
[
84
]
.
Muon candidates are required to pass either the “loose” or the “tight” WP of an identification algorithm using criteria such as the quality of the global fit and the geometrical matching between the tracks in the tracker and the muon spectrometers.
Isolation criteria are imposed on the muon candidates to reject those originating from hadronic decays.
The isolation variable used for this purpose is defined as the scalar
\pt
sum of reconstructed charged and neutral particles within a cone of radius
\Delta R=\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\Delta\eta)^{2}+(\Delta\phi)^{2}}}
= 0.4, where
\phi
is the azimuthal angle in radians, around the muon candidate track, which is excluded from the sum, divided by the
\pt
of the lepton candidate.
Charged particles not originating from the primary vertex are excluded from this sum, and a correction is applied to account for the neutral components originating from pileup, as described in Ref.
[
85
]
.
Muons satisfying the tight (loose) WP and with relative isolation less than 15 (25)%,
\pt>20\ (28)\GeV
, and
\abs{\eta}<2.4
, are referred to as veto-muons (id-muons) in what follows.

Hadronic tau lepton candidates are reconstructed from one charged hadron and up to two neutral pions, or three charged hadrons and up to one neutral pion, consistent with originating from the decay of a tau lepton, using the hadrons-plus-strips algorithm
[
43
]
.
To distinguish between jets originating from quarks or gluons and genuine hadronic tau lepton decays, the discriminant of the
DeepTau

[
44
]
deep neural network algorithm is used.
The
\tauh
candidates are required to have
\pt>30\GeV
,
\abs{\eta}<2.4
, and are selected with the “medium” WP of the above discriminant, which has an efficiency of
{\approx}
70% and a misidentification probability of
{\approx}
1%.
In addition, the
\tauh
candidates are required to satisfy the “very loose” (“medium”) WP of the
DeepTau
discriminant against electrons (muons), which has an efficiency of
{\approx}
98 (99.8)% and a misidentification probability of
{\approx}
1.5 (0.04)%.
These
\tauh
candidates are used to identify a region enriched in
\PZ\to\PGt\PGt
events, where the calibration factors for the
DisTau
identification efficiency are measured, as described in Section
0.4.2
.

The missing transverse momentum vector,
\ptvecmiss
, is computed as the negative vector
\ptvec
sum of all the PF objects in an event and its magnitude is denoted as
\ptmiss

[
86
]
.
The
\ptvecmiss
is modified to account for the energy calibration of all the PF candidates in an event, clustered into jets or not.

0.4.2
Displaced
\tauh
candidates

The
DisTau

[
45
]
graph neural network algorithm is trained to distinguish between signal jets originating from displaced
\tauh
candidates (
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
) and those from the fragmentation of light-flavored, charm, and bottom quarks, and gluons (background jets).

The algorithm, based on the
ParticleNet

[
48
]
architecture, represents the particles in a jet as a “point cloud” and performs dynamic graph convolution operations
[
87
]
on the properties of each jet constituent and its nearest neighbors.
The features provided as input to the network are the same as those used in the
DeepTau
algorithm
[
44
]
.
The
DisTau
algorithm is optimized to identify
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
produced within the tracker volume of the CMS detector.
The algorithm exploits features relevant for both hadronic
\PGt
decays (such as the number of charged hadrons and their isolation properties), as well as displaced
\PGt
decays (such as the impact parameters of the charged constituent tracks).
Displacements of less than 10
\mm
result in reduced discrimination between
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates and background jets, while for displacements above around 500
\mm
, the
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidate cannot produce enough hits in the tracker to be distinguished from the track of the
\sTau
that produced it, leading to a lower identification efficiency.

The distributions of
DisTau
scores for signal and background jets are presented in Fig.
2
. The “loose”, “medium”, and “tight” WPs of the
DisTau
algorithm are denoted as WPL, WPM, and WPT, with signal efficiencies of about
{>}99
, 98, and 80%, respectively, measured in simulated events for a signal hypothesis of
m_{\PSGt}=200\GeV
and
c\PGt_{0}=100\mm
.
Their respective misidentification probabilities, evaluated in simulated
\ttbar
events, are approximately 7, 1, and 0.06%.
The efficiencies (misidentification probabilities) quoted here are inclusive,
\ie
, measured with respect to all signal (background) jets in the simulated sample.
The lower thresholds on the tagger score corresponding to the three WPs are 0.05, 0.7, and 0.99, respectively.
To be chosen as a
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidate, a jet selected with the criteria mentioned in Section
0.4.1
is also required to satisfy
\abs{d_{xy}}>2\mm
,
\hat{d}_{xy}>5
, and pass the WPL selection.

\PGm\tauh

As there are no truly signal-like
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates in SM processes, the
\tauh

Following a strategy similar to that in Ref.
[
88
]
, events are selected online with a single-muon trigger, and required to have exactly one id-muon and one
\tauh
candidate with opposite electric charges, angular separation
\Delta R(\PGm,\tauh)>0.5
, invariant mass of the
\PGm\tauh
system between 60 and 80
\GeV
, transverse mass
\mT(\PGm,\ptvecmiss)<60\GeV
, and
D_{\zeta}(\PGm,\tauh,\ptvecmiss)>-25\GeV
, with

defined as
[
89
,
88
]

D_{\zeta}(\PGm,\tauh,\ptvecmiss)=\ptvecmiss\cdot\hat{\zeta}-0.85[\ptvec(\PGm)+\ptvec(\tauh)]\cdot\hat{\zeta},

(1)

where
\hat{\zeta}
is the unitary vector oriented along the bisector of the muon and the
\tauh
candidate transverse momenta,
\ptvec(\PGm)
and
\ptvec(\tauh)
, respectively.
The transverse mass for two objects labeled “a” and “b” is defined as

\mT^{2}(\text{a},\text{b})=m^{2}_{\text{a}}+m^{2}_{\text{b}}+2(\et^{\text{a}}\et^{\text{ b}}-\ptvec^{\text{ a}}\cdot\ptvec^{\text{ b}}).

(2)

Here the masses (transverse momenta) of the particles are denoted by
m_{\text{a}}
and
m_{\text{b}}
(
\ptvec^{\text{ a}}
and
\ptvec^{\text{ b}}
), respectively, and
\et^{2}=m^{2}+\pt^{2}
.
The
\mT(\PGm,\ptvecmiss)
formulation is then obtained by replacing the generic particles with a muon and a massless particle of transverse momentum
\ptvecmiss
.

Correction factors are estimated using the “tag-and-probe” technique described in Refs.
[
90
,
44
]
.
The muon serves as the “tag” in this procedure, while the
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidate nearest to the
\tauh

is treated as the “probe”.
The distribution of the
DisTau
score for the probes in the
\PGm\tauh

.
To measure the simulation-to-data correction for a given WP, a maximum likelihood fit is performed on the invariant mass distribution of the
\PGm\tauh
system, simultaneously for events that pass or fail the chosen WP.
The correction to the
DisTau
efficiency is then treated as an anticorrelated parameter across the two fit regions and corrected in simulation to match the one in data.
The correction factor per
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidate for WPT is evaluated from the fit to be within 10% of unity for all years with an uncertainty of 0.15.
The leading contribution to this uncertainty arises from the statistical uncertainty in the number of events that pass the WPT selection.
The behaviour of the SFs with increasing probe jet
d_{xy}
has also been studied, and no significant dependence is observed.

0.5
Event selection

In order to reject the vast majority of events that are easily identifiable as incompatible with the signal topology, we define a baseline region (BR) containing events in both the signal-enriched search region (SR) and the sideband regions that are used for estimating the background contribution in the SR.

Events in the BR are selected with high-level triggers requiring a large transverse momentum imbalance:
\ptmiss>100\GeV
in 2016 and
\ptmiss>120\GeV
in 2017 and 2018.
The efficiency of the
\ptmiss
triggers increases from about 70% at 120
\GeV
to a plateau of almost unity at 250
\GeV
.
Offline, events are required to have
\ptmiss>120\GeV
and exactly two
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates.
Events are rejected if they have any veto-electron, veto-muon, or
\PQb
-tagged jet.
The leading (higher-
\pt
) and subleading (lower-
\pt
)
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidate jets in an event are referred to as
j1
and
j2
, respectively.
The background contribution from jets produced in noncollision processes, such as beam halo interactions, is greatly mitigated by requiring events to satisfy the beam halo filters
[
86
]
.
Since beam halo jets usually populate the plane defined by the beam axis and
\phi=0
or
\pi
, their contribution is further reduced by requiring events to satisfy
\Delta\phi({\text{j1}},{\text{j2}})>0.5
.
In order to mitigate the impact of increased noise in the ECAL endcaps observed during the 2017 data-taking period, events with
\HT^{\text{soft, fwd}}>50\GeV
are vetoed.
Here
\HT^{\text{soft, fwd}}
is defined as the scalar sum of the
\pt
of all soft (uncorrected
\pt<50\GeV
) jets in the forward region (
2.65<\abs{\eta}<3.139
).
Since jets in signal processes are typically harder and in the central region, this selection has a negligible effect on signal events, while suppressing background events.
The requirement on
\HT^{\text{soft, fwd}}
thus helps to improve sensitivity to the signal, and is applied to all data-taking periods.

A separate CR enriched in
{\PW}\text{+jets}
events (WCR) is selected using single-muon triggers.
Offline, events are required to satisfy the aforementioned beam halo filters, have exactly one id-muon, no additional veto-muons or veto-electrons,
\ptmiss>50\GeV
,
60<\mT(\PGm,\ptvecmiss)<120\GeV
, and exactly two
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates satisfying the same requirement on
\Delta\phi({\text{j1}},{\text{j2}})
as in the BR.
The region thus defined is about 85% pure in
{\PW}\text{+jets}
events.

Single-muon triggers are also used to identify another separate CR enriched in
DY+jets
(DYCR).
Offline, events are required to satisfy the aforementioned beam halo filters, have exactly two id-muons with opposite charges, no additional veto-muons or veto-electrons, dimuon invariant mass between 70 and 120
\GeV
, and the same requirement on the
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates as the WCR.
This region is found to be approximately 96% pure in
DY+jets
events.

The contamination from signal processes in WCR and DYCR is found to be negligible (
{<}1\%
) using simulation.
Events satisfying the BR, WCR, and DYCR selections are categorized into the T0, T1, and T2 regions based on whether zero, one, or two jets, respectively, passed the
DisTau
WPT, as illustrated in Table
0.5
.
For example,
BRT0
includes events in the BR satisfying the T0 selection.
We use
BRT2
as the SR, whereas
BRT0
,
BRT1
, WCR, and DYCR are CRs used for background estimation and validation, as described in Section
0.6
.
All the regions discussed here are mutually orthogonal.

The dominant sources of
\ptmiss
in the signal events would be the weakly interacting gravitinos, whose kinematic properties are correlated with those of the visible objects, namely, the
\tauh
candidates.
In contrast,
\ptmiss
in the SM background processes primarily originates from neutrinos.
This difference can be exploited by first constructing the transverse mass
\mT(\text{vis},\text{inv})
, defined in Eq.
2
, with the visible (“vis”) and the invisible (“inv”) particles.
The distribution of
\mT(\text{vis},\text{inv})
presents a maximum at the mass of the parent of the visible and the invisible particles when there is only one source of missing momentum in the system.
To account for pair-produced particles where both have visible and invisible decay products, the “stransverse mass (
\mTii
)”
[
91
,
92
]
is defined as:

\mTii^{2}(\text{vis1},\text{vis2},\ptvecmiss)=\min_{\ptvec^{\text{ inv1}}+\ptvec^{\text{ inv2}}=\ptvecmiss}[\max\{\mT^{2}(\text{vis1},\text{inv1}),\mT^{2}(\text{vis2},\text{inv2})\}].

(3)

Since the momenta of the individual invisible particles in Eq. (
3
) are unknown,
\ptvecmiss
is divided into two components (
\ptvec^{\text{ inv1}}
and
\ptvec^{\text{ inv2}}
) in such a way that the value of
\mTii
is minimized.
If
\mTii
is computed using the two
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates as the visible objects, “vis1” and “vis2”, then its upper limit in the signal will be the stau mass.
This is different from the SM background processes.
For example, in
\ttbar\to\PQb\PAQb\PWp\PWm
events, where both the
\PW
bosons decay to a lepton (visible) and a neutrino (invisible), the upper limit is at the
\PW
boson mass.
In searches where the masses of the invisible particles are unknown, the calculation of
\mTii
requires an assumption on their masses
[
93
]
.
In this analysis, we consider the gravitino to be massless when calculating
\mTii
.

In addition to
\ptmiss
and
\mTii
, the
\pt
of the subleading
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidate (
p_{\text{T,\ j2}}
) is found to be useful to discriminate between signal and background.
Distributions of these three variables for events within the SR are presented in Fig.

.
The background distributions have been estimated using the procedure described in Section
0.6
.
Signal events with different
m_{\PSGt}
populate different regions of the parameter space.
For example, regions with low (high)
\ptmiss
,
\mTii
, and
p_{\text{T,\ j2}}
are sensitive to signals with low (high)
m_{\PSGt}
.
In order to have high sensitivity over a wide range of
m_{\PSGt}
, selected events are categorized into eight bins in the measured
\ptmiss
,
\mTii
, and
p_{\text{T,\ j2}}
, as shown in Table

0.6
Background estimation

The dominant background in this search arises from jets that are misidentified as
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates.
Such jets can be produced in SM processes from
\Pp\Pp
collisions, or in noncollision processes, such as beam halo interactions.
The latter contribution is greatly mitigated with the selections discussed in Section
0.5
.
Jets arising from beam halo are mostly trackless and preferably populate the forward (high
\abs{\eta}
) regions.
Any residual contamination from beam halo is expected to be negligible as
DisTau
relies heavily on the properties of charged PF candidates in jets, and jets are required to have at least one charged PF candidate (
\ie
a track) and be reconstructed within
\abs{\eta}<2.1
, as discussed in Section
0.4.1
.
The contribution to the background yield from electrons, muons, and
\tauh
candidates from SM processes being misidentified as
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates is estimated from simulation to be negligible,
\ie
, with an event yield lower than 0.1 across all bins defined in Table
0.5
, because of the requirements on veto-electrons and veto-muons, and on the
d_{xy}
of the jets, as described in Sections
0.4.1
and
0.5
.
Therefore, they are not included in the background estimation.

Simulated samples are not suitable for estimating the misidentified-jet background from SM processes owing to the limited number of events entering the SR, especially in the tails of the search variable distributions presented in Section
0.5
.
Therefore, we employ a technique fully based on control samples in data for evaluating the SM background arising from jets that are misidentified as
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates.
The procedure has been developed by combining the strategies used in Refs.
[
36
,
94
,
95
]
.
In order to describe the method, the following quantities are to be considered:

•

Misidentification probability
f
, defined as the probability of a jet passing WPT, provided it has passed WPL:

f=n_{\text{jet}}(\text{WPT})/n_{\text{jet}}(\text{WPL}).

(4)

The probability of a jet passing WPL but failing WPT is then
1-f
.

•

N
: total number of events where exactly two jets pass WPL.

•

N_{\text{T0}}
,
N_{\text{T1}}
, and
N_{\text{T2}}
: number of events satisfying
T0
,
T1
, and
T2
criteria, respectively, as described in Table
0.5
.

Noting the above definitions, the following equations can be established:

\displaystyle N_{\text{T0}}

\displaystyle=(1-f_{1})(1-f_{2})N,

(5a)

\displaystyle N_{\text{T1}}

\displaystyle=[f_{1}(1-f_{2})+(1-f_{1})f_{2}]N,

(5b)

\displaystyle N_{\text{T2}}

\displaystyle=f_{1}f_{2}N.

(5c)

Here
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are the misidentification probabilities of the
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidate with higher and lower
\pt
, respectively.

Taking the ratio between the subequations of Eq. (
5
), the following quantities can be obtained:

•

Prediction of
N_{\text{T1}}
from
N_{\text{T0}}
:

N_{\text{T10}}\equiv N^{\text{pred}}_{\text{T1}}=\frac{f_{1}(1-f_{2})+(1-f_{1})f_{2}}{(1-f_{1})(1-f_{2})}N_{\text{T0}}.

(6)

•

Prediction of
N_{\text{T2}}
from
N_{\text{T0}}
:

N_{\text{T20}}\equiv N^{\text{pred}}_{\text{T2}}=\frac{f_{1}f_{2}}{(1-f_{1})(1-f_{2})}N_{\text{T0}}.

(7)

•

Prediction of
N_{\text{T2}}
from
N_{\text{T1}}
:

N_{\text{T21}}\equiv N^{\text{pred}}_{\text{T2}}=\frac{f_{1}f_{2}}{f_{1}(1-f_{2})+(1-f_{1})f_{2}}N_{\text{T1}}.

(8)

Equation (
4
) is used to measure the misidentification probability
f_{\text{W}}
from data, in the WCR described in Section
0.5
.
An alternate measurement,
f_{\text{DY}}
, is performed in the DYCR in data as a cross-check, and is found to agree with
f_{\text{W}}
within the statistical uncertainties.

The misidentified-jet background predictions in BRT1 and the SR are obtained using
f_{\text{W}}
parametrized in two dimensions, in bins of
\pt
and
\abs{d_{xy}}
of the jet.
Their values are, approximately, in the 10–20 and 5–10 (5–10 and 30–40)% ranges with increasing
\pt
(
\abs{d_{xy}}
), depending on the data-taking period.

We use BRT1 as the primary region for validating the background estimation as it is adjacent to the SR, while having a negligible signal contamination of
{\lesssim}1\%
, compared to the predicted background.
Figure
5
shows the observed event yields in BRT1 compared with the signal and background predictions, the latter (obtained using
N_{\text{T10}}
) being found to be consistent with the observed data.
Additional validations are performed by using
f_{\text{W}}
(
f_{\text{DY}}
) to predict the yields in DYCRT1 and DYCRT2 (WCRT1 and WCRT2) and comparing them with the observed data in the corresponding regions.
These comparisons are also found to agree within the uncertainties.

The primary background prediction in the SR is obtained using
N_{\text{T21}}
.
An alternate measurement is performed using
N_{\text{T20}}
, serving as a cross-check against potential signal contamination in BRT1, which would have an impact on the estimation of
N_{\text{T21}}
.
The two estimates are found to agree within uncertainties, and the difference between them is propagated as a systematic uncertainty, as reported in Section
0.7
.

Sources of theoretical uncertainties affecting the signal simulation include the PDFs and missing higher-order corrections.
The effect of these are estimated from the variation in signal yields from alternate PDF sets
[
56
,
96
,
57
]
, and from independently varying the factorization (
\mu_{\text{F}}
) and renormalization (
\mu_{\text{R}}
) scales up and down by a factor of two with the constraint
0.5\leq(\mu_{\text{R}}/\mu_{\text{F}})\leq 2
to account for the uncertainty arising from missing higher-order corrections.
The
SysCalc
package
[
97
]
is used for this purpose.

The experimental sources affecting the signal include the jet energy scale and jet energy resolution, the effect of unclustered energy in calculating
\ptmiss
, pileup reweighting, integrated luminosity, and the uncertainty in the modeling of the
\ptmiss
trigger.
The last uncertainty is taken to be the difference from unity of the simulation-to-data correction to the trigger efficiency for events with
\ptmiss<250\GeV
, estimated with events selected by a single-muon trigger. For events with higher
\ptmiss
, the statistical uncertainty is used.
The simulation is reweighted to make its pileup vertex distribution identical to that of the data.
The uncertainty in estimating the number of pileup interactions is estimated by varying the total inelastic cross section by
\pm
4.6%
[
98
]
.
This is propagated as an uncertainty in the pileup reweighting factor applied to the simulation.
The uncertainty in the measured integrated luminosity amounts to 1.2, 2.3, and 2.5% in 2016, 2017, and 2018
[
99
,
100
,
101
]
, respectively.
In addition, the uncertainty in the correction factor for the
DisTau
identification efficiency described in Section
0.4.2
, is also taken into account.

All sources of systematic uncertainties affecting the signal are considered uncorrelated across data-taking years, except for those from theory and the integrated luminosity uncertainty.
The theory uncertainties are treated as fully correlated across the years, and the integrated luminosity uncertainty is treated as partially correlated, which results in a value of 1.7% on combining the years.

The following systematic uncertainties are propagated to the prediction of the final background yields.
Since the predicted background in the BR is obtained using
f_{\text{W}}
, which is measured in a
{\PW}\text{+jets}
enriched region, one needs to account for how differences between WCR and BR can impact the extrapolation of the misidentification probability in the BR (
f_{\text{BR}}
).
These can arise from differences between the two regions in the flavor composition of the parton corresponding to the jet that is misidentified as a
\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidate.
We use simulations of the SM processes described in Section
0.3
to estimate the relative difference
\abs{f_{\text{W}}^{\text{MC}}-f^{\text{MC}}_{\text{BR}}}/f_{\text{W}}^{\text{MC}}
, which is found to be about 10%.
This is treated as an uncertainty in
f_{\text{W}}
correlated across data-taking years and propagated to the background prediction.
The statistical uncertainty in
f_{\text{W}}
is also propagated to the prediction of the background yields.
In addition, the difference between the primary and alternate background predictions, as discussed in Section
0.6
is taken as an uncertainty.
These last two sources of uncertainties are treated as uncorrelated across data-taking years.

The observed data in the BRT1 region for the first
19\fbinv
of 2016 data taking were observed to be lower than the estimated background by a factor of 0.7 uniformly across all bins.
The discrepancy can possibly be attributed to the saturation effects observed in the APV25 readout chip preamplifier used in the tracker
[
102
]
, during the same data-taking period.
Therefore, an additional correction of
0.7\pm 0.3
is applied to the SR in that portion of the data set, with the uncertainty being taken as the deviation of the factor from unity.
This correction has a negligible effect on the final results described in Section
0.8
.

m_{\PSGt}

c\PGt_{0}

The upper limits become weaker for
c\PGt_{0}
below about 10
\mm

\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates have smaller displacements and resemble background jets.
For
c\PGt_{0}
above a few hundred millimeters, the stau decays in the outer layers of the tracker, which reduces the reconstruction efficiency of
\tauh^{\text{dis}}

\PGt

\tauh^{\text{dis}}

\abs{d_{xy}}

c\PGt_{0}

m_{\PSGt}

\tauh^{\text{dis}}
candidates and the
\ptmiss
in the events, which causes the upper limits to become more stringent.

Summary

A search for the direct pair production of long-lived superpartners of the tau lepton (staus) has been performed in final states with two hadronically decaying tau leptons (
\tauh
) using data collected by the CMS detector in 2016–2018, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 138
\fbinv
.
The potentially large background from misidentified jets in the fully hadronic final state is efficiently suppressed by the
DisTau
neural network algorithm, specifically designed to identify displaced
\tauh
candidates.
In the maximally mixed (mass-degenerate) scenario, stau masses,
m_{\PSGt}
, in the 126–260 (90–425)
\GeV
range are excluded for a proper decay length,
c\PGt_{0}
, of 50
\mm
, and stau proper decay lengths in the range 21–94 (6–333)
\mm
are excluded for
m_{\PSGt}=200\GeV
.
The development of a dedicated algorithm targeting displaced
\tauh
signatures has led to a significant improvement in the experimental sensitivity.
These results improve the exclusion limits compared to previous searches
[
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
, and extend the parameter space explored in the context of supersymmetry.
