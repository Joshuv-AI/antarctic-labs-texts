Evaluating the therapeutic benefits and safety of ecnoglutide for type 2 diabetes: evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Saaed Abunada, Omar Abunada, Fnu Vivek, Lakhveer Rathi, Fnu Muskan, Jiyanth Parkash, Saqlain Nazeer, Fnu Anchal, Fnu Danial, Kashish Turesh, Amjad Ali, Faizan Qureshi, Mahir Tesfaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a growing global health challenge, necessitating effective therapies that improve glycemic control and promote weight loss. Ecnoglutide, a novel cAMP-biased GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) receptor agonist, has emerged as a promising therapeutic option.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was conducted following PRISMA and AMSTAR-2 guidelines. Electronic databases were searched up to February 2026. Eligible studies included adult patients with T2DM comparing ecnoglutide with placebo or active comparators. Outcomes included glycemic parameters, anthropometric measures, metabolic indices, and safety. Data were pooled using a random-effects model, and certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE.ResultsThree RCTs comprising 979 participants were included. Ecnoglutide significantly reduced HbA1c (MD -0.57%, 95% CI -0.86 to -0.29) and fasting plasma glucose (MD -1.11 mmol/L, 95% CI -1.58 to -0.65), with a higher proportion achieving target HbA1c (RR 1.57, 95% CI 1.37–1.80). Significant reductions in body weight (MD -1.83 kg) and waist circumference (MD -1.53 cm) were also observed. Improvements in insulin resistance and lipid parameters were noted. Exploratory subgroup analyses did not establish a clear dose response relationship, while short-term trials showed larger glycemic reductions compared with longer follow up. Adverse events were more frequent but not statistically significant, with no increase in serious adverse events.ConclusionEcnoglutide demonstrates significant efficacy in improving glycemic control and weight-related outcomes in T2DM, with a safety profile comparable to existing GLP-1 receptor agonists. However, larger and longer-term studies are needed to confirm its cardiovascular benefits and long-term safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13098-026-02250-9.

Introduction

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) has become a significant public health concern, affecting millions of people globally, and its prevalence is increasing dramatically worldwide. Importantly, T2DM accounts for over 96% of all diabetes cases [1]. Notably, 537 million people worldwide were living with diabetes in 2021 (1 in 10 adults), representing a global age-standardized prevalence of 6.1%.This reflects a substantial increase of 90.5% rom 3.2% n 1990, which could rise to 783 million by 2045. The prevalence is projected to escalate to 9.8%,potentially affecting an estimated 13.1 billion individuals. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is by far the most common form of diabetes, accounting for approximately 90% f cases globally [2].

T2DM is a complex metabolic disorder primarily characterized by insulin resistance (IR) and beta-cell dysfunction, which results in chronic hyperglycemia and disrupts normal glucose homeostasis. The pathophysiology of T2DM involves a combination of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors that collectively contribute to the development and progression of the disease. Insulin resistance, which mainly affects the skeletal muscle, liver, intestines, and adipose tissues, is a core feature of T2DM [3].

Multiple generations of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have been developed, beginning with exenatide, which has a short half-life (~ 2–3 h) requiring twice-daily dosing. Longer-acting agents such as liraglutide (~ 13 h) allow once-daily use, whereas newer agents such as Semaglutide and Dulaglutide have half-lives exceeding 100 h, enabling once-weekly administration [4, 5]. These long-acting GLP-1 RAs improve glycemic control and reduce the treatment burden. For instance, semaglutide has demonstrated significant HbA1c reduction (~ 1.6%) and weight loss (~ 6.1 kg) in clinical trials [6].

Although the currently available GLP-1 RAs have demonstrated impressive treatment outcomes, further improvements in this class of drugs are possible. The current limitations of GLP-1 peptide analogs include the tolerability of gastrointestinal side effects, challenges in managing injections or oral dosing around meals, and the costs and scalability of drug manufacturing. Furthermore, there are still patient populations whose glucose levels and body weight cannot be adequately controlled by current therapies [6].

Ecnoglutide (XW003) is a modified GLP-1 peptide containing an alanine-to-valine substitution at position 8 and an 18-C fatty acid conjugation at the lysine 30 side chain [7]. The ecnoglutide valine substitution also biases GLP-1 receptor signaling towards cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) induction over β-arrestin recruitment and receptor internalization. Therefore, ecnoglutide differs from the approved single GLP-1 peptide analogs, which are full agonists of both pathways. Dual incretin receptor agonists (such as GLP-1/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide [GIP] analog tirzepatide) also show cAMP bias [7].

In view of these promising findings, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of ecnoglutide in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus through comparisons with both placebo and active treatment options. The primary focus of the analysis was on key clinical endpoints, including glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and body weight. In addition, secondary outcomes such as fasting plasma glucose, measures of insulin resistance, lipid parameters, and safety profiles including adverse events are examined to provide an integrated assessment of its therapeutic role in current diabetes management.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with the AMSTAR 2 tool and PRISMA guidelines, adhering to established standards for the rigorous conduct and transparent reporting of research syntheses [8, 9]. These guidelines ensured clear documentation of our methods and strengthened the reliability and reproducibility of our findings, ultimately supporting the overall credibility of our conclusions. As per the TITAN Guidelines 2025 governing transparent AI implementation in scholarly publications, AI assistance was confined exclusively to linguistic enhancement during manuscript development, with no involvement in the study design, data collection, data analysis, or results interpretation [10].

Literature search and study identification

A systematic and exhaustive search of electronic databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, ScienceDirect, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Google Scholar, was performed until February 2026. The search strategy incorporated a combination of keywords and relevant terms such as “Ecnoglutide,” “GLP-1 receptor agonist,” “type 2 diabetes,” and “randomized controlled trial.” To ensure completeness, the reference lists of pertinent reviews and eligible studies were manually screened for additional records (see Supplementary Table 1 for the full strategy).

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote X7.5 for the deduplication process. Screening was conducted in two phases. Initially, two independent reviewers evaluated the titles and abstracts for relevance. This was followed by a full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Any discrepancies during the selection process were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer.

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: [1] randomized controlled trial design; [2] adult participants diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus; [3] comparison of ecnoglutide with placebo or another active intervention; and [4] reporting of at least one quantitative outcome related to glycemic or metabolic parameters, including HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, insulin resistance, body weight, or adverse events.

Studies were excluded if they were observational, non-randomized, preclinical, or single-arm in design, or if they lacked a comparator group with measurable outcomes. Narrative reviews, editorials, and conference abstracts were excluded.

Data collection and outcome measures

Relevant data were systematically extracted using a pre-designed spreadsheet. The extracted variables included study identifiers (author, year, trial registration), sample size, baseline participant characteristics (age, body mass index, HbA1c levels, duration of diabetes), intervention details (dosage, duration), comparator information, and reported outcomes.

The primary efficacy outcomes included indicators of glycemic control and insulin function, including glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-hour postprandial glucose, seven-point self-monitored blood glucose (SMBG), fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR. Secondary outcomes included anthropometric parameters such as body weight and waist circumference, along with lipid profile measurements (LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides). Safety outcomes were also evaluated, including total adverse events, serious adverse events, and adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation [11].

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers to minimize errors, and disagreements were resolved through consensus.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan version 5.4). Continuous variables were summarized using mean differences (MDs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), whereas dichotomous outcomes were reported as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. A random-effects model was employed for all pooled analyses to account for the expected clinical and methodological variability across studies.

In studies featuring multiple intervention arms compared with a single control group, adjustments were made to prevent duplication of control data. Specifically, the control group sample size was evenly distributed across comparisons, while the original means and standard deviations were preserved in accordance with the Cochrane methodological guidance.

Statistical heterogeneity among studies was quantified using the I² statistic, with values exceeding 50% interpreted as substantial heterogeneity [12]. To evaluate the stability and reliability of the pooled estimates, sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach, in which individual studies were sequentially removed to assess their influence on the overall results [13].

Both placebo-controlled and active-comparator trials were incorporated into the primary analysis to ensure a broad evaluation of the therapeutic effects of ecnoglutide. The consistency of treatment outcomes across different comparator types was examined, and no meaningful variations were observed, supporting the robustness of the combined estimate. Similarly, trials from both phase 2 (dose-ranging) and phase 3 (confirmatory) stages were included, with comparable effect sizes observed across phases, further strengthening their validity.

Predefined subgroup analyses were performed to explore the potential sources of heterogeneity. These analyses were stratified according to ecnoglutide dosage (0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.2 mg) and duration of follow-up, categorized as short-term (≤ 6 months) and long-term (≥ 6 months). Statistical significance for all analyses was defined as a two-sided p-value of < 0.05.

Risk of bias evaluation

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [14]. Each study was evaluated across key domains, including the randomization process, allocation concealment, blinding procedures, completeness of outcome data, and risk of selective reporting. Independent assessments were conducted by two reviewers, and any inconsistencies were addressed through discussion.

Assessment of evidence certainty

The overall certainty of evidence for the primary outcomes was evaluated using the GRADE framework. This approach considers multiple factors, including risk of bias, heterogeneity, indirectness of evidence, imprecision of estimates, and potential publication bias. Based on these criteria, the strength of evidence was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low [15].

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic search yielded a total of 172 records from five databases: Google Scholar (n = 130), ScienceDirect (n = 20), PubMed (n = 10), Cochrane Library (n = 9), and ClinicalTrials.gov (n = 3). Following the removal of 62 duplicate entries, 110 unique studies remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 90 were excluded for failing to meet the predefined inclusion criteria. Subsequently, 20 articles underwent full-text evaluation, leading to the exclusion of 17 studies due to adherence to eligibility or PICO criteria. Ultimately, 3 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included in the meta-analysis [16–18]. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

The included trials were published between 2024 and 2026 and involved a total of 979 participants, with 665 assigned to the ecnoglutide group and 314 to placebo or active comparator groups. Among these, two were phase 3 trials and one was a phase 2 trial. Specifically, Dalong Zhu (2024) was a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial; Yang He (2025) was an open-label, active-controlled phase 3 trial (EECOH-2); and Dalong Zhu (2026) was a double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial (EECOH-1).

The mean age of participants ranged from 49 to 54 years across treatment arms, with males accounting for 58% and females 42% of the study population. Baseline HbA1c values ranged from 8.39% to 8.67%, while mean BMI ranged between 25.8 and 27.23 kg/m². Ecnoglutide was administered subcutaneously at doses ranging from 0.4 mg to 1.2 mg. The primary endpoints across studies included changes in HbA1c from baseline and percentage change in bodyweight. Further details of study characteristics are provided in Tables 1 and 2.

Baseline Characteristics

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool. Overall, 2 studies were rated as low risk of bias, while 1 study (Yang He 2025) was rated as high risk due to its open-label design. All studies demonstrated adequate randomization using computer-generated sequences or interactive web response systems with appropriate stratification by baseline HbA1c or BMI. Allocation concealment was ensured through centralized randomization systems in all trials. The main methodological concern was performance bias in Yang He 2025, where blinding of participants and personnel was not possible due to differences in injection devices. No concerns were identified regarding incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, or other sources of bias across all included studies. The risk of bias is presented in Fig. 2A and B and detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Primary outcomes

Glycemic control outcomes

Across the included studies, Ecnoglutide demonstrated significant improvements in glycemic parameters. HbA1c (%) was significantly reduced (MD = −0.57, 95% CI −0.86 to −0.29, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3), with a comparable reduction observed when expressed in mmol/mol (MD = −8.22, 95% CI −13.97 to −2.47, p < 0.01) (Fig. 4). Additionally, a greater proportion of participants in the Ecnoglutide group achieved target HbA1c levels compared to controls (RR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.37 to 1.80, p < 0.01) (Fig. 5).

Fasting plasma glucose levels were significantly decreased (MD = −1.11, 95% CI −1.58 to −0.65, p < 0.01) (Fig. 6), alongside reductions in 2-hour postprandial glucose (MD = −1.96, 95% CI −3.47 to −0.45, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.1). Similarly, seven-point SMBG values showed a significant decline (MD = −0.87, 95% CI −1.14 to −0.59, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.2).

Insulin sensitivity

Ecnoglutide was associated with improvements in insulin-related indices. Fasting insulin levels were reduced (MD = −0.43, 95% CI −1.47 to 0.60, p = 0.41) (Supplementary Fig. 1.3), and HOMA-IR also showed a significant decrease (MD = −0.48, 95% CI −0.83 to −0.14, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.4).

Secondary outcomes

Anthropometric outcomes

Treatment with Ecnoglutide resulted in significant reductions in bodyweight (MD = −1.83, 95% CI −2.13 to −1.53, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.5) and percentage bodyweight change (MD = −2.49, 95% CI −2.90 to −2.07, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.6). Waist circumference also decreased significantly (MD = −1.53, 95% CI −1.88 to −1.18, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.7). Moreover, a higher proportion of participants achieved predefined weight-loss targets in the Ecnoglutide group (RR = 2.10, 95% CI 1.77 to 2.49, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.8).

Lipid profile

Ecnoglutide was associated with changes in lipid parameters. Percentage reductions from baseline were observed for Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C) (MD = −0.08, 95% CI −0.15 to −0.00, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.9) and triglycerides (MD = −0.27, 95% CI −0.40 to −0.14, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.10). Similarly, High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C) increased more in the Ecnoglutide group (MD = 0.02, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.04, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1.11).

Safety outcomes

Adverse events were more frequently reported in the Ecnoglutide group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (RR = 1.07, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.16, p = 0.15) (Supplementary Fig. 1.12). No significant differences were observed in serious adverse events (RR = 1.22, 95% CI 0.74 to 2.02, p = 0.81) (Supplementary Fig. 1.13) or in adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation (RR = 1.17, 95% CI 0.51 to 2.69, p = 0.71) (Supplementary Fig. 1.14).

Sensitivity analyses and heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was evaluated for all outcomes, with sensitivity analyses performed for those demonstrating substantial variability (I² > 50%). Exclusion of individual influential studies significantly reduced heterogeneity across several outcomes. Specifically, removal of Dalong Zhu 2026 reduced heterogeneity for HbA1c and 2-hour postprandial glucose, while exclusion of Yang He 2025 decreased heterogeneity for fasting plasma glucose. Despite initial variability, pooled estimates remained stable, and both the direction and statistical significance of effects were preserved (Supplementary Figs. 2.1–2.4).

Subgroup analyses by dosage

Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to examine potential dose-response relationships across outcomes, with studies stratified by Ecnoglutide dose (0.4 mg, 0.6 mg, 0.8 mg, and 1.2 mg). While numerical differences were observed across dose levels for several outcomes, the test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant for any outcome, including HbA1c % (Chi² = 0.73, p = 0.87), fasting plasma glucose (Chi² = 3.34, p = 0.34), 2-hour postprandial glucose (Chi² = 0.13, p = 0.72), fasting insulin (Chi² = 0.40, p = 0.53), HOMA-IR (Chi² = 0.01, p = 0.94), bodyweight (Chi² = 2.18, p = 0.54), percentage bodyweight change (Chi² = 1.04, p = 0.31), waist circumference (Chi² = 2.48, p = 0.12), LDL-cholesterol (Chi² = 0.04, p = 0.84), HDL-cholesterol (Chi² = 0.25, p = 0.61), and triglycerides (Chi² = 0.11, p = 0.74). Accordingly, no statistically supported dose-response relationship could be established for any outcome. Notably, the 0.4 mg and 0.8 mg subgroups were each represented by a single study arm, limiting the reliability of comparisons at these dose levels (Supplementary Figs. 3.1–3.12).

Subgroup analysis by follow-up duration

Outcomes were stratified by follow-up duration into short-term (≤ 6 months) and long-term (≥ 6 months) subgroups. Contrary to an expected duration-dependent pattern, short-term follow-up was associated with numerically larger reductions for most glycemic outcomes. For HbA1c (%), the short-term subgroup showed a mean reduction of −1.33 (95% CI −1.59 to −1.07) compared with − 0.25 (95% CI −0.33 to −0.16) in the long-term subgroup, with a highly significant subgroup difference (Chi² = 59.44, p < 0.0001, I² = 98.3%). A similar pattern was observed for HbA1c (mmol/mol), where the short-term subgroup showed a larger reduction (MD = −14.44, 95% CI −19.45 to −9.44) compared with the long-term subgroup (MD = −2.59, 95% CI −3.95 to −1.24), with a significant subgroup difference (Chi² = 20.05, p < 0.0001). Fasting plasma glucose also showed a larger short-term reduction (MD = −1.94, 95% CI −2.33 to −1.56) compared with long-term (MD = −0.46, 95% CI −0.65 to −0.28), with a significant subgroup difference (Chi² = 46.49, p < 0.0001). Similarly, 2-hour postprandial glucose demonstrated a greater reduction in the short-term subgroup (MD = −4.88, 95% CI −6.32 to −3.44) than in the long-term subgroup (MD = −0.59, 95% CI −1.05 to −0.12), with a significant subgroup difference (Chi² = 30.84, p < 0.0001).

For fasting insulin and HOMA-IR, subgroup differences by follow-up duration were not statistically significant (Chi² = 1.21, p = 0.27 and Chi² = 0.22, p = 0.64, respectively), and neither short-term nor long-term subgroups showed consistent statistically significant effects for fasting insulin.

Anthropometric outcomes including bodyweight, percentage bodyweight change, and waist circumference showed comparable reductions across both follow-up durations, with no statistically significant subgroup differences (p = 0.84, p = 0.81, and p = 0.45, respectively), indicating that the magnitude of effect on anthropometric measures was relatively stable regardless of treatment duration.

Lipid parameters including LDL-cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, and triglycerides also showed minimal variation between short-term and long-term subgroups, with non-significant subgroup differences across all three outcomes, consistent with the main analysis findings (Supplementary Figs. 4.1–4.12).

GRADE for key outcomes

The certainty of evidence for most glycemic outcomes (HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose) was rated as moderate, primarily due to considerable heterogeneity among studies (I² ranging from 84% to 95%). Bodyweight-related outcomes demonstrated high certainty, reflecting consistent effect sizes, low heterogeneity, and precise estimates. Overall, these findings suggest that the intervention significantly improves both glycemic control and anthropometric measures, although heterogeneity in glycemic outcomes warrants cautious interpretation (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials involving 979 participants demonstrates that ecnoglutide provides significant improvements in glycemic control, body weight, and metabolic parameters in type 2 diabetes mellitus. These benefits were consistent across phase 2 and phase 3 studies, as well as across placebo and active comparators, supporting the robustness of the findings. Dose-dependent effects were observed for key outcomes, with greater efficacy at higher doses, while longer treatment duration further enhanced clinical benefits. Despite some heterogeneity in glycemic outcomes, sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of the results, highlighting the potential of ecnoglutide as an effective therapeutic option.

The mechanism by which ecnoglutide controls diabetes is fundamentally the same as other GLP-1 receptor agonists: it stimulates glucose-dependent insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells, suppresses inappropriate glucagon release, slows gastric emptying, and reduces appetite but the cAMP-biased signaling may enhance the potency or durability of these effects [7].

Relative to control groups, a substantially greater proportion of individuals receiving ecnoglutide achieved target glycemic thresholds (HbA1c < 7%), underscoring its efficacy in both treatment-naïve patients and those on background metformin therapy [19]. This effect may be attributed to its sustained pharmacodynamic activity and high receptor binding affinity. A head-to-head preclinical study has shown that at the same dose level, ecnoglutide significantly reduced blood glucose and HbA1c more than semaglutide, which has been shown to be more effacious in reducing HbA1c than dulaglutide [17].

The dose-dependent weight loss observed in our analysis (MD = −1.83 kg overall, with higher efficacy at 1.2 mg to 2.4 mg doses) mirrors the trajectory seen with semaglutide, though the magnitude in our pooled result is slightly lower than the top-line results of the SLIMMER trial (which reached 13.2%) [20]. This discrepancy is likely due to the inclusion of shorter-term studies (20–24 weeks) in our analysis. Interestingly, our subgroup analysis confirmed that weight loss is duration-dependent, suggesting that ecnoglutide requires prolonged administration (48 + weeks) to reach its maximum therapeutic plateau, similar to tirzepatide [21].

One of the most encouraging findings of this meta-analysis is the safety profile. While ecnoglutide was associated with higher rates of adverse events (RR = 1.07), the lack of statistical significance in serious adverse events (SAEs) or treatment discontinuation (RR = 1.17) distinguishes it from some earlier GLP-1RAs that faced high dropout rates due to gastrointestinal distress. The “biased signaling” mechanism may indeed contribute to a more favorable tolerability profile by reducing the rapid receptor internalization that often triggers acute nausea [7]. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the evidence is derived from a limited number of trials with relatively short follow-up durations. Consequently, rare but clinically important adverse events cannot be reliably assessed. Further long-term and post-marketing studies are warranted to more comprehensively establish the safety profile of Ecnoglutide.

Exploratory dose-stratified analyses did not reveal a statistically significant dose–response relationship across glycemic, anthropometric, or lipid outcomes. While numerical differences were observed, the limited representation of certain dose levels (0.4 mg and 0.8 mg each from a single study arm) restricts the reliability of these comparisons. Larger, well-powered trials across multiple dose tiers are needed to clarify whether efficacy varies meaningfully by dose. In contrast, subgrouping by follow-up duration showed consistently greater reductions in glycemic outcomes in short-term trials (≤ 6 months) compared with long-term trials (≥ 6 months). Significant subgroup differences were observed for HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, and postprandial glucose. These findings may reflect stronger early treatment effects, attenuation over time due to physiological adaptation, or declining adherence during longer follow-up. They also highlight potential differences in trial design and reporting between shorter and longer studies. Overall, while ecnoglutide demonstrates robust short-term efficacy, the durability of these effects remains uncertain, and future confirmatory trials with extended follow-up are required to establish long-term effectiveness and optimal dosing.

Ecnoglutide distinguishes itself from established GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide and liraglutide through its unique cAMP-biased signaling, which is designed to provide more potent and sustained receptor activation [22]. Clinical data suggests that ecnoglutide may offer superior efficacy; for instance, it has demonstrated significantly greater HbA1c reductions compared to dulaglutide and more robust weight loss than maximum-dose liraglutide [7]. While it shares the typical gastrointestinal side-effect profile of the class, its composition of entirely natural amino acids is intended to simplify manufacturing and potentially reduce the risk of long-term immunogenicity.

An important consideration in interpreting these findings is the substantial variability in baseline HbA1c levels across the included trials, with some studies enrolling participants with near-normal glycemic values. Such heterogeneity in baseline metabolic status may influence treatment responsiveness, as individuals with lower baseline HbA1c have limited potential for further glycemic reduction. This may partially attenuate the pooled effect size and contribute to between-study variability. In clinical practice, the magnitude of benefit with ecnoglutide is likely to be more pronounced in patients with suboptimal glycemic control, and treatment decisions should be individualized based on baseline glycemic status, comorbid conditions, and therapeutic targets.

The findings of this meta-analysis highlight ecnoglutide as a promising next-generation incretin-based therapy. Its robust glycemic efficacy and notable impact on weight reduction may support the achievement of individualized metabolic targets, particularly in patients with obesity or insulin resistance. However, the lack of cardiovascular outcome data within the included studies limits the ability to determine its potential cardioprotective effects. Further well-designed trials are needed to evaluate the cardiovascular and renal benefits of ecnoglutide, as well as its real-world effectiveness, including long-term adherence and treatment persistence in routine clinical practice.

Limitations

This meta-analysis should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the relatively small number of randomized controlled trials and limited sample size reduce the statistical power to detect rare adverse events and may affect the robustness of subgroup analyses. Additionally, the inclusion criteria restricted studies to published trials, which introduces a potential risk of publication bias, as unpublished or negative studies may not have been captured, potentially overestimating the treatment effect.

Second, variability in trial design, including differences in patient demographics, baseline characteristics, dosing regimens, and duration of follow-up, contributed to substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity. Although subgroup analyses based on treatment duration partially explained this variability, the overall high heterogeneity (I² = 87%) limits the generalizability of pooled estimates.

Third, the inclusion of open-label trials introduces potential performance and detection bias, although sensitivity analyses demonstrated minimal impact on the overall effect size. Furthermore, differences in background therapies and adherence patterns across studies may have confounded the observed outcomes.

The inclusion of both placebo-controlled and active-comparator trials may introduce clinical heterogeneity, as differences in comparator efficacy can influence the magnitude of pooled treatment effects. Although effect direction was consistent across studies, this approach may limit the precision and interpretability of combined estimates. The open-label design of the Yang He. (2025) trial may have introduced performance and detection bias, particularly affecting subjective outcomes such as adverse events and weight-related behaviors. This potential bias may influence the pooled estimates and should be interpreted with caution.

Our study combined dose-ranging phase 2 trials with confirmatory phase 3 trials. While this approach increased the available sample size and statistical power, it may have introduced heterogeneity and potentially inflated effect sizes due to differences in trial design, objectives, and patient populations. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, and future analyses restricted to phase 3 confirmatory trials may provide more precise effect size estimates.

In particular, the higher incidence of gastrointestinal adverse events, consistent with other GLP-1 receptor agonists, may influence treatment adherence in real-world settings. Finally, the reliance on aggregated study-level data rather than individual patient data limits the ability to perform more precise adjustments for confounding variables and to explore patient-level effect modifiers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis provides evidence that ecnoglutide is an effective GLP-1 receptor agonist for improving glycemic control, reducing body weight, and enhancing metabolic parameters in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Exploratory subgroup analyses did not establish a clear dose–response relationship, while short-term trials showed larger glycemic reductions compared with longer follow-up. While the overall safety profile appears consistent with the GLP-1RA class, the current evidence base remains limited by short follow-up durations and a small number of trials. Therefore, although ecnoglutide shows promise as a next-generation incretin-based therapy, further large-scale, long-term studies are required to confirm its cardiovascular and renal benefits, as well as its sustained safety and real-world effectiveness.
