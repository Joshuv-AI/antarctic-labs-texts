Examining the Relationship Between Early Feeding Difficulties and Language Development: A Longitudinal Study
Authors: Simone Poulin, David H. McFarland, Stefano Rezzonico, Natacha Trudeau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIncreasing evidence suggests that interactive feeding is an important variable in language development, with early disruptions in feeding potentially having long‐term consequences. This longitudinal experiment characterized early patterns of feeding difficulties and language development in full‐term, typically‐developing infants across the developmental milestones of 8, 12, 18, 24, and 54 months. Parent‐directed assessments were used to assess feeding difficulties and language abilities between 8 and 24 months and a clinical language assessment was administered at 54 months. Results revealed that certain feeding difficulties, such as poor saliva control at 18 months, correlate negatively with expressive vocabulary size and may signal co‐occurring and later occurring less developmentally advanced language skills at 18 and 24 months. Others, such as food selectivity, are characteristic of children with less developmentally advanced language skills at 24 months. Together, these findings suggest that feeding difficulties may reduce opportunities for language stimulation and/or reflect underlying oral‐motor developmental vulnerabilities that also affect language development.

Introduction

One of the key goals in research on language/communicative disorders is to understand the foundational skills that scaffold speech and language development. Our own research has focused on interactional mother–infant physiological synchrony (e.g., McFarland et al. 2020) and the potential role of feeding as providing key moments of communicative interaction and language stimulation (Malas et al. 2015, 2017; McFarland, Poulin, et al. 2020). There are several reasons to consider the potential role of feeding as a key factor in building language skills. Caregiver–infant feeding is a crucial moment of social interaction (Feldman 2015; Kaye 1977) and language stimulation (Aukrust and Snow 1998; Leech 2021; Leech et al. 2018; Snow and Beals 2006). Verbal interactions during solid feeding provide particularly rich language input (Weizman and Snow 2001; Zimmerman et al. 2019), even compared to other interactive contexts such as toy play, often considered the gold standard language stimulation context. Maternal speech during mealtimes has greater lexical diversity (Zimmerman et al. 2019) and contains more rare words (Weizman and Snow 2001) than play. The higher frequency of rare words during mother–child mealtime interactions is correlated with children's later higher receptive vocabulary skills (Weizman and Snow 2001).

Despite the potential of feeding as a setting for language enrichment, when feeding difficulties are present, this optimal context for language stimulation may be compromised, as disrupted mealtimes adversely impact caregiver–infant communicative interactions and consequently, early language development. For example, parent–child mealtime communicative exchanges tend to focus on corrective comments on feeding behaviors when feeding difficulties are present (Harding et al. 2013). This reduces children's exposure to the normally rich, diverse parental language input during mealtimes related to food characteristics and enjoyment in infants (Harding et al. 2013; Thompson et al. 2024) or abstract topics beyond the here‐and‐now in older children (Leech 2021; Leech et al. 2018; Snow and Beals 2006). Mother–child conflicts also increase in the presence of feeding difficulties, particularly those related to food selectivity (Lucarelli et al. 2003). Such conflicts may contribute to long‐lasting negative interactive cycles, in which a child's food selectivity and parents’ nonresponsive and coercive behaviors reinforce one another (Jansen et al. 2017; Mallan et al. 2018). Given that low parental responsiveness in parent–child interactions has been shown to be associated with lower child language outcomes (Levickis et al. 2023; Tamis‐Lemonda et al. 2001), food selectivity may interfere with typical language development by hindering important language‐learning opportunities tied to contingent, meaningful, and developmentally‐appropriate parental responses to children's communicative acts and behaviors during mealtimes.

It is not surprising then, that early disruptions in feeding may negatively impact language development (Adams‐Chapman et al. 2013, 2015; Crapnell et al. 2015; Lainwala et al. 2020; Motion et al. 2001; Putnick et al. 2022; Somaraki et al. 2024; Wolthuis‐Stigter et al. 2017; Yardımcı‑Lokmanoğlu et al. 2024; Zimmerman and Rosner 2018). Prior history of feeding difficulties in infancy is associated with a range of later speech and language concerns in both preterm and full‐term children, including caregiver‐perceived speech‐language delays before Age 3 (Zimmerman and Rosner 2018) and limited expressive vocabulary (fewer than three words) as early as 18 months (Motion et al. 2001). These findings suggest that infants who experience early feeding difficulties are more likely to show less developmentally advanced language abilities over time. Population‐based cohort data reinforce this idea. In large cohorts of preterm and full‐term children, a greater number of feeding difficulties at 18, 24, and 36 months increased the odds of communication delays (likely including language delays), with the strength of the associations increasing with child age (Putnick et al. 2022), and late introduction of solids (after 10 months) predicted smaller vocabularies at 24 months (Somaraki et al. 2024). Evidence from preterm infants further underscores the prognostic value of early feeding behavior. Abnormal sucking patterns have been shown to predict poorer performance on standardized language tests through the toddler and preschool years (Wolthuis‐Stigter et al. 2017; Yardımcı‑Lokmanoğlu et al. 2024), including lower comprehension at Age 5 (Wolthuis‐Stigter et al. 2017). These associations extend to clinically significant outcomes, with early feeding difficulties increasing the likelihood of language delays by 18–26 months (Adams‐Chapman et al. 2013; Lainwala et al. 2020) and expressive and receptive vocabulary delays by 30 months (Adams‐Chapman et al. 2015). Retrospective clinical studies of children later diagnosed with developmental language disorders without co‐occurring neurodevelopmental disorders, motor deficits, or history of prematurity provide converging support. In these samples, roughly half of children (48%–53%) had a documented history of early feeding difficulties, most frequently characterized by food selectivity issues and challenges in food transition (Malas et al. 2015, 2017). Compared to the 20% general population estimates of Lindberg et al. (1991), children with developmental language disorders are 3.77 times more likely to have experienced early feeding difficulties (Malas et al. 2017), reinforcing the idea that such difficulties may have long‐term consequences for language development and may contribute to later language disorders. Additional evidence from neurodevelopmental conditions characterized by high rates of motor and communication difficulties, such as cerebral palsy, autism, and developmental language disorders, highlights a similar pattern of co‐occurrence. In cerebral palsy, lower expressive language abilities are predictive of more pronounced feeding dysfunction (Malandraki et al. 2022), while in autism the joint occurrence of feeding difficulties and speech‐language disorders exceed that expected from the independent prevalence of each (Neumeyer et al. 2019). Taken together, these converging findings across population‐based cohorts, preterm samples, clinical developmental language disorder groups, and neurodevelopmental conditions suggest that early feeding difficulties represent subtle developmental disruptions that may shape divergent language trajectories.

Feeding difficulties may not only disrupt caregiver–infant interactions (Byrne et al. 2017; Jansen et al. 2017; Lindberg et al. 1996; Mallan et al. 2018), reducing opportunities for rich and responsive verbal input and joint engagement that support early language learning (Anderson et al. 2021; Fu and Zheng 2025; Madigan et al. 2019), they may also reflect underlying oral‐motor vulnerabilities that may constrain speech sound development (Nip et al. 2009), vocabulary (Kehoe et al. 2018), and grammar. Less mature oral movements such as slower or less coordinated movements of the tongue, the lip, or the mandible may impede accurate articulatory positioning (Green et al. 2002) and the acquisition of new speech sounds (Nip et al. 2009). Given that children tend to produce words containing sounds already in their phonetic repertoire (Schwartz and Leonard 1982; Stoel‐Gammon 1985), this may in turn restrict children's ability to learn new words (Kehoe et al. 2018). Early oral‐motor disruptions that affect vocabulary growth may in turn influence grammatical development, as vocabulary provides the foundational elements for grammar (Bates et al. 1988; McGregor et al. 2005). Parents also tend to provide less lexically diverse and syntactically complex contingent input in response to less developmentally advanced infant oral productions (Elmlinger et al. 2025). These oral‐motor‐based constraints may further negatively impact children's language development by disrupting the quality of linguistic input they receive.

Consistent with the premise that oral‐motor vulnerabilities may constrain language development, research indicates clear associations between early oral‐motor skill and language outcomes. Lower abilities to imitate oral movements at 21 months have been found to correlate cross‐sectionally to lower expressive vocabulary and grammatical abilities at 21 months (Alcock and Krawczyk 2010) and longitudinally to lower performance on an omnibus test of expressive and receptive language abilities at 36 months (Alcock and Connor 2021). Oral‐motor vulnerabilities, along with broader gross and fine motor deficits (Brumbach and Goffman 2014; Hill 2001; Hsu and Bishop 2014; Sack et al. 2022; Sanjeevan and Mainela‐Arnold 2019; Ullman et al. 2020; Vuolo and Goffman 2018; Vuolo et al. 2017), have also been documented in older children with developmental language disorders, suggesting that the influence of oral‐motor vulnerabilities on language development may extend beyond infancy and toddlerhood. Compared to typically‐developing peers, children with developmental language disorders frequently demonstrate poorer control and coordination of the lips and tongue (Noterdaeme et al. 2002), as well as challenges with speech production and organization (Benham et al. 2018; Goffman 1999). Taken together, these findings from toddlers and older children with developmental language disorders suggest that feeding‐related oral‐motor vulnerabilities may play a role in shaping language trajectory both directly, by constraining speech sound production, vocabulary growth, and grammatical development, and indirectly, by reducing the quality of caregiver linguistic input, potentially contributing to the developmental relationship between feeding difficulties and language.

Within this theoretical/experimental context, the present longitudinal study was designed to prospectively study developmental interactions between feeding difficulties and language development in typically‐developing children, with the goal of determining how age, differences in language development, and feeding difficulties interact over time. Such information will add to our comprehension of the developmental expression of the relationship between these seemingly diverse behaviors and the specific roles of feeding difficulties in shaping divergent language trajectories. Given the focus on a nonclinical population, feeding difficulties were operationally defined as difficulties “that are usually clinically subthreshold, often signaled by parents, and typically without serious medical or nutritional consequences” (McFarland, Poulin, et al. 2020, 22). This is in contrast to pediatric feeding disorders, which are age‐inappropriate feeding abilities and oral intake that results in significant medical, nutritional, and/or psychosocial consequences (Goday et al. 2019).

Our specific experimental objectives were twofold. First, to characterize feeding difficulties in relation to language development by examining their occurrence across developmental age and language status and by assessing their cross‐sectional and longitudinal correlations with continuous indices of language development. Second, to determine whether feeding difficulties increased the likelihood that children's concurrent and later language skills would meet established criteria for classification as less developmentally advanced (Desmarais et al. 2008; Henrichs et al. 2011; Reilly et al. 2009; Thordardottir et al. 2010, 2011; Trudeau et al. 2008). Considering these objectives and research suggesting that food selectivity disrupts caregiver–infant mealtime interactions (Byrne et al. 2017; Jansen et al. 2017; Lindberg et al. 1996; Lucarelli et al. 2003; Mallan et al. 2018) and that underlying oral‐motor vulnerabilities can constrain speech sound and language development (Kehoe et al. 2018; Nip et al. 2009) and elicit simplified caregiver language input (Elmlinger et al. 2025), two experimental hypotheses were advanced. The first was that both food selectivity issues and feeding difficulties reflecting poor oral motor control, such as chewing difficulties or poor saliva management, would be among those most frequent in children with less developmentally advanced language skills and would correlate cross‐sectionally and longitudinally with continuous indices of language development. The second was that these feeding difficulties would also increase the likelihood that children's concurrent and later language skills would be less developmentally advanced.

Methods

Participants

Participants were 8‐month‐old, typically‐developing infants from monolingual French‐speaking homes. Eight months was selected because it is the youngest age at which Quebec French language abilities can be assessed using a standardized parent‐report instrument (Trudeau et al. 2008). Restricting the sample to monolingual French‐speaking homes minimized the influence of language differences and multilingualism (Hoff et al. 2012; Thordardottir and Brandeker 2013). The questionnaire used for identifying feeding difficulties was developed and content validated in Quebec French only (McFarland, Poulin, et al. 2020).

One hundred and forty infants between the ages of 7 months 3 weeks and 8 months 4 weeks were recruited through the birth lists of the Sainte‐Justine University Hospital Center, through 15 Montreal parent Facebook groups, a webpage of the University of Montreal, and through social and professional networks. Infants were included if (1) they were from monolingual French‐speaking families (i.e., exposed to French at least 80% of the time) (Trudeau et al. 2008; Trudeau and Sutton 2011), (2) born at term (37 weeks of gestation or later), and (3) without known or suspected craniofacial, visual, cognitive, or auditive impairments, neurodevelopmental delays or disorders, genetic disorders, or neurological lesions based on parent report and the French version of the Ages and Stages Questionnaire‐Third Edition (Council on Children With Disabilities 2006; Squire and Bricker 2016; Wilson et al. 2012). All recruitment and experimental procedures were approved by the ethics committee of Sainte‐Justine University Hospital Center (file number: 2016–990), and written consent was obtained from one of the primary caregivers.

Three (2%) of the participants were later diagnosed with autism spectrum disorders as reported by parents at the 54‐month measurement period. This led to the exclusion of their data from all analyses. Feeding and/or language measurements were not performed for 11 (8%) children at 12 months, 13 (9%) children at 18 months, 26 (19%) children at 24 months, and 56 (41%) children at 54 months due to either parental withdrawal from the study (n = 1 at 12 months, n = 2 at 18 and 24 months, and n = 5 at 54 months) or nonresponsiveness to contact attempts (n = 10 at 12 months, n = 11 at 18 months, n = 24 at 24 months, and n = 51 at 54 months). As a result, the study sample included 137 infants at 8 months (98% retention rate), 126 children at 12 months (90% retention rate), 124 children at 18 months (89% retention rate), 111 children (79% retention rate) at 24 months, and 81 children at 54 months (58% retention rate).

Procedures

The Feeding Difficulties Questionnaire (McFarland, Poulin, et al. 2020) and the Quebec version of MacArthur–Bates Communicative Development Inventories (MBCDI) (Trudeau et al. 2008) were used to quantify feeding difficulties and language abilities, respectively, at 8, 12, 18, and 24 months (see Figure 1). The MBCDI was also used to determine children's language status at 12, 18, and 24 months, classifying them as having less developmentally advanced language skills or not according to established normative criteria (Desmarais et al. 2008; Henrichs et al. 2011; Reilly et al. 2009; Trudeau et al. 2008). At 54 months and because the MBCDI is suitable only for assessing language in children up to 30 months, the standardized sentence imitation task titled Le grand déménagement (Royle and Thordardottir 2003), was used to quantify language abilities and determine language status. The test was administered by the first author, a licensed speech‐language pathologist and native speaker of Quebec French, in a single testing session that lasted approximately 15–20 min. Standardized administration and scoring procedures were followed as outlined in Royle and Thordardottir (2003) and Thordardottir et al. (2010, 2011).

These clinical assessments could not be completed due to the COVID pandemic.

Measurement Instruments and Data Preparation

Feeding Difficulties Questionnaire

The Feeding Difficulties Questionnaire (McFarland, Poulin, et al. 2020) is a parent‐report instrument designed to assess feeding difficulties directly from caregivers using rigorous methodological standards. The instrument was developed and content validated according to the guidelines for health measurement scales construction outlined by Streiner and Norman (2008), involving collaboration between clinical experts, elementary school teachers, and parents of young children (McFarland, Poulin, et al. 2020). This collaborative approach ensured that assessment items accurately measured the intended feeding constructs, utilized accessible language free from clinical jargon, and were comprehensible to parents with a Grade 6 reading level.

The final instrument comprises 33 Likert‐type items (the focus of the study) and two open‐ended questions (McFarland, Poulin, et al. 2020). Thirty of the Likert‐type items assess individual feeding difficulties previously identified in retrospective studies of children with developmental language disorders (Malas et al. 2015, 2017), distributed across four domains: sucking difficulties (10 items), food transition difficulties (12 items), food selectivity difficulties (five items), and salivary control difficulties (three items). The remaining three Likert‐type items assess broader feeding‐related constructs including food appreciation, mealtime behaviors, and parental concerns regarding feeding, drawing on established measures in the literature (Archer et al. 1991; Crist and Napier‐Phillips 2001; Ramsay et al. 2011; Wardle et al. 2001; Wright et al. 2006). The open‐ended questions provide caregivers opportunity to report additional concerns or contextual information regarding their child's feeding patterns.

All Likert‐type items employ a 5‐point response scale ranging from 1 (very rarely/not at all) to 5 (very often/absolutely). To minimize acquiescence bias, 11 of the 33 items used an inverted response scale (Streiner and Norman 2008); these responses were recoded to the non‐inverted scale prior to analyses. Parental responses were entered into a spreadsheet‐based data management system. Missing responses (657 out of 16,434 [4.0%]) or dual responses (eight out of 16,434 [0.05%]) were excluded from all analyses. The distribution of missing data was nonrandom, with the majority of the missing responses (610 of 657) occurring on the 10 sucking difficulty items, with frequency increasing with child age (10 missing responses at 8 months, 20 at 12 months, 180 at 18 months, and 400 at 24 months). This pattern likely reflects normative developmental changes in feeding. Although solid foods are typically introduced between 4 and 6 months (Arvedson et al. 2020; Fewtrell et al. 2017), breast milk and/or formula generally remain a primary source of nutrition until approximately 12 months (Health Canada 2025). By 18 and 24 months, however, many children are no longer bottle‐ and breastfeeding. Caregivers’ qualitative comments in the open‐ended questions of the questionnaire and direct communication with the first author indicated that these items were often omitted once their child had stopped bottle‐ and breastfeeding. Accordingly, and as suggested by one reviewer, responses to the 10 sucking difficulty items at 18 and 24 months were excluded from the analyses.

Language Assessment

Early language development (8–24 months): The Quebec French versions of the standardized parent‐report MBCDI (Trudeau et al. 2008) were used to measure expressive vocabulary development across the first 2 years of life (Desmarais et al. 2008; Reilly et al. 2009). The MBCDI: Words and Gestures was administered at 8 and 12 months and the MBCDI: Words and Sentences was administered at 18 and 24 months. Although infants at 8 months typically produce few or no words, the expressive vocabulary measure was included at this age to provide longitudinal assessment continuity across all measurement periods and to align with comparable longitudinal research of early communication development and related developmental domains (e.g., Cadime et al. 2017; Nip et al. 2011).

For vocabulary quantification, expressive vocabulary size was calculated as the total number of words parents reported their child producing in the 408‐word vocabulary checklist of the MBCDI: Words and Gestures (at 8 and 12 months) and in the 680‐word vocabulary checklist of MBCDI: Words and Sentences (at 18 and 24 months) (Trudeau et al. 2008). This measure provided a continuous indice of language development throughout infancy and toddlerhood.

The MBCDI was also used to determine whether children's language skills met established criteria for classification as less developmentally advanced at 12, 18, and 24 months. Children were classified as having less developmentally advanced language skills at 12 and 18 months if expressive vocabulary fell at or below the normative 10th percentile (Desmarais et al. 2008; Henrichs et al. 2011; Reilly et al. 2009; Trudeau et al. 2008). At 24 months, less developmentally advanced language skills were defined as expressive vocabulary at or below the 10th percentile and/or an absence of word combinations, determined by parental endorsement of “not yet” in response to the MBCDI's trichotomous choice question about word‐combining abilities (Charkaluk et al. 2019; Reilly et al. 2009). The inclusion of absent word combinations as a marker of less developmentally advanced language skills at 24 months was based on evidence linking this feature to later developmental language disorders (Rudolph and Leonard 2016) and its status as an indicator of early language vulnerability relevant to the investigation of feeding difficulties and language outcomes. The MBCDI could not be used to determine whether children had less developmentally advanced language skills at 8 months because a normative 10th percentile cut‐off for expressive vocabulary has not been established for this age group (Trudeau et al. 2008). Summary statistics for these language measures are reported in Table 2.

Later language development (54 months): Language abilities at 54 months were assessed using the sentence imitation task Le grand déménagement (Royle and Thordardottir 2003), a standardized measure that simultaneously evaluates multiple components of language abilities, including speech perception, vocabulary knowledge, and grammatical competence (Klem et al. 2015; Thordardottir et al. 2010; Ward et al. 2024). The task involves presenting children with a narrative context containing target sentences, which they are instructed to repeat (Royle and Thordardottir 2003; Thordardottir et al. 2010, 2011). The total number of words repeated out of a total of 134 is calculated and converted to a percentage score, with lower scores indicating language skills at the lower end of the developmental spectrum (Ward et al. 2024). This continuous metric provided a single, integrated assessment of language abilities across phonological, lexical, and syntactic domains, thus allowing for comprehensive characterization of language development in the early school period.

This task was also used to determine whether children's language skills met established criteria for classification as less developmentally advanced at 54 months. Children were classified as having less developmentally advanced language skills at 54 months if their percentage score on the sentence imitation task fell at or below the cut‐off established by Thordardottir et al. (2011). This cut‐off corresponds to the percentage score one standard deviation below the mean of the published normative sample (Thordardottir et al. 2010) and has the highest diagnostic accuracy for identifying developmental language disorders in Quebec French‐speaking preschool children (Thordardottir et al. 2011). Summary statistics for this language measure are reported in Table 2.

Data Analyses

Feeding Difficulty Patterns and Language Status

Feeding difficulties were operationally defined as parental responses of four or five on any item of the Feeding Difficulties Questionnaire, consistent with prior work identifying “above threshold” feeding concerns in both typically‐developing (Hubbard et al. 2014; Schmitt et al. 2008; Toyama and Agras 2016) and neurodivergent children (Hubbard et al. 2014; McFarland, Poulin, et al. 2020; Schmitt et al. 2008). For the 10 sucking difficulty questionnaire items, the proportion of above threshold responses was calculated at 8 months for the full sample and, at 12 months, separately for children with and without less developmentally advanced language skills. For the remaining 23 questionnaire items, the proportion of above threshold responses was calculated at 8 months for the full sample and at 12, 18, and 24 months separately by language status.

Cross‐Sectional and Longitudinal Associations Between Feeding Difficulties and Language Skills

To examine associations between feeding difficulties and language outcomes, groups of feeding items that covaried were first identified using principal component analyses (PCA) on Feeding Difficulties Questionnaire responses (Benfer et al. 2017; Field 2018; Putnick et al. 2022; Samara et al. 2010). PCA with oblique rotation (direct oblimin), conducted in SPSS (version 27), yielded four empirically‐derived clusters: general mealtime difficulties, poor saliva management, difficulties in chewing and/or issues with food selectivity, and difficulties in sucking (see Table 3). These clusters were treated as aggregated variables in subsequent analyses to reduce the number of correlations and to limit collinearity among individual feeding items that could bias regression results. A child was classified as exhibiting a PCA‐derived feeding category if at least one item within that cluster received an above‐threshold response. For example, poor saliva management was coded as present if any of drooling, having difficulty controlling saliva, and/or having a lot of saliva in the mouth was rated 4 or 5.

Two complementary statistical approaches were then used. First, point‐biserial correlations were computed to assess cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations between the presence/absence of the PCA‐derived categories of difficulties in sucking at 8 and 12 months and general mealtimes difficulties, poor saliva management, and difficulties in chewing and/or issues with food selectivity at 8, 12, 18, and 24 months and continuous language outcomes. These outcomes included (1) MBCDI‐derived expressive vocabulary size at 8, 12, 18, and 24 months and (2) percentage scores on the sentence imitation task at 54 months. Point‐biserial correlation was selected because it is appropriate for testing associations between dichotomous and continuous variables, and statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Second, logistic regression analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.3) using the “glm” function of the “lme4” package (Bates et al. 2015) to quantify the odds of co‐occurring or later occurring less developmentally advanced language skills as a function of the PCA‐derived feeding categories. For these analyses, the presence/absence of the categories of difficulties in sucking at 8 and 12 months and general mealtimes difficulties, poor saliva management, and difficulties in chewing and/or issues with food selectivity at 8, 12, 18, and 24 months served as independent variables, while early language status (presence/absence of less developmentally advanced language skills) at 12, 18, and 24 months served as the binary outcome variables. Logistic regression was not carried out for the 54‐month language status because only two children (2.5%) met criteria for less developmentally advanced language skills at 54 months (see Table 2), a percentage of occurrence substantially lower than the 7.4% reported in previous epidemiological work (Tomblin et al. 1997). In cases of quasi‐complete separation, Firth logistic regression was substituted using the “logistf” function of the “logistf” package (Heinze et al. 2023) to obtain bias‐reduced estimates (Heinze 2006). Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05, and results were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

Among children with less developmentally advanced language skills, the three most frequently occurring feeding difficulties were drooling (Question 28; 22% [4/18]), having lots of saliva in the mouth (Question 29; 22% [4/18]), and difficulty controlling saliva (Question 30; 22% [4/18]), all related to poor saliva management.

Among children with less developmentally advanced language skills, the three most frequently occurring feeding difficulties were picky eating (Question 23; 23% [5/22]), sensitivity to food temperatures or textures (Question 24; 18% [4/22]), and difficulties with the introduction of purees (Question 11; 10% [2/21]).

Discussion

Interactive feeding is a key moment for social interaction and language stimulation (Feldman 2015; Kaye 1977; Leech 2021; Leech et al. 2018; Snow and Beals 2006; Weizman and Snow 2001; Zimmerman et al. 2019) and subclinical, parent‐reported feeding difficulties can create suboptimal language‐learning environment during mealtimes (Byrne et al. 2017; Harding et al. 2013; Jansen et al. 2017; Lindberg et al. 1996; Mallan et al. 2018) and/or reflect underlying poor oral‐motor control that shape early speech and vocabulary development (Kehoe et al. 2018; Nip et al. 2009) and caregiver language input (Elmlinger et al. 2025). The present longitudinal investigation provides evidence for developmental interactions between feeding difficulties and language in full‐term, typically‐developing infants, with both mealtime environmental and oral‐motor influences potentially contributing to this association as discussed below and summarized in Figure 3.

Summary of hypotheses, findings, and explanation/implications.

Results revealed that poor saliva management at 18 months was negatively correlated with expressive vocabulary size at both 18 and 24 months. Pattern analyses similarly revealed that, at 18 months, the three most frequently occurring feeding difficulties in children with less developmentally advanced language skills were all related to poor saliva management, a pattern that contrasts sharply with the food selectivity issues most frequently seen in children without less developmentally advanced language skills at the same age. Regression analyses further revealed that children with poor saliva management at 18 months were over six times more likely to be identified with less developmentally advanced language skills at 18 months and four times more likely at 24 months. These findings with typically‐developing children are generally consistent with previous research in toddlers and preschoolers with significant neurodevelopmental and motor disorders (Chang et al. 2012).

The fact that poor saliva management at 18 months correlates negatively with expressive vocabulary size at 18 and 24 months and increases the likelihood of less developmentally advanced language skills both concurrently at 18 months and later at 24 months may reflect the contribution of underlying oral‐motor vulnerabilities to early language development. From a developmental motor perspective, children typically develop sufficient oral‐motor control to manage saliva effectively by 15–18 months (Chaléat‐Valayer et al. 2016; Fairhurst and Cockerill 2011; Meningaud et al. 2006). Therefore, poor saliva management at 18 months may indicate delays in oral‐motor control development and its association with adverse language outcomes may arise from the constraints these delays impose on sound inventory and syllable shapes (Nip et al. 2009) ultimately resulting in reduced early expressive vocabulary and grammar (Kehoe et al. 2018). Caregivers may also provide less diverse and complex language input in response to these less advanced oral productions (Elmlinger et al. 2025), potentially establishing a bidirectional loop that hinders communicative development. Taken together, these results suggest that poor saliva management at 18 months may represent subtle developmental disruptions that may shape divergent language trajectories even in the absence of known biomedical risk factors.

Although pattern analyses revealed that picky eating was among the most frequently occurring feeding difficulties in both children with and without less developmentally advanced language skills at 24 months, it was particularly prominent in those with less developmentally advanced language skills, occurring more than twice as often and ranking as the most frequent feeding difficulty. The previous retrospective data of Malas et al. (2015, 2017) similarly revealed that food selectivity issues were among the most frequently documented feeding difficulties in the clinical files of older children with developmental language disorders. It is therefore possible that the negative impact of food selectivity on language development, through less lexically diverse parent input (Harding et al. 2013) and negative, emotionally charged mealtime interactive cycles in which both food selectivity and nonresponsive parental behaviors reinforce one another (Jansen et al. 2017; Mallan et al. 2018) may not be evident until later in development. This interpretation is consistent with that of Cardona Cano et al. (2015), who suggested that persistent or later‐onset picky eating may be more strongly shaped by parent–child interaction dynamics than early‐onset picky eating that resolves quickly. Skinner et al. (1998) also observed that children's emerging abilities to verbally communicate food dislikes between 16 and 20 months coincided with an increase in nonresponsive and coercive parental behaviors during mealtimes, further supporting the idea of later‐occurring impacts of food selectivity on language development. In fact, verbal expressions of food dislikes in later part of toddlerhood (Skinner et al. 1998) and increasing autonomy in self‐feeding (Arvedson et al. 2020; Carruth et al. 2004) may make food selectivity more salient and disruptive during mealtimes, which in turn may perturb established feeding routines, confront caregivers’ expectations that mealtime behaviors would be less of a battle with age (Harris et al. 2018), escalate their emotional stress and frustration (Harris et al. 2018; Wolstenholme et al. 2020), challenge their efforts to maintain responsive practices, and ultimately lead to less responsive parental behaviors and less lexically diverse parental input. Further evidence for the longer‐term negative effects of food selectivity on language development comes from the longitudinal work of Putnick et al. (2022), who reported that the associations between feeding difficulties (primarily related to food selectivity) and communication delays strengthened between 18 and 36 months. Future research should extend beyond the early developmental window examined here and use longitudinal or cross‐sectional designs to investigate how these dynamics unfold across later toddlerhood and early childhood.

When looking at the correlations between early feeding difficulties from 8 to 24 months and later language abilities at 54 months, no significant association emerged. At present, we can only speculate as to why longer‐term developmental links were not observed. One possible explanation is that the influence of early feeding difficulties on language development is restricted to the first few years of life. However, this interpretation seems unlikely given prior findings showing high percentages of early feeding difficulties in children later diagnosed with developmental language disorders (Malas et al. 2015, 2017) and longitudinal evidence that abnormal sucking patterns predict reduced language comprehension at Age 5 in preterm children (Wolthuis‐Stigter et al. 2017). Further, negative mealtime interactional dynamics appear to intensify over time rather than diminish (Cardona Cano et al. 2015). Given that 41% of children with less developmentally advanced language skills at 24 months were lost to the 54‐month follow‐up, and that less developmentally advanced language skills at this age are associated with an increased likelihood of poorer later language outcomes (Hammer et al. 2017; Reilly et al. 2010), a more plausible explanation is that the sample assessed at 54 months included relatively few children with language abilities at the lower range of the developmental spectrum. This underrepresentation is reflected in the lower occurrence of children with less developmentally advanced language skills in our sample at 54 months (2.5%) compared with the 7.4% widely‐cited prevalence reported by Tomblin et al. (1997) despite our rather large experimental sample. Additional research with even larger samples extending beyond our target population of French‐speaking children from the province of Quebec and with extended follow‐up periods could be used to clarify any longer‐term consequences of early feeding difficulties on language development.

In conclusion, this longitudinal study adds to the growing body of literature supporting developmental cross‐system interactions between feeding–swallowing and language across diverse pediatric populations and age groups (e.g., Adams‐Chapman et al. 2013, 2015; Crapnell et al. 2015; Lainwala et al. 2020; Malandraki et al. 2022; Malas et al. 2015, 2017; McFarland, Poulin, et al. 2020; Motion et al. 2001; Neumeyer et al. 2019; Somaraki et al. 2024; Wolthuis‐Stigter et al. 2017; Yardımcı‑Lokmanoğlu et al. 2024). It focused on a key developmental window (8–24 months) during which both feeding and language abilities rapidly emerge (Arvedson et al. 2020; Carruth and Skinner 2002; Chaléat‐Valayer et al. 2016; Delaney et al. 2021; Fairhurst and Cockerill 2011; Gisel 1991; Meningaud et al. 2006; Reilly et al. 2009; Rudolph and Leonard 2016; Trudeau et al. 2008), while also assessing language abilities at a later stage when they are more stable and complex (Bishop et al. 2017). A key methodological strength was the exclusion of known or suspected developmental vulnerability factors, which are known to independently influence both feeding and language development (Bishop et al. 2017; van Noort‐van der Spek et al. 2012; Pados et al. 2021; Reilly et al. 1996; Sharp et al. 2013) and shape their interactions (Adams‐Chapman et al. 2013; Malandraki et al. 2022; Neumeyer et al. 2019; Wolthuis‐Stigter et al. 2017). The demographic composition of the study sample further mirrored that of the Quebec population in terms of sex and family size (Statistics Canada 2017, 2024), and our language data from 8 to 24 months were consistent with previous studies (Bouchard et al. 2009; Reilly et al. 2009; Trudeau et al. 2008). The present findings indicate that specific patterns of feeding difficulties are associated with differences in early language development. These results suggest that feeding difficulties may disrupt language‐rich mealtime interactions and/or reflect underlying oral‐motor vulnerabilities that shape language development.
