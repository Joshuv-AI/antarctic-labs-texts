ABSTRACT

Background and Indications

The indications for performing a lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) concomitantly with anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) continue to evolve, with authors throughout the literature citing numerous clinical and radiographic findings known to increase rates of graft failure. A recent international consensus statement on the indications for the addition of lateral extra-articular augmentation procedures (LEAP) to ACLR reported strong consensus (>90% support) for LEAP in the revision setting, presence of a grade 3 pivot shift or Lachmann, knee hyperextension, skeletal immaturity, a planned return to pivoting sports, and in active patients younger >25 years using nonhamstring autografts. 11 Consensus (75%-89.9% support) was reported for the inclusion of LEAP in patients with chronic ACL deficiency, posterior tibial slope >12°, and a history of contralateral ACL injury. 11 Other findings that have been suggested for indicating LEAP—such as smaller diameter grafts, female sex, radiographic findings consistent with anterolateral knee injuries, and concomitant meniscal injuries or procedures—did not reach consensus. 11 This same group provided unanimous (100%) agreement for the inclusion of LEAP in patients >25 years undergoing ACLR with hamstring autograft, citing the evidence provided by the STABILITY study. 11

Level 1 evidence, provided by the STABILITY study, suggests a clinical benefit from the addition of a LET in young athletes (≤25 years ) undergoing ACLR with hamstring (HS) autograft, in which 2 of the following 3 criteria are met: (1) grade ≥2 pivot shift; (2) generalized ligamentous laxity (>4/9 Beighton score); and (3) intended return to cutting or pivoting sports postoperatively. 2 In 2020, this multicenter, randomized clinical trial found that ACLR and LET in this setting led to a 4% rate of graft rupture at 2 years postoperatively compared with 11% with ACLR alone. 2 This study's findings led to a renewed enthusiasm for the inclusion of LEAP for patients undergoing ACLR. Subsequent high-level studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses have been similarly supportive of the inclusion of LEAP, leading to improved rotational stability and reduced rates of graft rupture postoperatively.6,8-10 An ongoing study is being undertaken to further refine the indications for the addition of a LET with ACLR.

The universal use of LET during primary ACLR is not recommended, as the benefits are clearest in patients with high-risk demographics or anatomical risk factors for graft rupture. 11 Theoretical complications from a LET, including increased pain, stiffness, or overconstraint and iatrogenic arthrosis, have not been demonstrated to occur at an increased incidence at short- or mid-term follow-up, although longer-term follow-up is more limited. 4

Technique Description

Operative Sequence

The location for LET graft fixation is just proximal and posterior to the lateral epicondyle of the femur, which exists in proximity to the extra-articular aperture of the ACL femoral tunnel. Anatomical and cadaveric studies have, likewise, suggested a high potential risk of convergence between these procedures. In one cadaveric study, the tines of a compression staple utilized for onlay LET fixation were found to violate the ACL femoral tunnel in 8 of 20 knees. 7 Another study utilizing an interference screw for inlay fixation reported a 70% rate of LET and ACL femoral tunnel convergence. Therefore, using an appropriate operative sequence, technique, and fixation modalities is important when performing a LET concomitantly with ACLR, given the risk of interference and potential loss of graft fixation. Notably, this risk may be more or less clinically relevant depending on the method of ACL femoral tunnel drilling and the modalities of ACL and LET fixation. Surgical Technique

With the knee in 90° of flexion, an approximately 3 to 4 cm longitudinal incision is created in line with the Gerdy Tubercle, approximately 1 cm from the posterior border of the iliotibial (IT) band. Dissection is carried down to the IT band, and a mobile window is briefly created circumferentially within the incision. The posterior border of the IT band should be clearly defined. At a location proximal to the LCL and just anterior to the posterior border of the IT band, the intended LET graft size may be marked with the use of a 10 mm push rod (Arthrex Inc) coated in ink from a sterile marking pen, which is imprinted on the IT band in this position. A 15-blade scalpel is then utilized to create 2 parallel, longitudinal incisions in a distal-to-proximal direction, reaching 8 cm proximal to the Gerdy tubercle. Alternatively, a double-bladed knife may be utilized to ensure a consistent width for LET graft harvest. The longitudinal incisions are only taken proximally at this time to avoid the potential for iatrogenic injury to the LCL more distally. The graft is then released proximally. A looped suture with a needle (FiberLoop; Arthrex Inc) is used to place several running, locking sutures at the proximal extent of the graft to allow control during completion of the LET graft harvest and to facilitate eventual graft passage deep to the LCL. With the graft under control, the surgeon may then complete the LET graft dissection by extending both longitudinal limbs distally in a parallel fashion with Metzenbaum scissors until reaching the Gerdy tubercle.

Next, the anterior and posterior borders of the LCL are identified. A curved hemostat is passed deep to the LCL in a proximal-to-distal direction and utilized to shuttle the graft in a distal-to-proximal direction deep to the LCL.

While performing “tunneloscopy” to confirm that femoral tunnel violation does not occur, a double-knotless, all-suture anchor (Arthrex Double Knotless Knee FiberTak; Arthrex Inc) is placed just proximal and posterior to the lateral epicondyle.

Once the ACL graft is passed and fixed, attention is returned to the LET graft fixation. The LET graft is first passed between both suture loops of the double-knotless, all-suture anchor in a distal-to-proximal direction. With the knee in 30° of flexion and neutral tibial rotation, the knotless mechanism of 1 suture is cinched down to achieve provisional surface fixation of the graft. The second suture loop is then twisted 180° on itself before passing the LET graft back through the loop in a proximal-to-distal direction. The graft is then carried back proximally around the suture loop before the second suture is cinched down to create a “cruciate” suture configuration. The remaining graft may also be sutured to itself distally using No. 1 Vicryl suture to reduce graft prominence and provide back-up fixation. The authors recommend reapproximation of the IT band and confirmation of hemostasis before closure to reduce the risk of postoperative hematoma. Further modification to this technique has been proposed in which the second—more distal—suture loop is again twisted 180° on itself before passing the graft back through the loop distally. This time, the graft is held distally while the second suture loop is cinched to create a “suture staple” construct to mitigate graft slippage.

Results

The authors do not advocate for any adjustment to a standard, validated ACLR rehabilitation protocol or the eventual timing of return to sport with the concomitant performance of a LET. In the absence of meniscal repair, a standard ACLR rehabilitation protocol is provided, allowing patients to weightbear as tolerated and to perform early range-of-motion exercises.

Discussion/Conclusion

Technical descriptions in the literature have utilized a variety of modalities for LET fixation— including staples, a screw-and-washer, hard- and soft-body suture anchors for onlay fixation, and interference screws and suspensory fixation for inlay fixation. 3 A recent cadaveric study comparing the biomechanical performance of onlay using a compression staple, onlay using an all-suture anchor, and inlay with an interference screw for LET fixation found no significant difference in maximum load-to-failure between the all-suture anchor and interference screw, which both demonstrated superior maximum load-to-failure compared with the compression staple. 3 Given the biomechanical noninferiority and reduced risk of tunnel convergence with surface fixation utilizing an all-suture anchor, the authors advocated for its use in comparison to inlay fixation with interference screws, which has been demonstrated to result in a 70% rate of ACL femoral tunnel convergence and may confer greater risk of physeal injury in skeletally immature patients.3,5 Further clinical study comparing 56 patients undergoing LET using onlay suture anchor fixation (n = 26) and inlay fixation with an interference screw (n = 26) during revision ACLR did not show differences in pain at the LET site, patient-reported outcome scores, or instrumented biomechanical testing. 1
