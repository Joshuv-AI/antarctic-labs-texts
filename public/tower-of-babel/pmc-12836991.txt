Enhancing unique identification of patients with malaria using biometric fingerprinting in Papua, Indonesia
Authors: Liony Fransisca, Enny Kenangalem, Reynold Rizal Ubra, Benedikt Ley, Ric N Price, Jeanne Rini Poespoprodjo, Nicholas M Douglas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnique patient identification is necessary for detecting malaria recurrence and facilitates individualised treatment. Reliable patient identification in settings with poor information technology infrastructure is challenging. A before-and-after study was conducted to investigate whether the use of biometric fingerprinting alongside an existing physical ‘malaria card’ system improved unique identification of patients with malaria at a busy, publicly-funded clinic in Papua, Indonesia.MethodsA three-phase study was conducted at Wania clinic in Timika over a 16-month period. In Phase 1 (the ‘pre-intervention period’—3 months), patient identification practices using the standard ‘malaria card’ system were documented. In Phase 2 (‘training’—6 months), fingerprinting was introduced, troubleshooting was undertaken, and biometric data were gradually accrued. In Phase 3 (‘consolidation’—7 months), fingerprinting continued to be incorporated into routine clinic practice. The main outcome of interest was the proportion of malaria patients visiting the clinic’s ‘Malaria Corner’ who could be linked to a pre-existing malaria card number in Phase 1 versus Phase 3. Analyses were descriptive.ResultsDuring the 16-month study period, 7471 patients with malaria visited the Malaria Corner at Wania clinic, 1487 in Phase 1 (80.3%), 3228 in Phase 2 (59.6%), and 2756 in Phase 3 (59.1%). The proportion of patients who attended the Malaria Corner with a malaria card was 33.1% (492/1487) in Phase 1, 36.3% (663/1828) in Phase 2, and 46.9% (938/2001) in Phase 3. Overall, 56.6% (1828/3228) of patients attending the Malaria Corner had biometric fingerprinting in Phase 2 and 72.6% (2001/2756) in Phase 3. The proportion of all patients attending the Malaria Corner who could be linked to a pre-existing malaria card number increased from 44.4% (660/1487) in Phase 1 to 48.9% (1348/2756) in Phase 3 (difference = 4.5%, 95% Confidence Interval (CI) 1.4–7.7%). In Phase 3, 55.8% (1117/2001) of patients who had fingerprinting were linked to a pre-existing malaria card number (difference compared with Phase 1 = 11.4%, 95% CI 8.1–14.8%). Of the 2714 patients who were offered biometric fingerprinting for the first time, 0.39% (6/1556) refused in Phase 2 and 0.26% (3/1158) refused in Phase 3. Challenges in implementation included unreadable fingerprints, particularly among children.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the potential use of biometrics to improve patient identification in resource-limited settings and to streamline workflows. Expanding biometric systems to include complementary methods, such as facial recognition, could further address challenges in uniquely identifying specific patient groups, such as young children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05749-0.

Background

The province of Papua in eastern Indonesia remains highly endemic for malaria. In this area, local Plasmodium vivax strains typically relapse at intervals of 3 to 4 weeks, consistent with equatorial patterns [1]. Recurrent episodes of malaria can be due to antimalarial treatment failure, reinfection, or in the case of P. vivax, relapses. Treatment failure can arise from drug resistance, inadequate doses of schizontocidal or hypnozoitocidal drugs, or because the patient does not adhere to a complete course of medication. When antimalarial treatment of patients with P. vivax malaria is unsupervised, more than 50% of patients have recurrent malaria within 6 months [2]. Patients with recurrent malaria are at greater risk of anaemia, inpatient admission, and early mortality [3–5]. Multiple episodes of malaria are also associated with pre-term labour, low birthweight, anaemia, and long-term cognitive impairment in affected children, resulting in significant economic loss to families and communities [6–11]. Identifying patients who re-present early to clinics with recurrent infection can help healthcare providers tailor patient management to reduce further episodes of malaria, including doubling the total dose of primaquine to 7 mg/kg for patients with P. vivax malaria and directly observing treatment [12]. Tracking patients through multiple recurrences can be highly informative for policymakers and implementers evaluating the effectiveness of malaria prevention and control programs [13, 14].

Physical cards provided to patients after their initial encounter with the health care system have been used in many settings to assist with re-identifying patients who subsequently return to the same clinic. In malaria-endemic areas, this process is particularly valuable for detecting malaria recurrence. In Papua, Indonesia, this approach is called the ‘malaria card’ system and was first introduced as part of a health systems strengthening initiative in January 2019 at 5 publicly-funded clinics. At a patient’s first presentation with malaria, a physical malaria card is provided which contains a newly assigned unique identification number, the patient’s personal identifiers (clinic, medical record number, name, age, tribe, sex, and address) as well as history of malaria diagnoses, treatment and G6PD status (if available). The patient is asked to bring the card to all future clinic visits to assist in reidentification and appropriate clinical management. The introduction of the malaria card system improved patient identification greatly, and in the last quarter of 2021, more than 70% of malaria patients attending one of the clinics presented with a card [15]. However, this system has multiple deficiencies including a high potential for the cards to be lost, forgotten or shared, potential for duplication or misassignment of card numbers and an inability to detect repeat visits at different clinics. Identification errors resulting from these deficiencies dilute the apparent effect of interventions aimed at reducing the risk of recurrent malaria and hinder quality, individualised healthcare.

A biometric approach to patient identification has the potential to reduce the challenges of lost or forgotten malaria cards. If multiple clinics can access the same centralised database, biometric testing also has the potential to assist with the identification of patients presenting for healthcare at different clinics. Novel biometric systems are now available that are simple to administer in environments with poor electricity supply and limited internet connectivity. In 2018, Indonesia began using fingerprint biometrics at National Health Insurance partner hospitals to simplify administrative processes and improve the quality of health services. The aim of this intervention was to reduce patient queues and decrease fraudulent insurance claims by enhancing the unique identification of patients [16–18]. Other countries, such as Malawi and Kenya, have also used biometry to assist patient identification in their public health care systems [19–21]. In Ghana, a ‘biometrics against malaria campaign’ adopted fingerprinting to track the vaccination status of children and maximise vaccine coverage [22–24]. However, the use of biometrics in routine healthcare requires strict data protection measures to prevent breaches, maintain patient trust, and comprehensively address security concerns and privacy issues that could otherwise negatively impact public acceptance [25].

The current study was conducted to determine whether biometric fingerprinting, as an adjunct to the pre-existing malaria card system, could improve unique identification of patients with malaria at a busy, publicly-funded clinic in Timika, Papua, Indonesia, and whether fingerprinting was feasible and acceptable to patients. The study was nested within a larger health system strengthening study named SHEPPI (Strengthening Health Systems for Effective Primaquine in Papua, Indonesia), which is ongoing at 5 clinics in Mimika Regency.

Methods

Site details

This study was conducted at Wania clinic in the downtown area of Timika Municipality. Prior to the start of the study, Wania clinic saw 7000 cases of malaria annually [15]. Timika is in the lowlands of Papua, with a population of approximately 143,000 people [26], primarily consisting of native highland and lowland Papuans. Timika has a tropical, equatorial climate with perennial rainfall and consistently warm temperatures. Plasmodium falciparum, P. vivax, Plasmodium malariae, and Plasmodium ovale are co-endemic, with the majority of malaria cases caused by P. falciparum and P. vivax in approximately equal proportions. Malaria due to any Plasmodium species is treated with dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine. Primaquine is given for radical cure in patients with P. vivax or P. ovale infections, typically at a total dose of 3.5 mg/kg divided over 14 days.

Study design

The study was conducted in three phases over a 16-month period (chosen for pragmatic reasons to end just prior to the commencement of the SCOPE study [27] at the same clinic). In the ‘pre-implementation’ period (Phase 1—2 June until 10 September 2022), staff at Wania clinic followed standard procedures for patient identification using the pre-existing malaria card system at the Malaria Corner, where patients with confirmed malaria also received malaria education, medical treatment, and supervision of their first dose of antimalarial treatment. The process relied on patients to bring their physical malaria cards with them if they had been issued one previously. If patients failed to provide a malaria card at presentation, staff searched a database (provided as a Microsoft Excel® spreadsheet) to resolve patients’ identities by matching the patient’s names, sex, and age with previous records as per pre-existing procedures. For the purposes of this study, clinic staff were given access to patient identification data from all five local clinics that were participating in the local health system strengthening programme to facilitate identification of patients who may have attended alternative local healthcare providers. The Malaria Corner at the clinic was managed by local clinic staff.

In the ‘training’ period (Phase 2—12 September 2022 to 11 March 2023), identification data (name, sex, year of birth, medical record number, and malaria card number) for approximately 50,000 patients from all five clinics were imported into a biometric MySQL® database (Cipta Informatics Solutions, Indonesia) used to gather fingerprint information. During this phase, patients presenting to the clinic with suspected malaria attended a biometric station whilst awaiting blood film or malaria rapid diagnostic test results. Those with positive malaria results were then asked to attend the Malaria Corner. The biometric table was operated by trained local administrative staff and supported by a study field worker (Fig. 1). Two fingerprints were obtained for each patient, with priority given to the left and right ring fingers since these were regarded as having a lower likelihood of damage from manual labour. If a patient did not have a ring finger, the index finger was used instead. For patients whose identities could be verified by matching name, age, and sex, the data generated by fingerprint scanning were assigned to the patient’s pre-existing malaria card number in the biometric database. If a patient had no prior malaria record or their identity was uncertain, a new malaria card number was generated (Appendix 1).

Service flow in the different phases at Wania clinic

The biometric database (data entry, storage, and back-up) was managed entirely by clinic staff. An information sheet was placed at the biometric desk explaining the purpose of the study and the potential benefits for the clinic and patients. This information sheet also emphasised that patients could refuse fingerprinting without it affecting the health services provided by the clinic.

During the ‘consolidation’ period (Phase 3—13 March to 14 October 2023), patient identification processes at the intervention clinic continued as per Phase 2. However, data from Phase 3 were delineated for the purposes of statistical comparisons since sufficient biometric identification information was predicted to have accrued during Phase 2 to ensure meaningful impact on accuracy and comprehensiveness of patient identification. Throughout Phases 2 and 3, a study field worker completed a recapitulation record for each patient detailing the patient identification methods used (Appendix 2).

Study resourcing

To ensure sufficient human resources for fingerprint scanning, two administrative staff were trained to manage the biometric desk before the start of Phase 2, one of whom was assigned to a primary role, and the other acted as a backup. Clinic staff received a one-time training session to achieve proficiency. During the first 2 weeks of biometric testing, fingerprinting was supervised by a research staff member to ensure that clinic staff could perform tasks independently and efficiently.

Fingerprint scanning was undertaken using a Fingerspot U.are.U Flexcode 4500® scanner (Digital Persona, Indonesia), and data were stored on a dedicated laptop with biometric MySQL database software. The scanner was connected to the laptop, and through a bridging programme, could access the fingerprint database. New scan results were entered into the laptop's local database in real-time without needing an internet connection.

Quality control procedures included cleaning the fingerprint plate whenever smudges or debris were visible. If a fingerprint was unreadable, staff asked the patient to re-scan after cleaning the fingertip with an alcohol swab and allowing it to dry. All failed scan attempts were recorded, along with the reason for failure.

Both the laptop and the biometric database were password-protected. Monthly backups were made to an external hard drive. All hardware was securely stored after hours, with access limited to the two dedicated study staff members. Apart from patient identification, healthcare provision and documentation, including laboratory diagnosis, were unchanged and reflected standard clinic practice.

Study outcomes and statistical analysis

The primary outcome of interest was the proportion of all malaria patients attending the Malaria Corner who could be linked with a preexisting malaria card number. Assuming that 583 malaria patients would present to Wania clinic per month (7000 per year), 70% of whom would attend the Malaria Corner, 1225 patients were predicted to be available for analysis in Phase 1 and 2858 in Phase 3. Assuming a baseline malaria card linkage proportion of 45% (based on analysis of the clinic’s previous surveillance data) and a 5% increase in overall linkage between Phases 1 and 3 (conservatively assuming that 20% of the non-linked patients would have presented to the clinic before and that addition of fingerprinting would improve identification by one quarter), the predicted sample size would allow the difference in linkage proportion to be estimated with a precision of ± 3.3%. In a pre-planned subanalysis, the proportion of patients who could be linked with a pre-existing malaria card was assessed in the subset of patients in Phase 3 who attended the Malaria Corner and had biometric fingerprinting. Other secondary outcomes included the proportion of patients presenting to clinic without a malaria card who could be linked to a pre-existing malaria card number, the probability of successful fingerprint acquisition by age group, the proportion of fingerprinted patients who had duplicate malaria card numbers detected and the proportion of patients who refused fingerprinting. The primary and secondary outcomes were compared between Phase 1 and Phase 3, where applicable. Analyses are presented descriptively as proportions (with numerators and denominators) and absolute differences (with 95% confidence intervals). Hypothesis testing was not done. Graphing and analyses were done in STATA® version 18.0 (College Station, Texas, USA).

Ethical approval.

Ethical approval for this study was provided by the Human Research Ethics Committees of Gadjah Mada University (KE/FK/1113/EC/2020) and Menzies School of Health Research (HREC 2019–3368). Data confidentiality was prioritised and the Caldicott Principles for data use were adhered to. The biometric database, managed by the intervention clinic staff, followed routine data protection protocols and excluded medical histories. Fingerprints were only recorded after verbal consent and were securely stored on password-protected devices, accessible only to designated staff.

Results

During the 16-month study period (2/6/22 to 14/10/23), 24,933 patients were tested for malaria at Wania clinic, of whom 47.9% (11,934) were diagnosed with malaria (Fig. 2). The malaria test positivity rate was 41.1% (1852/4509) in Phase 1, 56.0% (5417/9673) in Phase 2, and 43.4% (4665/10,751) in Phase 3. Overall, 7471 patients with malaria attended the Malaria Corner: 1487 in Phase 1 (80.3%), 3228 in Phase 2 (59.6%), and 2756 in Phase 3 (59.1%).

Of the patients who were known to have presented to Wania clinic previously but did not bring a malaria card with them, 78.8% (189/240) could be linked to a pre-existing malaria card number in Phase 1 compared to 89.7% (183/204) in Phase 3, an 11.0% (95% CI 4.3–17.6%) absolute increase. The remaining patients were either issued a new card number or were not assigned a malaria card number at all.

The addition of biometric fingerprinting to the malaria card system enabled patients with duplicate malaria card numbers to be identified. In Phase 2, 1.4% (26/1828) of patients who attended the biometry table were found to have more than one malaria card number and in Phase 3 the corresponding proportion was 0.9% (18/2001). In Phase 2, 2.8% (52/1828) of patients who had fingerprint testing presented with someone else’s malaria card, and this decreased to 1.1% (22/2001) in Phase 3.

Acquisition of fingerprint data

During Phase 3, 42.1% (843/2001) of patients who visited the biometry table had had their fingerprints scanned during prior visits compared to 14.9% (272/1828) in Phase 2. The proportion of patients attending the biometry table who required a new fingerprint recording decreased from 78.6% (1436/1828) in Phase 2 to 52.7% (1055/2001) in Phase 3 as the comprehensiveness of the fingerprint database increased.

The proportion of patients who had previously had biometric testing, but required rerecording of their fingerprints due to a lack of biometric matching, fell from 1.3% (24/1828) in Phase 2 to 0.6% (12/2001) in Phase 3. Reasons for needing to rerecord fingerprints included device-related issues such as poor previous scan quality, software bugs (subsequently fixed with a software patch), and improper finger placement. Non-modifiable patient-related factors hindering fingerprint collection included worn or damaged finger ridges due to aging, manual labour, injuries to the finger(s), and modifiable patient-related factors included dirty, oily or sweaty skin. Feasibility and acceptability of fingerprinting

The commonest reasons for not being able to acquire fingerprints in young children (< 5 years of age) in Phases 2 and 3 were: child “crying or fussy” (49/404, 12.1%), child sleeping and not woken (8/404, 2.0%), and fingerprints “unreadable” (9/404, 2.2%). In 68.3% (276/404) of cases, no specific reason was recorded. Of the 2714 patients who were offered biometric testing for the first time, 0.39% (6/1556) refused fingerprinting in Phase 2 and 0.26% (3/1158) in Phase 3. In total, 77.8% (7/9) of those declining fingerprinting were adults > 15 years old.

Discussion

Unique patient identification is important for ensuring appropriate patient care, but it is notoriously difficult to achieve in resource-limited settings [28, 29]. Biometric identification has the potential to overcome many of the pitfalls of hard copy identification cards, and fingerprinting is amongst the simplest of the available methods [30, 31]. In the current study, the impact of introducing fingerprinting was investigated at a busy, publicly-funded clinic in Timika, Papua. The addition of biometric fingerprinting alongside the existing malaria card system improved linkage of patients to pre-existing malaria card numbers, strengthened data integrity, and was both feasible and acceptable to patients.

Overall, linkage to a pre-existing malaria card number improved modestly following the introduction of fingerprinting in Phase 2; the impact was almost certainly diluted by frequent non-attendance at the biometry table. Greater linkage was seen in the subset of patients who attended both the Malaria Corner and the biometry table (an absolute increase of 11.4% over Phase 1). This underscores the importance of maximising coverage of fingerprinting to realise its potential effect. The impact of fingerprinting on patient re-identification would be expected to increase over time as the comprehensiveness of the fingerprint database increases; thus, a longer study period would likely have resulted in a greater apparent impact.

The transition to independent operation of the biometry table by clinic staff occurred within two weeks, indicating that the system was user-friendly and practicable once operational. Automated biometric identification also anecdotally reduced the burden on Malaria Corner staff who previously had to conduct laborious manual identity searches in addition to their primary roles as patient educators. Fingerprinting was highly acceptable to patients, with refusal rates below 0.5% in both Phases 2 and 3, and this willingness of patients to engage with the biometric system likely reflects their trust in the clinic setting. A separate qualitative study is warranted to formally evaluate health staff and patients’ acceptance of fingerprinting.

While fingerprinting was generally well-received and helped resolve ambiguous identity in the setting of non-matching or duplicated malaria card numbers, instances of failed fingerprint matching occurred. Issues with unreadable fingerprints caused by modifiable factors such as dirty, oily, or sweaty skin were infrequent in Phase 3 and could be further mitigated through improved staff training and provision of simple equipment such as alcohol swabs and soap to clean the fingers. Unreadable fingerprints caused by non-modifiable factors, such as the effects of manual labour and scarring/trauma, could potentially be addressed by using an alternative and complementary means of biometric identification, such as facial recognition [32], contactless fingerprint recognition [33] or a multimodal biometric system [34–36].

Challenges with children’s fingerprints, which are still developing and harder to record accurately [37, 38], were anticipated. Most unrecorded or unreadable fingerprints among infants and young children were attributed to behaviours such as fussiness, crying, or sleeping during visits. These observations also emphasise the importance of implementing alternative identification strategies to ensure inclusivity and accurate patient tracking [39]. Combining fingerprinting with ear or iris recognition could enhance the reliability of patient identification [40].

The Flexcode 4500® fingerprint scanner used in this study was relatively inexpensive (approximately USD 75 per unit) and readily obtainable. The same device has also been used in Indonesian hospitals participating in the National Health Insurance Programme. At the time of implementation, public clinics in Timika lacked cloud-backup capability due to poor infrastructure, and medical records have remained predominantly paper-based. Nevertheless, Wania clinic has plans to transition to an electronic health information system in the future, with the potential for linkage to the biometric database and eventual integration of an online backup system.

An important limitation of this study was the use of a single intervention clinic, limiting generalisability to clinics in different epidemiological settings. It was not possible to delineate the relative impact of fingerprinting versus the impact of a greater proportion of patients bringing malaria cards to the clinic as the study progressed. This study found that malaria card number linkage was substantially higher in Phase 3 compared with Phase 1, specifically in patients who did not bring a malaria card to the clinic, indicating that fingerprinting was likely an important contributor to enhanced patient re-identification. Biometric log recording errors by study staff may have occurred and will have diluted the apparent impact of fingerprinting. Finally, the relatively short timeframe of the study limited the opportunity to observe the full effect of a comprehensive fingerprint database, likely underestimating its long-term benefits. The study also has notable strengths. Despite the relatively short timescale, a large number of patients were included, which allowed precise assessments of changes in malaria card linkage. Furthermore, the study was conducted in a real-world setting with an early handover of biometric procedures to clinic staff, demonstrating the feasibility of integrating these systems into routine practice.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the potential of biometric fingerprinting to improve patient identification and streamline workflows in resource-limited settings. There were challenges with unreadable or unobtainable fingerprints in young children, and this warrants further studies to see if alternative or additional biometric identification strategies may offer benefits over fingerprinting.
