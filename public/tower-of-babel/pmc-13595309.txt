Prevalence and Associated Factors of Gastrointestinal Helminths Infection in Domestic Ruminants in and Around Gondar City, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Wudneh Ayele, Nega Yismaw Alemu, Sefinew Alemu Mekonnen, Moges Maru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGastrointestinal helminth (GIH) infections are major health problems that cause economic losses in Ethiopia; however, information on their prevalence and associated factors in domestic ruminants is limited in and around Gondar city, Northwest Ethiopia.ObjectivesTo estimate the prevalence, identify GIH based on egg morphology and assess its associated factors in domestic ruminants.MethodsCross‐sectional study was conducted from December 2024 to June 2025. A total of 384 domestic ruminants were sampled using simple random sampling techniques. Faecal samples were collected from each animal and examined using coprological examination techniques.ResultsThe overall prevalence was 56.5%. Strongyle‐type nematodes (most common 37.3%), Strongyloides, Trichuris, Fasciola, Paramphistomum, Moniezia spp. and Nematodirus (least common 1.4%) were identified. Multivariable logistic regression showed significant associations between GIH infection and species, sex, age, study area sites, and management system (P < 0.05). Sheep had 16 times higher odds of infection than goats, and cattle also had increased odds (AOR = 9.74). Females were more likely to be infected than males (AOR = 25.65), and young animals had twice the odds of adults (AOR = 2.01). Extensive and semi‐intensive systems had higher infection risks (AOR = 7.31 and 3.21) than intensive systems. Animals in peri‐urban kebeles also had a higher infection risk (AOR = 2.46) than those in urban kebeles.ConclusionsGastrointestinal helminth infections are highly prevalent in the study area, with host and management factors influencing their occurrence. Therefore, the control measures should focus on the potential associated factors, including management system, age, and species of animals.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal helminth (GIH) infections are among the most significant parasitic diseases of domestic ruminants worldwide, causing substantial productivity losses through reduced weight gain, lower milk yield, impaired reproduction, anaemia, diarrhoea, increased susceptibility to secondary infections, and even mortality in severe cases, particularly among young or poorly managed animals (Taylor et al. 2016). These infections are especially widespread in tropical and subtropical regions, where ruminants are commonly affected by nematodes (example: Haemonchus, Trichostrongylus, Ostertagia, Cooperia, Teladorsagia, Nematodirus, and Oesophagostomum); trematodes (Fasciola and Paramphistomum); and Cestodes (Moniezia) (Hoste et al. 2010; Zajac and Garza 2020; Urquhart et al. 2021).

Epidemiological studies in various countries have reported different prevalence rates. For example, studies have found prevalence rates of 59.8% in cattle slaughtered at Shendi abattoir in Sudan (Sulieman et al. 2017), over 60% in communal grazing systems in South Africa (Tsotetsi et al. 2013), and 59.33% in ruminants in northern Iran (Rahimi et al. 2020).

In Ethiopia, the prevalence of gastrointestinal parasitic infections remains high but varies considerably across regions and production systems. Recent studies have reported prevalence estimates ranging from 21.4% in Kurmuk District to 88.6% in Dire Dawa. Specifically, prevalence was reported as 21.4% in Kurmuk District, 40.8% in Bale Zone, 64.1% in Boloso Sore, 64.3% in Jimma, 66.7% in Diguna Fango District, Wolaita Zone, 69.0% in Arsi Negele, 72.5% in Andabet, and 88.6% in Dire Dawa (Fufa et al. 2011; Alemayehu et al. 2015; Alemu et al. 2019; Abdisa et al. 2021; Kalacho and Kunta 2024; Regassa et al. 2006; Teshome et al. 2013; Abebe et al. 2008).

The occurrence of GIH infections in animals varies across geographical areas and host groups, reflecting differences in environmental conditions, management practices, and host characteristics. In Ethiopia, reported prevalence has varied among locations, with an estimated 40.7% in Hawassa and 67.2% in Hosanna (Amanuel and Bekele 2023; Tiele et al. 2023). Such variation may be associated with differences in climatic conditions, grazing systems, and husbandry practices, which influence the exposure of animals to infective parasite stages. Infection also varies among host‐related factors such as age, sex, body condition, and management system reported to be associated with infection (Amanuel and Bekele 2023; Tiele et al. 2023; Dugassa et al. 2018).

Gastrointestinal helminth infections are a major constraint to livestock production in Ethiopia, with prevalence varying across agro‐ecological zones due to differences in climate, altitude, grazing systems, and management practices. Livestock production in the study area is primarily based on mixed crop‐livestock farming systems, where cattle, sheep, goats, and equines commonly share communal grazing lands (Mekuriaw and Harris‐Coble 2021). These mixed species grazing practices may increase exposure to infective helminth stages and affect the transmission dynamics and distribution of gastrointestinal helminths. However, localised epidemiological data remain scarce, hindering the development of effective, context‐specific control strategies. Thus, understanding the prevalence and associated factors of GIH infections in this area is essential for designing effective, targeted control measures.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to estimate the prevalence of gastrointestinal helminth infections, identify the most common gastrointestinal helminths based on faecal egg morphology, and assess the factors associated with the prevalence of gastrointestinal helminth infections in domestic ruminants in and around Gondar city, Northwest Ethiopia.

Materials and Methods

Description of Study Area

The study was conducted in and around Gondar city (Figure 1), located in the central Gondar zone of the Amhara Regional State in Northwest Ethiopia. The town is situated at geographical coordinates of 12.6 N and 37.47°E, with an altitude of 2133 metres above sea level. It is located 739 kilometres far from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. Rainfall varies from 880–1172 mm with the average annual temperature of 20°C.

Map of the study area.

The area is characterised by three seasons: the wet season (Kiremt) from June to September, the dry season (Bega) from October to January, and the short rainy season (Belg) from February to May. The short rainy season is highly variable, and rainfall may be intermittent or totally absent in some periods, and hence this season is considered as a dry season. The city and its surrounding kebeles’ climate falls under the classification of ‘Weynadega’ (Mekonnen 2019). The livestock population of the study area is estimated to be 82,029 cattle, 2,695 sheep, and 22,590 goats (Central Gondar Zone Livestock and Fishery Office 2024).

Study Population

The study populations were domestic ruminants kept in and around Gondar city. The study animals included in the study were cattle, sheep, and goats. Animals of both sexes managed under intensive, semi‐intensive, or extensive systems were considered. Information collected for each animal included species, sex, deworming history, body condition, and age. Body condition was categorised as poor, medium, or good. The scoring systems used were those of Nicholson and Butterworth (1986) for cattle and Russel (1984) for sheep and goats. The age of cattle was estimated by examining their teeth, as described by Pope (2021), and by asking the owners. For sheep and goats, age was determined from dental eruption patterns (Abegaz and Awgichew 2009) together with information from the owners. For easier data handling, age was grouped into categories. Sheep and goats under one year were classified as young, and those one year and older as adults. Cattle aged 1–3 years were grouped as young, while those older than 3 years were classified as adults. During the sampling period, no cattle under one year of age were available for random sampling; therefore, only cattle aged ≥1 year were included, based on availability rather than intentional selection.

Study Design and Period

A cross‐sectional study was conducted from December 2024 to June 2025 to estimate the prevalence of gastrointestinal helminth infections in domestic ruminants and to identify associated factors based on coprological examination in and around Gondar city.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Methods

Since no previous studies had considered all domestic ruminants in the study area, an expected prevalence of 50% was used. The sample size was calculated using the formula described by Thrusfield (2018): n=[1.962xPexp(1−Pexp)]/d2

Where n is the required sample size, Pexp is the expected prevalence, and d is the desired absolute precision. Based on this formula, the total sample size was determined to be 384 domestic ruminants.

In and around Gondar City, urban and peri‐urban areas were purposively selected based on transportation accessibility and livestock population density. Within each selected urban and peri‐urban area, kebeles were chosen using a simple random sampling method. Accordingly, three urban kebeles and two peri‐urban kebeles were randomly selected. The peri‐urban kebeles were Bilajik‐Dabirka and Gonderoch Mariyam, while the urban kebeles were kebele 18, kebele 19, and kebele 20 (Ayra). The total sample size of 384 domestic ruminants was proportionally distributed among the five kebeles according to the number of animals in each kebele and the representation of different species within those kebeles. This ensured that the sampling reflected the actual species composition and population structure of each area. Within each kebele, individual animals were selected using a simple random sampling technique (lottery method) for faecal sample collection. Both animals with a history of deworming within the past three weeks (as there were unconfirmed reports of helminthiasis in animals following recent deworming within the previous three weeks by animal health professionals) and those not recently dewormed were included in the current study.

Sample Collection, Transportation and Coprological Methods

Faecal samples were collected directly from the rectum of cattle using a water‐lubricated gloved hand. When faeces were not readily available, gentle rectal massage was performed to stimulate defecation. In sheep and goats, samples were obtained using the lubricated gloved index finger. To minimise variation associated with sampling time, faecal samples were consistently collected in the morning throughout the study period. In addition, information on management practices was obtained through face‐to‐face interviews using open‐ended questions, supported by direct field observations and a standardised checklist. Deworming history was recorded based on owners' reports of recent anthelmintic use, including the type of treatment (oral or injectable) and the route of administration. To confirm that the reported treatments were specifically anthelmintic rather than drugs administered for other purposes, farmers were interviewed using structured recall questions. The questions covered the route and frequency of administration, appearance or colour of the preparation, injection site, formulation, and local names of commonly used anthelmintics in the study area, including ivermectin 1%, albendazole, levamisole, triclabendazole, and oxyclozanide. Farmers were able to correctly identify the anthelmintic drugs used, and they collectively refer to these drugs as Madlebia, which logically means that the drug kills feed‐competing helminths, and thus animals gain body weight relatively faster. Each sample was properly labelled with the animal's identification (species, age, sex, body condition score), date, and site of collection, then placed in a universal bottle containing 10% formalin for preservation. Faecal samples were transported to the Veterinary Parasitology Laboratory, College of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Sciences, University of Gondar, for gastrointestinal helminth egg detection. Upon arrival, simple test tube flotation (using saturated NaCl solution, specific gravity 1.2) and sedimentation techniques were performed according to standard procedures (Hansen and Perry 1994; Urquhart et al. 2021). For both methods, 3 g of faeces were mixed with 42 mL of solution (NaCl for flotation, tap water for sedimentation), filtered through double‐layered gauze, and processed accordingly. Flotation preparations were left for 10–15 min before microscopic examination, while sedimentation samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min before examining the sediment. Slides were examined under 10× magnification. Helminth eggs were identified based on morphological characteristics using standard identification keys. Morphologically indistinguishable trichostrongylid eggs were recorded as strongyle‐type eggs. Samples not examined within 24 h were refrigerated at +4°C and processed the next morning.

Data Management and Analysis

The raw data was entered, cleared and coded in Microsoft Excel 2010, and analysis was performed by using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise percentages, mean and prevalence. Variables significant at p ≤ 0.25 in univariable analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model. A forward LR approach was used, and variables with p < 0.05 were retained in the final model. Collinearity among explanatory variables was assessed during multivariable modelling. In cases of strong collinearity, the variable with greater biological plausibility and model contribution was retained. The results' statistical significance was assessed using a 95% confidence interval, and a P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Limitations of the Study

A limitation of this study is that it was conducted over a limited period as a cross‐sectional study design and therefore did not assess seasonal variations in gastrointestinal helminth infections. Furthermore, parasite identification relied on egg morphology, preventing species‐level identification of some helminths, particularly strongyle‐type parasites. In addition, faecal samples were collected once from each animal during the morning to ensure consistent sampling times. However, variability in egg shedding among infected animals and the possibility of intermittent egg excretion may have caused some infections to go undetected, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence.

Results

Prevalence of Gastrointestinal Helminth Infection in Domestic Ruminants

The overall prevalence of gastrointestinal helminth infection in domestic ruminants was 56.5% (217/384; 95% CI: 51.3–62.0). Among host species, sheep showed the highest prevalence (62.4 %) (95% CI: 54.6%–70.2%), followed by a closer prevalence rate in cattle (61.8%) (95% CI: 53.5%–70.1%), while goats had a comparatively lower prevalence (41.3%) (95% CI: 31.8%–50.8%) (Figure 2).

Prevalence of gastrointestinal helminth parasites in each domestic ruminant species.

Regarding the distribution of helminth infection across the five kebeles, the findings show notable variation. Among the kebeles included in this study, Blajig‐Dabirka had the highest prevalence rate at 68.1%, followed closely by Gonderoch‐Mariam at 65.6%. The lowest prevalence was observed in kebele 18, with a rate of 47.1% (Table 1). Of the five kebeles, Blajig‐Dabirka and Gonderoch‐Mariam are peri‐urban areas, with a combined prevalence of 67.1% (104/155) (Table 2). In contrast, Ayra (kebele 20), kebele 18 and kebele 19 are urban kebeles, recording a combined prevalence of 49.3% (113/229) (Table 3).

The gastrointestinal helminths identified included strongyle‐type nematodes, Strongyloides, Nematodirus, Trichuris, Fasciola, Paramphistomum, and Moniezia spp. Among these helminth parasites, Strongyle‐type nematodes exhibited the highest proportional prevalence (37.3%), whereas Nematodirus showed the lowest prevalence (1.4%) (Table 1).

Out of the 217 infected domestic ruminants, 176 (81.1%) were infected with a single gastrointestinal helminth taxon, while 41 animals (18.9%) harboured mixed infections involving two or more helminth genera (Table 1). Mixed infections were identified when faecal samples from individual animals contained eggs of multiple helminth genera within the same microscopic field. The most frequent combinations involved strongyle‐type eggs co‐occurring with other helminths, including Moniezia, Trichuris, Strongyloides, Capillaria, Fasciola, and Nematodirus. Both dual and triple helminth infections were observed, indicating concurrent exposure to multiple parasite species within the same host.

Morphological Identification of Helminth Eggs

Parasitological techniques revealed distinct morphological characteristics of different helminth eggs (Figure 3). Strongyle‐type nematode eggs were ovoid with a thin, translucent shell enclosing a morula (Figure 3A). Strongyloides eggs were ellipsoid, with a thin wall containing a larva (Figure 3B). Nematodirus eggs were markedly larger than other strongyles, barrel‐shaped, and typically contained 2–8 cells (Figure 3C). Trichuris eggs were lemon‐shaped, thick brown shells with bipolar plugs (Figure 3D). Capillaria eggs were also barrel‐shaped, with striated shells, flattened ends, and conspicuous bipolar mucoid plugs (Figure 3E).

Among the trematodes, Fasciola eggs were identified as broadly ellipsoidal, golden yellow in colour, and operculated at one end (Figure 4A). Paramphistomum eggs were found to be large, oval, operculated at one end, and bluish‐grey (Figure 4B). Associated Factors of Gastrointestinal Helminth Infection in Domestic Ruminants

Univariable logistic regression analysis showed that the prevalence of gastrointestinal helminth infection differed significantly across several host‐ and management‐related factors. Accordingly, species, sex, age, management system, study site (kebeles clustered as urban and peri‐urban) and history of deworming were significantly associated with gastrointestinal helminth infection (P < 0.05) (Table 2). Compared with goats, sheep (COR = 2.36; 95% CI: 1.41–3.93) and cattle (COR = 2.30; 95% CI: 1.36–3.89) had higher odds of infection. Female animals were more likely to be infected than males (COR = 13.56; 95% CI: 7.91–23.25). Young animals had higher odds of infection compared to adults (COR = 1.75; 95% CI: 1.16–2.63). Animals managed under semi‐intensive (COR = 4.73; 95% CI: 2.37–9.43) and extensive systems (COR = 6.46; 95% CI: 3.32–12.58) were more likely to be infected than those under intensive management. A history of deworming showed a strong crude association with infection, as non‐dewormed animals had significantly different odds of infection compared to dewormed animals (COR = 13.56; 95% CI: 7.91–23.25). Furthermore, a statistically significant association was observed between animals kept in peri‑urban and urban areas and the occurrence of helminth infection (COR = 2.09; 95% CI: 1.37–3.20). Body condition score was not significantly associated with the infection, and were excluded from further multivariable analysis.

Variables with p ≤ 0.25 in the univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. After adjustment for potential confounders, species, sex, age, and management system remained independently associated with gastrointestinal helminth infection (Table 2). Compared with goats, sheep (AOR = 16.16; 95% CI: 7.06–37.01) and cattle (AOR = 9.74; 95% CI: 4.66–20.37) had significantly higher odds of infection. Female animals were at markedly increased risk compared to males (AOR = 25.65; 95% CI: 13.07–50.34). Young animals were approximately two times more likely to be infected than adults (AOR = 2.01; 95% CI: 1.16–3.49). Management system remained a strong predictor of infection, with animals under extensive management (AOR = 7.31; 95% CI: 2.90–18.46) and semi‐intensive management (AOR = 3.21; 95% CI: 1.30–7.98) having significantly higher odds of infection compared to those managed intensively. In addition, the odds of helminth infection were significantly higher among animals kept in peri‑urban areas than among those kept in urban areas (AOR = 2.46; 95% CI: 1.38–4.37).

Although deworming history was significantly associated with gastrointestinal helminth infection in univariable analysis, it was excluded from the final multivariable model due to Collinearity with sex.

Discussion

Prevalence of Gastrointestinal Helminth Infection in Domestic Ruminants

In the current study, the overall prevalence of gastrointestinal helminth infections among domestic ruminants in the study area was found to be 56.5% (217/384; 95% CI: 51.3–62.0). Out of the 217 infected domestic animals, 37.3% (81/217) harboured Strongyle‐type nematodes. Other nematode genera detected were Strongyloides (1.8%; 4/217), Nematodirus (1.4%; 3/217), and Trichuris (8.8%; 19/217). Furthermore, trematode and cestode infections were observed, with proportional prevalences of Fasciola (2.8%; 6/217), Paramphistomum (8.3%; 18/217), and Moniezia (20.7%; 45/217). The presence of mixed gastrointestinal helminth infections in nearly one‐fifth of infected animals underscores the complexity of parasite transmission dynamics in the study area. The predominance of strongyle‐type eggs in most mixed infections suggests that these helminths share ecological and management‐related risk factors, such as communal grazing, ongoing pasture contamination, and limited use of strategic deworming practices (Taylor et al. 2016; Urquhart et al. 2021). Mixed infections reflect repeated or simultaneous exposure to infective stages of multiple helminth species, a pattern typical of extensive production systems where animals graze on the same pastures for extended periods (Soulsby 1982; FAO 2003).

From an epidemiological and control perspective, polyparasitism has significant clinical and management implications. Concurrent helminth infections can worsen nutritional losses, disrupt host immune responses, and increase overall disease severity compared to single‐species infections (Maizels and Yazdanbakhsh 2003; Steel et al. 2012). Additionally, mixed infections complicate diagnosis and control because helminth species differ in pathogenicity, anthelmintic susceptibility, and seasonal transmission patterns. The co‐occurrence of nematodes with trematodes (Fasciola) and cestodes (Moniezia) indicates overlapping transmission routes and inadequate integrated parasite control strategies, highlighting the need for comprehensive control approaches that explicitly address polyparasitism rather than targeting individual parasite taxa in isolation (Kaplan and Vidyashankar 2012; Taylor et al. 2016).

In the present study, the overall prevalence of gastrointestinal helminths among domestic ruminants was 56.5%, a figure that falls well within the range of reports from Ethiopia and abroad. Comparable findings have been documented in small ruminants at Kuarit district (56.25%) and in another cross‐sectional survey where prevalence was approximately 55%–56%, both of which used similar coprological examination techniques and seasonal sampling designs (Fentahun 2020). Similarly, the overall prevalence observed in the present study is comparable to the findings of Rahimi et al. (2020), who reported 59.33% in abattoirs of Mazandaran Province, Northern Iran, and Sulieman et al. (2017), who recorded 59.8% in Shendi Abattoir, River Nile State, Sudan. Consistent results have also been documented in Ethiopia, where Yihdego (2017) reported 59.5% in cattle around Bahir Dar, and Shimelis and Derso (2016) recorded 59.63% in small ruminants in Enemay Woreda, East Gojjam. These consistencies suggest that ecological conditions, diagnostic approaches, and mixed host sampling may have contributed to the close agreement between our study and theirs.

However, the prevalence observed in the present study was lower than those reported in previous studies conducted in various regions of different countries, including 67.7% in Pastoral Communities of Ondo State, Nigeria (Grace and Aderotimi 2024); 71.32% in rural areas of southern Rajasthan, India (Choubisa and Jaroli 2013); 90.8% in the Coastal Savannah zone of Ghana (Squire et al. 2019); 69.6% in Western Oromia, Ethiopia (Regassa et al. 2006); 94.1% in the Abattoir Market, Jos South Local Government Area, Plateau State, Nigeria (Yohanna et al. 2019); 66.9% in Jakiri, Bui Division, Northwest Region of Cameroon (Ntonifor et al. 2013); and 83.87% in and around Faizabad (Uttar Pradesh), India (Verma and Jaiswal 2017). In contrast, the prevalence reported in this study is higher than 51% reported by Raza et al. (2007) in Southern Punjab, Pakistan, and 30.7% reported by (Malathi et al. 2021) in the Srikakulam district, Andhra Pradesh, India. This may be attributed to differences in geographical and climatic conditions, as well as variations in animal management and husbandry practices.

In the current study, peri‐urban kebeles had a greater frequency of helminth infection (67.1%) than urban kebeles (49.3%). The likelihood of infection was more than doubled for animals in peri‐urban locations (AOR = 2.46; 95% CI: 1.38–4.37). Similar results have been documented in Tanzania (Mhoma et al. 2010) and Ethiopia (Alkadir and Ayana 2024). Evidence at the landscape level that semi‐natural, resource‐rich environments support helminth persistence and transmission in contrast to heavily urbanised settings lends additional credence to these findings (Froeschke and Matthee 2014). Furthermore, this trend may reflect environmental and management factors typically associated with peri‐urban livestock production. Animals in peri‐urban areas often graze on open or communal pastures where soil contamination is greater, manure management is less regulated, and multiple herds mix freely, all of which increase exposure to infective helminth stages. In contrast, livestock in urban kebeles are more likely to be kept in confined housing, receive more consistent veterinary care, and have less contact with contaminated grazing fields. These factors collectively explain the significantly higher prevalence observed in peri‐urban kebeles.

The most prevalent group of helminthic parasites infecting the examined domestic ruminants was nematodes, followed by cestodes and trematodes. This finding is in agreement with the report of (Yohanna et al. 2019; Husen et al. 2018). The results also showed that the most prevalent helminths were Strongyle type nematodes (37.3%). This finding is in agreement with different findings (Dogo et al. 2017; Teshome et al. 2020; Tulu and Lelisa 2016). The high prevalence of strongyles could be attributed to the favourable climate of the study area, which provides optimal moisture and temperature for the eggs to hatch and develop into infective larvae outside the host.

Associated Factors of Gastrointestinal Helminth Parasites in Domestic Ruminants

The present study evaluated the association between GIH infections and host‐ and management‐related risk factors using univariable logistic regression and multivariable logistic regression models. In the univariable logistic regression, species, sex, age, management system, and history of deworming were significantly associated with gastrointestinal helminth infection (P < 0.05).

In the current study, body condition score (BCS) was not significantly associated with GIH infection using univariable logistic regression analysis (p > 0.05). Although BCS was initially evaluated as a potential risk factor, its lack of statistical association suggests that it did not independently influence the occurrence of GIH infection in the study population.

Importantly, BCS may represent an outcome of helminth infection rather than a predisposing risk factor, particularly in cross‐sectional studies. Gastrointestinal helminths can impair nutrient intake and utilisation, leading to gradual loss of body condition, especially in chronic infections (Taylor et al. 2016). This raises the possibility of reverse causality, where infection affects body condition rather than poor body condition increasing susceptibility to infection (Thrusfield 2018). Consequently, the interpretation of BCS as an independent risk factor is limited in cross‐sectional designs, which may explain its exclusion from the multivariable model.

Although deworming history was statistically significant in the univariable logistic regression, it was excluded from the multivariable logistic regression model due to collinearity (redundancy) with sex. In practical terms, this means that in the dataset, deworming history was so strongly patterned by sex that the model could not separate the independent effects of deworming from those of sex. Therefore, the final adjusted model retained the predictors that contributed unique explanatory power: species, sex, age, and management system, while deworming history could not be estimated independently because it did not add additional information beyond sex.

After adjustment, the multivariable logistic regression analysis indicated that species remained independently associated with GIH infection, with sheep and cattle showing higher odds of infection compared with goats. This pattern is biologically plausible and often attributed to differences in feeding ecology: sheep and cattle predominantly graze and therefore ingest infective larvae from pasture (Raza et al. 2007), while goats browse higher vegetation and may experience reduced exposure to infective larvae deposited on grass swards (Gilbert and Dwyer 2024). In pastoral and mixed production systems, species differences in pasture contact and grazing height commonly translate into higher nematode exposure in grazers than browsers, especially under communal or heavily contaminated grazing conditions (Phetla et al. 2024). However, other studies have reported different species patterns, including lower prevalence in sheep and higher prevalence in goats, potentially reflecting differences in immunity development, husbandry, ecology, nutrition, or immunity and sampling context (Choubisa and Jaroli 2013; (Ntonifor et al. 2013).

Sex was one of the strongest independent predictors of infection in the multivariable model, with females having markedly higher odds of GIH infection than males. Female animals were 25.65 times more likely to harbour GIH infections compared with males (AOR = 25.65; 95% CI: 13.07–50.34; p < 0.001). This finding agrees with previous reports (El‐Seify et al. 2007; Khan et al. 2010; Shimes and Derso 2016; Negasi et al. 2012). A higher infection burden in females is commonly attributed to physiological stress during pregnancy, parturition, and lactation, which may reduce immunity and increase susceptibility to helminths. Additionally, females are often retained longer in herds for breeding purposes, which might lead to prolonged exposure to infective stages in endemic environments. In contrast, other studies have found higher prevalence in male ruminants, possibly due to differences in ranting behaviour, grazing exposure, or management (Sulieman et al. 2017; Habtemichael et al. 2018; Teshome et al. 2020). The strong association observed in the current analysis indicates that sex‐related physiological and/or management differences likely contributed substantially to infection risk in the study area.

Young animals had significantly higher prevalence (73.12) than adults (44.64%). Young animals had twice the odds of GIH infection compared with adults (AOR = 2.01; 95% CI: 1.16–3.49; p = 0.013). This result agrees with reports of Teshome et al. (2020) and Kemal and Terefe (2013), who reported a significantly higher prevalence in young ruminants. This may be associated with immature immunity and limited prior exposure in younger animals, whereas adults may develop partial immunity following repeated exposure. However, some studies have reported higher prevalence in adults (El‐Seify et al. 2021) in Egypt, possibly due to cumulative exposure over a longer lifespan, or have found no age‐related differences (Ntonifor et al. 2013) in Cameroun. These inconsistencies highlight the importance of local ecology and husbandry conditions.

The management system was also independently associated with GIH infection. Compared with animals kept under intensive management, those managed semi‐intensively had more than threefold higher odds of infection (AOR = 3.21; 95% CI: 1.30–7.98; p = 0.012), while animals under extensive management were 7.31 times more likely to be infected (AOR = 7.31; 95% CI: 2.90–18.46; p < 0.001). This result agrees with the findings of Squire et al. (2019) and Habtemichael et al. (2018). This may be due to extensively managed domestic ruminants having greater exposure to contaminated pastures and communal grazing areas, which increases the risk of parasite transmission. Additionally, these systems often lack proper housing, clean water, and adequate nutrition, which can weaken the animals' immune systems and make them more susceptible to gastrointestinal helminth infections.

Overall, the wide range of parasite taxa found in the current study and the high prevalence of gastrointestinal helminth infections highlight the necessity of context‐appropriate control measures in and around Gondar city. Control efforts should focus on risk‐based and targeted anthelmintic treatment, with special attention to sheep and cattle, female animals, young stock, and animals managed under semi‐intensive and extensive systems, since species, sex, age, and management system were important independent risk factors.

Conclusion and Recommendations

This study revealed an overall prevalence of 56.5% for gastrointestinal helminth infections in domestic ruminants in and around Gondar city, Northwest Ethiopia. Strongyle‐type nematodes, Strongyloides, Nematodirus, Trichuris, Fasciola, Paramphistomum, and Moniezia species were identified. Among individual parasites, Strongyle‐type nematodes were the most frequently observed, while Nematodirus was less commonly observed. Mixed helminth infections were found in 18.9% of infected animals, as confirmed by microscopy showing eggs from multiple genera, with dual and triple species detected in the same field. Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that species, sex, age, study area sites and management system are key independent risk factors for gastrointestinal helminth infection in domestic ruminants. Therefore, based on these findings, the following recommendations are made: species‐specific, age‐targeted, and management‐based parasite control strategies are warranted, with particular emphasis on females and animals managed under extensive grazing systems. Further studies using advanced diagnostic tools should be conducted to quantify the burden of gastrointestinal helminth parasites (Supporting material).
