Dynamic Expert-Guided Model Averaging for Causal Discovery
Adrick Tench, Thomas Demeester

Abstract
Understanding causal relationships is critical for healthcare. Accurate causal models provide a means to enhance the interpretability of predictive models, and furthermore a basis for counterfactual and interventional reasoning and the estimation of treatment effects. However, would-be practitioners of causal discovery face a dizzying array of algorithms without a clear best choice. This abundance of competitive algorithms makes ensembling a natural choice for practical applications. At the same time, real-world use cases frequently face challenges that violate the assumptions of common causal discovery algorithms, forcing heavy reliance on expert knowledge. Inspired by recent work on dynamically requested expert knowledge and LLMs as experts, we present a flexible model averaging method leveraging dynamically requested expert knowledge to ensemble a diverse array of causal discovery algorithms. Experiments demonstrate the efficacy of our method with imperfect experts such as LLMs on both clean and noisy data. We also analyze the impact of different degrees of expert correctness and assess the capabilities of LLMs for clinical causal discovery, providing valuable insights for practitioners.

1
Introduction

Many applications in healthcare require not just accurate predictive models, but a robust understanding of the underlying causal mechanisms. Structural Causal Models (SCMs) such as Bayesian networks (BNs) are a popular choice for applications such as clinical decision support systems, due to their inherent interpretability and capacity for counterfactual and interventional reasoning
(
Kyrimi et al. 2021
)
. Causal discovery aims to learn SCMs from observational data, potentially revealing valuable new causal relationships for clinical research and practice. To this end, there has been significant interest in applications of causal discovery in healthcare
(
Shen et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Zahoor et al. 2025
;
Petrungaro et al. 2025
)
.

Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for determining causal relationships, but conducting RCTs is often costly and difficult, if not impossible. Causal discovery can point the way towards the most useful clinical trials to perform and provide insight in cases where clinical trials are infeasible. However, would-be practitioners of causal discovery face a great diversity of methods, with no clear indicators of which method is the best for their use-case
(
Kitson et al. 2023
)
.

We therefore seek to provide a general-purpose method for ensembling in causal discovery that can flexibly incorporate a diverse array of learning algorithms with expert domain knowledge. Our primary contributions are threefold:

•

We provide a general model averaging strategy that uses dynamically requested expert knowledge to mediate discrepancies between models. To our knowledge, our method is the first to explore the use of expert knowledge for model averaging or ensembling in causal discovery.

•

We evaluate the performance of our method as an ensembling approach for causal discovery against several baselines on well-known, real-world graphs, using both clean and noisy data to better reflect the challenges of real-world applications of causal discovery.

•

We assess the use of LLMs and investigate the impact of varying degrees of expert correctness on our method, helping establish to what extent imperfect experts can be useful in mediating model averaging.

2
Related Work

2.1
Assumptions and data noise

The theoretical bases for many causal discovery algorithms rely on assumptions that are frequently impossible to verify in practice, such as causal sufficiency (the absence of latent confounders). A plethora of algorithms exist that attempt to address different possible assumption violations. For example, the well-known FCI algorithm
(
Spirtes et al. 2000
)
works in the presence of latent confounders and can provide some insight in the presence of selection bias
(
Spirtes et al. 2013
)
; the SAT-based approach of
Hyttinen et al. 2013
handles latent confounders and cyclicity; and the hill-climbing based approach of
Liu and Constantinou 2022
is shown to improve performance in the face of missing data, including data Missing Not At Random. However, such approaches typically only address one or two assumptions while leaving others untouched. Furthermore, analysis of the performance of well-known causal discovery algorithms under data noise that violates various common assumptions reveals inconsistencies in algorithm performance across metrics and noise types
(
Constantinou et al. 2021
)
.

2.2
Ensembling in causal discovery

The performance of individual causal discovery algorithms can vary widely and unpredictably
(
Kitson et al. 2023
)
, making the improved robustness provided by ensembling
(
Zhou 2025
)
a natural choice for applying causal discovery in practical settings. However, to our knowledge, little research has been done exploring the best methods for performing ensembling or model averaging in causal discovery.
Constantinou 2019a
provides a method for model averaging that greedily adds edges by order of the most counts, avoiding cycles as the graph is constructed.
Aslani and Mohebbi 2023
treat ensembling as an optimization problem, using a distance measure between graphs to maximize the marginal contribution of the edges in the final graph.
Khatibi et al. 2024
present their strongest results from a weighted ensemble of existing causal discovery algorithms. However, their approach relies on access to unrealistic metadata about the ground-truth causal graph that would not be available in a practical setting.

2.3
Expert knowledge

Prior work has typically focused on including expert knowledge as prior constraints
(
Constantinou et al. 2023
)
. A recent contribution from
Kitson and Constantinou 2025
explored using dynamically-requested expert knowledge in the score-based tabu algorithm. Unlike our approach, their method is intended for use with a human expert, and evaluation largely focused on a setting with clean data and a perfect (simulated) expert.

There has also been much interest in using LLMs as experts to enable causal discovery
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
.
Vashishtha et al. 2025
present a strong case for restricting LLMs to answering causal ordering questions, and provide a method for prompting LLMs to obtain a causal order over a whole graph, to be used as prior constraints.
Khatibi et al. 2024
introduce a general framework for using an LLM to refine the output of a causal discovery algorithm. In line with the dynamic knowledge method of
Kitson and Constantinou 2025
,
Tao et al. 2025
use active requests to an LLM to augment continuous optimization algorithms (e.g.
Zheng et al. 2018
’s NOTEARS).

Inspired by these contributions, we explore the use of imperfect experts (such as LLMs) to dynamically answer causal ordering questions, improving model averaging across an ensemble of varied causal discovery algorithms.

Methods

3.1
Expert Model Averaging

We treat the problem of ensembling as one of model averaging: given a set of causal models
M
and an expert
e

m
that best matches the (unknown) ground-truth model
g

M
are generated, our method provides flexibility in the inclusion of different algorithms, making it widely applicable across different domains and data types.

We provide pseudocode for our model averaging strategy in Algorithm

x\rightarrow y

M

Inspired by the model averaging strategy of
Constantinou 2019a
, our method greedily adds edges that avoid creating cycles by order of most occurrence in the component models
M
. However, crucially, our method treats the existence of a direct connection separately from the orientation of that connection: when determining the counts by which edges will be added, any direct edge between two nodes
x
and
y

x\rightarrow y,y\rightarrow x,x-y

\theta_{1}
that determines what percentage of component models
M
must contain a connection between
x
and
y
for an edge to be added to
m

\theta_{1}
but below 0.5 (a majority vote), we query the expert
e
to potentially reject the presence of an edge between
x
and
y
.

3.2
Simulated Expert

We use a simulated expert in our experiments. Based on the known ground-truth model
g
, the expert provides correct answers to
acceptConnection
and
determineOrientation
queries in some fixed percentage of cases. As we focus on the potential impact of causal ordering questions, our simulated expert provides answers based on presence of some chain
x\rightarrow...\rightarrow y
rather than merely a direct edge
x\rightarrow y
. From the perspective of graphical accuracy, a correct causal ordering answer from our simulated expert may therefore not result in a correct edge in the final graph (e.g. if there is a chain
x\rightarrow...\rightarrow y
but no edge
x\rightarrow y
in
g
, when answering “correctly” our expert will not block an edge between
x
and
y
). We choose to take this approach to better reflect the limitations of eliciting accurate expert knowledge in practice. Finally, we note that when
determineOrientation
is called for two variables with no causal relation in the ground truth model
g
, an arbitrary orientation is returned.

We report several evaluation metrics, namely the Balanced Scoring Function (BSF)
(

Our results show broad improvements in BSF and F1 score, typically improving over baseline methods by at least 10% on both clean and noisy data (closer to 5% on noisy data compared to other ensembling methods). Our method further provides a comparable recall to other ensembling methods with much higher precision. The relative SHD also typically improves, especially over other ensembling approaches. Finally, our method improves in BSF, recall, and F1 over Tabu-AL on both clean and noisy data by at least 9% on average, demonstrating the value of using dynamic expert knowledge for ensembling compared to augmenting an individual algorithm.

While individual algorithms still often have higher precision, this is balanced by the greatly improved recall of our method. A higher edge threshold
\theta_{1}
can easily be used to achieve higher precision at the cost of lower recall, as shown in
4.4

4.3
Impact of expert correctness

While our main results focus on a simulated expert with
80\%
correctness, we examine the performance of simulated experts with correctnesses
\in\{50\%,60\%,70\%,80\%,90\%,100\%\}
. We find a linear relationship between expert correctness and the performance of our method, across all metrics, networks, and sample sizes, under both clean and noisy data. Figure
plots the improvements across metrics, averaged over all runs, compared to the Bayesys model averaging approach as a static baseline. Individual plots are available in Appendix
E
.

4.4
Impact of edge threshold

Figure
shows the impact of the edge threshold
\theta_{1}
on graphical accuracy metrics. As expected, precision increases with the edge threshold while recall decreases. The BSF and F1 scores also decrease with a higher edge threshold, leading to our choice of
\theta_{1}=0.0
as default.

4.5
LLM as expert

In addition to a simulated expert, we conduct experiments with an LLM expert, using OpenAI’s gpt-5-nano-2025-08-07 for all experiments. Full details of the prompts used for
acceptConnection
(existence) and
determineOrientation
(orientation) queries are available in Appendix
F
. While we average all results across 20 seeds (consistent with our other experiments), calls to the LLM were cached across seeds and sample sizes for cost saving purposes, such that only one call to each of
acceptConnection
and
determineOrientation
is made for the same variables. To reduce ordering sensitivity, we query the LLM with both possible argument orders, and use the LLM’s choice only if it is consistent across both orders; otherwise, we fall back to a majority vote among the ensemble models

.

5
Conclusion

We provide a flexible approach for model averaging in causal discovery using dynamically requested expert knowledge. Experiments demonstrate the improvements of our approach over both ensembles without expert knowledge and individual algorithms using dynamically requested knowledge, on both clean and noisy data. Further experiments study the impact of expert correctness on our method and establish the viability of using an LLM as an expert. To our knowledge, this study constitutes the first method for leveraging expert knowledge in the ensembling of causal discovery algorithms.

Our work has several important limitations. Our experiments involve only synthetic, categorical data. While our model averaging approach is general and should be applicable to other types of data (e.g. continuous or mixed), provided appropriate choices are made for the component algorithms, this is not yet verified empirically. Furthermore, while we include experiments on noisy data, the synthetic noise we use cannot be expected to cover all real-world conditions.

Future work can seek to address these limitations by employing our method on different data types and noise distributions, including on real data. Additional possibilities include using our model averaging method across multiple runs of the same chosen algorithm rather than across an ensemble of disparate algorithms, providing improved stability (for non-deterministic algorithms or algorithms sensitive to variable ordering) or efficiency (across different data partitions). Finally, while to our knowledge this work is the first to explore the use of dynamic expert knowledge for model averaging, we foresee great potential for future exploration along this avenue.

Appendix B
Selection of edge and orientation thresholds

Keeping the correctness of our simulated expert fixed at 80%, we test our method with all combinations of edge threshold
\theta_{1}\in\{0.0,0.1,0.2,...,1.0\}
and orientation threshold
\theta_{2}\in\{0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9,1.0\}

\theta_{1}=0.0,\theta_{2}=0.7

Appendix F
LLM prompts

We provide our prompt templates below. The relevant substitutions (e.g. network and variable descriptions) are included in our code, available at
https://anonymous.4open.science/r/expert-cd-ensemble-3281/
. We use the following prompt for orientation queries:

We are currently constructing a causal graph for a dataset covering <dataset description>. You are an <expertise description>. Assume that there is a plausible causal relationship between the following two variables (possibly indirect). Your task is to determine the most plausible *causal ordering* between them. Causation may be direct or indirect (via unobserved or observed mediators).

The two variables under consideration are:

’’<X>’ with possible values <X values>, described as <X description>

’’<Y>’ with possible values <Y values>, described as <Y description>

Before answering, consider:

- Which variable is more plausibly upstream.

- Whether intervening on one would reasonably be expected to change the other.

- Whether one variable represents a more downstream outcome. Note that aggregated variables are downstream of (and directly caused by) what they aggregate.

- Whether either variable primarily serves as evidence of underlying processes rather than a driver.

A: ’<X>’ is a cause (possibly indirect) of ’<Y>’ (<X> ->* <Y>).

B: ’<Y>’ is a cause (possibly indirect) of ’<X>’ (<Y> ->* <X>).

Provide a brief causal analysis (2-4 sentences), then conclude with:

"The correct choice is: <A/B>"

And the following for existence queries:

We are currently constructing a causal graph for a dataset covering <dataset description>. You are an <expertise description>. Your task is to determine whether there is a plausible *causal relationship* between two variables. The relationship may be direct or indirect, and may involve unobserved mediators. You are not asked to determine direction at this stage.

The two variables under consideration are:

’’<X>’ with possible values <X values>, described as <X description>

’’<Y>’ with possible values <Y values>, described as <Y description>

Assume that:

- The true causal graph may include unobserved variables.

- Observed associations alone do not determine causal ordering.

Before answering, consider:

- Whether the variables are part of the same underlying (domain-relevant) mechanism.

- Whether there exists a specific, domain-supported causal mechanism by which changing one would plausibly alter the other, beyond general shared factors.

- Whether any observed association is more likely explained by confounding or selection effects.

- Whether the variables operate at compatible levels (e.g., trait vs symptom, background vs outcome).

- Whether the variables are better understood as parallel consequences of broader factors rather than causally linked to each other.

A: There is a plausible causal relationship between ’<X>’ and ’<Y>’.

B: There is no meaningful causal relationship between ’<X>’ and ’<Y>’; any association is likely due to shared causes or noise.

Provide a brief causal analysis (2-4 sentences), then conclude with:
"The correct choice is: <A/B>"

For the ALARM network, we include the following additional text in the prompts for orientation queries:

Additionally, note that:

- Variables that represent errors or system failures are root causes of other measurements and not downstream of anything.

- Measurements, indicators, or monitoring outputs depend on underlying physiological states and cannot cause them.

Likewise, we include the following additional text in the prompts for existence queries:

Choose A only if at least one of the following clearly applies:

- one variable directly produces the other through a known mechanism

- one variable is an upstream structural or pathological cause of the other

- one variable is a downstream aggregate or consequence of the other

Appendix G
LLM memorization of benchmark networks

As noted in
4.5
, we acknowledge the potential that the LLM has seen, and possibly memorized, the benchmark networks in its training data. We conduct the following case study as a limited assessment of the degree to which this has occurred, prompting the chosen LLM (
gpt-5-nano
) to provide the full structure of the networks ASIA, ALARM, and SimSUM:

Provide the structure of the <network name> bayesian network as a list of edges. Format the answer as a python list of tuples strings, i.e. [(’x’, ’y’), ...], where (’x’, ’y’) indicates an edge x -> y in the network. Do your best even if you don’t believe you have the network memorized. Answer only with the edge list.

To avoid ambiguity in the networks, we use the following substitutions for ¡network name¿: ’ASIA’, ’ALARM clinical monitoring’, and ’SimSUM patient encounters for respiratory diseases’.

The response for ASIA is almost perfect, merely eliding the aggregate variable ’either’, indicating a high degree of memorization:

[(’Asia’, ’Tuberculosis’), (’Smoking’, ’Lung Cancer’), (’Tuberculosis’, ’XRay’), (’Lung Cancer’, ’XRay’), (’Tuberculosis’, ’Dyspnea’), (’Lung Cancer’, ’Dyspnea’), (’Bronchitis’, ’Dyspnea’), (’Smoking’, ’Bronchitis’)]

The LLM refused to provide a response for ALARM, even after adding the text from ”Do your best…” on (not initially included in the prompt), answering with an empty list.

The following response was provided for SimSUM. It contains 22 edges compared to SimSUM’s 35, and 20 nodes compared to SimSUM’s 16, with only 5 of the 20 (25
\%
) directly corresponding to nodes in SimSUM.

[(’age’, ’pneumonia’), (’smoking’, ’pneumonia’), (’exposure’, ’pneumonia’), (’age’, ’asthma_exacerbation’), (’sex’, ’asthma_exacerbation’), (’smoking’, ’copd_exacerbation’), (’acute_bronchitis’, ’cough’), (’pneumonia’, ’cough’), (’pneumonia’, ’fever’), (’pneumonia’, ’sob’), (’fever’, ’cbc_wbc’), (’cbc_wbc’, ’pneumonia_diagnosis’), (’cxr_result’, ’pneumonia_diagnosis’), (’pneumonia_diagnosis’, ’antibiotics’), (’pneumonia_diagnosis’, ’bronchodilators’), (’asthma_exacerbation’, ’wheeze’), (’wheeze’, ’sob’), (’sob’, ’pulse_ox’), (’resp_rate’, ’pulse_ox’), (’pulse_ox’, ’disposition’), (’antibiotics’, ’disposition’), (’bronchodilators’, ’disposition’)]

Overall, these results point to high memorization for the ASIA network, but low or none for ALARM and SimSUM.

Appendix H
LLM performance on clinical networks

As seen in Section
4.5
, the LLM performs much better on the ASIA and SimSUM networks than the ALARM network. Like ASIA and SimSUM, the orientation of arrows in the ALARM has a realistic causal interpretation (from diagnosis to evidence rather than the reverse)
(
Beinlich et al. 1989
)
, which should allow an LLM to accurately answer orientation queries. However, the structure of the ALARM network may involve modeling choices that make it difficult for an LLM to accurately determine the existence of causal relationships between certain variables. For example, in the ALARM network the variables
KINKEDTUBE
and
SHUNT
are not in a causal relationship, although an LLM may reasonably expect a kinked tube to be a possible (indirect) cause of a pulmonary shunt by impacting ventilation. Such issues also hold for other pairs of variables (e.g.
VENTLUNG
and
SHUNT
), but cannot be expected to wholly account for the LLM’s difficulties with the ALARM network, given performance also decreased on orientation queries relative to the other networks.
