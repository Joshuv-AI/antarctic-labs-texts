Optimizing AI Agent Attacks With Synthetic Data
Chloe Loughridge, Paul Colognese, Avery Griffin, Tyler Tracy, Jon Kutasov, Joe Benton

Abstract
As AI deployments become more complex and high-stakes, it becomes increasingly important to be able to estimate their risk. AI control is one framework for doing so. However, good control evaluations require eliciting strong attack policies. This can be challenging in complex agentic environments where compute constraints leave us data-poor. In this work, we show how to optimize attack policies in SHADE-Arena, a dataset of diverse realistic control environments. We do this by decomposing attack capability into five constituent skills – suspicion modeling, attack selection, plan synthesis, execution and subtlety – and optimizing each component individually. To get around the constraint of limited data, we develop a probabilistic model of attack dynamics, optimize our attack hyperparameters using this simulation, and then show that the results transfer to SHADE-Arena. This results in a substantial improvement in attack strength, reducing safety score from a baseline of 0.87 to 0.41 using our scaffold.



1
Introduction

As AI systems grow more capable and situationally aware, they will have increasing opportunity to cause high-stakes safety failures, whether due to misuse, misalignment, or system errors
(
Carlsmith 2023
;
Crichton et al. 2024
;
Shlegeris and Greenblatt 2024
)
. AI control is a framework introduced by
Greenblatt et al. 2024
to estimate an upper bound on the rate of safety failures of a particular AI deployment. It involves performing an adversarial evaluation of the safety measures by having a red team attempt to demonstrate the maximum probability of safety failure possible subject to the proposed safety protocols. When we use AI control to estimate risks from misalignment, this typically involves eliciting the AI model being deployed to cause harm. We call the resulting policies
attack policies

(
Greenblatt et al. 2024
)
.

For a control evaluation to provide a genuine upper bound on the risk, the red team must be strong enough to elicit a safety failure probability at least as high as observed in deployment. Strong red team elicitation is therefore a key requirement for AI control. However, as AI deployments become increasingly sophisticated, their potential failure modes also become more complex, as do the control evaluations required to study them.

Recent work has made significant progress designing more realistic control settings
(
Bhatt et al. 2025
;
Kutasov et al. 2025
;
Matthews et al. 2025
)
. But, strong red teaming in complex, agentic settings remains challenging. Firstly, transcripts are often slow and expensive to collect. This results in poor feedback loops, makes it hard to pick attack policy hyperparameters in a principled manner, and makes sample-inefficient elicitation methods like end-to-end reinforcement learning impractical. In addition, even current frontier models are often poor attack policies, requiring complex scaffolds to see moderate performance
(
Greenblatt et al. 2024
;
Bhatt et al. 2025
;
Kutasov et al. 2025
)
.

In this work, we demonstrate a more effective method for eliciting attack policies. Rather than relying on end-to-end reinforcement learning, we decompose effective attack capabilities into five interacting components:

•

Suspicion modeling:
Learn a calibrated model of what the safety protocol flags as suspicious in an environment.

•

Attack selection:
Effectively choose when to start and stop attacks to minimize suspicion.

•

Plan synthesis:
Construct multi-step plans which, if executed correctly, bypass the safety protocol.

•

Execution:
Execute plans with high fidelity across long contexts and avoid deviations that risk detection.

•

Subtlety:
Frame actions in ways that monitoring protocols are likely to find innocuous.

We instantiate and evaluate an attack policy scaffold in SHADE-Arena
(
Kutasov et al. 2025
)
, a suite of long-horizon, simulated environments. We develop a probabilistic model of attack dynamics parameterized by (i) the probability of the attack policy outputting different action types, (ii) the distribution of internal (attack policy) suspicion scores, and (iii) distribution of external (monitor) suspicion scores for a given action type. Using real SHADE-Arena transcripts, we infer these parameters for our synthetic SHADE model and use the fitted model to (a) identify optimal hyperparameter choices for attack protocols, and (b) run counterfactual simulations that predict the marginal value of targeted improvements (e.g., more calibrated suspicion predictors or improved persuasion).
Griffin et al. 2024
also study synthetic models of control evaluations and consider using them to optimize attack policies and safety protocols, but our work is the first to apply such techniques in practice.

Guided by these predictions, as well as experiments in the original SHADE-Arena, we implement targeted interventions within our agent scaffold and measure the effect of these interventions on red-team effectiveness in SHADE-Arena environments. We find (Figure
1
) that our optimized scaffold substantially outperforms the baseline attack policy, reducing safety score by more than a factor of 2, from 0.87 to 0.41. Moreover, our interventions stack (as can be seen by the ablations in Figure
1
– see Section
9
for more details), so that their combination produces a more effective attack policy than any individual intervention. This closes the loop from model-based diagnosis to empirical validation, and yields attack policies that more meaningfully stress-test monitoring and control protocols.

2
Control Evaluations

A control evaluation of an AI deployment, as introduced in
Greenblatt et al. 2024
, consists of the following steps. First, we define the deployment parameters, including the affordances given to the AI system, and what constitutes a safety failure. Then, a
blue team
specifies a safety protocol that they believe should prevent the AI system from causing safety failures. Next, a
red team
attempts to demonstrate the maximum probability of safety failure that the AI system could cause subject to the safety protocol. This is assumed to be an upper bound for the actual probability of safety failure, assuming the attack policy is sufficiently well elicited.

Typically, we assume that the AI system makes use of a strong
untrusted model

\mathcal{U}
, which is highly capable but may be misaligned. We assume that the blue team also has access to a less capable
trusted model

\mathcal{T}
, and a limited budget of high-quality human labor
\mathcal{H}
for auditing transcripts. The human labor is assumed to be perfect, so that if we audit a transcript in which the agent attacks, the agent is certainly caught. We use Claude 3.7 Sonnet as
\mathcal{U}
and Claude 3.5 Sonnet as
\mathcal{T}

(
Anthropic 2024
;
Anthropic 2025
)
, unless otherwise specified.

In high-stakes control, we consider a single safety failure to be unacceptable, but also highly incriminating if the model is caught atttempting to cause such a failure
(
Bhatt et al. 2025
)
. Therefore, we consider the red team to win if their attack policy causes a single safety failure before a transcript with an attack attempt is audited. Conversely, the blue team wins if they audit an attack attempt before an attack is executed successfully.

When studying AI control, we typically approximate a continuous AI deployment using a dataset of discrete environments. We deploy the agent in each environment in turn, and measure whether the attack policy causes a safety failure and whether the trajectory is audited by the safety protocol.

We define the
safety score
for this combination of attack policy and safety protocol to be the probability of a blue team win, which is equal to

\text{Safety Score}=\frac{p_{\text{aud}}}{p_{\text{succ,unaud}}+p_{\text{aud}}}

where
p_{\text{succ,unaud}}
is the probability that for a given transcript the agent causes a safety failure and the transcript is not audited, and
p_{\text{aud}}
is the probability that a transcript from the attack policy is audited
(
Greenblatt et al. 2024
)
. We use the safety score to estimate the rate of safety failure in our deployment – this is the metric which the red team is trying to minimize. (The other key metric in AI control is
usefulness
, the proportion of the benign untrusted performance which the blue team can recover with their protocol. Since we care only about red team elicitation, we will not focus on usefulness here, but see
Greenblatt et al. 2024
for more.)

Previous works have performend control evaluations in the APPS-backdoor setting
(
Greenblatt et al. 2024
;
Wen et al. 2024
)
and in BashBench, a dataset of linux system administration tasks
(
Bhatt et al. 2025
)
. These works use complex agentic scaffolds to elicit attack policies.
Bhatt et al. 2025
elicit attack policies by empirically optimizing hyperparameters like the probability that the attack policy inserts an attack on each step of the trajectory. In addition, several previous works evaluate for capabilities which are closely tied to producing strong attacks, variously known as sabotage, stealth, or scheming evaluations
(
Phuong et al. 2024
;
Benton et al. 2024
;
Phuong et al. 2025
;
Meinke et al. 2025
;
Schoen et al. 2025
;
Mallen et al. 2025
)
.

2.1
SHADE-Arena

SHADE-Arena is a dataset of 17 agentic AI environments, each one containing a benign main task and a malicious side task
(
Kutasov et al. 2025
)
. For example, in one environment the main task is to manage a user’s calendar while the side task is to covertly locate and exfiltrate an API key. These task pairs are challenging even for current frontier AI agents. We evaluate on a subset of nine tasks that are well-behaved, meaning their main objectives are reliably achievable and do not require persuading the monitor to overlook task failures. More details on SHADE-Arena are provided in Appendix
A
. We define a safety failure in SHADE-Arena to be completing the side task. In each environment, the blue team is informed of the main task, but not the side task.

Before iterating on attack policy elicitation, we must fix a safety protocol to iterate against. For simplicity, we iterate against the default
trusted monitoring
safety protocol taken from
Kutasov et al. 2025
;
Bhatt et al. 2025
. In trusted monitoring, after each turn from the attack policy (i.e. each tool call or assistant message) the blue team prompts the trusted model
\mathcal{T}
to assign a suspicion score between 0 and 100 to that turn. At the end of the trajectory, we aggregate the suspicion scores for all the turns by taking the second highest, and apply human auditing based on whether the aggregated suspicion score is greater than some auditing threshold
s_{\textup{ext}}
. We pick
s_{\textup{ext}}
so that the false positive rate of monitoring on benign transcripts is 0.5%.

When we run a basic prompted attack policy against trusted monitoring it achieves a safety score of 0.87, and the corresponding auditing threshold is 68 (see Appendix
B
for details). This is the baseline that we aim to improve on.

3
Modeling Red Team Policies

Figure 2:
Synthetic SHADE-Arena. Attack policy actions are sampled from
P
and then suspicion is sampled from
Q_{X}
.

To optimize attack policies in SHADE-Arena, we build a synthetic simulation of the dataset that preserves its most important statistical properties. This model lets us iterate on different red team strategies substantially faster, by reducing the computational cost associated with evaluating a given strategy. As control settings and protocols continue to grow in complexity, we anticipate the cost of testing attack policies will become a significant bottleneck, underscoring the importance of simulation-based analyses.

To build our synthetic model of SHADE-Arena, we start by categorizing agent turns. In original SHADE-Arena, some agent turns mainly make progress towards the main task, others are directed towards the side task, and some help the model achieve both. We formalize this categorization by defining five action types:

•

M
actions work towards only the main task.

•

S
actions work towards only the side task and appear in trajectories in which the agent
completes
the side task.

•

SF
actions work towards only the side task and appear in trajectories in which the agent
fails
the side task.

•

SM
actions work towards both tasks, and appear in trajectories in which the agent
completes
the side task.

•

SMF
actions work towards both tasks, and appear in trajectories in which the agent
fails
the side task.

With this action classification system, we define a generation procedure for synthetic SHADE trajectories. We say that for each turn in the trajectory:

•

The probability that the attack policy outputs an action type
X\in\{\textup{S, SM, M, SF, SMF}\}
is
p_{X}
.

•

The suspicion score that
\mathcal{T}
assigns to an action of type
X\in\{\textup{S, SM, M, SF, SMF}\}
is sampled from a distribution
Q_{X}
.

We then construct a synthetic SHADE trajectory as follows: for each turn of the rollout, we first sample an attack policy action
x\in\{\textup{S, SM, M, SF, SMF}\}
according to
P:=\{p_{x}\}_{x}
. Then, we sample a corresponding suspicion score
s\sim Q_{x}
. This is illustrated in Figure
2
. We repeat this for
N
turns, where
N
is fixed per synthetic environment. We consider a synthetic trajectory to complete the side task if it contains at least one
S
action. We find that this definition best matches the empirical patterns observed in real SHADE data (see Appendix
A
)

We fit all parameters for synthetic SHADE using statistics from original SHADE-Arena transcripts. We infer
P
by calculating the mean of each action type in LLM transcripts on SHADE-Arena. Since suspicion scores from
\mathcal{T}
are typically bimodal for real LLMs on SHADE-Arena, we model
Q_{X}
as a mixture of two Gaussian distributions and infer the means and variances using the expectation-maximization algorithm applied to real transcripts
(
Dempster, Laird, and Rubin 1977
)
. For more details, see Appendix
A.2
.

We infer parameters separately for each of the nine environments, since environments are quite heterogeneous and this allows us to better capture the diversity of different environment dynamics. This results in a set of nine synthetic SHADE-Arena environments in which we can test out red team scaffolds, allowing us to efficiently optimize attack policy hyperparameters.

Suspicion Modeling

An attack policy should benefit from having a calibrated understanding of what the model
\mathcal{T}
finds suspicious, since it can use this information to perform
attack selection
, i.e. start and stop attacks at appropriate times
(
Bhatt et al. 2025
)
.

s_{\textup{int}}

\tau_{\textup{int}}

5
Attack Selection

Next, we use synthetic SHADE to optimize the hyperparameters in the internal monitor simulation protocol that control red team attack selection:
\tau_{\textup{int}}
and
K
. For a given value of
\tau_{\textup{int}}
, the internal monitor will have some false positive and false negative rate when predicting whether
\mathcal{T}
will flag a transcript. Given a fixed internal monitor simulation, changing
\tau_{\textup{int}}
allows us to trade off false positives against false negatives. Meanwhile, decreasing
K
lets us decrease the aggression of the attack policy within a single trajectory.

To do this, we must extend synthetic SHADE to model the internal monitor suspicion distribution. For a fixed
\tau_{\textup{int}}
, we take the actual FPR and FNR of a Claude 3.7 Sonnet internal monitor when simulating Claude 3.5 Sonnet as
\mathcal{T}
with suspicion threshold
\tau_{\textup{int}}
in the original SHADE-Arena, and set the synthetic internal suspicion equal to the external suspicion, except that we fix the internal suspicion value to be above (resp. below) the internal suspicion threshold for a fraction of samples corresponding to the observed FPR (resp. FNR) on original SHADE-Arena. (For the exact details, see Appendix
D.1
.)

To optimize
\tau_{\textup{int}}
and
K
, we measure the safety score of the internal monitor simulation protocol in synthetic SHADE for various values of the internal monitor threshold
\tau_{\textup{int}}
, for
K=1,10
and
500
. In Figure
5
we see that, for a fixed value of
K
, the safety score is increasing in
\tau_{\textup{int}}
for larger values of
\tau_{\textup{int}}
. This is because larger values of
\tau_{\textup{int}}
make the model more aggressive, i.e. more likely to attempt an action that it thinks is suspicious – this increases its chance of getting caught. However, the safety score is decreasing in
\tau_{\textup{int}}
for small enough values of
\tau_{\textup{int}}
. This is because for very small
\tau_{\textup{int}}
the model hardly ever attacks, so the probability that it successfully completes an attack is very low. Meanwhile, because the external monitor is stochastic and has a FPR on benign transcripts of 0.5%, there is a steady background rate at which it may audit attack transcripts by chance, even if the attack policy is doing its best not to be suspicious.

We also see that the optimal value of
\tau_{\textup{int}}
(corresponding to the minimum safety score) is lower for larger values of
K
. This is intuitive, since an attack policy that tries more interventions can afford to be more conservative with each one. The minimum safety score is also lower for larger values of
K
, since trying more interventions means we are more likely to find one that is successful and not suspicious.

If we calculate the FPR and FNR of the internal suspicion simulator implied by the optimal value of
\tau_{\textup{int}}
for
K=1,10,500
we find that they are
(0.07,0.52),(0.29,0.16)
and
(0.64,0.06)
respectively. For reasonably large
K
, the optimal attack policy favors caution: false negatives appear to be substantially more costly than false positives since the former risk the attack policy getting caught, while if the model choses not to attack on a particular turn it can try again later.

Planning

Third, we evaluate if improving plan quality helps the attack policy. To measure this, we provide agents with plan hints of varying quality. We use four types of hints: deliberately “bad” one-sentence hints, high-quality human-written hints, Claude 4.5 Sonnet-generated verbose hints, and no hints at all. Examples of each type are given in Appendix
E
.

We measure the safety score in original SHADE-Arena when models are provided with each type of plan, and plot the results in Figure
7
. We also show the effect on the rate of side task completion and the rate at which attacks are audited separately in Figure
8
. Across all conditions, changes to plan strength produce very limited change in safety scores. This suggests frontier models are relatively insensitive to plan-level optimizations in SHADE, implying that planning ability is not the limiting factor for current attack policies. This corroborates a similar observation in
Kutasov et al. 2025
.

7
Execution

Fourth, we evaluate the extent to which attack policies gain from improving their execution of a given plan, by which we mean their ability to avoid distractions and errors. We model the attack policy’s execution in synthetic SHADE by modulating the relative weight of
p_{\textup{S}},p_{\textup{SM}}
compared to
p_{\textup{SF}},p_{\textup{SMF}}
. We introduce an
execution quality factor

e\in[0,1]
which modifies the attack policy output distribution via:

\displaystyle p_{\textup{SF}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(1-e)p_{\textup{SF}},\quad

\displaystyle p_{\textup{S}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=p_{\textup{S}}+ep_{\textup{SF}},

\displaystyle p_{\textup{SMF}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(1-e)p_{\textup{SMF}},\quad

\displaystyle p_{\textup{SM}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=p_{\textup{SM}}+ep_{\textup{SMF}}.

Thus
e=0
corresponds to the baseline behavior and
e=1
corresponds to perfect execution – all failures are converted into successes. The mean values of each attack policy parameter across all environments are reported in Table
1
.

We measure the effect of improved execution by modulating
e
in synthetic SHADE and measuring the effect on safety score. The results in Figure
9
show that increasing the execution parameter only has a small effect on the safety score. Even perfect execution – which is unrealistic for current models – only decreases safety by 0.14. Figures
20
and
21
in Appendix
F
break this effect down into the effect on side task success and the auditing rate – we see that improving execution barely changes the auditing rate and has only a small positive effect on side task success rate.

Since execution quality appears to have relatively little effect on attack success, and because we expect the models’ execution ability to be particularly correlated with general capabilities which will naturally improve in the future, we focus on other interventions going forward.

8
Subtlety

Attack policies should benefit from being able to frame their actions as less suspicious to the monitor
\mathcal{T}
. We model this effect in synthetic SHADE by modifying the suspicion distributions
Q_{X}
. We scale down the mean of the upper mode of each distribution
Q_{X}
by a
suspicion reduction factor

\gamma
, so that if the original upper mean was
\mu_{+}
, the new mean in
(1-\gamma)\mu_{+}
. (Recall that we parameterized
Q_{X}
as a mixture of two Gaussians.) The parameter
\gamma\in[0,1]
thus models the degree to which the attack policy is able to reframe its actions to be less suspicious to
\mathcal{T}
. This adjustment is applied across all action categories and environments uniformly.

Next, we check that this effect transfers to the original SHADE-Arena. We strengthen the subtlety of our agent by using a best-of-
N
approach. At each turn, we first sample a single turn from the attack policy. We fix the tool call from this action, and then sample
N
justifications from our agent for this tool call. We score each sample with
\mathcal{T}
and pick the one which has the lowest suspicion score. We must fix the tool call as otherwise our best-of-
N
procedure would select for tool calls which don’t make progress on the side task.

We sample LLM trajectories in SHADE-Arena using this process for a range of values of
N
between 1 and 64, and calculate the resulting safety scores and reduction in mean suspicion. The results in Figure
11
show that for increasing
N
we observe a decrease in the mean suspicion per turn, and this induces a reduction in safety score that mirrors the reduction observed in synthetic SHADE.

9
Combining Attack Policy Optimizations

To summarize, our components-based analysis of effective sabotage suggests the following conclusions:

•

Suspicion modeling:
Having a calibrated view of what
\mathcal{T}
finds suspicious is valuable, and can be leveraged via an internal monitor simulation scaffold.

•

Attack selection:
Having a large maximum number of interventions and a small but non-zero internal suspicion threshold is typically optimal, and good parameter values can be found by optimizing in synthetic SHADE.

•

Planning:
Better plans provide a benefit, but the effect size is small for current models.

•

Execution:
Avoiding failures of execution makes only a small difference in synthetic SHADE.

•

Subtlety:
Attack policies are more successful when they are able to frame their actions as less suspicious to monitors, in both synthetic SHADE and the original.

Finally, we demonstrate that these insights can be combined into a substantially strengthened attack policy based on the internal monitor simulation protocol. We use synthetic SHADE to pick improved hyperparameter values for the internal suspicion threshold
\tau_{int}
and the maximum number of interventions
K
. We pick
K=10
to balance the effect observed in Section
5
, where increasing
K
leads to reduced safety score, with the fact that larger
K
leads to a narrower minimum in Figure
5
which we expect to be less able to accurately target (due to unavoidable differences between our synthetic model and the original SHADE-Arena). Given
K=10
, we then pick
\tau_{\textup{int}}=4.0
to minimize the simulated safety score. We also set
N=64
since Section
8
suggests safety score is monotonically decreasing in
N
.

With our final scaffold, the attack policy using Claude 3.7 Sonnet achieves a safety score of 0.41 (see Figure
1
), compared to a baseline of 0.87 with no scaffold. To demonstrate that each component of our scaffold is contributing to this improvement, we also run several ablations:

(i)

Reduced calibration:
We replace Claude 3.7 as the internal monitor simulation by GPT-4o. GPT-4o is worse calibrated than Claude 3.7 at predicting
\mathcal{T}
(AUROC of 0.69 compared to 0.82).

(ii)

Decreased Subtlety:
We run with
N=2
instead of
N=64
. This will increase the mean suspicion of attack policy turns.

(iii)

Increased internal suspicion theshold:
We run with
\tau_{\textup{int}}=40
instead of
\tau_{\textup{int}}=4.0
. This makes the attack policy more aggressive, harming attack selection.

We plot the results in Figure 1, aggregated over five environments rather than nine because our full attack policy scaffold is substantially more compute-intensive.

We find that each of these ablations produces a higher safety score than our combined scaffold – reduced calibration, increased suspicion and increased internal suspicion threshold (aggression) lead to increases in safety score of 0.18, 0.16 and 0.09 respectively – showing that our interventions stack.

10
Discussion

We decomposed the capabilities required for a strong attack policy into five key skills: suspicion modeling, attack selection, planning, execution and subtlety. We then provided an attack policy scaffold – the internal monitor simulation – within which we could separately optimize each of these capabilities and test the downstream impact on safety score. Some of these skills we could optimize directly, while others we optimized using the help of a synthetic model for SHADE-Arena. This model allowed us to optimize hyperparameters in the internal monitor simulation protocol much more effectively. We then showed that these lessons transfered back to the original SHADE-Arena, and stacked to produce a strong overall attack policy.

As AI deployments become more sophisticated, we anticipate that the control evaluations required to estimate their risk will also become increasingly complex. We therefore believe it is very likely that frontier control evaluations will continue to operate in the highly data-limited regime, where our ability to collect appropriate statistics and optimize the parameters of our attack policies and safety protocols is constrained by the compute requirements of collecting large numbers of long agentic transcripts.

Therefore, practical methods for approximate optimization of attack policy scaffolds will continue to be highly relevant for future control evaluations, even as models become smarter and compute costs decrease. In the future, we look forward to additional work expanding on the methods developed here, perhaps by iterating on more elaborate attack policy scaffolds, or against more sophisticated blue team protocols, or by developing synthetic models which more closely capture the dynamics of the relevant control evaluation.

D.1
Details on Synthetic Simulation of False Positive Rate and False Negative Rate

We induce a particular false negative rate
fnr
in our synthetic attack protocol as follows:

On each trajectory turn, we sample a suspicion score
s\sim Q_{x}
. If
s>\tau_{int}
, then with probability
fnr
, we sample a coefficient
\alpha
uniformly from
[0.7,0.9]
, and we set the internal suspicion score for the turn to
\alpha*\tau_{int}
. We set the external suspicion score for the turn to
s
.

We use a similar procedure for inducing a particular false positive rate
fpr
:

On each trajectory turn, we sample a suspicion score
s\sim Q_{x}
. If
s<\tau_{int}
, then with probability
fpr
, we sample a coefficient
\beta
uniformly from
[1.1,1.5]
, ad we set the internal suspicion score for the turn to
\beta*\tau_{int}
. We set the external suspicion score for the turn to
s
.

This allows us to approximately but not perfectly induce a particular false negative and false positive rate in our synthetic
internal monitor simulation
attack protocol.
