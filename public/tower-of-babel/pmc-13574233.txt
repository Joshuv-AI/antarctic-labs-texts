How to manage medullary thyroid carcinoma: current treatment strategies
Authors: Katerina Saltiki, Olga Karapanou, Marina Michalaki, Grigoris Effraimidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a rare thyroid malignancy, representing approximately 2–5% of all thyroid cancers. It develops from the parafollicular C cells of the thyroid gland. Calcitonin, produced by parafollicular C cells, is a highly reliable biomarker for both diagnosis and disease monitoring. MTC commonly spreads to cervical lymph nodes and can also lead to distant metastases. Approximately 25% of MTC cases are hereditary, occurring as part of multiple endocrine neoplasia syndromes. Inherited forms are driven by germline mutations in the RET proto-oncogene. Genetic testing enables identification of RET carriers, whilst the risk associated with specific RET variants helps determine the optimal timing for prophylactic or therapeutic surgery. Surgical management remains the cornerstone treatment for MTC. Nevertheless, recurrent or metastatic disease continues to pose therapeutic challenges, requiring individualized strategies such as locoregional interventions, radiotherapy and systemic therapies. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors, along with selective RET inhibitors, represent the main therapeutic strategies for patients whose tumours show rapid progression. This review summarizes current surgical, locoregional and systemic treatment strategies for sporadic and hereditary MTC, focusing on the role of multi-kinase and selective RET inhibitors.

Introduction

Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a rare thyroid malignancy arising from the parafollicular C cells of the thyroid. It accounts for 2–5% of thyroid cancers. Each year, approximately 1200 cases of MTC are diagnosed in the USA; 1 however, its incidence may be greater than previously estimated. In approximately 0.5–1.3% of multi-nodular goitres, undetected micro-MTCs are found largely due to routine calcitonin screening in patients with nodular thyroid disease and the widespread use of high-resolution ultrasound. 2–5

MTC cells primarily secrete calcitonin, which is the most sensitive biochemical marker for the diagnosis and post-operative follow-up of MTC. 6 In addition to calcitonin, C cells release several other peptides, including chromogranin, neurotensin, somatostatin and histamine. Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) is also a useful marker of tumour burden. 7 Furthermore, three key histopathological parameters reflecting tumour proliferative activity and tumour aggressiveness are incorporated into the International Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma Grading System (IMTCGS): the Ki-67 proliferation index, the mitotic activity and the presence of necrosis. 8–10 Additionally, the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Tumour-Node-Metastasis (TNM) staging system (8th edition) classifies MTC into stages I–IV according to the extent of the primary tumour (T), regional lymph node involvement (N) and the presence of distant metastases (M).

Approximately 25% of cases have a hereditary origin, most commonly associated with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 syndromes (MEN2A and MEN2B). These familial forms are caused by germline gain-of-function mutations in the RET proto-oncogene. 6,11 Genetic screening is recommended for all individuals diagnosed with MTC as it is essential for uncovering previously unrecognized hereditary cases and for detecting possible RET carriers within MEN2 families. 12 Somatic mutations can also be identified in sporadic MTCs. According to the COSMIC database, somatic RET mutations are identified in 45–70% of tumours, whilst RAS gene mutations (HRAS, KRAS, NRAS) occur in 16–20%. 13

The primary therapeutic approach to MTC is thyroidectomy, with or without lymph node dissection, depending on disease extent. In asymptomatic RET mutation carriers, the timing of prophylactic thyroidectomy is determined by the aggressiveness associated with the specific affected RET codon. 6 The 2015 guidelines also specify that, in the case of a moderate-risk RET mutation, the possibility of treating patients with surgery should be based on neck ultrasound and calcitonin values, both basal and stimulated; 6 this is also specified in several papers. 14–16 Achieving complete tumour removal during the initial operation is crucial for cure. Key prognostic factors include post-operative calcitonin levels, stage at diagnosis, tumour size and lymph node involvement. 17–20 Most patients with undetectable post-operative calcitonin experience no recurrence and have excellent long-term outcomes. The clinical course varies in cases where post-operative calcitonin remains elevated. Calcitonin and CEA doubling times provide valuable predictive information regarding disease progression and survival. Furthermore, RET carriers should undergo assessment for additional manifestations of the syndrome, including pheochromocytoma and primary hyperparathyroidism.

Locoregional interventions are beneficial for controlling metastatic lesions, serving either as adjuvant or palliative treatment depending on the site and the number of metastases. For advanced or metastatic MTC, two tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) – vandetanib and cabozantinib – have received approval and offer clinical response and disease stabilization. 21,22 More recently, highly selective RET inhibitors have emerged as effective treatment options for patients with RET-mutated MTC and are already being implemented in clinical practice. 23,24 For patients with drug resistance and advanced progressive disease, additional novel therapeutic agents are currently under investigation. This review focuses on current treatment strategies for MTC, from surgery and genetic counselling to newly approved multi-kinase and selective RET inhibitors.

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

Sporadic MTC most commonly presents as a palpable thyroid nodule or cervical lymph node. 17 At diagnosis, cervical lymph-node metastases (involving the central compartment with or without the lateral compartments) are present in 30–60% of patients; upper mediastinal involvement, seen in locally invasive tumours, is associated with a worse prognosis. 25,26 Approximately 10% of patients present with distant metastases at the time of diagnosis. Metastases most commonly involve the liver, lungs and bones and less frequently the brain and skin. 27

Systemic symptoms occur in approximately 10% of patients and primarily include diarrhoea and flushing (due to secretion of calcitonin, calcitonin-related peptide or other bioactive substances). More rarely (0.7%), Cushing’s syndrome due to ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) secretion has been reported. 28 Ultrasound imaging reveals features suspicious for malignancy; however, these findings are non-specific for MTC. MTC can be identified in less than 50% of cases when the diagnosis is based on the main risk stratification systems. Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) does not detect MTC in all cases; it has been reported that, in 31–54% of patients, FNA was not able to diagnose MTC. 29–31 Moreover, in some of these cases, a recommendation to perform FNA is absent, potentially resulting in a missed or delayed diagnosis. 32,33 Thus, calcitonin levels remain a more reliable parameter for the earlier diagnosis and treatment of patients with MTC. 29

Calcitonin is the main marker for MTC diagnosis and early management. 2,3 However, compared with guidelines by the European Thyroid Association (ETA), those of the American Thyroid Association (ATA) are less supportive of routine calcitonin screening due to concerns about cost-effectiveness, limited specificity with false-positive results mainly related to C-cell hyperplasia and non-thyroidal causes of hypercalcitoninaemia, as well as the potential for overdiagnosis of indolent micro-MTCs 6,34. Thus, the ETA recommends one-time calcitonin testing in all cases of nodular thyroid disease, whereas the ATA is less supportive of routine universal screening. 6,34,35 For accurate diagnosis of MTC, both the ATA 6 and European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) 36,37 guidelines recommend FNA biopsy with calcitonin washout measurement. Micro-MTC and C cell hyperplasia cannot always be reliably distinguished. 38 Calcitonin-negative or low-calcitonin MTCs are rare and are thought to result from poor tumour differentiation, often correlating with more aggressive disease. 39–41 It should be noted that calcitonin levels may also be elevated in other malignancies, such as small cell lung carcinoma and carcinoid tumours, as well as in non-malignant conditions such as hypercalcaemia, autoimmune thyroid disease, pregnancy and renal failure.6 However, in these situations, calcitonin levels typically do not show an increase following calcium or pentagastrin stimulation. 42 Inconsistencies have been observed in the threshold values for stimulated calcitonin levels. 43

Before surgery, especially in suspected familial cases, plasma or urinary catecholamines and metanephrines should be assessed, with adrenal imaging performed if results are positive. If a pheochromocytoma is diagnosed, it must be removed prior to MTC surgery. Chromogranin A is usually elevated only with high tumour burden. When elevated alongside moderately increased calcitonin, it indicates pheochromocytoma or another neuroendocrine tumour. 44

Histopathological evaluation plays a pivotal role in prognostic assessment and risk stratification of MTC. In addition to the TNM staging system (AJCC, 8th edition), the IMTCGS (introduced and validated in a multicentre cohort in 2022), 45 provides an independent histopathological grading framework. This two-tier grading system classifies MTC as high grade when at least one of the following adverse histopathological features is identified: a mitotic index ≥5 per 2 mm2, a Ki-67 proliferation index ≥5%, or tumour necrosis. Tumours lacking all three features are classified as low-grade tumours. The IMTCGS has been proposed as an independent prognostic marker for predicting disease-specific survival and as a marker for distant metastasis-free survival and locoregional recurrence-free survival. 46 Nevertheless, its clinical value is important mainly for patients with a relatively lower risk of recurrence and disease-specific mortality whilst it aids in the identification of the small subset of cases that exhibit more aggressive clinical behaviour. 46 Furthermore, a prognostic nomogram incorporating several clinical and histopathological factors has been validated and has demonstrated enhanced predictive performance, suggesting a potential role in guiding clinical decision making and patient management. 47 Multifocality is not associated with differences concerning the tumour grade according to a retrospective cohort analysis. 48

Familial disease and genetics

Approximately 25–30% of MTC cases are inherited and occur in the context of MEN2A, MEN2B or familial MTC.34 In MEN2A, nearly all patients develop MTC, with pheochromocytoma occurring in ~50% and primary hyperparathyroidism in 25–30%.34 Rare manifestations include cutaneous lichen amyloidosis and Hirschsprung disease. MEN2B is a rare, more aggressive syndrome with early-onset MTC, often in infancy. Around half of patients with MEN2B develop pheochromocytoma, and most show a distinct phenotype characterized by mucosal neuromas, ocular abnormalities, skeletal deformities and widespread ganglioneuromatosis of the aerodigestive tract. 6

The gene responsible for hereditary MTC is the RET proto-oncogene, located on chromosome 10q11.2. The RET gene spans 21 exons and encodes a tyrosine kinase transmembrane receptor; it was first described by Takahashi et al. in 1985 (Figure 1). 49 The majority of known mutations occur in exons 5, 8, 10, 11 and 13–16 and their identification is important for the design of genetic testing panels for hereditary MTC in affected patients. 34,50 The majority of typical MEN2A cases carry mutations in exon 10 (codons 609, 611, 618 and 620) and exon 11 (codons 630 and 634), 51 whilst, in >95% of MEN2B cases, the mutation is localized in exon 16, codon 918 and, rarely, in exon 15, codon 883. 6 New RET mutations continue to be discovered and, to date, more than 150 germline RET variants have been reported and are available in genetic databases. Somatic RET mutations are detected in approximately 60% of sporadic MTC cases and have been associated with higher proliferation rates and worse outcomes. 52,53

The 2012 ETA guidelines recommend RET gene analysis in all patients with either sporadic or familial medullary thyroid carcinoma. 34 The wide application of genetic testing has resulted in the recognition of previously undiagnosed hereditary disease in cases amongst those initially considered as sporadic. 50 Moreover, identification of a RET mutation within a family enables cascade genetic screening of relatives, facilitating early diagnosis. Thus, a timely prophylactic thyroidectomy in asymptomatic gene carriers has significantly improved the disease outcome. 54 The mutation location, patient age and basal calcitonin levels are key determinants in deciding the appropriate timing for prophylactic thyroidectomy. 55 Recently, an Italian study showed that active surveillance in RET carriers with moderate or high-risk mutations is safe and can delay thyroid surgery until adolescence or adulthood. 56 For MEN2B cases where MTC is highly aggressive and develops in the first year of life, early detection and surgical treatment improve survival. 57

A genotype–phenotype correlation has been demonstrated in MEN2 syndromes. 34,58 The ATA guidelines classify RET mutations into three risk categories based on MTC aggressiveness: highest (ATA-HST), high (ATA-H) and moderate (ATA-MOD) (Table 1). 6 ATA-HST includes patients with MEN2B (mutation in RET codon M918T), ATA-H includes patients with MEN2A with a C634 RET mutation and ATA-MOD includes the remaining patients with familial MTC. 6 The ATA guideline recommendations for prophylactic thyroidectomy for RET mutation carriers are shown in Table 1. Age-appropriate prophylactic thyroidectomy, guided by mutation risk classification and calcitonin levels, improves disease-free survival. 59

Treatment

Surgical intervention constitutes the cornerstone of MTC management. 6,36 A total thyroidectomy is indicated in both hereditary and sporadic forms of the disease, given the bilateral distribution of parafollicular C cells and the potential for multifocal tumour development, even in non-familial cases. In selected sporadic, non-invasive, non-metastatic, unifocal MTC cases without desmoplasia or nodal involvement, hemithyroidectomy with or without ipsilateral central neck dissection may be a curative and risk-reducing option. 60,61 Careful patient selection is the key factor in decision making for hemithyroidectomy. A recent retrospective European study reported the feasibility of lobectomy as first-line therapy for patients with RET-negative MTC using pre-operative calcitonin levels and thyroid ultrasound features, as these tools have a good accuracy in detecting the affected lobe and support the possibility for a more conservative surgery. 62 According to these findings, a prospective study was conducted in patients with cN0 sporadic apparently unifocal MTC (n=54) who underwent total thyroidectomy and central lymph node dissection; only one patient had bilateral disease and two patients had both ipsilateral and contralateral central lymph nodes; however, central ipsilateral lymph node involvement was present in 12 patients (22.2% of cases). 63 These data support previous findings of a study conducted in China where patients with small (≤2 cm) unilateral sporadic MTC who underwent hemithyroidectomy showed comparable structural and biochemical outcomes with patients undergoing total thyroidectomy even in cases with high-risk factors (including high pre-operative calcitonin levels, multifocal disease, lymph node metastases, somatic RET M918T mutation and desmoplasia). Thus, for small unilateral sporadic MTCs, hemithyroidectomy may be considered an alternative treatment that does not compromise prognosis whilst avoiding additional complications associated with total thyroidectomy. 64 Further prospective randomized clinical trials are needed to assess lobectomy as a potential option for achieving biochemical cure in patients with unilateral disease. 65,66

Pre-operative serum calcitonin and CEA play a critical role in operative planning as they correlate strongly with tumour burden and regional lymph node invasion, determining the appropriate extent of initial surgery. 6,67,68 According to the ATA guidelines, the extent of surgery should be determined based on comprehensive risk stratification according to ultrasonographic findings, pre-operative serum calcitonin levels, and, in familial cases, the specific RET mutation according to the ATA risk classification for familial cases. 6 Central neck dissection at initial surgery is advised even when ultrasound shows no nodal disease, since microscopic lymph node involvement is often present at diagnosis. 6,69 Pre-operative confirmation of cervical lymph node involvement warrants dissection of both the central and lateral neck compartments. 26 In patients with pre-operative calcitonin >200 pg/mL and ipsilateral nodal disease, contralateral neck dissection can be considered but is rarely curative, limiting its overall utility. 6,70 In locally advanced disease, tumour debulking is recommended; however, complete resection is often not achievable. Thoracic surgery may be required for upper mediastinal nodal involvement. 6

In individuals harbouring germline RET mutations, clinical outcomes are generally more favourable, most likely reflecting earlier detection and timely surgical intervention. When basal serum calcitonin concentrations remain within the reference range – excluding patients with MEN2B – the indication for prophylactic lymph node dissection remains controversial. Prior to undertaking thyroidectomy, biochemical and/or radiological evaluation must be performed to rule out the presence of pheochromocytoma. If a unilateral adrenal tumour is identified, adrenalectomy is warranted. The risk of developing a metachronous contralateral pheochromocytoma within a decade is estimated to range between 60% and 80%. 6,34 Consequently, adrenal cortex-preserving surgical approaches have been proposed in selected centres to mitigate the risk of long-term adrenal insufficiency. 71 When primary hyperparathyroidism occurs, surgery is guided by findings and involves excision of a single adenoma or 3.5 parathyroid glands. 72 The algorithm for MTC evaluation and management is shown in Figure 2.

Early diagnosis and optimized surgical management are essential for improving survival and achieving cure in patients with MTC. Prognosis is influenced by tumour size, nodal invasion, pre-operative and post-operative calcitonin levels, age, disease stage, the AJCC TNM system and the MTC Grading System (IMTCGS). 9 Optimal outcomes and the highest 10-year survival are achieved after successful initial surgery. 6,65

Persistent disease occurs in up to 50% of cases. Five-year survival is ~80–97%, and 10-year survival, depending on stage, has been reported at ~75% in older studies. 26,73–75 Specifically, the 10-year survival rate is ~95% for stage I, 93% for stage II, 71% for stage III and 20–40% for stage IV or metastatic disease. 6,76 Sporadic MTC has a poorer prognosis than familial forms, except for MEN2B; 77,78 although, this was not always observed. 79 Adherence to ATA guidelines reduces local reoperations and improves cure rates. 18

Post-operative calcitonin and CEA are the most sensitive biomarkers for detecting persistent or progressive disease.36,44,73,80–82 They should be measured 2–3 months postoperatively. Undetectable post-operative calcitonin is associated with a recurrence risk of 0–5%. 73,74,83 Disease recurrence is more frequent in patients with post-operative calcitonin levels within the normal range than in those with undetectable calcitonin (approximately 25% versus 3%). 84 Patients with elevated post-operative basal calcitonin levels or advanced-stage disease require closer follow-up, 85 although a substantial proportion of cases with nodal metastases and/or elevated post-operative calcitonin achieve long survival. 81

In patients with persistent disease and post-operative calcitonin >100–200 pg/mL, localization of structural disease is recommended to guide management. 86 Tumour progression should be confirmed using imaging modalities consistent with those used at baseline. Disease progression, as well as the emergence of metastatic lesions, should be assessed according to RECIST criteria, particularly in patients receiving systemic therapy. 87–89 The 2020 ESMO guidelines recommend 18F-DOPA PET/CT at initial diagnosis in patients with serum calcitonin levels >500 pg/mL or when distant metastases are suspected, as well as in patients with persistent or recurrent MTC and calcitonin levels >150 pg/mL. 90 FDG-PET/CT is primarily reserved for aggressive disease, whereas 68Ga-somatostatin analogue PET/CT has limited use. 91

The gold-standard markers of disease progression are calcitonin and CEA doubling times. A calcitonin doubling time <2 years correlates with structural progression. 80,92 Fluctuations of 20–30% in calcitonin levels between measurements can occur without indicating progression. Increasing CEA despite falling post-operative calcitonin suggests dedifferentiation and more aggressive disease. 93 Additional biomarkers, including procalcitonin, carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) and, potentially, microRNAs have demonstrated clinical significance in the evaluation of advanced or dedifferentiated MTC. 94–96 At follow-up, calcitonin levels >400–500 pg/mL are associated with a high likelihood of distant metastases, mainly involving the lungs, liver and bones. When present, 10-year survival is about 20–40%.6

Management of metastatic and/or recurrent disease

For patients presenting with locoregional disease recurrence, surgical management remains the principal therapeutic approach. Pre-operative evaluation should comprehensively assess both the anatomical extent of recurrent tumour involvement and the potential presence of distant metastatic spread, as these factors critically influence therapeutic decision-making. When the recurrent disease is deemed technically amenable to complete resection, pre-operative intervention may achieve durable structural disease control and contribute to sustained local remission. 97 Whether primary tumour resection benefits patients with metastatic MTC is still debated. A study of 186 metastatic cases found that patients with one or two metastases who underwent surgery had better overall and cancer-specific survival. 98

The use of post-operative external beam radiotherapy is controversial. 99 In cases of locoregional disease, it has been associated with a higher mortality. 100 Local treatment modalities, such as radiofrequency ablation, radioembolization or microwave ablation, may be applied to lymph node, lung, bone and liver metastases. 6,101–103 For hepatic metastatic involvement, locoregional ablative strategies may be applied with the intent of achieving a cytoreductive effect, mitigating tumour-related symptomatology and restraining the progression of dominant or high-volume lesions. For osseous metastatic disease, external beam radiotherapy constitutes an effective palliative modality, conferring substantial analgesic benefit, decreasing the likelihood of pathological fracture and preserving functional capacity. In the context of intracranial metastatic spread, surgical resection and/or stereotactic radiosurgical techniques should be evaluated on an individualized basis, contingent upon clinical status and disease burden. Localized therapeutic approaches are preferably undertaken prior to the commencement of systemic therapy. In slowly progressive disease, such interventions may facilitate transient disease stabilization and delay the initiation of systemic treatment. In rare cases of advanced MTC complicated by ectopic ACTH secretion resulting in Cushing syndrome, the overall prognosis is generally poor 6,104. Management of severe hypercortisolism may necessitate pharmacological inhibition of adrenal steroidogenesis or, in carefully selected patients, bilateral adrenalectomy to achieve adequate biochemical and clinical control. 104

Optimal management also necessitates comprehensive symptomatic control. Antidiarrhoeal agents, most commonly loperamide, are routinely administered, whereas analgesic regimens are tailored to address pain attributable to metastatic involvement. Although somatostatin analogues may partially ameliorate secretory diarrhoea, they do not exert a disease-modifying effect in metastatic MTC cases.6 Conventional chemotherapy and radioiodine are ineffective in MTC. Therefore, patients with advanced, symptomatic or rapidly progressive metastatic disease should be considered for targeted therapy. 87,105

A considerable subset of individuals with disseminated metastatic disease demonstrates extended intervals of minimal or absent radiographic progression. These patients should be monitored every 6 months, with assessment of calcitonin and CEA doubling times at each follow-up visit. For the decision for initiation of systemic therapy, the primary factors to take into consideration are tumour burden and the rate of disease progression. Progression is defined according to RECIST criteria as either a ≥20% increase in the sum of the longest diameters of target lesions or the emergence of one or more new lesions within a 12-month period.6 Systemic therapy is not indicated in patients exhibiting isolated biochemical progression in the absence of structural disease. Furthermore, it should not be administered to asymptomatic individuals with low-volume metastatic disease, a stable clinical course or a minimal risk of developing local complications. 6,106 Genetic analysis of the primary tumour mutational profile is critical for guiding therapeutic decision making.

Recent advances in MTC molecular pathways and the RET kinase domain structure have significantly enhanced our understanding of disease oncogenesis (Figure 1). 107,108 MTCs may harbour a range of driver mutations, predominantly involving RET and RAS genes. Somatic RET mutations occur in 40–70% of sporadic tumours, most commonly the RET M918T variant (approximately 85%). 109 Recent evidence indicates that somatic RET mutations correlate with features of more aggressive primary tumours and with shorter distant metastasis-free survival.6,52 In approximately 70% of cases lacking RET mutations, activating mutations in RAS genes (predominantly HRAS and KRAS) are detected in roughly 20% of tumours. 6,110,111 Finally, TP53 mutations are independently associated with reduced overall survival and cancer-specific survival. 112 RET signalling plays a crucial role in oncogenic processes, including tumour progression, metastases, resistance, apoptotic mechanisms and angiogenesis. 113 These data underscore the significance of RET inhibition as a therapeutic target 114 mainly in cases harbouring either germline or somatic RET mutations. 115,116 Furthermore, RAS pathway activation represents an alternative oncogenic driver, particularly in sporadic MTCs classified as intermediate risk. 111,114,117

Molecular analysis of the primary tumour for somatic RET mutations is not currently considered a standard routine clinical practice in all sporadic MTC cases. Somatic RET alterations are identified in approximately 60% of primary tumours, whereas their prevalence increases to 75–80% in metastatic disease. Specific somatic RET variants, particularly the highly prevalent M918T RET mutation in exon 16, have been associated with a more aggressive clinical phenotype, an increased likelihood of lymph node invasion, distant metastases and poorer overall prognosis, although not always independently of tumour grade and disease stage. 52,112 The optimal timing and clinical indications for somatic RET testing remain a matter of ongoing debate. In particular, the utility of testing at the time of initial diagnosis versus in advanced-stage disease, when patients may be candidates for TKI therapy, continues to be evaluated. 118,119 Given the emergence of selective RET inhibitors, identification of RET somatic alterations has become increasingly important for therapeutic stratification and individualized treatment planning. Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and ESMO guidelines recommend somatic RET testing in all patients with recurrent, unresectable, locally advanced or metastatic sporadic MTC who are candidates for systemic therapy as well as in those with a high structural tumour burden. Nevertheless, implementation of these recommendations is influenced by the availability and accessibility of molecular diagnostics across different healthcare systems, while cost-effectiveness should be considered. Next-generation sequencing represents the preferred methodology for detecting somatic RET alterations. Molecular analysis can be performed using fresh-frozen specimens, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumour tissue, or circulating tumour DNA obtained through liquid biopsy approaches, a minimally invasive alternative for genomic profiling and longitudinal disease monitoring. 119

The development of targeted therapeutic strategies in the treatment of MTC has primarily focused on TKIs. These agents function as multi-kinase inhibitors (MKIs), exerting their antitumour effects through inhibition of aberrantly activated tyrosine kinase receptors or other components of intracellular signalling cascades that drive tumorigenesis and angiogenesis. The main molecular targets of TKIs are RET kinase and VEGFR (which are overexpressed in MTC cells and tumour vessels) together with other molecules associated with oncogenic and angiogenetic processes (e.g. EGFR, MET/HGF, RAS, mTOR, FGFR, PDGFR). 120,121 Patients with symptomatic or rapidly progressive metastatic disease are candidates for targeted therapy; the aim of therapy is disease stabilization and potentially the improvement of overall survival. 6,106,122,123 It should also be mentioned that TKIs exert mainly cytostatic effects and are less cytotoxic. 124

Two TKIs, vandetanib and cabozantinib, have received approval from both the FDA and EMA for the treatment of progressive metastatic MTC. Vandetanib targets VEGFR2, VEGFR3, RET and EGFR. In the pivotal phase III ZETA trial, vandetanib demonstrated a significant improvement in progression-free survival (PFS) compared with placebo (30.5 versus 19.3 months) and achieved higher objective radiographic response rates (45% versus 13%). These benefits were observed regardless of baseline factors, including RET mutation status, disease progression rate, anatomical distribution or overall disease burden. 125 In the subgroup of patients with symptomatic and progressive disease at baseline (184/331), the efficacy was comparable for PFS (hazard ratio [HR], 0.43; 95% CI, 0.28 to 0.64; p<0.0001); an objective response rate (ORR) of 37% was observed in the treatment arm versus 2% in the placebo arm. The safety profile did not differ. 126 Real-world studies have also confirmed the efficacy of the drug, demonstrating long-term clinical benefit in a substantial proportion of patients (mainly younger ones or cases without important structural progression before treatment initiation). 103,127–131 The EMA requires validation of a RET gene mutation before initiating vandetanib, as data indicates a lack of sufficient clinical benefit in patients without RET alterations.

Cabozantinib represents the second approved TKI. The drug targets multiple kinases, that is, VEGFR2, RET, c-MET, KIT and Tie-2. 132 The efficacy of cabozantinib was demonstrated in the phase III EXAM trial, a randomized controlled study in patients with progressive metastatic MTC. Compared with placebo, cabozantinib significantly improved PFS (11.2 versus 4.0 months) and achieved higher objective radiographic response rates (28% versus 0%). These benefits were observed irrespective of patient age, baseline tumour burden, disease location, progression rate or prior exposure to TKIs. 22 Several retrospective analyses have suggested a less favourable outcome in patients receiving cabozantinib; this observation is possibly explained by its administration in more aggressive MTC cases, frequently as second-line treatment after disease progression on vandetanib. 128 In a randomized trial in progressive metastatic MTC, cabozantinib 140 mg/day (capsules, the approved regimen for MTC) was compared with 60 mg/day (tablets, approved for other solid tumours); the study showed no inferiority in PFS and ORR (33% in both arms). The 60 mg/day arm was associated with fewer adverse events (AEs) as well as with reduced rates of dose reductions (69% versus 81%) and discontinuations due to AEs (23% versus 36%). 133 Both drugs have been associated with stabilization in 30% and partial disease regression in 35% of cases. 22,134 Assessing the impact of TKIs on overall survival in MTC is challenging, given that many patients exhibit a relatively indolent disease course and prolonged life expectancy. Upon meaningful disease progression, TKI treatment should be reassessed, considering alternative targeted therapy, local treatment for oligo-progressive disease or clinical trial enrolment, as appropriate.

TKIs are associated with significant AEs in 30–60% of patients. 6,105. Common, generally manageable toxicities include non-specific systemic symptoms, cutaneous reactions, gastrointestinal disturbances, hypertension and transient elevations in TSH, which can typically be addressed symptomatically. Serious AEs are uncommon, occurring in approximately 2% of patients. The toxicity profiles of vandetanib and cabozantinib differ, with QT interval prolongation representing the major concern for vandetanib, precluding its use in patients with a baseline QTc >450 ms in men and >470 ms in women, whereas cabozantinib is more commonly associated with gastrointestinal fistulas and severe haemorrhagic events. Dose reductions are required in approximately 35% of patients receiving vandetanib and up to 79% of those treated with cabozantinib, whilst permanent treatment discontinuation occurs in 12% and 16% of patients, respectively. 6 Importantly, the presence of RET mutations in both familial and sporadic MTC cases may serve as a predictive biomarker for response to TKI therapy. 135 In general, MKIs exhibit variable affinity for different receptor targets, a feature that may underlie their off-target effects and associated AEs. In addition, in a subset of MTC cases, their antiangiogenic activity may contribute to clinical efficacy independently of RET mutational status. 124,136

Two novel selective RET inhibitors, selpercatinib and pralsetinib, which target mainly RET protein, received FDA and EMA approval for the treatment of advanced metastatic RET-mutant MTCs (Figure 1). 108,137 Selpercatinib (LOXO-292) is a highly selective, ATP-competitive small-molecule RET inhibitor that potently targets the RET receptor, whilst demonstrating minimal activity against other kinases, including weak inhibition of VEGFR2. 136 The drug exhibits high selectivity for RET alterations, including point mutations, gene fusions and the V804M mutation, which has been implicated in resistance to other TKIs. 138 Preliminary findings from the LIBRETTO-531 phase III, randomized clinical trial (NCT04211337), initially reported in 2020, 139 have recently been published. Patients with RET-mutant MTC with disease progression were enrolled comparing selpercatinib as first-line therapy with physician’s choice of cabozantinib or vandetanib (control group). At 12 months, PFS was 86.8% in the selpercatinib group and 65.7% in the control group, and the overall response was 69.4% and 38.8%, respectively. Dose reductions due to AEs occurred in 38.9% of patients receiving selpercatinib versus 77.3% in the control group, whilst treatment discontinuation rates were 4.7% and 26.8%, respectively. 23 Real-world data demonstrated high efficacy and favourable tolerability, supporting the use of the drug as the preferred first-line targeted therapy for RET-driven MTC in routine clinical practice. 140

Another highly selective RET inhibitor, pralsetinib (BLU-667), has also shown remarkable efficacy in RET-mutant tumours, including cases with drug resistance to other TKIs. 136 The phase I/II ARROW (an open-label multi-centre trial) demonstrated robust responses in RET-mutant MTC, including cases harbouring the V804M/L gatekeeper mutation, with an ORR of 65% in previously treated patients and 75% in TKI-naive patients. At 18 months, PFS was 71% and 85% and duration of response was 90% and 86%, respectively. The safety profile was consistent with that observed for selpercatinib. 141 Similar results have been reported in an updated analysis of the ARROW trial 142 as well as in real-life studies. 143 At present, the agent is no longer approved for the treatment of RET-mutant MTC in Europe and the USA. To date, the choice of first-line treatment in progressive metastatic MTC depends on RET-mutation status. In RET-mutated disease, selpercatinib is used as first-line treatment; in RET-wild type tumours, vandetanib or cabozantinib may be offered as first-line treatment. 144

Other MKIs are currently being evaluated in ongoing studies. 145 Pazopanib (targeting c-KIT, FGFR, PDGFR and VEGFR), lenvatinib (a potent inhibitor of FGFR) and anlotinib (targeting VEGFR, FGFR, PDGFR, c-KIT, RET) have demonstrated partial responses in phase II clinical trials. 146–152 In a small real-world cohort, lenvatinib demonstrated promising efficacy as salvage therapy for advanced progressive metastatic MTC. Thus, the drug may represent a treatment option for patients with limited therapeutic alternatives, particularly those with RET-negative disease and no access to selective RET-targeted therapies. 152 Although the majority of patients with MTC initially respond to MKIs, disease progression may occur over time. 123,153 This may occur due to pre-existing or acquired resistance to a specific TKI. In addition, the different conformational structure of TKIs as well as the different way that TKIs and selective RET inhibitors bond to RET may influence treatment response. 154

Treatment with MKIs is frequently associated with AEs, although most are mild to moderate and can be effectively managed with supportive measures. Selpercatinib has a more favourable toxicity profile than other MKIs. However, ‘unconventional’ emerging AEs have been recently reported 155–159. Chylous effusions have been described as a novel treatment-emergent AE of selpercatinib in 7% of cases. 156,158 Mild cases may be managed with surveillance, whereas symptomatic cases generally are improved after fluid drainage and dose reduction, supporting a causal association. 158 Real-world data suggest that gastrointestinal AEs and abdominal pain in patients under selpercatinib may be under-recognized. They are likely related to bowel-wall oedema (including late-onset gastric and small-bowel oedema) although, for most cases, this AE is self-limiting and manageable with dose modification. 159 Selpercatinib may induce hypothyroidism in levothyroxine-treated athyreotic patients through impaired D2-mediated T3 production, occasionally requiring liothyronine supplementation. 160 A case report described obliterative bronchiolitis as a rare pulmonary AE associated with selpercatinib. 155 In another real-world study, hypertension and erectile dysfunction were the predominant AEs. Grade ≥3 toxicities occurred in 37.5% of patients, with treatment modification and discontinuation required in 22.6% and 9.7% of cases, respectively. 140 Erectile dysfunction has also been reported in another study recently. 157

Several mechanisms underlying resistance in RET-driven tumours have been described, including target-dependent mechanisms involving RET alterations and mechanisms mediated by bypass signalling pathways. 153,161 Amongst RET alterations associated with drug resistance, the most frequently observed is the V804M/L gatekeeper mutation, 162 whereas less commonly reported variants include S904F, I788N, solvent-front mutations (G810S/R/C), Y806C/N and the β2-strand substitution V738A. 135,154,163–165 Although selpercatinib and pralsetinib retain efficacy against V804-mediated resistance, they show reduced activity against other RET variants (i.e. V738A, Y806C/N and G810C/S). 154 Furthermore, activation of bypass signalling pathways may also contribute to drug resistance, involving upregulation of downstream oncogenic cascades (i.e. RAS/RAF/MAPK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, AKT2 amplification and molecular alterations such as NRAS, Q61K mutations, MDM2-mediated p53 inhibition and MET or FGFR1 amplification). 162,166–168 The tumour microenvironment 169 and microRNAs 170,171 may be implicated in drug resistance as well.

To overcome the aforementioned limitations, novel therapeutic agents are currently under investigation and may demonstrate improved efficacy. 137 Next-generation selective RET inhibitors are under evaluation in phase I/II clinical trials, aiming to overcome resistance driven by various RET alterations. These include agents such as LOXO260, BOS172738 and TPX-0046 (which are designed to overcome resistance to gatekeeper mutations V804L and V804M and the solvent-front mutation G810R) 137,172,173 as well as APS03118, HM06/TAS0953 and EP0031, which are in early clinical development. 173 For TPX-0046, the clinical trial has been recently terminated because of unfavourable changes in the overall risk–benefit profile. Combination therapies may enhance efficacy by targeting multiple oncogenic signalling pathways. 165 The combination of selpercatinib with the MET–ALK–ROS1 inhibitor crizotinib may present a strategy to overcome acquired resistance to selpercatinib. 174 Additional pathways under study as potential therapeutic targets include NOTCH signalling, 175 delta-like protein 3 (targeted by rovalpituzumab) 176 and an apoptosis-related mechanism (targeted by imipridone). 177 A case report demonstrated promising activity of tarlatamab (a DLL3-targeted bispecific T cell engager therapy used in advanced neuroendocrine malignancies) in RET-altered MTC after progression under selpercatinib, achieving a complete response without grade ≥3 treatment-related AEs. A modified dosing and monitoring strategy may improve its safety while maintaining efficacy, though further studies are required to establish its role in MTC. 178 Finally, RAS inhibitors are currently being investigated in small phase I clinical trials. 114,117,175,179

In addition to TKIs and selective RET inhibitors, alternative therapeutic strategies in advanced MTC include peptide receptor radionuclide therapy, tumour vaccines and immunotherapy. Radiolabelled agents (such as anti-CEA antibodies, DTPA derivatives and 90Y-labelled octreotide analogues) have shown some efficacy but are limited by considerable toxicity. 6,180 Ga-SSA PET/CT or octreotide scan may identify SSTR expression in patients who may be candidates for these treatments. 90,124 In a recent systematic review of 220 patients with metastatic MTC treated with various peptide receptor radionuclide therapy agents (such as 90Y-DOTATOC, 177Lu-DOTA-TATE, 111In), the biochemical response rate and ORR were 37.2% and 10.6%, respectively. 181

Immune reactivation against cancer cells has transformed cancer treatment strategies, yet immunotherapy shows comparatively limited efficacy in MTC. 182,183 The MTC tumour microenvironment is thought to be relatively immunologically inert, whilst expression of immune inhibitory molecules (PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA4, TIM3, LAG3, TIGIT) shows variability amongst reported studies. 185,186 Although immunotherapy has been suggested for some progressive MTC cases, 186 a study of 190 patients found low expression of immune-checkpoint molecules and no effect on prognosis, in line with earlier findings. 185,187–189 Ongoing trials (such as NCT03246958 and NCT03072160) continue to evaluate immunotherapy in MTC. Humanized anti-CEA monoclonal antibodies have been investigated in early-phase clinical trials. 190,191

Conclusions

The prognosis of MTC has improved substantially over recent decades, largely attributable to earlier detection and refinements in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Contributing factors include the more frequent identification of nodular thyroid disease through imaging, advancements in high-resolution cervical ultrasonography, the broad implementation of molecular genetic screening and optimization of surgical techniques. Amongst these advances, the widespread availability of germline genetic testing represents the most important, as it enables systematic screening, risk stratification and timely prophylactic or early therapeutic intervention in hereditary disease. Germline RET analysis should be performed in all individuals diagnosed with MTC, irrespective of family history, including those with apparently sporadic disease, given the possibility of unrecognized hereditary syndromes.

Although the clinical trajectory of MTC is heterogeneous, it is most commonly characterized by relatively indolent progression. In the setting of advanced or metastatic disease, the introduction of targeted systemic therapies has further modified the therapeutic landscape. MKIs, such as vandetanib and cabozantinib, as well as selective RET inhibitors for RET-altered tumours, selpercatinib and pralsetinib, have been approved for the treatment of metastatic MTC. Selective RET inhibition, in particular, has demonstrated clinically meaningful efficacy.

Determining the optimal timing for initiation of systemic targeted therapy should be individualized. Current practice generally reserves treatment for patients with documented structural progression based on RECIST criteria and/or clinically significant symptomatic burden. A comprehensive understanding of MTC tumorigenesis, together with elucidation of the molecular determinants and structural mechanisms underlying acquired drug resistance, is essential to support the rational design of next-generation targeted therapies for MTC and other RET-driven malignancies.
