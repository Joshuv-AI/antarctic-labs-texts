A generalisation of the signal-to-noise ratio using proper scoring rules
Jochen Bröcker, Eviatar Bach

Abstract
A generalised concept of the signal-to-noise ratio (or equivalently the ratio of predictable components, or RPC \operatorname{RPC} ) is provided, based on proper scoring rules. This definition is the natural generalisation of the classical RPC \operatorname{RPC} , yet it allows one to define and analyse the signal-to-noise properties of any type of forecast that is amenable to scoring, thus drastically widening the applicability of these concepts. The methodology is illustrated for ensemble forecasts, scored using the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), and for probability forecasts of a binary event, scored using the logarithmic score. Numerical examples are demonstrated using synthetic data with prescribed signal-to-noise ratios as well as seasonal ensemble hindcasts of the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) index. For the synthetic data, the RPC \operatorname{RPC} statistic as well as the scoring rule–based ones agree regarding which data sets exhibit anomalous signal-to-noise ratios, but exhibit different variance, indicating different statistical properties. For the NAO data, on the other hand, the results among the different statistics are more equivocal.

Introduction

The signal-to-noise “paradox” was first described in
Scaife and Smith 2018
in the context of ensemble forecasts on seasonal timescales.
It refers to a situation in which the correlation between ensemble mean and actual verification is larger than the correlation between ensemble mean and individual ensemble members.
That the ensemble mean is better able to predict the verification than the ensemble members might indeed seem paradoxical, although it has to be kept in mind that in this context the prediction skill is quantified in terms of correlation rather than any form of error.
Following
Bröcker et al. 2023
, we will henceforth refer to such a situation as an
anomalous
signal-to-noise ratio.

The signal-to-noise ratio (
\operatorname{SNR}
) as a statistical diagnostic is not new.
To our knowledge it was first introduced to the climate literature in
Madden 1976
.
The idea of estimating
\operatorname{SNR}
s from ensembles by partitioning the ensemble spread into “signal” and “noise” was considered by various authors and is reviewed in
Rowell 1998
.
Although estimated
\operatorname{SNR}
s can indeed be used to diagnose normal or anomalous signal-to-noise ratios, there are alternative (yet equivalent) diagnostics.
The
ratio of predictable components
(
\operatorname{RPC}
) statistic, for instance, was introduced in
Eade et al. 2014
.

In spite of widespread use of the
\operatorname{RPC}
, a thorough analysis of the statistical properties of the
\operatorname{RPC}
or equivalent diagnostics is still outstanding.
It is not clear, for instance, when the
\operatorname{RPC}
of a given data set (or more precisely an estimator of the
\operatorname{RPC}
) is “significantly” larger than one; a proper testing methodology would have to be able to answer that question.
The present paper aims to contribute to the development of this methodology.

The precise motivation for this paper is threefold.
Firstly, we want to widen the spectrum of possible statistics for diagnosing an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio.
A greater flexibility in choosing test statistics is likely to be beneficial, rather than just relying on the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
. In particular, other test statistics may have better sampling properties or more accurately capture the probabilistic information contained in the forecasts.
Secondly, the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
is only applicable to real-valued verifications.
This is a serious limitation, since there are various other types of verifications such as spatial or categorical ones, which as of yet cannot be analysed in terms of the
\operatorname{RPC}
.
Thirdly, by placing the
\operatorname{RPC}
in a more general information-theoretic framework, we hope to illuminate its theoretical origin and assumptions involved in its use.

We propose to generalise the
\operatorname{RPC}
by considering instead the ratio of two
skill scores
, namely the skill score of the forecast against a verification drawn from the forecast, versus the skill score of the perfectly recalibrated forecast against the actual verifications.
In common with previous authors,
skill score
in this paper refers to the score of a forecast, relative to the score of a reference forecast, typically a climatological forecast.
To score forecasts we use proper scoring rules
(
Bröcker and Smith 2007a
;
Bröcker and Smith 2007a
)
, as these provide a coherent framework for forecast evaluation, see also
Dawid 2007
for the scoring rule–based notion of entropy, and
Takaya et al. 2025
for its use in a climate context.

In Section
2
we introduce the ratio of skill scores (
\operatorname{RSS}
), the proposed generalisation of the
\operatorname{RPC}
, after we briefly recalled the notion of proper scoring rules and their properties in Subsection
2.1
.
The
\operatorname{RPC}
can be applied to any type of forecast that is amenable to scoring.
In Subsection
2.2
we demonstrate that this definition is the natural generalisation of the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
.
In Section
3
we briefly discuss some implications of an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio (these apply likewise to the general and the classical definition.)
Numerical examples are discussed in Section
4
.
We consider ensemble forecasts, scored using the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), and probability forecasts of a binary event, scored using the logarithmic score.
We compare the
\operatorname{RSS}
s with the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
in the context of synthetic data sets as well as data from an operational seasonal forecasting system.
Section
5
concludes.

2
Mathematical framework

2.1
Proper scoring rules

Let
E
be some space which, for the purpose of this paper, can be taken as either the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or simply a finite set
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{K}\}
.
Further, let
\mu,\nu
be probability distributions over
E
which the reader might think of as densities (in the case of
E=\mathbb{R}^{d}
) or probability vectors (of dimension
K
in the case of
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{K}\}
).
A
scoring rule
over
E
is a function
s(\mu,y)
taking as input such a probability distribution
\mu
, and some
y\in E
which is a possible value for the observation or verification.
The value
s(\mu,y)
measures how well
\mu
forecasts
y
. We adopt the convention of
negatively oriented
scores: a smaller score indicates a better forecast.

For any scoring rule
s
and probability distributions
\mu,\nu
we define the associated
expected score

\mathcal{S}(\mu,\nu):=\int s(\mu,y)\nu(y)\;\mathrm{d}y,

(1)

and the
entropy

\mathcal{E}(\mu):=\mathcal{S}(\mu,\mu).

(2)

We furthermore define the
divergence
associated with
s
through the formula

\mathcal{D}(\mu,\nu):=\mathcal{S}(\mu,\nu)-\mathcal{E}(\nu).

(3)

The scoring rule is
proper
if
\mathcal{D}(\mu,\nu)\geq 0
, and
strictly proper
if
\mathcal{D}(\mu,\nu)=0
implies
\mu=\nu
.
Scoring rules have to be proper in order to be interpreted as measures of forecast performance
(
Bröcker and Smith 2007a
;
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
)
.
We note that the scoring rule being strictly proper implies that
\mathcal{D}
is in fact a statistical divergence
(
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
;
Bach et al. 2025
)
, so it can be regarded as a sort of distance or discrepancy between two probability distributions, although
\mathcal{D}
is not symmetric in its arguments.
We henceforth fix a proper scoring rule
s
.

A
probability forecast
is a probability distribution
f
on
E
which at the same time depends on some random variable
\omega
; this dependence reflects the fact that forecasts typically incorporate observations, through data assimilation, that have been collected in the past, and those observations are contaminated by random error.
Those data can be thought of as a random variable
\omega
in a potentially very high-dimensional space.
We could indicate this dependence on
\omega
with a subscript
f_{\omega}
, although we will use this notation sparingly to avoid clutter.

Next we fix a random variable
Y_{\omega}
, with values in
E
, which we call the
verification
.
It might seem impractical that we let
Y_{\omega}
and
f_{\omega}
depend on the same random variable
\omega
, but it turns out to be more convenient to collate all “randomness” in a single variable.
This does not mean that
Y_{\omega}
and
f_{\omega}
contain the same information, since they may depend on different components of
\omega
.
Furthermore,
\omega
will have some distribution, but we have no need to write it explicitly.
Integrals over
\omega
will be denoted by
\mathbb{E}(\ldots)
; that is, as expectation values.

Given a verification
Y_{\omega}
and a probability forecast
f_{\omega}
we define another probability forecast
\pi_{\omega}
, the
recalibration
of
f
, as the conditional distribution of
Y
given
f
, namely

\pi:=\mathcal{L}(Y|f),

(4)

where here
\mathcal{L}
stands for “law”, in this case the conditional law of
Y
given
f
.
If
E
is just a finite set of alternatives, then we are conditioning on a finite dimensional vector with random entries.
Conditioning on a more general random object (if
f
is a distribution) is possible using the common mathematical definition of the conditional expectation
(see, e.g.,
Breiman 1992
, Ch.4)
, although it cannot be done using the familiar Bayes rule.
Note that
\pi
is a probability forecast over
E
as well; it is the forecast of
Y
that optimally uses the information contained in
f
.
We furthermore define
\overline{\pi}:=\mathbb{E}(\pi)
, and likewise
\overline{f}:=\mathbb{E}(f)
; both
\overline{f}
and
\overline{\pi}
are probability distributions over
E
but they do not depend on
\omega
and therefore are not random.

Since
Y
is a random variable (we could write
Y_{\omega}
), then so is
s(f,Y)
, and if
f
or
\pi
are random probability forecasts, then
S(f,\pi),\mathcal{E}(\pi),\mathcal{D}(f,\pi)
, etc. are also random, so it makes sense to consider expectations of these quantities.
Firstly, we have the important identity

\mathbb{E}(s(f,Y))=\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{S}(f,\pi)),

(5)

which follows from

\mathbb{E}(s(f,Y))=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(s(f,Y)|f))=\mathbb{E}\left(\int s(f,y)\pi(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)=\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{S}(f,\pi)).

(6)

We now recall a decomposition of the expected score on the left-hand side of Equation (
5
), which reads as

\mathbb{E}(s(f,Y))=\mathcal{E}(\overline{\pi})-\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{D}(\overline{\pi},\pi))+\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{D}(f,\pi)).

(7)

The three terms on the right-hand side are called, respectively, the
entropy
, the
resolution
, and the
reliability
of
f
(although it must be kept in mind that the definition of these terms refers not only to
f
but also to
Y
).
It follows from the propriety of the scoring rule
s
that all terms involving
\mathcal{D}
are non-negative (although note that the resolution term carries a minus sign).
The reliability term is zero if
f=\pi
, and in general it quantifies the extent to which this fails to be true on average.
The stronger the agreement, the smaller the reliability term, and the better the overall score.
The resolution term is zero if
\overline{\pi}=\pi
, and in general it quantifies how much
\pi
diverges from its average
\overline{\pi}
.
More divergence means a larger resolution term and a
better
overall score.
For a proof of Equation (
7
) and more details regarding the interpretation, see
Bröcker 2009
.

Given a probability forecast
f
, it makes sense to score it against a hypothetical or synthetic verification
Z
which is drawn from the forecast
f
itself; that is,
Z\sim f
.
The resulting score will be referred to as the
self-score
of
f
.
In this case,
f
will be equal to its own recalibration as the relation (
4
) holds in the form
f=\mathcal{L}(Z|f)
; we have defined
Z
so as to render this true.
The score
\mathbb{E}(s(f,Z))
can be decomposed as before, but the reliability term will be zero, so Equation (
7
) holds in the form

\mathbb{E}(s(f,Z))=\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{E}(f))=\mathcal{E}(\overline{f})-\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{D}(\overline{f},f)).

(8)

With these preparations, we make the following definition.

Definition 1

Given a probability forecast
f
, the quantity

\operatorname{SSC}(f):=\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(f)}{\mathcal{E}(\overline{f})},

(9)

is called the self--skill score
1
1

1

We use the word “skill score” as it typically refers to a score
relative
to some benchmark, e.g., climatology.
of
f
.
Given, in addition, a verification
Y
, we say the forecast
f
exhibits an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio (with respect to the verification
Y
and the scoring rule
s
) if
\operatorname{SSC}(\pi)<\operatorname{SSC}(f)
.

Equivalently, we define the
ratio of skill scores
for scoring rule
s
as

\operatorname{RSS}_{s}=\frac{\operatorname{SSC}(f)}{\operatorname{SSC}(\pi)},

(10)

and say that the forecast exhibits an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio with respect to verification
Y
and scoring rule
s
if
\operatorname{RSS}_{s}>1
.

Roughly speaking, an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio means that the “actually achievable” skill score of
f
(through proper recalibration) is better than the “anticipated” skill score of
f
that would materialise if verifications
Y
were drawn from
f
.

2.2
Classical definition of the signal-to-noise ratio

The point of this section is to show that the definition of an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio in Section
2.1
is the natural generalisation of the classical definition as given by various authors.
This classical definition refers to the
ratio of predictable components
or
\operatorname{RPC}
, and the signal-to-noise ratio is defined as anomalous if
\operatorname{RPC}>1
.
We will show that the generalised and the classical definitions agree under the following circumstances (to be made precise below):

1.

For the scoring rule in Definition
1
we use the mean-squared error of the forecast mean.

2.

The forecast means of
f
and of
\pi
are in a linear relationship, or in other words, the mean-square optimal regression of the forecast mean onto the verification is linear.

To properly state our results, we define for any probability distribution
\mu
on
E
the

\begin{split}\text{Mean}\qquad m(\mu)&:=\int x\mu(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\\
\text{Variance}\qquad v(\mu)&:=\int x^{2}\mu(x)\;\mathrm{d}x-m(\mu)^{2}.\end{split}

(11)

We stress that if
\mu
is a probability forecast, then
m(\mu)
and
v(\mu)
will be random variables.

Definition 2

Let
f
be a probability forecast and
Y
a verification.
The classical ratio of predictable components
(
Weisheimer et al. 2019
, see, e.g.,)
is defined as

\operatorname{RPC}=\frac{r(m(f),Y)}{r(m(f),Z)},

(12)

where
r(m(f),Y)
is the Pearson correlation coefficient between
m(f)
and the observation
Y
, while
r(m(f),Z)
is the Pearson correlation coefficient between
m(f)
and a random variable
Z
drawn from the forecast
f
.
The forecast
f
exhibits an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio if
\operatorname{RPC}>1
.

Broadly speaking, the definition states that the forecast
f
exhibits an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio if
m(f)
correlates better with the verification than with draws from itself.
We also note that the denominator in Equation (
12
) can be written as
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(m(f)^{2})/v(\bar{f})}
.

As announced, we will now clarify the connection between two notions of an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio as in Definitions
1
and
2
.

Theorem 1

Let
f
be a probability forecast and
Y
a verification, and let
\pi
be the recalibration of
f
with respect to
Y
.
Suppose that

1.

\mathbb{E}Y=m(\overline{f})=0
,

2.

m(\pi)=a+b\,m(f)
, with
a,b
determined so as to minimise
\mathbb{E}((Y-m(\pi))^{2})
.

Then
\operatorname{RPC}\geq 1
if and only if
\operatorname{RSS}_{\sigma}(\pi)\geq 1
, where
\operatorname{RSS}_{\sigma}
is understood with respect to the specific scoring rule
\sigma(f,y):=(y-m(f))^{2}
.
In particular the two notions of an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio (from Defs.
1
resp
2
) agree in this case.

A few remarks are in order:

1.

Due to the definition of
\pi
we have
m(\pi)=\mathbb{E}(Y|f)
, so Assumption 2 in Theorem
1
amounts to saying that
\mathbb{E}(Y|f)
, which will generally be a nonlinear function of
f
, is actually a linear function of
m(f)
.

2.

The scoring rule
\sigma(f,y)
is indeed proper but not strictly proper.

3.

In “ensemble forecast” terms,
m(f)
is the ensemble mean,
v(f)
the ensemble spread, and
\mathbb{E}(m(f)^{2})
the variability of the ensemble mean, sometimes referred to as the “signal”.

4.

Although both
\operatorname{RPC}=1
and
\operatorname{RSS}_{s}=1
have the interpretation of a normal SNR, their magnitudes cannot be directly compared for an anomalous SNR, not even for the specific score
\sigma
; in general
\operatorname{RSS}_{\sigma}
will not be numerically equal to the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
.

We will now sketch the proof of Theorem
1
.
Some details will be given in the Appendix.
First we note that

r(m(f),Y)=r(m(\pi),Y),

(13)

simply because
m(\pi)
is a linear function of
m(f)
due to Assumptions 1, 2 in the theorem.
Next it follows from the definition of
m(\pi)
that

r(m(\pi),Y)=\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}(m(\pi)^{2})}{v(\bar{\pi})}},

(14)

and the same is true for
r(m(f),Z)
as was remarked already just after Definition
2
.
We have proved that

\operatorname{RPC}^{2}=\frac{\mathbb{E}(m(\pi)^{2})/v(\bar{\pi})}{\mathbb{E}(m(f)^{2})/v(\bar{f})}.

(15)

Turning to the explicit form of
\operatorname{SSC}
in case of the scoring rule
\sigma
, one finds (see Appendix) that

\operatorname{SSC}(f)=\frac{v(\bar{f})-\mathbb{E}(m(f)^{2})}{v(\bar{f})}=1-\frac{\mathbb{E}(m(f)^{2})}{v(\bar{f})},

(16)

and the same for
\pi
.
Comparing Equations (
15
) and (
16
), we find that
\operatorname{RPC}^{2}>1
if and only if
\operatorname{RSS}_{\sigma}>1
, as claimed.

We conclude from this theorem that by replacing
\operatorname{RSS}_{\sigma}
for
\operatorname{RPC}
we obtain an entirely equivalent definition of an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio.
Yet our interpretation of
\operatorname{RSS}_{\sigma}
applies just as well if we replace
\sigma
with any other proper scoring rule
s
.
We can therefore interpret the corresponding definitions of an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio as generalisations of the classical definitions.

3
An anomalous signal-to-noise ratio—so what?

It is often stated, somewhat imprecisely, that an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio implies that the ensemble mean is better in forecasting the actual observation than the individual ensemble members.
It must be kept in mind though that the performance is “better” only in a relative sense, since our generalised formulation of an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio (which includes the classical definition) is in terms of
skill scores
, that is of performance
relative
to the predictive performance of climatology.
It would therefore be more accurate to describe the classic anomalous signal-to-noise ratio as a situation in which the error between the recalibrated ensemble mean and the observation, relative to the variance of the observation, is lower than the error between the ensemble mean and the ensemble members, relative to the variance of the ensemble members.
This is also evident from the characterisation (
15
) of the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
in the proof of Theorem
1
.

We can conclude that since the definition of an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio refers to the skill score rather than the score itself, anomalous signal-to-noise ratios might occur because either the score of the recalibrated forecast against actual observations is better than expected, or because the climatology of the actual observations has a larger entropy than expected, or a combination of both.
In general, these two issues cannot be disentangled without further information or assumptions.

For instance we may assume (or better still, confirm statistically) that the overall score of
f
against actual observations is equal to what we called the self-score of
f
, that is the score of
f
against hypothetical observations drawn from
f
itself
(see, e.g.,
Bröcker et al. 2023
;
Weisheimer et al. 2019
, where this assumption is made)
.
For the quadratic scoring rule
\sigma
this would mean

\mathbb{E}(v(f))=\mathbb{E}(v(\pi))+\mathbb{E}((m(f)-m(\pi))^{2}),

(17)

which is a special case of Equation (
7
) or alternatively follows from a direct calculation.
Since the second (reliability) term on the right-hand side is nonnegative, we find

\mathbb{E}(v(f))\geq\mathbb{E}(v(\pi)).

(18)

In ensemble terms, this says that the ensemble spread is larger than the error of the recalibrated ensemble mean.
This relation combined with a
normal
signal-to-noise ratio allows to conclude that

\mathbb{E}(m(f)^{2})\geq\mathbb{E}(m(\pi)^{2}),

(19)

as is easily seen, meaning that the actually achievable signal of
f
(through proper recalibration) is smaller than the anticipated signal of
f
(i.e. the signal we would see if verifications were drawn from
f
).

However, if the signal-to-noise ratio is
anomalous
, in contrast, the converse relation to (
19
) does
not
necessarily hold!
It follows directly from the definition of
\operatorname{SSC}
that there are two ways in which the signal-to-noise ratio can be anomalous: either because the climatology of the actual observations has a larger variance than the averaged forecast, that is
v(\overline{f})\leq v(\overline{\pi})
, or because the score of the recalibrated forecast against actual observations is better than expected, that is
\mathbb{E}(v(f))\geq\mathbb{E}(v(\pi))
, or both.
The latter case however is already guaranteed by relation (
18
), so it is impossible to decide whether
v(\overline{f})
is larger or smaller than
v(\overline{\pi})
in this situation, at least without further data analysis.

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we discuss several numerical experiments to compare estimators of the new
\operatorname{RSS}
concept for different scoring rules and forecast types, including the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
, for ensemble forecasts.
In Section
4.2
we present experiments using synthetic data with known (classical)
\operatorname{RPC}
, that is, the
\operatorname{RPC}
can be controlled in these data.
Subsequently in Section
4.3
, we will consider seasonal hindcasts and reanalysis of the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) index.

Both data sets comprise ensemble forecasts and corresponding real-valued verifications.
This allows the application of the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
.
Furthermore, we will consider the
\operatorname{RSS}
for the
continuous ranked probability score
(CRPS), a probabilistic scoring rule that can be applied directly to real-valued ensembles and the corresponding verifications (see Section
4.1.1
).
We also consider binary verifications and corresponding probability forecasts; these will be generated from the real-valued forecasts discussed earlier by thresholding.
The logarithmic score will be used for those experiments (see Section
4.1.2
).
Note that we will obtain different versions of the
\operatorname{RSS}
, depending on which score is used, yet for the same forecasting system.
We expect that an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio in a forecasting system is a fundamental statistical property of that system which, if present, should be picked up by different scoring rules, amount of data permitting.

When analysing operational forecast data, one does not have access to the optimally recalibrated forecasts
\pi
, which is a theoretical construction based on certain mathematical assumptions.
Recall that
\pi
is a (potentially highly complicated and nonlinear) function which maps instances of the forecast
f
onto the actual probability distribution of the verification
Y
, conditional on the forecast
f
; it might be thought of as the optimal postprocessing of
f
.
Evidently,
\pi
can only be approximated, based on the available data.
There is a plethora of probabilistic postprocessing methods available; see, for instance,
Vannitsem et al. 2018
for an overview and further references regarding the postprocessing of ensembles.
The specific method selected for approximating
\pi
will of course influence the statistical properties of the resulting estimator of
\operatorname{RSS}
.
A detailed analysis of these properties, and in particular the influence of estimating
\pi
, will be subject of future research.
In the following experiments, we will employ rather simple, and most likely suboptimal, approaches to estimating
\pi
.
The details will be discussed below.

4.1
Experimental setup

In all the following experiments, we use bootstrap resampling
Efron and Hastie 2016
across the time indices (that is, the forecast–verification pairs for each time are sampled with replacement) to approximate the sampling distribution of each statistic, in particular our estimates of the
\operatorname{RPC}
and
\operatorname{RSS}
.
We use the
scoringrules
Python library
(
Zanetta and Allen 2024
)
for computing scoring rules and the SciPy library
(
Virtanen et al. 2020
)
for optimisation (see below for more details).
The open-source Python code for the experiments performed here is available at
https://github.com/eviatarbach/snp
.

In the following two subsections we discuss the scoring rules and corresponding
\operatorname{RSS}
to be used for the ensemble forecasts with real-valued verifications, and for the probability forecasts with binary verifications.
Furthermore, we provide details as to how we approximate the optimally calibrated forecast
\pi
.

4.1.1
Ensemble forecasts and the CRPS

We consider verifications
\{y_{n};n=1,\ldots,N\}
and corresponding ensemble forecasts
\{x^{(k)}_{n};n=1,\ldots,N,\;k=1,\ldots,K\}
, both consisting of real numbers.
We will write the time index as a subscript, while superscripts enumerate the ensemble members.
Consequently,
N
is the number of forecast–verification pairs in our data set, while
K
is the number of ensemble members.

To score ensemble forecasts we use the
continuous ranked probability score

(
Brown 1974
;
Wilks 2019
, CRPS; see)
, a strictly proper scoring rule.
In its original form, the CRPS is defined for a real-valued verification
y
and a forecast
f
given as a cumulative distribution function (CDF), and can be written as

\operatorname{CRPS}(f,y)=\int(f(x)-H(x-y))^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x,

(20)

where
H
is the Heaviside step function.
This scoring rule has become popular in meteorological forecast verification, since it (and its multidimensional extensions) are fairly easy to evaluate, in particular for ensemble forecasts, as we will now see.

An ensemble forecast
\{x^{(k)}\}_{1\leq k\leq K}
can be transformed into a piecewise constant CDF
f
through

f(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{1}\{x^{(k)}\leq x\},

(21)

where
\mathbb{1}
is the indicator function.
Subsequently, we take the CRPS of an ensemble
\{x^{(k)}\}_{k\leq K}
to be the CRPS of the corresponding CDF defined through Equation (
21
).
(We will also write
f
to refer to the ensemble since the CDF and the ensemble convey exactly the same information.)

In order to compute the
\operatorname{RSS}
we need to find
\pi
defined in Equation (
4
).
We recall that mathematically speaking,
\pi
is a function which maps the original forecast
f
onto another forecast which is perfectly calibrated.
In the present case, this means that
\pi
transforms the original ensemble onto a perfectly calibrated ensemble.
As discussed, we do not have direct access to
\pi
, so we need to approximate it by fitting a parametric model to the data.
We will restrict ourselves here to a very simple linear approach.
More specifically, we shift the mean of
f
according to
m(\pi)=a+b\,m(f)
while leaving the ensemble spread around the mean the same.
We determine
a
and
b
so as to minimise the averaged CRPS (over all forecast–verification pairs) of the transformed ensemble, namely
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{CRPS}(\pi_{n},y_{n})
.
The minimisation is carried out using the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) optimisation method.
Note that this approach is “ensemble–aware” in that the resulting ensemble is still exchangeable
(
Bröcker and Kantz 2011
)
.

4.1.2
Binary probability forecasts and the logarithmic scoring rule

In binary forecasting problems, the verification
Y
can only take the values
0
or
1
(or more generally two arbitrarily labelled categories).
Probability forecasts for such problems provide a number
f\in[0,1]
representing the forecast probability of the outcome
Y=1
, while
1-f
is the probability of outcome
Y=0
.
Such forecasts can be scored using the
logarithmic scoring rule

Good 1952
;
Wilks 2019
, a proper scoring rule given by the formula

\operatorname{LS}(f,Y)=-\log|p+Y-1|.

(22)

Again we need to find
\pi
, which in the present case comprises a function from the unit interval to the unit interval.
(Readers familiar with the reliability diagram
Bröcker and Smith 2007b
may visualise
\pi
as the perfect recalibration curve.)
We approximate
\pi
through a logit model

\operatorname{logit}(\pi)=a+b\operatorname{logit}(f),

(23)

with parameters
a,b
, where
\operatorname{logit}
is the logit transformation

\operatorname{logit}(p)=\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right).

(24)

We choose
a
and
b
to minimise the total logarithmic score
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{LS}(\pi(f_{n}),y_{n})
over the forecast–verification archive.
The logarithmic score is infinite in case that
Y=1
yet
\pi=0
, or if
Y=0
yet
\pi=1
.
Now
\pi=0
or
1
happens if and only if
f=0
or
1
, respectively, irrespective of the values of
a,b
.
In order for this problem not to spoil the optimisation, we set
f_{n}=\epsilon
in case
f_{n}=0
, and
f_{n}=1-\epsilon
in case
f_{n}=1
, and we set
\epsilon=10^{-2}
.
We note that in our experiments, the possible values of the forecast
f_{n}
are discrete, and in particular values between
0
and
\epsilon
or between
1-\epsilon
and
1
cannot occur (if we set
\epsilon
sufficiently small).

4.2
Simulations for synthetic data

In this section, we discuss numerical experiments using synthetic data with known characteristics.
In particular, we are able to specify the value of the true
\operatorname{RPC}
, and thus whether the data exhibits an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio or not.
These data comprise ensemble forecasts and corresponding verifications; we will also consider binary verifications with corresponding probability forecasts derived from these data.

To generate the data, we first draw a verification
Y
from
\mathcal{N}(m_{Y},\sin(\phi)^{2})
, where
\phi
is prescribed.
The mean
m_{Y}
is itself drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,\cos(\phi)^{2})
. As a result,
Y
has overall variance equal to one.
The forecast
f
is defined by an ensemble drawn from
\mathcal{N}(m_{f},\sigma_{f}^{2})
. The mean
m_{f}
is set to be
m_{f}=cm_{Y}
, where
c
is a prescribed parameter.
The variance (or ensemble spread)
\sigma_{f}^{2}
is set so as to match the expected error in the ensemble mean, that is we define
\sigma_{f}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}((m_{f}-Y)^{2})=\sin(\phi)^{2}+(1-s)^{2}\cos(\phi)^{2}
.
It is easy to see that
\cos(\phi)=r(m_{Y},Y)
is the correlation between
m_{Y}
and the verification
Y
(and is furthermore equal to
r(m_{f},Y)
).
We define
\psi
so that
\cos(\psi)=r(m_{f},Z)
is the correlation between the mean
m_{f}
of
f
and a draw
Z
from
f
.
Consequently, we have that the
\operatorname{RPC}=\cos(\phi)/\cos(\psi)
. Note, however, that any specific generated instance of the synthetic verification and ensembles will generally have an empirical
\operatorname{RPC}
that differs from this analytical value.

From the ensemble, we also form binary forecasts and verifications.
As verification we use the label “1” if
Y>0
and “0” else.
To form the forecast
f
, we transform the ensemble members into binary labels in the same fashion and then use as the probability forecast the empirical frequency of the label “1” among the ensemble members.

5
Conclusions

A generalised concept of the ratio of predictable components (RPC) is presented, based on proper scoring rules rather than merely linear correlations.
The guiding idea is to define the
\operatorname{RPC}
as the ratio of two
skill scores
, namely the skill score of the forecast against a verification drawn from the forecast, versus the skill score of the perfectly recalibrated forecast against the actual verifications.
It is shown that this definition is the natural generalisation of the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
, but it can be applied to any type of forecast that is amenable to scoring, not only real-valued ensemble forecasts and verifications.
This drastically widens the applicability of these concepts.

As examples, we consider ensemble forecasts, scored using the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), and probability forecasts of a binary event, scored using the logarithmic score.
The numerical examples show that the new
\operatorname{RSS}
’s basically deliver the same message as the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
, thus demonstrating that they can indeed be considered suitable generalisations of the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
.
At the same time however, the experiments already indicate that the variance of the different statistics can be very different.
For instance in the context of an actual seasonal hindcast data set (of the North Atlantic Oscillation index), the
\operatorname{RSS}
based on the continuous ranked probability score exhibits a variance which is a mere 10% of the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
’s variance.
This evidences that depending on the problem at hand, a ratio of skill scores potentially exhibits much better statistical properties than the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
, and thus permit more confident statements as to whether a given forecasting system exhibits an anomalous signal-to-noise ratio or not, despite using the same data.
A future area of work will therefore be the analysis of the sampling properties of scoring rule–based
\operatorname{RSS}
’s.
(In fact, this analysis is arguably incomplete even in the case of the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
, although see
Bröcker et al. 2023
for an analysis linking the variance of the classical
\operatorname{RPC}
to the correlation between ensemble mean and verification, which is comparatively weaker in seasonal forecasts as opposed to medium-range forecasts.)
A second area of future research is to consider more powerful methods for estimating
\pi
, the optimally recalibrated forecast.
In the present work we restricted ourselves to very simple linear recalibration (with a logit link in the case of forecast probabilities) which is probably warranted given the rather limited amounts of data.
It seems clear however that there is large scope here for methods of machine learning to obtain better estimates of
\pi
.

Appendix A
Proof of Equation (
16
)

We need to prove that for the scoring rule
\sigma(f,y):=(y-m(f))^{2}
we have

\operatorname{SSC}(f)=\frac{\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{E}(f))}{\mathcal{E}(\bar{f})}=\frac{v(\bar{f})-\mathbb{E}(m(f)^{2})}{v(\bar{f})},

(25)

the first equality just being the definition of
\operatorname{SSC}
.
Now

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}(f)&=\int\sigma(f,y)f(y)\;\mathrm{d}y,\\
&=\int(y-m(f))^{2}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}y,\\
&=\int(y^{2}-2ym(f))f(y)\;\mathrm{d}y+m(f)^{2},\\
&=\int y^{2}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}y-m(f)^{2}.\end{split}

Taking the expectation value
\mathbb{E}(\ldots)
, we find

\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{E}(f))=\int y^{2}\bar{f}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y-\mathbb{E}(m(f)^{2})
.
But
\int y^{2}\bar{f}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y=v(\bar{f})
, showing that
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{E}(f))=v(\bar{f})-\mathbb{E}(m(f)^{2})
.
Along similar lines it can be shown that

\mathcal{E}(\bar{f}))=v(\bar{f})
.
Note that we assume here that
m(\bar{f})=0
to simplify the calculations, but the statements remain correct if
m(\bar{f})\neq 0
.
