On the interaction of dilatancy and friction in the behavior of fluid–saturated sheared granular materials: a coupled Computational Fluid Dynamics–Discrete Element Method study Preprint: AIP/POF
Bimal Chhushyabaga, Behrooz Ferdowsi

Abstract
Frictional instabilities in fluid-saturated granular materials underlie critical natural hazards such as submarine landslides and earthquake initiation. Distinct failure behaviors emerge under subaerial and subaqueous conditions due to the coupled effects of mechanical deformation, interparticle friction, and fluid interactions. This study employs three-dimensional coupled Computational Fluid Dynamics–Discrete Element Method (CFD-DEM) simulations to investigate the collapse and runout dynamics of dense and loose granular assemblies across these settings. Parametric analyses reveal that pore pressure evolution plays a central role in governing failure mechanisms: dense assemblies stabilize through dilation, while loose assemblies undergo rapid compaction and fluidization, particularly under subaqueous conditions. Spatiotemporal analyses of coarse-grained fields further highlight strain-rate-dependent behavior driven by evolving porosity and effective stress. Both environments exhibit rate-strengthening behavior that scales with the inertial number ( I n I_{n} ) and viscous number ( I v I_{v} ), though driven by distinct mechanisms: subaerial systems are dominated by interparticle contact networks, whereas subaqueous systems are influenced by fluid drag, pore-pressure buildup, and lubrication. An analytical solution for excess pore pressure is compared with breaching-induced pressure distributions from CFD-DEM simulations, using input parameters derived from numerical triaxial DEM tests. The model captures fluid–particle coupling effectively, reproducing comparable excess pore pressures at steady state, while early-time discrepancies underscore the complexities of transient interactions. These findings advance the understanding of failure mechanics in saturated granular media and support the development of physics based models for mitigating hazards associated with subaqueous granular flows.

I
Introduction

Submarine slides and slope failures contribute significantly to the transport and redistribution of sediments from the continental shelf into the deep sea. This is a process that also often involves the formation of turbidity currents. Similar to subaerial landslides, submarine slides also have different modes of sliding that can create particular flow dynamics and different potentials for subsequent transport. These modes of failure include liquefaction, hydraulic cracking of sediment deposits, slip/sliding on a failure surface
Pinet et al. 2008
;
Blum et al. 2010
;
Edwards, Lee, and Field 1995
. An additional, more complex, and less studied mode of submarine flow is known as breaching flow slides or retrogressive slope failures. It is arguable that this mode of sliding may be a type of slow, gradual frictional slip and, in essence, a form of sliding on a failure surface. Field and experimental studies have demonstrated that submerged granular systems, as opposed to subaerial ones, experience distinct failure modes, including liquefaction slides, abrupt frictional slides, and slow breaching flow slides, driven by the interplay of mechanical, frictional, and fluid-mediated processes
Rauter 2021
;
Montellà et al. 2023
.

A few recent experimental and continuum mechanics-based modeling studies by You et al.
You, Flemings, and Mohrig 2012a
;
You, Flemings, and Mohrig 2014a
;
You et al. 2014
illuminated the main processes that control the breaching phenomenon. They investigated the failure and sliding mechanics of steep subaqueous sediment deposits using laboratory flume experiments that were instrumented with pore pressure sensors and a sonar imaging system (to image the progress of breaching/sliding failure fronts). You et al..
You, Flemings, and Mohrig 2012a
;
You, Flemings, and Mohrig 2014a
;
You et al. 2014
have shown that breaching takes place in dense fine-grained sediments (such as sandy silts) and that it is caused by a temporary build-up of negative pore pressure due to transient dilation of dense fine-grained sediments at the onset of sliding, the production of negative pore fluid pressure temporarily increases the effective stress and shear resistance of materials, which in turn arrests an otherwise developing failure surface. The subsequent dissipation of negative pore pressure results in a continuous process of slow sediment destabilization so that slow breaching continues.
You, Flemings, and Mohrig 2014a
further showed there exists a spectrum of breaching type failure itself, which can be interpreted as a gradual transition from full breaching flow slides to dual-mode failure (alternate, cyclic changes between abrupt sliding and slow breaching), to abrupt frictional sliding, as the sediment deposit thickness increases for a given deposit length (in the primary direction of sliding). While some aspects of these processes have been demonstrated using continuum models of friction-fluid-coupled media, the underlying physical mechanisms, particularly the transient interplay between dilatancy and friction, remain poorly understood under earth’s near-surface stress conditions. Moreover, the mathematical and computational frameworks required to accurately model such coupled processes across scales are still underdeveloped and actively evolving
Soulaine 2024
.

Dilatancy refers to the tendency of granular materials to change volume when subjected to shear deformation
Reynolds 1885
;
Bolton 1986
;
Kabla and Senden 2009
. This behavior plays a critical role in granular failure processes due to its direct coupling with pore-pressure evolution
Goren et al. 2010
. The resulting interaction governs transitions from stable creep to localized failure and strongly influences the mobility and run-out of landslides
Finnegan and Saffer 2024
. These transitions are often observed within shear zones, regions of concentrated deformation and elevated shear stress, which typically develop in response to externally applied loads or internal heterogeneities in the granular structure
Dey et al. 2016
. As deformation continues within these zones, changes in porosity and grain fabric promote frictional weakening
Wan and Guo 1999
, while pore-pressure feedbacks amplify instability by reducing the effective normal stress
Deng et al. 2020
. This coupling between mechanical and hydraulic responses creates conditions conducive to dynamic failure
Iverson 2005
. However, not all shear zones evolve toward instability; under certain conditions, dilatant hardening may increase the system’s resistance to slip, thereby stabilizing deformation. Such behavior has been linked to the occurrence of slow slip events, which are characterized by quasi-static rupture and recurrence intervals that match field observations
Segall et al. 2010
. The mechanical response of shear zones is further influenced by their initial density: dense zones typically undergo dilation during early deformation stages, with porosity eventually stabilizing after sufficient shear displacement
Iverson 2005
;
Garagash and Rudnicki 2003
. Environmental conditions, particularly saturation, also play a key role in modulating dilatant behavior. Under submerged conditions, the presence of pore fluid reduces interparticle friction
Legros 2002
, introduces buoyant uplift forces
Cassar, Nicolas, and Pouliquen 2005
, and lowers the effective weight of the material, thereby enhancing its potential for downslope mobility
Vescovi, Marveggio, and Di Prisco 2020
. These factors ultimately govern the flow dynamics of landslides, which may exhibit free-fall, inertial, or viscous regimes depending on the Stokes number (
S_{t}
) and the density ratio (
r
)
Courrech du Pont et al. 2003
.

At the microscale, dense suspensions exhibit viscoplastic rheology governed by frictional and collisional interactions, evolving as a function of the viscous number,
I_{v}

Boyer, Guazzelli, and Pouliquen 2011
;
Buettner, Guo, and Curtis 2020
. Shear zone behavior is also modulated by shear rate
Marone, Raleigh, and Scholz 1990
, prevailing stress state
Iverson et al. 2000
;
Aharonov and Sparks 2002
, and the evolving balance between dilation and compaction. Collectively, these factors shape the stress–strain response and sliding stability of saturated granular systems
Hsiao et al. 2017
. In this context, the viscoinertial number offers a unified framework for characterizing rheological transitions in mono and bidisperse granular flows
Cui et al. 2020
.

To understand the complex mechanisms driving granular failure, researchers integrate empirical observations with theoretical frameworks, laboratory experiments, and advanced numerical models. Laboratory studies confirm that porosity and dilatancy critically govern frictional strength, pore-pressure evolution, and landslide mobility. For instance, loose soils tend to liquefy abruptly under shear, whereas dense packings dilate, lowering pore pressure and resisting motion
Iverson et al. 2000
. Submerged granular collapse experiments reinforce these trends: loose beds produce rapid, shallow flows, while dense configurations yield slower, more coherent deposits due to enhanced fluid–grain coupling
Rondon, Pouliquen, and Aussillous 2011
. Classical theories such as Rowe’s stress-dilatancy framework offer insight into strength evolution under confinement but overlook key influences such as stress magnitude, void ratio, and initial packing conditions
Wan and Guo 1999
. To address these limitations, modern constitutive models incorporate state parameters and density-dependent dilatancy to better capture evolving material behavior.

Advances in CFD-DEM modeling have further enabled high-resolution exploration of fluid–solid interactions under landslide-relevant conditions
Pham et al. 2018
. These simulations show that pore-pressure evolution governs shear localization: dilation induces suction and stabilizes dense packings, while compaction elevates pore pressure and promotes failure in looser configurations. Drainage and permeability conditions strongly modulate these responses, with dilation near drained boundaries increasing effective stress and compaction near impermeable zones reducing shear resistance
Aharonov et al. 2013
. CFD-DEM studies also reveal distinct flow regimes, from grain-inertial to fluid-inertial to viscous, each shaped by the Stokes number (
S_{t}
) and the density ratio (
r
), with important implications for landslide runout, energy dissipation, and deposition morphology
Jing et al. 2019
. Semi-resolved models further resolve vortex structures and fluid-mediated grain interactions, improving predictions of runout extent and final deposit structure
Chen and Zhang 2023
. Notably, bidisperse configurations, particularly those with coarse-over-fine layering, enhance segregation and kinetic energy transfer, promoting longer runouts, whereas reversed or randomly mixed beds tend to inhibit mobility
Huang et al. 2025
. Finally, the column aspect ratio influences vortex formation and segregation intensity; in submerged conditions, increased viscous drag reduces runout distances and delays recompaction
Shademani, Blais, and Shakibaeinia 2021
.

Despite advances, the transient coupling of friction, pore pressure, and dilatancy in fluid-saturated granular systems, critical for flow initiation and localization, remains poorly understood. Hence, this study addresses these gaps using CFD-DEM simulations to investigate the roles of dilatancy, initial porosity, and frictional resistance in the shear deformation of dry and submerged granular systems. Systems with two initial packing densities are examined under subaerial and subaqueous conditions, incorporating Hertzian contact laws. We first detail the numerical model setup and boundary conditions, followed by a comparative analysis of failure kinematics and runout distances. We analyze spatiotemporal variations in coarse-grained stress, strain, and pore-pressure fields to identify emerging failure mechanisms and the evolution of shear zones. Through simulations, we explore how spatial dilation affects pore-pressure evolution and breaching in both dense and loose beds. We then characterize the evolution of friction, porosity, and pore pressure as functions of deviatoric strain rate and dimensionless numbers, including the inertial number and viscous number, offering a unified, strain-rate-dependent description of granular flow behavior across both dry and submerged conditions. A two-dimensional analytical pore-pressure model is employed later in the study to compare breaching erosion rates and pressure distributions. Triaxial DEM isotropic unloading tests are conducted to quantify isotropic compressibility and dilation strength. Dilation is modeled as a function of mean effective stress and the ratio of major to minor principal effective stresses.

II
Numerical approach : Coupled Computational Fluid Dynamics with the Discrete Element Method (CFD-DEM)

We use a numerical technique that combines Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) with the Discrete Element Method (DEM) to simulate fluid-particle systems. This approach enables detailed modeling of particle-fluid interactions by combining CFD’s ability to simulate fluid flows with DEM’s capability to track the motions and collisions of individual particles
El-Emam et al. 2021
. Below, we provide the details of the model setup and computations used in this study. We use the open-source code MFiX to simulate fluid–particle systems, which employs a hybrid Euler–Lagrange framework for multiphase flows, solving the fluid phase on an Eulerian grid and treating particles as discrete Lagrangian entities
Garg et al. 2012
;
Musser et al. 2021
. The CFD and DEM solvers are coupled through momentum exchange between the fluid and particles. Fluid motion is governed by the Navier–Stokes equations and is solved using a predictor–corrector method with an approximate projection scheme. Particle trajectories are determined by Newton’s laws, integrated using a forward Euler scheme with additional collision handling. Interpolation and deposition operators facilitate the coupling between the fluid and particle phases.

III
Mechanism of failure

The failure mechanisms of granular columns differ significantly between subaerial and subaqueous conditions, with the initial packing fraction playing a crucial role in these behaviors. Subaqueous systems exhibit reduced runout distances and slower failure for both dense and loose configurations due to fluid interactions, with dense models being the most stable, a result consistent with previous studies
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Montellà et al. 2023
. In contrast, subaerial systems fail more rapidly and exhibit greater sensitivity to packing fraction
Nguyen, Bui, and Fukagawa 2015
. Loose models undergo more pronounced and extended collapses, while dense models fail abruptly but with limited spreading. Fig.
2
compares velocity profiles at two time steps (0.04 s and 0.4 s) to illustrate the contrasting failure and runout behaviors across dense and loose configurations in both environments. In subaqueous systems, fluid interactions, such as drag and pore pressure feedback, moderate failure dynamics, leading to slower progression and reduced energy dissipation compared to subaerial cases, although final runout distances may be similar in some cases
Zhang et al. 2020
.

The dense subaqueous model (Fig.
2
(a) and Fig.
2
(b)) shows minimal runout, with a maximum of 0.054 m, which is approximately 61% shorter than the maximum runout observed in subaerial conditions. The loose subaqueous model (Fig.
2
(c) and Fig.
2
(d)) exhibits a slightly longer runout of 0.062 m, yet still 59% shorter than its subaerial counterpart. These reductions underscore the dampening influence of fluid on collapse extent. As shearing initiates, dilation occurs due to interlocking grains pushing against one another, increasing the pore pressure and mobilizing peak shear resistance. This interparticle interlocking elevates the internal friction angle and temporarily raises shear strength
Montellà et al. 2023
. Following this peak, the friction angle decreases, and failure progresses gradually with limited velocity magnitudes throughout the collapse.

Granular rheology in shear zone

V.1
Dry condition

Frictional behavior in subaerial granular systems is strongly influenced by strain rate and the inertial number (

In contrast, loose subaerial materials display lower friction at low strain rates due to limited and transient grain contacts. While porosity in granular media is generally expected to vary with strain rate due to the competing effects of compaction and dilation, it can remain relatively stable over certain regimes. This constancy arises when the system achieves a balance between structural compression and dilation, or when strain rates are too low to significantly disrupt the grain arrangement. V.2
Fluid saturated condition

Subaqueous granular rheology is governed by the intricate interplay between solid grains and the surrounding fluid. In these systems, friction emerges from both interparticle contact forces and hydrodynamic interactions, introducing greater complexity compared to dry granular materials.

Loose subaqueous granular materials exhibit a strain-rate-dependent response characterized by two distinct regimes (Fig.
7
(b)). At the onset of shearing, increasing strain rates lead to pore-pressure buildup, which enhances friction through fluid drag and the development of reinforced contact networks. Conversely, at lower strain rates, typically observed in the later stages of shearing, fluid lubrication dominates. This lubrication reduces friction by facilitating easier particle rearrangement. The observed rate-strengthening behavior underscores the importance of fluid-mediated forces in controlling deformation within loosely packed systems.

Dense subaqueous materials respond differently, exhibiting comparatively lower friction with increasing strain rate (Fig.
7
(b)), primarily due to the development of negative pore pressure at the onset of shear. As strain rate continues to rise, fluid drag becomes increasingly influential, and pore-pressure effects become more evident, reflecting the evolving interaction between the solid framework and the interstitial fluid. When friction is plotted against the viscous number (
I_{v}
), dense materials tend to stabilize at higher
I_{v}
values, showing reduced frictional variability. This plateau suggests that viscous forces play a moderating role in the frictional response under steady fluid–particle interactions. In contrast, loose materials display a more pronounced increase in friction with rising
I_{v}

Although porosity in fluid-saturated granular materials might be expected to vary with strain rate, observations show that porosity can remain nearly constant over a moderate range of strain rates (Fig.
7
(d)). This behavior arises when the system reaches a dynamic balance between compaction and dilation, or when the imposed strain rates are too low to significantly alter the microstructure.
Amarsid et al. 2024
similarly found that porosity, governed by the viscous number, remains largely unaffected at lower
I_{v}
values, consistent with the trends identified here.

A direct comparison of dry and saturated systems highlights the critical role of fluid presence and initial structure in governing shear response: while subaerial systems rely on interparticle contact mechanics, subaqueous systems are shaped by a complex feedback between fluid drag, pore pressure, and microstructural evolution.

VI
Analytical solution for excess pore pressure using poromechanics

During breaching, a dynamic equilibrium emerges between pore pressure dissipation and continuous dilation
You, Flemings, and Mohrig 2012b
;
Alhaddad et al. 2024
. As pore pressure dissipates, the effective stress within the slope deposit increases, stabilizing the sediment. However, for retrogressive failure to persist, this dissipation must be sustained by ongoing dilation. The resulting feedback mechanism, where slope failure induces dilative strain that regenerates negative excess pore pressure, helps maintain the breaching process. Importantly, this dynamic decouples the steady-state pore pressure distribution from the deposit’s intrinsic permeability, revealing an underexplored aspect of sediment failure behavior
You, Flemings, and Mohrig 2012b
;
Alhaddad et al. 2024
.

To formalize this equilibrium, the analytical solution developed by
You, Flemings, and Mohrig 2012b
describes the pore pressure evolution coupled with dilation during the breaching process. This formulation predicts the steady-state negative pore pressure field, showing that the system can self-regulate pore pressure independently of permeability once dilation-driven feedback is active.

Comparisons with large-scale laboratory experiments and numerical models confirm this feedback mechanism’s role in sustaining breaching and highlight deviations during the transient initiation phase
Alhaddad et al. 2024
;
Alhaddad et al. 2023
. The same analytical formulation is applied in this study to evaluate whether the CFD–DEM simulation results for excess pore pressure in both dense and loose subaqueous granular models follow the theoretical predictions, particularly at steady state. By extracting simulation data at various locations and times, we directly compare the evolution and final distribution of pore pressure against the analytical model.

Furthermore, recent hydromechanical models of subduction zone sediments
Nikolinakou et al. 2023
demonstrate that shear-induced overpressures, in addition to changes in mean stress, significantly control pore pressure evolution. Although applied in a tectonic context, these findings conceptually reinforce that dilative strain and shear play a dominant role in transient and steady-state pore pressure fields, complementing observations from breaching processes.

VI.1
Pore pressure evolution during breaching

The evolution of excess pore pressure during breaching reveals distinct responses in dense and loose subaqueous granular systems, governed by the initial packing density and fluid–particle interactions. Fig.
9
presents the spatial and temporal variations across monitored points A–L (A–F for the dense model shown in Fig.
9

9

In the dense model, wall removal initiates an abrupt drop in pore pressure, followed by a gradual rise as fluid redistributes and particles rearrange (Fig.
9
(b)). This transient suction phase results from the system’s resistance to compaction. Over time, pore pressure stabilizes, reflecting the balance between fluid expulsion and interparticle contact forces. At point D, the temporal evolution of excess pore pressure (Fig.
9
(c)), plotted in Lagrangian coordinates across multiple time intervals (0.01 s, 0.1 s, 0.4 s, and 0.8 s), shows an initial negative pressure that progressively increases toward a steady state. This behavior aligns well with analytical predictions, with minor deviations during the transient phase attributed to localized heterogeneities and nonlinear fluid–particle coupling.

In contrast, the loose model exhibits a sharp rise in pore pressure following wall removal, driven by void collapse and rapid fluid compression (Fig.
9
(e)). The high initial pressure reflects the system’s susceptibility to compaction, followed by redistribution and a gradual decline toward equilibrium. At point J, the temporal pressure profile (Fig.
9
(f)) begins elevated and stabilizes over time. While the steady-state matches analytical solutions well, significant deviations are observed during the transient stage, underscoring the complex dynamics of loosely packed systems.

These comparisons confirm that the analytical solution, despite its simplifying assumptions, effectively captures the steady-state behavior of excess pore pressure for both dense and loose subaqueous breaching scenarios. The good agreement observed at later times across different monitored points supports the applicability of the poromechanical framework in predicting long-term pore pressure evolution. While transient mismatches highlight the complexity of fluid–particle interactions during early deformation, the steady-state comparison underscores the robustness of the analytical model in capturing the essential physics governing pore pressure regulation during breaching.

VII
Conclusion

This study investigated the collapse and runout behavior of granular materials in subaerial (dry) and subaqueous (fluid-saturated) environments, with a particular focus on the roles of initial packing density and fluid–particle interactions. Three-dimensional CFD-DEM simulations revealed that dense packings form narrower shear zones and exhibit reduced runout distances due to higher resistance to deformation. In contrast, loose packings undergo broader deformation and longer runouts as a result of easier compaction.

In subaqueous conditions, these trends are amplified: dense materials fail gradually through dilation and the generation of negative pore pressures, whereas loose materials experience rapid failure driven by compaction and transient positive pore pressures. The evolution of porosity plays a central role in controlling deformation behavior, fluid migration, and mechanical stability. Dense systems dilate during shear, drawing in fluid and increasing resistance, while loose systems compact, expelling fluid and decreasing stability. These behaviors are governed by the strain rate and local stress conditions. The granular rheology of subaerial and subaqueous models under dense and loose conditions further provides critical insights into the interplay between friction, strain rate, and dimensionless parameters such as the inertial number (
I_{n}
) and viscous number (
I_{v}
). Both environments exhibit a general rate-strengthening behavior, though driven by distinct mechanisms: in subaerial systems, friction increases modestly with
I_{n}
due to enhanced contact networks and force-chain persistence, while in subaqueous systems, frictional evolution is influenced by the competing effects of fluid drag, pore-pressure buildup, and lubrication. Dense materials tend to maintain nearly constant porosity at low
I_{n}
and
I_{v}
, reflecting a dynamic balance between compaction and dilation, whereas loose materials exhibit more pronounced porosity changes and frictional variability at higher strain rates. These rheological trends highlight the sensitivity of granular strength to both fluid presence and initial structure.

Simulation results were consistent with analytical predictions of excess pore pressure, demonstrating the model’s ability to capture the complex coupling between fluid flow and granular deformation at steady state, while transient mismatches highlighted the complexity of fluid–particle interactions during early deformation. Overall, the study demonstrates that granular failure dynamics are highly sensitive to initial structure and hydromechanical interactions. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of natural phenomena such as landslides, sediment transport, and seabed instabilities. To further enhance predictive capability, future work should incorporate time-dependent effects, spatial heterogeneity in material properties, and comparison against field-scale observations. In addition, to better capture the stick-slip behavior associated with frictional evolution, we plan to derive rate-and-state parameters from small-scale direct shear experiments. These parameters will then be integrated into a frictional rheology model based on the rate-and-state framework, enabling more accurate representation of transient shear responses in fluid-saturated granular systems.
