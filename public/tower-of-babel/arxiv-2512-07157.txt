In this section we prove

Theorem 3.1
.

(with hypotheses as in
1.1
) Fix
i\geq 0
. There exists
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear maps

\mathcal{Q}^{m}\colon H^{i}(G,R)\rightarrow H^{i}(G,R)
for

with
\mathcal{Q}^{0}
being the identity such that for any
s\in S
and
\alpha\in H^{i}(G,R)
we have

\mathcal{Q}^{m}(s\alpha)=\sum_{a+b=m}\mathcal{P}^{a}(s)\mathcal{Q}^{b}(\alpha).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{m}\colon R\rightarrow R
be the Steenrod operators on
R
.
We work with the chain complex
C^{*}(G,R)
, see
2.4
. For
\psi\in C^{n}(G,R)
define
\mathcal{Q}^{m}(\psi)=\mathcal{P}^{m}\circ\psi
. Clearly
\mathcal{Q}^{m}\colon C^{*}(G,R)\rightarrow C^{*}(G,R)
are
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear maps.
Also as
\mathcal{P}^{0}
is the identity we obtain that
\mathcal{Q}^{0}
is the identity.
If we show that it is a chain map then we have
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear maps
\mathcal{Q}^{m}\colon H^{i}(G,R)\rightarrow H^{i}(G,R)
.
To do this let
\psi\in C^{n}(G,R)
. Then

\displaystyle(d^{n}(\mathcal{Q}^{m}(\psi))(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n+1})

\displaystyle=g_{1}\mathcal{P}^{m}(\psi(g_{2},\ldots,g_{n+1}))+

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{P}^{m}(\psi(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i}g_{i+1},\cdots,g_{n+1}))

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n+1}\mathcal{P}^{m}(\psi(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})).

We also have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{m}(d^{n}((\psi))(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}^{m}(g_{1}(\psi(g_{2},\ldots,g_{n+1})))+

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{P}^{m}(\psi(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i}g_{i+1},\cdots,g_{n+1}))

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n+1}\mathcal{P}^{m}(\psi(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})).

Notice

g_{1}\mathcal{P}^{m}(\psi(g_{2},\ldots,g_{n+1}))=\mathcal{P}^{m}(g_{1}(\psi(g_{2},\ldots,g_{n+1}))).

Thus
\mathcal{Q}^{m}\colon C^{*}(G,R)\rightarrow C^{*}(G,R)
are
F_{q}
-linear chain maps and so induce
F_{q}
-linear maps
\mathcal{Q}^{m}\colon H^{i}(G,R)\rightarrow H^{i}(G,R)
.

Let
\alpha=[\psi]\in H^{i}(G,R)
and let
s\in S
. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{m}(s\psi)(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}^{m}(s\psi(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{a+b=m}\mathcal{P}^{a}(s)\mathcal{P}^{b}(\psi(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i})).

\displaystyle=\sum_{a+b=m}\mathcal{P}^{a}(s)\mathcal{Q}^{b}(\psi)((g_{1},\ldots,g_{i})).

The result follows.
∎

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{Q}^{m}(t\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a+b=m}\mathcal{P}^{a}(t)\mathcal{Q}^{b}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}^{m}(t)\alpha+\sum_{a+b=m,\ \text{and}\ a<m}\mathcal{P}^{a}(t)\mathcal{Q}^{b}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}^{m}(t)\alpha.

The result follows.
∎

We now give

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Fix

. Also

. By Proposition
2.3

4.2
we get that

is a
\mathcal{P}^{*}
-invariant ideal of

, 11.4.4]

∎

In this section we give

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We note that if
d\leq 3
then
S
is Cohen-Macaulay (see
[
10
]
) and so we have nothing to prove. Also if
p
does not divide order of
G
then
S
is Cohen-Macaulay, and so we have nothing to prove. So we assume that
p
divides
|G|
. If
d\geq 4
then by a result of Ellingsrud-Skjelbred
[
3
, 3.9]
we have

0ptS\geq\min\{\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}V^{P}+2,d\},

where

p
-subgroup of
G

[

, 8.2.1]

0ptS\geq 3
. Thus
H^{j}_{S_{+}}(S)=0
for

.

We use the Ellingsrud-Skjelbred spectral sequence with
\mathfrak{a}=S_{+}
and
M=R
. We have two first quadrant spectral sequences

(\alpha)\colon\quad\quad E_{2}^{p,q}=H^{p}_{S_{+}}(H^{q}(G,R))\Longrightarrow H^{p+q}_{S_{+}}(G,R),\ \text{and}

(\beta)\colon\quad\quad\mathcal{E}_{2}^{p,q}=H^{p}(G,H^{q}_{S_{+}}(R))\Longrightarrow H^{p+q}_{S_{+}}(G,R).

We first analyse the spectral sequence
(\beta)
. We note that as
R
is a finite
S
-module we have
H^{q}_{S_{+}}(R)=H^{q}_{R_{+}}(R)=0
for
q\neq d
. So the spectral sequence
(\beta)
collapses. In particular
H^{n}_{S_{+}}(G,R)=0
for
n<d
.

Next we analyse the spectral sequence
(\alpha)
.
We note that for
q>0
we get by Theorem
1.2
and
2.15
that

(*)

\mathbf{d}_{d,0}\in\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_{S}E_{2}^{p,q}}\quad\text{for $q>0$}.

For
r\geq 3
we have that
E^{p,q}_{r}
is a sub-quotient of
E^{p,q}_{r-1}
. So we get that

(**)

\mathbf{d}_{d,0}\in\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_{S}E_{r}^{p,q}}\quad\text{for $q>0$ and $r\geq 3$}.

For the convenience of the reader we first give a detailed proof of

\mathbf{d}_{d,0}\in\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_{S}H^{3}_{S_{+}}(S)}
when
d\geq 4
.
We have
E_{2}^{3,0}=H^{3}_{S_{+}}(S)
.
Note

. As
E^{3,0}_{\infty}

E^{3,0}_{\infty}=0
.
So we have a surjective map

By (**) and
2.14

\mathbf{d}_{d,0}\in\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_{S}E_{3}^{3,0}}

We have an exact sequence

E_{2}^{1,1}\rightarrow E_{2}^{3,0}\rightarrow E_{3}^{3,0}\rightarrow 0.

By (*),
2.14
and the fact that
\mathbf{d}_{d,0}\in\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_{S}E_{3}^{3,0}}
we get that

\mathbf{d}_{d,0}\in\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_{S}E_{2}^{3,0}}
. The result follows.

Now let
3\leq j\leq d-1
. We have
E^{j,0}_{2}=H^{j}_{S_{+}}(S)
.
Note
E^{j,0}_{j+1}=E^{j,0}_{\infty}
. As
E^{j,0}_{\infty}

.
So we have a surjective map

.
Next we have an exact sequence

By (**),
2.14
and the fact that
\mathbf{d}_{d,0}\in\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_{S}E_{j}^{j,0}}

\mathbf{d}_{d,0}\in\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_{S}E_{j-1}^{j,0}}

Iterating we obtain
\mathbf{d}_{d,0}\in\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_{S}E_{2}^{j,0}}
