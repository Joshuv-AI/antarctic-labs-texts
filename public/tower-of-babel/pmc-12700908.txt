Upper limb function and quality of life in Duchenne muscular dystrophy: a cross-sectional study in Chile
Authors: Daniela Avila-Smirnow, Carla Pérez, Odalie Moscoso, Nayadeth Lucero-González
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) impairs motor function and health-related quality of life (HRQoL), yet data from low- and middle-income countries are scarce. We assessed upper limb functionality with the Upper Limb Functionality Evaluation Scale (EEFES) and HRQoL with the SF36v2 in Chilean patients with DMD at late ambulatory and nonambulatory stages, comparing them with healthy age-matched controls. This cross-sectional study included 17 patients with genetically or biopsy-confirmed DMD and 12 controls. Median upper limb functionality was 51%; proximal domains were most impaired (0%) while distal domains remained preserved (100%). Compared with controls, patients reported markedly lower physical functioning (15% vs. 100%) and general health (62% vs. 82%), with a reduced physical component summary (40 vs. 58), but higher mental component summary (58 vs. 50). Age correlated negatively with upper limb performance (ρ = − 0.73; p = 0.0013), whereas corticosteroid use correlated positively (ρ = 0.73; p = 0.0163). Physical Function 10 (PF10) and General Health 2 items showed the strongest associations with upper limb performance. In this first Chilean cohort using EEFES and SF36v2, DMD patients demonstrated significant physical but relatively preserved mental health. These findings align with international reports and highlight potential sensitive endpoints for clinical trials in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27480-1.

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X-linked disorder characterized by progressive loss of motor function and health-related quality of life (HRQoL). In high-income countries such as Italy1,2 , the United States3,4 , and the United Kingdom5 , upper limb function and HRQoL have been extensively studied. In contrast, data from developing regions—particularly Latin America—remain scarce6,7 . Differences in early diagnosis, standards of care, corticosteroid use, rehabilitation access, nutrition, assistive devices, and availability of disease-modifying therapies may influence outcomes across regions8 .

Upper limb function in DMD is typically assessed using the Performance of Upper Limb (PUL) scale, Brooke Upper Extremity Scale, or Motor Function Measure (MFM)9 . A less commonly used tool, the Upper Limb Functionality Evaluation Scale (EEFES), was developed and validated in Chile10. This accessible, self-instructive instrument evaluates activities of daily living (ADLs) and has been applied to DMD cohorts.

HRQoL in DMD is often measured with the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) and, less frequently, the Short Form 36 version 2 (SF36v2)11 . The SF36v2 assesses eight domains—physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health (role physical), bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems (role emotional), and mental health—summarized into the physical component summary and mental component summary. Unlike the PedsQL, which is limited to individuals up to 25 years, the SF36v2 applies from adolescence through adulthood12 , a critical advantage given the extended life expectancy in DMD due to improved standards of care13–15 and disease-modifying therapies16 . There is limited validation evidence for most tools used to assess HRQoL, and none are disease-specific. This underscores the need to evaluate and adapt HRQoL instruments for use in diverse settings, particularly in underrepresented regions such as Latin America17.

In nonambulatory stages, upper limb function becomes essential for independence in ADLs, fine motor control, and wheelchair operation. However, few studies have explored the relationship between upper limb function and HRQoL in adolescents with DMD18 . This study aimed to characterize upper limb function in Chilean adolescents with DMD in late ambulatory and nonambulatory stages using the EEFES, evaluate HRQoL via the SF36v2, and examine associations between these measures in a middle-income setting.

Methods

Study approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile and the South Eastern Metropolitan Health Service. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Study design

This was a cross-sectional observational study evaluating upper limb functionality, HRQoL, and associations between these factors in patients with DMD. HRQoL was assessed in a DMD subgroup and in an age- and sex-matched group of healthy controls, as depicted in Fig. 1. Participants

Male patients aged 12–25 years with genetically or biopsy-confirmed DMD were recruited, along with age-matched healthy male controls. Exclusion criteria included noncooperative behavior or cognitive limitations precluding participation. Recruitment occurred between March 2022 and January 2024 through outpatient clinics at Red de Salud UC Christus and Complejo Asistencial Dr. Sótero del Río, Santiago, and via DMD patient associations. Complejo Asistencial Dr. Sótero del Río serves approximately 8% of Chile’s population. Participants were classified into three groups: healthy controls (Control), all DMD patients (DMD_tot), and DMD patients with SF36v2 data (DMD_SF36).

Healthy controls were recruited through acquaintances and relatives of healthcare personnel, as well as through social media and public postings.

Variables

Demographic, anthropometric, and clinical data were collected, including DMD-related complications (loss of ambulation, scoliosis, spinal fusion, ventilatory support), current treatments (corticosteroids, disease-modifying therapies), and psychological health. Upper limb functionality was assessed via the Upper Limb Functionality Evaluation Scale (EEFES), and muscle strength via manual muscle testing (MMT) using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale. HRQoL was assessed via the SF36v2 in both DMD and control groups.

Muscle strength and upper limb functionality

MMT evaluated shoulder abductors, elbow flexors/extensors, and wrist flexors/extensors (MRC scale 0–5)19. All assessments were performed by the same neuromuscular neurologist.

EEFES comprises four dimensions (proximal, middle, distal, and functional); items were scored in the dominant limb, except for the proximal dimension (bilateral scoring). Total scores were expressed in absolute terms and percentages (maximum score = 120). A self-trained occupational therapist experienced in DMD administered EEFES.

The EEFES scale was selected because, unlike the PUL, Brooke Upper Extremity Scale, and MFM scales—which primarily assess motor strength and performance—it includes a specific functionality domain that captures the daily-life use of the upper limbs. Moreover, the EEFES was developed by our research team and is the standard tool routinely used in our center for the assessment of patients with DMD. It is also a self-trainable instrument, accessible through a dedicated website, and freely available for both clinical and research use10. However, a limitation of this instrument is its reduced cross-study comparability, as most international research in DMD relies on the PUL scale as the standard outcome measure.

HRQoL assessment

HRQoL was measured via the SF36v2 (0–100 scale), validated for Chilean populations (QualitiMetrics license)12,20 .

The SF36v2 was selected because it provides an assessment of HRQoL allowing comparison with the general population. PedsQL 4.0 and the PedsQL 3.0 Neuromuscular Module21,22 are also validated in Spanish-speaking cohorts, nevertheless SF36v2 is the only one formally validated in Chile, demonstrating robust psychometric properties including construct validity and internal consistency in local populations12.

Patients ≥ 15 years with sufficient cognitive ability completed the test. Eight domains and two summary components (physical and mental) were analyzed. Controls self-administered the test; DMD patients received evaluator assistance due to functional limitations. Scores are reported as absolute, percentage, and normalized values (mean = 50, SD = 10).

Statistical analysis

Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution were described as means, standard deviations, and 95% confidence intervals. Non-normally distributed variables were described as medians, interquartile ranges (IQRs), and confidence intervals, except for EEFES results, for which confidence intervals were not calculated since the objective was descriptive rather than inferential—specifically, to characterize rather than test differences between the DMD_tot and DMD_SF36 groups.

Comparisons of non-normally distributed continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, applying the false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons between independent groups. The FDR correction was not applied when comparing dependent groups. For comparisons between the DMD_SF36 and control groups, the effect size (r) was calculated.

Spearman correlation coefficients assessed associations among variables (general and psychological health, EEFES items/dimensions, SF36v2 items/domains/components).

Bias control and sample size

Red de Salud UC Christus is a private tertiary care center that primarily serves patients from middle-income backgrounds, whereas Complejo Asistencial Dr. Sótero del Río is a public hospital providing care to both middle- and lower-income populations. Private institutions generally offer greater access to specialized health care in Chile. Differences in socioeconomic background and access to medical services may have introduced potential selection bias in the study population.

Patients with DMD were either attending school or had completed their studies and were no longer formally enrolled in the education system. In contrast, all controls were actively enrolled, either in school or at university. This factor may also have introduced a bias in patient selection. However, the observed difference in educational level is consistent with the clinical reality in Chile, where patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) rarely can pursue higher education, in contrast to healthy individuals.

All evaluations were standardized and performed by trained professionals using validated tools. Data collection and analysis were anonymized. Groups were numerically coded for statistical analysis to minimize handling bias.

No formal sample size or power calculation was performed. A convenience sample of 15–20 DMD patients was used for upper limb evaluation, with 10–15 patients and matched controls assessed for HRQoL. The sample size was estimated based on the number of patients under regular follow-up at the two participating centers and those potentially recruitable from DMD patient associations during the study period, including all eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria. This approach is consistent with previous studies in rare neuromuscular disorders where recruitment feasibility determines sample size.

Results

Participants

Between March 2022 and January 2024, 17 patients with DMD (DMD_tot, all DMD patients) and 12 healthy age-matched males (Control) were recruited. All DMD patients underwent clinical evaluation, manual muscle testing (MMT), and assessment with the Upper Limb Functionality Evaluation Scale (EEFES); 11 of these patients (DMD_SF36, DMD patients with SF36v2 data) also completed the Short Form 36 version 2 (SF36v2) questionnaire; six patients were excluded because they were younger than 15 years of age or presented cognitive limitations. All healthy controls underwent clinical assessment and completed the SF36v2 as depicted in Fig. 1.

Data were complete for all participants except for one control subject (missing height and weight) and one DMD patient (missing muscle strength data); these missing data were excluded from the relevant analyses.

Mean body mass index (BMI) was 22.07 ± 4.43 kg/m2 in DMD_tot, 23.43 ± 4.91 kg/m2 in DMD_SF36, and 21.09 ± 3.61 kg/m2 in controls, with no significant between-group differences (p = 0.457).

Mean age at symptom onset was 2.76 ± 1.56 years in DMD_tot and 2.82 ± 1.47 years in DMD_SF36 (p = 0.929). Depression was diagnosed in 8/17 patients in DMD_tot, 5/11 in DMD_SF36 and none of the patients in the control group (p = 0.010). Spinal fusion had been performed in 4 patients per group (p = 0.376); disease-modifying drugs were used by 3 patients per group (p = 0.439). Cardioprotective drugs were prescribed to 11 patients in DMD_tot and 7 in DMD_SF36 (p = 0.632). Corticosteroid therapy was received by 14 patients in DMD_tot and 9 in DMD_SF36 (p = 0.671). Nocturnal ventilatory support was used by 6 patients in DMD_tot and 5 in DMD_SF36 (p = 0.442).

Muscle Strength and EEFES

In the DMD_tot group, median MRC scores were: shoulder abductors, 2 (IQR = 1–4); elbow flexors, 3.5 (IQR = 2–4); elbow extensors, 3.5 (IQR = 2–4); wrist flexors, 4 (IQR = 4–5); and wrist extensors, 4 (IQR = 3.75–5) as summarized in Table S3.

In the DMD_SF36 group, shoulder abduction strength was 2 (IQR = 1–3.75); elbow flexors, 3.5 (IQR = 2–4); elbow extensors, 3.5 (IQR = 2.75–4); wrist flexors, 4.5 (IQR = 4–5); and wrist extensors, 4.5 (IQR = 4–5).

No significant differences were observed between groups for any muscle group (all p > 0.86).

EEFES results, as detailed in Fig. 2 and Table S1, showed a median total functionality score of 51% (IQR = 28–71%) in DMD_tot and 53% (IQR = 35–74%) in DMD_SF36 (p = 0.972). The proximal domain scored 0% (IQR = 0–25%) in both groups (p = 0.861). Middle domain scores were 80% (IQR = 24–96%) in DMD_tot and 66% (IQR = 38–94%) in DMD_SF36 (p = 0.861). Distal domain scores were 100% (IQR = 63–100%) in DMD_tot and 100% (IQR = 79–100%) in DMD_SF36 (p = 0.992). Discussion

We assessed upper limb functionality and HRQoL in adolescents with DMD, mostly in non-ambulatory stages, from a middle-income country. While overall functionality was reduced, only certain EEFES dimensions and HRQoL domains were significantly affected. Age was associated with deterioration in both upper limb function and HRQoL, whereas corticosteroid and disease-modifying drug use correlated with better upper limb function. Our findings align with reports from high-income countries, despite the use of a different functional assessment tool, and reveal novel associations with specific HRQoL dimensions.

Our results showed a mean upper limb functionality of 51% using the EEFES scale. Comparable data from high-income countries are lacking, though studies using other tools, such as the PUL scale, reported 44% functionality in similar age groups 1 . Standardized cross-country assessments are needed to enable robust comparisons across different income settings.

In line with prior PUL 2.0 findings23,24, proximal limb function was more impaired than distal function. A possible ceiling effect was noted in the distal and functional EEFES domains, limiting sensitivity to subtle changes but aligning with the distal preservation typical of DMD. Our patients showed 0% proximal function on EEFES, whereas a PUL-based study reported 16%. Nevertheless, these results are not directly comparable, as differences in scale sensitivity, patient age, or disease stage may have influenced the observed outcomes.

The observed age-related decline in EEFES scores matches longitudinal evidence of progressive upper limb loss1 and Brooke scale trends9.

Corticosteroid use correlated with better function, as in larger PUL 2.0 studies3,25. However, a causal association cannot be directly established given the cross-sectional design of our study. A longitudinal study associated corticosteroid use with slower PUL decline and preservation of key HRQoL abilities, consistent with our findings, and linking hand-to-mouth movements with physical and social HRQoL domains. Despite the small sample, disease-modifying drugs were also linked to improved distal function, consistent with prior reports26. Although disease-modifying therapy use was rare (n = 3), its potential benefit warrants further investigation.

Compared with controls, DMD patients reported poorer physical health, consistent with disease progression, and older age predicted worse physical functioning scores. Depression was not associated with HRQoL. A study using the SF36v2 found reduced physical functioning and general health in patients with DMD as in our study; unlike our results, social functioning and mental health were also impaired18 . They suggest that targeted physical support may preserve quality of life. Interestingly, healthy adolescents scored lower in mental health than DMD patients. Similar age-related physical functioning PF decline with preserved MH mental health has been reported in European SF36v2 studies18,27. Another study using the PedsQL similarly reported that DMD patients can maintain good HRQoL through adaptive mechanisms over time28 . Protective factors such as social support and resilience have been shown to promote better quality of life in patients with DMD29 .On the contrary, recent Chilean studies indicate over 60% of children and adolescents reporting significant symptoms of depression, anxiety, or stress30,31. These findings may partially account for the lower mental health HRQoL observed in our cohort on the SF36v2, even in the absence of formally diagnosed psychiatric disorders.

Prior work with SF36v2, and other motor-function scales has shown links between upper limb function and HRQoL, particularly in social functioning and physical functioning domains as we observed in our study18. In our cohort, EEFES correlated most strongly with the items physical functioning 10 (self-care), general health 1 (general health perception), and general health 2 (current vs. prior health), hilighting their potential value in clinical monitoring and trial endpoints.

Reports from other low- and middle-income countries help contextualize our findings. Brazilian patients scored 56.8% on PUL 1.2 and 76.2% on PUL-Br6,32, while Indian patients—generally younger—reported HRQoL scores near 77% using the PedsQL Neuromuscular Module33. Despite limited resources, HRQoL and upper limb function decline in patterns similar to those in high-income settings8. Prioritizing physical functioning and general health domains, especially physical functioning 10, general health 1, and general health 2, may optimize interventions.

A previous survey, conducted in a setting with limited resources, identified several determinants of quality of life among people with disabilities: higher education, living in a family household, stable employment, being aged 18–25 years (compared to under 18), and access to rehabilitation34 . In Chile, these factors should be further explored in the future.

This study’s limitations include its small sample size, typical in rare disease research, and the challenge of comparing across functional assessment tools with differing structures.

Conclusion

Using the EEFES instrument, we identified markedly reduced upper limb functionality in adolescents with Duchenne muscular dystrophy, with the greatest impairment in proximal function. Specific SF36v2 items showed high sensitivity and strong correlations with upper limb function, supporting their potential as targeted outcome measures in clinical trials and for longitudinal monitoring. The exceptionally low proximal scores in our cohort may reflect limited access to therapies, although other factors could also have contributed, highlighting the need to further investigating the implementation of standards of care in Chile. EEFES, being cost-free and easy to administer, appears particularly suited for clinical and research use in Latin America.
