Different irrigation solutions in calcium hydroxide removal: Do high-frequency ultrasonic and laser activation make the difference?
Authors: Ayşe Tuğba Eminsoy Avcı, İpek Eraslan Akyüz, Hüseyin Sinan Topçuoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to comparatively evaluate the effectiveness of different irrigation solutions and activation techniques in removing calcium hydroxide (CH) from standardized artificial grooves located in the apical third of root canals. Ninety extracted single-rooted human maxillary central incisors were instrumented using a standardized preparation protocol. Artificial grooves were created in the apical third of split roots and filled with CH paste. Specimens were randomly divided into two irrigation groups: 1.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) + 0.1% octenidine dihydrochloride (OCT) or 1.5% NaOCl + 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Each group was further subdivided according to the activation technique: conventional needle irrigation (CNI), passive ultrasonic activation (PUA), or laser-activated irrigation (LAI). Data were analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05). Significant differences were observed among the groups (p < 0.001). OCT combined with PUA showed the lowest residual CH scores and performed significantly better than OCT + CNI. OCT demonstrated superior CH removal compared with EDTA under CNI and PUA conditions. The OCT + PUA protocol was the most effective approach for CH removal.Keywords: Calcium hydroxide removal, Laser activated irrigation, Octenidine dihydrochloride, Passive ultrasonic activation

Introduction

The primary objective of endodontic treatment is to promote the healing of periapical tissues by eliminating vital and necrotic pulp tissues, microorganisms, and microbial toxins from the root canal system [1]. The complex anatomical configuration of the root canal system makes it difficult for mechanical instrumentation alone to contact all canal surfaces and achieve complete debridement [2]. Therefore, the success of root canal treatment largely depends on the application of an effective irrigation and disinfection protocol in conjunction with mechanical preparation [3]. However, despite the completion of chemomechanical procedures, residual microorganisms within the root canal system may persist, leading to refractory infections and treatment failure [4]. Consequently, the use of interappointment intracanal medicaments has become an indispensable component of endodontic therapy [1].

Calcium hydroxide (CH) is the most commonly used intracanal medicament in endodontics due to its high pH, broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, tissue-dissolving properties, and ability to induce hard tissue formation [3, 5]. CH exerts its antimicrobial effect by inactivating lipopolysaccharides in the bacterial cell wall and releasing hydroxyl ions thereby creating an environment unfavorable for microbial survival [6]. However, despite its well-documented clinical benefits complete removal of CH from the canal walls prior to obturation is of critical importance. Residual CH remnants on the canal walls may hinder the penetration of root canal sealers into the dentinal tubules, reduce the bond strength of the filling material to dentin, and promote apical leakage, thereby adversely affecting the long-term success of treatment [3, 5, 7, 8]. In addition it has been reported that remaining intracanal medicament residues may alter the physical properties and setting reactions of root canal sealers [3].

The most commonly used approach for the removal of CH involves the application of irrigation solutions such as sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) using conventional needle irrigation (CNI) [3, 8]. EDTA is a potent chelating agent capable of dissolving the smear layer and inorganic CH by binding calcium ions [8]. However numerous studies have demonstrated that irrigation performed solely with a syringe and needle is insufficient to completely remove CH from hard-to-reach areas of the root canal system particularly irregularities in the apical third, isthmuses, and lateral canals [2, 8, 9]. The effectiveness of needle irrigation is limited by the depth of needle insertion and the canal diameter which restricts adequate irrigant flow and agitation in the apical region [4, 10]. These limitations have prompted the development of various activation techniques and alternative irrigation solutions aimed at enhancing the efficacy of root canal irrigation.

In recent years various devices have been developed to enhance the effectiveness of root canal irrigation including sonic, ultrasonic, and laser activated systems. Passive ultrasonic activation (PUA) is one of the methods widely regarded as a gold standard in the literature, as it increases irrigant contact with the canal walls and improves its efficacy by generating acoustic streaming and cavitation effects [2, 5]. Recently introduced wireless ultrasonic devices such as the Ultra X (Changzhou Sifary Medical Technology, China) operate at high frequencies of approximately 45 kHz, aiming to generate strong acoustic microstreaming and thereby achieve more effective cleaning in complex root canal anatomies [1, 5].

Laser activation has also emerged as a promising approach for root canal disinfection and intracanal medicament removal. Diode lasers equipped with thin fiber tips can reach deep within the canal and enhance cleaning efficacy by inducing cavitation and thermal effects in irrigation solutions [11, 12]. Irrigants activated by laser energy can generate shock waves that dislodge debris and medicament residues from the canal walls [9, 10].

Regarding irrigation solutions, concerns over the potential for dentin erosion and the cytotoxic effects of EDTA have encouraged the investigation of more biocompatible alternatives [2, 7]. In this context octenidine dihydrochloride (OCT) has gained attention in dentistry due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and low toxicity. In addition, OCT has demonstrated potential in smear layer removal and improving dentinal tubule cleanliness, which may influence the interaction between the dentin surface and root canal sealers [13]. Although the effectiveness of OCT particularly when used in combination with NaOCl on smear layer and intracanal medicament removal has not yet been fully elucidated, its tissue-friendly properties render it a promising alternative for root canal irrigation. As a secondary objective, the effectiveness of OCT in intracanal medicament removal was also evaluated. This was a secondary objective to investigate whether it had this potential.

While numerous studies exist in the current literature regarding the removal of CH there is limited research comparing the efficacy of next generation activation systems in combination with different irrigation protocols.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to comparatively evaluate the effectiveness of three different activation techniques (CNI, PUA and LAI) in combination with two different irrigation regimens (1.5% NaOCl + 0.1% OCT and 1.5% NaOCl + 17% EDTA) for the removal of CH medicament using a standardized artificial groove model created in human teeth. The first null hypothesis of the study is that there will be no statistically significant difference between the different irrigation solutions in the removal of CH. The second null hypothesis of the study is that there will be no statistically significant difference between the various activation techniques in the removal of CH.

Materials and methods

Sample size and power analysis

The sample size for this study was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on power analysis referenced from data of a similar study in the literature, the effect size (f) was set at 0.52, with a type I error probability of 5% (α = 0.05) and a power of 95% (1–β = 0.95) [14]. The calculations indicated that a minimum of 14 specimens per group were required to detect a statistically significant difference between the groups. To compensate for potential specimen loss and to maintain the statistical power of the study, a total of 90 single-rooted human maxillary central incisors were included, with 15 teeth allocated to each group.

Selection and preparation of samples

For this study, caries-free, single-rooted, single-canal maxillary central incisors extracted for periodontal reasons were selected. Soft tissue remnants and adherent debris on the root surfaces were removed using periodontal curettes and the teeth were stored in distilled water until use. The crowns were sectioned at the cementoenamel junction using diamond discs to obtain a standardized root length of 16 mm. The working length of the root canals was determined as 1 mm short of the point at which a #10 K-type file (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was visible at the apical foramen. The root canals were prepared up to an apical size of #40 using rotary systems (Race Evo, FKG Dentaire, Switzerland) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Between each file change, the root canals were irrigated with 2.5% NaOCl solution.

A 2.5% NaOCl solution was used during the instrumentation phase to facilitate routine chemomechanical preparation, whereas a 1.5% NaOCl solution was used during the experimental irrigation protocol following calcium hydroxide placement to standardize the experimental conditions and minimize the potential influence of NaOCl concentration on the comparative evaluation of EDTA and OCT.

Artificial groove model creation and medicament placement

Following completion of root canal shaping the specimens were prepared using the split-tooth model to simulate hard-to-reach areas of the root canal system such as isthmuses and lateral canals. Longitudinal grooves were created on the buccolingual surfaces of the roots using diamond discs, and the specimens were split into two halves with a chisel.

Standardized artificial grooves were created in the apical third of one root half at a fixed distance of 3 mm from the canal lumen with dimensions of 4 mm in length, 0.2 mm in width, and 0.5 mm in depth [15]. The artificial grooves were then filled with CH paste (UltraCal XS, Ultradent, St. Louis, MO, USA) and the root halves were reassembled using cyanoacrylate adhesive. Access cavities were filled with temporary filling material. After placement of the calcium hydroxide paste, the grooves were inspected using periapical radiography to confirm complete filling of the standardized cavities. The apical foramina were sealed with wax to simulate a closed-system environment. The specimens were then incubated at 37 °C in a 100% humid environment for 7 days.

Experimental groups and irrigation protocols

At the end of the incubation period, the temporary filling materials were removed from the root canals. For randomization, all specimens were assigned unique identification numbers, and the numbered specimens were randomly drawn from an opaque container and allocated to the six experimental groups (n = 15 per group). In all specimens, a total volume of 20 mL of active irrigant was used during the irrigation procedure, and the application time was standardized to 5 min.

Group 1 (NaOCl + OCT)

Specimens in this group were irrigated first with 1.5% NaOCl (10 mL) followed by 0.1% OCT (10 mL) (Schülke & Mayr GmbH, Germany).

Group 2 (NaOCl +EDTA)

In this group the specimens were irrigated respectively with 1.5% NaOCl (10 mL) and then with 17% EDTA (10 mL).

In both groups, an intermediate irrigation with 5 mL of distilled water was performed between NaOCl and the subsequent irrigant (EDTA or OCT) to minimize potential chemical interactions. A final irrigation with 5 mL of distilled water was then performed to terminate any remaining chemical interactions and remove residual irrigants.

Irrigation activation methods

All irrigation and activation procedures were performed by the same experienced operator to minimize operator-related variability. Due to the distinct nature of the experimental protocols, operator blinding to group allocation was not feasible.

In both main irrigation groups, the following activation techniques were applied in order to enhance the effectiveness of the irrigation solutions. Activation was performed in three 20-second cycles with 10-second intervals between cycles, resulting in a total activation time of 60 s.

Conventional Needle Irrigation (CNI): In this group no additional activation device was used. The irrigation solutions were delivered into the root canal using 30-gauge closed end, side vented endodontic needles (NaviTip, Ultradent, UT, USA). The needle was passively inserted to a depth 1 mm short of the working length and the irrigant was injected using gentle 2–3 mm up and down movements without binding to the canal walls allowing adequate backflow of the solution.

Conventional Needle Irrigation (CNI): In this group no additional activation device was used. The irrigation solutions were delivered into the root canal using 30-gauge closed end, side vented endodontic needles (NaviTip, Ultradent, UT, USA). The needle was passively inserted to a depth 1 mm short of the working length and the irrigant was injected using gentle 2–3 mm up and down movements without binding to the canal walls allowing adequate backflow of the solution.

Passive Ultrasonic Activation (PUA): A wireless ultrasonic activation device (Ultra X, Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) was used. Flexible tips with a size of 25/0.02 (Gold/Silver tip) were inserted into the root canal to a position 1 mm short of the working length.

Passive Ultrasonic Activation (PUA): A wireless ultrasonic activation device (Ultra X, Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) was used. Flexible tips with a size of 25/0.02 (Gold/Silver tip) were inserted into the root canal to a position 1 mm short of the working length.

Laser Activated Irrigation (LAI): A diode laser device with a wavelength of 940 nm (Epic X, Biolase Inc., CA, USA) was used for laser activation. A 200-µm diameter fiber optic tip (E200), suitable for endodontic applications, was employed during the activation procedure. A new 200-µm fiber optic tip was used for each specimen to avoid potential variations in laser delivery associated with fiber degradation, carbonization, or repeated use. The output power was set to 1 W according to the device console and was not independently verified using an external power meter. The laser parameters were set to continuous-wave mode with an output power of 1 W. Continuous-wave mode was selected to provide continuous energy delivery and predominantly photothermal activation of the irrigant, rather than the high-peak-power photoacoustic streaming mechanism associated with pulsed erbium laser systems such as Er/PIPS. This setting is consistent with previously reported endodontic protocols using 940-nm diode lasers at approximately 1 W in continuous-wave mode [16]. The fiber tip was advanced to a position 1 mm short of the working length and activated. During each 20-s activation cycle, the fiber was repeatedly moved from the apical to the coronal direction using continuous circular (spiral) movements at a speed of approximately 2 mm/s. After each coronal withdrawal, the fiber was repositioned apically and the movement was repeated continuously until completion of the 20-s activation period. This procedure was performed for three 20-s cycles with 10-s intervals between cycles, resulting in a total laser activation time of 60 s per specimen. A total nominal energy delivery of 60 J per specimen. Considering the 200-µm fiber diameter, the nominal cumulative radiant exposure at the fiber output surface was approximately 191 kJ/cm². Because the fiber tip was continuously moved within the root canal, this value represents the nominal radiant exposure at the fiber output surface rather than the actual local energy density delivered to a specific area of the canal wall or irrigant.

Evaluation of calcium hydroxide removal efficiency

After completion of the irrigation and activation procedures the root canals were dried using paper points. The root halves were then separated again and the CH residues within the artificial grooves were examined under an operating microscope (Zeiss OPMI Pico, Oberkochen, Germany) at 25× magnification. The obtained images were transferred to a computer using a digital camera.

The amount of CH residue remaining on the canal walls was evaluated using a four-grade scoring system described by Lee et al. [17]. Score 0; there was no medicament in the groove, Score 1; medicament was present in less than half of the groove, Score 2; medicament was present in more than half of the groove, Score 3; the groove was entirely filled with medicament.

Two endodontists with five years of clinical experience evaluated the extent of CH removal within the grooves. The evaluators were blinded to the group allocation during the scoring procedure. In cases of disagreement the images were re-evaluated and a consensus score was determined.

Statistical analysis

This study was analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) software package. The distribution of residual CH scores was assessed using descriptive statistics and was found to be non-normally distributed (Table 1). Intergroup comparisons among the six experimental groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When statistically significant differences were detected, subgroup analyses were conducted. Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction, where applicable. Comparisons between EDTA and OCT within the same activation technique were carried out using the Mann–Whitney U test. Descriptive data were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A statistically significant difference was observed among the six experimental groups with respect to residual CH scores (Kruskal–Wallis test, p < 0.001), as illustrated in Fig. 1.

When the EDTA groups were evaluated separately, no statistically significant difference was detected among the different activation techniques (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.059).

In contrast, a significant difference was observed among the OCT subgroups (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.040). Pairwise comparisons revealed that the OCT + PUA group demonstrated significantly lower residual CH scores than the OCT + CNI group (p = 0.013). In contrast, no significant differences were found between the LAI and other OCT groups.

Comparisons between EDTA and OCT, conducted under identical activation techniques, revealed that OCT resulted in significantly lower residual CH scores under both CNI (p = 0.005) and PUA (p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant difference was detected between the EDTA and OCT in LAI groups (p = 0.072).

Regardless of the activation method, OCT resulted in significantly lower residual calcium hydroxide scores than EDTA (p < 0.001). Regarding activation methods, both PUA and LAI performed significantly better than CNI (p = 0.006 and p = 0.014; respectively), whereas no significant difference was observed between PUA and LAI (p = 0.926). Discussion

In this study the effectiveness of different irrigation solutions and activation systems in the removal of CH was evaluated using an artificial groove model which is widely employed in the literature. This model was preferred because it allows standardized simulation of hard-to-reach areas of the root canal system and enables direct visualization of residual material before and after irrigation. Numerous studies have emphasized that no single technique is capable of completely removing CH from the canal walls [1, 8]. Our findings are consistent with this general consensus as varying amounts of medicament residue were observed in all experimental groups.

One of the most notable findings of the study was that the use of OCT particularly when combined with CNI and PUA resulted in statistically significantly lower residual CH scores compared with EDTA. Traditionally EDTA has been regarded as a chelating agent that enhances the solubility of CH by binding calcium ions [18]. However concerns regarding its potential to cause excessive dentinal erosion and its cytotoxic properties have prompted the search for alternative agents [19]. OCT is well known for its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and tissue friendly characteristics [20, 21]. Findings suggest that OCT may serve not only as an antiseptic but also as a strong alternative to EDTA in the medicament removal process when combined with appropriate activation methods. Moreover, the superiority of OCT over EDTA was not only statistically significant but also demonstrated a moderate-to-large effect size (r = 0.49), suggesting that this difference may be of practical clinical relevance. In particular, the OCT + PUA combination yielded the lowest residual scores (median: 0), indicating that this protocol may possess superior cleaning potential under the experimental conditions. OCT is not a chelating agent; therefore, its effectiveness may be related to physicochemical properties such as improved wettability, reduced surface tension, and enhanced penetration into irregular canal areas. Furthermore, the sequential use of NaOCl and OCT may facilitate the mechanical displacement of calcium hydroxide residues through improved irrigant dynamics. Since these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study, further investigations examining the physicochemical interactions between OCT, NaOCl, and calcium hydroxide are required.

With respect to activation systems CNI demonstrated the lowest effectiveness in both irrigation solution groups. This can be attributed to the limited flow dynamics and the formation of a dead water zone created by needle irrigation in the apical region [4, 22]. During needle irrigation the effective action of the irrigant generally extends only 1–2 mm beyond the needle tip which may allow CH to remain within the apical third and artificial grooves [14, 23].

In contrast ultrasonic activation with Ultra X significantly enhanced cleaning efficacy particularly when used in combination with OCT. The Ultra X device generates strong acoustic microstreaming and cavitation effects within the irrigant [24]. These physical phenomena increase the wall shear stress exerted by the solution on the canal walls thereby facilitating the mechanical detachment of medicament residues [25, 26]. Güven et al. and Adl et al. reported that Ultra X achieved superior CH removal in the apical region compared with conventional PUA or sonic systems such as the EndoActivator [1, 8]. Results are consistent with these reports supporting the notion that the high operating frequency of Ultra X may provide an advantage in dislodging medicaments from anatomically complex areas.

LAI also emerged as an effective method in our study; however, no statistically significant difference was observed between EDTA and OCT within the LAI group. Unlike erbium laser systems, which primarily rely on photoacoustic and cavitation effects, the 940-nm diode laser used in the present study mainly acts through photothermal energy transfer to the irrigant. This photothermal activation may enhance irrigant dynamics, improve penetration into inaccessible areas of the root canal system, and facilitate the displacement of calcium hydroxide remnants. Previous studies have demonstrated that diode laser activation can enhance irrigant penetration, improve canal cleanliness, and increase sealer penetration into dentinal tubules through thermal activation and enhanced fluid movement [27, 28]. Therefore, it is conceivable that the physical effects generated by diode laser activation partially masked the chemical differences between EDTA and OCT, resulting in comparable calcium hydroxide removal efficacy between the two irrigants.

When compared with other studies in the literature, Arıcan et al. reported that PUA and sonic activation (EDDY) were more effective than CNI, whereas Edwards et al. identified LAI as the most effective method in the apical region [8, 10]. The superior success exhibited by Ultra X in combination with OCT in the present study indicates that the high frequency of the device maximizes the penetration and debridement capacity of the antiseptic solution. Similarly a study by Güven et al. found that Ultra X and Eddy systems were more effective than EndoActivator and Endosonic Blue [1].

However the present study has several limitations. As an in vitro investigation it may not fully replicate the complex interactions with vital tissues, periapical pressure conditions, and the anatomical variability of the root canal system encountered in clinical practice. In addition the exclusive focus on artificial grooves located in the apical third provides limited information regarding the overall cleaning efficacy throughout the entire root canal system. This scoring system has been widely used in the literature for the evaluation of CH removal due to its practical applicability, greater reproducibility, and high interobserver agreement compared with other scoring methods [29–32]. However this scoring system does not allow accurate assessment of CH removal in terms of depth. In this study both observers performed the scoring independently and in cases of disagreement they consulted to reach a consensus.

Conclusion

This study has shown that OCT demonstrated superior performance compared with conventional EDTA in the removal of CH from artificial grooves in the apical region. Among the activation methods evaluated PUA and LAI provided more effective cleaning than CNI. Notably the combination of OCT and PUA was identified as the most successful protocol for minimizing CH residues. These findings suggest that combining high-frequency ultrasonic activation with alternative antiseptic solutions may be advantageous for clinicians when preparing a cleaner dentin surface prior to the placement of bioactive materials and root canal sealers.
