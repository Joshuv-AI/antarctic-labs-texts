Harmonized Acquisition of Connectome MRI and Clinical Data for Stroke, Parkinson's Disease and Glioma: Delphi‐Derived Protocol Recommendations by the European Academy of Neurology
Authors: Thomas Berger, Lorenzo Pini, Alessandro Salvalaggio, Simone Cauzzo, Christine Klein, Maria Lolich, Alberto Picca, Dauren Ramankulov, Maria A Rocca, Roberta Rudà, Alessandro Sodero, Alessandro Tessitore, Thomas Truelsen, Roland Wiest, Gereon R Fink, Elena Moro, Philippe Ryvlin, Maurizio Corbetta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction and AimsThe European Academy of Neurology (EAN) joined the Horizon Europe EBRAINS 2.0 research consortium to co‐develop recommendations for prospective acquisition and integration of clinical and research core multi‐scale human datasets for connectome analysis in stroke, Parkinson's disease (PD), and glioma (GBM).MethodsUsing an online Delphi survey method, expert consensus was sought on 28 statements for each of the following protocols in stroke, PD and GBM: clinical core/research brain MRI, minimum/extended clinical outcomes, minimum/extended cognition assessments, case report forms (CRF). The level of agreement for each statement was predefined as ≥ 80%. 536 experts from four EAN Scientific Panels (SP) (SP Neuro‐oncology [n = 70], SP Movement Disorders [n = 206], SP Neuroimaging [n = 112], SP Stroke [n = 148]) were invited to participate in this Delphi process.ResultsTwo Delphi voting rounds were conducted to reach the predefined level of agreement (≥ 80%; range: 80.49%–100%) for all items to establish consensus on protocol recommendations for stroke, PD and GBM. Cumulative response rate from SP experts was 25% (n = 135 from 30 European countries) for the first, and 32% (n = 173 from 35 European countries) for the second Delphi voting round.DiscussionThese consented protocols provide a pragmatic framework for harmonizing multi‐scale data acquisition for connectome analyses in stroke, PD, and GBM. Further, these protocols allow future modular extensions for other datasets, e.g., neurophysiological data or non‐imaging biomarkers, and serve as a role model for brain network analyses in other neurological disorders.Keywords: connectome, data harmonization, data sharing, digital health, network neuroscience

Introduction

The increasing availability of large‐scale, multimodal health data presents valuable opportunities to advance neurological research and improve clinical care in Europe. However, meaningful integration and analysis of such data, i.e., conventional, functional and advanced imaging, clinical measures and their outcomes, require harmonized acquisition protocols and standardized procedures across institutions and countries. Variability in clinical and research practices (particularly in the acquisition, management, and sharing of neurological data) remains a significant barrier to reproducibility, interoperability, and large‐scale collaborative research within Europe.

Within this context, the EBRAINS 2.0 consortium partnered with the European Academy of Neurology (EAN) to support a specific EBRAINS 2.0 research task, namely the development of standard operating procedures (SOPs) to standardize acquisition protocols and facilitate harmonization of prospective health data across both routine clinical and research settings, enabling consistent approaches to data collection for connectome analyses for three common neurological conditions: stroke, Parkinson's disease (PD), and glioma, specifically glioblastoma (GBM). These three conditions were predefined by the scope of EBRAINS 2.0 Work Package 2 and were selected as complementary exemplars: stroke and glioma represent two focal disorders of distinct etiology and constitute the pillar of the task, whereas PD serves as a proof‐of‐concept for neurodegenerative conditions. This design was intended to provide prototype protocols that can subsequently be extended to other neurological and psychiatric diseases.

To ensure that the proposed protocols reflect broad European neurological expert consensus and are feasible across diverse clinical institutions, including research settings, a structured consensus methodology is required. The Delphi method is a well‐established technique for systematically eliciting and refining expert opinion through iterative rounds of evaluation and feedback, allowing consensus to emerge among geographically distributed specialists [1, 2].

The resulting consented protocols for clinical core/research brain MRI, minimum/extended clinical outcomes, minimum/extended cognitive assessments, and case report forms are intended to serve as recommended templates for the acquisition, management, and analysis of large‐scale, multimodal human datasets. By promoting harmonized data practices, the proposed framework aims to facilitate data acquisition in routine settings, enable collaborative research, enhance reproducibility, and support the development of advanced analytical approaches in European neurology.

While the clinical protocol is intended to provide a feasible acquisition framework (≤ 20 min) suitable for routine clinical settings, ensuring standardized structural imaging and minimum clinical/cognitive assessments across European centers, the research protocol is designed to enable comprehensive connectome reconstruction through advanced multi‐modal imaging sequences (≤ 40 min), including resting‐state functional brain MRI, multi‐shell diffusion‐weighted imaging, and perfusion mapping, supporting network‐level analyses of brain structure and function in stroke, PD, and GBM.

A central rationale for the development of harmonized research protocols lies in the growing recognition that the brain connectome (i.e., the comprehensive map of structural and functional connections), represents a shared biological substrate underlying diverse neurological diseases (Figure 1). Although stroke, PD and GBM, have fundamentally different etiology, in each of them the analysis of the connectome provides valuable insights into network disruption and reorganization that affect clinical presentation, progression and prognosis [3, 4, 5, 6].

In stroke, the brain connectome provides a framework for understanding how focal vascular lesions produce widespread network disruptions far beyond the site of injury. This phenomenon, known as “diaschisis”, manifests as altered functional and structural connectivity between remote, structurally intact brain regions [7, 8]. Critically, the degree of post‐stroke functional connectivity normalization has been identified as a key biomarker of recovery, with the emergence of a reorganized “recovery connectome” supporting regained functions rather than a simple restoration of pre‐stroke network architecture [8, 9].

In GBM, the connectome perspective has revealed that brain tumors cannot be considered purely focal pathologies but rather diseases of large‐scale brain networks. GBM infiltrates and perturbs structural and functional connectivity beyond visible tumor margins, producing remote network disruption analogous to stroke‐related diaschisis [3, 5]. Importantly, structural connectivity measures from normative (and harmonized) imaging protocols have demonstrated prognostic value, with white matter tract density at the lesion site significantly predicting overall survival [10, 11, 12]. Moreover, experimental evidence has shown that glioma cells form functional synapses with neurons and exploit activity‐dependent plasticity mechanisms, positioning the tumor as an active pathological node within the connectome [13].

In neurodegenerative diseases, the connectome is a central feature [4, 14, 15, 16], serving as a potential biological substrate for the spread of misfolded proteins and with therapeutic implications [17, 18]. Regarding PD, α‐synuclein aggregates, the molecular hallmark of this disorder, are hypothesized to propagate along structurally and functionally connected brain networks in a “prion‐like” manner, thereby determining the onset and progression of both motor and non‐motor symptoms of the disease [19]. Consistent with this, longitudinal analyses suggest that connectivity changes may also precede cognitive symptoms, supporting a model in which connectome disruptions mediate cognitive decline [20]. More recently, emerging evidence links alterations in the so‐called somato‐cognitive action network with PD pathophysiology, in which hyperconnectivity between disease‐affected nuclei and this large‐scale circuit correlates with disease severity and treatment response [6].

These findings underscore the need for standardized multi‐modal acquisition protocols (both conventional, functional, and advanced imaging for connectome measurements) to enable network‐based prognostic and therapeutic approaches in stroke, neuro‐oncology, and neurodegenerative conditions.

Methods

Consensus on best‐practice protocols for prospective data acquisition was developed using a structured Delphi panel methodology involving iterative rounds of expert evaluation for stroke, PD and GBM combined with three protocol tiers (“Core”, “Extended”, and “Case Report Form”). The candidate sequences and protocol items were first developed centrally and in a shared manner by the EBRAINS 2.0/WP2 group, constituting a common transdiagnostic framework. From this shared pool, the Delphi voting was subsequently performed separately for each condition by the respective expert panels.

For each neurological condition, a total of seven protocols underwent the Delphi process:

1. Clinical Core MRI Protocol; 2. Research Core MRI Protocol; 3. Minimum Clinical Outcome Protocol; 4. Extended Clinical Outcome Protocol; 5. Minimum Cognition Assessment Protocol; 6. Extended Cognition Assessment Protocol; 7. Case Report Form (CRF).

Each item/question of the seven protocols offered five agreement categories (strongly agree/agree/neutral/disagree/strongly disagree) and a feedback/comment text field.

Steering Group and Delphi Process Multi‐Step Workflow

A Delphi process Steering Group composed by TB (EAN), MC (EBRAINS 2.0, Work Package [WP] 2 lead), LoP (WP2), PR (WP2), RR and AP (co‐chairs EAN SP Neuro‐oncology), CK and AT (co‐chairs EAN SP Movement Disorders), AS and TT (co‐chairs SP Stroke), MR and RW (co‐chairs EAN SP Neuroimaging), LuP, ML and DR (EAN Scientific Department‐EAN Head Office), prepared and developed the Delphi survey items, analyzed the voting results, integrated all comments/feedback, and wrote the final analysis of all Delphi survey rounds, the summary of consensus results and the final report.

The steering group pre‐defined consensus for every single protocol item/question as ≥ 80% agreement on “strongly agree” and “agree”.

Design, Content and Procedure of the Survey

To develop the consensus‐based protocol criteria, this study followed an iterative, quasi‐anonymous, multistage approach with controlled feedback according to the Delphi technique [21, 22, 23].

Initial voting items were developed from suggested protocols for clinical core/research MRI, minimum/extended clinical outcomes, minimum/extended cognition assessments, and CRFs received by the WP2 EBRAINS 2.0 partners for the three specified diseases.

Delphi voting questionnaires were set up in Survey Monkey: Online Survey Software.

With the invitation letter, accompanied by a cover sheet outlining Delphi's aims in the context of this project and a full description of the procedure, the SP experts received the SurveyMonkey link to access the first Delphi voting round. In the second round, a document with detailed feedback on responses from the first Delphi voting round was shared.

Protocol items, which did not reach the predefined ≥ 80% threshold of agreement (cumulative of “strongly agree” and “agree”) in the first voting round, were re‐asked, newly phrased, or newly added (based on participants' comments in the first voting round) by the steering group for the second voting round.

For the second voting round, participants were asked to refrain from voting “neutral” unless they intended to abstain. Thus, following an appropriate and usual methodological approach, for every question in the second voting round, the number of participants who had voted “neutral” was subtracted from the total number of participants for every question. In consequence, voting results for (strong) agreement/(strong) disagreement were recalculated based on the final number of participants (total voters for a particular question minus “neutral voter” for that particular question).

Delphi Panel Recruitment and Participants

The expert members of four EAN SP, i.e., Stroke, Movement Disorders, Neuro‐Oncology, and Neuroimaging represent broad geographical inclusion of subject‐matter expertise from 48 EAN member countries and ensure diverse clinical and research perspectives. All experts from the above‐mentioned four EAN SP were, thus, approached with a personalized electronic invitation to participate in this Delphi process. The final Delphi panel consisted of those SP members who declared no conflicts of interest and were interested to join their disease‐specific Delphi survey and to contribute to consensus development through structured, iterative feedback. Members of the SP Neuroimaging were invited to join all disease‐specific Delphi voting rounds.

Results

In total, 536 members of the four EAN SPs were invited to participate in this Delphi process. The cumulative response rate across the three disease protocols was 25% (n = 135 from 30 European countries; response rates from the respective SP between 16% and 19%) for the first Delphi and 32% (n = 173 from 35 European countries; response rates from the respective SP between 22% and 25%) for the second Delphi voting round.

Stroke Protocols

260 members of the EAN SPs Stroke (n = 148) and Neuroimaging (n = 112) were invited to validate the 7 stroke protocols. The results are presented below.

First Voting Round

The first Delphi voting round included 42 experts (response rate of 16%) representing 22 countries, all with recognized clinical and imaging expertise in stroke. A total of 16 out of 27 items reached the predefined consensus threshold of ≥ 80% agreement and were therefore excluded from the second voting round. In addition, based on participant feedback and review by the steering group, one item was exchanged for another test (Patient Health Questionnaire should replace the Geriatric depression scale), and three new items were introduced, resulting in a total of fourteen items included in the second voting round (eleven retained from the first round and three newly added).

Second Voting Round

The second Delphi voting round included 56 experts (response rate of 22%) representing 25 countries, reflecting sustained international engagement across both voting rounds.

In the second voting round, 14 questions were evaluated. After excluding “neutral” responses, a consensus of ≥ 80% agreement was achieved for all remaining questions except one, thereby completing the validation process for the seven stroke protocols.

Final Validated Protocols

PD Protocols

318 members of the EAN SPs Movement Disorders (n = 206) and Neuroimaging (n = 112) were invited to validate the 7 PD protocols.

First Voting Round

The first Delphi voting round included 62 experts (response rate of 19%) representing 27 countries, all with recognized clinical and imaging expertise in movement disorders, specifically PD. A total of 17 out of 25 items reached the predefined consensus threshold of ≥ 80% agreement and were therefore excluded from the second voting round. In addition, based on participant feedback and the steering group's review, no new items were introduced, resulting in a total of 8 items included in the second voting round.

Second Voting Round

The second Delphi voting round included 80 experts (response rate of 25%) representing 31 countries, reflecting sustained international engagement across both voting rounds.

Further, for one question, ≥ 80% agreement was achieved for the question to be deleted from the protocol. For two items, the predefined agreement threshold was not reached.

GBM Protocols

182 members of the EAN SPs Neuro‐Oncology (n = 70) and Neuroimaging (n = 112) were invited to validate the seven protocols for GBM.

First Voting Round

The first Delphi voting round included 32 experts (response rate of 27%) representing 14 countries, all with recognized clinical and imaging expertise in Neuro‐Oncology, specifically in glioma. A total of 22 out of 28 items reached the predefined consensus threshold of ≥ 80% agreement and were therefore excluded from the second voting round. In addition, based on participant feedback and the steering group's review, two new items were introduced, bringing the total to eight in the second voting round (six retained from the first round and two newly added).

Second Voting Round

The second Delphi voting round included 41 experts (response rate of 35%) representing 19 countries, reflecting sustained international engagement across both voting rounds.

In the second voting round, eight questions were evaluated. After excluding “neutral” responses, a consensus of ≥ 80% agreement was achieved across all remaining 8 questions, thereby completing the validation process for the seven glioma protocols.

Discussion

Consensus on core and extended protocol recommendations for stroke, PD, and GBM was achieved through a structured Delphi process involving brain MRI parameters, clinical outcomes, cognitive assessments, and CRF items. The resulting recommendations reflect broad expert agreement from a total of 35 European countries and define standardized data elements suitable for implementation across clinical and research settings in Europe.

These consensus‐based protocols provide a pragmatic framework for harmonized multi‐scale data acquisition to support neuroimaging, clinical, and neuropsychological analyses in routine clinical practice and in research applications across three major neurological diseases. The following sections discuss how the specific protocol components align with current evidence on both clinical features and network‐level mechanisms for each condition.

Clinical Core Protocols

The Core MRI protocols were designed to characterize key lesion features, including location and volume (in stroke and GBM), as well as associated pathological changes such as perilesional edema and blood–brain barrier disruption (GBM).

In PD, the core protocol has the main purpose of ruling out any underlying cause of symptomatic parkinsonism (e.g., vascular, traumatic, space‐occupying or toxic brain injury, hydrocephalus) and to support the identification of imaging markers of atypical Parkinsonism (e.g., the hummingbird sign, hot‐cross bun sign and putaminal atrophy) and early indicators of dementia, such as medial temporal lobe atrophy. Importantly, the core MRI protocol also provides the basis for indirect connectome analyses, obtained by embedding lesions (stroke, GBM) or regions of atrophy (PD) into normative structural and functional atlases derived from healthy populations [24, 25, 26]. These analyses allow identification of structurally and functionally disconnected brain regions and networks. Disconnection mapping can then be used for behavioral correlations both cross‐sectionally by identifying which disrupted networks best explain specific clinical or cognitive impairments, or longitudinally by predicting outcomes such as functional recovery after stroke, survival in GBM, or the onset of dementia in PD. We note, however, that the indirect lesion network mapping approach has recently been subject to important methodological and biological criticisms [27, 28]. This limitation further underscores the value of the patient‐specific connectome measurements enabled by the research protocols proposed here. A key strength of the core clinical MRI protocol is its efficiency: all sequences can be acquired in less than 20 min, enabling seamless integration into routine clinical workflows. Moreover, all sequences are standard on the most widely used MRI platforms, facilitating broad implementation across centers. It was beyond the scope of these recommendations to provide specific parameter recommendations (i.e., repetition time, echo time, inversion time or flip angle prescriptions), since MR protocol parameters vary across scanners and vendors as well as during hardware innovation cycles. Rather, our intention was to enable vendor agnostic and robust protocol recommendations to create a pre‐specified and balanced reading environment, as well as to facilitate automated and large‐scale quantitative image analysis across Europe. Our recommendations target harmonization at the point of acquisition (prospective harmonization). This is complementary to well‐established post hoc statistical (e.g., ComBat) or more recent deep‐learning harmonization methods [29], which aim to remove residual scanner‐ and site‐related variance after data collection. Prospective standardization reduces the non‐biological variability that such methods must subsequently model, an advantage that is particularly relevant for pathological datasets.

Research Connectome Core and Acquisition Principles

The research MRI protocol for each pathology was designed to enable direct physiological and structural assessment of disease effects on brain networks through resting‐state fMRI (for functional connectivity) and multi‐shell diffusion‐weighted imaging (for structural connectivity and microstructural white‐matter features). The central diffusion recommendation is the principle of acquiring multi‐shell, multi‐directional data, rather than any specific set of b‐values. These principles are shared across all three diseases. Multiple diffusion shells enable advanced non‐Gaussian diffusion models beyond the basic tensor (e.g., NODDI, DKI) and distributed gradient directions improve crossing fibers identification for robust tractography. The scheme reported here (b = 300/1000/2000 s/mm2, with approximately 10/32/64 directions per shell) is provided as one feasible example that balances modeling flexibility against a clinically realistic acquisition time. The precise shell and direction values may be adapted to the local scanner and research question without compromising harmonization. A similar general principle applies to fMRI, where a minimum duration is recommended for stable connectivity estimation. In both diffusion and functional imaging, isotropic voxels (equal size along all axes) are preferable to avoid orientation‐dependent biases in tractography and provide uniform spatial resolution for functional connectivity, respectively. Where appropriate, perfusion imaging further provides complementary metabolic information. These advanced measures are expected to become increasingly important as machine learning based‐approaches, such as foundation models, explanaible AI, and deep learning (especially convolutional and graph neural networks increasingly used for imaging), are integrated into classification and diagnostic pipelines, as they offer a substantially richer set of discriminative features. As with the clinical protocol, all sequences included in the research protocol are available on the major scanner platforms and can be acquired within a feasible time window (< 40 min). The 10‐min minimum for resting‐state fMRI reflects a consensus‐derived suggestion rather than a fixed threshold. This range offer a reasonable reliability trade‐off, while longer acquisitions are more reliable and enable more nuanced analyses. Clinical and cognitive protocols were designed to provide standardized characterization of neurological impairment and its longitudinal evolution across pathologies, as well as their impact on cognition, functional status, and disability. Impairment, function, and disability represent the three core axes of the World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning framework and are all recognized as critical outcome domains for evaluating the effectiveness of emerging therapies.

Disease‐Specific Considerations

For stroke, the research protocol is designed to capture the connectome signatures that have been shown to drive both acute impairment and long‐term recovery. The inclusion of resting‐state functional MRI and multi‐shell diffusion imaging enables the computation of both functional and structural connectomes. Current evidence demonstrates that stroke‐related behavioral deficits are strongly shaped by alterations in large‐scale brain networks rather than by lesion volume alone, and that the degree of functional connectivity normalization at follow‐up represents a meaningful biomarker of recovery potential [9, 30, 31, 32]. Furthermore, a recent longitudinal study has revealed that structural connectivity undergoes widespread degeneration in the months following stroke, while functional recovery appears to depend on synaptic reweighting of residual structural connections, a finding that underscores the complementary value of both diffusion and functional MRI sequences included in these protocols [33]. The addition of non‐contrast imaging (pCASL/ASL) and MR angiography in the stroke research protocol further enables the assessment of cerebrovascular architecture and its relationship with the structural and functional connectome, providing a multi‐modal characterization of the post‐stroke brain. Clinical outcome measures, including the NIHSS at admission and at 24–48 h and the mRS at 3 months, align with the time points at which informative connectivity changes have been documented [34], facilitating the development of predictive models linking acute network disruption to functional recovery trajectories.

For GBM, these protocols address the growing recognition that brain tumors produce a “disconnectome‐like” condition in which network‐level alterations critically shape clinical outcomes [3, 5]. The inclusion of multi‐shell diffusion imaging and resting‐state functional MRI enables the computation of both structural and functional connectivity measures that have demonstrated significant prognostic value. Notably, structural disconnection derived from white matter tract density at the lesion site has been shown to outperform conventional clinical predictors of overall survival, including lesion volume, degree of surgical resection, and genetic markers [10]. In contrast, indirect functional connectivity measures derived from normative datasets appear to have limited clinical predictive value in GBM [35], reinforcing the importance of patient‐specific, direct connectome assessments as enabled by the present protocols. Furthermore, the DSC T2* perfusion sequence included in the clinical core protocol provides information on cerebral blood volume that complements the connectomic assessment. The extended cognitive assessment battery (MoCA, ROCF, TMT, phonemic fluency, digit span) captures the cognitive domains most affected by network disruption in GBM patients, while the CRF items on genetic markers (IDH, MGMT) and WHO classification enable the integration of molecular and network‐level data for comprehensive prognostic modeling. The homogenized protocol will further enable the incorporation of automated and longitudinal lesion segmentation at larger scale and prepare for successful federated learning initiatives across Europe, as previously demonstrated in international network cooperations [36, 37].

For PD, the protocols are designed to capture the progressive network degeneration that characterizes this neurodegenerative condition. The resting‐state fMRI sequence in the research protocol enables the assessment of functional connectivity disruptions that have been consistently reported in PD [38], including altered connectivity between frontal and posterior cortical regions and disrupted integration within the default mode and sensorimotor networks [39, 40, 41]. The multi‐shell diffusion imaging protocol allows for the computation of structural connectomes and microstructural parameters that can reveal the axonal pathways along which α‐synuclein pathology may propagate [19, 42]. The SWI/SWAN sequence included in the clinical core protocol provides additional information through visualization of the nigrosome‐1 signal, which has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in the diagnosis of PD, making it a reliable imaging biomarker for distinguishing affected patients from healthy individuals. Finally, the clinical outcome scales (UPDRS, PDQ‐8) and extended assessments (SCOPA‐AUT) are well‐suited for tracking the motor and some non‐motor manifestations of network dysfunction across disease stages. In addition to the MoCA test, widely recognized as a comprehensive screening tool for cognitive impairment in patients with PD, the extended cognitive battery, including the ROCF, the Stroop Test, and the TMT, captures visuoconstructional ability, visual memory, and executive function deficits. These domains have been closely linked to connectivity alterations in PD and may serve as potential early predictors of cognitive decline and progression to dementia [43].

Cross‐Disease Design and Harmonization

A key strength of the present framework lies in its cross‐disease design. By adopting harmonized acquisition protocols across stroke, GBM, and PD, these recommendations enable direct comparisons of connectome alterations across conditions with fundamentally different etiologies. This approach is supported by accumulating evidence that these diseases share common patterns of network dysfunction, including the characteristic trajectory from initial hyperconnectivity to progressive network failure [44]. The shared MRI sequences (3D T1, T2‐FLAIR, resting‐state fMRI, multi‐shell DWI) across all three disease protocols allow for the extraction of comparable brain features, facilitating cross‐disease analyses. Crucially, resting‐state fMRI, for capturing the functional connectome, and multi‐shell DWI, for the structural connectome, represent the connectome core common to all three conditions. Furthermore, the use of MoCA as a minimum cognitive assessment provides a standardized cognitive endpoint that can be related to network measures across disease boundaries. This cross‐disease analytical capability is particularly relevant for identifying shared vulnerability patterns, such as the preferential involvement of network hubs and rich‐club connections observed across neurological disorders, and for developing transdiagnostic network‐based biomarkers that may inform therapeutic strategies applicable across multiple conditions.

Importantly, the framework is designed to remain adaptable, allowing modular extensions to incorporate additional data domains such as neurophysiological measures or non‐imaging biomarkers. Beyond the diseases addressed here, this approach may serve as a model for developing network‐oriented study frameworks for other neurological disorders.

These protocols will provide European neurologists with robust procedures for operating and securely sharing sensitive health data, while also strengthening data owners' confidence in their validity for both research and clinical applications. The broader vision is to enable not only major hub hospitals in tertiary medical centers, but also smaller spoke hospitals across Europe to actively participate in clinical research through GDPR‐compliant platforms for sensitive data sharing. By contributing to large‐scale AI models, Europe can leverage the unique strengths of its national health systems to play a leading global role in medical artificial intelligence and advanced health analytics.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although 536 experts from four EAN Scientific Panels were invited, the cumulative response rate was modest. This attrition may introduce self‐selection bias, with responders potentially differing from non‐responders (e.g., greater interest or expertise in connectomics), and participation may have been unevenly distributed across countries and panels despite representation from around thirty countries; future iterations may benefit from broader consultation. Second, some imaging metrics (e.g., diffusion‐based) should be interpreted with caution in pathological brains, as lesions, oedema, or tumor infiltration can increase measurement uncertainty, so structural connectome estimates require careful interpretation. Finally, the protocols were not benchmarked against existing large‐scale imaging initiatives (e.g., the Human Connectome Project); however, within EBRAINS 2.0 a multimodal connectome in healthy controls is being prospectively acquired and will provide a physiological reference against which disease‐related connectome alterations can be calibrated in future work.

Conclusions

In conclusion, by enabling standardized, multi‐modal data acquisition across European centers, the present framework facilitates the transition from traditional lesion‐based or region‐specific analyses to a network‐based understanding of neurological disease. This paradigm shift, from studying isolated brain regions to investigating large‐scale network dynamics, has the potential to reveal shared mechanisms of vulnerability and resilience across neurological conditions and to support the development of personalized, connectivity‐informed therapeutic interventions. The EBRAINS 2.0 research infrastructure, with its capacity for large‐scale data integration, multi‐center collaboration, and advanced computational modeling, provides the ideal platform for realizing this connectome‐based vision of European neurology.
