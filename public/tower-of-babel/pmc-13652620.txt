Reimagining UBE: Midline Biportal Endoscopic Approach with “Under the Cave” Decompression Technique for Lumbar Canal Stenosis—A Technical Note and Clinical Pearls
Authors: Atmaranjan Dash, Anurag Reddy Kancharla, Karansinh Parve Patil, Bhushan Meshram, Amaresh Prahallad, Adithya Vardhan Guduru, Shailesh Hadgaonkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this study was to describe the technique for midline biportal endoscopic decompression (MBED) for lumbar canal stenosis by a new “under the cave” decompression technique: a paradigm shift in biportal endoscopic decompression. Laminectomy with/without fusion and facet-preserving microforaminotomy has been performed as conventional surgical treatments for lumbar canal stenosis (LCS). Recently, endoscopic spinal surgery has been introduced as a minimally invasive therapeutic modality of LCS by several authors. Here, we describe the surgical steps and technical pearls of MBED with “under the cave” decompression technique in LCS, successfully treated with a modification to the novel unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) spine surgery technique, that is, by using a more midline approach using primary two portals. The authors included patients who presented with chronic onset of back pain and neurogenic claudication symptoms. They were diagnosed with LCS from magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography preoperatively. The surgery was performed via unilateral approach to achieve bilateral compression without additional skin incisions by “under the cave” decompression technique and primary two portals. This technique ensures complete decompression of the spinal canal as compared to the microscopic, tubular, and traditional UBE. Midline modification of the conventional BED done through the “under the cave” technique permits clear visualization of ipsilateral and contralateral sublaminar and foraminal areas. Owing to this enhanced exploratory capability it gives us a good outcome in terms of complete decompression of the ipsilateral and contralateral exiting roots while avoiding fusion in the elderly. The authors suggest that the new MBED, which is minimally invasive, is a good alternative treatment option for degenerative LCS.

Introduction

Unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) has been applied to address a variety of spinal pathologies, such as discectomy for lumbar disc herniation, laminotomy for degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis, lumbar interbody fusion for disc degeneration or spondylolisthesis, and cervical foramen stenosis, all of which demonstrated good clinical efficacy and satisfactory outcomes. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7

Lumbar stenosis is a common degenerative process among the elderly and can significantly limit their quality of life. Central canal stenosis results from the hypertrophy flavum and the inferior facet articular process of the cephalic vertebra. Lateral recess and foraminal stenosis are the result of the hypertrophy of the superior facet articular process of the caudal vertebra along with flaval hypertrophy. In case of lumbar canal stenosis (LCS), there is reduction in the flaval elasticity and increased fibrosis making it thickened and fold into the spinal canal under the lamina (telescoping), causing further narrowing of the canal. 8

The ligamentum flavum hypertrophy is considered a major contributor to the development of canal stenosis and its surgical removal can relieve the symptoms effectively. The subfacetal flavum, that is, the lateral ligamentum flavum, which usually causes compression over the exiting root, is inaccessible in the open, microscopic, tubular surgery and if left over results in incomplete decompression.

In this article, we have proposed a modification of the conventional UBE—the midline endoscopic biportal decompression (MEBD) with under the cave decompression technique, which the authors see as a modification of the over-the-top (OTT) approach. This concept reimagines the midline not as a single anatomical point but as a zone with multiple docking options, primarily favoring the left midline approach for most cases.

This minimally invasive technique is a revolutionary endoscopic technique, which uses a high-resolution endoscopic system which is only 4 mm, MEBD with “under the cave” technique delivers a clear, bright, and magnified surgical field, enabling surgeons to navigate the inaccessible zones, perform delicate surgical procedures, as well as advance the scope very close into the contralateral lateral recess and decompress the exiting root, aiding in the removal of the entire ligamentum flavum along with minimal destruction of the facet joints, that is, facet preserving. Hence, assisting in complete decompression without excessive soft tissue damage and avoiding the need for fusion in the elderly.

Endoscopic surgery has a steep learning curve and is technically demanding, having a high incidence of complications in the early learning curve. 9 10 The most common complication being dural injury, followed by epidural hematoma, incomplete decompression, and restenosis. 11

This article aims to explain that all these complications can be reduced by adopting the MBED technique with “under the cave approach.” Owing to the versatility of this technique, we can go sublaminar, release the flavum from the lamina, and navigate the scope through the gap created (like going through the tights of a cave) making it easier to approach these inaccessible areas and achieve complete decompression of the roots.

Surgical Technique

Side Determination and Side of Approach

In the initial cases, we approached the patient from the symptomatic side, but later all the cases were done from the left side with the surgeon standing on the left side of the patient as we achieved good bilateral lateral recess and foraminal decompression, resulting in adequate decompression of the bilateral exiting roots from the left side.

Equipment Used

During surgical procedures, we used 4-mm spherical diamond burrs (Medtronic, Midas Rex); 5-mm shaver (Stryker); 3.5-mm bendable diamond burs, and 0° 4.0-mm diameter arthroscopes (Arthrex). To control intraoperative bleeding, 90° 3.75-mm radiofrequency (RF) probe (BONSS) was used.

We also used specially designed serial dilators by the author himself (Gesco, Dr. Dash UBE set), rotating Kerrison punches (3.4 mm), pituitary forceps (standard and upbite), nerve hook, and variable angled chisels and curettes ( Figs. 1 and 2 ).

Discussion

LCS is the most common indication for surgery of the spine in patients over the age of 65 years. 12 13 Lumbar stenosis pathophysiology involves a complex degenerative process that leads to compression of neural elements from a combination of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, intervertebral disc herniation, and facet thickening with arthropathy of the capsule soft tissues. With recent advances in imaging, it has become evident that most of these pathologic compressive changes typically occur at the level of the interlaminar window. 14

The traditional treatment of lumbar stenosis, which has been used with varying degrees of success, involves resection of spinous process, lamina, portions of facet joints and capsule, and the ligamentum flavum, that is, decompressive laminectomy, medial facetectomy, and foraminotomy.

Although open surgeries provide adequate decompression, anatomical supporting tissues including muscles and ligaments, may suffer adverse effects from a traditional open decompressive laminectomy. Back pain and muscular atrophy following surgery might result from injury to the surrounding tissues. 15

Hence, minimally invasive surgical techniques have been developed to avoid destruction to the posterior stabilizing structures and minimize tissue damage. Bilateral decompression via unilateral laminotomy was the most frequently used decompression method in degenerative LCS (DLCS).

However, a microscopic approach includes the need for dissection of muscle and difficulties in contralateral visualization and complete decompression in patients. It also restricts the surgeon's view outside the spinal canal and limits the range of motion of the surgical instruments, making it difficult to decompress the contralateral exiting and traversing nerve roots.

In contrast, UBE has the advantage of handling and movement of the instruments freely, plus allowing the use of standard laminectomy instruments through the working portal, making it similar to open microscopic surgery but without retraction of muscle and maintaining the facet integrity. 4 16

The traditional UBE technique proposed by Dr. Jwo-Luen Pao, has two skin incisions taken along the medial pedicle line, separated by 2 to 3 cm ( Figs. 1 and 6C ). He uses serial dilators up to 10 mm to split the paraspinal muscles, enlarge the instrument portal, and gently detach the soft tissues off the interlaminar space. 1

In contrast, the authors' reason for adopting a more midline approach (MBED technique), helped in avoiding any muscle entrapment or injury to the paraspinal muscles and directly entering the son's space. Eventually assisting the surgeon to reach the spinolaminar junction fast and achieve triangulation with ease, even in cases of severe canal stenosis (shorter “incision-to-target” distance) ( Fig. 13 ).

Black and red arrows showing the viewing and working portal access to the site of laminotomy without disturbing the multifidus muscle ( B ).

Next is the facet joints complex (including the synovial facet joint and the joint capsule), which is the most important among the posterior stabilizing structures. Biomechanical tests have demonstrated that more than 50% of facet joint destruction can lead to segmental instability. During lumbar decompression surgery, the extent of preservation of the bilateral facet joint is an important factor for maintaining spinal stability. 17

In the UBE technique, when decompression is performed using unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression, the approach side experiences greater facet joint damage compared to the opposite side. With the conventional UBE technique, the preservation rate of the facet joint was 80% on the approach side and 90% on the opposite side. 1 In their study, they have given that the facet joint preservation was 92.9% on the contralateral side and 84.2% on the approach side. It is unavoidable that facet joint destruction is more severe on the approach side. 1

The author claims that this ipsilateral facet destruction also can be prevented with the MBED technique. As being more toward the midline gives more freedom for the scope to access the ipsilateral side facets as compared to the traditional UBE technique where the incisions are taken along the medial pedicle line. Hence, the lateral recess decompression can be done with little facet undercutting like the contralateral side (mirror image decompression) ( Figs. 14 and 15 ). Fig. 14 shows the pre- and postoperative computed tomography images of facet joints showing almost 100% preservation amounting to equal facet preservation on both sides. Fig. 15 shows postoperative three-dimensional reconstruction images of the same patient. As this was written as a technical note, we could not include other postoperative scans as we do postoperative scans only in case any postoperative complications arise.

For these technical reasons, the author has chosen the MBED technique, which is a variation of the conventional UBE decompression (UBED), integrating both microscopic unilateral laminotomy and bilateral arthroscopic decompression.

Coming to the stenosis of the lumbar canal, the ligamentum flavum in the subfacetal areas, that is, the lateral ligamentum flavum, which the author refers to as the “blind spot” (subfacetal ligamentum flavum), is not accessible due to severe facet hypertrophy in cases of severe canal stenosis, particularly in the upper inner quadrant of the contralateral side adjacent to the undersurface of the lamina and medial to the SAP.

This flavum is believed to be the main cause for compression over the exiting root and is left unattended in the microscopic, tubular, or endoscopic surgeries with the OTT technique. The OTT technique involves performing a unilateral laminotomy and tilting the table to decompress the contralateral side by traversing over the top of the dural sac. While effective, this technique has its limitations—primarily the need for extensive bone removal, plus its inability to address deep-seated blind spots. This subfacetal flavum can only be removed by cutting the facet (facetectomy), eventually leading to fusion of the segment.

This challenge can be resolved using our MBED technique and the use of a 4-mm endoscope, helping us navigate these inaccessible zones coining what we call “the under the cave decompression method,” which enhances the visualization of both the ipsilateral and contralateral subfacetal areas with minimal undercutting of the facet joint. This concept mirrors a caver's journey by working beneath the laminar shell, without violating its outer structural integrity. This improvement provides us with greater flexibility, enables easy navigation of the scope through the space formed by the chisel (like navigating through the narrow tunnels of a cave), and a superior quadrant-to-quadrant decompression, effectively addressing stenosis in all four corners of the canal while preserving the integrity of the facet joints.

This under the cave technique has advantages of:

Excellent visualization of the contralateral, sublaminar, and foraminal areas by easily shifting the endoscope, bringing it close to the lateral recess, which is often underestimated in extent. True lateral recess decompression extends from the medial border of the SAP to the tip of SAP—the territory often inadequately decompressed in conventional approaches.

Excellent visualization of the contralateral, sublaminar, and foraminal areas by easily shifting the endoscope, bringing it close to the lateral recess, which is often underestimated in extent. True lateral recess decompression extends from the medial border of the SAP to the tip of SAP—the territory often inadequately decompressed in conventional approaches.

Preservation of facet integrity compared to the OTT decompression, which may require partial resection of facet to access the contralateral side. With under the cave technique, resection is near zero, making it an excellent choice for patients at risk of postoperative instability or in patients where motion preservation is prioritized ( Figs. 14 and 15 ).

Patients with severe stenosis typically develop hypertrophied and distorted spinous processes and facet joints. These abnormalities result in a relatively limited area between the spinous process and the facet joint. Decompression with unilateral laminotomy becomes extremely difficult and may result in extensive damage of the ipsilateral and contralateral facet joint. These challenges may be addressed by removing additional bone from the base of the spinous process to facilitate sublaminar decompression and gain access to the contralateral recess.

These modified procedures shift the laminotomy window contralaterally, minimizing drilling on the ipsilateral lamina and facet joint.

Effective decompression is the main concern in the management of DLCS. Adequate decompression if not performed including the lateral recess, there can be early restenosis. 18 Studies cite revision rates of 8 to 22% for LCS decompressed endoscopically, often attributable to residual compression in these blind spots.

Our under the cave decompression technique can achieve good results with adequate decompression and visualization of the contralateral and ipsilateral exiting and traversing roots.

Once again, this visualization was possible due to the clarity of 0° 4-mm endoscope and the exploratory capacity of “under the cave” approach, which was sufficient to safely decompress the contralateral recess under direct vision. In the author's opinion, this visualization and decompression was superior to that of a unilateral microendoscopic decompression, where such an extreme contralateral angle of visualization is difficult to obtain.

In comparison to the microscopic and microendoscopic tubular decompression, the incidence of dural injuries is less in biportal endoscopy, as it has a better visualization and fluid from the continuous pressure irrigation enables slight compression of the dura and widening of the contralateral epidural space during the procedure. 19 20

But the most common type of complication in “over the top” technique was dural tears. It is generally agreed that the opposite lateral spinal canal may require significant dural sac retraction through the unilateral approach, increasing the risk of dural tear or nerve injury. 21 The chances of dural injuries in our surgical technique is very less as most of the under the cave work is done using the chisel.

Dural tear, if any, was managed by the “hanky in the pocket” technique—in which a dural substitute that is at least 30% larger than the dural defect is used. We insert it into the defect, tucking it underneath the tear margins (like handkerchief into the pocket). It acts as a ball-valve seal, thereby preventing cerebrospinal fluid leak.

UBED is the most widely adopted concept in minimally invasive spine surgery. 22 23 24 But the traditional over the top technique still misses to completely remove the flavum from the blind spot. Based on cadaveric studies for UBED, the decompression should be effective on both the ipsilateral and contralateral sides. 16 But due to the limited access to the contralateral side, the ipsilateral side is better decompressed. 25 Most studies suggest approaching from the dominant symptoms side to ensure effective decompression. However, in our method the under the cave technique provides good decompression of both ipsilateral and contralateral sides while maintaining the facet joint integrity.

To conclude as to why MBED works better in canal stenosis, the following are the points:

Conclusion

Midline biportal endoscopic surgery by the “under the cave” approach with the help of clear magnified surgical view and usage of surgical instruments familiar to most spine surgeons, can achieve sufficient bilateral lateral recess clearance, adequate decompression of neural elements, and effective clearance of the flavum, with the advantage of preserving the facet joint integrity, thereby preventing postoperative spinal instability and the need for fusion in the elderly.

To conclude, the author quotes “Shifting the approach from the side to the center not only enhances your surgical access but your surgical confidence as well.” A balance between adequate decompression and preservation of segmental stability is the ideal treatment of DLCS, which can be achieved by the MBED technique with under the cave decompression approach—a technique that can redefine triangulation, simplify access, and enhance symmetry.
