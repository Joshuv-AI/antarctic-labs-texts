Perceived algorithmic recommendation environments and the weakening of aesthetic autonomy among design students
Authors: Keyan Liu, Xuanming Hong, Qianling Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn visual environments shaped by algorithmic recommendation, design students’ aesthetic judgement may increasingly align with platform logic, and their visual expression may show convergent tendencies. This study proposes a structural model examining the relationships among perceived algorithmic recommendation environments, aesthetic mediatization, aesthetic homogenization, and the weakening of aesthetic autonomy. Using survey data and structural equation modeling, the study tests the relationships among three platform-environment variables and aesthetic outcomes at the levels of judgement and expression. The results suggest that perceived algorithmic recommendation environments are indirectly associated with the weakening of aesthetic autonomy, with aesthetic mediatization playing a central mediating role. The findings indicate that platform-driven visual environments may be associated with reduced aesthetic autonomy among design students, and offer implications for cultivating aesthetic independence and media literacy in design education.Keywords: Algorithmic recommendation, Aesthetic autonomy, Aesthetic homogenization, Aesthetic judgement, Design education, Structural equation modelingSubject terms: Cultural and media studies; Cultural and media studies; Mathematics and computing; Science, technology and society

Introduction

Algorithmic recommendation systems refer to computational models embedded in content distribution environments—such as social media platforms and short-video applications—that filter, rank, and personalize vast amounts of information for users1. Within this environment, users experience a passive mode of content reception in which the sequence and visibility of information are determined by algorithmic logic rather than intentional choice2.

Existing research shows that algorithmic recommendation improves the efficiency of attention allocation, making users more likely to consume easily accessible content, while simultaneously contributing to preference polarization, content convergence, and uniform modes of aesthetic expression3,4. Recent work in computational design and creative industries further shows that algorithmic ranking and personalized recommendation directly reshape creative outputs by reinforcing platform dominant visual patterns, narrowing stylistic variability, and incentivizing creators to imitate high-visibility aesthetics5. At a broader level, bibliometric analyses of education digitalization research indicate that recent scholarship has increasingly concentrated on platforms, artificial intelligence, and digital literacy within higher education, while paying comparatively less attention to students’ evaluative and aesthetic judgement processes6. Although recommendation increases efficiency, it also fosters phenomena such as filter bubbles and echo chambers7. In brief, filter bubbles narrow the range of visible information through personalized filtering, whereas echo chambers reinforce similar views within relatively homogeneous environments. Together, these conditions may reduce exposure to diverse perspectives. Prior studies generally suggest that algorithmic recommendation environments are associated not only with the scope of content exposure but also with aesthetic judgement, value formation, and cognitive processing8. Ukkola (2025) observes that recommendation strengthens ideological convergence by placing users in homogenous information environments, intensifying opinion polarization9. Hussain, Aslam, and Imran (2025) find that users with strong dependence on recommendation algorithms tend to exhibit convergent aesthetic standards10. Overall, while improving efficiency, algorithmic systems also generate cognitive bias, limit choice, and homogenize aesthetic outcomes.

Taken together, these studies suggest that algorithmic recommendation affects not only the distribution of information, but also the mediated environments within which aesthetic judgement is formed. When users repeatedly encounter similar visual languages, stylistic paradigms, and creative logics, their aesthetic judgement may become increasingly shaped by platform-conditioned feedback. In this sense, the dynamics of homogenization may be understood less as a direct consequence of technology itself than as an outcome of the mediated environments constructed by recommendation systems.

Despite this progress, existing research remains limited in several ways. Most studies focus on public communication, political expression, or mass behavior, while comparatively little work addresses design students—a population characterized by intensive use of visual platforms, heightened aesthetic sensitivity, and strong reliance on external creative resources. In the learning and creation process, design students actively use social media platforms to obtain inspiration, examples, and trends11,12, and their aesthetic judgement may be shaped by dominant platform content and platform-based evaluative cues13–15. At the same time, under conditions of digital-intelligent transformation, algorithmically mediated design environments, visual trend prediction, and intelligent design tools are becoming increasingly central to the design industry16,17, making critical understanding of platform-based recommendation environments an increasingly important capability for design students18.

Design students therefore represent a theoretically meaningful group for examining how algorithmic recommendation shapes aesthetic preferences. Their dependence on platform logics makes them a useful case through which the relationships between algorithmic environments and aesthetic judgement can be explored. Understanding this population’s aesthetic transformation is important for a broader understanding of visual culture production in the algorithmic context.

Accordingly, this study takes design students as the research object and proposes a structural equation model linking perceived algorithmic recommendation environments, aesthetic mediatization, aesthetic homogenization, and the weakening of aesthetic autonomy. The aim is to examine the structural relationships through which platform-based recommendation environments are associated with aesthetic judgement and expression. The study contributes to the literature by addressing a theoretical gap in research on algorithmic recommendation in art and design education, while also offering practical implications for cultivating aesthetic independence and media literacy among design students. Because the present study is based on self-reported questionnaire data, its empirical focus is not on algorithmic mechanisms in a direct technical sense, but on students’ perceived algorithmic recommendation environments and the ways in which these perceived environments are associated with aesthetic judgement and creative expression.

Research hypotheses and model construction

Research hypotheses

Based on the theoretical analysis above, this study organizes the perceived influence of algorithmic recommendation environments into two parts—an “algorithmic environment layer” and an “aesthetic impact layer”—and develops the corresponding research hypotheses.

Algorithmic environment layer

In studies on the effects of algorithmic recommendation, the “information cocoon”, “filter bubble”, and “echo chamber” are three widely discussed concepts. In general, these concepts point to the same phenomenon: under the combined influence of algorithms and social networks, the information encountered by audiences becomes increasingly singular and similar, resulting in closed knowledge structures and bias. Based on existing perspectives, this study understands these three phenomena as behavioral manifestations of algorithmic influence at different levels and accordingly proposes three dimensions: Content Homogeneity, Reference Dependence, and Source Closure.

Content homogeneity

Content Homogeneity refers to the high similarity observed in color, composition, subject matter, and visual style among the works encountered by design students during browsing and creation, as well as the frequent practice of using popular works as reference templates for minor adjustments or re-production. The classic concept of the “information cocoon” emphasizes that audiences tend to select information aligned with their own preferences and exclude alternative content, resulting in an information environment that is closed and homogeneous19. In this study context, Content Homogeneity represents the outcome of such closure at the visual content level.

Reference dependence

Reference Dependence refers to design students’ tendency to rely on popular or mainstream design cases on digital platforms when forming aesthetic judgements and making creative decisions. The concept of the “echo chamber” describes the process through which viewpoints are repeatedly reinforced within homogeneous groups20. Reference Dependence can be regarded as the behavioral expression of echo chambers in design practices: platform popularity and social feedback constitute the “echo”, reducing active exploration of non-mainstream visual resources.

Source closure

Source Closure refers to the concentration of information sources on a single platform or a single information stream, accompanied by a lack of cross-platform comparison and screening. The notion of the “filter bubble” highlights the deep filtering of information sources by algorithms, narrowing the range of visible information21. Therefore, Source Closure can be regarded as the deep filtering mechanism operating at the “information source level”. Source Closure determines the boundary of aesthetic resources accessible to students and serves as the channel-level variable through which algorithms affect the aesthetic ecosystem.

Taken together, these three dimensions describe how perceived algorithmic recommendation environments may shape the aesthetic input of design students at the levels of visible content, reference practices, and information sources. Prior studies on information cocooning, echo chambers, and algorithmic filtering suggest that recommendation systems may narrow the range of accessible information, reinforce repeated exposure to similar content, and increase reliance on dominant evaluative cues19–21. Consistent with this line of reasoning, Anwar et al. (2024) distinguish between the shrinkage of differences between users and the scope of information available to individual users, suggesting that recommendation systems may promote homogenization without necessarily implying complete informational closure. In this sense, Content Homogeneity, Reference Dependence, and Source Closure may be understood as related but distinct dimensions through which students experience platform-based recommendation environments. This line of reasoning provides a theoretical basis for the following discussion of aesthetic impact.

Aesthetic impact layer

Aesthetic mediatization

Aesthetic Mediatization is not an independent concept in the traditional sense, but rather an extension of mediatization theory into the field of aesthetic judgement. Mediatization theory argues that social practices—including culture, cognition, and aesthetics—gradually become embedded in the operational logic of media systems over the course of technological development, leading individuals to increasingly rely on media-provided frameworks and value systems in judgement, expression, and interaction22–24. Philosophical aesthetics has also questioned the assumption that aesthetic properties are directly given through raw visual perception. Teles (2025) argues that at least some aesthetic properties of artworks are visible only within historically formed visual paradigms, through which viewers learn how to recognize and organize aesthetic features. This perspective suggests that aesthetic judgement is not a neutral sensory process but is guided by socially and historically structured modes of seeing, providing theoretical support for understanding how evaluative frameworks shape aesthetic perception25.

Viewed together, these perspectives suggest that aesthetic judgement may gradually become oriented toward externally structured evaluative frameworks rather than arising solely from immediate individual perception. Extending mediatization theory to the aesthetic domain, Aesthetic Mediatization can be understood as a shift in aesthetic judgement from being “based on individual aesthetic experience” to being “based on media logic” under the influence of media and technological systems—for example, using popularity metrics, platform preferences, interaction ranking, and algorithmic trends as references for evaluating visual quality22,26,27. This “platform-based aesthetic judgement” is particularly salient in algorithm-driven visual environments. Through filtering and ranking visible content, algorithms shape the point of entry into aesthetic information; over time, users’ aesthetic experiences gradually become isomorphic with the mainstream paradigms presented by platforms, resulting in dependence on platform logic.

For design students, Aesthetic Mediatization is especially relevant in everyday learning and creative practice. On the one hand, students rely heavily on social media platforms for inspiration and trend detection in learning and creation, which may make their aesthetic criteria more responsive to popular styles, interaction metrics, and trending content circulated on these platforms24. On the other hand, the platform-based organization of visibility, popularity, and evaluation may shape how students form aesthetic judgements, making them more likely to draw on platform preferences rather than rely solely on independent judgement. Over time, these tendencies may shift aesthetic activity away from professional training and personal experience and align it more closely with media logic.

From the perspective of the algorithmic environment layer, Content Homogeneity, Reference Dependence, and Source Closure jointly constitute the “reshaped aesthetic input structure” experienced by students under algorithmic environments. Content Homogeneity indicates highly convergent visual information; Reference Dependence reflects reliance on platform paradigms in aesthetic judgement; and Source Closure shows that channels of visual input are increasingly concentrated. The intensification of all three may cause students’ aesthetic judgement to rely more heavily on algorithm-driven content ecologies and thus increase the level of Aesthetic Mediatization.

Based on this theoretical logic, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1a: Content Homogeneity has a positive effect on students’ level of Aesthetic Mediatization.

H1b: Reference Dependence has a positive effect on students’ level of Aesthetic Mediatization.

H1c: Source Closure has a positive effect on students’ level of Aesthetic Mediatization.

Aesthetic homogenization

From the perspective of cognitive psychology, Aesthetic Homogenization can be explained through schema theory. Cognitive schemas refer to the knowledge structures formed through long-term experience that individuals use to process external stimuli; they shape how people understand, interpret, and reproduce external information28,29. During long-term visual input, repeatedly presented structured content strengthens existing aesthetic schemas, leading individuals to rely on those schemas when engaging in new expressive tasks, which in turn results in convergence of stylistic expression28. Schemas not only shape how people understand visual information, but also serve as default organizational structures in the generation of new works. Therefore, continuous exposure to similar visual paradigms leads aesthetic expression to become more consistent in structure and style29.

Algorithmic recommendation environments further reinforce this process. Research indicates that platform algorithms continually narrow the types of content users encounter through personalized recommendations, click feedback, and interaction weighting, resulting in clear trends of homogenization in online culture and value orientation, accompanied by weakened critical thinking and increased confirmation bias5,30.

In the context of this study, Aesthetic Homogenization is defined as the phenomenon in which algorithm-dominated visual input continuously reinforces particular aesthetic schemas, causing design students’ aesthetic judgement and creative expression—such as in color, composition, and style—to converge. It is not only an important psychological outcome of Aesthetic Mediatization, but also provides a structural foundation for the subsequent weakening of aesthetic autonomy. Existing studies suggest that repeated exposure to similar visual materials, stronger reliance on dominant platform cases, and reduced diversity of inspiration sources may jointly reinforce stable aesthetic schemas.

Based on this reasoning, the study proposes the following hypotheses:

H2a: Content Homogeneity has a positive effect on students’ level of Aesthetic Homogenization.

H2b: Reference Dependence has a positive effect on students’ level of Aesthetic Homogenization.

H2c: Source Closure has a positive effect on students’ level of Aesthetic Homogenization.

H3: Aesthetic Mediatization has a positive effect on students’ level of Aesthetic Homogenization.

Weakening of aesthetic autonomy

In media and cultural studies, “aesthetic autonomy” generally refers to an individual’s ability to maintain a relatively independent stance in aesthetic judgement and creative practice—namely, to evaluate based on personal experience and value standards rather than relying excessively on market feedback, platform algorithms, or others’ opinions13,31. Under algorithmic recommendation environments, researchers are increasingly concerned that this autonomy is being eroded. On the one hand, people’s “psychological dependence” on recommendation systems continues to grow, with a tendency to regard algorithms as knowing better than themselves “what counts as good content”, thereby outsourcing selection and judgement to platforms32,33.

On the other hand, the opacity and one-directionality of algorithmic curation are also understood to weaken individuals’ “voice” and capacity for intervention. Ordinary users find it difficult to understand how algorithms define “content worth seeing”, and lack practical means to alter this structure, making them more likely to passively accept the limited options offered by platforms31. This line of research suggests that while algorithmic news and content recommendation increase efficiency, they may also reduce users’ sense of control over the information selection process, rendering their value judgements more vulnerable to platform logic and commercial objectives13.

Drawing on these perspectives, this study defines the Weakening of Aesthetic Autonomy as a tendency observed in algorithmically dominated media environments: when making aesthetic judgements and creative decisions, design students find it increasingly difficult to uphold their independent standards, and instead rely more on platform popularity, algorithmic recommendations, and others’ feedback for selection and evaluation. Mechanistically, Aesthetic Mediatization means that students increasingly use platform indicators—such as view counts, likes, and recommendation positions—as references when evaluating works, resulting in aesthetic judgement being “mediated” by media logic. Over time, this weakens adherence to professional principles and personal aesthetic positions. At the same time, increasing Aesthetic Homogenization compresses the aesthetic space to which students have access and within which they can imagine; differences between works shrink, and the cost of maintaining distinctiveness rises, which further reduces their psychological motivation and perceived ability to sustain autonomous judgement13,30.

Based on the above, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H4: Aesthetic Mediatization has a positive effect on the level of Weakening of Aesthetic Autonomy among students.

H5: Aesthetic Homogenization has a positive effect on the level of Weakening of Aesthetic Autonomy among students.

Research design and methods

Questionnaire design

This study adopts a seven-point Likert scale to measure respondents’ level of agreement with each indicator. A score of 1 indicates “strongly disagree”, 2 “disagree”, 3 “somewhat disagree”, 4 “neutral”, 5 “somewhat agree”, 6 “agree”, and 7 “strongly agree”. Given the self-reported nature of the questionnaire, the variables in this study are intended to capture respondents’ perceived platform environments and reported tendencies, rather than directly observable properties of recommendation systems themselves.

To ensure conceptual relevance and measurement consistency, the study employed a structured questionnaire as the primary survey instrument. Prior to the formal survey, a small-scale pretest was conducted with a group of design students to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, and relevance of the questionnaire items. Based on participant feedback, minor adjustments were made to the wording of several items and response options to better align with the platform-based design context. The pretest data were not included in the final analysis.

The full questionnaire, including all item wordings and scale descriptions, is provided as a supplementary file. All construct items were developed with reference to established literature and were adapted to fit the context of undergraduate design students’ platform use, aesthetic judgement, and creative practice. More specifically, the original conceptual meanings of the cited constructs were retained, while the wording of individual items was adjusted to reflect the visual-platform environment examined in this study. The same questionnaire and response format were used across both offline and online data collection to ensure procedural consistency. In addition to the measurement indicators of the model, the questionnaire also collected basic demographic information, including gender, year of study, major and platforms used. Sample collection

To enhance sample validity, the study adopted both offline and online questionnaire distribution. To improve participation and response quality, modest compensation was provided in both modes. Offline questionnaires were distributed on site at the School of Design, Jiangnan University, in Wuxi between November and December 2025, while the online survey was disseminated through digital channels such as design-related student groups and Xiaohongshu. After data cleaning, 234 valid responses from undergraduate design students were retained for analysis, meeting the basic sample requirements for structural equation modeling. All participants were informed of the purpose of the study and participated voluntarily.

Among the 234 valid responses retained after data cleaning, 52 were identified as offline responses from Wuxi and 182 as online responses, accounting for 22.2 and 77.8% of the final sample, respectively.

Data analysis strategy

The study employed partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) to test the hypothesized relationships among the latent constructs. PLS-SEM was considered appropriate because the study focuses on examining the structural associations among multiple latent variables in an exploratory model, and the analysis includes assessments of measurement reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and predictive relevance. The structural model was evaluated using path coefficients, coefficients of determination (R2), and Q2_predict values, and significance testing was conducted through bootstrapping.

All participants took part voluntarily, and all responses were collected anonymously without personally identifiable information.

Data analysis

Reliability and validity tests

Before evaluating the structural model in the PLS-SEM analysis, the measurement model was assessed in terms of internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. In addition, common method bias was examined using Harman’s single-factor test. The results showed that five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, and the first factor accounted for 34.636% of the total variance, which is below the commonly used threshold of 40%, suggesting that common method bias is unlikely to be a serious concern in the present study. Cronbach’s α values for all constructs were above 0.70, and the composite reliability (CR) ranged from 0.834 to 0.896, meeting the reliability standards for structural equation modeling. The average variance extracted (AVE) values ranged between 0.626 and 0.741, exceeding the 0.50 threshold, indicating that the measurement items explain a substantial proportion of the variance within each latent construct and that convergent validity is adequate.

In addition, all standardized factor loadings were significant and above 0.70. The square root of AVE for each construct was greater than its corresponding inter-construct correlations, demonstrating good discriminant validity. Overall, the measurement model exhibits satisfactory reliability and validity, and thus allows proceeding to structural modeling. Assessment of structural model

The explanatory power and path estimation results indicate that the model shows good statistical performance. The explained variance for the outcome variable Aesthetic Homogenization reached R2 = 0.545, suggesting that Content Homogeneity, Reference Dependence, Source Closure, and Aesthetic Mediatization jointly explain approximately 54.5% of the variance in the convergence of aesthetic expression. The R2 value for Weakening of Aesthetic Autonomy was approximately 0.320, indicating that Aesthetic Homogenization and Aesthetic Mediatization jointly account for about one-third of the variance in the weakening of aesthetic autonomy, which represents a moderate level of explanatory power.

In addition, the Q2_predict values were all positive (Aesthetic Homogenization: Q2 = 0.537; Aesthetic Mediatization: Q2 = 0.259), and the overall model goodness-of-fit was GoF = 0.503, indicating that the model possesses predictive relevance and acceptable overall model fit, and that the structural relationships are statistically meaningful. Regarding path coefficients, most hypotheses were statistically supported. Content Homogeneity was positively associated with Aesthetic Mediatization (β = 0.329, p < 0.001) and was also positively associated with higher levels of Aesthetic Homogenization (β = 0.148, p = 0.012), suggesting that greater concentration in platform content presentation is related to shifts in students’ aesthetic judgement tendencies and convergence in visual expression. Reference Dependence was significantly associated with both Aesthetic Mediatization (β = 0.174, p = 0.010) and Aesthetic Homogenization (β = 0.130, p = 0.020), indicating that stronger reliance on platform content as an aesthetic reference is related to the externalization of aesthetic evaluation and symbolic imitation.

In comparison, Source Closure was significantly associated with Aesthetic Mediatization (β = 0.162, p = 0.027), but its association with Aesthetic Homogenization did not reach statistical significance (β = 0.074, p = 0.136), suggesting that concentration of information sources is more closely related to modes of aesthetic judgement than to convergence in visual expression itself.

With respect to the relationships among aesthetic-processing variables and aesthetic outcomes, Aesthetic Mediatization showed the strongest association with Aesthetic Homogenization (β = 0.563, p < 0.001), representing the largest standardized path coefficient in the model. This suggests that convergence in creative style is more likely when aesthetic evaluation increasingly relies on platform logic. Meanwhile, Aesthetic Homogenization was significantly associated with the Weakening of Aesthetic Autonomy (β = 0.244, p = 0.005), suggesting that convergence in creative expression is related to reduced ability to maintain personal aesthetic standards and explore stylistic boundaries. Aesthetic Mediatization was also directly associated with the Weakening of Aesthetic Autonomy (β = 0.368, p < 0.001), suggesting a significant association between externalized aesthetic judgement logic and reduced aesthetic autonomy (Table 7).

Model testing

Taken together, the analytical results support the hypothesized structural relationships among the variables in the model. Discussion

Main findings: associations among perceived algorithmic recommendation environments, aesthetic mediatization, and aesthetic autonomy

This study examines the relationships among perceived algorithmic recommendation environments, aesthetic mediatization, aesthetic homogenization, and the weakening of aesthetic autonomy, and tests a hypothesized chain of structural associations among these variables. The results suggest that Content Homogeneity, Reference Dependence, and Source Closure are all significantly associated with students’ levels of Aesthetic Mediatization, while Content Homogeneity and Reference Dependence are also positively associated with Aesthetic Homogenization. Taken together, these findings support the view that different dimensions of perceived platform environments are related not only to students’ aesthetic judgement tendencies, but also to changes in aesthetic expression.

Further analysis suggests that Aesthetic Mediatization occupies a central position in the hypothesized model. It is significantly associated with the Weakening of Aesthetic Autonomy and is also linked to it indirectly through Aesthetic Homogenization. In this sense, the findings are consistent with a broader pattern in which perceived algorithmic recommendation environments are associated first with shifts in aesthetic judgement tendencies and then with convergence in aesthetic expression, while the weakening of aesthetic autonomy appears within this overall structure of relationships.

Among the reported associations, the strongest path in the model is the relationship between Aesthetic Mediatization and Aesthetic Homogenization. This suggests that when aesthetic judgement becomes increasingly oriented toward platform-based evaluative logic, convergence in visual expression may also become more likely. One possible interpretation is that when students increasingly regard highly visible or highly approved platform content as an aesthetic benchmark, platform recognition may gradually become intertwined with aesthetic value. Under such conditions, aesthetic judgement may become more dependent on external evaluative systems than on personal experience or independently formed standards.

The findings are consistent with existing discussions on how algorithmic curation weakens aesthetic autonomy. Algorithms reshape visible content and evaluative structures, leading students to rely more on platform logic rather than personal aesthetic standards in aesthetic judgement31. This phenomenon may be understood through the perspective of Aesthetic Mediatization. Aesthetic Mediatization implies that aesthetic standards are no longer generated independently of media, but within media systems themselves. Especially in visual creation, algorithmic filtering and sorting shape “what visual content can be seen”, and further determine “which visual forms are worth imitating”. Thus, media not only provide content, but also produce an “aesthetic consensus”.

Taken together, these findings further support the interpretation that Aesthetic Mediatization occupies a central position in the hypothesized model.

Differential effects of perceived algorithmic recommendation environments: input homogeneity and source closure are not equivalent

Contrary to expectations, Source Closure does not significantly predict Aesthetic Homogenization, but it is significantly associated with Aesthetic Mediatization. This pattern of partial support suggests an important distinction: structural singularity in aesthetic inputs may not be directly associated with convergence in outputs, but may instead be more closely related to shifts in aesthetic judgement tendencies.

In other words, even when information sources are highly concentrated, students may still pursue stylistic differentiation in their creations; however, as their aesthetic judgement becomes increasingly aligned with platform logic, such differentiation tends to remain at the level of “stylistic variation” rather than “expansion of aesthetic boundaries”. This helps explain a common real-world phenomenon: many design works appear different on the surface, yet their aesthetic logic and visual symbols continue to revolve around platform trends, resulting in a pattern of “representational difference and structural isomorphism”.

These findings are consistent with the possibility that convergence in aesthetic logic may precede convergence in aesthetic expression. They also align with research on recommendation environments suggesting that filtering processes do not directly determine behavioral outcomes, but may limit opportunities to encounter new information and thereby increase reliance on platform-based evaluative standards21. When information sources are highly concentrated, individuals may develop similar judgements about “what counts as good design” and “what constitutes correct aesthetics”, while their creative outputs may still retain some degree of variation. From this perspective, Source Closure appears to be associated first with Aesthetic Mediatization and only indirectly with the Weakening of Aesthetic Autonomy, rather than with a direct replication of visual outputs36.

Therefore, the relationship between Source Closure and Aesthetic Homogenization is better understood not as a direct association, but as an indirect pattern of structural relationships in which Source Closure is associated with Aesthetic Mediatization, which is in turn associated with the Weakening of Aesthetic Autonomy.

In addition, the results suggest that different dimensions of perceived algorithmic recommendation environments may be associated with aesthetic outcomes in different ways. Content Homogeneity may contribute to a more unified aesthetic reference system through repeated exposure to mainstream visual templates, thereby being associated with convergence in creative outcomes. By contrast, Source Closure may limit opportunities for updating aesthetic schemas, reducing the cognitive basis for cross-paradigm comparison and encouraging aesthetic judgement to rely more heavily on existing “platform paradigms”, which may appear as “convergence within apparent difference”34. This interpretation is also consistent with schema theory, which suggests that the updating of schemas depends on the input of new external information; when knowledge sources are highly restricted, schemas may become more fixed, resulting in convergence in aesthetic judgement without necessarily producing direct visual imitation28,29.

A model of interpretation: from platform logic to aesthetic autonomy

Research by Ahmmad et al. suggests that recommendation algorithms may shape the sources of aesthetic input through filtering processes, which may in turn be associated with content homogeneity and the weakening of aesthetic autonomy4. Combined with the results of this study, these findings suggest that the relationship between perceived algorithmic recommendation environments and aesthetic outcomes may be better understood as a sequential pattern of associations. Within this pattern, platform content structures and information sources are associated with students’ aesthetic judgement tendencies, while creative expression may gradually align with dominant platform styles, manifesting as Aesthetic Homogenization and the weakening of aesthetic autonomy.

This may be understood as a pattern in which changes in judgement tendencies are associated with convergence in visual expression, while the weakening of aesthetic autonomy appears within this broader set of structural associations.

This result suggests that the more consequential role of perceived algorithmic recommendation environments may lie not only in what students encounter, but also in how they come to judge what is aesthetically valuable. Aesthetic autonomy may be weakened not simply through exposure to isolated visual content, but through increasing reliance on platform-based evaluative logic in aesthetic judgement. Therefore, simply expanding sources of reference material does not necessarily guarantee the development of aesthetic autonomy; if the principles of judgement remain tied to platform logic, diverse inputs may still be associated with similar aesthetic outputs.

From an educational perspective, this finding implies that the focus should not be on “providing more reference materials”, but on guiding students to understand the basis of aesthetic judgement and establish independent evaluative frameworks. Only when students can clearly articulate their aesthetic reasoning, rather than simply adopting platform logic, can genuine aesthetic independence and diversity be achieved.

Conclusion and future research

Academic contributions and practical implications

This study examines the weakening of aesthetic autonomy among design students in relation to perceived algorithmic recommendation environments and highlights the relationships among content structure, aesthetic judgement tendencies, and aesthetic outcomes. Unlike previous research that primarily describes aesthetic trends or creative outcomes, this study focuses on changes in aesthetic judgement and highlights the potential role of Aesthetic Mediatization in understanding how technological environments are associated with the weakening of aesthetic autonomy. The findings are best understood as reflecting a sample situated in the current Chinese design-education context, where visually oriented platform use and algorithmically mediated content exposure are highly salient in students’ everyday learning and creative practices.

This framework suggests that the influence of algorithmic recommendation may not be reflected in direct stylistic convergence alone; rather, convergence may be more closely associated with the externalization of aesthetic judgement criteria.

On a practical level, the findings offer guidance for design education in algorithmic environments. For design students, platform algorithms have become the dominant channel through which visual information is received. The results indicate that simply increasing reference materials or expanding sources of information is insufficient for strengthening aesthetic autonomy. More importantly, design education should guide students to understand the basis of aesthetic judgement and develop personal evaluative standards. This suggests a shift in pedagogical emphasis from “providing more materials” to “cultivating interpretive ability and critical judgement”, enabling students to articulate why certain visual forms are aesthetically valuable. Such training helps students maintain aesthetic autonomy in highly mediatized environments rather than search for standard answers embedded in platform logic.

Furthermore, the analytical model proposed in this study offers a perspective for subsequent research—namely, to analyze platform-related influences on creative domains by examining “modes of judgement” rather than “creative outcomes”. By analyzing the relationships between Aesthetic Mediatization, Aesthetic Homogenization, and aesthetic autonomy, the study provides a theoretical foundation for future research on the interaction between design creation and technological environments. The study outlines a possible interpretive path in which convergence in aesthetic logic may precede convergence in visual outcomes, highlighting that the influence of algorithmic recommendation in design education may lie less in simple visual imitation than in the externalization of aesthetic judgement criteria.

Limitations and future research

Because this study relies on cross-sectional self-report survey data, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of statistically significant associations rather than definitive causal relationships. Although the structural model is consistent with the hypothesized relationships, it does not by itself establish temporal order or causal transmission. Future research may strengthen causal inference by using longitudinal designs, experimental approaches, or multi-source data.

This study has several limitations that offer directions for future research. First, the sample size was limited. Although statistically meaningful, sampling bias may still exist. Second, the sample contained a gender distribution of approximately 2:8, which appears imbalanced but reflects a realistic educational context. According to publicly available statistics, female students comprise more than 70% of the population in art-related disciplines38. The gender ratio in this study therefore reflects the current situation in design education rather than sampling error. However, gender may influence aesthetic judgement, and future work could examine model stability using more gender-balanced samples.

In addition, the sample consisted primarily of design students from several domestic universities, resulting in a relatively concentrated sample composition and limited broader representativeness. The findings should therefore be understood as grounded in a specific Chinese higher-education context, rather than as a basis for unrestricted generalization to all design students. Given that aesthetic judgement may be shaped by educational and cultural contexts, future research could include institutions from different regions and cross-cultural settings to examine how perceived algorithmic recommendation environments are associated with aesthetic tendencies under different aesthetic traditions.

Future research may deepen the inquiry in two directions. First, the psychological and learning consequences of weakened aesthetic autonomy warrant further investigation. For example, mechanisms such as self-doubt, value uncertainty, creative anxiety, or self-devaluation triggered by Aesthetic Homogenization may produce chain reactions in real learning environments. Second, from the perspective of educational intervention, it would be valuable to examine training methods that reduce the impact of Aesthetic Mediatization—such as empirical observation, aesthetic interpretation training, or small-sample reference strategies—and to develop pedagogical approaches that strengthen independent aesthetic judgement and critical visual literacy.
