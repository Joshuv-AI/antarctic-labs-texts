The F. John model and Cummins’ equations for freely floating objects
David Lannes, Martin Oen Paulsen

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The floating body problem

The derivation of equations for ship motions based on linear potential flow is a historical problem that dates back to the 19th century. Two significant contributions to this field were made by Froude in 1861
[
27
]
, who examined a ship rolling in waves, and Krylov, who investigated motions with six degrees of freedom
[
44
,
45
]
. In their work, the dynamics of the floating object were modeled as a damped harmonic oscillator, which marked an important step toward developing a general theory. However, the derivation was based on strict assumptions that neglected the effects of diffracted waves and those generated by the ship itself. A large community set out to find an appropriate formulation of the linear floating body problem, and one of the first systematic approaches was developed by John in 1949 for motions with six degrees of freedom.

In a two-part series, John
[
39
,
40
]
gave a general formulation of the linear floating body problem. The floating body problem describes the interaction between a fluid and a partially immersed solid under the assumptions:

-

The amplitude of the surface elevation is assumed to be small.

-

The motion of the solid is assumed to be free or forced with small amplitude.

-

The fluid is governed by the linear Bernoulli equation.

Under these assumptions, John neglected the nonlinear effects due to the variations of the fluid domain to arrive at a set of equations which can be stated, in the two-dimensional case, as

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\zeta-\partial_{z}\phi=0&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D}\\
\partial_{t}\psi+{\mathtt{g}}\zeta=0&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D},\end{cases}

where
{\mathtt{g}}
is the gravity,
\Gamma^{\rm D}
consists of two flat finite intervals or half-lines corresponding to the part of the surface of the fluid in contact with the atmosphere when the fluid is at rest,
\zeta
is the free surface elevation, and

\phi
is the velocity potential. This latter is determined in terms of its trace
\psi
on
\Gamma^{\rm D}
and of the velocity
{\mathcal{V}}^{\rm w}
of the floating object by solving

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\Delta\phi=0&\text{in}\quad\Omega\\
\phi=\psi&\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\mathrm{D}}\\
\mathbf{n}^{\rm w}\cdot\nabla\phi=\mathbf{n}^{\rm w}\cdot{\mathcal{V}}^{\rm w}&\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\mathrm{w}}\\
\mathbf{n}^{\rm b}\cdot\nabla\phi=0&\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\rm b},\end{cases}

where
\Omega
denotes the fluid domain at rest, while the wetted part of the object is labeled
\Gamma^{\rm w}
, and the bottom of the fluid domain is denoted
\Gamma^{\rm b}
(see Figure
1
). The first equation in (
1.1
) is the linearization of the classical kinematic boundary condition at the free surface, while the second one is the linearization of the trace of Bernoulli’s equation at the free surface.

Since the object is a rigid body, its velocity
{\mathcal{V}}^{\rm w}
can be expressed in terms of the time derivative of
{\mathtt{X}}(t)=(\tilde{x}(t),{\tilde{z}(t),\theta}(t))^{\rm T}
, where
\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{z}
denote the horizontal and vertical displacements of the center of mass of the object and
\theta
its rotation. When the object is freely floating, the vector
{\mathtt{X}}
is determined by Newton’s equations, the acting forces and torque acting on the object being its weight and the hydrodynamical force/torque exerted by the fluid. These equations are provided in Section
2
.

The work of John marked a period with several foundational works on the formulation and dynamics of the floating body problem
[
35
,
70
,
67
,
71
,
64
,
73
]
, culminating in the seminal work of Cummins in 1962
[
17
]
. He was able to link the formulation by Krylov and John, and proposed a description of the motion of the object as a set of coupled integro-differential equations for the degrees of freedom of the object (three in dimension two, and six in dimension three). In the present two-dimensional case, it takes the form

(1.3)

({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})\ddot{\mathtt{X}}+\rho{\mathtt{g}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}(t-\tau)\dot{\mathtt{X}}(\tau)\>\mathrm{d}\tau+{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}=\mathcal{F},

where
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}_{a}
are the mass and added mass matrices,
\mathcal{K}
is a matricial kernel that accounts for the memory effect of the fluid,
\mathcal{C}{\mathtt{X}}
is the hydrostatic force, and
\mathcal{F}
is the force resulting from the waves acting on the object.
The matrices
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}_{a}
and the kernel
{\mathcal{K}}
only depend on the geometry of the object, while the matrix
{\mathcal{C}}
also depends on the position of its center of mass. The analytical description of these terms is presented in Section
2
, where we revisit the derivation of F. John’s model and generalize it to allow for the presence of a (possibly emerging) topography. The derivation of Cummins’ equations in such general configurations is done in Subsection
3.3
. Despite being oversimplified in many respects, these equations remain one of the primary methods used in naval engineering and marine renewable energy applications
[
62
,
56
,
26
,
3
,
32
]
, through widely used computer programs such as WaveAnalysisMIT
[
52
]
, which enables large-scale numerical simulations of floating structures with complex geometries.

A drawback of John’s model is that it neglects nonlinear, viscous, and rotational effects, which are needed to assess maximum loads on the structure in extreme sea conditions
[
50
]
. One way to include these effects is based on computational fluid dynamics (CFD) using versions of the Navier-Stokes equations
[
65
,
25
]
. This approach is computationally much more expensive, as one has to deal with highly separated flows with large-scale differences between the floater and mooring systems, coupled with a non-Gaussian wave field
[
41
]
. For these reasons, the CFD project cannot account for a full sea state and is only used in very specific cases.

A compromise between linear theory and CFD computations was recently put forward in
[
49
]
. Here, the models still neglect viscosity and vorticity but include nonlinear effects.
The presence of a body is accounted for in the water wave equations by applying a constraint on the surface elevation at the object’s location. This approach therefore relates the floating body problem to the wide literature on congested flows
[
24
,
28
,
66
,
55
,
29
,
19
]
. It can be used with vertically averaged models that allow for faster numerical simulations while accounting for nonlinear effects. We direct interested readers to
[
9
,
8
,
36
,
12
,
5
,
7
,
4
,
37
]
for both numerical and analytical studies of various vertically averaged models. Despite active study of these models, it remains an open question whether their solutions are close to those of the original physical system.
The first step in addressing this question is to establish the well-posedness of John’s problem. This is the first objective of the paper and will in turn be used to justify the Cummins equations.
Moreover, since deriving asymptotic models classically involves a loss of derivative from an asymptotic expansion, see for instance
[
48
]
, one would need to establish high regularity of the solutions. Therefore,
a second goal of the paper is to investigate the regularity properties
of the solutions to F. John’s problem.

1.2.
Related results

While in the absence of floating objects and more generally of boundaries, the water waves equations are now well understood, the situation is far more complex in configurations where the free boundary of the fluid meets a solid boundary. T. Alazard, N. Burq and C. Zuily considered in
[
2
]
the case of a channel delimited by vertical walls; the surface is then the graph of a function over a finite interval. Their approach is based on a 1910 paper by Boussinesq
[
11
]
, where the problem is transformed into a problem on the torus using a simple reflection and periodization procedure. Extending the solution this way naturally induces singularities around the point of reflection, resulting in a domain with a Lipschitz boundary. However, by imposing the compatibility condition
\partial_{x}\zeta=0
at the contact points at
t=0
and observing that this condition is trivially propagated by symmetry reasons, the authors were able to improve the regularity of the surface down to a point where the periodic water waves equations are known to be well posed
[
1
]
.

For non vertical walls, for instance in the presence of an emerging bottom, this argument does not work. This configuration was studied in the work of de Poyferré,
[
23
]
, who derived
a priori
estimates for the water waves equation in the case of an emerging bottom, provided that the contact angle between the free surface and the emerging bottom remains small. A similar condition is imposed by Ming and Wang
[
58
,
59
,
60
]
who proved the well-posedness in the same situation, but in the presence of surface tension. In the related context of corner singularities for the water waves equations, similar conditions also appear
[
42
,
74
,
14
]
. The smallness condition on the contact angles is due to the fact that the analysis of the velocity field implies the resolution of a Laplace problem on a domain with corners. For such elliptic problems, it is known that corner singularities appear and destroy the standard elliptic regularity properties observed in smooth domains
[
38
,
30
,
22
,
13
,
43
,
51
]
. There is no smallness assumption on the contact angles in the works of I. Guo and I. Tice
[
34
,
33
]
who studied the contact problem for the Navier-Stokes equations with surface tension, nor in the work of E. Bocchi, A. Castro and F. Gancedo
[
6
]
on the Muskat problem with contact points, but this issue is still present in these references, and is the reason why the authors have to work in a low regularity setting.

The issues of regularity discussed above are also at the core of the mathematical analysis of F. John’s problem, and a layer of complexity is added by the fact that the free surface is not connected, which imposes to work in non standard functional spaces.
From the time John published the second paper in 1950
[
40
]
, which demonstrated that the system produces a unique solution under the assumption that all motions are time-harmonic with sufficiently large frequency, and other technical assumptions, most subsequent research has concentrated on time-harmonic motions
[
57
,
46
]
. The first well-posedness result of John’s problem for unsteady waves in an optimal functional setting and an analysis of the regularity properties of the solutions was only recently established for a fixed object
[
47
]
. The case of an object having the shape of a half-disk and allowed to move only vertically in a fluid of infinite depth was recently considered in
[
63
]
. Using the explicit expression of the velocity potential in that configuration, the authors proved the well-posedness of F. John’s problem at low regularity and assuming that the velocity potential, and not only its gradient, is in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. This latter assumption was also made in
[
47
]
to establish the higher regularity properties of the solution. In this paper, we address the case of general geometric configurations of a solid allowed to move along three degrees of freedom, of general initial sea states, and investigate as well the issue of the regularity of the solutions. We also derive and investigate Cummins’ equations and, in particular, whether the convolution kernel
{\mathcal{K}}
makes sense in all possible configurations, in spite of the possible presence of corner singularities.

To provide a more comprehensive description of the main results, we must first write down John’s equations and make the coupling with Newton’s equations precise. This process will be detailed in the following subsection.

1.4.
Description of the results and organization of the paper

In this paper, we revisit the derivation of the Cummins equations
[
17
]
in the general configurations allowed by Assumption
1.1
and give the first well-posedness result for (
1.3
). The rigorous derivation of Cummins’ equations is a consequence of the well-posedness of John’s problem and of the regularity properties of its solutions. This well-posedness theory is based on several results that are of independent interest.

Section
2
is dedicated to new reformulations of John’s problem, as presented in equations (
1.1
)-(
1.4
). In this section, we introduce the Dirichlet-Neumann map studied in
[
47
]
and use it to reformulate (
1.1

and inspired by the classical Zakharov-Craig-Sulem formulation of the water-waves problem
[
75

15
,
16
]

{\Gamma}^{\rm D}
, is expressed in terms of Kirchhoff potentials. Through the Kirchhoff potentials, we provide an analytical formulation of the added mass effect
\mathcal{M}_{a}
hidden in equation (
1.4

{\mathcal{H}}(\zeta,\psi,{\mathtt{X}},{\mathtt{V}})=\frac{1}{2}{\mathtt{g}}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi G_{0}\psi+\frac{1}{2\rho}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}\cdot{\mathtt{X}}+\frac{1}{2\rho}({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a}){\mathtt{V}}\cdot{\mathtt{V}}.

In Subsection
2.3

{\mathcal{H}}
. Under certain conditions on the coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
,

can be used to construct a semi-Hilbert space adapted to the mathematical analysis of F. John’s problem. We relate these conditions on the matrix
{\mathcal{C}}
to classical equilibrium criteria used in ship construction.

In Section
3
, we develop an abstract framework for John’s problem and establish its well-posedness, which in turn is used to justify the Cummins equations. The well-posedness is obtained in the semi-Hilbert space associated with the Hamiltonian, and that can be characterized as

{\mathbb{X}}=L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3},

where
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
is the trace space associated with the Beppo-Levi space
\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)

3.1

3.1

{\mathbb{X}}
. These elements are utilized in Subsection
3.2

{\mathbb{X}}

[
47

{\mathbb{X}}

Theorem 1.3
.

The initial value problem for John’s problem is well-posed in the energy space.

Having this result at hand we turn to the derivation of Cummins equations. In Subsection
3.3

1.3

Theorem 1.4
.

The initial value problem for the Cummins equations is well-posed in the energy space.

This result is of independent interest and is used in this paper to study the regularity of John’s problem in the case of a vertical wall in the neighborhood of each corner. Specifically, we are able to propagate the compatibility conditions at each corner in time and show that we have higher regularity for restricted motions of the object:

Theorem 1.9
.

The initial value problem for John’s problem is well-posed in higher Sobolev norms for right angles at the contact points under compatibility conditions on the data, and assuming further that one of the following cases holds

-

The object is only allowed to move vertically.

-

The center of mass
G
is such that
z_{G}=0
and the object is only allowed to move by vertical translation and rotations around
G
.

We note that the restriction on degrees of freedom of the object is due to the creation of singularities for horizontal motions. More precisely, the Kirchhoff potential corresponding to horizontal translations has a singularity which is described explicitly by Theorem
1.8
, and that limits the regularity of the solutions to John’s problem and Cummins’ equations.

2.
Hamiltonian reformulations

In this section, we propose several reformulations of F. John’s problem (
1.1
)-(
1.4
). Using the Dirichlet-Neumann operator and the Kirchhoff potentials introduced in Section
2.1
and taking advantage of the added mass effect, a convenient reformulation is proposed in Section
2.2
. We then show that this formulation admits a non-canonical Hamiltonian structure, which can be transformed into a canonical Hamiltonian structure by a simple change of unknowns. In general, this Hamiltonian does not have a sign. We therefore impose conditions that are classical in naval engineering on the position of the center of mass and of the so-called meta-centers, in order to ensure that the Hamiltonian is positive, even if it remains non-definite. Under such conditions, the Hamiltonian can be used to define a semi-normed functional space adapted to the problem.

2.1.
The Dirichlet-Neumann operator and the Kirchhoff potentials

The homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)
is a natural functional space to work with since velocity potentials that correspond to velocity fields of finite kinetic energy are in this space. We recall that it is defined as

\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)=\{\phi\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega),\nabla\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}\},

with associated semi-norm
\|\phi\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)}=\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. The space
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
corresponding to the traces on
\Gamma^{\rm D}
of all functions in
\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)
was introduced in
[
47
]
, where its is proved that the mapping

(2.1)

\mbox{Tr}^{\rm D}:\phi\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)\mapsto\phi_{|_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}}\mbox{ on }\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})

is well defined, continuous, onto, and admits a continuous right-inverse. It is not necessary to characterize further this functional space at this step; this will be done later in Section
3
. We can now define the Dirichlet-Neumann operator.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega
,
\Gamma^{\rm D}
and
\Gamma^{\rm N}
be as in Assumption
1.1
. The Dirichlet-Neumann operator

is defined as

G_{0}\>:\>\begin{cases}\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{D}})&\rightarrow(\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{D}}))^{\prime}\\
\psi&\mapsto\partial_{z}\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}},\end{cases}

where
(\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{D}}))^{\prime}
is the dual of
\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\mathrm{D}})
and
\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)
is the unique variational solution of

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}=0\qquad\hskip 8.5359pt\text{in}\quad\Omega\\
\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}=\psi\qquad\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\rm D}\\
\partial_{n}\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}=0\qquad\hskip 5.69046pt\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\rm N}.\end{cases}

Remark 2.2
.

The existence of a variational solution
\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)
to (

) for

is given by Proposition 9 in
[
47
]
. Also, the definition of the normal derivative
\partial_{z}\psi^{{\mathfrak{h}}}
on

should be understood in its weak variational meaning
[
54
]
.

We now turn to define the Kirchhoff potentials and the added mass matrix.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\Omega
,
\Gamma^{\rm D}
and
\Gamma^{\rm N}
be as in Assumption
1.1
and recall that
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})^{\rm T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is defined in (
1.5
).

i.
We write

, where

,

are the

defined as the unique solution in

of the boundary value problem

(2.3)

\begin{cases}\Delta K_{j}=0&\text{in}\quad\Omega,\\
K_{j}=0&\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\rm D},\\
\mathbf{n}^{\rm w}\cdot\nabla K_{j}=\kappa_{j}&\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\mathrm{w}},\\
\mathbf{n}^{\rm b}\cdot\nabla K_{j}=0&\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\rm b}.\end{cases}

ii.
The
added mass
matrix

is defined as the
3\times 3
matrix with entries
\rho\int_{\Omega}\nabla K_{i}\cdot\nabla K_{j}
, for
1\leq i,j\leq 3
.

Using these two definitions, we can decompose the solution
\phi
to the boundary value problem (
1.2
) with
{\mathcal{V}}^{\rm w}
derived in Appendix

A.5
), namely,

{\mathcal{V}}^{\rm w}=(\dot{\tilde{x}},\dot{\tilde{z}})^{\rm T}-\dot{\theta}r_{G_{\rm eq}}^{\perp}

to write the potential under the form

(2.4)

\phi=\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}+{\mathtt{V}}\cdot{\bf K},

where we recall that

2.2.
Reformulation of Fritz John’s model

According to the decomposition (
2.4
), the quantity
\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}\partial_{t}\phi{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
that appears in Newton’s equation (
1.4
) can be written

\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}\partial_{t}\phi{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}\partial_{t}\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}+\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}(\dot{\mathtt{V}}\cdot{\bf K}){\boldsymbol{\kappa}}.

Recalling that
\kappa_{j}={\bf n}^{\rm w}\cdot\nabla K_{j}
on
\Gamma^{\rm w}
, it follows from Green’s identity and the fact that
\partial_{t}\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}=\partial_{t}\psi=-{\mathtt{g}}\zeta
on
\Gamma^{\rm D}
that

\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}\partial_{t}\phi{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\rho{\mathtt{g}}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\zeta\partial_{z}{\bf K}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a}\dot{\mathtt{V}};

the second component corresponds to the added mass effect, and together with (
2.4
), it allows us to reformulate Fritz John’s model (
1.1
)-(
1.4
) under the form

(2.5)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\zeta-G_{0}\psi={\mathtt{V}}\cdot\partial_{z}{\bf K}&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D}\\
\partial_{t}\psi+{\mathtt{g}}\zeta=0&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D},\end{cases}

with
{\mathtt{V}}
solving the reformulated Newton’s equations

(2.6)

\begin{cases}{\mathtt{X}}^{\prime}&={\mathtt{V}}\\
({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})\dot{\mathtt{V}}&=-\rho{\mathtt{g}}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\zeta\partial_{z}{\bf K}-{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}},\end{cases}

where we recall that
{\mathtt{X}}=(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{z},\theta)^{\rm T}
,
{\mathtt{V}}=(\widetilde{x}^{\prime},\widetilde{z}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})^{\rm T}
,
{\mathcal{M}}=\mbox{diag}({\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{i}})
, while
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
are given by (
1.5
) and
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a}
by Definition
2.3
. The system of equations (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) is the reformulation of F. John’s model that we work with in this article.

Remark 2.4
.

It is straightforward to generalize our analysis to the case where an external force and torque
{\mathcal{F}}^{\rm ext}=((F^{\rm ext})^{\rm T},T^{\rm ext})^{\rm T}
is applied to the system. The second equation in (
2.6
) must then be replaced by

({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})\dot{\mathtt{V}}=-\rho{\mathtt{g}}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\zeta\partial_{z}{\bf K}-{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}+{\mathcal{F}}^{\rm ext}.

2.3.
Hamiltonian structures

Let us introduce the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{V}})
defined as

(2.7)

{\mathcal{H}}(\zeta,\psi,{\mathtt{X}},{\mathtt{V}})=\frac{1}{2}{\mathtt{g}}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi G_{0}\psi+\frac{1}{2\rho}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}\cdot{\mathtt{X}}+\frac{1}{2\rho}({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a}){\mathtt{V}}\cdot{\mathtt{V}};

here, we write by abuse of notation
\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi G_{0}\psi=\langle\psi,G_{0}\psi\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})^{\prime}}
. The mapping
{\mathcal{H}}
is well defined and
{C}^{1}
on
L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and one readily checks that its variational gradient with respect to
(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{V}})
is given in
L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times(\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D}))^{\prime}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
by

\nabla{\mathcal{H}}{(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{V}})}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathtt{g}}\zeta\\
\frac{1}{\rho}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}\\
G_{0}\psi\\
\frac{1}{\rho}({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a}){\mathtt{V}}\end{pmatrix}.

In order to show the (non-canonical) Hamiltonian structure of the equations (
2.5
)-(
2.6
), we need to introduce the skew-symmetric matrix
J
defined as

J=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf 0}_{4\times 4}&-J_{1}\\
J_{1}^{*}&{\bf 0}_{4\times 4}\end{pmatrix}

with

J_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho(\partial_{z}{\bf K})^{\rm T}({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}\\
{\bf 0}_{3\times 1}&\rho({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

and

J_{1}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}1&{\bf 0}_{1\times 3}\\
\rho({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}\ell_{\partial_{z}{\bf K}}&\rho({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

where
\ell_{\partial_{z}{\bf K}}:L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is defined as
\ell_{\partial_{z}{\bf K}}\zeta=\int_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}\zeta\partial_{z}{\bf K}
.
It is now straightforward to check the Hamiltonian structure of the equations.

Proposition 2.5
.

The equations (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) can be equivalently written under the form

\partial t\begin{pmatrix}\zeta\\
{\mathtt{X}}\\
\psi\\
{\mathtt{V}}\end{pmatrix}+J\nabla{\mathcal{H}}{(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{V}})}=0;

in particular, for regular solutions, the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}
is a conserved quantity.

Remark 2.6
.

The Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}
corresponds to the total energy of the fluid-solid system in the linear approximation considered here. Its first two components are obviously positive; the last one is also positive since
{\mathcal{M}}
is a definite positive matrix and
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a}
is a Gram-matrix and is therefore symmetric positive. As seen in Remark
A.3
, the third component is not always positive. However, under the condition

(2.8)

c_{zz}c_{\theta\theta}-c_{z\theta}^{2}>0,

one has
{\mathtt{X}}\cdot{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}\geq c_{0}(\widetilde{z}^{2}+\theta^{2})
for some
c_{0}>0
. From the conservation of the Hamiltonian for regular solutions, it then follows that
|\zeta|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}
,
|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}
,
\widetilde{z}
,
\theta
and
{\mathtt{V}}
remain bounded for all times. This is not necessarily the case if (
2.8
) is not satisfied. Condition (
2.8
) corresponds to the stability condition in the classical static stability of ships
[
61
]
. Even under this condition, the conservation of the Hamiltonian does not imply a control of the vertical displacement
\tilde{x}
.

It was remarked in
[
72
]
with formal variational arguments that the wave-structure interactions should have a canonical Hamiltonian structure. This can be deduced from the above analysis for Fritz John’s model. Indeed, since
-{\mathtt{g}}\zeta=\partial_{t}\psi
on
\Gamma^{\rm D}
, the second equation in (
2.6
) can be written

\big[({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a}){\mathtt{V}}+\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi\partial_{z}{\bf K}\big]^{\prime}=-{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}},

which motivates the introduction of the variable
{\mathtt{W}}:=({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a}){\mathtt{V}}+\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi\partial_{z}{\bf K}
. Using this variable instead of
{\mathtt{V}}
, the model (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) can written
(
1.1
)-(
1.4
) under the form

(2.9)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\zeta-G_{0}\psi=({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}\big({\mathtt{W}}-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi\partial_{z}{\bf K}\big)\cdot\partial_{z}{\bf K}&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D},\\
\partial_{t}\psi+{\mathtt{g}}\zeta=0&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D}\end{cases}

with
{\mathtt{V}}
solving the reformulated Newton’s equations

(2.10)

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathtt{X}}&=({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}\big({\mathtt{W}}-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi\partial_{z}{\bf K}\big)\\
\dot{\mathtt{W}}&=-{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}.\end{cases}

The following proposition shows that (
2.9
)-(
2.10
) has a canonical Hamiltonian structure with canonical conjugate variables
(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
and
(\psi,{\mathtt{W}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
for the Hamiltonian
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{W}})
deduced from

{\mathcal{H}}(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{V}})
by replacing
{\mathtt{V}}=({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}\big({\mathtt{W}}-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi\partial_{z}{\bf K}\big)
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{W}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\mathtt{g}}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi G_{0}\psi+\frac{1}{2\rho}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}\cdot{\mathtt{X}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\rho}({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}\big({\mathtt{W}}-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi\partial_{z}{\bf K}\big)\cdot\big({\mathtt{W}}-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi\partial_{z}{\bf K}\big).

Proposition 2.7
.

The equations (
2.9
)-(
2.10
) can be equivalently written under the form

\partial t\begin{pmatrix}\zeta\\
{\mathtt{X}}\\
\psi\\
{\mathtt{W}}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}{\bf 0}_{4\times 4}&-{\rm Id}_{4\times 4}\\
{\rm Id}_{4\times 4}&{\bf 0}_{4\times 4}\end{pmatrix}\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}{(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{W}})}=0.

in particular, for regular solutions, the Hamiltonian
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
is a conserved quantity.

Remark 2.8
.

i.
In
[
72

is defined as
{\mathtt{W}}={\mathcal{M}}{\mathtt{V}}+\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}(\partial_{t}\phi){\boldsymbol{\kappa}}

3.
Well-posedness of F. John’s model in the energy space and Justification of Cummins equations

In this section, we introduce a semi-normed functional space based on the Hamiltonian introduced in the previous section; this functional setting is well adapted to study the well-posedness of F. John’s model in Subsection
3.2
. Building on this result, we revisit the derivation made by Cummins
[

in Subsection

We have already mentioned the space

in Section

as the range of the trace mapping

. This space was characterized in

, for

and

Definition 3.1
.

Let
I
be a finite interval or a half-line.

i.
We define
\widetilde{L}_{\rm loc}^{1}(I)=\{f\in L_{\rm loc}^{1}(I)\>:\>\exists\tilde{f}\in L_{\rm loc}^{1}(I),\tilde{f}_{|_{I}}=f\}
.

ii.
For
0<r<1
, we define
\dot{H}^{r}(I)
as

\dot{H}^{r}(I)=\{f\in\widetilde{L}_{\rm loc}^{1}(I),|f|_{\dot{H}^{r}(I)}<\infty\}

with

|f|_{\dot{H}^{r}(I)}^{2}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{I}\int_{I}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2r+1}}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y&\mbox{ if }I\mbox{ is a finite interval},\\
\displaystyle\int_{I}\int_{I\cap B(y,1)}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2r+1}}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y&\mbox{ if }I\mbox{ is a half-line}.\end{cases}

iii.
For all

, we just take

.

Remark 3.2
.

i.
When

is a half-line, then

differs from the standard homogeneous semi-norm defined as
|f|_{{H}_{\rm hom}^{r}(I)}^{2}=\int_{I}\int_{I}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2r+1}}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

in the second integral defining the semi-norm. We refer to

for an in depth analysis of screened Sobolev spaces.

ii.
For all

, we can write

, with

and

. The semi-norm of

can be equivalently defined as

|f|_{\dot{H}^{r}(I)}^{2}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\partial_{x}^{j+1}f|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}&\mbox{ if }\sigma=0,\\
\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\partial_{x}^{j+1}f|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}+|\partial_{x}^{k}f|_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}(I)}&\mbox{ if }0<\sigma<1.\end{cases}

Following
[

]
, we also define such homogeneous spaces on domains with various connected components, such as

. The important fact in the definition below is that the semi-norm associated with

relates all the connected components through the constants

is a half-line is of no importance, as shown in

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega
be as in Assumption

with

.
For all

, we then define

\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})=\{f\in\widetilde{L}^{1}_{\rm loc}(\Gamma^{\rm D}),\quad f_{|_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\pm}}}\in\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\mathcal{E}}_{\pm})\},

endowed with the semi-norm

|f|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}=|f|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\mathcal{E}}_{-})}+|f|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\mathcal{E}}_{+})}+|\overline{f}_{+}-\overline{f}_{-}|.

We now show that we may view F. John’s model as the evolution of a skew-symmetric operator in this functional setting.

3.1.
Abstract reformulation of F. John’s model

We show here that there exists a semi–Hilbert space
{\mathbb{X}}
such that F. John’s model (

)-(

) can be reformulated in compact form as

(3.1)

\partial_{t}{\bf U}+{\bf A}{\bf U}=0

with
{\bf U}=(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}}^{\rm T},\psi,{\mathtt{V}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
{\bf A}
an unbounded skew-symmetric operator on
{\mathbb{X}}
. We define
{\mathbb{X}}
as

(3.2)

{\mathbb{X}}=L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3},

endowed with the semi-scalar product

\qquad\langle{\bf U}_{1},{\bf U}_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{X}}=\frac{1}{2}{\mathtt{g}}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathtt{g}}\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\psi_{1}G_{0}\psi_{2}+\frac{1}{2\rho}{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}_{1}\cdot{\mathtt{X}}_{2}+\frac{1}{2\rho}({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a}){\mathtt{V}}_{1}\cdot{\mathtt{V}}_{2},

for all
{\bf U}_{1},{\bf U}_{2}\in{\mathbb{X}}
, and we write
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{X}}
the associated semi-norm; in particular, for all
{\bf U}\in{\mathbb{X}}
, one has
\|{\bf U}\|_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}={\mathcal{H}}({\bf U})
, with
{\mathcal{H}}
the Hamiltonian defined in (
2.7
). According to the comments made in Remark
2.6
, the following assumption is needed to ensure that
\|{\bf U}\|_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
is always positive.

Assumption 3.4
.

The coefficients of the matrix
{\mathcal{C}}
defined in (
1.5
) are such that (
2.8
) is satisfied, namely,

c_{zz}c_{\theta\theta}-c_{z\theta}^{2}>0.

Remark 3.5
.

Even under this assumption,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{X}}
is only a semi-norm; indeed,
\|{\bf U}\|_{\mathbb{X}}=0
if and only if there are constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that
\psi=C_{1}
and
\widetilde{x}=C_{2}
.

We also define the operator
{\bf A}
as

(3.3)

{\bf A}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf 0}_{4\times 4}&A_{1}\\
A_{2}&{\bf 0}_{4\times 4}\end{pmatrix}

with

A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-G_{0}&-\partial_{z}{\bf K}^{\rm T}\\
{\bf 0}_{3\times 1}&-{\rm Id}_{3\times 3}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathtt{g}}&{\bf 0}_{1\times 3}\\
({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}\ell_{\partial_{z}{\bf K}}&({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})^{-1}{\mathcal{C}}\end{pmatrix},

where we recall that
\ell_{\partial_{z}{\bf K}}\zeta=\int_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}\zeta\partial_{z}{\bf K}
.
The following proposition shows that
{\bf A}
is skew-symmetric for the scalar product associated with
{\mathbb{X}}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
{\bf A}
be as defined in (
3.3
).

i.
The equations (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) can be written under the abstract form (
3.1
).

ii.
The domain of
{\bf A}
defined as
D({\bf A})=\{{\bf U}\in{\mathbb{X}},{\bf A}{\bf U}\in{\mathbb{X}}\}
is given by

D({\bf A})={H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\dot{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

iii.
The operator
{\bf A}
is skew-symmetric for the scalar product associated with
{\mathbb{X}}
, that is,

\forall{\bf U}_{1},{\bf U}_{2}\in{D}({\bf A}),\qquad\langle{\bf A}{\bf U}_{1},{\bf U}_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{X}}=-\langle{\bf U}_{1},{\bf A}{\bf U}_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{X}}.

Proof.

The first and third points follow from direct computations. For the second point, the only non-trivial things to check are that

•

(i) If
\zeta\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
then
\int_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}\zeta\partial_{z}{\bf K}
is well defined.

•

(ii) If
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
and
{\mathtt{V}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
are such that
-G_{0}\psi-\partial_{z}{\bf K}\cdot{\mathtt{V}}\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
then
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
.

The first point follows immediately if we can prove that each component
\partial_{z}K_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq 3
of
\partial_{z}{\bf K}
is in
L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. Now, by definition,
K_{j}
solves the mixed boundary value problem (
2.3
), with smooth Neumann data on
\Gamma^{\rm N}
and homogeneous Dirichlet data on
\Gamma^{\rm D}
. Since the angles at the corners between
\Gamma^{\rm N}
and
\Gamma^{\rm D}
are assumed to be strictly smaller than
\pi
, we know by the elliptic regularity theory on corner domains (see for instance
[
22
]
) that
K_{j}\in H^{3/2+\epsilon}(\Omega)
for some
\epsilon>0
. It follows from the trace theorem that
\partial_{z}K_{j}\in H^{\epsilon}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\subset L^{2}((\Gamma^{\rm D}))
, which proves the claim and therefore point (i).

Since we now know that
\partial_{z}{\bf K}\cdot{\mathtt{V}}\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
, we are left to prove that if
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})

is such that
G_{0}\psi\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
then
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. This was proved using Rellich’s identities in Proposition 3.8 of
[
47
]
.
∎

3.2.
Well-posedness of Fritz John’s model in the energy space

We now apply the theory from the previous section to demonstrate the well-posedness of the floating body problem described by (
3.1
) within the energy space defined by (
3.2
).

The following theorem proves the well-posedness in
{\mathbb{X}}
of the initial value problem associated with (
3.1
), with further time regularity if the initial data is in
D({\bf A})
, where we recall that by Proposition
3.6
, one has
D({\bf A})={H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\dot{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
,
\mathbb{X}
defined by (
3.2
), and suppose the coefficients of
\mathcal{C}
satisfy Assumption
3.4
. Then for any
\mathbf{U}^{\rm in}=(\zeta^{\rm in},({\mathtt{X}}^{\rm in})^{\rm T},\psi^{\rm in},({\mathtt{V}}^{\rm in})^{\rm T})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{X}
, there is a unique solution

\mathbf{U}=(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}}^{\rm T},\psi,{\mathtt{V}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}\in C({\mathbb{R}}_{+};\mathbb{X})

to (
3.1
) with initial condition
{\bf U}(0)={\bf U}^{\rm in}
.
Moreover, we have that
{\mathtt{V}}=\dot{X}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, and the solution conserves the energy

(3.4)

\forall\>t\geq 0\quad\|{\bf U}(t)\|_{\mathbb{X}}=\|{\bf U}^{\rm in}\|_{\mathbb{X}}.

If in addition
{\bf U}^{\rm in}\in D({\mathbf{A}})
, then
{\bf U}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};\mathbb{X})
and
{\mathtt{V}}=\dot{X}\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

Remark 3.8
.

i.
We recall that the conserved energy coincides with the Hamiltonian, that is,
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{U})(t)=\|{\bf U}(t)\|_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
.

ii.
By Duhamel’s formula, the theorem can be extended to the non-homogeneous case

(3.5)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}{\bf U}+{\bf A}{\bf U}={\bf F},\\
{\bf U}_{|_{t=0}}={\bf U}^{\rm in}.\end{cases}

with
{\bf F}\in C({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{X}})
; the solution then satisfies the energy estimate

\|{\bf U}(t)\|_{\mathbb{X}}\leq\|{\bf U}^{\rm in}\|_{\mathbb{X}}+\int_{0}^{T}\|{\bf F}(t)\|_{\mathbb{X}}\>{\rm d}t.

This allows for instance to cover the case where an external force is applied to the floating object, see Remark
2.4
.

Proof.

The proof is inspired by Theorem 4 in
[
47
]
; the main difference is that the adherence of zero in the semi-normed space
{\mathbb{X}}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
here, while it was isomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}
in
[
47
]
. This means that there is an underdeterminacy of one additional constant, corresponding to horizontal translations. We mainly focus on this aspect of the proof and only sketch the other points: a priori estimates and a duality argument for the construction of a weak solution, and an approximation argument to deduce the regularity of the solutions. The first step is provided by the lemma, which shows that regular enough solutions satisfy an energy estimate; such solutions are called strong solutions.

Lemma 3.9
.

For any
T>0
, there exists
C>0
such that for all
\mathbf{V}\in H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{X})\cap L^{2}([0,T];D({\bf A}))
there holds, for all
0\leq t\leq T
,

(3.6)

|\mathbf{V}(t)|_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}\leq C\big(|\mathbf{V}(0)|_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}|(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{A})\mathbf{V}(s)|_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}\>\mathrm{d}s\big).

Moreover, if
(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{A})\mathbf{V}=0
then
|\mathbf{V}(t)|_{\mathbb{X}}=|\mathbf{V}(0)|_{\mathbb{X}}
for all
0\leq t\leq T
.

Proof.

Since
{\bf V}
has enough regularity to use the skew-symmetry of the operator
{\bf A}
, this is a direct consequence of the third point of Proposition
3.6
.
∎

The next step is to construct a weak solution to the non-homogeneous initial boundary value problem (
3.5
). This is where we have to lift the underdeterminacy related to horizontal translations.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
T>0
,
{\bf F}\in L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
and
{\bf U}^{\rm in}\in{\mathbb{X}}
. There exist a weak solution
{\bf U}\in L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
to (
3.5
), which also solves (
3.5
) almost everywhere.

Proof.

The space
L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
is not a Hilbert space. The adherence
K
of zero for the
L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
semi-norm consists, by Remark
3.5
, of all
(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}}^{\rm T},\psi,{\mathtt{V}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}\in L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
such that
\zeta,\widetilde{z},\theta,{\mathtt{V}}
vanish identically, while
\psi=C_{1}
and
\widetilde{x}=C_{2}
with
C_{1},C_{2}\in L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{R}})
. This means that if
{\bf V}_{1},{\bf V}_{2}\in L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
are such that
\|{\bf V}_{1}-{\bf V}_{2}\|_{\mathbb{X}}=0
then
{\bf V}_{1}={\bf V}_{2}
up to two functions of time, while in the case of a fixed object considered in
[
47
]
it is up to only one function of time. The quotient space
L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})/K
is a Hilbert space and, as in Lemma 9 in
[
47
]
, one can use a duality method based on the energy estimate of Lemma
3.6
to obtain that there exists
\widetilde{\bf U}\in L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
such that for all
{\bf V}\in H^{1}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})\cap L^{2}([0,T];D({\bf A}))
such that
{\bf V}(T)=0
, there holds

\int_{0}^{T}\langle{\bf F}(t),{\bf V}(t)\rangle_{\mathbb{X}}{\rm d}t+\langle{\bf U}^{\rm in},{\bf V}(0)\rangle_{{\mathbb{X}}}=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\widetilde{\bf U}(t),(-\partial_{t}-{\bf A}){\bf V}(t)\rangle_{{\mathbb{X}}}{\rm d}t.

However, since
L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
is only semi-normed, this does not imply that
\widetilde{U}
solves (
3.5
) almost everywhere; we only get that there is an element
{\bf k}\in K
such that

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bf U}+{\bf A}\widetilde{\bf U}={\bf F}+{\bf k}

almost everywhere. Remark now that for all
\widetilde{\bf k}\in K
, one has
{\bf A}\widetilde{\bf k}=0
and therefore

\partial_{t}(\widetilde{\bf U}+\widetilde{\bf k})+{\bf A}(\widetilde{\bf U}+\widetilde{\bf k})={\bf F}+\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bf k}+{\bf k}

holds almost everywhere. Choosing
\widetilde{\bf k}=\widetilde{\bf k}(0)-\int_{0}^{t}{\bf k}(s){\rm d}s
, with
\widetilde{\bf k}(0)={\bf U}^{\rm in}-\widetilde{\bf U}(0)
(which is constant since
\|{\bf U}^{\rm in}-\widetilde{\bf U}(0)\|_{\mathbb{X}}=0
), the function
{\bf U}=\widetilde{\bf U}+\widetilde{\bf k}
is a weak solution satisfying the conditions of the lemma.
∎

For the conclusion, one can show, using mollifiers in time as in Lemma 10 of
[
47
]
, that a weak solution furnished by Lemma
3.10
is the limit in
C([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
of a sequence of strong solutions, from which one deduces that it is unique, belongs to
C([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
, and satisfies the energy estimate. In order to prove that
{\mathtt{V}}
belongs to
C^{1}
rather than
C^{0}
, we use the second equation in (
2.6
) to observe first that
\dot{\mathtt{V}}\in C^{0}
and therefore
{\mathtt{V}}\in C^{1}
.

The proof of the additional regularity under the assumption that
{\bf U}^{\rm in}\in D({\bf A})
is a particular case of Corollary
4.2
below, and we therefore refer to the proof of this corollary.

∎

3.3.
Derivation and well-posedness of the Cummins equations

In this section, we rigorously derive the Cummins equations
[
17
]
from John’s problem. In doing so, we make explicit the excitation force associated with the wave field and prove the well-posedness of these integro-differential equations by using our result on Fritz John’s model. To be precise, we
introduce the radiative potentials
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\rm rad}=(\zeta^{\rm rad}_{1},\zeta^{\rm rad}_{2},\zeta^{\rm rad}_{3})^{\rm T}\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})^{3})
and
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{\rm rad}=(\psi^{\rm rad}_{1},\psi^{\rm rad}_{2},\psi^{\rm rad}_{3})^{\rm T}\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})^{3})
defined as the solution of the initial value problems

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\zeta^{\rm rad}_{j}-G_{0}\psi^{\rm rad}_{j}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\psi^{\rm rad}_{j}+{\mathtt{g}}\zeta^{\rm rad}_{j}=0\end{cases}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad(\zeta^{\rm rad}_{j},\psi^{\rm rad}_{j})_{|_{t=0}}=(\partial_{z}K_{j},0),

for
j=1,2,3
and where
{\bf K}=(K_{1},K_{2},K_{3})^{\rm T}
are the Kirchhoff potentials introduced in Definition
2.3
. We note that since
\partial_{z}K_{j}\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
(see the proof of Proposition
3.6
), the solution
(\zeta^{\rm rad}_{j},\psi^{\rm rad}_{j})
to the above system is well defined and unique in
C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D}))
by Theorem 4.5 of
[
47
]
, whence our assertion that
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\rm rad}_{j}\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})^{3})
. Similarly, for all
(\zeta^{\rm in},\psi^{\rm in})\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
, we define the exciting wave field
(\zeta^{\rm exc},\psi^{\rm exc})\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D}))
as the solution to

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\zeta^{\rm exc}-G_{0}\psi^{\rm exc}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\psi^{\rm exc}+{\mathtt{g}}\zeta^{\rm exc}=0\end{cases}\quad\mbox{ with }\quad(\zeta^{\rm exc},\psi^{\rm exc})_{|_{t=0}}=(\zeta^{\rm in},\psi^{\rm in}).

Consider now
{\bf U}=(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{V}})\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathbb{X}})
to be the unique solution furnished by Theorem
3.7
to the Cauchy problem associated with (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) and with initial data
{\bf U}^{\rm in}=(\zeta^{\rm in},{\mathtt{X}}^{\rm in},\psi^{\rm in},{\mathtt{V}}^{\rm in})\in{\mathbb{X}}
. With the notations just introduced and using the Duhamel formulation associated with (
4.1
), we can decompose the surface elevation as

(3.7)

\zeta(t,\cdot)=\zeta^{\rm exc}(t,\cdot)+\int_{0}^{t}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\rm rad}(t-\tau)\cdot\dot{\mathtt{X}}(\tau){\rm d}\tau

and plug it into (
2.6
) to obtain the Cummins equation,

(3.8)

({\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm a})\ddot{\mathtt{X}}+\rho{\mathtt{g}}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{K}}(t-\tau)\dot{\mathtt{X}}(\tau){\rm d}\tau+{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}}={\mathcal{F}}^{\rm exc},

where the kernel
{\mathcal{K}}\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathcal{M}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}}))
and the excitation force
{\mathcal{F}}^{\rm exc}\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
are given by

(3.9)

{\mathcal{K}}(t)=\int_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}\partial_{z}{\bf K}(x,0)\otimes{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\rm rad}(t,x){\rm d}x\quad\mbox{ and }\quad{\mathcal{F}}^{\rm exc}(t)=-\rho{\mathtt{g}}\int_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}\zeta^{\rm exc}(t,x)\partial_{z}{\bf K}(x,0){\rm d}x,

Remark 3.11
.

Recalling that by construction
\partial_{z}{\bf K}(x,z=0)={\bf\zeta}^{\rm rad}(t=0,x)
, we can rewrite the kernel as

{\mathcal{K}}(t)=\int_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\rm rad}(0,x)\otimes{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\rm rad}(t,x){\rm d}x;

this shows that for
t\geq 0
small enough, one has
{\mathcal{K}}(t)>0
.

Remark 3.12
.

The original derivation of
[
17
]
was done under the assumption of the fluid being initially at rest, with an arbitrary and unspecified exciting force, and without topography. Here, the exciting force corresponds to the wave field that would be generated by the initial data
\zeta^{\rm in}
and
\psi^{\rm in}
if the solid were fixed, which is the situation considered in
[
47
]
. Our result also generalizes the Cummins equations in the presence of non trivial topography, with a possibly emerging bottom.

The Cummins equations (
3.8
) are integro-differential equations on the position/rotation vector
{\mathtt{X}}
. The following theorem proves its well-posedness.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and suppose the coefficients of
\mathcal{C}
satisfy Assumption
3.4
. Let
(\zeta^{\rm in},\psi^{\rm in})\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
and
{\mathcal{K}}\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathcal{M}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}}))
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm exc}\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
be as defined in (
3.9
). Then for any
{\mathtt{X}}^{\rm in}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
{\mathtt{V}}^{\rm in}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, there exists a unique solution
{\mathtt{X}}\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
to (
3.8
) satisfying the initial conditions
{\mathtt{X}}(0)={\mathtt{X}}^{\rm in}
and
\dot{\mathtt{X}}(0)={\mathtt{V}}^{\rm in}
.

If in addition
(\zeta^{\rm in},\psi^{\rm in})\in H^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
, then
{\mathtt{X}}\in C^{3}({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

Proof.

We have proved in the derivation of the Cummins equations that if
{\bf U}=(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}},\psi,{\mathtt{V}})\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathbb{X}})
solves (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) then
{\mathtt{X}}
solves the Cummins equation. The reverse is true, since if
{\mathtt{X}}
solves the Cummins equation, one just has to take
{\mathtt{V}}=\dot{\mathtt{X}}
,
\zeta
given by (
3.7
) and
\psi
by a similar decomposition formula to obtain a solution to (
2.5
)-(
2.6
). The result therefore follows from Theorem
3.7
.
∎

In general, the solution to the Cummins equation is no more regular than
C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
if
(\zeta^{\rm in},\psi^{\rm in})\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
and
C^{3}({\mathbb{R}}^{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
if
(\zeta^{\rm in},\psi^{\rm in})\in H^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
. This is proved in Section
6
, where we also show that there is a singularity in the Kirchhoff potentials for horizontal translations at higher regularity. We first study the issue of higher order regularity in an abstract functional framework.

4.
Abstract higher order regularity in the general case

In order to study higher order regularity of solutions to (
3.1
), it is convenient to introduce a scale of functional spaces adapted to the problem.
We know by Corollary 2 in
[
47
]
that
G_{0}
admits a self-adjoint realization on
L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
with domain
H^{1}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. Since it is also positive, it admits a square root, denoted
G_{0}^{1/2}
, which is such that
|G_{0}^{1/2}\psi|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}^{2}=\langle\psi,G_{0}\psi\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1/2}\times(\dot{H}^{1/2})^{\prime}}
. Since this latter expression makes sense for
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
, we still use the notation
|G_{0}^{1/2}\psi|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}
for such
\psi
even though they are not necessarily in
L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. More generally, if
n
is an odd integer, we write

\forall n=2l+1,\qquad|G_{0}^{n/2}\psi|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}^{2}=\langle G_{0}^{l}\psi,G_{0}(G_{0}^{l}\psi)\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1/2}\times(\dot{H}^{1/2})^{\prime}},

which is well defined whenever
G_{0}^{l}\psi\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
; by abuse of language, we then say that
G_{0}^{n/2}\psi\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. We can now introduce the appropriate functional spaces.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
.

i.
We define

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})

\displaystyle=\{f\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D}),\quad\forall 1\leq j\leq n,\quad G_{0}^{j/2}f\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})

\displaystyle=L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\cap\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D}),

respectively endowed with their canonical semi-norm and norm.

ii.
The space
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
is defined as

{\mathbb{X}}^{n}={\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{(n+1)/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

and is endowed with the semi-norm

\|{\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}^{n}}=\|{\bf U}\|_{\mathbb{X}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(|G_{0}^{j/2}\zeta|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}+|G_{0}^{(j+1)/2}\psi|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}),

where
{\bf U}=(\zeta,{\mathtt{X}}^{\rm T},\psi,{\mathtt{V}}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
.

We implement the strategy used in
[
47
]
. In particular, we use the energy estimate (
3.4
) to deduce a control on higher-order time derivatives and trade them for higher-order spatial regularity for initial data in
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
. However, due to the motion of the object there is an additional source of singularity compared with the case of a fixed object considered in
[
47
]
. More precisely, the regularity of the Kirchhoff potentials
{\bf K}=(K_{1},K_{2},K_{3})^{\rm T}
introduced in Definition
2.3
is a limitation to the regularity of the solution.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\Omega
, and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and suppose the coefficients of
\mathcal{C}
satisfy Assumption
3.4
. Let also
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and assume that

(\partial_{z}{\bf K})_{|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{(n-1)/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D}).

Then for all
{\bf U}^{\rm in}\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
, there exists a unique solution
{\bf U}\in\cap_{j=0}^{n}C^{j}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{X}}^{n-j})

to (
3.1
) with initial condition
{\bf U}(0)={\bf U}^{\rm in}
,
and one also has
{\mathtt{V}}=\dot{\mathtt{X}}\in C^{n+1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
; moreover, there exists
C>0
independent of
{\bf U}^{\rm in}
such that

\forall t\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{j=0}^{n}\|\partial_{t}^{j}{\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}^{n-j}}(t)+|{\mathtt{V}}^{(n+1)}(t)|\leq C\|{\bf U}^{\rm in}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}^{n}}.

Proof.

It is convenient to isolate the equations satisfied by
U=(\zeta,\psi)^{\rm T}
, namely, (
2.5
), which we can rewrite in compact form as

(4.1)

\qquad\quad\partial_{t}U+AU=F,

with

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-G_{0}\\
{\mathtt{g}}&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad F=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathtt{V}}\cdot\partial_{z}{\bf K}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

We will use the fact for all
U^{\rm in}\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
and
F\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D}))
, there exists a unique solution
U\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D}))
to (
4.1
) with initial value
U^{\rm in}
; this is shown in Theorem 4 of
[
47
]
and can also easily be deduced from the proof of Theorem
3.7
.
Let us also notice that if
U
is a regular solution to (
4.1
), one can use the equation to express time derivatives in terms of space derivatives, so that for all
n\geq 1
, there holds

\partial_{t}^{n}U=(-A)^{n}U+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}(-A)^{k}(\partial_{t}^{n-1-k}F).

In particular, at
t=0
, one should have
\partial_{t}^{n}U=(\partial_{t}^{n}U)^{\rm in}
, with

(\partial_{t}^{n}U)^{\rm in}=(-A)^{n}U^{\rm in}+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}(-A)^{k}(\partial_{t}^{n-1-k}F)_{|_{t=0}}.

We divide the proof of the corollary in two steps. We first prove the time regularity, namely, that
\mathbf{U}\in C^{n}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
and that moreover
{\mathtt{V}}\in C^{n+1}({\mathbb{R}})
. In a second step, we deduce space regularity.

Step 1.
Under the assumptions of the corollary, we prove by a finite induction that for all
0\leq j\leq n
, one has

(4.2)

\mathbf{U}\in C^{j}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad{\mathtt{V}}\in C^{j+1}({\mathbb{R}}).

For
j=0
, this is exactly the result stated in Theorem
3.7
. Let
0\leq j\leq n-1
and assume that (
4.2
) holds for all integers
j^{\prime}\leq j
; we now prove that it also holds for
j+1
. From (
4.2
)
j
, we know that
{\mathtt{V}}\in C^{j+1}({\mathbb{R}})
; since in addition
\partial_{z}{\bf K}\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
(this was established in the proof of Proposition
3.6
) we deduce from the above expression for
F
that
\partial_{t}^{j+1}F\in C([0,T];L^{2}\times\dot{H}^{1/2})
. Under the assumptions made in the statement of the corollary, we also get that
(\partial_{t}^{j+1}U)^{\rm in}\in L^{2}\times\dot{H}^{1/2}
. As explained above, there exists therefore a unique solution
U_{j+1}\in C([0,T];L^{2}\times\dot{H}^{1/2})
to the initial value problem

(4.3)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}U_{j+1}+AU_{j+1}=\partial_{t}^{j+1}F,\\
(U_{j+1})_{|_{t=0}}=(\partial_{t}^{j+1}U)^{\rm in}.\end{cases}

The function
\widetilde{U}
defined as

\widetilde{U}(t,x)=U^{\rm in}(x)+\dots+\frac{1}{(j)!}t^{j}(\partial_{t}^{j}U)^{\rm in}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{j}}{j!}U_{j+1}(s,x){\rm d}s

belongs therefore to
C^{j+1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D}))
. Since moreover
\partial_{t}^{j+1}\widetilde{U}=U_{j+1}
, one deduces after substitution in (
4.3
) that

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}(\partial_{t}^{j+1}\widetilde{U})+A(\partial_{t}^{j+1}\widetilde{U})=\partial_{t}^{j+1}F,\\
(\partial_{t}^{j+1}\widetilde{U})_{|_{t=0}}=(\partial_{t}^{j+1}U)^{\rm in}.\end{cases}

Integrating
j+1
times in time and remarking that by construction all the boundary terms cancel at
t=0
, one gets that
\widetilde{U}
solves the same initial value problem as
U
, so that, by uniqueness,
U=\widetilde{U}
, so that
U\in C^{j+1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\times\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D}))
. The fact that
{\mathtt{V}}\in C^{j+2}
is then obtained as in the proof of Theorem
3.7
, using (
2.6
). This ends the proof of the induction.

Step 2.
Space regularity. From the previous step, we have a solution
\mathbf{U}\in C^{n}([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
to (
3.1
) with initial data
{\bf U}^{\rm in}
. We readily deduce that for all
0\leq j+k\leq n
,
(-{\bf A})^{k}\partial_{t}^{j}{\bf U}\in C([0,T];{\mathbb{X}})
also solves (
3.1
) but with initial data
(-{\bf A})^{j+k}{\bf U}^{\rm in}
, and satisfies the energy estimate, namely,

\forall t\geq 0,\qquad\|(-{\bf A})^{k}\partial_{t}^{j}{\bf U}(t)\|_{\mathbb{X}}=\|(-{\bf A})^{j+k}{\bf U}^{\rm in}\|_{\mathbb{X}}.

Now, from the definition of
{\bf A}
and using the assumption that
(\partial_{z}{\bf K})_{|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{(n-1)/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
, one readily checks that
{\bf A}:{\mathbb{X}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{X}}^{j-1}
is a well defined and continuous operator for all
1\leq m\leq n
. It follows that

(4.4)

\forall t\geq 0,\qquad\|(-{\bf A})^{k}\partial_{t}^{j}{\bf U}(t)\|_{\mathbb{X}}\leq\|(-{\bf A})^{j+k}{\bf U}^{\rm in}\|_{\mathbb{X}}\lesssim\|{\bf U}^{\rm in}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}^{n}}.

In order to obtain the estimate of the corollary, it is therefore enough to prove that

(4.5)

\forall 1\leq k\leq n,\quad\|{\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}^{k}}\lesssim\sum_{0\leq k^{\prime}\leq k}\|(-{\bf A})^{k^{\prime}}{\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}}.

In order to prove this inequality, let us first remark that

\displaystyle\|{\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}^{k}}

\displaystyle=\|{\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}}+\sum_{1\leq k^{\prime}\leq k}\big(|G_{0}^{(k^{\prime}-1)/2}\zeta|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}+|G_{0}^{(k^{\prime}-1)/2}G_{0}\psi|_{L^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|{\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}}+\sum_{1\leq k^{\prime}\leq k}\big(|G_{0}^{(k^{\prime}-1)/2}\zeta|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}+|G_{0}^{(k^{\prime}-1)/2}(G_{0}\psi+\partial_{z}{\bf K}\cdot{\mathtt{V}}|_{L^{2}}\big)+\|\partial_{z}{\bf K}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1/2}}|{\mathtt{V}}|.

Remarking that the first and third components of
(-{\bf A}){\bf U}
are respectively given by
G_{0}\psi+\partial_{z}{\bf K}\cdot{\mathtt{V}}
and
-{\mathtt{g}}\zeta
, this implies that

\|{\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}^{k}}\lesssim\|(-{\bf A}){\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}^{k-1}}+(1+\|\partial_{z}{\bf K}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1/2}})\|{\bf U}\|_{{\mathbb{X}}}.

Iterating this inequality, and using the assumption that
\|\partial_{z}{\bf K}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1/2}}<\infty
, one readily obtains (
4.5
), which concludes the proof of the corollary.

∎

5.
The case of small angles at the contact points

In the previous section, we established higher order regularity, but in the abstract functional space
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
. In general, these spaces are of finite codimension and defined through the cancellation of various linear forms used to define the coefficients of the singular terms in the singular expansion of variational solutions for elliptic equations in corner domains; see for instance
[
20
]
in a different context. When the contact angles are small, the analysis is simpler and we can characterize
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
in terms of more standard Sobolev spaces. To do so, we need some basic properties on the trace mapping
\mathrm{Tr}^{\rm D}:\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
defined in (
2.1
). This is done in Subsection
5.1
. Moreover, we need to revisit elliptic estimates provided in
[
30
,
21
]
for small angles in the homogeneous functional setting, this is done in Subsection
5.2
. The characterization of the spaces
{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}
and
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{(n+1)/2}
, and therefore of
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
, in terms of standard Sobolev spaces is then derived in Subsection
5.3
.
These results are then used in Subsection
5.4
where we show higher order regularity of John’s problem when the contact angles are small. Lastly, we stated the corresponding results for the Cummins equations in Subsection
5.5
.

5.1.
Properties of the trace mapping
\mathrm{Tr}^{\rm D}

We prove here that the restriction to
\dot{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
of the trace mapping
\mathrm{Tr}^{\rm D}:\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
defined in (
2.1
), is continuous with values in
\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
for all
s>1/2
,.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and let
s>1/2
. The trace mapping
\mathrm{Tr}^{\rm D}:F\in\dot{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)\mapsto F_{|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}}\in\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
is well defined and continuous.

Proof.

For
s>1/2
one has by Proposition 5 in
[
47
]
that
\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})=\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\cap\big(\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\mathcal{E}_{-})\times\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\mathcal{E}_{+})\big)
. Since
\mathrm{Tr}^{\rm D}:\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
is well defined and continuous by Theorem 1 in
[
47
]
, and recalling that
|f|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\mathcal{E}_{\pm})}=|\partial_{x}f|_{H^{s-1/2}({\mathcal{E}}_{\pm})}
, the result follows from the estimate
|\partial_{x}F_{|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}}|_{H^{s-1/2}({\mathcal{E}}_{\pm})}\lesssim\|\partial_{x}F\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}
which is a consequence of the trace theorem in standard Sobolev spaces since
s>1/2
.
∎

The fact that the mapping considered in Proposition
5.1
is onto is a direct consequence of the following proposition, whose proof is omitted as it is a straightforward generalization of the proof of Theorem 2.21 in
[
47
]
, which corresponds to the special case
s=0
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and let
s\geq 0
. Then there exist a continuous mapping
\mathbf{E}:\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\rightarrow\dot{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
such
\mathrm{Tr}^{\rm D}\circ\mathbf{E}=\mathrm{Id}_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\rightarrow\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}
.

5.2.
Elliptic estimates for corner domains with small angles

We now present two elliptic regularity results for the problem with mixed boundary conditions and to the Neumann problem in the case of small angles. We extend classical results provided by
[
22
,
30
]
to unbounded domains and, in the case of the mixed problem, we allow for Dirichlet data in
\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
– and not only in the standard Sobolev space
{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and let
s>1/2
. If the angles at each corner point between
\Gamma^{\rm N}
and
\Gamma^{\rm D}
are strictly smaller than
\frac{\pi}{2s}
then for all

\psi\in\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
, the variational solution
\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}
to the mixed boundary value problem (
2.2
) is in
\dot{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
and there is a constant
C>0
independent of
\psi
such that

(5.1)

\frac{1}{C}|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}\leq\|\nabla\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq C|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}.

In particular, one has
G_{0}\psi\in{H}^{s-1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
and here exists another constant
C^{\prime}>0
independent of
\psi
such that

(5.2)

|G_{0}\psi|_{{H}^{s-1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}\leq C^{\prime}|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}.

Remark 5.4
.

The estimate (
5.2
) also holds for
s=1/2
as a consequence of Rellich identity, see Proposition 3.7 in
[
47
]
.

Proof.

The extension
{\bf E}\psi\in\dot{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
of
\psi
furnished by Proposition
5.2
, satisfies by the trace theorem

(5.3)

|\mathbf{n}\cdot(\nabla{\bf E}\psi)_{|_{\Gamma^{\rm N}}}|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm N})}\lesssim\|\nabla{\bf E}\psi\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})},

Then
v=\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}-{\bf E}\psi
solves

\begin{cases}\Delta v=-\Delta{\bf E}\psi&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
v=0&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D}\\
\partial_{\rm n}v=-\partial_{\rm n}{\bf E}\psi&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm N};\end{cases}

since the angles of the contact angles are smaller than
\frac{\pi}{2s}
, we can conclude by Theorem 8.13 and Section 8.C of
[
21
]
(see also Theorem 5.1.3.1 in
[
30
]
) that
v\in H^{s+1}(\Omega)
and that one has

\displaystyle\|v\|_{H^{s+1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla{\bf E}\psi\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}+|\partial_{\rm n}{\bf E}\psi|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm N})}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}.

Together with Proposition
5.1
, this easily implies the second inequality in (
5.1
). The first one follows from the fact that
|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}}=|\partial_{x}\psi|_{H^{s-1/2}}
and the trace theorem applied to the function
\partial_{x}\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}
in standard Sobolev spaces since
s>1/2
.

Finally, (
5.2
) follows directly from the estimate (
5.1
) and the trace theorem.
∎

The next result concerns the reverse inequality, where we show improved regularity of the Dirichlet data through the control of the Dirichlet-Neumann map. A similar result was provided in
[
47
]
for
s=n/2
with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\ast}
(see Proposition 3.10 and proof of Corollary 4.14). However, when
s\geq 1
it was also assumed that
\psi\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
when
\Gamma^{\rm D}
is unbounded. This assumption is removed here.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and let
s\geq 1
. If the angles at each corner point between
\Gamma^{\rm N}
and
\Gamma^{\rm D}
are strictly smaller than
\frac{\pi}{s}
then
for all
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
such that
G_{0}\psi\in H^{s-1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
, one actually has
\psi\in\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
and there holds

(5.4)

|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}\leq C\big(|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}+|G_{0}\psi|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}\big),

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\psi
.

Remark 5.6
.

The case
s=1/2
is proved using a Rellich identity in Proposition 3.8 in
[
47
]
.

Proof of Proposition
5.5
.

We prove the proposition in the configuration of Figure
1
, where
x_{\rm L}
is finite and
x_{\rm R}=\infty
; the adaptation to the other admissible configurations is straightforward.
The proof is based on elliptic estimates for a Neumann problem, and in order to work in a homogeneous functional setting, we need the following Poincaré-type inequality.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
, with
x_{\rm L}>-\infty
and
x_{\rm R}=\infty
. Suppose further,
a>x_{\rm r}
such that
\Gamma^{\rm w}
is located on the half-plane
\{x<a\}
. Let also
s\geq 1
. If
u\in\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(\Omega)
is harmonic then its rescaled vertical average
\widetilde{u}
defined for all
x>a
as

\widetilde{u}(x)=\int_{-1}^{0}u(x,-b(x)z)\>\mathrm{d}z,

satisfies the estimate

(5.5)

\|u-\widetilde{u}\|_{{L^{2}(\Omega\cap\{x>a\})}}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}\widetilde{u}\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega\cap\{x>a\})}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\leq C\|\nabla u\|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Omega)},

for some constant
C>0
independent of
u
.

Proof of Lemma
5.7
.

The first term is bounded by the gradient in
L^{2}(\Omega)
using a simple version of the Poincaré inequality,

\|u-\widetilde{u}\|_{{L^{2}(\Omega\cap\{x>a\})}}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{{L^{2}(\Omega)}}.

For the second term, we use the fact that
u
is harmonic on
\Omega\cap\{x>a\}
to get that the function
u^{\sharp}
defined on
(a,\infty)\times(-1,0)
by
u^{\sharp}(x,z)=u(x,-b(x)z)
satisfies an elliptic equation of the form

\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\sharp}+\partial_{z}(p_{1}(z,b)\cdot\nabla u^{\sharp})+p_{2}(z,b)\cdot\nabla u^{\sharp}=0,

where
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are two vectors satisfying

p_{1}(0,b)=0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|p_{j}\|_{W^{l-1}(\Omega\cap\{x>a\})}\leq C(|b|_{W^{l,\infty}},\inf_{x>a}\frac{1}{b(x)}),

the exact expression of these two vectors being of no importance. Since
\widetilde{u}(x)=\int_{-1}^{0}u^{\sharp}(x,z){\rm d}z
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}\widetilde{u}=\int_{-1}^{0}\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\sharp}=\big[p_{1}(-1,b)(\nabla u^{\sharp})_{|_{z=-1}}\big]-\int_{-1}^{0}p_{2}\cdot\nabla u^{\sharp},

where we used the fact that
p_{1}(0,b)=0
. Using the trace theorem to control the first term of the right-hand side, we get that

\|\partial_{x}^{2}\widetilde{u}\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega\cap\{x>a\})}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla u\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega)},

where we used also the fact that
\|\nabla u^{\sharp}\|_{H^{r}((a,\infty)\times(-1,0))}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{H^{r}(\Omega)}
, with
r=s-1/2,s
.
To conclude with, it follows from the above, and from the fact the that
H^{s-1/2}(\Omega)
is continuously embedded in
H^{s-1}(\Omega)
.
∎

To prove the estimate for
s\geq 1
, it is enough to show that if
\phi
is variational solution of

\begin{cases}\Delta\phi=0&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
\partial_{\rm n}\phi=G_{0}\psi&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D}\\
\partial_{\rm n}\phi=0&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm N},\end{cases}

then it satisfies the following bound

(5.6)

\|\nabla\phi\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\|\nabla\phi\|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Omega)}+|G_{0}\psi|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}\big).

Indeed, since
|G_{0}\psi|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})^{\prime}}\lesssim|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}
, we have that
\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}
, and we may use induction and the interpolation inequality
\|\nabla u\|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega)}^{1/2}\|\nabla u\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}^{1/2}
to find that

\|\nabla\phi\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}+|G_{0}\psi|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}\big),

for
s\geq 1
. Using the trace estimate in Proposition
5.1
, we deduce that
\phi_{|_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}}
is bounded in
\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
by the right-hand side of (
5.4
). Now, we just have to observe that
\phi=\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}+c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
to obtain that
\psi=\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}_{|_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}}=\phi_{|_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}}+c_{0}
also satisfies (
5.4
).

To prove (
5.6
), we introduce a smooth cut-off function
\chi_{+}
such that
\chi_{+}=1
for
x<a
and
\chi_{+}=0
for
x>a+1
, for some
a>x_{\rm r}
. Moreover, to keep control of
\nabla\phi
, we will introduce corrections of the mean. In particular, denoting
\Omega_{a}=\Omega\cap\{x<a+1\}
, we write
\langle\phi\rangle
the average of
\phi
over
\Omega_{a}
,

(5.7)

\langle\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{a}|}\int_{\Omega_{a}}\phi(x,z)\>\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}z,

for
x>a
, we also recall that
\widetilde{\phi}
denotes the rescaled vertical average of
\phi
. We can now decompose
\phi
as

\displaystyle\phi=

\displaystyle\big[\chi_{+}\langle\phi\rangle+(1-\chi_{+})\widetilde{\phi}]+\chi_{+}(\phi-\langle\phi\rangle)+(1-\chi_{+})(\phi-\widetilde{\phi})

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm 0}+\phi_{\rm I}+\phi_{\rm II}.

Step 0.

Estimate on
\phi_{\rm 0}=\chi_{+}\langle\phi\rangle+(1-\chi_{+})\widetilde{\phi}
. We now use that
\phi_{\rm 0}
only depends on the horizontal variable and observe that for
s\geq 1
there holds,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\phi_{\rm 0}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\nabla\phi\|_{{H}^{s-1}(\Omega)}+\|\langle\phi\rangle-\widetilde{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\cap\mathrm{supp}(\partial_{x}\chi_{+}))}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}\widetilde{\phi}\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega\cap\{x>a\})}.

Then using that
\partial_{x}\chi_{+}
is supported in
\{a<x<a+1\}
, the usual Poincaré inequality
\|\langle\phi\rangle-\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{a})}\leq C\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{a})}
, and Lemma
5.7
we find that

\|\nabla\phi_{\rm 0}\|_{{H}^{s}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\nabla\phi\|_{{H}^{s-1/2}(\Omega)}.

Step 1.

Estimate of
\phi_{\rm I}=\chi_{+}(\phi-\langle\phi\rangle)
.
Using the support of
\chi_{+}
, there is a bounded domain
\widetilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega
whose boundary
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}=\Gamma^{\rm D}\cap\{x<a+2\}\cup\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\rm N}
coincides with the boundary of
\Omega
for
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\chi_{+})
and is smooth for
x\in(a+1,a+2)
. Then we have that
\phi_{\rm I}
satisfies

\begin{cases}\Delta\phi_{\rm I}=f_{\rm I}&\mbox{ in }\widetilde{\Omega}\\
\partial_{\rm n}\phi_{\rm I}=\chi_{+}G_{0}\psi&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D}\cap\{x<a+2\},\\
\partial_{\rm n}\phi_{\rm I}=0&\mbox{ on }\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\rm N},\end{cases}

with

f_{\rm I}=-\chi_{+}^{\prime\prime}(\phi-\langle\phi\rangle)-2\chi_{+}^{\prime}\partial_{x}\phi.

On bounded domains with contact angles smaller than
\frac{\pi}{s}
we may again apply
[
30
,
21
]
to find that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\phi_{\rm I}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega_{a})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f_{\rm I}\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega_{a})}+|\chi_{+}G_{0}\psi|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D}\cap\{x<a+2\})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Omega)}+|G_{0}\psi|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})},

where we also used Lemma
5.7
in the last estimate.

Step 2.

Estimate of
\phi_{\rm II}=(1-\chi_{+})(\phi-\widetilde{\phi)}
.
Let
\widetilde{b}
be an extension of
b_{|_{x>a}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\widetilde{b}
never vanishes and is equal to some negative constant
b_{0}
for
x<a-1
. We may extend the domain
\Omega\cap\{x>a\}
to the strip
\mathcal{S}=\{(x,z),\widetilde{b}(x)<z<0\}
. Still denoting by
\phi_{\rm II}
the extension by
0
of
\phi_{\rm II}
to
{\mathcal{S}}
, we have

\begin{cases}\Delta\phi_{\rm II}=f_{\rm II}&\mbox{ in }\mathcal{S}\\
\partial_{\rm n}\phi_{\rm II}=(1-\chi_{+})G_{0}\psi&\mbox{ on }{\mathbb{R}}\times\{0\},\\
\partial_{\rm n}\phi_{\rm II}=0&\mbox{ on }\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\rm b},\end{cases}

with
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\rm b}=\{(x,z),z=\widetilde{b}(x)\}
and

f_{\rm II}=-\chi_{+}^{\prime\prime}(\phi-\widetilde{\phi})-2\chi_{+}^{\prime}\partial_{x}(\phi-\widetilde{\phi})+(1-\chi_{+})\partial_{x}^{2}\widetilde{\phi}.

Applying classical regularity estimates for this elliptic problem on a smooth strip gives

\displaystyle\|\nabla\phi_{\rm II}\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{S})}\lesssim\|f_{\rm II}\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega)}+|(1-\chi_{+})G_{0}\psi|_{H^{s-1/2}({\mathbb{R}})}.

To conclude, we use the support of
\chi_{+}
and we estimate
f_{\rm II}
using Lemma
5.7
to deduce that

\|\nabla\phi_{\rm II}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\nabla\phi\|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Omega)}+|G_{0}\psi|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})},

which concludes the proof of (
5.6
).

∎

5.3.
Characterization of
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
for small angles

The spaces
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
are related, but not always identical, to
\dot{H}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
and
{H}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. They are always the same for
n=1,2
; when
n
is higher, this also remains true if a smallness assumption is made on the contact angles. In general, they are different due to the lack of elliptic regularity in corner domains, see for instance Section
6
when the contact angles are
\pi/2
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma
be as in Assumption
1.1
.

i.
For
n=1,2
, one has
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})=\dot{H}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})={H}^{n/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
.

ii.
If
n\geq 3
and the angles at the contact points between
\Gamma^{\rm N}
and
\Gamma^{\rm D}
are all strictly smaller than
\pi/(n-1)
, then the same conclusion holds.

Remark 5.9
.

In the case
n=3
, one has
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{3/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\subset\dot{H}^{3/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
. However, the reverse inclusion is false. See Section
6.3
below when the contact angles are
\pi/2
. In fact, we will see in the proof below that for
n\geq 3
that there holds
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\subset\dot{H}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
if the angles at the contact points are strictly less than
\frac{2\pi}{n-1}
.

Proof.

For the proof we note that
n=1
is obvious, and the case
n=2
was established in the proof of Proposition
3.6
. By induction it is therefore enough to assume that
\dot{H}^{j/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})=\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{j/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
for
j=1,\dots,n-1
and show that this implies
\dot{H}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})=\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
.

Let us first prove that
\dot{H}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\subset\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
. To that end, we let
\psi\in\dot{H}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
and use (
5.2
):

|G_{0}\psi|_{{H}^{(n-2)/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}\leq C^{\prime}|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})},

for
n\geq 3
and angles strictly less than
\frac{\pi}{n-1}
. Since we have by the induction assumption that
{H}^{(n-2)/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})={\mathcal{H}}^{(n-2)/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
, we also have that
G_{0}^{k/2}(G_{0}\psi)\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
for all
1\leq k\leq n-2
or equivalently that
G_{0}^{k/2}\psi\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
for all
3\leq k\leq n
. Since this property also holds for
k=1,2
, we have proved that
\psi\in\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
.

Next, we turn to the proof of
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})\subset\dot{H}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
. In this case, we suppose
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
such that
G_{0}^{k/2}\psi\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. This implies that
G_{0}^{k/2}(G_{0}\psi)\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
for all
1\leq k\leq n-2
, that is,
G_{0}\psi\in\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{(n-2)/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
and, using the induction assumption,
G_{0}\psi\in\dot{H}^{(n-2)/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
. Since we also get from the case
n=2
that
G_{0}\psi\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
, we deduce that

G_{0}\psi\in{H}^{(n-2)/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
. We can therefore use Proposition
5.5
to conclude that
\psi\in\dot{H}^{n/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
for angles strictly smaller than
\frac{2\pi}{n-1}
. Combining the two inclusions, concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Now, with this proposition at hand we can provide higher regularity result in standard Sobolev spaces for the solution provided by Theorem
3.7
.

6.
The case of right angles at the contact points

We have established in Corollary
4.2
the propagation of the
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
regularity under an assumption on the Kirchhoff potentials. However, it is in general not obvious to translate this regularity into a regularity measured in standard Sobolev spaces. In the previous section, this was done in the case of small angles. We pursue here this program in the case where the contact angles are assumed to be equal to
\pi/2
. In such a configuration, we are able to describe the corner singularities of the harmonic extension
\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}
and to characterize the spaces
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

We derive in Subsection
6.1
new elliptic estimates in the homogeneous functional setting for the harmonic extension
\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}
; in particular, we provide in Subsection
6.1.2
a singularity decomposition of this harmonic extension that could be of independent interest. These results are then used in Subsection
6.2
to establish higher order ellipticity properties of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator
G_{0}
. The space
{\mathcal{H}}^{n/2}
and
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{(n+1)/2}
involved in the definition of
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
can then be easily characterized in Subsection
6.3
. The applications of these results to the space regularity of the solution to F. John’s problem is then proposed in Subsection
6.4
. In particular, the singularity decomposition is used to show the lack of regularity in some cases.

6.1.
Higher order regularity for the mixed problem

We are interested here in the mixed problem (
2.2
) that defines the harmonic extension
\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}
, namely,

(6.1)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}=0\qquad\hskip 8.5359pt\text{in}\quad\Omega,\\
\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}=\psi\qquad\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\rm D},\\
\partial_{n}\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}=0\qquad\hskip 5.69046pt\text{on}\quad\Gamma^{\rm N}.\end{cases}

As already said, it was shown in
[
47
]
that this problem admits variational solutions in
\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)
for data
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
.
Let us now consider a more regular data
\psi\in\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
, with
s\geq 0
and
\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
as defined in Section
3
. Due to the corner singularities of
\Omega
, it is in general false that
\nabla\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\in H^{s}(\Omega)
. Indeed, it is well known
[
10
,
30
,
22
,
31
,
43
]
that singular terms arise in general at the corners. It is possible to impose conditions on the data in order to cancel the coefficients of these singularities, hereby ensuring that
\nabla\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\in H^{s}(\Omega)
, but such conditions are nonlocal and it is difficult to assess whether a given boundary data
\psi
satisfies them (see
[
30
,
21
]
, and
[
18
]
in a related context).

However, the case of contact angles equal to
\pi/2
is a special case, not covered for instance in
[
30
]
. In terms of the Mellin transform, the singularity does not come from the spectral problem but from the lack of injectivity modulo polynomials in the sense of
[
22
]
. We show below with elementary tools that the conditions to remove the singularities can be reduced to simple compatibility conditions. We are also able to provide an explicit description of the singularities when these conditions are not satisfied.

6.1.1.
The case of compatible data

We recall that
{\mathtt{C}}
denotes the set of all finite contact points.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\Omega
be as in Assumption
1.1
and assume moreover that all the angles at the contact points are equal to
\pi/2
. Let
s\geq 0
.

i.
We say that
\psi\in\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
is compatible at order
s
if the following holds for all
{\mathtt{c}}=(x_{\mathtt{c}},0)\in{\mathtt{C}}
,

\begin{cases}\partial_{x}^{2l+1}\psi({\mathtt{c}})=0&\mbox{ for all }l\in{\mathbb{N}}\mbox{ such that }2l+1<s\\
|\cdot-x_{\mathtt{c}}|^{-1/2}\partial_{x}^{s}\psi\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})&\mbox{ if }s\in 2{\mathbb{N}}+1.\end{cases}

ii.
We denote by
\dot{H}^{s+1/2}_{\rm c.c.}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
the set of all
\psi\in\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
that are compatible at order
s
, endowed with the semi-norm

|\psi|_{\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}=\begin{cases}|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}&\mbox{ if }s\not\in 2{\mathbb{N}}+1,\\
|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}+\sum_{{\mathtt{c}}\in{\mathtt{C}}}\big||\cdot-x_{\mathtt{c}}|^{-1/2}\partial_{x}^{s}\psi\big|_{L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}&\mbox{ if }s\in 2{\mathbb{N}}+1.\end{cases}

iii.
We similarly define
{H}^{s+1/2}_{\rm c.c.}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
by replacing
\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
by
{H}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
everywhere in point
ii
.

The proposition below shows that if the data
\psi\in\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
then
\nabla\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}
has the maximal regularity, namely
\nabla\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\in H^{s}(\Omega)
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\Omega
,
\Gamma^{\rm D}
and
\Gamma^{\rm N}
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and assume moreover that all the contact angles are equal to
\pi/2
.
Let
s\geq 0
and
\psi\in\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. Then the variational solution
\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\Omega)
to
(
6.1
) satisfies
\nabla\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\in H^{s}(\Omega)
and there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
\psi
such that

\|\nabla\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq C|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}_{\rm c.c.}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}.

In particular, one has for any
s\geq 1/2
and
G_{0}\psi\in{H}^{s-1/2}_{\rm c.c.}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
that there exists another constant
C^{\prime}>0
independent of
\psi
such that

|G_{0}\psi|_{{H}^{s-1/2}_{\rm c.c.}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}\leq C^{\prime}|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}_{\rm c.c.}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}.

Proof.

The higher regularity result is based on two lemmas. The first one constructs an extension
\psi^{\rm ext}
of
\psi
which is harmonic near the corners and satisfies the Neumann boundary condition on
\Gamma^{\rm N}
near the corners, and such that the mapping
\psi\mapsto\psi^{\rm ext}
is well defined and continuous with respect to homogeneous semi-norms.
The proof of this lemma is based on an extension of
\psi
to the real line and an analysis of the singularities of the Poisson kernel associated with this extension. The proof is postponed to Appendix
B
for the sake of clarity. We denote
\Omega_{x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r}}
the portion of the fluid domain located below the segment
(x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r})\times\{0\}
,

\Omega_{x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r}}=\Omega\cap\{(x,z),x_{\rm l}<x<x_{\rm r}\}.

Lemma 6.3
.

Under the assumptions of the proposition, there exists
h_{1}>0
such that for all
s\geq 0
and
\psi\in\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
, there exists a function
\psi^{\rm ext}\in\dot{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
, harmonic in
\Omega\backslash\overline{\Omega_{x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r}}}
, such that
\psi^{\rm ext}=\psi
on
\Gamma^{\rm D}
and
\partial_{n}\psi^{\rm ext}=0
on
\Gamma^{\rm N}\cap\{z>-h_{1}\}
, and such that

(6.2)

\|\nabla\psi^{\rm ext}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq C|\psi|_{\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})},

for some constant
C
independent of
\psi
.

Having the extension in hand, we will now use it to study
u=\phi-\psi^{\rm ext}
:

(6.3)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=h&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D}\\
\partial_{\rm n}u=g&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm N},\end{cases}

where
h=-\Delta\psi^{\rm ext}
and
g=-\partial_{\rm n}\psi^{\rm ext}
which are both supported away from the corner. In other words, we simply need to prove that the elliptic regularity follows if the data is supported far from the corner. We state this as a general result in the next lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Under the assumptions of the proposition, let
s\geq 1
, and
h\in H^{s-1}(\Omega)
and
g\in H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm N})
. Let also
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be the variational solution of the elliptic boundary value problem

\begin{cases}\Delta u=h&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm D}\\
\partial_{\rm n}u=g&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm N}.\end{cases}

Then if
g
is supported on
\Gamma^{\rm N}\cap\{z<-h_{1}\}
for some
h_{1}>0
and if
h
is supported in
\Omega_{x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r}}:
, one has
u\in H^{s+1}(\Omega)
and moreover

\|u\|_{H^{s+1}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\|h\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega_{x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r}})}+|g|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm N})}),

for some
C>0
independent of
g
and
h
.

Proof of the lemma.

We do the proof in the case where
-\infty<x_{\rm L}
so that
{\mathcal{E}}_{-}
is finite, and
x_{\rm R}=\infty
so that
{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
is infinite. The other configurations are treated similarly. Note also that by interpolation, it is sufficient to prove the lemma for
s=k
, with
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
.

Taking
h_{1}>0
smaller if necessary, we can assume that
\Gamma^{\rm N}\cap\{z>-h_{1}\}
is a union of vertical segments. Let
\theta
be a smooth compactly supported function on
{\mathbb{R}}_{-}
such that
\theta(z)=1
for
-\frac{h_{1}}{2}\leq z\leq 0
and
\theta(z)=0
for

z\leq-h_{1}
, and let
\chi_{-}
be as in the proof of Lemma
6.3
. We can then write

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\theta\chi_{-}u+\theta(1-\chi_{-})u+(1-\theta)u

\displaystyle=:u_{\rm I}+u_{\rm II}+u_{\rm III}.

We prove the three components are in
H^{k+1}(\Omega)
and satisfy the estimate of the lemma. To do so, we proceed by induction and show that if
u\in H^{l}(\Omega)
for some
1\leq l<k
, then the three components are in
u\in H^{l+1}(\Omega)
.

Step 1.

Estimate of
u_{\rm I}
. The function
u_{\rm I}
solves the elliptic boundary value problem

\begin{cases}\Delta u_{\rm I}=h_{\rm I}&\mbox{ in }(x_{\rm L},x_{\rm l})\times(-h_{1},0)\\
u_{\rm I}=0&\mbox{ on }(x_{\rm L},x_{\rm l})\times\{0\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad(x_{\rm L},x_{\rm l})\times\{-h_{1}\}\\
\partial_{\rm n}u_{\rm I}=0&\mbox{ on }\{x_{\rm L}\}\times(-h_{1},0)\cup\{x_{\rm l}\}\times(-h_{1},0),\end{cases}

with

h_{\rm I}=2\theta^{\prime}\partial_{z}u+\theta^{\prime\prime}u,

where we used the fact that
\chi_{-}
is constant and
h
identically vanishes on
\Omega_{-}=\Omega\cap\{x\in{\mathcal{E}}_{-}\}
. Letting
L=x_{\rm l}-x_{\rm L}
, the strategy is to use the Boussinesq symmetrization introduced in Section
B.2
to extend
u_{\rm I}
and
h_{\rm I}
to the periodic strip
{\mathcal{S}}_{-}={\mathbb{R}}/{(2L{\mathbb{Z}})}\times(-h_{1},0)
where the elliptic theory is standard.

The Boussinesq symmetrizations of
u_{\rm I}
and
h_{\rm I}
, respectively denoted by
u_{\rm I}^{\rm B}
and
h_{\rm I}^{\rm B}
are obtained by taking the even reflection of
u_{\rm I}
and
h_{\rm I}
across
\{x=x_{\rm L}\}
and then the
2L
-periodization of the resulting functions. Remark now that on
\{x=x_{\rm l}\}
, one has for all integer
m
such that
2m+1<l-1
,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2m+1}h_{\rm I}

\displaystyle=2\theta^{\prime}\partial_{z}\partial_{x}^{2m+1}u+\theta^{\prime\prime}\partial_{x}^{2m+1}u

\displaystyle=2\theta^{\prime}(-1)^{m}\partial_{z}\partial_{z}^{2m}\partial_{x}u+\theta^{\prime\prime}(-1)^{m}\partial_{z}^{2m}\partial_{x}u

\displaystyle=0,

where we used the fact that
u
is harmonic on
{\mathcal{S}}_{-}
and that
\partial_{\rm n}u=\partial_{x}u=0
on

\{x_{\rm l}\}\times(-H_{1},0)
. We can then deduce as in Section
B.2
that
h_{\rm I}^{\rm B}\in H^{l-1}({\mathcal{S}}_{-})
. Since
u_{\rm I}^{\rm B}
is in
H^{1}({\mathcal{S}}_{-})
and is obviously a variational solution of

\begin{cases}\Delta u_{\rm I}^{\rm B}=h_{\rm I}^{\rm B}&\mbox{ in }{\mathcal{S}}_{-},\\
u_{\rm I}=0&\mbox{ on }{\mathbb{R}}/(2L{\mathbb{Z}})\times\{0\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad{\mathbb{R}}/(2L{\mathbb{Z}})\times\{-h_{1}\},\end{cases}

we can use standard elliptic regularity on this periodic strip to deduce that
u_{\rm I}^{\rm B}\in H^{l+1}({\mathcal{S}}_{-})
and therefore that
u_{\rm I}\in H^{l+1}(x_{\rm L},x_{\rm l})\times(-h_{1},0)
, with the estimate

\|u_{\rm I}\|_{H^{l+1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|u_{\rm I}\|_{H^{l}(\Omega)}.

Step 2.

Estimate of
u_{\rm II}
. Since
u_{\rm I}
solves an elliptic value problem on the half strip, namely,

\begin{cases}\Delta u_{\rm II}=h_{\rm II}&\mbox{ in }(x_{\rm r},\infty)\times(-h_{1},0)\\
u_{\rm II}=0&\mbox{ on }(x_{\rm r},\infty)\times\{0\}\mbox{ and }\quad(x_{\rm r},\infty)\times\{-h_{1}\},\\
\partial_{\rm n}u_{\rm II}=0&\mbox{ on }\{x_{\rm r}\}\times(-h_{1},0),\end{cases}

with
h_{\rm II}=2\theta^{\prime}\partial_{z}u+\theta^{\prime\prime}u
, the method of Step 1 can easily be adapted to get

\|u_{\rm II}\|_{H^{l+1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{l}(\Omega)}.

Step 3
.
Estimate of
u_{\rm III}
. There exists a smooth domain
\widetilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega
whose boundary coincides with the boundary of
\Omega
for
z<-\frac{h_{1}}{2}
and a function
g^{\rm ext}\in H^{k-1/2}(\partial\widetilde{\Omega})
that coincides with
(1-\theta)g
for
z<-\frac{h_{1}}{2}
and is equal to
0
for
z>-h_{1}/2
; the function
u_{\rm III}
then solves a Neumann problem on a smooth domain, namely,

\begin{cases}\Delta u_{\rm III}=h_{\rm III}&\mbox{ in }\widetilde{\Omega}\\
\partial_{\rm n}u_{\rm III}=g^{\rm ext}&\mbox{ on }\partial\widetilde{\Omega},\end{cases}

where
h_{\rm III}=h+2\theta^{\prime}\partial_{z}u+\theta^{\prime\prime}u
is in
H^{l-1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
if
u\in H^{l}(\Omega)
. Using standard elliptic regularity theory and the Poincaré inequality, we deduce that
u_{\rm III}\in H^{l+1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
and therefore in
H^{l+1}({\Omega})
since it identically vanishes for
z>-\frac{h_{1}}{2}
. Moreover, we have that
g^{\rm ext}
satisfies

|g^{\rm ext}|_{H^{l-1/2}(\partial\widetilde{\Omega})}\lesssim|g|_{H^{k-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm N})},

and since
h
is supported in
\Omega_{x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r}}
, one readily gets the estimate

\|u_{\rm III}\|_{H^{l+1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|h\|_{H^{k-1}(\Omega_{x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r}})}+|g|_{H^{k-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm N})}+\|u\|_{H^{l}(\Omega)}.

Step 4
.
Conclusion
. From the three previous step, we conclude that
u\in H^{l+1}(\Omega)
if it is in
H^{l}(\Omega)
, if
1\leq l<k
. Starting from the variational solution (
l=1
), a finite induction and the estimates of the three steps yield the result.
∎

We can now proceed to the proof of the proposition. We make the decomposition
\phi=\psi^{\rm ext}+u
where
u
solves (
6.3
) with
h=-\Delta\psi^{\rm ext}
satisfies the assumption of Lemma
6.4
as well as

\|h\|_{H^{s-1}(\Omega_{x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r}})}\leq\|\nabla\psi^{\rm ext}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)};

since moreover
g=-\partial_{\rm n}\psi^{\rm ext}
we get by a classical trace estimate that

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}\psi^{\rm ext}|_{H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm N})}\lesssim\|\nabla\psi^{\rm ext}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}.

By Lemma
6.4
, we therefore get that for all
s\geq 1
, one has

\|u\|_{H^{s+1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\nabla\psi^{\rm ext}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}.

This estimate is also true for
s=0
which corresponds to the variational estimate; it holds also for
s\in(0,1)
by interpolation. With Lemma
6.3
, we therefore get that for all
s\geq 0
we have

\|u\|_{H^{s+1}(\Omega)}\lesssim|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}_{\rm c.c.}}.

which concludes the proof of the first part of Proposition
6.2
.

For the second part, we recall from Proposition 3.8 in
[
47
]
that it holds in a more general setting when
s=1/2
. For
s>1/2
we use that
\partial_{x}\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}=0
on
\Gamma^{\rm N}\cap\{z>-h_{1}\}
, together with the trace theorem on polygons (see more specifically Theorem 1.5.2.4 in
[
30
]
for the special case of a quadrant) that
G_{0}\psi=(\partial_{z}\psi^{\mathfrak{h}})_{|_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}}
belongs to
{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s-1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
and that
|G_{0}\psi|_{{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s-1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}\lesssim\|\nabla\psi^{\mathfrak{h}}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}
; together with the first point of the proof, this concludes the proof of the proposition.

∎

Remark 6.11
.

One can compute

G_{1}(x)=-\frac{2}{\pi}\big(x\arctan(x)-\frac{1}{2}\ln(x^{2}+1)\big)

and therefore

G_{1}[z](x)=-\frac{2}{\pi}\big(x\arctan(\frac{x}{z})-\frac{1}{2}z\ln((\frac{x}{z})^{2}+1)\big).

Since
G_{-1}(0)=-\frac{1}{\pi}
, the first singular function

takes the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{1}(x,z)

\displaystyle=G_{1}[z](x)-\frac{2}{\pi}z\ln(-z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\big(z\ln(x^{2}+z^{2})-2x\arctan(\frac{x}{z})\big).

Proof.

According to Proposition
6.8
, we can write

We know by Proposition

Proceeding as in the proof of Lemma

{\mathbf{X}}^{{\mathtt{c}}_{\rm r}}_{[2l+1]}-(1-\chi_{-})\exp(z|D|)\chi_{[2l+1]}(\cdot)\in H^{\infty}(\Omega).

We also get from classical arguments (Lemma 5.1.1.1 in
[

]

(1-\sum_{{\mathtt{c}}\in{\mathtt{C}}}\Theta_{\mathtt{c}}){\mathbf{X}}^{{\mathtt{c}}_{\rm r}}_{[2l+1]}\in H^{\infty}(\Omega)
; observing that

(\sum_{{\mathtt{c}}\in{\mathtt{C}}}\Theta_{\mathtt{c}})(1-\chi_{-})=\Theta_{{\mathtt{c}}_{\rm r}}

(6.5)

{\mathbf{X}}^{{\mathtt{c}}_{\rm r}}_{[2l+1]}-\Theta_{{\mathtt{c}}_{\rm r}}\exp(z|D|)\chi_{[2l+1]}(\cdot)\in H^{\infty}(\Omega).

We now need the following lemma; we recall that
{\mathcal{S}}_{2l+1}(x,z)
has been defined in Definition

.

Lemma 6.12
.

Let
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then there exists a function
R_{2l+1}\in C^{\infty}(\{(x,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},z\leq 0\})
such that there holds

\big(\exp(z|D|)\chi_{[2l+1]}\big)(x)={\mathcal{S}}_{2l+1}(x,z)+R_{2l+1}(x,z).

Proof of the lemma.

Recall that for all
z<0
, we have the explicit Fourier transform

{\mathcal{F}}\big[G_{-1}{[z]}(\cdot)\big](\xi)=\exp(z|\xi|),

so that

\exp(z|D|)\chi_{[2l+1]}=G_{-1}{[z]}*\chi_{[2l+1]}.

Remarking that
\partial_{x}^{2l+2}\chi_{[2l+1]}=2\delta+r_{2l+1}
, where
\delta
denotes the Dirac mass and
r_{2l+1}
a smooth compactly supported function,
we obtain that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2l+2}\big(\exp(z|D|)\chi_{[2l+1]}\big)(x)

\displaystyle=(G_{-1}{[z]}(\cdot)*\big(\partial_{x}^{2l+2}\chi_{[2l+1]})\big)(x)

\displaystyle=g(x,z),

where
g(x,z):=2G_{-1}[z](x)+(G_{-1}{[z]}*r_{2l+1})(x)
. Let us now introduce the induction relation for sequences of functions
(a_{2n+1})_{n\geq-1}
defined on the half-line
\{z<0\}
,

(6.6)

a_{-1}(z)=2G_{-1}[z](0),\quad\mbox{ and for all}\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}},\quad a_{2n+1}^{\prime\prime}(z)=-a_{2n-1}(z);

note that if
(a_{2n+1})_{n}
and
(\widetilde{a}_{2n+1})_{n}
are two sequences of functions satisfying (
6.6
), then for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

a_{2n+1}-\widetilde{a}_{2n+1}
is a polynomial in

of order
2n+1
.
We want to prove by induction that there exists a sequence of smooth functions
a_{2k+1}
satisfying (
6.6
) and such that for all
0\leq k\leq l+1
, one has for all
z<0
,

\partial_{x}^{2(l+1-k)}\big(\exp(z|D|)\chi_{[2l+1]}\big)=\sum_{0\leq k^{\prime}<k}a_{2(k-k^{\prime})-1}(z)\frac{x^{2k^{\prime}}}{(2k^{\prime})!}+I^{2k}(g(\cdot,z)),

where we denote by
I
the integration operator defined for all
f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}})
by
I(f)(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f
.
If the above property is true for
k\leq l
, then integrating twice with respect to

and remarking that

\partial_{x}^{2(l-k)+1}\big(\exp(z|D|)\chi_{l}\big)(0)=0
for all
z<0
because
\partial_{x}^{2(l-k)}\big(\exp(z|D|)\chi_{l}\big)
is regular and even, one obtains

(6.7)

\partial_{x}^{2(l-k)}\big(\exp(z|D|)\chi_{l}\big)=\alpha(z)+\sum_{0\leq k^{\prime}<k}a_{2(k-k^{\prime})-1}\frac{x^{2k^{\prime}+2}}{(2k^{\prime}+2)!}+I^{2k+2}(g(\cdot,z)),

where
\alpha(z)=\partial_{x}^{2(l-k)}\big(\exp(z|D|)\chi_{l}\big)(0)
. Since the left-hand side is harmonic, and using the fact that since
g
is also harmonic
and satisfies
\partial_{x}g(0,z)=0

, one has

\Delta[I^{2(k+1)}(g(\cdot,z))](x)=\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}g(0,z),

we obtain after applying the Laplace operator to the above identity that

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k^{\prime}<k}a_{2(k-k^{\prime})-1}\frac{x^{2k^{\prime}}}{(2k^{\prime})!}+\alpha^{\prime\prime}(z)+\sum_{0\leq k^{\prime}<k}a^{\prime\prime}_{2(k-k^{\prime})-1}\frac{x^{2(k^{\prime}+1)}}{(2(k^{\prime}+1))!}+\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}g(0,z).

Recalling that
g(0,z)=2G_{-1}[z](0)=a_{-1}(z)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=-\big[a_{2k-1}+\sum_{1\leq k^{\prime}\leq k}(a_{2(k-k^{\prime})-1}+a^{\prime\prime}_{2(k-k^{\prime})+1})\frac{x^{2(k^{\prime})}}{(2(k^{\prime}))!}\big]

\displaystyle=-a_{2k-1},

since the coefficients

satisfy (
6.6
). It follows that if we set
a_{2k+1}=\alpha
, then (
6.6
) is satisfied up to

. The relation (
6.7
) then shows that the induction assumption is satisfied at

\big(\exp(z|D|)\chi_{[2l+1]}\big)=\sum_{0\leq k^{\prime}\leq l}a_{2(l+1-k^{\prime})-1}\frac{x^{2k^{\prime}}}{(2k^{\prime})!}+G_{2l+1}[z]+I^{2l+2}(G_{[-1]}[z]*r_{2l+1}).

Now, considering successive primitives of the function

, one gets that any solution of the induction relation (

a_{2n+1}(z)=2G_{-1}(0)(-1)^{n+1}\frac{z^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\ln(-z)+p_{2n+1}(z),

for all

,

and where

is a polynomial of order

∎

It follows from (

.

∎

6.2.
Higher order ellipticity of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator

It was proved in
[
47
]
that if
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
is such that
G_{0}\psi\in L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
, then one has
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
, with the estimate

|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{1}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}\leq C\big(|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}+|G_{0}\psi|_{L^{2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}\big).

This result was established through a Rellich identity which does not allow us to extend this ellipticity property to more regular spaces. The following proposition shows that
G_{0}\psi
remains elliptic of order one on
H_{\rm c.c.}^{s-1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})
for all
s\geq 1/2
; the above inequality corresponds to the particular case
s=1/2
.

Proposition 6.13
.

Let
\Omega
,
\Gamma^{\rm D}
and
\Gamma^{\rm N}
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and assume moreover that all the contact angles are equal to
\pi/2
.
Let also
s\geq 1
. For all
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
such that
G_{0}\psi\in H_{\rm c.c.}^{s-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
, one actually has
\psi\in\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
and there holds

|\psi|_{\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}\leq C\big(|\psi|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}+|G_{0}\psi|_{{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s-1/2}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})}\big),

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\psi
.

Proof.

As in proof of Proposition
6.2
, we consider the case where
{\mathcal{E}}_{-}=(x_{\rm L},x_{\rm l})
is finite while
{\mathcal{E}}_{+}=(x_{\rm r},\infty)
is a half-line; the adaptation to the other possible configurations is straightforward. The proof relies on a Neumann extension property and on a regularity result for an elliptic problem with Neumann boundary conditions, with data that vanish near the corners.

The proof of the following extension lemma is postponed to Appendix
B
for the sake of clarity.

Lemma 6.14
.

Under the assumptions of the proposition, there exists
h_{1}>0
such that for all

Under the assumptions of the proposition, there exists

.
Let also

.

Denoting

as in Lemma

\displaystyle u=

\displaystyle\big[\chi_{+}\langle u\rangle+(1-\chi_{+})\widetilde{u}]+\theta\chi_{-}(u-\langle u\rangle)+\theta\chi_{+}(1-\chi_{-})(u-\langle u\rangle)

\displaystyle+(1-\theta)\chi_{+}(u-\langle u\rangle)+(1-\chi_{+})(u-\widetilde{u})

\displaystyle u_{\rm 0}+u_{\rm I}+u_{\rm II}+u_{\rm III}+u_{\rm IV}.

In order to prove that

,

Step 0.

Estimate on

Step 1.

Estimate of

with

h_{\rm I}=2\theta^{\prime}\partial_{z}u+\theta^{\prime\prime}(u-\langle u\rangle),

where we used the fact that
\nabla\cdot{\bf h}=0
on
(x_{\rm L},x_{\rm l})\times(-h_{1},0)
.
We let
L=x_{\rm l}-x_{\rm L}
, and consider as in the proof of Lemma
6.4
the Boussinesq extensions
u_{\rm I}^{\rm B}
and
h_{\rm I}^{\rm B}
(see Section

) of
u_{\rm I}
and
h_{\rm I}

\begin{cases}\Delta u_{\rm I}^{\rm B}=h_{\rm I}^{\rm B}&\mbox{ in }{\mathcal{S}}_{-}\\

For all

can be obtained as follows

\displaystyle\|\nabla\partial_{x}^{l^{\prime}}u^{\rm B}_{\rm I}\|_{L^{2}({\mathcal{S}}_{-})}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{-}}\partial_{x}^{l^{\prime}-1}h_{\rm I}^{\rm B}\partial_{x}^{l^{\prime}+1}u^{\rm B}_{\rm I}\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\Big(\|\partial_{z}\partial_{x}^{l^{\prime}-1}u\|_{L^{2}(R_{-})}+\|\partial_{x}^{l^{\prime}-1}(u-\langle u\rangle)\|_{L^{2}(R_{-})}\Big)\|\partial_{x}^{l^{\prime}+1}u^{\rm B}_{\rm I}\|_{L^{2}({\mathcal{S}}_{-})},

Estimate of

Step 3.

Estimate of

where one can find a regular bounded domain

Step 4.

Estimate of

.
Proceeding as in Step 2 of the proof of Proposition

Step 5.

Conclusion.

We deduce from the previous steps that

From the interpolation inequality

\|\nabla u\|_{H^{l}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{H^{l-1}(\Omega)}+\|\mathbf{h}\|_{H^{l}(\Omega)}.

By a finite induction on

In Section

6.4.
Higher order regularity of the Fritz John’s problem with vertical walls

In Corollary
5.10
we proved a higher regularity of the floating body problem in
\mathbb{X}^{n}
under the assumption that the Kirchhoff’s potentials introduced in Definition
2.3
satisfy
(\partial_{z}\mathbf{K}){|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}}\in\mathcal{H}^{(n-1)/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. In the case of vertical walls, we can use Proposition

to identify

{\mathcal{H}}^{(n-1)/2}
with

, and it possible to check whether this condition is satisfied. It turns out that the situation is different for the different components of
{\bf K}
. In the statement of the following lemma, we use the notation
H^{\infty}_{\rm c.c.}(\Omega):=\bigcap\limits_{n\geq 1}H_{\rm c.c.}^{(n-1)/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
.

Lemma 6.17
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Gamma^{\rm D}
be as in Assumption
1.1
, and assume moreover that all the contact angles are equal to
\pi/2
. Let the Kirchhoff potentials
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
, and
K_{3}
be defined as in Definition

. Then

(\partial_{z}K_{1}){|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}}\notin{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad(\partial_{z}K_{2}){|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}}\in H^{\infty}_{\rm c.c.}(\Omega);

for
K_{3}
, the result depends on the position of the center of mass
G=(x_{G},z_{G})
,

\displaystyle(\partial_{z}K_{3}){|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}}\notin{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\rm D})

\displaystyle\mbox{ if }z_{\rm G}\neq 0,

\displaystyle(\partial_{z}K_{3}){|_{\Gamma^{\rm D}}}\in H^{\infty}_{\rm c.c.}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\mbox{ if }z_{\rm G}\neq 0.

Remark 6.18
.

The proof provides the explicit expression of the singularity of
\partial_{z}K_{1}
near the contact points. This is a great numerical interest to get a better precision on the computation of the right-hand side in Newton’s equation (
2.6
).

Proof.

We first consider the regularity of
K_{2}
. By definition,
K_{2}
is harmonic and vanishes on
{\Gamma}^{\rm D}
, and its normal derivative vanishes on
\Gamma^{\rm b}
. Since the walls are assumed to be vertical near the contact points, one also has
\partial_{\rm n}K_{2}=\pm{\bf e}_{x}\cdot{\bf e}_{z}=0

K_{2}\in H^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and therefore
(\partial_{z}K_{2})_{|_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}}\in H^{\infty}({\Gamma}^{\rm D})

(\partial_{z}K_{2})_{|_{{\Gamma}^{\rm D}}}
is compatible at any order in the sense of Definition
6.1
is then again a consequence from the trace theorem on quadrants (Theorem 1.5.2.4 in
[
30
]
).

For
K_{1}
, since
\partial_{\rm n}K_{1}=\kappa_{1}=\pm 1
on
\Gamma^{\rm w}\cap\{z>-h_{1}\}
, we can decompose
K_{1}
as

K_{1}=K_{1,1}+K_{1,2}+K_{1,3}

with

, with
\chi_{[0]}
as in Notation

and

\partial_{\rm n}K_{1,3}=g&\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{\rm N},\end{cases}

wihere

Let us finally consider the case of

and the situation is the same as for

while

\begin{cases}\Delta K_{3,2}=h&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\

Remark A.2

The domain
\Omega^{\rm w}
is the volume of fluid displaced by the object in the equilibrium configuration, that is, the volume delimited by
\Gamma^{\rm w}
and the horizontal axis

\rho|\Omega^{\rm w}|={\mathfrak{m}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad x_{B}=x_{G_{\rm eq}},

where

The velocity of a point at the boundary of the solid can be expressed in terms of the motion of its center of gravity
{\mathcal{V}}_{G}
and its angular velocity
\omega

A.1.
Velocity of a point of the boundary of the object

We recall that we denote the position at time
t
of the center of gravity of the object by
G(t)=G_{\rm eq}+(\widetilde{x}(t),\widetilde{z}(t))^{\rm T}
, where
\widetilde{x}(t)
and
\widetilde{z}(t)
denote the horizontal and vertical displacement of the center of mass from its equilibrium position. We also denote by
\theta(t)
the rotation of the object with respect to its equilibrium position around the
(Oy)

(Oxz)
plane, we use the anti-trigonometrical orientation for
\theta

M(t)=(X(t),Z(t))^{\rm T}
the position at time

M_{\rm eq}=(X_{\rm eq},Z_{\rm eq})^{\rm T}
at equilibrium, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}X(t)-x_{G}(t)\\
Z(t)-Z_{G}(t)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta(t)-\theta(0))&\sin(\theta(t)-\theta(0))\\
-\sin(\theta(t)-\theta(0))&\cos(\theta(t)-\theta(0))\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X(0)-x_{G}(0)\\
Z(0)-Z_{G}(0)\end{pmatrix}

(A.2)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta(t))&\sin(\theta(t))\\
-\sin(\theta(t))&\cos(\theta(t))\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X_{\rm eq}-x_{G,{\rm eq}}\\
Z_{{\rm eq}}-z_{G,{\rm eq}}\end{pmatrix}.

Time differentiating the first identity, one deduces an expression for the velocity
{\mathcal{V}}^{\rm w}
of a point of the boundary

(A.3)

\forall(x,z)\in\Gamma^{\rm w},\qquad{\mathcal{V}}^{\rm w}(t,x,z)={\mathcal{V}}_{G}(t)-\omega(t){\bf r}_{G(t)}^{\perp}(x,z),

where
{\mathcal{V}}_{G}(t)=(\dot{\widetilde{x}(}t),\dot{\widetilde{z}}(t))^{\rm T}
and
\omega(t)=\dot{\theta}(t)
denote the velocity of the center of mass the angular velocity, while

{\bf r}_{G(t)}(x,z)=(x-x_{G(t)},z-z_{G(t)})^{\rm T}
.

A.2.
Newton’s laws

Both
{\bf U}_{G}
and
\omega
are determined by Newton’s laws; in order to state these laws in the present context, let us note that the resulting force and torque exerted by the atmosphere and the fluid on the object are, respectively

{\bf F}=\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)}(P-P_{\rm atm}){\bf n}^{\rm w}(t){\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad T=-\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)}(P-P_{\rm atm}){\bf r}_{G(t)}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf n}^{\rm w}(t){\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}(t),

where
\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)
is the immersed part of the object at time
t
,
{\bf n}^{\rm w}(t)
the outward unit vector to
\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)
. Newton’s laws then take the following form

\begin{cases}\mathfrak{m}\dot{\mathcal{V}}_{G}&=-\mathfrak{m}{\mathtt{g}}\mathbf{e}_{z}+{\mathbf{F}},\\
\mathfrak{i}\dot{\omega}&=T,\end{cases}

where
{\mathfrak{m}}
is the mass of the object and
{\mathfrak{i}}
its inertia coefficient.

A.3.
Linearized Newton’s laws

Since we are interested in small motions of the object around its equilibrium position, we derive linear approximations of the above formulas. Throughout this section, we write
A\sim B
if
A=B
up to quadratic and higher order terms in
\widetilde{x}
,
\widetilde{z}
and
\theta
. We first get

(A.4)

{\mathcal{V}}^{\rm w}(t,x,z)\sim{\mathcal{V}}_{G}(t)-\omega(t){\bf r}_{G_{\rm eq}}^{\perp}(x,z),

Since the pressure is given by Bernoulli’s law,

-\frac{1}{\rho}(P-P_{\rm atm)}=\partial_{t}\phi+{\mathtt{g}}z,

we can decompose

{\bf F}=-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)}\partial_{t}\phi{\bf n}^{\rm w}(t){\rm d}\Gamma_{j}^{\rm w}(t)-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)}{\mathtt{g}}z{\bf n}^{\rm w}(t){\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}(t),

and a similar decomposition for
T
. In the linear approximation, the integral of the first term can be taken over the position
\Gamma^{\rm w}
of the immersed part of the object at equilibrium,

-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)}\partial_{t}\phi{\bf n}^{\rm w}(t){\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)\sim-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}\partial_{t}\phi{\bf n}^{\rm w}{\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w},

but this is not the case for the second component. In order to describe its contribution, one must derive an approximation of the position of
\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)
in terms of
\widetilde{x}
,
\widetilde{z}
, and
\theta
. Neglecting quadratic and higher order terms in (
A.2
), one gets

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle\sim X_{\rm eq}+\widetilde{x}(t)+\theta(t)(Z_{\rm eq}-z_{G,{\rm eq}}),

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle\sim Z_{\rm eq}+\widetilde{z}(t)-\theta(t)(X_{\rm eq}-x_{G,{\rm eq}});

similarly, one checks that
{\bf n}^{\rm w}(t)\sim{\bf n}^{\rm w}-\theta(t)({\bf n}^{\rm w})^{\perp}
. Therefore, the second component of
{\bf F}
can be approximated as

-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)}{\mathtt{g}}z{\bf n}^{\rm w}(t){\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)\sim-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}{\mathtt{g}}\big[z+\widetilde{z}(t)-\theta(t)(x-x_{G,{\rm eq}})\big]{\bf n}^{\rm w}{\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}+\theta(t)\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}{\mathtt{g}}z({\bf n}^{\rm w})^{\perp}{\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}.

If we denote by
\Omega^{\rm w}
the region delimited by
\Gamma_{j}^{\rm w}
and the horizontal axis
\{z=0\}
, which corresponds to the area of fluid displaced by the solid in the equilibrium configuration, we have by Green’s identity and Archimedes’ law (
A.1
) that

-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)}{\mathtt{g}}z{\bf n}^{\rm w}(t){\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}(t)\sim\big({\mathfrak{m}}{\mathtt{g}}-\rho{\mathtt{g}}(x_{\rm r}-x_{\rm l})\widetilde{z}+\rho{\mathtt{g}(}\int_{x_{\rm l}}^{x_{\rm r}}(x-x_{\rm eq}){\rm d}x)\theta\big){\bf e}_{z}.

From these approximations of the two components of
{\mathbf{F}}
, we infer that

{\mathbf{F}}\sim-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}\partial_{t}\phi{\bf n}^{\rm w}+\big({\mathfrak{m}}{\mathtt{g}}-\rho{\mathtt{g}}(x_{\rm r}-x_{\rm l})\widetilde{z}+\rho{\mathtt{g}(}\int_{x_{\rm l}}^{x_{\rm r}}(x-x_{\rm eq}))\theta\big){\bf e}_{z}.

Proceeding similarly for the torque, we first observe that
{\bf r}_{G(t)}(X,Z)={\bf r}_{G_{\rm eq}}(X_{\rm eq},Z_{\rm eq})-\theta{\bf r}_{G_{\rm eq}}(X_{\rm eq},Z_{\rm eq})^{\perp}
, and therefore
{\bf r}_{G(t)}(X,Z)\cdot{\bf n}^{\rm w}(t)^{\perp}\sim{\bf r}_{G_{\rm eq}}(X_{\rm eq},Z_{\rm eq})\cdot({\bf n}^{\rm w})^{\perp}
, so that

T\sim\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}\partial_{t}\phi{\bf r}_{G}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf n}^{\rm w}{\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}+\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}{\mathtt{g}}\big[z+\widetilde{z}(t)-\theta(t)(x-x_{G,{\rm eq}})\big]{\bf r}_{G}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf n}^{\rm w}{\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}.

Since
\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}z{\bf r}_{G}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf n}^{\rm w}=-\int_{\Omega^{\rm w}}(x-x_{G,{\rm eq}})=|\Omega^{\rm w}|(x_{B}-x_{G_{\rm eq}})=0
by Archimedes’ law, and using Green’s identity, we get

\displaystyle T\sim

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}\partial_{t}\phi{\bf r}_{G}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf n}^{\rm w}{\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}+\rho{\mathtt{g}}(\int_{x_{\rm l}}^{x_{\rm r}}(x-x_{G,{\rm eq}}){\rm d}x)\widetilde{z}

\displaystyle-\rho{\mathtt{g}}\big(\int_{x_{\rm l}}^{x_{\rm r}}(x-x_{G,{\rm eq}})^{2}{\rm d}x+\int_{\Omega^{\rm w}}(z-z_{G,{\rm eq}}){\rm d}x{\rm d}z\big)\theta.

Denoting
{\mathtt{X}}(t)=(\widetilde{x}(t),\widetilde{z}(t),\theta(t))^{\rm T}
and
{\mathtt{V}}(t)=\dot{\mathtt{X}}(t)
and with
\boldsymbol{\kappa}=(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})^{\rm T}
as defined in (
1.5
), the linearized Newton’s equations can therefore be written as

{\mathcal{M}}\dot{\mathtt{V}}=-\rho\int_{\Gamma^{\rm w}}\partial_{t}\phi\boldsymbol{\kappa}{\rm d}\Gamma^{\rm w}-{\mathcal{C}}{\mathtt{X}},

with
{\mathcal{M}}={\rm diag}({\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{i}})
and

{\mathcal{C}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&c_{zz}&-c_{z\theta}\\
0&-c_{\theta z}&c_{\theta\theta}\end{pmatrix},

and

\displaystyle c_{zz}

\displaystyle=\rho{\mathtt{g}}(x_{\rm r}-x_{\rm l}),

\displaystyle c_{z\theta}

\displaystyle=c_{\theta z}=\rho{\mathtt{g}}\int_{x_{\rm l}}^{x_{\rm r}}(x-x_{G,{\rm eg}}){\rm d}x,

\displaystyle c_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=\rho{\mathtt{g}}\int_{x_{\rm l}}^{x_{\rm r}}(x-x_{G,{\rm eq}})^{2}{\rm d}x+{\mathfrak{m}}{\mathtt{g}}(z_{B}-z_{G,{\rm eq}}),

where we used Archimedes’ law and the definition of the center of buoyancy
B
to simplify the expression for

.

Remark A.3
.

i.
If the object is symmetric with respect to the vertical axis passing through

, then
c_{z\theta}=c_{\theta z}=0
.

ii.
Under the condition

the matrix

iii.

the quantity

is called

[
61
]
. The positivity condition of the previous point can therefore be expressed as a condition on the relative position of the meta-center and the vertical coordinate of the center of gravity. If the object is symmetric, it simply states that the meta-center must be above the center of gravity.

Lastly, we recall that
{\mathtt{V}}
are coupled with the fluid equations via

The idea of the proof of Lemma

B.1.
The spaces
\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})
and
\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\mathbb{R}})

For the sake of clarity, we denote
{\mathbb{R}}_{2L}={\mathbb{R}}/{(2L{\mathbb{Z}})}

We can now define

Definition B.3
.

Let

. We define the space

The case of the full line is treated below and is defined via the Fourier transform:

(B.3)

\hskip-28.45274pt\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad\hat{f}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-ix\xi}f(x)\>\mathrm{d}x.

s\in[0,1/2)
. From the previous observation, with
r=s+1/2
, and the definition of
f^{\rm B}
we find that

\displaystyle|f^{\rm B}|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim|f|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(I)}^{2}+\int_{-\delta}^{0}\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{|f^{\rm B}(x)-f^{\rm B}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2s+2}}\>\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\int_{L-\delta}^{L}\int_{L}^{L+\delta}\frac{|f^{\rm B}(x)-f^{\rm B}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2s+2}}\>\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Since the second and third terms of the right-hand side can be treated similarly, we just focus on the second one. Since for all
y\in(-\delta,0)
, one has
f^{\rm B}(y)=f(-y)
and because
(x+y)^{-(2s+2)}\leq(x-y)^{-(2s+2)}
when
x,y\in(0,\delta)
, we can perform the change of variable
y\mapsto-y
in the integral to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-\delta}^{0}\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{|f^{\rm B}(x)-f^{\rm B}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2s+2}}\>\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{|f(x)-f(-y)|^{2}}{|x+y|^{2s+2}}\>\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq|f|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(I)}^{2},

so that we can conclude that
|f^{\rm B}|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})}\lesssim|f|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(I)}
.

Step 2.
Case
s\in[1/2,1)
. We observe for
s=1/2
that

\displaystyle|f^{\rm B}|_{\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})}^{2}=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(nk_{L})^{2}|\widehat{f^{\rm B}}(n)|^{2}=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\widehat{\partial_{x}f^{\mathrm{B}}}(n)|^{2},

so that, by Parseval identity,
|f^{\rm B}|_{\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})}\leq|\partial_{x}f|_{L^{2}(I)}=|f|_{\dot{H}^{1}(I)}
. For
s\in(1/2,1)
we remark that on
(-L,L)
, one has
\partial_{x}f^{\rm B}=(\partial_{x}f)^{\rm odd}
, where
(\partial_{x}f)^{\rm odd}
denotes the
2L
periodization of the odd reflection of
f
with respect to the origin. We then argue, as in the previous step, to see that

\displaystyle|f^{\rm B}|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{2L}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim|f|_{\dot{H}^{s+1/2}(I)}^{2}+\int_{-\delta}^{0}\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{|(\partial_{x}f)^{\rm odd}(x)-(\partial_{x}f)^{\rm odd}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2s+2}}\>\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\int_{L-\delta}^{L}\int_{L}^{L+\delta}\frac{|(\partial_{x}f)^{\rm odd}(x)-(\partial_{x}f)^{\rm odd}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2s+2}}\>\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

For the second term, we find that

\displaystyle\int_{-\delta}^{0}\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{|(\partial_{x}f)^{\rm odd}(x)-(\partial_{x}f)^{\rm odd}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2s+2}}\>\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{|x|^{2(s-1/2)}}|\partial_{x}f|^{2}\>\mathrm{d}x,

and since
0<s-1/2<\frac{1}{2}
we may use Hardy’s inequality (see for instance Theorem 1.4.4.4. in
[
30
]
) to control the last term by
|\partial_{x}f|_{\dot{H}^{s-1/2}(I)}^{2}
. The last term is treated similarly.

Step 3.
Case
s=1
. Arguing as in the previous case, we find that

|f^{\rm B}|_{\dot{H}^{3/2}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})}^{2}\lesssim|\partial_{x}f|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(I)}^{2}+|x^{-\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{x}f|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,\delta])}+|(L-x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{x}f|^{2}_{L^{2}([L-\delta,L])}.

Step 4.
Case
s\in(1,3/2)
. We proceed exactly as in Step 2, but since one now has
\frac{1}{2}<s-1/2<1
, Hardy’s inequality requires that
\partial_{x}f(0)=\partial_{x}f(L)=0
.

Step 5.
Case
s\geq 3/2
. First observe that if
\partial_{x}f(0)=\partial_{x}f(1)=0
then
\partial_{x}^{2}f^{\rm B}=(\partial_{x}^{2}f)^{\rm B}
. For
s=3/2
, one can then easily compute that

\displaystyle|f^{\rm B}|_{\dot{H}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(nk_{L})^{2}(1+(nk_{L})^{2})|\widehat{f^{\rm B}}(n)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\widehat{\partial_{x}f^{\mathrm{B}}}(n)|^{2}+\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\widehat{(\partial_{x}^{2}f)^{\mathrm{B}}}(n)|^{2}.

The first term corresponds to the case
s=1/2
, and for the second one, we can use Parseval’s identity to finally conclude that
|f^{\rm B}|_{\dot{H}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})}\lesssim|\partial_{x}f|_{H^{1}(I)}
.
For
3/2<s<5/2
, the same analysis as in Step 1 can then be performed. For
5/2\leq s<7/2
, one proceeds as in Steps 2, 3, and 4, adding the additional condition on
\partial^{3}_{x}f(0)=\partial^{3}_{x}f(L)=0
for
s\geq 3
. This scheme is repeated for
2k\leq s+1/2<2k+2
for
k\geq 2
.

∎

B.3.
Proof of Lemma
B.1

First, we do the proof in the case where
{\mathcal{E}}_{-}=(x_{\rm L},x_{\rm l})
is finite while
{\mathcal{E}}_{+}=(x_{\rm r},\infty)
is infinite. All the other configurations can be treated with simple adaptations. We also denote by
\psi_{-}
and
\psi_{+}
the restriction of
\psi
on
\mathcal{E}_{-}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
respectively.
Secondly, we construct explicit harmonic extensions of
\psi_{-}
and
\psi_{+}
whose horizontal derivatives vanish on the vertical lines issued from the contact points. These extensions are then used to construct
\psi^{\rm ext}
.

Step 1.

Extension of
\psi_{-}\in\dot{H}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{E}_{-})
, with
s\geq 0
.
Under the assumptions of the lemma, we know from Proposition
B.7
that the Boussinesq extension
\psi_{-}^{\rm B}
belongs to
\dot{H}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})
with
L=x_{\rm l}-x_{\rm L}
. Let
H_{0}>0
be large enough so that
\Omega
is contained in the strip
{\mathbb{R}}\times(-H_{0},0)
, and introduce the periodic strip
{\mathcal{S}}_{-}={\mathbb{R}}_{2L}\times(-H_{0},0)
. Then we define the extension
\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}:{\mathcal{S}}_{-}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
via the Poisson kernel:

\displaystyle\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}(x,z)=\exp(z|\mathrm{D}|)\psi_{-}^{\rm B}(x)=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{z|n|}\widehat{\psi_{-}^{\rm B}}(n)e^{ink_{L}x}.

Clearly,
\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}
is harmonic. Since
\psi_{-}^{\rm B}
is even we have that
\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}
reduces to a cosine series and gives

(B.7)

\partial_{x}\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}=0\mbox{ on }\{x_{\rm L}\}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{-}\cup\{x_{\rm l}\}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{-}.

By classical properties of Poisson kernels, we also have
\|\nabla\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}\|_{H^{s}({\mathcal{S}}_{-})}\lesssim|\psi_{-}^{\rm B}|_{\dot{H}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})}
.
We then infer from Proposition
B.7
that

\|\nabla\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}\|_{H^{s}({\mathcal{S}}_{-})}\lesssim\begin{cases}|\psi_{-}|_{\dot{H}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{E}_{-})}&\mbox{ if }s\not\in 2{\mathbb{N}}+1,\\
|\psi_{-}|_{\dot{H}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{E}_{-})}+|(|x-x_{\rm L}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}+|x-x_{\rm l}|^{-\frac{1}{2}})\partial_{x}^{s}\psi_{-}|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E}_{-})}&\mbox{ if }s\in 2{\mathbb{N}}+1.\end{cases}

Step 2.

Extension of
\psi_{+}\in\dot{H}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}({\mathcal{E}}_{+})
. We define the strip
{\mathcal{S}}_{+}={\mathbb{R}}\times(-H_{0},0)
. Then we define the extension
\psi_{+}^{\rm ext}:{\mathcal{S}}_{+}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as

\displaystyle\psi_{+}^{\rm ext}(x,z)=\exp(z|\mathrm{D}|)\psi_{+}^{\rm B}(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(e^{z|\xi|}\widehat{\psi_{+}^{\rm B}}(\xi)\big)(x).

Clearly, we have by Plancherel’s theorem and Proposition
B.7
that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\psi_{+}^{\rm ext}\|_{H^{s}({\mathcal{S}}_{+})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\psi_{+}^{\rm B}|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\begin{cases}|\psi_{-}|_{\dot{H}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{E}_{-})}&\mbox{ if }s\not\in 2{\mathbb{N}}+1,\\
|\psi_{-}|_{\dot{H}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{E}_{-})}+||x-x_{\rm r}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{x}^{s}\psi_{-}|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{E}_{-})}&\mbox{ if }s\in 2{\mathbb{N}}+1,\end{cases}

while the condition

(B.8)

\partial_{x}\psi_{+}^{\rm ext}=0\mbox{ on }\{x_{\rm r}\}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{-}

can be verified by direct computations.

Step 3.

Extension of
\psi\in\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. We will now glue the two functions above together to construct the final extension on
\Gamma^{\rm D}
. To do so, we introduce a smooth cut-off function
\chi_{-}:[x_{\rm L},\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
\chi_{-}(x)=1
for
x\in\mathcal{E}_{-}
,
\chi_{-}(x)=0
for
x\in{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
. Then define
\psi^{\rm ext}
as

(B.9)

\displaystyle\psi^{\rm ext}(x,z)

\displaystyle=\chi_{-}(x)\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}(x,z)+(1-\chi_{-}(x))\psi_{+}^{\rm ext}(x,z).

Clearly, we have that
\partial_{\rm n}\psi^{\rm ext}=0
on
\Gamma^{\rm N}\cap\{z>-h_{1}\}
for
h_{1}>0
is chosen small to ensure that
\Gamma^{\rm N}\cap\{z>-h_{1}\}
is a union of vertical segments. It therefore only remains to prove (
B.1
). To do so, we add and subtract
c_{1}
which is the average of
\psi_{-}^{\rm B}
over one period and
c_{2}
being the average of
\psi_{+}^{\rm B}
on some finite interval:

(B.10)

\nabla\psi^{\rm ext}={\mathbf{v}}_{\rm I}+{\mathbf{v}}_{\rm II},

with

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}_{\rm I}=

\displaystyle\chi_{-}(x)\nabla\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}+(1-\chi_{-}(x))\nabla\psi_{+}^{\rm ext}-(\nabla\chi_{-})(c_{2}-c_{1}),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}_{\rm II}=

\displaystyle(\nabla\chi_{-})(\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}-c_{1})-(\nabla\chi_{-})(\psi_{+}^{\rm ext}(x,z)-c_{2}).

From Steps 1 and 2, and Definition
3.3
, one directly gets that

(B.11)

\|{\mathbf{v}}_{\rm I}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim|\psi|_{\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}.

For the estimate on
{\mathbf{v}}_{\rm II}
we use that
\nabla\chi_{-}
is supported on
(x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r})
to obtain

\|{\mathbf{v}}_{\rm II}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\psi_{-}^{\rm ext}-c_{1}\|_{H^{s}((x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r})\times(-H_{0},0))}+\|\psi_{+}^{\rm ext}-c_{2}\|_{H^{s}((x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r})\times(-H_{0},0))}.

Both terms are estimated in the same way, where we use the identity

\psi_{j}^{\rm ext}=\psi_{j}^{\rm B}(x)-\int_{z}^{0}\partial_{z}\psi_{j}^{\rm ext}(x,z^{\prime})\>\mathrm{d}z^{\prime},

for
j=1,2
to deduce that

\displaystyle\|\psi_{j}^{\rm ext}-c_{j}\|_{H^{s}((x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r})\times(-H_{0},0))}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\psi_{j}^{\rm B}-c_{j}|_{L^{2}(x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r})}+\|\nabla\psi_{j}^{\rm ext}\|_{H^{s}((x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r})\times(-H_{0},0))},

where we also used the Minkowski inequality. To conclude, we use Theorem 3.5 in
[
53
]
, which states for all finite interval
I
one has

|f-\frac{1}{|I|}\int_{I}f|_{L^{2}(I)}\lesssim|f|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(I)}.

Then, since the constants
c_{j}
appearing in the definition of
\dot{H}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
do not depend on the choice of the interval (see Proposition 3 in
[
47
]
), we get together with Step 1 that

(B.12)

\|{\mathbf{v}}_{\rm II}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim|\psi|_{\dot{H}_{\rm c.c.}^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma^{\rm D})}.

Estimate (
6.2
) therefore follows from (
B.10
), (
B.11
), and (
B.12
). This concludes the proof of Lemma
6.3
.

B.4.
Proof of Lemma
B.2

Let
f_{\pm}
be the restriction of
f
on
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}
. Then we argue as in the proof of Lemma
6.3
, keeping the same notations; the only change is on the nature of the extension of the boundary data, since we now have to deal with non-homogeneous Neumann rather than Dirichlet conditions. As above,
h_{1}>0
is chosen such that
\Gamma^{\rm N}\cap\{z>-h_{1}\}
is a union of vertical segments. We also take
H_{0}
large enough to have
\Omega
strictly included in the strip
{\mathbb{R}}\times(-H_{0},0)
.

Step 1.

Neumann extension of
f_{-}\in H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{E}_{-})
. A function
F_{-}
, harmonic in the periodic strip
{\mathcal{S}}_{-}={\mathbb{R}}_{2L}\times(-H_{0},0)
and such that
\partial_{z}F_{-}=f_{-}
on
\mathcal{E}_{-}
is given by

\displaystyle F_{-}(x,z)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\sinh((z+H_{0})|\mathrm{D}|)}{\cosh(H_{0}|\mathrm{D}|)}\frac{1}{|\mathrm{D}|}f_{1}^{\rm B}\Big)(x).

Also, proceeding as in Step 1 in the proof of Lemma
B.1
in Section
B.3
, we observe that

(B.13)

\partial_{x}F_{-}=0\mbox{ on }\{x_{\rm L}\}\times(-H_{0},0)\cup\{x_{\rm l}\}\times(-H_{0},0),

and that

\displaystyle\|F_{-}\|_{H^{s+1}({\mathcal{S}}_{-})}

\displaystyle\lesssim|f_{-}^{\rm B}|_{{H}^{s-1/2}({\mathbb{R}}_{2L})}

\displaystyle\lesssim|f_{-}|_{{H}^{s-1/2}_{\rm c.c.}({\mathcal{E}}_{-})}.

where we also used Proposition
B.7
and Remark
B.8
for the final estimate.

Step 2.

Neumann extension of
f_{+}\in H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}({\mathcal{E}}_{+})
. The same formula as in the previous step furnishes a function
F_{+}
, harmonic in the strip
{\mathcal{S}}_{+}={\mathbb{R}}\times(-H_{0},0)
, such that
\partial_{z}F_{+}=f_{+}
on
{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
, and that satisfies
\partial_{x}F_{+}=0\mbox{ on }\{x_{\rm r}\}\times(-H_{0},0)
as well as the estimate

\|F_{+}\|_{H^{s+1}({\mathcal{S}}_{-})}\lesssim|f_{+}|_{{H}^{s-1/2}_{\rm c.c.}({\mathcal{E}}_{-})}.

Step 3.

Neumann extension of
f\in H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma^{\rm D})
. As in Step 3 of the proof of Lemma
B.1
, we introduce a smooth cut-off function
\chi_{-}:[x_{\rm L},\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
\chi_{-}(x)=1
for
x\in\mathcal{E}_{-}
,
\chi_{-}(x)=0
for
x\in{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
. We then define
F
as

(B.14)

\displaystyle F(x,z)

\displaystyle=\chi_{-}(x)F_{-}(x,z)+(1-\chi_{-}(x))F_{+}(x,z).

This extension satisfies
\partial_{\rm n}F=0
on
\Gamma^{\rm N}\cap\{z>-h_{1}\}
and by construction there holds

\quad\partial_{z}F=f_{\pm}\quad\text{on}\quad\mathcal{E}_{\pm},

and moreover
\Delta F\in H^{s}(\Omega)
and vanishes outside
\Omega_{x_{\rm l},x_{\rm r}}
.
We are therefore left to prove (
B.2
), which follows easily by the previous two steps.
